Adsorption of toxic metals from greywater using coconut husk biochar and spent green tea
Authors: Abudu Ballu Duwiejuah, Emmanuel Frimpong Adjei, Elliot Haruna Alhassan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe discharge of wastewater into ground and surface waters can cause human and ecological health problems, hence eco-friendly, sustainable and cost-effective methods for removing toxic metals from wastewater are required. The study employed coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea to adsorb cadmium (Cd), chromium (Cr) and lead (Pb) from greywater. The adsorption capacity of coconut husk biochar ranged from 88.70 % to 98.20 % for cadmium, 78 %–96 % for chromium and 95.71 %–99.29 % for lead whilst the spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea ranged from 94.03 % to 96.87 % for cadmium, 52 %–74 % for chromium and 98.52 %–99.48 % for lead. The maximum adsorption capacity (Qe) of coconut husk biochar ranged from 235.64 to 1132.40 mg/g for Cd, 1.31–8.80 mg/g for Cr and 58.85–415.80 mg/g for Pb. Lead demonstrated the highest affinity for the binding of coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea adsorbent with a trend of Pb > Cd > Cr. Coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea adsorbent were suitable for the toxic metals adsorption in the greywater. Cr showed different removal efficiencies where coconut husk biochar showed more effective removal than spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea. The application of coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea in wastewater treatment is a green technology means and can lead to zero pollution of freshwater.Keywords: Chromium, Coconut husk biochar, Eco-friendly adsorbent, Green technology, Greywater

Introduction

The quality of the world's water is being depleted because of the continuous discharge of a variety of organic and inorganic pollutants, including dyes, surfactants and hazardous metals [1]. Also, personal care products and waste from municipalities find their ways into water bodies hence polluting the water bodies and groundwater [2]. Wastewater has the tendency in polluting not only water bodies but underground water and the environment which poses a high risk to the public health [3]. Managing wastewater is one of the social and environmental challenges in developing countries. The contaminants in wastewater have a high degree of persistence and have the potential to change into resistant forms, which are harmful to human health and the environment [4].

Toxic metals toxicity implies the unwanted concentration which is found naturally on earth and becomes concentrated as a result of human activities. This enters into the soil to plant to animals then to human tissue through inhalation, ingestion, and dermal exposure through handling, and can bind to, and interfere with the functioning of the vital cellular component [5]. Consequently, it is important to reduce their concentrations in environment and wastewater.

Greywater can be treated by removing the toxic metals to supplement the available freshwater resources which would benefit humans [6]. Undoubtedly, there are many standard techniques for treating wastewater and eliminating hazardous metals from the environment. Over the years other removal techniques such as activated carbon adsorption, electrocoagulation, ion exchange, precipitation and packed bed filtration have all been used to treat wastewater, but they are very expensive and leave behind a lot of contaminants that are dangerous to both human health and the environment [7]. Hence, adsorption as one of the easiest, cheapest, and less time-consuming will be a better candidate as it is more eco-friendly method for wastewater treatment. In the process of adsorption an adsorbent is usually required. The procedure of adsorption could be a feasible process of treatment, due to its favourable efficiency for toxic metals removal from wastewater and water [8].

Biochar as an adsorbent has many surface functional groups, a porous structure, a large specific surface region, and mineral components, which make it an effective adsorbent for removing toxic metals such as cadmium, chromium and lead from aqueous solutions [9]. Various natural waste product such as coconut husk, rice husk, sawdust, plant origin, and oil palm shell, cork biomass among others which are low-cost adsorbents and have proven to be suitable for wastewater treatment [3]. Beside, the composition of cellulose and lignin as major constituents, the adsorbent from agricultural wastes also have other polar functional groups such as aldehydes, alcohols, carboxylic, ketones, ether and phenolic groups [10]. Electron pair donating process would lead to the toxic metals binding to form complexes with the toxic metal ions in solution [11]. This was evident with coconut husk biochar that showed Hg(II) removal efficiency of 92 % and 98 % of Hg(II) at 1.0 mg/L Hg(II) solution at lower mass of 0.01 and 0.05 g, respectively [12]. For raw coconut husk at contact times of 90–150 min, Cr ions removal percentage ranged from 60 % to 65 % and Ni ions was 80 %–100 % [10].

In Ghana, a large number of individuals drink tea at home, and a large number of vendors sell it in areas where employees can buy it. Waste from drinking tea is simply dumped afterward this add to the environmental issues. The removal of heavy metals from wastewater using adsorption methods and other inexpensive materials has been covered in a number of recent studies. The ability of tea waste to remove heavy metal pollutants has recently increased its popularity. Cellulose, hemicelluloses, lignin, condensed tannins, and structural proteins make up the majority of the insoluble cell walls of tea leaves [13].

Removing toxic metals such as cadmium, chromium and lead from greywater using low-cost adsorbent such as coconut husk and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea would help improve public health and the environment. Coconut shells and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea are abundance in Ghana and are left in open fields or spaces to rot. The application of raw powdered of agricultural waste in systems of water treatment faces challenges due to low mechanical strength and mobility. This challenges can be address through production of biochar from agricultural wastes. Coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea are simple, readily available, cheap, easy and green technology that can easily be adopted and upscale for a practical application in aqueous environment for removal of pollutants. This makes it a better candidate for the adsorption of contaminants. This research therefore explored eco-friendly and low-cost coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea for the toxic contaminants removal from the greywater.

Materials and methods

Greywater sampling Collection

The greywater samples were collected from three sources such as bath taps, laundry facilities, and hair salons in Nyankala Campus of the University for Development Studies and stored in polypropylene bottles. These samples were composited or mixed to have a uniform mixture and used for the experiment. Hand gloves, ice chest, and plastic bottles for sampling of greywater were properly cleaned. The sampling technique employed was purposive sampling.

Coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea Collection and preparation

The coconut husks were collected from farms in the Kumasi Metropolis in the Ashanti Region of Ghana. The coconut husks were removed manually from the fruits, cleaned from external materials and weighed. Weighted husks were into earthen pots and kept in the muffle furnace for the pyrolysis at 350 °C for 30 min pyrolysis time. The process was continued still 1 kg of the coconut husk biochar was obtained. The coconut husk biochar was crushed and sieved with 2 mm standard sieve.

Spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea were gathered from tea shops and restaurants and cleansed. This was done to guarantee that the residual solution turns colourless. Spent tea is likely to contain some colorants, tannins, proteins, and polysaccharides [2]. The idea behind washing them was to get rid of any contaminants, following colour removal, the item was left to air dry for 24 h. It was now prepared for use as an adsorbent. Orhevba et al. [14] assert that the removal effectiveness of tea wastes depends on the size of the adsorbent particle and the readily available active sites.

Batch experiment for coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea

The greywater using glass funnel and Whatman's qualitative filter paper (125 mm) was filtered and then stored in a sampling bottle. The initial quantities of cadmium, chromium, and lead were then measured. The concentrations of the metals in greywater samples were 6.00 mg/L for cadmium, 0.05 mg/L for chromium and 2.10 mg/L for lead, and 100 mL of greywater and 0.50 g dosage of coconut husk biochar was taken in conical flask which was shaken via a rotatory orbital shaker at for 1 h. Similar experiment was carried out for cadmium, chromium and lead at toxic metal concentrations of 6.00 mg/L, 0.05 mg/L and 2.10 mg/L at 100 mL of greywater at 1.00 g, 1.50 g, 2.00 g, 2.50 g, 3.00 g and 3.50 g of coconut husk biochar was shaken via a rotatory orbital shaker at for 1 h, respectively.

Similar experiments were carried out using spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea as affordable adsorbent. The concentrations of the toxic metals in greywater samples were 6.00 mg/L, 0.05 mg/L and 2.10 mg/L for cadmium, chromium and lead, respectively, and 100 mL of greywater and a dosage of 0.50 g of spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea adsorbent was taken in conical flask was shaken via a rotatory orbital shaker at for 1 h. Similar experiment was carried out for cadmium, chromium and lead at metal concentrations of 6.00 mg/L, 0.05 mg/L and 2.10 mg/L, respectively at 100 mL of greywater at 1.00 g, 1.50 g, 2.00 g, 2.50 g, 3.00 g and 3.50 g of spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea adsorbent was shaken via a rotatory orbital shaker at for 1 h. All the adsorption experiments were performed at a pH range of 6.63 and 6.67 at a temperature of 25 °C. The elutes were delivered to the Ecological Laboratory of the University of Ghana for analysis using an Atomic Absorption Spectrophotometer (AAS) by PerkinElmer PIN Accle 900T GRAPHITE (Waltham, United States of America).

Results and discussion

Removal efficiency of metals by coconut husk biochar

Coconut husk biochar adsorption efficiency of cadmium at a concentration of 6.00 mg/L of the greywater ranged from 88.70 % to 98.20 %. Chromium at a concentration of 0.05 mg/L of the greywater was adsorbed by coconut husk biochar that ranged from 78 % to 96 %. Lead at a concentration of 2.10 mg/L of the greywater was adsorbed by coconut husk biochar that ranged from 95.71 % to 99.29 % (Table 1). Coconut husk biochar had a good affinity for Cd, Cr and Pb. In this experiment, coconut husk biochar in the greywater removed cadmium quite effectively at the various doses. The surface area of the biomass and the number of possible binding sites increased as the adsorbent dosage was increased [18]. The rate of lead removal was tested at concentrations of 0.50, 1.00 and 2.00 g of adsorbent, and the uptake increased with increasing concentrations of the adsorbent material [19]. According to Ravulapalli and Kunta [20], the rate of removal of Pb peaked at 99.20 % at 0.20 g, and then remained steady as the amount of adsorbent increased. The adsorption capacity gradually grew until it peaked at 1.30 mg/g at 0.15 g, after which it gradually dropped. At low dosages, Pb in the solution fully used the adsorbent sites, increasing the adsorption capacity and improving the removal rate. The adsorption equilibrium between the modified biochar and the solution was reached after the amount of adsorbent was sufficiently increased. As a result, the adsorption capacity declined as the adsorbent dose increased but the adsorption rate remained consistent [21].

In this experiment, coconut husk biochar in the greywater removed cadmium quite effectively at doses of 0.50, 1.00, 1.50, 2.00, 2.50, 3.00 and 3.50 g. According to Regmi et al. [22], the dosage response for cadmium was examined by changing the concentration of the adsorbent HTC biochar from 0.10 g/L to 2.00 g/L. The temperature, agitation, beginning 40 mg/L metal concentration, pH level of 5, contact time of 24 h, and all other parameters were held constant. The amount of cadmium that could be absorbed increased as the solid to solution ratio was upsurged due to the coconut husk biochar vacant adsorbent sites. This was evident as similar coconut husk biochar showed Hg(II) removal efficiency of 92 % and 98 % of Hg(II) at 1.0 mg/L Hg(II) solution at lower mass of 0.01 and 0.05 g, respectively [12]. Electrostatic interaction will usually occur between cadmium, chromium and lead and the coconut husk biochar structure and composition. Activated coconut husk biochar showed the highest level of cadmium adsorption at 1.00 g/L and 2.00 g/L, the metal was eliminated to levels of around 80 % and almost 100 %, respectively [23]. Also, Cr ions removal percentage ranged from 60 % to 65 % and Ni ions was 80 %–100 % for raw coconut husk at contact times of 90–150 min [10].

Removal efficiency of metals by spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea

The adsorption efficiency of cadmium at a concentration of 6.0 mg/l of the greywater by spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea ranged from 94.03 % to 96.87 % (Table 1). The adsorption efficiency of chromium at a concentration of 0.05 mg/l of the greywater by spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea ranged from 52 % to 74 % (Table 1). The adsorption efficiency of lead at a concentration of 2.1 mg/l of the greywater by spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea ranged from 98.52 % to 99.48 % (Table 1). Spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea had a good and robust affinity for Cd, Cr and Pb in this experiment. In this experiment, spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea in the greywater removed cadmium quite effectively at various doses of adsorbents. As the adsorbent concentration rises, more adsorbent particles surround the metal ions or the fraction of adsorbent particles to metal ions rises; as a result, more ions are attracted to the surfaces of these particles [24]. According to Waseem et al. [25], it is evident that adsorption increases as the mass of the sorbent increases. When the concentration of tea waste was increased from 5 to 15 g/L, chromium's adsorption capacity decreased from 48.82 mg/g with 61 % removal to 23.51 mg/g with 88 % removal. This is because the sorbent's greater surface area makes more adsorption sites available at higher doses, resulting in a higher removal of chromium [26].

Comparatively, coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea exhibited similar effectiveness in the removal efficiency for Cd, Cr and Pb in the graywater. Cr showed different removal efficiencies where coconut husk biochar showed more effective removal than spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea. This could be attributable to the chemistry of Cr and the nature of biomass. The spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea showed a bit of high efficiencies towards Cd and Pb compare to coconut husk biochar. This could be attributable to the chemistry of Cd and Pb and the nature of biomass. The residues of coconut husk biochar have huge surface areas and superior mechanical strengths. This experimental investigation supports the establishment of treatment facilities using coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea at the community or household levels in nations.

Langmuir adsorption isotherm of coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea

The experiment employed Langmuir adsorption isotherm (Fig. 1, Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6) to calculate the toxic metals' ability to bind onto the coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea in the greywater. Zhang et al. [27] found that using the Langmuir isotherms model to eliminate a gas molecule onto a corresponding solid surface was the best method. Langmuir isotherms are most commonly utilised in the adsorption of atoms onto a uniform surface, which could be a crystal or a solid having adsorptive qualities [28]. The isotherms are mostly utilised to investigate and appraise the maximum capacity of toxic metal adsorption as well as the type of metal-adsorbent interaction [29]. The maximum adsorption capacity (mg/g) onto the coconut husk biochar ranged from −3.21 mg/g to 1.71 × 10−3 mg/g (Table 2). RL for Cd was 3.24, Cr was 0.99 and Pb was 3.25 by the coconut husk biochar (Table 2). For the three metals in the experiment, Qmax (mg/g) which is the maximum adsorption capacity of spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea ranged from −1.50 × 10−4 mg/g to 2.70 × 10−3 mg/g (Table 2). RL for Cd was −1.10, Cr was 0.99 and Pb was 1.10 by spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea. R2 values for Langmuir adsorption isotherm for the spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea for cadmium, chromium and lead ions were 0.0117, 0.8446 and 0.0946, respectively (Fig. 4, Fig. 5, Fig. 6). The Langmuir adsorption, according to Guo and Wang [30], should take place in a single layer with no connections and no steric hindrance between molecules. This is due to the fact that as the distance from the adsorbent surface rises, the intermolecular forces rapidly decrease.

The Langmuir isotherm is used when the adsorption process is monolayered with a fixed number of sites [16]. This type and nature of adsorption take place on the surface of the adsorbent were ascertained using this model. Langmuir constant reflects how much the adsorbent interacts with the adsorbate. A bigger constant value suggests a strong association between the adsorbent and the adsorbate whilst a smaller value shows a weak interaction [31]. All R2 values for the Langmuir and Freundlich isotherms had an R2 value more than 0.97, according to a related study by Xie et al. [32]. For the adsorption of tea waste, Cd, Pb, and Cr all fit the Langmuir isotherm model while Cr fit the Freundlich isotherm model. Pb showed a higher maximum adsorption capacity than Cr and Cd. In a comparable work, Xie et al. [32] determined that the maximum adsorption capacities for Pb2+, Cd2+, and Cu2+ under ideal conditions were 33.49, 16.87, and 0.25, respectively.

Freundlich adsorption isotherm of coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea

The suitability of the experiments with coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea adsorbent surface sites which are heterogeneous and the toxic metals adsorption was evaluated using the Freundlich adsorption isotherm (Fig. 7, Fig. 8, Fig. 9, Fig. 10, Fig. 11, Fig. 12). In the gas phase, this isotherm often fits processes of adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces [17]. High enough pressure leads in the infinite limit in the Freundlich adsorption isotherm, which implies it does not suit a large variety of data from adsorption tests [17]. The 1/n values of coconut husk biochar attained for toxic metals were −0.59, 0.10 and −0.03 for Cd, Cr and Pb, respectively (Table 2). N values of coconut husk biochar for toxic metals ranged from −35.46 to 9.84 (Table 2). KF (L/mg) values of coconut husk biochar for all the experiments ranged from 4.58 to 711.23 (Table 2). Freundlich isotherm correlation coefficient of coconut husk biochar for the toxic metals ranged from 0.0028 to 0.1183. The 1/n values obtained for spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea adsorbent were −0.11, 2.50 and 0.52 for Cd, Cr and Pb, respectively (Table 2). N values for spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea adsorbent for the metals ranged from −9.40 to 0.41 (Table 2). KF (mg/g) values of spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea adsorbent for all the experiments ranged from 18.16 to 45258.50 (Table 2). Freundlich adsorption isotherm correlation coefficient values of spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea adsorbent for metals ranged from 0.0014 to 0.7734 (Fig. 10, Fig. 11, Fig. 12).

The adsorption process between the biochar adsorbents made from coconut husk and tea waste and the hazardous metals under research (Cd, Cr and Pb) was described using the Freundlich adsorption isotherm. The 1/n for Cd and Cr were above unity, indicating that biochar made from coconut husk had cooperative adsorption. The fact that chromium's1/n was greater than unity indicates that tea waste was used in cooperative adsorption. Cadmium and lead had 1/n values that were lower than unity, indicating that metal ions first occupied sites with higher energies before moving on to sites with lower energies during adsorption on heterogeneous surfaces. Cd and Cr fit the Freundlich isotherm model whilst Pb fit the Langmuir isotherm model for spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea adsorption.

Effect of pH of the greywater on the removal efficiency of coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea

The pH of the wastewater has a significant impact on the adsorbents' surface charge and the degree to which different toxins ionise during the remediation process when using both unconventional and common adsorbents. In the process of removing hazardous metals from wastewater, pH is crucial. For all the hazardous metals under consideration, the pH at which the studies were carried out demonstrated extremely high removal efficiencies. When conducting sorption experiments, the impact of pH is a crucial element that is extensively researched [33]. The pH plays a significant role in the sorption of a specific proportion of chromium from an aqueous medium using sorbents and sorption. Additionally, a crucial factor in affecting adsorption effectiveness is temperature. High temperatures increase the mobility of metal ions [34]. The presence of a large number of hydroxyl ions in aqueous solutions at higher pH values acts as a barrier between the negatively charged surface of biochar and ions with a negative charge, limiting the sorption of chromium [35].

Conclusion

The low-cost coconut husk biochar was able to effectively remove the metals from the greywater. Coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea were suitable for the adsorption effect of toxic metals in the greywater. Cr showed different removal efficiencies where coconut husk biochar showed more effective removal than spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea. This could be attributable to the chemistry of Cr and the nature of biomass. The spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea in the other hand showed a bit of high efficiencies towards Cd and Pb compare to coconut husk biochar. This could be attributable to the chemistry of Cd and Pb and the nature of biomass. This experimental investigation supports the establishment of treatment facilities using coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea at the community or household levels in nations. The application of coconut husk biochar and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea in treatment of wastewater is a means of green technology and can lead to zero pollution of freshwater. Toxic metals in wastewater and greywater can be removed using biochar made from coconut husks and and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea. Further research should be conducted using the fast pyrolysis of the coconut husk biochar and and spent impra ginseng flavoured green tea to assess the adsorption efficiency of toxic metals in greywater.
