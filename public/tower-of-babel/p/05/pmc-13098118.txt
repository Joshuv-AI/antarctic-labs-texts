Variability in Perceived Healthcare Data Quality Across Tanzanian Regional Referral Hospitals: A Hospital‐Based Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Rogate Phinias, Mikidadi Muhanga, Joshua Malago
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimHealthcare data quality is critical for informed decision‐making and effective healthcare management. However, variability in perceived data quality exists across different healthcare facilities. This study aimed to explore the differences in healthcare data quality perceptions among healthcare workers across four dimensions: data accuracy, completeness, timeliness, and consistency.MethodsBetween August and October 2024, we conducted a cross‑sectional survey of 336 healthcare workers, selected via simple random sampling from the eight RRHs. Participants completed a validated questionnaire (Cronbach's α ≥ 0.83 across dimensions). We used descriptive statistics to summarize mean scores by hospital, and dimension scores were analyzed using one‑way ANOVA, with partial η² as an effect‑size measure and Tukey HSD post‑hoc tests to identify specific inter‑hospital differences.ResultsSignificant hospital‑level differences emerged for accuracy (F(7, 328) = 5.03, p = 0.026, η² = 0.097), completeness (F(7, 328) = 8.65, p < 0.001, η² = 0.156), timeliness (F(7, 328) = 6.07, p = 0.006, η² = 0.115), and consistency (F(7, 328) = 5.41, p < 0.001, η² = 0.103). Post‑hoc analyses showed that H8 and H2 consistently outscored other hospitals: both led on completeness (vs. all peers), and both exceeded H6 (the lowest‑rated) on accuracy, timeliness, and consistency.ConclusionPerceptions of data quality vary markedly across RRHs in Tanzania, with medium‑to‑large effect sizes indicating substantive differences. However, the cross‑sectional, perception‑based design precludes causal inference and lacks objective data‐quality verification. Future work should integrate objective metrics (e.g., audit logs, error rates) and apply a guiding framework such as Donabedian's structure–process–outcome to identify the facility‑level determinants driving these perceptual gaps and their impact on patient‑care outcomes.

Introduction

Healthcare data quality is a fundamental pillar in the effective functioning of healthcare systems worldwide [1]. The quality of healthcare data directly impacts patient management, health service planning, resource allocation, and evidence‐based policymaking [2]. As healthcare systems face increasing demands, the need for accurate, complete, consistent, and timely data has never been more critical. This is particularly true for Regional Referral Hospitals (RRHs), which serve as key referral centers for a large population and are tasked with managing complex patient information across diverse healthcare services [3, 4, 5, 6]. These hospitals often operate at the intersection of local and national healthcare needs, thus presenting unique challenges for data management. High‐quality healthcare data is essential not only for improving patient care but also for ensuring the efficient allocation of resources, optimizing healthcare delivery, and supporting informed clinical decision‐making [7].

Despite its importance, maintaining high‐quality data remains a significant challenge in healthcare settings in developing countries. Inaccuracies in documentation, incomplete reporting, delayed updates, and inconsistencies in record‐keeping are common issues that can severely undermine the integrity of healthcare data [8, 9, 10]. These data quality issues can lead to misguided clinical decisions, delayed interventions, and inefficiencies in hospital management [11, 12]. Researchers and policymakers typically rely on four key dimensions to assess healthcare data quality: accuracy, ensuring that data correctly reflects real‐world events; completeness, verifying that all required data elements are fully recorded; timeliness, ensuring that data is recorded and updated within an appropriate time frame to support real‐time decision‐making; and consistency, ensuring uniformity and coherence of data across various hospital records, reports, and databases [13, 14].

These data quality dimensions, while well‐established, highlight the complexity of managing healthcare data in diverse and often resource‐constrained settings. In Tanzania, RRHs, in particular, face unique challenges as they frequently serve as the highest‐level healthcare facilities in their regions, handling large volumes of patient data that must be processed in a timely and accurate manner [15, 16]. Ensuring high‐quality data in these settings is not only essential for direct patient care but also for improving national health statistics, which guide public health strategies, disease surveillance, and policy development [6].

Despite significant efforts to standardize data collection and improve management practices, variability in healthcare data quality persists across hospitals, and particularly within RRHs. Differences in data quality can affect healthcare decision‐making, leading to inefficiencies in service delivery and health reporting [4, 17]. Understanding the extent of this variability is crucial for strengthening data management systems and ensuring reliable health information systems across RRHs.

One of the most pressing concerns in the context of RRHs is that although these hospitals are governed by national health policies and guidelines, some RRHs are able to maintain highly reliable and standardized data management practices, while others struggle with inconsistent or incomplete records, which undermine the effectiveness of national health policies and interventions [6, 10, 18]. This variation presents a challenge not only for hospital administrators but also for policymakers who rely on accurate data to make informed decisions that affect large populations.

Existing research on healthcare data quality has primarily focused on national data trends or district‐level healthcare facilities, with limited attention given to the variability in data quality within urban‐based RRHs [4]. The gap in the literature was particularly concerning, as urban RRHs are typically better resourced and more integrated into the broader national healthcare infrastructure, making them key players in shaping health outcomes. This study sought to address this gap by examining data quality variability across eight urban‐based RRHs, systematically analyzing the extent to which healthcare data quality differs across these hospitals and identifying patterns in data management practices.

The primary aim of this research is to assess how hospital staff perceive healthcare data quality within their institutions. While previous studies have largely focused on identifying the factors influencing data quality [4, 11], this study took a different approach by providing a descriptive analysis of how data quality is perceived across hospitals, which offer important insights into the current state of data management in RRHs. By doing so, this research contributes to a better understanding of the factors that shape data management practices and highlights the areas in need of improvement. Moreover, this study informs future research and policy interventions aimed at enhancing healthcare data quality in RRHs, ultimately strengthening healthcare delivery at the regional level.

Material and Methods

Study Setting, Period and Design

The research utilized a cross‐sectional design conducted between August and October 2024 across eight selected Regional Referral Hospitals (RRH) in Tanzania. The country has 28 RRHs, and in this research, a multi‐stage sampling method was used to select the eight RRHs. Initially, all 28 RRH were identified. In the subsequent stage, eight administrative zones were recognized, leading to the random selection of one RRH from each zone. As shown in Figure 1, the RRHs chosen were Mount Meru (Northern zone), Mwalimu Nyerere Memorial (Lake zone), Maweni (Western zone), Singida (Central zone), Morogoro (Eastern zone), Mbeya (South‐West highland zone), Sokoine (Southern zone), and Iringa RRH (Southern Highland zone). This approach was implemented to ensure randomness and the ability to generalize findings from the study. The participant group included all healthcare workers engaged in the entry of patient data from the moment of patient admission to their eventual discharge.

Data Collection and Measurement

Data were collected through a self‐administered questionnaire specifically designed to evaluate healthcare workers' perceptions of healthcare data quality. We assessed data quality in terms of accuracy, completeness, consistency, and timeliness of data entry. Table 1a clearly outlines the measurement of perceived healthcare data quality. We categorized the levels of perceived healthcare data quality based on percentiles, dividing the data into three distinct quality categories: Low, Moderate, and High. According to Table 1b, this classification was based on three percentile ranges: data quality was deemed low if it fell below the 33.33rd percentile, moderate if it ranged from the 33.33rd to the 66.66th percentiles, and high if it exceeded the 66.66th percentile. This mirrors the methodological convention employed by Gazmararian et al. [19] and Muhanga M [20], who used identical percentile cut‑offs to classify healthcare survey data. While this approach is reproducible and allows relative comparison across facilities, the cutoffs remain distribution‐based rather than criterion‐referenced and should therefore be interpreted as indicative rather than absolute thresholds. In the absence of established external benchmarks for perceived data quality, this method provides a pragmatic classification; however, future studies should validate these categories against objective data quality standards. Prior to the main survey, we conducted a pilot test at one RRH to assess each item's validity and used the results to refine the questionnaire. The final instrument demonstrated strong internal consistency, with Cronbach's alpha values all above 0.70: Data Accuracy (α = 0.904), Data Completeness (α = 0.871), Data Timeliness (α = 0.833), and Data Consistency (α = 0.839).

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were pre‑specified and conducted in IBM SPSS Statistics 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY) after data entry and cleaning in Microsoft Excel 2019. We first generated descriptive statistics (frequencies, percentages, means ± SD) to summarize respondent demographics and mean scores on each data‑quality dimension. A one‑way analyses of variance (ANOVA [21]) were used to test for differences in perceptions across the eight RRHs for each dimension, accuracy, completeness, timeliness, and consistency using two‑sided tests with a significance threshold of α = 0.05. We report F‑statistics with numerator (df₁ = k – 1 = 7) and denominator (df₂ = N – k = 328) degrees of freedom, exact p‑values formatted per SAMPL guidelines (p < 0.001; 0.001 ≤ p < 0.01 to three decimals; p ≥ 0.01 to two decimals; p > 0.99), and proportion of variance explained (partial η²) effect sizes [22]. Where ANOVA was significant, Tukey's honestly significant difference (HSD) post‑hoc tests identified specific pairwise contrasts. No subgroup or sensitivity analyses were pre‑specified; any additional comparisons are exploratory. Results are presented in tables and figures to display frequencies, percentages, mean differences, and effect sizes.

Figure 2 shows that most RRHs report moderate HDQ levels, with a notable number of RRHs having a higher proportion of high HDQ cases (H1, H2 and H8). However, low HDQ levels are consistently minimal across all RRHs, indicating generally favorable data quality but with room for improvement, especially in achieving higher data quality in some areas.

Perceived Healthcare Data Quality By Dimensions

Table 4 presents the perceived healthcare data quality by dimensions; The perceived accuracy of healthcare data across the RRHs had a mean score of 3.54 (SD = 0.97), reflecting a generally moderate to high level of confidence among respondents in the recorded data's accuracy. While most respondents expressed confidence in the data's accuracy, some variability was noted, as indicated by the standard deviation. This suggests that although the majority felt the data was accurate, there were still variations in perceptions among different hospitals. Similarly, the perceived completeness of healthcare data had a mean score of 3.52 (SD = 0.82), indicating that respondents considered the data completeness to be moderate. Most respondents agreed that patient records were generally complete, though a slight inconsistency was evident. Regarding data timeliness, the mean score of 3.58 (SD = 0.74) suggests that respondents largely viewed data updates as timely, though discrepancies were observed across hospitals. Finally, the perceived consistency of healthcare data scored a mean of 3.24 (SD = 0.68), indicating that respondents were somewhat neutral or slightly dissatisfied with the consistency of the data. The relatively low standard deviation suggests there was a consistent perception across the respondents, with fewer extreme variations in opinion.

Variation in Perceived Data Quality Across RRHs

As shown in Table 5, One‑way ANOVAs revealed that perceived data quality varied significantly by hospital across all four dimensions: accuracy (F(7, 328) = 5.03, p = 0.026, partial η² = 0.097), completeness (F(7, 328) = 8.65, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.156), timeliness (F(7, 328) = 6.07, p = 0.006, partial η² = 0.115), and consistency (F(7, 328) = 5.41, p < 0.001, partial η² = 0.103). Post‑hoc Tukey tests showed that for accuracy, H8, H7, and H2 all scored significantly higher than H6 (with no other pairwise differences); for completeness, H8 and H2 outperformed every other hospital; for timeliness, H2 and H8 were rated timelier than H6 and H7 (while the remaining hospitals did not differ); and for consistency, H2 and H8 again scored above H6, with no further significant contrasts among the other sites.

The remaining hospitals (H1, H3, H4, H5 and H7) fall under the moderate category. On the other hand, H6 was consistently rated lower, leading to their classification as low data quality hospital.

Discussion

This study highlights meaningful variation in healthcare workers' perceptions of data quality across RRHs, suggesting that data accuracy, completeness, timeliness, and consistency are not experienced uniformly across facilities. Importantly, the analysis was intentionally limited to describing perceptual variability and did not examine the organizational or technical mechanisms underlying these differences. Factors such as data management practices, technological infrastructure, resource allocation, and staff training, widely recognized as influential in the literature, were therefore beyond the scope of this study but may help explain the observed patterns [8, 9, 10].

The observed variations in perceived data quality are consistent with findings from previous studies, which have highlighted disparities in data quality perceptions across healthcare settings [10, 23]. For example, hospitals H8 and H2, which are perceived to have high data quality, may be distinguished by superior data collection processes, enhanced health information systems, and training for healthcare workers in emotional intelligence. The training helps them maintain emotional stability and manage data more effectively. This suggests that these hospitals might have been successful in implementing certain practices or strategies that enhance data quality. Conversely, hospital H6, which was ranked lower in data quality, may face challenges in these areas, though the specific reasons for these differences are not identified in this study. The variability observed in the ratings across the hospitals underscores the need for further research that investigates the factors contributing to these differences.

Perceived differences in data accuracy, completeness, and timeliness have important implications for healthcare delivery, as these dimensions underpin clinical decision‐making, continuity of care, and timely responses to emerging health needs. Where these aspects of data quality are weak, patient care may be compromised through delayed, incomplete, or inappropriate decisions. These findings reinforce existing evidence that deficits in routine health data quality can negatively affect service delivery and patient outcomes [24].

Interestingly, while hospitals H8 and H2 were classified as having high data quality, their performance varied across different dimensions. For instance, although they received high ratings for data completeness and consistency, they did not consistently excel in data accuracy and timeliness. These inaccuracies and delays may stem from issues related to data entry practices. Delays in data entry due to workflow blocks or reliance on manual processes can hinder timeliness, even if the data is ultimately accurate [25, 26]. Additionally, maintaining high consistency across multiple departments might sometimes compromise the strict accuracy of individual entries, particularly if data entry is standardized but not rigorously checked for errors [27, 28]. This finding underscores the complexity of achieving high data quality across multiple dimensions, even in hospitals that are generally viewed as having robust data management systems.

On the other hand, a low data quality hospital might have faced issues with both data accuracy and consistency. These hospitals may struggle with unreliable or incomplete data entry, inadequate training for staff, or a lack of access to necessary technology, all of which can contribute to lower data quality ratings [10]. While the current study does not investigate these underlying factors, it is clear that improving these dimensions of data quality should be a priority for these hospitals. Future studies could explore the specific barriers faced by hospitals with lower data quality ratings, allowing for targeted interventions to improve their data management processes.

Facilities classified as having moderate data quality represent important opportunities for improvement. While their performance suggests a functional baseline, gaps remain that may be addressed through targeted quality‐improvement strategies. Adopting proven practices from higher‐performing facilities, such as standardized data procedures, enhanced staff capacity, and routine feedback mechanisms, may help strengthen data accuracy, completeness, timeliness, and consistency [5].

Study Limitations

A key limitation of this study is its exclusive reliance on self‐reported perceptions of data quality, without objective validation using standardized data quality indicators. As a result, the findings reflect perceived rather than actual data accuracy, completeness, or consistency, which limits the strength of inferences about data quality outcomes. Additionally, the study did not incorporate objective measures or organizational‐level variables (e.g., training, data systems, or validation mechanisms) that could corroborate or explain these perceptions. Future research should combine perceptual measures with objective data quality metrics to enhance methodological rigor.

Study Implications and Area for Further Study

Given the importance of high‐quality healthcare data for informed decision‐making and patient care, addressing the variability in data quality across hospitals is critical. This study suggests that hospitals with lower perceived data quality, such as H6, should prioritize interventions to improve their data management systems and staff training programs. Moreover, hospitals that were classified as moderate performers (H1, H3, H4, H5 and H7) should also strive to enhance their data practices to align more closely with the high‐quality benchmarks set by H8 and H2.

Ultimately, while this study provides valuable insights into the variability in perceived healthcare data quality, further research is needed to explore the factors driving these differences while triangulating with objective data. Understanding the root causes of data quality variability would enable healthcare organizations to adopt more targeted, evidence‐based strategies for improving data quality across different institutions. Additionally, to guide these investigations, future studies should adopt a conceptual framework such as Donabedian's structure–process–outcome model or established data governance frameworks to systematically assess how facility‑level factors (e.g. infrastructure, staff capacity, governance structures) influence data quality perceptions and downstream health outcomes [29]. Understanding these underlying causes can help design targeted interventions aimed at improving data quality in hospitals that are perceived to have lower standards. Nevertheless, the findings of this study underscore the need for continuous monitoring and improvement of healthcare data quality across all hospitals to ensure accurate, timely, and consistent data that can support better healthcare outcomes in Tanzania.

Conclusion

This study highlights meaningful inter‐hospital variation in perceived data quality, suggesting that facility‐level practices play a critical role in shaping how healthcare data are produced and managed. Based on these findings, hospitals, particularly lower‐performing facilities, should prioritize targeted staff training in data management, strengthen supervision and feedback mechanisms, and standardize data validation procedures within routine M&E systems. At the policy level, health authorities could use perception‐based assessments as a screening tool to identify facilities requiring support, while complementing them with objective data quality audits. Together, these steps may help translate perceived data quality gaps into actionable quality‐improvement strategies.
