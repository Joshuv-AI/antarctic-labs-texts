The relationship between nurses’ cultural competence and cultural humility levels and their perception and understanding of human dignity
Authors: Ayşe Eminoğlu Güven, Sebahat Kuşlu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe nursing profession rests on an ethical foundation based on respect for the right to health of individuals and the protection of human dignity under all circumstances. Human dignity is at the center of nursing care, recognizing that each individual is unique, valuable, and worthy of respect. This ethical principle becomes even more critical in health care services where cultural differences are felt intensely. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between nurses’ cultural competence and cultural humility levels, as well as their perception and understanding of human dignity.MethodsThe study was conducted as a descriptive-correlational research. This study was conducted with 318 nurses working in a public hospital in Gaziantep, Türkiye. The data were collected using the “Health Workers Cultural Competence Scale”, “Cultural Humility Scale”, and “Perception and Understanding of Human Dignity in Nursing Scale”. Descriptive statistics, Mann-Whitney U, Kruskal-Wallis, and Spearman correlation tests were used to analyze the data.ResultsThe mean age of the nurses was 28.74 ± 5.29 years, and 71.4% were female. Most participants had a bachelor’s degree (72.0%), and 51.9% were single. Nurses had high levels of awareness and sensitivity (28.16 ± 3.66), resource ownership and registration (17.00 ± 4.88), attitude (37.16 ± 10.11), patient-centered communication (11.29 ± 3.01), interaction and practice orientation (24.28 ± 3.20), self-assessment (37.41 ± 6.96), cultural humility (77.53 ± 13.05), and perception and understanding of human dignity (201.07 ± 25.61). Positive but generally weak relationships were found between cultural competence, cultural humility, and the perception of human dignity (r: 0.22–0.34; p < 0.05).ConclusionIt was observed that nurses with high levels of cultural competence and humility more strongly adopted a care approach that respects human dignity. In this context, it is recommended that nursing education content be developed to include both affective and behavioral dimensions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12912-026-04828-0.

Introduction

Diversity is the one thing that every individual in the world has in common [1]. This diversity can be observed in various aspects, including appearance, psychological state, living environment, the food we eat and drink, beliefs, values, attitudes, and behaviors. Culture is an important concept that comes at the beginning of these diversities and interacts with many of them. Culture is defined as the beliefs, values, and norms that guide a group’s decisions and behaviors [2]. Cultural competence is a set of adaptive behaviors, attitudes, and policies that enable effective communication between systems, institutions, professionals, and individuals [3]. Six conditions must be fulfilled for cultural competence to be formed. These are openness, awareness [4], desire, cultural knowledge [5], cultural sensitivity, and cultural encounter [3]. Societies are gradually transforming into multicultural structures due to the growing presence of people from diverse cultures, migration, expanding travel opportunities, and other factors. In this multicultural environment, it is essential for cultural competence and care that the social, cultural, religious, and linguistic differences of the individuals receiving healthcare are recognized, respected, and addressed [3]. However, being culturally competent about other cultures is considered unattainable; instead, the concept of cultural humility is emphasized [6]. Cultural humility in the context of healthcare refers to acknowledging the impact of cultural characteristics on health and illness and integrating this understanding into treatment and care [7]. To carry out this process, nurses should consider the culture of the individual they care for from the individual’s perspective, be aware of this, and be respectful of every culture [6]. In cultural humility, the individual’s perspective and interpretation of their own culture are in question. In contrast, in cultural competence, the nurse understands the individual’s culture before establishing a relationship with them [8].

The concept of honor, which is defined as “respect, honor, self-respect, dignity, izzetinefis” or “personal value, honor, reputation on which the respect shown by others is based” [9], constitutes the essence of nursing care. It is the most fundamental right of every patient and must be respected. Otherwise, patient satisfaction will decrease, interpersonal relationships will deteriorate, and patient-oriented care may be disrupted [10]. Therefore, protecting and enhancing human dignity in diverse cultural environments is the most crucial ethical responsibility of nurses [11]. However, nurses face difficulties in fulfilling this task due to reasons such as migration, multiculturalism, and globalization. Especially in multicultural societies, generalizing the characteristics of a particular culture to the whole society may create problems regarding justice and cultural approaches in health services and social contexts [12]. Therefore, culturally competent, humble, and dignified health care services should be provided in all societies, especially in multicultural societies.

To guide this research, a review of studies on human dignity was conducted. A survey conducted to determine the factors affecting patient dignity found that nurses’ knowledge of how to protect patient dignity and of the factors that impact it should be increased [13]. In a study conducted to determine the views and experiences of Turkish nurses regarding care that respects human dignity, nurses stated that it is essential not to expose the patient’s body and to respect the patient’s privacy [14]. In another study, nurses considered the patient’s honor unique [15]. When the literature is examined, although there are different studies on human dignity [13–15], there is no study on the relationship between the concepts of cultural competence and cultural humility with the levels of perception and understanding of human dignity. In this direction, the present study aimed to determine the relationship between nurses’ cultural competence and cultural humility levels and their perception and understanding of human dignity.

Method

Type of research

This study was designed as a descriptive and correlational study.

Time of research

The research was conducted between 10.03.2025 and 20.05.2025.

Population and sample of the study

The study was conducted in a public tertiary-care hospital located in Gaziantep, Türkiye. The hospital is one of the largest healthcare institutions in the region and provides a wide range of medical services, including emergency care, surgical units, internal medicine clinics, and specialized healthcare services. At the time of the study, approximately 2000 nurses and midwives were employed at the hospital. The study population consisted of all nurses working in this institution. A convenience sampling method was used. Nurses who were actively working in clinical or administrative units during the data collection period and who agreed to participate voluntarily were included in the study. Nurses who were on leave, not actively working during the study period, or who declined to participate were excluded from the study. The sample size was calculated using the G*Power 3.1 program. Since the study had a correlational design, the calculation was performed using the expected correlation coefficient reported in previous studies [16]. Assuming a small effect size (r = 0.20), a significance level of α = 0.05, and a power of 0.95, the minimum required sample size was calculated to be 272 nurses. In addition, considering recommendations in the literature to increase the sample size by 10–15% to prevent potential data loss from incomplete forms or study withdrawals [17, 18], a larger sample size was targeted. In this context, the study was completed with 318 nurses who agreed to participate.

Data collection

The research data were collected face-to-face between March 10 and May 10, 2025, using a questionnaire developed by the researchers. It was explained to the people in a short text at the beginning of the questionnaire form that no information that would reveal their identities was requested in the questionnaire form, that the study would be used only for scientific purposes, and that the objective answers to the survey questions were to reveal the importance of the subject. It took an average of 15 min to complete the questionnaire form.

Data collection tools

The data were collected through the “Information Form” created by the researchers and the “Healthcare Professionals Cultural Competence Scale (HPCCS)”, “Cultural Humility Scale (CHS)” and “Perception and Understanding of Human Dignity in Nursing Scale (SHDPUN)” in the literature.

Information form

The Information Form was developed specifically for this study by researchers, based on a review of the nursing literature [19]. The form has not been published previously and was created solely to collect sociodemographic and professional background data relevant to the current study. The form consists of a total of 14 questions, including participants’ age, gender, marital status, income level, number of children, educational status, place where they have spent most of their lives, work style, satisfaction with their profession, weekly working hours, length of time in the profession, the clinic where they work, participation in cultural practices, and whether they have received training on cultural practices. The form is provided as (Supplementary File 1).

The healthcare professionals cultural competence scale

The scale adapted by Korkmaz et al. (2022) is a five-point Likert-type scale consisting of 43 items and six sub-dimensions. Each sub-dimension is evaluated separately. The score of each sub-dimension is obtained by summing the scores obtained from the sub-dimension questions and dividing by the number of questions in that sub-dimension. Low scores obtained from each sub-dimension indicate low levels of cultural competence, while high scores indicate high levels of cultural competence. Awareness and Sensitivity sub-dimension 1 (items 1–8); Resourcefulness and Recording sub-dimension 2 (items 9–13); Attitude sub-dimension 3 (items 14–23). Patient-centered communication sub-dimension 4 (items 24–26); Interaction and Practice Orientation sub-dimension 5 (items 27–34); Self-Assessment sub-dimension 6 (items 35–43). Awareness and Sensitivity sub-dimension, Resourcefulness and Recording sub-dimension, Attitude sub-dimension, Interaction and Practice Orientation sub-dimension, and Self-Assessment sub-dimension were scored as “Strongly agree = 5”, “Agree = 4”, “Neutral = 3”, “Disagree = 2”, “Strongly disagree = 1”. The patient-centered communication sub-dimension was scored as follows: “Never = 1”, “Rarely = 2”, “Occasionally = 3”, “Very often = 4”, and “Always = 5”. Scale items 1, 2, 28, 29, 30, 32, and 34 are reverse-scored. Awareness and sensitivity sub-dimension includes the health worker’s cultural awareness and sensitivity; resource ownership and recording sub-dimension consists of the health worker’s resource access and documentation for cultural diagnosis; attitude sub-dimension: expressing attitudes towards recognising or understanding culture; patient-centred communication sub-dimension: communicating with the patient and his/her family for treatment and the participation of the patient and his/her family; interaction sub-dimension: evaluating the effect of mutual interaction on care outcomes; self-assessment sub-dimension: includes the health worker’s self-assessment and awareness of his/her competencies for providing cultural care. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the Turkish version of the scale ranges from 0.79 to 0.94 [20]. The Cronbach’s alpha value of the scale in this study was 0.902.

Cultural humility scale

The scale developed by Foronda et al. (2021) was adapted into Turkish by Kuşci and Oğuz Duran (2022) [21, 22]. The scale consists of 19 items ranging from 1 (Never) to 5 (Always) on a 5-point Likert scale. The scale consists of three sub-dimensions: Perspective on Differences, Self Attribution, and Results of Cultural Humility. Perspective on Differences: Items 1–7; Self Attribution: Items 8–11; Results of Cultural Humility: 12–19. There is no reverse item in the scale. Scores 19–38 on the scale indicate that the respondent is rarely culturally humble, 39–75 points indicate that the respondent is sometimes culturally humility, 76–85 points indicate that the respondent is usually culturally humility, and 86–95 points indicate that the respondent is always culturally humility. Cronbach’s Alpha, the internal consistency coefficient for the scale, was 0.82 [22]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the scale in this study was 0.965.

Scale human dignity perception and understanding in nursing

It was developed by Yıldırım and Akın Palandöken in 2021, comprising three factors (perception, understanding, and care) and 43 items. The items of the 5-point Likert-type scale are scored as 1: “Strongly Disagree”, 2: “Disagree”, 3: “Undecided”, 4: “Partially Agree”, 5: “Agree”. There are only positive expressions in the scale items, and there are no reverse-coded items. The highest score that can be obtained from the scale is 215, the highest score that can be obtained from the understanding sub-dimension is 75, the highest score that can be obtained from the care sub-dimension is 75, and the highest score that can be obtained from the perception sub-dimension is 65. The total Cronbach’s alpha was 0.910. This value was calculated as 0.864 for the Understanding Subscale, 0.826 for the Care Subscale, and 0.766 for the Perception Subscale [23]. The Cronbach’s alpha of the scale in this study was 0.988.

Data evaluation

The Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) version 25.0 was used to analyze the data. Descriptive statistics of the data obtained from the research were presented as arithmetic mean, standard deviation, number, percentage, mean-rank, minimum, and maximum values. The suitability of the scores obtained from the scales and sub-dimensions used in the study to a normal distribution was examined using the Shapiro-Wilk test, Skewness and Kurtosis values, histograms, and detrended graphs. It was observed that a normal distribution was not provided. Because the data did not meet the assumption of normality, and a ceiling effect was observed in the Scale Human Dignity Perception and Understanding in Nursing scores, non-parametric statistical tests were preferred. The Mann-Whitney U test was used for two-group categorical variables, and the Kruskal-Wallis test for three- or more-group categorical variables. A Bonferroni correction was applied to pairwise comparisons of variables with significant differences across three or more groups. The relationship between two quantitative variables that are not normally distributed was analyzed using Spearman’s rank correlation. The internal consistency of the scales was evaluated using Cronbach’s alpha. For statistical tests, p < 0.05 was considered significant. Hierarchical multiple regression analysis was used to examine the independent contribution of cultural competence subdimensions and cultural humility to the perception of human dignity, after controlling for demographic and professional variables. Prior to the regression analysis, multicollinearity was assessed using tolerance and variance inflation factor (VIF) values, with tolerance > 0.20 and VIF < 10 considered acceptable thresholds.

Ethical aspects of the research

Ethical approval was obtained from Gaziantep Islam Science and Technology University Non-Interventional Clinical Research Ethics Committee (Decision no: 388.36.02). During the study, the ethical guidelines and principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki were strictly adhered to, and the study was conducted in accordance with international research ethics standards. Written informed consent was obtained from the nurses participating in the study.

The mean age of the nurses was 28.74 ± 5.29 years; 71.4% were female and 51.9% were single. Most nurses had a bachelor’s degree (72.0%), 43.7% reported that their income was equal to their expenses, the average number of children was 0.69 ± 0.99, and 76.1% had spent most of their lives in the province (Table 2).

The nurses had worked in the profession for an average of 5.68 ± 5.44 years. More than half had a mixed working schedule (53.1%), 82.7% reported liking their profession, and 44.3% worked 40–47 h per week. In addition, 53.8% worked in internal services, 62.3% reported engaging in cultural practices in their daily lives, and 36.2% had received training on cultural competence and approaches to individuals’ cultural practices (Table 3).

No significant differences were found between gender and the scale scores (p > 0.05). Married nurses had significantly higher mean ranks in the Patient-Centered Communication and Self-Assessment subdimensions (p < 0.05). Nurses with postgraduate education demonstrated higher Awareness and Sensitivity scores, whereas those who graduated from health vocational high schools had lower Cultural Humility scores than nurses with associate or bachelor’s degrees (p < 0.05).

Nurses whose income was lower than their expenses showed higher scores in the Perspective on Differences subdimension (p < 0.05). Nurses who had spent most of their lives in the province had higher scores in Resourcefulness and Recording, Attitude, and Patient-Centered Communication, whereas those who had lived mostly in villages or towns had higher scores in Interaction and Practice Orientation, Self Attribution, and the Cultural Humility Scale (p < 0.05).

Regarding professional characteristics, nurses who liked their profession had significantly higher scores in Patient-Centered Communication and Self-Assessment (p < 0.05). Nurses working more than 72 h per week tended to have lower Attitude, Patient-Centered Communication, and Self-Assessment scores but higher Interaction and Practice Orientation scores (p < 0.05). Nurses working in administrative units showed higher scores on the Perspective on Differences and Cultural Humility Scale than nurses working in operating theatres (p < 0.05).

Nurses who reported engaging in cultural practices in their daily lives had higher scores on Resourcefulness and Recording and Perspective on Differences (p < 0.05). Similarly, nurses who had received training on cultural competence showed significantly higher scores in several cultural competence subdimensions and the Cultural Humility Scale (p < 0.05). In addition, nurses who had received cultural competence training had lower scores on the Scale of Human Dignity Perception and Understanding in Nursing compared with those who had not received such training (p < 0.05).

Correlation analysis revealed moderate-to-strong positive correlations among age, number of children, and years of professional experience. In addition, the number of children was positively associated with the Self-Assessment subdimension, and years of professional experience were positively correlated with Self-Assessment and Self-Attribution (p < 0.05). Cultural competence subdimensions were weakly to moderately correlated with cultural humility, and several cultural competence subdimensions were weakly to moderately associated with human dignity perception. A weak but significant positive relationship was found between cultural humility and human dignity perception (p < 0.05) (Table 4).

A hierarchical multiple regression analysis was conducted to examine the independent predictors of nurses’ perception of human dignity. In the first step, demographic and professional variables (age, gender, years of professional experience, and training status) were entered into the model. This model explained 6.7% of the variance in perceptions of human dignity (R²: 0.067, F: 5.605, p < 0.001). In the second step, cultural humility and the relevant subdimensions of cultural competence (Awareness and Sensitivity, Interaction and Practice Orientation, and Self-Assessment) were added to the model. The overall model explained 27.9% of the variance in human dignity perception (R²: 0.279, F: 14.972, p<0.001). Cultural humility (β: 0.266, p < 0.001), Awareness and Sensitivity (β: 0.252, p < 0.001), Interaction and Practice Orientation (β: 0.180, p < 0.001), and training status (β: 0.194, p < 0.001) were significant predictors of human dignity perception (Table 5). Multicollinearity diagnostics indicated that tolerance values ranged from 0.20 to 0.94, and VIF values ranged from 1.06 to 5.01 (Table 5).

Discussion

In this study, which aimed to investigate the relationship between nurses’ cultural competence and cultural humility levels and their perceptions and understanding of human dignity, it was found that nurses’ cultural competence levels were above average, and they generally demonstrated cultural humility. In addition, nurses’ perceptions and understanding of levels of human dignity were found to be well above average. Culturally competent care that respects human dignity has been associated with higher patient satisfaction and improved care experiences. For this reason, individual or group training aimed at improving nurses’ cultural sensitivity may help strengthen their cultural competence and cultural humility [24].

Individual-specific care is based on patient-centered communication and approach. This form of communication, based on trust, respect, and cooperation with the patient, may facilitate the patient’s active participation in the treatment and care process and may contribute to improved clinical outcomes [25]. The findings revealed that the patient-centered communication and self-assessment scores of married nurses were significantly higher than those of single nurses. This situation may be associated with the development of empathy, patience, and mutual responsibility in long-term relationships such as marriage. In this context, it may be suggested that married nurses could reflect the emotional intelligence, communication, and self-assessment skills developed in their personal lives in their professional roles. However, Cankaya and Eris (2020) reported in their study among nurses and midwives that single individuals had higher levels of emotional intelligence than married individuals. This difference may be due to variables such as the average age, professional seniority, or marital status of the individuals in the sample of Cankaya and Eris’s study [26]. At this point, more detailed qualitative research may be necessary to determine how communication and self-evaluation skills evolve with marital status.

In this study, nurses with postgraduate degrees demonstrated higher levels of awareness and sensitivity compared to those with other educational levels. In addition, nurses who graduated from a health vocational high school had lower levels of cultural humility than nurses who graduated from associate degree and bachelor’s degree programs. According to the literature, the cultural humility levels of associate degree nurses were lower [27]. Cultural competence and intercultural nursing courses are primarily included in undergraduate or graduate programs in Turkey, as in many countries. In a study on this topic, it was determined that 58.7% of 206 universities in Turkey have nursing departments, and 45.4% of these departments include culture courses. Additionally, it was noted that the course hours and content of culture courses in nursing undergraduate programs were insufficient [28]. In line with these findings, curricula can be updated to include courses that aim to increase cultural sensitivity and competence across all levels of nursing education. Additionally, the hours and content of these courses should align with specific standards, a crucial step in developing cultural sensitivity and competence.

It was determined that nurses with incomes below their expenses had a significantly more positive perspective on differences than those with incomes above their expenses. Individuals whose income is less than their costs may show a more sensitive and positive approach to environmental differences. A study conducted by Piff et al. (2010) demonstrated that individuals from lower socioeconomic classes were more generous, charitable, trustworthy, and helpful than their upper-class peers [29]. In this context, individuals’ socioeconomic life experiences may be useful for developing a diversity-sensitive approach in the nursing profession.

In this study, nurses who spent most of their lives in the city center had higher scores on “Resourcefulness and Recording”, “Attitude”, and “Patient-Centered Communication” than those who lived in villages or towns. This may be explained by the fact that nurses living in the city have access to more professional resources, more educational opportunities, and greater experience in patient-centered communication. On the other hand, nurses who spent most of their lives in villages or towns had higher scores in “Interaction and Practice Orientation”, “Self Attribution”, and Cultural Humility. This finding suggests that nurses working in rural areas adopt a humble, open approach, as they frequently encounter diverse cultural structures and form closer, more intimate relationships with individuals.

Occupational satisfaction refers to the physical, social, and spiritual fulfillment that individuals experience from their work. Occupational satisfaction is a factor that increases efficiency and quality [30]. Therefore, a profession is expected to create sufficient satisfaction for individuals. Nurses who reported satisfaction with their profession showed higher Patient-Centered Communication and Self-Assessment scores. In another study supporting the research findings, it was reported that the patient-centered care competencies of individuals who liked their profession were significantly higher than those who did not [31]. Based on this study and the literature, it may be beneficial to positively change the professional image to increase satisfaction with the nursing profession. Additionally, developing strategies to enhance nurses’ professional satisfaction is a crucial step toward improving patient safety and quality of care.

It was observed that the mean ranks of the Attitude, Patient-Centered Communication, and Self-Assessment sub-dimensions were generally lower among nurses with weekly working hours exceeding 72 h; however, this finding should be interpreted with caution, given the small sample size in this subgroup (n = 10). In comparison, the mean ranks of Interaction and Practice Orientation sub-dimensions were significantly higher. This situation may be linked to the effects of long working hours on the quality of nursing care and the practice environment. Namely, as the weekly working hours increase, so does interaction and orientation. However, there is a decrease in patient-centered communication, attitude, and self-assessment skills due to intensive work and fatigue. A study conducted in Thailand found that prolonged working hours were positively correlated with communication errors [32]. According to a study conducted in China, satisfaction and quality of care decreased as the duration of night shifts increased. It was reported that nurses working overtime experienced less satisfaction and lower quality of care across all shift types [33]. In line with these findings, it is recommended that international action plans to eliminate the nursing shortage and reduce nurses’ workload be implemented immediately. Otherwise, patient satisfaction, job satisfaction, quality of care, and health outcomes may be negatively affected.

It was found that nurses working in administrative units had significantly higher mean ranks in the Perspective on Differences, the Results of Cultural Humility sub-dimension, and the total rank average of the Cultural Humility Scale compared to nurses working in the operating theatre. This may be attributed to the fact that nurses working in administrative units experience less stress and have a lower workload compared to nurses in other units. An intense workload and stress may lead to a decrease in communication and empathetic skills. Cultural humility skills can only emerge when there is enough thinking and time. These findings may indicate that workload and work environment could influence communication and cultural interaction in nursing practice, and that reducing stress and excessive workload may support the development of communication and cultural skills.

In this study, nurses engaged in cultural practices in their daily lives demonstrated higher scores in “Resource Ownership and Recording” and “Perspective on Differences.” According to the literature, individuals who are actively engaged in cultural practices tend to be more open to diverse cultures and exhibit better cultural competence and intercultural sensitivity [34]. This paves the way for more effective and sensitive care delivery in nursing practice. Therefore, programs could be implemented and organized for nurses and nursing students to develop their skills in communicating effectively with patients from diverse cultural backgrounds, thereby increasing their cultural awareness and contacts. Additionally, it may be recommended to incorporate more cultural awareness, cultural humility, and intercultural communication into nursing education.

It was found that nurses who had previously received training on issues related to cultural competence/approach to cultural practices of individuals had significantly higher mean ranks of Resource Ownership and Recording, Attitude, Patient-Centered Communication, Self-Assessment, Perspective on Differences sub-dimension, and Cultural Humility Scale total rank average compared to those who did not receive training and partially received training. In a study conducted in Turkey, cultural sensitivity and skills of nurses trained in cultural care were found to be significantly higher [34]. In another study, cultural competence training given to nurses was found to effectively improve cultural competence [35]. In light of these findings, it is recommended that cultural courses be added to nursing curricula and that nurses enhance their cultural knowledge and skills through vocational training.

It was determined that nurses who had previously received training in cultural competence and approaches to individuals’ cultural practices had lower total scores on the Human Dignity Perception and Understanding in Nursing Scale than those who had not received such training or had received only partial training. However, the literature indicates that individuals who receive cultural education tend to be more sensitive, empathetic, and respectful toward cultural differences and to approach the individuals in their care with respect for human dignity [34, 35]. The finding of a result contrary to the literature in this study may suggest that the training received by the nurses in the sample did not lead to the expected changes in knowledge, attitudes, and behaviors. In addition, nurses who received such training may have developed greater self-awareness and critical reflection skills, which may have led them to evaluate their professional practices more honestly, a pattern consistent with international evidence suggesting that cultural humility training may initially lower perceived scores by fostering self-reflexivity rather than enhancing self-confidence [27, 36]. For these reasons, the findings should be interpreted with caution, and future studies should examine the effects of training using different methodological approaches. It is recommended that nurses be supported with case-based and practice-based learning methods that enable change not only at the cognitive level but also at the affective and behavioral levels. Furthermore, when evaluating the effectiveness of training, it is important to monitor not only short-term outcomes but also long-term changes in professional attitudes and care practices.

A positive and weakly significant relationship was found between the number of children of nurses and the self-assessment sub-dimension, as well as between the years of employment and the self-assessment and self-attribution sub-dimensions. According to the literature, the self-assessment abilities of nurses with more than 10 years of experience are higher than those of nurses with less experience [37]. In addition to long-term professional experience, life experiences, such as parenthood, are believed to provide individuals with empathy, sensitivity, and awareness, thereby enhancing their self-assessment skills. In this context, nurses’ personal life experiences and professional seniority may be positively associated with their cultural skills. These results suggest that not only formal education but also experience-based learning opportunities should be considered in developing nurses’ assessment and self-assessment skills.

In this study, it was determined that there was a positive and weak relationship between the sub-dimensions of the Cultural Competence Scale, “Resource Ownership and Recording” and “Self-Assessment”, and the Cultural Humility Scale. The “Resource Ownership and Recording” sub-dimension encompasses an individual’s behaviors, such as accessing cultural information, utilizing resources, and recording it. The development of these skills may support the ability to accept others’ cultural knowledge and experiences and to be open to learning, which is a fundamental component of cultural humility. Similarly, the relationship between the “Self-Assessment” sub-dimension of cultural competence and cultural humility is related to the nurse’s awareness of his/her values, prejudices, and beliefs. In the literature, it is emphasized that the basis of cultural humility lies in an individual’s tendency to question their own culture, to accept and learn about its rights and wrongs [38]. Therefore, it is expected that individuals with high self-assessment skills will also have high levels of cultural humility. In light of these findings, nurses need to understand their own cultural backgrounds, be open to diverse cultural perspectives, and make informed evaluations.

It was determined that there was a weak-to-moderate positive relationship between the Awareness and Sensitivity, Interaction and Practice Orientation, and Self-Assessment sub-dimensions of the Cultural Competence Scale and the Perception and Understanding of Human Dignity in Nursing Scale. This finding suggests that nurses who are culturally aware and sensitive may be more likely to adopt an understanding care that respects human dignity. Again, nurses with a high self-assessment level are more respectful and understanding towards the cultural attitudes and behaviors of both themselves and the individuals they care for. In this context, these findings suggest that cultural competence may be associated with more respectful and dignity-oriented nursing care; therefore, emphasizing care that respects human dignity, along with cultural competence, may be beneficial.

The concept of cultural humility refers to approaching other cultures with humility and respect, free from prejudice [38]. Respect for human dignity is widely recognized as a fundamental ethical principle in nursing practice [39]. The findings of this study indicated a positive but weak association between the Cultural Humility Scale and the Perception and Understanding of Human Dignity in Nursing Scale. This relationship may indicate that cultural humility and the perception of dignity intersect through an unconditional respect for the differences and unique existence of each individual. In this context, the findings highlight the potential importance of strengthening cultural humility skills in nursing practice to support dignity-centered care. Furthermore, hierarchical regression analysis showed that cultural humility remained significantly associated with nurses’ perception of human dignity even after controlling for demographic and professional variables, further supporting its role as an important component of dignity-centered nursing care. Cultural competence and cultural humility, while distinct constructs, are complementary rather than interchangeable [36]; cultural competence provides the knowledge and skills base for cross-cultural interactions, whereas cultural humility adds a reflexive, person-centered orientation that may be more closely aligned with dignity-respecting care. The weak but significant relationships observed in this study may reflect this complementary dynamic and are consistent with international evidence indicating that cultural humility remains an area requiring development across diverse healthcare settings [27, 40]. Multicollinearity diagnostics also supported the robustness of the regression model. VIF values for the primary predictors of interest, including cultural competence subdimensions and cultural humility, ranged from 1.14 to 1.37, indicating no problematic collinearity. The somewhat higher VIF values observed for age and years of professional experience (4.97 and 5.01, respectively) reflect the expected overlap between these two demographic variables, as nurses with more years in the profession tend to be older. However, both values remained below the commonly accepted threshold of 10 [41], and since these variables functioned as control variables rather than primary predictors, the substantive interpretation of the findings was not affected.

An important methodological consideration relates to the ceiling effect observed in the Scale of Human Dignity Perception and Understanding in Nursing. A substantial proportion of participants scored at or near the maximum possible value, thereby restricting the range of variation for this variable. From a statistical perspective, restricted variability tends to attenuate Pearson and Spearman correlation coefficients, as both indices depend on the dispersion of scores around the mean. When the variance of one variable is reduced due to a ceiling effect, the magnitude of its observed correlations with other variables is mathematically constrained, even when a true underlying association exists. Therefore, the generally weak-to-moderate correlation coefficients observed in this study, particularly those involving the Human Dignity Perception and Understanding Scale, may underestimate the actual strength of these associations. This consideration should be taken into account when interpreting the findings, and future studies are recommended to employ measurement tools with greater sensitivity in the upper range of the scale or to include more heterogeneous samples to capture broader score variability.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Strengths

This study is a rare examination of three essential concepts that complement one another: cultural competence, cultural humility, and the perception of human dignity in nursing. The holistic evaluation of the relationships between the concepts offers original contributions to the literature.

Conclusions and recommendations

The findings of this study indicate that nurses demonstrated above-average levels of cultural competence and generally high levels of cultural humility. In addition, nurses’ perception and understanding of human dignity were relatively high. These findings suggest that the participating nurses reported culturally competent, humble, and dignity-oriented attitudes that may be reflected in their clinical practice.

In the study, positive and significant relationships were found between “Awareness and Sensitivity”, “Interaction and Practice Orientation” and “Self-Assessment” sub-dimensions of the cultural competence scale and perception and understanding of human dignity; between “Resourcefulness and Recording” and “Self-Assessment” sub-dimensions and cultural humility; and between cultural humility and perception of human dignity. The findings suggest that higher levels of cultural competence and cultural humility are associated with a stronger perception and understanding of human dignity. However, the observed relationships were generally weak to moderate; therefore, the results should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal influence. In this context;
