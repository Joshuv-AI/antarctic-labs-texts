The association of Zip Code-level income with cardiovascular disease burden in the Bronx: a two-decade analysis
Authors: Priyansh Shah, Kathleen Allen, Daniel Spevack, Megan Woody, Kristina Chae, Aarti Patel, Michael Schaefer, Sanjay Doddamani, Spencer Raub, Sharmila Sarkar, Robert J. Ostfeld
Published: 2026

Introduction

Cardiovascular disease remains the leading cause of death in the United States. Clinical care accounts for less than one-fifth of modifiable health outcomes, while socioeconomic and behavioral factors contribute the majority [1,2]. Lower socioeconomic status is inversely associated with cardiovascular disease across broad geographic scales [3], and neighborhood-level social determinants are increasingly incorporated into risk assessment, most notably through the optional zip code-based Social Deprivation Index in the PREVENT equation [4]. Two questions remain under-examined: whether a continuous income-disease association is detectable within a single, uniformly disadvantaged locale, where neighborhood variation is obscured in county-level or national analyses; and whether the income effect is uniform across race and ethnicity.

The Bronx offers a useful natural setting for both questions. It has the lowest per-capita income and the highest poverty rate of the 62 counties in New York State, yet its per-capita income varies 3.35-fold across its 25 zip codes [2]. We examined the association between zip code-level income and the prevalence of diabetes mellitus (DM), hypertension (HTN), congestive heart failure (CHF), and coronary artery disease (CAD); tested whether the association is modified by race and ethnicity; and assessed its persistence using contemporary public data.

Methods

Using Clinical Looking Glass (Montefiore Medical Center; Moses, Weiler, and North divisions), we identified Bronx outpatients aged 18 years or older seen between January 1, 1996 and September 8, 2006 with a primary or secondary ICD-9 diagnosis of DM, HTN, CHF, or CAD, using the first visit as the index date. Age, sex, self-identified race and ethnicity (White, Black, Hispanic, or Other), and residential zip code were recorded; case-definition specificity, confirmed by physician review of 100 randomly selected charts per diagnosis, ranged from 95 to 98 percent. The 25 Bronx zip codes were dichotomized a priori at the 2000 Census mean per-capita income into 13 lower-income (Group A) and 12 higher-income (Group B) zip codes. We computed disease prevalence and unadjusted odds ratios (Group B reference), stratified odds ratios by age, sex, and race/ethnicity, and regressed adjusted disease odds on per-capita income across the 25 zip codes, with the highest-income zip code as the reference (odds ratio 1).

For contemporary corroboration, we regressed Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Population Level Analysis and Community Estimates (CDC PLACES) 2025 ZIP Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA)-level prevalence of DM, HTN, and CAD on American Community Survey (ACS) 2019 to 2023 ZCTA per-capita income across the same 25 zip codes, using ordinary least squares (n = 25) [5]. Because PLACES small-area estimates use county-level poverty as a covariate, ZCTA-level ACS income is an independent axis. Spatial stability of the income gradient was assessed with the Spearman rank correlation between 2000 Census and 2023 ACS per-capita income. The study was determined to be exempt by the institutional review board as an analysis of de-identified data.

Results

All 220,844 eligible patients were included, representing all 25 Bronx zip codes. Group A comprised 106,487 patients with a mean per-capita income of $10,215, and Group B comprised 114,357 patients with a mean of $18,646 (p < 0.001). Group A patients were younger (42.2 versus 47.2 years) and more often Hispanic (51.0% versus 30.5%). Prevalence was higher in Group A for all four conditions: DM 9.0% versus 6.7%, HTN 17.0% versus 12.5%, CHF 1.6% versus 1.1%, and CAD 2.4% versus 2.2% (all p at or below 0.05). Unadjusted odds ratios were significant for DM (1.36; CI 1.32 to 1.41), HTN (1.43; 1.40 to 1.47), and CHF (1.46; 1.36 to 1.57), and were directionally consistent for CAD (1.06; 0.999 to 1.12) (Fig. 1C). Stratified analysis revealed effect modification by race/ethnicity (Fig. 1B). The income gradient held across all race strata for HTN, CHF, and CAD. For diabetes, however, the gradient was present in White patients (odds ratio 1.57) and in the Other group (1.26) but was absent or reversed in Black patients (0.87) and Hispanic patients (0.94); it was preserved across age and sex strata. The Bronx income geography was highly stable: the Spearman correlation between 2000 Census and 2023 ACS per-capita income was 0.89 (p < 0.0001; Fig. 1A). In contemporary data, PLACES 2025 ZCTA prevalence was inversely associated with ACS 2019 to 2023 per-capita income for DM (r-squared 0.79), CAD (0.31), and, more weakly, HTN (0.18) (Fig. 1D).

Discussion

Within a single, uniformly disadvantaged borough, zip code-level income was continuously and inversely associated with the burden of all four cardiovascular conditions at fine spatial scale, without a threshold even at the lowest incomes, and this gradient persisted across two decades. Although this association is well established nationally, demonstrating a continuous, dose-dependent gradient within the poorest county in New York State, in individual-level data from 220,844 patients, and showing that the income geography and disease gradient remain reproducible twenty years later, extends prior work. The absence of a threshold suggests no income floor below which further disadvantage ceases to confer cardiovascular risk, relevant to how deprivation is modeled in risk equations.

This gradient likely reflects structural mediators concentrated in lower-income Bronx neighborhoods, including limited access to care, food insecurity, environmental exposures, lower health literacy, and psychosocial stress, which act on cardiovascular risk beyond insurance coverage (Fig. 1E) [7]. Its persistence across the pre-Affordable Care Act period, the Medicaid expansion, and the post-pandemic years suggests that insurance expansion alone has been insufficient to close the within-borough gap and that place-based structural interventions are needed [3].

Among the individual outcomes, one finding merits emphasis. The association between income and diabetes was modified by race and ethnicity, remaining protective in White and Other patients but absent in Black and Hispanic patients, even as the gradient for the other three conditions held across all groups. This replicates a prior finding of inverse income-diabetes relationship in White men and women and in Black women but not Black men, and extends it across four conditions, indicating a modification specific to diabetes rather than a general artifact [6]. Because race captures income-related risk inconsistently while zip code income tracks it consistently across diseases, these data support the shift from race-based toward place-based risk assessment in the PREVENT equations [4].

Multi-domain measures such as the Social Vulnerability Index and Area Deprivation Index capture vulnerability beyond income and are a natural next step; we used per-capita income for its policy relevance and comparability across the interval. This study has limitations. It relies on a single health system, although all 25 zip codes were represented and the catchment area is broad, and case ascertainment used ICD-9 codes, mitigated by a confirmed specificity of 95 to 98 percent. The historical primary cohort is available only as archival summary outputs, which precludes a fully adjusted multivariable odds ratio or a formal income-by-race interaction term; we therefore present stratified estimates alongside independent contemporary corroboration, which is itself ecological. Within these constraints, zip code income is durably and inversely associated with cardiovascular disease across a uniformly disadvantaged borough, and its persistence over two decades, with the race modification of the diabetes association, underscores that place-based measures may capture socioeconomic cardiovascular risk more consistently than race/ethnicity.
