Beyond the boundaries of microbial diversity in radioactive environments: novel microbial species isolated from a former silver-uranium mine’s radon-saturated waters
Authors: Gabriela Kapinusova, Kunal Jani, Petr Pajer, Alena Filipova, Pavel Ulbrich, Tomas Cajthaml, Michal Strejcek, Jachym Suman, Ondrej Uhlik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Svornost mine in Jáchymov (Joachimstahl), Czech Republic, historically the world’s first radium mine and formerly a silver-uranium mine, is currently the deepest facility exploiting radon-saturated waters. These geologically ancient waters, approximately 13,000 years old, exhibit unique physicochemical properties and are generally considered inhospitable to life. Nevertheless, they harbor phylogenetically and evolutionarily distinct microorganisms. A previous culturomic survey yielded nine Gram-stain-negative, rod-shaped, aerobic bacterial isolates. In this study, a polyphasic taxonomic approach, including 16S rRNA gene phylogeny, core-genome analyses, and comparisons of genome indices such as average nucleotide identity (ANI), average amino acid identity (AAI), and percentage of conserved proteins (POCP), together with physiological and chemotaxonomic characterization, confirmed the taxonomic novelty at species level of these nine strains. The novel strains span five bacterial classes (α-, β-, and γ-Proteobacteria, Cytophagia, and Flavobacteriia) and eight genera, and are herein proposed as Hymenobacter abyssi J193T (= DSM 113871T =CCM 9301T), Rhizobacter abyssi J219T (= DSM 113781T =CCM 9293T), Aquabacterium abyssi J223T (= DSM 113847T =CCM 9303T), Aquincola abyssi J276T (= DSM 113828T =CCM 9294T), Flavobacterium abyssi J372T (= DSM 113872T =CCM 9299T), Phenylobacterium abyssi J367T (= DSM 113838T =CCM 9302T), Phenylobacterium fontinale J426T (= DSM 113839T =CCM 9296T), Mesorhizobium abyssi J428T (= DSM 114114T =CCM 9297T) and Pseudomonas fontinalis J452T (= DSM 113783T =CCM 9298T). These findings highlight the potential of radon-saturated ancient waters as a source of novel microbial diversity, offering insights into the evolution of bacteria under extreme, spatially isolated environmental conditions.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12866-026-05379-9.Keywords: Microbial diversity, Novel bacterial taxa, Phylogenetic novelty, Mineral springs, Radon-saturated waters

Background

Remote and pristine environments have recently gained increased attention as valuable sources of phylogenetically novel lineages and microorganisms possessing unique metabolic and ecological traits [1–4]. Such ecosystems, often characterized by extreme physical or chemical conditions, provide selective pressure that provokes microbial adaptation and diversification. However, the microbial diversity of such habitats remains largely unknown and many of the phylogenetic branches found in these habitats are represented by few or no cultured isolates.

One such intriguing site is the former silver and uranium mine in Jáchymov, Czech Republic, where four springs with elevated concentrations of radon and other dissolved elements create specific geochemical conditions favoring the growth of previously undescribed microbial taxa. From a previous culturomic survey targeting microbial diversity in Jáchymov radon springs, nine phylogenetically novel bacterial isolates were successfully obtained [5]. The isolates represent five bacterial classes α-, β-, and γ-Proteobacteria, Cytophagia, and Flavobacteriia and belong to eight genera: Hymenobacter, Mesorhizobium, Pseudomonas, Rhizobacter, Aquincola, Aquabacterium, Phenylobacterium and Flavobacterium.

Members of the genus Hymenobacter, fam. Hymenobacteraceae, are typically red or pink pigmented bacteria that are often resistant to desiccation and UV radiation [6, 7]. They are widely distributed in terrestrial and extreme environments, including cold deserts, permafrost [7–9], and irradiated habitats [6, 10]. Mesorhizobium, fam. Phyllobacteriaceae, includes bacteria that are commonly capable of nitrogen fixation and are mostly associated with plant roots or rhizosphere environments. They can also be found in bare soil and aquatic ecosystems [11–13].

The genera Pseudomonas (fam. Pseudomonadaceae), and Rhizobacter with Aquincola (fam. Burkholderiaceae) comprise metabolically versatile, motile, aerobic bacteria capable of degrading a variety of aromatic compounds and xenobiotics. They inhabit soil, water, rhizosphere, and host-associated niches where they play important roles in bioremediation, plant growth promotion and nitrogen fixation [14–19]. Filamentous bacteria of Aquabacterium [20–22], fam. Sphaerotilaceae; stalked/prosthecate cells of Phenylobacterium [23–25], fam. Caulobacteraceae; and yellow-pigmented members of Flavobacterium [26–28], fam. Flavobacteriaceae, are usually abundant in aquatic and soil habitats where they contribute to the degradation of complex organic polymers and biofilm formation.

In the present study, these nine novel Gram-stain negative isolates are subjected to comprehensive phenotypic, chemotaxonomic, and genomic characterization in order to clarify their taxonomical categorization, and ultimately, nine novel species are proposed, with these isolates as their type strains.

Methods

Site description and bacterial isolation

All the strains described in this study were isolated during a comprehensive microbial analysis of radon-saturated springs at the former silver-uranium Svornost mine in Jáchymov, Czech Republic (50°22′22.9″N 12°54′42.1″E). Strain J193T was isolated from the spring Agricola (water yield 10 l/min., temperature 29 °C, radioactivity 20 kBq/l); strains J219T, J223T and J276T from the spring Běhounek (300 l/min., 36 °C, 10 kBq/l); strains J367T, J372T were isolated from the spring C1 (30 l/min., 29 °C, 11 kBq/l) and strains J426T, J428T, J452T from the spring Curie (30 l/min., 29 °C, 5 kBq/l) by using an inoculum created by concentrating 20 l of every spring water into 25 ml. The detailed isolation process and subsequent strain acquisition is described in Kapinusova et al. 2022 [5].

Taxonomic classification of novel strains

16S rRNA gene phylogeny

Preliminary taxonomic assignments of novel strains were inferred from 16S rRNA gene phylogenetic analysis [29]. For genomic DNA extraction, strains were cultivated for 24–96 h on solid media according to strain preference (J193T, J367T, J372T, J426T, J452T on 1/10 PCA, 24–96 h, 28 °C; strains J219T, J223T, J276T on R2A, 24–48 h, 37 °C; strain J428T on R2A, 72 h, 28 °C). The isolated pure cultures were subsequently harvested, and the genomic DNA was extracted using the PureLink™ Genomic DNA Kit (Invitrogen) in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions.

Amplification of the 16S rRNA gene was performed using the universal primers 8–27F (5´-AGAGTTTGATCMTGGCTCAG-3´) [30] and 1509–1492R (5´-GYTACCTTGTTACGACTT-3´) [30]. The preparation of the reaction mix, PCR conditions and the sequencing procedure of the resulting amplicons are described in Kapinusova et al., 2022 [5]. All sequences were quality-checked, trimmed, and aligned using MEGA X [31]. Nearly complete 16S rRNA gene sequences (> 1300 bp; Table 1) were compared against the EzBioCloud 16S rRNA gene sequence database [32] to determine the closest phylogenetic relatives and assess sequence similarity.

The closest type strains based on the 16S rRNA gene sequence similarity

To elucidate the evolutionary relationships between the novel taxa and their closest type strains, more than 20 of the most similar 16S rRNA gene sequences were retrieved from the EzBioCloud database and aligned based on their secondary structures using EzEditor2 [33]. Phylogenetic trees were subsequently reconstructed in MEGA 11 [34] employing the Kimura two-parameter model [35] and the secondary structure-aligned sequences. Both Neighbor-Joining [36] and Maximum-Likelihood [37] algorithms were used to infer phylogenetic relationships. The robustness of the tree topologies was evaluated by bootstrap analysis with 1,000 replications, and the percentage of replicate trees in which the associated taxa clustered together was indicated. Evolutionary rate heterogeneity among sites was modeled using a discrete gamma distribution with five categories.

Genomic analysis

Genomic analysis was conducted as described by Kapinusova et al. [5]. Briefly, the high-quality DNA was processed for the whole genome library preparation according to Lopez-Echartea et al., 2021 [38]. The obtained libraries were pooled in equimolar concentrations prior to sequencing on an Oxford Nanopore Technologies MinION platform using a FLO-MIN106 flow cell. Guppy (v3.2.10) was used to perform the base-calling of nanopore sequencing signals and Porechop- and Filtlong-based for quality filtering. The quality-filtered reads (≤ 500 bp) were used for genome assembly using the Canu assembler (v1.7) [39]. The polishing of the Canu assemblies was carried out using Medaka (v1.4.3) (https://github.com/nanoporetech/medaka). CheckM, available on the Kbase server, was used to assess the quality and the completeness of the genome assemblies [40, 41]. Genome annotations for the inference of the functional potential of the bacteria were carried out using the combination of Prokka and PATRIC online servers [42–44]. The genome assembly and annotation processes are described in more detail elsewhere [5].

The phylogenetic relationships between the studied strains and their closest relatives were further inferred using the UBCG pipeline, which employs a core gene-based phylogenomic approach [45]. The UBCG analyses were based on a concatenated alignment of 92 conserved single-copy housekeeping genes [5]. To further assess the taxonomic novelty of the isolates, genome-wide similarity indices were calculated, including average nucleotide identity (OrthoANI), average amino acid identity (AAI), and the percentage of conserved proteins (POCP). These metrics were obtained using the EzGenome ANI calculator [46], EzAAI [47], and Galaxy Protologger [48].

Physiology and chemotaxonomy

Microscopic and macroscopic morphology

Macroscopic characteristics (color, shape and surface) of all strains were assessed on solid media (R2A or 1/10 PCA or 1/5 TSA according to each strain’s preference). Transmission electron microscopy was used to examine microscopic characteristics such as cell shape, cell size, presence of flagella and other extracellular structures. Standard Gram-staining procedures as well as the KOH string test were used for Gram-stain examination.

Determination of growth requirements

The temperature dependence of growth was studied on solid media inoculated and placed in different incubators (4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24, 28, 32, 37, 42, 45 °C) to determine the temperature range of each strain’s growth ability; and in liquid media cultured in a narrower temperature range (26, 28, 32, 34, 37 °C) for 24–72 h to determine the optimum growth temperature. Growth capability in the presence of NaCl (0.1, 0.5, 1, 1.5, 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5, 4, 5, 6, 7%w/v) was determined using a series of solid media with adjusted NaCl concentrations. Similarly, solid media with adjusted pH (using 0.5 M phosphate, citrate, or glycine buffer) were used to determine the pH optimum for growth (4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11). For strains that could grow in liquid media (all except J193T and J372T), solid media were replaced by liquid media of preferred composition with the same adjusted pH. Each experiment was carried out in two independent runs, each time in triplicates. Test results were evaluated 5 and 10 days after inoculation to determine if cells could grow under stress but with extended cultivation time. In order to examine anaerobic growth, inoculated medium plates of strain preference were cultivated under anerobic conditions using an anaerostat and AnaeroGen 2.5 l atmosphere generation system (ThermoFisher Scientific).

Enzymatic profile assessment and determination of substrate utilization capacity

The enzymatic profile was examined using the API ZYM kit (bioMérieux, France) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, for each strain, bacterial suspension (5 McFarland units) was added to each strip well. The inoculated strips were incubated at the temperature preferred by the strain tested (28–37 °C). Cultivation was terminated by adding reaction solutions and subtracting the color change. Instead of the recommended 4 h, all tests were evaluated after an extended period of time (1–3 days, depending on the growth rate of the strains). The oxidase test was performed with OXItest (Erba Lachema) by immersion of a test strip in the freshly grown colony. The catalase test was carried out as a reaction of the fresh culture with 3%v/v H2O2. Substrate assimilation was determined using a GEN III MicroPlate™ (Biolog, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. For each strain, a bacterial suspension (0.5 McFarland units) was prepared by mixing the freshly grown bacterial culture and the chromogenic medium supplied with the kit. Inoculated 96-well plates were incubated at the preferred temperature of each strain. The evaluation of the test was carried out every 24 h for one week.

Fatty acid methyl ester (FAME) analysis

To determine the FAME profile, each strain was inoculated onto several R2A or 1/5 TSA plates according to their preference and cultured at each strain’s optimum temperature for 24–96 h depending on growth rate. Once the cultures were grown, cells were harvested from all plates, washed with 0.9% saline solution, and lyophilized until 100 mg of freeze-dried cells were harvested. Extraction and gas chromatography analysis of fatty acids were carried out as described elsewhere [49]. Briefly, the lyophilized biomass was saponified in 10% potassium hydroxide in methanol at laboratory temperature overnight. The obtained fatty acid fraction was divided into an alkalic solution (pH 9) and diethyl ether in order to remove basic and neutral components. The aqueous phase with fatty acids was acidified to pH 2 and extracted with hexane and then methylated using boron trifluoride in methanol [50, 51]. All obtained GC mass spectra were scanned within the m/z range of 50–600. By comparison of retention times and fragmentation patterns with the standards, the structures of the FAMEs were confirmed.

Results and discussion

The taxonomic classification of novel strains

Whole-genome sequencing and comprehensive genomic analyses were performed for all strains, providing detailed insights into their genomic characteristics. The sizes of the genomes ranged from 3.53 to 6.38 Mbp. The G + C content was > 70% in two strains, with a maximum value of 71.7% in strain J223T, and > 60% in five strains, while in one strain the G + C content was below 50% (J372T, 42.4%), as detailed in Supplementary table S1. At the level of phyla, these nine strains were classified into Pseudomonadota (n = 7) and Bacteroidetes (n = 2). The 16S rRNA gene similarity of 6/9 strains to the closest type strains cataloged in the RefSeq RNA database (NCBI) NCBI database and EZBioCloud 16S rRNA gene sequence database was lower than the suggested species delineation cut-off of 98.65% [52] (Table 1). However, strains J193T, J276T and J452T could be assigned to the recently described type species based on 16S rRNA gene similarity > 98.7%. Specifically, strain J193T was related to Hymenobacter guriensis BT594T, strain J276T to Aquincola agrisoli MAHUQ-54T and strain J452T to Pseudomonas campi S1-A32-2T. Nevertheless, the ANI values of all strains compared to their relatives, using either the GTDB database or NCBI type strains as references, were substantially lower than the suggested taxa delineation threshold of > 95% (Table 2). The only exception was the strain J276T, sharing an ANI of > 95% with Aquincola tertiaricarbonis MIMtkpLc11, a species that has not been validly published. Thus, strain J276T still meets the above mentioned criterium as all other type strains. Furthermore, our analyses suggest that strain MIMtkpLc11 was misclassified as Aquincola tertiaricarbonis; even the authors of the original publication, which was a draft genome sequence announcement [53], acknowledged that the similarity between the 16S rRNA gene sequences of strains MIMtkpLc11 and A. tertiaricarbonis L10T is only 98.36%. This value is below the threshold for assigning two strains to the same species. In fact, the similarity between the 16S rRNA gene sequences of strains MIMtkpLc11 and Aquincola agrisoli MAHUQ-54T exceeds the reported value of 98.36%. In any case, our data indicate that strain MIMtkpLc11 should be classified in the same species as strain J276T, which is presented herein.

Phylogenetic and genomic analyses, including 16S rRNA sequencing, phylogeny reconstruction based on secondary structure of 16S rRNA gene (Supplementary figures S1-S3, S5, S7-S10), ANI, and POCP (Table 3), consistently indicate the taxonomic placement of the investigated strains. Specifically, strain J193T was classified in the genus Hymenobacter, strains J367T and J426T in the genus Phenylobacterium, strain J372T in the genus Flavobacterium, strain J428T in the genus Mesorhizobium and strain J452T in the genus Pseudomonas. Nonetheless, the core gene-based phylogenetic assessment revealed that all strains formed distinct branches from their related taxa (Supplementary figures S4 and S6 in this article; Figure S2 - Figure S11 published in Kapinusova et al. 2022 [5]). For strains J219T, J223T, J276T where the taxonomic categorization based on ANI and POCP did not clearly place them in an existing genus within the order Burkholderiales, the AAI analysis was performed (Table 4). Based on the results of all analyses, the type strain most closely related to J219T was classified as Rhizobacter gummiphilus NS21T, with 77.77% ANI, 58.97% POCP and 71.99% AAI. Thus, strain J219T was assigned to the genus Rhizobacter. However, none of the analyses provided a clear picture of the taxonomic classification of strains J223T and J276T. The affiliation of strain J223T to the genus Aquabacterium was therefore mainly based on the 16S rRNA gene secondary structure (Supplementary figure S3) and the core gene-based phylogenetic tree (Supplementary figure S4), in which strain J223T clustered with other members of the genus Aquabacterium. The same approach was applied to strain J276T, for which the 16S rRNA gene secondary structure (Supplementary figure S5) and the core gene-based phylogenetic tree (Supplementary figure S6) revealed clustering of J276T with other species of the genus Aquincola.

Physiology and chemotaxonomy

Strains J193T, J219T, J367T, J372T and J426T were non-motile rods, while strains J223T, J276T, J428T and J452T were motile rods or coccoid rods with single polar or multiple flagella (Fig. 1). The majority of the characterized strains were able to grow in liquid media. The preferred media were R2A and 1/5 Tryptic Soy Broth (TSB). For strains J219T and J452T, LB medium was also suitable. However, no growth was observed for J193T and J372T in any of the tested liquid media, even when supplemented with solid supports such as sea sand, glass beads, or low-concentration agar [54, 55]. All strains thrived on solid media. Specifically, 1/10 PCA and 1/5 TSA were preferred by strains J193T, J372T and J452T; 1/5 TSA by strains J367T and J426T; and R2A by strains J219T, J223T, J276T, J428T. The temperature range at which each strain grew corresponded to the temperature of the hot springs in which the strains naturally occur: 20–32 °C for strains J193T and J372T and 20–37 °C for the other strains (Table 5). Compared with the closest type strains of the Hymenobacter genus, which are mainly isolated from cold deserts or glaciers [7, 9, 56], strain J193T cannot thrive at temperatures as low as 4–10 °C (Supplementary table S2). Conversely, strain J219T exhibited growth at 44 °C (Supplementary table S3). Strains J223T and J276T were found to have preferred mesophilic temperatures similar to other representatives of the genera Aquabacterium (Supplementary table S4) and Aquincola (Supplementary table S5). However, no match was found with representatives of the genus Caldimonas and Calidifontimicrobium, which prefer temperatures of 50 °C or higher [57, 58]. Finally, strain J426T was able to grow at temperatures as low as 12 °C (Table 5, Supplementary table S6).

All of the novel Jáchymov strains exhibited the ability to grow within a wide pH range of 6–10 or 6–11. These findings are consistent with those of all the closest type strains, except for strain J372T, which showed greater resistance to higher pH values than its closest type representatives (Supplementary table S7). Only strains J219T and J223T exhibited resistance to the presence of NaCl in the medium at concentrations of ≤ 3.5%w/v (Supplementary table S3) and ≤ 4%w/v (Supplementary table S4), respectively. Strains J376T, J426T, J428T and J452T tolerated concentrations ranging from 1.5 to 2.5%w/v. The growth of strains J193T, J276T, and J372T required the lowest NaCl concentration of ≤ 1%w/v. When these tolerances were compared with those of the closest type strains, it was found that strains J219T, J223T, J367T and J426T were the only ones that were more tolerant than their closest relatives (Supplementary table S3, S4, S6). Conversely, strain J428T exhibited reduced tolerance to the presence of NaCl in comparison with other Mesorhizobium species (Supplementary table S8). No growth of any strain was observed under anaerobic conditions, even after a two-week cultivation period. Therefore, all of the novel strains characterized here are classified as aerobic.

The utilization of various substrates and basic enzymatic profile determination

According to the results of the API ZYM test, all Jáchymov strains produce esterase (C4) and leucine arylamidase (Table 5, Supplementary tables S2-S9). A positive, or at least weakly positive, reaction was confirmed even for alkaline phosphatase, esterase-lipase (C8) and naphthol-AS-BI-phosphohydrolase. Conversely, none of the strains can produce α-galactosidase or α-fucosidase. Strain J193T along with the closest Hymenobacter species were positive for alkaline phosphatase, leucin-arylamidase, valine-arylamidase, acid phosphatase and negative for α-chymotrypsin, β-galactosidase, β-glucuronidase, α-mannosidase and α-fucosidase. Strain J219T corresponded with the closest type species of the genus Rhizobacter that exhibited positive reaction for esterase (C4), esterase-lipase (C8), leucin-arylamidase, naphtol-AS-BI-phosphohydrolase and negative for trypsin, α-galactosidase, β-glucuronidase. The absence of β-glucuronidase, α-glucosidase, β-glucosidase, N-acetyl‑β‑glucosaminidase, α-mannosidase and α-fucosidase indicated the taxonomic similarity between strain J223T and representatives of Sphaerotilaceae, and Comamonadaceae (Supplementary table S4). Strain J276T and its closest Burkholderiaceae and Comamonadaceae type strains were positive for oxidase, esterase (C4), esterase-lipase (C8), leucine-arylamidase and naphthol-AS-BI‑phosphohydrolase, but negative for β-galactosidase. Strains J367T and J426T, similarly to their closest type Phenylobacterium species, produce alkaline phosphatase, but they cannot produce lipase (C14), α-galactosidase, β-glucuronidase or α-fucosidase. Strain J372T showed a positive reaction to leucine-arylamidase, but negative for α-chymotrypsin and N-acetyl‑β‑glucosaminidase. The same was true for the closest Flavobacterium type species [27, 59, 60]. Even though the strain J452T showed a narrow enzymatic profile, the positive reactions for esterase-lipase (C8), leucine-arylamidase, and negative reactions for α-chymotrypsin, α-galactosidase, β-galactosidase, β-glucuronidase, α-glucosidase, β-glucosidase, N-acetyl‑β‑glucosaminidase, α-mannosidase and α-fucosidase were consistent with the enzymatic profile of the closest Pseudomonas type species [14, 61, 62]. Only the strain J428T did not produce an enzyme that corresponded with those produced by Mesorhizobium species. This difference is most likely due to the different isolation sources, since other Mesorhizobium species are known to be associated with roots rather than being water-borne bacteria [11, 13, 63].

Substrate assimilation and basic sensitivity test to antimicrobial compounds, as determined using a GEN III MicroPlate™, showed that all strains except J276T had the common ability to utilize only a narrow spectrum of substrates (Table 5). Specifically, strain J193T was able to utilize only d-gluconic acid, glucuronamide, α-ketoglutaric acid, α-ketobutyric acid, acetoacetic acid, and was sensitive to all the antimicrobial compounds tested. Strain J223T also expressed poor metabolic activity, utilizing only d-fructose-6-PO4, α-ketoglutaric and acetoacetic acid. On the contrary, strain J276T exhibited a broad range of metabolic activities, utilizing the majority of the tested substrates. Strains J219T and J223T showed great resistance to a variety of antimicrobial compounds. Strain J219T was only sensitive to guanidine HCl, lithium chloride, and sodium bromate, whereas strain J223T was sensitive to 1% sodium lactate, lithium chloride, d-serine, potassium tellurite, and sodium bromate. For detailed information on the substrate assimilation ability of the Jáchymov strains and their closest type strains, refer to Supplementary tables S2–S9.

Fatty acid profile

Analysis of the fatty acid profile revealed a feature common to all the novel Jáchymov strains: the presence of palmitic acid (C16:0, Supplementary table S10). The highest concentrations were found in strain J193T, where it was also the most dominant acid, accounting for 52.8% of the total, followed by J223T, where it appeared as the second most abundant acid with 37.4% (Supplementary table S10). The concentration of palmitic acid was also high in other strains, namely J276T (29.4%), J219T (25.4%) and J367T (20.8%), while in strains J426T, J428T and J452T ranged between 13.5% and 16.6%. The only exception was strain J372T, for which C16:0 represented just 4.1% of the total profile. The significant presence of C16:0 in the Jáchymov strains can be explained by the fact that some bacteria use sphingolipids synthesized from palmitic acid to resist the oxidative stress [64–67], which is typical of Jáchymov radon waters.

Another most frequently found fatty acid in the Jáchymov strains, with the exception of strain J426T, was C16:1 ω7c, which had a concentration ranging from 1.5% to 39.1%. In strains J367T and J426T, C18:1 ω7c was the most abundant fatty acid, reaching 72.4% and 64.2% of the total, respectively. Together with C16:0, which was also an abundant fatty acid, this phenomenon proved to be typical of the genus Phenylobacterium [68, 69]. The major fatty acid in J428T was also C18:1 ω7c, and its high representation is also typical of other species of the genus Mesorhizobium [70–72]. Although the fatty acid composition of Pseudomonas species varies greatly, the dominance of C18:1 ω7c (42.5%) in strain J452T was consistent with those of Pseudomonas flavocrustae with 46.6% [73] and Pseudomonas glareae with 36.5% [74]. Strain J372T had the most distinct profile compared to all Jáchymov strains. However, its profile corresponded to those of other Flavobacterium strains. For example, iso-C15:0 was the most abundant fatty acid in both J372T (47.9%) and Flavobacterium okayamense [75]. Anteiso-C15:0, found in strain J372T at a concentration of 10.1%, was one of the major fatty acids in Flavobacterium soyangense [76]. The presence of fatty acids cyclo-C17:0, C16:0, C18:1 ω7c and C16:1 ω7c in strain J219T corresponded to the fatty acid profile of other Rhizobacterium species [17, 77, 78]. In addition, the profiles of strain J193T and other strains of the genus Hymenobacter showed a clear matching pattern when C16:0 and C16:1 ω7c were present. The taxonomic classification of J276T to the genus Aquincola was supported by the presence of C16:1 ω7c as a major fatty acid in this strain and in the species A. rivuli [79] and A. amnicola [80]. Finally, the fatty acid profile of strain J223T, dominated by C18:0, differed from other Aquabacterium members, where this fatty acid was either completely absent or present in only small percentages.

Final categorization of Jáchymov novel strains to the existing genera

Based on the results of the 16S rRNA gene phylogeny reconstructions, ANI, and POCP similarities to the closest type strains, here we propose strain J193T as a novel member of the Hymenobacter genus; strain J372T as a novel member of the Flavobacterium genus; strain J367T together with J426T as novel members of the Phenylobacterium genus; strain J428T as a novel member of the Mesorhizobium genus; and strain J452T as a novel member of Pseudomonas genus.

According to ANI, POCP, and AAI similarities, as well as the main chemotaxonomic features (such as the basic enzymatic profile and fatty acid composition), here we propose strain J219T as a novel member of genus Rhizobacter.

According to 16S rRNA gene and core genome phylogeny reconstructions, as well as physiological and enzymatic profiling, we propose that strain J223T is a novel member of the Aquabacterium genus.

Finally, as indicated by the 16S rRNA gene and core-genome phylogeny reconstructions, POCP and AAI similarities, together with chemotaxonomic features of enzymatic profile and fatty acid composition, here we propose strain J276T as novel member of the Aquincola genus.

Description of Hymenobacter abyssi sp. nov

Hymenobacter abyssi (a.bys’si. L. gen. n. abyssi, from the depth). Cells are Gram-stain-negative, non-spore-forming, non-motile, 1.1 μm wide and 2.7 μm long rods (Fig. 1a). After 5 days of cultivation on 1/5 TSA or 1/10 PCA medium, cells form 4–6 mm large, mucoid, pink colonies. No growth is observed in liquid media. Growth occurs in a temperature range of 20–32 °C with optimum 30 °C, on media with pH 6–11, and in a presence of ≤ 0.5%w/v NaCl. The bacterium is oxidase and catalase negative. In the API ZYM test it is positive for alkaline phosphatase, esterase (C4), esterase lipase (C8), lipase (C14), leucin-arylamidase, valine-arylamidase, cystine-arylamidase, acid phosphatase, naphthol-AS-BI-phosphohydrolase, α-glucosidase and N-acetyl-β-glucosaminidase activity, but negative for α-chymotrypsin, α-galactosidase, β-galactosidase, β-glucuronidase, α-mannosidase and α-fucosidase. Weak activity was observed for trypsin and β-glucosidase. The utilized substrates are d-gluconic acid, glucuronamide, α-ketoglutaric acid, α-ketobutyric acid and acetoacetic acid. C16:0 (52.8%) and C16:1 ω7c (39.1%) acids are the major fatty acids. The genome size is 5.664 Mbp, with a DNA G + C content of 59.96%. The type strain is J193T (= DSM 113871T =CCM 9301T).

Description of Rhizobacter abyssi sp. nov

Rhizobacter abyssi (a.bys’si. L. gen. n. abyssi, from the depth). Cells are Gram-stain-negative, non-spore-forming, non-motile and 0.5 μm wide x 1–1.6 μm long rods (Fig. 1b). Colonies grown on R2A medium are after 2 days of cultivation yellowish, circular, and 1–2 mm in diameter. Growth occurs in a temperature range of 12–44 °C with optimum 37 °C, on media with pH 6.5–10, and in a presence of ≤ 3.5%w/v (optimum 0.5-1%w/v) NaCl. The bacterium is oxidase positive and catalase negative. In the API ZYM test it is positive for alkaline phosphatase, esterase (C4), esterase lipase (C8), leucin-arylamidase, naphthol-AS-BI-phosphohydrolase, α-glucosidase, but negative for valine-arylamidase, cystine-arylamidase, trypsin, α-chymotrypsin, acid phosphatase, α-galactosidase, β-galactosidase, β-glucuronidase, β-glucosidase, N-acetyl-β-glucosaminidase, α-mannosidase and α-fucosidase activity. Weak activity was observed for lipase (C14). Using Biolog GEN III test, the bacterium assimilates d-turanose acid, d-glucose-6-PO4, d-fructose-6-PO4, l-histidine, d-glucoronic acid, glucuronamide, α-keto-glutaric acid, d-malic acid, tween 40, α-hydroxybutyric acid, β-hydroxy-d,l-butyric acid, acetoacetic acid and formic acid. The bacterium is resistant to 1% sodium lactate, lincomycin, vancomycin, nalidixic acid, aztreonam, fusidic acid, rifamycin SV, tetrazolium violet, sodium butyrate, d-serine, minocycline, tetrazolium blue, potassium tellurite. Cyclo-C17:0 (32.6%), C16:0 (25.4%), C18:1 ω7c (16.7%) and C16:1 ω7c (16.5%) acids are the major fatty acids. The genome size is 5.524 Mbp, with a DNA G + C content of 67.70%. The type strain is J219T (= DSM 113781T =CCM 9293T).

Description of Aquabacterium abyssi sp. nov

Aquabacterium abyssi (a.bys’si. L. gen. n. abyssi, from the depth). Cells are Gram-stain-negative, rod shaped, non-spore-forming and motile by means of single-polar flagellum. C18:0 (39.6%), C16:0 (37.4%), cyclo-C17:0 (17.3%) acids are the major fatty acids. The genome size is 4.656 Mbp, with a DNA G + C content of 71.73%. The type strain is J223T (= DSM 113847T =CCM 9303T).
