Associations of Ginkgo biloba extract exposure with 12-month cognitive trajectories in amyloid PET–positive mild cognitive impairment and Alzheimer’s disease: a pooled retrospective cohort study
Authors: YoungSoon Yang, Yong Tae Kwak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlzheimer’s disease (AD) and mild cognitive impairment (MCI) show considerable heterogeneity in treatment response, underscoring the need for individualized therapeutic approaches. Ginkgo biloba extract is commonly used in cognitive disorders, but its efficacy in amyloid biomarker-confirmed populations remains unclear. To evaluate the association between Ginkgo biloba extract exposure and 12-month cognitive and functional change patterns in amyloid PET–positive MCI/AD, and to assess whether baseline plasma MDS-OAβ (amyloid oligomerization tendency) is associated with cognitive trajectory subgroup membership.MethodsIn this 12-month retrospective study, 165 drug-naïve amyloid PET–positive patients (83 with documented Ginkgo biloba extract use in routine care and 82 without documented Ginkgo use) were assessed for changes in Korean Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE-2), Clinical Dementia Rating–Sum of Boxes (CDR-SB), Korean Instrumental Activities of Daily Living (K-IADL), and plasma MDS-OAβ levels. K-means clustering was used to define three cognitive trajectory subgroups, and exploratory one-vs-rest adjusted logistic regression analyses were performed to examine baseline variables associated with trajectory group membership.ResultsThree cognitive trajectories were identified: Improver (n = 36), Stabler (n = 113), and Decliner (n = 16). Compared with patients without documented Ginkgo use, patients with documented Ginkgo exposure showed significantly greater cognitive gains (ΔK-MMSE-2 + 1.42 vs. − 0.30, p = 0.012), less IADL decline (p < 0.001), and greater reductions in MDS-OAβ levels (p < 0.001). Baseline plasma MDS-OAβ did not significantly differ among the trajectory groups and was not interpreted as a robust independent predictor of trajectory membership. Ginkgo use differed significantly across trajectory groups and was least frequent in the Decliner group.ConclusionsIn this amyloid PET–positive MCI/AD cohort, Ginkgo biloba extract exposure was associated with more favorable 12-month cognitive, functional, and plasma MDS-OAβ changes. However, baseline MDS-OAβ should be regarded as an exploratory biomarker rather than a validated predictor of improvement or Ginkgo-related benefit.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12906-026-05469-1.

Background

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the leading cause of dementia and a growing global health burden as societies age. Although monoclonal antibodies that target amyloid or tau now offer hope of slowing pathology, most patients still rely on symptomatic therapies—chiefly cholinesterase inhibitors (ChEIs) such as donepezil—which yield only modest, short-lived cognitive benefits and show wide inter-individual variability [1]. This therapeutic gap has rekindled interest in Ginkgo biloba, a botanical extract with antioxidative, anti-inflammatory, vasoregulatory, anti-apoptotic, and potentially anti-amyloidogenic actions [2–4].

Evidence for Ginkgo remains mixed [5]. Large prevention trials in cognitively normal elders (GEM, GUIDAGE) were negative—likely because many participants lacked underlying AD pathology and follow-up was limited [6, 7]. By contrast, later studies restricted to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) or mild-to-moderate dementia have been more encouraging. Kandiah et al. [8] showed that Ginkgo enhanced donepezil’s effects on cognition and neuropsychiatric symptoms; Özge et al. [9] and García-Alberca et al. [10] reported additive cognitive gains when Ginkgo was combined with standard pharmacotherapy in MCI and early AD. Our group has added two amyloid biomarker-confirmed studies. In drug-naïve AD, adding Ginkgo to donepezil produced larger 12-month improvements on the Korean Mini-Mental State Examination (K-MMSE-2) and greater reductions in plasma oligomeric amyloid-β (MDS-OAβ) than donepezil alone [11].

A separate amyloid-positive MCI cohort showed superior cognitive and daily-function preservation with Ginkgo monotherapy versus other cognitive enhancers, again accompanied by a marked MDS-OAβ decline [12]. Collectively, these data suggest that Ginkgo may confer benefit across the AD continuum—especially when AD pathology is biomarker-verified. Yet therapeutic heterogeneity persists: even in biomarker-defined populations, only a subset improves. Precision medicine therefore now asks not merely whether a treatment works but for whom it works. Identifying patient-level modifiers of response is crucial for tailoring care and enriching trials. Plasma MDS-OAβ is a promising candidate biomarker. This enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)-based assay quantifies the tendency of circulating Aβ monomers to oligomerize—a dynamic step closely linked to neurotoxicity—providing information that static Aβ42 levels cannot [13, 14]. Our prior work showed that reductions in MDS-OAβ paralleled cognitive gains with Ginkgo, hinting at mechanistic relevance [11]. Conceptually, we viewed plasma MDS-OAβ as an index of an individual’s amyloid oligomerization tendency—an early, dynamic step closely linked to neurotoxicity—which may differ even among amyloid PET–positive patients. We hypothesized that Ginkgo biloba extract could modulate this oligomerization tendency through complementary neuroprotective and potentially anti-aggregation actions reported in prior studies, thereby reducing oligomer-related toxicity and translating into heterogeneous short-term clinical courses. Under this working model, changes in MDS-OAβ may provide exploratory information on amyloid oligomerization dynamics during follow-up; however, whether baseline MDS-OAβ identifies patients more likely to benefit from Ginkgo requires formal biomarker-by-treatment interaction analyses.

Therefore, in this retrospective registry-based study, we aimed to examine whether Ginkgo biloba extract exposure in routine clinical care was associated with 12-month cognitive and functional trajectory patterns in amyloid PET–positive MCI and AD patients. We used trajectory-defined subgroups, an established approach for capturing heterogeneity in longitudinal clinical patterns [15], rather than defining response solely by predefined minimal clinically important difference thresholds, which may vary across outcomes and clinical contexts in Alzheimer’s disease [16, 17]. We also explored whether baseline plasma MDS-OAβ, a marker of amyloid oligomerization tendency, was associated with subsequent trajectory subgroup membership. By integrating biomarker-confirmed AD status with trajectory-defined clinical heterogeneity, this study sought to provide hypothesis-generating evidence for more individualized therapeutic stratification in early AD.

Methods

Participants and study design

This retrospective observational study used data from the Soonchunhyang Dementia Registry, an institutional database maintained by the Dementia Clinic at Soonchunhyang University College of Medicine since March 2020. The present analysis pooled participants from two retrospective, registry-based source cohorts conducted within the Soonchunhyang Dementia Registry: one cohort of amyloid PET–positive patients with mild cognitive impairment and one cohort of amyloid PET–positive patients with mild Alzheimer’s dementia. These two source cohorts were mutually exclusive, and no participant was included in both cohorts. Before pooling, patient identifiers and registry records were reviewed to confirm the absence of duplicate or overlapping participants. The detailed screening, exclusion, and inclusion process for the two retrospective source cohorts and the final pooled cohort is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1. The registry systematically collects clinical and neuropsychological data from patients undergoing dementia evaluation, including demographic information, medical and neurological history, laboratory tests (e.g., apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype), structural neuroimaging, and neuropsychological test batteries. The index visit/date was defined as the date of the baseline cognitive and clinical assessment, at which K-MMSE-2, CDR-SB, K-IADL, and plasma MDS-OAβ were measured. Inclusion and exclusion criteria were applied at this index visit, and patients whose index visit occurred between February 2020 and May 2025 were screened for eligibility. Eligible participants met the following inclusion criteria: (1) treatment-naïve at the index visit; (2) met the National Institute of Neurological and Communicative Disorders and Stroke and the Alzheimer’s Disease and Related Disorders Association (NINCDS-ADRDA) criteria [18] for probable Alzheimer’s disease or diagnosis of MCI according to Petersen et al. criteria [19]; (3) were amyloid PET-positive on 18 F-FC119S PET/CT; and (4) had complete clinical and biomarker data for a 12-month follow-up period. Patients were excluded if they had major psychiatric or neurological comorbidities, poor adherence, or incomplete follow-up. Documented donepezil use was observed almost exclusively among patients diagnosed with Alzheimer’s dementia. Therefore, documented medication use may reflect clinical judgment, patient/caregiver preference, and other factors related to prognosis.

Ginkgo exposure and control definition

Ginkgo exposure was defined retrospectively based on medical records. Patients were categorized as the Ginkgo-exposed group if they had documented use of Ginkgo biloba extract 240 mg/day during routine clinical care from the index visit through the 12-month follow-up assessment. Patients without documented Ginkgo biloba extract use during the same period were categorized as the non-Ginkgo control group. Medication exposure and continuity were verified through prescription records and follow-up medical records.

Clinical and functional assessments

Primary clinical outcomes included cognitive, functional, and biomarker measures. The main 12-month outcome measures were changes from baseline to follow-up in K-MMSE-2, CDR-SB, K-IADL, and plasma MDS-OAβ. Cognitive function was assessed using the K-MMSE-2. Global disease severity was measured using CDR-SB. To evaluate functional status in instrumental activities of daily living (IADLs), we used the Korean Instrumental Activities of Daily Living Index (K-IADL). This ADL tool assesses a patient’s independence in areas such as shopping, managing finances, using transportation, and medication management. The K-IADL has been validated in Korean dementia populations and provides a quantitative measure of functional decline that complements cognitive assessments [20]. These evaluations were conducted at baseline and again after approximately 12 months. Changes in scores from baseline to follow-up were calculated for each outcome measure, including K-MMSE-2, CDR-SB, K-IADL, and MDS-OAβ levels (e.g., K-MMSE-2 Δ, CDR-SB Δ, K-IADL Δ, MDS-OAβ Δ). Baseline assessments also included years of education, sex, age, number of APOΕ ε4 alleles, and plasma MDS-OAβ levels.

Plasma MDS-OAβ measurement

Amyloid oligomerization tendency was assessed using the Multimer Detection System for Oligomerized Aβ (MDS-OAβ), a proprietary ELISA-based assay. This test measures the potential of plasma Aβ monomers to form toxic oligomers—a dynamic biomarker distinct from static measures like Aβ42 concentration. Plasma samples were thawed at 37 °C for 15 min, incubated with synthetic Aβ peptides (PeopleBio Inc.) for 48 h at 37 °C, and processed in 96-well plates alongside standard curves. Chemiluminescence substrate (Rockland Immunochemicals Inc.) was added and read on a Victor 3 spectrophotometer. Chemiluminescence signals were first recorded as relative luminescence units (RLU), and final MDS-OAβ values were calculated as concentration-equivalent values in ng/mL using the assay standard curve according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cognitive trajectory classification via clustering

Cognitive and functional trajectories over 12 months were analyzed using k-means clustering applied to z-score–standardized change scores in K-MMSE-2 and CDR-SB. Because the registry provided two primary time points, baseline and 12 months, model-based longitudinal trajectory approaches (e.g., latent class growth analysis, group-based trajectory modeling, and growth mixture modeling) that estimate trajectory shapes across three or more repeated measures were not well suited to the data structure. Accordingly, k-means clustering was used to identify multi-outcome 12-month change patterns in an assumption-lean manner. Standardization was performed to ensure comparability between K-MMSE-2 and CDR-SB change scores. The optimal number of clusters was evaluated using the elbow method based on the total within-cluster sum of squared errors across candidate k values. After clustering, descriptive labels were assigned according to the direction and magnitude of standardized K-MMSE-2 and CDR-SB changes. The resulting cluster assignments were subsequently used for descriptive group comparisons and exploratory one-vs-rest adjusted logistic regression analyses.

Statistical analyses

Continuous variables were compared using independent t-tests or one-way ANOVA, while Chi-square tests were used for categorical variables. Between-group comparisons (e.g., Ginkgo vs. non-Ginkgo, or across trajectory classes) assessed differences in demographic, cognitive, functional, and biomarker variables. To address potential baseline imbalance and confounding by indication between the Ginkgo-exposed and non-Ginkgo control groups, inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) based on propensity scores was performed as a supplementary analysis. Propensity scores were estimated using clinically relevant baseline variables, including age, sex, education, APOE ε4 allele count, baseline K-MMSE-2, CDR, CDR-SB, K-IADL, and baseline MDS-OAβ. Covariate balance before and after IPTW was assessed using absolute standardized mean differences. IPTW-weighted comparisons of 12-month outcome changes were also performed using weighted linear regression with robust standard errors. The clustering procedure is described in the Cognitive Trajectory Classification section. As an exploratory analysis, we performed separate one-vs-rest adjusted logistic regression analyses to examine baseline variables associated with membership in each trajectory group. In each model, one trajectory group was compared with the remaining two groups combined. Because these analyses involved multiple exploratory contrasts and the Decliner group was small, the results were interpreted cautiously and were not used as confirmatory predictor models. Independent variables included: age, sex, education years, APOΕ ε4 allele count, baseline K-MMSE-2, CDR, CDR-SB, K-IADL, MDS-OAβ, and Ginkgo use. Results were reported as regression coefficients (β), odds ratios (ORs), 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and p-values. All statistical computations were conducted using SPSS version 24.0 (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA).

Reporting guideline

This observational study was reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement. A completed STROBE checklist is provided as Additional File 5.

Results

Comparison of baseline characteristics and 12-month clinical changes according to Ginkgo use

A total of 165 amyloid PET-positive patients were included in this study, 83 patients with documented Ginkgo biloba use (Ginkgo group) and 82 patients without documented Ginkgo use (non-Ginkgo group). Baseline demographics and clinical variables were comparable between the two groups (Table 1). The mean age was 68.8 ± 10.1 years in the Ginkgo group and 70.2 ± 9.4 years in the non-Ginkgo group (p = 0.361). The proportion of female participants, years of education, APOΕ ε4 gene copy number, baseline K-MMSE-2 scores, CDR, CDR-SB, K-IADL, and plasma MDS-OAβ levels did not significantly differ between groups. Over the 12-month follow-up period, the Ginkgo group showed significantly more favorable changes in K-MMSE-2 (+ 1.42 ± 4.11 vs. − 0.30 ± 4.57, p = 0.012), K-IADL (–0.10 ± 0.22 vs. 0.01 ± 0.14, p < 0.001), and plasma MDS-OAβ (–0.12 ± 0.11 vs. − 0.00 ± 0.05, p < 0.001) compared to the non-Ginkgo group. Differences in changes in CDR and CDR-SB were not statistically significant between the two groups. In the supplementary IPTW analysis, measured baseline covariates were well balanced between the Ginkgo-exposed and non-Ginkgo control groups after weighting, with all post-weighting absolute standardized mean differences below 0.10 (Supplementary Table 1). IPTW-weighted outcome analyses showed findings generally consistent with the unweighted analyses. Ginkgo exposure remained significantly associated with greater K-MMSE-2 improvement, less K-IADL worsening, and greater MDS-OAβ reduction. CDR remained non-significant, whereas CDR-SB reached statistical significance after weighting (Supplementary Table 2).

Trajectory-based cognitive classification

To better capture heterogeneity in cognitive outcomes, K-means clustering analysis was performed using 12-month changes in K-MMSE-2 and CDR-SB. Based on the elbow method (Fig. 1), a three-cluster solution was selected. The clusters were labeled as Improver (Cluster 1, n = 36), Stabler (Cluster 2, n = 113), and Decliner (Cluster 3, n = 16) (Fig. 2). As shown in Table 2, the Improver group demonstrated a mean K-MMSE-2 gain of + 6.19 and a mean CDR-SB reduction of − 3.19 over 12 months. The Stabler group exhibited minimal changes (ΔK-MMSE-2 = − 0.12, ΔCDR-SB = − 0.01), while the Decliner group showed significant deterioration (ΔK-MMSE-2 = − 7.25, ΔCDR-SB = + 5.69). These outcome patterns are consistent with the descriptive labels assigned to each cluster.

Clinical, biomarker, and ginkgo exposure differences by trajectory group

The clinical characteristics of each trajectory group are summarized in Table 3. Baseline K-MMSE-2 scores differed significantly among groups (p < 0.001), with the Improver group starting at a lower cognitive level (18.8 ± 3.9) compared to the Stabler (26.5 ± 2.8) and Decliner (23.7 ± 3.3) groups. Similarly, the Improver group had significantly higher baseline CDR scores than both the Stabler and Decliner groups, indicating greater disease severity at baseline. K-IADL scores also varied significantly across groups (p < 0.001), with the Improver group showing the greatest degree of functional impairment at baseline. Despite their more severe clinical status at baseline, this group demonstrated relatively favorable changes in cognitive and functional outcomes over time. Baseline plasma MDS-OAβ levels did not significantly differ among the groups (p = 0.413). Importantly, baseline MDS-OAβ was numerically lowest in the Improver group and highest in the Decliner group; therefore, the descriptive data did not support interpreting higher baseline MDS-OAβ as a marker of subsequent improvement. However, changes over 12 months were numerically more favorable in the Improver and Stabler groups compared to the Decliner group, although these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Ginkgo use by trajectory group

Ginkgo use differed significantly across the three trajectory groups (p = 0.013). Specifically, 44.4% of the Improver group and 56.6% of the Stabler group had documented Ginkgo use, compared to only 18.8% in the Decliner group. Post hoc pairwise comparisons revealed that Ginkgo use was significantly more common in the Stabler group compared to the Decliner group (p = 0.030), suggesting that documented Ginkgo use was less frequent among patients in the Decliner trajectory.

Predictors of trajectory group membership

Before interpreting the exploratory regression models, multicollinearity diagnostics were performed for the baseline severity variables (Supplementary Table 3). Exploratory regression analyses were performed to examine baseline variables associated with trajectory group membership (Supplementary Table 4). The fully adjusted model included age, sex, education, APOΕ ε4 allele count, baseline K-MMSE-2, CDR, CDR-SB, K-IADL, MDS-OAβ, and Ginkgo use. In these exploratory models, baseline K-MMSE-2 was associated with Stabler and Improver membership, and documented Ginkgo use showed an inverse association with Decliner membership. Baseline MDS-OAβ was not interpreted as a robust predictor of trajectory membership because the descriptive comparisons showed no significant group difference and the regression estimates were imprecise. The regression results are presented as exploratory analyses.

Discussion

This study aimed to evaluate the clinical relevance of Ginkgo biloba use in patients with amyloid PET-positive Alzheimer’s disease (AD), particularly focusing on its association with cognitive and functional trajectories over a 12-month period. By applying trajectory analysis rather than a conventional MCID-anchored approach, this study attempted to identify patient subgroups (Improver, Stabler, Decliner) and explore baseline variables associated with these cognitive patterns, with a special focus on plasma oligomerized Aβ (MDS-OAβ) levels and Ginkgo use.

Baseline measured covariates were generally comparable between the Ginkgo-exposed and non-Ginkgo groups, and supplementary IPTW analysis further supported adequate balance in measured baseline characteristics. However, follow-up data showed that the Ginkgo group had more favorable 12-month changes in K-MMSE-2, K-IADL, and plasma MDS-OAβ levels than the non-Ginkgo group. These findings suggest that Ginkgo exposure in routine clinical care was associated with more favorable cognitive, functional, and biomarker change patterns over 12 months.

Trajectory analysis further revealed three distinct clusters based on cognitive and functional outcomes: Improver, Stabler, and Decliner. A trajectory-based approach may be useful in this setting because cognitive change in early AD is multidimensional and may not be fully captured by a single MCID threshold. By incorporating correlated changes in K-MMSE-2 and CDR-SB, this approach can identify clinically interpretable patterns of improvement, stability, and decline. However, because the classification was based on two time points, the resulting groups should be interpreted as data-driven 12-month change patterns rather than definitive long-term disease trajectories. This approach offers several advantages. First, it respects the longitudinal and multidimensional nature of AD progression, incorporating both cognitive and functional changes. Second, it allows data-driven identification of response subtypes without requiring predefined thresholds. Third, comparing baseline characteristics across trajectory groups helps identify variables that differ across subgroups and may be associated with treatment responsiveness. Exploratory adjusted regression analyses were performed to examine baseline variables associated with trajectory group membership. However, because baseline severity differed substantially across trajectory groups and the Improver group had greater room for improvement, individual baseline severity coefficients were interpreted cautiously rather than as confirmatory predictors. In addition, given the lower baseline cognitive scores and greater baseline clinical severity of the Improver group, regression to the mean and measurement variability may have partly contributed to the observed improvement in this trajectory. Because this model included multiple correlated baseline severity measures and the Decliner group was small, the analysis was considered exploratory rather than confirmatory. In essence, trajectory analysis enables us to move beyond the binary question of whether Ginkgo “works” to a more refined question: “Which patients are most likely to benefit, and what distinguishes them?” The mean K-MMSE-2 and CDR-SB changes for each group strongly supported the labeling of these clusters. Notably, Ginkgo intake was highest in the Stabler group and lowest in the Decliner group, with a statistically significant overall group difference. Post hoc analysis confirmed significantly higher Ginkgo use in the Stabler group than in the Decliner group (p = 0.030), which supports the notion that Ginkgo use was associated with less decline.

Exploratory adjusted regression analysis was performed to examine baseline variables associated with cognitive trajectory group membership. Ginkgo use showed an inverse association with Decliner trajectory membership, although this finding should be interpreted cautiously. Baseline plasma MDS-OAβ did not significantly differ among trajectory groups and was numerically lowest in the Improver group. Therefore, the present data do not support baseline MDS-OAβ as a robust predictor of improvement or trajectory membership. In the revised exploratory fully adjusted one-vs-rest logistic regression models, baseline K-MMSE-2 was associated with Stabler and Improver trajectory membership, whereas documented Ginkgo use showed an inverse association with Decliner trajectory membership. However, these findings should be interpreted cautiously because the analyses were exploratory, involved multiple one-vs-rest contrasts, and the Decliner group was small. Accordingly, baseline MDS-OAβ and individual coefficients from the fully adjusted models should be regarded as exploratory rather than confirmatory predictors of trajectory membership or Ginkgo-related benefit.

These results suggest that Ginkgo exposure may be associated with more favorable cognitive trajectories, whereas the role of baseline MDS-OAβ remains exploratory and should be interpreted cautiously. While donepezil is known to stabilize symptoms in early AD, the additional effect of Ginkgo appears to provide cognitive benefit or at least better maintenance in select individuals. The significant reduction in MDS-OAβ among Ginkgo users suggests a possible mechanistic role in modulating amyloid-related neurotoxicity. This aligns with previous preclinical studies indicating that Ginkgo extract reduces Aβ aggregation and oxidative stress [21, 22], and may improve mitochondrial function [23]. The inclusion of instrumental ADL measures also adds to the robustness of this study. While cognitive tests like K-MMSE-2 and CDR assess mental status, changes in K-IADL provide insight into real-world functional outcomes, which are often more meaningful to patients and caregivers. The Ginkgo group not only showed better cognitive outcomes but also less functional decline, which strengthens the argument for its utility.

Despite these strengths, certain limitations must be acknowledged. First, the study’s observational and retrospective nature limits causal inference. Although the groups were largely comparable at baseline and IPTW was used to improve balance in measured baseline covariates and to provide supplementary weighted outcome analyses, documented Ginkgo use was non-randomized and determined by clinician judgment and patient/caregiver preference, raising the possibility of confounding by indication and residual confounding. Therefore, residual confounding due to unmeasured factors cannot be excluded. Such factors may include motivation, adherence behavior, health-seeking behavior, or engagement in daily activities, any of which could have contributed to better cognitive and functional maintenance independently of a direct pharmacological effect. This treatment-by-indication bias may have influenced both treatment selection and 12-month outcomes (e.g., clinicians may have been less likely to prescribe Ginkgo to patients perceived as likely to decline). Therefore, while Ginkgo use was associated with better outcomes, the findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of a causal treatment effect. The fully adjusted regression model should also be interpreted cautiously because the Decliner group was small, with only 16 participants and 3 Ginkgo users. Thus, the inverse association between Ginkgo use and Decliner membership may have been influenced by sparse events and overfitting, and should be regarded as exploratory. Donepezil use was not included as a baseline predictor because it was closely tied to diagnostic status and disease stage, being used mainly in patients with AD dementia rather than MCI. Including donepezil use could therefore have introduced confounding by disease stage and treatment indication. Secondly, although plasma MDS-OAβ appears promising as a biomarker, its clinical adoption remains limited. The MDS-OAβ assay is relatively novel, and although previous studies have validated its diagnostic utility [24], its prognostic or therapeutic monitoring value requires further longitudinal validation. In the present analysis, baseline MDS-OAβ did not robustly predict trajectory membership. Its prognostic or treatment-modifying value therefore remains exploratory and requires further validation in larger prospective studies. Third, the absence of a randomized, placebo-controlled design limits causal inference. Treatment decisions were made in routine clinical settings, and although this introduces variability, it also enhances the ecological validity of the findings. Lastly, the generalizability of these findings may be limited to similar populations, i.e., patients with early-stage, amyloid PET-confirmed AD who are drug-naïve and receiving care in a specialized dementia clinic. Broader application across different ethnicities, healthcare systems, and disease stages will require replication and expansion.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this study provides evidence that Ginkgo biloba use is associated with favorable cognitive and functional outcomes over 12 months in amyloid-positive patients with early-stage Alzheimer’s disease, including those with mild cognitive impairment. The use of trajectory analysis provided a refined description of heterogeneous 12-month clinical change patterns and allowed exploratory assessment of baseline variables associated with trajectory group membership. Ginkgo exposure was associated with more favorable 12-month cognitive, functional, and plasma MDS-OAβ changes, and Ginkgo use was least frequent in the Decliner trajectory group. However, baseline MDS-OAβ did not robustly predict trajectory membership and should not be interpreted as a validated marker of Ginkgo-related benefit in this study. These findings suggest that Ginkgo exposure may be associated with favorable 12-month cognitive, functional, and biomarker changes in amyloid PET–positive MCI/AD. Future prospective studies with formal biomarker-by-treatment interaction analyses are needed to determine whether amyloid oligomerization tendency can identify patients more likely to benefit from Ginkgo therapy.
