Bifidobacterium bifidum enhances hepatic mitochondrial β-oxidation and ameliorates MAFLD in a high-fat diet rat model
Authors: Fatemeh Dashti, Ava Bolandparvaz, Anahita Panji, Zahra Hojatifar, Mahdi Alinejad, Mahtab Mehboodi, Mohammad Hassan Maleki, Afsaneh Mirshekari, Elham Nadimi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), recently reclassified as metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD), is closely linked to mitochondrial dysfunction and impaired lipid metabolism. Bifidobacterium bifidum has emerged as a promising probiotic candidate for restoring metabolic balance, yet its mitochondrial-targeted mechanisms remain underexplored. This study investigates the role of B. bifidum in modulating hepatic mitochondrial β-oxidation pathways and key transcriptional regulators involved in fatty acid metabolism. MAFLD was induced in male Sprague-Dawley rats using a high-fat diet and streptozotocin (STZ). Following disease induction, B. bifidum was administered over two treatment durations (6 and 14 weeks). Liver function tests, lipid profiles, and stereological analyses were performed, and hepatic gene expression of UCP2, CPT1A, PGC-1α, PPAR-α, and PPAR-γ was evaluated using quantitative RT-PCR. B. bifidum treatment significantly reduced serum triglycerides, total cholesterol, and LDL-C levels, while showing a non-significant upward trend in HDL-C levels. Gene expression analysis revealed that B. bifidum restored downregulated PGC-1α, CPT1A, and PPAR-α expression and normalized elevated UCP2 and PPAR-γ levels, suggesting enhanced mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation. Histological and stereological assessments confirmed structural improvements in liver tissue, including reduced steatosis and improved hepatocyte morphology. These findings provide new mechanistic evidence that B. bifidum exerts hepatoprotective effects by reprogramming mitochondrial lipid metabolism through the PPAR-α/PGC-1α/CPT1A axis. This probiotic may offer a novel, mitochondria-targeted therapeutic strategy for managing MAFLD and related metabolic disorders. Further studies are warranted to evaluate strain-specific effects and long-term outcomes in clinical settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-22467-4.

Introduction

The liver is a vital metabolic organ and frequently becomes impaired in setting of chronic metabolic disease, such as type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM). In these patients, persistent insulin resistance and hyperglycaemia are closely linked to hepatic lipid accumulation, ultimately contributing to development of fatty liver disease1,2. Recently, the condition historically known as non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) was redefined as metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) to better reflect its relationship with systemic metabolic derangements, including obesity, dyslipidaemia, and insulin resistance3. MAFLD has emerged as a global health concern, affecting up to 35% of the adult population, depending on geographical and lifestyle factors2,4.

Despite the high prevalence of MAFLD and its increasing burden on healthcare systems, effective pharmacological treatments remain limited. Current therapeutic strategies largely rely on lifestyle modification and metabolic control, yet these approaches often yield inconsistent results or are difficult to maintain long-term5,6. This has led to growing interest in microbiota-targeted therapies, especially probiotics, which are known to modulate gut-liver communication and have demonstrated potential in alleviating metabolic liver damage. Emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role of the gut-liver axis in MAFLD pathogenesis. Gut microbiota dysbiosis, increased intestinal permeability, and the resulting translocation of microbial products such as lipopolysaccharides (LPS) contribute to hepatic inflammation, lipid accumulation, and insulin resistance7,8. Modulating this axis has therefore become a promising therapeutic approach to restore metabolic homeostasis and prevent disease progression. Within this context, microbiota-targeted strategies, particularly probiotics, have attracted increasing interest as potential interventions for MAFLD.

Among the various probiotic genera, Bifidobacteria have been widely studied for their immunomodulatory and gut barrier-enhancing properties. In particular, Bifidobacterium bifidum, a dominant commensal in the human gastrointestinal tract, has been shown to influence glucose metabolism, reduce inflammation, and improve obesity-related outcomes in preclinical models9,10. However, the precise molecular mechanisms through which B. bifidum may benefit liver metabolism, especially at the level of mitochondrial function, have not been thoroughly explored.

Mitochondrial dysfunction is a well-recognized feature in MAFLD pathogenesis. One of the key abnormalities involves the impairment of mitochondrial β-oxidation, the process by which fatty acids are metabolized for energy. Disruption of this pathway leads to lipid accumulation, oxidative stress, and hepatocellular injury. Several nuclear and mitochondrial genes govern β-oxidation in the liver. Among these, PGC-1α (peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha) plays a central role in promoting mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism. Decreased hepatic expression of PGC-1α has been associated with increased steatosis and reduced energy efficiency11,12. CPT1A (carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A) is another critical component, functioning as the rate-limiting enzyme for mitochondrial fatty acid entry. Its downregulation is commonly observed in MAFLD models and contributes to impaired lipid clearance13,14. UCP2 (uncoupling protein 2), located in the mitochondrial inner membrane, modulates oxidative phosphorylation but may also reduce ATP production when upregulated, an effect frequently observed in steatotic livers15,16. Moreover, a series of animal experiments revealed that in normal conditions, there is a low expression level for UCP2, whereas there is a very high upregulation in NAFLD and Non-Alcoholic Steatohepatitis (NASH) conditions17. The transcriptional regulators PPAR-α and PPAR-γ, members of the peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor family, further influence mitochondrial β-oxidation, with PPAR-α enhancing fatty acid catabolism and PPAR-γ being upregulated in pathological fat accumulation18.

While probiotics have been extensively studied in the context of gut microbiota and systemic inflammation, very few studies have focused on how probiotics like B. bifidum modulate the hepatic mitochondrial β-oxidation mechanism at the gene expression level. Understanding this mechanism could provide a more targeted and mechanistic framework for microbiota-based therapies in metabolic liver disease.

This study aims to investigate the regulatory effects of Bifidobacterium bifidum on key genes involved in mitochondrial fatty acid β-oxidation in a rat model of MAFLD induced by high-fat diet and streptozotocin (STZ). Specifically, we examined how B. bifidum administration affects the expression of PGC-1α, CPT1A, UCP2, and PPAR-α/γ, as well as liver structure and biochemical markers of lipid metabolism. By focusing on mitochondrial gene regulation, this study provides a novel mechanistic insight into the potential of B. bifidum as a therapeutic agent in metabolic liver injury.

Methods

Preparation of Bifidobacterium bifidum probiotic

Bifidobacterium bifidum (ATCC®29521) was obtained commercially and revived by culturing in MRS (deMan Rogosa Sharpe) medium (Merck, Germany). A 1 ml portion of the culture was transferred to 50 ml of fresh MRS medium and incubated at 37 °C with agitation at 250 RPM. The optical density of the culture at 600 nm was measured to estimate bacterial growth. Subsequently, the bacterial population was quantified through serial dilutions on solid agar, followed by counting colonies. Animals were treated daily with 1 ml of water containing 1 × 109 CFU of Bifidobacterium bifidum for 6 and 14 weeks in the morning19.

Animal treatment and sample collection

This study utilized forty male Sprague-Dawley rats, each weighing approximately 180 ± 10 g. They were allowed free access to sufficient diet and water and a temperature of 24 ± 2 °C with alternate 12-hour dark/light cycles. The research protocol was approved by the institutional research board (IRB) of Shiraz University of Medical Sciences, Shiraz, Iran. Male rats were chosen specifically because of known differences in how male and female rodents respond to high-fat diet (HFD) challenges, as well as their respective compensatory energy expenditure mechanisms20. The rats were randomly divided into four groups, with 10 rats in each group.

The Control group was given a standard laboratory diet; the MAFLD group was fed an HFD through gavage for 14 weeks (See Table 1). At the 8th week, these rats received an intraperitoneal (IP) injection of 35 mg/kg of streptozotocin (STZ) in citrate buffer (0.1 mol/L, pH 4.5); Treated group 1 (MAFLD-Bifidum-6) was also fed an HFD for 14 weeks, injected with 35 mg/kg STZ at the 8th week, and received oral administration of Bifidobacterium bifidum for 6 weeks (up to the 14th week); Treated group 2 (MAFLD-Bifidum-14) was given an HFD for 14 weeks, injected with 35 mg/kg STZ at the 8th week, and recieved continuous oral administration of Bifidobacterium bifidum throughout the entire 14 weeks of the experiment.

After completing the treatment period, all rats underwent a 6-hour fasting period before being sacrificed21. A high-fat diet (HFD) was prepared for the animals, consisting of 83% fat, 11% carbohydrates, and 6% protein, resulting in a total caloric content of 5420 Kcal (Table 1)22–24. STZ was freshly prepared in citrate buffer (pH 4.5, 0.1 M) and administered intraperitoneally at a dose of 35 mg/kg, following 8 weeks of dietary manipulation25. The impact of STZ was evaluated by analyzing non-fasting blood glucose levels using a glucometer one week after its administration26,27.

Throughout the study, the animals’ body weight was measured weekly. After weighing, the rats underwent a 15-hour fasting period, followed by anaesthesia using an intraperitoneal injection of 100 mg/kg ketamine and 10 mg/kg xylazine (Merck, Germany). Euthanasia was performed in accordance with the university’s ethics committee guidelines, by placing rats in a CO2 chamber.

Blood samples were collected from the heart, and livers were then removed and rinsed with cold normal saline. The weight and volume of the livers were measured immediately to calculate the liver Indices (liver weight/body weight × 100) and for further stereological assessments. A portion of the liver tissues was then rapidly frozen in liquid nitrogen and stored at -80 °C for Real-Time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR). The remaining tissue samples were fixed in 10% formalin buffer for subsequent stereological and pathological analysis.

Measurement of biochemical parameters

Blood samples were collected into tubes and then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min. The resulting serum was stored at − 20 °C for subsequent analysis of various biochemical parameters. Serum levels of Fasting Blood Glucose (FBG), lipid profile components (triglycerides [TG], Total Cholesterol [Chol], High-Density Lipoprotein [HDL], and Low-Density Lipoprotein [LDL]), and liver markers (Aspartate Aminotransferase [AST], Alanine Aminotransferase [ALT], and Alkaline Phosphatase [ALP]) were measured using Pars Azmoon diagnostic kit from Tehran, Iran. The measurements were conducted on a Perestig24i auto-analyzer following the manufacturer’s instructions28.

Quantitative reverse transcriptase-polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR) assessments

The RT-qPCR was used to analyze the mRNA expression levels of PGC-1α, CPT1A, UCP2, and PPAR-α/γ. The analysis was conducted with the QuantStudio 5 real-time PCR system. Total RNA was extracted from the liver tissue using the TRIzol extraction kit from Yekta Tajhiz Azma, Tehran, Iran, and subsequently reverse transcribed into cDNA with a cDNA synthesis kit from the same manufacturer. The expression levels of GAPDH were measured in each sample to serve as the endogenous control.

Amplification reactions were performed using the SYBR Green master mix (Amplicon, Denmark). All samples were run in triplicate, and the mean of triplicates was used for analysis. Deionized distilled water (ddH2O) was included as a no-template control (NTC). The relative quantity of each mRNA was determined using the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method29,30. Primer sequences were designed using Allele ID software (version 7.73) and are listed in Table 2.

Stereological and histopathological assessment

The researchers apply unbiased stereology to examine structural alterations in various tissues. They employ stereological evaluations to accurately quantify the of the extent of these changes. For stereological and liver index analyses, six animals per group were randomly selected. This sample size is consistent with established stereological practices, where 5–6 animals per group are considered sufficient to provide unbiased and statistically valid estimates of organ and cellular parameters, given the labor-intensive and highly detailed nature of these methods31,32.

Estimating the liver volume

The primary liver volume (V Primary liver) was determined using the Scherle method33. In this method, the liver was suspended with thin threads to ensure that it does not touch the sides or bottom of the container. It was then submerged in a bottle containing normal saline. The weight of the bottle was recorded before and after the liver was added. The volume of the liver, measured in cubic centimeters (cm3), was calculated by subtracting the initial weight from the final weight and dividing the result by the specific gravity of normal saline, which is 1.0048 g/cm334. The formula used to calculate the primary volume of the liver was as follows:

Here, IW refers to the Initial Weight of the bottle containing normal saline (cm3), while FW (Final Weight) represents the total weight of IW combined with the liver tissue immersed in it.

Preparation of hepatic tissues for histopathological and stereological analyses

The liver tissues were prepared for stereological analyses using the orientation method to obtain Isotropic Uniform Random (IUR) Sections35,36. Following this, the liver samples were embedded in paraffin, and sections of 5 and 25 μm thicknesses were obtained using a microtome. These sections were then stained with H&E dye (Merck, Germany). The rate of tissue volumetric shrinkage was calculated37. Finally, the total volume of the liver was determined using the following formula38:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${V_{Total\; Liver}} = {V_{Primary \; Liver}} \times \left( 1 - {Volume \; shrinkage} \right)$$\end{document}

where V Primary Liver represents the initial volume of the liver.

Estimating the volumetric density of liver constituents

A video microscopy system manufactured by Nikon in Japan was utilized to analyze 5 μm sections of rat liver. It was examined and scanned 8 to 14 microscopic fields in each liver sample, ensuring even spacing along the X and Y axes with the help of a stage micrometer. A point probe with 25 points was then positioned over the image of the ample section displayed on monitor (See Fig. 1).

The volume density (Vv) of tissue components, such as hepatocytes and sinusoids, was assessed using the point counting method and the following equation39:

In this equation, the P Component represents the total points counted in the component’s profile, while P Reference denotes the total points in the reference space.

Estimating the number of hepatocyte nuclei and Kupffer cells (KCs)

The quantification of hepatocyte and Kupffer cell (KC) nuclei in tissue sections is carried out using the “optical dissector method,” which involves a detailed process to ensure accuracy. This quantification process was performed using a microscope (Nikon E200) equipped with a numerical aperture of 1.30 and a 40× oil immersion objective, and connected to a microcator (Heidenhain, Germany) along with Stereolite software (SUMS, Iran).

The equations used to evaluate the numerical density (Nv) and the total number of hepatocyte nuclei and Kupffer cells are as follows40,41:

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N_V} = \Sigma Q/(\Sigma P \times \Sigma A \times {\text{h}}) \times (t/{\text{BA}})$$\end{document}

\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${N_{Total}} = {{N_V}} \times {V_{Total \; liver}}$$\end{document}

In these equations, “∑Q” denotes the total number of counted cells, while “∑A” signifies the combined areas of the unbiased counting frames. Furthermore, “h” represents the height of the dissector (15 μm), “t” is the average section thickness of all fields assessed by a microtome, and “BA” stands for the block advance, which is the thickness of the section set at 25 μm on the microtome.

Statistical analyses

The study provides average values along with standard deviations. The Shapiro-Wilk test was applied to assess the normality of data distribution, and Levene’s test was used to verify the homogeneity of variances across groups. Both assumptions were met prior to conducting parametric analyses. Statistical analyses and graph creation were performed using GraphPad PRISM version 9.5.0. To determine statistical significance among multiple treatment groups, a one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was conducted, followed by Tukey’s post hoc test. Changes in body weight over time were evaluated using two-way repeated measures ANOVA, which considered the interactions between treatment and time. A P-value of less than 0.05 was deemed statistically significant.

Results

Effects of Bifidobacterium bifidum on fasting blood glucose and lipid profile

According to Table 3, serum levels of fasting blood glucose (FBG), triglycerides (TG), total cholesterol (TC), and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C) were significantly elevated after 14 weeks of high-fat diet (HFD) and streptozotocin (STZ) injection in rats with MAFLD, compared to the control group (p < 0.0001). However, the administration of Bifidobacterium bifidum resulted in a significant reduction in TG, TC, and LDL-C levels in both MAFLD-bifidum-6 (p < 0.01) and MAFLD-bifidum-14 (p < 0.001) groups when compared to the MAFLD group. Nevertheless, there was no significant reduction in FBG levels observed in the MAFLD-bifidum-6 and MAFLD-bifidum-14 groups relative to the MAFLD group.

Additionally, the serum level of high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL) was significantly lower in the MAFLD group compared to the control group (p < 0.001). Although the administration of Bifidobacterium bifidum appeared to increase HDL levels compared to the MAFLD group, this increase was not statistically significant.

Effects of Bifidobacterium bifidum administration on liver indices

The research investigated changes in serum levels of liver indicators, including ALT, AST, ALP, and LDH, among all experimental groups. As shown in Table 4, serum levels of ALT, AST, ALP, and LDH in the MAFLD group were significantly higher compared to the control group (p < 0.001). Conversely, the administration of Bifidobacterium bifidum led to significant decreases in these serum levels for both the MAFLD-bifidum-6 group (p < 0.01) and the MAFLD-bifidum-14 group (p < 0.001) when compared to the MAFLD group. These findings suggest that Bifidobacterium bifidum can provide a protective effect against elevated liver function markers induced by the high-fat diet and STZ injection in MAFLD rats.

Improvement of genes associated with mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation through the administration of Bifidobacterium bifidum

In Fig. 2, there was a significant downregulation in the mRNA expression levels of PGC-1α (Fig. 2A), CPT1A (Fig. 2B), and PPAR-α (Fig. 2D) in the MAFLD group compared to the control group by 72%, 80.5% and 65% respectively. However, the mRNA expression levels of UCP2 (Fig. 2C) and PPAR-γ (Fig. 2E) were significantly increased in the MAFLD group compared to the control group by 2.1 and 1.6-fold, respectively (p < 0.0001). Administering Bifidobacterium bifidum to MAFLD-induced rats for 6 and 14 weeks significantly enhanced the mRNA expression levels of PGC-1α, CPT1A, and PPAR-α, compared to the MAFLD group (p < 0.0001), while markedly decreasing the mRNA expression levels of UCP2 (p < 0.0001) and PPAR-γ (p < 0.001).

Assessment of liver weights and liver indices

It should be noted that liver weight and liver index were determined in six rats per group, as these animals were also used for stereological analyses. This approach aligns with previous stereological studies, where a sample size of 5–6 animals per group has been shown to yield reliable and unbiased quantitative results.

In the MAFLD group, the liver weight was significantly higher compared to the control group (p = 0.0005). After 14 weeks of administering Bifidobacterium bifidum, there was a notable reduction in liver weight (p = 0.0040) (Fig. 3A).

The liver Index was calculated by dividing each rat’s liver weight by the total body weight and then multiplying by 100 (Fig. 3B). The liver indices of the MAFLD group were higher (4.84 ± 0.26 g) compared to the control group (3.90 ± 0.27 g). Additionally, it is important to note that both the MAFLD-bifidum-6 and MAFLD-bifidum-14 groups exhibited significantly lower liver indices (3.99 ± 0.22 g and 4.18 ± 0.16 g, respectively) compared to the MAFLD group.

The total body weight of animals in each group is presented in Supplementary Table 1, confirming that differences in liver weight and liver index were independent of overall body mass.

Stereological findings: the effects of Bifidobacterium bifidum administration on liver tissue structure and cells

The data presented in Fig. 4 shows that the total liver volume in the MAFLD group was significantly greater (39%) than in the control group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4A). Furthermore, compared to the control group, the volumes of sinusoids and hepatocytes in the MAFLD group increased by 100% and 40% respectively (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4B and C).

Administering Bifidobacterium bifidum resulted in a significant decrease in the total liver volume by 11.3% (p < 0.05), sinusoids by 25% (p < 0.0001), and hepatocytes by 12% (p = 0.0005) in MAFLD-bifidum-6 rats compared to the MAFLD group. Additionally, after 14 weeks of administering Bifidobacterium bifidum, total liver volume decreased by 20% (p < 0.001), sinusoids saw a 36% reduction (p < 0.0001), and hepatocytes decreased by 20% (p < 0.001) in MAFLD-bifidum-14 rats compared to MAFLD rats.

In the MAFLD group, the number of hepatocyte nuclei increased by 1.7-fold compared to the control group (p < 0.0001). However, this number declined following B. bifidum after 6 and 14 weeks by 33% and 49%, respectively, in comparison with the MAFLD group (p < 0.0001) (Fig. 4D). Conversely, the number of Kupffer cells significantly decreased by 57% in MAFLD rats compared to the control group (p < 0.0001). Nevertheless, after administering B. bifidum for 6 and 14 weeks, this decrease was significantly reversed, with improvements of 61% and 100%, respectively, in the MAFLD-bifidum-6 and MAFLD-bifidum-14 groups (p < 0.01) (Fig. 4E).

Histopathological evaluation of liver tissues

Histopathological evaluations revealed substantial signs of hepatocellular damage, including pronounced macrovesicular steatosis, hepatocellular ballooning, and inflammatory cell infiltration in the livers of HFD + STZ-induced MAFLD rats (Fig. 5B). In contrast, the liver tissues of the control group showed no such pathological alterations, exhibiting a normal hepatic lobular architecture, intact sinusoidal structure, and healthy hepatocytes with centrally located nuclei (Fig. 5A).

Interestingly, the MAFLD-Bifidum-6 group shows partial improvement in hepatic injury. While mild microvesicular steatosis and limited ballooning were still present, the overall hepatic architecture appeared more preserved, and inflammatory infiltration was significantly reduced compared to the untreated MAFLD group (Fig. 5C). Remarkably, the most substantial histological improvement was observed in the MAFLD-bifidum-14 group. Long-term administration of Bifidobacterium bifidum resulted in near-complete restoration of hepatic structure, with minimal steatosis, absence of ballooning, and reduced inflammatory infiltration (Fig. 5D). These morphological differences suggest that prolonged probiotic supplementation may effectively attenuate both hepatocellular and sinusoidal structural damage in MAFLD conditions. These qualitative findings were consistent with our stereological results (Fig. 4), which quantitatively demonstrated significant reductions in hepatocyte hypertrophy, sinusoidal dilation, and restoration of Kupffer cell numbers in B. bifidum-treated groups.

Discussion

This study provides novel mechanistic insights into the hepatoprotective effects of Bifidobacterium bifidum in a rat model of metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) induced by high-fat diet (HFD) and streptozotocin (STZ). Most notably, we identify the modulation of mitochondrial β-oxidation-related genes (PGC-1α, CPT1A, UCP2, PPAR-α, and PPAR-γ) as a key central axis through which B. bifidum exerts its therapeutic effects. This mechanistic focus on mitochondrial gene regulation distinguishes our study from earlier probiotic research, which has largely emphasized microbiota diversity, inflammatory modulation, or gut barrier enhancement.

Our findings reveal that B. bifidum significantly upregulated hepatic expression of PGC-1α, CPT1A, and PPAR-α, while also normalizing aberrantly elevated PPAR-γ and UCP2 expression in MAFLD-induced rats. These molecular changes were correlated with improvements in histological parameters (sinusoid volume, hepatocyte nuclei count, Kupffer cells) and serum markers of liver function (ALT, AST, ALP, LDH) and lipid metabolism (TG, TC, LDL-C, HDL-C). Taken together, these results indicate that B. bifidum enhances hepatic mitochondrial function and fatty acid oxidation, thereby mitigating steatosis and metabolic stress.

The gut-liver axis is increasingly recognized as a pivotal driver of MAFLD, wherein microbial dysbiosis contributes to hepatic inflammation, lipid accumulation, and insulin resistance7. Recent studies have implicated gut-derived endotoxins and alcohol-producing Klebsiella pneumoniae in the pathogenesis of steatosis via the promotion of intestinal permeability and systemic inflammation42. B. bifidum, in contrast, has been associated with beneficial shifts in the gut microbiota, such as the enrichment of Faecalibaculum and Lactobacillus, and suppression of harmful genera like Tyzzerella and Escherichia-Shigella43.

While previous research has addressed the microbiota-level effects, the novelty of this study lies in identifying a precise mitochondrial signaling axis, specifically the PPAR-α/PGC-1α/CPT1A pathway, as a direct molecular target of B. bifidum. This pathway is a cornerstone of hepatic fatty acid oxidation. PPAR-α is a nuclear receptor that governs the transcription of genes critical for lipid uptake and β-oxidation, including CPT1A, the rate-limiting enzyme responsible for transporting long-chain fatty acids into mitochondria for degradation44. Our findings of decreased PPAR-α and CPT1A in the MAFLD model, and their restoration by B. bifidum, are consistent with studies showing that genetic or pharmacological enhancement of CPT1A activity reverses steatosis and reduces hepatic triglyceride levels45,46.

PGC-1α, a master regulator of mitochondrial biogenesis and oxidative metabolism, also plays a crucial role. In MAFLD, its downregulation is linked to lipid accumulation and decreased ATP generation. We observed that B. bifidum treatment re-established hepatic PGC-1α expression, implying a rejuvenation of mitochondrial capacity. Wang et al. (2024) similarly reported that pharmacologic activation of the PPARα/PGC-1α axis mitigates NAFLD progression through enhancement of mitochondrial function47.

PPAR-γ, though beneficial in peripheral insulin sensitivity, is known to promote hepatic lipogenesis and steatosis when overexpressed in the liver. Elevated PPAR-γ levels can induce UCP2 expression, which mediates proton leak in the mitochondrial inner membrane. While this may reduce oxidative stress, sustained UCP2 overexpression can impair mitochondrial efficiency and lead to hepatocyte pyroptosis. In line with these reports, we observed upregulation of PPAR-γ and UCP2 in the MAFLD group, which were normalized by B. bifidum treatment. This aligns with the findings of Zhong et al. (2018) and Xu et al. (2013), who demonstrated that UCP2 suppression alleviates liver damage and metabolic stress in high-fat-fed or diabetic rodent models48,49.

A particularly innovative aspect of this study is the integration of the emerging microbiota–mitochondria axis, wherein gut-derived metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs) modulate host mitochondrial gene expression. SCFAs, particularly butyrate and acetate, have been shown to activate hepatic AMPK and stimulate PGC-1α transcription, thereby enhancing mitochondrial biogenesis and fatty acid oxidation. Although our study did not directly quantify SCFA concentrations or their downstream signaling, prior studies by Gao et al. (2009) and Hu et al. (2016) have demonstrated that butyrate and acetate enhance hepatic PGC-1α via GPR43-AMPK activation. Future work should measure SCFA levels in serum and feces and correlate them with mitochondrial gene expression to establish a causal connection50,51.

Despite these promising results, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, although we based the dosage and treatment duration of B. bifidum on previous studies, this may not represent the optimal therapeutic window. Dose-response studies are needed to refine both efficacy and safety profiles. Second, while we used a single B. bifidum strain, probiotic effects are often strain-specific. Differences in epithelial adhesion, SCFA production, bile salt hydrolase activity, or immunomodulation may explain varying efficacy. Future comparative studies could assess strain genomics and phenotypes to optimize strain selection for mitochondrial-targeted therapy.

Moreover, we did not evaluate gut colonization efficiency. Probiotic engraftment depends on multiple host factors, including baseline microbiota composition, diet, and mucosal immunity. The absence of data on colonization limits our ability to establish a direct host-microbe relationship. Future studies using strain-specific qPCR or 16 S rRNA sequencing could better elucidate colonization patterns and their impact on host response.

Another important limitation of our study is the lack of protein-level validation or functional metabolic assessment. While RT-PCR provides valuable transcriptional insights, the absence of Western blotting, ATP quantification, or mitochondrial respiration assays (e.g., Seahorse analysis) constrains causal interpretation. Acknowledging this limitation, our findings provide a strong molecular rationale for targeted future studies involving these tools. Additionally, the use of gene knockout models (e.g., PPAR-α- or CPT1A-deficient mice) or germ-free animals selectively colonized with B. bifidum would be instrumental in confirming the dependency of observed effects on specific pathways.

Altogether, our study highlights B. bifidum as a potent modulator of hepatic mitochondrial gene expression and introduces the PPAR-α/PGC-1α/CPT1A signaling axis as a novel mechanistic target in MAFLD therapy. By restoring fatty acid oxidation and mitochondrial function, B. bifidum appears to halt disease progression at its metabolic origin. These findings suggest a broader paradigm wherein probiotics can reprogram host mitochondrial networks via the gut-liver axis, offering a promising avenue for therapeutic development.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that Bifidobacterium bifidum mitigates MAFLD progression and is associated with restoration of mitochondrial fatty acid oxidation at the transcriptional level. Specifically, B. bifidum enhanced the expression of PPAR-α/PGC-1α/CPT1A, while normalizing PPAR-γ and UCP2 expression. These transcriptional changes were accompanied by improvements in hepatic structure and serum metabolic profiles, suggesting that B. bifidum may influence host mitochondrial lipid metabolism via thePPAR-α/PGC-1α/CPT1A axis. However, as our findings are based solely on mRNA analyses, they should be interpreted as preliminary mechanistic evidence. Protein level validation and functional assay of mitochondrial activity will be essential in future studies to confirm the functional activation of this pathway. Collectively, our results highlight the therapeutic potential of B. bifidum while understanding the need for further validation to establish its precise role in microbiota-mitochondria communication and MAFLD therapy.
