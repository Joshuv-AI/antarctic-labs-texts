Gender and surface effects on tiebreak tactics in Grand Slams: A machine learning–enhanced point-by-point analysis
Authors: Qiushi Wang, Yixiong Cui, Yan Chen, Haoyang Liu, Dapeng Bao, Wangwang Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeTiebreaks in professional tennis are high-pressure situations in which tactical execution may influence match outcomes. This study investigated how gender and playing surface are associated with tactical behavior and point outcomes during tiebreaks in Grand Slam singles matches.MethodsUsing a dataset of 51,665 tiebreak points from 4347 matches played between 2011 and 2024 across the four Grand Slam tournaments, we applied a two-level analytical framework. First, set-level comparisons were conducted across 12 subgroups defined by gender, surface, and outcome. Second, point-level XGBoost models were developed separately by gender and surface, and SHAP values were used to interpret the contribution of 13 tactical predictors. To examine the effect of within-tiebreak dependency, an additional tiebreak-level grouped validation was performed. Sensitivity analyses were also conducted after excluding super tiebreaks.ResultsSet-level analyses showed significant winner–loser differences in several technical-tactical indicators, particularly Serve Points Won, Winners, and Unforced Errors, with patterns varying by gender and surface. Under the original random split, point-level model accuracy ranged from 0.634 to 0.710 and AUC from 0.625 to 0.747 across subgroup models; under tiebreak-level grouped validation, the corresponding ranges were 0.637–0.700 and 0.595–0.752. SHAP analysis identified PointServer as the most consistently important predictor across subgroup models, whereas the relative importance of other variables—including RallyType, ServeWidth, ReturnDepth, and net-related indicators—varied by gender and surface. Sensitivity analyses excluding super tiebreaks showed trivial effect sizes at both the set and point levels.ConclusionTactical behavior in Grand Slam tiebreaks was associated with gender and surface context. Male grass-court tiebreaks were more strongly characterized by serve-dominant patterns, whereas female clay-court contexts showed greater relevance of rally control and error management. These findings provide retrospective, interpretable evidence on context-specific tactical tendencies and may serve as a useful reference for match preparation, post-match review, and performance analysis.

Introduction

Tennis is an open-skill, opponent-constrained sport in which performance is shaped not only by technical execution and physical conditioning but also by context-specific tactical decision-making.1 In particular, the tiebreak—recognized as a pivotal moment that determines set and match outcomes—places exceptional psychological and tactical demands on players, requiring precise integration of physical execution and spatial judgment on every point 2, 3, 4. Both standard 7-point tiebreaks and 10-point match tiebreaks are defined within the official rules of tennis as short-format scoring systems designed to resolve sets or matches under high-pressure conditions.5 Although they differ in length and match context, both formats share similar point-by-point competitive structures, including alternating serve sequences and immediate scoring consequences. Therefore, they can be considered comparable high-pressure scoring contexts for tactical analysis.6,7

Among the external variables influencing tactical behavior, court surface plays a pivotal role by altering movement dynamics and ball–racket interactions. Hard, grass, and clay courts differ significantly in terms of friction, ball speed, and bounce characteristics.2,8,9 Grass courts, with their low bounce and fast pace, favor serve-dominant patterns and one-shot winners, facilitating rapid offensive transitions.4,6 Clay courts, by contrast, promote long rallies and defensive baseline play due to their high friction and slow bounce.8,10 Hard courts provide an intermediate profile that supports both power-serving and cross-court exchanges, allowing for tactical flexibility.2,9,11

Beyond surface effects, gender differences also significantly influence tactical structures and point-winning strategies. Male players typically possess greater serve velocity and explosive strength, often favoring aggressive tactics such as serve-and-volley or cross-court finishing strokes.2,9,12 In contrast, female players are characterized by consistent rally construction, effective rhythm control, and strong backhand execution—especially on clay and hard surfaces where extended rallies are common 13, 14, 15. Notably, men show greater reliance on “first-strike” tactics such as serve-and-volley or aggressive cross-court finishes, while women typically construct points through longer rallies and rhythm control, particularly on slower surfaces.16, 17, 18 These patterns underscore how tactical behaviors are shaped by the combined structure of gender and surface, influencing critical scoring dynamics in pressure situations.

Despite growing interest in tennis analytics, most existing studies rely on whole-match or set-level aggregate statistics, with few targeting micro-level point-by-point dynamics—particularly within tiebreak contexts.3,19,20 Previous work on tiebreak performance has examined psychological momentum,21 error patterns under pressure during critical points,22,23 and serve–return asymmetries in tiebreak formats.24 However, large-scale point-by-point modeling of tactical variables remains limited. Studies that jointly consider court surface, gender-specific playing styles, and tactical sequences during tiebreaks are scarce, leaving important gaps in understanding how these factors interact to shape scoring outcomes under pressure. Few investigations have incorporated the three-way interaction of gender, surface, and tactical behavior into an integrated analytical framework, so the mechanisms linking contextual variables to point outcomes remain underexplored. To address this gap, it is essential to construct large-scale point-level datasets derived from real-world Grand Slam tiebreaks, enabling detailed examination of how tactical choices influence scoring probabilities under varying gender–surface configurations. Advances in machine learning—particularly the XGBoost algorithm—offer robust tools for modeling nonlinear interactions, performing feature selection, and generating accurate predictions in complex behavioral datasets.17,25 When paired with SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations), researchers can quantify the marginal effect of each tactical behavior on scoring outcomes and compare importance rankings across gender and surface.3,26 However, systematic applications of such interpretable machine learning techniques remain rare in high-stakes tennis scenarios such as tiebreaks.

To address these gaps, this study analyzes all men's and women's singles tiebreaks played at the four Grand Slam tournaments from 2011 to 2024. The selection of this time window is grounded in the increasing availability of point-by-point tracking and electronic match records across the four Grand Slam tournaments during the study period.27,28 This development made it possible to assemble a large-scale point-level dataset for more detailed analysis of player behavior across hard, grass, and clay courts, although the completeness of higher-resolution variables varied across tournaments and court settings. Accordingly, a multi-level analytical framework was developed to examine tiebreak tactics at both aggregated (set-level) and point-specific levels. This approach aims to uncover context-dependent performance patterns and provide a data-driven foundation for the development of targeted strategies, coaching interventions, and surface- or gender-specific training programs.

Methods

Sample and data

All procedures conformed to the Declaration of Helsinki. Ethics approval was not required, as only de-identified, publicly available secondary data were used. Point-by-point data were extracted from main-draw singles matches at the Australian Open, French Open, Wimbledon, and US Open (2011–2024). Only matches that included at least one standard tiebreak (7-point format) or 10-point match tiebreak (Tiebreak-10) were retained, following the definitions outlined in ITF rules.5 Matches with missing data (e.g., serve information), retirements, or walkovers were excluded. Records showing internal inconsistencies or out-of-sequence scoring were manually flagged and removed. The final dataset included 4347 tiebreaks and 51,665 points (50,603 standard tiebreaks; 1062 Tiebreak-10s). Distribution across Grand Slams was as follows: Australian Open (11,858), Wimbledon (16,886), French Open (10,254), and US Open (12,667). Although Wimbledon contributed substantially more tiebreaks than the other Grand Slams, we accounted for this potential imbalance by conducting all analyses within surface × gender subgroups and, where necessary, applying stratification to minimize any disproportionate influence of a single tournament. All three surfaces were well represented, with ≥100 valid tiebreaks per surface.

To assess the robustness of the analytical framework, both standard 7-point tiebreaks and 10-point match tiebreaks were included. A sensitivity analysis was conducted by comparing the full dataset with a subset containing only standard 7-point tiebreaks. Key variables were examined to determine whether the inclusion of 10-point tiebreaks influenced the distribution of tactical variables and the structural patterns of point outcomes.

Study design and variable handling

Only matches including at least one standard (7-point) or match (10-point) tiebreak were retained.

Set-level analysis

Each tiebreak was treated as a unit. Twelve subgroups were formed based on Gender (Male/Female), Outcome (Win/Loss), and Surface (Hard, Clay, Grass)—e.g., M-W-H denotes “Male–Winner–Hard.” Prior to analysis, all variables were tested for normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test (p > 0.05 indicates normality).29 Normal variables were compared using independent-sample t-tests, while non-normal variables used Mann–Whitney U tests. Effect sizes were reported as Cohen's d or correlation r, using standard thresholds for interpretation.30,31 A p-value <0.05 indicated statistical significance.

Point-level analysis

Each point during the tiebreak was treated as an individual observation and modeled using six XGBoost classifiers stratified by gender and surface. Model training initially used an 80:20 random train/test split with default hyperparameters (100 iterations; objective = “binary:logistic”; eval_metric = “AUC”), and performance was evaluated using Accuracy, Balanced Accuracy, AUC, and confusion matrices. Because points within the same tiebreak are not fully independent,21 an additional tiebreak-level grouped validation was performed, with all points from the same tiebreak assigned exclusively to either the training or test set. The same predictors, XGBoost settings, and SHAP procedure were used for direct comparison with the original random-split models. Some point-level predictors were not available for all observations. Missingness was concentrated in higher-resolution spatial and technical variables, whereas core point-context variables were largely complete. This pattern suggests that data completeness depended mainly on differences in electronic recording, point-by-point notation detail, and tracking support across tournaments, years, and court settings rather than on random omission.32 XGBoost was retained because it can accommodate missing predictor values during tree construction without prior imputation.33 Accordingly, observations with partially missing predictor values were retained whenever possible. Feature contributions were interpreted using SHAP (SHapley Additive Explanations), following Lundberg & Lee (2017),34 and extended to tennis contexts by Wang et al. (2025).35

To ensure clarity, “Winning player” and “Losing player” refer to the eventual winner and loser of the tiebreak, respectively. Only non-outcome variables were used in point-level modeling. All statistical analyses were conducted using R (v4.3.2), with core packages including xgboost, caret, SHAPforxgboost, dplyr, and ggplot2. Additional plots and figure refinements (e.g., radar charts, heatmaps) were performed using GraphPad Prism (v10.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).

Results

Set-level analysis results

Statistical comparisons between tiebreak winners and losers across gender and surface groups yielded multiple significant differences in technical-tactical variables.

Serve performance metrics showed consistent differences. In the male–grass subgroup, 1st Serve Won% was 85.96% for winners and 62.22% for losing player; Ace% was 11.24% vs. 7.30%. In the female–clay subgroup, Unforced Errors (%) differed by 0.13 units between winning player (0.12) and losing player (0.25). Return-related indicators also showed marked differences. Return 1st Won% in the female–clay group was 54.20% (winning player) vs. 24.77% (losing player), while Net Approach Won% was higher in all male subgroups, e.g., male–hard: 81.32% (winning player) vs. 54.99% (losing player). Additional variables such as Max Hotstreak and Game Point were also elevated in winning player. For example, male–grass: Max Hotstreak = 3.44 (winning player) vs. 2.02 (losing player); Game Points = 1.79 vs. 0.34.

Validation strategy analysis

To examine the potential impact of within-tiebreak dependency on model evaluation, an additional grouped validation was performed at the tiebreak level, such that all points from the same tiebreak were assigned exclusively to either the training or test set. Under grouped validation, accuracy ranged from 0.637 to 0.700 and AUC ranged from 0.595 to 0.752 across the six subgroup models. The differences in AUC between the original random split and the grouped validation ranged from −0.030 to +0.022 (Table 2). The ranking of key SHAP features remained broadly comparable between the two validation strategies, as shown in Supplementary Table 3.

Tiebreak format sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses were conducted at both the set level (derived variables) and the point level (raw variables) by excluding all super tiebreaks (10-point format). At the set level, no significant differences were observed between the full dataset and the dataset including only standard 7-point tiebreaks across all variables (all p > 0.05). Mean differences were minimal (Δ ≈ 0), and effect sizes were trivial (|d| < 0.01) (see Supplementary Table 4). At the point level, mean differences between datasets were also small across all variables (Δ values close to zero). Effect sizes were consistently trivial (|d| < 0.05) (see Supplementary Table 5).

Discussion

This study developed a three-factor analytical framework (gender × surface × tactical behavior) to examine how contextual factors shape tiebreak outcomes in Grand Slam singles. Using all men's and women's matches from 2011 to 2024 that featured either standard or 10-point tiebreak formats, we combined set-level statistics with point-level machine learning models. The main findings are twofold. First, at the set level, serve-related performance was a consistent determinant of success, with the largest winner–loser differences observed in male players on grass, where first-serve points won, ace rate, and net-approach success were particularly pronounced. In contrast, female players on clay were distinguished more by unforced errors, return points won, and greater rally distance, underscoring the importance of error reduction and rally management on slower surfaces. Second, at the point level, Point Server was the most decisive predictor of scoring outcomes—especially in male grass and clay tiebreaks—while other features such as Rally Type, Serve Width, and Return Depth showed context-dependent importance across gender–surface pairings. Together, these results indicate that tactical success in tiebreaks is not governed by a single universal rule, but instead emerges from surface-dependent and gender-specific interaction patterns that can be quantified using interpretable machine learning.

Set-level analysis

At the Set level, behavioral differences between winners and losers demonstrated high statistical significance, modulated by the dual influence of surface and gender. First, Serve Points Won was significantly higher in winners across all conditions, with the most substantial difference in the male grass group. This pattern aligns with previous findings that highlight serve-dominant advantages on low-friction surfaces like grass 8,9,16. The phenomenon aligns with grass's physical properties—low friction and fast bounce—enhancing the server's dominance while reducing the receiver's preparation time, thereby increasing the likelihood of direct point wins on serve.3 These findings are consistent with previous notation studies emphasizing serve dominance on grass. Second, Winners and Unforced Errors were key distinguishing variables between winners and losers, showing variation by gender and surface. In the female clay group, Unforced Errors had the most significant inter-group difference. Large differences in unforced errors on clay courts support the relevance of error reduction strategies in extended rallies.8,10,18 This finding is aligned with the physical and psychological demands unique to clay, such as aerobic endurance and emotional resilience.10 Conversely, in the male grass group, Winners were significantly more frequent among victors, revealing a capacity to terminate points quickly through offensive play.2,16 Furthermore, Net Points Won and Distance Run illustrated gender-specific tactical pathways. In the male grass group, winners had significantly higher net success rates, reflecting the continued tactical efficacy of the “Serve-and-Volley” approach, supported by greater explosive strength and racquet-handling capacity in men.16,26 In contrast, in the female clay group, losers ran significantly more than winners, indicating that higher physical output does not necessarily yield scoring advantage. Instead, excessive movement may result from rhythm loss or persistent defensive play, contributing to point losses.15,17,36 This suggests that endurance-based strategies on clay must be paired with effective rhythm control and error reduction. In sum, Set-level analysis illustrates how gender and surface jointly shape outcome-defining tactical structures, particularly through variables such as serve quality, error control, and net play efficiency—highlighting the critical role of tactical adaptability during pivotal match stages.

Point-level modeling

Point-level XGBoost models showed moderate predictive performance across the six subgroup models. Under the original random split, accuracy ranged from 0.634 to 0.710 and AUC from 0.625 to 0.747, whereas the corresponding grouped-validation ranges were 0.637–0.700 and 0.595–0.752. This suggests limited optimistic bias under the original random split, while the overall performance pattern remained comparable under grouped validation. SHAP interpretability further revealed the marginal contributions and structural ranking of each tactical variable under varied gender and surface conditions. PointServer emerged as the dominant predictor across subgroup models, and its top-ranked position remained broadly stable under the additional grouped validation. This result is consistent with grass's structural reinforcement of serve dominance, where the point outcome is often predictable based solely on whether the eventual tiebreak winner served the point 3,8,9,16 Another prominent variable was Rally Type, whose SHAP values were notably higher in female models—especially on clay and grass. This suggests that female players may rely more heavily on rally construction during non-serve points, leveraging rhythm control and extended exchanges to generate tactical advantages.13,14,18 In conjunction with the Set-level findings on Unforced Errors and Distance Run in the female clay group, it can be inferred that women on slow surfaces tend to pressure opponents through pacing and spatial control, yet success in this tactic requires high stamina and error regulation.10 Additionally, certain tactical variables displayed surface- and gender-dependent contributions, revealing their context-specific functionality. Serve Width had significantly higher SHAP values on grass, suggesting that lateral serve variation creates greater tactical disruption—especially on fast, low-bounce surfaces where it reduces return success rates9 and breaks the returner's timing to increase winner probability.3 Meanwhile, Return Depth showed heightened importance in female clay groups, consistent with analyses demonstrating deeper returns and longer rallies on clay for women.37 Set No also showed elevated importance on grass, possibly reflecting tactical behavior shifts due to physical fatigue or psychological pressure as the match progresses,1,12,17 such as more conservative serving or altered attack rhythms.1 Return Depth had its strongest predictive power in the female clay group and weakest in the male grass group. This suggests that on slower surfaces, deep returns help prolong rallies and gain positional control, forming a core strategy for women. In contrast, on grass, where points are shorter, the same strategy loses efficacy.20 These findings emphasize that the scoring value of tactical variables should be interpreted in light of specific gender × surface configurations, supporting the view that tactical success depends on contextual adaptability rather than universal rules.13 Overall, point-level models not only validate findings from Set-level analysis but also quantify the strength and structural function of tactical behaviors across contextual frameworks. The subgroup findings should also be interpreted with some caution. Although the analyses were stratified by gender and surface, surface and tournament were not fully separable in the present dataset, because grass-court tiebreaks were predominantly drawn from Wimbledon and clay-court tiebreaks from Roland Garros. As a result, some effects described as surface-related may also partly reflect tournament-specific characteristics. This issue is particularly relevant in smaller subgroup models, where sample imbalance may amplify apparent differences.

In addition, the incomplete availability of several higher-resolution spatial and technical predictors should be considered when interpreting subgroup-specific predictor importance. Because missingness was concentrated in variables requiring more detailed electronic notation and tracking support, their relative contribution may be more sensitive to differences in data availability across tournaments,32 years, and court types. Accordingly, these subgroup-specific patterns are best interpreted as context-dependent tendencies rather than strictly isolated surface effects. The additional tiebreak-level grouped validation provides a more stringent assessment of the point-level modeling strategy. As expected, model performance changed slightly relative to the original random split, suggesting that random partitioning may introduce limited optimistic bias when points from the same tiebreak appear in both training and test sets. Importantly, however, the grouped validation did not materially alter the main findings. Performance remained within a comparable range, and the ranking of key SHAP features was largely preserved. This indicates that the principal interpretative conclusions of the study are not driven by the original validation strategy, but reflect relatively stable tactical patterns across subgroup models. This highlights the diversity and non-linearity of tactical behavior under varied match conditions, providing a useful reference for contextualized strategy development and training design.

The sensitivity analyses showed consistent results across both set-level and point-level analyses after excluding super tiebreaks. The absence of significant differences at the set level, together with trivial effect sizes at both analytical levels, suggests that the inclusion of super tiebreaks does not meaningfully influence the observed patterns. These findings indicate that the distribution of point-level behaviors and the aggregated performance indicators remain comparable regardless of tiebreak format.

This study focused exclusively on Grand Slam singles tiebreaks and provides novel, interpretable insights into gender–surface–tactic interactions using XGBoost and SHAP. While the dataset was extensive, clay and grass courts had fewer tiebreak samples than hard courts, potentially limiting subgroup generalizability.27 In addition, super tiebreaks (10-point format) accounted for a relatively small proportion of the total sample (approximately 2%), which may limit their contribution to overall statistical estimates. Future research specifically targeting super tiebreak contexts may provide additional insights into potential format-specific adaptations. Additionally, as not all matches were played on center courts with advanced tracking systems, certain spatial or technical variables (e.g., ServeWidth, ReturnDepth) were partially missing. In addition, tournament and surface were partly confounded in the present dataset, particularly for grass and clay courts, and some subgroup models were based on relatively smaller samples. Furthermore, several higher-resolution spatial and technical predictors were only partially available, which may have reduced the stability of variable-level estimates in some subgroup models and should be considered when interpreting subgroup-specific and surface-related differences. Although robust statistical preprocessing and model design were applied to minimize bias from missing data,38 this remains a structural limitation. In addition, although an extra tiebreak-level grouped validation was performed, point-level observations in tennis remain embedded within broader hierarchical contexts, including player characteristics, match dynamics, and prior performance, which cannot be fully captured within a single modeling framework. Moreover, the analysis lacked shot-level information such as forehand/backhand speed, spin, or stroke type, shot placement during rallies, and did not account for athlete-specific physiological measures (e.g., energy output, acceleration). These constraints restrict assessment of within-point tactical dynamics and physical load variation.28,39,40 Future research should incorporate richer spatiotemporal and biomechanical data to capture a more complete picture of performance under pressure.39,41 Including player ranking, match context, and fatigue-related indicators would further improve model precision and ecological validity.42

Practical applications

From an applied perspective, the present findings are most relevant for retrospective tactical evaluation and context-specific performance profiling, with set-level comparisons and SHAP-based point-level interpretation providing a useful framework for identifying tactical tendencies. Such information may support match preparation, opponent analysis, post-match review, and targeted training design. However, because the analysis was retrospective and did not include forward-looking or external validation, the findings should be interpreted as explanatory rather than real-time predictive.

Conclusion

This study combined set-level statistical analysis and point-level machine learning to examine how gender and surface shape tactical behavior and point outcomes in Grand Slam tiebreaks. Across subgroup models, PointServer emerged as the most consistently important predictor, while other tactical variables—including rally structure, serve placement, return depth, and net-related indicators—showed context-dependent contributions that varied by gender and surface. Set-level comparisons further showed that winners and losers differed significantly in several key performance variables, particularly Serve Points Won, Winners, and Unforced Errors. Overall, the findings suggest that tactical behavior in tiebreaks is influenced by the interaction of gender and surface context. Male grass-court tiebreaks were more strongly associated with serve-dominant patterns, whereas female clay-court contexts showed greater relevance of rally control and error management. These results are best understood as retrospective, interpretable evidence of context-specific tactical tendencies, and may provide a useful reference for match preparation, post-match review, and performance analysis under pressure.
