Evaluation of the efficacy and safety of a modified anesthetic protocol using dexmedetomidine, midazolam, and butorphanol in mice
Authors: Natsuki KIDA, Haruto TAKEDA, Kazuhiro SAKAMOTO, Masaki WATANABE, Nobuya SASAKI, Atsushi ASANO
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWe aimed to evaluate the efficacy and safety of a modified injectable anesthesia protocol combining dexmedetomidine, midazolam, and butorphanol (dMMB) compared with the conventional medetomidine-based protocol (MMB) in C57BL/6J mice. Animals were assigned to three groups (n=5 per group for each sex): MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0), dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0), and dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) mg/kg. We assessed anesthetic induction and recovery times, depth scores, heart rate, SpO2, and body temperature, and administered atipamezole 40 min post-anesthesia to evaluate temperature recovery. Blood glucose levels were also measured. Both dMMB protocols produced anesthetic effects comparable to MMB, but recovery was significantly faster than MMB. Duration of surgical anesthesia in males (mean ± 95% confidence interval) was 141 ± 11 min for MMB, 112 ± 8 min for dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0), and 89 ± 10 min for dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0). In females, durations were 109 ± 43 min, 76 ± 12 min, and 78 ± 16 min, respectively. In addition, hypothermia was less severe, particularly with the lowest dexmedetomidine dose (0.1/6.0/10.0). Furthermore, females had a significantly shorter duration of surgical anesthesia than males. Blood glucose levels increased under all anesthetic protocols, with males showing higher values than females, while no significant differences among anesthetic protocols were detected in either sex. No additional adverse effects were observed. These findings indicated that low-dose dexmedetomidine-based anesthesia provides effective sedation while improving safety by reducing hypothermia and facilitating rapid recovery.Keywords: animal welfare, butorphanol, dexmedetomidine, injectable anesthesia, midazolam

INTRODUCTION

The use of laboratory animals for scientific research and educational purposes requires careful consideration of animal welfare. Anesthesia during surgical procedures is essential not only for ethical reasons but also for the success of experiments [1]. Balanced anesthesia, which combines multiple agents to achieve optimal sedation, analgesia, and muscle relaxation, is widely recognized as a safe and effective approach due to its complementary pharmacological effects and reduced adverse reactions [16].

In rodents, particularly mice and rats, anesthesia is commonly achieved using either injectable or inhalation methods. Inhalation anesthesia typically employs isoflurane or sevoflurane, whereas injectable anesthesia has traditionally relied on ketamine combined with xylazine. However, ketamine abuse has become a social concern [18], leading to strict regulations on its use in several countries, including Japan. As a result, an alternative injectable protocol combining medetomidine (an α2-adrenoceptor agonist), midazolam (a GABA-A receptor agonist), and butorphanol (an opioid receptor agonist)—known as the triple combination anesthesia (MMB), which provides deep sedation and analgesia—has been widely adopted for small laboratory animals [7,8,9, 11, 14, 15]. Although the triple-combination anesthetic does not include any agents formally classified as general anesthetics, its ability to induce anesthesia through central nervous system mechanisms remains incompletely understood. Nevertheless, recent c-Fos–based studies [28] have suggested that this anesthetic mixture may exert central nervous system–suppressive effects comparable to those of isoflurane.

One advantage of this protocol is that atipamezole can be used as an antagonist of α2-agonists to reverse anesthesia and facilitate rapid recovery [10]. However, triple-combination anesthesia is associated with adverse effects such as hypothermia, decreased SpO2, and hyperglycemia [6, 17, 19, 21], which necessitate further refinement. Several modified protocols adjusting drug doses have been reported. For example, reducing medetomidine to 0.2 mg/kg while increasing midazolam and butorphanol to 6 and 10 mg/kg, respectively, significantly suppressed post-anesthetic hypothermia [22], as hypothermia is primarily attributable to the dose-dependent effect of medetomidine, an α2-adrenoceptor agonist, on thermoregulation. Nevertheless, detailed evaluations of anesthetic depth, physiological changes, and recovery profiles have been limited.

Recently, the use of dexmedetomidine as a substitute for medetomidine has increased in small animals [5]. Dexmedetomidine is the dextrorotatory enantiomer of medetomidine, whereas medetomidine is a racemic mixture that contains levomedetomidine, which exhibits markedly lower pharmacological activity. Therefore, dexmedetomidine is expected to exert more selective and potent effects as an α2-adrenoceptor agonist compared with medetomidine [13]. Based on these considerations, this study aimed to compare the conventional and other modified MMB-based protocols [24, 25] with two dexmedetomidine-based modifications (dMMB) to clarify their anesthetic characteristics, physiological effects, and safety profiles in mice. Furthermore, the study design was developed in accordance with the principle of the 3Rs (Replacement, Reduction, and Refinement) to ensure the ethical use of laboratory animals. Particular emphasis was placed on reduction by limiting the sample size to n=5 per group, which was deemed sufficient for exploring the feasibility and preliminary efficacy of the dMMB protocol. Given these considerations, the present investigation is characterized as a pilot study, intended to generate foundational data to inform future large-scale experiments while minimizing animal use.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

C57BL/6J mice (male and female, 8–12 weeks old; n=30 total; Japan SLC, Shizuoka, Japan) were used in this study. Animals were housed in an AAALAC International-accredited facility at Kagoshima University under controlled conditions (temperature: 20–26°C; humidity: 30–70%; 12-hr light/dark cycle, lights on at 07:00). Mice were kept in cages (KN-60101, Natsume, Tokyo, Japan) with paper bedding and plastic tunnels, and provided ad libitum access to commercial chow (CE-2, CLEA Japan, Tokyo, Japan) and water. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals (8th edition, National Academy of Sciences, USA) and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Kagoshima University (approval number: VM24051). After the experiment, the mice were euthanized by CO2 inhalation.

Anesthetic protocols

The injectable anesthetics used in this study were medetomidine (Medetomin Injection, Meiji Animal Health, Kumamoto, Japan), dexmedetomidine (Dexdomitor, Zenoaq, Kooriyama, Japan), midazolam (Midazolam Injection NIG, Nichi-Iko Pharmaceutical, Toyama, Japan), and butorphanol (Vetorphale, Meiji Animal Health). Mice were randomly assigned to three groups (n=5 per sex per group): MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0), consisting of medetomidine 0.5 mg/kg, midazolam 4.0 mg/kg, and butorphanol 5.0 mg/kg; dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0), consisting of dexmedetomidine 0.25 mg/kg, midazolam 4.0 mg/kg, and butorphanol 5.0 mg/kg; and dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0), consisting of dexmedetomidine 0.1 mg/kg, midazolam 6.0 mg/kg, and butorphanol 10.0 mg/kg. All drugs were diluted in sterile saline (Otsuka, Tokyo, Japan) to achieve a dose of 0.1 mL per 10 g body weight and administered subcutaneously at the dorsal neck. Drug preparation and administration were blinded to the experimenter.

Assessment of anesthetic depth and physiological parameters

Before anesthesia, body weight, heart rate (HR), oxygen saturation (SpO2), and body temperature were recorded. After injection, immobilization time, loss of righting reflex (LORR), and recovery of righting reflex (RORR) were measured. Anesthetic depth was scored every 5 min for 150 min based on five reflexes: righting, forelimb withdrawal, hindlimb withdrawal, tail pinch, and corneal reflex (score: 0 =present, 1 =absent). HR, SpO2, and body temperature (rectal temperature) were monitored every 5 min for 60 min using PhysioSuite with MouseSTAT and RightTemp modules (Kent Scientific, Torrington, CT, USA) on a warming pad (set target to 37°C body temperature).

Blood glucose measurement

Twenty min after anesthesia, 1–2 µL of blood was collected from the tail tip and glucose concentration was measured using a veterinary glucometer (Foracare, Tokyo, Japan).

Recovery assessment

To promote recovery from anesthesia and objectively evaluate arousal, atipamezole (Kyoritsu Seiyaku, Tokyo, Japan), an α2-adrenoceptor antagonist, was administered subcutaneously 40 min after anesthesia at a dose equivalent to medetomidine or twice the dexmedetomidine dose, based on manufacturer recommendations and previous studies [24, 25]. Specifically, atipamezole was diluted with sterile saline to 0.05 mg/mL and administered at 0.5 mg/kg for the MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0) and dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0) groups, and at 0.2 mg/kg for the dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) group. These doses corresponded to administration volumes of 0.1 mL per 10 g body weight for the 0.5 mg/kg dose and 0.04 mL per 10 g body weight for the 0.2 mg/kg dose. Immediately after administration, mice were transferred from the warming pad to a plastic platform to clearly observe the effect of atipamezole. Body temperature was measured at 0, 30, 60, 90, 120, and 180 min after atipamezole administration.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± 95% confidence interval. Two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used to analyze immobilization time, LORR, RORR, surgical anesthesia duration, and blood glucose levels, with sex and drug treatment as factors. Levene’s test and the Shapiro–Wilk test were performed to assess the homogeneity of variance and the normality of distribution across groups. When significant differences (P<0.05) were detected in either normality or homogeneity of variance, a nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dwass–Steel–Critchlow–Fligner (DSCF) post hoc comparisons was applied. HR, SpO2, and body temperature were analyzed by repeated-measures ANOVA followed by Tukey–Kramer post hoc tests. The assumption of sphericity was tested using Mauchly’s test, and when this assumption was not met, the Greenhouse–Geisser correction was used to adjust the degrees of freedom for the F-tests. Anesthetic depth scores were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test with DSCF post hoc comparisons. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05. Analyses were performed using Jamovi 2.6.45; power analysis was conducted with G*Power 3.1.9.6.

RESULTS

Comparison of three triple-combination anesthetic protocols: induction time, LORR, and RORR

We selected RORR as the primary endpoint for assessing anesthetic efficacy, along with immobilization time, and LORR as the secondary endpoint. The effects were compared among the following groups: MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0), dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0), and dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) (Fig. 1). To assess the normality and homogeneity of variance, the Shapiro–Wilk test and Levene’s test were performed. For immobilization time, the Shapiro–Wilk test yielded a P-value of 0.302, and Levene’s test yielded P=0.601. For LORR, the Shapiro–Wilk test resulted in P=0.196, and Levene’s test resulted in P=0.066. Therefore, these parameters were analyzed using a two-way ANOVA. In contrast, for RORR, the Shapiro–Wilk test yielded P=0.563, and Levene’s test yielded P=0.032. Consequently, a nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test was applied. No significant differences were observed in immobilization time or LORR among the three protocols for either sex. However, the RORR duration was significantly shorter in females compared to males {P=0.008, ε2 (epsilon squared, effect size for Kruskal–Wallis test)=0.239}. In addition, RORR was shorter in dexmedetomidine-based groups compared with the medetomidine group. For example, in males, the mean RORR was 152.2 ± 17.3 min for MMB versus 85.7 ± 12.4 min for dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0), representing a 56.3% reduction. In females, RORR was 113.1 ± 41.1 min for MMB and 67.6 ± 21.2 min for dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0), a 59.8% reduction. Kruskal–Wallis test also revealed significant effects of drug treatment (P=0.009, ε2=0.679) in males. According to the results of the DSCF post hoc comparisons, significant differences were observed in males between MMB and dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0), as well as between dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0) and dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0). In contrast, no significant differences were found among the groups in females (P=0.070, ε2=0.380).

Comparison of anesthetic depth

Anesthetic depth was assessed using an index based on five reflexes: righting, forelimb withdrawal, hindlimb withdrawal, tail pinch, and corneal reflex, scored every 5 min for 150 min (Fig. 2). Both male and female mice exhibited earlier recovery of reflexes in dexmedetomidine-based protocols compared to medetomidine-based protocols. In males, dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0) showed significantly lower scores than MMB at 55–125 min post-injection, and dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) was significantly lower than MMB at 115–130 min. Females demonstrated similar trends, with significant differences between MMB and both dMMB groups from approximately 105 to 140 min post-injection.

Duration of surgical anesthetic level

To further examine the detailed transition of anesthetic depth, and following previous studies, an anesthetic index of ≥4 was considered to indicate attainment of a surgical anesthesia level [25], and the duration for which this level was maintained was compared among the three protocols as a primary endpoint parameter (Fig. 3). In males, mean durations were 141 ± 11 min for MMB, 112 ± 8 min for dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0), and 89 ± 10 min for dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0). In females, durations were 109 ± 43 min, 76 ± 12 min, and 78 ± 16 min, respectively. The Shapiro–Wilk test yielded P=0.007, and Levene’s test yielded P=0.001. Therefore, a nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test was applied instead to two-way ANOVA. Kruskal–Wallis test revealed significant effects of sex (P=0.006, ε2=0.260) and drug treatment (P=0.002, ε2=0.899 in males, P=0.008, ε2=0.691 in females). Furthermore, sex-specific post-hoc comparisons revealed significant differences between dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0) and dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) (P=0.023), MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0) and dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0) (P=0.023), and MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0) and dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) (P=0.023) in males, and between MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0) and dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0) (P=0.023), and MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0) and dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) in females (P=0.023) (Fig. 3).

Heart rate and SpO2 changes

Heart rate and SpO2 were monitored for 60 min post-injection (Fig. 4). In males, MMB tended to show lower heart rates than dMMB during the first 40 min, whereas SpO2 was slightly higher in dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0). In females, no consistent trends were observed. The repeated-measures ANOVA for SpO2 values with Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied because the assumption of sphericity was violated (male, P<0.001; female, P=0.016). For males, it was indicated that a significant main effect of time (P=0.004, η2P=0.260). However, the interaction between protocol and time (P=0.240, η2P=0.183) and the main effect of protocol (P=0.099, η2P=0.320) were not significant. Similarly, in females, a significant main effect of time was observed (P=0.003, η2P=0.261), while the interaction between protocol and time (P=0.051, η2P=0.254) and the main effect of protocol (P=0.342, η2P=0.164) showed a similar trend to that in males.

Recovery of body temperature after atipamezole administration

To compare body temperature recovery after administration of the reversal agent, atipamezole was administered 40 min after anesthesia, and changes in body temperature were monitored (Fig. 5). Because the use of a warming pad can maintain body temperature at 37°C, mice were transferred from the warming pad to a plastic platform immediately after atipamezole administration to clearly observe temperature changes. As the assumption of sphericity was violated in both sexes (male, P=0.018; female, P=0.035), repeated-measures ANOVA with Greenhouse–Geisser correction was applied. The analyses revealed main effects of drug treatment and elapsed time, as well as their interaction. In males, significant effects were observed for time (P<0.001, η2P=0.866), the interaction between protocol and time (P<0.001, η2P=0.836), and protocol (P<0.001, η2P=0.976). Similarly, in females, significant effects were found for time (P<0.001, η2P=0.777), the interaction between protocol and time (P<0.001, η2P=0.717), and protocol (P<0.001, η2P=0.687).

DISCUSSION

This study aimed to improve the commonly used triple-combination injectable anesthesia protocol (MMB protocol), which employs medetomidine, midazolam, and butorphanol. We compared the standard MMB protocol with two modified protocols (dMMB protocols) in which medetomidine was replaced by dexmedetomidine, focusing on anesthesia depth, duration of surgical anesthesia, HR, SpO2, and body temperature recovery following reversal by atipamezole. Our findings revealed that anesthesia using dexmedetomidine exhibited several distinct advantages over the medetomidine-based method. Specifically, the dMMB protocol provided faster recovery from anesthesia, shorter maintenance of surgical depth, and superior temperature recovery. These characteristics were most apparent in the dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) protocol.

Comparison of anesthetic parameters showed no differences among the three protocols in time to immobilization or LORR, indicating similar induction times. The newly examined dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) protocol achieved rapid induction comparable to the conventional method, confirming its suitability for procedures requiring prompt anesthesia. Previous studies reported that dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0) produced LORR durations equivalent to MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0) in both male and female C57BL/6J mice [24]. In contrast, an MMB protocol using medetomidine 0.2 mg/kg, midazolam 6 mg/kg, and butorphanol 10 mg/kg—comparable to dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0)—resulted in significantly shorter LORR than MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0) [22], which is inconsistent with our observations. The cause of this discrepancy remains unclear, and whether dexmedetomidine-based protocols consistently shorten induction time compared to medetomidine-based protocols requires further investigation.

Analysis of RORR demonstrated that dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) group recovered significantly faster than the MMB group in males. Time-course assessment of anesthesia depth revealed similar trends and indicated that dMMB groups became lighter earlier than MMB. Surgical anesthesia duration was shortest with dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) in males, while in females, dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0) was significantly shorter than MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0), and dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) was comparable to dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0). These findings align with recent reports on dMMB anesthesia in mice [24]. Several studies have investigated the pharmacological differences between dexmedetomidine and medetomidine in animals. Comparisons of their analgesic and sedative effects indicate that, although both drugs exert similar effects on nociceptive withdrawal reflexes in dogs and cats, the duration of sedation tends to be longer with medetomidine than with dexmedetomidine [3, 20]. These findings align with the results of the present study and suggest that this phenomenon may be consistently observed across species other than mice. In a study conducted in dogs, administration of the optical isomer levomedetomidine did not induce behavioral changes; however, at high concentrations, it enhanced bradycardia and attenuated the sedative and analgesic effects of dexmedetomidine [13]. Therefore, rather than exerting direct pharmacological effects, levomedetomidine contained in medetomidine likely influenced the pharmacodynamic or pharmacokinetic actions mediated by dexmedetomidine through α2-adrenoceptors [4], which may explain why the dMMB protocol resulted in a shorter anesthesia duration compared to the MMB protocol. These findings suggest that dMMB protocols, particularly those using low-dose dexmedetomidine, are well suited for short-time procedures. For experimental procedures that require extended durations, anesthesia protocols should be tailored to the specific requirements of the procedure, which may include repeated administration or the use of inhalant anesthetics. Additionally, no significant difference was observed in RORR among the three protocols in females (P=0.070, ε2=0.380). However, the moderate effect size (ε2=0.380) suggests that protocol-related differences may exist and could be detected with increased sample size.

Our results also revealed sex-related differences in anesthetic duration between MMB and dMMB protocols, highlighting the importance of considering sex in injectable anesthesia design. Previous studies reported that gonadectomy of post-pubertal male mice increases resistance to volatile anesthetics, whereas acute testosterone administration enhances anesthetic sensitivity [23]. Similarly, a recent study reported that MMB/dMMB anesthesia tends to be shorter in females than in males [24], which is consistent with our findings and suggests a potential role of testosterone in modulating anesthetic sensitivity. Zhang et al. demonstrated that estrogen receptor A in GABAergic neurons of the medial preoptic area contributes to sevoflurane sensitivity in male mice [29]. Midazolam acts as a GABA-A receptor agonist. Future studies should clarify how hormonal environments influence sensitivity to midazolam-based protocols. Moreover, sex differences in drug-metabolizing enzyme expression are well established [26], highlighting the need for research into sex-related variations in the pharmacodynamics and pharmacokinetics of injectable anesthetics.

No significant differences in HR or SpO2 were observed among the three protocols. Although recent study reported higher SpO2 in males with dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0) compared to MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0) [24], our results showed no significant differences between these protocols. Effect size analysis indicated low statistical power for HR and SpO2 (see Limitations section below for details), suggesting that Type II errors cannot be excluded for these non-significant findings. Nevertheless, dMMB preserved physiological parameters similar to MMB and showed no adverse events, thereby supporting its safety profile. In contrast, body temperature recovery following atipamezole reversal was significantly faster with dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) than with the other protocols in both sexes. Previous reports indicated that MMB (0.2/6.0/10.0), equivalent to dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0), required less time for temperature recovery than MMB (0.3/4.0/5.0) [22], consistent with our findings. In addition, a significant difference in body temperature was observed between dMMB (0.25/4.0/5.0) and MMB (0.5/4.0/5.0) in males, which is consistent with recent reports [24]. MMB has been reported to cause severe hypothermia [21], an effect likely attributable to the action of medetomidine, an α2-adrenoceptor agonist [22]. The present findings indicate that, although dMMB also induces hypothermia similar to MMB, reducing the dose of dexmedetomidine can prevent excessive temperature decline. Therefore, the dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) protocol can be considered a safer anesthetic method in terms of hypothermia prevention and temperature recovery during emergence from anesthesia. In the present analysis, the body temperature measured immediately after atipamezole administration at 40 min following anesthesia in male mice was significantly lower in the dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0) group than in the other two groups. The factors responsible for this temperature difference among the three protocols, however, remain unclear. Tashiro et al. previously reported that body temperature in male mice without thermal support decreases in a medetomidine dose-dependent manner [22]. In the present study, we did not monitor post-anesthetic temporal changes in body temperature without the use of a warming pad. Moreover, we did not assess the return of the righting reflex (RORR) following atipamezole administration and thus were unable to directly compare the three protocols. Therefore, future studies should address these limitations and perform detailed comparative analyses under conditions aligned with those of previous investigations.

Reported adverse effects of triple-combination anesthesia include hyperglycemia and corneal opacity [17, 19]. In this study, blood glucose levels did not differ among protocols. Hyperglycemia observed with the triple-combination injectable anesthesia protocol is attributable to suppression of insulin secretion from pancreatic β-cells by medetomidine/dexmedetomidine, both of which are α2-adrenoceptor agonists [2, 27]. Recent studies suggest that dexmedetomidine does not affect ATP-sensitive potassium (KATP) channels, voltage-dependent potassium channels, or voltage-dependent calcium channels, but rather inhibits signaling pathways mediated by α2-adrenoceptors or the exocytosis of insulin granules, thereby potentially suppressing glucose-stimulated insulin secretion in pancreatic β-cells [12]. Particular caution is warranted when MMB/dMMB anesthesia is used in diabetic model animals or in studies involving blood biochemical measurements. Ocular cloudiness was not observed, consistent with previous reports in C57BL/6 mice [24], though precaution is required in susceptible strains.

The primary limitation of this study is the insufficient sample size (n=5 per group), which resulted in low statistical power and consequently limits the reliability of non-significant findings. Specifically, the power for analyses of heart rate (HR) and blood oxygen saturation (SpO2) was considerably low (HR: power=0.357; SpO2: power=0.543), indicating that Type II errors (false negatives) cannot be ruled out. Future studies should conduct a priori power analyses and adopt a minimum sample size of n=8–10 per group to achieve adequate statistical power (≥0.80). Although we verified the assumptions of ANOVA (normality, homogeneity of variances, and sphericity for repeated measures) and implemented alternative approaches when necessary, including the use of the nonparametric Kruskal–Wallis test and adjustment of P-values, future research should continue to adopt such measures to ensure the validity of statistical inferences. Limitations of this study also include evaluation of strategies for prolonged anesthesia, such as re-administration or inhalation adjuncts. Future studies should address pharmacokinetics, reproducibility across strains, and additional physiological parameters (e.g., hormonal fluctuations) to optimize anesthesia protocols. In addition, certain aspects related to the evaluation of anesthetic recovery following atipamezole administration were not examined in this study. As these unaddressed aspects represent limitations of the present study, they should be elucidated in future investigations.

In summary, we compared conventional MMB anesthesia with two dMMB variants, evaluating induction time, anesthesia depth, maintenance duration, physiological parameters, and recovery. Our findings demonstrate that dMMB (0.1/6.0/10.0), using low-dose dexmedetomidine, achieves induction and sedation comparable to MMB while significantly shortening recovery time and reducing hypothermia risk. HR and SpO2 remained stable, indicating similar cardiovascular and respiratory safety. These results suggest that dMMB offers advantages over MMB in terms of animal welfare, particularly for short surgical procedures. As an injectable protocol without ketamine, dMMB also holds practical value from regulatory and welfare perspectives.
