Balancing Safety and Efficacy: Uncemented Versus Cemented Total Hip Arthroplasty in the Elderly
Authors: Robert J. Carangelo, Sara E. Strecker, Mark Shekhman, Dan Witmer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background and Objective:The increasing use of total hip arthroplasty (THA) in elderly patients has led to debate regarding optimal femoral fixation. Although press-fit (noncemented) fixation remains common, there is concern regarding its association with increased periprosthetic fracture risk in elderly populations with compromised bone quality. We evaluated the outcomes and complications of cemented versus press-fit fixation in a well-matched, elderly cohort.Materials and Methods:This was a retrospective study of 1449 patients who, between January 2020 and December 2023, underwent a primary, elective, unilateral THA. Patients were subdivided by decade (70 to 79, 80 to 99-year-old groups). Demographics, clinical factors, surgical parameters, and postoperative patient outcomes, including fracture within the first 90 days, were evaluated. Predictive modeling was used to evaluate risk factors for cementation.Results:Eleven periprosthetic fractures occurred exclusively in the noncemented group (P = 0.186). The calculated number needed to treat indicated that in patients aged 70 to 99 years, 168 cemented procedures would prevent one periprosthetic fracture; this number decreased to 60 in the 80 to 99 year subgroup. Older age, lower body mass index, female sex, anterior approach, and low preoperative Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcomes Score all increased cementation risk.Conclusion:Although noncemented THA remains generally safe for elderly patients, it does carry an increased risk of periprosthetic fracture, a serious complication with notable morbidity. Orthopaedic surgeons should thoroughly counsel elderly patients regarding fracture risks associated with noncemented femoral fixation. Our large, well-matched cohort adds robust evidence supporting careful patient selection and discussion of cemented fixation benefits in this population.

Total hip arthroplasty (THA) is one of the most common and successful orthopaedic procedures performed worldwide providing substantial improvements in the quality of life for patients with severe osteoarthritis.1-3 The rate of THA has increased substantially in the past 15 years in the United States, with a 22% increase between 2009 and 2019.4,5 The design of the replacement implants has evolved to increase patient comfort and reduce complication rates, while also increasing implant survivability.6-9

Historically, early designs featured a cemented stem using poly methyl methacrylate to adhere the femoral component to the bone within the intramedullary canal.10 Press-fit fixation uses osseous integration to affix the implant to the bone in noncemented stem designs, allowing the stem to integrate.11 Most THAs performed today in the United States are noncemented. Data from the 2016 to 2017 National Readmissions Database showed that in primary elective THA, approximately 7.9% received a THA with a cemented femoral implant, whereas 92.1% received an noncemented THA.12 The 2024 American Joint Replacement Registry shows that overall, in 2023, 5.1% of THAs were cemented, with a higher percentage of cemented stems in older populations; 6.5% of stems are cemented in 70 to 79 year olds, but that number rises to 34.6% in patients older than 90 years.13

The US census estimates that by 2030, older Americans will make up 21% of the population, and by 2060, that number is expected to be 25%.14 Controversy surrounds whether it is safe to use a noncemented THA prosthesis in this older population, primarily due to poor bone quality, which correlated with advancing age, as more than 20% of those older than 65 years have been shown to be osteoporotic.15,16 More and more elderly patients are being treated with noncemented THA implants.15 The concern regarding noncemented fixation in the elderly population, particularly females, is an increased rate of periprosthetic fracture and stem subsidence.17,18 The National Health Service in England, for example, recommended in 2020 that at least 80% of patients older than 70 years should receive a cemented or hybrid prosthesis.19

Although noncemented femoral implants have been shown to have improved survival in a younger age group (55 to 74 years), recent studies have shown no difference in long-term survival compared with cemented implants in patients older than 75 years.20,21 When these noncemented implants do fail, it is often due to proximal bone loss and reduced bone density in the lateral trochanter, which can lead to periprosthetic fracture within this population.15 Registry studies have shown that noncemented THAs have a higher rate of revision due to fracture and aseptic loosening.22,23

The purpose of this study is to evaluate primary outcomes for cemented and noncemented THA implants in an elderly population, subdivided by decade (70 to 79, 80 to 99), at a single high-volume tertiary center. Patients in the same age range who underwent a THA with cemented femoral fixation were compared with those who underwent a noncemented femoral fixation. Postoperative outcomes, such as Hip Disability and Osteoarthritis Outcomes Score for Joint Replacement (HOOS JR), were assessed, as well as complications including periprosthetic fracture. We hypothesized that THA using noncemented femoral fixation would have a similar recovery to a cemented cohort, with a higher risk of periprosthetic fracture.

Methods

This retrospective study was approved by the institutional review board (HHC—2023- 0010) and took place at a large, urban, orthopaedic specialty hospital. Unilateral, elective, primary THA patients, between 70 and 99 years, were included. Conversion and revision patients were excluded, as were those who underwent a THA due to cancer, trauma, or fracture. Only those who had surgery between January 1, 2020, and December 30, 2023, were included, with totaled 1,449 patients (40% of all THAs done during that period). Overall, 2,131 patients younger than 70 years were also operated on in the same time frame who were not included.

All patients underwent a medical optimization program before surgery, as well as risk stratification. Patients from eight fellowship-trained, private practice surgeons were assessed. For most patients (1,315 patients, 90.8%), a posterior approach was used, with an anterior approach used in 133 patients (9.2%). Most surgeries were done manually (1,097, 75.7%), but some cases used computer navigation (281, 19.2%) or robotic assistance (69, 4.7%). Implants from Zimmer BioMet (Zimmer), Stryker (Stryker), and Smith & Nephew (Smith & Nephew) were used, depending on surgeon preference, and all implants used noncollared stems. The use of noncemented or cemented femoral fixation was an intraoperative decision and was surgeon dependent. Perioperatively, all patients received intravenous antibiotics and tranexamic acid. Physical therapy was started on postoperative day 0, with all patients encouraged to bear weight as tolerated.

Demographic data, including medical history, implant characteristics, and inpatient stay parameters, like physical therapy outcomes, were extracted from the electronic medical record. Complications within the first 90 days postsurgery were also assessed. Surveys, such as the HOOS JR, were collected using FORCE Therapeutics, which is an online engagement platform (Force Therapeutics, New York, NY). A minimally clinically important difference threshold of 18 patients for the HOOS JR was chosen per Hung et al.24

Single outcome measures, like readmission due to fracture or discharge to a skilled nursing facility (SNF) were compared using chi-squared tests of proportion or Fisher exact tests. Pain level, surgical time, and other continuous variables were compared using Student t-tests. Univariate logistic regression models were used to determine potential risk factors for having a cemented implant. Variables in the initial regression modeling included age, sex, body mass index (BMI), approach, preoperative HOOS JR scores, Risk Assessment and Prediction Tool (RAPT) scores, the presence of a bone density scan within 1 year before the index surgery, and the use of technology during surgery. A nonparametric receiver operator curve analysis was done where variables with an area under the curve over 0.7 were considered to be predictors, and a cut point was calculated for all variables of interest. All statistics were completed using STATA (Version 17, Statcorp, College Station, TX) or Microsoft Excel 2016 (Microsoft, Redmond, WA). A result of P < 0.05 was used to determine statistical significance.

Results

Overall, 1,449 patients met the criteria and were included in the study, as seen in Table 1. Table 1 shows demographics for all patients at the time of surgery, sorted by age group, with 365 patients aged 80 to 99 years and 1,084 patients aged 70 to 79 years. The THA population is overwhelmingly female and non-Hispanic White. Those in the older age group have a lower BMI and markedly worse (P < 0.001) RAPT and Charlson comorbidity index scores, indicating that they are, as a whole, a sicker population. Physical therapy parameters also echoed this conclusion, with lower ambulation distances (161 vs. 183 ft, P < 0.001), and patients were more than twice as likely to be discharged to a SNF (19.7% vs. 7.7%). Older patients were also twice as likely to return to the emergency department (ED; 15.3% vs. 7.7%) or be readmitted within 90 days (9.9% vs. 4.8%). HOOS JR scores were compared between both age groups, which showed that patients received the same benefit, although the older group started with lower scores. A similar percentage of patients in both groups (73 vs. 72%) met the minimal clinically important difference. As there were more than double the amount of cemented hips in the older age group, patients with cemented and noncemented hips were first striated by age, then compared.

No differences were seen in length of stay or postoperative acute care unit between any cohort, although the surgical time was longer in the cemented groups, due to the cement needing time to cure. Patients in the cemented group in both cohorts were more likely to receive a cemented implant if an anterior approach was used (P < 0.001).

Patients in the 80 to 99 year group with a cemented THA had markedly higher pain at rest than those in the age matched noncemented group (P = 0.027), although this difference was not seen when pain levels associated with activity were assessed (P = 0.132; Table 3). No differences were found in pain scores at rest or with activity in the cemented versus the noncemented groups in the 70 to 79 cohort (Table 2). Physical therapy parameters, like ambulation distance, timed up and go score, and the Activity Measure for Post-Acute Care subscores, were not different between the noncemented and cemented THA groups in any of the age-matched cohorts tested.

No differences were seen in discharge disposition, return to the ED within 90 days, or readmission within 90 days between the cemented and noncemented groups in age-matched cohorts, although a much higher percentage of patients in the older age group returned to the ED or were discharged to a SNF. All fractures seen within 90 days occurred in the noncemented group, with 11 patients overall returning to the operating room. Of these fractures, five occurred in patients between 80 and 99 years and six occurred in patients between the ages of 70 and 79 years. This was not statistically significant (P = 0.120). No intraoperative fractures were found. Dorr classification for these patients showed two with type A, six with type B, and three with type C. Vancouver classification was also assessed for these fractures. The majority (nine) were type B2, and the rest were Ag. All patients who fractured had a posterior approach used during their surgery. No one brand of implant was overrepresented in this cohort, and all fractures occurred in implants with noncollared stems. Of those patients who experienced a fracture, 81.8% were female and 18.2% were male. In our cohort of patients older than 70 years, an additional 168 patients would need to be treated with a cemented implant to prevent one periprosthetic fracture. The number needed to treat with a cemented arthroplasty drops to 60 patients in the 80 to 99 age group to prevent one periprosthetic fracture.

Predictive modeling was used to determine risk factors for cementation. Modeling showed that age, BMI, sex, preoperative HOOS JR scores, and the approach were factors that contributed to a patient having a cemented implant. RAPT score, preoperative bone density scans, and the use of technology were not found to be predictive. Women were four times more likely to have a cemented implant. Those who had an anterior approach THA were six times more likely to be cemented. Low BMI, low preoperative HOOS JR scores, and older age were all risk factors for cementing (Table 4).

Discussion

THA offers pain relief and increased mobility for patients of all ages. As the population as a whole ages, so does the prevalence of degenerative joint disease and the demand for a THA procedure.25,26 We found that in our population, patients receiving a cemented THA were likely to be older, have an anterior approach hip, and female compared with the noncemented cohort. This is consistent with the findings of Chen et al,12 which showed that cemented implants were more likely to be used in women older than 70 years with more medical comorbidities. The decision to cement is surgeon dependent and occurs at the time of surgery. Vitamin D levels and bone density scans are not routinely run on our patients. Although no differences were found based on age in who had the dual-energy x-ray absorptiometry compared with who did not (P = 0.123), the percentage of patients who had a bone density scan was very small and could be improved upon. Cemented implants were also more likely to be used in patients where an anterior approach was used to complete the arthroplasty. This is likely due to a lower surgeon threshold for cementing due to the increased fracture risk associated with the anterior approach.27 We did not see differences in postoperative acute care unit time or length of stay in our patient population, as striated by decade. More pain was seen initially in the cemented group in the 80 to 99 cohort, but this was not seen in the other cohorts. The differences in ED utilization, facility discharge, timed up and go, and ambulation distance differ only between age groups and do not differ between press fit or cement. In all cases, the older group had worse outcomes. Bone cement implantation syndrome is a rare complication that can occur in older fragile patients, but no cases of this were seen in our cemented patients.

Patient-reported outcomes (PROs) were overwhelmingly similar between the groups, although preoperative PROs were lower as the population aged. Older age is associated with less improvement in PROs,28 although some association with cemented implants compared with noncemented implants has been postulated29; we did not see a difference in PROs between the cemented and noncemented cohorts. We did note that for every 1-point decrease in the HOOS JR scores below 43.3, the patient was 2% more likely to require a cemented implant, through this is likely not a practical measure in application.

Although noncemented fixation in THA is commonly employed, our data highlight a clinically relevant increase in periprosthetic fracture risk among elderly patients. Specifically, no fractures occurred in our cemented cohort, whereas eleven fractures occurred exclusively in the noncemented cohort, underscoring the potential severity of this complication. The calculated number needed to treat further emphasizes this risk: 168 patients aged 70 to 99 years would require cemented implants to prevent one fracture, a number that drops markedly to 60 in patients aged 80 to 99 years.

Although this study provides insights into this elderly patient population, it does have inherent limitations. This is a single-center study including multiple surgeons and is retrospective in design, which may limit how generalizable the findings are to the population as a whole. Our study population was limited in diversity, with the majority being non-Hispanic Whites, who often have higher rates of osteoporosis compared with other ethnicities, and subsequently higher fracture risk.30 Future research could examine more diverse patient populations. In addition, no fractures or dislocations were seen in the cemented group, which due to how rare this complication is, can make comparisons difficult and was underpowered to determine true fracture rate. In addition, although almost all periprosthetic fractures locally are referred to our hospital, it is possible that a patient may have had a fracture and been treated elsewhere, and some patients could have been lost to follow-up. This study was not able to tease out the impact of noncemented implant design on fracture risk, as there were only 11 fractures but, implant design may also play a role.

In conclusion, our study demonstrates that although press-fit THA implants are generally safe and yield comparable PROs to cemented implants, they do pose an increased risk of periprosthetic fracture in elderly patients. Given the notable morbidity associated with fractures, patients must be appropriately counseled regarding this risk, with the potential addition of prehabilitation or nutritional programs to mitigate this risk. The findings from this large urban data set reinforce the importance of individualized patient assessment and highlight the protective effect of cemented fixation against periprosthetic fracture in elderly populations.
