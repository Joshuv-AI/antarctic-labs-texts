Selective real-time disulfide reduction with simultaneous fluorescent labeling of a humanized anti-cocaine mAb
Authors: Terence L. Kirley, Andrew B. Norman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
A procedure for rapid screening and development of facile and limited partial reduction, with simultaneous fluorescent labeling, of a humanized anti-cocaine mAb was devised and characterized. This employed a standard soluble protein reductant, tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP), paired with an alkylating agent that can be used without removal of the reductant, yielding a fluorescent adduct of the generated cysteine. This reaction can also be monitored by absorbance in a nanophotometer, allowing for rapid, simple screening of reduction and alkylation conditions using small mAb amounts and volumes. The degree of reduction is readily controllable, and yields results similar to limited reductions followed by separate labeling reactions, as recently reported using immobilized TCEP, and a less reactive phosphine reductant, triphenylphosphine-3,3′,3″-trisulfonic acid (TPPTS). The overall structure of the mAb is not perturbed, and the thermal stability of the Fab portion of the mAb, where most selective disulfide reductions occur, is only minimally decreased. The antigen (cocaine) binding and binding thermodynamics are not changed, as demonstrated by isothermal titration calorimetry. However, differential scanning fluorimetry demonstrated that the thermal stabilization of the Fab domain by cocaine binding is dramatically decreased after reductive labeling to a level of 5.44 modified cys/mAb, suggesting the importance of light-heavy chain disulfide bonds for the cocaine-induced thermal stabilization of the mAb Fab. Methods described in this work should aid in the selective reduction and labeling of disulfides in mAbs and other proteins, assisting the assignment of structural and functional importance to specific protein disulfide bonds.

Introduction

Cocaine use disorder affects more than 1.3 million adults in the US alone, with the one year relapse rate estimated at about 60 % (from the 2023 National Survey on Drug Use and Health (NSDUH) data). In 2022 alone, 27,569 people in the US died from cocaine-related overdoses (NIDA drug overdose deaths facts). Globally, the United Nations lists cocaine 4th in terms of worldwide numbers of drug users, behind only cannabis, opioids, and amphetamines, in that order (https://news.un.org/en/story/2022/06/1121472). There is no current US FDA approved drug for cocaine use disorder, and thus a treatment for cocaine use disorder remains an important unmet medical need. To address this need, a high (nM) affinity anti-cocaine mAb (named “h2E2”) is under development in our laboratory for the potential treatment of cocaine use disorders [[1], [2], [3]].

Limited and selective labeling of antibodies has many experimental and therapeutic applications, one of which is the generation of antibody-drug conjugates (ADCs), which have most commonly been generated via conjugation to lysine or cysteine residues on antibodies. ADCs have become very important for the treatment of many cancers, and are the standard of care for metastatic advanced breast cancer [4]. The combination of ADCs with immune checkpoint inhibitors for the treatment of breast cancer is an important extension of their use in breast cancer therapy [5]. ADCs also have great potential for delivering prodrugs for cancer therapies [6]. Although a 2025 review indicates that there were 14 ADCs approved by the FDA as of the end of 2024 [7], there were no ADCs approved by the FDA in 2024, continuing a disturbing trend of low FDA approval of ADCs observed in 2023. One reason for this trend is the difficulty in synthesis of ADCs which display the requisite stability, drug payload amount, and degree of conjugation homogeneity, despite much study and effort to improve the synthesis, stability, homogeneity, and therapeutic utility of ADCs. This is both true for ADCs in general [8], and for ADCs based on conjugation via cysteine residues in the mAb, which residues can be introduced by mutation or by selective reduction of mAb disulfide bonds [9]. Thus, advances in cysteine-based conjugation methods to generate ADCs such as described in this study are important, both for the generation of experimental mAb probes and for future antibody therapeutics.

We previously characterized the limited labeling of lysine residues of this anti-cocaine mAb by fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC [10]). Other methods have been developed to label antibodies, including methods based on lysine-selective biotin labeling [[11], [12], [13]], alkylation of endogenous free cysteine residues and labeling after partial reduction of mAb disulfides [14], introduction of non-native free cysteine by site directed mutagenesis followed by labeling [15], and multi-step complex labeling of the antibody hinge region [14,16].

We recently developed methods and procedures for limited and selective reduction of the anti-cocaine mAb using the less reactive water-soluble phosphine, triphenylphosphine-3,3′,3″-trisulfonic acid (TPPTS) reductant in solution, or tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine (TCEP) covalently linked to agarose beads [17]. However, neither of these partial reduction methods allow real-time monitoring of disulfide reduction, and both methods also require removal of the reducing agents prior to subsequent alkylation and fluorescent labeling of the generated cysteines with fluorescein maleimide, or with most other cysteine-selective chemical modification reagents [17]. This can lead to lower yields, as well as unwanted air re-oxidation of the reduced cysteine residues to disulfides, and possible mAb aggregation, occurring after reduction but prior to alkylation and labeling.

In the current work, methods and procedures for in-solution selective reduction of this mAb under mild conditions were developed, which allow simple real-time monitoring of the progress of the reaction, using small amounts and small (μl) volumes of the mAb. This simplifies screening and optimization of different reaction conditions, buffers, and reaction times, to facilitate the attainment of the desired degree of disulfide reduction and labeling.

A standard reductant was chosen, i.e., tris (2-carboxyethyl) phosphine hydrochloride (TCEP), and an alkylation reagent highly selective for cysteine was employed (ABD-F), which generates a cysteine adduct that is both a chromophore and a fluorophore, and which can be used simultaneously with TCEP and other phosphine reductants to alkylate the freshly reduced disulfides, without requiring separation from, or inactivation of, the reductant. TCEP is superior to tributyl phosphine (TBP) for this limited and selective disulfide reduction, since TCEP is more water soluble than TBP, as well as being far less toxic, although TBP may be better for attaining complete reduction of all the disulfide bonds, due to its hydrophobicity, allowing access to and reduction of non-exposed and difficult to reduce disulfide bonds under denaturing conditions [18]. The sulfhydryl-selective reagent, 4-(aminosulfonyl)-7-fluoro-2,1,3-benzoxadiazole (ABD-F) was utilized in this study, since it is non-fluorescent before reaction with sulfhydryls, and reacts rapidly and selectively with cysteine to generate a cysteine adduct which both absorbs at 385 nm, and fluoresces (385 nm excitation, 520 nm emission) [[19], [20], [21]].

This ABD-F alkylation reaction can be monitored in real time at 385 nm, using small volumes and amounts of mAb and a nanophotometer. Various times and TCEP concentrations were employed to reduce and label the mAb to various extents (i.e., various degrees of labeling (DOL), in this case [ABD-cys]/[mAb]). Anti-cocaine mAb labeled in this manner to varying extents was then characterized by non-reducing and reducing SDS-PAGE, agarose native gel electrophoresis, differential scanning fluorimetry (using 2 distinct dye probes), and isothermal titration calorimetry to assess the effects of the limited and selective disulfide bond reduction and ABD-F alkylation on the structure and stability of the mAb, as well as on the characteristics and thermodynamics of antigen (cocaine) binding. The relationship between the fluorescence and absorbance of the ABD-F modified mAb was also examined, leading to some interesting and important observations. The advantages and limitations of the methods and reagents used are briefly discussed.

This selective reduction of mAb disulfides should be applicable to many antibodies, and especially to mAbs of the IgG1 class, which is a very common immunoglobulin class for therapeutic mAbs, since all these IgG1 antibodies have the same 16 disulfide bonds present in the same approximate locations. This strongly suggests that these disulfides would share the same relative sensitivity to partial reduction, and thus our methods and results would be expected to be applicable to many mAbs used for research and therapeutic purposes.

Experimental procedures

Materials

The h2E2 anti-cocaine monoclonal antibody was supplied by the manufacturer, Catalent, as previously described [1]. TCEP-HCl was purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific (catalog PG80080), and ABD-F was from TCI America (catalog A5597), via ThermoFisher Scientific. DTT and all electrophoresis chemicals were from BioRad. 10 mM stock solutions of cocaine in distilled water were made from solid cocaine-HCl (obtained from the Research Triangle Institute) as described [3]. Briefly, stock cocaine solutions were generated by weighing solid cocaine-HCL (formula weight = 339.82 g/mol), and are expressed as the free base cocaine concentrations, with these concentrations being confirmed by measuring cocaine absorbance at 232 nm (the extinction coefficient for cocaine at 232 nm is 13,000 M−1cm−1). Zeba Spin 0.5 ml 7k MWCO size exclusion desalting columns (catalog 89882) were from ThermoFisher Scientific. Sephadex G-50 size exclusion chromatography beads were purchased from Sigma, and disposable columns used for buffer exchange and reagent removal were obtained from BioRad (Econo-Pac 14 columns, catalog # 7321010EDU). Polypropylene Eppendorf tubes (catalog 05-0408-129), borosilicate glass 12 × 75 mm test tubes (catalog 14-961-26), and MOPS buffer were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific. Ultrapure agarose for the protein native gels was from BRL Life Technologies (electrophoresis grade, catalog 5510UA). PBS buffer concentrate (10X) was purchased from Cambrex (BioWhittaker, without calcium or magnesium, catalog 17-517Q). Sypro orange 5000X dye stock was obtained from Invitrogen/Life technologies, and the solid 4-(4-(dimethylamino)styryl)-N-methylpyridinium iodide dye (4-Di-ASP iodide, DASPMI) was purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific (Invitrogen/Life Technologies catalog # D-288). A DASPMI stock dye solution of 20 mM was made by dissolution of the solid dye in dry DMSO. The Sypro orange and DASPMI dye stock DMSO solutions were stored light protected at −20°C. The Applied Biosystems 48 well RT PCR plates (catalog 4375816) and the MicroAmp plate sealing optical adhesive transparent film (catalog 4375928) used in the StepOne RT PCR used to perform the DSF analyses were obtained from ThermoFisher Scientific.

Methods

TCEP/ABD-F simultaneous reduction and alkylation of h2E2 mAb

4.33 mg/ml (30 μM) h2E2 mAb was incubated at 22 °C for various times in 100 mM MOPS pH = 7.4 buffer, in the presence of a constant concentration of ABD-F (2 mM). In most experiments, varying final concentrations of TCEP reductant were then added, the samples mixed, and after 10–15 s 2–5 μl aliquots of each sample were monitored at 385 nm using an Implen N60 nanophotometer using its built-in kinetics program, recording absorbance every 15 s for 1 h. In some experiments, 2–5 μl aliquots of the reaction solution were taken at various times for dilution into PBS buffer (to a total volume of 1.0 ml) to measure the fluorescence at 22 °C of the formed ABD-cysteine adduct (exciting at 385 nm and measuring the emission at 520 nm) using a Hitachi F-2000 fluorescence spectrophotometer. The formation of ABD-cys was quantitated using the ABD-cys extinction coefficient of 7800 M−1cm−1 at 385 nm [19,20], using either the Implen N60 nanophotometer for small volumes (2 μl), or a Beckman DU800 spectrophotometer (and a micro cuvette) for larger volume (100 μl) samples.

Separation from excess reagents and buffer exchange of partially reduced and ABD-F reacted mAb

In some experiments, reactions were stopped using size exclusion chromatography, employing either 0.5 ml Zeba spin columns (for 100 μl reactions), or 9.5 ml Sephadex G-50 columns (for 1.0 ml reactions), and exchanging the partially reduced and labeled mAb into either 50 mM MOPS 7.4 buffer (for the 100 μl samples) or PBS buffer (for the 1.0 mL samples, to be used in the subsequent DSF and ITC analyses), while removing the excess reagents. G-50 sizing column fractions containing mAb were pooled in glass test tubes for further analyses. The concentration of ABD-cys was quantitated as described above (using the ABD-cys extinction coefficient at 385 nm of 7800 M−1cm−1), and the mAb concentration was calculated using a mAb extinction coefficient of 219,500 M−1cm−1, and a correction factor for the absorbance of ABD-cys at 280 nm of 0.37, i.e., the absorbance of ABD-cys at 280 nm is 0.37 x the absorbance of ABD-cys at 385 nm: 0.37 x 7800 M−1cm−1 = 2890 M−1cm−1 , which is the calculated molar extinction coefficient for ABD-cys at 280 nm.

SDS-PAGE analysis of control and labeled mAb samples

4 μg of each mAb sample were analyzed on 7–12 % gradient acrylamide SDS-PAGE gels according to Laemmli [10], after dilution to 0.2 mg/ml mAb and boiling for 3 min in either reducing (+ 100 mM DTT) or non-reducing (+ 20 mM NEM) sample buffer. The SDS-PAGE gels were photographed for fluorescence using a 365 nm transillumination light source and an Azure 280 gel imager, and the fluorescent bands quantified using ImageJ software. The gels were stained for 1 h with Coomassie blue, and destained overnight, basically as described by Laemmli [22], prior to photography.

Agarose native gel analysis of control and selectively reduced and labeled mAb samples

The 100 mM histidine/100 mM MES pH 6.2 buffer native agarose gel protein system [[23], [24], [25]] was used to detect potential aggregation of partially reduced and labeled mAb, as well as possible changes in the electrophoretic migration rate of the mAb caused by disulfide reduction and labeling. 5 μg of each mAb sample was loaded per well on a 1 % agarose horizontal slab gel after dilution of samples with 50 % glycerol/50 % pH 6.2 native gel buffer. The gels were electrophoresed at 72 V for 3 h at room temperature (changing the gel running buffer after running for 1.5 h), using crystal violet dye as the tracking dye. The agarose gels were stained for 1 h with Coomassie blue, and destained overnight, basically as described by Laemmli [22].

Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) of TCEP/ABD-F treated h2E2 mAb

Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) was performed using a ThermoFisher Scientific/Life Technologies StepOne RT PCR, using “Rox” dye calibration settings (fluorescence excitation at 485 nm, with emission at 610 nm), and the passive reference “quencher” designated as none [10]. For each sample, duplicate 20 μl aliquots in pH 7.4 PBS buffer were loaded onto the 48 well plate, with all samples containing 2.5 μM h2E2 anti-cocaine mAb in pH 7.4 PBS buffer, and either 50 μM DASPMI or a 1:500 dilution of commercial (5000X) Sypro orange dye stock, and varying final concentrations of cocaine, ranging from 0 to 1000 μM. DSF plates containing the samples were heated from 35 °C to 95 °C at a rate of 0.45 °C/min, (a heating ramp rate of 0.3 %) as previously described [26]. It is important to note that there is no fluorescence spectral overlap between the ABD-cys label covalently bound to the mAb and either the Sypro orange or the DASPMI dyes, permitting DSF analyses of ABD-F labeled mAb protein based on these dyes.

Isothermal titration calorimetry (ITC) of TCEP/ABD-F treated h2E2 mAb to assess cocaine binding

After partial reduction and labeling, all samples for ITC analyses were buffer exchanged into pH 7.4 PBS using G-50 Sephadex sizing columns, as described above. The exact same PBS buffer was also used to dilute the 10 mM stock cocaine solution and each of the h2E2 mAb samples analyzed by ITC. The ITC experiments were carried out at 20 °C in a MicroCal VP-ITC instrument in PBS buffer as was previously described [27]. Before fitting the ITC data, each ITC integrated peak from each cocaine ITC injection was blank corrected by subtracting the average area of the integrated peaks from the final 5 injections of the ligand (i.e., using the integrated cocaine ligand injection peak data obtained after the mAb binding sites were fully saturated with cocaine ligand). These blank corrected data were analyzed and fitted to a one binding site model using the Origin 7.0 software supplied with the instrument [10,27,28]. Cocaine dissociation constants (Kd values) were calculated as the reciprocal of the association constants (K values) calculated by the Origin software, and the Gibbs free energy values (ΔG) were derived using the equation, ΔG = ΔH – TΔS.

Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) is used to detect both protein thermal denaturation and ligand stabilization of protein domains. Specifically, for the h2E2 mAb, DSF has been used to characterize the binding of cocaine and cocaine metabolites to the mAb [26]. Therefore, DSF was used to assess possible negative consequences on the stability of the protein domains in this mAb, and the effects of cocaine binding on mAb thermal stability, both employing the DASPMI rotor dye (Fig. 7) and the more commonly used Sypro orange dye (Fig. 8). DASPMI dye DSF has been used to calculate binding affinities of cocaine and cocaine metabolites at elevated temperatures for this anti-cocaine mAb, since the DASPMI dye only binds to and reports on the part of the Fab domain of the mAb which binds cocaine [26]. Thus, in Fig. 8A, this destabilization of the cocaine-induced stabilization of the binding domain can also be seen, but it is less obvious, due to the Sypro orange dye also binding to the Fc domain of the mAb (unlike the DASPMI dye, which does not bind to Fc [26]), giving rise to multiple, poorly resolved, dFl/dT DSF peaks in the absence of cocaine (Fig. 8A). Discussion

A major objective of this study was to develop a simple and rapid method to screen for and optimize the partial selective reduction of the h2E2 anti-cocaine mAb under mild conditions, employing a very commonly used and effective water-soluble reductant, TCEP. This was accomplished by simultaneous labeling of the generated cysteine residues by an excess of the cysteine selective reagent, ABD-F. This permits real time monitoring of the degree of reduction by simple absorbance measurements, which can be done using nanoliter spectrophotometers, allowing screening data to be obtained quickly using small volumes and amounts of mAb (or other disulfide-containing proteins).

Fig. 1 is an example of such a screening experiment. Absorbance kinetic data can be obtained using 1–2 μl of sample, and fluorescence data can also be acquired on the same sample by taking μl aliquots from the sample on which the nanoliter absorbance kinetics measurements are performed, thereby using as little as 20 μl of total mAb reaction solution to obtain both absorbance and fluorescence kinetic data. The kinetics of reaction can also be monitored by either fluorescence or absorbance after the mAb is separated from excess reagents (Fig. 2). However, it is noted that there is some background (i.e., signal generated with no TCEP present) 385 nm absorbance generated in both cases, as evidenced by the non-zero absorbance detected in the nanoliter spectrophotometer (Fig. 1, Panel A) as well as after reagent removal (Fig. 2, the black (non-zero absorbance at 385 nm) symbol measured at 0 mM TCEP). Notably, there is no such 0 mM TCEP background when ABD-cys fluorescence is measured, either without removing reagents (Fig. 1B, “0 mM TCEP” data) or after removal of excess reagents (Fig. 2, where zero fluorescence is measured for the 0 mM TCEP treated mAb sample). The fact that the background 385 nm absorbance is not eliminated by removal of reagents after reaction is also evident in the 0 mM TCEP treated sample spectra shown in Fig. 6.

We noted that the very faint yellow color of a freshly made 40 mM stock solution of ABD-F in DMSO becomes slightly more yellow with time of storage, even at −20 °C, indicating some change in the reagent which likely contributes to the observed background 385 nm absorbance. This is consistent with the black, 0 mM TCEP, trace in Fig. 1A rising slightly above zero, even in the absence of mAb, due to some slight modification of ABD-F in buffer alone within 1 h at 22 °C. However, the retention of some 385 nm absorbance even after separation of excess reagents from the TCEP/ABD-F treated mAb is likely due to some small amount of reaction of ABD-F with other functional groups on proteins, as it has been reported previously that ABD-F can also react with phenolic hydroxyl groups (tyrosine residues) and amino groups, although with greatly reduced efficiency [29]. Another explanation for this background 385 nm absorbance could be that the anti-cocaine mAb contains some cysteine that is not correctly disulfide linked, giving rise to some free cysteine mAb residues which could be labeled by ABD-F. This seems unlikely, however, since there is no fluorescence detected in the 0 mM TCEP/ABD-F treated mAb. This strongly suggests that the small amount of non-selective, background, reaction of ABD-F with the mAb detected by absorbance is due to a small reactivity of ABD-F with tyrosine or amino groups, and that the ABD adduct formed with tyrosine and amino functional groups is not fluorescent, unlike the ABD-cysteine adduct. This also indicates that calculation of the number of ABD-cys per mAb based on 385 nm absorbance will be slightly overestimated, and that the magnitude of this effect can be estimated, and corrected for, by measuring the slight increase in absorbance at 385 nm which occurs in the absence of any TCEP reductant during the reaction.

In terms of the selectivity of the TCEP disulfide bond reduction obtained using the methods described in this study, the gels in Fig. 3, Fig. 6 show that, at the lower concentrations of TCEP, incorporation of the ABD label is consistent with the selective reduction of the disulfides linking each light chain to its associated heavy chain. If only these 2 disulfides were specifically and completely reduced and alkylated, and no other disulfides were reduced, this would give rise to a theoretical DOL of 4 ABD-cys/mAb, which is approximately the DOL seen after reaction with 0.2 mM TCEP in Fig. 2, and intermediate between the 2.64 and 5.44 ABD-cys/mAb observed after reaction with 0.1 and 0.5 mM TCEP, respectively. This disulfide bond selectivity would also lead to the observed preferential labeling of the light chain at low concentrations of TCEP (Fig. 3D), as well as the appearance of the H/H/L and H/H antibody species at low concentrations of TCEP, due to reduction of the two LC-HC disulfides (Fig. 3, Fig. 6). Taken as a whole, the SDS-PAGE gel data in Fig. 3, Fig. 6 at the lower concentrations of TCEP (< 0.3 mM TCEP) are very similar to the gel and stoichiometry data published using TPPTS or TCEP-agarose for selective reduction of this mAb, followed by fluorescein maleimide labeling [17], indicating a similar level of selectivity of mAb disulfide bond reduction.

Differential scanning fluorimetry (DSF) was used to assess the effects of varying degrees of TCEP reduction and ABD-F labeling on the mAb. Both the DSF experiments and the ITC cocaine binding experiments were performed in PBS buffer to allow direct comparison with previously published results. In DSF experiments, both Sypro orange and DASPMI dyes were used, since the Sypro dye is the most commonly used dye in DSF, and binds to hydrophobic patches exposed upon thermal unfolding of the protein leading to an increased dye fluorescence due to a more hydrophobic environment for the dye [30]. The DASPMI dye is a rotor dye, meaning its fluorescence is dependent on the rotational restriction of the dye via binding to sites on the protein exposed by thermal unfolding [31]. An advantage of using the DASPMI dye with the anti-cocaine mAb is that this dye was previously shown to only bind to the Fab portion of the mAb, and not to the Fc part of the mAb [26] (which is likely to also be the case for other mAbs having human sequence heavy chain Fc regions). Partly because of this, DSF data obtained with the DASPMI dye has been used to calculate relative affinities of this antibody for cocaine and cocaine metabolites, since binding of cocaine stabilizes the binding portion of the mAb Fab arms against thermal denaturation, thereby increasing the Tm (melting temperature) of that portion of the mAb, as a function of the binding and the concentration of cocaine [26].

In the absence of cocaine, both dyes revealed a small, TCEP concentration-dependent, decrease in TmD for the mAb. Thus, using the DASPMI dye, the TmD temperatures for the cocaine binding domain of the mAb decreased from 70.3 to 69.6 to 69.0 °C going from 0 to 0.1 to 0.5 mM TCEP (a ΔTmD from the 0 mM TCEP mAb of 0.7 °C and 1.3 °C for 0.1 and 0.5 mM TCEP samples, respectively). In contrast to these small TmD changes observed in the absence of cocaine, large thermal shifts in the TmDs for the mAb labeled using 0.5 mM TCEP are seen in saturating concentrations of cocaine (using 0.5 mM TCEP results in almost all the interchain disulfides connecting the mAb heavy chains to the light chains to be reduced and labeled, see Fig. 3, Fig. 6). Thus, using DASPMI DSF (Fig. 7B), for the 0 mM TCEP treated sample, the cocaine stabilized TmD peak is at 79.3 °C, and this TmD is decreased to 73.0 °C after reduction with 0.5 mM TCEP (a large ΔTmD of 6.3 °C). The 0 mM TCEP sample cocaine-stabilized TmD of 79.3 °C agrees very well with that measured earlier for the control mAb under these conditions, i.e., 79.32 °C [26]. The DASPMI DSF 0.1 mM TCEP treated mAb sample (Fig. 7B, green traces) shows the presence of both the 0 and 0.5 mM TCEP Fab species (i.e., the 79.3 °C and the 73.0 °C TmD peaks), indicating that in the 0.1 mM TCEP treated mAb there is partial conversion of the Fab portion of the mAb molecules to the species that is only poorly thermally stabilized by the binding of cocaine. This is consistent with only some of the disulfides connecting the mAb heavy chains to the light chains being broken at this 0.1 mM TCEP concentration (as seen in Fig. 3, Fig. 6).

The effects of different concentrations of TCEP on the DSF results in the presence of saturating cocaine seen in Fig. 7, Fig. 8 could have resulted from a change in the cocaine binding affinity or the underpinning thermodynamic forces driving the binding of cocaine to the mAb. However, the ITC results shown and quantitated in Fig. 9 indicate there are no differences in cocaine binding or binding thermodynamics among the 0, 0.1, and 0.5 mm TCEP treated mAb samples at 20 °C. The calculated affinities (Kd values) are all in the 7–9 nM range, and the ΔH and ΔG values are all approximately −13400 cal/mol and −10900 cal/mol, respectively, and there is no change in the measured number of cocaine binding sites per Fab (about 0.87). In addition, these binding parameters all agree well with previously published control anti-cocaine mAb values [10,17,27,28], including ITC data obtained using the TPPTS and TCEP-agarose partial reduction methods, which resulted in a Kd of approximately 10 nM and ΔH and ΔG values of approximately −13300 cal/mol and −10700 cal/mol, respectively, for the control mAb in that study [17]. Thus, there is no effect of TCEP selective partial reduction and ABD-cys labeling up to at least a concentration of 0.5 mM TCEP on the binding of cocaine at 20 °C as measured by ITC. However, there is a major effect of 0.5 mM TCEP reduction and labeling on the degree of cocaine-induced thermal stabilization of the cocaine binding domain as measured by DSF. This suggests that the light chain-heavy chain interchain disulfide bonds are not critical for cocaine binding at low temperatures, but are important for the cocaine binding-induced stabilization of the Fab cocaine binding domain against thermal unfolding.

The advantages of the present method compared to those previously published methods for partial, selective reduction of protein disulfides include: (1) the present methods can be followed in in real time using μl volumes of sample to facilitate optimization of conditions; (2) the unreactive ABD-F reagent, unlike most fluorescent labeling reagents, is non-fluorescent before reaction; (3) the present method does not require an additional step of removal of excess reduction reagent prior to labeling, which could lead to re-oxidation of cysteine and/or the formation of non-native disulfides during subsequent handling procedures; (4) reaction with ABD-F does not change the charge on the protein, unlike fluorescein [10] and other labeling reagents (see the native gel in Fig. 4); and (5) ABD-cys labeling does not interfere with Sypro orange or DASPMI fluorescent dye-based DSF analyses (Fig. 7, Fig. 8).

In conclusion, we describe a simple and rapid method for screening and optimizing the partial selective reduction of an anti-cocaine mAb. We employed low concentrations of a very commonly used and effective, water-soluble phosphine reductant, TCEP, under mild and rapid reaction conditions. This limited reduction is coupled with the simultaneous fluorescent labeling of the generated cysteine residues by the cysteine selective reagent, ABD-F, without removal of the reductant prior to alkylation. We characterized the effects of various degrees of partial reduction and labeling of the mAb in terms of its structure, stability, and antigen binding function. The DSF data demonstrates that the limited reduction and alkylation of the resultant cysteine residues resulted in very little change in the thermostability of the mAb, and the ITC data indicate no change in the antigen binding characteristics of this anti-cocaine mAb. These results are very important for the suitability of this method for generating antibody drug conjugates (ADCs), since perturbation of the structure or function of the mAb after conjugation of a label or drug should be minimized or eliminated, so that the conjugated antibody is not deleteriously affected in either its structure or function, which could decrease its efficacy and utility.

This selective reduction of mAb disulfides should be generally applicable to many antibodies, and especially to mAbs of the common IgG1 class, since these antibodies have the same 16 disulfide bonds present in the same approximate locations, suggesting very similar differential sensitivity to reduction of these bonds. Importantly, many therapeutic mAbs are human or humanized IgG1 immunoglobulins (like the anti-cocaine mAb used in this study), making these techniques and findings of selective reduction of specific disulfides reported here likely to be applicable to many mAbs. Thus, the techniques and analyses presented here will aid in the selective reduction and labeling of disulfides in mAbs and should be useful for making antibody drug conjugates, which are not disruptive of overall antibody structure and function. This selective reduction may also prove effective for the evaluation of the structural and functional importance of specific disulfide bonds in other classes of proteins.
