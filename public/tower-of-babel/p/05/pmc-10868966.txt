New-Onset and Exacerbation of Psoriasis following COVID-19 Vaccination: A Systematic Review of Case Reports and Case Series
Authors: Aisyah N. F. Arifin, Antoninus Hengky, Maureen Widjaja, Lorettha Wijaya
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Although uncommon, several cases of new-onset and exacerbation of psoriasis have been reported after coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) vaccination. This study aims to systematically review reports regarding new-onset and exacerbation of psoriasis to identify features related to that condition following COVID-19 vaccination.Materials and Methods:Three databases were utilised, namely, PubMed, Google Scholar, and EBSCOHost to search for literature published until June 2022. We used the following MeSH terms: “psoriasis,” “exacerbation,” “induced psoriasis,” and “COVID-19 vaccination.” The included literature must report the case/(s) of psoriasis exacerbation in previously-in-remission or induced psoriasis in individuals receiving any dose of COVID-19 vaccines regardless of the type.Results:Among 33 selected studies, an overall mean age was 57.52 ± 15.92 years. Female (n = 40; 54.8%) was more frequently reported than male (n = 33; 45.2%). In terms of vaccine types, Pfizer is the most commonly reported (49 cases; 67.1%), followed by Astra-Zeneca (13 cases; 17.8%), Moderna (10 cases; 13.7%), and CoronaVac (1 case; 1.4%). The onset of psoriasis after receiving the COVID-19 Vaccines was varied, ranging from 1 to 90 days (Mean ± SD: 15.75 ± 18.22). Plaque psoriasis was most commonly reported, followed by pustular, guttate and other types of psoriasis. Reported cases were frequently treated with topical steroids and monoclonal antibodies monotherapy.Conclusions:Although the cases of new-onset and exacerbation of psoriasis after COVID-19 vaccination is quite scarce, the possibility of occurrence should be acknowledged and managed properly; and patients should be informed, particularly ones with history of psoriasis without discouraging patients to take vaccine.

Introduction

Coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) has been a global pandemic since the start of 2020. The confirmed cases of COVID-19, as of June 2022, there have been 539.893.858 cases worldwide, including 6.324.112 deaths, reported to WHO. A significant part of the strategy to terminate the pandemic is to use immunisation.[1] According to WHO, up to January 2022, COVID-19 has a wide variety of vaccine types, differentiated by vectors and production methods. The main types of COVID-19 vaccines are messenger RNA (mRNA) vaccine, vector vaccine, and protein subunit vaccine.[2] Vaccines are regulated to ensure the quality, safety and efficacy using clinical trial data, manufacturing and quality control processes. Although studies showed the overall efficacy of all the available vaccines, with the hastened pace of vaccine production, the probability of undesired adverse effects was not observed thoroughly.[3]

Cutaneous reactions to COVID-19 vaccines were also reported. Delayed major local reactions were the most common, followed by local injection site reactions, urticarial eruption and morbilliform eruption.[4] Another adverse skin reaction reported was psoriasis. Psoriasis has been described as uncommon adverse events after many types of vaccination before, including tetanus-diphtheria, pneumococcal, Bacillus Camette-Guerin (BCG), smallpox and influenza. Although uncommon, several cases of new-onset and exacerbation of psoriasis have been reported after COVID-19 vaccination.[5]

Given the importance of the COVID-19 vaccines in this pandemic era and its adverse effects that can cause psoriasis, we aim to systematically review reports regarding new-onset and exacerbation of psoriasis to identify features related to that condition following COVID-19 vaccination.

Materials and Methods

This systematic review is registered on PROSPERO with registration number CRD42022357440.

In this systematic review, we identified studies that investigate or report psoriasis exacerbation or induced psoriasis in individuals receiving COVID-19 vaccination.

Eligibility criteria

We selected research articles that investigate or report the case of psoriasis exacerbation in previously-in-remission or induced psoriasis in individuals receiving any dose of COVID-19 vaccines regardless of the type. We excluded studies that investigate or report cases involving patients with active psoriasis. The literature should be a case report or case series in design. Letters or correspondence that contains report regarding psoriasis exacerbation or induced psoriasis after COVID-19 vaccination with sufficient information will also be included in order to avoid missing important information regarding this topic since the case is statistically rare. Reports with the same health centre and demographic will be excluded to avoid including the same patients. We considered papers available in English and Indonesian.

Literature search

We utilise three databases, namely PubMed, Google Scholar and EBSCOHost to search for literature published until June 2022. We used the following terms: “psoriasis,” “exacerbation,” “induced psoriasis,” and “COVID-19 vaccination.” Research titles were screened by two authors to eliminate studies that did not match the eligibility criteria, followed by abstract and full-text review of selected studies. Disagreements between the two authors were resolved through discussion with the third author.

Data extraction

Among selected studies, we extracted author, year of publication, number of participants, age, sex, type of psoriasis exacerbation or induced psoriasis, type of COVID-19 vaccine, dose of the vaccine, onset, history of psoriasis, history of psoriasis treatment, other pre-existing conditions, treatment for exacerbation and clinical outcome. The data were extracted by two independent co-authors, double-checked to ensure matching and organised by the third author.

Risk of bias

For quality assessment, we utilised The Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal checklist for case series and case reports.

Until June 2022, a total of 85 articles were identified, 37 from PubMed, 43 from Google Scholar, and 5 from EBSCOHost [Figure 1]. Characteristics of individuals experiencing induced psoriasis or exacerbation

Sex

Female (n = 40; 54.8%) was more frequently reported than male (n = 33; 45.2%).

Vaccine type

Both the exacerbation of psoriasis and new-onset psoriasis were found after vaccination using Pfizer, Moderna, Astra-Zeneca and CoronaVac vaccinations, with Pfizer being the most commonly reported (49 cases; 67.1%), followed by Astra-Zeneca (13 cases; 17.8%), Moderna (10 cases; 13.7%) and CoronaVac (1 case; 1.4%).

Vaccine dose

Psoriasis was reported to appear after the first dose, second dose, third dose, both the first and second dose, and after all three doses of COVID-19 Vaccines. Reports stated that there were 20 patients who got it after the first dose (27.4%), 44 patients got it after the second dose (60.3%), 3 patients got it after the third dose (4.1%), 5 patients got it after both the first and second doses (6.8%) and only one case reported getting it after all three doses, repeatedly (1%).

History of psoriasis

History of psoriasis was more frequently reported among the participants than the absence of it. Exacerbation of psoriasis in patients with a history of psoriasis was found in 62 patients (84.9%) with 27 of them (81.8%) being male, and 35 of them (87.5%) being female. New onsets of psoriasis were found in 11 patients (15.1%); with 6 of them (18.2%) being male and 5 (12.5%) being female.

History of COVID-19

Among the selected case reports included in this systematic review, only 15 out of 33 studies reported a history of COVID-19. A total of 5 cases from 2 studies have a history of COVID-19, while 14 cases from 13 studies reported no history of COVID-19.

Type of psoriasis

Of the 73 cases in 33 studies, 36 patients (49.3%) were reported to have plaque psoriasis. Thirteen patients (17.8%) reported pustular psoriasis, 7 (9.6%) reported guttate psoriasis. Meanwhile, 10 patients (13.7%) were reported as having other types of psoriasis, including nail psoriasis and erythrodermic psoriasis. Lastly, 7 patients (9.6%) reported unspecified psoriasis types.

Onset after vaccine

It was found that the onset of psoriasis after receiving the COVID-19 vaccines was varied, ranging from 1 to 90 days. From all cases, an average of 15.75 ± 18.22 days was obtained. In male participants, the average onset was 19.91 ± 21.01 days, which was longer than the female participants with the average onset of 12.28 ± 14.99 days.

Comorbidity

Twenty-six studies reported comorbidity status, which totaled 45 cases [Table 3]. The most common comorbidity is hypertension, at 13 cases (28.89%). Followed by 5 cases of type 2 diabetes mellitus (11.11%), 3 cases of COPD (6.67%), and 2 cases of obesity (4.44%). Sixteen cases did not have comorbidities (35.56%), and 28 cases (38.36%) did not provide comorbidity status.

Treatment and clinical outcomes

The most common treatment option utilised for new onset and exacerbation psoriasis after COVID-19 vaccination were topical steroids and monoclonal antibodies monotherapy [Table 4]. There were 16 cases treated using topical steroids, with a clinical outcome of improvement after 1–4 months, and there were 9 cases using monoclonal antibodies, resulting to improvement after 10 days to 4 months.

Discussions

We found that there were 62 cases of exacerbated psoriasis and 11 cases of new-onset psoriasis after COVID-19 vaccination. The mean age of individuals experiencing exacerbation or new-onset psoriasis after COVID-19 vaccination was 57.52 ± 15.92 years, with males having mean age slightly older than females (60.18 ± 14.68 years vs 55.33 ± 16.74 years). These findings are similar to psoriasis in the United States population, which was most frequently diagnosed at 50–59 and 60–69 years.[38] Among the reported cases, females had shorter onset than males (12.28 ± 14.99 vs 19.91 ± 21.01).

Although uncommon, psoriasis had previously been reported as an adverse effect of vaccination. The provocative effects of vaccination on psoriasis continues to be described in today’s COVID-19 era.[39] Individuals suffering from exacerbated psoriasis who were vaccinated with H1N1 influenza vaccines, showed predominant T-helper 1 response. According to previous studies, there were an increase in IL-6 and T-helper 17 cell development after BCG vaccines and tetanus-diphtheria.[40] Aside from the immune system-provoking effects of BCG and tetanus-diphtheria vaccines, it is also known that BCG and pneumonia vaccination increases levels of IL-2, IL-12, Tumour Necrosis Factor (TNF)-α, and Interferon (IFN)-γ, which are major causes of the immunopathogenesis of psoriasis by promoting epidermal proliferation and IL-8 to stimulate neutrophil ingression.[4142] Another postulated theory for psoriasis following COVID-19 vaccination was considered due to the changes in eosinophil counts and T-helper cell dysregulation that trigger the release of downstream cytokines that play a key role in the development of epidermal changes in psoriasis.[4344] All of these pathological responses contribute to the pathogenesis of psoriasis lesion, which is considered to be primarily Th-1 disease.[45] However, the pathological pathways through which COVID-19 vaccination induces psoriasis lesion still remains undefined.

The association between psoriasis and COVID-19 vaccine type or dose is still unknown and no study has really addressed their association. Further investigations and large controlled studies are still needed to explain the relationship between psoriasis and the COVID-19 vaccines. However, some studies have reported that psoriasis can be triggered by COVID-19 vaccines.[39] COVID-19 vaccines that are currently in use are mostly mRNA-based or use adenoviruses as vectors. To date, McMahon DE et al. documented 2 cases of exacerbated psoriasis (unknown type) in 414 patients with cutaneous reactions after the Moderna and Pfizer vaccination.[4] Several studies reported that, after receiving the AstraZeneca-Oxford COVID-19 vaccines, there was increased production of TNF-α and IFN-γ by CD4+ T cells,[46] which are cytokines that can trigger inflammatory cascades of psoriasis.[47] Vaccines are also known to provoke the immune system to form an immunological pathway that may worsen the state of psoriasis.[41]

Among reported cases, the most common comorbidities found were hypertension and diabetes mellitus. A study from a German statutory health insurance found that the most common metabolic syndrome comorbidity for psoriasis are arterial hypertension, hyperlipidemia, obesity, and diabetes mellitus.[48] The more severe the psoriasis, the stronger this association is shown. Psoriasis may stimulate the hypothalamic-pituitary axis, which is associated with central obesity, hypertension, and insulin resistance. The state of chronic inflammation is commonly found in these pathologies.[49] Vaccines may trigger immunological disorders and it should be given carefully in psoriatic patients with comorbidity associated with chronic inflammation.[49]

There are multiple treatment modalities for psoriasis according to the AAD (American Academy of Dermatology), and they can be adjusted depending on each patient’s severity and preferences. AAD recommended potent, class II and class III topical corticosteroids, long-term use of topical vitamin D analogues (including calcipotriene/calcipotriol, calcitriol, tacalcitol and maxacalcitol), tazarotene, methotrexate, acitretin, and more.[5051] The medication given to patients with exacerbated psoriasis and new-onset psoriasis post-COVID-19 vaccination is the same as with general psoriasis treatments. Wei N et al. used the combination between monoclonal antibody (ixekizumab, risankizumab and tildrakizumab), topical steroids (clobetasol, triamcinolone) and phototherapy, or the combination of monoclonal antibody and topical steroid, or even just topical steroid alone for some cases of patients with new or exacerbated psoriasis after receiving COVID-19 vaccination, and most patients showed improvement.[29]

Due to the scarcity of the reported case occurrence, we could not include trials or longitudinal studies because there is no publication on this topic. Therefore, we were unable to determine causality or even association of new-onset and exacerbation of psoriasis, but with this systematic review, we managed to compile some characteristics that are more commonly reported, which may or may not be associated. This is the first systematic review investigating this topic, which included 33 selected published studies we found in the databases.

Conclusions

Although the incidence of new-onset and exacerbation of psoriasis after COVID-19 vaccination is quite rare, the possibility of occurrence should be acknowledged and managed properly; and patients should be informed, particularly ones with the history of psoriasis, while endeavouring to not discourage the patients from taking the vaccine. The causality of psoriasis following COVID-19 vaccination is still yet to be determined due to the scarcity of data, analysis using a larger sample would be needed to explore this topic further.
