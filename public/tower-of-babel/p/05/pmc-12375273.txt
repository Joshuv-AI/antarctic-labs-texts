Reverse vaccinology-based identification and in silico characterization of immunogenic membrane proteins of Salmonella Typhimurium as novel vaccine targets against multidrug-resistant infections
Authors: Tooba Ume Habiba, Zahid Hussain, Fatima Asghar, Wajeeha Nawaz
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

The disease disproportionately affects developing nations, where young children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals face high risks of severe morbidity and mortality. Its ability to evade host immune defenses and acquire multidrug resistance (MDR) exacerbates global public health challenges. Background

Salmonella is a life-threatening enteric pathogen with a wide range of hosts, including humans. It is a Gram-negative, rod-shaped, facultative anaerobe that possesses flagella aiding in motility [1]. The Salmonella genus comprises only two species, S. enterica and S. bongori but includes nearly 2,700 serovars [2]. Among these, S. Typhimurium is of particular clinical importance, responsible for a variety of human diseases worldwide [3]. In addition to transmission via contaminated food and water, S. Typhimurium can also spread via contact with infected animals and poor hygiene practices [4]. As a major cause of invasive non-typhoidal salmonellosis (iNTS), it induces acute gastro-intestinal illness characterized by mucosal invasion and symptoms ranging from mild diarrhea to severe febrile illness. In vulnerable populations, including young children, the elderly, and immunocompromised individuals, these infections frequently progress to bacteremia, elevating risks of systemic complications [5]. Once the bacteria enter the bloodstream, they can disseminate to other organs, causing focal infections in the bones (osteomyelitis), joints (acute arthritis), the endocardium (endocarditis), and lungs (pneumonia) [6]. Increasing reports of multidrug-resistance (MDR) further complicate treatment by reducing the efficacy of commonly used antibiotics, resulting in prolonged infection duration, increasing complication rates, and raising healthcare costs. According to the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, an estimated 509,976 incident cases of invasive non-typhoidal salmonella (iNTS) occurred worldwide in 2021 [7].

The immune response to S. Typhimurium includes both innate (non-specific) and adaptive (specific) defense mechanisms. The gut epithelium serves as the first line of defense by trapping the bacteria [8]. Additionally, mucus and secretory immunoglobulins (Ig) in the intestinal lumen neutralize the pathogen. Phagocytic cells internalize bacteria and initiate intracellular degradation via phagolysosome formation. Bacterial internalization triggers a cascade of immune responses including cytokine and chemokine release, which recruit immune cells to infection site [9]. To initiate cellular immunity, dendritic cells (DCs) present processed antigens to naïve T cells. They activate CD4 + T helper cells and CD8 + cytotoxic T cells which recognize and kill infected cells directly [10]. B cells produce antibodies (e.g. IgG and IgM) that promote bacterial clearance by phagocytes. Following infection, some T and B cells differentiate into memory cells, enabling a more rapid and effective immune response in the future [11].

S. Typhimurium has evolved multiple strategies to evade host immune defenses. Both invasive and non-invasive strains can cross the intestinal barrier [12, 13]. Once internalized by phagocytes, Salmonella forms a membrane-bound compartment called the Salmonella- containing vacuole (SCV) that prevents lysosomal degradation. The SCV provides a safe environment for bacterial replication, which contributes to severe intestinal inflammation. S. Typhimurium can remain in the mucosa owing to its tolerance to inflammation [14]. It resists host-derived antimicrobial peptides by upregulating specific virulence mechanisms. For instance, upon sensing antimicrobial peptides it activates the PhoP-PhoQ system, promoting survival under stressed environments [15, 16]. Due to these strategies, S. Typhimurium has developed resistance to multiple antibiotics such as ampicillin, chloramphenicol, streptomycin, sulfonamide, and tetracycline, classifying it as MDR. Regulatory systems such as PhoP-PhoQ mediated antigen shielding complicates vaccine development, highlighting the need to identify accessible targets like membrane proteins [17].

Traditional vaccines targeting flagella and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) lack cross-strain efficacy and durable mucosal immunity [18]. Membrane proteins, however, are ideal targets due to surface accessibility, enabling B- and T-cell recognition, robust humoral and cellular responses against intracellular pathogens like S. Typhimurium. Despite their critical role in host-pathogen interactions, membrane proteins have been largely overlooked because of structural complexity and technical challenges. To overcome these limitations, reverse vaccinology, a genome-based computational approach, combined with advanced in silico techniques offers an effective means to identify novel vaccine targets [19]. The present research applies these computational tools to find potential vaccine candidates against MDR strains of S. Typhimurium. It specifically seeks to identify epitopes within highly immunogenic membrane proteins for subunit or multi-epitope vaccine development. These findings advance computational vaccinology and may facilitate the development of safer and more effective vaccines against S. Typhimurium.

Methods

Sequence retrieval

The complete proteome of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium strain LT2 was retrieved from the NCBI Reference Sequence database https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/datasets/genome/GCF_000006945.2/ on March 8, 2021 [20]. At the time of retrieval, the dataset included 4,446 annotated proteins. The strain LT2 was chosen for this study due to its well-characterized status as a model of foodborne pathogens, with a fully sequenced and annotated genome.

Prediction of sub-cellular localization of proteins

Membrane protein prediction was conducted using the SOSUI platform https://harrier.nagahama-i-bio.ac.jp/sosui/mobile/ with default parameters to identify and isolate membrane-associated proteins. The tool analyzes structural motifs to differentiate extracellular proteins from intracellular and secreted counterparts. Subcellular localization and subsequent prioritization steps were performed on the complete dataset of 4,446 proteins [21].

Antigenicity assessment

The antigenic potential of the proteins was evaluated using the VaxiJen v2.0 server http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html. It is a widely used alignment-independent, machine learning-based tool that predicts antigenicity by analyzing the physicochemical properties of proteins [22]. To ensure stringent selection, a conservative threshold of ≥ 0.9 was applied. Based on the scoring system of VaxiJen, where values closer to 1.0 indicate higher confidence in the antigenic nature of the protein, a threshold of ≥ 0.9 represents a conservative and high-specificity filter, ensuring the selection of only strongly antigenic candidates for further analysis. Proteins meeting or exceeding this threshold were prioritized for downstream assessments.

Allergenicity assessment

Allergenicity was evaluated using AllerTOP v1.1 https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/allertop/ with default settings [23]. AllerTOP is a bioinformatics platform designed to predict potential allergic responses of proteins in humans. It applies smart algorithms and a database of known allergens to make quick and reliable assessments. Excluding allergenic proteins at this stage prioritizes vaccine safety, reduces risks of hypersensitivity, and focuses immune responses on protective epitopes.

Homology assessment of selected proteins with the human proteome

To eliminate potential autoimmunity risks, homology between selected proteins and the human proteome was assessed using PSI-BLAST (Position-Specific Iterated BLAST) on the NCBI BLASTp platform against Homo sapiens (TaxID: 9606) https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi?PAGE=Proteins [24]. Proteins having similarity with human sequences were excluded from further consideration.

Prediction of toxicity

Toxicity of selected candidates was assessed using ToxinPred https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/toxinpred/ via the SVM-based prediction method with default settings [25]. The analysis utilized a binary classification model (toxic versus non-toxic) trained on the Swiss-Prot dataset. To ensure vaccine safety, any predicted B-cell or T-cell epitopes overlapping with toxic residues will be excluded. Checking toxicity of vaccine candidates is immensely important for designing safer vaccines.

Immune simulation analysis

The immunogenic potential of proteins was assessed using C-immsim https://kraken.iac.rm.cnr.it/C-IMMSIM/index.php. It uses Celada-Seiden model which is agent-based approach to predict innate, humoral and cell mediated immune response Simulations were conducted with default immunological parameters, except for the antigen input, where a vaccine with no lipopolysaccharide (LPS) was selected [26]. Immunogenicity was determined based on multiple parameters: high primary and secondary antibody titers (especially IgG and IgM) and elevated levels of key cytokines such as interferon gamma (IFN-γ) and interleukins (IL-2, IL-10 and IL-12) indicative of a Th1-biased response. This step allowed differentiation of immunogenic versus non-immunogenic candidates, ensuring that only candidates capable of eliciting robust immune activation were prioritized for downstream vaccine development.

B-cell epitope prediction

Linear B-cell epitopes were predicted using the ABCpred server https://webs.iiitd.edu.in/raghava/abcpred/ with threshold score of 0.51 [27], followed by evaluation of the predicted epitopes for antigenicity using VaxiJen v2.0 http://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/vaxijen/VaxiJen/VaxiJen.html and allergenicity using AllerTOP v1.1 https://www.ddg-pharmfac.net/allertop/. Additionally, B-cell epitope predictions were refined using the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) B-cell epitope prediction tool http://tools.iedb.org/bcell/ using the Bepipred Linear Epitope Prediction 2.0 method [28]. This multi-tool approach helped identifying antigenic, non-allergenic B-cell epitopes.

T-cell epitope prediction

T-cell epitope prediction was performed using the Immune Epitope Database (IEDB) https://www.iedb.org/. For prediction of Major Histocompatibility Complex class I (MHC-I) epitopes, which present intracellular antigens to cytotoxic T-cells, the MHC-I Binding tool http://tools.iedb.org/mhci/ was employed with the Artificial Neural Network (ANN 4.0) algorithm [29]. The human species was selected as the host, with the complete HLA allele reference set included. For Major Histocompatibility Complex class II (MHC-II) epitopes, which present extracellular antigens to helper T-cells, the MHC-II Binding tool http://tools.iedb.org/mhcii/ was employed using the NN-align 2.3 (NetMHC II 2.3), specifically targeting the human HLA-DR locus. These analyses aimed to identify highly immunogenic peptides capable of eliciting robust cellular immune responses, thus helping prioritize epitope candidates with strong T-cell activation potential [30].

Prediction of physiochemical properties of proteins

The physicochemical properties of the selected proteins were analyzed using Expasy ProtParam https://web.expasy.org/protparam/ [31, 32]. This tool calculates key parameters such as molecular weight and stability providing insights into protein behavior under varying conditions. To detect transmembrane helices, the TMHMM 2.0 tool https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/TMHMM-2.0/ was employed using default settings [33]. It identifies membrane-spanning regions through hidden Markov models. Functional annotation of the proteins was performed using UniProtKB https://www.uniprot.org/uniprotkb [34]. Finally, tertiary structures were modeled using the SWISS-MODEL server https://swissmodel.expasy.org/interactive enabling homology-based prediction of 3D conformations for downstream analyses [35].

Conservation analysis of vaccine candidates in MDR S. Typhimurium strains

To confirm conservation of selected vaccine candidates in MDR S. Typhimurium, BLASTp https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi?PAGE=Proteins analysis was performed against the genome of the MDR strain S. Typhimurium DT104 (TaxID: 85569) [36]. Protein sequences retrieved from UniProt were aligned using NCBI BLASTp with default parameters. Proteins exhibiting 100% sequence identity and full query coverage were considered conserved. This step ensured the relevance of the selected proteins in clinically important MDR isolates. A complete overview of the computational pipeline is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Results

Sequence retrieval

The complete proteome of Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium strain LT2, comprising 4,446 annotated proteins, was retrieved from the NCBI Reference Sequence database. This full dataset served as the starting point for downstream computational analyses aimed at identifying potential vaccine candidates.

Prediction of subcellular localization of proteins

All 4,446 proteins were analyzed using the SOSUI server to predict subcellular localization. Based on SOSUI’s classification, 1,228 proteins were identified as membrane-associated. These membrane proteins were selected for further analysis as they are more likely to be accessible to host immune surveillance and thus relevant for vaccine targeting.

Antigenicity assessment

To identify membrane proteins with strong antigenic potential, all 1,228 candidates predicted by the SOSUI server were evaluated using VaxiJen v2.0, with a stringent threshold score of ≥ 0.9. This high-specificity cutoff was selected to ensure robust antigen prediction. As a result, 15 proteins were predicted to possess high antigenic potential and were shortlisted for subsequent allergenicity assessment. The antigenicity profiles of these proteins are illustrated in Fig. 2.

Antigenicity scores and allergenicity profiles of 15 selected S. Typhimurium proteins. Each bar represents the antigenicity score of an individual protein (identified by UniProt ID). Proteins classified as allergens are marked in red, while non-allergens are shown in green. This visualization highlights potential vaccine candidates based on high antigenicity and non-allergenic properties

Allergenicity assessment

The 15 antigenic membrane proteins were further analyzed using AllerTOP to evaluate their allergenic potential. Two proteins (Q9SQH7 and Q7CPB5) were predicted to be allergens and were therefore excluded from further analysis. The combined antigenicity and allergenicity profiles of shortlisted proteins are summarized in Fig. 2.

Homology assessment of proteins with the human proteome

All 13 proteins with ≥ 0.9 antigenicity and non-allergenic profiles were analyzed for sequence similarity to the human proteome Homo sapiens (taxid: 9606). None of the selected proteins exhibited significant homology with human proteins, suggesting a low risk of cross-reactivity and autoimmune responses.

Prediction of toxicity

Toxicity analysis using ToxinPred revealed that one protein, Q7CQW9, contained two potentially toxic residues at positions 19 and 20, with toxicity scores of 0.09 and 0.13, respectively. To mitigate potential safety concerns, only epitopes located outside these toxic regions will be retained for subsequent immunogenicity and allergenicity assessments. All other candidate proteins were predicted to be non-toxic, indicating that the majority of the shortlisted proteins are suitable for further vaccine development.

Immune simulation analysis

The 13 shortlisted proteins were subjected to immune simulation analysis using the C-ImmSim tool to evaluate their potential to elicit strong adaptive immune responses. This step was essential for identifying candidates capable of inducing effective humoral and cellular immunity, including antibody production and T-cell-mediated cytokine release. Representative simulation results for one promising candidate Q7CQG8 are shown in Fig. 3 highlighting immunoglobulin dynamics and cytokine release profiles.

T-cell epitope prediction

T-cell epitopes for both MHC class I and MHC class II molecules were predicted using the IEDB tool, with the results presented in Tables 2 and 3 respectively. Each predicted epitope was further evaluated for antigenicity using VaxiJen and for allergenicity using AllerTOP. The most promising epitope per protein was selected based on the lowest predicted IC50 value, with values below 50 nM considered indicative of strong MHC binding and high immunogenic potential. After this step, based on the integrated analysis of B-cell and T-cell epitopes, candidate Q7CQ85 was excluded for further analysis due to (i) the absence of B-cell epitopes (Table 1), (ii) poor MHC II compatibility indicated by high IC50 value 143 nM (Table 3).

Conservation analysis of vaccine candidates in MDR S. Typhimurium strains

All selected proteins showed 100% sequence identity with homologs in S. Typhimurium DT104, confirming their conservation in a well-known MDR clinical isolate. This supports the potential applicability of the identified proteins as vaccine targets against both drug-sensitive and resistant strains.

Discussion

S. Typhimurium is an important zoonotic pathogen that leads to gastroenteritis, sepsis, and focal infections in humans. It is MDR and several vaccines have been developed for animals such as Live Attenuated Vaccines (Poulvac ST and Salenvac-T) [37]. However, no licensed vaccine currently exists for human use. In this study, a comprehensive in silico approach was employed to identify highly immunogenic membrane proteins as potential vaccine candidates against S. Typhimurium. A workflow of whole research strategy is shown in Fig. 1. Starting with 4,446 proteins of S. Typhimurium, membrane proteins were prioritized because of their relevance as vaccine targets due to surface exposure and immune accessibility [38]. Antigenicity profiling of 1,228 membrane proteins using VaxiJen (threshold ≥ 0.9) identified 15 proteins (Fig. 2) with scores surpassing known antigens such as OmpA (0.7–0.8) and flagellin (0.6–0.7) [39]. These 15 highly antigenic proteins were then tested for allergenicity which resulted in exclusion of two allergens i.e. Q9SQH7 and Q7CPB5 (Fig. 2).

Immune simulation of the remaining 13 proteins using C-Immsim revealed interactions with immune components. As shown in Fig. 3, immune simulation of the candidate Q7CQG8 revealed a strong initial response with sustained immunoglobulin levels and cytokine activities. The antibody titer analysis, summarized in Fig. 4, revealed that P0A1E5, Q7CQW9, and Q7CQG8 elicited the strongest immune responses, with high IgG and IgM titers, indicating their potential as vaccine candidates. Q8ZPK9, Q05595, Q8ZMZ1, and Q7CPR0 showed moderate responses, suggesting partial immunogenicity. In contrast, Q7CPZ9, Q8ZN08, and Q7CPM3 exhibited minimal or absent antibody titers, confirming their poor immunogenicity. Cytokine analysis shown in Fig. 5 revealed that Q7CQW9, P0A1E5, Q7CQG8, and Q05595 induced high IFN-γ and IL-12 levels, indicating a strong Th1 immune response essential for combating intracellular pathogens like S. Typhimurium. Q8ZPK9, Q7CPM3, and Q7CPR0 exhibited moderate cytokine levels, suggesting a balanced but weaker response. Of the 13 proteins, Q7CPZ9, Q8ZN08, and Q7CPM3 were excluded due to poor immunogenicity, evidenced by low antibody titer and reduced cytokine levels.

The 10 proteins were then analyzed for B and T cell epitopes. ABCpred server predicted linear B cell epitopes regions in antigen sequences. Except for Q7CQ85, all proteins showed B cell epitopes and their scores ranged from 0.57 to 0.90 (Table 1). Scores above 0.80 suggest strong confidence in epitope potential. IEDB predictions further validated these findings and confirmed the absence of B-cell epitopes in Q7CQ85 (Fig. 6) [40]. For Q7CQW9, that had toxicity at 2 positions, it was made sure that all selected epitopes were located outside these toxic regions, confirming their safety.

Physiochemical properties were assessed using ProtParam, TMHMM, and UniProt (Table 4). UniProt analysis revealed that the protein candidates Q7CQX2, Q7CQW9, P0A1E5, and Q8ZLN8 were surface-exposed and accessible to the immune system while the presence of lipoproteins in Q7CQX2 and Q7CQW9, supports their role in immune recognition, which is a key factor in designing an effective vaccine. These findings suggest that these proteins can play a crucial role in developing a targeted immune response. The molecular weight of proteins ranges from 4.0 to 18.8 kD (Table 4), which is advantageous for vaccine design, as smaller proteins are generally easier to express, purify, and formulate. Their low molecular weight enhances solubility and stability, making them well-suited for subunit vaccine platforms [41]. The isoelectric point (pI) values ranged from 4.48 to 10.38 (Table 4), however, proteins with a pI closer to neutral (6–7) such as Q7CQW9 might be better tolerated and soluble in logical conditions [42]. Q7CQX2, Q7CQG8, Q7CPR0, and Q8ZLN8 exhibited aliphatic index values above 100 (Table 4), indicating high thermostability which is an advantageous feature for vaccine design, as such proteins are typically more stable during production, storage, and delivery [43]. The negative GRAVY scores observed for several proteins (Q7CQW9, Q8ZPK9, P0A1E5, Q8ZMZ1) suggest they are hydrophilic, which may enhance solubility during recombinant expression and promote immune recognition due to increased surface accessibility [44]. Proteins with 1 or more TMHs (Q7CQ85, Q7CQX2, Q8ZPK9, Q7CQG8, Q05595, Q7CPR0, Q8ZLN8) might be more suitable as subunit vaccines, especially those with a single TMH that remains exposed to the immune system [45]. Fig. 7 illustrates the 3D structures of the selected proteins, highlighting their structural diversity and spatial organization, which may play a key role in their antigenic properties and interaction with immune receptors. Finally, conservation analysis revealed that all selected proteins were 100% identical in the MDR strain Salmonella Typhimurium DT104, highlighting their potential as broad-spectrum vaccine candidates across both susceptible and drug-resistant isolates.

Among the nine shortlisted candidates, P0A1E5 has been previously investigated as a vaccine target due to its role in Salmonella biofilm formation and immune evasion. Disrupting curli-mediated pathogenesis could reduce S. Typhimurium colonization, transmission, and antibiotic-resistant infections, particularly in high-risk populations [46]. Notably, Q7CQW9, a peptidoglycan-associated lipoprotein, emerges as a compelling vaccine candidate due to its dual role as a structural anchor and TLR2 agonist, which synergistically enhances immune activation through adjuvant-like effects. Its high conservation across MDR Salmonella strains (e.g., DT104) underscores its potential as a broad-spectrum target. In contrast, the remaining candidates represent novel targets with no prior reports of vaccine applications against Salmonella or related pathogens. These proteins are amenable to multiple platforms such as for epitope specificity and DNA vaccines for broad cellular immunity. Alternatively, nanoparticle-based formulations (e.g., lipid or polymer nanoparticles) could improve antigen stability, dendritic cell targeting, and adjuvant co-delivery. Therefore, based on in silico analysis, the present study has screened novel and potential targets as vaccine candidates which can induce humoral and cellular response. Building on the prioritized candidates identified here, subsequent studies should focus on expression of these proteins and validation of immunogenicity through murine challenge models. Adjuvant-formulated candidates could then advance to preclinical efficacy trials, aligning computational predictions with functional immune outcomes. However, several key challenges remain such as optimal epitope linkage strategies, appropriate adjuvant selection to enhance immunogenicity, and the development of efficient delivery systems to ensure stability and targeted immune activation. This research can be extremely beneficial to design more effective and broad spectrum vaccine against S. Typhimurium and to save millions of lives across the globe.

Conclusion

S. Typhimurium continues to pose a significant global health threat, especially in developing regions, with no approved human vaccine despite prolonged research efforts. Existing strategies, including live attenuated and subunit vaccines, face notable limitations. Live vaccines pose a risk of reversion to virulence in immunocompromised individuals, while subunit vaccines often exhibit limited serovar coverage and are less effective against MDR strains. In contrast, this study leverages a robust in silico pipeline to prioritize nine conserved, membrane-associated antigens which are Q7CQX2 (Putative outer membrane lipoprotein), Q7CQW9 (Peptidoglycan-associated lipoprotein), P0A1E5 (Curli major subunit), Q8ZPK9 (Acid shock protein), Q7CQG8 (Phage shock protein B), Q05595 (Cobalt ABC transporter substrate-binding protein CbiN), Q8ZMZ1 (Gifsy-1 prophage protein [Phage Gifsy-1]), Q7CPR0 (Inner membrane protein) and Q8ZLN8 (Periplasmic protein). These candidates were selected based on their strong antigenicity, non-allergenicity, non-toxicity and ability to stimulate strong humoral and cellular immune responses. The findings support the development of a multi-epitope vaccine capable of addressing current shortcomings and enhancing protection against MDR S. Typhimurium. This approach holds promise for protecting vulnerable populations, such as children and immunocompromised individuals, thus advancing vaccine design efforts to address this critical public health challenge.
