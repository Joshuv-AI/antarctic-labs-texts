Salmonella vertebral osteomyelitis with bilateral psoas myositis in a patient with newly diagnosed diabetes: a case report and literature review
Authors: Le Viet Nghia, Do Xuan Tien, Cam Ba Thuc, Doi Ngoc Anh, Ngo Manh Linh, Nguyen Quoc Phuong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Highlights•First reported case of Salmonella vertebral osteomyelitis with bilateral psoas myositis.•Occurred in a newly diagnosed diabetic patient without gastrointestinal symptoms.•Initial treatment with ceftriaxone-ciprofloxacin failed; however, meropenem-ciprofloxacin achieved success.•Early magnetic resonance imaging and blood culture were crucial for accurate diagnosis and treatment.•Emphasizes suspicion of Salmonella vertebral osteomyelitis in diabetic patients with fever and back pain.

Introduction

Non-typhoid Salmonella (NTS) infections typically manifest as self-limiting gastroenteritis in immunocompetent individuals. However, invasive Salmonella infections with extraintestinal manifestations can occur, particularly in patients who are immunocompromised [1]. An estimated 535,000 cases of invasive NTS infections occurred in 2017, with a mortality rate of 11.5% [2].

Salmonella vertebral osteomyelitis (SVO) is an extremely rare complication, occurring in 0.8% of all Salmonella infections and accounting for only 0.45% of osteomyelitis cases [3,4]. Notably, a review by Huang et al. [4], who analyzed 67 cases of SVO, reported a gradual increase in incidence in recent decades, possibly related to the growing number of immunocompromised patients and improved diagnostic methods.

Salmonella can spread hematogenously and preferentially adheres to the vertebral bodies owing to the dense capillary network and slow circulation. The specialized vascular system of the vertebrae, with terminal arteries lacking collateral circulation, increases the risk of local ischemia and necrosis when infection occurs [5].

The symptoms in patients with SVO are often nonspecific, leading to diagnostic challenges and treatment delays. Unlike typical Salmonella gastroenteritis, patients with SVO often lack gastrointestinal symptoms, with diarrhoea reported in only 17.91% of cases [4]. The absence of gastrointestinal symptoms, combined with the rarity of the disease, often results in misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis.

Diabetes mellitus is an important risk factor for invasive Salmonella infection, observed in 20.9% of SVO cases [4]. The mechanisms leading to increased susceptibility include decreased gastric acid concentration, autonomic neuropathy affecting intestinal motility, impaired neutrophil function, and cell-mediated immunity impairment related to hyperglycemia.

Herein, we present a case of SVO with bilateral psoas myositis in a patient with newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus and discuss the clinical challenges, diagnostic methods, and treatment considerations.

Case presentation

A 56-year-old Vietnamese woman presented to our emergency department with high fever (39 °C), chills, and severe back pain for 2 days. The patient reported a sudden onset of fever accompanied by chills, severe back pain radiating to both lower extremities with movement difficulties, and pain exacerbated by changes in position. No gastrointestinal symptoms such as nausea, vomiting, diarrhoea, or abdominal pain were noted. After 2 days without improvement, the patient continued to experience high fever and back pain and presented to our hospital for examination and admission. The patient’s medical history included vestibular disorders. She had not traveled, had no known exposure to animals, and had not recently consumed any unusual food.

At admission (day 1), the vital signs showed fever (38.5 °C), tachycardia (92 bpm), normal blood pressure (120/80 mmHg), and a respiratory rate of 18/min. Physical examination revealed severe back pain (visual analogue [VAS] score 8), movement difficulties with hip flexion and spinal rotation, a positive Lasègue sign at approximately 60° bilaterally, muscle spasm of the paraspinal muscles at L3-L5, and diffuse pain at L3-L4. No abnormalities were observed in cardiovascular, respiratory, gastrointestinal, genitourinary, and neurological examinations.

Based on the clinical presentation, laboratory results, MRI findings, and positive blood culture, the patient was diagnosed with Salmonella bacteremia complicated by L3 vertebral osteomyelitis and bilateral psoas myositis.

The patient received treatment with the following course:Days 1-2: Symptomatic treatment with meloxicam 15 mg intramuscularly and gabapentin 600 mg, and diabetes management with gliclazide 60 mg daily. Ceftazidime was initially administered as empirical antibiotic therapy.Days 3-7: Antibiotic therapy was initiated with ceftriaxone 2 g intravenously combined with ciprofloxacin 800 mg intravenously daily for 5 days.Days 8-21: Owing to persistent fever (38 °C) and severe back pain (VAS score 8/10) after 5 days, the antibiotic regimen was changed to meropenem 3 g intravenously combined with ciprofloxacin 800 mg daily. After 4 days of the modified regimen, the fever resolved, back pain decreased (VAS reduced from 8 to 4), and laboratory results improved (white blood cell count decreased to 16 × 10⁹/L; CRP decreased to 58 mg/L). After 3 weeks of hospitalization, the patient was discharged with complete fever resolution, back pain reduced to VAS 2, normal ambulation, and improved laboratory results (white blood cell count 7.7 × 10⁹/L; CRP 16 mg/L). She continued oral ciprofloxacin 1 g daily for 4 weeks after discharge.

Follow-up: 1 month later, the patient showed complete clinical resolution, with normal inflammatory markers and no complications.

Discussion

SVO is a rare but serious clinical condition. The three serovars most commonly causing osteomyelitis are S. typhimurium, S. enteritidis, and S. typhi [4,6,7]. In our case, the organism was identified as Salmonella sp., notable for its susceptibility to most antibiotics, which contrasts with the increasing global trend of antibiotic-resistant Salmonella strains.

Diabetes mellitus is the most prominent risk factor for SVO, being prevalent in 20.9% of cases [4]. Our patient had newly diagnosed type 2 diabetes mellitus (HbA1c, 9.2%), which likely contributed to increased infection severity and the risk of dissemination. Hyperglycemia impairs multiple aspects of immune function, including neutrophil phagocytosis, bactericidal activity, and cell-mediated immunity.

SVO often presents insidiously with nonspecific symptoms. According to the review by Huang et al., fever occurs in 65.67% of cases, local symptoms in 68.66%, while diarrhoea is present in only 17.91%; however, no cases of psoas muscle inflammation were documented. Our case aligned with this pattern, presenting with fever, severe back pain, and an absence of gastrointestinal symptoms [4].

The bilateral psoas myositis observed in our patient represents an uncommon complication of SVO. Spread from the vertebrae to the psoas muscle can occur via (i) direct extension through the anterior longitudinal ligament, (ii) the Batson venous system, or (iii) rupture of a prevertebral abscess [5,8]. Bilateral involvement is particularly rare and suggests extensive local inflammation or hematogenous spread.

The psoas involvement in our case was diagnosed by MRI, which showed prominent bilateral enhancement with T2-weighted high-signal imaging, indicating active myositis. This finding has important clinical implications, as psoas involvement may prolong recovery time and require extended treatment.

We conducted a literature search on PubMed and Embase for articles published from 1990 to 2025 using the following keywords: “Salmonella vertebral osteomyelitis,” “psoas myositis,” and “bilateral psoas abscess.” The search was limited to articles published in English. Additionally, the reference lists of relevant reviews were screened. No prior reports describing bilateral psoas myositis associated with SVO were identified.

Treatment of Salmonella osteomyelitis requires prolonged antibiotic therapy. The 2015 Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines recommend 6 weeks of treatment, emphasizing the use of highly bioavailable oral antibiotics as an alternative to intravenous therapy. Commonly selected antibiotics include third-generation cephalosporins, fluoroquinolones, or carbapenems in severe cases [9,10].

In our case, the patient did not respond to ceftriaxone combined with ciprofloxacin, despite susceptibility on antibiotic testing. The failure could be attributed to poor drug penetration at the site of bone infection, biofilm formation by Salmonella, uncontrolled diabetes reducing treatment efficacy, or minimum inhibitory concentration values close to resistance breakpoints. Meropenem was selected owing to clinical failure with the initial regimen. Switching to meropenem combined with ciprofloxacin led to marked clinical improvement after 4 days, with a rapid reduction in inflammatory markers. The patient was de-escalated early to oral ciprofloxacin following clinical improvement to optimize cost-effectiveness in a resource-limited setting.

SVO is primarily managed medically, with surgery reserved for specific indications, including spinal cord compression with paralysis, large abscesses not responding to percutaneous drainage, or spinal instability [[11], [12], [13]]. In our case, medical management was appropriate, given the absence of spinal cord compression, lack of large abscess formation, and good response to the modified antibiotic regimen.

Differential diagnoses include tuberculous spondylitis, fungal spondylodiscitis, malignancy with secondary infection, and other pyogenic spondylodiscitis. However, imaging features such as acute marrow oedema, absence of thin or longitudinal paraspinal abscess, and clinical acuity favored pyogenic infection over tuberculosis in this case. Eventually, positive Salmonella blood cultures provided a unifying diagnosis.

Our case has limitations, including the lack of specific serotyping of the Salmonella isolate and the absence of follow-up MRI due to financial constraints. However, clinical resolution remained complete at follow-up.

This case report has some limitations. First, specific Salmonella serotype identification was not performed owing to technical and financial constraints at our provincial hospital, limiting epidemiological correlation. Second, minimum inhibitory concentration values were not determined, which could have provided insight into the treatment failure of initially susceptible antibiotics. Third, additional inflammatory markers such as erythrocyte sedimentation rate and procalcitonin were not available. Fourth, the 1-month follow-up period was shorter than the 6 to 12 months recommended by the Infectious Diseases Society of America guidelines for monitoring vertebral osteomyelitis. Finally, a follow-up MRI was not performed owing to financial constraints, although clinical resolution was complete.

Conclusion

SVO with bilateral psoas myositis represents a rare disease entity that can occur without gastrointestinal symptoms, particularly in patients with uncontrolled diabetes mellitus. The bilateral psoas involvement adds diagnostic complexity and may require modified treatment approaches. Early diagnosis based on clinical presentation combined with MRI and blood culture is crucial for identifying the cause and guiding appropriate treatment. Recognition of this rare disease entity is necessary to avoid diagnostic delays, thereby limiting the risk of complications and improving patient outcomes.
