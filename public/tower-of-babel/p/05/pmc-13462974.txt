Prokaryotic Schlafen proteins cleave tRNAs during type III CRISPR immunity
Authors: Pedro Weickert, Yang Liu, Jonathan Strecker
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchlafen nucleases restrict viral infection in mammals by cleaving self RNAs, however, their function and mechanism in prokaryotic immunity is unknown. Here, we uncover CRISPR-associated Schlafen (Cash) proteins containing a Schlafen domain fused to Csx15, an uncharacterized member of Rossmann-like nucleotide-binding sensors. Cash is activated by cyclic tetra-adenylate (cA₄) produced during type III CRISPR interference and induces cell toxicity by cleaving tRNAs, primarily in the T-loop. Cryo-electron microscopy structures of Chloroflexi bacterium Cash reveal an inactive dodecamer, the formation of a filament upon cA₄ binding to align catalytic interfaces, and the molecular basis of substrate recognition and cleavage in a tRNA–bound complex. We identify numerous families of prokaryotic Schlafen proteins associated with diverse antiviral defense systems and characterized by unique sensor domains. This work highlights tRNA depletion by Schlafen nucleases as an evolutionary recurring antiviral strategy and reveals mechanistic differences between Cash and human Schlafen members.Subject terms: Structural biology, Bacteriophages, CRISPR-Cas systems, Bacterial structural biology

Introduction

Over the past decade, striking parallels have emerged between prokaryotic and eukaryotic antiviral immunity, revealing evolutionary connections and conserved immune strategies1. Examples include the production of cyclic nucleotide messengers by cGAS-like enzymes to activate downstream effectors2, pore-forming complexes that trigger cell death3, pattern recognition by NOD-like receptors4,5, and the generation of chain-terminating nucleotides to block viral replication6.

Mammalian Schlafen (SLFN) nucleases are induced by interferon7 and suppress viral infection by cleaving host tRNAs and rRNAs to disrupt protein translation8–10. SLFN11 is the best‑characterized member in humans and restricts HIV infection by cleaving tRNAs at the base of the acceptor stem in a codon-biased manner10,11. SLFN11 is recruited to stalled replication forks, can bind single-strand DNA, and is regulated by phosphorylation to form an active dimer11,12. Structures of SLFN11 reveal a single tRNA bound in the central channel between two SLFN11 subunits11,12. Mammalian SLFNs participate in cell proliferation, immune cell differentiation, and tumor biology, and several lack catalytic residues, suggesting non-nuclease functions13–15. However, although Schlafen domains are present in bacteria and archaea16,17, their function and mechanism outside mammals is less characterized18.

Metagenomic sequencing and protein functional prediction through genetic co-association have uncovered numerous new antiviral factors, including previously unrecognized functions of CRISPR-Cas systems16,19. Type III CRISPR systems, for example, generate secondary messenger molecules through the Palm domain of Cas10, which activate downstream effectors containing CRISPR-associated Rossmann fold (CARF) or SMODS-associated and fused to various effector domains (SAVED) proteins20,21. These include DNA- and RNA-targeting nucleases such as Csm6 and Card120–22, the NADase Cat123, the ATP deaminase Cad124, proteases25,26, and the pore-forming protein Cam127, highlighting the diverse strategies employed by CRISPR systems to block viral spread.

In this study, we characterize the structure, function, and mechanism of a prokaryotic Schlafen homolog and uncover a new mechanism by which type III CRISPR systems provide immunity.

Results

Cash proteins are activated by type III CRISPR systems and induce cell toxicity

While investigating type III CRISPR loci, we identified an uncharacterized protein containing a Schlafen nuclease domain and a C-terminal domain with homology to Csx15 (Fig. 1a, b). Csx15 is related to the CARF/SAVED family of Rossmann-like nucleotide-binding sensors despite exhibiting low sequence identify16 (Supplementary Figs. 1, 2). We named this protein Cash (CRISPR-associated Schlafen), and in contrast to the nuclease-inactive Schlafen family members, including mouse Slfn215, alignment of the nuclease domains displayed clear conservation of a catalytic triad11 (Fig. 1b, Supplementary Fig. 3a, b). Given the association with type III CRISPR systems, we hypothesized that Cash might be activated by Cas10-produced cyclic oligoadenylates (cOA). We utilized the established Mycobacterium tuberculosis type III-A (or Csm) CRISPR system, which produces all cOA messengers and has previously been used to characterize CARF effectors22. We generated Escherichia coli strains carrying plasmids encoding MtbCsm and Cash orthologs under an arabinose-inducible promoter and challenged strains with target plasmids (Fig. 1c). We observed a severe growth defect with two of the four tested Cash orthologs that depended on the presence of the crRNA target sequence, suggesting a potential abortive infection mechanism (Fig. 1d, Supplementary Fig. 3c). We decided to further characterize Cash from Chloroflexi bacterium (CbCash) and observed that mutation of the catalytic residues abolished the growth defect (CbCash-E17Q/D22N; Fig. 1d). To test whether the Schlafen domain targets RNA, we extracted and analyzed small RNAs from E. coli liquid cultures by denaturing gel electrophoresis. We observed a smear of small RNA products that was dependent on the presence of the crRNA target sequence and CbCash catalytic residues (Fig. 1e, Supplementary Fig 3d). We performed small RNA sequencing using adapter ligation to preserve precise RNA ends, which revealed an enrichment of tRNA fragments in CbCash-expressing cells compared to catalytic mutant controls (CbCash-E17Q/D22N) after 1 hour of induction (Fig. 1f, Supplementary Fig 3d). We detected cleavage of twenty-eight E. coli tRNAs, and analysis of the resulting 3′ RNA ends revealed cleavage sites that primarily mapped to the T-loop.

CbCash is a cA₄-triggered tRNA endonuclease

To gain mechanistic insight into the properties of CbCash, we purified wild-type and catalytic mutant CbCash-E17Q/D22N protein (Supplementary Fig. 4a) and generated a pool of forty-eight tRNAs encoded in E. coli using in vitro transcription (Supplementary Data 1). Cas10 generates a range of cyclic oligoadenylates20,21, and we first incubated CbCash with different cyclic nucleotides to determine the specific messenger required for activation. We successfully reconstituted tRNA cleavage in vitro and observed that CbCash activity was dependent on the addition of cyclic tetra-adenylate (cA₄) and its catalytic residues (Fig. 2a). In vitro activity of SLFN11 requires Mn2+ as a cofactor11; however, we observed CbCash activity with either Mn2+ or Mg2+ as the divalent cation (Supplementary Fig. 4b). CbCash showed the strongest activity of the four orthologs tested and also cleaved native E. coli tRNA (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d).

Gel electrophoresis of in vitro CbCash reactions revealed a primary cleavage site yielding fragments of approximately 55 and 20 nucleotides with additional minor products. (Fig. 2a, Supplementary Fig. 4d). RNA sequencing confirmed this cleavage to be in T-loops, consistent with in vivo results, while some tRNAs were also cleaved in the anticodon loop (Fig. 2b). Sequence analysis of cleaved anticodon loops revealed a UC motif following the cleavage site, matching the conserved T-loop sequence (Fig. 2c). At higher concentrations, CbCash cleaved anticodon loops in a larger number of in vitro–transcribed tRNAs (Supplementary Fig. 4e).

In addition to tRNA, we also observed in vitro cleavage of native and in vitro–transcribed 16S rRNA, but not 5S or 23S, and identified four cleavage positions in 16S rRNA by sequencing (Supplementary Fig. 5a–d). None of these four sites were observed in RNA sequencing from cells expressing CbCash, suggesting it is not a likely source of toxicity in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 5d).

To further characterize CbCash specificity, we focused on in vitro–transcribed tRNAIle(CAU) as a substrate, which is efficiently cleaved in both the anticodon and T-loops (Supplementary Fig. 5e). Substitutions of both nucleotides flanking each site with adenine impaired cleavage and CbCash had no detectable activity on a double-loop mutant (Fig. 2d). Single mutations at U54 and U55 in the T-loop also abolished cleavage, indicating these are critical for activity (Supplementary Fig. 5f, g).

CbCash forms an inactive dodecamer

To understand the molecular mechanism underlying CbCash activation, we turned to structural studies using single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM). First, we determined the structure of apo–CbCash at 2.8 Å and observed a D2‑symmetric dodecamer assembled from six homodimers (Fig. 3a, Supplementary Fig. 6a–d). Within each dimer unit, the Csx15 domains form a tight interface stabilized by a reciprocal E207–R264 salt bridge, explaining the robustness of the interface (Fig. 3b). Decomposition of the D2 dodecamer revealed four symmetrical units, each in which CbCash dimers engage an additional monomer through Csx15. The oligomerization of dimers depends on two distinct interfaces. The first lies between neighboring symmetry units and is formed by a dense salt‑bridge network with putative residues pairs E292–R100, R287–D125, R158–E292, and E129–R230 (Fig. 3c). The second resides within each symmetry unit, where the CbCash dimer contacts a single monomer through a hydrogen‑bonding network donated by Arg154 and Arg158 to backbone carbonyls in the L124–C126–P127 segment (Fig. 3d).

Mass photometry of CbCash corroborated this architecture, revealing a ~ 386 kDa species consistent with a dodecamer (12 × 33.4 kDa monomers = 401 kDa theoretical), along with a minor ~67 kDa dimer. Disruption of either oligomerization interface (R158A/E292A or D125A/R287A) prevented dodecamer assembly (Fig. 3e) and disruption of the salt-bridge network (E129A/R230A, R158A/E292A, D125A/R287A, R100A/E292A) or Csx15 dimer interface (E207A/R264A) all abolished tRNA cleavage in vitro (Fig. 3f, Supplementary Fig. 6e).

Filamentation of CbCash upon cA₄ binding

Next, we turned to the active state and observed that the addition of cA₄ converted CbCash dodecamer particles into long, uniform filaments within seconds, which were readily visible by cryo-EM (Fig. 4a). The structure was determined at 2.4 Å and revealed that the filament is built from repeating segments, each composed of two parallel Schlafen dimers (Fig. 4b, Supplementary Fig. 7a–e). Adjacent segments are joined end to end and rotated by ~72°, such that five segments complete one helical turn with a pitch of ~288 Å. Each helical segment contains two cA₄ molecules located at the dimeric Csx15 interfaces, one in an outer pocket and one in an inner pocket. The adenine bases of each ligand are accommodated within two aromatic sites formed by both Csx15 subunits, where Phe241 and Phe275 provide π-stacking interactions (Fig. 4c, d). In the inner site, cA₄ binding repositions Arg204, which is solvent exposed in apo–CbCash, so that it rotates into the pocket to engage an adenine ring through a cation–π interaction, forming an Arg204–Glu292 salt bridge that links the two parallel dimers within each segment (Fig. 4e).

Within each segment, the two dimers form a stacked interface but adopt distinct orientations at the Schlafen domains relative to the stable Csx15 core. The dimer bound to the outer cA₄ remains in the ‘closed’ conformation and is structurally similar to apo–CbCash; however, the adjacent dimer with buried cA₄ undergoes a substantial conformational change, moving the Schlafen domain into an ‘open’ position that exposes the catalytic residues (Fig. 4f). This open–closed interface is stabilized by a network of charged contacts, including putative salt bridges R19–E102, D148–R85, K61–D79, and R154–D125. Alanine substitutions at these pairs reduced or abolished tRNAIle(CAU) cleavage, demonstrating that this interface is required for activation, as did mutation of the cA4 binding pocket (F275A; Fig. 4g, Supplementary Fig. 7f).

tRNA recognition and cleavage by CbCash

To determine how CbCash engages its substrate, we pre-incubated protein with tRNAIle(CAU) and mixed it with cA4 on grids. We observed similar filaments to the cA4–bound state and determined the structure at 2.6 Å (Supplementary Fig. 8a, b). The map reveals two additional stem‑loop densities inserted into adjacent Schlafen pockets along the filament, which align to the anticodon and T-loops (Fig. 5a, b). Binding of tRNA occurs only through the ‘open’ Schlafen domains present in dimers with buried cA₄, highlighting the importance of the conformational change within each segment. We could only unambiguously assign density for two loops of tRNAIle(CAU) and note a distortion of the geometry in complex compared to an apo yeast tRNAPhe structure11 (Supplementary Fig. 8c).

Closer views show the scissile phosphate immediately 5′ to the conserved uridine (U55 in the T‑loop) positioned in the catalytic pocket formed by the E–D–K triad (E17, D22, K24) and a bound Mg2+, with R25 lining the pocket. An analogous configuration is observed for the anticodon loop in the other pocket. Consistent with this active‑site geometry, mutations in the catalytic center (E17Q, D22N, K24A) eliminated tRNA cleavage in vitro, and as previously noted, base substitutions of U55 likewise abolished cleavage (Fig. 5d, Supplementary Fig. 5f, 8d). Together with our biochemical identification of a cleavage motif, these data indicate that CbCash recognizes the canonical U‑turn architecture in tRNA loops, using it to pre‑organize the backbone so that the scissile phosphodiester is positioned within the E17–D22–K24/ Mg2+ catalytic center for hydrolysis28.

Diverse Schlafen proteins linked to prokaryotic defense systems

To investigate other instances of Schlafen domains in prokaryotes, we performed PSI-BLAST searches using the CbCash nuclease domain (residues 1-163) as a query, clustered hits at 50% sequence identity, and extracted gene neighborhoods from the contigs to identify potential associations with defense systems29. We extracted Schlafen domains from each protein to generate a phylogenetic tree with 926 members, and additional domains were annotated using HHpred30 (Fig. 6a, Supplementary Data 3). Outside of Cash, we identified seven clusters of Schlafen domains linked to diverse defense systems, including short prokaryotic Argonautes31 (Cluster 6), and direct fusions with methyltransferases found in restriction modification systems32 (Cluster 3). A large number of Schlafen homologs contained ATPase domains, often in combination with DNA-binding domains, and were tightly associated with nuclease-based defense systems, including BREX33 (Cluster 2), restriction–modification (Cluster 7), and type I CRISPR systems (Cluster 1). Additional type III CRISPR-associated Schlafen proteins were identified that lack a Rossman-like nucleotide-binding domain and instead contain NACHT domains, which are found in NOD-like receptors and regulate oligomerization4,5 (Cluster 5). Finally, a large portion of Schlafen homologs in the tree contain C-terminal domains of unknown function and are associated with restriction–modification systems (Cluster 4). Together, these data highlight the diverse regulatory architectures that have evolved around Schlafen domains in prokaryotic defense and point to new opportunities for uncovering mechanisms of Schlafen nuclease activation.

Discussion

In this work, we describe the function, structure, and mechanism of Cash proteins and observe tRNA depletion by Schlafen nucleases as an evolutionarily recurring antiviral strategy. Our biochemical and structural data indicate that CbCash undergoes substantial reorganization upon activation and preferentially cleaves the tRNA T-loop (Fig. 6b), revealing key differences to known Schlafen family members. Eukaryotic SLFN11, for example, targets the base of the acceptor stem and cleaves at a single site approximately 10 nucleotides from the 3′ end12. Despite this distinct biochemical activity, structural studies of SLFN11 reveal a second nuclease center near the T-loop that aligns with the cleavage site observed in CbCash12. In contrast to SLFN11, which preferentially targets type II tRNAs8,10,34, CbCash exhibits broad activity and targets a UUC motif within the T-loop, with cleavage observed in both in vitro–transcribed and native E. coli tRNA. In mature tRNAs, this sequence corresponds to the conserved TΨC motif, where pseudouridine (Ψ) stabilizes the U-turn structure and is found in almost all tRNAs across organisms35 (Supplementary Fig. 9). Cleavage of self-tRNAs is a known immune strategy in bacteria, but most nucleases target specific tRNAs. For example, AriB from the PARIS defense system36 and PrrC37, which both target tRNALys, and PtuAB38 from retron-Eco7, which cleaves tRNATyr.

Structurally, CbCash uses a two-layered regulatory mechanism that suppresses basal activity and enables activation-dependent cell toxicity. In the absence of cA₄, CbCash assembles into a highly stable, D₂-symmetric dodecamer composed of Csx15-bridged dimers (Fig. 3a–d). This architecture constrains the motion of the Schlafen domains and occludes the tRNA-binding grooves, consistent with our inability to detect tRNA engagement in the absence of cA₄ by cryo-EM (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). Upon cA₄ addition, CbCash rapidly polymerizes into filaments, a phenomenon also observed in other CARF/SAVED family members23,25. In this filamentous state, neighboring Schlafen domains align in parallel and expose an active catalytic center capable of engaging tRNA. Strikingly, on-grid mixing experiments reveal that this transition occurs within seconds, suggesting that CbCash can trigger an immediate response to type III CRISPR signaling during bacteriophage infection. Mechanistically, this control differs from the prevailing model for mammalian SLFNs, whose activity is primarily regulated by dimerization, controlled through post-translational modification12. CbCash instead introduces an upstream oligomerization step to stabilize the inactive state, followed by a downstream, polymerization-driven amplification step upon cA₄ binding.

To our knowledge, this work represents the first mechanistic characterization of a Csx15-fused effector16,39, and despite limited sequence homology to CARF and SAVED domains16, we find it also responds to cyclic nucleotides. We note several interesting differences in our structure compared to other cA4-activated effectors. In the deaminase Cad1, cA4 binding induces a large conformational change in the CARF domain that activates the enzyme24; however, we did not observe comparable changes in Csx15 and detected only a minor reorientation of an arginine side chain (Supplementary Fig. 10c, d). In the Lon-SAVED protease, which also forms filaments, cA₄ directly mediates the filament-stacking interface25 (Supplementary Fig. 10e). This interaction is not observed in the CbCash filament, where cA4 contacts only the bound CbCash dimer, but not the parallel dimer. Together, these observations suggest that CbCash represents a distinct mode of cA₄-dependent regulation compared with known CARF and SAVED domains. A recent preprint describes Csx15 proteins that possess cA₄ ring nuclease activity and non-enzymatic variants that can form oligomers with cA₄-mediated stacking40, highlighting a potential diversity of Csx15 function and activation mechanisms.

The association of Schlafen domains with other prokaryotic defense systems highlights their importance in antiviral responses and reveals diverse regulatory units that are likely to modulate nuclease activation. Our phylogenetic analysis identifies previously undescribed defense-associated Schlafen homologs, and we note that more than fifty percent of these proteins are linked to restriction–modification systems and other nucleases17 such as type I CRISPR systems (Fig. 6a). Future work will be required to define the activation mechanism for these enzymes. It is tempting to speculate that such homologs may detect specific structures generated by DNA cleavage, reminiscent of the single-stranded DNA sensing role proposed for the human SLFN11 helicase domain41. One such system was recently described and consists of a bacterial Schlafen protein fused to an immunoglobulin-like domain42. In this system, recognition of the T5 bacteriophage tail protein triggers oligomerization and cleavage of the anticodon loop of several host and phage tRNAs. The authors also uncovered Schlafen architectures that may overlap with systems identified here, highlighting the prevalence of Schlafen nucleases in antiviral immunity and their distinct modes of regulation.

Together, our characterization of Cash reveals mechanistic parallels between bacterial Schlafen defense systems and their mammalian counterparts, extends our understanding of CRISPR-activated immune functions, and suggests that Schlafen domains have evolved as a recurring solution for nucleic acid immunity in bacteria and eukaryotes.

This study has several limitations. Our cellular characterization of CbCash relies on heterologous expression in E. coli and a non-cognate Csm complex, and the specificity and kinetics of cleavage in the native Chloroflexi bacterium remain unclear. Host tRNA sequence and modification state may alter the substrate preference we observe, and whether phage tRNAs are also targeted remains an open question. Our cryo-EM reconstructions capture two stable states but do not resolve how the dodecamer transitions into the filament, whether through complete disassembly or a sliding rearrangement. We are currently unable to determine whether the dodecamer is required for filament formation and tRNA cleavage, as our mutations impair both, and future work will be needed to determine whether these functions can be separated. Whether these higher-order filaments form in the native host is unknown. Finally, our work contributes to our understanding of the evolutionary diversity of bacterial Schlafen enzymes. We propose that these diverse systems mediate defense through tRNA cleavage; however, the specific triggers and activation mechanisms remain to be defined

Methods

Cloning and mutagenesis

All genes were codon-optimized for expression in E. coli, and gene fragments were ordered from Twist Bioscience for construction by Gibson assembly. All mutants used in this study were generated using either Gibson assembly or the KLD Enzyme Mix (NEB). PCRs were performed with Phusion Flash High-Fidelity polymerase (Thermo Scientific) using primers from Azenta (Supplementary Data 1). All cloning was performed in E. coli Turbo cells (NEB, C2984H), and plasmids were sequence-verified by Nanopore sequencing (Azenta).

For protein purification, cash genes were cloned into a pET vector containing an N-terminal TwinStrep–SUMO affinity tag and an T7 promoter. For defense assays, an operon containing MtbCsm was codon-optimized and cloned into a pCOLA plasmid containing an arabinose-inducible pBAD promoter with a ribosome-binding site between each gene (cas10, csm2–5, and cas6). Cash homologs were cloned into pACYC plasmids containing pBAD promoters, and a gene containing the Csm target sequence was cloned into pET with a pBAD promoter. pMtbCsm contains a short CRISPR array under transcriptional control of a J23119 promoter with five direct repeats and four unique spacers matching the target RNA. All gene sequences and accession numbers are provided in Supplementary Data 1.

Plasmid interference assay

E. coli BL21(DE3) cells (Sigma-Aldrich, CMC0016) were electroporated with pMtbCsm and plasmids expressing each of the cash homologs and selected with chloramphenicol and kanamycin. Transformed cells were made competent using the Mix & Go! Competent Cell Kit (Zymo Research), and 100 μl of competent cells were transformed with 100 ng of target or non-target plasmids. After transformation, cells were recovered for 1 h at 37 °C in LB Lennox broth, gently pelleted, resuspended in 100 μl of LB Lennox, and serially diluted twofold. Three microliters of each dilution were spotted onto plates containing ampicillin, kanamycin, and chloramphenicol and either 0.25% (w/v) glucose or 0.25% (w/v) arabinose. For an example of the presentation of full photographed plate, see the Source Data file.

RNA extraction and sequencing

For E. coli RNA extraction, cells transformed with pMtbCsm, pCash, and pTarget plasmids were grown in 20 ml of LB Lennox containing ampicillin, kanamycin, chloramphenicol, and 0.25% glucose overnight at 30 °C. Cultures were pelleted and washed with 50 ml of distilled water to remove residual glucose, pelleted again, and the entire pellet was inoculated into 50 ml of fresh medium containing antibiotics and 0.25% arabinose. Cultures were grown at 37 °C, and 10 ml aliquots were collected, pelleted, and frozen at the indicated times.

Pellets were resuspended in 2 ml of TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen) and incubated for 10 min with intermittent vortexing. Chloroform (500 μl) was added, the mixture was vortexed again, and after a 15 min incubation, the tubes were centrifuged at maximum speed. The upper aqueous phase (100 μl) was transferred to a fresh tube, and RNA was precipitated with isopropanol. Pellets were resuspended in 100 μl of distilled water (total RNAs). Small RNAs were isolated using the RNA Clean & Concentrator-5 kit (Zymo Research, R1013), including the DNase I treatment step.

After elution in RNase-free water, 300 ng of RNA from each condition was mixed with RNA loading dye (NEB, B0363S) and loaded into the wells of a 12-well Novex 15% TBE–Urea gel (Invitrogen). Gels were prerun in 0.5× TBE at 200 V for 15 min, samples were loaded, and electrophoresis continued for 45 min at 200 V. Gels were then stained in 0.5× TBE buffer containing SYBR Gold (Invitrogen) and imaged using the ChemiDoc MP imaging system (Bio-Rad).

Two hundred nanograms of extracted RNA were treated with T4 polynucleotide kinase (NEB) and RNA 5′ pyrophosphohydrolase (RppH) in 50 mM HEPES, pH 7.5, 50 mM NaCl, and 5 mM MgCl₂ at 37 °C for 6 h. The reaction was supplemented with additional PNK and ATP and incubated for an additional 1 h at 37 °C. Reaction mixtures were purified using the RNA Clean & Concentrator kit (Zymo Research) according to the manufacturer’s protocol. One hundred nanograms of purified RNA were used for library preparation with the NEBNext Small RNA Library Construction Kit (NEB) following the manufacturer’s instructions, using unique barcode indexes. The resulting libraries were purified with AMPure XP beads (Beckman Colter, A63881) and sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq 2000 or an Illumina Miseq instrument.

Protein expression and purification

E. coli BL21(DE3) cells (NEB, C2527H) were transformed with pET_Twin-Strep–SUMO plasmids encoding different cash homologs or mutants. Cells were grown in 1 L of Terrific Broth (IBA) supplemented with ampicillin to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.5 with vigorous shaking at 37 °C. The temperature was then lowered to 18 °C, IPTG was added to a final concentration of 250 μM, and cultures were incubated overnight. The following morning, cultures were pelleted and stored at −80 °C.

Cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 500 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol) supplemented with 1 mM PMSF, 2 μg ml⁻¹ aprotinin, 5 μM leupeptin, and 1 mM TCEP. Lysozyme (100 mg) was added to facilitate lysis, and the suspension was sonicated (5 min total, 3 s on/7 s off, 70 W). The lysate was clarified by centrifugation at 38,000 × g for 30 min at 4 °C, and the supernatant was incubated with gentle mixing for 1 h at 4 °C with 1 ml of Strep-Tactin Superflow Plus resin (Qiagen, 30004).

The resin was transferred to a chromatography column (Bio-Rad, 7321010) and washed with 10 column volumes (CV) of wash buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 1 M NaCl, 5% glycerol), followed by 20 CV of lysis buffer. The resin was resuspended in 2 ml of elution buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol) supplemented with 1 mM TCEP and Ulp1 protease to cleave the SUMO tag, and incubated on ice for 20 min. The flow-through was collected, and the resin was washed with 10 CV of low-salt buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 5% glycerol), which was pooled with the eluate.

The combined sample was loaded onto an ÄKTA Pure system and passed through a RESOURCE Q column (Cytiva, 17117701). The flow-through was collected and concentrated using a centrifugal filter unit (Millipore, UFC905008). Protein concentrations were determined by A₂₈₀, aliquoted, and flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen. Protein purity was assessed by SDS–PAGE, resolving 1 μg of protein on a gradient gel (Invitrogen, XP04122) and staining with InstantBlue Coomassie (Abcam, ISB1L).

In vitro tRNA cleavage assays

In vitro assays were performed with 100 nM protein unless otherwise stated. Standard reactions were carried out at 37 °C for 15 min using 200 ng of RNA template in Schlafen buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 100 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl₂) and 1 μM of cA₄ (or other secondary messenger) in 10 ul final volume. All secondary messengers tested were obtained from Biolog. Samples were resolved as described for in vivo RNA experiments (15% TBE–Urea gels), with half of each reaction loaded. For total RNAs, 250 ng of RNAs extracted as described before (TRIzol extraction) were used for the assay. 6% TBE-Urea gels were used for resolving larger RNAs.

tRNA sequences and relevant primers used are provided in Supplementary Data 1. tRNAs and rRNAs (template amplified from E. coli genomic DNA) were in vitro transcribed using the HiScribe T7 High Yield RNA Synthesis Kit (NEB, E2040S) and purified with RNA Clean XP beads (Beckman Colter, A63987). Isoleucin tRNA variants were generated by overlapping PCR of two oligos that contained the T7 promoter and the corresponding mutation. E. coli tRNAs used to compare different systems were extracted as described above, except that following TRIzol extraction, long RNAs were selectively precipitated with 2 M lithium chloride. The supernatant was transferred to a fresh tube, precipitated with isopropanol, digested with DNase I, and reprecipitated using ethanol and sodium acetate. For Fig. 2b and S4d, we purchased E. coli tRNAs, which contain contaminating ribosomal RNA, from NEB (NEB, N6845S) and incubated them with the mentioned concentrations of CbCash but scaled up the reaction to 50 ul (1 ug of total RNA). 10 ul were resolved on a gel, and the rest was used as an input for library preparation (see sequence analysis section). For an example of the presentation of full scan blots, see the Source Data file.

Cryo‑EM sample preparation and data collection

Apo–CbCash was purified as described above, except that glycerol was omitted during the ion-exchange step and all procedures were performed in cryo-EM buffer (25 mM HEPES pH 7.5, 200 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl₂). Apo–CbCash (2 mg ml⁻¹) was applied to Quantifoil R1.2/1.3, 300-mesh Au holey-carbon grids (Quantifoil).

For the cA₄-bound complex, cA₄ was added to apo–CbCash to a final concentration of 20 μM and incubated for 30 min at room temperature before vitrification. For the cA₄/tRNAIlᵉ(CAU) -bound complex, apo–CbCash (2 mg ml⁻¹) was incubated with tRNAIlᵉ(CAU) at 100 μM for 10 min at 4 °C, followed by the addition of cA₄ to 20 μM immediately before vitrification.

Grids were blotted and plunge-frozen in liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Data were collected on a Titan Krios microscope operated at 300 kV and equipped with either a Gatan K3 direct electron detector or a Falcon 4 detector (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Movies were recorded using Legion software at physical pixel sizes of 0.93, 0.844, or 0.649 Å per pixel, with 5-s exposures fractionated into 50 frames and a total dose of ~50 e⁻ Å⁻².

Image processing and 3D reconstruction

Dark‑subtracted movies were gain‑normalized. Motion correction and dose‑weighting were performed in cryoSPARC using Patch motion correction. The contrast transfer function (CTF) was estimated with Patch CTF in cryoSPARC43. Particles were autopicked using the Blob Picker (80–160 Å diameter) and extracted. Approximately 200,000 particles were extracted for the apo CbCash dataset and ~1,000,000 for the cA₄-bound and cA₄–tRNAIle(CAU) ‑bound datasets. Subsequent two‑dimensional classification, multi‑class ab initio modeling, heterogeneous 3D refinement, and non‑uniform refinement of the best class were carried out (Supplementary Figs. 6–8). For CbCash complexes, local CTF refinement followed by local refinement in cryoSPARC yielded the final maps.

Cryo‑EM model building and refinement

For CbCash complexes, an initial model guided by an AlphaFold2 prediction was built de novo and manually adjusted in Coot44. Residue 1 and residues 295–298 were not modeled. The model was refined in real space with Phenix45. For cross‑validation, the final model was refined against one half‑map, and model–map Fourier shell correlation (FSC) curves were generated with the comprehensive validation module in Phenix45. Phenix and MolProbity were used to validate the final model46. Structure figures were generated in ChimeraX47.

Sequence analysis of tRNA cleavage

Small RNA libraries that harbor NEBNext Small RNA index barcodes were sequenced on an Illumina NextSeq or Illumina MiSeq. Base calls were demultiplexed, and 3′ adapters were removed with Cutadapt48 using the adapter sequences specific to the small RNA library preparation. Adapter‑trimmed reads were aligned with srnaMapper49 to a 48‑tRNA reference library or a 48-tRNA plus 5 s/16 s/23 s rRNA reference library, for which we first constructed a pseudo‑genome from the component reference sequences. Following alignment, the 3′ termini of mapped reads were enumerated to generate per-position counts, and tRNAs with fewer than 20 mapped reads were filtered out. Z-scores at each genomic location were then computed from these 3′‑end counts. Positions with Z-score >1 were visualized with custom scripts. Unless otherwise stated, tools were run with default parameters. All scripts that are used in this analysis are available on Zenodo.

Bioinformatic analysis of Schlafen homologs

The CbCash Schlafen domain (amino acids 1–163) was used as a seed for homology searches using NCBI PSI-BLAST. We performed two iterations with a maximum of 20,000 target sequences (ClusterNR) against the bacterial (TaxID: 2) and archaeal (TaxID: 2157) databases. The FASTA output was processed with CD-HIT using a maximum sequence identity cutoff of 0.5. We identified 4913 Schlafen homologs and retrieved ±10 kb of their genomic contexts from NCBI as annotated GenBank records.

Defense Finder29 was used to identify all known defense systems in the vicinity of Schlafen homologs, and the system closest to each query coding sequence was used to assign association. A total of 926 homologs were categorized as defense-enriched. NCBI’s Conserved Domain Database (CDD) was used to annotate the Schlafen domains (AlbA2 or equivalent) of each homolog; missing domains were added manually using HHPred. Domains were extracted for each protein, resulting in a FASTA file containing the Schlafen domains of each original sequence. Phylogenetic analysis was performed on these 926 AlbA-domain protein sequences. Sequences were aligned with MAFFT v7.526 (--auto) and trimmed with trimAl (-gappyout), yielding a final alignment of 108 amino acid positions. Maximum- likelihood inference was performed with IQ-TREE v3.1.050 under the LG + R4 model, with branch support assessed using 1000 SH-aLRT replicates and 1000 ultrafast bootstrap replicates with --bnni. The resulting tree was visualized in iTOL51, and terminal leaves were pruned for visualization purposes only, yielding a final displayed tree of 890 sequences; protein lengths were added as annotations. Domains of selected candidates were classified and delimited using HHPred30. Sequences used for phylogenetic analysis and defense-system assignments are provided as Supplementary Data 3. Sequence alignments in other figures were visualized in Geneious and formatted using ENDscript52.
