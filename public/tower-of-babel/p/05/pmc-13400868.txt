Ayush systems in India- understanding the Awareness, Utilization, and Out-of-Pocket Expenditure (OOPE): Insights from the 79th round of National Sample Survey (NSS)- 2022–23
Authors: Shobhit Kumar, Shrestha Hazra, Abhijit Dutta, Deepak Londhe, Raman Kaushik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAyush systems form a crucial component of India's healthcare system. Despite sustained policy attention, the implementation of the National Ayush Mission scheme and progress in the sector, challenges persist in the availability and utilization of Ayush services in several States/UTs. Drawing on the National Sample Survey (79th Round, 2022–23) on Ayush, this study examines state-wise variations in awareness, usage, and out-of-pocket expenditure (OOPE) on Ayush medicines, alongside the availability of infrastructure, human resources, and financial burden.MethodsA descriptive cross-sectional analysis was undertaken using NSSO 79th Round data, supplemented with information on Ayush dispensaries and registered practitioners. Population coverage per dispensary and per practitioner served as proxy indicators for infrastructure and workforce availability. States/UTs were categorized by availability levels and usage relative to the national average (496 users per 1000). Cross-tabulations and comparative categorizations were used to examine inter-state variation.ResultsNational awareness exceeded 95%, while usage remained around 50%, with substantial variation across States/UTs. 20 States/UTs reported above-national average usage and sixteen below. Infrastructure availability varied widely, with only five States/UTs meeting high dispensary coverage and thirteen exhibiting very low coverage. Practitioner availability was similarly uneven, ranging from zero in several Northeastern States to strong density in Kerala and Maharashtra. Higher availability did not consistently correspond with higher usage. OOPE averaged ₹523 per capita annually, with significant inter-state differences, indicating non-linear associations.Recommendations and conclusionDespite widespread awareness, Ayush utilization remains uneven due to infrastructure gaps and workforce disparities. Strengthening infrastructure and human resources, improving quality of care, expanding digital systems, enhancing public financing, education and training, promoting evidence-informed communication, and reinforcing research–practice linkages are essential to improve access, utilization and financial protection.Keywords: Ayush, Awareness, Healthcare utilization, Out-of-pocket expenditure, NSS, NSSO

Introduction

The Ayush (Erstwhile Indian System of Medicine and Homoeopathy), encompassing Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, and Homoeopathy, has long been an integral part of Indian culture, practiced even before the formal healthcare system was established. The integration of Indian Systems of Medicine and Homoeopathy (ISM&H) into primary healthcare has been endorsed by expert committees, starting with the Mudaliar Committee, which recommended using indigenous doctors for vertical health programs. Later national policies (1983, 1989, 2002) recognized ISM's potential to improve healthcare access, especially in rural areas [1].

The institutionalization of ISM&H led to the formation of the Department of Ayush in 2003, later elevated to a Ministry in 2014, strengthening infrastructure and integration into public health. Initiatives like the National Rural Health Mission (NRHM)'s mainstreaming strategies and the National Ayush Mission aimed to improve accessibility, affordability, and sustainability of care. The National Health Policy 2017 further recognized Ayush as integral to India's health system, promoting preventive health, standardization, integration with conventional healthcare, and scientific validation of its practices.

With a global rise in wellness trends, the uptake of Ayush medicine in India has been increasing steadily, particularly as people seek care through traditional systems of medicine in addition to conventional medicine. The Ayush market has seen remarkable growth, expanding from USD 2.85 billion in 2014 to USD 43.4 billion in 2023, while exports have also doubled from USD 1.09 billion to USD 2.16 billion during the same period [2]. The accessibility to Ayush services is improving through provisions under the National Ayush Mission (NAM) related to strengthening Ayush public health infrastructure and improving medicines availability [3].

Recently, the National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) conducted an exclusive all-India survey on Ayush between July 2022 and June 2023, as part of the 79th round of the National Sample Survey (NSS). This survey is the first of its kind and covers the entire Indian Union, excluding a few inaccessible villages in the Andaman & Nicobar Islands. The broad objectives of the survey were to collect information on the awareness of people about the traditional system of healthcare (Ayush system of medicine); uses of Ayush for prevention or treatment of ailments; and, awareness of the households about home remedies, medicinal plants, local health tradition/folk medicine [4].

Drawing on survey data, this paper analyses the levels of awareness and patterns of utilization of Ayush medicines across various States and Union Territories (UTs) in India and investigates the systemic factors that might be associated with these trends. The study examines the distribution of Ayush public health infrastructure and human resources in relation to awareness and usage, employing cross-tabulation as the primary analytical method. Infrastructure availability is assessed using the number of Ayush dispensaries as a proxy indicator, based on data from the National Health Profile (2023) [5]. Similarly, the availability of human resources is estimated using state-wise data on registered Ayush practitioners. Both indicators are measured against projected population figures to derive comparative estimates. This paper aims to investigate the disparity between awareness and actual usage of Ayush medicines across various States and Union Territories in India. It assesses how the availability of Ayush infrastructure influences usage patterns and analyzes the variation in Out-of-Pocket Expenditure (OOPE) on Ayush medicines across different states. The study also explores how the availability of Ayush infrastructure and human resources influences OOPE levels. Ultimately, it recommends policy actions that can enhance access and usage of Ayush services while reducing the financial burden on individuals.

Methods

This study draws upon data from the 79th Round of the National Sample Survey (NSS) on Ayush: Awareness and Utilization, conducted by the National Sample Survey Office (NSSO) between July 2022 and June 2023. This was the first all-India survey exclusively focused on Ayush systems, encompassing Ayurveda, Yoga & Naturopathy, Unani, Siddha, Sowa-Rigpa, and Homoeopathy. The survey covered 1,81,298 households, including 1,04,195 in rural areas and 77,103 in urban areas, representing the entire Indian Union (excluding a few inaccessible villages in the Andaman & Nicobar Islands).

Data source and variables

Data were extracted from the NSS factsheets, and concerned report related published by the Government of India. The key variables analyzed include:

1)Awareness of Ayush: Percentage of population aware of any Ayush system, as defined by NSS.

Awareness of Ayush: Percentage of population aware of any Ayush system, as defined by NSS.

2)Usage of Ayush: Percentage of population reporting use of any Ayush system for prevention or treatment in the last reference period.

Usage of Ayush: Percentage of population reporting use of any Ayush system for prevention or treatment in the last reference period.

3)Out-of-Pocket Expenditure (OOPE): Average annual per-person expenditure incurred for Ayush treatment during the last 365 days preceding the survey.

Out-of-Pocket Expenditure (OOPE): Average annual per-person expenditure incurred for Ayush treatment during the last 365 days preceding the survey.

4)Infrastructure Availability: Number of Ayush dispensaries per State/UT obtained from National Health Profile 2023, against the projected population (Census-based estimates).

Infrastructure Availability: Number of Ayush dispensaries per State/UT obtained from National Health Profile 2023, against the projected population (Census-based estimates).

5)Human Resource Availability: Number of registered Ayush practitioners per State/UT from Ayush in India 2022 [6], against the projected population.

Human Resource Availability: Number of registered Ayush practitioners per State/UT from Ayush in India 2022 [6], against the projected population.

Analytical framework

A descriptive cross-sectional analysis was conducted using cross-tabulation to examine inter-state variations in awareness, utilization, and OOPE. The relationship between Ayush infrastructure, human resource availability, and usage was analyzed through comparative categorization:

1)Dispensary availability: Population covered per dispensary, classified as High (<10,000), Moderate (10,000–25,000), Low (25,000–50,000), and Very Low (>50,000).

Dispensary availability: Population covered per dispensary, classified as High (<10,000), Moderate (10,000–25,000), Low (25,000–50,000), and Very Low (>50,000).

2)Practitioner availability: Population covered per registered practitioner, categorized as High (<1000), Moderate (1000–5000), Low (5000–10,000), Very Low (>10,000), and Nil.

Practitioner availability: Population covered per registered practitioner, categorized as High (<1000), Moderate (1000–5000), Low (5000–10,000), Very Low (>10,000), and Nil.

3)Usage and OOPE: Both are classified as above or below the national average.

Usage and OOPE: Both are classified as above or below the national average.

The data were presented through state-wise comparative tables and categorical distributions to illustrate patterns and associations. The analysis emphasized descriptive and relational interpretation, acknowledging the survey's design as a non-experimental, population-based dataset.

The indicators such as availability of Ayush dispensaries and registered practitioners across States/UTs has been used to see their relation with awareness, usage and out-of-pocket expenditure. The use of such indictors is consistent with health systems analysis frameworks of the World Health Organization, particularly in contexts where granular data on facility functionality, and quality of care are not uniformly available. These indicators provide a comparable, first-order approximation of service availability and potential access across such heterogeneous settings.

Defining awareness: As per NSSO, a member of the household (aged 15 years or more) is considered as “aware of Ayush” if she/he has taken treatment using Ayush anytime or is aware about medicinal plants, home remedies/traditional practices for treatment or prevention of ailments and is/was engaged by profession to Ayush or has heard about at least one of the Ayush systems.

•About 95% of India's population is aware of the Ayush system, with rural awareness ranging 87–99% (except Arunachal Pradesh at 77%) and urban awareness above 86% across all States/UTs.

While awareness across States/UTs seems to be significantly high, this awareness does not always translate into widespread usage (prevention or treatment of disease). The gap between awareness and usage is 42.9% in rural areas, while it is 41.3% in urban areas. In rural areas, it varies from 3.2% to 71.8%. In urban areas, while the minimum gap is 5.4%, the maximum is 78.8%. The gap (%) has been classified into four categories for rural and urban, and accordingly, the distribution of the States/UTs has been outlined in Table 1 to determine their relative position. It can be observed that, in most States/UTs, the gap between awareness and usage of Ayush is similar in rural and urban areas, except in a few cases (e.g., Chhattisgarh, Telangana, and Lakshadweep, which have higher rural gaps; Goa, which has a higher urban gap). However, rural awareness–usage gaps exceed those in urban areas in 22 States/UTs (highest at 18.3% in Lakshadweep), while urban gaps are higher in 14 States/UTs (highest at 10.6% in Goa); gaps are generally higher among males than females, with a few exceptions.

A total of 36,068 Ayush dispensaries are available across all 36 States/UTs in India and the average population covered by a dispensary is 1,32,044 at the national level.

There is a huge variation across States/UTs in terms of the population covered by a dispensary, ranging from 3778 in Lakshadweep to 31,65,000 in Manipur, where only one Ayush dispensary is available. Infrastructure, workforce, and the usage of Ayush

To examine the relationship between the availability of Ayush medicines and their usage, states were categorized based on population coverage per dispensary and per registered practitioner, alongside usage levels relative to the national average of 496 users per 1000 population. The heatmaps (Fig. 1) reveal that higher dispensary availability—indicated by lower population coverage—does not necessarily lead to higher usage, suggesting a complex interplay among access, awareness, and user preferences. For instance, states like Chhattisgarh and Nagaland record higher-than-average usage despite low dispensary density, while Punjab and Telangana display low usage even with similar levels of availability. Larger states such as Bihar, Gujarat, and Tamil Nadu also show high usage despite significant population pressure per dispensary, whereas Maharashtra and Uttar Pradesh exhibit low usage, likely due to supply-side bottlenecks. Practitioner availability shows similar inconsistencies (Fig. 2). Kerala, with a high concentration of registered Ayush practitioners, reports usage above the national average, while Maharashtra, despite comparable practitioner density, reports low usage. Among states with moderate practitioner coverage (1000–5000 population per practitioner), usage varies widely: Bihar, Gujarat, and Rajasthan show high uptake, but Uttar Pradesh and West Bengal do not. In states with 5000–10,000 people per practitioner, Chandigarh shows higher usage, whereas Andhra Pradesh, Meghalaya, and Tripura remain below average. Notably, Nagaland records high usage despite very low practitioner density (>10,000 people per practitioner), suggesting strong community reliance on traditional systems. Conversely, states like Assam and Jharkhand, with low practitioner availability and weaker awareness, continue to show low usage.

Discussion

Principal findings

This study provides the first national-level descriptive analysis of Ayush awareness, utilization, infrastructure availability, human resource distribution, and OOPE using the NSS 79th Round Survey and other government reports. Awareness of Ayush systems is nearly universal across India, yet only about half the population reports actual usage, revealing a persistent awareness-usage divide. Considerable state-level variability exists in the availability of Ayush dispensaries and registered practitioners, with 24 States/UTs showing low to very low infrastructure coverage and several Northeastern States reporting no registered practitioners. Usage patterns do not always correspond with infrastructure or practitioner density. Similarly, OOPE varies widely, with one-fifth of States/UTs reporting expenditures above the national average of ₹523 per person per year.

The findings of this study reveal a complex and often non-linear relationship between Ayush awareness, availability, utilization, and financial burden across Indian states. Despite near-universal awareness, actual usage remains around 50%. The analysis shows that higher dispensary density or greater practitioner availability does not consistently translate into higher usage: states such as Chhattisgarh and Nagaland demonstrate high usage despite low infrastructure coverage, while better-served states like Punjab and Telangana report low uptake, reflecting varying demand-side dynamics. Likewise, Kerala, with one of the best practitioner-to-population ratios, exhibits high usage, yet Maharashtra, despite similarly high practitioner availability, shows lower usage. The patterns of OOPE further reinforce this complexity. Some states with robust infrastructure, such as Goa and Kerala, report high OOPE, whereas others like Gujarat and Tamil Nadu maintain low OOPE despite limited infrastructure. The four OOPE usage patterns identified across states (low–low, low–high, high–low, and high–high) illustrate that financial burden and utilization are shaped not only by availability but also by system efficiency, reliance on private providers, and user behaviour.

While infrastructure and practitioner density are important factors, Ayush usage is also influenced by several other factors such as quality of services, availability of drugs, cultural acceptance, institutional support, integration with mainstream public health systems, and perception about safety, and efficacy of drugs [3,[7], [8], [9], [10]].

Overall, the interpretation suggests that accessibility, affordability, and cultural acceptability interact in diverse ways across regions, underscoring the need for both system strengthening and demand-side interventions to enhance equitable utilization of Ayush services.

Alignment with national and global policy frameworks

The findings of this study closely align with national priorities and global policy directions for traditional medicine, while highlighting certain structural challenges that remain crucial to strengthening the Ayush sector. The WHO Traditional Medicine Strategy 2014–2023 emphasized improving governance, enhancing safety and quality, and integrating traditional medicine into national health systems to advance universal health coverage [11]. India's experience reflects strengthening in these areas due to wide disparities in Ayush infrastructure, uneven practitioner availability, and variable financial protection, all of which mirror the global concerns outlined by WHO. These patterns are clearly reflected in Fig. 3, which illustrates the heterogeneity in infrastructure and human resource availability across states grouped by usage levels. Among the 20 States/UTs with above-national-average usage, 45% possess moderate to high infrastructure, 60% have moderate to high practitioner availability, yet 30% have no registered practitioners at all. In contrast, among the 16 States/UTs with below-average usage, only 18.8% have moderate to high infrastructure, 56% have moderate to high practitioner availability, and 12.5% report no practitioners. Notably, in 30% of high-usage states that lack practitioners, two-thirds (66.7%) have moderate to high infrastructure, suggesting that physical access to facilities may exert a stronger influence on utilization than practitioner availability alone (Fig. 3). It is also observed from the National Health Profile-2023 that states lacking registered practitioners also generally lack Ayush educational institutions, except Puducherry.

The currentWHO Traditional Medicine Strategy 2025–2034 [12] further calls for digital health integration, people-centred service delivery, stronger evidence ecosystems, and enhanced workforce competencies. The integration of digital health tools, notably the Ayush Health Management Information System (HMIS) and telemedicine platforms, alongside the inclusion of Ayush therapies within public insurance schemes, will help bridge access gaps and reduce financial burdens, especially in underserved regions.​ The Government of India launched the National Ayush Mission (NAM) in September 2014 to provide better access to Ayush services. The mission has laid foundational policy frameworks and driven measurable progress. For instance, the number of beneficiaries availing services under Ayushman Arogya Mandirs-Ayush (launched under NAM in 2020) has increased exponentially from 1.5 crore in 2021 to 8.9 crore in 2023 [13].

However, there are certain bottlenecks due to varying state-level implementation capacities, budget allocation, and insufficient granularity in data reporting, which obscure rural-urban and regional inequalities. Furthermore, the acute shortage of registered Ayush practitioners in many Northeastern states and Union Territories stems from the absence of local educational institutions and training infrastructure, an issue that requires urgent alignment with national education policies such as the National Education Policy 2020.

Future directions

To substantially enhance the uptake and effectiveness of Ayush services across India, a comprehensive, multi-layered approach is imperative. The following strategic measures are suggested:

1.Address Infrastructure Disparities and HR availability: The recently released IPHS norms for Ayush provide a comprehensive framework for standardizing Ayush infrastructure, human resources, and service delivery. States and UTs should undertake a systematic gap analysis to assess the current availability of infrastructure, workforce, and other essential resources against these benchmarks. Based on the findings, they should set short, medium, and long-term targets to progressively bridge these gaps and ensure equitable access to quality Ayush services.

States and UTs should undertake a systematic gap analysis to assess the current availability of infrastructure, workforce, and other essential resources against these benchmarks. Based on the findings, they should set short, medium, and long-term targets to progressively bridge these gaps and ensure equitable access to quality Ayush services.

2.Focus on Service Quality: In addition to expanding infrastructure, States and Union Territories must prioritize the quality of Ayush service delivery through existing public health facilities. This entails ensuring the consistent availability of quality medicines and diagnostics, strengthening supply-chain mechanisms, ensuring adoption of well-defined SoPs and clinical governance, developing robust patient record and information systems, and ensuring human resource availability in line with Ayush IPHS guidelines. These measures are critical for enhancing patient trust, ensuring continuity of care, and facilitating the effective integration of Ayush services within the broader public health system.

Limitations

This study uses descriptive cross-tabulations and does not employ multivariate statistical modelling; hence the findings indicate associations, not causality. NSS data are self-reported and subject to recall and reporting bias. State-level aggregation masks intra-state variation, and no district-level or socioeconomic stratification was possible. Quality, functionality, and service readiness of Ayush facilities remain unassessed. Further, use of proxy indicators such as population per dispensary and practitioner represent supply-side capacity and do not capture variations in service readiness, or practitioner engagement.

Conclusion

These findings demonstrate that while awareness of Ayush systems is nearly universal, actual usage remains modest and uneven across states. Availability of dispensaries and practitioners influences usage and OOPE but does not independently determine them; factors such as quality of services, cultural norms, trust, and service quality play equally important roles. Strengthening infrastructure and human resources in line with IPHS norms, improving quality of care, expanding digital health platforms, and integrating Ayush within public financing frameworks are essential for enhancing accessibility and reducing OOPE. Addressing both supply and demand-side barriers will be crucial for positioning Ayush as an effective contributor to India's path toward universal health coverage.
