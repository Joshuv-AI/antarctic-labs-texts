Development and validation of a deep learning model for radiographic classification of pediatric femoral neck fractures

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe Delbet–Colonna (DC) classification guides treatment of pediatric femoral neck fractures (PFNFs) but relies on clinical experience. No deep learning (DL) model has been developed and validated to differentiate between PFNFs and proximal femoral growth plates (PFGPs) and classify PFNFs via DC classification, in order to overcome this limitation.Materials and methodsX-ray data including the annotations of 5555 PFGPs, 1306 PFNFs with various DC types, and 257 pediatric subtrochanteric femoral fractures (PSFFs), were prepared to construct a DL model based on the you-only-look-once (YOLO) model with wavelet transform (WT) and attention mechanism (AM) architectures. Two senior-level pediatric orthopedic surgeons (POS) performed the annotations independently by referring to the postoperative X-rays. The annotations were finalized if there were no differences. Otherwise, the two POS discussed and determined the final annotation. Thirty-one POS with different experience assessed the external testing dataset twice, without (first) and with (second) YOLO-WTAM model assistance. The rating performances of the YOLO-WTAM model and POS were evaluated. The kappa value reflecting reliability was obtained using a Fleiss kappa analysis.ResultsAccording to the internal testing dataset, the area under the curve for different annotations ranged from 0.94 to 0.99. According to the external testing dataset, in the second round, the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV) were greater than those in the first (P < 0.001): 79.17–87.16%, 83.30–87.47%, 94.68–96.57%, 74.62–79.44%, and 92.97–95.55%, respectively. Senior-level POS exhibited superior accuracy (P = 0.021), sensitivity (P = 0.013), specificity (P = 0.039), PPV (P = 0.004), and NPV (P = 0.025) in the first round but not in the second. The kappa value improved among residents (+27.36%), junior-level (+17.03%), mid-level (+26.66%), and senior-level (+17.07%) POS.ConclusionsThe YOLO-WTAM model can accurately differentiate between PFNFs and PFGPs and classify different DC types of PFNFs. This improves POSs’ rating performance and reduces the need for experience in classifying PFNFs.Level of evidenceLevel III.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s10195-026-00953-3.

Introduction

Pediatric femoral neck fractures (PFNFs) are uncommon, accounting for 0.3–0.5% of pediatric fractures [1, 2]. If not diagnosed or managed appropriately, they can lead to serious complications, such as avascular necrosis of the femoral head in 30% of cases [1, 2]. PFNFs are categorized using the Delbet–Colonna (DC) classification, which is used to guide treatment and evaluate prognosis [2–4].

Published report and previous research (Supplementary Material 1) have shown that the DC classification depends on clinical experience [5], and given the rarity of PFNFs, errors in assessment are relatively frequent, leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment [6, 7]. Innovative solutions are needed to accurately and reliably classify PFNFs without relying solely on experience [5].

Deep learning (DL) technology is excellent for the automated, accurate assessment of pediatric orthopedic radiographs [8–12]. However, previous studies have primarily focused on binary fracture detection [11–18]. Few have evaluated DL technology’s performance in classifying multiple fracture types owing to the complexity of multiclass detection [11–18]. Additionally, the performance of DL technology is correlated with the size of the training dataset [19–21], yet no previous studies have systematically explored DL-based DC classification of PFNFs owing to the rarity of PFNFs [11, 12].

These injuries occur adjacent to the proximal femoral growth plates (PFGPs), which exhibit radiological features similar to fracture lines. Therefore, DL models must be able to discern the differences between PFGPs and PFNFs to prevent misclassification of PFGPs as fracture lines [4, 22, 23].

Previous reports have demonstrated that the YOLO model, wavelet transform (WT), and attention mechanism (AM) have enhanced capabilities for extracting fine-grained image features and discriminating subtle intersubtype differences [24–32]. Therefore, we hypothesized that a hybrid YOLO-WTAM architecture, an unreported DL model, could accurately identify DC types of PFNFs.

The aim of this multicenter study was to develop a YOLO-WTAM-based DL model that can automatically and accurately perform DC typing of PFNFs to eliminate dependency on clinical experience. Furthermore, the clinical efficacy of YOLO-WTAM model was evaluated using an external testing dataset.

Materials and methods

After receiving approval from the Institutional Review Board (approval no. 2025228), we conducted a retrospective review of the clinical records and anteroposterior or lateral pelvis X-rays of 1149 patients with PFNFs (30.1% of the total patient population, with 1598 preoperative X-rays) and 137 patients with pediatric subtrochanteric femoral fractures (PSFFs) (3.6% of the total patient population, with 271 preoperative X-rays). These patients were treated surgically in 14 institutions. Additionally, we reviewed the records of 2536 children with normal hip joints (NHJs) and PFGPs (66.4% of the total patient population, with 4731 X-rays) in these institutions.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients with PFNFs and PSFFs ≤ 17 years old at the time of injury, (2) complete pre- and postoperative X-rays, and (3) complete clinical and radiographic data.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a diagnosis of slipped capital femoral epiphysis (SCFE) or pathological fracture, (2) concomitant metabolic bone diseases, syndromes, or neuromuscular diseases, (3) X-rays taken with nonstandard projections or of poor quality (low resolution or hip joints masked by casts or braces), and (4) immediate postoperative X-rays of patients with PFNFs and PSFFs, in which it is difficult to identify the locations of the fracture lines.

A total of 97 participants (2.5%) were excluded for the following reasons: a diagnosis of SCFE (n = 7; 0.19%), pathological fractures (n = 12; 0.32%), X-rays in nonstandard projections (n = 31; 0.83%), or poor-quality images (n = 47; 1.3%).

PFNF types

The DC classification [4] classifies PFNFs into four types according to fracture line location: transepiphyseal (type I), transcervical (type II), cervicotrochanteric (type III), and intertrochanteric (type IV).

A previous study has validated the use of expert consensus on hip radiographs [33]. On the basis of this study [33], two POS (F.C. and A.A.) with over 20 years of experience classified PFNFs independently. They referred to the postoperative X-rays of PFNFs that were treated surgically to confirm the location of the fracture lines before determining the DC classification. This was especially important for severely displaced PFNFs as the locations of the fracture lines were difficult to identify.

Two POS consistently identified the DC subtypes of 1459 (97.5%) preoperative PFNFs X-rays, classifying 49 (3.3%) as type I, 742 (49.6%) as type II, 505 (33.8%) as type III, and 163 (10.9%) as type IV. The remaining 37 X-rays (2.5%) could not be classified owing to inconsistent results between the two POS. This includes 20 X-rays (1.3%) with fracture lines passing through both the transcervical and cervicotrochanteric regions of the femoral neck and 17 X-rays (1.1%) with fracture lines passing through both the cervicotrochanteric and intertrochanteric regions of the femoral neck.

Datasets

The preoperative X-rays of patients with PFNFs, PSFFs, and children with NHJs and PFGPs, collected from 14 institutions, were used to build the dataset for DL model construction and the external testing dataset. In particular, the dataset for DL model construction was collected from 13 institutions (92.86%), and the external testing dataset was collected from another 1 institution (7.14%). The detailed constitutions of the two datasets are as follows:

Dataset for DL model construction

The dataset consisted of 6331 X-rays (97.6%) of patients with PFNFs and identified DC types, including type I (41 X-rays, 0.63%), type II (645 X-rays, 9.94%), type III (476 X-rays, 7.34%), and type IV (144 X-rays, 2.22%); 37 X-rays (0.57%) of PFNFs with unidentified DC types; 257 X-rays (3.96%) of PSFFs; and 4731 X-rays (72.92%) of children with NHJs and visible PFGPs.

External testing dataset

The external testing dataset consisted of 157 preoperative X-rays (2.4%) of patients with PFNFs: 8 (0.12%) with type I fractures, 97 (1.5%) with type II, 29 (0.45%) with type III, and 19 (0.29%) with type IV. Additionally, 4 (0.06%) X-rays were obtained from patients with PSFFs.

X-ray cropping

The X-rays of the two aforementioned datasets were cropped manually to include the superior margin of acetabulum (superiorly), femur (inferiorly), skin (laterally), and medial margin of acetabulum (medially). Non-relevant regions including patients’ demographic information were excluded.

Annotation of the X-ray dataset for DL model construction

After the X-rays were cropped, the regions of interest (ROIs) were annotated with bounded boxes using the MakeSense online image annotation tool (https://www.makesense.ai/); this included annotating fracture lines and PFGPs in different X-rays. One POS (F.C.) annotated the X-rays, and then a second POS (A.A.) checked their work. If there were no differences, the annotation was finalized. Otherwise, the two POS discussed the case and determined the final annotation together. If they could not reach an agreement, the cases were excluded.

If PFGPs were visible on X-rays of patients with PFNFs or PSFFs, then the PFGP and fracture line were annotated separately in per X-ray. The detailed annotation targets, methods, and locations are shown below:

Fracture line annotation

The proximal and distal fracture lines were annotated within one bounded box without covering the PFGPs.

PFGP annotation

PFGPs were annotated within a single box without covering the fracture lines.

Finally, fracture line annotations were conducted on 1306 preoperative PFNF X-rays, including 41 (3.13%) type I, 645 (49.39%) type II, 476 (36.45%) type III, and 144 (11.03%) type IV fractures, as well as on 257 PSFF X-rays. Additionally, a total of 5555 PFGPs were annotated in the X-ray dataset.

DL model construction

Annotation division of the dataset for DL model construction

A total of 1563 fracture line annotations (21.96%) and 5555 PFGP (78.04%) annotations from the dataset for DL model construction were randomly split into training (4979 annotations; 69.95%) and internal testing (2139 annotations; 30.05%) datasets (7:3 ratio) at the patient level. Specifically, the training dataset included 28 type I (0.56%), 451 type II (9.06%), 333 type III (6.69%), and 100 type IV (2.01%) PFNF fracture line annotations, as well as 179 PSFF fracture line annotations. Furthermore, the training dataset included 3888 PFGP (78.09%) annotations.

Additionally, the internal testing dataset contained 13 type I (0.61%), 194 type II (9.07%), 143 type III (6.69%), and 44 type IV (2.06%) PFNF fracture line annotations, as well as 78 PSFF (3.65%) fracture line annotations. Moreover, it contained 1667 PFGP (77.93%) annotations.

DL model

The YOLO11 model and the architecture of WT and AM (YOLO-WTAM model) were integrated to construct the DL model in the current study. By employing discrete WT, the method extracted texture features across various orientations. This process enhanced subtle fracture cues within high-frequency subbands as well as cortical discontinuities. Additionally, a residual channel-spatial dual AM in the neck section adaptively emphasized pathological features while suppressing noise (Fig. 2).Fig. 2YOLO-WTAM model construction flowchart

YOLO-WTAM model training and performance evaluation

The image sizes of the training and internal testing datasets were adjusted to 640 × 640 pixels before constructing the YOLO-WTAM model. The model was then trained using the training dataset. The initial learning rate was set to 0.01, and 300 training epochs were implemented using a stochastic gradient descent optimizer with a batch size of 32 and an NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 GPU (NVIDIA, USA). The model was constructed using a cross-entropy loss function.

Finally, the performance of the YOLO-WTAM model was evaluated using the internal testing dataset. Specifically, the differences between the bounding boxes reflecting PFNFs types, PSFF fracture line locations, or PFGPs as generated by the model and those annotated manually were compared using the following metrics: intersection over union (IoU), mean average precision (mAP) at an IoU of 50% (mAP@50), mAP at an IoU ranging from 50 to 95% (mAP@50–95), precision, and recall. Larger IoU, mAP@50, mAP@50–95, precision, and recall values indicate the superior performance of the model. Additionally, receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis was performed to calculate the area under the curve (AUC) for different annotations, reflecting the sensitivity and specificity of the model.

Comparison between the YOLO-WTAM model and POS assessment performance of the external testing dataset

First round

Thirty-one POS who were blinded to the fracture line locations (PFNFs or PSFFs) and the detailed DC types of PFNFs in the external testing dataset were asked to review the dataset with randomly distributed X-rays and record the corresponding fracture line locations (PFNFs or PSFFs) and the detailed DC types of PFNFs independently. The POS included residents (n = 10; 32.3%), junior-level (n = 6; 19.4%) with < 5 years of experience, mid-level (n = 7; 22.6%) with 5–10 years of experience, and senior-level (n = 8; 25.8%) with > 10 years of experience.

Second round with YOLO-WTAM model assistance

One month after the first round, the 31 POS were asked to review the dataset with newly randomly distributed X-rays. They were instructed to provide detailed descriptions of the fracture line locations (PFNFs or PSFFs) and the DC types (PFNFs) independently. These raters referred to the results generated by the YOLO-WTAM model when evaluating each X-ray included in the external testing dataset before determining their assessment outcomes.

In particular, the DC classification descriptions in the forms of drawings and radiographs were sent to the raters 1 week before they reviewed the dataset in each round so that they could become familiar with it.

Evaluation of the radiographic assessment performance

The YOLO-WTAM model and the two rounds of radiographic assessment performed by 31 POS were evaluated according to the metrics reported in similar studies involving the construction of a DL model [33–36], including the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value (PPV), and negative predictive value (NPV). Higher values of these metrics indicated better radiographic assessment performance. Reliability among POS with different levels of experience was also evaluated on the basis of the outcomes.

Statistical analysis

Data are expressed as numerical variables, frequencies, percentages, and means with standard deviations. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 26 (SPSS, Chicago, IL, USA).

One-sample t-tests were performed to compare the accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV of the YOLO-WTAM model with those of residents and junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS following each round of radiographic evaluation.

Paired-sample t-tests were used to compare the differences in radiographic assessment performance between the first and second rounds among residents and junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS for accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV.

One-way ANOVA was used to assess the differences in accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV among residents and junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS following each round of radiographic rating.

Fleiss kappa analysis was performed to calculate the kappa value (poor: 0.0–0.2; fair: 0.21–0.4; moderate: 0.41–0.6; substantial: 0.61–0.8; excellent: 0.81–1.0) to evaluate the reliability of the radiographic rating performances among residents and junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS [37].

The level of statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Second round with the YOLO-WTAM model assistance

With the assistance of the YOLO-WTAM model, one-sample t-tests revealed no significant differences in accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, and NPV between the YOLO-WTAM model and residents, junior-level POS, mid-level POS, or senior-level POS (Fig. 5). The average percentage of the consistency of their answers with the answers provided by the YOLO-WTAM model among residents, junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS was 86.4%, 85.2%, 88.8% and 85.4%, respectively. Notably, the YOLO-WTAM model’s specificity was significantly greater than that of residents, while mid-level and senior-level POS had superior PPV performance (Fig. 5).

Improvements in POSs’ rating performances

The results of the paired-samples t-tests indicated that, on average, the accuracy (79.17–87.16%, t = 8.032, p < 0.001), sensitivity (83.30–87.47%, t = 4.884, p < 0.001), specificity (94.68–96.57%, t = 7.595, p < 0.001), PPV (74.62–79.44%, t = 5.486, p < 0.001), and NPV (92.97–95.55%, t = 9.417, p < 0.001) of POS with different levels of experience were significantly superior to those obtained in the first round. Figure 5 shows the detailed rating performance improvements among residents, junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS regarding accuracy, specificity, PPV, and NPV with the assistance of the YOLO-WTAM model.

Discussion

Accurate DC classification of PFNFs is essential for optimizing treatment strategies and prognosis assessments [2, 3]. However, the accuracy of the DC classification depends on clinical experience, and many specialists cannot classify PFNFs into their respective subtypes quickly and accurately owing to the rarity of the injury and a lack of experience [5–7, 38]. In this context, we developed and validated a YOLO-WTAM model to automatically and accurately classify PFNFs according to the DC system on X-rays, thereby improving classification performance of PFNFs and minimizing reliance on clinical experience.

Studies have shown that PFGPs can be misdiagnosed as fracture lines owing to similar radiographic characteristics [22, 23, 39]. Our results show that the YOLO-WTAM model can distinguish between PFGPs and PFNFs; this may be due to the adequate sample size of training dataset and the architecture of the model. Reports show that larger sample sizes and model adaptability contribute to DL model performance [19–21]. In our study, annotations of 5555 PFGPs and 1563 fracture lines were prepared by experienced POS to construct the YOLO-WTAM model. Furthermore, previous studies have shown that the YOLO model, WT, and AM architecture enhance the ability to capture and distinguish fine-grained imaging features [24–32].

The YOLO-WTAM model performed better than residents and junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS in the first round, possibly because it is difficult to accurately classify DC subtypes on preoperative PFNF X-rays, where the fracture line may be difficult to locate, especially in cases of severe rotated or angulated displacement [4, 40, 41]. However, fracture line locations are clearer on postoperative X-rays [2, 40, 41]. In our study, two experienced POS evaluated the DC subtypes of the dataset for YOLO-WTAM model construction on postoperative PFNF X-rays. Therefore, the performance of the YOLO-WTAM model in classifying PFNFs according to the DC classification on preoperative X-rays was significantly superior to that of POS.

Type I PFNFs are rare, and only 28 X-rays were included in the training dataset. However, the YOLO-WTAM model demonstrated high sensitivity and specificity in detecting type I PFNFs, with an AUC of 0.94. The model’s performance may be the reason. Integration with WT captures convolutional imaging features, whereas AM focuses on learning imaging features [24–32]. Nevertheless, the accuracy of the YOLO-WTAM model in detecting type I PFNFs was 66.7% owing to the small samples. Despite this limitation, all the raters achieved 100% accuracy of type I PFNFs diagnosis according to the two-round radiographic rating performance of the external testing dataset (Supplementary Material 2), except for 1.2% of type I PFNFs being misdiagnosed by residents in the first round. These findings demonstrate that type I PFNFs can be easily diagnosed and that the YOLO-WTAM model improves residents’ performance, although its accuracy still needs improvement.

Notably, the classification performance of residents, junior-level, mid-level, and senior-level POS in the second round was significantly superior to that in the first. This finding may have been due to diagnostic subjectivity and uncertainty among POS in the ratings caused by the aforementioned difficulties, which limited the accurate classification of PFNFs according to the DC classification system [40, 41]. Furthermore, the YOLO-WTAM model was trained on the basis of the training dataset with PFNF types accurately and objectively evaluated by two experienced POS. Therefore, the answers provided by the YOLO-WTAM model were more likely to be considered as standard references when the POS classified the PFNFs using the DC system.

The YOLO-WTAM model was not trusted by all POS for classifying fractures according to the DC classification. Instead, they relied on independent thinking and critical evaluation, resulting in different rating performance values. In our study, the DC subtype rating performance of residents was significantly inferior to that of the YOLO-WTAM model in the second round. Moreover, compared with the YOLO-WTAM model, mid-level and senior-level POS exhibited significantly improved performance in terms of PPV. These findings further emphasize the importance of clinical experience in accurately classifying DC subtypes; then, we constructed the YOLO-WTAM model on the basis of a training dataset prepared by experienced POS.

This study has several limitations. Firstly, the fracture lines of PFNFs and PSFFs were not further confirmed according to computed tomography data. Secondly, we only evaluated the clinical efficacy of the YOLO-WTAM model among 31 POS with different levels of experience. Nevertheless, the 31 POS raters we selected were rationally divided and represent the largest total number compared with previous studies involving DL models for evaluating pediatric orthopedic images [11, 12]. Thirdly, the training dataset had a small sample size of type I PFNFs owing to the low incidence of this injury. Nevertheless, the YOLO-WTAM model exhibited exceptional sensitivity and specificity in identifying type I PFNFs (AUC: 0.94). Fourthly, the YOLO-WTAM model’s PFNF typing performance according to the DC classification was not compared with those of other models. Despite these limitations, to the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in which a multiclass classification DL model was developed to accurately identify the DC classification of PFNFs on the basis of a training dataset collected from multiple institutions [11, 12, 42–45]. In the future, our DL model, as well as those developed by others, should be evaluated among raters with various specialties from multiple institutions using larger sample sizes.

In conclusion, our YOLO-WTAM model can accurately differentiate between PFGPs and PFNFs and classify fractures according to the DC classification. Additionally, our model improves the DC rating performances of POS with various levels of experience, thus minimizing reliance on experience for accurate classification of PFNFs.
