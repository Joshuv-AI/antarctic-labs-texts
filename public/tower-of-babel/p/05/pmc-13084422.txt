Biowaste to Bioactive Hydroxyapatite–Organic Hybrid Coatings with ECM-Like Topography and Mechanical Integrity for Multifunctional Titanium Implant Coatings
Authors: Gopi Srinivasan, Anushiya Manickam, Jeevadharani Murugan, Sivasakthi Sivakumar, Surendiran Mohan, Wan Mohd Norsani Bin Wan Nik
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of multifunctional coatings for orthopedic and dental implants remains critical to achieving long-term corrosion resistance, osteoconductivity, and antibacterial performance. Hydroxyapatite (HAP), although bioactive, often exhibits brittleness and limited functional integration when used as a standalone coating. Herein, HAP derived from the caprine femur bone was sequentially modified with 5-amino 4-imidazolecarboxamide hydrochloride (AICA), sodium alginate (SA), and polyacrylonitrile (PAN) to form composite coatings (HAP, m-HAP-1, m-HAP-2, and m-HAP-3), which were deposited onto plasma-treated titanium substrates via electrophoretic deposition. The optimized m-HAP-3 coating exhibited reduced crystallite size with partial amorphization, an ECM-like surface morphology, balanced wettability, and improved mechanical integrity. Thermal analysis confirmed the mineral stability with controlled organic degradation. Electrochemical studies revealed an enhanced corrosion resistance and favorable charge transfer behavior. Additionally, m-HAP-3 demonstrated dose-dependent antibacterial activity against Pseudomonas aeruginosa. These results indicate that m-HAP-3 is a stable, bioactive, and antibacterial coating suitable for orthopedic and dental implant applications.

Introduction

Biomedical implants are integral to modern healthcare, providing durable and biocompatible solutions for restoring physiological function and improving patient quality of life. , Titanium (Ti) and its alloys are widely regarded as gold-standard implant materials owing to their excellent mechanical strength, corrosion resistance, and biocompatibility. − However, the bioinert nature of Ti surfaces limits direct bone bonding, often resulting in delayed or insufficient osseointegration, which can compromise long-term implant stability. Consequently, surface modification strategies are essential to enhance the biological performance of titanium-based implants. Hydroxyapatite (HAP, Ca10(PO4)6(OH)2), a bioactive calcium phosphate ceramic closely resembling the mineral phase of natural bone, is extensively employed as a coating material to improve osseointegration. − HAP exhibits excellent bioactivity, osteoconductivity, nontoxicity, and nonimmunogenicity. − Although synthetic HAP is produced via chemical precipitation, hydrothermal treatment, sol–gel techniques, mechanochemical synthesis, microemulsion, and ultrasonic irradiation, these methods are often costly and environmentally burdensome. − As a sustainable alternative, HAP is derived from natural sources such as mammalian bones (e.g., bovine, caprine, camel,), marine sources (e.g., fish scales and bones), shell sources (e.g., eggshells, seashells), and minerals like limestone. Among these, caprine-bone-derived HAP is particularly attractive due to its high purity, optimal Ca/P ratio (∼1.67), favorable porosity, and energy-efficient extraction process. , Moreover, its utilization contributes to waste valorization and environmental sustainability.

Despite these advantages, conventional HAP coatings suffer from inherent brittleness, low fracture toughness, and weak adhesion to titanium substrates. Thermal expansion mismatch between HAP and Ti can induce residual stresses, leading to microcracking, delamination, and coating failure under physiological loading conditions. − In addition, HAP lacks intrinsic antibacterial activity, increasing susceptibility to bacterial colonization and peri-implant infection. To address these limitations, composite coatings incorporating bioactive and mechanically reinforcing components have emerged as an effective strategy. In this context, 5-amino-4-imidazolecarboxamide hydrochloride (AICA), sodium alginate (SA), and polyacrylonitrile (PAN) offer complementary functionalities. AICA provides antibacterial activity and enhances corrosion resistance by forming a protective interfacial layer. SA, a naturally derived anionic polysaccharide, improves hydrophilicity and biocompatibility while promoting cell adhesion and osteogenic response. , PAN contributes high mechanical strength and toughness, mitigating the brittleness of HAP and improving coating adhesion to titanium substrates. Unlike inorganic dopants, these organic constituents enhance the multifunctionality without compromising cytocompatibility. Furthermore, plasma surface activation combined with electrophoretic deposition (EPD) enables the fabrication of uniform, adherent, and bioactive composite coatings. Additionally, implant surfaces in clinical settings may be exposed to drug-rich physiological environments, particularly in patients receiving calcium supplements or antidiabetic medications. Evaluating the coating stability under such conditions is therefore important for assessing long-term implant performance. The synergistic integration of caprine-derived HAP with AICA, SA, and PAN yields a multifunctional coating that addresses the mechanical, biological, and antimicrobial limitations of conventional HAP systems, offering a clinically relevant approach to improving titanium implant durability, osteoconductivity, corrosion resistance, and infection prevention.

Materials and Methods

Materials Procurement and Preparation

Caprine (goat) bones were sourced from a local abattoir and used as the primary calcium source for HAP synthesis. Analytical-grade chemicals and reagents were used throughout the study. AICA, PAN, and SA were procured from Sigma-Aldrich and BLD Pharma, India, with a purity of >99%. Titanium (Ti) metal substrates were procured from Raghavendra Engineering, Chennai. All solvents and deionized (DI) water used in the synthesis and post-treatment processes were of analytical grade and used without further purification.

Synthesis of HAP from the Caprine Femur Bone

Biogenic HAP was prepared from caprine femur bones through a multistep process. The bones were first thoroughly cleaned with double-distilled water to remove residual soft tissue, cut into fragments, and defatted by stirring in acetone for 60 min to remove lipids. The defatted fragments were then treated with 2% acetic acid for 60 min to remove collagen and other nonmineral proteins, followed by repeated washing with double-distilled water until neutral pH was achieved, yielding 7.102 g of fine crushed bone. The cleaned bone powder was calcined in a programmable high-temperature furnace under an inert nitrogen atmosphere, with the temperature increased at 5 °C min–1 to a final range of 600–900 °C and held for 2 h to ensure complete decomposition of organic matter and conversion to HAP. After controlled cooling, 4.614 g of HAP was obtained, corresponding to a 64.9% yield from the fine crushed bone, while the overall yield from the raw bone to HAP was approximately 41%.

Synthesis of the HAP-Based Biocomposite

AICA functionalized solution was prepared by dissolving 0.2 g of AICA in 10 mL of deionized water (20 mg mL–1), and the pH was adjusted to 7–8. The solution was stirred for 30 min at room temperature and sonicated in a bath for 10 min to ensure a complete molecular dispersion. For the polymer matrix, 0.2 g of PAN was dissolved in 10 mL of DMSO to form a viscous solution. Separately, 0.4 g of sodium alginate (SA) was dissolved in 10 mL of deionized water (40 mg mL–1, pH 6–7). The SA solution was gradually added to the PAN solution at a 2:1 SA/PAN weight ratio under constant stirring at 500 rpm. Ionic cross-linking was achieved by adding 5 mL of 2% CaCl2 solution (0.2 g in 10 mL of water, final Ca2+ concentration: 0.01 M), followed by aging for 1 h to allow gel network formation. Thermally processed HAP (0.4 g, 40 mg mL–1) was incorporated into the polymer blend, followed by mechanical stirring at 600 rpm and 15 min of bath sonication to ensure a uniform dispersion. The AICA solution was added dropwise to the HAP/PAN-SA mixture to obtain a final HAP/AICA/SA/PAN weight ratio of 2:1:2:1. The resulting mixture was stirred for 8 h and aged overnight at room temperature. Drying was performed in an air oven at 55 °C for 48 h, yielding the final composite (m-HAP-3). The same procedure was applied to prepare m-HAP-1 and m-HAP-2, adjusting the component ratios according to their respective formulations.

Preparation and Coating of Titanium Implant Substrates

Titanium implant substrates (10 × 10 × 3 mm3) were embedded in Araldite, leaving a 1 cm2 exposed surface for electrodeposition. Surface preparation involved mechanical polishing with progressively finer silicon carbide (SiC) papers (600 grit to 2000 grit) to achieve a smooth finish. The polished specimens were then ultrasonically cleaned in acetone for 30 min to remove surface contaminants and improve coating adhesion. Surface activation was performed using atmospheric-pressure, low-temperature nitrogen plasma treatment at 0.2 MPa for 300 s, generating surface oxide layers and increasing surface energy to enhance coating adherence. The synthesized materials, including HAP, m-HAP-1, m-HAP-2, and m-HAP-3, were dispersed in deionized water to prepare stable suspensions and subjected to electrophoretic deposition (EPD). Deposition parameters such as suspension concentration, applied voltage, electrode separation, and time were systematically optimized to achieve thin and uniform coatings on the plasma-treated titanium substrates. During EPD, the titanium substrate served as the anode, while a platinum electrode functioned as the cathode with a 10 mm separation between electrodes. Deposition was performed at an applied voltage of 4 V for 2 h. After deposition, the coated substrates were air-dried and thermally cured at 55 °C for 48 h to stabilize the composite layer. The resulting coatings were then subjected to structural, morphological, mechanical, electrochemical, and application-based evaluations for dental implants.

Characterization Techniques

XRD

The crystalline phases of hydroxyapatite and the corresponding composite powders were identified by X-ray diffraction using a PANalytical diffractometer equipped with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5418 Å). Measurements were carried out at an operating voltage of 40 kV and a current of 40 mA. Diffraction patterns were collected over a 2θ range of 10–60° with an angular increment of 0.02°.

FT-IR and UV–Vis Spectroscopy

Infrared spectra were recorded to examine functional groups and molecular interactions using an ATR-FTIR spectrometer over the range 400–4000 cm–1. All samples were analyzed in the dried powder form. Optical absorption behavior was investigated by UV–visible spectroscopy between 200 and 800 nm using aqueous sample dispersions.

FESEM with EDX

Surface morphology of the coated titanium substrates was examined by using field-emission scanning electron microscopy operated at an accelerating voltage of 10 kV. Elemental composition was determined by energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy. Prior to imaging, samples were sputter-coated with a thin gold layer to suppress charging effects.

HR-TEM and SAED

The nanoscale structure and crystallinity of the composite powders were investigated by using high-resolution transmission electron microscopy. For sample preparation, powders were dispersed in ethanol by ultrasonication, and a drop of the suspension was deposited onto carbon-coated copper grids. Selected area electron diffraction patterns were recorded to confirm the crystallographic features.

Atomic Force Microscopy

Surface topography and roughness of the deposited coatings were analyzed by atomic force microscopy in the tapping mode under ambient conditions. Measurements were performed by using silicon probes over a scan area of 5 × 5 μm2.

Contact Angle

Wettability of the coatings was evaluated through static contact angle measurements using the sessile drop method. A 1 μL droplet of deionized water was placed on the sample surface, and the contact angle was recorded at room temperature.

Thermal Analysis

Thermal behavior of the composite powders was assessed by thermogravimetric analysis under a nitrogen atmosphere. Samples were heated from ambient temperature to 900 °C at a constant heating rate of 10 °C min–1.

Vickers Hardness

The mechanical integrity of the coatings was determined by using Vickers microhardness testing. Indentations were made under applied loads ranging from 50 g to 1 kg with a dwell time of 14 s. Sufficient spacing between adjacent indents was maintained to avoid overlapping deformation zones.

Preparation of Simulated Body Fluid (SBF)

Simulated body fluid was prepared according to a widely accepted biomimetic formulation to replicate the ionic composition of human blood plasma. The solution contained (in mM) Na+ 142.0, K+ 5.0, Mg2+ 1.5, Ca2+ 2.5, Cl– 147.8, HCO3 – 4.2, HPO4 2– 1.0, and SO4 2– 0.5.

For preparation, analytical-grade salts were sequentially dissolved in deionized water maintained at 36.5 and 37 °C under continuous stirring. Tris(hydroxymethyl)­aminomethane was added as a buffering agent, and the pH was adjusted to 7.40 at 37 °C using 1.0 M hydrochloric acid. The final volume was adjusted to 1 L, and the solution was stored at 4 °C and used within 1 week.

Electrochemical Analysis

Electrochemical performance was evaluated by potentiodynamic polarization, electrochemical impedance spectroscopy, and cyclic voltammetry in a SBF at 37 °C using a three-electrode cell. The coated titanium substrate acted as the working electrode, while platinum and Ag/AgCl electrodes served as the counter and reference electrodes, respectively. Potentiodynamic polarization tests were conducted from −0.25 to +0.75 V relative to the open-circuit potential at a scan rate of 1 mV s–1. Impedance measurements were performed over a frequency range of 105–10–2 Hz using a sinusoidal perturbation of 10 mV. For drug-loaded cyclic voltammetry, 3 mL of an aqueous solution containing calcium citrate and metformin was introduced into the SBF to represent a drug-enriched physiological environment, while measurements in pure SBF were used as controls. CV scans were recorded between −0.2 and +1.2 V at 50 mV s–1 for 30 successive cycles.

Antibacterial Studies

Antibacterial performance of the synthesized powders was assessed by an agar well diffusion assay against Pseudomonas aeruginosa. Bacterial cultures were grown to the mid logarithmic phase and adjusted to approximately 106 CFU mL–1 before being uniformly spread on Mueller–Hinton agar plates. Sterile wells of 6 mm diameter were filled with 50 mg of the test powders. Plates without samples and those containing standard antibiotic discs were used as negative and positive controls, respectively. After incubation at 37 °C for 24 h, antibacterial activity was quantified by measuring the diameter of the inhibition zones. All experiments were conducted in triplicate.

Results and Discussion

Evaluation of Crystallinity and Particle Size of HAP and m-HAP-3 Using TEM and XRD

X-ray diffraction measurements collected over a 2θ range of 10°–60° were used to evaluate the phase constitution and lattice characteristics of the HAP-based coatings. The diffraction pattern of pristine HAP (Figure a) indicates a biphasic structure dominated by hexagonal apatite (P63/m), which is consistent with JCPDS 96-900-1234. The appearance of weak monoclinic features (P21/c), evidenced by peak splitting near 2θ ≈ 33° and 46° (JCPDS 96-900-1264), suggests incomplete lattice relaxation. Such crystallographic deviations are typically linked to point defects and local structural disorder, which can generate internal stress fields within the apatite lattice.

XRD patterns of (a) HAP and (b) m-HAP-3 coatings.

By comparison, the m-HAP-3 composite (Figure b) exhibits a predominantly hexagonal apatite structure with no detectable monoclinic contributions. Diffraction features corresponding to carbonated hydroxyapatite (CHA) and fluorapatite (FA) agree with JCPDS 96-900-3553 and 96-900-1233, respectively. The incorporation of CO3 2– and F– ions induces subtle peak shifts and broadening, reflecting localized lattice distortion accompanied by overall strain redistribution rather than accumulation. This substitution-mediated lattice adjustment contributes to improved structural stability while limiting excessive crystallite growth. Transmission electron microscopy provides complementary nanoscale evidence supporting the XRD findings. The m-HAP-3 composite exhibits an irregular particle morphology with agglomerated domains and indistinct grain boundaries (Figure ), consistent with constrained crystal growth imposed by interfacial interactions. The corresponding selected area electron diffraction (SAED) pattern displays diffuse rings. In contrast, literature reports describe pristine HAP as consisting of discrete, spherical nanoparticles with sharp SAED rings, indicative of higher crystallinity. The close agreement between Scherrer-derived crystallite size (22.22 nm) and the TEM particle dimensions (26.7 nm) shown in Figure , together with smaller UDM-derived values (5.36 nm), highlights the role of internal strain in refining crystallite domains within the composite.

TEM and SAED images showing core–shell structured composite (m-HAP-3) particles with uniform spherical morphology.

Particle size distribution histogram of the synthesized composite (m-HAP-3), showing a Gaussian distribution with an average particle diameter (2r) of 24.21 nm based on 70 measured particles.

These structural characteristics have direct implications for biomedical performance. Crystallite refinement and controlled lattice strain increase surface heterogeneity and defect density, providing favorable sites for protein adsorption, cell attachment, and apatite nucleation in physiological environments. At the same time, strain accommodation within the nanocrystalline framework enhances the mechanical reliability by distributing applied stresses and limiting crack initiation under cyclic loading. Collectively, the XRD and TEM results demonstrate that lattice imperfection control, crystallite growth restriction, and strain modulation contribute in a mechanistically consistent manner to the functional performance of the m-HAP-3 coating in implant applications. ,

FT-IR

FT-IR analysis identified key functional groups in the HAP composite coatings, confirming molecular interactions among its constituents. As shown in Figure , HAP exhibited characteristic phosphate (PO4 3–) vibrational modes at 1086 and 1018 cm–1 (ν3 asymmetric stretching), 961 cm–1 (ν1 symmetric stretching), and 560–600 cm–1 (ν4 bending), along with a sharp OH stretching band at 3575 cm–1, indicating high crystallinity. The librational OH mode at 628 cm–1 further supported the phase purity of stoichiometric HAP. The FT-IR spectra of the HAP-based composites retained HAP’s phosphate peaks while incorporating distinct vibrational bands of AICA, PAN, and SA. The nitrile (–CN) stretching at 2247 cm–1 confirmed PAN, while the carbonyl (CO) stretching at 1604 cm–1 corresponded to AICA. Carboxylate (–COO–) absorptions were attributed to SA, with conjugated CC and CN vibrations at 1434 cm–1 indicating structural stability. Aliphatic C–H stretching at 2921 cm–1 confirmed PAN and SA contributions, while a broad absorption band centered at ∼3360 cm–1 was attributed to overlapping O–H and N–H stretching vibrations, reflecting extensive hydrogen bonding among hydroxyl, amine, and carboxylate functionalities within the composite matrix. The weak feature observed near 3730 cm–1 was associated with free or weakly hydrogen-bonded surface hydroxyl groups arising from adsorbed moisture or exposed OH species on the apatite surface. The transformation of CHA and FAP within the composite was evident from spectral modifications, including the appearance of carbonate peaks at ∼870 cm–1 (ν2 CO3 2–) and 1410–1465 cm–1 (ν3 CO3 2–), confirming B-type CHA with CO3 2– replacing PO4 3–. The PO4 3– stretching peak at 960 cm–1 shifted to a higher wavenumber, with intensified bands at 1040–1090 cm–1, indicating FAP formation. The reduced intensity of OH stretching at 3570 cm–1, along with peak narrowing, suggested lattice stabilization through CO3 2– and F– incorporation, enhancing the crystallinity and bioactivity.

Proposed Interaction Mechanism of m-HAP-3

Figure presents a schematic of the anticipated interactions between the AICA-based hydrogel network (HAP, SA, and PAN) and the TiO2 layer of titanium. The coating is stabilized through hydrogen bonding, electrostatic attraction, and chelation involving Ca2+, phosphate groups, carboxyl functionalities, nitrogen moieties, and surface oxygen species. The heterocyclic structure of AICA introduces additional coordination sites, which strengthen interfacial adhesion and contribute to the coating stability. These interactions are consistent with the morphological uniformity and compositional integration observed in SEM, EDX, and AFM, and they provide a mechanistic basis for the enhanced mechanical and electrochemical responses discussed in later sections.

UV–Vis Spectroscopy

The UV–visible absorption spectra of pristine HAP and its modified composite coatings were recorded to investigate the molecular interactions within the composite framework. The UV–vis spectrum obtained for HAP, as shown in Figure a, exhibited strong absorption bands at approximately 215 and 265 nm, which are characteristic of (PO4)3– group transitions within the HAP lattice. , Upon the incorporation of AICA (Figure b), a noticeable red shift was observed, with λmax shifting to the range of 225–275 nm. This shift is attributed to the π → π* and n → π* transitions introduced by the aromatic and heterocyclic nature of AICA, as well as its possible chelation with calcium ions in the HAP matrix. Further, the addition of SA in the composite (Figure c) led to a broader absorption band spanning approximately 240–285 nm. The slight red shift and reduced intensity compared to m-HAP-1 suggest the presence of hydrogen bonding and electrostatic interactions between the (–COO) and (–OH) groups of SA with the modified framework, which likely disrupted the extended conjugation to some extent. Interestingly, the final composite m-HAP-3 (Figure d) showed an even broader absorption feature in the range of 260–300 nm, although with the lowest absorbance intensity among all samples. This indicates a moderate red shift in the absorption maximum, likely influenced by the partial conjugation and (–CN) functionalities in PAN. The decreased intensity and spectral flattening are possibly due to the surface passivation effects and enhanced molecular dispersion introduced by PAN. The progressive red shift and spectral flattening across the composite series correlate with the XRD and TEM evidence of crystallite refinement, lattice distortion, and partial amorphization, indicating that the optical response arises from intrinsic structural modification rather than surface adsorption alone. The absence of visible-region absorption confirms optical neutrality and chemical stability, supporting the suitability of the composite coatings.

UV–vis spectra of (a) HAP, (b) m-HAP-1, (c) m-HAP-2, and (d) m-HAP-3 coatings showing the progressive changes in absorption behavior with each composite modification.

FESEM

FESEM analysis was used to examine the surface morphology of the HAP-based composite coatings. As shown in Figure c, the pristine HAP coating showed loosely packed nanorod structures with irregular shapes and a highly granular surface, typical of biogenic HAP. After adding AICA, the m-HAP-1 (Figure d) coating became denser with improved particle cohesion, indicating effective interaction between the organic molecules and the HAP surface. Incorporation of SA further modified the morphology, producing a porous, sponge-like structure in m-HAP-2 (Figure e) that can support fluid penetration and mass transport. In contrast, the (Figure f) composite m-HAP-3 exhibited a compact and uniform surface with reduced microscale porosity due to the formation of a continuous polymer network. This compaction improves the coating cohesion, mechanical stability, and adhesion to the titanium substrate. Importantly, the reduced porosity observed in FESEM reflects microscale densification and does not imply a loss of surface bioactivity. FESEM mainly captures microscale features, while biologically relevant nanoscale characteristics are better evaluated using AFM. Cross-sectional SEM images (Figure ) showed that all composite coatings formed a uniform, well-adhered layer on the titanium substrate, with thicknesses ranging from 13.51 to 15.34 μm depending on composition. The coating–substrate interface was continuous and free of voids or delamination, confirming a strong mechanical anchorage. This enhanced adhesion is attributed to plasma treatment of the titanium surface (Figure b), which created surface roughness and micropits that promoted mechanical interlocking during deposition. In contrast, the untreated titanium (Figure a) surface appeared to be relatively smooth with visible machining marks.

Cross-sectional SEM images of the HAP-based composite coating showing a uniform and compact layer with a thickness of approximately 13.61 μm. (Left) Grayscale SEM image with thickness measurement; (Right) false-colored image for better structural visualization.

EDX

EDX analysis was performed to evaluate the elemental composition of the HAP-based composites, focusing on Ca/P and F/Ca atomic ratios. The spectra (Figure ) confirmed the presence of Ca, P, O, C, N, and F, while elemental mapping showed a uniform distribution across both HAP and modified samples. Pure HAP exhibited a Ca/P ratio of 1.61, close to the ideal value, along with a very low F/Ca ratio (0.0076), indicating good phase purity and slight calcium deficiency beneficial for biological resorption. , Incorporation of AICA reduced the Ca/P ratio to 1.55, suggesting the formation of lattice defects that may enhance ion transport and osteogenic activity. This was accompanied by an increase in the F/Ca ratio to 0.125, reflecting effective fluoride incorporation. With the addition of SA and PAN, the Ca/P ratio gradually increased to 1.56 and 1.63, respectively, approaching ideal stoichiometry and indicating improved structural stability. The lower F/Ca ratios observed in SA- (0.091) and PAN-containing (0.0646) composites suggest controlled fluoride levels, which are important for maintaining biocompatibility.

Energy-Dispersive X-ray Spectroscopy (EDX) analysis with elemental mapping and SEM micrographs of (a) HAP and (b) m-HAP-3 coatings.

Contact Angle

The contact angle measurements were conducted to evaluate the surface wettability of the developed coatings, which is critical for protein adsorption, cellular adhesion, and overall biointegration. As shown in Figure , the Ti–B substrate exhibited a high contact angle of 96.4°, indicating its intrinsic hydrophobic nature, which may hinder initial biological interactions. Ti–P showed a significantly reduced contact angle of 56.7°, demonstrating improved surface energy and hydrophilicity, which enhances cell attachment and bioactivity. When HAP was coated onto titanium, the contact angle further decreased to 43.2°, confirming the hydrophilic contribution of HAP’s surface hydroxyl (–OH) groups. However, the incorporation of AICA into the HAP matrix resulted in an increased contact angle of 86.5°, likely due to molecular interactions and the presence of hydrophobic functional groups. Upon further addition of SA alongside AICA and HAP, the contact angle decreased to 72°, indicating an improvement in surface wettability due to the carboxyl (–COO–) and hydroxyl functionalities of SA. , In the final composite formulation addition of PAN, the contact angle was measured to be 58°, suggesting an optimized balance between hydrophilicity and controlled wettability, which is crucial for facilitating biological fluid interactions, enhancing osseointegration, and promoting long-term implant stability.

TGA–DTA

Thermal analysis of the HAP synthesized from caprine bone and the m-HAP-3 biocomposite coating was carried out by using TGA–DTA to evaluate their thermal stability and degradation profiles. As shown in Figure a, the HAP coating material demonstrated excellent thermal resilience, with only ∼2.7% weight loss up to 850 °C, primarily due to desorption of adsorbed moisture near 51 °C, as shown by a minor endothermic deviation in the DTA curve. The absence of any significant decomposition events suggests high crystallinity and phase purity, which is characteristic of well-processed biologically derived apatite. In contrast, the m-HAP-3 (Figure b) coating exhibited a multistep thermal degradation profile, reflecting the presence of organic polymers within the inorganic matrix. An initial weight loss (∼4.2%) was observed between 46–86 °C, which is attributed to surface and bound water loss. This was followed by prominent degradative events between 100 and 420 °C, corresponding to the sequential thermal breakdown of AICA, SA, and PAN. These stages are marked by endothermic peaks in the DTA curve at ∼71 °C, 110 °C, and 332 °C, characteristic of organic–inorganic hybrid composites. The final residue of ∼42.7% at 843 °C signifies the preservation of the HAP phase despite extensive organic decomposition. These results corroborate the XRD and SEM data, which indicated a transition from well-defined nanorod morphology in the HAP coating to a compact, polymer-reinforced structure in the m-HAP-3 composite. The m-HAP-3 thermal behavior reflects not only the degradability of organic additives but also the thermal persistence of the mineral core, suggesting its suitability for biomedical applications, where thermal robustness and structural integrity are crucial.

TGA–DTA analysis of (a) HAP and (b) m-HAP-3 coatings.

AFM

AFM was employed to investigate nanoscale surface topography and roughness, which play a decisive role in protein adsorption, cell adhesion, and osteogenic signaling. The HAP coating (Figure a) exhibited a granular and uneven surface characterized by nanoscale protrusions resulting from anisotropic crystal growth. This nanoscale roughness enhances the surface area and promotes protein adsorption, thereby facilitating early osteoblast attachment. However, the surface displayed a limited nanoscale interconnectivity. Following AICA incorporation (m-HAP-1, Figure b), the surface became denser and more compact, with reduced nanoscale porosity and tighter surface features. While this compaction slightly reduced wettability due to the semihydrophobic nature of AICA, the increased structural coherence may favor osteoconductivity by improving cell–surface mechanical interactions. With the addition of SA (m-HAP-2, Figure c), the surface evolved into a stratified and interconnected network resembling an extracellular matrix (ECM) architecture. This nanoscale porosity and roughness enhanced hydration, protein adsorption, and biomolecular interactions. The abundance of carboxyl and hydroxyl functional groups likely facilitated hydration layer formation, supporting integrin-mediated cell adhesion and early osteogenic signaling.

Atomic Force Microscopy (AFM) topographical images of synthesized calcium phosphate-based composite coatings: (a) HAP, (b) m-HAP-1, (c) m-HAP-2, and (d) m-HAP-3 composite layers.

In the final composite m-HAP-3 (Figure d), compared with HAP and m-HAP-1, the surface exhibited a moderately increased roughness, while being significantly smoother than m-HAP-2, indicating a relatively uniform morphology with retained nanoscale interconnectivity and controlled heterogeneity. Despite the reduced microscale porosity observed in SEM, AFM confirms the preservation of nanoscale features that are known to govern osteoblast attachment, focal adhesion formation, and osteogenic differentiation. The combination of hydrophilic domains from HAP and SA with the semihydrophobic PAN matrix resulted in balanced wettability, closely mimicking native ECM interfaces. Collectively, the AFM results demonstrate that controlled surface compaction at the microscale does not impede osteogenesis, provided that nanoscale roughness and interconnectivity are maintained. , This hierarchical surface architecture reconciles the FESEM and AFM observations and aligns with the current understanding that nanoscale topography, rather than large open porosity alone, is a dominant regulator of osteogenic response.

Electrochemical Studies

The electrochemical behavior of bare titanium (Ti–B) and the coated samples (HAP, m-HAP-1, m-HAP-2, and m-HAP-3) was systematically studied in simulated body fluid (SBF) by using potentiodynamic polarization (Tafel analysis), electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS; Nyquist, Bode, and phase angle plots), and cyclic voltammetry (CV). The results revealed significant improvements in the corrosion resistance and interfacial stability of Ti after surface modification with hydroxyapatite-based hybrid coatings.

Polarization Analysis

The polarization curves demonstrated a clear trend of improved corrosion resistance upon coating modification. Bare Ti exhibited the highest corrosion current density (i corr), indicating its susceptibility to rapid electrochemical degradation in SBF (Figure ). HAP-coated Ti showed moderate improvement, which was attributed to the presence of a bioactive ceramic barrier. The incorporation of AICA (m-HAP-1) further decreases i corr, reflecting the role of the polymeric phase in hindering electrolyte penetration. The addition of sodium alginate (m-HAP-2) resulted in a more compact and cross-linked structure, which shifts the corrosion potential (E corr) toward more noble values. The m-HAP-3 coating exhibited the lowest corrosion current and the most positive E corr, confirming its superior ability to passivate the Ti surface and suppress electrochemical dissolution.

EIS

The Bode and Nyquist plots support the Tafel findings. In (Figure ), bare Ti shows a single, small semicircle in the Nyquist spectrum, characteristic of a thin and unstable passive film. In contrast, the coated samples exhibited enlarged semicircle diameters, indicating a higher charge transfer resistance (R ct) and enhanced interfacial stability. Among them, m-HAP-3 showed the largest impedance arc, signifying the most effective corrosion barrier. In (Figure ), the Bode magnitude plots show that |Z| at low frequency increases in the following order: Bare Ti < HAP < m-HAP-1 < m-HAP-2 < m-HAP-3. The corresponding phase angle spectra revealed broader and higher peaks for the hybrid coatings, suggesting improved capacitive behavior due to the formation of a more protective, multilayered film. m-HAP-3, in particular, exhibited a wide phase angle plateau near 80°, indicating strong barrier properties and effective prevention of ionic diffusion through the coating.

CV Analysis

Cyclic voltammetry (CV) was used as a preliminary electrochemical tool to evaluate the surface stability of the m-HAP-3 composite coating in simulated body fluid (SBF) under both baseline and drug-exposed conditions. Measurements in drug-free SBF were carried out to determine the intrinsic electrochemical behavior of the coating, while drug-loaded CV experiments were performed by adding 3 mL of an aqueous solution containing calcium citrate and metformin to SBF to simulate a therapeutically relevant environment commonly encountered in clinical scenarios involving calcium supplementation and antidiabetic treatment. In drug-free SBF, the coating exhibited relatively higher anodic and cathodic current densities, reaching approximately ±2.4 mA/cm2, indicating an increased electrochemical activity and active interfacial charge transfer. Gradual shifts in peak positions observed over successive cycles suggest dynamic surface processes, such as ion exchange and interfacial rearrangement (Figure a). In contrast, the drug-loaded system (Figure b) showed substantially reduced and more stable current responses, confined within ±0.3 mA/cm2, with minimal variation across the cycles. The decrease in current density is attributed to the adsorption of calcium citrate and metformin molecules onto the coating surface, which promotes partial surface passivation and limits charge transfer at the electrode–electrolyte interface. − It is important to note that CV provides qualitative and comparative insight into interfacial electrochemical behavior rather than absolute corrosion rates; therefore, these results are interpreted as complementary to potentiodynamic polarization and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy measurements. The observed stabilization of the electrochemical response in the presence of therapeutic agents is consistent with previous studies on surface-modified titanium- and zirconia-based biomaterials, where tailored surface chemistry enhanced electrochemical stability. Overall, the m-HAP-3 coating demonstrates stable charge transfer behavior under both standard and drug-rich conditions, supporting its potential relevance as a corrosion-resistant surface modification for titanium implant applications.

Antibacterial Activity

Bacterial adhesion and biofilm formation are critical obstacles for the success of orthopedic implants, especially with P. aeruginosa, , which is known for its persistence and resistance on biomaterial surfaces. In Figure , the antibacterial potential of the synthesized composites (m-HAP-1, m-HAP-2, m-HAP-3) was tested using an agar well diffusion assay at concentrations ranging from 20 to 80 μg/mL. No inhibitory effect was observed at 20–60 μg/mL; at 80 μg/mL, m-HAP-3 and m-HAP-1 generated inhibition zones of 12.0 ± 1.0 mm, while m-HAP-2 showed 9 ± 1.0 mm, compared to 24 ± 1.0 mm for gentamicin. These findings indicate that antibacterial activity is concentration-dependent and likely results from a combination of released ions, the antimicrobial action of AICA functional groups, and surface charge interactions. Alongside their bone-supportive properties, these composites show promise as multifunctional coatings that both encourage osteointegration and reduce early bacterial colonization.

Conclusion

This study establishes that HAP derived from the caprine femur bone can be effectively tailored through the sequential incorporation of AICA, SA, and PAN, producing a multifunctional composite with improved structural and biological characteristics. The coatings fabricated on plasma treated titanium substrates using electrophoretic deposition (EPD) showed a uniform coverage and strong substrate adhesion. XRD results of the m-HAP-3 coating material confirmed reduced crystallite size and partial amorphization, indicating that organic modifiers regulated crystal growth and enhanced compatibility within the ceramic phase. Spectroscopic and microscopic analyses further revealed the successful integration of organic moieties, leading to a densified, homogeneous surface with ECM-like features and balanced hydrophilicity, which are favorable for protein adsorption and cellular interactions. Mechanical evaluation confirmed stronger adhesion and durability, while thermal studies showed the stability of the mineral phase together with controlled degradation of the organic components. Electrochemical tests highlighted the enhanced corrosion resistance of m-HAP-3-coated titanium that was reflected in its more noble potential and lower current density than that of bare titanium, along with improved charge transfer behavior in both drug-loaded and unloaded conditions. Additionally, antibacterial assays demonstrated dose-dependent inhibition of P. aeruginosa, supporting the material’s role in infection prevention. Collectively, the findings confirm that the m-HAP-3 composite, when coated on a surface-activated titanium implant via EPD, provides a favorable physicochemical and biological interface with strong osteoconductive potential, making it a promising material for reliable and long-lasting orthopedic and dental implant applications.
