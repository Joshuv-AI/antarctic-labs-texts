Explainable AI for malware analysis: a systematic review of benchmark datasets, traffic-oriented detection, and interpretability methods
Authors: Bowen Yu, Wanting Zhang, Yishen Liu, Awais Ahmad, Waeal J. Obidallah, Yousef Ahmed Alduraywish, Farhan Ullah, Kenji Yoshigoe, Yue Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In recent years, malicious software (malware) has remained a persistent and evolving threat, affecting both personal and organizational devices across diverse computing environments. This article presents a systematic review of recent progress in malware analysis over the past 5 years, with particular emphasis on benchmark datasets, traffic-oriented detection settings, deep learning methods, and explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) approaches. Representative benchmark resources discussed in the reviewed literature include CIC IoMT 2024, CIC-MalMem-2022, and CICMalDroid 2020, which reflect different malware-analysis contexts such as IoMT/network traffic, memory-based Windows malware behavior, and Android malware analysis. The review examines how prior studies characterize malicious behaviors, evaluate detection paradigms, and employ interpretability techniques such as Local Interpretable Model-Agnostic Explanations (LIME) and Shapley Additive Explanations (SHAP) to improve model transparency and analyst understanding. In addition to summarizing the practical strengths of these approaches, the article discusses current limitations related to interpretability overhead, dataset dependence, scalability, and deployment constraints. The review concludes by identifying major research gaps and outlining future directions for building more scalable, explainable, and trustworthy malware-analysis systems.

The audience it is intended for

The target audience for this study includes cybersecurity researchers, malware analysts, academics in artificial intelligence and machine learning, and professionals interested in malware detection and defense technologies on Windows and Android platforms. This article aims to provide these readers with a comprehensive literature review covering the latest advances in malware analysis over the past 5 years, with particular attention to network-based detection techniques, deep neural network models, and interpretable artificial intelligence (XAI) mechanisms.

To ensure methodological rigor, this study was designed as a systematic review. The guiding research question was: “How have interpretable AI techniques, particularly LIME and SHAP, been applied to malware detection in Windows and Android environments between 2019 and 2025?”

Search strategy

A comprehensive literature search was conducted across multiple academic databases, including Google Scholar, IEEE Xplore, ACM Digital Library, and SpringerLink, covering the period from January 2019 to March 2025. Search terms combined malware-related keywords (e.g., malware detection, Windows malware, Android malware, malicious traffic analysis, IoT malware) with interpretability-focused terms (e.g., explainable artificial intelligence (AI), XAI, LIME, SHAP, deep learning for malware detection). Additional studies were identified through citation chaining of relevant articles.

Study selection

Titles, abstracts, and keywords were first screened to exclude irrelevant studies. Full-text assessment was then conducted to evaluate eligibility against the inclusion/exclusion criteria. The selection process followed PRISMA 2020 guidelines, and the results are summarized in the PRISMA flow diagram (Fig. 1). Specifically, 13,600 records were identified (12,960 from databases and 640 from other sources). After removing 2,100 duplicates, 11,500 records remained for title and abstract screening, of which 10,700 were excluded. A total of 800 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility, and 629 were excluded for reasons such as lack of malware-detection focus (n = 235), platform mismatch (n = 128), insufficient XAI/interpretability (n = 186), or methodological limitations (n = 80). Ultimately, 171 studies were included in this systematic review.

Introduction

The widespread adoption of the Internet has significantly reshaped communication, commerce, and daily life, driving the evolution of cyber threat landscapes. It has profoundly transformed communication, commerce, and daily life. However, along with these advancements, cyber threats have escalated in both scale and complexity, with malware emerging as one of the most prevalent attack vectors. Malware, defined as software intentionally designed to disrupt, damage, or gain unauthorized access to digital systems, encompasses diverse variants such as viruses, worms, trojans, spyware, ransomware, and rootkits. These threats often operate covertly, altering system configurations, exfiltrating data, or enabling remote control, thereby posing significant risks to individuals, organizations, and critical infrastructure. Traditional malware detection methods rely heavily on signature-based or heuristic techniques, but these approaches have become increasingly inadequate when facing evolving and polymorphic attacks. In recent years, machine learning (ML) and deep learning (DL) methods have been widely adopted in this domain, demonstrating notable improvements in accuracy and adaptability (Amin et al., 2020; Aurangzeb & Aleem, 2023). However, the “black-box” nature of these models introduces a new challenge, namely the lack of interpretability. Despite their strong predictive performance, this lack of transparency creates a critical research problem: analysts cannot determine why a model flags certain samples as malicious, which limits trust, hinders forensic investigation, and prevents reliable deployment in real-world security environments. Security analysts often cannot fully understand why a model classifies a particular file as malicious or benign, which weakens trust, accountability, and practical deployment in mission-critical environments. According to recent global statistics, an average of 5.4 billion malware attacks occurs annually. The most common causes of cyberattacks last year were ransomware (19%), malware (17%), and phishing attacks (17%). According to industry statistics, the number of malware detections increased from 183 million in 2017 to approximately 493 million in 2022. In some regions, mobile malware specifically targeting Windows and Android platforms is increasingly prevalent. Upon identifying malware, it is crucial to deploy appropriate detection mechanisms. Much like personal firewalls or antivirus software, IT security professionals employ enterprise-level cybersecurity solutions to monitor and protect organizational networks against these threats (Statista, 2024a; UpCity, 2024; Statista, 2024b).

To address these limitations, explainable artificial intelligence (XAI) has emerged as a promising solution. XAI techniques aim to clarify the logic behind model decisions, thereby enhancing transparency, accountability, and user trust. In the cybersecurity domain, XAI not only enables experts to identify the key features of malicious behavior but also supports proactive defense strategies, improves dataset curation, and facilitates regulatory compliance. Among the various XAI methods (Alani & Awad, 2022), this study focuses on Local Interpretable Model-agnostic Explanations (LIME) and SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP). The reason for selecting these two methods is that they provide a unique interpretability value that cannot be easily replaced by other approaches. Traditional detection methods, such as those based on signatures, heuristic rules, or static and dynamic analysis, mainly address the question of “whether detection can be made,” but they fail to answer, “why this judgment was made.” Similarly, while deep learning models offer high accuracy, they operate as black boxes and lack transparency. By contrast, LIME and SHAP are not new classification algorithms but model-agnostic interpretability techniques. Accordingly, one of the primary objectives of this study is to systematically evaluate how these interpretability tools can reveal the internal decision processes of malware detection models and provide actionable insights for security analysts. They reveal the contribution of individual features to predictions, enabling researchers and security analysts to understand classification rationales and summarize key feature importance. LIME excels at local explanations (e.g., false positive analysis), while SHAP offers theoretically stable global and local interpretations. LIME is particularly effective at providing local explanations, which are useful for analyzing false positives or borderline samples, whereas SHAP is theoretically more stable and capable of offering consistent explanations at both global and local levels. The combination of the two strikes a balance between single-sample interpretability and importance of global features, aligning with the current trend toward trusted AI and explainable detection in cybersecurity. This contribution is particularly significant because interpretable detection systems are increasingly required for regulatory compliance, operational reliability, and the development of trustworthy AI-driven cybersecurity frameworks. Against this backdrop, this study investigates malware detection through the lens of XAI, with particular emphasis on evaluating and visualizing feature importance using representative datasets. This review therefore examines how prior studies combine ML/DL models with LIME and SHAP to improve interpretability, transparency, and practical trustworthiness. To guide this effort, we formulate the following central research question: How can LIME and SHAP be effectively applied to enhance the transparency, reliability, and practical usability of machine-learning-based malware detection systems across diverse computing platforms?

This comprehensive dataset includes tens of thousands of labeled network traffic samples derived from 18 simulated cyberattacks conducted in a realistic IoMT (Internet of Medical Things) testbed. The testbed comprises 40 devices—25 physical and 15 virtual—communicating via multiple healthcare-relevant protocols such as Wi-Fi, Message Queuing Telemetry Transport (MQTT), and Bluetooth (Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity, 2024).

These tools offer local and global interpretability by highlighting key features contributing to model decisions, thereby enhancing transparency—especially critical in high-risk cybersecurity domains.

Probing malicious traffic: definition and research significance

Malware spreads through multiple channels, with malicious network traffic serving as the primary vector. This type of traffic involves intentionally manipulated data transmissions that allow attackers to exfiltrate sensitive information, gain unauthorized access, or disrupt critical systems. By acting as the conduit for delivering harmful payloads, malicious traffic plays a decisive role in the operational success of modern cyberattacks.

A deeper understanding of these transmission mechanisms is crucial for developing effective defense strategies. Unlike traditional static malware, contemporary threats often leverage dynamic and adaptive traffic behaviors, making them more difficult to detect using conventional methods. Malicious traffic analysis provides insights into malware behavioral patterns and serves as a practical basis for designing adaptive detection frameworks to address evolving attack strategies.

Furthermore, malicious traffic is closely tied to widely used attack vectors such as phishing emails, malicious websites, and exploit toolkits. Phishing campaigns often use misleading attachments or embedded links to lure users into executing harmful software. Drive-by download attacks enable silent installation of malware, while exploit kits automate vulnerability targeting and payload delivery. Analyzing these traffic patterns reveals how cyberattacks evolve from initial access to full system compromise, which is essential for developing intelligent and explainable defensive models.

In this context, probing malicious traffic is not only about identifying attack signatures but also about uncovering the underlying mechanisms that enable persistence and escalation. This research therefore establishes a clear rationale for combining malicious traffic analysis with explainable AI technologies to enhance transparency, strengthen detection capabilities, and provide a foundation for proactive cyber defense.

Furthermore, various types of malwares frequently display analogous characteristics in their malicious traffic, which can be observed through specific protocol attributes. Certain malware is known to utilize protocols, such as ARP and DNS. Analyzing malicious traffic is essential for identifying and categorizing similar behaviors exhibited by malware. This process is illustrated in Fig. 2, which depicts a typical Network-based Intrusion Detection System (N-IDS) architecture. This type of traffic serves as a fundamental mechanism through which an attack infiltrates a system, compromising data integrity, gaining unauthorized control, or exfiltrating sensitive credentials. Additionally, malware can often be differentiated based on the protocols they target. By recognizing and comprehending the nature of malicious traffic, one can significantly enhance the understanding and prevention of malware threats.

Relation to malicious traffic

Malware manifests in various forms, yet it fundamentally necessitates an effective delivery mechanism to achieve its intended target. Among these mechanisms, malicious network traffic has emerged as the predominant channel for the dissemination of malware. Cyber attackers frequently utilize malicious websites or advertisements containing harmful links, compromised websites that initiate drive-by downloads, and exploit toolkits that leverage software vulnerabilities to inject malicious code.

In addition to traditional network-layer vectors, malware propagation can also occur at the application and system levels. For instance, SQL injection attacks manipulate web inputs to provoke unintended database operations, while side-channel attacks exploit physical signals, such as power consumption or execution time, to extract sensitive information. Document-based malware, often concealed within PDFs or Office files, continues to pose significant risks in both enterprise and governmental settings. Furthermore, software supply chain attacks introduce malicious code through vulnerable dependencies, effectively circumventing internal security measures (Lawson, 2009; Singh, Tapaswi & Gupta, 2020; Andreoli et al., 2023).

These evolving methodologies illustrate the increasing complexity associated with malware dissemination, thereby amplifying the necessity for sophisticated and interpretable detection systems. Consequently, this study concentrates on the application of XAI techniques to identify patterns and behaviors inherent in malicious traffic, with the objective of enhancing threat comprehension and defense capabilities.

System architecture.

Methodology: dataset and preprocessing

Methodological clarification. Because the revised manuscript is positioned as a systematic review rather than as a standalone original experimental study, it does not present a newly specified model architecture, training/validation pipeline, hyperparameter-selection procedure, or reproducibility benchmark as primary contributions. Instead, this section summarizes representative benchmark datasets and interpretability practices reported in prior studies in order to support literature synthesis across malware-analysis settings (Klein, 2021; Korine & Hendler, 2021). The complete environment configuration has been documented to enable replication without deviation. Data preprocessing, statistical analysis, and model interpretation were implemented using standard scientific libraries, including Pandas and Scikit-learn for data handling, Matplotlib for visualization, and the official LIME and SHAP packages for interpretability analysis. This configuration ensured reproducibility and computational efficiency throughout the study. Statistical evidence indicates a significant escalation in the incidence of malware, rising from 183 million cases in 2017 to 493 million in 2022, thereby highlighting the expanding magnitude of cyber threats. The removal of malware is often a complex endeavor; even post-detection, it may necessitate extensive cleaning of the system’s registry, file system, or master boot record (MBR). In extreme instances, a complete reinstallation of the system may be the only viable solution to eradicate lingering threats.

In response to these increasingly severe cyber threats, numerous organizations have implemented Cyber Threat Intelligence (CTI), a data-centric methodology designed to identify emerging threats. Nevertheless, conventional CTI frequently depends on manual analysis and signature-based detection, which proves inadequate in keeping up with rapidly evolving malware variants, including polymorphic and zero-day attacks.

This research aims to bridge this gap by incorporating Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) into the malware detection framework, with the objective of enhancing transparency, bolstering detection reliability, and facilitating a more proactive approach to cybersecurity (Zhang, Ragunathan & Jia, 2014; Xu, Fang & Yang, 2021).

Considering the necessity to comprehend the mechanisms of malware propagation, this research underscores the significance of examining malicious traffic, which serves as an indicator of the fundamental dissemination patterns of malware. To facilitate this examination, the CIC IoMT dataset 2024 was chosen due to its clarity, organization, and availability to the public. Scope clarification. Although CIC IoMT 2024 is discussed in detail as a representative benchmark for traffic-oriented malware analysis, the present review does not perform a formal cross-dataset evaluation, temporal concept-drift simulation, or stability test of feature-importance patterns under distribution shift. Accordingly, findings related to feature relevance and interpretability should be understood as literature-synthesized observations within the reviewed benchmark contexts, rather than as evidence of validated generalization across heterogeneous environments or time periods. This section delineates the team’s analysis and data visualization derived from this dataset, with the objective of advancing the understanding of malware transmission behaviors (Laurenza, Lazzeretti & Mazzotti, 2020).

By accessing the website and searching for the keyword “CIC IoMT dataset 2024” in the search bar, a realistic benchmark dataset is obtained, which is intended to support the development and evaluation of medical IoT (Internet of Things) security solutions. The CIC IoMT 2024 dataset (Canadian Institute for Cybersecurity) is a publicly available benchmark with 4.2 million network traffic records (benign and malicious) from a medical IoT environment, including 85 features across basic connection, content-based, and statistical categories (e.g., source/destination IP, packet length, flow duration). The dataset includes 85 network traffic features, covering categories such as basic connection information (e.g., source/destination IP, protocol, timestamp), content-based features (e.g., packet length, flow duration, inter-arrival time), and statistical features (e.g., mean, standard deviation, flow bytes, packet counts). Additionally, we provide a detailed breakdown of class distributions, feature categories, and label mappings to ensure that dataset characteristics are transparent and reproducible for future benchmarking. The dataset encompasses multiple attack categories, including but not limited to DDoS, brute force, botnet activity, port scanning, and command-and-control traffic. Each record is labeled with its corresponding attack type or benign behavior, making it suitable for supervised learning and model interpretation. The data were captured in a controlled network environment using real IoT devices, ensuring both authenticity and reproducibility.

The usability of this dataset lies in its timeliness and relevance. Timeliness: Malicious attack methods are bound to undergo more changes and exhibit new features as time progresses, rather than remaining static. Utilizing the most recent dataset helps avoid studying obsolete or already updated and relatively useless data. Relevance: This dataset records the traffic table formed by a certain medical IoT device being subjected to malicious attacks over a period. Starting from this aspect can demonstrate the characteristics of different malicious attacks, which is highly relevant to this research (Hsu et al., 2006).

Secondly, by adopting a key-point analysis approach, we focused only on those items that were associated with abnormal data, excluding some items with weak regularity. We statistically recorded all such items. However, this was insufficient for analysis. Therefore, we also recorded the timestamp of all abnormal data occurrences. Thus, we had the number of abnormal occurrences and the timestamp of each abnormal occurrence in each dataset.

Interpretive value of dataset-reported feature patterns in prior studies

Prior studies suggest that interpretable malware-analysis frameworks can help reveal nuanced behavioral patterns in malicious traffic. In the reviewed literature, LIME and SHAP are widely used to provide feature-level attributions that help explain why models distinguish between normal and abnormal network flows. This perspective also helps interpret reported anomalies in traffic behavior, as sharp fluctuations in DDoS- and DoS-related activity are often associated with bursty packet-generation behaviors in high-intensity flooding attacks. Similar temporal irregularities have been documented in prior studies on IoT-based cyberattacks, where heterogeneous device communication patterns often lead to uneven attack visibility across time windows.

Additionally, prior studies frequently identify packet-length variance, flow duration, and inter-arrival time as influential features in intrusion-detection tasks, suggesting that these variables are well-established behavioral indicators of malicious activity. Taken together, these consistencies indicate the potential value of XAI-based insights for model auditing, debugging, and analyst interpretation. From a practical standpoint, the interpretability discussed in the reviewed literature carries notable implications for real-world cybersecurity operations. The ability to pinpoint which traffic characteristics contribute to malicious classifications enables security analysts to perform more targeted incident investigations, refine firewall or IDS rules, and generate more accurate threat intelligence reports. To make this operational relevance more explicit, a practical interpretation workflow can be summarized as follows. First, a detection model flags a traffic flow, process, or sample as suspicious. Second, LIME or SHAP highlights the most influential features contributing to that prediction, such as abnormal flow duration, packet-length variance, inter-arrival irregularity, unusual API-call behavior, or permission-related anomalies. Third, the analyst maps these high-contribution features to concrete behavioral hypotheses, for example volumetric flooding activity, suspicious command-and-control communication, privilege misuse, or evasive runtime behavior. Fourth, these explanations can guide operational actions, including prioritizing alerts for triage, refining firewall or IDS rules, updating detection signatures, isolating endpoints for deeper inspection, or initiating forensic review. Finally, the explanation record may also support model auditing, analyst reporting, and later rule adjustment. In this sense, XAI outputs are valuable not only as interpretive aids, but also as structured decision-support signals for operational security practice. In IoMT environments, where device heterogeneity and resource constraints are common, XAI-driven transparency may support the deployment of lightweight and more trustworthy detection modules for rapid decision-making. Overview of Existing Malware Detection Methods 1.Signature detection based on signature codes utilizes known malware digital identifiers to identify suspicious behavior. Typically, these identifiers are stored in a database as a list of breach indicators (IOCS) that are used to identify potential violations. Although IOCS are very effective for identifying malicious activities, they are inherently passive. As a result, CrowdStrike employs an attack indicator (IOA) approach to proactively identify ongoing cyberattacks (Dugyala et al., 2022).2.Static file analysis examines the code without running the file for signs of malicious intent. A variety of elements, including file names, hash values, strings (such as IP addresses), and header data, can be evaluated to determine whether the file is malicious. While static file analysis provides a good initial screening tool, experienced security teams employ additional techniques to detect advanced malware that is difficult to identify at static analysis stage (Ball et al., 2006).3.Dynamic malware analysis executes suspected malicious code in a securely isolated environment—a so-called sandbox. This closed system allows security experts to observe and study the behavior of malware while avoiding the risk of it infecting systems or escaping to corporate networks (Or-Meir et al., 2019). Moreover, the analysis of dynamic behavior has become crucial considering the growing prevalence of polymorphic and evasive strategies employed by contemporary malware (Gebrehans et al., 2025).4.Dynamic monitoring involves the observation of many file operations, such as renaming or deleting commands, to detect signs of tampering or corruption. Dynamic monitoring is often implemented with file integrity monitoring tools, which track and analyze file system integrity through a combination of passive forensic auditing and active rules-based monitoring.5.File extension Block list/Block litigation file extension refers to the sequence of characters after the period of the file name, which is used to identify the file format. An attacker may use this classification to encapsulate malware. Therefore, a common security measure is to put known malicious file extension types on the block list to prevent users from inadvertently downloading or using dangerous files.6.Application allow/allow litigation as opposed to blocking litigation, organizations improve security by authorizing the system to use only approved application lists. While this approach is effective in preventing malicious applications, its strict limitations can reduce an organization’s operational efficiency and flexibility (Raje, 1999).7.Malware honeypots/honeypot files trigger malware attacks in a controlled and non-threatening environment by simulating software applications or application programming interfaces (apis). Similarly, honeypot files serve as decoy files, designed to attract and detect potential attackers. In doing so, security teams can analyze attack tactics and develop or refine anti-malware strategies based on them to address specific vulnerabilities, threats, or attackers (Nawrocki et al., 2016).8.Checksum/cyclic redundancy check (CRC) is a technique for performing calculations on a dataset, such as a file, to verify its integrity. CRC is one of the most used checksums, implemented through the analysis of data values and their locations. While checksums are effective in identifying data corruption, they are not infallible in determining whether data has been tampered with Nawrocki et al. The availability of LIME and SHAP

Or-Meir et al. (2019) asserted that both static and dynamic analysis methodologies are essential for the identification and comprehension of malware behaviors within contemporary settings. This section presents the team’s solutions to the current situation and to combat malware. The initial and essential step in formulating subsequent defense strategies against advanced threats is the implementation of effective malware detection (Tahir, 2018). However, in recent years, with the promotion of AI technology to machine learning and deep learning technology, the complexity of detection models is increasing, such as AI-based security detection evasion. Making its predictions difficult to interpret. This “black box” problem greatly increases the difficulty of detecting and preventing malware, making it difficult for security teams to respond to new types of attacks (Filiol, 2006). In this context, interpretive AI methods such as LIME and SHAP, provides a powerful tool for understanding the predictions of complex models. LIME reveals the feature influence of specific data points through local linear models, while SHAP’s game-based theory calculates the feature, which is helpful to both the whole and the part of the prediction results (Slack et al., 2020). These methods provide insight into key behavioral characteristics of malware, such as API calls, network traffic, and permission requests, and evaluate the reliability and limitations of detection models based on this. In addition, LIME and SHAP can be used to identify potential detection blind spots, optimize protection strategies, and enhance the ability to interpret the mechanisms of malware transmission (Nguyen et al., 2021). A comparative analysis has demonstrated that both LIME and SHAP offer more robust explanatory insights compared to conventional machine learning models that utilize TF-IDF feature (Pradhan, Navaneeth & Aditya, 2025). Therefore, the understanding of LIME and SHAP, combined with code and AI technology, into malware analysis can not only improve the efficiency of the model, but also play an important role in dealing with the rapidly evolving, more complex and changeable malware threats. Next, the team will specifically discuss the role of LIME and SHAP in detecting and identifying malware.

The integration of XAI methods such as SHAP and LIME has also been explicitly applied to malware analysis pipelines. Pradhan conducted a comparative study of these two techniques in conjunction with hashing-based feature extraction, showing that SHAP provides robust global feature importance while LIME contributes local interpretability at the instance level. Their work highlights how different XAI tools can complement each other to improve trust and usability of AI-driven cybersecurity models (Pradhan, Navaneeth & Aditya, 2025).

Focusing on Android environments, Lee investigated the robustness of explainable deep learning models for malware detection using network flow features. Their study evaluated Deep Neural Network (DNN), 1D-Convolutional neural network (CNN), and bidirectional long short-term memory (BiLSTM) architectures on the CICAndMal2017 dataset, with BiLSTM achieving the best performance on unseen samples. Model predictions were interpreted using LIME and SHAP, which enabled the identification of both robust features (e.g., TCP flags, initial window sizes) and weak, manipulable ones. By retraining models with only robust features, they achieved 71% F1-score for detection and 57% for classification, while enhancing interpretability and resilience to evasion. These findings highlight the trade-off between accuracy and robustness, emphasizing that transparent, dependable, and adversarial resilient models are critical for the practical deployment of AI-driven malware detection in Android ecosystems (Najibi & Bidgoly, 2025; Taher et al., 2023).

It approximates the behavior of complex models around specific data points by building simple linear models, and researchers can obtain the characteristics of the data points by observing and analyzing the performance of the models. SHAP

SHAP is an interpretive artificial intelligence method that provides global and local explanations for complex models based on Shapley values in game theory. It explains the decision-making process of the model by calculating the marginal contribution of each feature to the model prediction and then quantifying the importance and direction of influence of these features (Salih et al., 2023).

This discovery, after guiding feature engineering optimization, increased the detection rate by 15%. However, this scheme has a bottleneck in computational efficiency. Verified by the NVIDIA Tesla V100 test, the operation time consumption of SHAP is typically higher computational cost than lightweight local explainers, and it requires supporting hardware acceleration to achieve engineering application.

This strong positive correlation among process count, number of loaded DLLs, and handle utilization can be explained by the operational characteristics of malware during execution. When a malicious program is launched, it often initiates multiple concurrent processes to increase its persistence and spread within the system. Each new process typically requires the loading of dynamic link libraries to support specific functionalities such as file manipulation, network communication, or privilege escalation. This naturally increases both the number of DLLs loaded and the total handle count. In addition, malicious activities often involve the intensive use of system resources for maintaining backdoors, establishing command-and-control channels, and hiding malicious payloads within legitimate processes. This coordinated consumption of processes, libraries, and handles reflects a behavioral pattern inherent to many modern malware families, rather than a random artifact of the dataset. Therefore, the observed strong positive correlation provides not only a statistical feature relationship but also a behavioral explanation that can inform future malware detection strategies and rule design.

Despite the proliferation of explainable AI methodologies, their assessment frequently occurs within idealized contexts. This study developed a testing environment that emulates authentic conditions utilizing the CIC IoMT 2024 dataset to assess the performance of explainable AI technologies in the detection of malicious traffic. We selected five representative attack datasets: DDoS ICMP Flood, DDoS UDP Flood, DoS TCP Flood, DoS ICMP Flood, and MITM ARP Spoofing, each characterized by distinct attack patterns and operational complexities. By simulating various traffic scales, attack frequencies, and temporal distributions, we effectively replicated common high-threat scenarios encountered in the Internet of Things (IoT) environment (He et al., 2020).

The evaluation results for the two predominant interpretation methods, LIME and SHAP, reveal distinct advantages and limitations. LIME is noted for its intuitive explanations and user-friendly visualizations, making it particularly accessible for non-technical audiences. However, its local approximation mechanism incurs considerable computational overhead, especially when applied to large-scale traffic data. Additionally, the reliance on random sampling for perturbation data introduces a degree of volatility in the interpretation results, which typically yield qualitative analyses.

Conversely, SHAP, grounded in game theory principles, offers a more rigorous mathematical framework for explanations, albeit requiring users to possess a certain level of technical expertise to comprehend the Shapley value. Its computational complexity is relatively high, particularly in the context of high-dimensional data and tree models. Nevertheless, SHAP demonstrates superior stability and repeatability in its interpretations, allowing for precise contribution values to be assigned to each feature, thereby facilitating more detailed quantitative analyses.

In summary, when the accessibility of explanations is paramount, LIME is the preferable option. Conversely, for scenarios necessitating in-depth and stable analyses, SHAP presents greater advantages, albeit at the cost of increased computational demands. At the same time, the present review does not define or benchmark formal interpretability evaluation criteria such as explanation consistency, stability, or robustness across repeated trials or distribution shifts. Instead, the interpretability discussion remains literature-synthesized and descriptive in scope, with these dimensions identified as important directions for future work in XAI-based malware analysis. From a review perspective, this computational trade-off can be interpreted along three practical dimensions: explanation latency, memory consumption, and scalability to high-dimensional traffic data. First, SHAP typically incurs higher explanation latency because the computation of feature contributions requires repeated estimation of marginal effects across many feature combinations, whereas LIME usually provides faster local approximations for individual instances. Second, SHAP may impose greater memory demands when explanations are generated over large feature spaces or multiple samples, especially in cybersecurity settings involving rich traffic descriptors, API-call traces, or memory-behavior indicators. Third, scalability becomes a major concern when these methods are applied to high-dimensional malware-analysis data, where the number of features, interactions, and correlated attributes can significantly increase computational burden. In this sense, the choice between LIME and SHAP should not be guided only by interpretive stability, but also by the operational context in which explanations are expected to be generated. These findings serve as practical guidance for researchers in selecting appropriate interpretative tools based on specific requirements, including response time, depth of interpretation, and model transparency. Another important limitation concerns generalization under dataset shift. In malware-analysis settings, feature-importance patterns may vary across network environments, device ecosystems, collection periods, and traffic distributions. As a result, explanations derived from one benchmark context may not remain stable when the underlying feature distribution, attack composition, or temporal behavior changes. This issue is especially relevant in IoMT and traffic-oriented datasets, where heterogeneous communication patterns and evolving attack strategies can affect both predictive behavior and interpretability outcomes. Despite the proliferation of explainable AI methodologies, their assessment frequently occurs within idealized contexts. Several optimization strategies have been discussed in the literature to reduce this overhead. These include feature-space reduction before explanation, instance-level sampling rather than full-dataset explanation, approximate or subset-based SHAP computation, and the selective use of local explanations only for high-risk or high-uncertainty predictions. In operational environments such as IoMT traffic monitoring, a practical compromise may be to use lightweight local explanation methods for rapid alert triage, while reserving computationally intensive SHAP-based analysis for offline investigation, forensic review, or model-auditing stages. This trade-off-oriented perspective is important because interpretability in cybersecurity must remain not only accurate and stable, but also deployable under realistic resource and time constraints. Looking ahead: the technological development path of explainable artificial intelligence in cybersecurity defense

The value of this review lies in consolidating recent evidence on explainable malware detection across multiple benchmark settings and platform contexts (Serpanos et al., 2021). By synthesizing how LIME and SHAP are used for local and global interpretation, the review highlights both their practical promise and their methodological constraints, including computational overhead, dataset dependence, and deployment challenges in resource-constrained or real-time environments. Rather than claiming a fully validated new detection framework, the present study identifies recurring gaps in the literature and uses them to motivate a more focused future research agenda. Accordingly, the following directions are organized around improving interpretability efficiency, strengthening robustness under dataset shift, and enhancing the operational usability of XAI in cybersecurity defense. In this sense, the contribution of the review is not the introduction of a new benchmarked model, but the structured synthesis of current evidence and the clarification of high-priority directions for future XAI-driven malware-analysis research. To avoid an overly broad future-work discussion, the directions below are prioritized according to the main limitations exposed in the present review, namely computational overhead, limited generalization evidence, insufficient operational grounding, and the need for privacy-aware and robust XAI deployment. First, more work is needed on computationally efficient XAI for high-dimensional malware-analysis data. A practical hypothesis is that lightweight or approximate explanation strategies can preserve most of the interpretive value of SHAP and LIME while reducing latency and memory burden to a level more suitable for operational deployment. A preliminary evaluation design would compare explanation methods under standardized runtime, memory, and feature-space conditions across traffic-oriented benchmark datasets. Second, future research should examine generalization and explanation stability under dataset shift. The central hypothesis is that feature-attribution patterns derived from one benchmark environment may not remain stable across different device ecosystems, network conditions, attack compositions, or collection periods. A feasible preliminary design would involve cross-dataset comparison, temporal split validation, and consistency analysis of explanation outputs under distributional change. Third, XAI for malware analysis should be made more operationally actionable for analyst-facing decision support. The key hypothesis is that explanation outputs become more useful when they are explicitly linked to downstream actions such as alert prioritization, firewall or IDS rule adjustment, signature updating, and forensic escalation. A preliminary study design could evaluate workflow-oriented explanation interfaces in simulated SOC scenarios or analyst-in-the-loop review settings. Fourth, future work should further explore robust and privacy-aware XAI frameworks. This includes federated XAI settings, where interpretability must be preserved without centralizing sensitive data, as well as adversarial robust explanation methods that remain reliable under obfuscation, evasion, or poisoning attempts. In recent years, strengthening malware detection and network intrusion detection systems has become very important. Naeem propose an optimization-based graphical approach to improve malware detection performance on consumer devices through an ensemble learning model (Aldehim et al., 2023). At the same time, Ullah explored an intrusion detection method that combines big multimodal data with transfer learning, and optimized network security strategies in the IoT environment through game theory (Naeem et al., 2024). The same research team also developed the NIDS-VSB system, which utilizes Spark big data optimization and transfer learning to perform efficient intrusion detection for Connected Vehicle (VANET) scenarios. On the other hand, Zhao propose a hybrid approach based on transfer learning and generative adversarial samples (GANs), which further improves the accuracy and robustness of malware detection through graphical representation (Ullah et al., 2024a). These studies show that the combination of advanced machine learning technology and big data optimization methods can effectively address modern network security challenges and provide efficient and reliable protection schemes for complex network environments (Ullah et al., 2024b). Another recent advancement in explainable malware detection has focused on adaptability to unseen malware variants. Khan proposed a lightweight, interpretable Random Forest–based system trained on a single malware subtype (Transponder from the Spyware family) yet capable of detecting 15 unseen malware subtypes from the CIC-MalMem-2022 dataset. The system achieved over 99.8% accuracy with an average processing time of 5.7 µs per file, demonstrating both efficiency and scalability. Importantly, the framework relied on only the top five most influential features, improving interpretability while reducing computational overhead. The incorporation of SHAP further enhanced model transparency, allowing security analysts to understand decision-making processes. This work not only demonstrates the feasibility of generalizing malware detection to novel subtypes with minimal training data but also underscores the necessity of integrating accuracy, efficiency, and interpretability into future malware detection frameworks (Zhao et al., 2025).

Pham introduced a multimodal Windows malware detection framework that integrates hybrid static and dynamic analysis with enriched graph representations. The approach leverages CNNs for static PE features and GNNs for dynamic API call sequences, enriched by BERT-based embeddings for semantic context. Importantly, the framework incorporates XAI methods (SHAP, LIME, PGExplainer) to interpret classification outcomes, providing both global and local explanations of malicious behaviors. Their results demonstrated near-perfect accuracy (99.9%) while significantly improving transparency, positioning it among recent representative explainable solutions for Windows malware detection (Madamidola, Ngobigha & Ez-zizi, 2025).

More recently, Zhang introduced an innovative hybrid framework that integrates Transfer Learning (TL) with Explainable AI (XAI) for malware detection (Zhang et al. 2022a). By leveraging pre-trained neural networks, the system effectively adapts to emerging malware with minimal training data, addressing the challenge of data scarcity in evolving threat landscapes. The integration of XAI methods further ensures transparency and trustworthiness, enabling security analysts to understand and validate the model’s predictions. Experimental evaluations across multiple datasets confirmed that this approach not only improves detection accuracy but also provides interpretable insights into decision-making processes. This work represents a significant step forward in balancing performance and interpretability, reinforcing the role of hybrid TL–XAI strategies in combating dynamic cyber threats (Pham et al., 2025).

By combining multiple deep learning models with SHAP-based explanations, the framework achieved over 99% accuracy and demonstrated resilience against various attack scenarios. Their work highlights how explainable AI enhances feature attribution and trustworthiness in critical infrastructure contexts, particularly within ITS environments where both reliability and transparency are indispensable (Dominic & Ranjan, 2025).

Building on this line of research, Wazid developed an explainable deep learning-enabled malware attack detection framework for IoT-enabled ITS, further integrating XAI into model evaluation. Their system not only provided robust classification accuracy but also delivered interpretable insights into influential traffic features, facilitating security analysis and practical deployment in intelligent transportation environments. This underscores the applicability of XAI in domains requiring both real-time response and model accountability (Ciaramella et al., 2025b).

Beyond desktop and mobile environments, researchers have investigated domain-specific deployments. Alohali developed a two-stage malware detection model for Internet of Vehicles (IoV), combining BiLSTM-MHSA classifiers with SHAP-based explanations to enhance transparency and accuracy in vehicular networks. This demonstrates the versatility of XAI approaches in emerging intelligent transportation ecosystems, where both privacy and interpretability are critical (Wazid et al., 2025).

In the context of IoT security, Sharma proposed a deep learning-based ensemble intrusion detection framework (DLEX-IMD) to address malware attacks in IoT-enabled environments. The model was trained and evaluated on benchmark datasets, achieving 99.96% accuracy and an F1-score of 0.999. To enhance trust and interpretability, the framework incorporated LIME explanations, which justified the reliability of its training process by highlighting influential features in classification outcomes. Compared with several existing schemes, DLEX-IMD consistently outperformed alternatives, thereby illustrating the effectiveness of combining deep ensemble learning with XAI techniques for IoT malware detection (Alohali et al., 2025).

Towards the later stage of related work, Bagirov conducted a comprehensive investigation into robust malware detection using XAI, focusing on Android malware detection with interpretable machine learning models. Using the CICMalDroid dataset, the study applied Logistic Regression and multiple tree-based algorithms, achieving an overall F1-score of 94%. More importantly, the research emphasized that XAI does not merely serve to improve predictive accuracy but also provides valuable interpretability by identifying critical features influencing classification outcomes. This contribution demonstrates that enhancing model transparency and reliability is essential for practical deployment, further reinforcing the need for explainable approaches in malware analysis beyond pure performance metrics (Mittal et al., 2025).

Conclusion

This article systematically investigates recent malware analysis and detection technologies for Windows and Android platforms, with a focused emphasis on the role of XAI in enhancing modern detection frameworks. By combining a structured review of state-of-the-art methods with experimental observations on multiple publicly available datasets, this study derives concrete conclusions regarding the effectiveness, limitations, and practical value of XAI-assisted malware detection.

The main findings of this work confirm that while conventional network-based and behavioral-feature-driven detection approaches remain effective for identifying malicious activities, they exhibit inherent limitations in scalability, adaptability to evolving malware variants, and decision transparency. Our analysis demonstrates that deep-learning-based models achieve strong detection performance but function largely as black boxes, which restricts their practical usability in operational cybersecurity environments. In contrast, the integration of XAI techniques—specifically LIME and SHAP—enables clear attribution of model decisions to high-impact behavioral and traffic features, thereby transforming opaque detection outcomes into interpretable and actionable insights.

A key result of this study is that XAI-based interpretation can effectively uncover the underlying behavioral logic of malware attacks, distinguish dominant malicious characteristics from redundant features, and support the validation of detection rules across heterogeneous attack scenarios. The experimental observations further indicate that explainability mechanisms significantly enhance analyst trust and situational awareness, bridging the gap between high detection accuracy and real-world deployment requirements. These findings highlight that interpretability is not merely an auxiliary property, but a functional component that directly improves the reliability and usability of malware detection systems.

Nevertheless, this work also identifies critical unresolved challenges. The rapid emergence of polymorphic malware, adversarial crafted samples, and zero-day threats continues to undermine the generalization capability of existing models, including XAI-enhanced frameworks. Moreover, the increasing prevalence of IoT, cloud-based, and mobile infrastructures introduces highly heterogeneous traffic patterns, placing additional computational and scalability constraints on both detection and explanation modules.

In conclusion, this study establishes that the deep integration of explainable artificial intelligence with traditional and deep-learning-based malware detection techniques constitutes a necessary and promising direction for next-generation cyber-defense systems. By simultaneously improving detection effectiveness and decision interpretability, XAI-driven frameworks provide a more transparent, trustworthy, and operationally viable foundation for malware defense across personal devices, enterprise networks, and critical infrastructure. Future research should prioritize computationally efficient explainability, broader real-world data coverage, privacy-preserving collaborative learning, and adversarial robustness to further advance the development of scalable and trustworthy malware detection systems. The findings synthesized in this review should be interpreted as literature-based observations rather than as fully validated claims of causal performance improvement, broad external generalizability, or formally benchmarked interpretability robustness.
