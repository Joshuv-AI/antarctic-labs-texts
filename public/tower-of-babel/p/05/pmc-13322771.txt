Automated impaction in uncemented total hip arthroplasty
Authors: Milos Brkljac, Eliseu Bandeira, Shrey Sharma, Borna Guevel, Justin Cobb, Kartik Logishetty
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimsIntraoperative periprosthetic femoral fractures (IPFs) are a serious complication of total hip arthroplasty (THA), typically attributed to surgical technique. Manual impaction provides tactile feedback but varies with surgeon fatigue and experience. Automated impaction systems offer consistent energy delivery, potentially improving safety and efficiency. This study aimed, through a systematic review and meta-analysis, to compare the risk of IPF and operating times between automated and manual impaction during primary uncemented THA.MethodsWe performed a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of primary uncemented THA studies comparing automated and manual impaction techniques. Outcomes included IPFs and operating time. Pooled odds ratios (ORs) were calculated for IPF risk, and differences in mean operating time between techniques were analyzed using mean differences with 95% CIs.ResultsComparative data were available from five studies (n = 2,607 THAs). Meta-analysis included three studies (n = 2,377), with 1,408 hips in the automated group (14 IPFs) and 969 hips in the manual group (three IPFs). There was no significant difference in IPF risk between techniques (pooled OR 1.29; 95% CI 0.52 to 3.21; p = 0.59). Operating time was reported in three studies. The pooled mean difference suggested a shorter operating time with automated impaction (−1.34 minutes; 95% CI −15.60 to 12.92; p = 0.72).ConclusionAutomated impaction was not associated with a lower risk of IPF or operating time compared with manual techniques in primary uncemented THA. Fracture risk and efficiency may instead depend on femoral component–femur compatibility and implant sizing. Further randomized studies incorporating diverse femoral component designs and automated impaction systems, alongside formal economic evaluation, are required.Cite this article: Bone Jt Open 2026;7(7):838–844.Keywords: Periprosthetic femoral fracture, Hip arthroplasty, Intraoperative femoral fracture, Automated impaction, Cementless femoral stem, Total hip replacement, Primary uncemented total hip arthroplasty, Powered impaction, uncemented total hip arthroplasty, femoral components, hips, IPF, periprosthetic femoral fractures, fatigue, total hip arthroplasty (THA), femur, randomized controlled trials, periprosthetic fracture

Introduction

Intraoperative fractures during femoral canal preparation or femoral component insertion can have serious consequences, occurring in up to one in 20 primary total hip arthroplasties (THAs).1 Conventionally, both steps are performed by striking sequential broaches and the femoral component impactor, respectively, with a mallet to achieve press-fit of the uncemented component. The surgeon relies on technical skill and experience to deliver each impact with the appropriate force and trajectory. Close attention to acoustic changes in pitch is essential, as these may indicate cortical contact and signal that an additional strike could result in fracture.2 These fractures span a wide clinical spectrum, from clinically occult injuries identified only on postoperative imaging and frequently managed with protected weightbearing in the presence of a stable femoral component,3 to intraoperatively recognized fractures that increase operating time and necessitate immediate fixation or revision femoral component implantation. Such injuries may significantly alter the postoperative course, ranging from delayed mobilization to early revision surgery, and are associated with increased mortality.4

Automated impaction devices were introduced to improve the consistency of canal preparation and femoral component insertion.5 They aim to provide a controlled delivery of force, mitigate for a surgeon’s inconsistency in delivering measured force, and reduce surgeon fatigue.6 These devices may also help lower the risk of work-related injuries among surgeons.7 Additionally, it has been suggested that automated impaction may shorten operating times and reduce the risk of IPFs.8 As the risk of IPFs is multifactorial, influenced by patient- and implant-related factors,9 the force with which a surgeon impacts the broach varies substantially, and is also affected by mallet type and off-axis strikes. Despite ongoing uncertainty regarding their benefits and limitations, the uptake of automated impaction devices in THA has increased substantially, particularly in the USA.10

These devices differ in technical specifications, ergonomics, and noise profiles, and currently there are no comparative data between individual systems.11 Our aim was to objectively quantify intraoperative fracture risk and operating time by conducting a meta-analysis of studies comparing automated impaction devices with manual impaction.

Methods

Data sources

A systematic review of MEDLINE and EMBASE was conducted from database inception to 2 December 2024. The review protocol was prospectively registered with PROSPERO (CRD42024622187) and conducted in accordance with PRISMA guidelines (Figure 1). The literature search strategy was developed using the PICO framework with advice from a specialist librarian (see Acknowledgements); full search terms are provided in Appendix 1. Citation searching of all included full-text articles was performed to identify additional relevant studies not captured by the initial search.

PRISMA flow diagram of study identification, screening, and inclusion for automated impaction in primary uncemented total hip arthroplasty.

Eligibility criteria

An initial scoping search identified a single randomized controlled trial; therefore, this review was limited to peer-reviewed cohort studies and case series. Eligible studies reported intraoperative periprosthetic femoral fracture (IPF) rates in patients undergoing primary uncemented THA in which an automated impaction device was used and the implant was specified. Studies were included if early fractures (occurring intraoperatively or identified on immediate postoperative radiographs) could be isolated from overall periprosthetic fracture rates, if all IPFs were reported including those not requiring surgical intervention, and if the use of an automated impaction device was explicitly documented.

Revision procedures, hemiarthroplasty, hip resurfacing, bilateral-specific studies, learning-curve analyses, studies evaluating other forms of automation, and studies limited to specific patient populations (e.g. avascular necrosis or inflammatory arthropathy) were excluded. Conference abstracts, duplicate cohorts, and systematic reviews were excluded to prevent duplication of data.

Study selection

All abstracts and full-text articles were independently screened by two authors (SS and EB), with disagreements resolved by consensus. Where required data were missing, corresponding authors were contacted to request additional information. A third reviewer (MB) independently verified all included studies.

Quality assessment

Study quality was independently assessed bytwo authors (SS and EB) using the Newcastle-Ottawa Scale (NOS), modified for intraoperative fracture studies.12 This tool evaluates three domains: selection of study cohorts, comparability between groups, and outcome assessment. Studies were additionally graded using the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality (AHRQ) system.13 Disagreements were resolved by consensus. Particular attention was given to potential sources of bias including retrospective study design, imbalance in surgical approach or implant selection, and incomplete reporting of intraoperative fracture detection methods.

Statistical analysis

IPF risk was pooled using a random-effects model with inverse-variance weighting, a restricted maximum-likelihood (REML) estimator for between-study variance, and a continuity correction of 0.5 for zero-event studies. Operating time was analyzed using a random-effects model with Hartung–Knapp adjustment. Where SDs were not reported, they were estimated using the method described by Shi et al.14 Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. All analyses were conducted using R (R Core Team, 2023; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Austria) with the meta package.

Results

Study selection and characteristics

The systematic review identified seven relevant studies.8,15-20 Five studies compared automated and manual impaction techniques in primary uncemented THA, comprising a total of 5,656 hips. All studies evaluated the KINCISE automated impaction device used in conjunction with the ACTIS femoral component (DePuy Synthes, USA); one study additionally included the CORAIL femoral component (DePuy Synthes). Data on intraoperative periprosthetic fracture rates and operating time were extracted where available.

Meta-analysis

Comparative data were available from five studies (n = 2,607). Meta-analysis included three studies (n = 2,377), comprising 1,408 hips in the automated impaction group (14 IPFs) and 969 hips in the manual impaction group (three IPFs). There was no significant difference in intraoperative fracture risk between groups (pooled odds ratio 1.29; 95% CI 0.52 to 3.21; p = 0.59; I² = 0%) (Figure 2). The point estimate suggested a possible higher fracture risk with automated impaction, although the wide CI indicates substantial uncertainty.

Forest plot of intraoperative periprosthetic fracture risk comparing automated and manual impaction in primary uncemented total hip arthroplasty. OR, odds ratio.

Operating time was reported in three studies. The pooled mean difference suggested a shorter operating time with automated impaction (−1.34 minutes; 95% CI −15.60 to 12.92; p = 0.72), although this difference was not statistically significant (Figure 3).

Discussion

In this systematic review and meta-analysis, automated impaction was not associated with a lower risk of intraoperative periprosthetic femoral fracture or shorter operating time compared with manual impaction in primary uncemented THA. Fracture rates were low in both groups, and no statistically significant difference in operating time was identified. Overall, the available evidence does not demonstrate a clear intraoperative advantage of automated over manual impaction.

Accurate femoral component sizing and achieving an adequate fit are essential in uncemented implants to minimize micromotion and facilitate bony ingrowth.21 An undersized femoral component may loosen under repeated loading and, if not integrated, may initiate and propagate a fracture.22 More commonly, this process can manifest as thigh pain which often warrants revision surgery.23,24 Only one included study evaluated the effect of automated impaction on implant sizing using radiological measurements.15 Thalody et al15 assessed canal-fill ratio (CFR) and coronal alignment, to evaluate implant sizing. They reported that automated impaction produced higher CFRs, more consistent alignment, and a tendency towards the insertion of larger femoral components. A recently published study outside our review timeframe examined similar canal-fill metrics and found that automated impaction yielded improved canal fill and, on average, larger femoral components, although these differences did not reach statistical significance.25 The tendency for automated devices to insert larger femoral components has also been reported by Thomason et al,8 who found that automated impaction resulted in a statistically significant increase in femoral component size compared with the preoperatively templated size. Bhimani et al20 similarly observed larger femoral components in the automated impaction group, although the appropriateness of sizing was not assessed relative to radiological canal filling or preoperative templating. Despite femoral component oversizing being recognized as a risk factor for IPF,26,27 this did not confer an increased fracture rate in any of these studies. This suggests that automated impaction devices may enable more consistent canal filling without increasing fracture risk. While encouraging, it should be noted that all studies used the KINCISE system and either the Corail or the ACTIS collared, triple-tapered short femoral component; therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to other femoral component designs.21-23 Further work is needed to determine whether automated impaction devices reduce postoperative loosening by enabling more accurate femoral component sizing. This is particularly relevant as the adoption of newer automated systems increases and a broader range of femoral component geometries are implanted using these devices.

In a recent review, Kutzer et al10 identified four main devices currently available: the KINCISE (DePuy Synthes), HAMMR (Zimmer Biomet, USA), orthodrive (De Soutter Medical, UK), and the Woodpecker (Integrated Medical Technologies, USA).10 At present, only the KINCISE and Woodpecker systems have published clinical data. Although our search strategy was robust, it did not retrieve the study by Fujii et al,28 which compared 80 THAs performed with the Woodpecker pneumatic broaching system with 89 performed using manual broaching. Unlike powered impaction systems that deliver discrete strikes, the Woodpecker employs continuous high-frequency pneumatic oscillations to seat the broach, representing a distinctly different preparation technique. Its exclusive evaluation with the INITIA (Kyocera, Japan) taper-wedge femoral component further contributes to methodological heterogeneity. Equally, its omission is unlikely to influence our conclusions, as no fractures occurred in either cohort and operating time was not reported. Although part of their review, the Woodpecker was not evaluated for maximum noise production.

Automated impaction devices may produce similar noise levels to those of manual techniques,10 but it is uncertain whether their acoustic profiles still enable the surgeon to detect the critical change in pitch that can indicate an appropriately sized femoral component,29 or warn of a potential impending fracture.2 Instrumented hammers have been employed to quantify impaction forces, and their integration with acoustic monitoring may enable real-time thresholds that prevent further impaction when high-risk acoustic signatures are detected.30 Until then, surgeons must remain adept at interpreting acoustic cues, recognizing that automated devices produce distinct sound patterns that may require specific familiarity.

Despite their widespread use in the USA, no published studies have evaluated the health-economic implications of automated impaction systems compared with manual impaction. By contrast, robotic-assisted arthroplasty has been assessed using formal cost-effectiveness frameworks that incorporate capital acquisition and maintenance costs, per-case disposable costs, operating theatre time, and downstream economic consequences related to complications, revision surgery, and length of stay. Several analyses have additionally reported cost per quality-adjusted life year and modelled the influence of institutional case volume on economic viability.31 No comparable analyses exist for automated impaction devices. Although these systems are marketed as reducing surgeon fatigue, there is similarly an absence of evidence assessing their effect on intraoperative efficiency or surgical throughput. Similarly, fatigue may also play a role in the surgeon achieving satisfactory femoral component-canal fit.32 Long-term data evaluating whether improvements in femoral component–canal fit translate into reduced rates of loosening and late periprosthetic femoral fractures remain awaited, but may ultimately support the wider adoption of automated devices if proven. In the absence of demonstrable reductions in fracture risk or operating time compared with manual impaction, broader adoption of this new technology within cost-conscious healthcare systems is likely to depend on future evidence that has yet to emerge. Nevertheless, potential advantages for the surgeon and outcomes not examined in this analysis may still prove to be clinically relevant.

The limited evidence base is likely to be one factor contributing to the more gradual uptake of automated impaction systems outside the USA. Although registry data demonstrate a rapid increase in the use of the ACTIS femoral component within the American Joint Replacement Registry,33 the use of automated impaction devices themselves is not currently recorded, and their prevalence cannot be determined from publicly available data. Consequently, relationships between implant selection and impaction technique cannot be assessed using existing registry datasets.

All three analyzed studies assessed difference in age, sex, BMI, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) grade.8,16,20 Bhimani et al20 and Thomason et al8 showed no statistically significant differences between the automated impaction and manual groups in either of these domains. In contrast, Strait et al16 reported that patients in the manual broaching group were marginally older and had a slightly higher preoperative ASA grade than those in the automated group, although the absolute differences between groups were small. All three studies included procedures performed exclusively through the anterior approach, which enhanced comparability between cohorts but limited the generalizability of the findings. Preoperative diagnoses were only reported by Bhimani et al20 and Thomason et al,8 and reflected the typical distribution of indications for THA, with osteoarthritis accounting for the majority of cases.33,34 Bone quality was reported only by Thomason et al;8 however, Bhimani et al20 noted that the single calcar fracture observed in their series occurred in an osteoporotic female.

This study represents the first meta-analytic comparison of automated and manual impaction techniques; however, the analysis is limited by the small number of eligible studies. The small number of intraoperative fracture events limits statistical precision and the ability to detect small differences between impaction techniques resulting in wide confidence intervals and increasing the risk of a type II error; pooled estimates should therefore be interpreted with caution. While the point estimate favoured manual impaction, the wide confidence interval indicates that the true effect remains uncertain. Similarly, the wide confidence intervals for operating time suggest substantial between-study variability, and the pooled mean difference of less than two minutes is unlikely to represent a clinically meaningful reduction in operating time. Only one randomized controlled trial was available,8 and it evaluated a single surgical approach—an important determinant of intraoperative periprosthetic fracture risk.35 Furthermore, only one automated impaction device was included. While this enhances comparability across studies, it limits the generalizability of the findings to the broader range of devices currently in clinical use. Finally, heterogeneity arose from a single study that included the Corail femoral component, the design and fixation philosophy of which differ from the ACTIS, and the results of which could not be distinguished due to pooled data.8

Future studies should incorporate proper randomization between manual and automated impaction and evaluate the effects of different surgical approaches and, critically, surgeon experience.36 Cost-benefit analyses should also be undertaken to determine whether any observed reductions in fracture risk or improvements in operating theatre efficiency translate into meaningful economic value. Finally, radiological evaluation is required to clarify the interplay between automated impaction and the concurrent adoption of short, triple-tapered C3 femoral components,32 and to establish their safety relative to established designs. Concurrently, radiological assessment of femoral component fit, together with long-term follow-up examining loosening and late periprosthetic fracture rates, may reveal important differences between uncemented femoral component designs.

As these technologies become more widespread, there is a potential risk of skill attrition, particularly among surgeons trained predominantly with devices perceived to be safer. This may be most consequential in revision arthroplasty, where increased complexity demands a highly refined tactile sense to avoid complications. Although newer systems such as the KINCISE V2 are approved for use in revision surgery and offer both extraction and impaction functions,37 a widening skill gap may nonetheless emerge—one that technology alone may be unable to bridge.

In conclusion, automated impaction was not associated with a lower risk of intraoperative periprosthetic femoral fracture or shorter operating time compared with manual techniques in primary uncemented THA using predominantly the ACTIS femoral component. However, the limited number of studies and fracture events introduces substantial uncertainty around the pooled estimate. Current evidence suggests that outcomes may depend more on femoral component–femur compatibility through femoral component selection than on impaction method alone. Larger, well-designed studies with radiological assessment, long-term follow-up, and economic evaluation are needed to clarify whether automated impaction provides meaningful clinical or system-level benefit.

Take home message

- Automated impaction did not reduce intraoperative periprosthetic femoral fracture risk or operating time compared with manual impaction in primary uncemented total hip arthroplasty.

- Further adequately powered randomized controlled trials, including economic evaluation, are required before widespread adoption can be justified.
