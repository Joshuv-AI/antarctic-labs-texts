Surgical Management of Congenital Lung Malformations: Indications, Timing, and Outcomes
Authors: Chang Hyun Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Congenital lung malformations (CLMs) represent a heterogeneous group of developmental anomalies increasingly detected through prenatal imaging. The optimal management of asymptomatic patients remains controversial. This review summarizes current evidence regarding surgical indications, timing, techniques, and outcomes. Although some studies report low rates of symptom development, longer-term data suggest that many initially asymptomatic patients eventually become symptomatic and require surgery. Delayed intervention after infection is associated with increased operative difficulty and morbidity. Additionally, the potential risk of malignant transformation, though rare, supports consideration of early resection. Minimally invasive approaches, particularly thoracoscopic surgery, have demonstrated superior postoperative recovery, musculoskeletal outcomes, and pulmonary function compared with thoracotomy. While lung-sparing procedures are increasingly utilized, careful patient selection is essential to minimize residual disease. Overall, early elective resection using minimally invasive techniques appears safe and may optimize long-term outcomes in patients with CLMs.

Introduction

Congenital lung malformations (CLMs) comprise a heterogeneous group of developmental anomalies, including congenital pulmonary airway malformation (CPAM), bronchopulmonary sequestration (BPS), congenital lobar overinflation (CLO), bronchogenic cysts, and hybrid lesions. With advances in prenatal imaging, particularly fetal ultrasonography, the detection of these lesions has markedly increased [1-3]. Although many CLMs remain asymptomatic during early life, a substantial proportion may later present with recurrent respiratory infections or, in rare cases, malignant transformation [4,5]. These potential complications continue to drive ongoing debate regarding the optimal management of CLMs.

Historically, surgical resection has been the cornerstone of treatment for symptomatic CLMs, providing both definitive management and the opportunity for histopathological confirmation [5-7]. With the advent and widespread adoption of minimally invasive surgical techniques, particularly video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS), there has been a shift in clinical practice toward early intervention, including in asymptomatic patients [8-10]. The potential benefits of early resection have been highlighted, including prevention of infectious or malignant outcomes, preservation of lung capacity, and clarification of diagnosis [5,6,8]. However, key aspects—including the optimal timing of surgery, selection of surgical approach, and the indications for resection in asymptomatic individuals—remain subjects of ongoing debate.

This review provides a comprehensive overview of current surgical approaches to CLMs, with particular focus on patient selection, optimal timing of intervention, surgical outcomes, and postoperative pulmonary function. By synthesizing recent surgical evidence and long-term follow-up data, this review aims to inform best clinical practices and guide decision-making in the management of these complex congenital anomalies.

Methods

This review was conducted through a structured literature search using PubMed and Google Scholar databases. Relevant studies published up to December 2024 were identified using combinations of the following keywords: congenital lung malformation, congenital pulmonary airway malformation, bronchopulmonary sequestration, congenital lobar overinflation, bronchogenic cyst, surgical resection, thoracoscopy, video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery, pulmonary function, and pleuropulmonary blastoma. Reference lists of identified articles were additionally reviewed for relevant citations. Studies were selected based on relevance to surgical indications, operative techniques, oncologic risk, and postoperative pulmonary outcomes.

The institutional experience described in this review is based on a retrospective analysis of 1,056 consecutive patients who underwent surgical resection for CLMs at Seoul National University Hospital between January 2000 and December 2023. Patients were included if they had a confirmed diagnosis of CLM and underwent pulmonary resection at our institution. Data on operative approach, type of resection, postoperative complications, and hospital stay were extracted from prospectively maintained institutional records.

Surgical indications and preoperative considerations

Most CLMs are detected during routine prenatal ultrasound screening, and accordingly the majority of cases are diagnosed before birth [3,6,7]. However, the accurate diagnosis of the lesion—whether it is a CPAM, BPS, CLO, or bronchogenic cyst—usually requires confirmation by postnatal computed tomography (CT) imaging and surgical pathology [11,12]. For neonates who present after birth with severe clinical signs such as respiratory distress or mediastinal compression caused by a large intrathoracic lesion, early surgical intervention is indicated [13-15].

In contrast, because most affected infants remain asymptomatic, definitive treatment is often deferred until later in childhood. Given this context, the need for prophylactic surgical resection in asymptomatic cases remains a matter of debate. On one hand, researchers opposing surgery argue that surgery is unnecessary for asymptomatic lesions [16,17], citing the low incidence of infection [18-22] and the very low observed rate of malignant transformation associated with these malformations [23].

On the other hand, proponents of early intervention emphasize the substantial long-term risk of recurrent pulmonary infections [19,21,24-27], the potential for future malignant development [28], and the increased rate of postoperative complications if surgery is delayed until after an infection has developed [29]. Therefore, they advocate for elective surgical removal of the lesion despite the absence of symptoms. In this review, we examine the evidence underpinning both of these opposing perspectives.

Symptom development and surgical indications in asymptomatic CLM patients

In CLM, the most frequent clinical manifestations are related to infection, and patients can also present with pneumothorax or respiratory distress. The argument that asymptomatic pediatric patients do not require surgery is often based on the reported low incidence of symptom development in this population. For instance, Ng et al. [18] reported that among 72 patients with CLM followed for 5 years, only 5% developed symptoms. Similarly, a meta- analysis by Stanton et al. [30] found that only 3.2% of neonates with CLM became symptomatic during a 1-year follow-up, indicating a relatively low rate of symptom onset.

However, these studies have limitations, including small sample sizes, relatively short follow-up durations, and the exclusion of patients who underwent surgical resection, meaning they focused mainly on cases expected to have a relatively favorable prognosis. In contrast, other studies with longer follow-up periods have reported symptom development in 24%–86% of patients, with a substantial fraction of initially asymptomatic patients eventually requiring surgical intervention. For example, Criss et al. [19] reported that among 39 initially asymptomatic patients, 33% developed symptoms and all of those patients underwent surgical treatment; similarly, Wong et al. [31] noted that 86% of 60 asymptomatic patients eventually became symptomatic and required surgical resection. Although the exact rates of symptom onset and the need for surgery vary widely between studies—owing to differences in patient cohorts, follow-up protocols, and duration—taken together, reported rates vary markedly according to follow-up duration, inclusion of surgically treated patients, and definitions of symptomatic disease. Consequently, the efficacy of surgical intervention in preventing subsequent pneumonia warrants further investigation in future studies.

Surgery after infection

A delayed, symptom-driven strategy has been advocated by some groups; however, surgery performed after pulmonary infection is technically more demanding and is associated with worse perioperative outcomes. Respiratory infection (most commonly pneumonia) in patients with CLM results in an intense inflammatory reaction in the lung and surrounding tissues. As a result of this inflammatory response, the lung tissue becomes more edematous and fragile, making it more susceptible to surgical injury. Furthermore, secondary adhesions around the vessels, bronchus, and fissures are very common, and dissection along these critical structures becomes difficult, often necessitating open thoracotomy.

Previous studies consistently indicate that surgical resection performed after the onset of symptoms in pediatric patients with CLMs is associated with increased operative complexity and postoperative morbidity (Table 2) [29,38-41]. Yuan et al. [38] demonstrated that preoperative overt infection was associated with greater intraoperative blood loss, longer operative time, and a higher rate of conversion to open surgery. Several subsequent studies reported poorer postoperative outcomes in symptomatic patients, including higher overall and respiratory complication rates, most commonly pneumonia, as well as prolonged hospital stays [39-41]. In addition, Elhattab et al. [29] found that symptomatic patients had higher conversion rates, longer operative times, and increased antibiotic requirements, although overall complication rates were comparable to those in asymptomatic patients. Taken together, these findings suggest that delaying surgical intervention until symptom development may not be a safe strategy for the management of asymptomatic CLMs.

Risk of development of malignant tumor

Another important rationale for recommending surgical resection in asymptomatic patients with CLMs is the potential risk of developing lung malignancy. In selected cases, the risk of malignancy may represent an important indication for surgical resection, potentially comparable to or exceeding the risk associated with recurrent respiratory infections. Although the overall incidence of lung malignancy in patients with CPAM is relatively low, it has been reported to be clinically significant under certain circumstances. Tumors arising in association with CLMs account for approximately 4% of all primary pulmonary malignant tumors in children [23].

The most frequently reported malignancy associated with CPAM is pleuropulmonary blastoma (PPB), while mucinous adenocarcinoma has also been described. The incidence of PPB in patients with CPAM type 4 has been reported to be as high as 4% in some series [42]. However, a large multicenter study including 521 patients demonstrated no cases of PPB among 344 patients with prenatally diagnosed lesions, whereas a markedly higher incidence of PPB (8.7%) was observed in patients whose lesions were not detected prenatally [43].

These findings raise several important questions regarding the relationship between CPAM and the development of PPB. First, PPB may arise postnatally through a mechanism distinct from prenatally detectable CPAM. Second, current prenatal diagnostic tools may be insufficiently sensitive to identify CPAM lesions that later give rise to PPB. Finally, the absence of PPB in prenatally diagnosed cases may reflect the fact that many of these patients undergo early surgical resection, thereby preventing malignant transformation. Further studies are required to clarify these possibilities.

Mucinous adenocarcinoma has been reported almost exclusively in CPAM type 1 and occurs at a low incidence of less than 1% [44]. Because most available data are derived from case reports and small case series, the true incidence and natural history of this malignancy remain poorly defined. Nevertheless, existing studies suggest that mucinous cell clusters within type 1 CPAM may serve as precursor lesions for malignancy [45] and are frequently associated with KRAS gene mutations [46].

The mortality rate of PPB has been reported to reach approximately 20%, and outcomes can be particularly poor in certain patient subsets [42,44,47]. Prognosis is closely related to the patient’s age at diagnosis and the histologic type of the tumor. In general, older age at diagnosis is associated with a higher likelihood of detecting PPB at a more advanced, solid stage. PPB is believed to progress over time from a purely cystic form (type I) to a mixed cystic–solid form (type II), and eventually to a purely solid tumor (type III). The median ages at diagnosis have been reported as approximately 8–10 months for type I, 35 months for type II, and 41–44 months for type III disease. Type III PPB carries the worst prognosis, with a reported 5-year survival rate of approximately 53% [48].

PPB is characterized by a biological tendency to evolve from a less aggressive cystic lesion into a highly aggressive solid sarcoma over time. Accordingly, older age at diagnosis likely reflects progression to a more malignant stage, which directly contributes to decreased survival [49]. Therefore, early detection during infancy, particularly before 1 year of age, followed by complete surgical resection at the cystic stage represents the most critical strategy for improving survival.

In this context, when a conservative management approach is selected for patients with congenital cystic lung malformations, clinicians must remain constantly aware of the potential risk of malignant transformation. The development of a growing cystic lesion or the appearance of a solid component should prompt strong consideration of early surgical intervention. These findings further support a proactive surgical approach in selected patients, particularly when reliable long-term surveillance cannot be ensured.

Timing of surgery and postoperative pulmonary function

From a surgical safety perspective, multiple studies indicate that early resection can be performed without increased perioperative risk. Duron et al. [50] reported that timing of surgery did not significantly affect operative duration, estimated blood loss, postoperative pneumothorax, or length of hospital stay, concluding that early elective surgery is as safe as delayed intervention in asymptomatic patients. Similarly, Conforti et al. [41] found that even very young infants operated on before 3–6 months of age did not experience higher complication rates or prolonged hospitalization compared with older children. Collectively, these data suggest that young age alone should not be considered a contraindication to surgery and that early resection may reduce the cumulative risks of infection or malignant transformation.

A major rationale for early surgery is the potential for compensatory lung growth during infancy and early childhood. Classical developmental studies indicate that alveolar multiplication is most active within the first 2 years of life, after which alveolar proliferation markedly slows and lung growth becomes primarily driven by expansion of existing alveoli [51,52]. This developmental window provides an anatomical basis for the concept that resection performed early may maximize the potential for true compensatory alveolar growth. Long-term clinical follow-up supports this notion: Lau et al. [53] reported that children who underwent thoracoscopic lobectomy for CPAM achieved pulmonary function comparable to healthy controls 5 years after surgery, suggesting effective functional adaptation of the remaining lung.

However, the long-term functional benefit of early surgery is not universally supported. Keijzer et al. [54] found no significant differences in spirometric outcomes (forced expiratory volume in one second [FEV1] and forced vital capacity [FVC]) between children operated on before versus after 2 years of age, although interpretation was limited by the small sample size. Furthermore, more recent physiologic and imaging studies raise concerns that compensatory “growth” may not always translate into completely normal lung function. Willers et al. [55] showed that although residual lung expands structurally into the resection space, functional magnetic resonance imaging revealed localized reductions in ventilation and perfusion that were not captured by conventional spirometry. Likewise, Dincel et al. [56] demonstrated higher lung clearance index values and subtle peripheral airway dysfunction in surgically treated patients despite relatively preserved global lung volumes, indicating persistent ventilation inhomogeneity.

These observations suggest that compensatory lung development after infant lobectomy may restore lung volume effectively yet may be accompanied by disproportionate airway growth or persistent small-airway impairment, a phenomenon described as dysanaptic growth [57]. Longitudinal studies also indicate that early postoperative adaptation initially relies on overinflation, whereas true alveolar multiplication may contribute more substantially after 2 years [58]. Therefore, although early surgery is widely considered safe and generally favored from a lung developmental perspective—maximizing the opportunity for compensatory growth—clinicians should remain aware that structural expansion does not necessarily guarantee complete functional normalization, and subtle residual deficits may persist even when conventional spirometry appears normal. This highlights the importance of distinguishing structural lung expansion from true functional recovery when evaluating long-term outcomes after early lung resection. This distinction is particularly important when counseling patients and families regarding the expected long-term outcomes after early resection.

Surgical techniques

VATS is now widely regarded as the preferred minimally invasive approach for the resection of CLMs, largely due to its minimally invasive nature and its potential to reduce long-term morbidity in growing children. Compared with traditional open thoracotomy, thoracoscopy avoids rib spreading and extensive muscle division, thereby minimizing postoperative pain, shortening hospitalization, and providing superior cosmetic outcomes. Cano et al. [59] first highlighted that VATS in asymptomatic infants could prevent thoracotomy-related musculoskeletal sequelae such as chest wall asymmetry, scoliosis, and rib fusion, which are particularly concerning during periods of rapid thoracic growth.

Long-term follow-up studies have consistently demonstrated that thoracoscopy offers significant advantages in preserving musculoskeletal development. Lawal et al. [60] evaluated 62 infants and children undergoing pulmonary resection (VATS vs. thoracotomy) with a mean follow-up of 3.8 years and reported substantially fewer thoracic deformities and scoliosis in the VATS group, along with better shoulder mobility and greater cosmetic satisfaction. Similarly, a large series of 144 thoracoscopic lobectomies reported no cases of scoliosis, chest wall deformity, or muscle weakness over up to 10 years of follow-up, reinforcing VATS as an effective strategy to avoid thoracotomy-associated growth disturbances [61]. More recently, Lam et al. [62] confirmed these findings in CPAM patients, demonstrating markedly lower rates of winged scapula (13% vs. 58%) and better preservation of thoracic symmetry after thoracoscopic resection compared with open surgery.

Beyond musculoskeletal outcomes, thoracoscopy may also provide superior long-term pulmonary function. Lau et al. [63] compared pulmonary function testing nearly a decade after surgery and found significantly higher FVC, FEV1, and diffusing capacity of the lung for carbon monoxide values in children treated with VATS compared with thoracotomy. The reduced pulmonary function observed after open surgery was attributed to subtle restrictive impairment likely related to respiratory muscle injury during thoracotomy. These results suggest that minimally invasive approaches may better preserve respiratory mechanics and functional lung capacity over time.

Evidence from larger datasets further supports the safety and efficacy of thoracoscopic surgery. A systematic review and meta-analysis by Adams et al. [64], encompassing over 1,626 pediatric resections, demonstrated a significantly lower overall complication rate and shorter hospital stay with thoracoscopy compared with thoracotomy, indicating at least equivalent, if not superior, perioperative safety. In parallel, newer minimally invasive platforms are emerging. Li et al. [65] reported that robotic-assisted pulmonary resections achieved comparable complication rates, chest tube duration, length of stay, and scar satisfaction relative to thoracoscopy, although operative times were longer. Collectively, current evidence strongly supports thoracoscopic resection as the preferred minimally invasive approach for pediatric CLMs, offering advantages in musculoskeletal preservation, pulmonary function, and postoperative recovery.

As with any advanced minimally invasive procedure, thoracoscopic resection for CLMs is associated with a learning curve. In the early phase of program establishment, operative times are typically longer and conversion rates to open thoracotomy are higher, reflecting the technical demands of operating in the small thoracic cavity of a neonate or infant. Park et al. [66] demonstrated that a dedicated thoracoscopic surgery program requires a cumulative experience of approximately 20–30 cases before operative times and conversion rates stabilize. These findings underscore the importance of centralized referral and dedicated surgical training in optimizing outcomes for CLM resection.

Sublobar resection

Lobectomy has traditionally been considered the standard surgical treatment for CLMs, primarily to ensure complete removal of abnormal tissue and minimize the risk of residual disease. However, increasing attention has been directed toward parenchyma-sparing approaches such as segmentectomy, given the importance of preserving functional lung tissue in growing children. Several studies suggest that lung-sparing procedures may offer functional advantages, particularly in the early postoperative period. Huang et al. [67], using propensity score–matched analysis, demonstrated better early pulmonary function recovery following segmentectomy compared with lobectomy, although pulmonary function normalized in both groups within 2 years. Similarly, Joo et al. [68] reported that thoracoscopic segmentectomy could be performed safely with a very low conversion rate and perioperative outcomes comparable to lobectomy, while older children undergoing segmentectomy showed better preservation of FEV1.

Despite these potential benefits, segmentectomy is technically more demanding and may be associated with slightly increased operative complexity. Cheng et al. [69], in a large cohort study, reported longer operative times and marginally greater blood loss in the segmentectomy group compared with lobectomy, although complication rates were similar between procedures. Earlier experience also highlighted technical challenges, including relatively high conversion rates during the initial adoption of parenchyma-sparing techniques [70], emphasizing the importance of surgical expertise and careful patient selection.

A key concern regarding sublobar resection is the potential risk of residual disease. Early meta-analyses suggested relatively high residual lesion rates after segmentectomy, approaching 15% [30]. However, more recent reviews have reported substantially lower rates of approximately 2%, because earlier series likely included non-anatomic resections, limited imaging, and early learning-curve cases. Anatomic segmentectomy may offer an advantage over wedge resection by reducing the risk of residual lesions, although robust comparative data remains limited. Most residual lesions appear clinically silent and are typically detected during surveillance imaging, with only rare reports of infection or malignant transformation. Emerging technologies, including fluorescence-guided surgery using inhaled indocyanine green, may further enhance the precision of lung-sparing resections by improving intraoperative identification of abnormal parenchyma [71]. Collectively, current evidence suggests that parenchyma-sparing surgery can be performed safely in selected patients while preserving lung function, although careful surgical planning remains essential to ensure complete resection.

Experience in Seoul National University Hospital

Seoul National University Children’s Hospital is a high- volume tertiary referral center for pediatric thoracic surgery. Between 2000 and 2023, a total of 1,056 patients with CLMs underwent surgical resection at our institution. A thoracoscopic surgery program was established in 2005, and since its introduction, the overall proportion of thoracoscopic procedures has reached 87%, increasing to 95% after 2010 (Table 3).

At our institution, the standard management strategy for CLMs is elective surgical resection regardless of symptom status, once patients reach 1 year of age. The specific timing and surgical approach are tailored to the severity of the lesion at presentation. In neonates presenting with significant mediastinal compression or shift and respiratory distress attributable to a large intrathoracic mass, urgent surgical intervention is indicated; in these cases, open resection via thoracotomy is typically performed owing to the technical demands of operating on a hemodynamically compromised neonate. In patients with moderate-to-large lesions causing partial mediastinal displacement but without overt respiratory compromise, early elective resection is performed at approximately 3 to 6 months of age using thoracoscopic techniques. For the majority of asymptomatic patients with incidentally detected CLMs, elective thoracoscopic resection after 1 year of age represents the standard of care at our institution. In cases where parents decline elective resection following thorough counseling, regular surveillance is conducted in the pediatric outpatient clinic, with careful monitoring for symptom development or radiologic progression.

When open surgery is required, thoracotomy is performed using a muscle-sparing technique that avoids division of the serratus anterior and latissimus dorsi muscles, thereby minimizing postoperative muscle weakness and reducing the risk of chest wall growth asymmetry. For thoracoscopic resection, the number of ports ranges from 1 to 3 and is determined based on the location and size of the lesion, the patient’s age and body habitus, and the anticipated operative complexity. One-lung ventilation is achieved under general anesthesia via selective blockade of the ipsilateral main bronchus using an endobronchial balloon blocker, as previously described by our group [72]. The most frequently used configuration is a 2-port technique, consisting of a 2-cm utility port placed in the sixth intercostal space and a 5-mm working port in the eighth intercostal space. Fissure development and lung parenchyma dissection are performed using an energy-sealing device (LigaSure; Medtronic). Vascular structures are ligated and divided using Hem-o-lok polymer clips (Teleflex) in combination with the LigaSure device. Bronchial transection and fissure completion are performed using a Signia powered stapling system (Medtronic).

To date, no perioperative mortality has been observed in this cohort. The overall postoperative complication rate was 5.2%, with prolonged air leakage being the most frequent complication, occurring in 2.3% of patients. No major complications, including respiratory failure or bronchopleural fistula, were encountered. During the initial phase of program establishment, operative times were longer and a considerable proportion of cases required conversion to open thoracotomy. As operative proficiency increased, however, both operative times and conversion rates progressively declined and subsequently stabilized in our series [66]. The thoracotomy conversion rate was 23.3% (7 of 30 patients) before 2010 and has since decreased to 0.9% (8 of 887 patients) after 2010 (Table 4).

Segmentectomies were performed in 189 patients, including 172 who underwent standalone segmentectomy and 17 who underwent segmentectomy in combination with lobectomy. Three patients underwent chest CT scans due to recurrent pneumonia after segmentectomy; however, no residual lesions were detected in these cases. At our institution, routine postoperative chest CT follow-up is not performed in asymptomatic patients. Only 1 patient, who underwent right upper lobectomy for CPAM with adenocarcinoma, developed a small residual lesion in the right middle lobe and subsequently underwent additional wedge resection during the follow-up period. No long-term mortality was observed during the follow-up period.

Conclusion

In conclusion, CLMs are associated with a meaningful long-term risk of infectious complications and, in selected subtypes, malignant transformation, supporting consideration of early elective resection before symptom development. Surgery performed after infection is consistently associated with increased technical difficulty and higher morbidity, reinforcing the limitations of a delayed, symptom- driven approach. Thoracoscopic resection has emerged as the preferred surgical strategy, offering superior musculoskeletal and pulmonary outcomes compared with open thoracotomy. Although parenchyma-sparing approaches are increasingly utilized, careful patient selection and surgical planning remain essential to ensure complete resection. While early surgery appears advantageous from a developmental perspective, complete functional normalization is not always guaranteed, highlighting the need for long-term follow-up. Overall, current evidence supports early minimally invasive resection as a safe and effective strategy in appropriately selected patients.
