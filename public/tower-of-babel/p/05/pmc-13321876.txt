Immunogenicity assays are biomarker assays: is the 3-tiered paradigm fit-for-purpose? An illustrative case study
Authors: Lauren F. Stevenson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThis perspective highlights challenges associated with implementation of the 3-tiered paradigm over the past two-plus decades. A case study is used to illustrate re-analysis of anti-drug antibody (ADA) data from a Phase 1 clinical study of a monoclonal antibody, applying a biomarker-oriented approach. Unlike traditional analyses that focus on “positive” responses defined by statistical cut points, this approach incorporates all data, including “negative” responses, to characterize complete response profiles.By leveraging signal-to-noise (S/N) and raw signal data from the screening tier, the analysis provides a more granular, contextualized view of immunogenicity. Placebo data further inform longitudinal variability within the study population. This approach demonstrably clarifies apparent baseline positivity, distinguishes true treatment-emergent responses, and reveals that some subjects classified as ADA-positive under the traditional paradigm reflect biological variability or low-level, clinically irrelevant responses.Continuous readouts such as S/N enhance interpretation relative to titers and enable earlier insight into response dynamics without requiring additional assay tiers. Collectively, these findings underscore limitations of the current paradigm, including potential inflation of incidence, loss of clinical context, and mischaracterization of program risk. A biomarker-based framework offers an opportunity to generate more informative immunogenicity data more efficiently, supporting improved decision-making and facilitating timely regulatory evaluation.

Background

Immunogenicity guidance in context

More than two decades ago, biotherapeutics were simpler in structure and complexity (e.g., monoclonal antibodies and recombinant proteins), yet less human(ized). Drug administration was primarily systemic, assays were relatively rudimentary, and technology platforms were still developing. Altogether, given the newness of the field, the relative clinical impact of immunogenicity incidence versus magnitude of immunogenicity response was not well understood. Limited experience meant limited data sets to inform what factors would truly differentiate high vs. low-risk programs – whether molecule design, manufacturing processes, mechanism of action, patient population, or some other parameter.

The field’s relative inexperience came into sharp relief when alarming safety events occurred with EPREX® (epoetin alfa; EPO). An unexpected increase in immunogenicity led to increased risk of development of neutralizing antibodies that cross-reacted with endogenous erythropoietin, leading to pure red cell aplasia in vulnerable patients [1–3]. Prior to these events, immunogenicity evaluations were not routine but instead tended to be reactive and performed only in response to an observed impact on pharmacokinetics (PK) or other clinical measures. However, the EPO case understandably triggered a change in thinking, with a heightened focus on ensuring prevention of similar events in the future. Developing a means to sensitively detect and characterize anti-drug immune responses was therefore deemed critical. It would provide a means to collect larger data sets that would better inform understanding of immunogenicity risks and clinical impacts. It is important to keep this context in mind. Guidance was written in response to tragic safety events that occurred with a specific molecule under specific conditions. The fear that unforeseen events could happen again led to a desire to detect any/all immunogenicity for all biologic therapeutics.

Today, we have the advantage of over 20 years of collected data and experience to inform our understanding. However, our efforts have included significant deviation from the original intent of guidance. Notably, the first sentence of the introduction section of FDA’s Guidance for industry: Immunogenicity Testing of Therapeutic Protein Products — Developing and Validating Assays for Anti-Drug Antibody Detection states: “This guidance provides recommendations to facilitate industry’s development and validation of assays for assessment of the immunogenicity of therapeutic protein products during clinical trials” [4]. Despite being written for this limited scope, this and related guidances have frequently been applied to both preclinical and clinical studies in support of the entire landscape of biologic (and some non-biologic!) therapeutics [5,6]. Many of these cases represent overapplication or outright misapplication of the guidance, since the context of use for preclinical studies (understanding ADA impact on PK and toxicokinetic (TK) parameters) differs from that for clinical studies (understanding benefit/risk for patients). Many new modalities and their attendant mechanisms of action are beyond what could ever have been imagined when guidance was first written. Furthermore, although risk-based approaches have been encouraged, in practice, regardless of perceived risk of a given molecule, three tiers of analysis (screen, confirm and titer) have been universally applied. Risk assessments quickly devolved to only considering when (not if) a neutralizing antibody assay should be conducted. Fortunately, this blanket adoption of the testing paradigm resulted in accumulation of a very large data set, which has informed some key learnings.

Broader conceptual shifts have also occurred. There is more widespread recognition that guidance was written for application in clinical studies, with most experts agreeing that ADA testing in preclinical studies should be implemented on a case-by-case basis as needed to understand exposure and interpretation of toxicity studies [9]. Additionally, discussions at multiple scientific forums indicate growing support for evaluating drug tolerance in proper context as the industry has accumulated countless cases where highly (arguably overly) drug tolerant assays for low-risk molecules have resulted in inflated reporting of incidence without correlation with clinical impact [18,19,22–24]. This confounds our ability to identify and characterize truly meaningful responses, leads to inappropriate comparisons between immunogenicity ‘rates’ across competitor molecules, and causes organizational anxiety that consumes time and resources, and can result in deprioritization of potentially promising molecules due to misperception of immunogenicity liability. Lastly, applying current guidance to new modalities is now better understood as misapplication that risks generating uninformative data sets and thereby not delivering the data required to address the specific needs of these molecules and modalities [25–27].

Our collected experience has also allowed us to build a better understanding of risk. Risk is typically evaluated by considering potential for eliciting an immune response and the resulting clinical impact. Many organizations have historically categorized a molecule’s risk by likelihood of incidence versus likely clinical impact with both aspects carrying equal weighting. This can erroneously designate drugs with very low risk of clinical impact as moderate risk [28–30]. In truth, risk is defined by clinical impact, not incidence.

Incidence of ADA positivity can be deliberately manipulated by assay platform selection and adjusting assay conditions to alter assay sensitivity, enhance or reduce drug tolerance, and more. Many programs have reduced their perceived immunogenicity burden by redeveloping their assays to be less sensitive. Incidence without impact is not informative; it is a distraction. By comparison, clinical impact is defined by safety signals, and/or loss of exposure, pharmacodynamic (PD) effects, and efficacy. Assays should be designed to reliably detect responses that lead to these effects. If we revisit the idea of risk categorization with a focus on clinical impact, then high-risk is easily identified by cases where there is real potential for impact on safety. Such molecules should be monitored early and often and via the most relevant assays [31–34]. Molecules with no definable risk for safety events would therefore be considered lower risk. If a given program is collecting ADA samples to be analyzed at a later date, this is a clear indicator that the molecule is not considered to be high-risk. Ultimately, molecules are either high risk, or not high risk.

Revisiting the origins of the 3-tiered paradigm

The canonical teaching is that the three-tiered paradigm is designed to ensure that no true positives are missed. The screening tier incorporates a statistical cut point with a 5% false positive rate to ensure there are no false negatives. The confirmation tier then corrects for any false positives that come through screening and finally, magnitudes of true responses are determined in the titration tier. As described, this gives the impression of a linear thought process from screen to confirm to titer, but in truth the thought process began with titers. When considering how best to measure immunogenicity, the nascent field of biotherapeutics naturally turned to the vaccine field, which was far more mature, and from there we borrowed the concept of measuring magnitude of response using titers [35,36]. However, unlike vaccines, which are designed to elicit large immune responses, biotherapeutics are designed with the intent to minimize immunogenicity. For biotherapeutics, it was expected that many, and hopefully most, samples would be ‘negative,’ so it would not be operationally efficient, nor time- and cost-effective, to titer all samples. Hence, screening and confirmation tiers arose to limit the number of samples to titer.

Interestingly, three tiers of analysis, along with the associated efforts required to generate their statistical cut points, have proven to be slow and expensive to execute, perhaps equaling or even exceeding the labor- and cost-intensiveness that titration of all samples might have incurred. The gravest unintended consequence of the current paradigm, though, is that the context of use (COU) for biotherapeutic drug development is not best served by titers. The COU for vaccine titers is to measure very large responses and those measurements do not require fine precision. Conversely, in drug development, it is important to understand very low (especially for high-risk molecules), low, moderate, and large to very large responses. We require an understanding of the full landscape of response development and relative magnitude of responses and this more granular level of understanding requires finer precision than titers can provide. For the COU of drug development, titers should never have been the starting place.

Despite these 20/20 hindsight insights, implementation of the 3-tiered paradigm for virtually all biologics programs for more than two decades has largely worked, albeit in a complicated fashion. It has also afforded us the opportunity to uncover some inherent pitfalls of the approach. Most strikingly, the current paradigm, which was intended to ensure that we do not miss anything, causes us to ignore and discard data. By making a binary call of positive or negative at the screening stage and thereafter ignoring the entirety of the ‘negative’ data set, we lose any/all insights the totality of screening data can provide. The current paradigm further confounds data interpretation: confirmation of very low responses, including false positives near statistical cut points that represent biological variability only, inflate immunogenicity incidence that does not correlate with clinical impact. High-sensitivity assays with low cut point factors, coupled with inherent imprecision of titers, can also give appearance of large magnitude responses from samples with low magnitude signals. Ultimately, final reported data provides an incomplete picture of the subjects’ response profiles and are presented in difficult-to-digest tables that do not provide an intuitive nor clear picture of immunogenicity at the individual or population level, leading one to wonder why we would choose to discard informative data.

Taken together, these issues cannot be resolved through incremental adjustments to cut points or tier execution, since they arise from a fundamental disconnect between the three-tiered paradigm and the COU for immunogenicity testing in drug development. Instead, a paradigm-level reframing of immunogenicity measurement and interpretation is warranted.

A better way?

Is there a better way? With our accumulated experience in the immunogenicity realm, along with concomitant experience implementing biomarkers in drug development, we are now equipped to note that immunogenicity is a biomarker. As defined in the BEST (Biomarkers, EndpointS, and other Tools) Resource, a biomarker is “a defined characteristic that is measured as an indicator of normal biological processes, pathogenic processes, or biological responses to an exposure or intervention, including therapeutic interventions [37]. Accordingly, immunogenicity is undeniably a biological response to a therapeutic intervention. Therefore, immunogenicity assays are biomarker assays. By adopting a ‘biomarker assay’ mind-set and focusing on the COU of identifying and characterizing clinically relevant immunogenicity for our drug programs, we can ensure that we develop and validate assays that are truly fit-for-purpose. While this may sound like a daunting reinvention of our field, there is good news. Simply mining the totality of data available from our screening assays with a biomarker perspective provides a means to understand preexisting immunoreactivity, treatment-emergent responses, magnitude of responses, and characterization of response levels that are clinically impactful, all without need for cut points or additional tiers of analysis. The following case study is intended as an illustrative example supporting the broader conceptual perspective discussed throughout this article. It highlights some common challenges that arise when putting the current 3-tiered paradigm into practice and demonstrates how adopting a biomarker assay approach to analyzing the screening data can bring clarity and context to data interpretation.

CASE study

A fully validated assay – but is it fit-for-purpose?

A Meso-Scale Discovery (MSD)™ bridging ADA assay with acid sample pretreatment was fully validated at a GLP compliant bioanalytical laboratory according to current immunogenicity guidance and published best practices to support clinical studies for a low-risk antagonistic monoclonal antibody therapeutic [4–6,21,22]. For this category of low-risk molecule, there are no direct safety concerns related to immunogenicity, with clinically relevant ADA responses being those of sufficient magnitude to impact drug exposure levels (PK) and thereby, potentially, efficacy.

Review of the validation data revealed that while the assay met or exceeded expected validation criteria for all assay parameters as dictated by guidance, from a fit-for-purpose perspective, there were clear indicators that the assay would likely detect a large number of clinically irrelevant responses, including false positives. Specifically, the cut point factors for all assay tiers were quite low, and within the analytical variability of the method (screening cut point or SCP = 1.08, CCP = 9.2% and titer cut point, or TCP = 1.18). This was likely attributable to (1) the selected assay conditions resulting in raw signals of blank samples used for cut point analysis close to instrument noise and thereby compromising differentiation of biological variance across samples and (2) removal of analytical and biological outliers from the cut point calculations. In addition, the assay was highly sensitive (<5.0 ng/ml) and drug tolerant (>1500:1 drug:ADA molar ratio).

Conclusions from review of the validation package were that while the assay will reliably detect robust, clinically meaningful ADA responses, it will also, by design, and in accordance with expectations set by current guidance, detect and report low-level responses with no clinical impact, false positives and clinically irrelevant biological ‘noise’ as positive responses that will thereby inflate incidence and confound correlation with clinical impact. For a molecule where clinical impact will require robust responses that impact drug exposure levels, this ‘fully validated’ assay will introduce unnecessary noise into the analysis and is arguably not ideally fit for the intended purpose.

Fully validated assay in practice – concerns from emerging Phase 1 ADA data

The fully validated MSD™ bridging ADA assay was used to assess immunogenicity in a Phase 1 clinical study that included both single-ascending-dose (Phase 1a) and multiple-ascending-dose (Phase 1b) cohorts. From a clinical perspective, the Phase 1 study was a success with no observed safety signals nor apparent impact on PK or PD. However, upon receipt of initial ADA sample analysis data, the project team, comprised of a group of cross-functional stakeholders, raised questions about the assay and/or the execution of the assay because the emerging data were confusing and causing alarm within the organization. Project team concerns included: observed false positive rate in screening assay of 20%, multiple baseline samples that confirmed positive and a placebo subject with apparently high titers that appeared to show a transient increase after dosing, despite PK data confirming that the subject had not been dosed. Furthermore, in subjects with apparent treatment-induced responses, there was no discernible correlation of titer magnitude with PK/PD, and samples from one subject with an apparently robust response were awaiting retest due to a failed confirmatory assay, leaving a gap in the early data set. Under pressure to make a rapid decision on the suitability of the assay, the project team did not have the luxury of time to await retests from multiple tiers.

Noting these concerns in the context of the known assay characteristics from validation, it was hypothesized that the disconcerting results were likely related to how data are generated and reported within the current paradigm, and that analysis of full subject profiles using S/N from the screening assay would provide a more comprehensive data set to test whether the assay was performing adequately and deemed suitable for supporting the remainder of Phase 1 or if the study samples would need to be retested after redevelopment and revalidation of the assay. Notably, the validation data set included extensive data from a wide range of PC concentrations as part of sensitivity and prozone analysis that together demonstrated a linear response relationship between PC concentration and S/N value (R2 = 0.993) spanning PC concentrations from 30 to 63,000 ng/mL (the highest concentration tested) with commensurate S/N range of 1.32–1244 (data not shown), indicating that S/N would provide a reliable measure of relative response magnitude.

Since this placebo subject was confirmed to not have been dosed in error, the response variability observed over the course of the study was concluded to represent analytical variability coupled with within-subject biological variability. Therefore, the dataset represents ‘no response.’ Unlike the S/N and raw signal datasets, the titer data make it difficult to draw the correct conclusion. This is because S/N and raw signal benefit from continuous response readouts, while titers are constrained by discrete dilution steps, which can only change in coarse increments and therefore inherently sacrifice precision. As a result, especially when signal levels are low to moderate, titers are unreliable for providing a true picture of biological and analytical variability. This effect becomes evident by observing the variability between titers measured on Days 1–22 (titers = 480) versus Days 64 and 85 (titers = 60), compared to S/N values across all of these days where the maximum % difference between values was 11.7% (2.65 on Day 15 vs 2.98 on Day 22) (Table 1). Consequently, for this data set representing within-subject biological variability and assay analytical variability in the absence of a true immunogenicity response, percent coefficient of variation (% CV) of titers across the subject profile was 65% compared to 19.2% and 19.9% for S/N and raw data values, respectively (Table 1). Not only do S/N and raw data values provide a more reliable picture of analytical and biological variability, they also provide a means to put relative magnitude of sample responses in context. Plotting S/N (or raw data) for assay controls along with subject profiles gives an immediately digestible visual impression of relative magnitude of response (Figure 1(B)). This comparator is not possible for titers as there is no commensurate titer data set for the controls. This inability to put titer values in context can complicate messaging to key stakeholders that may include research scientists, medical directors, project managers, executives, board members, and investors. For example, a titer value of 480 could be construed as high by stakeholders who have no knowledge of assay details, but low by the bioanalytical scientist who has insight into the raw data. It is the end-users, though, who require a more intuitive understanding of the response and with no contextual information, it is challenging to assuage their concerns. In contrast, when the same data point is presented as S/N of 2.74 with the added context that the average S/N of the 30 ng/mL PC is 1.35 and the 1000 ng/mL PC is 14.9, all stakeholders can quickly grasp that the response is low.

S/N reanalysis of baseline-positive treated subjects

Preliminary data from two baseline-positive, treated subjects were reanalyzed to determine if S/N could bring additional clarity to data interpretation. The first baseline-positive subject, herein termed BPS-1, was ADA positive at all time points, with a Day-1 pre-dose titer of 60 that peaked at 240 on Day 29 and returned to 60 by Day 85 (Table 2). According to current industry standard, the failure to see a >4x increase in titer would be interpreted as no evidence of a treatment-emergent effect [19,21]. However, examination of the titer profile gives an impression of a transient response and can be challenging to explain to key stakeholders (Figure 2(A)). By comparison, the S/N and raw data clearly demonstrated no treatment effect (Table 2 and Figure 2(B)). The overall conclusion is the same for both titer and S/N datasets: the variability of observed response is not due to treatment-emergent immunogenicity but instead represents a combination of analytical and within-subject biological variability. However, the precision of the S/N profile is very tight (CV = 5.8%) and reflects the variability seen in the raw data (CV = 14.0%) while titers again provide a misleadingly imprecise measure (CV = 65.3%). Furthermore, the current paradigm identified this subject as positive, leading to the performance of additional tiers of analysis that incurred more cost and time to ultimately provide a more variable data set that complicated data interpretation and messaging of final conclusions to key stakeholders who will still view this subject as both preexisting and post-dose ADA positive. This false positive will also erroneously contribute to the ADA positive incidence rate for the study and adversely impact downstream analyses that place this biologically negative subject in the ADA positive population. In addition to concerns about baseline positivity, the project team had also expressed concerns that while “high titers” were observed for BPS-2 and additional study subjects (data not shown) there were no apparent correlations with impact on PK or PD. While bioanalytical professionals appreciate that titer values can be misleading without understanding of MRD and discontinuous nature of titer data, key stakeholders typically lack this insight and therefore their perceptions need to be managed. Again, the availability of S/N data from control samples allows the study data to be presented in context. Therefore, to better represent relative magnitude of response, the S/N data for BPS-2 were plotted along with mean S/N response level from the 30 ng/mL low positive control (LPC) during study sample analysis (1.36) and the 156 ng/mL and 1000 ng/mL PCs (3.80 and 14.9, respectively) employed during assay validation (Figure 3(B)). These controls provide context for response magnitude and help build a more intuitive understanding of relative response levels compared to titers. This analysis showed that the maximum S/N of 7.04 (titer = 1920) observed in BPS-2 on Day 106 fell between the mean relative response levels of the 156 ng/mL and 1000 ng/mL controls. The raw data values further indicate the low-level nature of the response with the Day 106 sample measuring at 608 RLU (Table 3). Importantly, no quantitative conclusions are possible from this analysis as the PCs do not represent sample results. However, PC levels are used to evaluate assay sensitivity and their relative response levels do provide some context. In this case, the magnitude of response being well below that generated by the 1000 ng/mL PC suggests a relatively low-level response, especially in the context of a drug whose eventual target dose will maintain 10s-100s μg/mL of drug in circulation, where an ADA response level equivalent to a high affinity control antibody at 1 μg/mL would not be expected to have clinically relevant impact on exposure [38,39]. This is an important point because the goal of immunogenicity analysis is to detect and characterize clinically relevant responses, not to detect every potential molecule of ADA present. It is the pursuit of the latter that causes reporting of high incidence, clinically irrelevant responses that derail perceptions of the true immunogenicity profile of therapeutics and provide no meaningful insight for regulators, clinicians, or patients.

In the initial dataset provided for the Phase 1 ADA sample analysis, one subject was missing data from Days 29–57 due to a confirmatory assay failure that was awaiting repeat analysis. This subject was ADA negative at baseline, but positive at later time points (Days 85 and 106), with a high titer at Day 106 (1920) compared to other study subjects (Table 4). The missing data left a gap in understanding of response development (Figure 4(A)). To provide immediate insight into the response dynamics across all time points, S/N for all time points was plotted along with mean response levels of control samples. The S/N profile provided a complete picture of response development and relatively low magnitude of maximum response (Figure 4(B)). Once again, titer imprecision led to an impression of increasing response magnitude at the end of study with a 4-fold increase between Days 85 and 106. While this is not considered to be meaningful under the current paradigm, the superior precision of S/N and raw data measurements more accurately reflect the response dynamics, where the response on Day 106 was only 1.09x higher than Day 85. Based on evaluation of raw signal and S/N, without having to await additional tiers of analysis, the project team was able to appreciate that the response was not large and therefore there was no expectation for impact on PK. Phase 1 immunogenicity data summary

Conclusions from the S/N reanalysis of preliminary data from the Phase 1 study reassured the project team that the issues observed were associated with challenges related to the testing paradigm. From the perspective of sensitively characterizing the presence and magnitude of immunogenicity responses (as evidenced by S/N and raw signal data), the assay was performing well, but when executed under the current paradigm and in accordance with current regulator expectations, this highly sensitive, highly drug tolerant assay with low cut point factors was also, by design, detecting clinically irrelevant responses and false positives that represent biological variability [40]. With reassurance that the assay was performing in alignment with current regulator expectations, sample analysis was completed with the original assay, with application of an in-study cut point that helped eliminate some false positives and marginally reduced artificially inflated titer values. Additionally, the totality of the immunogenicity data for the Phase 1 study was further evaluated using S/N from the screening tier compared to the final data set produced by the current 3-tiered paradigm to determine whether the S/N data from the screening tier could provide equivalent, or even superior, information while saving time and resources consumed by additional tiers of testing.

Rethinking the paradigm

The three-tiered paradigm – context and challenges

The widely adopted canonical three-tiered immunogenicity paradigm did not arise from first principles thinking, with linear thought progression from screening to confirmation to titration. Rather, it originated from an early assumption that titers were the appropriate primary readout, borrowed from the vaccine field. However, while vaccine development focuses on intentionally eliciting large immune responses that can be adequately characterized by coarse measurements, biotherapeutic development seeks to minimize immunogenicity and requires sensitive characterization of the full range of responses, over time. The expectation that most samples would be negative rendered universal titration operationally impractical, prompting the introduction of screening assays, statistical cut points to control false negatives and confirmation tiers to eliminate false positives, ultimately yielding a three-tiered framework that is slow, costly, and misaligned with the context of use for drug development.

Application of this paradigm and insights from collected data over the last 20 plus years have identified common inherent challenges. Firstly, cut points cause us to discard and ignore contextual data that allow us to discern meaningful responses from clinically irrelevant responses and normal biological variability. Confirmation of very low responses near statistical cut points inflates immunogenicity incidence without correlation with clinical impact and inherent imprecision of titers compromises the ability to differentiate low- and moderate-level responses and develop correlations with clinical impact. The case study presented herein illustrates many of these challenges and presents an alternative approach where the totality of screening data is leveraged to generate complete subject response profiles that retain appropriate context to facilitate understanding of the full landscape of response development, dynamics and magnitude, and allow differentiation of true responses from biological noise, without the need for cut points or additional tiers of analysis.

Immunogenicity is a biomarker

Since immunogenicity is a biological response to a therapeutic intervention, it sits firmly within the BEST (Biomarkers, EndpointS, and other Tools) Resource definition of a biomarker [37]. As such, we can rethink our approach to measuring immunogenicity by leveraging all the knowledge gained and lessons learned from decades of implementation of biomarkers in support of drug development. Where the current three-tiered paradigm measures immunogenicity response magnitude using ADA titers, the approach presented here instead evaluates response magnitude directly through screening assay ADA signal-to-noise (S/N) values, allowing the full data set to be interpreted within a broader biological context. This approach of examining full S/N response profiles has been demonstrated to provide additional biological insight beyond conventional tier-based interpretation alone [15,16].

There are several advantages to adopting a biomarker assay approach to immunogenicity analysis. First and foremost, this approach focuses on the context of use, not one-size-fits-all, providing the scientific flexibility to address the varied needs of traditional as well as new and emerging modalities and ensuring that assays are designed to identify clinically relevant immunogenicity. The overarching COU for immunogenicity assays is to identify clinically relevant immunogenicity responses. It is appropriately dictated on a case-by-case basis for each program since the therapeutic being evaluated will directly shape assay performance needs [41]. More timely drug development decision making becomes possible because study data can be delivered more quickly when there is no need to wait for additional tiers of testing. Furthermore, immunogenicity data can be monitored in relative ‘real-time’ as is often done for PK data because there is no need to wait for enrollment of enough subjects to determine if the validation cut point is suitable. This is particularly important for high-risk programs where it is critical to monitor for early signals of immunogenicity.

Another critical advantage is that data interpretation utilizes the entire data set from all subjects and puts responses from dosed subjects in appropriate context with cross-sectional biological variability observed across baseline samples with added insight into longitudinal biological variability provided by the placebo population. These complete data sets are easily visualized through figures, as for PK/PD, and meaningful responses are readily discerned from ‘noise’ as clearly demonstrated by the presented case study. This allows transparent, comprehensive, and efficient review of data, turning reams of pages of data tables into a few pages of easily digested figures, facilitating both scientific and regulatory review. And, since data sets are complete, including responses from all time points for all subjects, the data can be mined as deeply as desired. Since regulators can see the totality of data, this alleviates any concerns of ‘missing something.’ The biological context provided by this approach allows efficient identification of true, treatment-induced responses and facilitates rapid identification of clinically relevant immunogenicity, saving time, money, and resources. Importantly, it further ensures the right information gets to the label (clinically relevant immunogenicity) versus reporting of incidence numbers that do not inform impact nor provide insight for clinicians and patients.

These complete data sets are also more intuitive for cross-functional stakeholders in drug development companies to understand, leading to savings in uncounted FTE hours of anxiety-driven organizational swirl that occurs when inflated incidence of clinically irrelevant responses lead to misperceptions of the program’s immunogenicity liability and even lead to deprioritization of promising programs. The realized savings in cost and resources can then be redirected to other value-added efforts, allowing innovator organizations to invest in additional promising lines of research and deliver greater overall value to patients.

Future perspective

The case study presented herein demonstrates common challenges inherent in the current paradigm, highlighting how its design is not compatible with serving the context of use for drug development, even for a relatively simple, traditional monoclonal antibody therapeutic. Therefore, it most certainly cannot serve the needs of the landscape of modalities and molecules currently in development or those yet to be imagined. For that future, we need scientific discretion, not dogma.

Biologics drug development will only continue to evolve and grow in diversity and complexity. To effectively address the needs of current and future drug programs will require careful consideration of context of use for all assays. Outdated notions of traditional silos of bioanalysis need to be abandoned. PK, immunogenicity and biomarkers, and the teams that support them, cannot continue to operate in parallel, but must integrate. Bioanalytical scientists also need to exit mental silos where prescriptive guidance is revered and blindly adhered to and embrace critical thinking, grounded in first principles. Immunogenicity is undeniably a biomarker. With each therapeutic program, we are essentially undertaking a new biomarker discovery project with the goal of discovering which immunogenicity (biomarker) responses have clinical impact. The answer will differ for each therapeutic program, and there can be no singular, prescribed approach that will be fit-for-all-purposes. Therefore, focus should be on developing a scientific thought framework of key considerations to ensure context of use is met, followed by the application of sound science to execute appropriately.

Moving toward such a framework will require industry-wide rethinking, willingness to reanalyze data sets with a fresh perspective, and alignment with regulators on how to develop, validate, and implement new approaches. If this sounds too complicated to operationalize and you find yourself immediately thinking of dozens of reasons why this is not possible, consider this insight from organizational psychologist Adam Grant. Grant maintains that when people say, ‘I can’t’ what they really mean is ‘I don’t want to’ or ‘I don’t know how to’ [42]. I believe we can. And we must. The only thing we cannot do is tell patients we wouldn’t even try.
