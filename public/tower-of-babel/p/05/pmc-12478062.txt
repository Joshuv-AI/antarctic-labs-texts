Low-GPX4 drives a sustained drug-tolerant persister state in TNBC by a targetable adaptive FSP1 upregulation

ABSTRACT
Metastatic relapses in Triple-Negative Breast Cancer (TNBC) patients with residual disease pose a significant clinical challenge. In this study, we longitudinally modelled cellular state transition from dormant drug-tolerant persister (DDTP) to proliferative (PDTP) cell state across TNBC subtypes. We identified specific molecular and phenotypic alterations that characterize the DTP states in TNBC cells that are maintained upon re-gaining proliferation. We found that Basal-Like proliferative DTPs stably acquired mesenchymal traits, while luminal androgen receptor-positive TNBC DTPs undergo partial Epithelial-to-Mesenchymal Transition (EMT). TNBC DTP cells exhibit reduced expression of glutathione peroxidase-4 (GPX4), conferring susceptibility to ferroptosis inducers. Mechanistically, GPX4 downregulation promotes EMT in TNBC, supported by an inverse correlation between GPX4 and EMT marker vimentin (VIM) expression that also serves as a predictor of survival in TNBC patients undergoing chemotherapy. The genetic, pharmacological, or chemotherapy-induced suppression of GPX4 in TNBC cells leads to robust upregulation of ferroptosis suppressor protein-1 (FSP1). The clinical significance of these findings is established by a strong predictive value of FSP1high/VIMhigh signature for worst survival and incomplete pathological response in chemotherapy-treated TNBC patients. Further, targeting FSP1 re-sensitizes cells to chemotherapy, while combined inhibition of FSP1 and GPX4 is selectively lethal in proliferative DTP TNBC cells by inducing ferroptosis.

Introduction

Triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) is the most aggressive and heterogeneous type of breast malignancy, comprising four distinct molecular subtypes each exhibiting unique clinical behaviours and responses to therapy [1]. TNBC subtypes are classified based on their transcriptional profiles and cell of origin. They include Basal-Like (BL1 and BL-2) tumors, which are more epithelial-like, Mesenchymal Stem-Like (MSL) tumors, which are enriched in tumor stem cells, and the Luminal Androgen-Receptor Positive (LAR) subtype, which arises from luminal cells expressing androgen receptors [2,3]. TNBC patients generally have worst prognosis than their non-TNBC counterparts due to higher rates of disease relapse, therapy resistance, and metastases [1,4,5]. Moreover, TNBC molecular subtypes respond differently to treatment, for example, BL-2 tumors exhibit higher chemotherapy resistance and LAR tumors display low pathologic complete response (pCR) rates as well as chemoresistance [3,6,7].

Conventionally, TNBC is managed with chemotherapeutic agents as the first-line treatment, typically in a neoadjuvant setting due to the absence of hormone receptor (Estrogen and Progesterone Receptors; ER, and PR) expression and HER2 overexpression [[8], [9], [10], [11]]. TNBC is characterized by a high proliferation index and generally responds well to chemotherapy, leading to significant rates of initial pCR [10,11]. However, a substantial number of patients who do not achieve pCR (approximately 50 %) harbor residual disease and experience early relapse within 2–5 years’ post-therapy, often with distant metastases [12]. Survival rates for TNBC patients with chemoresistant or residual disease are typically very poor [10,12]. Managing TNBC patients who fail to achieve pCR with first-line chemotherapy is particularly challenging due to the limited understanding of residual disease and the lack of alternative therapeutic options [13,14]. Therefore, there is an urgent need to elucidate the molecular and cellular characteristics of chemo-tolerance across TNBC subtypes to design more effective treatment strategies.

Cancer cells undergo drug-induced molecular reprogramming, entering a reversible drug-tolerant persister state that enables survival under high doses of drugs [16]. These adaptive state drive distinct phenotypic changes and alter dependency on specific molecular pathways [[17], [18], [19]]. For example, activation of the epithelial-mesenchymal transition (EMT) program, can suppress apoptosis and confer multidrug resistance across multiple cancer types [20,21]. Drug tolerant persister (DTP) cells can survive treatment without undergoing any permanent genetic changes and are reversible in nature. These cells can withstand chemotherapy or targeted drug therapy by transitioning into a reversible state that help in their sustenance by entering a dormant state designated as Dormant drug tolerant persister (DDTP). Upon the withdrawal of chemotherapy, they can regain back their ability to grow into a more active state, re-establishing drug sensitivity, contributing to cancer relapse in the patient, a stage that we refer as Proliferating drug tolerant persister cells (PDTP). DTP cells, exhibit altered metabolism to confer cellular plasticity through increased synthesis of polyunsaturated fatty acids (PUFA) that paradoxically make the cells susceptible to ferroptosis-mediated cell death via increased lipid peroxidation [17,22]. Furthermore, DTP cells often show impaired redox regulation, notably due to reduced expression of glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4), and other ROS clearing enzymes Superoxide Dismutase (SOD1, SOD2), Catalase (CAT), peroxiredoxins (PRDXs) and thioredoxin (TXN) [23,24].

The Mesenchymal and LAR subtypes of TNBC have been shown to be more susceptible to inhibition of glutathione biosynthesis pathways as compared to basal-like subtypes [25]. Additionally, most TNBC cells often upregulate acyl-CoA synthetase long-chain family member 4 (ACSL4), which promotes polyunsaturated fatty acid enrichment in cell membranes and correlates with increased sensitivity to ferroptosis [26]. Our previous research demonstrated that TNBC cells are intrinsically sensitive to ferroptosis-inducing agents such as Fin56, Erastin, and BRD4-targeted therapies, which trigger reactive iron accumulation and downregulation of GPX4 in both TNBC cells and mouse xenograft models [27]. This ferroptotic susceptibility is consistent with the protein expression profiles in human TNBC tumor tissues that have low GPX4, GSS (Glutathione synthetase), iron transporter protein ferroportin (Fpn-1/SLC40A1), and elevated levels of the iron-binding receptor FTH1 and ACSL4 [27]. Despite several ferroptotic regulatory proteins that are differentially expressed in TNBC, effective therapeutic targeting of the tumors that utilizes cellular vulnerability to ferroptosis is challenging due to a lack of tumor cell selectivity and widespread toxicity [28,29]. Identifying novel molecular targets that exploit the compromised function of key anti-ferroptotic proteins, such as GPX4, to enhance toxicity in drug-tolerant TNBC cells could provide the basis for a more effective therapeutic strategy [30]. Given the potential to exploit cellular sensitivity to ferroptosis in targeting drug-tolerant cancer cells, it is critical to investigate whether different molecular subtypes of TNBC enter a chemotherapy-tolerant state with shared or distinct vulnerabilities to ferroptosis inducers.

Ferroptosis suppressor protein 1 (FSP1), also known as AIFM2 (Flavoprotein apoptosis-inducing factor mitochondria-associated 2), is a CoQ oxidoreductase that is upregulated in multiple cancers resistant to ferroptosis upon GPX4 inhibition [28]. FSP1 upregulation has been associated with chemoresistance in hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC), including in HCC DTP cells following Cisplatin treatment. In addition, FSP1 is also known to have a role in promoting cell invasion, migration, and stem cell-like properties of DTPs [29]. Although FSP1 reportedly inhibits ferroptosis independently of GPX4 [28], reduced expression of FSP1 is associated with a higher GPX4 reliance [26,31]. These studies suggest the existence of a very finely tuned regulatory balance between between GPX4 and FSP1 in different cell types. Understanding the interdependent roles of these two key regulators of the ferroptosis pathway may uncover the vulnerable ferroptosis population of aggressive tumor cells that might give a therapeutic opportunity.

In this study, we modelled the longitudinal development of TNBC DTP cells evolving from dormant to proliferative states, using cell lines representing four TNBC subtypes exposed to diverse chemotherapeutics. We report that all TNBC subtypes generate DDTP cell populations that transit to PDTP phase with higher chemoresistance compared to the parental cells. DTP cells from BL-1/2 and LAR subtypes exhibited molecular traits belonging to EMT, evidenced by elevated EMT genes, including vimentin. Moreover, cellular and molecular analyses showed that TNBC PDTP cells shared common targetable cellular and biochemical alterations, including increased lipid reactive species and suppressed glutathione synthesis. GPX4 expression modulation in TNBC identifies its role in the regulation of mesenchymal traits, FSP1 expression, and chemoresistance. Human breast cancer tissue analysis, combined with in silico analysis of patient datasets, identified a reverse correlation of GPX4 with both FSP1 and vimentin expression, that is predictive of survival and chemotherapy response in aggressive TNBC.

Results

Chemotherapy induces a dormant DTP state in all TNBC subtypes that switch to a proliferative DTP state characterized by acquired drug resistance and increased proliferation

To investigate chemotherapeutic tolerance in TNBC, we established persister cell models using paclitaxel-, doxorubicin, and cisplatin in representative TNBC subtype (hereafter referred to as parental cells, PC) cell line (MDA-MB-468 for BL-1, HCC70 for BL-2, MDA-MB-231 and HS578T for MSL, and MDA-MB-453 for LAR) [16,32] (Fig. 1A). Dose-response curves determine the IC50 values (Fig. S1A–C) for each chemotherapeutic drug (Fig. S1D), and the drug concentrations that allowed survival of 15 % of the cell population post-treatment were selected (Fig. 1A). These residual cells that endured repeated high dose drug-treatments reflect minimal residual disease in human tumors [33,34]. Following drug withdrawal, longitudinally monitoring of post-therapy residual DTP cells revealed four distinct phases (Fig. 1B–D). Phase I (cytotoxic phase) occurred within 72 h post-paclitaxel treatment, marked by accelerated cell death in the presence of the drug (Fig. 1B and C). After drug removal, cells entered Phase II (cytostatic phase) during which the DTP cells remained mitotically dormant (DDTP) for 3–15 days. By around 15 days post-treatment, these DDTPs transitioned into Phase III (slow-dividing phase), gradually recovering mitotic activity (Fig. 1B and C). Finally, in Phase IV the cells resumed robust proliferation (proliferative DTPs: PDTPs), often demonstrating cell division rates higher than PC (Fig. 1C). For instance, HCC70 recovered DTP cells proliferate more than 2-fold compared to controls (Fig. 1C). Cells in Phase IV, called proliferative DTP (called PDTP now onwards), were used for further characterization. PDTP cells were successfully generated with paclitaxel (PDTP-P), doxorubicin (PDTP-D), and cisplatin (PDTP-C) across all four TNBC subtypes (Fig. S1E).

Morphological analysis using phase contrast microscopy analysis of DTP cells during phases II and III revealed significant sub type specific differences (Fig. 1B), particularly in the BL-1/2 subtype lines (MDA-MB-468 and HCC70), that remained stable even in PDTPs (Fig. 1B, Fig. S1E). Regardless of the chemotherapy agent used, MDA-MB-468 and HCC70 acquired drug tolerance with aquisition of a stable spindle-shaped morphology with loss of cell-cell contacts, which is suggestive of mesenchymal transition [19,21,36]. TNBC cell lines from the MSL subtype (HS578T and MDA-MB-231) showed minimal morphological changes. LAR cells (MDA-MB-453), transient lost cell-cell contacts in the DDTP state, which partially reverted in the PDTPs, regaining cell-cell contacts (Fig. 1B). Quantitative analysis such as cell aspect ratio and area confirmed significant morphological alterations in the BL and LAR subtypes ratio (Fig. 1F and Fig. S1H)

We investigated whether the TNBC DTP cell populations maintain drug tolerance in different phases (DDTP to PDTP) by re-challenging PC and DTPs with lethal doses of paclitaxel (Fig. 1C and S1F) and by analysing the dose-response curves to compare PC, DDTP and PDTPs in each TNBC subtype (Fig. 1G). We observed that more than 80 % of DDTP cells (from day 3, 6 and 15 post-treatment) from all TNBC subtypes were viable upon paclitaxel treatment (Fig. S1F) and show tolerance (Fig. 1D), whereas, HCC70 and HS578T PDTP cells show some regain of drug-sensitivity. Similar results were seen in dose response curves (Fig. 1G) that paclitaxel-derived DDTP and PDTP population could tolerate higher doses of paclitaxel, as significant cell death occurred with dose escalation as compared to PC. However, PDTP-P cells showed noticeable decrease in tolerance to paclitaxel compared to their corresponding DDTP (Fig. 1D–S1F, and 1G)s suggesting phenotypic reversibility of PDTP primarily through non-genetic mechanisms [32,33]. Previous studies have supported the concept that heterogeneous cell populations can contribute to distinct clonal evolutionary lineages through epigenetic reprogramming in DTP cells [16,35]. Given that drug-resistant cells exhibit enhanced tumorigenic potential after metastasis across various tumor types, including TNBC [36], we assessed the colony formation capacity of TNBC PDTP-P cells (Fig. 1H). Colony formation assays assays demonstrated that PDTP-P cells produced more colonies, even at a lower concentration compared to the PCs (Fig. 1H). In vivo, MDA-MB-468 PDTP-P cells implanted into the mammary fat pad of NOD/SCID mice formed tumors with higher weight (Fig. 1J)) and volume (Fig. 1K) than parental cells, with higher Ki67-positive nuclei, indicating increased proliferative potential (Fig. 1I–L).

Together, these results highlight that TNBC cells from all subtypes can survive chemotherapy in a dormant persister state and subsequently resume proliferation after drug withdrawal, leading to a more aggressive and resistant PDTP state (Fig. 1M). Identifying the molecular changes associated with the phenotypes observed in PDTP cell models may elucidate the mechanisms behind early and aggressive disease relapse clinically observed in TNBC tumors post-treatment [12].

Chemotherapy induces a stable mesenchymal state in BL-1 and BL-2 subtypes, whereas the LAR subtype undergoes a partial EMT with enhanced migration and invasive properties

Drug-induced molecular reprogramming often leads to EMT in epithelial tumors, a plastic state that is more resistant to therapy [20]. We observed distinct morphological changes that were initiated in DDTP and persisted in PDTP cells derived from BL-1/2 TNBC cell lines (MDA-MB-468, BL-1; and HCC-70, BL-2), indicative of an EMT (Fig. 1B–F and S1E,H). Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) analysis revealed that parental MDA-MB-468 and HCC70 cells exhibited epithelial morphology increased cellular area- and intact cell-to-cell contacts (magnified inserts), features entirely lost in their respective PDTP cells, regardless of whether these are generated by paclitaxel, doxorubicin, or cisplatin (Fig. 2A). In contrast, TEM analysis of LAR MDA-453 cells showed stronger cell-cell junctions (Fig. S2A). To further confirm EMT, we assessed EMT marker expression and localization. Immunofluorescence staining revealed a significant decrease in E-cadherin, along with a notable increase in vimentin expression, in both MDA-MB-468 and HCC70 cells (Fig. 2B–C). Western blot analysis showed substantial upregulation (up to 27-fold) of vimentin and loss of E-cadherin in BL-1 and BL-2 paclitaxel PDTP cells (Fig. 2D) as well as derived from doxorubicin and cisplatin treatments (Fig. S2B). Interestingly, in MDA-453, we found a substantial increase in vimentin with an increase in E-cadherin (Fig. 2D). Notably, these changes were also observed in DTPs of these TNBC lines (Fig. 2SC), representing each subtype. At the transcriptional level, VIM expression was consistently elevated in PDTPs across all TNBC lines. (Fig. S2D). We then compared EMT gene expression levels in paclitaxel PDTPs of MDA-468 and MDA-453. As shown in the heatmap in Fig. S2E, CDH1 (E-CAD) gene transcript was downregulated with upregulation of CDH2, VIM, and FN1 in MDA-468 PDTP-P compared to PC. Further, levels of major EMT transcription factors (ZEB1, ZEB2, SNAIL1, SNAIL2, TWIST1, and TWIST2) were upregulated in MDA-468 PDTP-P, establishing a complete EMT program in BL1 TNBC cells. However, in MDA-453 PDTP-P, we observed an intermediate response with upregulated CDH2, VIM, SNAIL1, and SNAIL2, while upregulated CDH1, possibly due to the downregulation of its known transcriptional repressors ZEB1 and ZEB2. These results indicate that LAR PDTP cells likely attained a hybrid epithelial-mesenchymal state [7]. Recent research highlighted the role of hybrid EMT in metastasis and secondary tumor formation in breast and other epithelial tumors, linked to cells capability to acquire an epithelial state (E-Cad+) despite maintaining mesenchymal features, unlike fully mesenchymal cancer cells [37]. We therefore assessed whether tumors formed from PDTP cells retain a mesenchymal state by performing E-cadherin and vimentin immunofluorescence staining on mouse xenograft tumor sections from MDA-468 PDTP-P and PC. We found that PDTP derived tumor cells expressed significantly lower E-cadherin and higher vimentin levels compared to those tumors formed by PC cells (Fig. 2E).

Drug-resistant residual disease in TNBC patients often correlates with distant metastasis and poor survival [13,38]. Given the observed widespread EMT induction in TNBC DTP cells, we further investigated their capabilities for migration and invasion, phenotypic characteristics associated with the high mesenchymal state in persister cells [20]. We found that PDTP cells exhibited higher migration rates and closed wounds more effectively than PC by wound healing assay (Fig. 2G–H). For instance, MDA-MB-468 PC, which typically shows minimal migratory behaviour, displayed a dramatic increase in collective cell migration upon transition to the PDTP state (Fig. 2G–H). Similarly, the migratory capacity of MSL cells was further enhanced in PDTP cells (Fig. 2G–H). These changes were accompanied by higher filamentous actin in PDTP cells (Fig. S2F). Further, we assessed the invasive properties of PDTP using in-vitro Matrigel-based assays and analysis of in-vivo lung metastasis in mice models. Paclitaxel-derived PDTP cells acquired up to a 5-fold increase in invasion capacity compared to respective PCs, irrespective of the TNBC cell subtypes, we tested (Fig. 2I) and formation of metastatic nodules in lungs of mice bearing PDTP-P orthotropic tumors (Fig. S2G) as compared to PC. In summary, these findings illustrate that chemotherapy induces the development of drug-tolerant persister cells that exhibit distinct phenotypes from their parental cells, predominantly characterized by a mesenchymal phenotype with consistent elevation of vimentin across all TNBC subtypes and PDTP cells correlate with enhanced migratory and invasive capability, in agreement with previous reports [[39], [40], [41], [42]] (Fig. 2J).

These cellular/biological characteristics align with the known metastatic potential of drug-resistant cancer cells [40,41,43] and contribute to the aggressiveness and therapeutic challenges of the disease.

PDTP cells display elevated reactive iron levels, impairing GPX4-mediated antioxidant defence, and enhanced susceptibility to ferroptosis inducers

A prominent feature of oxidative damage at the subcellular level is abnormal mitochondrial structure and function [14,16]. Electron micrographs of PC and PDTP cells revealed significant alterations in mitochondrial structures in PDTP cells (Fig. 3A), including condensed and shrunken mitochondrial with disrupted cristae, features which are characteristic of oxidative injury and lipid peroxidation [44]. We observed a higher number of damaged mitochondria in all TNBC PDTP-P cell lines (Fig. 3B), as well as other chemotherapy PDTPs, suggesting a consistent mitochondrial phenotype in TNBC PDTPs. To understand the dynamics of redox homeostasis in PDTP cells, we measured H2O2-induced ROS using H2DCFDA reagent in different PC and PDTP-P cell lines. As seen in Fig. 3C, H2O2 treatment induced significantly higher ROS levels in PDTP cells. Since mesenchymal phenotype is coupled with elevated production of lipids, including PUFA, which contributes to lipid ROS generation [45]. We found that PDTP-P cells exhibit increased cellular lipid content as measured by Oil Red O staining (Fig. 3D) and higher ROS generation upon treatment with ferroptosis inducers RSL3 (Fig. S3A) and Erastin (Fig. S3B) as determined by CellROX live cell staining assay. As these results suggested higher lipid ROS generation, we next checked lipid peroxidation using BODIPY 581/591C11 lipid probes, upon treatment with Erastin. As shown in Fig. 3E, Erastin treatment caused a marked red-to-green shift in the fluorescence of BODIPY 581/591C11 lipid probe in PDTP cells (Fig. 3E, 3SB), FACS analysis of these cells also indicate significantly more number of PE+/FITC+ cells in PDTP cells (Fig. S3C) indicating extensive lipid peroxidation compared to PC. We further analysed the extent of lipid peroxidation in PDTP cells in response to more specific GPX4 inhibitors GPX4-in-3 and GPX4-in-5 using FACS analysis of BODIPY 581/591C11-stained cells (Fig. 3F). GPX4 inhibitors resulted in significantly higher no. of MDA-468 PDTP-P cells undergoing lipid peroxidation (Fig. 3G), which is rescued by the pre-treatment of ferroptosis inhibitor Liprostatin-1 (Fig. 3F and G). Together, these findings suggest that drug-tolerant cells have compromised antioxidant defence, mainly relying on the GPX4-glutathione pathway, and may have increased susceptibility to ferroptosis [25,27]. To further investigate whether the heightened lipid ROS induction correlates with reduced cellular glutathione levels, we measured total cellular glutathione levels in these cells under steady-state conditions and after Erastin treatment (Fig. 3H). Interestingly, PDTP cells exhibited lower cellular glutathione levels compared to parental cells under untreated conditions, and Erastin treatment led to a severe depletion (∼80 %) in cellular glutathione levels (Fig. 3H). Lipid peroxidation in cells is typically facilitated by increased pools of reactive iron participating in the Fenton reaction, generating lipid ROS that contributes to ferroptotic cell death [46]. We subsequently measured total cellular iron content and its reactive labile state (Fe2+) under steady-state conditions (Fig. S3D) and after RSL3 treatment (Fig. 3I) in PDTPs and PCs. Cellular iron quantification assays revealed that the total intracellular iron content has a subtle but significant increase in total cellular iron levels in PDTP cells. Moreover, there was a significant increase (more than 2-fold) in labile iron levels (Fe2+) in all PDTP cells, especially upon RSL3 treatment (Fig. 3I and Fig. S3D). Similarly, we also analysed cellular Fe2+ in cells in response to GPX4-in-3 treatment using FerroOrange, a fluorescent probe that specifically detects and quantifies ferrous iron (Fe2+) in living cells. As seen in Fig. 3J, cellular Fe2+ is higher in PDTP cells, both untreated and GPX4-in-3 treated conditions, in comparison to PC. Based on the increased cellular ROS and iron levels in PDTP cells along with lower glutathione levels, it can be speculated that these cells might exhibit minimal resistance to ferroptosis inducers [27,46]. Therefore, we tested the response of PDTP cells generated by using distinct chemotherapies to ferroptosis inducers including RSL3, FIN56, and xCT blocker Erastin to check their potential to induce cell death. Results shown in Fig. 3M indicate that TNBC PDTP cells are more sensitive to inhibition of these ferroptosis inducers, thus illustrating a significant susceptibility to ferroptosis. Further, we checked the sensitivity of PDTPs to specific inhibitors of GPX4 (GPX4-in-5 and in-3) and Thioredoxin-1 (TRi-1) using dose-response viability assays. As seen in Fig. 3N and S3K, PDTP cells were significantly more sensitive to GPX4-specific inhibitors, while a minimal or no difference was observed between the IC50 values of PC vs PDTP cells. Importantly, these specific GPX4 inhibitors are significantly less toxic to normal mammary cells MCF10A as compared to TNBC PDTP cells (Fig. S3L). These findings collectively indicate the distinct status of glutathione pathway modulators, mainly GPX4 and NRF2 derived antioxidants in TNBC drug-tolerant persister cells, which can be selectively targeted by GPX4 inhibitors and other ferroptosis-inducing agents in the GSH pathway (Fig. 3O), thus representing a specific therapeutic opportunity.

GPX4 modulation in TNBC and PDTP cells reveals its role in mesenchymal phenotype and a compensatory induction of FSP1 expression

Our earlier work showed that TNBCs are intrinsically vulnerable to ferroptosis and express lower levels of GPX4 and GSS, key proteins in the ferroptosis pathway, compared to non-TNBC counterparts [33]. The above findings suggest that chemo-tolerance in TNBC is linked to GPX4 downregulation and increased labile iron pools, conferring susceptibility to glutathione pathway blockage. We, therefore, hypothesized that the phenotypes of DTPs are at least partially dependent on GPX4 suppression in TNBC. To explore this hypothesis, we adopted an approach to select TNBC cell subpopulations that survived acute GPX4 inhibition, to model cellular non-dependence on GPX4. We treated TNBC cell lines of different subtypes (MDA-468, HS578T, and MDA-453) with lethal doses of RSL3 (ranging from 1 to 3 μM) for 3–5 days and allowed RSL3-tolerant cells to expand, developing RSL3 PDTPs. Phenotypic characterization of RSL3 PDTPs across these TNBC cell lines revealed a significant increase in cell-cell contact formation and compact colony growth patterns, even in the MSL subtype, which typically does not form colonies with cell-cell contacts (Fig. 4A). Interestingly, RSL3-driven PDTP phenotype contrasts with those driven by chemotherapy notably with loss of cell-cell contacts during DTP generation (Fig. 4A). We then investigated the expression of GPX4 and EMT markers altered in chemotherapy-tolerant TNBCs in RSL3 PDTP and found that MDA-468 PDTP-RSL3 cells exhibited robust accumulation of E-cadherin at cell-cell contact junctions, reduced vimentin, and increased GPX4 expression compared with PCs (Fig. 4B and C). These results were corroborated by Western blot analyses, showing increased GPX4 levels in PDTP-RSL3 cells compared with PCs (Fig. 4D). To further investigate whether the phenotypic changes observed in PDTP-Ps are associated with GPX4, we modulated GPX4 expression levels in MDA-468 PC and PDTP-P cells using shRNA-mediated silencing and lentiviral-based overexpression approaches, respectively. Immunofluorescence staining and confocal microscopy analysis of GPX4 knockdown PC show significant downregulation of GPX4 together with decreased E-cadherin and concomitant increase in vimentin expression (Fig. 4E and F). Ectopic overexpression of GPX4 in PDTP-P cells resulted in more than a 2-fold increase in its expression (Fig. S4A) as compared to PC. More interestingly, GPX4 overexpressed PDTP-P cells significantly reduced the expression of vimentin (Fig. 4E and F).

We further hypothesized that TNBC cells surviving severe GPX4 suppression induced by high doses of RSL3 might upregulate glutathione-independent alternative compensatory pathways to counteract acute ferroptosis induction that may also operate in chemotherapy-DTPs. To explore this possibility, we analysed the expression of CoQ oxidoreductase FSP1 that has been shown to compensate for the absence of GPX4 and rescue sarcoma cells from ferroptosis [28]. As shown in Fig. 4G, RSL3 PDTP derived from MDA-468 and HS578T cells exhibited substantial increases in FSP1 expression, indicating its compensatory upregulation in the expanded cell population to survive ferroptosis due to acute GPX4 inhibition. Additionally, we investigated whether short-term inhibition of GPX4 could also result in FSP1 upregulation in TNBC cells. GPX4 blockage in MDA-468 and HS578T cells with RSL3 and FIN56 for 48 h led to FSP1 upregulation and concurrent decrease in GPX4 levels (Fig. 4H). To further establish that TNBC cells can upregulate FSP1 to compensate for GPX4 loss, we examined FSP1 protein levels following shRNA-mediated GPX4 knockdown. As depicted in Fig. 4I, GPX4 knockdown in TNBC cells resulted in robust upregulation (up to 74-fold) of FSP1 expression. Remarkably, the clinical relevance of this regulatory mechanism supported by analysis of breast cancer patient datasets in TCGA data supported by analysis of breast cancer patient datasets in TCGA data. Specifically, Basal-Like breast tumors showed high FSP1 (AIFM2) and low GPX4 expression at both mRNA (Fig. S4 B, C) and protein (Fig. S4 D, E) levels compared to non-Basal tumors. Furthermore, we found significant co-expression of FSP1 and GPX4 mRNAs in normal human breast tissues (n = 403; Fig. S4F), whereas this co-expression was disrupted in breast cancers (n = 1091; Fig. S4F) tissues, suggesting a cancer-specific molecular switch and interactions (Fig. S4G) between GPX4 and FSP1 under GPX4 loss or suppression in TNBC cells. In summary, these results highlight a strong co-regulation of epithelial/mesenchymal behaviour in relation to GPX4 protein in TNBC cells (Fig. S4J). Importantly, loss of GPX4 and/or its activity results in acute and robust upregulation of FSP1 in TNBC cells. This could be a crucial mechanism for tumor cell survival under GPX4 depletion and might contribute to drug resistance.

Further, we investigated whether a gene signature comprising the mesenchymal marker VIM, along with AIFM2, could serve as a predictive biomarker for pathological complete response (pCR) and RFS in TNBC patients treated with chemotherapy. To assess this, we performed ROC (Receiver Operating Characteristic) analysis using an ROC plotter to compute pCR and RFS in chemotherapy-treated TNBC patients, based on AUC (Area Under the Curve) values. We found that high co-expression of AIFM2-VIM in tumors of chemotherapy non-responders that can effectively predicted poor pathological response to chemotherapy in LN + high-grade TNBC patients (Fig. 5D), with a statistically significant predictability (p-value of 3.3e-04 and an AUC value of 0.791, Fig. 5E). Similarly, low co-expression of GPX4 and CDH1 in this patient cohort is an indicator of poor prognosis (Fig. S5D) Collectively, these results from breast cancer patient samples and TNBC patient data analysis strongly suggest that GPX4, VIM-AIFM2 genes and protein expression alterations linked to the development of TNBC PDTPs are robust predictors of chemotherapy response in patients with metastatic and high-grade tumors. These findings parallel the phenotypes we observed in TNBC DTPs, reinforcing their clinical relevance in TNBC patient management.

To further validate our experimental findings, we analysed a breast cancer gene expression dataset (GSE6434) available on Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO), which includes cohorts of breast cancer patients with docetaxel-sensitive and resistant tumors using GEOR. Differential gene expression (DEG) analysis, depicted in the volcano plot (Fig. 5F) comparing resistant versus sensitive tumors, revealed GPX4 as the second most downregulated gene (log2 fold change −4.02, p-value = 3.47E-04, log10). Evaluation of key regulatory genes associated with the mesenchymal state (MUC1, TGFB1, MMP20, MMP3, and MMP16) (Fig. S5E) showed differential expression between resistant and sensitive tumors, indicating that resistant tumors exhibit more mesenchymal-like characteristics, consistent with our in vitro findings (Fig. 2). Furthermore, we observed compromised expression of genes involved in cellular ROS management (NFE2L2 and GSS) and iron binding (FTH1) in resistant tumors (Fig. S5E), suggesting reduced capacity for ROS handling. Using the same dataset, we calculated the epithelial score (Fig. 5F) and found it to be significantly lower in resistant human breast tumors compared to sensitive ones. Collectively, this analysis supports the concept that chemo-resistant tumors are likely to have acquired a mesenchymal state (Fig. 2) and exhibit a compromised capacity for ROS management (Fig. 3). These findings reinforce the relevance of our in vitro observations and highlight potential mechanisms underlying chemotherapy resistance in breast cancer.

To further establish clinical correlations of GPX4 with markers of ferroptosis, epithelial and EMT states, and iron homeostasis, we analysed several bulk tumor gene expression databases (TCGA, METABRIC, Wang, Lu, and Ivshina) encompassing various breast cancer types. Specifically, we examined Basal-Like bulk RNAseq data from TCGA, single-cell RNA sequencing datasets from TNBC patients, and gene expression sets from TNBC patient-derived xenograft (PDX) models, as illustrated in Fig. 5G–I and S5F. Gene expression correlation dot-plots across different breast cancer datasets revealed that GPX4 expression is predominantly positively correlated with epithelial cytokeratin genes (KRT8, KRT18, and KRT19), while demonstrating negative correlations with mesenchymal markers and EMT regulators such as VIM, S100A4, CDH2, MMPs, SNAI2, ZEB2, and MET (Fig. S5F). Furthermore, GPX4 exhibited significant positive correlations with the GSS gene, and negative correlations with EMT scores and IREB2 within the TCGA RNAseq Basal-Like PAM50 cohort (Fig. 5G). Analysis of single-cell RNAseq data from TNBC patients (GSE176078) underscored robust negative correlations of GPX4 with EMT hallmarks across all patients (Fig. 5H), highlighting the clinical relevance of our findings in PDTPs that GPX4 can modulate epithelial and mesenchymal cellular behaviours differentially (Fig. 4). Similar trends for GPX4 expression were corroborated in patient-derived xenograft models of breast cancer from GSE183187 (Fig. 5I). Overall, these findings establish a robust association of GPX4 with the EMT phenotype across TNBC cell lines, human tumor samples, and PDX models, revealing insights that were not previously mapped in breast cancer research.

To explore the relationship between GPX4 and Vimentin at the protein expression level in breast cancer, we conducted dual immunofluorescence co-staining of paraffinized breast tissue sections encompassing normal breast tissue (NBT, n = 8), primary breast tumors (PT, n = 36), and lymph node metastases (MT, n = 10) (Fig. 5J). The confocal micrographs of immunofluorescence co-staining of GPX4 (green) and Vimentin (red) in representative breast cancer tissue sections revealed a predominantly mutually exclusive staining pattern of these two proteins in tumor cells. Across various tumor tissue sections, we observed degrees of Vimentin-positive cells interspersed within well-differentiated epithelial tumor masses that were largely positive for GPX4, with occasional cells showing strong co-expression of both proteins. To substantiate these observations, we analysed the co-localization coefficient to quantify the extent of GPX4 and Vimentin co-expression in breast tissue sections (Fig. 5L), supported by estimating the Manders' overlap coefficient (MOC) (Fig. 5K). Overlay images of the red and green channels from immunofluorescence co-staining demonstrated distinct patterns of expression, where analysis of MOC K1 and K2 indicated a highly significant negative correlation (Spearman's correlation, −0.8767, p < 0.0001) between GPX4 and Vimentin protein levels (Fig. 5K). These findings strongly align with our cellular and computational results. Moreover, we evaluated the relative co-localization coefficients in normal and tumor tissues to ascertain if the co-expression of GPX4 and Vimentin changes with tumorigenesis and metastasis. Dot plots in Fig. 5L highlighted that normal breast tissues exhibited significantly higher co-expression of GPX4 and Vimentin compared to both primary and metastatic tumors. Furthermore, GPX4 expression levels were elevated in primary and metastatic tumors relative to normal breast tissue (Fig. S5G), suggesting that changes in the expression ratio of these proteins may be predictive of breast tumorigenesis.

These findings underscore a negative correlation between GPX4 and Vimentin gene in breast tissue, implying opposing roles and regulatory functions at the cellular level, consistent with our experimental insights (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, Fig. 4). Additionally, the identified significantly increased co-expression in normal breast tissue suggests a potential role in maintaining tissue homeostasis. Most importantly, our data suggests a clinical significance of these findings to human TNBC patients, which suggests a strong predictive value of GPX4low and FSP1high/VIMhigh tumor cell expression state as a molecular signature for the worst outcome under chemotherapy.

Chemotherapy-induced FSP1 overexpression in TNBC is a therapeutic target that synergizes with chemotherapy and GPX4 inhibitor RSL3

As we have observed that the perturbations suppressing activity or expression of GPX4 can induce upregulation in FSP1 expression in TNBC cells (Fig. 4G–I) and that FSP1 expression levels are strongly associated with the prognosis of TNBC patients under chemotherapy (Fig. 5C–E), we investigated its role in TNBC proliferative and dormant DTP cells with a low-GPX4 state. First, we checked FSP1 protein expression levels in PC and paclitaxel-derived PDTP cells and consistently observed a robust increase (up to 400-fold of parental cells) in all TNBC subtypes (Fig. 6A). Interestingly, we found that FSP1 predominantly localized in the cellular cytoplasm (Fig. 6B). We then assessed whether other chemotherapeutic agents also induce FSP1 upregulation in TNBC cells apart from paclitaxel and occurs early in DTP cells, which is then sustained in proliferative DTP cells. For this, we followed FSP1 protein levels in TNBC cells treated with cytotoxic doses of chemotherapies for 48 h. Fig. 6C shows that treatment with paclitaxel, CPA, 5FU, and Cisplatin increased FSP1 levels in the TNBC cell lines. Surprisingly, doxorubicin suppressed the expression of FSP1 in TNBC cell lines tested. Results were further confirmed by IF staining of cells after 48h of chemotherapy treatment (Fig. 6D). We additionally analysed time-dependent changes of FSP1 levels in response to paclitaxel in all TNBC cell lines using WB analysis (Fig. 6E). Results suggest that FSP1 upregulation occurs as an early event, within 24 h of treatment and in some TNBC cells, it continues to increase with time. Next, we checked if the FSP1 upregulation observed in PDTP-P cells in vitro also occurs when cells form tumors in xenografts. We found a significantly higher FSP1 expression in tumors generated from MDA-468 PDTP-P cells compared with those from parental cells (Fig. 6F). To strengthen further our findings on FSP1 upregulation in TNBC tumors following chemotherapy treatment, we took a completely independent approach using cells established from the MMTV‐R26Met TNBC mouse model. The MMTV‐R26Met mice develop exclusively TNBC tumor types following an increased expression of the receptor tyrosine kinase MET in the mammary gland [47]. We reasoned that this model would be particularly pertinent also in relation to its unique feature to recapitulate resistance and molecular heterogeneity of TNBC patients. We used 4 different mouse TNBC cell lines derived from independent tumors to model Basal-like (MGT4, MGT9, and MGT11) and Mesenchymal stem-like (MGT13) subtypes, and determine their response to distinct chemotherapeutic agents after 48h of treatment (Fig. 6G). The percentage of cell viability shown in the heatmaps illustrates the heterogeneous response of these mice TNBC models to chemotherapeutic agents, with MGT9 and MGT11 cell lines being more resistant to treatments (Fig. 6G). We then analysed FSP1 expression in these mice TNBC cells post 48h of treatment and observed very similar results found in human TNBC cell lines (Fig. 6H), with increased FSP1 levels in treated cells (up to 29-fold in docetaxel-treated cells), apart from doxorubicin as in human cell lines. FSP1 expression is known to suppress ferroptosis under cellular lipid peroxidation stress conditions, including GPX4 inhibition [31], thus supporting cell survival. Therefore, we hypothesized that chemotherapy-induced high FSP1 expression in TNBC PDTP cells might contribute to chemoresistance. To test this possibility, we performed shRNA-mediated FSP1 downregulation and followed the response to different chemotherapeutic agents in MDA-468 PDTP-P compared with PC. As observed in Fig. 6I, FSP1 silencing substantially re-sensitized the PDTP-P cells to paclitaxel, also improving their response to cisplatin and 5FU. Colony formation assays further strengthened these observations (Fig. 6J). Very interestingly, we observed that shRNA-mediated GPX4 silencing drastically increases resistance to paclitaxel and cisplatin in MDA-468 PC (Fig. S6A). This is coherent with the observed FSP1 upregulation triggered by GPX4 knockdown (Fig. 4I) as well as paclitaxel and cisplatin treatment (Fig. 6C and D). Likewise, treatment of sodium selenate that increased endogenous expression of GPX4 and concomitant downregulation of FSP1 in PDTP (Fig. 6SB), reduced the resistance towards chemotherapies (Fig. S6C). As we have observed that most chemotherapy agents increase FSP1 expression in TNBC cells within 24h of treatment, we rationalized that inhibition of FSP1 in a post-chemotherapy setting might improve the response to chemotherapy agents in TNBC cells. We therefore, tested this hypothesis using dose-response matrix-based cell viability assays in which MDA-468 cells were treated with increasing doses of paclitaxel, doxorubicin, or cisplatin for 48h, and thereafter treated with two concentrations of iFSP1, a potent inhibitor of FSP1, for the next 24h (Fig. 6K). We observed a marginal benefit of adding iFSP1 post-chemotherapy in sensitizing cells to chemotherapy, as indicated by the heatmaps of percent cell viability presented in Fig. 6K. Since PDTP-P have high FSP1 expression and are more sensitive to iFSP1 (Fig. S6D) we pre-treated them with iFSP1 and as seen in Fig. 6L, FSP1 inhibition re-sensitized cells to all chemotherapies tested. In order to understand whether iFSP1 and chemotherapy combinations have synergistic effects to induce cell death in TNBC cells, we calculated Combination Index (CI) scores at different drug combinations in a dose-response matrix (Fig. 6K and L), as described previously [27]. We found that iFSP1 added at 25 μM (∼IC50) is acting in synergy with chemotherapeutic agents (CI < 1) in all 2h pre-treated cases (Fig. S6 G, H), as well as in 24h post-chemotherapy treated cases (Fig. S6 I,J).

Previous studies on cancer DTP cells suggested the possibility of targeting GPX4 due to their vulnerability to GPX4 inhibitors. However, this approach has a clinical limitation due to the toxicity associated with GPX4 inhibitors [17]. Our results indicate that monotherapy against GPX4 may be an inviable therapeutic approach for TNBC as it leads to a robust upregulation of FSP1, which contributes to chemoresistance. Therefore, we hypothesized that in TNBC, PDTP susceptibility to GPX4 inhibitors can be better exploited in combination with an FSP1 blockage. We tested this possibility by studying the dose response of RSL3 and iFSP1 combinations in PDTP-P compared with PC. Strikingly, we observed a robust effect of combined GPX4 plus FSP1 blockage exclusively in PDTP cells, with up to 93 % of cell death (Fig. 6M). The combination of RSL3 and iFSP1 worked synergistically (Fig. 6M, right panel; CI up to 0.22). Instead, parental cells were hardly affected (Fig. 6M and S6K). These results are further supported by the observation that FSP1 silencing produced acute vulnerability to RSL3 in PDTP cells (Fig. S6E), and interestingly, GPX4 knockdown cells have more susceptibility to iFSP1 (Fig. S6F), therefore, serving as a confirmation for the assumed RSL3 mode of action. Finally, we wanted to identify the mode of cell death in MDA-468 PDTP cells upon iFSP1 and RSL3 combination therapy. As both inhibitors target master regulators of the ferroptosis pathway, we performed a lipid peroxidation assay using BODIPY 581⁄591C11 fluorescent reporter lipid probe. As seen in fluorescence micrographs and the bar graph showing the red/green ratio in Fig. 6N, iFSP1 and RSL3 co-treatment very specifically induced lipid peroxidation in paclitaxel-selected PDTP cells, which increased within 12h of treatment (Fig. S6L). We then verified ferroptosis by rescue experiments with ferroptosis inhibitor Liproxstatin-1, PDTP-P cells pre-treated with Liproxstatin-1 showed significant recovery from cell death mediated by the iFSP1 and RSL3 combination (Fig. 6O). Since RSL3 can inhibit other molecules of the ROS pathway like PRXS and TXN1, we further validated these results with GPX4-in-3, GPX-in-5 (Fig. S6M–P), and TRi-1 (Fig. S6Q). These results suggest that FSP1 suppression specifically has synergy with GPX4 inhibition and not with the TXN-1 inhibitor (Fig. S6 M, N, and Q). Moreover, a combination of either RSL3 (Fig. S6R) or GPX4-in-3 (Fig. S6S) with iFSP1 is relatively non-toxic to normal mammary epithelial cells MCF10A. These results substantiate that TNBC PDTP cells can specifically be targeted by co-inhibiting FSP1 and GPX4 in a highly synergistic manner by induction of ferroptosis.

Finally, we looked into any possible correlation of our above findings in human breast cancers using the Kaplan–Meier curve of overall survival of breast cancer patients with high AIFM2 mRNA as well as somatic copy number alterations (SCNA) utilizing the METABRIC dataset (n = 995 patients). We observed that in stratified breast cancer patients with high AIFM2 expression (Fig. 6P) or copy number (Fig. S6T), with low GPX4 expression is an indication of poor overall survival. We found that these results also apply at the PAN cancer level. Fig. S6U shows that in PAN cancer patients (TCGA pan-cancer dataset) with AIFM2 low mRNA expression (n = 4373), when stratified into GPX4-high and GPX4-low expressing groups, the overall survival of patients with GPX4-high is significantly better than that of the AIFM2-low and GPX4-low groups. These results may suggest that high FSP1 with low GPX4 expression in breast cancers may have some clinical significance; however, these results warrant extensive validation in patients. Collectively, these results suggest that upregulated FSP1 in TNBC PDTP with a low-GPX4 state provides a remarkably potent opportunity for their therapeutic targeting due to a highly synergistic interaction between GPX4 and the FSP1 pair (Fig. 6Q).

Discussion

Recent reports across various solid tumor types confirm that a subset of tumor cells can persist in a dormant state under targeted or chemotherapeutic drugs, eventually regaining proliferation and leading to post-therapy tumor recurrence with metastasis [[14], [15], [16],46,48,49]. These therapy-tolerant cells may evolve through the selection of pre-existing resistant clones or significant molecular reprogramming during treatment, facilitating a cellular adaptive response [50,51]. Studies consistently indicate higher TNBC recurrence rates in patients with residual disease that strongly correlates with residual tumor burden with poor event-free survival globally [13,[52], [53], [54], [55]]. Our cellular modelling of DTPs using various chemotherapeutic classes corroborates these clinical findings. We observed that TNBC cells possess a significant capacity to withstand drug-induced stress to develop in DTPs and progress to an aggressive proliferative DTP state, this is irrespective of the molecular subtype (Fig. 7A). Furthermore, our cellular models reflect that post-therapy recurrence in TNBC often involves more resistant and metastatic disease thatcontributs to worse clinical outcomes [12,56]. Clinical studies widely acknowledge that chemotherapy resistance in TNBC primarily occurs through non-genetic mechanisms. Earlier investigations using methods such as in situ hybridization [57], genomic profiling [58,59], and tumor cytogenetic markers have highlighted that neoadjuvant therapy does not alter tumor genetic diversity but enriches tumor cells with mesenchymal phenotypes [57]. We show that TNBC PDTP cells exhibit a mesenchymal state with elevated vimentin expression across all subtypes (Fig. 7A). Recent studies, including those with anti-VEGF antibody Bevacizumab [59,60], indicate that non-mutational transcriptome changes predominantly contribute to drug-resistant tumor cell emergence. Similarly, analysis of pre- and post-neoadjuvant chemotherapy samples [60,61], and patient-derived xenografts [60] suggests that resistant genotypes and persister clonal populations, with distinct transcriptomic and proteomic profiles, may pre-exist and expand under therapeutic pressure. These studies, alongside our findings, suggest an evolution of DTP populations from pre-existing cells with significant molecular and phenotypic plasticity, including adaptations in EMT. Notably, BL-1/2 TNBC subtypes can undergo a stable EMT state, while luminal cells enter a partial EMT state. Studies on genetic mouse models indicate that partial EMT enhances metastatic potential, whereas complete EMT promotes drug resistance [37]. Given that LAR subtype tumors are particularly resistant to chemotherapy and exhibit high metastatic rates, understanding their unique ability to undergo transient or partial EMT states could elucidate mechanisms underlying poor clinical responses [37,62].

Cellular reprogramming in DTPs drives altered metabolic states crucial for cell proliferation, invasion, and therapy resistance. Recent research highlights how therapy-stressed and EMT cells adapt energy acquisition through OXPHOS [60], supporting survival and proliferation. DTP states in solid tumors alter reactive oxygen handling, iron, and lipid metabolism, shielding cells from ferroptotic cell death induced by iron and oxidative stress [24,63]. While it is known that TNBC cells are vulnerable to iron chelators [30,64], ferroptosis inducers [25,65], and deprivation of anti-oxidant pathway metabolites [25], our study highlights that PDTP cells exhibit exacerbated redox abnormalities, including elevated lipid ROS and depleted glutathione, with downregulated metabolic regulators like GPX4, HO-1, xCT, and NRF2 [27,30]. This metabolic reprogramming supports DTP acquisition and maintenance, enhancing cellular ROS levels crucial for proliferation and invasion. Vulnerability of TNBC PDTPs to GPX4 inhibitors or ferroptosis inducers like xCT inhibitors underlines the therapeutic potential of these strategies [66].

GPX4 suppression is widely noted in solid tumors during DTP states, yet the phenotypic dependence of the DTP state on compromised glutathione levels remains experimentally unclear. Our study identifies GPX4 as a key regulator of EMT in TNBC cells that is commonly upregulated during metastasis in various cancers [62]. Additionally, our study discovered that GPX4 suppression either genetic ort drug-induced promotes strong FSP1 upregulation in TNBC. This suggests the development of a quick molecular switch by cancer cells under low GPX4 -glutathione metabolic state to support survival under redox and chemotherapy stress, and to proliferate utilizing a glutathione-independent mechanism relying on ubiquinol as an alternative antioxidant (Fig. 7B). These findings also suggest a potential anti-tumor role for GPX4 in TNBC beyond its regulation of ferroptosis, particularly in suppressing metastasis. In-silico analysis of multiple TNBC patient datasets strongly supports an association between low GPX4 expression levels and poorer survival, with a significant negative correlation between GPX4 and EMT/metastatic markers like vimentin. Furthermore, we have identified a high co-expression of FSP1 and VIM genes, combining EMT phenotypes that very effectively predict chemotherapy response in high-grade TNBC tumors with metastasis (Fig. 7C).

Another very important finding of this study is to demonstrate that chemotherapy forces overexpression of FSP1 in TNBC DDTP and PDTP cells that contributes to chemoresistance. An earlier study has shown that upon GPX4 inactivation, FSP1 maintains growth in H460 lung cancer tumors in vivo [28]. Additionally, FSP1 upregulation is observed in cisplatin-resistant HCC, as well as in DTPs developed by treating HCC cells with cisplatin, where it is demonstrated to have a role in cell invasion, migration, and stem cell-like properties of DTP cells [44]. This is in line with our results in TNBC cells as well as clinical data in TNBC patients that the FSP1 expression is associated with worse survival. Furthermore, we also showed that a combination of iFSP1 and RSL3 is exclusively lethal in TNBC PDTP cells. Some other studies have also highlighted that iFSP1 treatment enhanced the sensitivity towards RSL3-induced ferroptosis in several cancer cell lines [31]. Another advantage of targeting FSP1 in cancer lies in the fact that, unlike GPX4, FSP1 has multiple drug-binding pockets [28], thus facilitating its targeting compared with GPX4. Notably, unlike GPX4 knockout in mice, which is embryonically lethal, FSP1-deficient mice develop normally and display no obvious phenotypes, allowing it to be a promising target with relatively less toxicity [44].

In conclusion, this study significantly advances our understanding of the distinct and shared phenotypes and vulnerabilities of PDTPs across different TNBC molecular subtypes, serving as representative models of recurrence-associated drug-resistant subpopulations in patients. There has been a critical gap in comprehending how dormant and proliferative states in TNBC DTPs develop following chemotherapy exposure [18], especially in highly heterogeneous and aggressive cancers like TNBC that have high rates of metastatic recurrence and chemoresistance … Our research provides systematic experimental insights and establishes a foundation to address key questions regarding TNBC DTPs and their adaptive responses to survive and withstand severe chemotherapy-induced stress. FSP1 overexpression emerges as one of the potential adaptive mechanisms in this direction. This knowledge is crucial for developing precise molecular approaches to target both dormant and proliferative DTP states. Further in-depth studies on TNBC DTP states are essential to translate findings into clinical strategies. Extended studies using robust patient-derived organotypic and mouse models that accurately represent TNBC subtypes, coupled with molecular profiling and mathematical modelling-based tools, will uncover unique intrinsic and extrinsic dependencies. These efforts aim to identify synthetic lethal therapeutic combinations to eliminate DTP and proliferative DTP cells effectively.

Cell culture

Different subtypes of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC) cell lines, including MDA-MB-468, MDA-MB-231, MDA-MB-453, HCC70, and HS578T, were originally procured from the American Type Culture Collection (USA). These cells were maintained in RPMI medium (Gibco BRL, USA) supplemented with l-Glutamine (2 mM), sodium bicarbonate (2g/litter), 10 % fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco BRL, USA) and penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL/0.1 mg/mL, respectively; Invitrogen, USA). The cells were cultured in a 5 % CO2 humidified incubator at 37 °C. All the cultures were routinely checked for mycoplasma using a commercially available kit from Biological Industries (Israel) and DAPI nuclear staining. For the validation non-tumorigenic epithelial cell line MCF-10A was used which was procured from IISER Pune, Maharashtra. These cells were cultured in the DMEM F-12 media supplemented with hEGF (20 ng/ml) (Sigma-E9644), Insulin (10μg/ml) (Invitrogen-12585014), cholera toxin (100 ng/ml) (catalogue no. C8052), hydrocortisone (1ug/ml) (catalogue no. H0888), penicillin/streptomycin (100 U/mL/0.1 mg/mL, respectively; Invitrogen, USA) and 5 % heat inactivated horse serum (Gibco-26050088).

TNBC mouse cell line culture

Mouse MGT cell lines were derived from individual MMTV-R26Met TNBC tumors and established as previously described [47]. Mouse TNBC cell lines MGT4, MGT9, MGT11, and MGT13 cells were cultured in complete DMEM/F12 (Dulbecco's modified Eagle's media/F12, 1:1, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with 10 % FBS, penicillin-streptomycin (100 U/mL/0.1 mg/mL), and a cocktail of supplements and growth factors as previously described [47] at 37 °C in a 5 % CO2 atmosphere. PCR-based assays were performed on all cell lines to verify that the cells are Mycoplasma-free.

Generation of DTP and PDTP TNBC models

Cells were treated with increasing doses of chemotherapeutic drugs (Paclitaxel, Doxorubicin, and Cisplatin) for 72 h. Cell viability assays were conducted, and IC50 values were determined for each drug using GraphPad 5.0 software as described previously [27,67]. To generate DTPs, cells were exposed to IC85-90 concentrations of each drug for 72 h. The surviving cells were then maintained in a drug-free medium to allow recovery and growth.

MTT assay

To determine the cytotoxic effect of drugs, dose-response experiments were performed. In a 96 well plate format, MDA-MB-468 (12000 cells), HCC70 (9000 cells), MDA-MB-231 (9000 cells), HS578T (3000 cells), or MDA-MB-453 (9000 cells) cells were seeded for 16 h. Cells were treated with drugs for 72 h with an increasing concentration of drugs. Cell viability was determined using MTT assays as described previously [27], till the visible formation of blue formazan crystals. After solubilisation of formazan crystals using 100 μl of stop solution, the Optical Densities (OD) were determined at 570 nm (reference wavelength 630 nm) using a Cytation5 microplate reader (Biotek, Agilent). Dose-response curves were generated using Graph-Pad Prism 5.0 software, and IC50 values were determined.

Colony formation assay

Cells were seeded up to 5, 10, and 15 % confluence and allowed to grow to form visible colonies. The media was replenished every three days. Colonies were fixed in 4 % PFA and stained with crystal violet. Photographs of the plates were taken using a 16.2 Megapixel digital camera (Nikon D7000) with a light box to illuminate the plate.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

To study the sub-cellular details using in-house TEM central facility of TMC-ACTREC, cells were grown as a confluent monolayer, fixed with 3 % glutaraldehyde, washed with 0.1 M of sodium cacodylate pH 7.4, and postfixed with 1 % osmium tetroxide. Cells were then dehydrated and processed for sample preparation. Grids were contrasted with alcoholic uranyl acetate for 30 s and dipped once in lead citrate. The grids were observed under a JEOL 1400Plus transmission electron microscope at an accelerating voltage of 120 KeV and 10000X. Images were then captured using a Slow Scan CCD camera (TRS, Germany).

Immunoblotting

Whole cell lysate of different cell lines for total protein was prepared in lysis buffer consisting 0.2 % Triton X- 100, 50 mM HEPES (pH 7.5), 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM MgCl2, 50 mM NaF, 0.5 mM NaVO3, 20 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1 mM phenylmethyl sulfonyl fluoride, leupeptin (10 μg/ml), and aprotinin (10 μg/ml) as described previously [30]. The total protein concentration of the cell lysate was estimated by Bradford assay (Bio-Rad, Hercules, CA). For Western blotting, equal amounts (up to 80 μg) of total cell lysates were loaded on an SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis system (Bio-Rad) and transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane, which was then processed for immunoblotting to detect specific proteins. The membrane was probed with Primary antibodies for GPX4, xCT, HO1, SOD1, E Cadherin, Vimentin (dilution, 1:1000), and GAPDH/alpha-tubulin/beta-actin (1:2000) as a loading control. After incubation with species-specific secondary HRP-labelled IgG (1:5000), membranes were developed using ECL reagent (Bio-rad), and the images were acquired on ChemiDoc Imaging System (Bio-Rad). Images were used for the densitometric analysis using the ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA), and the intensity of each band was normalized to its respective loading control. The data was presented as folds of control.

Isolation of total RNA and real-time PCR reactions

Cell lysates were prepared in RNA later buffer, and total RNA was isolated using Qiagen RNeasy Kit according to the manufacturer's instructions. cDNA was synthesized from 2 μg of RNA using the ABI High-Capacity Reverse Transcriptase Kit (Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies). A total of 10 ng of cDNA was used for the real-time PCR using ABI SyBr Green PCR Master mix (Applied Biosystems, Life Technologies) in QuantStudio 12K Flex Real-Time PCR System (Life Technologies) with gene-specific primers sets, β actin or GAPDH gene expression levels were used as for normalization in all experiments. The expression of target genes was plotted as a fold change of experimental controls after calculating the relative to GAPDH or Actin as a housekeeping gene using the 2(-ΔΔCt) method. The sequences of forward and reverse oligonucleotides used in this study are shown in Table S1.

Migration assays

Cells were seeded in 96-well plates at 90–100 % confluence and serum-starved overnight. Cells were treated with 5 μg/ml of Mitomycin C for 3 h. The scratch was made using a 200 μl sterile tip, and cells were stimulated with a complete medium containing 10 % FBS. Phase-contrast images of migrating cells at initial and different time points were captured using an inverted microscope (Optika-SRL, IM-5FLD, C–P6 fitted with a 6.3 Megapixel camera) at a 20× objective. The average distance travelled was quantified from 3 independent experiments, and data were plotted in percent wound closure and migration rate over time.

Invasion assay

Matrigel degradation and invasion assays were performed using Boyden chambers (BD Falcon) with 8 μm pore size in a 24-well format. Boyden transwell chambers were coated with 5 μg of the Matrigel suspended in 100 μl of the ice-cold media and incubated at 37 °C for 1 h. In 200 μl of serum-free media, 2 10ˆ5 cells were seeded in the Boyden chambers, and in the lower chamber, 450 μl of media containing 10 % of FBS was added to incubate for 16 h. The Boyden chambers were taken out, washed in PBS, and fixed in 4 % Paraformaldehyde (PFA) in PBS for 10 min. The chambers were then washed in PBS and stained in 0.03 % crystal violet solution for 10 min. Cells from the inner surface of the chamber were carefully removed using cotton swabs, and the chambers were washed with PBS and air dried. Images of the lower surface containing invaded cells were acquired from 10 different fields on an upright AxioImager Z1 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) using a 10× objective lens. The number of cells invaded was calculated from 3 independent experiments, and the data were plotted as folds of control.

Cell area and aspect ratio of cells

In order to describe morphological changes, we did area and aspect ratio measurements using Fiji ImageJ software. Binary images were created by thresholding the images to remove background noise. The area measurements were performed on the binary images using the 'Wand (tracing) tool’ in Fiji ImageJ. The aspect ratio of cells was calculated based on their major and minor axes. The major axis refers to the longest axis inside the cell, while the minor axis is the perpendicular axis to the major axis and is located in the middle of the cell when the cell shape resembles an oval. In cases where the cell shape is different from an oval, the major axis is still chosen as the longest axis inside the cell, while the minor axis is arbitrarily chosen to be perpendicular to the major axis and maximally located close to the relative cell center. To calculate the aspect ratio, the length of the major axis was divided by the length of the minor axis for each of the 100 randomly selected cells.

Immunofluorescence staining

Immunofluorescence staining of cells was performed as described earlier [68]. Briefly, cells grown on coverslips were treated as per the experimental procedures, washed with PBS, and fixed using either 4 % PFA in PBS (20 min at RT) or chilled methanol (10 min at −20 °C). After fixation, cells were washed 3 times with PBS and permeabilized with 0.25 % Triton X-100 in PBS for 10 min. Cells were incubated with primary antibody at 4 °C overnight, followed by PBS washes 3 times. Secondary antibody incubation with Alexa Fluor 488 and 568 was performed for 1 h at room temperature. Nuclear staining was done with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Sigma). Confocal microscopic images were acquired using a Leica confocal microscope at 63× oil immersion objective using the Leica Application Suit X (LAS X) software platform. Images were processed and analysed using LAS X software.

ROS measurements

Intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) were quantitatively measured in live cell samples using the cell-permeant reagent 2′,7′-dichlorofluorescein diacetate (H2DCFDA) from the Cellular ROS Assay Kit (Abcam, ab113851) and CellROX™ Orange (Thermo, C10443) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Cells were cultured overnight in a black-walled, clear-bottom 96-well plate. Cells were then treated with the indicated H2O2 (100 μM, 2 h) or ferroptosis inducer as indicated in the Figures. For estimation of ROS, cells were then stained with 10 μM H2DCFDA or 5 μM CellROX Orange reagent for 30 min at 37 °C protected from light. After washing the cells, the plate was read on a microplate reader (Cytation5 spectrophotometer, Biotek, Agilent) upon setting fluorescence at Ex/Em = 485/535 nm for H2DCFDA and 545/565 nm for CellROX Orange. Data are presented as folds of controls. The reading values were normalized to the number of living cells in each well.

GSH measurements

Total GSH levels in cells were detected by using a luminescence-based GSHGlo Assay reagent kit (Promega, catalog no. B6912) as instructed by the manufacturer. The assay quantifies total glutathione (reduced and oxidized forms) in cell lysates. To detect GSH, cell lysates of control and treatment experimental sets were prepared directly in the multiwall plate by adding 50 μl μl GSH-specific lysis buffer with the reagents provided in the kit, followed by a 5 min incubation, and then 50 50 μl of Luciferin Generation Reagent was added to each well. The mixture was incubated for 30 min at RT, and the assays were mixed and incubated for 30 min. After the incubation, 100 μl of Luciferin Detection Reagent was added to all wells and mixed. After a 15-min incubation, luminescence was measured using a multi-plate reader (Cytation5 spectrophotometer, Biotek). GSH content was calculated from luminescence measurements and presented as means ± SD fold change relative to controls.

Iron measurements

Cellular iron (Fe2+ and Fe3+) and total iron content were measured using the colorimetric Iron Assay Kit (Abcam, ab83366) according to the manufacturer's protocol. Briefly, up to 2.5 x 105 cells were grown and treated in 6-well plates. Post-treatment cells were washed with cold PBS and then lysed in 100 μl of iron assay buffer. Cell lysates (100 μl) were incubated with 5 μl of iron reducer for 30 min at 37 °C in a 96-well plate format. After the incubation, 100 μl of iron probe was added to detect ferrous and ferric ions. The absorbance was immediately measured at 593 nm with a microplate reader (Cytation5 spectrophotometer, Biotek). Results were normalized to protein content in cell lysates and expressed as means of fold change relative to control.

Lipid peroxidation assay

A fluorescence shift-based assay (Image-iT Lipid Peroxidation Kit, Cat. No. C10445, Thermo Fisher Scientific) was used to analyze the lipid peroxidation in live cells using the BODIPY 581⁄591C11 fluorescent reporter lipid probe as described previously [32,35]. Cells were plated in black wall clear bottom 96-well Microplates (Corning, cat. No. 3603) for 16 h, and proceeded for experimental control and treatments as indicated. The cells were then incubated with 10 μM lipid peroxidation sensor C11-BODIPY (581/591) in a complete medium and incubated for 30 min in a humidified incubator with 5 % CO2 at 37 °C temperature. Cells were washed with PBS, and 100 μl of fresh medium was added to the cells. Live cell imaging was performed using the IncuCyte S3 (Sartorius) for acquiring bright-field and fluorescence images at 10× magnification. Excitation/emission settings were 581/590 nm (Texas Red filter set) for the reduced dye and 488/510 nm (fluorescein isothiocyanate filter set) for the oxidized dye. The red-to-green fluorescence shift was used to detect lipid peroxidation in acquired images and the data was analysed using IncuCyte 2020A software.

Flow cytometry to detect lipid peroxidation

The BL-1 subtype cell line MDA-MB-468 was used as parental control (PC) and its subsequent drug-tolerant persister (PDTP) cells were cultured in standard conditions (37 °C, 5 % CO2) in complete growth medium. The cells were seeded in 6-well plates at densities of 1.5 × 105 cells/well (PC) and 1.0 × 105 cells/well (PDTP) and allowed to grow for 48 h prior to treatment. Only cultures not exceeding 72 h in age were used to ensure consistency. At the time of treatment, cell confluency was maintained between 70 % and 80 %. Trypsinization during seeding and harvesting was performed with care to avoid overexposure. Drug treatments were carried out in 3 mL of complete medium per well. Fresh working concentrations were prepared for each experiment. The following conditions were applied: GPX4in-5 at 0.75 μM for 3 h, GPX4in-3 at 1 μM for 3.5 h, Erastin at 4 μM for 4 h, and cumene hydroperoxide (positive control) at 200 μM for 2 h. When stock solutions were highly concentrated, intermediate dilutions were made to closely match the final working concentration. After treatment, cells were washed with PBS only if notable cell death was observed. Cells were then trypsinized (90 s for PDTP; 150 s for PC), centrifuged at 1400 RPM for 3 min, and the supernatant discarded. Lipid peroxidation was assessed using Image-iT™ Lipid Peroxidation Sensor (BODIPY™ 581/591C11; Thermo Fisher Scientific) at a final concentration of 1 μM, prepared fresh in complete medium. The cells were then incubated in 500 μL media containing the dye in each tube individually. Samples were gently mixed and incubated at 37 °C for 25 min. After the incubation, the cells were centrifuged until a compact had pellet formed further washed once with PBS, and resuspended in 500 μL PBS. Samples were transferred to FACS tubes and analysed immediately using flow cytometry. Lipid peroxidation levels were quantified based on fluorescence shift from red to green emission. The samples were analysed using Attune NxT by Life Technologies.

Oil Red O staining

MDA-MB-468 parental control (PC) cells and MDA-MB-468 drug-tolerant persister (PDTP) were seeded onto coverslips placed in 24-well plates and allowed to grow for 72 h. After 72 h, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed once with 1 mL of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS). The cells were fixed using 1 mL of 10 % formalin was added to each well and incubated at room temperature (RT) for 10 min. The initial fixative was then discarded and replaced with fresh 10 % formalin, and the cells were left to fix for 1 h at RT. After fixation, the formalin was removed, and cells were rinsed twice with distilled water (ddH2O). This was followed by a 5-min wash with 2 mL of 60 % isopropanol at RT further performed to prepare the cells for staining. After removing the isopropanol, cells were allowed to air dry completely. The lipid droplets were stained using 1 mL of Oil Red O working solution. This was added to each well and incubated at RT for 10 min. Later the excess stain was removed, and the cells were rinsed four times with ddH2O to eliminate background staining. The visualization of lipid accumulation in the cell was done using an upright AxioImager Z1 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) using a 40× objective lens at 20 different fields. For quantification of lipid content, any remaining water was thoroughly removed, and 1 mL of 100 % isopropanol was added to each well to elute the retained Oil Red O dye. The plates were gently shaken for 10 min, and the solution was pipetted up and down to ensure complete dye extraction. The eluted dye was transferred to 1.5-mL microcentrifuge tubes, and absorbance was measured at 500 nm using 100 % isopropanol as the blank. Oil Red O stock solution was prepared by dissolving 0.35 g of Oil Red O powder (Sigma, Cat# O-0625) in 100 mL of isopropanol, which was then followed by stirring and filtration through a 0.2 μm filter. The working solution was freshly prepared by mixing 6 mL of stock with 4 mL of ddH2O, incubating at RT for 20 min, and filtering again (0.2 μm). A 10 % formalin solution was made by diluting 27 mL of formalin stock (37 %, Merck, Cat# K36658003) with 63 mL of ddH2O and 10 mL of 10X PBS. Isopropanol solutions (100 % and 60 %) were prepared using reagent-grade isopropanol (Merck, Cat# K36543834)

FerroOrange staining

FerroOrange staining was performed in MDA-MB-468 parental control (PC) and MDA-MB-468 drug-tolerant persister (PDTP) cells to detect intracellular labile ferrous iron (Fe2+). Cells were seeded in 24-well plates on the coverslips and cultured in 37 °C, 5 % CO2 for 24 h. Lyophilized FerroOrange was thawed at RT and then centrifuged to collect the powder. It was further reconstituted by dissolving 24 μg of FerroOrange dye in 35 μL of dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO) to prepare a 1 mmol/L stock solution. Later 1 μmol/L working solution was freshly prepared by diluting the stock 1:1000 in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), in the dark. After removing the culture medium, cells were washed three times with PBS to remove excess FBS and incubated with the FerroOrange working solution for 30 min at 37 °C in the dark. Following incubation, cells were gently washed with PBS and further DAPI staining was done to stain the nucleus. Afterwards the cells were washed with PBS again and mounted on the glass slide and sealed. Further the cells were imaged using a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope. Imaging was performed at 100 × magnification, to capture intracellular iron distribution. Fluorescence was detected using appropriate laser settings for FerroOrange (excitation/emission ∼543/580 nm), and identical acquisition parameters were applied to all groups to ensure consistency. All staining was conducted under serum-free conditions to minimize background, and the working solution was freshly prepared for each experiment.

Whole scan imaging

Lung tissues from tumor-bearing mice injected with triple negative breast cancer cells line 468 PC and 468 PDTP were harvested for histological analysis. Lung tissues were dissected and fixed in 10 % neutral buffered formalin for 24–48 h. Fixed tissues were dehydrated through a graded ethanol series, cleared in xylene, and embedded in paraffin. Sections of 10 μm thickness were cut using a microtome, mounted on glass slides, and dried before staining. H&E staining was performed by deparaffinizing sections in xylene, rehydrating through graded ethanol, staining with hematoxylin, followed by differentiation and bluing, then staining with eosin. After dehydration and clearing, slides were mounted with DPX mounting medium. Whole slide imaging was conducted using a Tissue Scope iQ bright field slide scanner (Catalog number: iQ 0234) 0.25 μm/pixel at 40× magnification to capture high-resolution images of stained sections. Image analysis was performed using Qu-Path software, and high-resolution TIFF images were extracted from Qu-Path for further evaluation.

Breast cancer mouse xenografts

Female NOD/SCID mice 4–6 weeks old and weighing 18–22 g, were used in this study. Animals were housed at the institutional animal facility and maintained according to Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) standards, with unrestricted access to food and water. All experimental procedures were carried out strictly as per the approved protocols of the IAEC of TMC-ACTREC (proposal number: 47/2022). For orthotropic TNBC tumor generation, a suspension of 2.5 x 106 MDA-MB-468 PC or PDTP-P breast cancer cells in 50 μl of sterile PBS was bilaterally implanted into the fourth inguinal mammary glands of female mice (n = 6 per group). Tumor size was monitored bi-weekly using a digital Vernier caliper once the tumors became palpable, and body weight was recorded bi-weekly. Tumor volumes were calculated (width2 × length/2). Mice were sacrificed once the tumor volume approached the maximal permissible limits as per IAEC protocols. Tumors and visceral organs were excised and processed for immunofluorescence staining procedures as described elsewhere [82]. Imaging of stained tissues was performed using a 60× objective of Nikon's 10th generation AX/AX R confocal microscope and quantitation of mean fluorescence intensities in each channel was recorded. For histopathological studies of tumor and organ tissues, hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was performed on deparaffinized tissue sections. AxioImager Z1 microscope (Carl Zeiss, Jena, Germany) using a 4× objective lens. Student's t-test was applied for statistical analysis.

Tissue array immunofluorescence

The human breast cancer tissue array (catalog number: NBP2-30212, NOVUS) was deparaffinized and hydrated as per the manufacturer's instructions, followed by antigen retrieval in a heated tris-EDTA buffer (pH 9) using a microwave. Autofluorescence quenching was performed using CuSO4·NH4Cl (pH-6) for 15 min. Tissue arrays were permeabilized using 0.2 % Triton X-100 in Tris buffer for 15 min and then stained with primary antibody for vimentin and GPX4 (1:100 dilution). Fluorophore-tagged secondary antibodies (Alexa Fluor 568 anti-mouse IgG of Invitrogen, A-11004, and Alexa Fluor 488 anti-rabbit IgG of Invitrogen, A-11001) were used in a 1:200 dilution and incubated for 1 h at room temperature. DAPI was used for nuclear staining, and the coverslip was mounted using a Mowiol-based mounting medium. Immunofluorescence images were acquired on 60× objective of Nikon's 10th generation AX/AX R confocal microscope and quantitation of mean fluorescence intensities in each channel and colocalization coefficient (Pearson's colocalization coefficient) and contribution of each channel to the colocalization was estimated using Mander's overlap coefficients k1 and K2. These parameters were used for scoring the tissue staining using Nikon Elements AR analysis 5.42.00 software to estimate the colocalization coefficient. Data was presented in mean fluorescence intensity from three representative images per tissue section in each channel and their contribution to the colocalization.

Bioinformatics analysis

Pre-processing of Datasets: The Microarray (GSE6434) dataset was retrieved from the NCBI GEO database and was pre-processed to procure the gene-wise expression from the probe-wise expression matrix using respective annotation files for the mapping of probes to genes. For single-cell RNA sequencing data (GSE176078), the MAGIC (version 2.0.3) [96] imputation algorithm was used to recover noisy and sparse single-cell data using diffusion geometry. Appropriate platform annotation files were utilized to map individual reads to genes. RNAseq (GSE183187) raw counts were obtained from NCBI GEO and were used to align the reads with the hg38-human (or mm10-mouse) reference genome. They were then normalized for gene length and transformed to TPM (transcripts per million) values. Log2 normalization was carried out to acquire the final expression data. TCGA datasets were obtained using UCSC Xena tools.

Scoring Methods: To quantify the enrichment of epithelial and mesenchymal signatures, ssGSEA (single sample gene set enrichment analysis) was carried out on KS epithelial and KS mesenchymal gene lists, respectively, using the GSEAPY Python library. The normalized enrichment score (NES) for these gene sets was obtained wherein a higher Epi score denotes a more epithelial phenotype, whereas a higher Mes score indicates the enrichment of a mesenchymal phenotype. The EMT score was calculated by subtracting the Mes score from the Epi score. The gene set for the Hallmark ROS pathway, Hallmark Apoptosis pathway, and Hallmark OXPHOS pathway was obtained from MSigDB [69]. These gene sets were used to calculate the respective scores for the pathways using the ssGSEA method. The activity scores of Hallmark pathways and EMT signatures for the single-cell RNA sequencing datasets were computed using AU Cell (version 1.18.1) [70,71] from the R package ‘Bioconductor’ with default parameters.

Datasets

Gene and protein expression analysis of breast cancer patients were done based on the Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) breast cancer dataset. For Survival analysis, TCGA gene expression was used on Kaplan-Meier Plotter (https://kmplot.com/analysis), and Recurrence-Free survival (RFS) was analysed using median cut. For protein expression TCPA portal (https://tcpaportal.org/tcpa/survival_analysis.html) was used to derive survival, and Kaplan-Meier plots were derived with the BRCA data set containing 901 patient samples. ROC plots in chemotherapy-treated patients were generated using the ROC plotter portal (http://www.rocplot.org/site/index) [28]. Gene correlation plots were obtained using the cancer tool (CANCERTOOL (cicbiogune.es) using different breast cancer datasets.

Statistical analysis

All the in-vitro data are presented as mean ± SD from at least three different experimental repeats, unless otherwise mentioned. To compare between experimental groups, we used Student's t-test for the experiments. The reported p-values associated with dataset analysis (TCGA or TCPA) are generated using online portals. p-value <0.05 is considered significant.
