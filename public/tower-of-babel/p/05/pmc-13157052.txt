Sepsis-triggered proteolysis of profibrinolytic annexin A2 associated with microvasculopathy-related organ dysfunction
Authors: Hana I Lim, Michael Bender, Huigen Chen, Frances M West, Julio Lanfranco, Dena Almeida, Katherine A Hajjar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Sepsis-triggered proteolysis of A2 reduces cell surface fibrinolytic activity.•A2 proteolysis is associated with microvasculopathic organ dysfunction in humans.

Introduction

Sepsis is a dysregulated host inflammatory response to infection that causes systemic tissue injury extending beyond the original site.1 Affecting ∼1.7 million adults in the United States annually, it carries a mortality rate of 30% to 50% and often leads to long-term morbidity among survivors, including cardiovascular, renal, neurologic, and pulmonary dysfunction.2, 3, 4 Despite being a significant burden to the health care system, targeted therapies remain elusive, with current management limited to infection control and supportive care.

One key pathologic feature of sepsis is coagulopathy, driven by dysregulation changes in both prothrombotic and antithrombotic pathways.5, 6, 7 Coagulation proteins contribute not only to clot formation but also to inflammation via various signaling mechanisms.8, 9, 10, 11 However, clinical trials targeting fluid-phase coagulation proteins and natural anticoagulants, such as tissue factor pathway inhibitor, antithrombin, and activated protein C, have failed to reduce mortality and increased bleeding risk.12, 13, 14

Annexin A2 (A2) is a calcium-dependent phospholipid-binding protein that forms a heterotetramer with S100A10 (p11) and can serve as a fibrinolytic receptor. The A2 complex binds tissue plasminogen activator (tPA) at L8 to L13 within the N-terminal domain and plasminogen at K308-R309 near the C-terminus, thereby enhancing the activation of plasmin at the cell surface.15, 16, 17, 18 This complex promotes fibrinolysis through its assembly of plasminogen and tPA.19 In monocytes and macrophages, which play key roles in sepsis, A2 facilitates plasmin-mediated fibrin clearance, matrix metalloproteinase-9 activation, and pathogen elimination.18,20,21

Although a mouse model suggests that A2 deficiency impairs immune responses and microvascular function in sepsis,20 human studies remain limited. We hypothesized that A2 expression is decreased in human sepsis, possibly contributing to inflammation and microvasculopathy, thereby worsening disease severity.

Study design

Study participants and sample preparation

Under an institutional review board–approved protocol (1007011160), blood was collected from 65 adult patients with sepsis within 24 hours of admission to the Medical Intensive Care Unit at Weill Cornell Medicine (2011-2018) and from 27 healthy controls. All patients with sepsis met Sepsis-3 diagnostic criteria.22 Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) and platelet-poor plasma were isolated from whole blood via Ficoll-Paque centrifugation within 1 to 2 hours after blood draw according to a previously published protocol.23 PBMCs were cryopreserved for immunoblot and plasmin generation assays as previously described.24

Quantification of A2

Using previously described methods,25 washed PBMCs were resuspended and lysed on ice in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer (150mM Tris HCl, pH 7.5, 150mM NaCl, 1mM EDTA, 1% octylphenoxypolyethoxyethanol [Igepal CA-630; Sigma-Aldrich], and 1% sodium deoxycholate) containing a broad-spectrum protease inhibitor cocktail active against serine and cysteine proteases as well as metalloproteases and calpains (Roche; catalog no. 11836153001; 1:100 v/v dilution). Lysate proteins (20μg) were resolved on 4% to 20% linear gradient, reducing sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide electrophoresis gels (Invitrogen), and proteins were transferred to polyvinylidene difluoride membranes (Thermo Fisher Scientific; constant 110 V; 4°C; 90 minutes). The membranes were washed, blocked, and incubated with primary antibodies (4°C; 16 hours), and then washed again and incubated with secondary antibodies (20°C; 60 minutes). Primary antibodies included mouse monoclonal anti–human A2 (1:1000; Becton Dickinson; catalog no. 610069), directed against a large core domain epitope (Figure 1A) and used to quantify full-length A2, and rabbit monoclonal anti–human A2 (1:1000; Cell Signaling; catalog no. D11G2), directed against a restricted C-terminal epitope surrounding F307 and used to detect A2 fragments (Figure 1A). Glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) was used as a loading control and estimated using mouse monoclonal anti-human GAPDH (1:13 000; Meridian; catalog no. H86504M). Secondary antibodies were ovine horseradish peroxidase (HRP)–conjugated anti–mouse immunoglobulin G (IgG; 1:1000 to 1:13 000; GE Healthcare; catalog no. NA931) and donkey HRP-conjugated anti–rabbit IgG (1:2000; GE Healthcare; catalog no. NA934). Blots were developed with chemiluminescent HRP substrate (Enhanced Chmiluminescence, Promega). For estimation of full-length A2, pixel counts were acquired from scanned images of uppermost immunoblot bands whose intensity fell within the linear signal range of interest, using Image Studio Lite software, version 5.2. Protein ratios were calculated with respect to the corresponding GAPDH signal.

Plasmin generation assay

Frozen PBMCs were thawed, washed, incubated with ε-aminocaproic acid (Sigma; catalog no. A2504), and then washed and resuspended with bovine serum albumin, human glu-plasminogen (100nM; BioMedica Diagnostics; catalog no. 400), and tPA (20nM; Boehringer Ingelheim; Actilyse). Cleavage of a fluorogenic, plasmin-specific substrate (AFC-81 [D-Val-Leu-Lys-7 amino-4-trifluoromethylcoumarin] 125mM; MP Biomedicals; catalog no. 03AFC15605) in a final volume of 200μL was monitored via microplate fluorometry as previously described.24,25

Subcellular fractionation

Subcellular fractionation was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol (Pierce Biotechnology; catalog no. 78840). Lactate dehydrogenase (Santa Cruz; catalog no. SC133123) and lysosome-associated membrane protein-1 (LAMP-1; Novus; catalog no. NBP2-25154) were used as cytosol and membrane markers. In some experiments, purified human recombinant A2 bearing a C-terminal histidine tag (His-rA2)26 or commercial rA2 (R&D Systems; catalog no. 9409-AN-050) was incubated with PBMC lysate samples from patients with sepsis in the presence or absence of class-specific protease inhibitors.

Immunofluorescence staining

For evaluation of intracellular levels of proteins in monocytes, staining with mouse anti-A2 (Becton Dickinson; catalog no. 610069; recognizing full-length A2; dilution 1:200), rabbit anti–LAMP-1 (Novus; catalog no. NB120-19294; dilution 1:500), and goat anti–cathepsin D (Santa Cruz; catalog no. SC-6486; dilution 1:500) was performed. The secondary antibodies were donkey anti–mouse-Alexa488 (Invitrogen; catalog no. A-21202; concentration 1:500), donkey anti–rabbit-Cy3 (Jackson ImmunoResearch; catalog no. 711-165-152; concentration 1:300), and donkey anti–goat-Cy5 (Jackson ImmunoResearch; catalog no. -175-147; 1:100). Binary images were thresholded to immunofluorescence signals for quantification in Nikon Instruments Software-Elements software. The thresholded signal area was divided by the total area of the monocytes to obtain the percentage area of protein expression.

Cytokine enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays

Platelet-poor plasma was thawed on ice, and cytokine assays were performed in duplicate using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits for interleukin-1β (IL-1β) and interferon gamma (Qiagen; catalog no. SEH00171A and SEH00380A, respectively) and for IL-18 (Medical and Biological Laboratories International; catalog no. 7620) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Clinical data analysis

A2 levels were analyzed with respect to clinical variables, including the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) scores, using 1-way analysis of variance.

Ethics

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

Results and discussion

Expression of intact A2 and cell surface plasmin generation are reduced in sepsis

Analysis of PBMCs from patients with sepsis (n = 33) revealed significantly reduced levels of full-length A2 compared with A2 in samples from healthy controls (n = 25; Figure 1B-C). When PBMC immunoblots were probed with a monoclonal antibody directed against an epitope near the C-terminus of A2, we noted a consistent pattern of A2 degradation products in 35 of the 35 patients with sepsis (Figure 1D). The evidence of A2 degradation was almost entirely absent in a limited number of patients who were critically ill and did not have sepsis with hemorrhagic shock, pancreatitis, or acute respiratory distress syndrome (the latter not shown), suggesting it is relatively specific for sepsis (Figure 1E). A2 was undetectable in 1 patient (number 115; Figure 1E) and, in some samples, we noted a decrease in apparent molecular mass of the uppermost band (number 151, 761, and 659; Figure 1D) or appearance of a doublet at this location (number 401 and 518; Figure 1E), likely indicating partial proteolysis of the protein. A4 and A5 in septic lysates remained fully intact (Figure 1F). When we examined A2 fibrinolytic function in sepsis and septic shock using a previously described assay that measures A2-related, tPA-dependent, cell surface plasmin generation, we noted a significant reduction in both cohorts (Figure 1G), suggesting that loss of intact A2 was functionally significant. These data indicated that full-length A2 is reduced in some patients with sepsis and may account for loss of cell surface fibrinolytic activity.

To further delineate the mechanism of degradation of A2, we incubated exogenous His-tagged A2 with PBMC lysates, pretreated with or without a panel of class-specific protease inhibitors (Figure 1H). His-A2 was almost completely proteolyzed in samples treated with leupeptin, E64, EDTA, or pepsin (middle panel), whereas incubation with the serine protease inhibitor, phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride, prevented this proteolysis. This result suggested that sepsis-related proteolysis of exogenous A2 may require a serine protease.

To determine the subcellular location of the proteolytic event, we conducted subcellular fractionation studies (Figure 1I-J). Interestingly, proteolytic fragments of native A2 were recovered only in the membrane fraction of PBMC lysates and not the cytosol (Figure 1I). Furthermore, when exogenous recombinant A2 was added to purified membrane or cytosol fractions in the presence or absence of phenylmethylsulfonylfluoride, we noted serine protease-related proteolysis of A2 upon its exposure to the membrane fraction but not the cytosol fraction of the septic PBMCs (Figure 1J). Together, these data suggest that proteolysis of A2 in sepsis is a relatively specific event carried out by a membrane-associated serine protease.

A2 loss is associated with IL-18 elevation

To evaluate the degree of A2 proteolysis with respect to the level of inflammation, we assayed plasma IL-18 levels (Figure 2A). IL-18 was significantly elevated in patients with sepsis and especially sepsis-related shock. Moreover, levels of full-length A2 correlated inversely with IL-18 levels (Figure 2B). To determine whether reduced A2 levels found in sepsis reflected reduced intracellular A2 levels, we conducted quantitative immunostaining of monocytes from patients with sepsis (Figure 2C-D). We noted significantly reduced intracellular A2 in the septic samples. At the same time, quantitative immunofluorescence showed no difference in levels or distribution of LAMP-1 or cathepsin D (Figure 2E-F). Together, these data suggest that A2 proteolysis occurs intracellularly and is not associated with the degradation of other intracellular proteins. Furthermore, they suggest that the association between elevated IL-18 and reduced A2 would extend also to a reduction in plasmin generation, as we have shown previously24 that reduced A2 is associated with reduced A2-specific plasmin generation in other settings.

A2 reduction is associated with higher SOFA scores

Brief patient demographics and clinical outcomes are summarized in Figure 3A. Our patient population demonstrated higher SOFA scores and increased mortality in the septic shock cohort compared with sepsis without shock. Clinical outcomes in patients with sepsis in relation to organ-specific SOFA scores were compared with A2 levels in PBMC lysates. Healthy patients were assigned SOFA scores of 0. Interestingly, reduced A2 levels were observed in patients with organ-specific SOFA scores >1, especially in those with renal, pulmonary, neurologic, or cardiovascular dysfunction (Figure 3B-H). Groups that were underpowered did not show statistical significance but trended toward lower levels of A2. No significant association was found between A2 levels and either liver function (Figure 3F) or platelet counts (Figure 3G). These data support a link between A2 loss and microvascular injury affecting selected organ systems.

Here, we demonstrate for the first time that A2 undergoes targeted, sepsis-specific, intracellular proteolysis in human PBMCs, and its loss is associated with impaired cell surface–mediated fibrinolysis, highly active inflammation, and sepsis-related organ dysfunction. In previous publications, we and others have demonstrated that absence or blockade of A2 at the surface of endothelial cells and monocytes impairs cell-mediated fibrinolysis,15,18,19,27, 28, 29 highlighting the role of A2 heterotetramer as a fibrinolytic receptor on the cell surface. Even partial cleavage of the N-terminus is likely to render A2 inactive as a fibrinolytic receptor, given the critical binding sites for p11 and tPA that it harbors. Similarly, cleavage of the binding site for plasminogen at the C-terminus would also impair cell surface plasmin generation (Figure 1A). Indeed, investigation of tPA-dependent, cell-surface–mediated plasmin generation revealed severely decreased activity on the surface of septic PBMCs. Given the previous data that immunologic blockade of A2 with polyclonal IgG markedly reduces plasmin generation in multiple cell types, degradation of A2 likely causes loss of plasmin-generating activity in patients with sepsis.

Our studies suggest further that proteolytic cleavage of A2 occurs either near the N-terminus or within the core domain, as recognition by a core-directed antibody is lost, and because fragments are detected only by a C-terminal antibody that recognizes a limited epitope. The apparent lack of fragmentation of A2 in the cytosol fraction suggested that the proteolytic fragments remain bound to the phospholipid membrane through its calcium-regulated attachment to negatively charged phospholipids.30 More importantly, degradation of A2 was much more pronounced in sepsis compared with other nonseptic critical illnesses and not observed in other annexin family proteins, suggesting its specificity to sepsis and A2.

Reduced A2 expression correlated inversely with plasma IL-18, a key cytokine strongly associated with sepsis severity and a possible predictor of clinical course. In addition, patients with sepsis with SOFA scores >1 in renal, pulmonary, neurologic, and cardiovascular categories also had significant reductions in A2 expression. This pattern of organ damage is commonly seen in diseases resulting in microvasculopathy, such as small vessel vasculitis.31 Given the established role of the A2 system in maintaining vascular integrity and promoting plasmin generation,30,32 its loss may promote fibrin deposition and microthrombosis, leading to worse clinical outcomes.33 The lack of a statistically significant reduction in A2 in liver dysfunction suggests that liver sinusoids may be less susceptible to A2 reduction. The lack of association with platelet counts suggests that A2 proteolysis may not impact the primary hemostatic system, although any effect on platelet function remains to be determined. Interestingly, A2-null mice also exhibit fibrin accumulation in multiple organs, including the lung, liver, and kidney, further supporting this mechanism.19,32

In conclusion, A2 degradation is a hallmark of human sepsis, and A2 proteolysis should be explored further to establish its relationship to clinical severities and specificity for sepsis. Although several serine proteases (eg, plasmin34 and HtrA235) could be responsible for A2 proteolysis, the identity of the A2-cleaving enzyme remains to be determined. Other serine proteases activated in immunothrombosis, such as neutrophil elastase, could also be candidates, but neutrophil elastase cleaves C-terminal amino acids, largely ruling this out. Further measures to identify and inhibit the relevant protease could eventually offer a therapeutic strategy for improving patient outcomes in sepsis.
