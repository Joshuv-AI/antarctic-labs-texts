GSAman: A gene-structure annotation manual curator for functional genomics research
Authors: Chengjie Chen, Ya Wu, Kangning Feng, Hongsen Liu, Yi Liao, Zaohai Zeng, Fengqi Wu, Mengjin Tan, Xiaolong Xu, Songlin Huang, Zihao Wang, Shuyuan Tang, Weijian Sun, Jisen Zhang, Ling-Ling Chen, Rui Xia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractA high-quality reference genome requires not only accurate DNA sequences but also well-defined gene-structure annotations. However, many existing tools depend predominantly on automated pipelines that perform poorly when confronted with complex gene architectures, such as overlapping loci, alternative splicing patterns, and lowly expressed isoforms, resulting in incomplete or inaccurate annotations. To overcome these limitations, we developed GSAman, a standalone, ready-to-use tool that enables intuitive, what-you-see-is-what-you-get (WYSIWYG) editing of gene-structure annotations. In contrast to web-based platforms such as Apollo2, which generally require server deployment and do not provide full offline functionality, GSAman delivers a fully local, responsive interface for real-time annotation refinement, thereby improving accessibility across research settings. GSAman supports both fine-scale curations of individual genes and large-scale annotation of entire genomes. By enabling precise curation of gene models across varied genomic contexts, it directly facilitates downstream applications, including pan-genome construction, gene family evolutionary analyses, and precision crop enhancement. Using a telomere-to-telomere rice genome (MH63) annotation project as a case study, we demonstrate the practical utility of GSAman in producing a complete and accurate reference annotation, improving Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) completeness to 99.63% following manual curation. We believe GSAman will serve as a critical resource for advancing functional genomics across diverse species.Keywords: GSAman, Gene-structure annotation, Reference genome, Manual curation

Introduction

Advances in high-accuracy long-read sequencing technologies and the development of powerful assembly algorithms have enabled the routine generation of chromosome-level and even telomere-to-telomere (T2T) genome assemblies for many species.1,2,3 Consequently, genomics has shifted from a focus on structural assemblies to functional genomics. Recently, the field has entered a phase in which comprehensive functional analyses can be conducted across a wide range of species rather than being limited to traditional model organisms.4,5 In this context, the emphasis has moved beyond genome continuity to the accurate identification of functional elements, particularly protein-coding genes. Notwithstanding the substantial improvements in genome assembly quality, gene-structure annotations across most species remain incomplete or inaccurate and require significant refinement.6,7,8

Substantial efforts have been devoted to the development of algorithms for gene-structure annotation over recent decades, leading to the creation of numerous tools and pipelines such as GeneMark, AUGUSTUS, SNAP, MAKER, EVidenceModeler, Braker, Galba, and Helixer.9,10,11,12,13,14,15 These tools have significantly accelerated annotation workflows. However, when applied to functional genomics research, the resulting gene models often lack the accuracy and completeness required by experimental biologists. Even in well-studied model plants such as Arabidopsis thaliana and rice, annotation inaccuracies remain widespread.16 These structural errors can propagate through downstream analyses, introducing bias into gene expression profiling, ortholog identification, and variant interpretation, ultimately affecting research on trait association, evolutionary biology, and crop improvement and potentially distorting biological interpretation.

Multiple factors underlie these limitations. First, the intrinsic intricacy of genome organization, which includes abundant repetitive elements, transposable sequences, and pseudogenes, significantly complicates accurate gene annotation. This challenge is further compounded by the regulatory complexity of transcription, such as alternative splicing, variable transcription initiation and termination sites, and dynamic spatiotemporal expression patterns.7,17 Second, empirical evidence used for annotation is often incomplete. Lowly expressed transcripts may be missed, sequencing depth can vary across the genomic regions, and capture efficiency is frequently suboptimal. Third, as noted by George Box’s famous observation that “All models are wrong, but some are useful,” current gene prediction models are inherently limited and cannot achieve complete accuracy.18,19,20 One of the most effective ways to overcome these challenges is to integrate expert biological knowledge through manual review and curation of gene-structure annotations. Based on this principle, several curation tools have been developed, including Artemis, GenomeView, Apollo, Web-Apollo, and Apollo2.21,22,23,24,25 However, except for Apollo2, most of these tools are no longer actively maintained. Apollo2, which remains in use, is built upon the JBrowse genome browser and is designed for web-based deployment through a centralized server.26 Although this architecture facilitates collaboration, rapid local editing is constrained. Server configuration remains a substantial barrier, and docker-based installations can still be challenging for users with limited computational expertise.27 Furthermore, remote usage can be unreliable in the absence of a stable high-speed Internet connection, increasing the risk of crashes and repeated operations. Web-based platforms also face inherent limitations in interacting with local file systems and operating system APIs, restricting the implementation of advanced shortcuts and efficient editing workflows. These constraints highlight the need for a more accessible and responsive tool to support high-quality gene-structure annotation. Therefore, finding a better way to support researchers in obtaining high-quality gene-structure annotations is one of the pressing issues that needs to be addressed.

To meet this need, we developed GSAman, a standalone desktop application based on the widely used Integrative Genomics Viewer (IGV) platform.28 GSAman inherits IGV’s support for a broad range of genomic data types, including genome feature annotations, protein alignments, and RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) datasets from both short- and long-read platforms. In contrast to Apollo2, which is optimized for collaborative, server-based environments, GSAman is designed for local deployment, enabling fast and intuitive gene-structure annotation without requiring Internet access. To date, GSAman has been applied successfully in functional genomic studies across a variety of species, including cucumber (Cucumis sativus), sheep (Ovis aries), pineapple (Ananas comosus), pepper (Capsicum annuum), eggplant (Solanum melongena), and peach (Prunus persica) (Tables S1 and S2). In this study, we provide a comprehensive description of GSAman’s design and core functionalities, outline a streamlined strategy for rapid deployment, and demonstrate its utility through representative application examples.

Materials and methods

Quality assessment of genome assemblies and annotations

Genome assembly completeness was evaluated using Benchmarking Universal Single-Copy Orthologs (BUSCO) (v6.0.0) with the Viridiplantae single-copy ortholog dataset (viridiplantae_odb12).29 For protein-level evaluation, representative protein sequences per gene were retrieved using TBtools-II,30 and annotation completeness was calculated by executing BUSCO on these protein sequences with the same Viridiplantae dataset. To examine the validity of annotated protein-coding loci and to identify potentially spurious protein predictions, PSAURON (v1.0.8) was applied.31 This tool generates a machine-learning-derived score based on models trained across diverse plant and animal genomes to estimate whether a sequence represents an authentic coding region. Phylogenetic placement and visualization of annotated outputs were performed using tvBOT.32

Implementation logic of GSAman

The original IGV provides rapid local visualization of diverse genome-associated datasets and is widely used in genomics research. However, it does not support “on-the-fly” computation of analytical metrics (e.g., derivation of phasing scores from alignments of plant small RNAs) or interactive real-time modification of displayed annotations. IGV-sRNA was previously developed as a customized IGV variant for plant small RNA omics to overcome the first limitation, demonstrating the feasibility of extending the IGV source code.33 Building on this groundwork, this study introduces GSAman, which is also implemented within the IGV framework. The key advancement in GSAman is the extension of the AbstractTrack class (Figure 1A) and implementation of the Track interface, resulting in a new track category termed the “GSAman Track,” which enables direct interaction with gene-structure annotations. This track accepts gene annotation inputs in the GFF3 or GTF format (Figure 1B). During initialization, GSAman calls the “GXF Fix” function from TBtools (packaged as a dependency) to validate, calibrate, and complete the annotation file where needed. Subsequently, the processed data are stored in a local SQLite database.30 When a user inspects a specific genomic interval, GSAman triggers the “GXF View” function to render the annotation graphically, which is implemented through the JIGplot graphics engine in TBtools. Gene structures can be edited directly within the GSAman Track via mouse or keyboard input. Each action is automatically translated into an executable SQL statement and a reversible command, allowing instant execution while preserving the ability to undo prior steps (Figure 1D). This SQL-driven design inherently maintains a transparent annotation history, ensuring reproducibility and auditability in accordance with the Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, and Reusable (FAIR) principles.34 GSAman expands IGV’s native sequence analysis and assembly features by integrating multiple external tools and pipelines (Figure 1C), including AUGUSTUS, Softberry, FLASH, BLAT, faToTwoBit, and MIRA (https://github.com/DrMicrobit/mira),14,35,36 thereby providing enhanced support for gene prediction, sequence alignment, and local assembly.

Results

Gene-structure annotation quality across plant lineages

Using plants as a representative system, species spanning diverse evolutionary lineages were selected to evaluate genome assemblies and corresponding gene annotations (Figures 2A and S1). The BUSCO metrics showed that assembly completeness exceeded 90% for most taxa. The BUSCO completeness of gene annotations was near 90% in many cases. Nevertheless, numerous species, including Ceratophyllum demersum and Nelumbo nucifera (Figure 2), exhibited low annotation reliability (with PSAURON scores below 90%) despite high assembly and annotation completeness (Figure S1).29,31 Figure 2B presents four prevalent error categories in plant genome annotations: gene model fragmentation, adjacent gene fusion, exon omission or splice-site misassignment, and tandem-duplicate collapse.

The first major feature is the interactive editing of annotated gene structures, which facilitates precise adjustment of the input models. Users can modify exon boundaries by dragging them with the mouse while holding the Ctrl or Alt key, and all changes can be reversed using the undo function. Transcripts and exons can be added, deleted, merged, or split directly as discrete units. For coding sequences (CDSs), GSAman provides options to remove open reading frames (ORFs) or perform ORF prediction. Users can also manually define the start and end positions of CDS for individual transcripts. In addition, GSAman supports gene prediction within any selected genome interval. It integrates local execution of AUGUSTUS and can efficiently parse gene predictions from the Softberry web server. For rapid annotation, GSAman allows users to generate new gene models from multiple types of supporting evidence, including (1) conversion of sequence alignment results (either protein or nucleic acid) into annotations, (2) use of third-generation full-length RNA-seq alignment data or existing annotated transcripts as new gene models, and (3) local assembly of second-generation RNA-seq reads to generate novel transcripts and associated annotations.

The second core functionality of GSAman is its flexible interaction with both data and visualization, enabling efficient exploration of the supporting evidence for each annotated gene structure. Users can tag individual annotations, zoom in and out on specific genomic regions using the mouse wheel, or navigate directly to adjacent gene models for batch operations. The software automatically detects whether splice sites conform to the canonical GT-AG rule and assesses their support from RNA-seq evidence. Users can also customize annotation labels with informative tags, which facilitates biological interpretation, such as identifying tandemly repeated genes or candidate gene clusters involved in metabolic pathways. GSAman also offers robust data export capabilities. Users can (1) export software run logs to aid in error tracing or collaborative troubleshooting; (2) save updated GFF3 files for downstream analyses; and (3) export the current genome view, including sequences, gene models, and sequencing read alignments. All exported files are compact, making them suitable for collaborative sharing and archiving. Furthermore, GSAman provides an extensive set of keyboard shortcuts that support nearly all functions, allowing experienced users to perform most operations effectively without relying on the graphical interface.

To contextualize GSAman’s capabilities, we compared its key features with those of Apollo2 (Table 1). Both tools offer extensive gene-structure annotation editing functionality. However, while Apollo2 is designed for collaborative web-based usage, GSAman, as a standalone local application, offers several advantages. It requires no complex deployment, launches quickly, operates with minimal latency, and uniquely supports local ab initio gene prediction and short-read assembly.

A user-friendly strategy for manual curation of gene-structure annotations

GSAman is designed to facilitate rapid manual curation of gene-structure annotations, with particular emphasis on accessibility for researchers from diverse academic and technical backgrounds. Effective annotation curation typically requires three main types of reference evidence: ab initio gene predictions, transcriptome sequencing read alignments, and homologous protein alignments.37,38,39 However, generating transcriptome reads and homologous protein alignments continue to pose a practical challenge for many users, although these types of evidence are optional but frequently informative. To facilitate this process, two companion utilities were developed within the TBtools-II platform (Figure 4). For transcriptome alignment evidence, we developed a user-friendly TBtools-II plugin named “Target Genome Region Mapping.” This tool allows users to generate accurate transcriptome read mapping results for selected genomic regions using standard laptop computing environments (Figure 4A). The plugin operates by identifying all genome intervals with sequence similarity to the target region, merging them into a synthetic reference, and conducting RNA-seq read alignment against this composite sequence. Following mapping, the coordinates are translated back to the original genomic positions. Because homologous and repetitive sequences are incorporated into the mapping reference, the risk of mismatches arising from sequence redundancy is significantly reduced. Moreover, by reducing the effective reference sequence space, this mapping approach significantly reduces computational resource requirements and the overall alignment runtime. For homologous protein alignment evidence, GSAman leverages the recently introduced Miniprot software, which provides fast and accurate protein-to-genome alignment at large scales (Figure 4B).33 We have integrated Miniprot into a dedicated TBtools-II plugin, further streamlining the acquisition of homology-based annotation evidence.

Although GSAman is a standalone desktop application and does not offer real-time, multi-user network collaboration, it supports flexible strategies for distributed manual curation based on our project experience. Two common collaboration models are supported: (1) assigning multiple users to annotate the same genomic region, and (2) dividing the entire genome into segments to enable parallel annotation by different users. For the first model, users can utilize the “Export Curr. Region Data” function in GSAman to extract and share the current working interval with collaborators. In the second model, a project manager typically partitions the genome into intervals based on the number of participants, and each member is assigned a subset of the annotation task. After all segments have been curated, the results are merged into a final, unified annotation file. To facilitate this workflow, we developed two additional TBtools-II modules (Figure 4C): “GXF Split,” which divides annotation files into multiple parts based on user-defined parameters, and “GXF Merge,” which consolidates the curated files into a complete structural annotation dataset. Notably, the GXF Split function prevents direct overlap of assigned loci or intervals. However, user actions cannot always be fully anticipated. When multiple users edit the same locus, GXF Merge produces an “∗.conflict” file, allowing users to manually resolve discrepancies and determine which annotation should be retained.

Manual refinement of gene-structure annotations using GSAman is straightforward for most users (Figure 5). As outlined above, the preparation of input data can be streamlined using the two TBtools plugins provided in this study (Figures 5A and 5B). During routine operation, users simply import the required files, as in a conventional genome browser (Figures 5C and 5D). The GSAman mode is automatically activated upon loading a gene annotation file, prompting the user to confirm the selection. GSAman supports flexible manual editing of gene models through standard mouse and keyboard operations once enabled (Figures 5E and 5F).

Case study: Refinement of gene-structure annotations in the rice genome

To demonstrate the practical utility of GSAman, we organized a team to perform comprehensive manual gene-structure curation for the Oryza sativa xian/indica variety Minghui 63 (MH63), a staple parent in hybrid rice breeding with a gap-free, T2T genome assembly.40 Despite its high-quality genome sequence, the existing gene annotation for MH63 is compromised by several limitations, including (1) unannotated gene loci, (2) single genes erroneously split into multiple loci, (3) incomplete gene structures, (4) over-annotation of gene models, (5) inaccurate untranslated region (UTR) annotations, and (6) a lack of alternative splicing (AS) information (Figures 6A–6E). We manually refined the MH63 gene annotations using the GSAman platform, achieving a final annotation quality that surpasses the annotation of the widely used Nipponbare geng/japonica reference genome (Table 2; Figures 6 and S2).41 As illustrated in Figures 6A–6D, we curated four major types of annotation errors:

(1)Locus merging occurs when multiple distinct gene loci are erroneously combined into a single annotation. This can result in the merging of coding regions from separate genes, misassignment of start/stop codons within CDSs, or misinterpretation of short CDSs as UTRs, resulting in their omission. For instance, two transcripts initially annotated as OsMH_06G0467700 represented a case of coding-region fusion (Figure 6A). The merged locus was annotated as a MULE transposase. Manual curation separated it into OsMH_06G0467650 and a redefined OsMH_06G0467700. The latter retained its MULE family transposase annotation, whereas OsMH_06G0467650 was reannotated as a zinc finger CCCH domain-containing protein, homologous to OsLIC in Nipponbare, a transcriptional activator regulating rice architecture through brassinosteroid signaling.42

We curated 11,496 gene loci within the MH63 genome. The chromosome distribution of these curations is illustrated in Figure 6E. More than 80% of the corrected gene loci were supported by RNA-seq or Iso-seq (Isoform Sequencing) evidence (Figure 6F). To quantitatively assess the resulting annotation quality, we performed BUSCO analysis using two datasets. The proportion of complete BUSCOs increased from 86.9% to 99.6% for the embryophyta_odb10 dataset (containing 1,614 single-copy gene orthologs), which is 0.8% higher than the score for the Nipponbare reference genome. Similarly, the number of complete BUSCOs increased by 423 (from 4,329 to 4,752) for the poales_odb10 dataset (containing 4,896 single-copy genes), raising the completeness score from 88.5% to 97.0%. This result surpasses Nipponbare’s score of 90.5% by more than 6%, demonstrating a significant improvement in the quality of gene-structure annotation in rice genomes (Figure 6G). Collectively, these results represent a substantial improvement in the structural annotation of the MH63 genome, establishing a reliable foundation for downstream functional genomics research in rice.

Discussion

GSAman represents a practical solution for rapid deployment and gene-structure annotation

In the functional genomics era, genes serve as the fundamental units of biological research. As such, precise gene-structure annotation is as crucial as the accuracy of the genome sequence itself. Despite progress in automated and semi-automated annotation tools, most current methods still fall short of delivering the level of precision required for high-confidence functional genomics applications.46,47 Manual refinement, guided by expert biological knowledge, remains the most effective strategy for improving gene annotations. This approach is also instrumental in generating high-quality reference datasets for training gene prediction models, a strategy that has already proved successful in numerous studies (Tables S1 and S2). A key requirement in this process is the ability to perform intuitive, what-you-see-is-what-you-get (WYSIWYG) editing of gene models, which demands a stable, user-friendly, and efficient software platform.48 While several tools have been developed, only Apollo2 has continued to receive active maintenance and broad adoption. However, based on feedback from the research community, many biologists prefer tools that can be deployed and used rapidly without dependence on server infrastructure or web-based platforms. This preference motivated the development of GSAman. As a desktop application, GSAman offers a ready-to-use solution that eliminates complex deployment steps and provides a comprehensive suite of features for manual curation. Operating completely on the local machine, GSAman offers highly responsive interactions and seamless integration with other local software, markedly enhancing the efficiency of on-demand gene-structure refinement. When used in conjunction with the newly developed TBtools-II plugins, it facilitates the preparation of annotation evidence files and indirectly enables team-based curation workflows, supporting both individual and collaborative gene-structure curation tasks (Figure 4).

GSAman supports both efficient small-scale gene-structure annotation curation and the construction of high-quality reference gene-structure annotations

Accurate gene-structure annotation is a prerequisite for any gene function study, as it directly determines the fidelity of the corresponding coding sequence.20 The same principle applies to gene family analyses, where errors in structural annotations can lead to incorrect inferences about gene evolution, expression, or function. Despite their prevalence, many of these annotation errors persist in the literature, often due to a lack of accessible and effective curation tools, particularly for non-model organisms. GSAman addresses this critical gap by offering an efficient solution for correcting gene structures at the individual gene or gene-family level. Within just 2 years of its release, GSAman has been successfully applied to curate gene-structure annotations in a variety of species (Table S2), demonstrating its broad utility and ease of use. Its design allows researchers with limited computational backgrounds to make high-quality curations, thereby accelerating downstream functional analyses and improving the reliability of published annotations.

Following the initial development and small-scale internal validation of GSAman, we observed its rapid adoption for large-scale genome annotation curation tasks. Importantly, our team successfully applied GSAman to perform genome-wide structural annotation curations for both the gap-free lychee genome (unpublished) and the T2T pineapple genome within 1 month.49 To further demonstrate its capabilities, we conducted a comprehensive manual curation of gene structures in the T2T-level O. sativa (MH63) genome assembly.40 The resulting annotations, presented in Figure 6, are expected to provide an accurate and robust reference for future rice breeding and functional studies. Moreover, the successful applications of GSAman across multiple species (Table S1) further illustrate its versatility and reliability for genome-scale gene-structure annotation curation.

Limitations and future directions of GSAman

As a locally deployed desktop application, GSAman currently lacks the capacity for real-time synchronization of gene-structure curations at shared loci during network-based collaboration. Although simultaneous editing at the same locus is rarely required in practice, enhanced support for collaborative workflows remains an area for future development. To address this, we plan to implement a versioning system based on intermediate remote servers, using a model similar to Git repositories. This would facilitate efficient coordination and integration of large-scale annotation efforts across distributed teams. Additionally, with the increasing availability of haplotype-resolved genome assemblies, particularly for non-model and clonally propagated crops such as pineapple, banana, and cassava, gene function research is shifting toward comparative annotation across haplotypes. To meet this emerging need, a key future goal is to extend GSAman’s functionality to support simultaneous annotation curation across two or more haplotype genomes. Furthermore, with the ongoing advancement of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, particularly large language models (LLMs), there is considerable potential for evaluating gene-structure annotation quality and facilitating real-time, gene-focused exploration of genome-scale datasets.50 Incorporating LLMs into GSAman represents a promising strategy to expand its functionalities and progressively transform it into a more comprehensive genome browser platform.

Conclusion

We have developed GSAman, a standalone desktop application designed to support efficient and accurate gene-structure annotation curation. Its intuitive interface, extensive functionality, and integration with complementary tools enable both small-scale and genome-wide curation tasks. Demonstrated through its successful application to the T2T rice genome, GSAman provides a practical and powerful resource for advancing genome annotation quality across species. We believe it will serve as a valuable foundation for enabling robust functional genomics research in the years to come.

For Windows and macOS, the installer includes all necessary dependencies, such as TBtools-II libraries and third-party binaries, such as AUGUSTUS and BLAT. Linux users should configure third-party binaries in the system PATH following the user manual. The TBtools-II-based data preparation steps outlined in this manuscript are optional; GSAman can operate independently without the use of TBtools-II. The software efficiently manages large genomes and high-depth sequencing files on a standard consumer laptop (four CPU cores, 8 GB of RAM, and 120 GB of disk space). The new gene-structure annotation of the rice MH63 genome is also available at https://github.com/CJ-Chen/GSAman.
