Role of three-phase bone scintigraphy and fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography in monitoring hyperbaric oxygen therapy combined with conservative surgery for refractory osteomyelitis
Authors: Yu Nakai, Ken-Ichiro Sakata, Masayuki Shinohara, Yusuke Nakamura, Keisuke Nakamura, Tougo Tanabe, Taku Kimura, Takuya Asaka, Jun Sato, Yoshimasa Kitagawa
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground/purposeThe anti-inflammatory effect of hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) on osteomyelitis of the jaw has not been sufficiently quantified. In this study, we aimed to quantitatively evaluate the effects of HBOT and postoperative disease status in patients with jaw osteomyelitis treated with minimally invasive surgery combined with HBOT using nuclear medicine imaging.Materials and methodsThe study included 11 patients who underwent sequestrectomy or saucerization and preoperative HBOT in our department between 2008 and 2015. The underlying etiologies were dental infection-related osteomyelitis (four cases; hereinafter ODONT group), osteoradionecrosis (four cases; hereinafter ORN group), or medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (three cases; hereinafter medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) group). Quantitative assessments were performed using SUVmax from fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) and region-of-interest measurements from bone scintigraphy obtained before and after HBOT. Mean values and standard deviations were calculated, and pre-to post-treatment differences were defined as changes. Clinical cure was defined as the absence of symptom recurrence at 3 months postoperatively.ResultsThe overall cure rate at 3 months postoperatively was 90.9% (10/11 cases). The greatest magnitude of change was observed in the ODONT group for FDG-PET and in the ORN group for bone scintigraphy. Furthermore, post-treatment variability tended to be greater than pretreatment variability in the ORN group for FDG-PET and in the MRONJ group for bone scintigraphy.ConclusionNuclear medicine imaging may be helpful for quantitatively assessing the therapeutic effect of HBOT in jaw osteomyelitis and may contribute to improved treatment response evaluation.Keywords: Bone scintigraphy, Fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography, Hyperbaric oxygen therapy, Medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw, Osteoradionecrosis of the jaw

Introduction

Osteomyelitis of the jaws is a complex inflammatory condition within the oral and maxillofacial region.1 Accurately assessing the extent of necrosis, inflammation, and infection can be challenging, and refractory cases often require extensive surgical intervention, which may negatively impact quality of life.

The management of odontogenic osteomyelitis (ODONT) typically focuses on eliminating the source of infection and often involves early antibiotic therapy and surgical interventions, including tooth extraction. Conversely, treatment of medication-related osteonecrosis of the jaw (MRONJ) and osteoradionecrosis of the jaw (ORN) generally follows a phased approach, in which conservative and surgical modalities are applied stepwise according to the patient’s clinical presentation. This strategy reflects the underlying pathophysiology, such as impaired or absent bone turnover and compromised wound healing caused by bone-modifying agents or radiation-induced damage.2,3 However, the differential diagnosis of these conditions is often challenging. Chronic inflammation may obscure typical clinical symptoms, and histopathological findings often overlap, commonly exhibiting necrotic bone and nonspecific features of chronic inflammation.4 Furthermore, advanced osteomyelitis is often nonresponsive to conventional therapies, resulting in prolonged morbidity and an extended clinical course.4,5

Hyperbaric oxygen therapy (HBOT) has been widely applied in the management of various conditions.5–9 Recently, our department has combined HBOT with conservative surgical approaches for advanced cases of MRONJ and ORN.5,10 However, to the best of our knowledge, published studies evaluating the effectiveness of HBOT for these conditions remain limited, and this treatment has been established primarily as an adjuvant therapy.5

Nuclear medicine imaging modalities, including positron emission tomography/computed tomography (PET/CT) and bone scintigraphy, are widely used in the diagnosis of inflammatory diseases.11,12 In these conditions, fluorodeoxyglucose-(FDG) PET leverages the characteristic high glucose metabolism of inflammatory cells, resulting in FDG accumulation in infected or inflamed tissues, thereby enabling the visualization of the affected lesions. Conversely, 99mTcO4 (pertechnetate), used in bone scintigraphy, shows increased uptake in osteomyelitic bone due to elevated blood flow and enhanced capillary permeability mediated by inflammatory cells. This results in higher radiopharmaceutical accumulation in the affected region,13 enabling clear visualization of the diseased sites.14

In the present study, we aimed to evaluate the effects of HBOT on three types of osteomyelitis—ODONT, ORN, and MRONJ—by analyzing the maximum standardized uptake values (SUVmax) obtained from FDG-PET and the accumulation areas identified on three-phase bone scintigraphy.

Materials and methods

This study included 11 patients treated at the Department of Oral Diagnostics and Medicine, Hokkaido University Hospital, between 2008 and 2015 who were diagnosed with either ODONT, ORN, or MRONJ. All patients underwent conservative surgery (sequestrectomy or saucerization) combined with preoperative and postoperative HBOT.

HBOT was administered in a hyperbaric oxygen chamber (KHO–301B, Kawasaki Engineering, Kobe, Japan) according to the following protocol: compression at a constant rate of 0.146 MPa gauge pressure over 15 min, maintenance of this pressure for 60 min, and decompression over 20 min. Each patient received a total of 30 HBOT sessions, comprising 20 preoperative and 10 postoperative sessions, all performed during hospitalization. The treatment sequence combined 20 preoperative HBOT sessions, followed by conservative surgery, and concluded with 10 postoperative HBOT sessions (Fig. 1) (see Fig. 2).

A bilateral symmetrical region of interest (ROI) was positioned over the contralateral unaffected region as a control, and an additional ROI was placed on the frontal bone to represent background activity using ImageJ. The uptake ratio was calculated using the mean counts from the lesion (l), contralateral normal region (n), and background activity (b), based on a modified Jacobsson’s method: Uptake ratio=count(l)-count(b)count(n)-count(b).

Our treatment decisions were guided by clinical and radiographic findings. In asymptomatic cases, a conservative watch-and-wait strategy was adopted until spontaneous sequestrum expulsion occurred. In patients with a clearly demarcated sequestrum on radiographs for whom spontaneous expulsion was unlikely, minimally invasive removal of the necrotic bone and infectious tissue was performed. In cases of incomplete sequestrum formation accompanied by pain or acute symptoms, surgical removal of the necrotic bone was undertaken.

Each patient underwent FDG-PET (Gemini TF, Philips, Amsterdam, Netherlands) and three-phase bone scintigraphy both before and after preoperative HBOT. SUVmax was measured to quantitatively assess FDG uptake in the affected regions. For each three-phase bone scan (perfusion, pool, static), a rectangular region of interest (ROI) was defined over the lesion. A symmetrical ROI was positioned over the contralateral unaffected region to serve as a control, and an additional ROI was placed over the frontal bone to represent background activity. Image analysis was performed using ImageJ software. In all patients, conservative surgery (sequestrectomy or saucerization) was performed after completion of the preoperative HBOT. Postoperative outcomes were classified as healing or nonhealing according to the following predefined clinical criteria: Based on the definition of ORNJ proposed by Chronopoulos et al.,15 a case was considered cured if all ORNJ-related symptoms—including pain, gingival redness and swelling, purulent discharge, and intraoral bone exposure—resolved within 3 months after surgery, with no evidence of symptom recurrence during follow-up. Treatment failure (noncure) was defined as either improvement/stabilization or worsening of symptoms. Improvement or stabilization of symptoms was defined as partial clinical recovery in which symptoms improved after treatment but did not completely resolve, such as persistent bone exposure. Worsening of symptoms was defined as the absence of symptomatic improvement after treatment, accompanied by overall clinical deterioration. Furthermore, to assess the effects of different etiologies, patients with ODONT and MRONJ were also evaluated based on their clinical status at 3 months post-treatment.

Informed consent statement

This article does not contain any information that could identify any individual participants. Hence, consent for publication was not applicable.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using “Easy R” (EZR) software (version 1.70, Saitama Medical Center, Jichi Medical University, Saitama, Japan). The Kruskal–Wallis test was conducted to compare the magnitude of change (difference between pretreatment and post-treatment values) in the quantitative parameters among the ODONT, ORN, and MRONJ groups. A P-value of <0.05 was considered significant.

Regarding SUVmax, the highest mean values were observed in the ODONT group, with 5.04 before HBOT and 3.10 after HBOT, whereas the lowest values were observed in the ORN group, with 2.47 and 2.00, respectively (Tables 1 and 3). The MRONJ group had intermediate values of 4.54 pre-HBOT and 3.15 post-HBOT, respectively (Table 3). The SD differences were greater in the ODONT (1.94) and MRONJ (1.39) groups than in the ORN group (0.49) (Fig. 3).

In the bone scintigraphy ROI analysis, the mean values (pre-HBOT, post-HBOT) were 1.95 and 1.2 for the ORN group, 1.90 and 1.80 for the MRONJ group, and 1.83 and 1.63 for the ODONT group (Tables 1–3). The SD differences were largest in the ORN group (0.55) and smallest in the ODONT group (0.25), with the MRONJ group showing an SD difference of 0.29 (Fig. 4).

Additionally, comparison of the magnitude of change among the three groups revealed no significant differences in either the FDG-PET (P = 0.492) or bone scintigraphy findings (P = 0.279).

Discussion

The major types of osteomyelitis affecting the jawbones include ODONT, MRONJ, and ORN.4 Given that these conditions arise from different etiologies, their treatment approaches also differ.6

HBOT offers several potential clinical benefits, including: (1) antimicrobial effects through oxygenation of affected tissues, which promotes the production of reactive oxygen species; (2) acceleration of wound healing by promoting angiogenesis; and (3) enhancement of local blood circulation and reduction of hypoxia by decreasing gas volume caused by edema under increased pressure.11,16

However, a 2022 position paper issued by the American Association of Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeons concluded that the current evidence supporting HBOT remains limited.2 In our clinical experience, while improvements in soft tissue conditions were observed, complete healing of osteomyelitis-affected regions was not consistently achieved, highlighting the need for further examination.

In this study, healing was achieved in 10 of 11 patients (90.9%), with the single nonhealing case occurring in a patient with MRONJ. These results suggest a potential synergistic effect of conservative surgery combined with HBOT, particularly in mild forms of osteomyelitis across all types. Analysis of FDG-PET data revealed that the change (delta) in SUVmax pretreatment and post-treatment, as well as the difference in SD, was smallest in the ORN group. Conversely, the ODONT group showed the largest change in SUVmax, while the MRONJ group exhibited the largest difference in SD.

Pretreatment FDG SUVmax was the lowest in the ORN group. In bone scintigraphy analyses, ORN exhibited the highest ROI values and the largest pretreatment-to-post-treatment differences, whereas the MRONJ group showed a large SD difference in this modality. These observations likely reflect the distinct pathophysiological mechanisms underlying each disease. ORN is widely recognized as a condition characterized by hypoxia, hypovascularity, and hypocellularity induced by radiation therapy, resulting in vascular stenosis, reduced perfusion, and periosteal injury.17 Conversely, MRONJ arises from exposure to agents, such as bisphosphonates or anti-RANKL antibodies, with pathophysiology involving inhibition of osteoclast activity.18 and hypovascularization.19–21 Thus, these differences in underlying mechanisms may account for the observed variability in imaging and treatment outcomes of our study.

Previous studies have reported histopathological and imaging differences between ORN and MRONJ. In ORN, bone marrow is significantly compromised by radiation; histologically, ORN lesions have been reported to exhibit flattened, immature osteoclasts or, in some cases, a complete absence of identifiable osteoclasts. Similar differences have been reported in imaging findings.4,22

Periosteal reactions are commonly observed on CT scans in MRONJ but are typically absent in ORN. This absence is attributed to radiation-induced damage to surrounding soft tissues and the periosteum itself.23,24

Collectively, these findings indicate that ORN is associated with more extensive and severe vascular compromise than MRONJ. This impaired vascularization likely contributed to the observed reduction in FDG uptake and may also explain the limited efficacy of HBO in these cases. These observations are consistent with previous research showing significant decreases in SUVmax post-HBOT in patients with MRONJ.10

Conversely, comparison with the ODONT and MRONJ groups revealed that the SD of changes in SUVmax post-treatment was smaller in the ODONT group. This suggests that, being unaffected by radiation or pharmacological agents, patients in the ODONT group experienced less irreversible vascular compromise than those in the ORN and MRONJ groups, allowing for a more consistent therapeutic response to HBOT.

In the bone scintigraphy analysis, the difference in SD (pretreatment vs. post-treatment) was greatest in the MRONJ group, indicating that the therapeutic effect varied with disease severity. This variability may be partially attributable to the inclusion of patients with fibrous dysplasia in this group, as such patients may exhibit inherently elevated bone metabolism. Compared with the ORN group, the ODONT and MRONJ groups exhibited higher pretreatment SUVmax values and tended to show larger reduction post-treatment. These findings indicate that when the underlying disease causes minimal vascular and soft tissue damage, and healing is preserved, significant improvement can be achieved through combined HBOT and conservative surgery.

However, the observation that the ORN group exhibited the highest ROI values on bone scintigraphy warrants careful interpretation. Since the first phase (perfusion phase) of bone scintigraphy reflects blood flow activity, elevated pre-HBOT uptake may represent an acute inflammatory state. Given that the ORN cases in this study ultimately achieved healing post-treatment, this high uptake likely reflected an inflammation-induced increase in vascular permeability in regions where vascular compromise was not severe.

Additionally, the two imaging modalities employ different tracers and, therefore, may provide complementary information about the underlying pathophysiology. FDG-PET measures glucose metabolism, and in conditions such as joint inflammatory diseases and bone metastases, its uptake primarily reflects the metabolic activity of the inflammation or tumor itself rather than serving as an independent marker of bone metabolism.13,25–28 Cases that resulted in noncure, such as the patient with MRONJ (case 10), were notable. This patient had rheumatoid arthritis as an underlying condition and was receiving immunosuppressive therapy, including bucillamine and prednisolone. Therefore, whether immunosuppressants, alongside the osteoporosis drugs, influenced the osteomyelitis of the jaw could not be ruled out. The effectiveness of HBOT in patients with such complex comorbidities requires further study.

In contrast, bone scintigraphy shows increased uptake during the active inflammation phase due to higher blood flow and vascular permeability,13 although the uptake is primarily influenced by osteoblastic activity.28

Consequently, resolution of inflammation, such as that induced by HBOT, may inhibit the excessive inflammatory response, resulting in decreased signal intensity on imaging. Therefore, reduced visualization on imaging following HBOT should not be interpreted solely as a reduction in bone wound healing. Accurate assessment requires integrating findings from multiple imaging modalities alongside clinical and histopathological evaluations.

This study has several limitations. First, it was a single-center, retrospective evaluation with a small sample size, limiting the number of cases analyzed. Second, the correlation between clinical symptoms and imaging findings was insufficient, and broader comparisons with other imaging modalities were not feasible. The small sample size precluded robust statistical analysis, resulting in limited statistical power. Third, the precise mechanisms underlying the pathogenesis of ORN and MRONJ are yet to be fully understood, making it challenging to quantify the effects of HBOT, despite its potential utility in these conditions. Nonetheless, this study shows the feasibility of using nuclear medicine imaging to quantify treatment effects, suggesting that future studies may explore improvements achieved through less invasive treatment strategies. Larger studies employing additional nuclear medicine tracers are warranted. Furthermore, conventional planar bone scintigraphy has inherent technical limitations. As a two-dimensional modality, it poses challenges for accurate coregistration and anatomical localization compared to current mainstream three-dimensional (3D) modalities.

In cases where inflammation involves surrounding soft tissues in addition to bone, differentiating FDG uptake in soft tissue from that in bone on PET/CT scans can be difficult.29 This complicates the interpretation of imaging results, as decreases in tracer uptake may reflect either resolution of inflammation or reduced bone metabolic activity. While the former indicates progress toward healing, the latter could suggest impaired sequestrum formation or compromised bone wound healing.

Future research will involve prospective studies incorporating 3D imaging modalities, such as bone SPECT, alongside PET/CT and planar bone scintigraphy. These studies will also systematically correlate functional imaging findings with the corresponding CT, MRI, and histopathological results to enhance the diagnostic accuracy and quantitative assessment of osteomyelitis.

These preliminary findings demonstrate the potential utility of FDG-PET and three-phase perfusion imaging in monitoring the therapeutic effects of HBOT on osteomyelitis. Additionally, our results suggest that high-concentration oxygen can effectively penetrate the affected lesions in all types of osteomyelitis. Specifically, the ODONT group, which is not affected by medications or radiation therapy, may experience greater benefits than the ORN and MRONJ groups. Nevertheless, because these pathologies arise from distinct etiologies and involve complex clinical backgrounds, the universal application of this approach requires further case accumulation and validation. Although combining conservative surgery with HBOT represents a potentially promising treatment strategy for refractory osteomyelitis, further detailed studies are still necessary.
