Advocating for professional guidance on medical updates in gamete donation: A perspective from US-based genetic counselors
Authors: Rawan I. Awwad, Lauren J. Isley, Katherine Hornberger, Kara Baldwin, Lauri Black, Allison Boyd, Gina Davis, Meghan Dean, Heather Kamen, Suzanne Seitz, Daniela Young
Published: 2026

Introduction

Gamete donation has enabled countless individuals to build families and is a central component of third-party reproduction. It provides an alternative pathway for reproduction for individuals with infertility, as well as for individuals who cannot conceive through traditional means, including same-sex couples and those without a partner.1 This complex and meaningful process brings together a diverse group of stakeholders—gamete banks, fertility clinics, donors, recipients, and donor-conceived persons (DCP)—each contributing unique experiences, responsibilities, expectations, and perspectives.

Per the recommendations of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM), gamete donors are required to undergo comprehensive screening before donation, including a detailed personal and family medical history, infectious disease testing, genetic screening, and psychological evaluation.2 This information is shared with prospective recipients to support informed decision-making when selecting a donor. However, medical and genetic information is not static; it can evolve over time. Given that most gamete donors are nonidentified (previously referred to as anonymous), this presents a challenge: how should updated health information be managed and communicated among donors, recipients, and providers in a way that is timely, ethical, and clinically meaningful?

Recently, a sperm donation case in Europe has drawn global attention after a previously unrecognized genetic syndrome impacted many DCP and their families.3,4 After multiple children were diagnosed with early-onset cancers, the sperm donor’s semen was found to be mosaic for a disease-causing variant in the TP53 gene. This gene is associated with a dominant hereditary cancer syndrome called Li-Fraumeni syndrome, where a pattern of early or multiple cancers is often observed across generations, including breast cancer, bone and soft tissue cancers, brain tumors, leukemia, and adrenal gland cancer.5 Several elements of the case came under scrutiny, including regulations on how often a donor’s sperm can be used, given that roughly 200 children were conceived. However, one of the most notable issues was how the medical update was communicated: not all recipients were promptly contacted by their clinics or gamete banks, and many instead learned the information months later through social media or existing connections with other recipients.6

Similarly, in the United States, there have been instances of mismanaged medical updates in gamete donation. One widely publicized case involved an egg donor who, after unsuccessful attempts to contact her egg bank, turned to social media under the handle “sunny.in.season” to inform recipients of her diagnosis with Hereditary Hemorrhagic Telangiectasia. This is a dominant condition that causes abnormal blood vessel connections and can lead to chronic, severe bleeding.7 As a result, recipients received critical health information through informal channels without the benefit of professional guidance.

Based on the authors’ professional experiences, there is substantial variability in how gamete banks and clinics manage medical reports. Additional genetic testing of gamete donors may not always be feasible or permissible, and institution-specific policies often guide the evaluation and disclosure of medical updates. These policies may not be established by genetic or clinical professionals. The lack of consistency and standardization not only contributes to disparities in reproductive decision-making for an already vulnerable fertility patient population,8 but also has implications for the long-term health management of DCP, who have consistently expressed a strong interest in understanding their biological family’s medical histories.9,10

This commentary aims to highlight the complexities surrounding medical updates in gamete donation in the United States, the importance of involving genetic specialists in the process, and the need for standardization of medical update management through professional recommendations and guidance.

Understanding Medical Updates in Gamete Donation

In the context of gamete donation, a medical update refers to any newly identified health information, genetic test results, or diagnosis in a donor, a donor’s biological relative, or a donor-conceived pregnancy/person.11,12 These updates may carry implications for health risks, particularly for first- and second-degree relatives, and can inform future reproductive and medical management decisions.13,14

Examples of medical updates include a donor-conceived pregnancy with multiple birth defects, a gamete donor diagnosed with cancer suggestive of a genetic predisposition syndrome, and a donor’s parent newly diagnosed with a dominant genetic condition, such as Huntington disease or polycystic kidney disease. When shared appropriately, such updates can help: (1) assess and communicate recurrence risks; (2) guide reproductive choices; and (3) determine whether a donor’s gametes remain suitable for future donation. Understanding how to investigate and manage these updates is essential to providing appropriate and ethical care.

Medical updates may come to a gamete bank’s attention when donors and/or recipients reach out to report them or when a donor is medically re-evaluated before repeat donation. Similarly, medical updates may come to a fertility clinic’s attention when recipients reach out for additional cycles and/or treatment. As such, gamete banks and fertility clinics are major stakeholders in collecting, managing, and disseminating medical updates in donors and donor-conceived persons (DCP).

Currently, there is no formal or generally recommended professional framework for multidirectional sharing of medical updates among fertility clinics, donors, recipients, DCP, and gamete banks. Such a framework is essential to improving transparency and optimizing care and would ensure that relevant health information is communicated responsibly and consistently across all stakeholders.

Current Gaps in Practice

Several publications from the American Society for Reproductive Medicine (ASRM) address the topic of collecting and disseminating medical information from gamete donors. The 2019 ASRM Ethics Committee Opinion, Interests, Obligations, and Rights in Gamete and Embryo Donation, states that programs should strongly encourage donors and recipients to provide medical updates.15 Additionally, the 2024 ASRM Practice Committee Opinion, Gamete and Embryo Donation Guidance, makes recommendations on the frequency of updating mental health evaluations for gamete donors.2 Specific to frozen oocyte donation, the 2021 ASRM Ethics Committee Opinion, Ethical Issues in Oocyte Banking for Nonautologous Use, states that if a child is born with an inherited disease following the use of donor oocytes, both the woman who initially donated the oocytes and all recipients of these oocytes should be informed.16 The publication also lists additional responsibilities for egg banks, including tracking and reporting of outcomes and ensuring that the medical care and consenting requirements for donors are comparable to those of recipients. However, without clear guidance on the process of investigating and disseminating medical updates, as well as the most appropriate professionals who should be involved, programs face understandable challenges in defining best practices for managing medical updates.

For example, if a donor-conceived child is diagnosed with a genetic condition by a medical specialist, who should disclose this information to the fertility clinic and/or gamete bank? What type of consent process should be in place? Who assesses whether the risk to other DCP is increased, and whether sharing the medical update with other recipients is warranted? Which type of health care professional communicates this information to other recipients, and when is additional genetic testing on a gamete donor warranted? Where should this information be documented and saved, and who should have access to it?

Typically, gamete donors sign agreements to share evolving medical information with the gamete bank. Similarly, recipients sign agreements with the gamete bank that encourage them to share medical updates on DCP. However, clinics and gamete banks cannot require recipients to disclose pregnancy outcomes or the health status of DCP, nor can they guarantee donor responsiveness to follow-up inquiries. Legal and logistical constraints further limit access to comprehensive medical records.17 While these limitations are inherent to gamete donation, recommendations from professional societies would provide a valuable framework to support consistent, ethical, and practical approaches across the field.

The following real case examples of US-based gamete donation demonstrate some of the gaps in practice and underscore the need for a structured framework for medical update management:

Case example 1

A fertility clinic notifies a sperm bank that 2 of 5 embryos created with a specific donor show the same chromosomal microdeletion. The egg source tested negative. The clinic inquires whether additional testing of the sperm donor is available, but the bank declines, referencing internal policies against additional genetic testing of donors. Months later, another fertility clinic caring for a different recipient couple who used the same donor contacts the sperm bank to ask about any medical updates following the birth of a child with dysmorphic features and developmental delay. The gamete bank staff finds no documented updates and reports that outcomes associated with the donor have been normal.

This case highlights gaps in both the documentation of medical updates reported to the gamete bank and the sharing of this information with relevant stakeholders. At a minimum, such updates should be incorporated into the donor’s medical history profile to ensure accessibility for other recipients and their managing clinics. Additionally, inconsistent policies regarding follow-up genetic testing of donors underscore the need for clearer, professionally established guidance.

Case example 2

A fertility patient returns to her clinic for further treatment. She reports that her most recent egg donor-conceived pregnancy was diagnosed through prenatal ultrasound with a complex heart defect and spontaneously aborted at 23 weeks gestation. No genetic evaluation was performed on the fetus. The patient and her clinical team review the donor’s available family medical history and conclude that, given the apparently isolated and spontaneous nature of the event, notification to the gamete bank is not indicated.

The second case demonstrates the lack of incorporation of genetic specialists, such as genetic counselors or clinical geneticists, in assessing the medical report. Congenital heart defects can be isolated or associated with genetic syndromes. Depending on the underlying cause, recurrence risks may be elevated in biological relatives.18 In this case example, the patient’s clinical team is unaware of what additional medical updates may have been reported to the gamete bank or whether this reported diagnosis, when considered alongside other information that may be available, could be relevant for other families who have used the same donor. The working assumption appears to be that the condition is sporadic; however, this conclusion is made in the absence of a complete clinical and genetic picture. Even if this individual case does not ultimately alter donor suitability or medical management recommendations for other recipients, best practice supports proactive information sharing to ensure that all available medical history is accurately documented and accessible for the gamete donor and for informed decision-making by all parties involved.

Involving Genetic Professionals in Medical Update Management

There is an inherent variability in the severity and clinical significance of medical updates in gamete donation. Determining what is considered a reportable medical update can be complicated by the lack of integration of genetic professionals into this process. Genetic professionals, such as genetic counselors and clinical geneticists, are uniquely trained to interpret genetic information and test results, assess risks, and facilitate sensitive communication among stakeholders.19,20 Their involvement would enhance the quality and clarity of medical update investigations and ensure that families receive accurate and compassionate guidance.

When a medical update is received by a gamete bank or clinic, it may initiate an internal review process to gather relevant information, such as clinical documentation, genetic test results, and family history, from the donor, other gamete source, or DCP. These investigations are often complex and lengthy, and they require thoughtful coordination. In cases where further investigation and/or genetic testing is indicated, involvement from a genetics-trained provider, such as a certified genetic counselor, is essential to ensure appropriate consent, counseling, ordering, and interpretation.21,22

Practical and Ethical Considerations

Gamete donation presents unique ethical intricacies when managing medical updates, particularly due to the layered relationships and responsibilities among donors, recipients, and DCP. The concept of “duty to warn” is typically applied in clinical genetics to ensure that at-risk individuals are informed of potential health risks.23, 24, 25 It addresses the ethical responsibility of affected individuals to inform their family members about potential recurrence risks, enabling them to make informed decisions and pursue preventive measures. This concept becomes particularly complex in the context of nonidentified gamete donors. These are individuals who donate eggs or sperm without revealing their identity to the recipients or any resulting children.26 Unlike traditional family structures, the relationships among donors, recipients, and DCP may be mediated by gamete banks and clinics, with varying degrees of donor identity disclosure and contractual boundaries. From the donor’s perspective, there may be no ongoing relationship or sense of obligation to provide updated medical information. Efforts to recontact donors can be limited by prior consent agreements, privacy considerations, and outdated contact information.

On the recipient side, clinics cannot require individuals to report health outcomes or diagnoses in their donor-conceived children, even when such updates could have implications for other families using the same donor. These limitations, rooted in privacy laws, ethical considerations, and logistical realities, highlight the need for professional guidance that balances autonomy with the potential benefits of shared medical knowledge.27,28 A framework that includes options for de-identified reporting and clearly defined roles for genetic counselors could help navigate these challenges while respecting the boundaries of all parties involved.

Call to Action

As certified genetic counselors working in third-party reproduction, we recognize the urgent need for structured guidance on managing medical updates in gamete donation. We believe these efforts are best led by professional societies within the assisted reproduction and genetics fields and by organizations with deep expertise in the clinical nuances of third-party reproduction and medical genetics. Evidence-based recommendations developed by experienced medical and scientific professionals are more likely to result in thoughtful, ethical, and practical standards that can be consistently applied across the industry. In contrast, guidance shaped without such subject-matter expertise risks oversimplifying complex medical and ethical considerations.

We, therefore, call on professional societies to develop and disseminate recommendations that ensure meaningful, considerate, and clinically sound approaches to medical information sharing in gamete donation, guaranteeing that such guidance is shaped by those with appropriate medical expertise. To establish such guidance, we recommend the following:

Systematic collection of data demonstrating the value of investigating and reporting medical updates

There have been limited studies that address medical update investigations in gamete donation, including the value of additional genetic testing on donors. Lockwood et al29 (2024) reviewed 121 cases of DCP with positive genetic findings across 3 gamete banks that were managed between January 2021 and June 2023 to evaluate when donor testing was indicated. It was found that donor testing was performed in most cases and often confirmed the same variant as identified in the DCP (∼59%), with most variants being identified postnatally and classified as pathogenic or of uncertain significance. The study highlighted the importance and complexity of donor follow-up testing, as well as the challenges in interpretation, sample availability, and management.29

Isley et al30 (2016) conducted an 8.5-year retrospective review of outcomes and donor management, finding that routine screening cannot meaningfully reduce or eliminate risks from multifactorial conditions or undiagnosed autosomal dominant diseases in donor-conceived offspring. Similarly, Isley et al31 (2023) reviewed 30 cases of autosomal recessive conditions in donor-conceived offspring across 3 US sperm banks and found that most donors had undergone only limited, ethnicity-based carrier screening. Nearly half of the genes associated with these conditions were not included in the standard American College of Medical Genetics and Genomics expanded carrier screening recommendations, highlighting gaps in current guidelines. The findings showed that while expanded carrier screening can reduce risk, it cannot eliminate it, and they emphasized the importance of informing recipients about screening limitations and encouraging appropriate genetic counseling.31

Further data are needed to better demonstrate the importance of thorough investigation and communication of medical updates in gamete donation and to help inform future professional guidelines. Such data should include the following:•Types of medical updates that arise in gamete donation•Value of additional genetic testing on donors when indicated by a medical update•Effects of medical update investigation and dissemination on reproductive risk assessment and clinical management•Influence of medical updates on the management of remaining donor gametes and subsequent donor eligibility•Perceptions of recipients and DCP on receiving, and having access to, medical updates

Establishment of internal processes in gamete banks and fertility clinics to reflect best practices for handling medical updates

Although current US professional guidelines state the responsibilities of recipients and gamete banks in reporting new diagnoses of heritable conditions,15,16 a more robust and clearer framework to guide and standardize this process can reduce health care inequities. Best practices should include the following:•Clear informed consent language that outlines the possibility of future updates and the importance of maintaining contact with the gamete bank and/or fertility clinic•Protocols for investigating updates, including when to involve genetic specialists and how to manage incomplete information•Guidelines for disseminating clinically significant findings while protecting privacy•Criteria for reassessing donor eligibility based on new medical information

Consideration of a multidimensional approach in assessing and managing medical updates

A clear delineation of responsibilities for all stakeholders in gamete donation is necessary for the best outcomes. Although gamete banks, fertility clinics, and various medical specialists caring for DCP have their own sets of policies and protocols, guidelines must be established regarding duties to be informed when medical updates arise.

In addition, navigating medical updates in gamete donation is inherently complex. Perceptions of risk magnitude can be subjective, and stakeholders may respond differently to new information; what one person considers appropriate to share may not be viewed the same way by another. This variability highlights the need for clear, standardized policies that define when and how medical updates should be communicated.

Summary

Medical and genetic updates are an expected part of gamete donation and can empower individuals to make informed reproductive and health decisions, help gamete banks uphold standards for ongoing donation, and enable clinicians to provide appropriate counseling and care. We believe that formal guidance from professional societies on handling medical updates in gamete donation will help unify practices, reduce uncertainty, and ensure that all parties, including donors, recipients, gamete banks, clinics, and DCP, are supported in a transparent and ethical manner. This will optimize outcomes by enabling informed reproductive decisions and long-term health management.
