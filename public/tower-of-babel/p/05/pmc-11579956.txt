Robust Sequence Design Space for the Isothermal Exponential Amplification of Short Oligonucleotides
Authors: Yan Shan Ang, Lin‐Yue Lanry Yung
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractAdvances in isothermal amplification techniques have accelerated development in biosensing applications and the design of complex molecular devices. The exponential amplification reaction technique, or EXPAR, is uniquely positioned to process molecular information from short oligonucleotide strands (≈10 nucleotides length) typically encountered in molecular computing or microRNA detection. Despite its conceptual simplicity (requiring only a template strand and two enzymes), the issue of nonspecific background amplification has hindered broader adoption. In this work, a new system configuration is established at 37 °C to achieve significantly improved performance. Critical sequence motifs responsible for the excellent signal‐to‐background profile are identified and generalized as a universal adapter design framework. Orthogonal template sequences generated from the framework are implemented for a triplex reaction and successfully evaluated mixtures of multiple‐target inputs in a single‐step, one‐pot format without the need for exogenous agents.Keywords: enzymes, isothermal amplification, molecular evolution, nucleic acids, oligonucleotides

Introduction

Isothermal amplification techniques, such as loop mediated isothermal amplification (LAMP), recombinase polymerase amplification (RPA) and rolling circle amplification (RCA), have matured over the years as strong contenders to PCR for nucleic acid amplification.[ 1 , 2 , 3 , 4 , 5 ] These techniques typically require long templates (at least 100 nt) and are less suitable for shorter targets such as microRNA or synthetic barcodes used in molecular circuits.[ 6 , 7 ] The exponential amplification reaction (EXPAR) is uniquely positioned to work with short oligonucleotides (ca. 10 nt) to achieve high amplification factors of 106–109 achievable within minutes.[ 8 , 9 , 10 ] The basic concept is based on a self‐copying template strand flanking a nicking enzyme recognition site, and a pair of polymerase and nicking enzymes to double the amplicon per cycle (Figure 1A).

Schematics of the exponential amplification reaction (EXPAR). A) The reaction steps are: 1 – duplex formation between the single‐stranded EXPAR template and incoming input trigger (blue), whose 3′‐end primes 2 – the polymerase extension reaction to form a complete duplex strand from which 3 – the nicking enzyme cuts at the recognition site (grey) to 4 – generate amplicon, which can participate in the next round of amplification. B) The key features of the revamped EXPAR are highlighted, namely the use of Nt.AlwI nicking enzyme and Klenow fragment polymerase at 37 °C, and a universal adapter framework evolved from the general EXPAR template structure.

While there have been successful proof‐of‐concept demonstrations of its use for evaluating biomolecule inputs,[ 10 ] including the recent SARS‐Cov‐2 outbreak,[ 11 , 12 ] a critical analysis of the data generated often reveals the occurrence of nonspecific background generation at a dangerously fast rate close to that generated in presence of positive targets. Its susceptibility to nonspecific amplification has hindered the wider adoption of this technique, which ironically due to the conceptual simplicity involving only one template with two enzymes, leaves little degree of freedom for design optimization.[ 13 ]

Most efforts to suppress background amplification are centered on the belief that the main source of nonspecific amplification arises from the self‐priming of multiple template strands or via secondary structure.[ 14 , 15 ] Common strategies include the use exogenous additives, such as tetramethylammonium chloride or single‐stranded binding proteins, to modulate DNA hybridization and/or the interaction between polymerase and template strand.[ 16 ] Alternatively, modified template strands[ 17 , 18 , 19 ] and constrained 3‐letters nucleotide pool have been proposed.[ 20 ]

Other more elaborate design strategies involve the integration of EXPAR with peripheral DNA‐mediated modules, be it the use of exonuclease for tailored deactivation of undesired background reactions[ 21 ] or the addition of pseudotemplate strands as a sink to delay the onset of nonspecific amplification.[ 22 , 23 ] The combined design efforts have evolved the EXPAR concept into a polymerase‐exonuclease‐nicking enzyme (PEN) toolbox[ 24 , 25 ] which was recently demonstrated for complex molecular neural network computation.[ 26 , 27 ]

Notwithstanding that the strategies involving exogenous agents or oligo modifications have improved the differentiation between signal and background amplification, the key for achieving robust EXPAR performance while retaining the simplicity of the original concept fundamentally depends on the quality of the template sequence design. This was attempted by Qian et al. wherein machine learning algorithm was used to classify good and poor EXPAR template sequences, and identify specific sequence motifs favoring performance outcomes.[ 28 ] However, the progress of EXPAR development has yet to pick up significantly since then. Thus, we questioned if the conventional EXPAR configuration consisting of Vent (exo‐) and Nt.BstNBI, while feasible and widely reported, was not an optimal configuration.

In this work, we established, from ground up, a new EXPAR system configuration involving Klenow fragment and Nt.AlwI enzyme pair operating at 37 °C that exhibited significantly improved signal‐to‐background profile (Figure 1B). By testing a total of 83 EXPAR template sequences, we confirmed that having a different enzyme pair influenced the sequence design space for achieving robust EXPAR performance beyond just base pairing properties. Key sequence motifs critical for our proposed EXPAR configuration were identified, leading to the generalization of a universal EXPAR sequence adapter design framework. Orthogonal templates from our revamped EXPAR sequence pool could co‐exist in a three‐strand system for evaluating mixtures of multiple‐targets input in a one‐pot format. We believe that the sequence design concepts derived from this work will enable broader adoption of EXPAR as an isothermal amplification technique, particularly for processing molecular information from short oligonucleotides.

Results and Discussion

Overview of Sequence Screening Approach

The template sequence for the isothermal amplification reaction (EXPAR) was evolved over three design phases (Figure 2 ). We first established the strong dependence of the sequence design on the enzyme pairs selected by comparing the EXPAR performance using sequences and motifs previously reported,[ 28 ] and that newly generated using the NUPACK design tool.[ 29 ] Important sequence motifs for our specific enzyme pair were identified by decomposing a model template strand into two main regions, i.e., enzyme recognition site (consisting of 5 nucleotides (nt) design space at Position 10–14 for the Nt.AlwI nicking enzyme used in this study) and amplicon region, and testing 16 variants mutated systematically at specific sites. The critical sequence motifs were located at Position 9–14 which controlled the interaction between the polymerase and template strand. Finally, the core features identified were back tested on randomly selected template sequences, verified, and generalized in one instance as our proposed universal adapter framework. Template sequences generated using the universal adapter framework were mostly good performers, except for one extreme case of full CG content in the amplicon region.

Overview of the sequence design process. #1 – The basic template sequence comprised of the recognition site of the Nt.AlwI nicking enzyme (5′‐N/NNNN GATCC‐3′) in between two 10 nt amplicon repeating units (blue, numbered 1–10). #2 – Specific sequence motifs with high likelihood of generating template sequences with good performance were identified. The critical sequence motifs were located at Position 9–14 which encompassed both the repeating unit domain and nicking enzyme recognition site. #3 – the observations were generalized as one instance of universal adapter framework for generating EXPAR sequences with robust and good‐performance.

Performance of EXPAR Sequence Depends Strongly on Enzyme Pair

The premise of our study was that the performance of EXPAR sequences depends strongly on the specific pair of polymerase and nicking enzyme, and their interaction with the template strands as suggested in a handful of studies.[ 28 , 30 ] This premise deviated from most existing design efforts which focus on modulating the template base pairing properties, e.g. CG content and secondary structure, since the general belief is that the EXPAR background amplification arises primarily from the nonspecific binding of template strands.[ 10 , 15 , 16 ] In contrast, while enzymes have been acknowledged to exert an effect, they often play a secondary role during the design process and are studied only as part of optimization, e.g. by varying concentration or their relative ratio.

Considering that EXPAR generates short amplicons (typically 10 nt), we first explored the possibility for EXPAR to operate at a lower temperature than the conventional ca. 55 °C. A lower temperature could facilitate the ease of integration with other molecular circuit modules controlled by DNA duplex and hairpin gates as the risk of circuit leakage increases at higher temperature.[ 31 , 32 ] We chose 37 °C for its versatility to extend for other biomolecular systems such as to evaluate protein inputs.[ 33 ] Deviating from the conventional EXPAR configuration based on Vent(exo‐) polymerase and Nt.BstNBI nicking enzyme operating at 55 °C, an enzyme pair comprising of Klenow Fragment polymerase and Nt.AlwI nicking enzyme were chosen for their compatibility in terms of buffer composition and operating temperature of 37 °C.

We evaluated the performance of EXPAR sequences by adopting similar analysis framework reported by Qian et al. with slight modification. Two performance criteria, namely, difference in time between the 10% of the negative control or background signal (NC10) and 90% of the test signal (t90) as well as the point of inflection (POI) of NC (NCPOI), were used to classify the performance of the EXPAR sequences (Figure 3A). We prioritized the late onset of the negative control (POINC) over the amplification efficiency of positive reaction (previously defined as t90) used by Qian et al. as the issue of nonspecific background amplification have emerged as the dominant concern over the last decade of works. Sequences tested were either adopted from those previously identified by Qian et al. through a rigorous sequence screening process,[ 28 ] or freshly generated using the NUPACK design tool. 100 nm template was used in all reactions and 100 fM input was used to generate the test signal.

The EXPAR template sequences tested were grouped according to their design principles – Group L (sequences from literature), Group A (sequence motifs based on literature recommendation) and Group B (new sequences designed from NUPACK). A) Each sequence was classified into different performance category based on the criteria stated. Summary of the number of sequences classified in each performance category, alongside with representative signal evolution plots of the dominant category for B) Group L, C) Group A, and D) Group B.

The direct adoption of eight reported EXPAR sequences, except to replace the recognition site of Nt.BstNBI with that of Nt.AlwI, (Group L) resulted in non‐differentiable (N.D.) outcome between NC and test for all sequences (Figure 3B). Another 14 template sequences were designed on the recommended general sequence motifs.[ 28 ] The dominant category (7 out of 14) still remained as N.D. while only 3 out of 14 templates (i.e., strand A3, A7, and A13, Section S1, Supporting Information) were good performers (Figure 3C).

A fresh set of 17 EXPAR sequences was generated using the NUPACK design tool (Group B) which generates sequences with minimal secondary structure defects.[ 29 ] Key design specifications including temperature (37 °C), salt concentration (Na+ = 50 mm, Mg2+ = 10 mm), unpaired template structure, and minimum CG content of 0.6 were used. The dominant category (11 out of 21 candidates) was now flipped to good‐performing sequences (Figure 3D). Notably, the differential (NC10 – t90) of 10 of our good performers outperformed the highest differential previously reported (i.e., 12.9 min for sequence #356) despite using a 10‐fold lower amplicon input concentration, i.e., 100 fM.[ 28 ] Gel electrophoresis was used to identify the molecular species generated after 30 min reaction for one representative sequence of each design groups wherein only B17 (representing Group B) had yet to produce template‐amplicon duplex for the negative control (Figure S1, Supporting Information).

On the surface, this suggests the possibility to have some chance of a good EXPAR sequence design, albeit almost like flipping a coin, using purely a DNA design toolbox. Fundamentally, the improved outcome can be explained by the lack of sequence biasness previously imposed based on the design guidelines recommended for the convention EXPAR configuration.[ 28 ] However, they were less suitable for our current EXPAR configuration which necessitates the search for a different sequence design space.

Identifying Critical Sequence Motif Designs

Intrigued by the drastically different and apparent improved EXPAR performance with the alternative reaction configuration of 37 °C (instead of 55 °C) and different enzyme pair, we proceeded to compare features of the three groups of sequences designed (Groups L, A & B). The outcome of EXPAR performance depended most critically on specific sequence motifs shown in the following subsections. Other design features returned no clear impact on the EXPAR performance, such as the thermodynamics property of the full sequence (CG content and melting temperature) and by analyzing at the domain level (amplicon or recognition site) (Figures S2 and S3, Section S2, Supporting Information).

Overview of Approach to Identify Critical Sequence Motifs

We used a single good‐performing B17 strand as a reference design from which 11 direct variants were derived by modifying specific nucleotide sites followed by back testing prominent observations on other relevant template sequences. The effect of specific nucleotide(s) influencing the polymerase‐template interaction during the polymerization step was considered using the finger‐palm‐thumb model as a guide which is generally accepted for describing the interaction between the Klenow Fragment and its template strand (Figure S4, Supporting Information).[ 34 , 35 , 36 ] Since the finger region covers up to 11 nt downstream of the amplicon‐template duplex while the first 5 nt immediately downstream of the amplicon binding site is fixed by the nicking enzyme recognition site, only Position 9–14 can be freely designed (Figure 4A).

Importance of Nucleotide Identity at Position 9 and 10

We first focused on Position 9 and 10, which was part of the self‐repeating amplicon region, by changing specific nucleotides one at a time (Figure 4B). The CG content (both in terms of % composition and relative position) was kept unchanged by substituting T for A at Position 9 (Figure 4B(i)) and C for G at Position 10 (Figure 4B(ii)). The presence of adenine had the apparent effect of slowing down EXPAR while the presence of guanine accelerated the reaction. A single swap of TC to CT at Position 9 and 10 for B17‐M5 resulted in a sharp drop to N.D. performance (Figure 4B(iii)), which further illustrated the high degree of sensitivity at these two positions. This was however not observed when AC was swapped to CA (Figure 4B(iv)).

We back tested this observation using sequence B7 which similarly contained AC at Position 9 and 10 with AATA as part of the recognition site just like the sequence B17 used above. This B7 similarly behaved sluggishly as well with no signal generated even in presence of 100 fM amplicon input (Section S1, Supporting Information, Group B). By substituting A for T at Position 9 (B7‐M1), the signal was now observable within the experimental time scale of 2 h. Two other sequences with sluggish signal generation, B15 and B16, similarly contained AC at Position 9 and 10. We acknowledge that there were other sequences with adenine in either Position 9 and 10 with good (e.g., B12, B13, and B14) and non‐differentiable (e.g., A2 and A14) performance though it should be noted that these strands had other sequence combinations at Position 11–14 (part of recognition site).

On another note, guanine was not present at Position 9 or 10 in any of the NUPACK generated sequences and was only present in three out of eight Group L sequences (L4, L5, and L6) with non‐differentiable signal‐to‐background, suggesting that it is likely an unfavorable nucleotide which we exclude for future design iterations.

Importance of Position 9 and 10 Located at the Amplicon Generation Site

Since the amplicon region is self‐repeating, the nucleotides at Position 9 and 10 was found on both the incoming amplicon binding site and outgoing amplicon generation site (Figure S3B(i), Supporting Information). To determine if either position were more important, we further perturbed the sequence design by removing TC at the 3′‐end of either or both repeat units (Figure S3B(ii)–(iv), Supporting Information). Only the B17‐M10 strand, which retained TC in the outgoing site, exhibited comparable performance to the original B17 strand. B17‐M9 and B17‐M11 strands, which had TC removed from the outgoing site and effectively changed the nucleotides immediately downstream of the recognition site from TC to CC, exhibited much worse performance. This confirmed the greater importance of sequence design in the finger region (where the outgoing site is located) compared to the thumb region (where the incoming site was located). The thumb region is responsible for the binding of the polymerase to the duplex structure formed by primer with a minimum length of 7 nt to initiate strand extension.[ 36 ] Hence the removal of 2 nt in the thumb region, which only reduced the effective primer length from 10 to 8 nt, did not exert a significant impact.

Inclusion of Additional Thymine Immediately Downstream of Amplicon‐Template Duplex

We tested a side observation previously reported that having dATP as the first incorporated nucleotide promoted overall elongation efficiency.[ 6 ] In our case, the addition of one or two T as the immediate template nucleotide downstream of the amplicon binding site improved the signal differentiation slightly (Figure 4C(i,ii)). While this strategy did not always a yield favorable outcome across all the sequences randomly picked to have an additional T in the template (e.g., B7‐M2), it can potentially improve the signal‐to‐background differentiation significantly (e.g., B10) and is worth testing during the sequence refinement phase.

Nucleotide Positions 1–8 Less Critical

Having established that the EXPAR performance was highly sensitive to the identity of the Positions 9 and 10 nucleotides given specific recognition site sequence (Positions 11–14), we then perturbed the sequence of the remaining amplicon region (Position 1–8). Since all the templates generated by the NUPACK design tool tended to possess at least two contiguous stretches of cytosine, we tested if their absolute positions were critical by sequentially swapping the specific nucleotides in a pairwise manner (Figure 4D(i‐iii)). The qualitative classification of these template variants (B17‐M7‐V1 to B17‐M7‐V3) remained as good performance though there were subtle quantifiable changes to the signal‐to‐background differentiation (NC10 – t90). Lastly, we tested the possibility of extending the amplicon length beyond 10 nt while retaining TC at the critical position. Good performance was still achievable for template strands up to 13 nt amplicon length which offered greater design flexibility depending on the intended use case (Figure S3C, Supporting Information).

Generalization toward a Universal Adapter Design Framework

The desirable sequence design elements observed above were consolidated as key design features defining the framework of our universal adapter (Figure 5A). The most critical sequence motifs include defining the nicking enzyme recognition site as 5′‐C/AATA GATCC‐3′ and setting TC at Position 9 and 10. The presence of cytosine‐rich motif in the remaining amplicon region (Position 1–8) was retained and fixed at specific locations to simplify the design process. As such, only Position 1, 3 and 6 were available for random sequence generation. Furthermore, as discussed above, an optional thymine was added immediately downstream of the amplicon binding site.

B) Boxplot of the four major sequence design groups, namely, L for literature reported sequences, A for sequences based on motifs reported, B for sequences designed using the NUPACK design tool and U for sequences derived from the universal adapter framework. Sequences evolved manually (i.e., denoted with “‐Mx” or “‐Mx‐Vy” such as B7‐M2 or B17‐M7‐V1) were excluded from the plot. 26 out of 27 universal adapter sequences were classified minimally as good performers, of which representative signal evolution plots of C) excellent, D) good, and E) the only failed sequence are shown. The mean and standard deviation of the POI for each experimental setup is included within the plot (n = 3).

Twenty‐seven template sequences were generated using the universal adapter framework of which 26 were classified as good performers. Besides the categorial improvement, the signal‐to‐background difference was noticeably enhanced compared to the previous three groups (Figure 5B). In fact, 24 of the sequences exhibited excellent performance wherein the differential was at least 20 min, which was twice of our previous criterion of 10 min for good performance and exceeds that of the previously reported good performers for the conventional EXPAR configuration.[ 28 ] A representative plot of the excellent performance by U8 and good performance by U9 is shown in Figure 5C,D respectively. The only sequence failing to generate any signal was U10 which had guanine at all three remaining positions, representing the most extreme case of the universal adapter design (Figure 5E). Individual plots for other template sequences can be found in Figure S5 (Supporting Information).

As a proof‐of‐concept, the U8/A8 configuration was used to calculate the limit of detection by titrating six concentrations (0.1 fM to 10 pm) of A8 over three independent repeats. Using a cut‐off value as 3 standard deviations below the mean of the POI of the negative control, a limit of detection of 0.3 fM (ca. 18 copies/µL) was obtained (Figure S6, Supporting Information).

It should be noted that the EXPAR sequence design space is broader than defined within the scope of our proposed universal adapter framework. For example, the contiguous stretch of cytosine need not be fixed at the locations specified and the amplicon length need not be limited as 10 nt, which upon relaxation can open an exponentially bigger sequence design pool. As a feasibility study, we applied the EXPAR sequence design framework for detecting let‐7a microRNA with a 12 nt recognition length and showed that the inclusion of an additional TC sequence motif resulted in improved signal‐to‐background differentiation (Figure S7, Supporting Information).

Feasibility of Multi‐Strand EXPAR

As an ultimate test of the robustness of the EXPAR sequences defined thus far, eight excellent performers were further challenged under multi‐strand reaction conditions. As they were all derived from the same universal adapter framework, there was a high likelihood of sequence crosstalk arising from base pairing hybridization. Though the predicted crosstalk was minimum based on NUPACK analysis, it only sufficed as a first‐cut screen to eliminate incompatible combinations (Figure 6A). By reacting the template and amplicon strands pairwise experimentally in a combinatorial manner, a much more heterogeneous result was observed where most template strands were mis triggered by 10 pm of off‐targets (Figure 6B). This was likely due to the additional stabilizing effect of the enzymes on the DNA template strands which had inferentially played a dominant role in the outcome of all reactions performed thus far but were unaccounted for by NUPACK. Nonetheless, three out of eight template strands (i.e., U3, U4, and U7) were found to be sufficiently compatible with one another in a singleplex format for onward implementation in a multiplexed format (Figure 6C,D).

We then tested different combinations of amplicon inputs (A3 and/or A4 and/or A7, 10 pm each) in a one‐pot mixture where all three universal adapter template strands (U3, U4, and U7, 10 nm each) and their corresponding reporter strands (R3, R4 and R7, 20 nm each) co‐existed in a single reaction tube (Figure 7A). The evaluation outcome was quantified by taking the difference in the point of inflection (POI) of the negative control (i.e., absence of any amplicon input) and the specific input combination indicated at the bottom of Figure 7B. Each input combination generated three evaluation outcomes corresponding to three‐color channels – Cy5 from U3/R3, FAM from U4/R4 and HEX from U7/R7. An example of the reaction pathway leading to signal generation by the reporter strand is shown in Figure S8 (Supporting Information). Representative kinetics plots obtained from one repeat run are shown in Figure 7C. A one‐sample, one‐sided t‐test was performed on each outcome to determine if the difference in POI was statistically significant. The outcome with p < 0.05 was taken as positive which indicated the presence of the corresponding amplicon input. Using this analysis framework, the correct amplicon inputs were successfully identified for all seven combinations (Figure 7B).

This is an encouraging step forward for developing multiplexed EXPAR given the inherent difficulty to even achieve a sufficiently robust singleplex EXPAR. To the best of our knowledge, only two other works have demonstrated two‐[ 22 ] and four‐template[ 37 ] multiplexing in one‐pot format. It should be noted, though, that those works involve additional elements such as the inclusion of exonuclease and additional pseudo‐templates and cross‐inhibitory strands or Argonaute nuclease for specific reporter cleaving to achieve the binary and quadruplex system respectively.

Conclusion

In this work, we revamped the system configuration of EXPAR by using an alternative Klenow fragment and Nt.AlwI enzyme pair operating at 37 °C. Critical sequence motifs responsible for the significantly improved signal‐to‐background profile were identified over three design phases. The design guidelines culminated in the generalization in one instance as our proposed universal adapter design framework. Orthogonal templates generated from this universal adapter design could co‐exist in a three‐strand system for evaluating mixtures of multiple‐targets input in a one‐pot format without adding any exogenous agents. We are hopeful that our proposed EXPAR system configuration and the sequence design concepts elucidated from this work will enable the wider adoption of EXPAR as an isothermal amplification technique, particularly for processing short oligonucleotides involved in molecular computing or microRNA detection.

Experimental Section

Materials

The DNA oligonucleotides (oligo) used in this study were designed using the NUPACK web server[ 29 ] whenever applicable, and purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT) as 100 µm stock in 1 × Tris–EDTA buffer (1 × TE, pH 8.0) and stored at 4 °C. Reporter oligos, modified with fluorophore and quencher, were similarly purchased from IDT and stored in the dark at 4 °C. The DNA sequences for EXPAR template are listed in the Supporting.csv file.

All enzyme‐related reagents used in this study were purchased from New England Biolab: 10 × NEBuffer 2 buffer (#B7002S), 10 × rCutSmart buffer (#B6004S), 10 mm deoxynucleotide (dNTP) (#N0447), 10 U/µL Nt.AlwI nicking enzyme (#R0627S) and 5 U/µL Klenow fragment (3′→5′ exo‐) (#M0212). All buffers and dNTP were aliquoted as single‐use vials, while the enzymes were stored as‐delivered. All items were stored at −20 °C until use.

The following chemicals were used as received: Tris‐borate‐EDTA (10 × TBE) buffer pH 8.3 was purchased from 1st Base. SYBR gold nucleic acid gel stain (#S11494) and 10% normal goat serum (#50062Z) were purchased from ThermoFisher Scientific. 20 × EvaGreen Plus Dye (#31077) was purchased from Biotium.

Performing EXPAR Reaction

The assay reagents were prepared as a 2 × Mastermix consisting of 0.5 × NEBuffer 2 buffer, 0.5 × rCutSmart buffer, 50 µm dNTP, 0.2 U/µL Nt.AlwI nicking enzyme and 0.05 U/µL Klenow fragment (3′→5′ exo‐), aliquoted as single‐use vial (good for 10 reactions) and stored at −20 °C.

For each reaction, 1.0 µL of input strand (prepared in 10% normal goat serum) was added to 4.0 µL of 1 × rCutSmart buffer and incubated in a PCR tube. Next, one aliquot vial of 2 × Mastermix was thawed at room temperature for 5 min. During the sequence screening phase (singleplex format), 200 nm DNA template strand and 0.5 × EvaGreen Plus were then added to the 2 × Mastermix. For multi‐strand reactions, 20 nm of DNA template and 40 nm of reporter strand of each sequence design were all added in the same Mastermix (one‐pot format). Finally, 5.0 µL of the prepared 2 × Mastermix was added to each reaction and mixed thoroughly. All preparations were done at room temperature.

During the sequence screening phase, the reaction tubes were transferred to an isothermal real‐time fluorescence reader (Allsheng Gene‐8C) for incubation at 37 °C with fluorescence measurement (excitation = 470 nm, emission = 525 nm) every 15 s over 1–2.5 h. During the sequence testing phase for demonstrating cross‐strand / multiplexing capability, the reaction tubes were transferred to Bio‐Rad CFX‐96 for incubation at 37 °C with fluorescence measurement using the FAM, HEX, and Cy5 channels every 30 s over 1 h.

Data Analysis

During the sequence screening phase, the fluorescence kinetics curve was used to obtain three key parameters – point of inflection (POI), time of 10%, and 90% signal generation. The difference between the time of 10% signal generation of the negative control (NC10) where no input was added and time of 90% signal generation of the test set‐up (t90), typically at 100 fM of amplicon input, NC10 – t90, was used as the quantitative criteria to categorize the sequence performance.

During the sequence testing phase involving multi‐strand EXPAR, each test combination was repeated over three independent experiments and quantified by its POI. The difference in the POI of the negative control and the reaction consisting of specific input combination was used to represent the test outcome. A one‐sample, one‐sided t‐test was performed on each test outcome to determine if the difference in POI was statistically significant. The outcome with p < 0.05 was taken as positive which indicates the presence of the amplicon input corresponding to the template system represented by the specific fluorescence channel.

Different sequences exhibited different equilibrium fluorescence intensity value upon intercalating with EvaGreen Plus dye, or opening of reporter strand in different fluorescence channels, and across different instruments (Allsheng Gene‐8C and Bio‐Rad CFX‐96). To better represent the fluorescence kinetics curve for all sequences, the raw data was normalized by taking the difference between each data point and the minimum value divided by the difference between the maximum and minimum value for each kinetics curve obtained. Data normalization was used only for the graphical representation of the kinetics curve and did not affect the POI calculation which was independent of the absolute value of the raw data.
