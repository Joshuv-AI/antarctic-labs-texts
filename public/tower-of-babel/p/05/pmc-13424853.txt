Critical appraisal of “Preventing depression in older adults with chronic illnesses through the SMART Elderly Program: a quasi-experimental study in Central Thailand”
Authors: Muhammad Wasi Hassan, Tehseen Sajjad, Noor Un Nisa
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

We read with great interest the article by Toopboochakorn et al. [1] on the SMART (Smile, Mindfulness, Attitude, Relax, and Thinking) Elderly Program. The authors should be commended for developing a culturally tailored, theory-based intervention and demonstrating improvements in depression literacy, attitudes, and Thai Geriatric Depression Scale scores among older adults with chronic illnesses. Nevertheless, several methodological issues warrant further consideration.

The study analyzed outcomes at the individual level using analysis of variance, although allocation occurred at the subdistrict level, indicating a clustered design. Because only one subdistrict was assigned to each group, clustering effects could not be estimated reliably. Consequently, the findings should be interpreted cautiously. More explicit acknowledgment of the cluster-level allocation would strengthen the study’s methodological validity [2].

As commonly observed in quasi-experimental research, the intervention group received weekly face-to-face SMART sessions for six weeks, whereas the control group received routine care and a handbook. This imbalance in participant contact may have contributed to the reported improvements through attention and Hawthorne effects rather than the intervention itself [3]. Without an attention-matched control group, distinguishing the program’s true effectiveness from increased healthcare provider engagement remains difficult.

Additional details regarding intervention delivery and fidelity assessment would improve the report. Process evaluation, including implementation fidelity, facilitator training, participant adherence, and contextual adaptations, is essential for determining whether observed outcomes resulted from the intervention itself or variability in implementation. Inclusion of these data, or a parallel process evaluation, would substantially enhance the study’s interpretability and reproducibility [4].

Although the authors presented valuable findings on knowledge and attitudes, the analysis did not appear to fully address potential confounders such as socioeconomic position, prior health education, and healthcare access. These factors are known to influence “knowledge, attitudes, and practices” outcomes and may bias observed associations if not adequately considered. Adjusting for these variables, or at minimum discussing their potential impact, would strengthen the study’s internal validity and support more robust causal interpretation [5].

In conclusion, although the study provides valuable insights, its findings should be interpreted with caution. Cluster-level allocation, inadequate control of potential confounders, absence of fidelity assessment or process evaluation, and possible bias arising from unequal attention between groups may have influenced the reported outcomes. Future studies should address these methodological limitations to improve the validity and applicability of the findings.
