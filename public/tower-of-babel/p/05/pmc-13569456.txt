Fludarabine combined with treosulfan or total body irradiation as a conditioning therapy before allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation in acute lymphoblastic leukemia. A retrospective matched-pair analysis from the ALWP of the EBMT
Authors: Ryszard Swoboda, Myriam Labopin, Nicolaus Kröger, Matthias Stelljes, Thomas Schroeder, Peter Dreger, Gesine Bug, Andrew McDonald, Tülay Özçelik, Montserrat Rovira, Igor Wolfgang Blau, Desiree Kunadt, Régis Peffault de Latour, Werner Rabitsch, Matthias Eder, Marie Therese Rubio, Alexandros Spyridonidis, Zinaida Peric, Sebastian Giebel, Mohamad Mohty, Fabio Ciceri
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
There are very limited data on treosulfan (Treo)-based conditioning before allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (alloHSCT) in acute lymphoblastic leukemia. We aimed to compare fludarabine with Treo (FluTreo) or total body irradiation (FluTBI) in this setting. The final matched-pair analysis included ALL patients transplanted in complete remission after FluTreo (n = 153) or FluTBI (n = 431). The median patient age was 56.5 and 54.1 years, respectively. The allograft was derived from an unrelated (54.2% in FluTreo and 52% FluTBI group), matched sibling (30.7% and 32.5%) or haploidentical donor (15% and 15.5%). The incidence of acute graft-versus-host disease (GVHD) grade II-IV was 26.6% in the FluTreo and 30% in the FluTBI group (p = 0.43). The 2-year incidence of chronic GVHD was 33.5% and 40.7%, respectively (p = 0.23). The 2-year incidence of relapse and non-relapse mortality was 28.1% and 25.6% in the FluTreo and 25.1% and 20.4%, respectively, in the FluTBI group (p = 0.15 and p = 0.38). Leukemia-free survival and overall survival was 46.2% and 55% in the FluTreo and 54.5% and 61.3%, respectively, in the FluTBI group (p = 0.1 and p = 0.22). GVHD-free and relapse-free survival was 33.7% and 40.8%, respectively, in FluTreo and FluTBI patients (p = 0.06). In conclusion, there were no significant differences in transplant outcomes between the studied groups.

Introduction

Despite the progress in the management of patients with acute lymphoblastic leukemia (ALL), allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation (alloHSCT) still plays an important role in this setting, especially in patients with positive measurable residual disease (MRD) after induction and consolidation therapy [1]. The conditioning regimen before transplantation should be potent enough to eliminate lymphoblasts and enable engraftment, yet limited as much as possible to reduce transplant-related mortality. Although total body irradiation (TBI) is commonly considered a key component of conditioning therapy in ALL patients, there is no standard for both the TBI procedure, including dose, fractionation and dose rate, as well as additional chemotherapy administered in combination with irradiation [2]. TBI as a myeloablative conditioning in ALL patients is usually given at a dose of 12 Gy in six fractions, while cyclophosphamide (Cy) is the most common constituent added to this conditioning [2]. However, to minimize conditioning-related toxicity, TBI may be administered at a lower dose of 8 Gy, while Cy is replaced with fludarabine (Flu), a regimen which has shown encouraging results in several recent studies [3–5]. On the other hand, considering acute and long-term toxicities related to TBI, such as interstitial pneumonitis, endocrine dysfunction and secondary malignancies, some ALL patients, especially older ones, may benefit from TBI-free conditioning regimens, which are mainly based on busulfan (Bu) in combination with other components, such as Cy, Flu, thiotepa, melphalan or clofarabine [6–10]. Another cytotoxic agent with profound antileukemic and immunosuppressive properties is treosulfan (Treo), a precursor of L-diepoxybutane responsible for DNA alkylation, interstrand cross-linking and apoptosis [11, 12]. As a component of conditioning therapy, Treo is usually combined with Flu and given intravenously over three consecutive days at doses from 10 g/m2 to 14 g/m2 per day [13]. There are many studies evaluating Treo in conditioning regimens before transplantation in children and infants with different malignant and non-malignant diseases, such as ALL, primary immunodeficiencies, inborn errors of metabolism, haemoglobinopathies and bone marrow failure syndromes [14–18]. As for adult patients, almost all studies on Treo-based conditioning regimens concern patients with acute myeloid leukemia (AML) and myelodysplastic syndrome (MDS), with only a single study focusing on patients with chronic myeloid leukemia, myelofibrosis, lymphoma and multiple myeloma [19–36]. Although transplant outcomes after Treo-based conditioning are very encouraging, the data on its use in ALL patients are very scarce, with only one phase II study showing its value in this setting [37]. There are no studies comparing Treo-based conditioning with other regimens used in ALL patients, therefore, we aimed to compare Treo-based versus (vs.) TBI-based conditioning, both combined with Flu (FluTreo vs. FluTBI), in patients with ALL transplanted in first or second complete remission (CR1 or CR2).

Subjects and methods

Study design and data collection

This was a retrospective, registry-based, multicenter analysis using data from the registry of the Acute Leukemia Working Party (ALWP) of the European Society for Blood and Marrow Transplantation (EBMT). The EBMT represents more than 600 transplant centers, which are required to report all consecutive stem cell transplantations and follow-ups once a year. Data are entered, managed, and maintained in a central database and validated by verification of the computer printout of the entered data, cross-checking with the national registries, and on-site visits to selected teams. The study was approved by the ALWP of the EBMT institutional review board and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and Good Clinical Practice guidelines. The list of institutions contributing data to this study is provided in Supplementary Appendix 1. This study was reported in accordance with the STROBE guidelines.

Criteria for selection

We included adult patients (aged ≥18 years) with ALL who underwent their first alloHSCT in CR1 or CR2 between 2006 and 2022. Patients transplanted from a human leukocyte antigen-matched sibling donor (MSD), unrelated donor (URD) or a haploidentical donor (haploD) were included. Stem cell sources were peripheral blood or bone marrow. The conditioning regimens before alloHSCT were either TBI at a dose of 8–12 Gy plus Flu (FluTBI) or Treo at a total dose of 30–42 g/m2 plus Flu (FluTreo). The use of cord blood as a source of stem cells, as well as transplantations with ex vivo T-cell depletion, were exclusion criteria.

Statistical analysis

Because of the substantial difference in patient numbers between the two conditioning groups, as well as significant differences in demographic and transplant-related parameters, we used propensity score matching with a 1:3 ratio to reduce treatment assignment bias. All standardized mean differences were <=6%. For the matched-pair analysis, patients meeting the inclusion criteria were matched for disease status at alloHSCT, subtype of ALL (B-cell precursor [BCP]-ALL Philadelphia (Ph)+, BCP-ALL Ph-, T-cell precursor-ALL), donor type (MSD, URD, haploD), patient age, patient and donor cytomegalovirus (CMV) serostatus, year of alloHSCT and use of in vivo T-cell depletion. Leukemia-free survival (LFS) was the primary study endpoint. Secondary endpoints were: (1) overall survival (OS), (2) relapse incidence (RI), (3) non-relapse mortality (NRM), (4) incidence of acute and chronic graft-versus-host disease (aGVHD and cGVHD), (5) survival free from grade III-IV aGVHD, cGVHD, and relapse (GRFS) [38]. All endpoints were measured from the time of transplantation. Patients’ characteristics were compared using the Mann-Whitney or Kruskal-Wallis test for continuous variables, and the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. The probabilities of OS, LFS, and GRFS were calculated using the Kaplan-Meier estimate. The probabilities of RI, NRM, aGVHD and cGVHD were estimated using cumulative incidence curves [39]. Comparisons were performed using a Cox proportional hazards model [40], and cluster-robust standard errors were used to account for dependence between observations within matched pairs. Results were expressed as the hazard ratio (HR) with the 95% confidence interval (95% CI). All tests were two-sided with a type 1 error rate fixed at 0.05. Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R 4.0.2 (R Core Team (2020). R: A language and environment for statistical computing. R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria. URL https://www.R-project.org/).

Results

Patient, donor and transplant characteristics

In total, 2055 patients met the inclusion criteria (164 and 1891 patients in the FluTreo and FluTBI groups, respectively). Of these, 153 FluTreo patients and 431 FluTBI patients were included in the final matched-pair analysis. The median patient age was 56.5 and 54.1 years in the FluTreo and FluTBI groups, respectively. The male-to-female proportion was 51.6% to 48.4% and 52% to 48%, respectively. BCP-ALL Ph-, BCP-ALL Ph+ and T-cell precursor-ALL were diagnosed in 61 (39.9%), 42 (27.5%) and 22 (14.4%) patients in the FluTreo group (missing data for 28 patients), respectively, and in 168 (39%), 111 (25.8%) and 70 (16.2%) patients in the FluTBI group, respectively (missing data for 82 patients). About three-quarters of patients in each group were transplanted in CR1 (74.5% in FluTreo and 74.2% in FluTBI group), while peripheral blood was the dominant source of transplanted stem cells in both groups (90.8% and 87.9%, respectively). The allograft was derived from a URD in 54.2% and 52%, an MSD in 30.7% and 32.5% and a haploD in 15% and 15.5%, in the FluTreo and FluTBI group, respectively. AlloHSCT from a female donor to a male recipient occurred in 14.5% of the FluTreo and 18.1% of the FluTBI group. In vivo T-cell depletion was used in 90 patients (58.8%) in the FluTreo group and 243 patients (56.4%) in the FluTBI group, while post-transplant Cy was used in 25 (16.4%) and 95 (22.2%) patients, respectively. In the FluTreo group, Treo was used at a total dose of 30 g/m2 in 43 (28.1%) patients, 36 g/m2 in 22 (14.4%) patients and 42 g/m2 in 88 (57.5%) patients. In the FluTBI group, TBI was used at a dose of 8 Gy in 289 (67.1%) patients and >8 Gy in 142 (32.9%) patients. Transplant outcomes

Graft failure was diagnosed in 5 (3.5%) and 7 (1.7%) patients in the FluTreo and FluTBI group, respectively (results not shown). The 2-year RI and NRM were 28.1% and 25.6% in FluTreo patients and 25.1% and 20.4% in the FluTBI group, respectively (HR 1.31, 95% CI 0.91–1.88, p = 0.15 and HR 1.19, 95% CI 0.8–1.77, p = 0.38, respectively). LFS and OS were 46.2% and 55% in the FluTreo group and 54.5 and 61.3 in the FluTBI group (HR 1.25, 95% CI 0.96–1.63, p = 0.1 and HR 1.19, 95% CI 0.9–1.59, p = 0.22, respectively) (Table 2 and Fig. 1). The most frequent cause of death was original disease followed by infections and GVHD in both groups (Table 3). The 180-day incidence of aGVHD grade II-IV and III-IV was 26.6% and 13.7% in the FluTreo group and 30% and 9.3% in the FluTBI group (HR 0.87, 95% CI 0.6–1.24, p = 0.43, and HR 1.5, 95% CI 0.89–2.54, p = 0.13, respectively). The 2-year incidence of cGVHD and extensive cGVHD was 33.5% and 18.8% in FluTreo patients and 40.7% and 20.2% in the FluTBI group (HR 0.81, 95% CI 0.57–1.14, p = 0.23, and HR 1.04, 95% CI 0.65–1.68, p = 0.87, respectively). Discussion

In this study, we compared FluTreo with FluTBI as a conditioning therapy in ALL patients. We did not observe any significant differences in the main transplant outcomes or transplant-related toxicity between these regimens. To our knowledge, this is the first study comparing the use of Treo-based conditioning with other regimens before alloHSCT in this patient group. Considering the results of this study, Treo may enter the armamentarium of agents commonly used in the conditioning therapy in this setting. Moreover, the combination of Treo with Flu may be a valuable alternative for patients with contraindications for irradiation.

In vivo, Treo, as a prodrug, is subjected to non-enzymatic conversion to L-diepoxybutane, which is responsible for DNA alkylation and thereby cell apoptosis [12]. Contrary to Bu, it is soluble in water, which facilitates intravenous use [41]. The half-life of Treo is about 2 h, and there is a linear correlation between the area under the curve and maximum plasma cell concentration [41]. Originally, Treo was approved for the treatment of ovarian cancer [42], thereafter, it was tested at myeloablative doses in conditioning therapy before autologous HSCT in patients with various advanced malignancies [43]. Although Treo is commonly combined with Flu to enhance the immunosuppressive effect, its combination with Cy was primarily shown in 2005 to be an effective and well-tolerated myeloablative conditioning regimen in patients with hematological malignancies [44].

Treo in combination with Flu as a reduced-toxicity conditioning before alloHSCT has been investigated in many studies of non-homogenous populations comprising relatively small numbers of patients with various hematological malignancies, including MDS, AML, chronic lymphocytic leukemia, chronic myeloid leukemia, lymphoma, multiple myeloma and ALL [45–47]. Treo-based regimens have also been evaluated in more homogenous populations, but almost all of them involved patients with AML and/or MDS being transplanted both in CR and active disease from different types of donors, including haploD [19–23, 25–31, 33]. Many studies comparing Treo-based conditioning have shown a favorable toxicity profile and good transplant outcomes compared with Bu- or melphalan-based conditioning in AML/MDS patients [22, 25–27, 31–33]. On the other hand, there are only a few studies comparing Treo-based vs. TBI-based therapy before alloHSCT in this setting. Berning et al. retrospectively evaluated Treo at a total dose of 30 g/m2 in combination with Flu vs. TBI at a dose of 8 Gy with Flu before alloHSCT in AML and MDS patients [23]. The authors showed significantly lower 1-year NRM (1.9% vs. 9.5%, p = 0.029) in patients receiving the FluTreo regimen without any differences in other transplant outcomes, such as RI, relapse-free survival, OS and GVHD incidence [23]. Similar results were shown in another study restricted to AML patients transplanted in CR1, showing lower NRM in patients treated with a FluTreo regimen when compared to FluTBI 8 Gy (5.3% vs. 16.8%, p = 0.02) [28]. It is noteworthy that there was no difference in NRM in the subgroup of patients aged <55 years. However, the authors observed a higher probability of relapse in the FluTreo group (34.7% vs. 18.3%, p = 0.018) despite the fact that more than 70% of patients were treated with higher doses of Treo (36 g/m2 and 42 g/m2) [28]. The advantages of FluTreo over FluTBI regimens may result from better immune reconstitution after alloHSCT, which is manifested by earlier normalization of CD4 + T-cells and higher IgG levels at one and two years after transplantation in AML/MDS patients [20]. Moreover, patients treated with FluTreo suffered less frequently from high-grade mucositis and contrary to those treated with a FluTBI regimen, the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group performance score improved continuously by day +100 after transplantation [20]. Most recently, Pyka et al. showed good transplant outcomes with long-term survival (3-year OS of 58%) after Treo-based conditioning in a real-world cohort of older ( ≥ 50 years old) and/or comorbid AML patients. Importantly, Treo-based conditioning was also shown to eliminate the negative impact of pretransplant MRD-positivity on transplant outcomes in this setting [19].

Although Treo has a well-established position in conditioning therapy in AML/MDS patients, especially older ones, the data on its use in ALL patients are very scarce. More than 20 years ago, Fichtner et al. confirmed an in vivo antileukemic effect of Treo using human ALL models of pediatric origin xenotransplanted into mice [11]. Thereafter, Treo-based conditioning was shown to be effective and well-tolerated in six infants with high-risk ALL [48]. Similarly, based on another retrospective analysis on 71 pediatric patients with a median age of 9.1 years, Boztug et al. also concluded that Treo-based conditioning is an efficacious approach in this setting with a low transplant-related mortality of 14% [49]. In a large prospective phase III FORUM study, Peters et al. showed superiority of TBI in combination with etoposide over chemotherapy-based regimens including Flu/thiotepa with either Treo or Bu in high-risk ALL children aged >4 years, thereby establishing a standard of care in this group of patients [50]. Based on the latter study, Kalwak et al. compared Flu/thiotepa/Treo vs. Flu/thiotepa/Bu showing no significant differences in any transplant outcome in this cohort with 3-year OS, event-free survival, RI and NRM of 72%, 55%, 36% and 9%, respectively, with the Treo-based regimen [17]. According to these results, conditioning therapy with Flu/thiotepa with either Treo or Bu can be an alternative in ALL children aged >4 years with contraindication or lack of access to TBI [17].

Given the encouraging results of Treo-based conditioning in adult AML/MDS patients and children with ALL, it seems reasonable to assess its effect in adult ALL. Although single patients with ALL were incorporated into some analyses of Treo-based conditioning in non-homogenous cohorts with various diseases, it is impossible to draw any conclusions from those studies for patients with ALL. So far, only one phase II study on behalf of the German Cooperative Transplant Study Group and ALL Study Group (GMALL) has prospectively evaluated Treo-based conditioning before alloHSCT. They studied 50 ALL patients with a median age of 46.5 years [37]. The main inclusion criteria were CR and ineligibility for TBI. Patients received Treo in combination with etoposide and Cy as a conditioning therapy before transplantation. Similar to results in AML/MDS patients, a Treo-based regimen showed a favourable toxicity profile with a low 1-year NRM of 8%. Acute and cGVHD were observed in 53% and 41% of patients, while 2-year RI, disease-free survival and OS were recorded in 51%, 36% and 48% of patients, respectively [37]. Although we observed better outcomes in nearly all transplantation parameters following FluTreo conditioning, the NRM remained relatively high at 25.6% in this cohort, which may be attributed to a 10-year older median age of our study population. Moreover, the differences in transplant outcomes may indicate the role of agents co-administered with Treo.

This study has several limitations, including the lack of some clinical data, such as MRD status before transplantation or long-term toxicity, due to the retrospective nature of this analysis. The relatively low number of ALL patients treated with a FluTreo regimen before alloHSCT in the real-world forced us to include patients treated with all commonly used doses of Treo from 30 g/m2 to 46 g/m2. Similarly, the dose of TBI in the FluTBI group also differs, ranging from 8 Gy to 12 Gy, resulting in a non-homogenous patient population. Due to limited data, we were unable to perform sensitivity analyses stratified by Treo and TBI doses. The median patient age of approximately 55 years in this study population limits the extrapolation of these results to younger age groups.

In conclusion, we first compared Treo-based vs. TBI-based conditioning therapy prior to alloHSCT in adult ALL patients. Since we did not show any differences between FluTreo and FluTBI regimens in the main transplant outcomes, FluTreo may enter a list of commonly used conditioning therapies in ALL patients, particularly being indicated in those with contraindications to TBI. To determine the role of Treo in conditioning therapies in adult ALL patients, further studies, especially prospective ones with different co-agents and comparators, are warranted.
