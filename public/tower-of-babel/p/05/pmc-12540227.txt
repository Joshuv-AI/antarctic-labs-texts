Emerging photothermal agents combined with immunotherapy for cancer treatment

Introduction

Recent cancer statistics indicate that, in 2022, approximately 4,824,700 new cancer cases and 2,574,200 cancer-related deaths were reported in China [1]. Globally, cancer remains a leading cause of mortality, with the World Health Organization estimating over 19 million new cases and 10 million deaths annually. Modifiable risk factors such as tobacco use, alcohol consumption, chronic infections (e.g., HBV and HPV), obesity, and environmental carcinogens contribute significantly to tumour development through mechanisms involving genomic instability and chronic inflammation. Current clinical treatments for cancer encompass surgery, radiotherapy, chemotherapy, immunotherapy, and targeted therapy [2, 3]. The introduction of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs) has substantially reshaped the traditional treatment paradigms for many malignant tumours, demonstrating promising efficacy [4–7]. However, their effectiveness in solid tumours is often limited by hypoxic microenvironments, which contribute to immunosuppression [8].

Photothermal therapy (PTT) is a therapeutic modality that converts light into heat, which can be categorised into traditional PTT (≥ 45 °C) and mild PTT (MPTT) (41–45 °C). Illustratively, obstructive endobronchial carcinomas may be effectively managed with photocoagulation ablation, while hepatic tumours can similarly benefit from various forms of ablative therapy [9]. MPTT has gained broader application in recent years owing to its relatively lower temperature [10, 11]. Traditional PTT operates at higher temperatures, making it more effective in killing tumour cells; however, it also causes more damage to surrounding normal tissues. In contrast, MPTT is less damaging to normal tissues but correspondingly less effective at tumour cell eradication, and it is increasingly utilised as a regulatory mechanism [12].

ICIs target key negative regulators of T cell activity, enhance antitumor immune responses by blocking inhibitory signalling pathways such as programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1), programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1), and cytotoxic T lymphocyte-associated protein 4 (CTLA-4; Fig. 1). These agents have shown significant and durable clinical benefits in multiple cancer types, such as melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer [13]. Nevertheless, their effectiveness is often hampered in “cold tumours,” and the development of primary or secondary resistance remains a major clinical challenge.

Emerging evidence suggests that MPTT not only alleviates tumour hypoxia through vasodilation but also synergises with ICIs by converting immunologically “cold” tumours into “hot” phenotypes [14, 15] (Fig. 2). This therapeutic synergy arises from the dual ability of MPTT to induce immunogenic cell death and remodel the tumour microenvironment (TME), as demonstrated by the combination of near-infrared (NIR) photoimmunotherapy with anti-PD-L1 and CTLA-4 antibodies, which achieves complete responses in multiple murine cancer models [16]. Immunophotothermal therapy (IPTT) combines thermotherapy and immunotherapy to treat tumours through a dual mechanism of “physical killing + immune activation,” addressing issues related to tumour heterogeneity and immune escape.

The emergence of advanced photothermal agents (PTAs) has significantly accelerated the shift in cancer treatment. The tumour-specific accumulation of PTAs is predominantly achieved via two distinct strategies: passive and active targeting. Passive targeting exploits the enhanced permeability and retention (EPR) effect, enabling PTAs to accumulate within the leaky vasculature and impaired lymphatic drainage of the TME. Conversely, active targeting involves the conjugation of PTAs with specific ligands that recognise and bind to overexpressed receptors on tumour cell surfaces. While passive targeting is advantageous in terms of ease of preparation and wide applicability, active targeting offers superior targeting precision and improved cellular internalisation, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy [17, 18]. PTT activates both primary and secondary immune responses within the body, including the release of antigens, upregulation of PD-L1 expression, increased tumour T cell infiltration, and remodelling of the TME. As a result, PTT enhances tumour sensitivity to ICIs [19]. For instance, a hydrogel loaded with molybdenum disulfide and the immune adjuvant R837 (PVA-MoS2-R837, PMR hydrogel) significantly inhibited the recurrence of postoperative breast cancer in a preclinical model [20]. Crucially, contemporary PTAs designs overcome historical limitations related to targeting precision and thermal control, ushering in an era where IPTT could redefine clinical oncology [21, 22].

Given the growing interest in combining MPTT with ICIs, there is a urgent need to critically evaluate recent advances in PTAs, understand the mechanisms of synergy, and assess the clinical potential of IPTT. This review aims to summarise and analyse the latest developments in PTAs, with a focus on their synergistic applications with ICIs, underlying immunomodulatory mechanisms, and preclinical and clinical evidence. Furthermore, it discusses the current challenges and future directions for the clinical translation of IPTT, with the goal of guiding further research and therapeutic innovation in cancer immunotherapy.

PTT

Thermotherapy is a treatment modality in which heat is applied to the body using various heat sources to treat disease. PTT is a specific form of thermotherapy that utilises PTAs to convert light energy, typically NIR light, into heat, making it an effective approach for cancer treatment [23, 24]. However, several challenges remain, including limited penetration depth of light, heterogeneous heat distribution, off-target toxicity associated with conventional PTAs, and insufficient efficacy when used as a standalone treatment [25–28]. When employed as a standalone therapy, PTT often leads to uneven heat distribution within tumour tissues, complicating the achievement of complete tumour eradication.

PTT stimulates the expression of heat shock protein (HSP) genes, leading to increased HSP levels in the TME. HSP inhibitors bind to HSPs, disrupting DNA damage signalling in tumour cells, inhibiting the S and G2 phases of the tumour cell replication cycle, and interfering with tumour cell proliferation and repair processes [29–31]. Additionally, PTT enhances the activity of antigen-presenting cells, promotes T cell infiltration, and increases the expression of PD-L1 [32–34]. Moreover, its heating effect is not confined to the targeted tumour site; it can also induce an “abscopal effect,” activating the systemic immune response and thereby producing synergistic antitumor effects when combined with immunotherapy [35, 36]. PTT regulates the immune state of the TME by increasing the expression of HSPs and activating the immune system, making it a precision-guided immunomodulatory strategy for solid tumour ablation [37, 38]. Furthermore, PTT creates a type I TME characterised by PD-L1 overexpression and an abundance of tumour-infiltrating lymphocytes, positioning it as a promising candidate for complementing and enhancing the efficacy of ICIs [39, 40].

PTAs in IPTT

PTAs absorb light energy and efficiently convert it into heat. They are the key components responsible for the effective antitumor effects in PTT [23].

PTAs absorb light energy at specific wavelengths, typically NIR, and efficiently convert it into heat, raising the local temperature at the tumour site to induce apoptosis or necrosis of tumour cells [41]. The absorption wavelength of PTAs generally falls within the NIR-I window (750–1000 nm), although some also absorb in the red-light region (620–750 nm) and the NIR-II window (1000–1500 nm) [42, 43]. The NIR-II region exhibits lower light scattering and superior tissue penetration, making nanomaterials with strong absorption in the NIR-II window a promising area of research [44, 45]. Furthermore, the elevated temperature mediated by PTAs can promote the release of tumour antigens and activate the host’s antitumor immune response [46]. An ideal PTA should possess high photothermal conversion efficiency (PCE), good biocompatibility, stability, and functionalisable properties.

The performance of PTAs is influenced by several factors, including the wavelength of light absorption, material size, shape, and surface modifications [47]. PCE, a key indicator of PTA performance, plays a crucial role in determining its effectiveness. This efficiency is closely linked to the size and shape of the material [48]. For instance, gold nanoparticles (AuNRs) with a size of 15 nm exhibit a PCE of 100%, whereas AuNRs with a size of 100 nm show an efficiency of approximately 45% [49].

In recent years, nanotechnology-based photothermal materials have demonstrated significant potential for cancer treatment. Researchers have focused on developing nanomaterials with high PCE and excellent biocompatibility, such as gold nanorods [50], graphene nanosheets [51], and carbon nanotubes [52]. These materials can be functionalised for targeted delivery to tumour sites, thereby enhancing therapeutic efficacy. To further improve treatment outcomes, researchers have developed multifunctional nanoplatforms, such as nanosystems, that integrate photothermal, photodynamic, and drug delivery capabilities. These multifunctional platforms enable multimodal synergistic therapies, significantly improving antitumor effects [53].

PTAs are classified into three main categories based on their chemical composition and structural properties: inorganic PTAs, organic PTAs, PTAs based on biomaterials, and organic/inorganic hybrid systems [54, 55] (Table 1; Fig. 3).

Inorganic PTAs

Inorganic PTAs are a class of nanomaterials that can convert light energy into heat energy. Nanomaterials are characterised by nanoscale dimensions, typically ranging from 1 to 1000 nm. Inorganic PTAs play a significant role in tumour therapy due to their high performance and multifunctionality; however, challenges related to their biocompatibility, degradation, and penetration depth remain to be addressed [73, 74]. Beyond the biomedical field, inorganic PTAs also have promising applications in the energy and environmental sectors [75].

Metallic nanomaterials

Metallic gold is the most commonly used raw material for metallic nanomaterials due to its unique surface plasmon resonance effect, strong absorption in the NIR region, high PCE, and good stability [76].

Unlike traditional gold nanophotothermics, SiO2-coated gold nanorods can precisely control the temperature increase in tumour tissues (up to 45 °C), and when combined with anti-PD-L1 therapy, they achieve a 100% complete response [56]. For drug-resistant solid tumours, CeO2@GNSs/Myr-HA demonstrates considerable potential. Its combination of nano-enzymatic catalysis, PTT, and chemotherapy allows for precise photothermal ablation of tumour tissues via the accumulation of nanomaterials, showing enhanced synergistic efficacy in inhibiting tumour proliferation and achieving ablation [57]. Certain modified metal nanoparticles, conversely, can possess dual targeting capabilities, such as iron oxide nanoparticles modified by glucose oxidase (GOx) (Fe₃O₄@PDA-PEG-cRGD-AA@GOx) [58]. These nanoparticles accelerate iron deposition, leading to iron-induced death of tumour cells, and they also possess magnetic resonance imaging capabilities [77].

To overcome the limitations of NIR-I for deep tumour treatment, researchers have begun exploring the potential of NIR-II. Mn2+-BODIPY-based coordinated photo-immune nanoadjuvants can deliver Mn²⁺ to tumours and activate immunity by inducing immunogenic cell death (ICD) through NIR-II laser irradiation. Combined immunotherapy has demonstrated significant therapeutic effects on primary B16F10 tumours and their lung metastases, offering new insights into the design of medical nanomedicine materials [59].

Carbon-based nanomaterials

Carbon-based nanomaterials, including graphene-based nanomaterials [78] and carbon nanotubes [79], also hold significant potential for applications in oncology and antimicrobial therapies [80]. However, their effectiveness as PTAs for tumour treatment is often limited owing to intrinsic properties such as low PCE, insufficient biocompatibility, poor targeting, and challenges with in vivo clearance. To address these limitations, researchers have modified carbon-based nanomaterials with various functionalised moieties to expand their applications in PTT [81]. For instance, short single-walled carbon nanotubes functionalised with polyethylene glycolated phospholipids were found to be biologically non-toxic with extended circulation times, leading to complete tumour elimination in mice without any toxic side effects. In side-by-side experiments with gold nanorods, single-walled carbon nanotubes were injected at a 10-fold lower dose and irradiated with a 10-fold lower power, achieving highly efficient tumour elimination [60].

Semiconductor nanomaterials

Semiconductor nanomaterials, such as bismuth sulfide and bismuth selenide, have absorption bands in the NIR region that are suitable for light-to-heat conversion, making them of interest due to their low cost and ease of preparation [82]. However, single-semiconductor materials often suffer from the common drawbacks of inorganic PTAs, including poor biocompatibility, low degradability, and monofunctionality [83].

The limitations of single-semiconductor materials can be mitigated by strategies such as the addition of a shell. For example, chitosan-coated hollow copper sulfide nanoparticles conjugated with CpG oligodeoxyribonucleotides (HCuSNP-CpG) demonstrated effective photothermal immunotherapy in a mouse breast cancer model. Notably, HCuSNPs can be cleared from the body after laser irradiation, addressing the poor biocompatibility of inorganic PTAs [61].

Organic PTAs

Compared to inorganic PTAs, organic PTAs are less stable but offer several advantages, including cancer-targeting capabilities, multifunctionality, good biosafety, and biodegradability [84].

Organic small-molecule PTAs

Organic small-molecule PTAs are represented by indocyanine green, a commonly used clinical NIR fluorescent dye that absorbs NIR light to produce heat. However, its stability is poor, and it is prone to aggregation and degradation in physiological environments, which negatively impacts the efficacy of PTT [85]. Bionic nanoparticles containing elastin (C12-TPAE-AL nanoparticles) address the low PCE of organic PTAs by suppressing π–π stacking-induced fluorescence quenching, thereby enhancing NIR-II fluorescence brightness and achieving a PCE of 62.4%. This demonstrates the potential of organic PTAs for precise treatment of deep-seated tumours [62].

With the growing integration of photothermal therapy into immunotherapeutic strategies to potentiate antitumor immune responses, the demand for precise and real-time imaging modalities has significantly increased. Photoacoustic imaging (PAI), especially when augmented by nanophotonic agents, offers high-resolution insights into the dynamic changes within the TME. The incorporation of artificial intelligence (AI) further enhances this capability by enabling automated image analysis, early prediction of therapeutic responses, and personalised adaptive treatment planning. These advancements position AI-augmented PAI as a promising platform for guiding and assessing immunotherapeutic interventions in a clinical setting. For example, SPDI-based photothermal therapies have shown excellent results in lymph node localisation and cancer imaging, enabling ultrasensitive early thrombus imaging and facilitating multimodal imaging-guided cancer therapy [63].

Organic polymer PTAs

Some organic polymeric materials, such as conjugated polymers and dendritic macromolecules, can also be utilised as PTAs. Conjugated polymers exhibit good light absorption and charge transport properties, whereas dendritic macromolecules possess highly branched structures that can be modified to optimise their photothermal properties [86]. PCur/IND@Gel integrates PTT, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy, triggering a potent CD8+ and CD4+ antitumor immune response. It demonstrates advantages in delaying tumour progression and prolonging survival and can be applied to rapidly progressing tumours in patients with short survival times [64].

Biomaterial-based PTAs

Certain natural biomaterials and their biomimetics can be developed into PTAs. The application of biomimetic nanomaterials enables effective tumour remission with minimal toxicity, offering a promising approach for deep tumour therapy [87, 88].

Melanin, a natural pigment, possesses excellent light-absorbing ability and biocompatibility, effectively converts light energy into heat energy and is safe and biodegradable. The melanin-based biomimetic nanoplatform AMNP@CLP@CCM was able to cross the blood-brain barrier and specifically target glioblastoma, significantly improving the prognosis of patients when combined with ICIs [65].

RDIR1048 nanoparticles coated with erythrocyte membranes not only enhanced PTT but also reduced immune clearance, prolonged circulation time, and improved tumour targeting [66]. Researchers have attempted to combine bacterial engineering with nanomaterial properties, such as Pg@EV-H-T, for the treatment of glioma. This approach not only modulated the TME but also combined photothermal-activated immunity with an anti-PD-1 inhibitor, significantly delaying tumour growth and prolonging survival [67].

Organic/inorganic hybrid systems

In addition to the aforementioned categorisation, there are composites containing multiple components, some of which correspond to different material classes. The integration of different materials provides a promising strategy to overcome the limitations of single-material treatments, improve efficacy, and reduce off-target effects [89–92]. An example is the composite nanostimulator, comprising thermosensitive liposomes encapsulating CuS, CpG ODNs, and JQ1 [68]. It is characterised by the ability to perform spatiotemporally controlled drug release under NIR-II irradiation, as well as the ability of copper sulfide to cross the blood-brain barrier, demonstrating its potential for central nervous system IPTT. Some of these platforms can achieve precise targeting of specific tumours, such as Peptide Nanofiber-Bimetallic Nanosheet Hybrids (BMNS-PNFs) [69], which demonstrated kill rates exceeding 95% and 90% for the lung cancer cell lines HCC2279 and PC9, respectively. A Ranking of Efficiency Performance (REP) analysis yielded a REP value of 0.741. For tumours that are unsuitable for surgical resection, novel nanovaccine-based platforms consisting of fluorophore-loaded liposomes (IR-7-lipo) coated with multivalent immune adjuvants (HA-CpG) [70] offer new treatment modalities.

The combination of different materials often results in the integration of multiple functions, such as a PTA with dual targeting capabilities, like the manganese-bilirubin complex (MnBV), which demonstrated good drug targeting and photothermal properties in a triple-negative breast cancer model [71]. Ti₃C₂-MXene-Au Nanocomposites (A Multifunctional Tri-Therapy Platform) [72] enable synergistic PTT and enzyme dynamic therapy, coupled with photoacoustic and thermal dual-mode imaging for precise tumour visualisation. This tri-therapy platform combines PTT, enzyme dynamic therapy, and antitumor immune therapy, demonstrating robust features such as excellent biocompatibility, NIR-controlled targeting, significant cancer cell killing, and satisfactory biosafety both in vitro and in vivo.

Clinical translation and challenges

The partially synergistic mechanism of IPTT in tumour treatment has been elucidated in pioneering clinical studies [93] (Table 2 and Supplementary Table S1). The efficacy of IPTT varies significantly across different tumour types, influenced by intrinsic tumour biology and the composition of the TME. Tumours with high mutational burden and pre-existing immune infiltration, such as melanoma and non-small cell lung cancer, tend to respond more favourably to IPTT due to enhanced ICD and improved T cell priming following mild photothermal treatment. In contrast, tumours with a highly immunosuppressive TME, such as pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma and glioblastoma, often exhibit limited responses, attributed to low baseline T cell infiltration and persistent immune checkpoint expression.

While most research on IPTT remains preclinical, the combination of ICIs and PTT has shown significant potential [94–96]. This approach not only enhances immunotherapy efficacy against primary tumours but also inhibits distal metastasis, potentially improving patient survival rates [97–99]. The non-invasive, safe, and environmentally friendly nature of IPTT, along with promising results in experimental models (e.g., nanodrugs combined with agonists of toll-like receptors 7 and 8 for breast cancer [100]), suggests a bright future.

While multiple clinical trials of IPTT have been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov, only one Phase II trial (NCT02680535) evaluating AuroShell nanoparticles in combination with NIR laser therapy has published full results so far. Among 44 patients treated with this regimen, the technical success rate was 66% at 3 months and increased to 77% at 12 months. The majority of other ongoing studies are still in the recruitment or post-interventional analysis stage, highlighting the early-stage clinical development of IPTT strategies [101]. Prior to broad clinical implementation, it is essential to conduct comprehensive clinical trials to validate the therapeutic efficacy and safety of PTAs. Toward this end, the establishment of a clinical applicability scoring system—incorporating critical factors such as biocompatibility, source, PCE, biosafety, and biodegradability—could serve as a valuable tool for systematically evaluating and selecting the most promising PTAs. This approach would not only accelerate the development of effective photothermal immunotherapies but also contribute to the broader goal of improving cancer care on a global scale.

Challenges in clinical translation

Pharmacokinetic optimisation

Although strategies such as PEGylation [66] and biomimetic coatings [65] can enhance the circulation time and tumour accumulation of PTAs, their clinical translation requires Good Manufacturing Practice-compliant synthesis to ensure consistent quality, safety, and efficacy. This necessitates stringent control over raw materials, manufacturing processes, and quality testing, which could potentially increase production costs and complexity.

Dosimetry standardisation

Optimisation of laser parameters, particularly the selection of wavelength and power density, is of critical importance for achieving effective tumour ablation while minimising potential damage to adjacent healthy tissues. Therefore, establishing standardised protocols for laser dosimetry represents a key step in advancing the clinical application of thermal therapies.

Such standardisation not only enhances the reproducibility of treatment outcomes but also ensures consistent safety and efficacy across diverse clinical settings. In this context, the research by Dewhirst and colleagues holds pioneering significance [102, 103]. They not only provided a biological explanation for hyperthermia but also proposed the Cumulative Equivalent Minutes at 43 °C model, establishing a scientific basis for quantitatively assessing thermal damage.

The Cumulative Equivalent Minutes at 43 °C model provides a standardised framework for comparing different photothermal protocols and ensures reproducibility across various platforms and experimental conditions. This is especially important in translational studies where variability in laser systems, PTT agent biodistribution, and tumour heterogeneity can significantly affect therapeutic outcomes.

Furthermore, recent studies have emphasised the need for real-time temperature monitoring during PTT, using techniques such as thermocouples, infrared imaging, or noninvasive photoacoustic thermometry to refine dosimetry and improve treatment consistency.

Immune-related adverse events (irAEs)

The combination of PTT with ICIs enhances antitumor immunity but also increases the risk of irAEs [104]. These are inflammatory toxicities caused by systemic immune activation and commonly affect the gastrointestinal tract (e.g., colitis), lungs (e.g., pneumonitis), and liver (e.g., hepatitis). irAEs may occur during or after treatment and can resemble autoimmune diseases, requiring careful monitoring and timely intervention. Their pathogenesis involves loss of immune tolerance and potential cross-reactivity between tumour and self-antigens. Risk factors include ICI type, treatment duration, and combination regimens. Management typically involves glucocorticoids, with steroid-sparing agents used in refractory cases [105, 106].

As ICIs become more widely integrated into cancer care, standardised toxicity assessment and proactive management strategies are essential to ensure patient safety, particularly in the context of IPTT.

Future directions

Smart material design

Developing PTAs that respond to TME cues, such as pH, redox potential, or enzyme activity, can enable spatiotemporal control over PTT. This approach enhances tumour specificity, reduces off-target effects, and improves therapeutic outcomes [107, 108].

AI-Guided treatment planning

Integrating AI with PAI data [58, 109] can facilitate real-time tumour visualisation, treatment planning, and monitoring. AI algorithms can analyse imaging data to optimise laser parameters, predict treatment response, and personalise PTT strategies [104, 110–114]. PAI has emerged as a powerful tool for detecting brain tumours due to its unique combination of high optical contrast and deep ultrasound penetration. Notably, the integration of nanophotonic agents has significantly improved the resolution and sensitivity of PAI for deep-tissue imaging. The incorporation of AI further expands its diagnostic and therapeutic potential. AI demonstrates remarkable capabilities in image reconstruction, denoising, and enhancement, enabling more accurate delineation of tumour boundaries. Although AI-enhanced PAI holds great promise, several challenges remain, including the need for large, annotated PAI datasets to train robust models, the interpretability of AI predictions in clinical settings, and the regulatory approval of AI-augmented imaging platforms [115].

Personalised combinations

Identifying predictive biomarkers can guide the selection of optimal PTA-ICI combinations for individual patients. For instance, tumours with high PD-L1 expression may benefit more from anti-PD-1 therapy combined with PTT, whereas those with high CTLA-4 expression may respond better to anti-CTLA-4 therapy.

Conclusion

In the context of IPTT, both passive and active targeting strategies offer distinct advantages and limitations in terms of tumour enrichment, immune activation, and clinical translation. Passive targeting relies on the EPR effect, offering the benefits of simple preparation and broad applicability. However, its efficacy is significantly influenced by tumour vascular heterogeneity. In contrast, active targeting achieves higher targeting specificity through ligand–receptor interactions, which enhances tumour cell uptake and ICD, thereby improving synergy with ICIs. Although active targeting faces challenges related to fabrication complexity and clinical translation, its potential in precision medicine cannot be overlooked.

Future strategies should explore hybrid approaches that integrate both mechanisms to optimise targeting efficiency and advance the clinical application of IPTT. It is also important to recognise that several challenges remain in practical clinical implementation. These include balancing thermal efficiency, biodegradability, and targeting accuracy of PTAs, as well as the potential decline in target receptor expression over time due to tumour subtype variation and disease progression. Moreover, the inherent trade-off between laser penetration depth and temperature control must be addressed. Overcoming these translational barriers, while embracing future directions in smart material design, digital integration, and personalised medicine, will be crucial for fully realising the potential of this synergistic therapeutic approach and improving patient outcomes.
