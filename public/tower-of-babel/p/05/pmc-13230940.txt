PDSXray: A Benchmark Dataset for Pseudocoloring-Driven Domain Adaptation in Security X-ray Inspection
Authors: Xiaohao Zhang, Jiansen Qiao, Xianyu Wang, Wenjie Zhang, Jianxun Li, Yinxue Shi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn the field of security X ray imaging, existing datasets mainly focus on endogenous domain shifts caused by hardware differences but do not address pseudocoloring driven domain shift (PDS), a type of domain shift resulting from different pseudocoloring schemes across devices. To fill this gap, we construct and release a benchmark dataset designed for studying domain shifts in security X ray inspection, focusing on both PDS and endogenous differences. The dataset has two complementary subsets: a synthetic subset, which applies controlled hue transformations on an endogenous domain shift dataset to isolate and evaluate PDS independently; and an aggregated subset, which integrates multiple public security datasets into a unified format for verifying cross domain and cross device model generalization. All annotations follow standardized structures with detailed descriptions to ensure reproducibility and comparability. This dataset provides a benchmark for evaluating object detection algorithms under domain shifts and supports studies on model robustness and generalization in diverse X ray security scenarios.Subject terms: Physics, Mathematics and computing

Background & Summary

X-ray security screening is an essential technology in the field of public safety. With its non-destructive imaging capabilities, it plays a crucial role in detecting prohibited and dangerous items at key locations such as airports, customs, and public transportation hubs1–4. In recent years, the introduction of deep learning technologies has significantly improved the accuracy of automated detection. However, when deployed across different devices, there still exists a severe domain shift problem5. For instance, in our experiments, the same detection model achieved a detection rate of 92.5% for lighters on Manufacturer A’s device, but the accuracy plummeted to 31.6% when transferred to Manufacturer B’s device, resulting in a two-thirds loss of detection capability. This dramatic decline in cross-domain performance directly limits the widespread application of models in practical security scenarios.

This phenomenon is observed in various fields and is rooted in domain shift1,5. Domain shift refers to the statistical distribution differences between training data (source domain) and testing data (target domain). In medical X-ray imaging, similar issues of generalization across devices and centers have been reported: for example, deep learning models can suffer significant performance variation when tested across different X-ray devices or acquisition sites6. Security X-ray screening faces analogous device- and manufacturer-related distribution shifts, which motivates the need for benchmarks that explicitly capture such variability. A large number of datasets have addressed this issue and provided support in related studies7–11 as shown in the upper part of Fig. 1. In security screening, domain shift is commonly defined as “Endogenous Domain Shift” (EDS). This shift originates from hardware differences, such as detector parameters and X-ray energy, which result in minor visual differences but significantly impact model performance12 (as shown in the middle part of Fig. 1), and related datasets have studied this issue12. However, there is another factor, which has not received sufficient attention, whose impact on cross-device performance could be even more severe than existing EDS. We refer to this factor as “Pseudocoloring Driven Domain Shift” (PDS) (as shown in the lower part of Fig. 1), and to the best of our knowledge, no dataset has specifically addressed this issue.

Specifically, Pseudocoloring Driven Domain Shift (PDS) arises from non-standard pseudocoloring techniques employed by manufacturers. As shown in Fig. 2, the color differences of the same object on different devices are striking (e.g., metal appears blue on Device A and green on Device B). Pseudocoloring technology enhances human visual perception by converting X-ray images (i.e., atomic number images) into color images13. X-ray imaging distinguishes materials based on their absorption of X-rays, which correlates closely with the material’s atomic number (Z)13. The core principle of pseudocoloring is to use a Look-Up Table (LUT)14 or mapping function to transform atomic number differences into significant color differences. This technology aims to enhance image contrast or classify information rather than restore the true colors of objects, and it is widely applied in medical imaging13, remote sensing15, and industrial flaw detection16. In X-ray security screening, pseudocoloring is particularly common, especially in material-based encoding schemes: X-ray data is used to estimate the effective atomic number (Zeff) and density (ρ) of materials, assigning specific colors to different materials (e.g., organics, inorganics, metals)17,18. However, the lack of a global color mapping standard results in significant differences in pseudocoloring schemes across manufacturers or device models, causing the same object to be represented in completely different colors on different devices.

Imaging examples of three prohibited items under different pseudocoloring schemes. The pseudocoloring differences across devices cause significant color differences in the images, reflecting the impact of PDS.

Figure 3 demonstrates the X-ray imaging pipeline, where the differences in LUTs during the pseudocoloring stage are the fundamental cause of PDS. Unlike endogenous domain shifts (EDS), which are typically caused by hardware-induced visual changes, PDS is characterized by significant color differences for the same object across devices, disrupting the stable relationship between material and color and becoming a key factor in cross-device model performance19. In pathology, stain normalization is commonly used to standardize color appearance across laboratories and improve model generalization; whether a similar strategy is feasible in security X-ray imaging remains an open question, as dual-energy or multi-energy encoding, proprietary LUTs, and the strong link between pseudocolor and material semantics differ from the pathology setting. We therefore focus on providing a benchmark that characterizes and evaluates PDS, rather than on a single normalization approach. Specifically, let the atomic number imaging process of device d be represented as xg~Pg(x∣d),x∈Xg. When the atomic number distributions between different devices differ, i.e., Pg(x|di) ≠ Pg(x|dj), ∀di ≠ dj, we define this as EDS. If the atomic number image is converted to a color image xc=Cd(xg),xc∈Xc using a device-specific pseudocoloring mapping Cd:Xg→Xc, the LUT differences between devices cause the same atomic number input to exhibit different distributions in the color space, i.e., Pc(xc|di) ≠ Pc(xc|dj), ∀di ≠ dj. This difference not only causes changes in atomic number distribution but also disrupts the stability of the label conditional distribution, significantly reducing the cross-device model’s generalization capability.

The X-ray security imaging pipeline: EDS usually originates from hardware differences and often shares similar LUTs, whereas PDS arises from differences in LUTs at the software level, resulting in more severe domain shifts than simple EDS.

These quantification results also provide the core basis for the construction of PDS research benchmarks. Based on this, we have built the PDSXray benchmark dataset29, which contains two clearly defined subsets: The PDS-EDS-Synthetic Subset: Built based on the EDS dataset12, this subset applies software algorithms to perform hue rotation transformations on the existing DOMAIN2 and DOMAIN3 subsets of EDS, adding pseudocoloring variations while retaining the original EDS shift characteristics. This subset consists of 5 domains (1 source domain + 2 EDS subdomains + 2 PDS subdomains) and contains a total of 18,241 images, primarily used to verify the model’s ability to withstand pseudocoloring interference in “EDS+PDS mixed shift scenarios.” The PDS-Aggregated Subset: To better reflect real-world security screening scenarios and evaluate models’ performance against PDS changes, this subset integrates cross-device images from four publicly available datasets: EDS12, KDXray28, CLCXray26,30– 34, and PIDray24. Images are divided into “source domains” (EDS, KDXray, with common pseudocoloring schemes and high image quality) and “target domains” (CLCXray, PIDray, with lower image quality and distinctive color features). The subset also standardizes annotation formats, extracts consistent categories, and establishes new cross-dataset category mapping rules to create a standardized experimental benchmark. Subsequent testing on this subset with multiple YOLO35–37 models will validate its support for “model security PDS cross-domain performance evaluation."

Methods

PDS-EDS-Synthetic Subset Construction

We collected the EDS dataset12, which contains 10 categories of prohibited items across three domains (domain1, domain2, domain3). Domain1 serves as the source domain, while domain2 and domain3 are used as target domains to study endogenous domain shift. To simulate pseudocoloring-driven domain shift (PDS) on the existing EDS (endogenous domain shift) data and facilitate the decoupled analysis of the two types of shifts, we constructed the PDS-EDS-Synthetic subset. This subset preserves the EDS characteristics of the EDS dataset and introduces PDS variables via a custom pseudocoloring algorithm, creating an experimental benchmark that allows the independent quantification of the effects of both types of shifts.

The original EDS dataset uses custom TXT annotations (example: 00011.jpg knife 401 217 531 412), which are incompatible with mainstream object detection frameworks. After standardization, we used 4,814 images to construct domain1 as the source domain, following the common K-fold cross-validation38 partitioning method, with an 85:15 ratio for training/testing sets. We then extracted two independent subdomains, domain2 (4,377 images) and domain3 (5,028 images), from the original EDS dataset. These domains retain the endogenous shift introduced by hardware parameter differences, with color features unchanged. The remaining channels remain unchanged, and the annotation files directly reuse the original EDS subdomains.

Finally, as shown in Fig. 6, the PDS-EDS-Synthetic subset consists of 1 source domain D1 (domain1), 2 pure EDS subdomains D2, D3 (domain2, domain3), and 2 PDS subdomains D2(pds), D3(pds) (domain2_pds, domain3_pds), with 10 categories of prohibited items and a total of 18,241 images. This subset allows us to measure and compare the impact of EDS and the combined effect of EDS and PDS on model performance.

PDS-Aggregated Subset Construction

To better reflect real-world multi-device deployment scenarios and assess the impact of pseudocoloring (PDS) on cross-domain generalization, we explored existing datasets containing data with the same categories and integrated them to construct the PDS-Aggregated subset. We selected the EDS domain1 subdomain12 and KDXray12 as source domains, as they have higher image quality and pseudocoloring logic more in line with common industry settings. As target domains, we selected CLCXray and PIDray, which exhibit more significant pseudocoloring style differences and relatively lower image quality. This setup simulates the “common source domain training → deployment under special conditions in target domains” engineering scenario.

However, multi-source datasets exhibit three types of differences: inconsistent annotation formats (EDS uses custom TXT; KDXray uses VOC/XML; CLCXray and PIDray use COCO/JSON), inconsistent directory structures and naming conventions (whether images/annotations is used), and different category semantics (for instance, the “Knife” category in PIDray and CLCXray both share the “Knife” label, but PIDray mainly refers to common blades while CLCXray includes Swiss Army Knives, which do not meet our synonym mapping principle, so this category is discarded).

For annotation format standardization, we wrote multiple scripts, including Convert_EDStxt_to_yolo.py, Convert_VOC_to_yolo.py, and Convert_coco_to_yolo.py, and used yolo_transfer.py to remap annotations (core code is provided in the Supplementary Information). These scripts converted annotations from EDS, KDXray, CLCXray, and PIDray into YOLO-format .txt files. The output files follow a parallel images/labels structure, in line with the YOLO format, enabling the construction of the source and target domain datasets.

Technical Validation

To ensure the technical reliability of the PDS-Xray benchmark dataset, we used a two-step validation approach: automated checks on all files to establish full-dataset consistency, and manual verification on a stratified sample to assess annotation quality without extrapolating sample-level metrics to the entire dataset. First, we executed a series of system checks on all image and annotation files using an automated script, validate.py. These checks covered various aspects, including the validity of bounding box coordinates, abnormal size filtering, category consistency verification, and resolution compatibility validation. The bounding box coordinates for each annotation (‘xmin’, ‘ymin’, ‘xmax’, ‘ymax’) must be within the image boundaries to prevent annotations from exceeding the image limits. Small bounding boxes (e.g., width or height smaller than 5 pixels) are treated as anomalies and excluded from valid annotations. Additionally, all annotation categories must follow consistent naming conventions, and the annotation logic for the same class of objects across different images must remain consistent. Finally, we checked the ratio between the annotation coordinates and the actual resolution of the image, ensuring that annotation ratios are not distorted due to differences in image resolution. The results from these automated checks show that all annotation files are fully consistent with the target images and that annotation consistency is complete across the full dataset.

We then conducted manual verification on a 30% stratified sample of the dataset, selected to cover all subsets and categories proportionally. This manual review was carried out by two experts with over five years of experience in X-ray annotation, using a specialized script, show_labels.py, to perform a detailed comparison of each category in the dataset. The verification confirmed consistency and accuracy of annotations within that subset (no omissions, incorrect annotations, or redundant annotations) and that each annotation box fully encompassed the target object. In the verified 30% subset, all annotations conformed to the expected category mapping relationships, with an error rate below 0.5% in that subset. In line with standard practice for sample-based manual review, we do not extend this error rate to the full dataset, as the remaining 70% was not manually reviewed; reliability for the full dataset is supported by the automated checks applied to 100% of the annotation files. Detailed implementation can be found in the Supplementary Information along with the relevant scripts.

To validate the dataset’s utility in addressing domain shift issues, we performed baseline experiments on two subsets: PDS-EDS-Synthetic and PDS-Aggregated, and evaluated the dataset’s performance under these shifts by testing YOLO-based detectors, including YOLOv535, YOLOv1036, and YOLOv1137. The experiments used the original hyperparameters under the Ultralytics framework and were trained for 100 epochs. We used mean Average Precision at IoU threshold 0.5 (mAP@0.5) as the primary evaluation metric to verify the dataset’s cross-domain generalization ability; this metric is standard in object detection benchmarks.

The results indicate significant differences in sensitivity to domain shifts across categories, particularly in the KDXray → PIDray migration, where the performance of the Lighter category nearly completely vanishes. Lightweight models are more stable in cross-domain tasks, whereas larger models, while performing well in the source domain, show more severe performance drops in the target domain. Compared to CLCXray, cross-domain transfer with PIDray is generally more challenging, reflecting its more complex domain shift characteristics.
