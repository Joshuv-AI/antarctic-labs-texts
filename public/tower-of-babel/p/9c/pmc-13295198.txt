Bilateral Sprengel deformity with asymmetrical omovertebral connections in a child with Klippel–Feil syndrome: a rare case report
Authors: Zirvecan Güneş, Murat Danışman, Şenol Bekmez
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
An 8-year-old male patient with bilateral Sprengel deformity, characterized by a right-sided bony omovertebral bar and a left-sided fibrous band, in the context of Klippel–Feil syndrome is presented in this case report. The patient had limited shoulder movement, a short neck, and considerable cosmetic issues. The radiological evaluation showed the omovertebral structures to be asymmetrical and a cervical fusion at C2-C3. The patient underwent 2 separate surgical procedures: the right side was operated on when the patient was 8 years old, and the left side was operated on 8 months later. The procedures involved the resection of the omovertebral structures and modified Woodward procedures for scapular repositioning. The postoperative results showed a marked improvement in shoulder abduction and cosmetic appearance with no neurovascular complications. This report emphasizes the need for early diagnosis, comprehensive imaging, and individualized surgical planning in the management of complex congenital shoulder deformities.

Introduction

Sprengel deformity (SD) is a rare congenital anomaly resulting from the failure of the scapula to descend to its normal thoracic position during embryologic development. It is the most common congenital shoulder girdle abnormality and is typically unilateral; bilateral cases account for less than 10% of all instances.1

Sprengel deformity is frequently associated with other skeletal and systemic anomalies, particularly Klippel–Feil syndrome (KFS), which is characterized by the congenital fusion of cervical vertebrae.2 The omovertebral bar, which appears in 30%-50% of SD cases as a bony, cartilaginous, or fibrous body that links the scapula to the cervical spine, limits scapular mobility and causes significant functional impairment.3

Surgical intervention is recommended between the ages of 3 and 8 years for patients with significant functional and cosmetic impairment.4 Treatment aims to resect the omovertebral connection and reposition the scapula to a more caudal location using procedures such as the Woodward or Green techniques.5,6

The patient with KFS presents with a rare instance of bilateral SD that includes a right bony omovertebral bar and a left fibrous band. The case presents both diagnostic and surgical complexities of congenital musculoskeletal disorders and demonstrates how personalized multidisciplinary surgical treatment produces outstanding functional and cosmetic results.

Case presentation

An 8-year-old male presented with restricted shoulder mobility, a short neck, and difficulty performing overhead activities. The deformities had been noted since early childhood, but no prior interventions had been undertaken. The patient’s family history was unremarkable. The patient displayed a short neck with webbing and cervical rotation at 40° bilaterally and lateral bending at 20° during physical examination. The patient displayed elevated scapulae on both sides. Bilateral shoulder abduction was limited to 70° and thoracic kyphosis with thoracic movement compensation during arm elevation was observed. The upper extremity sensory and motor functions were normal during the neurological assessment.

The cervical spine fusion at C2-C3 matched KFS (Figure 1). The radiological assessment also included computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging (MRI). Computed tomography scans revealed that a right omovertebral bar linked the superomedial scapula to the C5 transverse process (Figure 2). The scapular elevation received a Rigault Grade 2 rating on both sides.7 The Cavendish classification system graded the patient at Grade 3 bilaterally because scapular elevation produced noticeable deformity and was observable from front and back perspectives. The MRI results confirmed that the left omovertebral band was fibrous and revealed normal spinal cord and visceral structures including the absence of renal and cardiac anomalies.

The surgical plan included 2 separate procedures. The first surgical intervention took place on the right side when the patient reached 8 years of age. The surgical goal involved improving both shoulder function and appearance through a modified Woodward procedure which included removing the omovertebral bar and repositioning the scapula. The patient underwent general anesthesia while positioned prone before posterior incision through C5-T8. The procedure included intraoperative neuromonitoring as a safety measure to prevent neurovascular injuries. After elevating the skin and subcutaneous flaps, the trapezius, rhomboid, and latissimus dorsi muscles were carefully dissected to expose the scapula. The trapezius was detached from the spinous processes from a caudal to cranial direction, and the rhomboid muscles were reflected while preserving their tendinous insertions for later reattachment. The omovertebral bar or fibrous band was identified and released using heavy scissors or bone biters and removed extraperiosteally. The scapula was then mobilized by releasing any adhesions and repositioned to a more caudal level (Figure 3). To provide support until soft tissue healing was complete, an “internal bracing” technique was utilized by passing an absorbable suture around a lower rib.8 This technique aims to provide temporary stabilization and support to the repositioned scapula during the critical period of soft tissue healing. A potential risk associated with this maneuver is pneumothorax; therefore, after passing the suture, a saline test was performed in the surgical field, and the absence of air bubbles confirmed that no pneumothorax occurred. Heavy nonabsorbable sutures also attached the trapezius muscle to the lower spinous processes.9

The second surgical procedure took place 8 months later on the left side. The left fibrous omovertebral band received careful dissection for excision which enabled complete scapular movement. A modified Woodward procedure was performed to reposition the scapula. The musculature received reattachment and the scapula was fixed at its correct thoracic level.

After surgery the patient received 3 weeks of immobilization followed by a physiotherapy protocol that included shoulder abduction exercises and strengthening of periscapular muscles. The patient obtained excellent functional recovery at the last follow-up evaluation which took place 19 months after right side surgery and 11 months after left side surgery. The right shoulder abduction reached 110° while the left shoulder abduction reached 100° (Figure 4). The surgical procedure resulted in major improvement of shoulder appearance and no neurovascular issues were detected (Figure 5). The family together with the patient reported high satisfaction regarding the functional and aesthetic outcome despite the development of a moderate hypertrophic scar at the incision sites. Written informed consent was obtained from the patient’s legal guardians for publication of this case report and any accompanying images.

Discussion

This case makes a unique contribution to the literature through its presentation of bilateral SD with asymmetrical omovertebral structures—a bony bar on the right and a fibrous band on the left. The patient’s unique anatomy added complexity to diagnosis and surgical planning. The patient underwent 2 surgical procedures with an 8-month interval to enable safer corrections and better rehabilitation. The modified Woodward technique with omovertebral structures resection and intraoperative neuromonitoring resulted in successful scapular repositioning. The patient maintained positive results in shoulder abduction along with proper scapular alignment and cosmetic results. The effectiveness of this customized surgical approach is demonstrated through both clinical images and radiological studies.

The routine use of clavicle osteotomy in the surgical correction of SD remains a topic of debate in the literature. Some authors suggest that an osteotomy provides a wider, safer arc for scapular descent, thereby minimizing the risk of stretch injury to the brachial plexus.10 But other surgeons achieve satisfactory results by avoiding clavicle osteotomy.11 However, this additional procedure is not without its own potential morbidity, including risks of nonunion, delayed union, and hardware-related complications. In the case, the decision to omit a clavicle osteotomy was based on 2 primary factors. First, the use of continuous intraoperative neuromonitoring provided real-time feedback on the integrity of the brachial plexus. This allowed for a controlled and carefully monitored scapular descent, ensuring that neurovascular structures were not compromised. Second, adequate caudal repositioning was achieved for both functional and cosmetic improvement without the need for clavicular release. The experience supports the perspective that while clavicle osteotomy remains an important tool in select severe cases, it may not be universally necessary, particularly when surgical correction is safeguarded by intraoperative neuromonitoring.

The Woodward procedure delivers long-lasting functional and aesthetic results. The literature shows that potential complications from the procedure include recurrence, motion restriction, and hypertrophic scarring.12,13 The patient has maintained stable correction and improved abduction at 19 months for the right side and 11 months for the left side without neurovascular complications or recurrence. The short duration of follow-up creates a limitation, but the results match previous studies and demonstrate why patients need a prolonged follow-up period to identify potential delayed complications.

The case demonstrates why it is important to diagnose and treat congenital shoulder anomalies early and to operate on them at the right time. Delayed treatment may result in persistent functional deficits, postural compensation, and psychosocial concerns related to appearance. Computed tomography and MRI are particularly useful for identifying associated anomalies such as cervical spine fusion and omovertebral morphology that directly affect surgical planning. Patients with KFS accompanying SD are at high risk for serious occult craniocervical anomalies, such as Chiari malformation or basilar invagination. As these conditions increase the risk of neurological injury during surgery, preoperative screening with cervical MRI is critically important. It is believed that this screening is an indispensable step in minimizing perioperative risks for all surgical candidates with KFS.

To the best of current knowledge, very few cases in the literature report bilateral SD with this degree of asymmetry in omovertebral anatomy. Pinheiro et al. reported an adult patient who had bilateral SD along with omovertebral bones and failed thoracic spinal segmentation. Although the patient showed symmetrical omovertebral bones and an exceptional presentation at age 19, their study reinforced the significance of omovertebral anatomy and spinal abnormalities for surgical planning.14 Pargas et al. reported 2 biological sisters who had SD, with one patient displaying bilateral involvement and the other patient showing unilateral involvement. The sibling with bilateral involvement displayed omovertebral connection asymmetry, which matched the current case findings. The study demonstrates genetic or familial involvement in the condition, although this link remains unproven, which enhances clinical importance for evaluating such infrequent deformities.15 the results are also consistent with the literature, which reports shoulder abduction gains ranging from 20° to 70° after surgery, confirming the reliability of the technique. The study provides significant information to current literature and demonstrates why individualized multidisciplinary care is essential for treating complex congenital musculoskeletal conditions.

A limitation of this study is the absence of standardized patient-reported outcome measures (PROMs) to quantitatively assess psychosocial benefits and quality of life. Although objective functional and cosmetic gains were documented, the positive psychosocial outcome was assessed subjectively through the high satisfaction expressed by the patient and his family. The use of PROMs is recommended in future studies for a more holistic evaluation of treatment success. Another limitation of the study is the follow-up period of 19 months for the right side and 11 months for the left side, which represents a relatively short follow-up period that limits the assessment of long-term functional and cosmetic results and the potential recurrence of scapular elevation during growth.

The optimal surgical timing for bilateral SD—whether to perform a staged or simultaneous correction—remains a topic with limited guidance in the current literature. The scarcity of comparative studies establishing a clear standard of care for this rare condition leaves the decision-making process to clinical judgment based on fundamental surgical principles. In the absence of high-level evidence, the approach was predicated on maximizing patient safety and facilitating the rehabilitation process. A staged approach was therefore chosen as the most cautious strategy to minimize the cumulative risk of a devastating bilateral neurovascular complication, such as a brachial plexus injury, and to avoid the significant functional challenges of simultaneous bilateral shoulder immobilization. It is believed that this patient-centered approach was the most appropriate for ensuring a safe and manageable recovery in the case.

This case report includes a rare and complex condition, which is a bilateral SD with asymmetrical omovertebral structures in the context of KFS. A multidisciplinary approach, preoperative planning, and individualized surgical techniques can result in important functional and cosmetic improvements and thus improve the quality of life of the patient.
