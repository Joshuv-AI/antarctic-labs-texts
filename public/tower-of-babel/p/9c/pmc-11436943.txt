RETRACTED ARTICLE: Microstructure and dry sliding wear evaluation of functionally graded coating deposited via atmospheric plasma spray
Authors: M Sathish, N Radhika, Bassiouny Saleh
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractSurfaces are commonly enhanced with wear-resistant coatings to improve their resistance to abrasion, erosion, and other forms of wear. These coatings play a crucial role in extending the lifespan and improving the performance of materials and components exposed to challenging conditions. The objective of the current study is to deposit thermally sprayed 3-layer TiC–12Co–10ZrO2/NiCoCrAlMo functionally graded coatings on SS410 substrates using atmospheric plasma spray. The results revealed that by employing appropriate ceramic proportions, the microhardness of graded coatings increased to 990.4 HV, which is approximately 3.21 times higher than that of the substrate. Furthermore, the tribological properties of graded coating exhibited superior resistance compared to the substrate, where process settings were varied. Specifically, graded coating samples subjected to the applied load, sliding distance, and sliding velocity showed improved wear resistance of 78.51%, 68.42%, and 76.66% respectively in extreme conditions compared with uncoated samples. The characterization of the worn surface revealed a sequential occurrence of wear mechanisms: abrasive, adhesive, and delamination. The formation of TiO2 and Cr2O3 tribo-oxide film protects the surface against the wear mechanism and is applicable in extreme wear environments.Keywords: Wear-resistant coatings, Functionally graded coating, Atmospheric plasma spray, Microstructure characterization, Microhardness, Wear resistanceSubject terms: Engineering, Materials science

Introduction

Wear-resistant coatings are specifically designed to protect surfaces from damage caused by various forms of mechanical stress, such as abrasion, erosion, friction, and more. These coatings find widespread application across a wide range of materials and components, serving to prolong service life, reduce maintenance costs, and improve overall performance1–3. Several factors, including operating conditions, material properties, and the specific requirements of the application, must be considered when selecting the best wear-resistant coating. With so many coating options available, care must be taken to ensure the coating’s compatibility with the intended use. Stainless Steel—Grade 410 (SS410), a widely used material, falls into the category of general-purpose 12% chromium martensitic stainless steels. This grade can be heat-treated to achieve a variety of mechanical properties. The properties of SS410 include excellent strength, hardness, and resistance to heat and corrosion in nature. Due to its adaptability, it may be used in a variety of applications, including pipelines, valves, nozzles, brake parts, automotive parts, and components in the petroleum distilling sector3,4. Though these grades of steel have good corrosion resistance, they are limited to poor wear resistance characteristics. The researchers focused on surface enhancement techniques, such as coating processes using hard transition materials, to improve the wear-resistant capabilities of the SS410 grade to withstand tough situations5,6. These hard phasing materials mainly come under carbides in the form of titanium, tungsten, chromium, and boron and oxides in the form of aluminum, zirconium, cesium, etc. which possess improved wear resistance and high-temperature resistance properties7–10. Among these materials, titanium carbide is well-known for its excellent wear-resistance properties, making it a popular material for various wear-resistant applications. Its high hardness makes it highly resistant to abrasive wear, making it suitable for cutting tools, wear-resistant coatings, and other high-wear applications. It revealed superior resistance to corrosion and oxidation, even at high temperatures, and was chemically stable. This property ensures its durability in harsh environments and makes it suitable for high-temperature applications11,12. In order to shield different surfaces from abrasion and wear, this carbide was a constituent of wear-resistant coatings that were sprayed using thermal spraying process. This material contributes to performing superior wear-resistant coatings and protecting the substrate surfaces and makes it a useful material for many sectors where wear, erosion, and abrasion are major concerns13–16.

Functionally graded coatings (FGCs) are more effective in withstanding wear-resistance applications due to their customized ceramic proportions, and gradient coating structure. These enhanced coating structures provide gradual transitioning properties in the coatings, which helps in the reduction of crack formation and propagation. Also, the presence of intermediate layer in the gradient structure improves strong adhesion bonding between the top layer and substrate, which helps in reducing the coating delamination during mechanical stresses17–19. These new composite coatings have characteristics that are specifically matched to a particular location due to minute variations in their compositions and coating structure across their volume. Since FGCs are a unique type of conventional composites, new research avenues have been made possible. The phases in the gradient coatings are evenly scattered to provide a smooth gradient structure20,21. The use of spraying methods significantly contributes to the creation of intermediate layers in the form of graded structures22,23. FGC has been extensively employed to prevent surface damage by attaining distinct attributes across various locations, making them appropriate for varying operational circumstances24–26. A flexible and effective method for improving the properties of the substrate material is most notably, extending its lifespan and functionality by depositing the FGCs with a gradient structure which improves high precision on its surface. In certain high-temperature situations, a composite mixture of ceramics with metallic elements combinations improves the surface properties and lifespan27,28. Metallic materials may be preserved and separated in a unique way using ceramic coatings. Recent research on coatings has confirmed that a graded structure of thin-layer coatings has a thermal fracture tolerance that is similar to that of a thicker coating system29,30. Nevertheless, to thoroughly assess the failure processes, a functionally graded thermal barrier coating (FG-TBC) design must be produced efficiently. The impact of developing fractures on the FGC focusing on its surfaces, graded coating layers, and substrate surface morphology were investigated31,32. It was determined that by modifying the substrate’s elasticity modulus and thermal expansion coefficient, surface crack impingements were reduced33. The research indicates that tension created by stress reduction and ongoing cyclic thermal stresses is the cause of fracture development in the multilayer ceramic layer coatings34–36.

According to the above review, wear and degradation can have a significant impact on the performance and lifespan of materials and components. As a result, finding effective solutions to increase wear resistance is critical. With this goal in mind, this paper focuses on the development of wear-resistant coatings, which play a critical role in protecting surfaces from mechanical stress damage. To achieve this goal, the study undertook the synthesis of 3-layer functionally graded coatings. These graded coatings consist of different layers, with TiC–12Co–10ZrO2 as the topcoat, a mixture of 50% TiC–12Co–10ZrO2 and 50% NiCoCrAlMo as the intermediate coat, and NiCoCrAlMo as the bond coat powders. The synthesis process involved utilizing the Atmospheric Plasma Spray (APS) technique to spray the resulting powder combination onto the SS410 steel substrate, resulting in an overall coating thickness of approximately 450 µm. Also, several characterization techniques were performed to evaluate the microstructure, phase orientation and its morphological properties of the ceramic powders and graded coating samples were attained. In addition, the coated and uncoated specimens were compared to evaluate the microstructural analysis and mechanical and tribological traits for this investigation. The objective of the research was to deliver the functionality of the gradient coatings by examining their hardness, wear-resistant behavior, and mechanisms.

Raw materials

In this work, TiC was employed as the predominant material in the gradient coating structure to increase the hardness and wear resistance properties of the coatings. In order to decrease the hard phases of carbide’s propensity, cobalt was included in TiC element to prevent fracture and increase toughness. This soft phase of cobalt acts as a binder matrix for the carbide particle and attains a strong bond and tough matrix in the coating structure. In addition, zirconium oxide was added to the top coat layer to resist the crack initiation and propagation during mechanical stress. Also, this element helps in increasing the adhesion between the ceramic and metallic combinations to resist wear and high-temperature situations. Accordingly, for the bond coat section nickel was the main alloying element due to its increased adhesion strength, and hardness, and reduces the brittle fracture in the gradient structure. Also, it provides great adhesion in the intermediate layers to prevent delamination and spallation of the coatings. Additionally, cobalt was known for its high-temperature strength and ductility which made them suitable for the bond coat compositions. Likewise, chromium was included in the composition due to its protective oxide layer formation during plasma spray, which helps in improving the oxidative and wear resistance. Also, for providing the diffusion barrier properties and preventing inter-diffusion between the bond coat and substrate, aluminum was included in this composition. Additionally, to enhance adhesion bonding and high-temperature strength in the coating, a minimum quantity of molybdenum was added to the bond coat composition. Due to the existence of an intermediate layer in the gradient structure, this layer improves the bonding strength, and fracture toughness, provides a strong supportive base, and resists crack propagation from the top coat layer. The bond coat layer protects the substrate while spraying improves adhesion between the metallic-ceramic interfaces, and reduces substrate oxidation. These material selections, as well as the functionally graded structure, were made to achieve the coatings’ desired wear resistance and performance characteristics. Atmospheric plasma spraying

The TiC–Co–ZrO2/NiCoCrAlMo functionally graded coatings with one intermediate layer were created using an air plasma system and the SULZER-Metco 3 MB plasma gun. Figure 2 depicts the process parameters of the Atmospheric Plasma Spray (APS) technique, providing a schematic view of the procedure. The following parameters were taken into account: (1) Carrier and Auxiliary Gases: A mixture of argon and nitrogen gases was used as the carrier and auxiliary gases to create plasma during the coating process, (2) Powder Feeder: Argon gas served as the carrier gas to transfer the powder particles from the powder feeder to the plasma torch, and (3) Cooling: Compressed air was utilized to cool both the substrate and coatings during the spraying procedure. Specific ranges for current density and particle size were observed to ensure the production of a dense coating structure in the APS technique. The current density was kept between 650 and 800A, and the particle size was kept between 40 and 70 μm. These parameters all help to achieve a dense, well-adhered coating structure. Figure 2b shows additional information about the 3-layer graded structure and its associated process. The figure depicts the coatings’ layered composition, which includes the top coat layer of TiC–Co–ZrO2, the intermediate coat layer of TiC–Co–ZrO2 and NiCoCrAlMo, and the bond coat layer of NiCoCrAlMo.

Microstructural analysis

The morphology of powders and microstructural analysis of graded coatings were examined using (Carl Zeiss Gemini 300, Germany) field emission scanning electron microscope with a voltage acceleration in the range of about 10–20 kV. Consequently, the SEM micrographs of both composite powder mixes and graded coated samples were employed before different levels of magnifications. The elemental composition and balance distribution for powders and graded coating samples were examined using EDS with the lens designed by Zeiss. Further, the Empyrean—Diffractometers with Cu Ka radiation of about 40 kV and 30 mA for determining the phase composition and intermetallic phase structure in the powders and coating samples with the scanning rate of 2°/step were analyzed. Additionally, using the Quanta 3D FEG equipment, Electron Back Scatter Diffraction (EBSD) was used to examine the grain structures and phase orientations of the coated materials.

Results and discussion

Characterization of coating powders

Characterization of FG’s coating powder is necessary to ensure the material’s intended qualities and its performance. The characterization gives information on different aspects that determine the overall features and functionality of the graded coating. An extensive analysis was conducted using SEM, EDS, and XRD methods to characterize the powders of titanium carbide (TiC), zirconium oxide (ZrO2), nickel (Ni), cobalt (Co), chromium (Cr), aluminum (Al), and molybdenum (Mo) for plasma spray coatings. SEM analysis found that TiC and ZrO2 powders showed angular shapes, which can improve mechanical interaction in coatings, whereas Ni, Co, Cr, Al, and Mo powders had mostly spherical or irregular morphologies, which were favorable for flowability and packing density. All of these powders had the same particle size distribution, which is necessary for reliable coating performance and thickness. According to surface topography studies, ceramic powders have slightly rougher surfaces that improve adhesion in the final coating, whereas metallic powders have flat surfaces that contribute to effective bonding and spraying. Figures 3a,b, 4a,b, and 5a,b illustrate the surface morphologies and particle size distribution of composite mixes of top coat, intermediate coat, and bond coat layer powders respectively. The existence of the main elements at high purity levels in each powder was verified by the EDS spectra. Whereas ZrO2 powders demonstrated a homogenous presence of Zr and O, TiC powders demonstrated a homogeneous distribution of Ti and C. Likewise, powders of Ni, Co, Cr, Al, and Mo showed predictable and constant elemental compositions free of notable impurities. For consistent performance and quality in plasma spray coatings, efficient synthesis and preparation procedures are essential, as seen by the homogeneous elemental distribution throughout all powders as shown in Figs. 3c, 4c, and 5c. The low level of impurities further confirms the powders’ appropriateness for high-performance uses. With all factors considered, the EDS analysis verified that these powders had the necessary chemical purity and composition for producing excellent plasma spray coatings. Microstructure analysis of coating surface

The microstructural analysis of the cross-sectional view of graded coatings using a TiC–12Co–10ZrO2 and a NiCoCrAlMo bond coat over a steel substrate reveals a sophisticated gradient in composition and microstructure designed to enhance performance. Starting from the steel substrate, the analysis shows a gradual transition in the microstructure through the bond coat and into the TiC top layer, which helps alleviate thermal stresses and improve adhesion. The bond coat displays a dense, fine-grained structure that serves as a diffusion barrier and provides oxidation resistance. Also, strong metallurgical bonding is observed at the interface with the steel substrate. The TiC particles, distributed within the graded layers, enhance hardness and wear resistance. Stronger and more constant molecular linkages exist between the coating and the substrate due to the material’s uniform distribution in the graded powder mixtures38,39. The graded coating’s cross-sectional view, as seen in (Fig. 6a), clearly distinguishes the three layers sprayed over the substrate. A uniform coating structure was obtained as a result coatings of each layer adhered strongly between the substrate to the TiC-graded layers with an overall coating thickness determined to be 450 µm as observed. According to layer morphology and thickness, distinct and well-defined layers of TiC top coat, intermediate coat, NiCoCrAlMo bond coat, and steel substrate were observed. This confirms successful deposition and appropriate thickness of each layer, which are essential for ensuring the designed properties of the coating.

Characterization of a cross-section view of TiC-based graded coated sample: (a) Cross-sectional view of 3 layer functionally graded coatings with all the layer details, (b) Line scan on the cross-sectional view of the graded coated sample.

A smooth and gradual transition in composition was observed between the layers; this reduces the thermal and mechanical stress concentrations between the graded layers and the substrate and enhances the coating’s longevity. Meanwhile, it was found that there were strong and defect-free interfaces were observed between the graded structure and the steel substrate. This improves adhesion and overall coating integrity and minimizes the possibility of delamination under stress and minimal interfacial defects. The graded layers also showed fine, uniformly dispersed grains and low levels of porosity, indicating increased mechanical strength and resistance to mechanical and thermal stresses. This enhances mechanical properties such as hardness and toughness and improves wear resistance. A low level of porosity shows higher density and strength within the graded structure, which leads to improved performance of the coating in service. Furthermore, it was observed that the graded layers exhibited minimal levels of crack propagation and that the detected cracks were not propagating across the graded layers. This outcome shows that there is a lower chance of coating failure and that the coating is resilient under operating circumstances. Additionally, the line scan analysis was performed on the cross-sectional graded coated sample which mapped the presence of each constituent along the interfacial layer as seen in Fig. 6b. Each component of the graded composite powders is confirmed to be present by the EDS analysis performed on the cross-sectional view of the graded coatings as seen in secptram1, 2, and 3. According to the findings, TiC composite-graded coatings have substantially better interfacial contact with the substrate than comparable materials. This enhancement is due to the substrate’s superior metallurgical compatibility with the TiC composite powder mixes. Because of this compatibility, each component is integrated well, creating a strong and long-lasting interface. Collectively, these findings suggest that graded coatings featuring TiC–12Co–10ZrO2 and NiCoCrAlMo bond coats offer superior mechanical properties, rendering them highly effective for protecting steel substrates in demanding environments.

Microhardness analysis

Significant data insights into the mechanical properties of the 3-layer TiC–12Co–10ZrO2/NiCoCrAlMo graded coatings were obtained from the microhardness evaluation of the coatings that were sprayed onto SS410 substrates using the APS technique. With a load of 100 g (HV0.1), the Vickers microhardness tester was used to measure the microhardness values for the graded coatings. The investigation showed that the hardness of the graded coating layers improved significantly. A comparison of the microhardness of graded coated samples and uncoated substrate material reveals significant differences. The microhardness of the graded coated samples changes gradually over the three layers of functionally graded coating. This gradual change indicates that the hardness transition from one layer to the next is smooth. In contrast, the microhardness of the uncoated samples differs significantly from that of the coated samples. As shown in (Fig. 8), the maximum microhardness observed in the graded coated samples is 990.4 HV, which represents a significant improvement over the base material’s microhardness of approximately 307.6 HV. This increase in microhardness corresponds to a hardness increase of approximately 3.21 times in the graded coated samples when compared to the uncoated substrate material. Likewise, the fracture toughness obtained for these graded coating sample was about 7.56 MPa m1/2 as shown in Fig. 8. This toughness was achieved due to the presence of cobalt and the fine distribution of ZrO2 particles contribute to improved toughness by hindering crack growth and providing mechanisms for crack deflection and energy dissipation.

Comparison of microhardness and fracture toughness of coated and uncoated samples.

Graded-coated samples exhibit enhanced microhardness and fracture toughness due to many contributing parameters. This gradient effect distributes mechanical stresses more uniformly and enhances the coating’s durability against cracking and delamination. Also, this effect reduces the thermal stresses induced at the interface section and enhances its overall longevity and functionality of the coating structure40. Meanwhile, the presence of hard phase elements such as TiC and ZrO2 of top layer with appropriate ceramic proportions and optimal deposition parameters of current density range maintained between 600 and 800A are responsible for the enhanced microhardness and toughness. These factors combined to produce a thick and well-bonded gradient structure with a low porosity level and flaws are achieved. As a result, this gradient coating structure greatly increases the surface hardness of SS410 substrates, which makes them appropriate for applications demanding strong wear resistance and mechanical robustness, according to the mechanical analysis41. This combination of hardness and toughness is essential for effective wear resistance, as it allows the coating to withstand both abrasive wear and impact stresses without catastrophic failure.

Improved wear resistance may be obtained from the graded coating structure because of the hard carbide phases and intermediate layer encompassed in the coating. Consequently, the hard surface coating of the top layer gives it abrasion resistance, and when it is loaded, the intermediate layer supports the top coat layer. Using a pin-on-disc tribometer, the wear tests were carried out between the graded coated samples and the uncoated samples. Impact of applied load

For evaluating the influence of load on wear and friction coefficient, the load applied to the sample has been modified while the sliding distance and velocity remain unchanged. The force applied was changed between 5, 10, 15, and 20 N at a constant rate of 5 N, while the distance was determined as 1000 m, and the velocity was set as 1 m/s. The wear rate and friction coefficient were increased gradually with the increments of load applied up to 15 N and a significant increment was observed at 20 N (Fig. 10a, b). Graded-coated samples exhibited 78.51% and 74.62% lower wear rate and friction coefficient respectively than uncoated samples, under the maximum applied load condition of 20 N with 1000 m sliding distance and 1 m/s sliding velocity. It is commonly known that wear resistance and hardness are related. Tougher materials have a lower wear percentage because they are often more resistant to friction and abrasion than softer ones. Studies conducted on graded coated samples revealed that there was a direct correlation between wear rate and hardness. The results of the investigation showed that compared to uncoated samples, graded coating samples worn down more slowly. This is due to the graded coatings’ enhanced hardness, which improved their wear resistance43. The results highlight the importance of hardness in assessing a material’s resistance to wear. The oxide layer’s resistance to abrasion was facilitated by the graded structure’s carbide phases and body-centered cubic (BCC) phase constituents including Cr and Mo. The wear rate of the graded coatings is decreased by these components and attains remarkable resistance to surface delamination and plastic deformation. Once the applied load increased from 5 to 20 N, the resistance to distortion was reduced whereas the built-up stresses were increased. This result implies that graded coatings work well to improve wear resistance by decreasing distortion and strengthening their capacity to bear higher applied stresses42,44.

Impact of sliding distance

With a load of 10 N, the distance was varied between 500, 1000, 1500, and 2000 m, and the sliding velocity was held constant at 1 m/s for the trend experiments on the hard steel disc at room temperature. The wear rate and friction coefficient were increased with increment of sliding distance in uncoated sample; however, a slight decrement was observed beyond the 1500 m distance on coated sample (Fig. 12a, b). Compared to the uncoated sample, the graded coating samples showed a 68.42% and 66.73% of lower wear rate and friction coefficient respectively under the maximum sliding distance condition of 2000 m. The uncoated samples exhibit more material loss, as seen by their increased wear rate. Material loss is higher in uncoated samples due to their finer microstructure and lower rigidity. In this analysis, a significant amount of material loss was observed during the process, which indicates the coated sample exhibited a higher wear rate than the uncoated sample. This significant amount of material loss in the graded coating is due to the presence of tiny voids and microcracks present in the coatings. From the Archard equation, it clearly indicates that the wear rate of a material decreases as its yield strength and hardness increase, respectively45. The study indicated that there was an association between hardness and wear rate, with higher hardness being correlated with lower wear rate. The graded coating was designed with a top layer consisting of “TiC-12Co-10ZrO₂”, which is known for its exceptional hardness. The XRD analysis of the top surface, as shown in Fig. 13, confirmed the presence of TiC, ZrO₂, and Co phases. These hard phases play a crucial role in enhancing the wear resistance of the coating, particularly under conditions of extended sliding distance. While it is true that the cross-sectional micrographs reveal a certain degree of porosity between the coating lamellae, it is important to emphasize that the wear resistance is significantly influenced by the hardness and toughness of the top layer. The TiC and ZrO₂ phases, in particular, contribute to a high degree of wear resistance by providing a robust barrier against material removal during sliding. The Co phase, acting as a binder, also supports this hard matrix, improving the coating’s overall integrity. The observed improvement in wear resistance with increasing sliding distance can be attributed to the hard and dense nature of the top layer, which mitigates the effects of microcracks and voids by distributing the applied load more effectively and resisting surface degradation. This is particularly evident in the graded coating structure, where the gradient in material properties allows for a gradual transition in toughness and hardness, thereby enhancing the overall durability of the coating. This study also highlighted the complex interaction between sliding lengths, hardness, and wear rate level. These complex interactions highlighted the need of taking into account several factors when evaluating a material’s wear resistance. For graded coatings based on TiC composite, the substrate surface required the coatings to adhere to in order for the wear process to occur. When the pin and surface come into contact during the wear process, the interlamellar bonding ratio between the splat creation of the graded coating structure drops. This drop in interlamellar bonding proportion is responsible for the enhanced wear resistance reported at greater sliding distances42,46. Meanwhile, the slight formation of TiO2 and Cr2O3 tribo-oxide film in the graded coatings serves as a protective barrier between the sliding surfaces and reduces direct interaction between the base materials. This layer reduces the occurrence of materials adhering to each other during the sliding condition which helps in minimizing the wear and surface damages. Due to the condition of self-repairing properties, once this formed tribo-layer wears off, as soon as a new oxide film formed continuously which acts as a barrier and supports wear resistance47.

Impact of sliding velocity

Variations in the sliding velocity of the samples were investigated in the study while keeping a constant applied load and distance. Figure 15 depicts the sliding velocities tested, which ranged from 0.5 to 2 m/s in 0.5 m/s steps. The applied load remained constant at 10 N, and the sliding distance was set at 1000 m. While compared to uncoated sample, it was observed that the graded coatings had 76.66% and 74.56% lower wear rate and friction coefficient respectively under the maximum sliding velocity condition of 2 m/s. This indicates that the graded coatings outperformed the uncoated sample in terms of wear resistance, especially at high sliding velocities. According to this analysis, the sliding velocity had a significant impact on the wear behavior of the uncoated samples, which indicates that the wear rate increased as the sliding velocity also increased. This is due to the higher interaction that occurs between the specimen material and the disc surface, which elevates the surface temperature to increase in the disc’s contact region. Thus the wear rate was increased in the uncoated samples due to this increased surface temperature and undergone significant plastic deformation. Conversely, the wear rate of the graded coating samples was decreased due to the increasing range of sliding velocity47. Also, this analysis possesses reduction in the wear range even at the higher trends of sliding velocity conditions. Likewise, this trend analysis emphasizes the intricate correlation between the wear rate, mechanical properties, and trending behavior of sliding velocities with the consideration of crucial factors assessing towards material’s wear resistance. The study’s reduced wear level rate even at greater sliding velocity trends suggests that gradient coatings have the ability to increase a material’s wear resistance48.

Conclusions

The SS410 substrate was coated with a TiC–12Co–10ZrO2/NiCoCrAlMo graded layer using the atmospheric plasma spray technique. The microstructure, hardness, and tribological performances of the graded coating and uncoated samples were analyzed.

In microstructural characterization, the graded coating structure showed distinct interfaces between the layers. The coatings showed significant adherence between the substrate and the main TiC coating layer, with an overall thickness of 450 μm.
