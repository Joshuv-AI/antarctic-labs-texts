Food is the Best Medicine (FBM): design and baseline findings from a multi-arm randomized controlled trial among food-insecure postpartum mothers in Texas
Authors: Aida Nielsen, Alexis Falzone, Christopher Reyes, Elizabeth Polinard, Victoria Threadgould, Nalini Ranjit, Martha Diaz Marin, Alejandra Gonzalez, Maria Tello, Andrea Abel, Joi Chevalier, Alicia Fischweicher, Alexandra van den Berg
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundFood is Medicine (FIM) interventions, which connect health-supporting food programs with healthcare systems, demonstrate substantial clinical and economic benefits. However, few FIM studies have focused on the health of postpartum mothers, a population facing unique vulnerabilities during a critical period. Food is the Best Medicine (FBM) is an 8-week, home-delivery-based FIM intervention targeting postpartum mothers experiencing food insecurity. Developed through partnerships between healthcare providers and community-based organizations, the FBM intervention integrates food delivery with supportive services. Here, we describe the research design and present baseline findings of a study evaluating the impacts of supplementing the FBM intervention with different sustainability-enhancing modalities.MethodsThe Food is the Best Medicine (FBM) study was a three-arm randomized controlled trial conducted in Central Texas. Food-insecure postpartum mothers (n = 150) were randomized to receive: (1) FBM-Standard (8-week food delivery), (2) FBM-CHW (standard delivery plus Community Health Worker (CHW) support), or (3) FBM-Virtual (standard delivery plus online educational platform). Primary planned outcome measures included food security and fruit, vegetables, and whole grain intake. Secondary planned outcome measures included nutrition security, breastfeeding rates, mental health, financial stress, and cooking behaviors. Baseline data were collected via telephone or online surveys prior to randomization.ResultsParticipants were predominantly Hispanic (71%) and Spanish-speaking (79%), with a mean household income of $20,620 (SD = $18,190). Most lived in households with multiple children (74%), in which the number of children and adults ranged from 1 to 15 and 1 to 9, respectively, and had limited formal education (38% without a high school diploma). Marital status was not collected. At baseline, 89% were food insecure. Depression screening indicated mental health concerns in 13% of participants. The 10-item Dietary Screener Questionnaire was used to assess fruit and vegetable intake. Mean fruit and vegetable consumption was 3.9 cups daily, but consumption of whole-grain bread and cooked whole grains was low, at about once a week.ConclusionsThe FBM study uses a multi-component, randomized design to evaluate variations of a FIM intervention among food-insecure postpartum mothers. The intervention incorporates food delivery, CHW support, and virtual education components. Ongoing analyses will assess the potential of these approaches to improve food security and related maternal health outcomes in the postpartum period.Trial RegistrationClinicalTrials.gov NCT06428578 (registered May 20, 2024).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40795-026-01406-0.

Background

Food is Medicine (FIM) interventions, which integrate health-supporting food programs with healthcare systems, demonstrate substantial clinical and economic advantages [1, 2].Previous studies show that medically tailored meals result in a 16% reduction in healthcare costs and a 50% decrease in hospitalizations, while also improving health outcomes for various conditions, including diabetes [3–7]. National models suggest FIM interventions could prevent 1.6 million hospitalizations annually and save $13.6 billion in healthcare expenditures [8]. Although FIM interventions have been widely studied in populations with chronic disease, few have focused on postpartum mothers [9–11].

The postpartum period presents unique challenges for all mothers, but particularly for those experiencing food insecurity (FI), defined as “lack of regular access to enough safe and nutritious food for normal growth and development and an active and healthy life.” [12, 13] Maintaining adequate intake of nutrient-dense foods is often challenging when coping with the physical, mental, and financial demands of caring for an infant, particularly for food-insecure mothers. Compared to food-secure postpartum mothers, food-insecure postpartum mothers have lower-quality diets, reduced breastfeeding rates, higher rates of mental health disorders, and greater weight retention [14, 15]. In communities with high food insecurity, particularly among low-income and ethnic minority populations, postpartum mothers face compounding vulnerabilities, including limited food access, social isolation, and healthcare barriers [16, 17].

Existing FIM programs offer promising solutions, but they also have limitations. Medically tailored meals are convenient but expensive and have limited participant choice, while produce prescriptions are more affordable and flexible but require greater participant effort in preparation. Programs that rely on voucher redemption at retail outlets or food banks may require more effort and time than is affordable for postpartum mothers. In addition, participant engagement remains a consistent challenge across all FIM programs [18, 19].

To address these barriers, partners in Texas co-developed the Food is the Best Medicine (FBM) program. Designed with a human-centered approach, FBM offered a home-delivered model intended to maximize accessibility during the critical postpartum period and was implemented through collaboration with key partners. Community partners included Ascension Seton Medical Center Austin (ASMCA), a non-profit, Level IV maternal high-risk hospital where participant recruitment took place; Farmshare Austin, a nonprofit that sources locally grown produce and prepares weekly produce/pantry boxes; and The Cook's Nook, a local business that prepares and delivers dietitian-certified meals from an USDA-inspected facility. The University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston provided program design, coordination, evaluation, and research oversight.

The FBM program, as initially conceived (FBM-Standard), provides food-insecure postpartum mothers with weekly deliveries of fresh, locally sourced, prepared meals and meal kits, along with $25 worth of fresh produce and shelf-stable foods, for an eight-week period. Although FBM-Standard helps alleviate the burden of meal preparation during a physically and emotionally demanding period, thereby reducing stress related to food access and ensuring short-term food security, it does not include components to ensure long-term behavior change. To address long-term sustainability and behavior change after cessation of food deliveries, the FBM team developed an enhanced version (FBM-CHW). This model incorporates community health workers (CHWs) who conduct home visits to provide educational sessions on food preparation skills, social support, and referrals to community resources. CHWs conduct individualized needs assessments, connect participants to resources such as Supplemental Nutrition Assistance Program (SNAP) or Special Supplemental Nutrition Program for Women, Infants, and Children (WIC), and provide nutrition guidance, recipes, and cooking support on demand. While WIC provides important supplemental nutrition benefits and education for eligible mothers and infants, it does not typically provide home-delivered prepared meals or intensive short-term food support during the immediate postpartum period. The FBM program was designed to complement, rather than replace, existing assistance programs by reducing barriers related to transportation, meal preparation, and food access during postpartum recovery. Recognizing the need for a scalable intervention that could be implemented across hospitals and communities nationally, the team also created a virtual version (FBM-Virtual), with identical educational materials, social support, and community referrals as the CHW version, but through technology-mediated platforms rather than in-person visits. This three-arm study included FBM-Standard with food delivery only, FBM-CHW with CHW support, and FBM-Virtual with online support. It enabled the systematic evaluation of how various support strategies affected the feasibility, engagement, and acceptability of food assistance for food-insecure postpartum mothers. The study also aimed to determine which delivery modalities were most effective in improving both short-term food security and long-term behavioral change while maintaining program feasibility and scalability.

The purpose of this manuscript is to present the design and baseline characteristics of the participants in a three-arm randomized controlled trial comparing the feasibility and impact of different versions of the FBM program on health and well-being outcomes, specifically food security status and diet quality, among low-income, food-insecure postpartum mothers in Texas.

Methods

Study design and setting

The FBM study was a three-arm randomized controlled trial (RCT) conducted in Travis County, Texas. The study was designed to compare three versions of a home food delivery program: FBM-Standard (food delivery only), FBM-CHW (food delivery plus community health worker support), and FBM-Virtual (food delivery plus access to a curated online platform providing nutrition education and community resources). The study protocol was approved by the University of Texas Health Science Center at Houston Institutional Review Board (HSC-SPH-23–0795) and is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (identifier: NCT06428578; registration date: May 20, 2024).

Study population and recruitment

The study population (n= 150) consisted of postpartum mothers residing primarily in Austin's Eastern Crescent, a low-income, majority-Hispanic area shaped by historical social and economic disadvantage and health disparities. In many Eastern Crescent zip codes, 25% of residents live below the federal poverty line with limited food access and SNAP under-enrollment rates of 61–80% [20, 21].

For this study, we included postpartum mothers who delivered a baby within the past 30 days at ASMCA—the county’s safety-net hospital, were between the ages of 18–45 years, were categorized as food insecure using the 6-item US Household Food Security Survey Module, and spoke English or Spanish [22, 23]. Mothers were excluded if they resided outside the 25 designated food-delivery zip codes or had dietary allergies that would preclude participation; participants with diabetes or prediabetes were accommodated with a modified meal plan provided by the food vendor.

Recruitment and randomization

Recruitment involved a two-pronged strategy at ASMCA, a Level IV maternal high-risk hospital that cares for birthing patients with the most complex maternal conditions (Fig. 1). Upon admission to ASMCA, all birthing patients are asked, “In the last 12 months, did you ever eat less than you felt you should because there wasn’t enough money for food?” Social workers and case managers in the perinatal units, including Labor and Delivery and the Neonatal Intensive Care unit, identified mothers who screened positive for food insecurity or required additional support. Eligible participants were provided with standardized program brochures and links to the application. ASMCA perinatal staff securely shared informed consent with the research team via encrypted email, and the enrollment coordinator subsequently conducted follow-up by phone and text to provide study information and promote participant engagement. An additional recruitment strategy involved distributing bilingual flyers with all discharge paperwork and displaying program flyers in staff breakrooms to educate nurses and other staff about the program. To accommodate food-delivery capacity constraints, a rolling recruitment model was employed, enrolling 4 mothers each week until the target of 150 participants was reached. Upon consent confirmation, participants were randomly assigned to study arms using a numbered randomization scheme generated in Python, maintaining a 1:1:1 ratio across the three study arms. Neither the researchers enrolling the participants nor the participants were blinded to the intervention assignment. Recruitment began in January 2024; the last participant completed the program in December 2024, and the last 3-month posttest data were collected in April 2025.

Intervention arms

The FBM-Standard group participated in an eight-week home delivery program, receiving weekly boxes containing six fully prepared culturally traditional meals, four meal kits requiring minimal preparation, $25 worth of shelf-stable foods, and locally grown produce, and recipe cards for the ingredients provided. Participants could customize their weekly boxes through online ordering, selecting from a variety of seasonally available produce and pantry staples (e.g., onions, beets, carrots, leafy greens, potatoes, peppers, tomatoes, apples, oranges, fresh herbs, eggs, cooked beans, oat milk, rice, noodles, etc.); if no customizations were made, a standard box was automatically provided. Boxes were prepared by local partners, Farmshare Austin and The Cook’s Nook, and delivered to participants’ homes every Friday.

The FBM-CHW group received the FBM-Standard intervention, along with two in-home visits and tailored phone calls from a bilingual community health worker (Fig. 2). All participants received a welcome call from the program coordinator upon enrollment and a follow-up check-in call following their first food delivery. The CHW conducted an additional phone-based needs assessment call within the first week of enrollment and scheduled the first home visit for week 2 and the second home visit for week 5, with confirmation calls made at the start of each respective week. If participants were unresponsive or requested to reschedule, additional call attempts were made prior to each visit. The CHW also provided customized support, including resource referrals and connections to health, nutrition, and social services. Referrals included resources such as SNAP, WIC, Medicaid/CHIP, food banks, rent and utility assistance, lactation support, and mental health services, among others.Fig. 2Eight-week intervention timeline and components. The FBM-Virtual group received the FBM-Standard intervention plus access to FoodWays, a bilingual online platform developed specifically for the study. FoodWays was not a structured curriculum but a curated repository of educational and community resources. It provided nutrition-related content, including cooking demonstrations, culturally relevant recipes, and practical nutrition information in both English and Spanish. While not exclusively tailored as a postpartum curriculum, the platform included materials relevant to postpartum nutritional needs alongside general healthy eating guidance. FoodWays also included information on food access resources, such as food bank locations and operating hours, as well as a “Healthy Living” section and a comprehensive community resource directory. The community resources section included referrals and information on employment support, child and infant services, legal aid, health insurance enrollment, food assistance programs (including SNAP and WIC), mental health services, housing support, and internet access. Participants assigned to the FBM-CHW and FBM-Virtual interventions were also provided with access to a private Facebook group, which facilitated peer support and allowed them to share updates about the program.

Data collection

Survey data were collected at baseline, immediately after the intervention (8 weeks after its start), and three months post-intervention, with post-test surveys including the same measures as the baseline survey to allow for direct comparison of outcomes over time. Baseline data were collected upon enrollment, prior to randomization, via either a telephone interview with research staff or an online survey, as preferred by the participant. The baseline survey assessed sociodemographic characteristics, food and nutrition security status, self-rated physical health and mental health, diet quality, dietary practices, home cooking behaviors, breastfeeding rates, and financial stress (Fig. 3) [22–32].Participants received $50 gift cards upon completing each of the three surveys, which were distributed electronically via email.Fig. 3FBM Program logic model. In addition, participants were asked to complete weekly surveys evaluating satisfaction with the food boxes, delivery, ordering process, food box consumption rates, reasons for uneaten food, and meal preferences. Participants who completed at least five of the seven surveys received a $20 gift card.

Primary planned outcomes

Household food security was assessed using the 6-item US Household Food Security Survey Module, a validated instrument designed to measure the level of food insecurity experienced within participants' households [22, 23, 33].The time reference was adjusted from the standard 12-month period to "previous 30 days" to capture more proximate changes. This was consistent with modifications made by other researchers while maintaining the validity of the instrument [34–37]. For example, participants were asked, “In the last 30 days, were you ever hungry but didn’t eat because there wasn’t enough money for food?” The sum of affirmative responses to all six questions determined the household’s raw score.

Diet quality was measured using items from the Dietary Screening Questionnaire (DSQ-10) to estimate intake in cups for fruits, vegetables, and a combined category of fruits and vegetables [38–40]. The DSQ was administered without modification and did not capture detailed information on specific types of fruits and vegetables consumed. Participants were, however, given the opportunity to provide additional detail on foods consumed through an open-ended question at posttest. Diet quality was operationalized as self-reported fruit and vegetable consumption, as HEI scores were not available. Additional items from the DSQ-26 were included to evaluate the frequency of consumption for specific food groups and items, such as whole grains, whole grain bread, and sugar-sweetened beverages. The screener specifically estimated daily intake for fruits, vegetables, salad, beans, fried and non-fried potatoes, lean and plant-based proteins, salsa, pizza, tomato sauce, whole grains, and whole-grain bread [7, 37]. The instrument also assessed the frequency of consumption of sugar-sweetened beverages over the past month. The response options for all questions included: never, 1 time per month, 2–3 times per month, 1 time per week, 2 times per week, 3–4 times per week, 5–6 times per week, 1 time per day, 2 or more times per day. The question pertaining to soda consumption, “During the past month, how often did you drink regular soda or pop that contains sugar?” had two additional response options: 4–5 times per day and 6 or more times per day [41].

Secondary planned outcomes

Nutrition security was assessed using two items adapted from the 4-item Nutrition Security Scale [24]. Participants were asked to rate the extent to which they agreed with the following statements: “We had to eat the same thing for several days in a row because we didn’t have money to buy other food,” and “We had to eat foods that were not good for my health because we couldn't get other food.” Responses used a five-point Likert scale (never = 1, rarely = 2, sometimes = 3, often = 4, always = 5). Scores from the two items were summed to create a total nutrition security score ranging from 2 to 10, with lower scores indicating better nutrition security.

Breastfeeding rates were evaluated using selected questions from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's Infant Feeding Practices Study II Postnatal Questionnaires (Infant Month 1 and Infant Month 2), which capture infant feeding behaviors in early postpartum [25]. Breastfeeding was a prespecified secondary outcome and was included as a distal maternal-infant health indicator, rather than a primary target of the intervention. Participants were asked: Are you breastfeeding? with the response options of: yes, exclusively; yes, breastfeeding and formula; no. For descriptive analyses, responses are presented in these three categories: exclusive breastfeeding, mixed feeding (breastfeeding and formula feeding), and exclusive formula feeding. For inferential analyses, mixed feeding and exclusive formula feeding will be combined into a single non-exclusively breastfeeding category due to the small number of participants exclusively formula feeding to ensure stable estimates. While the survey included an item assessing breastfeeding intent, this variable was excluded from analyses due to variability in postpartum enrollment timing across participants.

Self-reported general health was assessed with the question, “How would you describe your general health?” Responses were recorded on a 5-point Likert scale. For analysis, a binary variable was created by combining “poor” and “fair” into one category, and “good,” “very good,” and “excellent” into another.

Postnatal depression was measured using the Edinburgh Postnatal Depression Scale (EPDS), a validated 10-item screening instrument designed to identify symptoms common in mothers experiencing depression and anxiety during pregnancy and the year following childbirth [26, 42, 43]. Responses are scored on a scale from 0 to 30, with scores greater than 11 indicating potential depression. An example item was “I have felt sad or miserable,” with response options: Yes, most of the time; Yes, quite often; Not very often; No, not at all.

Financial stress was evaluated using three questions from the Financial Anxiety Scale: 1) I feel anxious about my financial situation, 2) I have difficulty sleeping because of my financial situation, and 3) I have difficulty concentrating on my school/or work because of my financial situation [27].Respondents could select from seven options: never, rarely, occasionally, sometimes, frequently, usually, always. " Never" and "rarely" were combined into one category and scored as 0, while "always" was scored as 6. Scores could range from 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating greater financial stress.

Financial strain was assessed using a single-item measure adapted from prior research on economic hardship in adult and low-income populations [28, 29]. Participants were asked, “I have trouble affording what I need each month,” with responses recorded on a five-point Likert scale ranging from 1 (“never”) to 5 (“almost always”). Higher scores indicate greater financial strain.

Home cooking practices were targeted because they are both cost-effective and associated with healthier dietary patterns compared to eating meals away from home [30, 31]. To assess home cooking frequency, items adapted from Wilson et al. asked how often participants or their family members prepared breakfast, lunch, and dinner from scratch during the past week (ex, In the past week, how often did you or anyone else in your family prepare lunch from scratch?) [32]. For analysis, responses from cooking lunch from scratch and cooking dinner from scratch were combined to form a binary home cooking variable: cooking more than 2 major meals or not cooking more than two major meals.

Statistical analysis

The sample size of 150 participants (with planned equal allocation to the three intervention arms) was determined based on a minimum detectable effect size of 0.5 standard deviation units, with a one-sided significance level of 0.05 and power of 80%, for comparison across any two arms. For the analysis of baseline data, we examined distributions of demographic characteristics and outcome measures in the full sample and within study groups, using frequencies to characterize categorical variables and means with standard deviations to describe continuous variables. Responses from selected variables were recoded into broader categories to ensure adequate cell sizes. Baseline comparisons between study arms, assessing the effectiveness of randomization, were calculated using analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. For significant omnibus tests, pairwise comparisons were conducted using Welch-adjusted post hoc tests, which account for unequal variances and sample sizes. All analyses were performed using Stata statistical software.

Breastfeeding rates

Most participants reported combining breastfeeding and formula (58%), while 32% of the mothers reported breastfeeding exclusively. No significant group differences were observed (Table 3).

Self-reported general health

When rating general health, 42% of participants reported "poor" or "fair" while 58% reported "good" or better. No significant differences were detected among the three groups (Table 3).

Postnatal depression

The mean postnatal depression score for all mothers was 5.08 (SD = 4.90), with 13% of participants (n = 19) scoring above the depression threshold (> 11), indicating having postnatal depression. No significant group differences were observed (Table 3).

Financial stress and strain

The mean financial strain score was 3.24 (SD = 1.20), indicating that participants experienced financial strain “sometimes” to “almost always”. The mean financial stress score was 5.07 (SD = 3.76), indicating moderate levels of financial stress. No significant group differences were detected among the three groups (Table 3).

Home cooking practices

Based on self-reported frequency of preparing meals from scratch, participants who cooked more than two major meals per week were classified as “frequent scratch cooks.” Overall, 72% met this criterion, and 93% reported confidence in basic cooking skills. No significant group differences were observed (Table 3).

Most respondents cooked from scratch two to three times per week (22% for breakfast and lunch, 15% for dinner). Only 11–15% reported daily scratch cooking for any meal, while 11–13% never cooked from scratch. The remainder cooked occasionally, typically one to six times per week.

Discussion

Maternal morbidity and mortality rates in the United States remain high compared with other high-income countries, with Texas ranking among the worst states [44–46]. Most maternal deaths occur postpartum, often from preventable causes such as cardiovascular disease and mental health conditions [44–47]. Food insecurity further compounds these risks: postpartum mothers with limited access to nutritious food face greater stress, higher rates of depression, and poorer diet quality, which can undermine recovery, breastfeeding, and long-term cardiovascular health [46, 48–50].These realities highlight the need for postpartum interventions that address both food insecurity and maternal mental health. The present intervention addresses maternal mental health through multiple pathways. Across all arms, the provision of nutritious foods may reduce food insecurity and financial strain, which are key drivers of stress and depressive symptoms in the postpartum period. The CHW arm may offer additional mental health benefits through personalized support, resource navigation, and consistent interpersonal engagement, which can enhance social support and self-efficacy. The virtual arm, while less intensive, still provides ongoing contact and access to resources that may mitigate isolation and stress.

Baseline findings from the FBM study revealed a highly vulnerable cohort of postpartum mothers, primarily Hispanic, Spanish-speaking mothers with limited formal education and low household incomes. Participants experienced substantial food and nutrition insecurity, financial strain, and stress. Randomization generally achieved balance across study arms; however, mothers in the FBM-Virtual group reported lower household incomes and higher levels of nutrition insecurity. These differences will be accounted for in subsequent outcome analyses.

It is notable that participants reported fruit intake consistent with or exceeding dietary recommendations at baseline, while vegetable intake remained below recommended levels. Given that participants were enrolled within 30 days of delivery and dietary recall captured the preceding 30 days, baseline dietary data likely reflect intake during the final weeks of pregnancy rather than the postpartum period. This is consistent with research indicating that women tend to adopt healthier dietary patterns during pregnancy, including higher fruit and vegetable consumption, compared to the postpartum period [51, 52]. Additionally, self-reported dietary intake may be subject to recall and social desirability bias, which could contribute to overestimation of intake. Despite relatively adequate fruit intake, suboptimal vegetable consumption highlights an important target for intervention within this population.

Lastly, the predominantly Hispanic composition of the sample reflects the patient population of ASMCA, a Level IV Maternal Care facility serving a low-income, uninsured/underinsured population, rather than an intentional recruitment target [53]. While explicit cultural tailoring was not a priori component of the intervention, several elements of the program were culturally responsive. Community health workers were bilingual and Hispanic, supporting culturally concordant engagement with participants. Cultural flexibility in food selection was also addressed through the grocery model: rather than receiving pre-selected produce and staple items, participants received weekly grocery credits redeemable through an online grocery platform, allowing them to choose foods aligned with their personal dietary preferences and cultural practices.

Innovation

FBM represented an innovative FIM approach by exclusively targeting postpartum mothers experiencing food insecurity, an understudied, high-priority population highlighted in recent systematic reviews and federal guidance. These reviews emphasized the critical need for FIM interventions among pregnant and postpartum populations, given their increased risk of unmet nutritional needs and the importance of adequate nutrition for maternal and infant health [11, 54]. While several FIM studies have focused on pregnant women, only one (Nourishing Beginnings) has extended into the postpartum period [9]. This gap is particularly concerning given the growing recognition of the "fourth trimester," i.e., the first three months postpartum, as a period of significant physical, emotional, and social transition when mothers face heightened nutritional needs while managing the demands of infant care and recovery from childbirth [55–57].FBM addresses this gap and aligns with recommendations to explore interventions that improve health outcomes, healthcare utilization, and long-term well-being among vulnerable populations.

A key feature of the FBM study was its evaluation of three program versions to compare different modes of support, all of which included weekly home-delivered food boxes containing prepared meals, meal kits, fresh produce, and pantry staple goods. While prior interventions for postpartum mothers have combined food with educational components, few have explicitly tested delivery mode. To our knowledge, only Nourishing Beginningspaired food delivery with CHW support, but it offered less frequent deliveries and did not include a virtual arm [45]. CHWs, as trusted community members with extensive knowledge of local resources, have been shown to improve healthcare utilization and outcomes, particularly among racial and ethnic minorities, and to positively influence diet, physical activity, preventive screenings, and chronic disease management [58–67]. In the FBM-In Person version, CHWs conducted individualized needs assessments, connected participants to resources such as SNAP or WIC, and provided nutrition guidance, recipes, and cooking support on demand. The FBM-Virtual version offered the same support via a bilingual online platform (English and Spanish), allowing for flexible, on-demand engagement. This three-arm design enabled the evaluation of how different support strategies influenced the feasibility, engagement, and acceptability of food for food-insecure postpartum mothers.

Home delivery can address multiple barriers commonly documented in Food Is Medicine programs, particularly for new mothers who face unique postpartum challenges such as physical recovery, sleep disruption, and managing new caregiving responsibilities [61]. These challenges can exacerbate issues like transportation limitations, mobility constraints, costs, lack of vehicle access, low literacy, and limited home cooking resources. Home delivery may also reduce difficulties in accessing farmers’ markets, redeeming vouchers, and obtaining culturally appropriate foods [68]. Redemption rates for FBM (98.7%) far exceed those of non-home-delivered programs (35–40%) [69], largely by eliminating transportation barriers. By delivering nutritious food directly to participants’ homes, FBM helps mitigate postpartum-specific barriers and supports more consistent, healthful dietary practices.

Finally, the intervention was implemented through a cross-sector partnership that combined clinical, community, agricultural, and academic expertise to create a community-centered model addressing both immediate food needs and longer-term resource and behavioral support. This framework could be adapted and scaled across diverse settings.

Strengths and limitations

Key strengths of FBM included its randomized controlled trial design, high recruitment and retention rates, and use of validated outcome measures. The three-arm design enabled direct comparison of support delivery modes within a common platform, providing insights into effectiveness and scalability. In addition, the cross-sector partnership across healthcare, community, and academic institutions fostered a sustainable model to guide future programs.

Several limitations should be noted. Behavioral outcomes may have been influenced by recall and social desirability bias, and recruitment from a single hospital in one county limits the generalizability of the findings to other settings. The study's focus on English and Spanish-speaking participants may not capture the experiences of other linguistic communities. ASMCA birthing patients who were ineligible to participate in FBM spoke languages that included Kinyarwanda, French, Pashto, and Vietnamese. Additionally, the eight-week intervention period, while intensive, may not provide sufficient time to observe longer-term behavior changes or sustained impacts on food security. Although the online platform was mobile-responsive, the virtual arm's reliance on technology access and digital literacy may have created participation barriers for some eligible participants, potentially limiting the representativeness of the findings for the most resource-constrained populations. Formal acculturation measures were not included; race/ethnicity and primary language spoken at home were collected as limited proxies, and future research with predominantly Hispanic samples should incorporate validated acculturation measures to better understand how acculturation may influence intervention outcomes. Finally, marital status and partner social support were not assessed in this study. Given that partner support has been associated with healthier dietary behaviors in the postpartum period, future studies should consider incorporating measures of social support from partners, as this may be an important factor in shaping food security and dietary outcomes among postpartum women [70].

Conclusion

Baseline findings demonstrate successful recruitment of food-insecure postpartum mothers experiencing substantial financial strain, nutrition insecurity, and health challenges. This study was designed to compare food delivery alone (FBM-Standard) with food delivery paired with either CHW or virtual support, both providing resource navigation and nutrition/cooking education on participant demand. This design establishes a pragmatic framework for examining how different support strategies can address immediate and longer-term needs. Importantly, the study focuses on an understudied, high-priority population and has strong potential for scalability.
