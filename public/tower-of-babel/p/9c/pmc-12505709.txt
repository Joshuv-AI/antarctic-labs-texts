The effects of physical exercise on adolescents’ antisocial behavior: the chain-mediated effects of good peer relationships and subjective wellbeing
Authors: Chaochao Wang, Lechen Liu, Min Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study examines the impact of physical exercise on adolescents’ antisocial behaviour, analysing the independent and sequential mediating roles of positive peer relationships and subjective wellbeing to elucidate the underlying mechanisms.MethodsUsing cross-sectional data from 7,272 adolescents, we conducted correlation analysis, OLS regression, and bootstrap-based mediation analysis (PROCESS Macro, Model 6) with 5,000 resamples to examine (1) the direct effect of physical exercise on antisocial behavior and (2) the independent and sequential mediation effects of positive peer relationships and subjective well-being. Stepwise regression and serial mediation modeling (physical exercise → peer relationships → subjective well-being → antisocial behavior) were applied to verify hypotheses, with bias-corrected 95% confidence intervals used to determine significance. All analyses were performed in SPSS 21.0.ResultsPhysical exercise showed a significant negative correlation with antisocial behaviour indicating its preventive effect. Positive peer relationships (mediating effect: 30.85%) and subjective wellbeing (14.89%) served as independent mediators, while also forming a sequential pathway: “physical exercise → peer relationships → subjective wellbeing → antisocial behaviour” (16.49%). The inhibitory effect was more pronounced among boys, only-children, boarding students, those with harmonious parental relationships, state-school attendees, rural students, and those with access to school sports facilities.ConclusionsPhysical exercise directly reduces antisocial behaviour while indirectly mitigating it through enhanced peer relationships and subjective wellbeing. These findings provide a theoretical basis for sports-based behavioural interventions, highlighting the need to integrate social interaction and emotional management strategies.Keywords: Physical exercise, Adolescents, Antisocial behaviour, Peer relationships, Subjective wellbeing

Introduction

Antisocial behaviour, alternatively termed deviant behaviour, refers to actions violating prevailing social norms, including truancy, violence, theft, substance abuse, and self-harm [1]. Adolescence constitutes a critical period for socialisation, during which external stressors and internal conflicts often manifest as emotional volatility, defiance, and confrontational conduct [2]. Substantial evidence links youth antisocial behaviours (e.g., school violence [3], theft [4], substance use [5, 6], and truancy [7]) to increased risks of criminality. Investigating these behaviours thus holds practical significance for prevention and rehabilitation.

Physical exercise is any bodily activity that involves planned, structured, and repetitive skeletal muscle contraction resulting in energy expenditure significantly above resting levels, performed to maintain or improve components of physical fitness or health [8]. Research demonstrates multifaceted benefits of adolescent physical exercise, spanning academic performance [9], interpersonal skills [10], cognitive development [11], mental health [12], self-efficacy [13], and social trust [14]. Scholarly consensus affirms an inverse relationship between sports participation and antisocial behaviour. Hoffmann’s social bond theory analysis of longitudinal educational data revealed positive associations between extracurricular sports and social ties, contrasting with negative links to antisocial conduct [15]. Spruit’s meta-analysis further corroborated physical activity’s protective effect against youth delinquency [16]. Recent evidence also suggests that physical activity may help reduce bullying victimization among children and adolescents, though its relationship with bullying perpetration appears less clear [17]. However, extant studies predominantly focus on Western contexts, leaving mechanisms underexplored. To address this geographical limitation, we analyse China’s national educational panel data, proposing:

H1: Physical exercise negatively correlates with adolescent antisocial behaviour.

H1: Physical exercise negatively correlates with adolescent antisocial behaviour.

Peer relationship theory posits that adolescents’ perceived connections with peers (encompassing acceptance, companionship, and reciprocity [18]) influence behavioural outcomes. Empirical studies associate physical activity with enhanced peer relationships, which foster social approval and self-esteem [19, 20]. Peer relationships refer to voluntary, reciprocal social bonds formed between individuals of similar age or developmental status, characterized by mutual affection, shared activities, and relative equality in interaction [21]. Positive peer ties improve school engagement among antisocial youths [22] and mitigate bullying [23]. Social learning theory further suggests that sports participation reinforces shared behavioural norms—prosocial or antisocial—through peer interactions [24]. Thus, we hypothesise:

H2: Positive peer relationships mediate the exercise-antisocial behaviour association.

H2: Positive peer relationships mediate the exercise-antisocial behaviour association.

Emotion regulation theory posits that individuals engage in behaviors to modulate their emotional experiences, thereby reducing negative affect and enhancing positive affect [25]. According to this theory, effective emotion regulation helps prevent impulsive, aggressive, or antisocial behaviors that may arise from emotional distress [26]. Crucially, subjective wellbeing—a key outcome of successful emotion regulation—is often cited in this framework as an indicator of emotional balance and life satisfaction. Subjective well-being (SWB) reflects individuals’ comprehensive evaluation of their social, familial, and life circumstances [27], encompassing both affective (positive and negative affect) and cognitive (life satisfaction) dimensions [28]. Longitudinal studies link sustained physical activity to elevated wellbeing in elderly populations [29], while leisure-time exercisers report higher life satisfaction than sedentary counterparts [30]. Buecker’s meta-analysis confirms this positive correlation across age groups [31]. Notably, adolescents with higher wellbeing exhibit lower bullying/cyberbullying rates [32], whereas perpetrators and victims alike report diminished wellbeing [33–35]. Consequently, we propose:

H3: Subjective wellbeing mediates the exercise-antisocial behaviour relationship.

H3: Subjective wellbeing mediates the exercise-antisocial behaviour relationship.

Self-determination theory (SDT) posits that the fulfilment of innate basic psychological needs – particularly autonomy, competence, and relatedness – is fundamental to human motivation, development, and wellbeing [36]. Within this framework, relatedness refers to the fundamental need to feel connected to, cared for by, and significant to others. Emerging evidence suggests peer relationships amplify wellbeing; for instance, school-based social ties reduce loneliness/anxiety among migrant youths [37]. Structural equation modelling confirms peer relationships’ partial mediation between exercise and wellbeing [38]. We therefore integrate these insights to propose a sequential mediation model grounded in SDT’s need satisfaction pathway. Within this framework: (1) Exercise serves as the initiating catalyst, often occurring in social contexts; (2) Peer relationships function as the primary social conduit, satisfying the fundamental psychological need for relatedness; (3) The fulfilment of this need through positive peer bonds subsequently elevates SWB; and (4) Enhanced SWB, characterized by greater positive affect, life satisfaction, and emotional stability, is theorized to reduce the propensity for antisocial behaviour. We therefore advance:

H4: Peer relationships and subjective wellbeing sequentially mediate exercise’s impact on antisocial behaviour.

H4: Peer relationships and subjective wellbeing sequentially mediate exercise’s impact on antisocial behaviour.

Methods and materials

Data sources

The China Education Panel Survey (CEPS), designed and implemented by the National Survey Research Center (NSRC) at Renmin University of China, is a nationally representative large-scale longitudinal survey project. Using a multi-stage stratified sampling approach, the study selected 438 classes from 112 schools across 28 county-level units in China, covering approximately 20,000 adolescents along with their parents, teachers, and school administrators. The CEPS data adhere to a probability sampling principle featuring stratification, multi-stage design, and probability proportional to size (PPS), ensuring national representativeness. To enhance data reliability and minimize respondent burden, the survey employs culturally adapted and psychometrically validated short-form scales for assessing key variables such as physical exercise and antisocial behavior. These scales were derived from established instruments and rigorously pretested in the Chinese adolescent population to ensure linguistic appropriateness, conceptual equivalence, and measurement validity within the local context. The survey encompasses variables such as students’ physical exercise, antisocial behavior, as well as family background and school characteristics that May influence antisocial behavior. After excluding samples with unsuccessful follow-ups or missing core variables, a total of 7,272 valid observations were retained for analysis.

Variable operationalisation

Dependent variable

The dependent variable in this study measures antisocial behaviour, constructed from responses to ten behavioural items in the CEPS questionnaire [40]. Participants were asked to report the frequency of specific behaviours during the preceding year, including: “swearing”, “quarrelling”, “fighting”, “bullying weaker classmates”, “short-tempered”, “inattentive”, “skipping classes, absenteeism, truancy”, “copying homework, cheating on exams”, “smoking, drinking”, “going to Internet cafes, game halls”. Each item was measured using a 5-point Likert scale ranging from “never” (1) to “always” (5). The composite score (range: 10–50) was derived by summing responses across all items, with higher values indicating more frequent antisocial behaviours.

Mediating variables

(1) Subjective wellbeing

As the CEPS questionnaire lacked direct measures of subjective wellbeing, we constructed a proxy using four affective states reported over the previous week: “depression”, “unhappiness”, “perceived”, “meaninglessness”, “sadness” [3]. Responses (“never” to “always”) were reverse-coded (5 − 1) and summed to create a composite score (range: 4–20), where higher scores indicate better subjective wellbeing (i.e., lower negative affect). This operationalisation is supported by established evidence demonstrating the inverse relationship between wellbeing and negative emotions [42].

(2) Peer relationships

Peer relationships were assessed using four questionnaire items measuring: “peer friendliness”, “positive class ethos”, “participation in school activities”, “school belonging” [43]. Responses ranged from “strongly disagree” (1) to “strongly agree” (4). The composite score (range: 4–16) was created by summing responses, with higher values indicating better peer relationships.

Control variables

The control variables in this study were selected from the CEPS “student questionnaire”, “parent questionnaire” and “principal questionnaire” in three dimensions: individual level, family level and school level. The variables were controlled. The basic characteristics of individuals included gender, only-child status, and whether or not they were boarding; the control variables at the family level were mainly the family’s economic status, household registration type, parents’ highest education level, parents’ educational expectations, and parents’ relationship. The control variables selected at the school level were school nature, school location, and school sports facilities (Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Data were organized and statistically analyzed using SPSS 21.0. The effects of physical exercise on adolescents’ antisocial behavior were examined using ordinary least squares (OLS) regression. To test the chain-mediated effects of good peer relationships and subjective well-being, we employed Model 6 (serial multiple mediation) in the PROCESS Macro with 5,000 bootstrap samples. This approach allowed us to assess both the direct effect of physical exercise on antisocial behavior and its indirect effects through the proposed mediators.

Most families were middle-income, with rural household registration type being slightly more common. Parental education levels were generally low, but family relationships were predominantly positive. Schools were overwhelmingly public and located in non-rural areas (Table 2).

The results show statistically significant (p < 0.01) negative associations between physical exercise and antisocial behavior across all models. Each one-standard-deviation increase in physical exercise corresponded to a 0.061-standard-deviation decrease in antisocial behavior (supporting Hypothesis 1). Model stability was evidenced by: (1) consistent coefficient magnitudes and significance levels across specifications, and (2) progressively increasing R² values with added controls and mediators.

Both positive peer relationships and subjective well-being exhibited significant negative associations with antisocial behavior (p < 0.01). A one-standard-deviation increase in positive peer relationships was associated with a 0.181-standard-deviation decrease in antisocial behavior, while a similar increase in subjective well-being was associated with a 0.311-standard-deviation decrease.

Among the control variables, gender, only-child status, boarding school attendance, parental expectations, parental relationship quality, school type (public/private), school location (urban/rural), and school sports facilities showed statistically significant associations with antisocial behavior. Specifically, boys, only children, boarding students, those with higher parental expectations, those from families with better parental relationships, those attending public or rural schools, and those with access to better sports facilities displayed lower levels of antisocial behavior. The remaining control variables did not reach statistical significance.

Discussions

This study examined the impact of physical exercise on adolescent antisocial behaviour through a survey of 7,272 adolescents, identifying a sequential mediation pathway involving positive peer relationships and subjective wellbeing. The results support hypotheses 1–4, consistent with prior research [15, 16]. Unlike previous studies, however, this research integrates both peer relationships and subjective wellbeing as sequential mediators in structural equation modelling, advancing the understanding of how physical exercise reduces antisocial behaviour.

Association between physical exercise and antisocial behaviour

The study reveals a significant negative association between physical exercise and adolescent antisocial behavior, with each standard deviation increase in exercise duration corresponding to a 0.061 standard deviation decrease in antisocial conduct. This aligns with existing research [44] showing that adolescents with antisocial tendencies often exhibit poor self-control, social withdrawal, impulsivity, and anxiety. Recent studies further highlight the broader mental health benefits of physical activity, demonstrating its role in mitigating anxiety, depression, and internet addiction—factors often comorbid with antisocial behavior [45]. For instance, physical activity has been shown to reduce sleep disorders in adolescents through its negative effects on anxiety and mobile phone dependence [46], and to alleviate internet addiction by improving inhibitory control and reducing anxiety [47]. These findings suggest that the antisocial behavior-reducing effects of exercise may be partially mediated by its positive impact on mental health and self-regulation. Team-based physical activities appear particularly effective in reducing such behaviors by fostering teamwork, social interaction, and psychological resilience [48].

This study advances current understanding by addressing key limitations in prior research. While previous work [49] found that aggressive sports participation increased psychological and physical aggression in specific groups (e.g., male university students), our comprehensive analysis of a broader adolescent population revealed an overall inhibitory effect of physical exercise on antisocial behavior, accounting for individual, familial, and school-level factors.

The benefits were particularly strong among boys, only-children, boarding students, those with higher parental expectations or positive parent-child relationships, and adolescents in rural state schools with adequate sports facilities. These subgroup findings not only confirm the general benefits of physical exercise but also demonstrate important population-specific variations, offering valuable insights for tailored intervention strategies.

Mediating effects of physical exercise on antisocial behaviour

This study demonstrates that positive peer relationships mediate the link between physical exercise and reduced antisocial behavior in adolescents. Research consistently shows that physical activity enhances peer relationships [50, 51], with inactive adolescents facing greater social exclusion risks. Regular participation in structured physical activities creates supportive environments that strengthen peer bonds through improved communication and cooperation [52]. The relationship is reciprocal—strong peer connections, marked by friendship and social support, also sustain exercise participation [53, 54]. Importantly, positive peer relationships significantly reduce antisocial behavior, consistent with evidence linking them to lower risks of bullying [55], truancy [56], and self-harm [57].

This study further establishes subjective wellbeing as a key mediator between physical exercise and reduced antisocial behavior. Robust evidence confirms that physical activity significantly enhances subjective wellbeing [31, 58, 59], improving both physical health and psychological states. Exercise-induced β-endorphin release promotes emotional stability and positive affect [60], while higher subjective wellbeing predicts better emotional regulation ([61] and adaptive coping strategies [62].

The wellbeing-antisocial behavior connection is well-documented: individuals with greater subjective wellbeing show lower rates of externalizing behaviors [32–34] and demonstrate stronger empathy [63] and social responsibility [64]. These findings suggest that exercise-based interventions should incorporate emotional regulation and social-emotional skill development to maximize their anti-aggression effects.

The serial mediating role of positive peer relationships and subjective wellbeing

This study reveals a sequential mediation pathway where physical exercise reduces antisocial behavior by first improving peer relationships, which then enhance subjective wellbeing. Physical activity creates structured social opportunities that strengthen peer bonds through teamwork and shared experiences [65, 66]. These improved relationships satisfy fundamental social needs [67], subsequently boosting wellbeing [31, 58] and creating a protective cycle against antisocial tendencies.

The identified serial pathway integrates social support theory with positive psychology perspectives. Existing evidence indicates that peer support buffers stress-related mental health risks [68], while individuals with higher subjective wellbeing are more likely to maintain positive social relationships [69], creating a mutually reinforcing dynamic. Notably, this chained mediation effect may be particularly pronounced among boarding students and rural adolescents, for whom physical activity assumes greater importance in facilitating peer interaction and emotional wellbeing due to their relatively constrained social environments [70].

Strengths: (1) Multipath mechanistic exploration: As the first study to examine peer relationships and subjective wellbeing as serial mediators, we identify a sequential pathway: physical exercise → peer relationships → subjective wellbeing → antisocial behaviour. This extends beyond single-mediator models, providing a more holistic understanding of physical activity’s psychosocial effects. (2) Population heterogeneity analysis: Stratified analyses revealed stronger antisocial behaviour reduction in boys, only-children, and boarding students, while highlighting moderating roles of familial (e.g., parental expectations, parent-child relationships) and school-level (e.g., rural state schools, sports facilities) factors. These insights support targeted interventions. (3) Theoretical integration: By combining social support theory (peer relationships) with positive psychology (subjective wellbeing), we clarify dual mechanisms—social interaction and emotional regulation—through which physical activity reduces antisocial behaviour, advancing understanding of “social-psychological-behavioural” linkages. Limitations: (1) Cross-sectional design: The data reveal associations but not causality. For example, adolescents with lower antisocial tendencies may self-select into physical activity. Longitudinal or experimental designs are needed to establish temporal relationships. (2) Unmeasured confounders: Potential influences such as genetic predispositions, neurocognitive functions (e.g., impulse control), or school disciplinary climates were not assessed.

Conclusions

This study, through a large-scale survey, has validated the inhibitory effect of physical exercise on adolescent antisocial behaviour while elucidating the serial mediation pathway in which positive peer relationships enhance subjective wellbeing, which in turn further reduces antisocial tendencies. The findings not only corroborate the beneficial effects of physical activity in promoting mental health and reducing problematic behaviours, but also highlight the synergistic mechanisms involving both social and emotional factors. Specifically, physical exercise fosters healthier peer interactions, which contribute to greater emotional wellbeing, ultimately leading to a decrease in antisocial behaviour—a sequential process that underscores the interconnected roles of social and psychological factors.

Notwithstanding the limitations inherent in its cross-sectional design and measurement methodology, the research provides empirical evidence to inform school-based interventions, family education strategies, and policy-making decisions. Future investigations should employ more rigorous experimental designs and interdisciplinary approaches to refine intervention strategies, thereby maximising the psychosocial benefits derived from physical exercise.
