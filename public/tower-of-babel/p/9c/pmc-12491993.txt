Relationship Between the Socio‐Demographic Index and the Burden of Depressive Disorders in Asia (2010–2021): A Population Based Study
Authors: Azam Ardeh, Jamile Shahverdi, Ali Ghiasi, Elham Goodarzi, Faezeh Zamani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsDepressive disorders represent a prevalent mental health challenge, posing a substantial threat to individuals' well‐being. This study aimed to explore the burden of depressive disorders in Asia from 2010 to 2021.MethodsThis study leverages population‐based data from the Global Burden of Disease Study in 2021. Metrics such as prevalence, incidence, and Disability‐Adjusted Life Years (DALYs) per 100,000 people were calculated using age‐standardized rates (ASR) with 95% uncertainty intervals (UI). The study also investigated the association between Socio‐Demographic Index (SDI) and the burden of depressive disorders using Pearson correlation analysis.ResultsIn 2021, Palestine exhibited the highest Age‐Standardized Incidence Rate(ASIR) (9361.0 per 100,000), Age‐Specific Prevalence Rate (ASPR) (7348.5 per 100,000), and Age‐Standardized DALYs Rate (ASDR) (1375.1 per 100,000). The highest percentage increase in 2010–2021 for ASIR (47%) and for ASDR (31%) was related to Myanmar, and ASPR (27%) was related to Tunisia. Notably, across all age groups, ASDR, ASIR, and ASPR were consistently higher in women compared to men. The results of Pearson analysis showed that there was a negative correlation between ASPR (r = −0.228), ASDR (r = −0.232), and ASIR (r = −0.227) indices with the SDI index, but this correlation was not statistically significant(p > 0.05).ConclusionThe findings highlight a disproportionately high burden of depressive disorders in South Asia, particularly in countries like Palestine and Iraq, where rising trends in DALYs and prevalence demand urgent attention. The significant negative correlation between ASPR and SDI suggests that lower socioeconomic development may exacerbate depressive disorders, even as higher‐SDI countries face their own mental health challenges.Keywords: Asia, burden, depressive disorders, temporal trend

Background

Depression stands as a substantial public health concern, exerting a detrimental impact across various dimensions of individuals' lives [1]. Globally, it ranks as the fourth leading contributor to the burden of diseases and, in 2020, is positioned as the second major disease [2].

According to the Global Burden of Diseases (GBD) study, the global incidence rate of depression reached 3588.2 per 100,000 people in 2019, signifying a substantial burden on a global scale [3], and significant variations in the prevalence of depression disorders are observed across countries [4].

Depressive disorders account for 37.1% of the burden of mental disorders in Asia, according to a study that analyzed data from 1990 to 2019 [5].

Although the age‐standardized prevalence and disability‐adjusted life years (DALY) rates in Asia have remained relatively stable over the past 30 years, the number of prevalences and DALYs have increased by 47.0% and 57.5%, respectively, which may be due to demographic changes, population growth, as well as aging [5, 6].

Previous major depressive disorder (MDD) studies reported that the proportion of people with MDD in South Asian countries was among the highest globally [7, 8]. A recent survey from India found the prevalence of MDD to be approximately 3.0% [9], previous studies reported higher figures in Pakistan [10], Bhutan [11], and Bangladesh [12] respectively.

Global Mental Health (WMH) findings highlight variations in depression prevalence among countries with low and middle income, reporting rates of 7.2%, 14.6%, 3.5%, and 3.3% in Iraq, Ukraine, China, and Nigeria, respectively [13, 14, 15, 16]. In contrast, countries with high economic levels exhibit rates of 10.5 in Spain, 6.7 in Japan, and 16.2 in America [17, 18, 19].

Subsequent to the occurrence of depression, individuals often grapple with a cascade of adverse events, including disruptions in work, education, family and marital relationships, as well as an increased susceptibility to drug abuse, self‐harm, and suicide. Recognizing these ramifications, policymakers play a pivotal role in fostering awareness of their country's mental health landscape and implementing crucial steps to enhance the healthcare system. Tailoring interventions to promote mental health necessitates a comprehensive understanding of the variations in mental illnesses across countries [20, 21, 22, 23].

While extensive studies have explored the prevalence and contributing factors of depression, there remains a dearth of investigations into the similarities and differences between different countries and depression, critical gaps remain [24]. First, most evidence derives from Western contexts, neglecting Asia's diverse socio‐demographic index (SDI) spectrum. Second, longitudinal analyses of SDI‐depression dynamics are scarce, despite Asia's rapid socioeconomic shifts (2010–2019). Third, gender disparities in depression burden within similar SDI tiers are understudied. Our study addresses these gaps by examining SDI‐depression relationships across three key metrics (DALYs, incidence, prevalence) while disaggregating data by sex and age. Thus, this study aims to delineate the epidemiological characteristics and burden of depression disorders in Asian countries.

Methodology

Data Sources and Study Design

The present study is a population‐based study. Our study utilizes data from the GBD 2020, which employs rigorous methods to address missing data and ensure cross‐country reliability. Data quality is ensured through standardized age‐adjusted rates (ASRs), 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), and cross‐validation against national health registries and WHO databases. GBD's Bayesian regression models further minimize bias from methodological heterogeneity across (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/gbd-results-tool). In this study, the burden of depression in the Asian countries was extracted from year 2010 to 2019 by gender and age groups(< 20, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44, 45–49, 50–54, 55–59, 60–64, 65–69, 70–74, 75–79, 80+ ).

Global Burden of Disease 2021 (GBD 2021)

The GBD 2021 data set encompasses annual incidence, DALY, age‐standardized incidence rate (ASIR), age‐standardized DALY rate (ASDR), and age‐specific incidence, stratified by sex, age, country, and territory [25]. The Global Burden of Disease 2021 (GBD‐2021) has calculated mortality rates, Years of Life Lost (YLL), Years Lived with Disability (YLD) and DALY for 23 age groups in men and women for 204 countries between 1990 and 2021 [26]. The detailed estimation framework for GBD 2021 has already been discussed [26, 27, 28].

Age‐Standardized DALYs Rate (ASDR), Age‐Standardized Incidence Rate (ASIR) and Age‐Standardized Prevalence Rate (ASPR)

This analysis used three measures of disease burden: incidence, prevalence, and DALYs. Incidence of a disease refers to the number of new cases of the disease in a given population in a given period of time. Prevalence refers to the number of cases of a disease in a population at a given point in time. DALY combines the impact of premature death and disability on a person's quality of life and is calculated as the sum of years of life lost due to premature death and years of healthy life lost due to disability. A DALY represents the loss of 1 year of full health.

Understanding the overall burden of disease on a population was calculated by dividing the number of new or existing cases or years lost by the population size. Age‐standardized estimates were used to adjust for differences in the age distribution of different populations to a standardized global reference population, which allowed comparisons of disease burden across populations [29, 30].

Geographic Units

In the GBD 2019 study, incidence, prevalence, mortality, and DALY in 204 countries and 21 regions and 7 superregions were grouped based on epidemiological similarity and geographic proximity, including all WHO member countries [31, 32]. The 7 super regions were (1) Southeast Asia, East Asia, and Oceania; (2) Sub‐Saharan Africa; (3) South Asia; (4) Latin America and the Caribbean; (5) North Africa and the Middle East; (6) Central Europe, Eastern Europe, and Central Asia; and (7) high income (including Australasia, high‐income Asia Pacific, high‐income North America, Southern Latin America, and Western Europe) [33, 34]. In this study, data related to depressive disorders in the regions of the Asian continent (East Asia, South Asia, Southeast Asia, Central Asia) were extracted and analyzed.

Depressive Disorders

For the purpose of this study, depressive disorders were defined based on the criteria outlined in the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders‐IV (DSM‐IV) and the International Classification of Diseases 10 (ICD‐10) [25]. In GBD 2021, depressive disorders encompassed Major Depressive Disorder (MDD): Major Depression Disorder (DSM‐IV‐TR: 296.21‐24, 296.31‐34; ICD‐10: F32.0‐9, F33.0‐9) and dysthymia (DSM‐IV‐TR: 300.4, ICD‐10: F34) [25].

Social Demographic Index

The Socio‐demographic Index (SDI) is a composite measure that integrates three key development dimensions: (1) lag‐distributed per capita income, (2) average educational attainment for individuals aged 15+ , and (3) total fertility rate among women under 25 [3]. These components were selected as they collectively capture a nation's human capital development and economic productivity potential. The SDI value ranges from 0 (lowest development) to 1 (highest development), with the global distribution showing a near‐normal distribution pattern.

For analytical purposes, countries were classified into five SDI quintiles based on established GBD classification thresholds that correspond to natural breaks in the global development spectrum:

High SDI (> 0.81): Representing the top 20% of nations with advanced economies and human capital.

Medium‐high SDI (0.70–0.81): Upper‐middle income countries with strong development indicators.

Medium SDI (0.61–0.69): Middle‐income countries in transitional development phases

Medium‐low SDI (0.46–0.60): Lower‐middle income countries with developing economies

Low SDI (< 0.46): The 20% least developed nations. These cut‐off points were determined through an iterative process in the GBD study.

Statistical Analysis

The Pearson correlation method was used to analyze the extracted data to check the correlation between the ASIR, ASPR and ASDR of depressive disorders and SDI. The significance level was p < 0.05. Analysis was performed using Stata software version 12 (Stata Corp, College Station, TX, USA).

Discussion

The results of the present study showed that South Asia has the highest incidence, prevalence, and burden of depressive disorders in Asia.

In 2016, the standardized prevalence of depressive disorders in South Asia was estimated to be 3.9%, which varied among countries, with Bangladesh: 4.4%, India: 3.9%, Pakistan: 3.0%, Nepal: 4.0%, and Bhutan: 3.7% [35].

South Asian countries have very low human resources for mental health, which affects the diagnosis, treatment and prevention of mental disorders [36, 37].

Furthermore, mental health is not well recognized as a health policy priority in those countries. Many Asian countries do not have effective policies, laws, strategic plans, programs and other measures to address mental health problems in their communities [37, 38].

In 2021, Palestine exhibited the highest ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR. The challenging conditions in Palestine, marked by civil unrest, displacement, political conflicts, and limited resources, contribute to immense stress and psychological pressure [39].

Furthermore, the study revealed that to Myanmar experienced the highest increases in ASIR and ASDR during the period from 2010 to 2021 which highlights the need for further research to comprehend the underlying causes of the rising prevalence, incidence, and DALY associated with depressive disorders.

The present study significant variations in the prevalence of depressive disorders among different countries, revealing higher rates in certain regions, particularly during the period from 2010 to 2021. This variation emphasizes the importance of identifying the underlying factors contributing to such differences. Potential explanations for these regional disparities include variations in cultural diversity and the implementation of intervention policies tailored to specific regions.

Our study showed that the prevalence and incidence of depressive disorders in men and women from 2010 to 2019 had a nearly constant trend, and an increase in the prevalence and incidence of depressive disorders was observed since 2019. The results of the study by Kim et al. showed that the incidence of anxiety disorders and depression after the COVID‐19 pandemic (2020–2021) was more severe than before the COVID‐19 pandemic (2018–2019) [40]. Therefore, it can be said that the COVID‐19 pandemic from 2019 onwards could be one of the reasons for the sudden increase in the prevalence and incidence of depressive disorders in this study.

Analyzing data from 2021, the study found that across all age groups, ASDR, ASIR, and ASPR were higher in women than in men. Studies have shown that women and the elderly suffer more from depression disorders [41]. Previous studies have also shown that the risk and burden of depression is higher in women compared to men [42, 43].

Existing evidence suggests that women may be more prone to depressive symptoms due to factors such as passive reactions to traumatic events, a higher likelihood of internalizing stress, and various biological, psychological, and environmental factors [44, 45].

Kader Maideen et al. [46, 47] showed that women consistently reported higher stress levels than men, making them 4.9 times more likely to experience depression. Moreover, women subjected to domestic violence exhibited a 7.09 times higher likelihood of depression compared to those without such experiences. Consequently, individuals facing violence in societies characterized by high population density and increased economic pressure tend to have less social support, leading to an elevation in their depression levels [48].

Hormonal fluctuations (e.g., premenstrual, postpartum, menopause) modulate serotonin systems and stress response, increasing vulnerability [49]. Gender roles in many Asian cultures disproportionately expose women to chronic stressors (caregiving burdens, domestic violence) while restricting coping resources [50].

The study results showed that the prevalence and incidence of depressive disorders are higher in older age.

Many studies on depressive disorders have shown that factors, including chronic illness, lower physical function, obesity, and cognitive decline, predict the risk of developing depressive symptoms [51, 52]. Social factors, including age and gender, are associated with depressive symptoms [52]. Previous research has also shown that greater participation in social activities can protect against depression by stimulating various body systems and strengthening attachment patterns throughout life, given that a person may have fewer social interactions with age, and the basis for depression in a person may increase [53, 54].

In our study, we observed a negative correlation between the prevalence, incidence, and burden of depressive disorders and the SDI. This finding aligns with previous research [3, 55]. For example, social and demographic factors such as reduced financial constraints, improved employment status [56], and higher Gross Domestic Product [57] have been associated with a decrease in the incidence of mental disorders, including depression.

The social and economic situation of the countries of the Asian continent, especially South Asia, significantly affects the prevalence of depression disorders through different mechanisms. High levels of poverty in South Asia are closely related to increased rates of depression. Economic problems limit access to basic needs, health care, and mental health services and lead to increased mental distress. Countries with higher rates of poverty tend to show higher prevalence rates of depressive disorders, as seen in data from the Global Burden of Disease Study, which shows different rates of depressive disorders in South Asian countries. Unemployment and job insecurity contribute to feelings of hopelessness and anxiety, which can exacerbate depressive symptoms [35, 58, 59].

The observed negative correlation between SDI and depression burden underscores the role of socioeconomic development in mental health promotion. While higher SDI nations benefit from stronger healthcare systems and social protections, low‐SDI countries face compounded risks from poverty, conflict, and weak infrastructure. However, the nonlinear relationship in some high‐SDI settings (e.g., East Asia's “depression paradox”) suggests that economic growth alone is insufficient without targeted mental health policies. Future interventions should address both material deprivation and psychosocial stressors [60, 61].

Several limitations should be considered in interpreting these results. The applicability of DSM‐IV and ICD‐10 criteria for depressive disorders may vary across cultural groups, as individuals may be reluctant to disclose mental health concerns due to stigma and discrimination. Additionally, the study lacks information about other risk factors, and the GBD study does not calculate the impact of depressive disorders on productivity, economic efficiency, and social‐emotional interaction. The lack of assessment of YLLs poses challenges in fully estimating health loss, given that they are not coded as causes of death in the GBD study based on ICD‐10 criteria. Additionally, indirect mortality attributable to depressive disorders, such as suicide, is not considered. The analysis in this study is correlational and does not establish causal relationships between the SDI and the burden of depressive disorders due to the observational and cross‐sectional nature of the data. The findings of this study rely entirely on secondary data from the 2019 GBD study, which has inherent limitations, including potential gaps in access to primary data—particularly in low‐income or conflict‐affected regions of Asia. Variations in mental health data reporting and diagnostic practices across countries may also impact comparability. Future research using longitudinal designs or primary data collection could help address these limitations and provide deeper insights into causal mechanisms.

Conclusion

The study reveals significant differences in the burden of depressive disorders across Asian countries, with Palestine showing the highest age‐standardized rates. These findings call for targeted and urgent policy action: governments in high‐burden regions should prioritize mental health budgets to expand accessible services, especially in conflict zones. Countries with low SDI should integrate depression screening into primary health care. Simultaneously, nationwide anti‐stigma campaigns and regional collaborations to share resources should be implemented. Such targeted SDI interventions, coupled with strong political commitment, are essential to address Asia's growing mental health challenges. Also, this study used Pearson correlation to examine the relationship and did not control for the effect of confounding variables. Future studies could use more advanced models such as multivariate regression to examine these relationships more closely.
