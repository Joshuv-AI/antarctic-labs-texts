Global burden trends of idiopathic epilepsy in young adults from 1990 to 2035: based on the global burden of disease study 2021
Authors: Yan Zhao, Yi Li, Mei Li, Yun Xu, Jiahui Zhang, Shuwei Qiu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundEpilepsy is a prevalent neurological condition that greatly affects the quality of life of those affected, with increasing rates of incidence and prevalence. This study aims to evaluate the global, regional, and national burdens of idiopathic epilepsy (IE) in individuals aged 15 to 49 from 1990 to 2021 via data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) Study and to project trends through 2035.MethodsUsing the GBD database 2021, we assessed the burden of IE through metrics involving incidence, prevalence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs). Statistical methods included joinpoint regression for annual percent change (APC), estimated annual percentage change (EAPC), and Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) modeling for projections.ResultsIn 2021, there were 11.48 million (95% UI, 8.35–14.75) young adults with IE, with global incidence, prevalence, mortality and DALY rates of 37.57 (95% UI, 26.28–50.70), 290.83 (211.42-373.58), 1.64 (1.38–1.83) and 185.09 (144.06–230.00) per 100,000 people, respectively. From 1990 to 2021, incidence and prevalence rates increased by 8.51% and 5.7%, respectively, but these increases were not statistically significant. In contrast, mortality and DALY rates significantly decreased by 15.42% and 12.67%, respectively. The 15–19 age group consistently had the highest incidence and prevalence rates, which declined with age. Regionally, high sociodemographic index (SDI) areas consistently presented the highest incidence and prevalence rates, whereas low-SDI regions always exhibited the highest mortality and DALY rates. Besides, males presented higher mortality rates than females. Forecasts for 2035 suggested that the global incidence and prevalence rates of IE would keep rising, whereas mortality and DALY rates were projected to decline.ConclusionThe global burden of IE among young adults remains substantial. To effectively address the evolving global impact of this neurological disorder, it is crucial to prioritize policies and allocate resources accordingly. This will enable the implementation of targeted interventions aimed at improving healthcare access, promoting early diagnosis, and providing treatment in low-resource areas, ultimately reducing future burdens.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-025-04431-x.

Introduction

Epilepsy is a prevalent neurological disorder characterized by recurrent seizures that can greatly affect the quality of life of those who suffer from it. Epilepsy patients face substantial social, vocational, physical, and psychosocial challenges due to premature death and disability, impacting both health systems and affected individuals and their families [1–3]. Its incidence has a bimodal distribution, with the highest risk found in infants and elderly individuals [4].

In the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) 2021, overall epilepsy was categorized into idiopathic epilepsy(IE) and secondary epilepsy(SE) [5]. Idiopathic epilepsy is defined to have an unknown cause with a genetic nature, while secondary epilepsy usually attributes to a known cause of the abnormality of the brain structure or chemistry [5]. In 2021, it was estimated that there were 24 million people suffering from idiopathic epilepsy and 28 million from secondary epilepsy, with the majority burden (> 80%) residing in low-income to middle-income countries [5]. Therefore, the GBD study offers a thorough framework for analyzing the epidemiological patterns associated with idiopathic epilepsy, enabling a detailed assessment of its impact across different populations.

To date, several extensive global studies have documented the epidemiology of idiopathic epilepsy at the global, regional and national levels, with a focus on trends in incidence, prevalence, mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) across all age groups [5–7]. These studies indicated that children aged 0–14 age group had the highest age-standardized incidence rate of epilepsy, whereas individuals over 70 years presented the highest age-standardized prevalence rate [6]. Research from the GBD database concerning the Chinese population revealed that the 15–49 age group had the highest number of deaths and DALYs compared with the other age groups [8]. The population aged 15–49 is the core working age group that is most actively involved in social and economic activities globally. Once they suffer from epilepsy, it will bring a heavy burden to their families and society. To date, however, no studies have comprehensively analyzed and projected IE trends in this age group at global and regional levels. Herein, utilizing the GBD database, this study aims to investigate the global, regional and national burdens of IE in the population aged 15–49 from 1990 to 2021 and forecast the future trend up to 2035.

Methods

Overview and data sources

This study used the 2021 GBD dataset, which provides extensive data on the global and regional burdens of 369 diseases and injuries, along with 88 risk factors across 204 countries and regions from 1990 to 2021. Information regarding idiopathic epilepsy in individuals aged 15–49 was obtained through the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) query tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/), which covers 204 countries and territories. The global IE burden in this age group was assessed on the basis of incidence, prevalence, mortality and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), with stratification by sex, age, region and sociodemographic index (SDI). DALYs are calculated by summing years of life lost (YLLs) due to premature death and years lived with disability (YLD) [9]. The SDI is categorized into quintiles on the basis of the geometric means of per capita income, total fertility rate, and average educational attainment, reflecting a country’s overall social and economic development [10]. The SDI values range from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating greater socioeconomic development. The GBD database classifies countries and regions into five categories on the basis of their SDIs: low, low-middle, middle, middle-high, and high. The data utilized in our analysis were downloaded on March 27, 2025. The age group of 15–49 years was further divided into the following categories: 15–19, 20–24, 25–29, 30–34, 35–39, 40–44 and 45–49. The study protocol was created in accordance with international standards for epidemiological research and received approval from the appropriate institutional review boards [11]. No individual-level data were used, and ethical approval was not necessary for the data mining of the GBD estimates [8].

Idiopathic epilepsy definition

Established by the International League Against Epilepsy (ILEA) in 1985, the definition of idiopathic epilepsy refers to recurrent, unprovoked seizures for which there is no identified underlying disease and is presumed to have a strong genetic basis. Our case definition includes cases of active epilepsy with at least one seizure in the previous five years, regardless of treatment [5, 8]. The diagnostic codes of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD) used for epilepsy were code 345 of the ICD-9, and codes G40-G41 of the ICD-10.

Statistical analysis

The rates of incidence, prevalence, mortality and DALYs, along with their 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs), were calculated per 100,000 people according to the GBD database statistics. To evaluate internal trends over distinct time periods, the joinpoint regression model was employed to compute the annual percent change (APC) and its 95% confidence intervals (CIs) [9]. This approach offers a comprehensive view of annual rate fluctuations, providing insights into year-to-year changes. The average estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) and its 95% CI were calculated via a log-transformed Linear regression model to examine temporal trends in the incidence, prevalence, mortality and DALYs from 1990–2021 [12]. The EAPC is especially valuable for identifying long-term trends, as it reveals whether rates generally decrease or increase over time, independent of short-term variations. An EAPC value and the upper Limit of its 95% CI less than 0 indicate a decreasing trend for the corresponding indicator, whereas an EAPC value and the lower Limit of its 95% CI greater than 0 indicate an increasing trend for the corresponding indicator [13]. Fitted curves were used to explore the relationships between disease burden indicators and the SDI. The Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) model was applied to forecast trends in the global burden of idiopathic epilepsy up to 2035 [8]. All analyses in this study were performed via R language version 4.4. 2 and visualized by JD_GBDR software V2.24, with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Prevalence

The global incidence trend of idiopathic epilepsy increased from 1990 to 2001, followed by a significant decline until 2015, after which it increased again through 2021 (Fig. 1B). In 1990, there were approximately 7.46 million (95% UI 5.40–9.68) prevalent cases of idiopathic epilepsy worldwide, which rose to 11.5 million (95% UI 8.35–14.8) by 2021, an increase of 53.98% (95% UI 30.21–80.11). Similarly, the prevalence rate increased from 275.15 (95% UI, 199.05-357.13) per 100,000 people in 1990 to 290.83 (95% UI, 211.42-373.58) per 100,000 people in 2021, reflecting no statistically significant change of 5.70 (95% UI, −10.62 to 23.63). The global EAPC value was − 0.06% (95% CI, −0.13–0.01%) (Table S1).

In terms of age subgroups, the prevalence was consistently highest in the segment 15–19, gradually declined as age increased, and diminished to the lowest in the 45–49 segment (Fig. 3A). In 2021, the 15–19 segment accounted for 17.1% of the total prevalence, with a corresponding rate of 343.38 per 100,000 people (95% UI, 227.47–487.20), whereas the 45–49 segment represented 11.7%, with a rate of 235.26 per 100,000 people (95% UI, 162.27-321.73) (Fig. 3A and S1B). Gender differences in prevalence rates were not significant across age groups, although the prevalence rates for males were consistently higher than that for females (Fig. 3B and S3).

Mortality

In contrast to incident trends, IE-related mortality exhibited a substantial decreasing trend from 1990 to 2012, followed by an increase from 2012 to 2017 and a subsequent decrease from 2017 to 2021. The lowest APC was recorded between 2003 and 2012, with a value of −1.348% (95% CI, −1.498% to −1.198%) (Fig. 1C). The lowest mortality rate was observed in 2021, at 1.64 (95% UI, 1.83–1.38) per 100,000 people (Fig. 1C). The global number of deaths attributed to IE in 1990 was 52503.81 (95% UI, 44526.44-57962.76), which increased to 64692.51 (95% UI, 54671.02-72386.47) by 2021, representing an overall increase of 23.21% (95% UI, 11.93–34.17%) (Table S2). However, the mortality rate decreased from 1.94 (95% UI, 1.64–2.14) per 100,000 people in 1990 to 1.64 (95% UI, 1.38–1.83) per 100,000 people in 2021, reflecting a decrease of −15.42% (95% UI, −23.17% to −7.90%). The EAPC was − 0.62% (95% CI, −0.66% to −0.57%) (Table S2).

In terms of age subgroups, the mortality was highest in the 35–44 segment, and was lowest in the segment 25–29 from 1990 to 2021 (Fig. 3B). In 2021, the 40–44 segment accounted for 15.2% of the total mortality rate, with a corresponding rate of 1.75 per 100,000 people (95% UI, 1.41–1.96), whereas the 25–29 segment represented 13.5%, with a rate of 1.55 per 100,000 people (95% UI, 1.23–1.79) (Fig. 3A and S1C). Gender differences in mortality rates were significant, with males exhibiting higher rates than females (Figs. 3B and 4A).

Discussion

Epilepsy is a prevalent neurological disorder with the incidence and prevalence rates displays a bimodal distribution, with peaks observed in both pediatric and geriatric populations. This comprehensive analysis of GBD data between 1990 and 2021 specifically examined the demographic cohort aged 15–49 years, revealing several critical trends regarding the burden of idiopathic epilepsy, as indicated by epidemiological metrics. Globally in 2021, we estimated that approximately 11.48 million individuals aged 15–49 had IE, with incidence, mortality, and DALY rates of 37.57, 1.64, and 185.09 per 100,000 people, respectively. Additionally, from 1990 to 2021, the incidence rate of IE in young adults worldwide showed a slight upward trend, while the prevalence remained stable, and both mortality and DALY rates experienced significant declines.

A key strength of this study was its demonstration of the yearly changes in all four indicators of IE among young adults from 1990 to 2021. Incidence and prevalence showed fluctuating patterns, with a rapid initial rise, followed by a decline, and then another increase. In contrast, mortality and DALY rates generally showed a downward trend. The rise in incidence rates was likely due to improvements in epilepsy diagnosis and access to medical care. Wide uses of diagnostic tools like long-term video-electroencephalography (EEG) [14], long-term home Video EEG [15], electroencephalographic wearable behind the ears [16] were beneficial for more detection of seizures. The popularization of genetic testing could discover more hereditary epilepsy syndromes, thus classifying some previously cryptogenic epilepsy as idiopathic epilepsy [17–19]. Some risk and environmental factors, such as alcohol use and air pollutants PM2.5 and NO2, were reported to be positively and significantly associated with epilepsy, contributing to the risk of epilepsy [20, 21]. The Global Campaign Against Epilepsy promoted countries to increase awareness and education about epilepsy, improve public attitudes and reduce stigma against epilepsy [22], which may consequently make more epilepsy patients to seek medical help and contribute to increase the incidence of epilepsy. As to the prevalence, it subsequently fluctuated with the change of incidence rate. Additionally, since 1990, mortality and DALY rates have consistently decreased, which was attributed to improved access to medical care, the availability of novel antiepileptic medications and increased surgical interventions [23]. We noticed the temporary decline of incidence rate from 1999 to 2011, with reasons still unknown. We speculated that the advancement of brain imaging technology in developing countries, especially the popularization of brain magnetic resonance imaging(MRI), could help detect intracranial lesions such as hippocampal sclerosis, tumors, gray matter ectopia, focal cortical dysplasia, etc., thus reclassifying some cases that were originally classified as idiopathic epilepsy [24].

Another strength of this study was illustrating the connection between four epidemiological indicators and SDIs among individuals aged 15–49 with IE at global, regional, and national scales. Specifically, we found that mortality and DALY rates were significantly inversely correlated with SDI levels across all these levels. In other words, areas and countries with lower SDIs experienced higher epilepsy mortality and DALY rates, while those with higher SDIs had lower rates. This is because high SDI countries and regions benefit from greater health awareness, better access to medical care, more epilepsy specialists and higher quality healthcare-resources often lacking in low SDI regions, especially rural ones [25–27]. Conversely, incidence and prevalence did not show exhibit the same patterns. Globally, high SDI countries showed the highest incidence rates, followed by low-income countries. This may be due to advanced diagnostic tools available in high SDI countries, such as brain MRI, video EEG and genetic testing, which aid in accurately diagnosing idiopathic epilepsy [5]. Regionally, the highest incidence was observed in Andean Latin America and Central Latin America, which are low- to middle-income regions, followed by Western Europe, a high SDI area. Nationally, Ecuador, with a middle SDI, had the highest incidence, followed by Germany, a high SDI country. It is speculated that low-income countries may lack adequate diagnostic methods, leading to misclassification of acquired epilepsy or symptomatic epilepsy as idiopathic epilepsy [28]. Therefore, public health policymakers should focus on enhancing healthcare infrastructure, improving treatment access, and implementing effective public health strategies in low- to middle-SDI regions to boost diagnostic accuracy.

Notably, the eastern sub-Saharan Africa region had the highest mortality and DALY rates related to epilepsy, indicating a substantial treatment gap compared to high-income or high-SDI regions. This gap is driven by factors such as limited diagnostic tools, a shortage of trained healthcare workers, insufficient availability of antiepileptic drugs [29], and fewer surgical treatment options [30]. Additionally, cultural beliefs and social stigma plays a crucial role in discouraging patients from seeking medical care [31]. Therefore, policymakers and global funding organizations should focus on addressing the epilepsy burden in these areas by implementing targeted interventions. These include increasing access to neuroimaging, training more epilepsy specialists, raising awareness about epilepsy, improving the supply and quality of anti-seizure medications and surgical treatments, as well as efforts to reduce poverty and enhance living conditions, all aimed at reducing epilepsy-related mortality and DALYs in low-resource countries [32].

Additionally, our study identified significant gender differences in the impact of epilepsy, with males showing statistically higher mortality rates and non-statistically higher incidence, prevalence, and DALY rates compared to females across different age groups and over the period from 1990 to 2021. Mortality rates in males generally increased with age, while those in females tended to decrease. However, this gender-related mortality trend difference observed in the 15–49 age group was not evident in the 50–69 and 70 + age groups [33]. Moreover, analysis of gender-specific mortality trends revealed that men consistently had higher mortality rates than women from 1990 to 2021, aligning with findings from overall population studies [34] and and data from Latin America and the Caribbean [27]. Several factors may explain the higher death rates in males. Primarily, males with epilepsy are more likely to be exposed to risk factors and injury causes such as alcohol consumption [21], occupational hazards [25], and traffic accidents [35]. Additionally, some research has indicated a higher incidence of SUDEP (Sudden Unexpected Death in Epilepsy) among males [36], which may be partly linked to alcohol use [37]. Furthermore, recognized sex differences in epilepsy and epileptogenesis—potentially due to sex-related differences in brain structure and neural networks—may make men more susceptible to epileptic seizures than women [38]. This gender gap highlights the importance of developing targeted interventions that cater to the distinct needs of both male and female patients, as well as addressing the social factors that influence these outcomes.

Using the Bayesian age-period-cohort (BAPC) predictive modeling, an increase in incidence and prevalence rates among youth and young adults is expected in the coming years, primarily driven by the 25–49 age group rather than the 15–24 group. This trend is likely linked to factors such as alcohol consumption [21], higher rates of complications and comorbidities [39], and SUDEP [36]. Meanwhile, death rates and DALY rates are projected to decline across the entire 15–49 age range. These forecasts highlight the need for proactive healthcare policies and resource planning to manage the anticipated rise in cases. Healthcare systems should focus on epilepsy care, especially in areas where the disease burden is predicted to grow.

This study has several Limitations that need to be recognized. Firstly, GBD 2021 depends on global data, which can be affected by factors such as changes in epilepsy diagnostic criteria, variations in data collection methods, and differences in the quality of data sources across countries with different SDI levels. These inconsistencies may influence the overall quality and dependability of the data used in this analysis. Secondly, the study utilized secondary data derived from GBD modeling rather than raw data; notably, epilepsy data from some low-income countries were not based on raw information but on estimates generated through GBD modeling, which could affect the reliability of the findings. Thirdly, this study did not evaluate the burden of secondary epilepsy, as GBD 2021 only provides data on idiopathic epilepsy. Finally, the projections should be viewed cautiously due to the inherent uncertainty in BAPC forecast models.

In summary, this research offers important insights into the challenges posed by idiopathic epilepsy in young adults, emphasizing the need for continued investigation and focused interventions. Gaining a thorough understanding of the changing patterns in epilepsy epidemiology is crucial for creating effective public health approaches to reduce its effects on both individuals and society.
