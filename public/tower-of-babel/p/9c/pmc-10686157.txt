Use of non‐canonical amino acids in genetic code expansion‐based therapeutics: Effects on mouse gut microbiota
Authors: Manyu Liang, Yanhong Yang, Jing Zhang, Yingjie Sun, Haixia Peng, Huawei Yi, Qin Wang, Xiaowei Gao
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractVaccines and cell therapeutics based on genetic code expansion are emerging. A crucial step in these therapeutic technologies is the oral administration of non‐canonical amino acids (ncAAs) to control pathogen growth and therapeutic protein levels in vivo. Investigating the toxicity effects of ncAAs can help identify more suitable candidates for developing genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics. In this study, we determined the effects of three ncAAs, namely, 4‐acetyl‐phenylalanine (pAcF), 4‐iodo‐phenylalanine (pIoF), and 4‐methoxy‐phenylalanine (pMeoF), commonly used in genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics, on the main organs, serum biochemical parameters, and gut microbiota in mice. We observed that pIoF and pMeoF significantly altered serum biochemical parameters to some extent. Moreover, the alterations in the mouse gut microbial composition were considerably greater after the oral administration of pIoF and pMeoF than after that of pAcF, compared with that in the control mice. These findings suggest that pAcF is more suitable than pIoF and pMeoF for application in genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics as it disturbs the physiological and gut microecological balance in mice to a lesser extent.

INTRODUCTION

Genetic code expansion can site‐specifically incorporate various non‐canonical amino acids (ncAAs) into proteins in vivo by employing an evolved heterogeneous and orthogonal transfer RNA (tRNA)/aminoacyl tRNA synthetase pair that exclusively recognizes and induces a charge to the corresponding ncAAs in cells (Davis & Chin, 2012; de la Torre & Chin, 2021; Wang et al., 2001). To date, over 200 ncAAs have been successfully incorporated into target proteins via genetic code expansion, and experts in the field have exploited the adaptability of this technology in various hosts, ranging from bacteria to mammalian cells and multicellular organisms (Chin, 2014; Brown et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2019). This technology has resulted in the emergence of new opportunities for the development of biomedicines and biomaterials (Gao et al., 2022; Manandhar et al., 2021; Sisila et al., 2023). For example, genetic code expansion allows the site‐specific PEGylation of protein drugs to improve their quality by avoiding heterogeneous reactive mixtures generated using canonical amino acids (cAAs) (Cho et al., 2011; Huang & Liu, 2018). Heterogeneous antibody–drug conjugates are typically generated using the reactive side chains present in cAAs, resulting in low potency and stability (Axup et al., 2012; Sochaj et al., 2015). Genetic code expansion can be used to overcome these limitations as the sites and number of drugs linked to the antibodies can be controlled precisely (Oller‐Salvia et al., 2018; Tian et al., 2014).

Genetic code expansion also offers a pioneering solution for vaccine development (Fok & Mayer, 2020; Si et al., 2016). Introducing an orthogonal translation system into the pathogen genome and performing genetic code expansion‐based ncAA mutagenesis in indispensable pathogenic proteins can generate derivative strains that require ncAAs (Mandell et al., 2015; Rezhdo et al., 2019). Such strains cannot grow in the absence of ncAAs in the human body after vaccination, and their growth in vivo can be strictly controlled based on the amount of the orally administered ncAAs. Immune responses can be easily regulated by controlling the replication and reproduction of pathogens using ncAAs in the human body (Figure 1). This strategy has hence been used to develop vaccines against human immunodeficiency virus, influenza, and tuberculosis (Fok & Mayer, 2020; Si et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2010).

Schematic illustration of genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics.

Recently, Chen et al. developed a genetic code expansion‐based strategy to control insulin expression in engineered cells; this strategy could ensure a favourable hypoglycaemic effect in mice fed ncAA‐containing food (Chen et al., 2022). Shi et al. successfully treated Duchenne muscular dystrophy in mice by restoring dystrophin expression using genetic code expansion (Shi et al., 2022). Their study demonstrated the potential of genetic code expansion using ncAAs in the treatment of monogenic nonsense mutation diseases. Indeed, genetic code expansion‐based strategies aimed at augmenting the effects of vaccination and therapy are currently emerging. However, an issue should be addressed before these treatments can be used in clinical settings. Although any ncAA that acts as a substrate for genetic code expansion can be used as an inducer of pathogen replication or protein expression, the effects of such ncAAs on host health should be carefully considered; hence, identifying ncAAs with safe and desirable pharmacokinetic properties for their biomedical application in genetic code expansion is a primary task.

Gut microbiota plays key roles in food digestion, drug metabolism, and immune and nervous system development (Ke et al., 2021). Gut microbiota dysbiosis not only induces intestinal diseases such as colitis and colorectal cancer but also extensively affects the endocrine and nervous systems and brain functions, resulting in conditions such as obesity, diabetes, cancer, and depression (Wu et al., 2022). Maintaining gut microbiota balance is crucial for host health; however, the gut microbiota could be remarkably influenced by drugs, and long‐term dysbiosis in gut microbiota could drastically increase health risks. Considering that ncAAs are generally administered orally to perform their roles in genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics, revealing their effects on the gut microbial community can help evaluate their safety profiles in humans.

To our knowledge, five ncAAs, namely, 4‐azido‐phenylalanine, 4‐acetyl‐phenylalanine (pAcF), 4‐iodo‐phenylalanine (pIoF), 4‐methoxy‐phenylalanine (pMeoF), and Nε‐2‐azidoethyloxycarbonyl‐L‐lysine (NAEK), have been used to develop genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics (Chen et al., 2022; Si et al., 2016; Wang et al., 2014). The use of these ncAAs had provided positive treatment results; however, their effects on host health have not been systematically investigated. Herein, we selected three commercially available and inexpensive ncAAs (pAcF, pIoF, and pMeoF) and investigated their effects on mice health, particularly on gut microbiota. Our study results can help identify appropriate ncAAs for developing genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics.

EXPERIMENTAL PROCEDURES

Animals and materials

Forty Kunming mice were obtained from the Experimental Animal Center of Southwest Medical University. pAcF, pIoF, and pMeoF were purchased from MERYER Co. Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total bilirubin, and creatinine assay kits were purchased from Mindray Co. Ltd. (Shenzhen, China). Catalase, glutathione, total superoxide dismutase (T‐SOD), and mouse interleukin 6 (IL‐6) enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kits were purchased from Elabscience Co. Ltd. (Wuhan, China). A stool DNA extraction kit was obtained from OMEGA Bio‐Tek (Georgia, USA). All other chemicals and reagents used in this study were of analytical grade.

Animal experiments

Mice were housed in standard non‐pathogenic conditions during the feeding experiment: 12‐h light/dark cycle, a temperature of 24 ± 1°C, and humidity of 55 ± 10%. Mice had ad libitum access to chow and water. After 1 week of acclimatization, 40 mice were randomly assigned to four groups: control (Ctrl), pAcF, pIoF, and pMeoF groups. The Ctrl group was orally gavaged with 200 μL of phosphate buffer, whereas the pAcF, pIoF, and pMeoF groups were orally gavaged with 200 μL of phosphate buffer supplemented with 20 mg of pAcF, 20 mg of pIoF, and 15 mg of pMeoF, respectively. The body weights of the mice were recorded during the experiment. At the end of the experiment, faecal samples were collected from each mouse for gut microbiota analysis. The mice were then sacrificed via anaesthetic overdose, and blood samples were collected to separate serum. The liver and kidney samples were removed for further analysis.

Haematoxylin and eosin staining

The liver and kidney samples were fixed in formalin and stained with haematoxylin and eosin. The images were visualized under the BA210 digital microscope (Motic, China).

Biochemical assay and ELISA

The levels of total bilirubin, creatinine, glutathione, and IL‐6, and the activities of alanine aminotransferase (ALT), total superoxide dismutase (T‐SOD), and catalase in the serum samples of each mouse were determined using the respective commercial kits per the manufacturer's instructions.

Gut microbiota analysis

Bacterial genomic DNA was extracted from the faecal samples using the stool DNA extraction kit per the manufacturer's instructions. The hypervariable regions (V3/V4) of the 16S rRNA gene were amplified via polymerase chain reaction (PCR) using the resulting genomic DNA as a template, as described previously (Wu et al., 2022). After quantification, PCR products were subjected to paired‐end sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform (Illumina, San Diego, CA, USA). The sequencing raw data can be found in the NCBI Sequence Read Archive database under the accession number PRJNA974183.

The raw sequencing data were first subjected to quality‐filtering; invalid reads were removed using previously reported criteria (Liang et al., 2022; Wu et al., 2020). Sequences with over 97% similarity were assigned to the same operational taxonomic unit (OTU). Operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were used to clarify gut microbial composition and diversity, and representative OTUs were annotated with taxonomic information using the RDP classifier algorithm. Venn diagrams were used to compare the number of OTUs among the four groups. The alpha diversity of the gut microbiota in the four groups was presented using ACE, Chao, Shannon, Sobs, and Simpson indices. Beta diversity was analysed using the QIIME 2 software and compared using weighted UniFrac‐based principal coordinate analysis (PCoA). The identified bacteria and their relative abundances at the phylum and genus levels in the four groups were determined using community bar plot analysis. Significant differences in the gut microbial composition at the phylum level among the four groups were identified using the Kruskal–Wallis H test. Significant differences in the gut microbial composition at the genus level between the Ctrl and experimental groups were identified using the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test. Distance‐based redundancy analysis was used to determine the association between gut microbiota and environmental factors.

Pharmacokinetic analysis

Mice were orally gavaged with a single dose of ncAAs solution, and the blood samples were collected at indicated time intervals. Serum samples were obtained after centrifugation and mixed with acetonitrile to precipitate protein. The supernatants were filtered and then analysed with a high‐performance liquid chromatograph–mass spectrometry (HPLC‐MS) system equipped with a C18 column (2.1 × 100 mm, 3.5 μm, Waters XBridge). The concentrations of the ncAAs were calculated with a standard curve.

Statistical analysis

Unless otherwise indicated, statistical analyses were performed using the GraphPad Prism software (v8.0) with the unpaired Student's t‐test. The data were presented as means ± SD. A p‐value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Biochemical effects of ncAAs on the mice

In a preliminary experiment, we orally administered 0, 5, 10, 15, and 20 mg of the corresponding ncAAs to mice to determine the tolerable dose of specific ncAA. One mouse died after being orally administered 20 mg of pMeoF for 10 days (n = 3), while other mice administrated other doses of ncAAs survived. Thus, 20 mg pMeoF was assumed to be toxic to mice after long‐term administration. Considering that the crucial factor determining the suppression efficiency of genetic code expression is the availability ncAAs in the cell, higher amounts of ncAAs may induce higher levels of therapeutic protein expression and cell replication. Hence, we used 15 mg of pMeoF and 20 mg of the other two ncAAs to perform the experiments. Figure 2A presents the schedule for the animal experiments. Figure 2B demonstrates the chemical structures of the three ncAAs used in this study. Compared with mice in the Ctrl group, the body weights of mice in the pAcF or pMeoF groups exhibited no obvious differences, whereas the mice in the pIoF group exhibited a relative decrease in body weight gain (Figure 2C).

Experimental schedule and effects of non‐canonical amino acids (ncAAs) on mouse organs. (A) Flow chart of the animal experimental schedule. (B) Chemical structures of the ncAAs used in the study. (C) Body weights of the four groups during the feeding experiments. (D) Haematoxylin and eosin staining results of the representative tissue sections in the four groups. Data are expressed as mean ± SD.

The safety evaluation of ncAAs should be addressed before they are utilized in disease management. Hence, we investigated the pathological effects of the three ncAAs on the main organs and serum biochemical parameters. Haematoxylin and eosin staining (Figure 2D) revealed no significant pathological differences in the kidneys and livers of mice in the three experimental groups compared with those in the Ctrl group. Total bilirubin levels were higher in the pAcF and pMeoF groups and lower in the pIoF group than in the control group; however, the differences were not significant (Figure 3A). There were no evident differences in creatinine levels and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) activity among the four groups (Figure 3B,C). However, glutathione levels were higher in the pIoF group than in the Ctrl group (Figure 3D). Given that glutathione serves as a pivotal indicator of liver functionality, the abnormally elevated glutathione levels after pIoF administration in mice could potentially signify the presence of liver cell damage. Furthermore, IL‐6 levels were significantly higher in the pIoF group than in the Ctrl group, which indicated that pIoF could cause inflammation in mice (Figure 3E). Total superoxide dismutase (T‐SOD) and catalase activities were significantly higher and lower, respectively, in the pMeoF group than in the Ctrl group (Figure 3F,G). The result suggested that the metabolism of reactive oxygen species in mice was disturbed by pMeoF. Taken together, although the three ncAAs do not cause any serious pathological changes in the main organs of mice, pIoF and pMeoF significantly affect some serum biochemical parameters.

Effects of ncAAs on the gut microbial diversity in mice

The gut microbiota in the samples from the four groups were determined using high‐throughput sequencing. According to the results of rarefaction analysis, shown in Figure S1, all curves reached a saturation plateau, ensuring sufficient sequencing depth to reveal most of the bacterial phylotypes in the samples. The total numbers of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) in the Ctrl, pAcF, pIoF, and pMeoF groups were 570, 579, 565, and 517, respectively. The unique numbers of OTUs in the Ctrl, pAcF, pIoF, and pMeoF groups were 10, 17, 19, and 2, respectively (Figure 4A). These results suggest that pMeoF slightly decreased the number of OTUs in the gut microbial community of mice after oral administration.

Gut microbiota diversity analysis in the four groups. (A) Venn diagram of the shared and unique number of operational taxonomic units (OTUs) among the four groups. (B) Alpha diversity indices of the gut microbial community in each of the four groups. Data are expressed as mean ± SD, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant (*p < 0.05 and **p < 0.01). (C) Principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) of the gut microbiome in each sample among the four groups. Principal component 1 (PC1) and 2 (PC2) accounted for 15.67% and 10.02% of the variance, respectively. The discrete degree of PC1 among the four groups is demonstrated in the right panel.

The α‐diversity analysis is used to reveal the species diversity of a microbial community, including richness and evenness. The ACE and Chao indices are often employed to reflect the community richness. The algorithms that calculate the ACE and Chao indices are different, but higher the Chao or Ace indices indicate a larger number of OTUs and a greater community richness. The ACE and Chao indices of the pMeoF group were significantly lower than those of the Ctrl or pAcF groups (Figure 4B). This result suggests that oral pMeoF administration reduces the richness of gut bacterial species in mice. The Simpson and Shannon indices are often used to reflect the community's richness and evenness, namely community diversity. A higher Simpson index means a lower community diversity, whereas a higher Shannon index means a higher community diversity. The Simpson and Shannon indices of the pIoF group were significantly lower and higher, respectively, than those of the Ctrl group, which indicates that pIoF administration could decrease the community diversity of the gut microbiota in mice. These results suggest that the alpha diversity of the gut microbial community in mice was significantly altered after the oral administration of pIoF and pMeoF. For β‐diversity analysis, the principal coordinate analysis (PCoA) was conducted. According to the result shown in Figure 4C, the samples in the pAcF and pMeoF groups overlapped with those in the Ctrl group. In contrast, the data points for most of the samples in the pIoF group exhibited dispersion from the points for those in the Ctrl samples. These results indicate that a significant structural dissimilarity in the gut microbial community in mice after the oral pIoF administration.

Effects of ncAAs on the gut microbial composition at the phylum level

Figure 5A demonstrates the relative abundances of the dominant phyla in the four groups. Bacteroidetes and Firmicutes were the two most abundant phyla in all four groups. However, the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes was lower than that of Firmicutes in the pIoF group, whereas that of Bacteroidetes was higher than that of Firmicutes in the other three groups. Figure S2 presents a bar plot of the dominant phyla in each sample among the four groups. The Kruskal–Wallis H test revealed that the abundances of Bacteroidetes, Firmicutes, and Actinobacteria differed significantly among the four groups (Figure 5B). The abundance of Bacteroidetes decreased in the pIoF group compared with that of the Ctrl group, while those of Firmicutes and Actinobacteria increased. A significant decrease in the Bacteroidota/Firmicutes (B/F) ratio was observed in the pIoF group (Figure 5C). There was no remarkable difference in the relative abundance of Proteobacteria (Figure 5D).

Effects of ncAAs on the gut microbial composition at the genus level

Figure 6 demonstrates the primary genera and their relative abundances in the gut microbiota of each sample in the four groups. Norank _f_Muribaculaceae and Lactobacillus were the two most abundant genera in all four groups (Figure S3). Compared with the Ctrl group, the relative abundances of Rikenella, norank_f_Erysipelotrichaceae, and norank_f_Desulfovibrionaceae were significantly lower, while those of Candidatus_Stoquefichus, unclassified_f_Erysipelotrichaceae, unclassified_f_Anaerovoracaceae, Caldicoprobacter, and Bacillus were significantly higher in the pAcF group (Figure 7A). The pIoF group had lower abundances of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, unclassified_f_Lachnospiraceae, Bacteroides, Alistipes, norank_f_Lachnospiraceae, Prevotellaceae_UCG‐001, Roseburia, Colidextribacter, norank_f_Oscillospiraceae, unclassified_f_Erysipelotrichaceae, norank_f_Ruminococcaceae, Parabacteroides, Oscillibacter, and Eubacterium_xylanophilum_group and higher abundances of Turcibacter, Bifidobacterium, Romboutsia, and Faecalibaculim than the Ctrl group (Figure 7B). The relative abundances of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Roseburia, Rikenella, Parabacteroides, Oscillibacter, norank_f_norank_o_RF39, Eubacterium_xylanophilum_group, UCG‐003, GCA‐900066575, Eubacterium_siraeum_group, Butyricicoccus, unclassified_c_Clostridia, norank_o_Rhodospirillales, A2, and Family_XIII_UCG‐001 were significantly lower in the pMeoF group than in the Ctrl group (Figure 7C). Thus, the number of significantly altered genera in the gut microbiota of mice administered pAcF, pIoF, and pMeoF were 8, 20, and 15, respectively. These results suggest that oral pAcF administration resulted only in minor alterations in the gut microbial community compared with the alterations caused by other two ncAAs. Interestingly, the relative abundances of the two probiotics Bifidobacterium and Lactobacillus were significantly higher in the pIoF group than in the control group (Figure S4).

Both pIoF and pMeoF significantly decreased the relative abundances of Lachnospiraceae_NK4A136_group, Roseburia, Rikenella, Parabacteroides, and Oscillibacter in mouse gut microbiota. Lachnospiraceae ferment plant polysaccharides into short‐chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which are important regulators of various biological processes (Boutard et al., 2014). A study reported that butyric acid, an SCFA, can efficiently inhibit colon cancer (Ai et al., 2019). The genus Roseburia is a group of butyric acid‐producing bacteria that could be beneficial for human health and treatment of diseases (Nie et al., 2021). Rikenella is commonly found in the gut microbiome, and a decrease in its abundance in the gut leads to decreased butyrate synthesis and disrupted metabolism (Nie et al., 2021). The genus Parabacteroides is regarded as a probiotic and exhibits beneficial effects on human health, including regulating weight gain, controlling hyperglycaemia, and ameliorating hepatic steatosis (Zeng et al., 2019). In addition, the relative abundance of Butyricicoccus, another butyrate producer with probiotic potential, was also decreased in the pMeoF group. Therefore, decreases in the relative abundance of these genera in mouse gut microbiota after administration of pIoF and pMeoF may affect host SCFA production and resulting in metabolic stress. Compared with pIoF and pMeoF, pAcF exerted the least effects in terms of alterations in the gut bacterial composition, implying little negative influences on host health.

Linear discriminant analysis effect size, including cladogram and linear discriminant analyses, was performed to reveal distinct alterations in the predominant taxon in the gut microbiota among the four groups (Figures 8 and S5). In the Ctrl group, the order Lachnospirales and family Lachnospiraceae contributed significantly to the differences among the three experimental groups. The phyla p_Bacteroidota, class Bacteroidia, and order Bacteroidales were the main sources of differences in the pAcF group, whereas the class Bacilli, family Burkholderiaceae, and genus Ralstonia were the main sources differences in the pIoF and pMeoF groups.

Associations between altered serum biochemical parameters and the gut microbiota

Correlations between altered serum biochemical parameters and the gut microbiota were determined using distance‐based redundancy and Spearman correlation heatmap analyses. As shown in Figure 9, most serum biochemical parameters were positively associated with the phylum Firmicutes and negatively associated with the phyla Verrucomicrobiota and Bacteroidetes. According to the Spearman correlation heatmap, the ALT activity was significantly and positively correlated with the genera norank_f_Ruminococcaceae and unclassified_f_Oscillospiraceae (Figure 10). Moreover, the creatinine level was significantly and positively associated with norank_f_norank_o_Clostridia_UCG‐014, Roseburia, unclassified_f_Ruminococcaceae, Lanchnospiraceae_UCG‐006, norank_f_UCG‐010, and Odoribacter and negatively associated with Enterococcus. The total bilirubin level was significantly and positively associated with Prevotellaceae_UCG‐001, Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group, Parabacteroides, and Ruminococcus and negatively associated with Turicibacter, Bifidobacterium, Enterococcus, Romboutsia, and Faecalibaculum. The glutathione level was positively associated with Turcibacter and negatively associated with norank_f_Erysipelotrichaceae, Rikenella, and norank_f_UCG‐010. Moreover, T‐SOD activity was significantly and positively correlated with Parabacteroides, Mucispirillum, norank_f_norank_o_RF39, and NK4A214_ group. Lastly, the IL‐6 level was significantly and positively associated with norank_f_norank_o_Clostridia_UCG‐014, Akkermansia, Muribaculum, Prevotellaceae_NK3B31_group, Ruminococcus, and norank_f_UCG‐010.

DISCUSSION

Genetic code expansion can revolutionize numerous aspects of biomedicine and provide novel strategies for treating metabolic and genetic diseases. The quality and efficiency of several classes of protein drugs, including growth hormones, cytokines, antibody‐drug conjugates, bispecific antibodies, and therapeutic protein vaccines, have been extensively enhanced in past decades with genetic code expansion (Wals & Ovaa, 2014). Cell therapeutics designed by control of host protein expression at the translational level with genetic code expansion open a new gate to treat human metabolic and genetic diseases, such as diabetes and monogenic diseases (Chen et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2022).

Genetic code expansion has facilitated the engineering of many pathogens into genetically modified organisms (GMOs) that depend on ncAAs (Fok & Mayer, 2020). GMOs remain viable and infective as wild‐type strains and have several advantages in the development of live‐attenuated vaccines. The biochemical properties and immunogenicity of the derivative strain are completely consistent with those of the wild‐type pathogen, and it can stimulate strong immune responses in the host. Furthermore, inserting multiple ncAAs into essential pathogenic proteins can effectively decrease the probability of revertant mutations and improve biological safety. The construction of a ncAA‐based nutrient‐deficient mutant strain is simpler and has a wider applicability than the traditional methods for obtaining attenuated mutant strains. Therefore, the use of genetic code expansion to construct ncAA‐dependent live pathogens can open new avenues for vaccine development.

Genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics need ncAA supply to achieve treatment outcomes; therefore, it is necessary to evaluate the biosafety profiles of ncAAs on the host before they can be transferred to the clinical trial stage. When the ncAA is administrated orally, more attention should be paid as it would be interacted with the host gut microbiota. Gut microbiota constitutes the most complex micro‐ecosystem in the host and performs biological functions as an “extra organ”. In general, it maintains dynamic equilibrium in life and could be significantly influenced by diet, drug use, stress, disease and the inhabit of the host. Gut microbiota dysbiosis affects the host's health through their metabolites, immunomodulation ability, and gut microbiota‐gut‐X axis (X: brain, liver, kidney, heart, and skin). Therefore, one merit of an ideal ncAA would be the minimal influence on gut microbial composition after oral administration.

In the present study, we investigated the pathological effects of three ncAAs commonly used for genetic code expansion in mice. The oral administration of these three ncAAs did not result in obvious pathological changes in the liver or kidney. According to our results, pIoF could cause liver injury, inflammation, and dysbiosis of intestinal microecology in mice as reflected by the increased levels of glutathione and IL‐6, and the alterations in gut microbiota (Figure 3D,E and 4). Obviously, pIoF has more adverse effects on mice than the other two ncAAs, and it may be the reason why pIoF administrated mice exhibited a lower body weight gain than that of the control mice (Figure 2C). Although no difference was detected in body weight gain after pMeoF administration, it significantly affected T‐SOD and catalase activities in mice, suggesting that the oxidation processes in the mice were disturbed (Figure 3F,G). Interestingly, pAcF did not significantly alter body weight gain rate or serum biochemical parameters in mice. In addition, pAcF showed a quicker serum clearance rate than the other two ncAAs (Figure S6).

In terms of gut microbiome, oral pIoF administration significantly increased the Simpson index and decreased the Shannon index, indicating that pIoF reduced the diversity of the gut microbial community in mice. PCoA revealed a remarkable structural rearrangement in the gut microbial composition of pIoF‐treated mice. pMeoF reduced the ACE, Chao, and Sobs indices, suggesting that the oral pMeoF administration can negatively regulate the species richness of the gut microbiota in mice. The B/F ratio is considered an indicator of the balance between gut microecology and host health status, and a decreased B/F ratio is associated with several pathological conditions, including colitis and depression (Wu et al., 2022). Oral pIoF administration significantly decreased the B/F ratio of the gut microbial community in mice, indicating that pIoF has adverse effects on mice health after long‐term treatment. Through bioinformatic analysis of the bacteria in mice gut, we found that most of the altered species in the pIoF and pMeoF treated groups are related to butyric acid production, with some of them possessing health‐promoting attributes. This result demonstrated that administration of pIoF and pMeoF would affect host SCFA production and cause metabolic stress.

Identifying ncAAs with no pathological properties for application in genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics is important for promoting therapies for clinical trials. In this study, we found that pAcF had no significant effects on the organs and serum biochemical parameters, and exhibited little effects on the gut microbial composition. Although pMeoF showed a better bioavailability than pAcF (Figure S6), it significantly altered the gut microbiota and some serum biochemical parameters in mice. Thus, pAcF may be more suitable than pIoF and pMeoF for applications in genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics. Previous studies about the safety evaluation of the ncAAs were focused on survival rate, body weight, blood chemical indexes, and pathological changes of the main organs in mice (Chen et al., 2022; Shi et al., 2022). Our results presented here provide new clues from the view of gut microbiota for the safe use of ncAAs during the preparation of genetic code expansion‐based vaccines and cell therapeutics. Although we focused on the influence of ncAAs on gut microbial composition here, the metabolisms of the ncAAs by gut microbiota and the effects of the resulting metabolites on host health should also be considered in the future. Furthermore, reproductive toxicity, carcinogenicity, mutagenicity, immunotoxicity, and neurotoxicity of the ncAAs must be determined before they can be used in clinical trials.
