Moderate electrical muscle stimulation during voluntary movement does not disrupt the sense of agency or ownership
Authors: Fumina Mori, Dai Yanagihara
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAlthough electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) is expected to alter the sense of agency (SoA) and sense of ownership (SoO) of a user’s body parts, as well as brain activity during passive movements, these phenomena have not yet been systematically examined. Therefore, we investigated the SoA, SoO, and brain activity in situations where participants moved their wrists voluntarily, or where EMS was presented either during voluntary movement or at rest. Eighteen healthy individuals (16 males and two females; age: 18–29 years) participated in this study. The EMS intensity was varied from the motor threshold to the maximum level at which participants did not experience pain or discomfort. SoA and SoO scores were comparable to those during voluntary movement alone and when EMS was presented during voluntary movement at medium or low intensities. The change of electroencephalography power in the alpha (8–12 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) bands could not reveal the effect of EMS. Based on our findings, EMS at medium or low levels appears to be favorable for maintaining SoA and SoO.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-026-57714-9.Keywords: Electrical muscle stimulation, Sense of agency, Sense of ownership, Electroencephalography, Wrist, MusclesSubject terms: Neuroscience, Physiology

Introduction

Electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) induces passive contraction of target muscles and has potential applications in exercise guidance and rehabilitation for inpatients and older adults1–4. Recently, EMS is expected to be utilized not only independently but also in combination with brain-computer interface (BCI) and robot-assisted therapy, serving as a more advanced mechatronics system and human-machine interface5–7. In these systems, it is important to maintain the user’s sense of agency (SoA), the feeling that “I am controlling my action” because the strength of SoA facilitates motor learning8.

The internal models for motor control compare the predicted sensory consequence of one’s action and actual sensory consequence9. When both consequences match, SoA is generated. The sense of ownership (SoO, the feeling that “the body experiencing this event is my body”) arises from the combination of cognitive processes and afferent sensory information reaching the brain from sensory organs10. EMS induces artificial movements regardless of the user’s intention, which changes actual sensory consequence and feedback. Hence, discrepancy arises between the brain’s prediction and the actual movement. In other words, the discrepancy, and its subsequent effects on SoA, SoO, and brain activity, may vary depending on EMS intensity, which dictates movement magnitude, and the presence or absence of motor intention.

Regarding SoA, most studies have evaluated it in terms of whether one perceives oneself as controlling the visual or auditory feedback resulting from one’s own movement11–15. Recently, several studies have evaluated SoA in relation to whether an individual perceives themselves as controlling an avatar’s or their own body movements2,5,7,16,17. Studies with EMS have adjusted the timing of EMS presentation in the button press task5,17, and controlled EMS by detecting motor intent via BCI6.

Most studies on SoO have focused on the rubber hand illusion, in which participants feel that tactile stimuli presented to a fake rubber or avatar hand are actually presented to their body18–22. Studies focusing on ownership of one’s own body have reported decreased SoO in patients with post-stroke effects or intracarotid amobarbital inactivation23,24. However, it remains unclear whether SoO changes in one’s own body parts, not artificial ones.

The pre-motor cortex, motor cortex, and posterior parietal cortex are involved as a network in the intention of movement and the perception of one’s own body25–27. Change in alpha band (mu rhythm) and beta band power in electroencephalography (EEG) reflect cortical activation in these motor and sensory areas28. Research on brain activity during situations where EMS induces movement is scarce29,30. In a study comparing EEG power during voluntary and EMS-induced movements, it was suggested that parts of the sensory-motor process during EMS-induced movement are also present during voluntary movement29. In a study of the relationship between EEG and SoA in voluntary movement, participants’ hand movements were synchronized with that of an avatar’s hand on a monitor (0–100%)16. This indicated the existence of brain activity related to SoA generation in the alpha and beta bands.

Despite extensive research, it remains unclear how EMS intensity and movement mode (voluntary movement alone, voluntary movement with EMS, or EMS alone) affect SoA, SoO, and brain activity. Therefore, in this study, we aimed to systematically investigate the effects of EMS intensities and movement modes (during movement or rest). We hypothesized that in cases of stronger EMS during rest, SoA and SoO would decrease, compared with those when performing voluntary movements without EMS. Concerning brain activity, we hypothesized that in such cases, the EEG power would differ, though whether it increases or decreases could not be determined from previous studies. This investigation may contribute to exercise guidance and rehabilitation as well as the development of technologies31,32 for sharing proprioception during movement.

Results

Survey scores

A survey was conducted to assess SoA, SoO, excitement, and fatigue in the V condition, where participants performed wrist flexion voluntarily; in the V + WS, V + MS, and V + SS conditions, where EMS was presented at three levels of intensity while participants performed wrist flexion voluntarily; and in the WS, MS, and SS conditions, where EMS was presented at three levels of intensity while participants were at rest. WS, MS, and SS indicated weak, medium, and strong EMS intensities, respectively. Weak EMS corresponded to the motor threshold level of palmar flexion; strong EMS was the maximum level at which participants did not feel pain or discomfort; and medium EMS was the intermediate level between weak and strong EMS. Participants verbally reported their feelings on a scale from 0 to 100, where 0 indicated “I did not feel like that at all”, and 100 indicated “I highly felt like that”. For SoA and SoO, two questions were asked for each construct. For excitement and fatigue, one question was asked per construct.

Discussion

When comparing between voluntary and other movements, SoA and SoO scores decreased in the WS, MS, SS, and V + SS conditions. This is thought to be due to the exogenous motor input from EMS causing a discrepancy with the motor prediction based on the internal model. Since we used explicit methods for answering questions, our findings may reflect participants’ cognitive biases34. However, we did not use implicit methods that reflect unconscious low-level brain processing. Therefore, it is not possible to strictly determine whether the score decrease resulted from sensory-level mismatch, such as timing or force modulation, or from awareness of EMS influence. Introducing intentional binding could allow for a more detailed elucidation of the factors causing the score decrease.

When comparing SoA and SoO scores between EMS intensities, the higher the intensity of EMS, the more the scores decreased, which supported our hypothesis. The analysis of the relationship between items revealed that SoA and SoO scores had medium positive correlations. The EMM values indicated that the SoA score showed a greater decrease than the SoO score. Although the SoO score of two participants did not change in any of the conditions, our findings are similar to the those of previous studies using rubber hands35,36. In these studies, SoA was influenced by the movement mode (active or passive) and was higher when participants performed the movement themselves. SoO was influenced by the positional congruence between the participant’s own hand and the rubber hand, and was higher when the position of both hands was congruent35,36. Both SoA and SoO were higher when the participant’s hand and the rubber hand moved synchronously35. In the present study, since the participant’s own hand was the target rather than a rubber hand, positional congruence was irrelevant. Instead, the movement mode and the synchrony between the predicted and actual movements were affected. This may have been the reason why SoA was more easily reduced by EMS.

The differences in SoA and SoO scores in the V + WS and V + MS conditions compared with the V condition were subtle. This suggests that EMS of medium or lower intensity level during voluntary movement is promising for application in rehabilitation or sports training. The survey score and correlations indicate that stronger EMS reduced SoA and SoO while increasing excitement. These findings suggest that the level of SoA and SoO required for motor learning may be in a trade-off relationship with excitement. Future studies using more game-like tasks or tasks beneficial for rehabilitation or sports training may enhance excitement even at low EMS.

We acknowledge three limitations in our investigation of SoA and SoO. First, it remains unclear whether participants became accustomed to the EMS through repeated task performance, or whether adaptation, repetition, time, and fatigue influenced scores. Scores in this study were obtained from surveys conducted once per task after EEG measurement. Therefore, regarding fatigue, participants may not have been able to strictly distinguish between fatigue arising from the EEG measurement and fatigue from the task itself. To clarify the effects of adaptation, repetition, time, and fatigue, it is necessary to conduct surveys multiple times per task and monitor changes in scores.

Second, we instructed the participants to look at a fixation point on the screen or a bar indicating their current wrist angle rather than at their own arm, to prevent noise from eye blinks or eye movements. SoO is generated by combining information reaching the brain from sensory organs such as vision, touch, and proprioception with cognitive processes10. Seeing one’s own arm is a more familiar situation than seeing information on angular changes on a monitor. The reason the SoO did not change for two of the participants may be due to an insufficient recognized discrepancy, because they did not see their own arm. Therefore, when observing one’s own arm while it is being moved, participants may become more aware of the discrepancy between the visual and proprioceptive information, potentially leading to a greater decrease in SoO.

The third limitation is the small sample size and restricted scope of samples and tasks. Participants were young healthy adults; hence, the effects of sex, age, or disease could not be investigated. Since only wrist movements were examined, different trends might be observed for movements involving other body parts or complex movements using multiple body parts. Larger sample size and sample and task scopes would be beneficial to further validate our findings.

Based on our findings and limitations, the following provisional guidelines are proposed for the use of EMS in rehabilitation and sports training.

At the proposed intensity, movements that match the ideal magnitude may not be induced. To enable users to voluntarily perform movements that match the ideal magnitude, visual guidance as well as EMS will likely be necessary. For example, an ideal system for learning proper movement using EMS and visual guidance may allow users to observe their own movements while viewing the ideal trajectory of body parts during exercise through Mixed Reality goggles, with EMS providing timely corrections to refine their form.

Compared with resting levels, EEG power decreased during the V, V + WS, V + MS, and V + SS conditions in both alpha and beta bands. Since EEG power decreases as cortical activity increases28, cortical activity during voluntary movement, regardless of EMS presence, is considered to be reflected in our EEG power. Our findings during voluntary movement were consistent with previous studies that examined EEG power during movement29. EEG power did not change compared with resting levels during WS, MS, and SS conditions in both alpha and beta bands, even though cortical activity could be driven by EMS. A previous study showed that alpha and beta power decreased even at sensory threshold levels, weaker than those used in our study. Therefore, we believe that cortical activity was supposed to be driven at our levels above the motor threshold. In our analysis, since participants were excluded if EMS artifacts were not fully removed, and the EMS period was excluded, it is unlikely that EMS artifacts influenced the EEG power. Therefore, it is possible that brain activity originating from EMS was not detected despite its existence because of the potential that such activity occurred during the excluded period or baseline acquisition method.

Our methods referenced the approaches investigating the relationship between SoA and EEG power, establishing separate periods for baseline and repeated movements16. Previous studies investigating EMS-induced EEG power changes alternated between baseline and a single movement29,30. The latter methods require over five times the measurement time, likely increasing participant burden. To gain a deeper understanding of the effects of EMS on SoA, SoO, and brain activity at different intensities and modes of movement, we first need to overcome the limitations of current evaluations of EMS effects on SoA and SoO. We then need to conduct the latter protocol after reducing the number of conditions to include only those where significant differences in SoA and SoO from voluntary movement are observed. In the future, optimization of the experimental protocol and a larger sample size might allow for EMS effect-related brain activity extraction.

Comparisons between conditions revealed significant differences in relative EEG power between the V condition vs. WS/MS/SS conditions. There were no differences between EMS intensities (Fig. 4). The reason for this was, as mentioned earlier, that EMS-only movement could not be detected—meaning its relative EEG power was nearly zero—while voluntary movement activity was detected, meaning its EEG power had decreased from resting. Therefore, the difference cannot be considered to reflect differences in brain activity. The lack of difference in EMS intensities is also thought to be due to the same reason.

Comparing between conditions, there was no difference in relative EEG power between the V vs. V + WS/V + MS/V + SS conditions. In the V + WS, V + MS, and V + SS conditions, there were time lags between the cue to start flexion and EMS onset. The range of the intra-individual trial-average time lag was 0.042–0.400 s, which indicates that some participants began flexing almost simultaneously with the cue, whereas others began flexing after seeing the signal. However, the likelihood of brain activity becoming blurred due to the time lag variation is considered low because the extracted epoch was always the period after the EMS offset, i.e., after EMS-induced movements. The maximum wrist angle during movement did not differ between the V, V + WS, V + MS, and V + SS conditions. This is reasonable because participants were instructed to move toward the maximum range of motion (ROM) from 0 degrees in those conditions. Regardless of the maximum movement angle, EMS presentation should cause a discrepancy between the predicted and actual movements, indeed, the SoA differed between the V and V + SS conditions in our survey. In a previous study examining EEG and SoA, higher SoA correlated with greater EEG power reduction16. Despite our SoA findings and the previous EEG power findings, no difference was observed between the V vs. V + WS/V + MS/V + SS conditions. This may stem from brain activity related to discrepancy or reduced SoA being excluded during analysis to counteract EMS artifacts. To detect the brain activity related to discrepancy or SoA, inducing movement through exoskeleton robots developed for rehabilitation7,37 may be beneficial.

We acknowledge two additional limitations in our investigation of EEG. First, we did not analyze the relationship between EEG, SoA, and SoO because we did not observe differences based on EMS intensity, contrary to our survey. In the future, by refining the baseline measurement and movement task, we may be able to isolate the effects of EMS and examine these associations during EMS. Second, we did not account for individual differences in EMS perception. However, such differences will likely cause variations in how individuals mentally compensate for discrepancies between predicted and actual movements, leading to differences not only in brain activity but also in the generation of SoA and SoO. Considering variations in EMS perception not only among healthy individuals but also across diverse populations may lead to a deeper understanding of the neural basis of SoA and SoO.

Previous studies have investigated methods for maintaining SoA and examined the relationship between SoA, SoO, EMS, and EEG. As previously mentioned, methods for providing EMS that shorten reaction times in switch-pressing tasks while maintaining SoA have been explored5,17. Moreover, changes in brain activity related to SoA and SoO when using avatar or rubber hands have also been investigated16,19. Brain activity during rest, when movement was induced by a single EMS intensity, have also been investigated29. However, the current study provides a novel, comprehensive framework by simultaneously evaluating SoA and SoO across various EMS intensities and movement modes. Notably, regarding SoO, unlike many prior studies with virtual or fake bodies, our approach focused on one’s own physical body parts.

We investigated the effects of EMS intensities and movement modes on SoA, SoO, and brain activity. As predicted, the higher the intensity of EMS, the more SoA and SoO decreased. Our findings indicate that EMS of moderate or low intensity level during voluntary movement is promising for maintaining SoA and SoO. However, we could not evaluate the influence of EMS on brain activity since brain activity originating from EMS may not have been detected. To understand SoA, SoO, and brain activity during EMS exercise in more detail, it is necessary to evaluate the effects of habituation on EMS, and repetition, duration, and fatigue on SoA and SoO, and to extract the effects of EMS on brain activity. Towards the development of EMS for sports training, rehabilitation, and sharing proprioception, future studies should evaluate SoO during movement while observing one’s own body and conduct assessments across a diverse range of users.

Methods

Participants

The study sample consisted of 18 healthy participants (16 males and two females; mean age: 21 ± 3.5 years, range: 18–29 years). The dominant hand was the right hand for all participants. The sample size was determined based on previous established studies employing similar experimental paradigms16,29,30. All participants provided written informed consent before the experiments. This study was approved by the Ethical Review Committee for Experimental Research Involving Human Subjects, Graduate School of Arts and Sciences, University of Tokyo, Japan and conducted in accordance with the Helsinki Declaration (2024).

Experimental setup

Participants sat on a height-adjustable chair. The distance between the chair and monitor (FlexScan EV2781, EIZO Corporation) was approximately 1 m. The chair was adjusted so that participants could sit comfortably with their feet on the floor. An armrest was placed on each participant’s right side. Participants placed their right arm on the armrest with the palm facing the left side so that their hand did not touch the armrest. They adjusted the height and position of the armrest to allow comfortable wrist movement.

EMS was performed using a constant-current electrical stimulator (USE-210; Unique Medical Co., Ltd.). While the participant repeatedly flexed their wrist, the experimenter touched the medial aspect of the ulnar side of the forearm and visually identified the area where the arm bulged most prominently during muscle contraction. Two disposable electrodes (SMP-300; METS INC., size 19 × 38 mm) were then attached to the skin at this location so that EMS induced palmar flexion in the same direction as when flexing the wrist voluntarily. Furthermore, the placement was fine-tuned by confirming whether flexion was elicited by EMS. The stimulation frequency was set at 50 Hz. The pulses were monophasic with a duration of 0.2 ms, and the number of trains was 10. The current intensity was adjusted individually for each participant, without changing the other parameters. Two levels of current intensity were investigated: the weak level corresponded to the threshold for palmar flexion, and the strong level was the maximum intensity at which participants did not feel pain or discomfort. The current intensity was increased in 0.1 mA increments starting from 0 mA. Each time the current was increased, participants were asked to answer yes or no regarding the presence of pain or discomfort. Additionally, when the intensity became stronger than the motor threshold level, the investigator confirmed whether flexion was elicited by EMS. If flexion was not elicited or a different movement was elicited, the placement of the electrode was tuned again and the current intensity was readjusted.

Wrist angles and EEG were recorded using a biosignal recorder (Polymate Pro MP6000, Miyuki Giken Co., Ltd.). A goniometer (4 Assist, Inc.) was attached by aligning its two sensors to sandwich the wrist joint, following the anatomical neutral position. Specifically, the sensors were carefully positioned along the longitudinal axes of the forearm and the third metacarpal bone while the wrist was held in a neutral posture. EEGs were recorded from 11 positions (F3, Fz, F4, T3, C3, Cz, C4, T4, P3, Pz, and P4) based on the 10–20 system (Fig. 6). The sampling rate was 1000 Hz.

Experimental paradigm

Measurement of the maximum angle of palmar flexion

Each participant’s maximum palmar flexion angle was measured after the goniometer was set up and before the task began. We instructed the participants to bend their wrist to its maximum ROM and maintain their wrist angle for 10 s. The average angle during this period was determined to be the maximum palmar flexion angle. This information was then used in the subsequent tasks.

Tasks

The tasks consisted of seven conditions in which participants either moved their wrist voluntarily or their wrist movement was intervened in or induced by EMS, and one resting condition. The EMS intensities for the weak and strong levels were those determined in the previous measurement, and the medium level was set as the midpoint between the weak and strong intensities. To standardize measurement times and the number of conditions across participants, we selected discrete intensity levels rather than continuous ones. The instructions for each task were displayed on a monitor using MATLAB (version: R2024b) and PsychToolbox (version: 3.0.19)38–40(Fig. 7).

V condition

Figure 7a shows the image of the V condition. First, the word “Move” was displayed in the center of the monitor for 5 s. For the next 5 s, the word “Ready” and bars were displayed in the center of the monitor. The white and black bars corresponded to the wrist angle. The left and right ends of the bar represented 90° palmar flexion and dorsiflexion, respectively. The length of the white bar was constant, while the length of the black bar changed in real time, corresponding to the participant’s wrist angle. The black bar extended and shortened when participants performed palmar flexion and dorsiflexion. The red line in the center of the white bar corresponded to 0°, and the red line on the left represented the participant’s maximum palmar flexion angle. Participants were instructed to adjust the wrist angle to make it 0° while looking at the black bar. The instruction was given to prevent large eye movements because such movements can generate noise in the EEG.

Leftward and rightward arrows were alternately displayed above the bars for 1 s, 21 times each. Participants were instructed to perform palmar flexion to the maximum angle while the leftward arrow was displayed and to straighten their wrist when the rightward arrow appeared. One trial consisted of one leftward and one rightward arrow and took 2 s, with the participant flexing their wrist once per trial. Participants were instructed to repeat bending their wrist to its maximum ROM and extending their wrist to 0° smoothly without stopping, comply with the arrows’ instructions, look at the black bar, and avoid blinking while the arrows were displayed. The task consisted of 21 trials to account for noise caused by body movement. In tasks involving EMS, participants tended to move their bodies instinctively during the first trial due to the initial EMS pulse. Therefore, one additional trial was included so that noise-free data from 20 trials could be used for analysis. Although EMS was not presented in the voluntary task, 21 trials were also conducted to prevent participants from noticing differences in trial numbers across tasks.

V + WS, V + MS, and V + SS conditions

Figure 7a shows the V + WS, V + MS, and V + SS conditions. The content displayed on the monitor and instructions given to participants were the same as those in the voluntary task. The wrist angle obtained from the goniometer was analyzed in real time during the task to detect flexion onset in each trial. EMS was presented automatically at flexion onset, which induced movement in the same direction immediately after voluntary flexion onset. Flexion onset was determined as the timing when the angle of palmar flexion increased by 3° from that at the beginning of the leftward arrow display, except when the palmar flexion angle did not increase by 3° within 1 s. The reason for selecting 3° was that in our preliminary experiments, using a criterion of less than 3° resulted in the EMS being presented due to slight hand tremors before the start of flexion.

WS, MS, and SS conditions

Figure 7b shows the WS, MS, and SS conditions. First, the word “Relax” was displayed in the center of the monitor for 5 s. Then, a fixation cross was displayed at the center of the monitor and EMS was presented automatically 21 times, once every 2 s. Participants were instructed to look at the cross while avoiding blinking and keeping their wrists relaxed.

Resting condition

Figure 7b also shows the resting condition. First, the word “Relax” was displayed in the center of the monitor for 5 s. Then, the fixation cross was displayed at the center of the monitor for 20 s. Participants were instructed to look at the cross while avoiding blinking and relaxing their wrists.

Procedure

Four measurements were performed (Table 1; Fig. 8). All participants underwent measurements in the order from the first to fourth run. In the first run, the V and resting conditions were alternately performed five times each. In the second to fourth runs, the V + WS/V + MS/V + SS, WS/MS/SS, and resting conditions were performed five times each. The order was randomized to prevent the same conditions from occurring consecutively. The order of conditions in each run differed among participants. The current intensities were weak, medium, and strong in the second, third, and fourth runs, respectively. It took 5.6 min in the first run and 10.7 min in the second to fourth run. The overall experimental paradigm was mostly based on previous research investigating brain activity and SoA during voluntary hand movement16,41. In each run, there were 105 trials per task and 100 s of rest in total as each task was carried out five times. During the measurements, participants’ back views were recorded using a web camera (C922n PRO HD, Logicool Co., Ltd.) at 30 fps. Video data were collected and used to check for extra body movements during analysis.

EEG

Preprocessing

A bandpass filter (0.5–40 Hz) was presented to the EEG data using Brainstorm (version: 06-May-2025)42. The bandpass frequency range was determined to include the frequency bands to be verified in the subsequent calculation of relative EEG power and to exclude the influence of power supply noise. In a preliminary experiment, we performed the same task with an electrooculography (EOG) electrode placed under the left eye in addition to the current setup. To remove the artifact of blinking, we performed independent component analysis (ICA) on the entire measurement period and decomposed the EEG into 11 components (same number of all EEG channels) using Brainstorm. We sorted components based on the correlation with EOG and visually inspected the components while simultaneously displaying EOG (Fig. S1). When EMS-associated high-amplitude artifacts were present, we could not stably extract the eye blink-derived component. Therefore, we decided not to perform ICA or artifact-subspace methods, posing a risk of distorting or eliminating the inherent EEG signal components. In the current task, we considered EEG contamination by eyeblink activity suppressed, since we minimized blinking through participant instruction.

The data were segmented into 1-s epochs for EEG power calculation. In the V + WS, V + MS, V + SS, WS, MS, and SS conditions, the epoch period was set to 1.0 s after every EMS offset, that is 0.22–1.22 s after the beginning of each trial, because the 0–0.22 s interval was contaminated by EMS noise. Under the V condition, the epoch was unified with that of the V + WS, V + MS, V + SS, WS, MS, and SS conditions. In the resting condition, data were divided into 1-s epochs throughout the duration of the condition. These 1-s periods were the time window used in the subsequent calculation of relative EEG power.

EEG amplitudes were visualized in Brainstorm42. To remove motion-related noise, we combined careful examination of EEG waveforms with verification using synchronized video. Since motion noise in this experiment manifested as atypical, steep potential changes, we did not set a uniform amplitude threshold. Instead, we extracted artifacts based on the morphological features of the waveforms. When unusual potential changes were detected, we individually verified the presence of actual head movement using the recorded video and manually excluded the epoch where head movement was confirmed. Trials in which EMS was not presented, due to the absence of a ≥ 3° increase in palmar flexion within 1 s, were also excluded from the V + WS, V + MS, and V + SS conditions. Regarding omitted epochs, video inspection resulted in zero omissions for 10 participants and three or fewer for eight others (Table S11). Due to lack of EMS, eight participants had zero omissions, while 10 had six or fewer (Table S12).

The total number of omitted epochs was zero to six in each condition across all the participants (Table S13). We verified whether each condition followed a normal distribution using the Lilliefors test. Since the results indicated that not all conditions were significantly parametric, we decided to perform the Friedman test. To examine the number of omitted epochs between the conditions, the number of omitted epochs was compared using the Friedman test (p < 0.05, Bonferroni-corrected) in MATLAB (). Post-hoc pairwise comparisons were performed using Bonferroni correction to account for multiple testing across the 55 pairs in MATLAB. Multiple comparisons revealed several pairs with significant differences (Table S14). However, these pairs were not included in the subsequent EEG power calculations or comparisons of power between conditions. Therefore, we determined that differences in the number of excluded trials would not affect subsequent analyses.

Calculation of relative EEG power

The power spectrogram of each 1-s epoch, which was segmented in the preprocessing, was computed using a short-time Fourier transform (spectral window: Hamming window of length 500; number of overlapped samples: 250; number of discrete Fourier transform points: 500) in MATLAB. The window length and number of discrete Fourier transform points were determined to achieve a frequency resolution of 2 Hz, allowing for the extraction of integer-valued data for each band. The number of overlapped samples was set to achieve a 50% overlap. Epoch-averaged power spectra were calculated for each condition and participant. Frequency-averaged power spectra were then computed for the alpha (8–12 Hz) and beta (13–30 Hz) frequency bands for each condition within each participant. We determined the frequency band and performed baseline processing by referring to analyses from a previous study16 that examined the SoA and brain activity during exercise. The relative power between each condition and the resting condition was expressed as the decibel difference. Note, the relative power was calculated as the difference between the power during the movement condition and resting condition obtained during the same run. This was done to standardize the length of the resting period used as a baseline and to mitigate the influence of potential power fluctuations or electrode impedance changes between runs. Specifically, the relative power of the V condition was the difference between the V and resting condition in the first run. The relative power of the V + WS or WS, V + MS or MS, and V + SS or SS conditions were similarly determined by the difference between the respective condition and the resting condition in the second, third, and fourth runs.

Data screening

Our aim was to evaluate how movement mode (voluntary movement alone, voluntary movement with EMS, or EMS alone) and EMS intensity affect brain activity. Voluntary movement typically produces an EEG power decrease16,29; however, the EEG power change during EMS at various intensities during movement or rest is not clear. If EEG power does not decrease during voluntary movement in a participant, the brain activity related to their movement may not be adequately extracted. Hence, it may not be appropriate to include them in the analysis. Therefore, for each channel we selected participants whose relative EEG power in the V condition was negative. Outliers were removed using the interquartile range method to exclude data with abnormally large relative EEG powers possibly due to long-lasting EMS-induced artifacts.
