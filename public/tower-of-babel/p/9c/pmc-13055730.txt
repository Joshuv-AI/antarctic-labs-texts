Specific Deficiency of Naïve CD4+ T Lymphocytes Characterizes Heart Failure and Heightens Mortality Risk in the HRS
Authors: David A. Zidar, Michael L. Freeman, Brigid M. Wilson, Sadeer Al‐kindi, Mina K. Chung, Douglas D. Gunzler, Sahil A. Parikh, Ashish A. Sharma, Carey L. Shive, Mukesh Jain, Jacek Skarbinski, W. H. Wilson Tang, Jarrod E. Dalton, Donald D. Anthony, Timothy A. Chan, Bharat Thyagarajan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) is associated with inflammation, but its specific immunophenotypic characteristics and relevant prognostic relationships are poorly established.MethodsIn the HRS (Health and Retirement Study; N=9566), we used multivariable logistic regression, linear regression, and Cox proportional hazard models to describe cross‐sectional relationships between HF, plasma biomarkers, leukocyte levels, lymphocyte subsets enumerated by flow cytometry, and all‐cause survival. We then analyzed real‐world lymphocyte levels, incident HF, and death among outpatients within the US Veterans Affairs Health System (N=38 565).ResultsHF was accompanied by elevated inflammatory cytokine levels as well as relative lymphopenia. This lymphopenia was due to marked reduction in the reservoir of naïve CD4+ T cells, whereas total CD8+ T‐cell numbers were not reduced, and the CD4+ effector memory niche was expanded. Importantly, among those without HF, total lymphocytes and naïve CD4+ T cells were each inversely related to aminoterminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide levels and signaled a higher mortality rate. Linkages between lymphocyte deficiency, HF, and survival were robust to adjustment for low physical activity and a variety of inflammatory biomarkers. In the Veterans Affairs system, real‐world lymphopenia (absolute lymphocyte count <1.2: ≈11.5% of outpatient complete blood cell counts) is associated with incident HF (adjusted hazard ratio [aHR], 1.60 [95% CI, 1.38–1.86]; P<0.001) and also portends a poor subsequent prognosis (aHR, 1.43 [95% CI, 1.20–1.70]; P<0.001).ConclusionsHF is associated with total lymphopenia due to a specific deficiency of naïve CD4+ T lymphocytes. Crude lymphopenia among the general ambulatory population can herald incident HF and poor subsequent survival.

Heart failure (HF) is known to be associated with heightened inflammation 1 , 2 through a variety of danger‐sensing pathways and via cytokine production by resident cardiac cells, including cardiomyocytes. 3 , 4 Less is known about the relationship between HF and lymphocyte homeostasis. At the cellular/molecular level, HF and its multiple secondary effects (eg, neurohormonal excess, oxidative/metabolic alterations) ultimately exert both stimulatory and immunosuppressive effects on lymphocyte activation, survival, and trafficking. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 At a population level, those with HF have a lower percentage of lymphocytes, 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 which is partially explained by a modest neutrophilia as well as an absolute lymphopenia. 14 Prognostically, these alterations can be highly relevant. In mediation analyses, more than half of the excessive mortality risk experienced by those with chronic HF during the first wave of the COVID‐19 pandemic was explained by preexisting leukocyte alterations reported in complete blood cell counts (CBCs) months before infection. 14

Lymphocytes include dendritic cells (DCs), natural killer (NK) cells, T lymphocytes and B lymphocytes, each further divisible into subsets with differing functions and physiologic implications. For instance, the expansion of antigen‐experienced T cells (ie, memory cells) can be seen after viral infections (eg, cytomegalovirus) and often coincides with a propensity for inflammatory tissue damage. In contrast, the niche of naïve T cells represent lymphocytes that have not been previously activated but harbor the breadth of antigen receptor diversity required to mount effective adaptive host responses to new exposures.

Using the HRS (Health and Retirement Study) 2016 VBS (Venous Blood Study), we sought to broadly analyze immunologic correlates (cytokine levels, leukocytes numbers, and lymphocyte subsets) in a large cohort of ambulatory adults to identify the immunophenotypic aspects of HF. Second, in a large real‐world population of US veterans without an HF diagnosis, we then examined the relationship between lymphopenia and incident HF and death. We find HF to be associated with lymphopenia, a relationship that holds after adjustment for a variety of potential clinical and inflammatory confounders. This relationship is due primarily to a marked reduction in circulating naïve CD4+ T‐cell levels. Naïve CD4+ cell counts were also inversely related to NT‐proBNP (N‐terminal pro‐B‐type natriuretic peptide) levels and survival in the general HRS population without HF. In the US Veterans Affairs (VA) health care system, lymphopenia was associated with a higher rate of HF and subsequent death. These findings establish for the first time unique immunologic characteristics that accompany HF and suggest relative deficiency in naive T cells and the heightened inflammation that each separately contribute to immunologic risk in those with clinical and preclinical HF.

Methods

Data Availability

HRS data are publicly available for download at https://hrs.isr.umich.edu/ as per study policies. VA data requests are considered in accordance with policies of the US Veterans Health Administration.

Study Approval

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Louis Stokes Cleveland Veterans Affairs Medical Center (Approval No. 1584022).

Study Design

This study included a biomarker analysis from the cohort of patients enrolled in the 2016 wave of the HRS. 15 The HRS was a longitudinal panel study that surveyed a representative sample of ≈20 000 people in America. These enrollees provided informed consent for data, blood collection, and periodic follow‐up. It was supported by the National Institute on Aging (U01AG009740) and the Social Security Administration and was conducted by the University of Michigan. We then conducted a real‐world analysis of lymphopenia and incident HF among US veterans receiving care within the VA health care system between January 2010 and December 31, 2019. These analyses did not require informed consent and were approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Louis Stokes Cleveland VA Medical Center.

HRS Study Population

This analysis is based on plasma and flow cytometry analyses from the HRS VBS. 15 Those missing a CBC (n=315) or having an absolute lymphocyte count >5.0×103/μL (n=52) were excluded, resulting in a final data set of 9566 individuals (Figure S1). Five‐year survival was ascertained through 2021 by household contact in subsequent waves and reported in the cross‐wave tracker file released in January 2024. HRS used a variety of methods to reach participants, including phone calls, in‐person visits, and mailings, to maximize recontact rates. Weighting was also used to account for missing data and mitigate potential bias introduced by loss to follow‐up.

Demographic features and clinical parameters included age, sex, race, ethnicity, diabetes, HF, active tobacco smoking, hypertension, dyslipidemia, prior myocardial infarction (MI), pulmonary disease, rheumatoid arthritis, cancer, alcohol use and number of drinks per day, and chronic kidney disease stage. Serum creatinine, blood urea nitrogen, and body mass index were used as continuous variables in adjusted models. Laboratory methods for these measurements have been previously described. 16 Chronic HF (N=291) was identified as a positive questionnaire response to PC036 (any heart condition), and either PC263 (ever had HF) or PC048 (ever had congestive HF), and PC050 (taking medicine for HF). Physical activity was based on the self‐reported assessment and scored as a 3‐tier categorical variable based on the response to PC225 (mildly energetic sports or activity participation: hardly ever/never, 1–4 times/month, or more than once a week). Investigations related to subclinical HF was performed using NT‐proBNP among those without HF (N=9022), expressed as quintiles or as standardized units after natural log transformation.

VA Prospective HF Cohort

To analyze the real‐world relationship between lymphopenia and incident HF, data were extracted from the VA Corporate Data Warehouse through the VA Informatics and Computing Infrastructure. This population (see Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials diagram, Figure S2) consisted of a random sample of 50 000 US veterans receiving care through the VA who (1) had an absolute lymphocyte count (ALC) as part of an outpatient CBC reported between January 1, 2010, and March 31, 2010, and limited to those patients who (2) had no HF, prior MI, or cancer (based on standard International Classification of Diseases, Ninth/Tenth Revision [ICD‐9/10] codes 17 ) documented at the time of the CBC (final N=38 565).

HRS Immunologic Markers

Methods and measurement details for assays used in the HRS 2016 VBS have been previously described. 16 Serum‐based assays used in this analysis included tumor necrosis factor receptor 1 (TNF‐R1), high‐sensitivity C‐reactive protein, interleukin‐6, interleukin‐1 receptor antagonist, and interleukin‐10. Cytomegalovirus exposure was determined via the detection of plasma antibodies. Leukocyte levels were measured from EDTA whole blood using a Sysmex XE‐2100 instrument (Sysmex America, Inc., Lincolnshire, IL) and included the absolute neutrophil count, absolute monocyte count, ALC, absolute basophil count, and absolute eosinophil count. Lymphocyte subsets were measured using flow cytometry and included DC counts (myeloid DC, plasmacytoid DC, and total), NK cell (CD56‐high, CD56‐low, and total), B lymphocytes (naïve, immunoglobulin D [Ig D]+ memory, IgD‐memory, and total), total T cells, CD4+ T cells (naïve, central memory [CM], effector memory [EM], EM expressing receptor antagonist [TEMRA], and total), and CD8 T cell (naïve, CM, EM, TEMRA, and total) counts as described previously. 16 Biomarkers that were skewed or kurtotic were natural log transformed (all except for absolute neutrophil count, ALC, absolute basophil count, total T cells, CD4+ T cells, naïve CD4+ T cells, and CD4+ CM T cells). Zero values were converted to 0.00001 to enable log transformations. Immune parameters were also expressed as standardized values to facilitate cross‐comparisons. Naïve CD4+ T‐cell levels were additionally analyzed as a categorical variable (according to tertiles).

Statistical Analysis

Means±SDs and 95% CIs were analyzed for continuous variables, and proportions were used to describe categorical variables. Data removal was used to handle missingness. To identify associations between prevalent HF and immunologic parameters, logistic regression was used, with individual biomarkers positioned as “predictor” variables recognizing that the underlying mechanisms are highly complex, adaptive, and likely to be multidirectional at the biologic level. We examined results from unadjusted models (model 1), models adjusted for demographic features (model 2: age, sex, race, ethnicity), and after additional adjustment for cardiovascular disease (CVD)/risk (model 3: hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, tobacco smoking, body mass index, prior MI, blood urea nitrogen, serum creatinine, diabetes or HF, and subsample weights). In a similar way, for analyses of subclinical disease in the HRS among those without prevalent HF, NT‐proBNP was used as the dependent outcome variable in linear regression models in which immunologic biomarkers were tested as predictor values in CVD/risk‐adjusted models (model 3).

As sensitivity analyses within the HRS cohort, we explored relationships between ALC or naïve CD4+ T‐cell levels and HF in models additionally adjusting for other comorbidities (model 4: model 3 plus cancer, lung diseases, rheumatoid arthritis, alcohol use and number of drinks per day, chronic kidney disease stage), inflammatory biomarkers (model 5: model 4 plus TNF‐R1, C‐reactive protein, interleukin‐6, interleukin‐1 receptor antagonist, interleukin‐10), prior cytomegalovirus exposure (model 6: model 5 plus cytomegalovirus), physical inactivity (model 7: model 6 plus physical activity), naïve CD4+ lymphocytes (model 8: model 7 plus naïve CD4+ T‐cell levels), and for all other lymphocyte subsets (model 9: model 7 plus myeloid DC, plasmacytoid DC, CD56‐high NK cells, CD56‐low NK cells, naïve B cells, IgD+ memory B cells, IgD− memory B cells, CD4+ CM, CD4+ EM, CD4+ TEMRA, CD8+ naïve, CD8+ CM, CD8+ EM, and CD8+ TEMRA).

Survival analyses were performed using multivariable Cox proportional hazard models in which immunophenotypic variables including ALC and naïve CD4+ T cells were considered as continuous predictors. ALC was also considered as a categorical variable, with lymphopenia defined according to clinical laboratory cutoff (ALC <1.2×109 cells/mL). Unadjusted and various adjusted models were analyzed controlling for the same variables as those listed above (models 1–9) for the HRS cohort.

Time‐to‐event analyses performed within the VA cohort also included examination of incident HF diagnosis within 3 years according to ALC strata. Competing risk models were used to analyze this incidence of an HF diagnosis versus death. Kaplan–Meier analyses were also used to illustrate HF‐free survival in which death before HF was a censoring event. Unadjusted models and models adjusting for age, sex, race, ethnicity, and comorbidities (Charlson comorbidity index score) were analyzed. A sensitivity analysis was performed, which also included other CBC‐reported leukocyte levels. Among those with incident HF, a landmark analysis was performed to compare time to all‐cause death after HF according to the original ALC strata.

In general, point estimates of effects and their 95% CIs are shown. Two‐sided P values without adjustment for multiplicity are also reported, with P<0.05 interpreted as statistically significant by convention, 18 although the results herein should be considered exploratory since there were no prespecified analyses.

Software

Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna Austria) and SPSS version 28, and visual outputs were generated using R, Prism (GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA), and BioRender (BioRender, Toronto, ON, Canada).

Cytokine Levels in HRS Participants With HF

To determine the extent to which chronic HF was associated with a proinflammatory milieu, we analyzed the levels of multiple cytokines in unadjusted and adjusted analyses. HF was associated with higher levels of TNF‐R1, interleukin‐6, interleukin‐1 receptor antagonist, interleukin‐10, and C‐reactive protein in unadjusted analyses, and observed differences were largely independent of demographic and clinical features (Figure 1; Table S1). For instance, the mean (natural log) of TNF‐R1 level was 7.75 and 7.41±0.40 pg/mL in those with and without HF, respectively (P<0.001). In analyses adjusted for demographics (ie, Model 2), 1 SD increase in TNF‐R1 was associated with 63% higher odds of having prevalent HF (adjusted odds ratio [aOR], 1.63 [95% CI, 1.51–1.77]; P<0.001). In models also adjusted for differences in prevalent CVD and risk factors (model 3), 1 SD increase in TNF‐R1 was associated with a 40% higher odds of having prevalent HF (aOR, 1.40 [95% CI, 1.19–1.64]; P<0.001).

Prevalent HF Is Associated With Lymphopenia and Specific CD4+ T‐Lymphocyte Subset Deficiencies

In general, cytokine levels correlated with each other but did not have strong relationships with lymphocyte levels (Figure S2). Therefore, we next sought to apply the same approach to analyze leukocyte subsets in those with HF. Relative neutrophilia and monocytemia was a feature of HF in univariable analyses, but this relationship was attenuated after adjustment for differences in demographics and CVD/risk (Figure 2; Table S2). In contrast, HF was associated with relative lymphopenia, a relationship that holds after adjustment for clinical/demographic features. For instance, the mean ALC was 1.78 and 1.96±0.69×109 cells/mL in those with and without HF, respectively (P<0.001). After demographic/clinical adjustment, HF remained independently associated with lower lymphocyte counts (aOR, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.68–0.91] per SD increase; P=0.001). Among lymphocyte subpopulations, HF‐associated lymphopenia was characterized by a contraction of T lymphocytes, B lymphocytes, and CD56‐low NK cells, whereas DC levels were preserved in adjusted analyses.

Within the T lymphocyte niche, HF was associated with reduced CD4+ T cells, whereas total CD8+ T‐cell levels were equal in those with and without HF (Figure 3; Table S3). This deficiency among CD4+ T cells was due in large part to a marked reduction in naïve CD4+ T cells. For instance, the mean naïve CD4+ T cell level in those with and without HF was 0.32 versus 0.43±0.30×109 cells/mL (P<0.0001). This difference remained significant after adjustment for demographics and CVDs and risk factors such that 1 SD increase in naïve CD4+ T cells was associated with 27% lower odds of having prevalent HF (aOR, 0.73 [95% CI, 0.62–0.87]; P<0.001). In contrast, CD4+ EM cells were higher in those with HF (aOR, 1.20 [95% CI, 1.03–1.40] per SD increase; P=0.021). CD4+ CM and CD4+ TEMRA cell numbers were similar in those with and without HF in adjusted analyses. Naïve CD8+ T cells, naïve B cells, and both subtypes of memory B cells were also reduced in HF in unadjusted and adjusted analyses.

Lymphocytes and NT‐ProBNP Levels in HRS Participants Without HF

To determine whether alterations in lymphocyte homeostasis are also linked to preclinical HF (ie, stage B), we analyzed the ALC and naïve CD4+ T‐cell levels according to NT‐proBNP quintiles among those without HF in the HRS population (Figure 4; Table S4). The mean ALC declined as a function of increasing NT‐proBNP quintile. Similarly, the mean naïve CD4+ T‐cell count was sequentially diminished in those with progressively higher NT‐proBNP quintiles. In adjusted analyses (model 3), the ALC (adjusted β of 1 SD increase in ALC, −0.10 [95% CI, −0.12 to −0.08] per 1 SD increase in NT‐proBNP; P<0.001) and CD4+ naïve T‐cell levels (adjusted β=−0.05 [95% CI, −0.04 to −0.07]; P<0.001) remained significantly reduced in relation to higher NT‐proBNP. CD4+ CM was also significantly reduced (adjusted β=−0.095 [95% CI, −0.07 to −0.11]; P<0.001) while CD4+ EM cells were elevated (adjusted β=0.03 [95% CI, 0.01–0.05]; P=0.008) in those with higher NT‐proBNP. These analyses suggest that HF‐associated alterations in T‐cell homeostasis may precede the clinical onset or recognition of the disease, and subclinical HF contributes to heterogeneity in T‐cell numbers in the general population.

Naïve CD4+ T‐Lymphocyte Levels and Death in the HRS

We next sought to analyze the relevance of lymphopenia, both crude ALC and naïve CD4+ T‐cell levels specifically, for long‐term survival (Table 3). One SD increase in ALC was associated with reduced death in unadjusted analyses (model 1: hazard ratio [HR], 0.67 [95% CI, 0.62–0.71]; P<0.001), analyses adjusted for demographics alone (model 2: aHR, 0.85 [95% CI, 0.79–0.92]; P<0.001), or models additionally controlling for cardiovascular differences (model 3: aHR, 0.81 [95% CI, 0.74–0.87]; P<0.001). Furthermore, additional adjustment for comorbidities (model 4), cytokine elevation (model 5), previous cytomegalovirus infection (model 6), and physical inactivity (model 7) did not significantly attenuate the relationship between total lymphopenia and reduced survival. Adjustment for naïve CD4+ T‐cell levels did attenuate this association (aHR, 0.917 [95% CI, 0.838–1.004]; P=0.061), suggesting that naïve CD4+ T cells are a major lymphocyte subset explaining the linkage between ALC and survival.

“Real‐World” Lymphopenia Is Prospectively Associated With Incident HF and Death

To explore the external, real‐world validity/applicability of these findings, we analyzed the incidence of HF in the VA system, according to lymphocyte levels obtained during outpatient encounters (Figure S3). Characteristics of this population are shown in Table S5. Among 38 565 patients without prevalent HF, prior MI, or history of cancer (mean age, 61.7 years, 7.9% women, 18.9% non‐White race, 7.9% Hispanic ethnicity, 11.5% lymphopenic [ALC<1.2 ×109/mL]), the incident diagnosis of HF was observed in 2.7%, 5.8%, 8.5%, and 15.3% within 1, 3, 5, and 10 years, respectively.

Figure 5A shows the time to the incident diagnosis of HF according to antecedent ALC levels, with death as a censoring event. The time to HF or death in those with and without lymphopenia is shown in competing risk models in Figure S4. The results of unadjusted and adjusted Cox proportional hazard models for time to incident HF (competing risk model) are shown according to ALC strata in Table 4 (leftward columns). Compared with those with an ALC >2.5×109/mL, lymphopenia was associated with a higher risk of HF in unadjusted analyses (HR, 2.55 [95% CI, 2.21–2.94]; P<0.001) and in models adjusted for demographics and comorbidities (aHR, 1.60 [95% CI, 1.38–1.86]; P<0.001). In these competing risk models, the corresponding risk of death without HF was also elevated in those with lymphopenia (aHR, 1.81 [95% CI, 1.58–2.06]; P<0.001). In contrast, lymphopenia was not associated with incident MI (aHR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.79–1.02]; P=0.109) out to 10‐year follow‐up. The strength of this lymphopenia/HF association was comparable to the association between HF and a decade of chronologic age (aHR, 1.53 [95% CI, 1.48–1.58] per 10 years; P<0.001) or between HF and 2 points on the Charlson comorbidity index (aHR, 1.30 [95% CI, 1.27–1.32] per point; P<0.001). When other leukocyte levels were added to the model, the risk of incident HF associated with lymphopenia was not diminished (aHR, 1.82 [95% CI, 1.36–2.42]), suggesting that this relationship was robust to other CBC‐reported inflammatory alterations.

Discussion

We analyzed the HRS to identify the immunologic characteristics associated with HF. In unadjusted analyses and in models controlling for demographics and other potential confounders, HF was associated with elevated cytokine levels but also associated with lymphopenia due primarily to marked reductions in naïve CD4+ T cells. Additionally, lymphopenia and reduced naïve CD4+ T cell levels were observed in those without HF in association with elevated NT‐proBNP levels. Lymphopenia, especially naïve CD4+ T‐cell lymphopenia, was strongly associated with death, even after accounting for demographic, clinical, and other immunologic features in the HRS. In the VA system, low lymphocyte levels were independently associated with higher rates of incident HF and a poor prognosis thereafter. Together, this large immunophenotypic analysis suggests that HF is characterized by disruptions in T‐cell homeostasis in addition to heightened inflammation. CBCs obtained during routine care may be sufficient to identify T‐cell dyshomeostasis and elevated HF risk and recognize those with a poor prognosis on this basis.

Immunophenotypic Characterization of HF

Cytokine elevation has been previously associated with HF and may be attributed to production from the heart as well as from generalized innate immune activation. 19 , 20 Among those with HF, the percentage of lymphocytes is an established prognostic risk factor. 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 , 13 However, a deep characterization of the immunophenotypic features that distinguish those with and without HF, including an examination of subsets that comprise the lymphocyte niche, has been lacking.

In this study, we identify a robust relationship between HF and absolute lymphopenia. Rather than a general loss of lymphocyte numbers, our results suggest some specificity to this phenomenon: Naïve CD4+ T cells, CD56‐low NK cells, and B cells were selectively diminished in HF, while DCs were maintained. Interestingly, CD4+ EM cells, which have proinflammatory characteristics, were expanded. Several direct and indirect mechanisms could be relevant to the naïve CD4+ lymphopenia. TNF‐R1 activation is a prototypical pathway for T‐cell apoptosis, 21 and interleukin‐6 can impair interleukin‐7–mediated naïve T‐cell replication. 22 Each was elevated in HF, but analyses meant to adjust for these pathways and other cytokines did not substantially attenuate the relationship between lymphopenia and HF or lymphopenia and death.

In addition to the proinflammatory milieu, several additional plausible mechanisms may account for the loss of circulating naïve T cells in HF. Under healthy aging conditions, naïve T cells with a broad repertoire of antigen specificity recirculate between the blood and lymphoid organs and undergo low‐grade replication to provide responsiveness to new pathogens. Altered hemodynamics, heightened adrenergic and neurohormonal signaling, and changes in homeostatic chemokine receptor regulation (ie, C‐C chemokine receptor type 7 and sphingosine‐1 phosphate receptors, ligands, and G‐protein–coupled receptor kinases) each accompany HF and could interact to disrupt lymphocyte recirculation and homeostasis. 7 , 23 , 24 , 25 Interleukin‐7, a cytokine critical for the proliferation and maintenance of this naïve pool, has been shown to be reduced in HF. 26 Reactive oxygen species, oxidized lipoproteins, nitric oxide production, Krüppel‐like factor expression, and other vasculoactive pathways are also altered in HF and have known effects on T‐cell physiology, including T‐cell hyporesponsiveness and upregulation of programmed cell death 1 expression in naïve T cells. 5 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Thus, a deeper examination of the direct and indirect effects of neurohormonal and cardiometabolic stimuli on the lymphocyte niche is justified in light of our findings.

Naïve CD4+ T Cells as a Novel Prognostic Factor in HF

Naïve CD4+ T cells are generated in the thymus during youth, and significant heterogeneity is known to exist in the maintenance of this pool during the aging process. 31 Altered T‐cell homeostasis and chronic innate immune activation are aspects of immunologic aging, but how these processes intersect with each other, with chronic disease, and with longevity remain incompletely understood. 31 , 32 , 33 , 34 New in this report is that HF (and NT‐proBNP elevation in the general population) is associated with marked naïve CD4+ T‐cell depletion, which is in turn linked to reduced survival. The large, diverse population of the HRS also enabled a rigorous examination of potential confounders including models adjusting for up to 40 different demographic, clinical, and inflammatory features.

This analysis is consistent with observations from our group and others showing that crude lymphocyte levels in ambulatory adults is a risk factor for death, 35 including death from CVD. 35 , 36 Here, we validate and extend our understanding of the relationship between lymphopenia and death. Our data suggest that crude lymphopenia and its associated risk may be a manifestation of diminished naïve CD4+ T cells rather than loss of lymphocytes in general. Diminished naïve lymphocytes may partially reflect HF physiology, and its risk is seemingly not a result of confounding from other variables such as cytomegalovirus, physical inactivity, and inflammation. These data add to the growing evidence that crude lymphocyte levels assist in the identification of those with T‐cell dyshomeostasis and guide population‐level studies and/or pragmatic immune‐profiling efforts.

Study Limitations

The strengths of this study include the multicohort analysis, the relatively detailed immunoprofiling available in the HRS, and sample sizes enabling adjustment for a broad spectrum of potential confounders. However, several limitations should be highlighted. The HRS does not include assessments of T‐cell function. The cellular biomarkers reflect levels enumerated from thawed cells recovered after cryopreservation. There may be additional inflammatory pathways of relevance that were unmeasured in this analysis. Since prevalent diseases were based on questionnaire responses, these analyses are subject to underreporting and/or misclassification. Detailed reporting of CVD characteristics (prior revascularization procedures, left ventricular ejection fraction, medication usage) is not available in the HRS. The extent to which these relationships hold for different endotypes of HF (preserved versus reduced ejection fraction, ischemic versus nonischemic, etc) and for different demographic groups will require additional study. Furthermore, the impact of HF therapies on these relationships and characterizing the complex operative mechanisms leading to naïve CD4+ T‐cell deficiency deserves special attention in future studies. Given that the observations herein were not prespecified, they should be regarded as hypothesis generating.

Summary

T‐cell homeostasis is altered in HF, resulting in differences in several specific subsets, ultimately manifesting as absolute lymphopenia. This lymphopenia primarily reflects deficiency in naïve CD4+ T cells. We find that naïve CD4+ T cells are specifically associated with prevalent HF and are associated with NT‐proBNP elevation and survival in those without HF. These relationships are not necessarily explained by heightened inflammation. Real‐world lymphopenia signals a higher risk of incident HF and death in the near term, observations with population health implications. Whether causal relationships exist between lymphocyte homeostasis, HF, and survival requires additional study.
