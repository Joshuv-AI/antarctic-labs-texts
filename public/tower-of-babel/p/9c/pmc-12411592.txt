Assessment of the role of probiotics in prevention of ventilator-associated pneumonia in neonates
Authors: Hager Ahmed Khodair, Heba Said Elmahdy, Lamiaa Khaled Zidan, Sara Mohamed Elashry, Abd Elrahman Mohamed Elmashad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeVentilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) is a severe complication in NICUs. It increases morbidity, mortality, and healthcare costs. The research purpose was to evaluate the preventive value of probiotics on the incidence of VAP among ventilated neonates.MethodsThis prospective randomized controlled study was done at the NICU of Tanta University Hospitals for one year. Eighty full-term neonates who required invasive mechanical ventilation for over 48 h were randomly divided into a probiotic group (n = 40) and a non-probiotic group (n = 40). Besides the standard treatment that was given to both groups, the probiotic group received a sachet containing 1 × 109 CFU of lactic acid bacteria twice a day, starting from the 1st day of recruitment until discharge. Neonates were screened for VAP incidence based on clinical and laboratory evidence.ResultsThe incidence of VAP was significantly lower in the probiotics group (20%) compared to the non-probiotic group (47.5%) with an OR of 0.28 (95% CI: 0.10–0.75). Additionally, administration of probiotics was associated with a significantly lower incidence of feeding intolerance, vomiting, and abdominal distension (17.5%, 12.5%, and 10.0% vs. 44.0%, 40.0%, and 44.0% in probiotic and non-probiotic groups, respectively) with OR = 0.26, 0.21, and 0.18, respectively. On the other hand, there was a significantly shorter duration of mechanical ventilation in the probiotic group over the non-probiotic group (MD = 10 days, 95% CI: 6.30–13.70). Similarly, NICU stay was significantly shorter in the probiotic group compared to the non-probiotic group (MD = 8 days, 95% CI: 3.29–12.71). Conclusion Probiotics seem to be effective in the prevention of VAP among mechanically ventilated neonates.Clinical trial registration: Clinicaltrials.gov/ NCT07001163; registered May 23, 2025. Introduction

Mechanical ventilation (MV) represents a crucial advancement in neonatology, offering essential life-saving support for neonates experiencing progressive respiratory distress and imminent respiratory failure. However, its application carries a significant risk of developing VAP, underscoring the challenges associated with this critical intervention [1]. VAP is the second most common healthcare-associated infection (HAI) in NICUs; it is associated with increased morbidity and mortality, prolonged hospital stays, and elevated healthcare costs [2].

The susceptibility of neonates to VAP is heightened by their physiological and immunological immaturity, making them more vulnerable to bacterial infections and related complications during their NICU stay [2].

The Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) defines VAP as requiring at least two consecutive days of invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV), coupled with evidence of deteriorating gas exchange and radiographic findings such as new or worsening pulmonary infiltrates. This stringent definition underscores the diagnostic complexities and challenges in standardizing VAP identification [3].

The lung, previously considered a sterile organ, is now recognized to harbor its microbiota, maintained through a dynamic balance of microbial immigration from the upper respiratory tract and elimination by host defense mechanisms. Disruption of this balance during MV may facilitate pathogenic colonization and subsequent infection, highlighting the need for innovative prevention strategies [4].

Traditional approaches to reduce VAP incidence have centered on bundled care practices, including strict hand hygiene, head-of-bed elevation, and comprehensive oral care. However, emerging research suggests that probiotics, defined as live microorganisms that confer health benefits when administered in adequate amounts, have shown potential in modulating immune responses, strengthening mucosal barriers, competing with pathogenic bacteria, and may offer a novel avenue for prevention [2, 5].

In neonatal care, probiotics have shown efficacy in reducing conditions, such as necrotizing enterocolitis (NEC) and late-onset sepsis in preterm infants [2]. However, their role in preventing VAP among mechanically ventilated neonates is still controversial. This study hypothesized that probiotics could be used as a preventive strategy against VAP in NICU settings.

Patients and methods

Trial design, setting, and ethical approval

This prospective randomized controlled research was conducted at the level 3 NICU of Tanta University Children's Hospital after authorization by the ethics council of the Faculty of Medicine, Tanta University, Egypt, with approval number (36264MS172/5/23). The informed permission of the parents of all recruited neonates was obtained. Additionally, this trial was registered with ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07001163) on May 23, 2025, and was conducted in accordance with the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. Enrollment criteria included full-term neonates (gestational age 37 to 42 weeks) who required mechanical ventilation for more than 48 h.

Study period, population, and interventions

Patients were enrolled from May 2023 to May 2024. During the research period, 205 neonates required mechanical ventilation of whom 80 term infants were enrolled in the study and the other 125 infants were excluded (Fig. 1) according to the exclusion criteria that included preterm neonates, neonates with early onset sepsis or pre-existing pneumonia, patient who was unfit for enteral intake, neonates with surgical causes of mechanical ventilation and infants with multiple congenital anomalies or suspected chromosomal anomalies.

CONSORT flow diagram

All patients were invasively ventilated by an endotracheal tube using a Neumovent mechanical ventilator (Neumovent Graphnet Ts, Tecme Corporation) with a heated humidification system and disposable ventilator circuits. The enrolled neonates were randomized to probiotic therapy in the form of powder sachets for oral suspension. These sachets contained 1 × 109 cfu of lactic acid bacteria (Bifidobacterium Animalis subsp. lactis BB-12). The content of one sachet was suspended in 5 ml of sterile water and given via orogastric tube twice daily (2 × 109 cfu/day), starting from the 1 st day of recruitment and continued till discharge. Also, the recruited cases of both groups received all routine care including VAP preventive measures as per hospital protocols and antibiotics therapy as necessary and under the direction of their admitting physicians, according to the international guidelines, the policy of the center for the most common organisms and modified according to the result of the culture and sensitivity.

Study definitions

The diagnostic criteria of VAP were monitored in all recruited neonates. According to the CDC [3], VAP was defined in neonates and infants under one year of age who were mechanically ventilated for more than 48 h with both worsening gas exchange and presence of radiographic evidence of new or worsening lung changes. Additionally, at least 3 of the following findings were also required: temperature instability (rectal temperature > 38° or < 35.5°), leukopenia (WBC < 4 × 109/L), leukocytosis (WBC > 15 × 109/L) with a left shift (> 10% band forms), new or increased purulent sputum or respiratory secretions, change in character of sputum, increased respiratory secretions requiring increased suctioning frequency, apnea, increased work of breathing (e.g., tachypnea, retractions, grunting, nasal flaring), wheezing, rales, rhonchi, bradycardia (< 100 beats/min) or tachycardia (> 170 beats/min). Feeding intolerance signs were also monitored. This study defines feeding intolerance as remaining gastric residual of at least 50% of the total feeding volume administered over the last 3 h on at least two occasions over 24 h. That condition was beyond the minimal enteral nutrition defined as total milk volume in the range of 10–20 ml/kg/day. The residual volume was determined from the enteral feeding record form.

Data gathering

Following admission and enrollment, full antenatal, perinatal, and postnatal histories were taken together with a thorough clinical examination. Gestational age and admission weight were documented. Complete blood count (CBC), C-reactive protein (CRP), liver and kidney functions, capillary blood gases (CBG), automated blood culture, and non-bronchoscopic broncho-alveolar lavage (NB-BAL) culture and sensitivity were done. Plain X-ray chest anteroposterior and lateral views were also done on the 1 st, 3rd, and 7th days of mechanical ventilation, and repeated when needed.

Study outcomes

The primary outcome was to evaluate the effect of probiotics on the VAP incidence among mechanically ventilated neonates. The secondary outcomes were to assess the clinical effect of probiotics supplementation on feeding intolerance, vomiting, abdominal distention, duration of invasive mechanical ventilation (calculated from the initiation of invasive ventilation until successful extubation or death), and duration of NICU stay among those neonates.

Sample size estimation

The sample size was calculated based on previously reported rates of VAP in neonates, which range between 20 and 35% in similar NICU settings [2]. For this study, a VAP incidence of 31% in the non-probiotic group was assumed [6]. A 25% absolute reduction was considered clinically meaningful and consistent with findings from earlier probiotic intervention studies [5]. Using 5% significance (α = 0.05) with 80% power (β = 0.2), the minimum required sample size was estimated to be 36 neonates per group. By considering a 15% probability of attrition rate, about 40 subjects in each group were considered (N total = 80).

Randomization

Participants were randomly assigned to either the probiotic group or the non-probiotic group using block randomization to ensure equal distribution throughout the study. A fixed block size of four was used, and all possible balanced permutations of group assignments within each block were computer-generated. To maintain allocation concealment and prevent selection bias, randomization was conducted by a designated research coordinator at the study site, and assignments were placed in identical non-consecutive coded packages, which were opened only after participant enrollment.

Blinding

Producing a matching inert placebo for the probiotic preparation was not feasible within our institutional resources. Despite the open-label nature of the study, the authors minimized bias using standardized clinical protocols across both groups. Additionally, all primary and secondary outcomes, such as NB-BAL, duration of NICU stay, and days of mechanical ventilation, were objective and assessed by individuals who were not involved in group allocation or patient care. This approach helped reduce the risk of both performance and detection bias.

Statistical data

The IBM SPSS software program version 20.0 (Armonk, NY: IBM Corp) was used to analyze the data once it was entered into the computer. Quantitative variables were described using their means and standard deviations. Categorical variables were described using their absolute frequencies and were compared using the Chi-squared test and Fisher's exact test, when appropriate. The Mann–Whitney test (for non-normally distributed data) and independent sample test (for normally distributed data) were used to compare continuous quantitative data of two groups; The level of statistical significance was set at 5% (P < 0.05). No interim analyses or predefined stopping rules were planned for this trial. The study was conducted according to the original protocol and completed as scheduled.

To address potential confounding variables, a multivariate logistic regression analysis was performed to evaluate the association between probiotic use and VAP incidence while adjusting for gender, gestational age, and birth weight. The analysis revealed that probiotic administration remained independently associated with a significantly reduced risk of VAP (adjusted OR = 0.29, 95% CI: 0.10–0.82, p = 0.019). In contrast, gender, gestational age, and birth weight were not significantly associated with VAP risk (p > 0.05 for all). These findings reinforce the primary analysis, indicating that the observed benefit of probiotics is not attributable to differences in baseline characteristics (see Online Supplement).

The incidence of VAP was significantly lower in the probiotics group compared to the non-probiotic group, as 20% of cases in the probiotics group had VAP, compared to 47.5% of neonates in the non-probiotic group (p = 0.017) with an odds ratio (OR) of 0.28 (95% CI: 0.10–0.75) (Table 2).

The administration of probiotics was associated with a significantly lower incidence of feeding intolerance, vomiting, and abdominal distension (17.5%, 12.5%, and 10.0% vs. 45.0%, 40.0%, and 42.5% in probiotic and non-probiotic groups, respectively) with OR = 0.26, 0.21, and 0.18, respectively. On the other hand, there was a significantly shorter duration of mechanical ventilation in the probiotic group over the non-group (P = 0.001), with a mean difference (MD) of 10.0 days (95% CI: 6.30–13.70). Similarly, NICU stay was significantly shorter in the probiotic group compared to the non-probiotic group with MD = 8.00 days (95% CI: 3.29–12.71; p = 0.003). Regarding the prognosis of the enrolled cases, probiotics therapy non-significantly reduced the mortality risk (crude OR = 0.202) (Table 2).

Discussion

In the current study, there was no statistically significant difference between the two groups regarding baseline characteristics, which suggests effective randomization and supports the attribution of the observed outcomes to the research intervention rather than to pre-existing differences.

Regarding the VAP incidence as the primary outcome of this current study, the incidence rate was significantly higher in the non-probiotic group (47.5%) compared to the probiotic group (only 20%), with the total incidence among all 80 cases being 33.75%. Beyond statistical significance, the magnitude of the observed effects highlights the potential clinical relevance of probiotic supplementation. Notably, the odds ratio for VAP incidence was 0.28 (95% CI: 0.10–0.75), indicating a significant 72% reduction in the likelihood of developing VAP among neonates who received probiotics. This finding is not only statistically significant but also clinically meaningful, as it indicates that probiotics might improve the clinical outcomes in NICU settings.

The present results are consistent with those reported by Roshanzamiri et al. [5], who investigated the preventative effects of probiotic prophylaxis on VAP in 72 mechanically ventilated pediatric patients. Their study showed a statistically significant reduction in VAP incidence in the probiotic group (21.05%) versus the placebo group (44.12%, p = 0.036).

Additionally, Branch-Elliman et al. [7] developed a cost–benefit model to determine the most cost-effective strategy to prevent VAP and examined 120 unique combinations of VAP prevention strategies. They documented that the application of prophylactic probiotics and subglottic endotracheal tubes was a cost-effective measure for the prevention of VAP. Therefore, Branch-Elliman et al. had recommended that the application of probiotics for VAP prevention should be used in clinical practice in the current healthcare circumstance.

Regarding NB-BAL culture results among both studied groups, and similar to other previous studies [5, 6], this current research found that bacterial colonization was insignificantly lower in the probiotic group than in the non-probiotic group (20% and 48% in the probiotic and non-probiotic groups, respectively).

The previous data could be explained by a range of potent mechanisms that reduce the respiratory colonization of virulent bacterial species. Firstly, probiotics contribute to the balance of gut microbiota as gut-derived bacteria can translocate to the lungs and cause infections [8]. Additionally, probiotics enhance the mucosal barrier function by increasing the production of mucins and tight junction proteins, which prevent the bacterial translocation to the bloodstream and subsequently to the lungs [9]. Furthermore, probiotics also produce antimicrobial substances like bacteriocins and organic acids that actively combat pathogens and, through competitive exclusion, probiotics occupy niches on mucosal surfaces, reducing the colonization and proliferation of potential pathogens that can cause VAP [10]. Lastly, probiotics modulate the host immune response by enhancing both innate and adaptive immunity [8].

Feeding intolerance is one of the most worrisome NICU complications and may contribute to the increasing hospital stay and mortality rates, especially in preterm and critically ill neonates. The possible mechanisms include GIT immaturity, dysbiosis, gut motility, or tissue alteration, due to inflammation or hypoperfusion [11].

Regarding GIT manifestations as secondary outcomes among the two studied groups, the current study found a statistically significant decrease in the incidence of feeding intolerance, vomiting, and abdominal distention among the probiotic group cases.

These results agree with the meta-analysis of Zhang et al. [11] that revealed a significantly reduced incidence of feeding intolerance in preterm infants who received probiotics, indicating a substantial improvement in gastrointestinal function.

The proposed mechanisms by probiotics to improve feeding tolerance include helping beneficial microbial flora to colonize the gut to prevent pathogen colonization, enhancing intestinal mucus barrier function, and modifying host responses to pathological microbial products. Also, probiotics enhance the innate immune defense by increasing the production of mucosal IgA, enhancing leukocyte phagocytosis, and reducing the production of inflammatory cytokines. Although many developed countries are already using probiotics routinely in preterm neonates for prevention of NEC, the specific mechanism of probiotic supplementation on gastrointestinal function is not yet fully clear [12, 13].

Regarding the duration of MV as another secondary outcome among the two studied groups, this study demonstrated a statistically and clinically significant reduction in both the duration of MV and the length of NICU stay among neonates receiving probiotic supplementation. Specifically, the median MV duration was 12.0 days (IQR: 9.5–17.5) in the probiotic group compared to 22.0 days (IQR: 13.0–27.0) in the non-probiotic group (p = 0.001). Similarly, NICU stay was significantly shorter in the probiotic group (27.0 days [IQR: 18.5–32.5] vs. 35.0 days [IQR: 30.0–45.0], p = 0.003).

These findings agree with Sharif et al. [14], who reported that probiotics administration had been associated with a reduction in the duration of mechanical ventilation, with a mean difference of 2.53 days fewer in the probiotic group compared to the control group. Additionally, these results agree with Gohr et al. [15] who reported prolonged duration of hospital stay (mean ± SD 40.3 ± 14.9 days) among non-probiotic group cases.

In fact, these observed reductions in the MV and NICU stay durations are clinically meaningful as they imply the probiotics'beneficial effects beyond gastrointestinal health, possibly through modulation of the gut-lung axis. Furthermore, the significant decrease in the NICU stay has profound implications for healthcare resource utilization and cost-effectiveness, especially in high-risk neonatal populations.

Regarding the prognosis of the cases as the final secondary outcome among the two studied groups, this current study found no significant difference between the two groups. These results coincide with Ibrahim et al. [16] who found that the administration of probiotics did not result in any improvement in the mortality rates.

Although this research data underscores the potential role of probiotics as a low-cost, biologically plausible intervention to improve outcomes and reduce the burden of intensive care in neonates, some limitations should be noted. Firstly, the study's single-center design may impact the external validity and generalizability of the results. Secondly, while the sample size was sufficient to detect the primary outcome, a larger cohort could enhance statistical power and facilitate more extensive subgroup analyses. The last limitation could be the lack of blinding or placebo control. However, this was a pragmatic, real-world trial designed to evaluate the clinical effectiveness of probiotic supplementation in a routine NICU setting, and although blinding was not feasible due to logistical constraints and the unavailability of a matching placebo formulation, objective outcome measures diminish the potential bias impact. Furthermore, outcome assessors and data analysts were not involved in patient care and were unaware of group assignments, which helped reduce detection bias.

Conclusions

VAP continues to be a significant complication in NICUs. The pathogenesis of VAP is multifactorial, and the measures to prevent this HAI should also entail multiple interventions or distinct steps in the care of newborns that operate synergistically. Probiotics therapy is one of the measures that seems very promising, with a significant decrease in the incidence of VAP.
