Neuro Data Hub: A New Approach for Streamlining Medical Clinical Research
Authors: Xu Han, Anton Alyakin, Shannon Ciprut, Cathryn Lapierre, Jaden Stryker, John Golfinos, Douglas Kondziolka, Eric Karl Oermann
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND AND OBJECTIVES:Neurosurgical clinical research depends on medical data collection and evaluation that is often laborious, time consuming, and inefficient. The goal of this work was to implement and evaluate a novel departmental data infrastructure (Neuro Data Hub) designed to provide specialized data services for neurosurgical research. Data acquisition would become available purely by request.METHODS:We established the New York University Langone Health Neuro Data Hub through collaboration between Department Leadership and Medical Center Information Technology, integrating it with Institutional Review Board workflows and an existing Epic electronic health record Datalake infrastructure. The system implementation included monthly departmental meetings and an asynchronous Research Electronic Data Capture–based request system. Data requests submitted between August 2023 and November 2024 were analyzed and categorized as basic, complex, or Natural Language Processing (NLP)–augmented, with optional visualization and database creation services. Request volumes, types, and execution times were assessed.RESULTS:The Hub processed 39 research data requests (2.6/month), comprising 3 basic, 22 complex, and 14 NLP-augmented requests. Two complex requests included visualization services, and one NLP request included database creation. Average request execution time was 36.5 days, with NLP-augmented requests showing increasing adoption over time.CONCLUSION:The Neuro Data Hub represents a paradigm shift from centralized to department-level data services, providing specialized support for neurosurgical research and democratizing access to institutional data. While effective, implementation may be limited by institutional information technology infrastructure requirements. This model could serve as a template for any form of medical-clinical research program seeking to improve data accessibility and research capabilities.

Medical progress is dependent on both clinical and basic science research. Many current methodologies of clinical research rely on clinical data acquisition from “the charts” by manual extraction. This manual extraction of data from electronic medical records is slow, inefficient, laborious, and often a major obstacle to research conduct in neurosurgery. The increasing integration of neurosurgical practices into larger health systems or hospitals can further complicate research efforts by making it difficult to access data stored on general systems usually in general data schema by means other than manual access of the electronic medical records.

More recently, with the rise of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies, there have also been efforts to extract large neurosurgical datasets for machine learning efforts.1 Although some of these efforts have drawn on the scientific literature itself2,3 and other efforts leverage large multi-center registries4,5 such as NeuroPoint or the National Surgical Quality Improvement Program (NSQIP),4-9 there is still a major gap between the accessibility of large datasets and the need for conducting machine learning or observational research with local data and patients.

Recently, medical centers and hospitals have created “datacores” to bridge this gap.10,11 Datacores are a centralized service consisting of honest brokers in hospital information technology (IT) to provision data resources throughout the medical center. Although this poses a potential solution to closing the data gap, there are still barriers to using these services. Most notably from a neurosurgical standpoint, centralized datacores suffer from a lack of domain expertise and medical knowledge and present a difficulty in translating neurosurgical needs into datacore service language. In 2022, New York University (NYU) Langone Health Medical Center Information Technology launched an initiative to rethink this centralized approach around Departmental Data Hubs—with each department running its own data service and the centralized datacore providing support. We present our results for the first year of operation of the NYU Langone Health Neuro Data Hub that has been developed and deployed within our department to provide data and data engineering resources to Neurosurgery Department–affiliated neurosurgeons, neurologists, and neuroscience researchers to address their unique scientific and engineering questions and data needs (Figure 1A).

METHODS

Preapprovals

No Institutional Review Board (IRB) was required for the establishment of the Neuro Data Hub. The Neuro Data Hub itself is integrated into the IRB workflow, with all requests requiring IRB approval and validation by the Neuro Data Hub staff. No procedures were performed as a part of this IT study.

Data Set

The Neuro Data Hub maintains a ticketing system that records data requests. This system was queried for the first year of operation from August 2023 to December 2024.

Request Types

Data requests were categorized into 3 major categories for the purposes of projecting wait times and determining service expense: basic, complex, and natural language processing (NLP). In addition to these 3 services, departmental members were allowed to request for assistance with data visualization or database creation.

Basic Data Egress

Basic data egress implies retrieving structured data from Epic for a well-defined cohort for which unique sample identifiers are provided. An example of a basic data egress is to query patient demographic information (gender, date of birth, zip code) given a list of patient medical record number. Basic data egress is predominantly executed through Structured Query Language (SQL) queries.

Complex Data Egress

Complex data egress requires the Neuro Data Hub team to retrieve structured data from Epic for IRB Approved research studies for a broadly defined cohort, for example, to identify all patients who have been diagnosed with glioma and underwent any surgical procedures for the past 5 years. Complex data egress is also predominantly executed through SQL queries.

NLP-Augmented Egress

NLP-augmented egress is a subtype of a complex egress that requires retrieving unstructured data from Epic and also extracting information from these data using NLP. Some examples of an NLP-augmented egress include identifying mutation from pathology reports, procedure details from operation notes, and patients self-reported symptoms from the history note. NLP-augmented requests use python-based regular expressions and large language models for processing, in addition to SQL queries for data extraction.

Data Visualization Request

Data visualization request is an add-on request that can be included with a data egress. Data visualization requires creating a dashboard that will update in real-time based on investigator selected criteria. An example of such a request is an interactive map that displays patient locations for High-Intensity Focused Ultrasound and Dynamic Functional Stimulation treatments, with users being able to filter the map to show either treatment group or both. The visualization layer is implemented through a Tableau dashboard, hosted on the NYU Langone Health infrastructure, maintaining direct connectivity to the enterprise Datalake for real-time query execution and visualization capabilities.

Database Creation Request

Database creation request is another add-on request that can be included with a data egress. It requires the Neuro Data Hub team to create a clinical database to track surgical cases, capturing key elements such as patient demographics, surgery timing, preoperative and postoperative diagnosis, and surgical notes. The system should allow surgeons to record and access comprehensive surgical records. Database infrastructure is supported by Research Electronic Data Capture (REDCap), with data ingestion processes facilitating the import of structured data files (Excel or CSV format) into the REDCap platform.

RESULTS

Personnel and Infrastructure

Neuro Data Hub team was established with one data engineer working full-time on this task and one clinical research coordinator working on it part time. Overall, the estimated cost of running Neuro Data Hub at our institution is $150 000 per year, consisting predominantly of the data engineer's salary and the time costs of the clinical research coordinator. The operating costs of compute and storage were comparatively negligible, as the service only produced a 3.5 GB of data over the first year of operations. No additional upfront or yearly costs were required since the service is based on already existing and adopted infrastructure such as Epic, NYU Langone Health Datalake, and NYU Langone Health Inference Node with hosted large language models. Since a workflow with querying data directly from the Datalake was established, no special license of permissions was required from Epic.

Neuro Data Hub Workflow

A workflow was established to manage data requests and ensure secure data dissemination. The protocol comprises 3 primary phases: request submission, verification, and data delivery (Figure 1B). This workflow was created by a committee consisting of Departmental Leadership and Medical Center Information Technology staff to emphasize the delivery of tailored data services to each requesting faculty member.

Request Submission

Two distinct channels for data request submissions were established. A secondary channel consists of a web-based submission request process and monthly departmental data science meetings. During these meetings, researchers can submit new requests and/or seek clarification on existing tickets. These meetings also serve as an opportunity for the Neuro Data Hub team to update researchers on the status of their ongoing projects and discuss technical challenges encountered. The primary channel was implemented through REDCap form to serve as an asynchronous method of requesting. The form submission requires information such as including requestor credentials, IRB approval documentation or Quality Improvement status, detailed research objectives, target population parameters, and specific variable requirements (Supplemental Digital Content 1 [http://links.lww.com/NS9/A59]).

Verification

In the next step, clinical research coordinator from the Clinical Research Division conducts reviews of IRB documentation for each submission to ensure regulatory compliance. This verification step was established as a mandatory prerequisite for data release if the study will be published.

Data Delivery

After the IRB status is confirmed, the engineering team begins processing the request. Data extraction is performed using the NYU Langone Health Datalake, which records all patients' electronic health record data ingested from the Epic system since 2016 when Epic is on live. At the time of submission, the number of unique medical record numbers in the system was over 10 million. The primary data processing pipeline uses SQL for initial data extraction and manipulation. For more complex data requests, Python-based scripts are implemented to supplement the SQL queries.

When text data are involved, we use different methods to reduce the impact of inhomogeneous language and terminology used. If the target text is a single word (eg, whether a patient's surgery note mentions “spinal”) or follows a pattern (eg, the size of the tumor, which is described as dim_1 * dim_2 * dim_3), it can be extracted by using simple regular expressions. When the phrases are free texts (eg, House Brackmann Index, usually written as HB, H&B), the texts were into a large language model hosted on a NYU Langone Health inference node which is Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act (HIPAA) compatible. We then retrieve the answers from the model and proceed with further data querying and/or processing.

On completion of the data query, the requested information is securely shared through OneDrive hosted by NYU Langone Health. After the delivery, requesters are responsible for checking the integrity of the results. For any result that is different from the requesters' expectations, data can be requeried by the data engineering team.

Year 1 Services

Between August 15, 2023, and November 15, 2024, Neuro Data Hub received a total of 39 data egress requests (2.6 per month). Three of these egress requests were basic, 22 were complex, and 13 were NLP-augmented (Figure 2). Two of the complex egress requests came with a request for visualization assistance, and one NLP-augmented request came with a request to build a permanent database (Figure 3). The average complete turnaround time across all requests was 36.5 days (1 to 182 days) (Figure 4). So far requests generated 3.5 GB of patient health information total (60 MB on average).

DISCUSSION

Our goal was to remove key difficulties and barriers to clinical research in the NYU Langone Health Department of Neurosurgery. NYU Langone Health Data Hub initiative presents an operational paradigm shift in how neurosurgeons access institutional data and data related services. In the Data Hub paradigm, data access and engineering resources are a fundamental service provided by the participating department to faculty members at cost. We present the results of the first year of operation of the NYU Langone Health Neuro Data Hub in the hope that we might inspire similar efforts in other neurosurgical departments. Observational studies are the mainstay of neurosurgical research and quality improvement, and the Data Hub paradigm recognizes that there needs to be a seamless interface between practicing surgeons and the data on them and their patients that is stored in institutional data resources.

Over the course of our first year of operation, there was a marked shift toward the NLP-Augmented Egress request type. We attribute this shift to 2 major factors. First, the NYU Langone Health Neuro Data Hub is integrated with an AI lab that specializes in NLP12-15 and Neuro Data Hub engineers sought to use this unique expertise. Second, as faculty members became aware of the possibility of extracting cohorts based on natural language requests such as “everyone who had a L3-4 TLIF for a Grade II spondylolisthesis,” this request rapidly became the most popular solution as it did not require surgeons to track individual patient identifiers or required information to be extracted from notes that was not typically standardized (eg, neurologic examinations).

Limitations

Although Neuro Data Hub is running successfully, there are still some limitations. The first limitation is generalization of such a service. NYU Langone Health is a pioneer in medical IT and has robust mechanisms for data storage, access, and governance. The Hadoop Datalake that the Neuro Data Hub draws on is predominantly populated by tables from Epic. Epic's Clarity and Caboodle data tables are relatively well documented compared with other electronic health record systems and are more easily integrated into other IT processes (imaging, coding, billing, etc.). Finally, NYU Langone Health provides access to substantial tools for NLP including an internal version of GPT-4. These NLP tools combined with the Neuro Data Hub's access to NLP expertise facilitate a number of services that might be harder to replicate at other institutions.

CONCLUSION

Neuro Data Hub represents a significant leap forward in medical research capabilities. By evolving to meet researchers' changing needs, from basic queries to advanced data management, it has become an invaluable tool in the pursuit of medical knowledge. This initiative not only streamlines access to critical patient data but also empowers clinicians and investigators to push the boundaries of their field. As Neuro Data Hub continues to develop, it promises to play a crucial role in advancing neurosurgical or any kind of medical care, ultimately benefiting patients and contributing to the broader landscape of medical innovation.
