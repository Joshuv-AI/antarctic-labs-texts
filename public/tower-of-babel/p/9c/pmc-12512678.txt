Adapting nursing education to improve care for persons with disabilities: a systematic review of curriculum changes and training effectiveness
Authors: Geneviève Opprecht, Sara Da Rocha Rodrigues, Minerva Rivas Velarde
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo ensure that nurses are equipped to provide effective care, it is essential to examine available evidence on how nursing curricula have adapted to meet the unique health needs of persons with disabilities. This study assesses how pedagogical strategies and interventions have changed in nursing education to develop disability competencies. It also examines whether such curriculum changes have been evaluated to assess their effectiveness in developing competencies.MethodsA systematic review based on a literature search in Web of Science, PubMed, Medline, PsycInfo, ERIC, CINAHL and Google Scholar. The study includes peer-reviewed papers, either qualitative or quantitative, that describe the development of disability-related education in undergraduate or postgraduate nursing training. With no time frame or publication restriction. The quality of the paper was assessed using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool (MMAT). 46 studies have met al.l inclusion criteria and were included in the final analysis.ResultsIn total, were 2803 articles found in the initial search across seven online electronic databases. Finally, a thorough review was conducted, incorporating 46 studies. Most of the studies come from high income countries. Among the 46 studies reviewed, 16 employed qualitative methodologies, 22 studies adopted quantitative descriptive designs, relying primarily on cross-sectional surveys and pre-post evaluations. Seven studies used mixed methods.Three major themes emerged from this analysis a) enhancing nurses’ understanding of disability, b) pedagogical strategies to work with persons with disabilities and their families in a clinical setting, and c) diverse approaches to clinical simulation in nursing education. The results show that most educational interventions are not assessing their impact in developing disability competencies.DiscussionMany interventions aim to raise disability awareness, challenge prejudice, and promote more inclusive attitudes toward individuals with disabilities. While these goals are important, there is limited evidence demonstrating their effectiveness in enhancing nursing practice competencies. Research on strategies that equip nursing students with the core skills needed to provide quality care to people with disabilities remains scarce.

Nurses, the largest and most influential group of healthcare professionals globally, are the backbone of patient care. [1, 2]. Their interventions in community health and hospital care are exceptionally vital for social groups that have been historically segregated from social and health systems and continue to report poor health outcomes, such as individuals with disabilities.

Over the last couple of decades, there has been a paradigm shift regarding rights and equity for persons with disabilities, including the right to health [3–5]. Disability results from the interaction between individuals with a health condition, for example, cerebral palsy, spinal cord injury, with personal and environmental factors, such as negative attitudes, inadequate infrastructure [6]. Disability is a relational phenomenon [7, 8]. Individuals with different impairments, such as sensory, motor, or intellectual disabilities, may benefit from diverse health interventions; however, they often encounter similar barriers. Therefore, this study considers disability broadly, encompassing various types of impairments to better understand the common challenges experienced across them.

There is growing evidence that persons with disabilities face significant health disparities, leading to poorer outcomes and early mortality. Some individuals with disabilities may die up to 20 years earlier than their non-disabled peers [9–11]. Additionally, they face double the risk of chronic conditions such as depression, asthma, diabetes, stroke, obesity, and poor oral health. These disparities result from decades of inequities and barriers within and outside the health system [12–15]. Evidence points to bias, prejudice, and stereotypes among healthcare providers contributing to these disparities [16]. Furthermore, health professionals are often not sufficiently trained to provide quality healthcare to individuals with disabilities [17].

In the USA, educators from medicine, nursing, and related fields created the Alliance for Disability in Health Care Education as a nonprofit organisation [18]. Recognising the need for shared standards, the Alliance proposed a set of 10 core competencies in disability for healthcare education. These competencies go from linguistics and communication, adequate clinical examination, and patient-centred care to ensuring accessibility and addressing access barriers. Such initiatives will enable the building of capacities across the health workforce following their rollout, and the potential impact is significant.

There is a growing awareness of the need to equip students with the necessary skills and competence to provide quality care for individuals with disabilities. Ali, Nguyen, Dennet, and Goez [19] analysed existing literature on disability curricula and their effects on undergraduate medical students. Their study demonstrates that implementing disability curricula significantly improves students’ attitudes, empathy, communication skills, and comfort in delivering care. Diverse teaching methods, including involving patients as mentors, were key to developing inclusive and impactful education. Furthermore, Evans, Lisiecka and Farrell [20] explore the impact educational interventions have on undergraduate nursing and medical students’ attitudes and empathy levels towards people with disability. Their results show that pedagogical strategies based on the transformative learning theory for disabled health courses improve attitudes toward disability among nursing students; however, the level of empathy did not improve as expected. In addition, Vi, Jiwa, Lunsky, and Thakur [21] review undergraduate training programs on intellectual disability across various healthcare professions to support the development of evidence-based curricula. Their findings indicate that the evidence is currently weak. Nevertheless, multimodal approaches to teaching were more likely to yield more positive results. These studies suggest that further high-quality research is necessary to identify more effective educational approaches that foster disability competencies. Changing perceptions among students is often alluded to as a main goal of teaching intervention; thus, this is important and overall positive, but it is insufficient to improve expertise in delivering health services to persons with disabilities. There is a need to rigorously evaluate teaching-related interventions and curriculum in terms of their effectiveness in developing disability competencies among students.

Nursing programmes and disability competencies

Higher education programs for nurses are designed to equip future nurses with the skills necessary to provide safe, high-quality, accessible, and person-centred care. This training aims to translate into better healthcare, positive health outcomes, and reduced healthcare costs. Despite improvements in overall healthcare for the general population, disparities between persons with and without disabilities persist. The literature highlights several challenges and areas that require improvement. For example, Ndengeyingoma and Ruel [22] found that practising nurses often did not feel competent to care for persons with disabilities, indicating a significant gap in their training and preparedness. Another study by Drainoni, Lee-Hood, Tobias, Bachmann, Andrew and Maisels [23] revealed that nurses’ lack of knowledge in handling disability-specific needs often led to suboptimal patient outcomes. Disability is an umbrella term encompassing various types of impairments, understanding this diversity is critical. For instance, Smeltze, Blunt, Marozsan, and Wetzel-Effinger [23] advocate for a broader approach to disability that encompasses intellectual, sensory, and intellectual disabilities in nursing programs. This is often lacking. Smeltze and colleagues also emphasise the need to build capacities in ethical and legal considerations regarding healthcare for persons with disabilities [24]. Disability-related content is scarce, and its delivery methods are mostly untested. Most reported interventions did not report on efficiency in disability competencies.

New technologies and learning in nursing programmes

New technologies, such as online modules and virtual reality in training, can enrich this learning process in several ways [25]. Online modules, for instance, can present diverse case studies illustrating how cultural and social attitudes affect the care provided to people with disabilities. Similarly, virtual reality simulations enable students to engage in scenarios involving patients from various cultural backgrounds, helping them develop a more profound and empathetic understanding of their specific needs [26]. Thus, we need to understand how effectively it engages students and facilitates the development of learning objectives. We have limited knowledge on their effectiveness, reach, and limitations in developing disability competencies.

Prioritising disability education in nursing programs can enable nurses to deliver quality care and remove barriers in the health system that could, in turn, contribute to reducing healthcare disparities for this population. Thus, to achieve systematic, comprehensive, and sustainable change in nursing education, we must assess how curricula are evolving, whether these changes reflect social shifts in disability rights, and whether the available literature on pedagogical strategies and nursing-based interventions has been proven effective in developing all-disability-encompassing competencies. This review aims to enable us to identify the published empirical evidence on the effectiveness of disability-specific training for nursing students at all levels. We will issue recommendations based on the results.

Methods

This is a systematic review. The study assesses curriculum design and delivery changes and what pedagogical strategies or interventions are more efficient in developing disability competencies across nursing professionals. It followed guidance from the Cochrane guidelines and the preferred reporting Items for systematic reviews and meta-Analysis. The PRISMA-P checklist is available in S1 Checklist.

Independent reviewers will conduct a literature search using the following databases: PubMed, Medline, PsycINFO, Education Resources Information Center (ERIC), Cumulated Index in Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), and Web of Science, as well as Google Scholar.

An experienced librarian developed a draft of the search strategy alongside the three authors (G.O, SDR, MRV). Risk of bias and methodological quality analysis

The methodological quality of the selected studies was assessed using the mixed methods appraisal tool [27]. This tool evaluated qualitative, quantitative, and mixed methods empirical studies. Furthermore, it has been tested and deemed appropriate in nursing literature [28]. Selected papers were assessed using over five components, which varied according to the nature of the study.

For qualitative studies, the assessment included the suitability of the qualitative approach, data collection, and analysis in addressing the research question, as well as the coherence supporting the result interpretation. Quantitative studies were evaluated based on sampling relevance and representativeness, measurement validity and reliability, risk of nonresponse bias, and the appropriateness of statistical analysis. Mixed-methods studies were assessed on the relevance of using such a design, the integration of qualitative and quantitative components, the consistency of combined interpretations, the explanation of differing findings, and the quality of each component.

The MMAT encourages not to estimate an overall score to rate the quality of each study but to analyse and discuss each criterion. Following the indications of the MMAT, studies were not excluded based on the methodological quality. Only those that met at least one MMAT criterion for their respective methodology were included in the analysis. Instead, the evaluation results are discussed and presented in the conclusions. Two of the authors jointly (SDR and GO) assessed the risk of bias and the quality of the articles. Disagreements were resolved by consulting (MRV) to achieve consensus.

Data management

Manuscripts identified through the search process, including their abstracts and full texts, will be uploaded to Rayyan Software (QCRI). This tool was designed to streamline the screening of titles, abstracts, and articles through semi-automated methods. Once uploaded, duplicates were eliminated from the Rayyan platform. Additionally, a data extraction form was created in Excel and tested in a pilot phase before implementation.

Data extraction and data synthesis

Two authors (GO, SDR) independently extracted data from the full text of eligible manuscripts using a predefined model. A senior researcher (MRV) resolved any uncertainties or divergences. The following data items were extracted from each publication: first author, journal and year of publication, country, the aim of the study, description of the pedagogical intervention, curriculum change, education intervention rationale, intervention and testing, sample size, which education context, any assessment or measurement of the intervention.

Results

The included studies were conducted across various countries, with the highest representation from the USA (40.4%), followed by the UK (10.6%), Canada (6.4%), Ireland (6.4%), and Australia (6.4%). Other countries such as Brazil, Taiwan, Norway, Scotland, Spain, Saudi Arabia, New Zealand, and Turkey each contributed smaller proportions of studies.

Among the 46 studies reviewed, 16 employed qualitative methodologies [30–45]), often using interviews, focus groups, or thematic analysis to explore student perceptions and lived experiences. 22 studies adopted quantitative descriptive designs [24, 46–52,, 53–67] relying primarily on cross-sectional surveys and pre-post evaluations. Seven studies used mixed methods [34, 68–73]. Interventions varied widely: eight studies implemented simulation-based strategies [33, 41, 44, 58, 59, 61, 65, 71], seven integrated disability topics into the curriculum [34, 35, 37, 43, 57, 69, 73], five developed e-learning modules [34, 49, 52, 58, 72], six involved clinical or service-learning placements [40, 42, 51, 60, 70, 73] and four used storytelling or patient testimony [30, 36, 38, 45].

Three major themes emerged from this analysis: a) enhancing nurses’ understanding of disability, b) pedagogical strategies to work with persons with disabilities and their families in a clinical setting, and c) diverse approaches to simulation in nursing education. Enhancing nurses’ understanding of disability

The nursing curriculum tends to inadequately cover the complex interplay of biological and social factors influencing the experiences of persons with disabilities. Research by Edwards and Nash [35], Norman [37], Trollor, Eagleson [57] shows that healthcare professionals, especially nurses, often have a limited understanding of disability. Diaz and Goyal [53] and Epstein, Stephens [36] demonstrate that incorporating disability theoretical models, particularly the social model into nursing curricula fosters inclusive practices and deepens understanding of the needs of disabled individuals. Temple and Mordoch [30] highlight gaps in training, particularly regarding intellectual disabilities, while Jukes and Aspinall [39] emphasise nurses’ leadership roles in community care. Alahmari, Rengaramanujam [46] and Gates and Statham [32] stress the importance of interdisciplinary teaching to reflect collaborative health professional practice.

Smeltzer and colleagues have extensively examined the role of disability content in nursing education. Smeltzer and Dolen argued two decades ago that nursing education remains predominantly rooted in the medical model. Subsequent studies [24, 64, 67, 75] confirm that a deficiency-centred approach still dominates, risking the perpetuation of negative perceptions while overlooking social and structural barriers that contribute to exclusion. They also advocate for including legal, social, and historical aspects of disability care within traditional lecture-based courses [64, 67] though they note the limited impact of theory without practical experience or clinical placements [56, 75]. Furthermore, their work also asks for caution against siloed, disability-specific approaches and calls for a more generic, holistic framework to better prepare nurses for inclusive care [24, 75]. In this line of work, Kronk and Colbert [33] proposed 12 core disability-related competencies via expert consultation across four dimensions: environment and care, communication, culture, and referral. These competencies are intended to bridge gaps in nursing curricula and reduce health disparities.

The results show that often single-session interventions are put forward by nursing programmes aiming to expose students to disability-related concepts, increase empathy, and reduce prejudice [46, 50, 54–56, 59, 66, 75], though no rigorous evidence exists to quantify their impact on learning outcomes. The evaluation of educational interventions across the 46 studies was conducted using a diverse range of instruments and strategies. Pre/post questionnaires or surveys were used by [33, 44, 46, 49, 51, 56, 58–61, 67, 70]; Attitudes Toward Disabled Persons (ATDP) [47, 50, 54, 56, 63, 66], Knowledge tests/quizzes [33, 34, 46, 49, 51, 58, 61, 69] Reflective writings/journals/essays [32, 36, 38, 41, 45, 70], Focus groups or interviews Diaz-Agea et al., 2022; Doody et al., 2012; Edwards & Nash, 2023; Epstein et al., 2020; Nash-Patel et al., 2023; Norman, 1998; Taveira et al., 2023; Temple & Mordoch, 2012), Observation and participation assessments [42, 44, 57, 71, 73], Course evaluations or institutional feedback [35, 37, 57, 69].

Working with individuals with impairments and their families in a clinical setting

Clinical placements are consistently presented as crucial learning tools, often leading to significant positive changes in students’ perspectives, better competence, and improved preparedness for real-world patient care. Fifteen studies explored clinical placements intervention. Nine demonstrated positive results [31, 35, 47, 54, 55, 69, 70, 73], while six indicated mixed effects [36, 43, 48, 49, 60, 72].

Edwards, Cron [55] and Epstein, Stephens [36] showed that direct interaction with patients with disabilities encourages positive attitudes and a better understanding of their complex needs. Furthermore, students gain enhanced empathy, a deeper understanding of the needs of people with disabilities, and an increased respect for their rights. Furthermore, clinical rotation facilitates the transition from a disease- or deficit-focused perspective to an individual-centred approach that emphasises their abilities [36, 55]. Atkins, Dugan [31] and Boyd, Bridge [69] reported that students report increased confidence in managing difficult situations after completing clinical placements in rehabilitation centres or specialised institutions. These clinical rotations provide a range of experiences, allowing students to familiarise themselves with various care environments and types of disabilities. [49] reported on the benefits of practical experience in different community settings that go beyond traditional clinical placement to include day activities centres and residential or community services. Their results show that exposure to various types of settings led to the development of adequate skills, thus highlighting the need for cautious planning in such rotations.

Clinical placements showed high efficiency in improving communication skills. Deasy, Doody [48] showed that students acquire essential communication skills to care for disabled individuals during these placements. Clinical rotations offer students the opportunity to apply the communication skills they have acquired in theory, particularly when interacting with patients, gathering information, and establishing a therapeutic relationship. Rasouli, Husby [43] investigated the use of technology to enhance communication with patients with disabilities. Results show that the students grasp the direct impact of adapted communication and the importance of integrating assistive technologies into nursing care. Thus, they also noted the need to train staff about ethical issues arising from the use of such technologies.

Clinical rotations, combined with ex-cathedra teaching interventions, resulted in significant positive changes in nursing students’ attitudes toward individuals with disabilities [66].

The structure of the clinical rotations varied greatly from one class session to an entire semester; there was no recommendation for a minimum or ideal time to see improvements in disability-related competencies. A key point in the process was enough supervision, support, and a debriefing session afterwards. Supervision by experienced nurses and establishing reflection periods are crucial for optimising learning during clinical rotations. Atkins, Dugan [31] study showed that the analysis of lived experiences, identification of strengths and weaknesses, and development of professionalism are elements facilitated by quality supervision and structured reflection. As highlighted by Edwards, Cron [55] group discussions after the clinical rotations allowed students to share their experiences and reflect on the communication challenges with disabled individuals, facilitating learning and improving their skills.

Although clinical rotations offer training potential, difficulties remain. Common obstacles include a shortage of suitable services that offer rotation placements, sometimes inadequate supervision, and a lack of preparation for specific situations [49]. Collaboration between training institutions and clinical placement sites is essential to ensure enriching and formative experiences for students. Clinical rotations have a significant influence on the perception of disability. [38] Identified common errors in clinical simulation in nursing students’ simulated clinical practice with autistic patients, suggesting insufficient practical training and knowledge gaps despite theoretical instruction. These findings suggest that while interventions may be present, their scope, depth, or implementation may not always be sufficient to achieve the desired changes.

Employing simulation as a teaching approach in nursing education

Simulation emerged as a crucial instructional method for nursing students, especially when caring for individuals with disabilities. Out of nine studies, eight reported positive outcomes, emphasising improvements in empathy, communication, and clinical reasoning [33, 44, 52, 59, 61, 65, 68, 71]. One study yielded inconclusive results [38]. The research reviewed highlights various methods with unique objectives, benefits, and drawbacks. Prominent techniques include high-fidelity simulation, virtual reality, standardised patients, role-playing, and experiential learning.

Goddard and Jordan [59], Bassett and Pickard [43], Levett-Jones, Lapkin [64], and Macaden, Smith [71] tested the effect of students’ simulation of physical and sensory impairments. Their results show that such activity has self-reported an immediate positive impact on reported attitudes towards patients with a disability. Haugland, Hartmann [62] Observed positive results of using videos and interviews as learning material in an online session to train health professionals on adaptive communication and developing adaptive physical activity plans for people with intellectual and developmental disabilities (IDD). Lewis, O’Boyle-Duggan [52] note that while role-playing is an engaging method, its realism is constrained by participant involvement and the effectiveness of the scenarios. In comparison, Nash-Patel, Anderson [45] role-playing combined with storytelling resulted in the positive development of relational skills.

Studies carried out by Van Boxtel, Napholz [68] and Boyd, Bridge [69] show that experiential learning provides significant realism by immersing students in real or simulated challenges. Boyd, Bridge [69] state that interacting with patients with intellectual or developmental disabilities nurtures empathy and enhances relationship-building skills. Van Boxtel, Napholz [68], demonstrated that having first-hand experience using wheelchairs increases awareness of the needs of physically disabled patients. Nonetheless, both studies noted that these methods necessitate significant resources and complicated logistics.

Research conducted by Diaz-Agea, Macias-Martinez [38] and McIntosh, Thomas [33] shows that standardised patients create a realistic experience using trained actors who perform scripted scenarios. These methods improve relational and therapeutic skills but are hindered by high costs and a lack of diversity. Virtual reality enables complete visual and auditory immersion in complex environments, including paediatric care [51]. Despite its adaptability and effectiveness, this technology remains expensive and necessitates specialised equipment. High-fidelity simulation, explored by Hencklein, da Silva [61] It is considered the most authentic method. It addresses sensitive topics, such as sexuality after spinal cord injury, by simulating physiological responses using advanced mannequins. However, its accessibility is restricted due to high costs and complex logistics required for its successful implementation.

The results report on the advantages of simulation with different resources. These are useful and more work will be needed to learn about the setups of such a tool and to evaluate learning outcomes adequately. The most positive results were found in a study run by Boyd, Bridge [69]. The Canadian-based study used simulation as part of a multi-phase experiential learning model, which included early exposure, simulation training to acquire clinical skills with standardised patients, and application in clinical settings. This led to significant improvements in self-reported comfort, confidence, and competence when providing care to the person with intellectual and development disabilities.

Limitations

This review was limited to publications in English and French, which may have excluded relevant studies in other languages and cultural contexts. We did not include non-peer-reviewed literature or institutional reports. Although quality was assessed, variations in journal standards may have influenced the consistency of included sources.

Discussion

This systematic review explored the evolution of nursing curricula and the educational strategies to foster disability-related competencies and evaluate their effectiveness. Several sub-questions fine-tune the analysis: What disability-related curricula have been implemented? What pedagogical strategies were employed? And how has their effectiveness been assessed?

Nursing practice is undergoing significant transformation due to population ageing and the rising global prevalence of disabilities. Nurses are increasingly expected to play a critical role in delivering quality care to people with disabilities, requiring robust knowledge and skills in physical, psychological, and intellectual disability care.

The literature analysis revealed that, although the available evidence is growing, there is a lack of robust data to evaluate the effectiveness of these interventions comprehensively. While a fair number of studies provide valuable insights into students’ perceptions and experiences, they often fail to offer definitive conclusions about the impact of educational interventions on competency development or even knowledge acquisition.

Lectures remain a predominant method for delivering foundational knowledge on disability rights, theoretical models, and ethical considerations in care [24, 56]. The literature reflects a growing awareness of the need to portray disability in its full complexity, moving beyond a narrow focus on pathology. Increasingly, disability rights and advocacy are being incorporated into nursing curricula. Although these efforts are still limited, they indicate a shift toward a more inclusive and nuanced understanding of disability. However, a truly inclusive approach would require the systematic integration of theoretical models, along with evidence-based practices aimed at improving training.

Simulation as a pedagogical recourse shows promise as an effective approach for strengthening students’ relational, communication, and technical skills. The results indicate a degree of effectiveness in simulation in improving communication, self-confidence, and clinical skills. Simulation exposes students to complex scenarios, prompting them to reflect on their actions and consequences. However, the effectiveness of the simulation depends on the quality of the scenario and the facilitation. As clear and structured as debriefing and feedback can be. The available evidence does not provide a baseline for the minimum number of scenarios or the key elements required for them to be effective. Additionally, there are no clear recommendations regarding the emphasis or number of hours these practices should occupy within a full nursing curriculum. Often, they are evaluated as isolated interventions, making it difficult to determine whether complementary curricular components influenced the outcomes.

Community-based placements, direct interactions with individuals with disabilities, and testimonies from those with lived disability experiences are often cited as pivotal in positively shaping students’ attitudes and confidence. The results showed positive impact of placements on students’ attitudes, confidence, and skills. Placements enable students to apply theoretical knowledge in real-world contexts, develop relational skills with individuals with disabilities, and gain an understanding of their needs and perspectives. Well-structured placements with opportunities for reflection are crucial. Moreover, placements should represent a diverse range of disability experiences. Unfortunately, it is difficult to extract concrete recommendations from the existing results, as many studies lack specificity and meaningful comparisons. For example, while some studies suggest that short placements are ineffective, they often fail to define what constitutes a “short” placement in terms of duration. As a result, there is no consensus on the minimum acceptable or ideal amount of time students should spend in placement to achieve meaningful learning outcomes. However, it can be inferred that well-structured placements, where students can meaningfully engage with service users under clear, consistent, and structured supervision, are highly advisable.

Evaluating the effectiveness of educational interventions reveals a paucity of robust data. This underscores the need for standardised tools to directly measure the competencies gained by students not only in terms of immediate learning outcomes, but also in assessing the long-term impact. It is essential to determine whether the knowledge acquired forms a correct and necessary foundation for future nursing practice.

Conclusion

The results of this review highlight the urgency of rigorously evaluating educational interventions related to disability. Across the globe, nursing programmes are dealing with the growing demand for training in different domains, yet have limited time to train students. It is essential that available teaching for disability-related content is well-utilised and effectively prepares students to deliver high-quality care. The findings indicate that both clinical placements and simulation can be effective in developing disability knowledge and competencies, yet they remain underutilised. Nevertheless, available data allow us to see a multi-phase learning model, which includes a theoretical basis rooted in human rights, well-structured simulation and clinical placement with detailed debriefing, and close supervision, can lead to improvements in confidence and competence when providing care to the person with disabilities

It is crucial to ensure that educational interventions are well-designed and adequately meet the needs of students, many of whom report feeling unprepared to care for individuals with disabilities. The development of rigorous evaluation tools will support the measurement of the impact of these interventions on students’ practical and relational competencies.

This systematic review highlights that nursing education on disability has yet to reach its full potential. Developing disability-related competencies among nursing students requires a diversified pedagogical approach integrating theory, practice, lived experiences, and critical reflection. Combining multiple teaching methods, adopting a biopsychosocial perspective on disability, and providing meaningful learning opportunities in real-world settings are crucial to fostering well-rounded, competent, and empathetic nursing professionals. Current evidence does not permit the formulation of concrete recommendations regarding the number of curriculum hours or specific interventions necessary to achieve successful outcomes. However, it does suggest that efforts must be well-structured and combine various pedagogical strategies to be most effective.

Intellectual disability has received more attention than other forms of disability. Progress on this area may allow us to explore the impact of specialised training. Furthermore, good practices in intellectual and developmental disability nursing have the potential to guide the development of education in other areas; thus, more needs to be known about their impact on healthcare delivery and their system-wide effects.

Disability is a large umbrella term. Most of us will likely experience disability at some point in our lives, as disability could occur due to a genetically related condition, be acquired due to injury or the natural ageing process. Most interventions claim to raise disability awareness, address prejudice, and change attitudes towards those with disabilities. While this is essential, more evidence of its effectiveness in improving nursing practice competencies is needed. Growing disability prevalence worldwide demands urgent action to adequately prepare future nurses with the knowledge, competencies and tools that will enable them to provide quality care for all.
