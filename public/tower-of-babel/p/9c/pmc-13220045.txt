Telomerase is induced during wound healing across species
Authors: David F Chang, Elizabeth A Olmsted-Davis, Aldona J Spiegel, John P Cooke, Biana Godin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
In this study, we show, for the first time, that levels of endogenous TERT, while expectedly very low in the healthy skin of three mammalian species (human, pig and mouse), are elevated by several orders of magnitude during cutaneous wound healing.

Dear Editor, Multiple well-orchestrated mechanisms that govern dermal wound healing have been extensively studied. However, the whole process has not been fully elucidated.1 Here, we present a potential new mechanism that may contribute to cutaneous wound healing in humans, as well as in other small and large animals. Telomerase reverse transcriptase (TERT), a catalytic subunit of telomerase, preserves telomeric length and chromosomal stability by adding telomeric repeats to DNA, protecting cells from senescence. Recent studies have confirmed other, noncanonical functions of TERT, including the maintenance of redox homeostasis, regulation of metabolism and protection from apoptosis.2

Generally, TERT is expressed at low levels in healthy epithelial adult tissues. While the mainstream scientific literature links uncontrolled TERT expression, specifically mutations in TERT, to tumorigenesis, several reports have shown that one of the essential functions of TERT is tissue regeneration, as has been confirmed in the liver,3 as well as alveoli, the brain and the pancreas.4 We have recently shown that exogenous induction of telomerase activity using human TERT mRNA lipid nanoparticles in an autologous human skin cell suspension (hSCS) enhanced cellular engraftment and proliferation of hSCS in a deep cutaneous wound model, while reducing cellular senescence and DNA damage.5

Serendipitously, we found that endogenous TERT is induced during skin wound healing. Interestingly, this phenomenon was seen in murine, porcine and human skin during the course of wound healing, suggesting that this is a highly preserved and essential wound-healing mechanism in skin across mammalian species.

Deidentified formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) human skin sections from two patients with healing surgical wounds and deidentified healthy adult human skin samples (participants aged 57–63 years) were either purchased or procured under the guidance of the Institutional Review Board (Appendix S1; see Supporting Information). No information about the number of days post-skin wounding was available. Wound tissues from full-thickness skin wounds created in pigs and mice were harvested 3–14 days postoperatively (Appendix S1).5,6 Unwounded healthy skin was used as a control. Mouse skin FFPE sections were collected from a previous study, as described in Chang et al. (Appendix S1).5 Pig skin FFPE sections were generated from a swine study (Appendix S1). FFPE sections were stained with haematoxylin and eosin for tissue morphology and immunostained with anti-TERT antibody (Figures S1, S2; see Supporting Information).

In this study, we found, for the first time, that endogenous TERT levels, while expectedly very low in healthy skin (5% of cells in human skin, 2% in pig, 3% in mouse), are elevated by several orders of magnitude during wound healing (66% in human, 71% in pig, 64% in mouse; Figure 1). The expression of TERT in cutaneous wounds in all three species was mainly seen in the viable epidermis layer and partially in the dermis. TERT staining colocalized with nuclear staining (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole). To assess whether TERT expression was present in the dermal vasculature and skin fibroblasts, skin wound tissues were further costained for CD31 or α-smooth muscle actin (SMA) antibodies, respectively. Some TERT-expressing cells colocalized with CD31+ dermal vasculature and α-SMA skin fibroblasts in the skin wound tissues, while most of the signal was scattered in the viable strata of the healing skin. Furthermore, as TERT expression has been reported to be increased in neutrophils and macrophages,7 particularly during inflammation, and owing to the fact that the haematoxylin and eosin staining pointed towards inflammatory infiltrates in the wounds, we also stained for CD66b and CD68 markers, respectively. While both cell populations were present in the healing wounds, their markers did not correspond with TERT expression.

Skin wounding activates a cascade of injury signals, or damage-associated molecular patterns, that enable cell proliferation, migration and differentiation, ultimately resulting in re-epithelialization and wound healing. While many aspects of the process have been clarified, mechanisms involved in skin wound healing have not been fully illuminated.1 Our data demonstrate for the first time that, in three different mammalian species, there is a prominent intrinsic expression of TERT, highlighting a potentially important role of this enzyme in dermal wound repair. It has been shown that TERT is essential for cell renewal in highly proliferative organs in mice (e.g. bone marrow, spleen and the male reproductive system) and is able to rejuvenate cells in a murine model of senescence, extending an animal’s lifespan.8 Telomerase transcription has been demonstrated to regulate nuclear ­factor-κB-­dependent gene expression critical to immune responses and cell migration,9 as well as epidermal stem-cell mobilization,10 all of which are necessary for wound healing. Additionally, telomerase is critical in multiple metabolic pathways activated during wound healing,2 which requires the maintenance of redox potential and mitochondrial function. We strongly believe that future studies to assess the contribution of telomerase to skin wound healing will shed more light on the mechanisms involved.
