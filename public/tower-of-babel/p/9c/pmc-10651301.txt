Role of hospital and patient factors in the outcome of reoperations for primary hyperparathyroidism: a retrospective multicenter cohort study
Authors: Claire Nomine-Criqui, Phi-Linh Nguyen-Thi, Sam Van Slycke, Ozer Makay, Laurent Brunaud, Anders Bergenfelz
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:There are few data on outcomes after reintervention for persistent or recurrent primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT). The authors hypothesized that the variation in outcomes at the hospital level after reoperation would be significant. After accounting for this variability, some patient-level clinical criteria could be identified to help inform treatment decisions in this patient population. The aim of this study was to determine whether there is significant variation in outcomes after reoperation for PHPT between hospitals (hospital-level analysis) and identify clinical factors (patient-level analysis) that influence postoperative outcomes.Materials and methods:This retrospective multicenter cohort study was performed using the Eurocrine registry. Data from 11 countries and 76 hospitals from January 2015 to October 2020 were extracted. A generalized linear mixed model was used to assess the variation in outcomes at the hospital level and to identify risk factors of postoperative outcomes at the patient level. The primary endpoint (textbook outcome) was achieved when all six of the following postoperative conditions were met: no hypocalcemia or persistent hypercalcemia, no laryngeal nerve injury, no negative exploration, no normal parathyroid gland only on histopathology, and no postoperative death.Results:Among 13 593 patients who underwent parathyroidectomy for PHPT, 617 (4.5%) underwent reoperative parathyroidectomy. At follow-up, 231 patients (37.4%) were hypocalcemic, 346 (56.1%) were normocalcemic without treatment, and 40 (6.5%) had persistent hypercalcemia. Textbook outcomes were achieved in 321 (52.0%) patients. The hospital-level variation in textbook outcome rates was significant (P<0.001), and this variation could explain 29.1% of the observed outcomes. The criterion that remained significant after controlling for inter-hospital variation was ‘a single lesion on sestamibi scan or positron emission tomography (PET) imaging’ (odds ratio 2.08, 95% confidence interval 1.24–3.48; P=0.005).Conclusion:Outcomes after reoperation are significantly associated with hospital-related factors. A ‘single lesion observed on preoperative sestamibi scan or PET’ appears relevant to select patients before reoperation.Keywords: hyperparathyroidism, operative, parathyroid glands, parathyroidectomy, primary, reoperation, surgical procedures

Primary hyperparathyroidism (PHPT) is a common endocrine disease with a prevalence ranging from 0.30 to 0.86% in the general population1–3. PHPT is associated with a variety of clinical manifestations (renal, skeletal, neuromuscular, gastrointestinal, neurobehavioral, and cardiovascular)4–7. Large cohort studies have demonstrated excess mortality in patients with PHPT6,8,9. Parathyroidectomy is the only definitive treatment for PHPT, with a postoperative decrease in associated complications compared with non-operative management3–5,9,10. Despite the high success rates, persistent or recurrent PHPT remains a common complication after the first parathyroidectomy3,5,11–17. In comparison with initial parathyroidectomy, reoperative parathyroidectomy is more challenging, with lower cure rates (80–98%) and higher morbidity rates (5–15%)5,11–14,17–24. As a result, many endocrine surgeons use stricter indications for subsequent operation than for initial surgery, and consensus guidelines before reoperation are lacking5,11,17. The majority of existing studies have focused on factors predictive of recurrence or persistence after initial surgery (volume–outcome relationship), and few studies have evaluated outcomes after reintervention3,14,16,17,24,25. In addition, the impact of quality of care provider variation on outcomes after reintervention has never been evaluated in these patients. Therefore, it is necessary to analyze the variation in outcomes between hospitals. Furthermore, the remaining patient-level criteria that may explain the observed outcomes among patients should be identified.

The objectives of this study were to determine whether there was a significant variation in postoperative outcomes between hospitals (hospital-level analysis) and, taking these results into account, to identify clinical factors (patient-level analysis) that influence postoperative outcomes after reintervention for PHPT.

Materials and methods

The study (retrospective multicenter cohort) was approved by an ethics committee (2018/725), and data extraction was approved (KVB 278-18) prior to data analysis. This work has been reported in line with the STROCSS criteria26 (Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A881). For this retrospective cohort study, the data were extracted on 31 October 2020.

Eurocrine database

The Eurocrine database is used to register operations for thyroid disease, primary and secondary hyperparathyroidism, adrenal surgery, paraganglioma, and neuroendocrine tumors of the gastrointestinal tract. The data were registered according to the predefined data fields. Baseline information, preoperative and operative variables, postoperative data, and follow-up data were registered. Data from the Eurocrine database have been audited in Sweden and Switzerland and are found to be valid and reliable18,27.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients who underwent surgery for PHPT between 1 January 2015 and 31 October 2020 were eligible for inclusion. The exclusion criteria were patients who underwent initial surgery for PHPT (first-time parathyroidectomy), patients with hereditary PHPT disease, patients with no data available for histology or calcium status at follow-up, patients treated with lithium, and patients with parathyroid cancer. Secondary and tertiary PHPT were registered in separate sections of the register and were thus excluded.

Variables extracted

Extracted data were: age, sex, preoperative and postoperative calcium levels, preoperative localization imaging procedures including positron emission tomography (PET) (18-F choline or C-11 methionine), intraoperative parathyroid hormone (IOPTH) measurement, frozen-section analysis, type of operation (focused, unilateral, bilateral), video-assisted operation, thoracic exploration and sternotomy, type of anesthesia, duration of operation, oral calcium or vitamin D at discharge, preoperative laryngoscopy before reoperation, laryngoscopy after reoperation at follow-up, hospital stay, pathology, need for reoperations before follow-up, time to follow-up, surgical-site infection, calcium level and status at follow-up, and mortality before follow-up.

Patients with illogical values for hospital stay (discharge date before operation date or hospital stay >2 months) and follow-up time (follow-up date before operation date or follow-up >5 years) were excluded from the analysis. Preoperative and postoperative laryngoscopy at follow-up were classified as recurrent nerve injury, normal, not performed, or missing data. For each participating hospital, the number of reoperations and the number of initial parathyroidectomies for PHPT performed during the same study period were collected.

Definitions

Calcium status at follow-up was reported as hypercalcemia (spontaneous), normocalcemia (without treatment), and hypocalcemia (calcium below the reference range without treatment or treatment with calcium or vitamin D analog therapy)18. Outcomes of the localization procedures were reported as follows: true preoperative localization of solitary adenoma (true positive, TP), false preoperative localization of solitary adenoma (false positive, FP), true preoperative prediction of multiglandular disease, false prediction of multiglandular disease in solitary adenoma, correct position of one pathological gland but multiglandular disease not predicted, or negative/inconclusive preoperative examination. For venous sampling, the procedure was reported as true lateralization of solitary adenoma (TP), false lateralization of solitary adenoma (FP), one gradient correct with bilateral multiglandular disease not predicted, or negative/inconclusive preoperative examination18.

Localization procedures were considered concordant when two or more procedures yielded the same results. A single lesion was defined as the observation of one pathological gland only (TP, FP, or correct position of one pathological gland but multiglandular disease not predicted) on one preoperative localization procedure. A solitary concordant focus (SCF) was defined as a concordant result on at least two preoperative examinations of TP, FP, or the correct position of a pathologic gland but unpredicted multiglandular disease (concordant findings for a single gland). A single lesion on sestamibi scan and/or PET imaging corresponded to the observation of a single pathological gland (TP, FP, or correct position of one pathological gland but multiglandular disease not predicted) on one of the two examinations or on both examinations, but without the obligation of concordance.

Textbook outcome

A composite quality measurement was used to assess the overall quality of care for patients undergoing a reoperation for PHPT28–30. Relevant outcome parameters reflecting an uneventful course at follow-up were defined as follows: no hypocalcemia or persistent hypercalcemia, no recurrent laryngeal injury during the reoperation, no negative exploration, no normal parathyroid gland on pathology, and no postoperative death. A patient was considered to have a textbook outcome when all these six criteria were met28,31.

Statistical analysis

Qualitative variables were expressed as percentages and quantitative variables as medians (interquartile range or IQR). Differences between the groups for continuous variables were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis test or Mann–Whitney U test. Chi-square and Fisher’s exact tests were used for the analysis of nominal variables, as appropriate. Statistical analyses were performed using the SAS software (SAS Institute, Inc, Cary, North Carolina, USA). Hierarchical logistic regression modeling was performed to analyze nested sources of variability in hierarchical data, considering the variability associated with each level of the hierarchy32,33. In this study, patients who underwent reoperative parathyroidectomy at a specific hospital were considered to share some common variances related to being managed at the same medical center (e.g. physicians may tailor their clinical practices with the result in less heterogeneity in the management). Consequently, patients managed in a particular institution could not be treated as truly independent of one another. In this context, the hierarchical linear modeling approach is considered a more appropriate nested data model than a traditional multiple linear regression32,34. The outcome variable was binary (patients with or without textbook outcome achievement). The variance partitioning coefficient of the hospital level was estimated to evaluate the proportion of level-specific contribution to the total outcome variance35,36. For example, if the hospital-level variance partitioning coefficient was 0.05, the proportion of the total variance in the textbook outcome attributable to the hospital level was 5%. Statistical significance was set at P<0.05.

Results

A total of 13 593 patients with PHPT were registered between 1 January 2015 and 31 October 2020. Among these patients, 806 (5.9%) underwent reoperation for sporadic PHPT. After excluding patients in accordance with the study protocol, 617 patients (4.5%) who underwent reoperative parathyroidectomy for PHPT in 11 countries and 76 hospitals were included in the study (Fig. 1). Each participating medical center included 1–85 patients (median, 4 patients, IQR 2–8). During the same inclusion period, the median number of first-time parathyroidectomies for PHPT was 109 (IQR 54–189) per hospital.

Patient characteristics and outcomes

The median age was 61 (54–71) years, and 493 patients were female (79.%) (Table 1). In total, 1316 preoperative localization procedures before reoperation were performed, corresponding to a median of 2 (2–3) procedures per patient (Supplemental Digital Content Table 1, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A882). Among the 504 patients in whom at least two preoperative localization procedures were performed (81.7% of the total cohort of 617 patients), 300 (59.5%) had a concordant positive result for a SCF (Supplemental Digital Content Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A882). Of the 516 patients in whom a sestamibi scan, PET imaging, or both were performed before reoperation, 388 (75.2%) had a single lesion on these scans (single).

All patients underwent reoperative parathyroidectomy corresponding to focused, unilateral, or bilateral neck exploration, in 187 (30.3%), 300 (48.6%), and 130 (21.1%) patients, respectively. Concomitant thyroid surgery was performed in 128 (20.7%) patients because of parathyroid disease in 79 patients and thyroid disease in the remaining 49 patients. Frozen sections and IOPTH levels were used during reoperations in 190 (30.8%) and 449 (72.8%) patients, respectively. Intraoperative nerve monitoring was used in 73 (11.8%) patients. The median duration of reoperation was 65 (43–103) min. At discharge, oral calcium was prescribed to 156 (25.3%) patients and vitamin D to 151 (24.5%) patients. The median hospital stay was 2 (1–2) days (missing data for 31 patients). Ambulatory surgery (0 days spent in the hospital) was registered in 12 patients. Postoperative hematoma requiring surgery occurred in five patients.

At follow-up, median 23 (4–49) days (missing data for follow-up duration in 30 patients), the median total calcium value was 2.35 (2.25–2.46) mmol/l (data missing in one patient). Recurrent laryngeal nerve damage and wound infection were registered in 35 (5.8%) (data missing in ten patients) and five (0.8%) patients, respectively. The median resected weight of parathyroid tissue was 0.90 (0.37–2.00) g. Some 419 patients (67.9%) had a histological diagnosis of parathyroid adenoma, with a median weight of 0.90 (0.40–1.90) g; in 39 patients (6.3%), the exploration was negative or a normal parathyroid gland was excised. Two patients died before the follow-up.

Overall, 40 (6.5%) patients had persistent hypercalcemia, 346 had normocalcemia without treatment (56.1%), and 231 had postoperative hypocalcemia (37.4%) (Table 2). A textbook outcome was achieved in 321 patients (52.0%) (Supplemental Digital Content Table 3, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A882).

Univariable analysis for textbook outcome

Patients with a textbook outcome had a higher incidence of SCF on at least two different preoperative localization procedures (64.5 vs. 53.8%, P=0.014) as well as a single lesion on a sestamibi scan or PET imaging (82.9 vs. 66.0%, P<0.001). During reoperation, IOPTH measurement was more frequently used in patients with postoperative textbook achievement (76.3 vs. 68.9%, P=0.039) (Supplemental Digital Content Table 4, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/JS9/A882).

Hierarchical logistic regression modeling

Hospital level (random intercept model)

In hospital-level analysis, the variation in textbook outcome rates between hospitals was significant (P<0.001), with odds ratios (ORs) ranging from 0.119 to 5.591 (Fig. 2). The distribution of the ORs corresponded to an S-shaped curve with the worst performing hospitals at the top and the best-performing hospitals at the bottom of the graph. The three hospitals with the lowest ORs (OR: 0.119, 0.160, 0.164) had significantly worse outcomes than the other centers (P<0.001). Similarly, the two hospitals with the highest ORs (OR: 3.711, 5.591) had better outcomes than the other centers (P<0.001).

Patient level (fixed effects)

After adjustment for hospital-level variation, the hierarchical analysis showed that the patient-level criteria typically used to explain postoperative outcomes (sex, SCF on at least two different preoperative location procedures, use of frozen section, IOPTH measurement, number of initial parathyroidectomies, number of reoperations, and the ratio between primary and reinterventions) could not explain the observed variation in ORs (Table 3). The only criterion that remained significant after controlling for inter-hospital variation was ‘a single lesion on a sestamibi scan or PET imaging’ (OR 2.08, 95% confidence interval 1.24–3.48; P=0.005).

Discussion

This retrospective multicenter cohort study examined outcomes after reoperative parathyroidectomy, reflecting the current clinical practice from more than 13 000 initial parathyroidectomies for PHPT2,3,13–17. The main findings of this study are that postoperative outcomes after reoperations were significantly influenced by hospital-related factors. This variation explained 29.1% of the observed outcomes. After adjustment for hospital-level variation, the criterion ‘a single lesion observed on preoperative sestamibi scan or PET’ was relevant for selecting patients for reoperation. The strength of this study is that it is based on a methodology adapted to nested data, while using the largest dataset of patients undergoing reoperation for PHPT published to date. For assessing differences in postoperative complication rates (textbook outcome) across hospitals after surgery, data were collected on random samples of patients nested within each hospital (random intercept model), with adjustment for covariates performed at the patient level (fixed effects)32,34.

Large cohort studies using administrative data, including more than 10 000 patients, have reported a need for reoperative parathyroidectomy ranging from 1.6 to 2.8%3,16,25. These cohorts may represent a selection of the actual total case volume and may be subjected to coding bias3,16,25. Current guidelines suggest that initial parathyroidectomy for PHPT should be associated with a cure rate of 95–99%4,5,10,11,20. This study showed that register data are actually at the lower end of this range, reflecting the day-to-day activities of the participating departments18. In agreement, a multi-institutional registry study reported the need for redo parathyroidectomy in 5.02% of 5860 patients who underwent initial parathyroidectomy17.

This study is the first to show that postoperative outcomes after reoperation for PHPT vary significantly between hospitals and that this variation can explain about 30% of the observed outcomes. Therefore, outcomes after reintervention cannot be considered solely as a function of patient-related criteria but are also significantly associated with hospital-related determinants that need to be identified more precisely. These other factors can be related to the hospital system or surgeon performance, as already reported for other pathologies35. Since the hospitals and surgeons were de-identified in the registry, it was not possible to analyze the characteristics of the care centers (size, environment) or surgeons (age, experience). However, in view of the importance of this analysis, which remains to be done in order to better define the ‘experienced surgeon’ as described in the literature, we believe that another study taking into account hospital characteristics (public or private activity, academic or not, presence of other subspecialties or not, etc.) and the surgeon’s qualities (age, experience, training, fellowship or not, type of trainees, possibility of assistance between surgeons, administrative responsibility, professional coach, etc.) would be useful4,10,37.

This study provides evidence that the exclusive use of cutoffs regarding the number of initial surgeries or reoperations to define the best-performing centers for revision parathyroidectomy is insufficient. Nearly all available literature demonstrated a self-evident correlation between higher surgical volume and better clinical outcomes after initial parathyroidectomy3,10,16,19,20,25. However, this correlation has never been evaluated for reoperations19,20. The present study showed that patient volume alone was not useful for selecting the best-performing centers. We acknowledge that it is necessary to better analyze the hospital-level criteria that might explain the observed variability to develop more relevant strategies for reinterventions for PHPT.

The first step before a reoperation for PHPT is to review previous imaging, surgical notes, and pathology10,11. There is a strong consensus that preoperative imaging is essential before reoperation, with a combination of imaging modalities to achieve concordance of at least two tests to significantly improve the success rate10,11,13,14,21,22,24,38,39. Accordingly, the percentage of patients undergoing fewer than two preoperative localization procedures was only 17.3% in this study, which is comparable with data from the CESQIP registry (24.1%)17. When the variation in outcomes between hospitals was considered, this study showed that a single lesion observed on preoperative sestamibi scintigraphy or PET imaging yielded better results than the concordance obtained between two preoperative examinations. We acknowledge that this new criterion needs to be confirmed by further studies, but it could be used to improve the selection of patients before reintervention, since it was significantly associated with better outcomes in this study.

Available data on reinterventions for persistence or recurrence of PHPT are derived mainly from high-volume institutions or surgeons, with mostly reported success rates at least 95%5,10,17,24,38,40. Contemporary multicenter-level data are scarce but report a rate of hypercalcemia persisting after reoperation closer to 10%17. This pan-European study showed that the rate of hypercalcemia after reoperation was 6.5%. Using a textbook outcome composite criterion, it was shown that only 52% of the patients could be considered cured without complications after parathyroid reintervention. The use of this composite criterion, considering variables often neglected in prior studies (nerve injury, hypocalcemia), is clearly relevant and likely closer to the clinical reality of patients than the sole normalization of hypercalcemia. The laryngeal nerve injury rate after reoperation for PHPT is reported to range from 2 to 15%5,17,22,23. The rate of hypocalcemia was considered as high as 21% in the 1980s and has decreased to less than 5% in recent decades after the use of minimally invasive approaches with less extensive neck dissection at initial parathyroidectomy17,22,41. We recognize that the rate of hypocalcemia after reoperation was fairly high in this study (37.4%)17. Although only patients with parathyroid insufficiency should be registered, some clinics prescribed calcium or vitamin D prophylactically18. Another explanation is that the follow-up time in this study was rather short, and longer follow-up would probably have been associated with lower rates of postoperative hypocalcemia17. Overall, data from the Eurocrine database show that the incidence of hypocalcemia is ~1.7 times more frequent after reintervention than after initial parathyroidectomy (37.4 vs. 22.1%)18.

This study has several limitations. There was no information regarding the initial surgical management of the included patients (imaging, operative notes, pathology, persistence, or recurrence). Therefore, it was not possible to incorporate these clinical data before reoperation into our analysis10. In addition, the available data did not allow us to differentiate between persistence and recurrence of PHPT. Second, the factors that define the hospital were lacking and this is a major drawback of the study. Furthermore, the follow-up period was short, which may have altered the incidence of postoperative hypocalcemia. Finally, as with all registry studies, there is a risk of erroneous registrations14,16–18. As 76 hospitals participated, there was a possibility of selection bias in the registrations. However, no systematic bias in the loss of data (missing) was detected18.

In the context of these limitations, we conclude that there is a significant variation between hospitals in the outcomes observed after reoperation for PHPT, unrelated to previously known factors. After adjusting for this variation, a single focus visualized on sestamibi or PET imaging can be used to select patients for reoperation. This new imaging strategy is simple to define and, above all, to use since it involves visualizing a single fixation observed prior to surgery on either sestamibi and/or PET imaging, but without the need for concordance. This study shows that this new imaging criterion performs better than the concordance observed between two imaging examinations prior to reintervention. However, we recognize that this new criterion needs to be tested with other datasets and validated in further studies.
