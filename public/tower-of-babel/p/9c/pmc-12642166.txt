Systematic review and meta-analysis of Staphylococcus aureus at the human-animal-environment Interface in eastern Africa: Prevalence, virulence genes, and antimicrobial resistance profile
Authors: Melkie Dagnaw Fenta, Abebe Belete Bitew, Beruktayet Wondu Demisse, Solomon Lulie Abey, Bemrew Admassu Mengistu, Mebrie Zemene Kinde, Kalkidan Getnet, Yitayew Demessie, Yesuneh Tefera Mekasha, Melaku Getahun Feleke, Atsede Solomon Mebratu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStaphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a major pathogen affecting humans, animals, and the environment (HAE) and poses a critical global health threat due to its ability to develop antimicrobial resistance (AMR), including methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA). Although numerous studies have reported on S. aureus in East Africa, the findings are scattered and vary in methodology, making it difficult to understand regional prevalence, AMR patterns, and virulence profiles. This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesizes existing evidence to provide a comprehensive overview, informing regional public health strategies, antimicrobial stewardship, and targeted interventions to control the spread of resistant S. aureus.MethodsFollowing PRISMA guidelines, a comprehensive search of major electronic databases (PubMed, African Journals Online, SCOPUS, M-libraries, Web of Science, and HINARI) was conducted for English-language cross-sectional studies published between 2000 and 2024 reporting S. aureus detection at the HAE interface or on occupational equipment in East Africa. Studies meeting predefined inclusion criteria underwent quality assessment using the Joanna Briggs Institute checklist. Pooled prevalence rates were calculated using a random-effects model meta-analysis, with heterogeneity assessed via I2 statistics and Cochran's Q test. Publication bias was evaluated using funnel plots and Egger's regression tests.ResultsFrom 4748 initial articles, 28 studies were included, revealing an overall S. aureus prevalence of 19 % (95 % CI: 14–25 %) across the HAE interface, exhibiting significant heterogeneity (I2 = 98 %). Subgroup analysis indicated the highest prevalence in Tanzania (26 %), followed by Ethiopia (19 %) and Kenya (18 %). AMR analysis demonstrated substantial resistance to penicillin (88.82 %), ampicillin (58.96 %), and tetracycline (45.73 %), with multidrug resistance (MDR) detected in 39.55 % of isolates and MRSA prevalence at 27.63 %. Key resistance genes, including mecA, blaZ, tet(K), and erm(C), were identified, alongside virulence genes like pvl and tsst-1.ConclusionThis review emphasizes the imperative need for interventions, including the enhancement of antimicrobial stewardship, the advancement of surveillance systems, and the implementation of One Health strategies to curtail the issues of AMR in S. aureus in East Africa.Keywords: Staphylococcus aureus, Antimicrobial resistance, Virulence genes, Meta-analysis, East Africa

Background

Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) is a gram-positive bacterium that colonizes humans, animals, and the environment [1]. Its presence at the human-animal-environment interface is concerning due to its antimicrobial resistance (AMR) and variable virulence factors [[2], [3], [4]]. S. aureus is a major human pathogen, responsible for infections ranging from mild skin conditions to severe diseases such as pneumonia, sepsis, and endocarditis [5]. It causes both community-acquired and healthcare-associated infections and is strongly linked to high rates of AMR in hospitals and communities [6,7]. Methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) is a major cause of hospital-acquired infections, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs), where it contributes to increased morbidity, mortality, healthcare costs, and resource utilization [[7], [8], [9], [10]]. Globally, MRSA accounts for a substantial proportion of infection-related deaths.

Staphylococcus aureus is prevalent in animals and causes diseases such as mastitis (in dairy cows), joint infections, and skin infections. This poses an economic challenge for the livestock industry [1]. When S. aureus infects animals, it forms biofilms, making eradication more difficult, as the biofilms allow bacteria to adhere to tissues and surfaces, exacerbating infections [2]. Thus, animals act as reservoirs of staphylococcal transmission to humans. It is an occupational hazard and farm workers, veterinarians, and abattoir workers are at risk [3]. S. aureus can persist in the environment, contaminating soil, water, and air. This contributes to transmission, spread, and persistence between animals and humans. S. aureus has frequently been reported in animal production and processing environments, including barns, stables, and slaughterhouses [2,4,5].

Researchers have continued to study S. aureus to inform public health strategies and to improve patient outcomes. A study in Guangzhou, China, isolated S. aureus from women and children and characterized them by spa typing, multi-locus sequence typing (MLST), virulence gene profiling, antimicrobial resistance testing, and analysis of staphylococcal chromosomal cassette mec (SCCmec) types [6]. While much attention has been paid to MRSA, the Methicillin-Susceptible S. aureus (MSSA) also remains important. The molecular epidemiology of S. aureus especially that of MRSA, has evolved rapidly in recent years. Vancomycin has long been the preferred antimicrobial for the treatment of S. aureus infections. However, the emergence of resistance in S. aureus strains poses a significant challenge to their continued clinical efficacy [7]. Recent investigations have revealed genetically diverse clonal populations of MRSA, providing insights into their origins [5,8,9].

Research in Eastern African countries (including Burundi, Kenya, Rwanda, Tanzania, and Uganda) has explored various aspects of S. aureus, revealing antimicrobial resistance in both human and animal populations [10]. A study in southwestern Uganda investigated S. aureus in bulk milk and raw milk products from pastoral communities and highlighted the prevalence of MRSA and other strains [4]. Staphylococcus aureus has epidemiologically emerged as a distinct clone in community settings in Africa [11], which was uncovered in another study in Ethiopia that focused on the clonality and antimicrobial susceptibility of MRSA isolates in clinical specimens from selected health facilities [12]. To identify spreading clones and lineages of MRSA and MSSA, researchers have used molecular techniques such as Multilocus Sequence Typing (MLST) [13], Pulsed-Field Gel Electrophoresis (PFGE) [9], and Spa typing [9], SCCmec Typing [14], and Whole Genome Sequencing (WGS) [[15], [16], [17]]. Such methods could help track the dissemination and understand the shifting epidemiology of S. aureus [9]. However, the continuous evolution of S. aureus has emphasized the need for ongoing surveillance and adaptation of these methodologies. The integration of advanced technologies facilitates rapid and accurate pathogen characterization, which is crucial for timely infection control [13,15,16].

There is considerable variability in the prevalence of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) in East Africa, with reported rates ranging from 31.5 % to 42 % among patients and healthcare workers in Uganda, 31 % to 82 % in Rwanda, 10 % to 50 % in Tanzania, and 53.4 % at Kenyatta National Hospital in Kenya [9]. MRSA poses major challenges due to antimicrobial resistance; even vancomycin, once a reliable option, now faces emerging resistance, while few approved anti-MRSA drugs remain available. Overall, antimicrobial resistance (AMR) in S. aureus is a global health concern [27,28], also evident in eastern Africa, and is driven by multiple factors. Within the One Health framework, which recognizes the interconnectedness of humans, animals, and the environment [27], S. aureus transmission highlights this complexity. Therefore, this systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate the pooled prevalence, resistance profile, and virulence genes of S. aureus at the human–animal–environment interface in East Africa, providing crucial insights for AMR management strategies in the region.

Methods

The study protocol was published online PROSPERO 2024 at https://www.crd.york.ac.uk/PROSPERO/view/CRD42024493284. This review was carried out following the guidelines outlined in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses (PRISMA) checklist [18] (Supplementary File 1).

Search strategy

The search was conducted between 20/08/2024 and 3/10/2024. A thorough search technique was developed to examine all relevant studies. To address our research objectives, we conducted a comprehensive search of PubMed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and carefully selected keywords. (“Staphylococcus aureus” OR “S. aureus”) AND ((“Prevalence” OR “Occurrence” OR “Epidemiology”) OR (“Antimicrobial resistance” OR “AMR” OR “Drug resistance” OR “Antibiotic resistance”) OR (“Virulence factors” OR “Virulence genes” OR “toxins” OR “adherence factors”)) AND ((“Human-animal-environment interface” OR “One Health” OR “Zoonosis” OR “Zoonotic transmission”) OR (“Livestock” OR “Cattle” OR “Sheep” OR “Goats” OR “Poultry” OR “Domestic animals” OR “Companion animals”)) AND (“East Africa” OR “Ethiopia” OR “Kenya” OR “Tanzania” OR “Uganda” OR “Somalia” OR “Rwanda” OR “Burundi” OR “South Sudan”). This search was structured to answer the following key questions: (1) what is the overall prevalence of Staphylococcus aureus isolates from the HAE interface in East Africa? (2) What is the overall proportion of Staphylococcus aureus isolates exhibiting antimicrobial resistance at the HAE interface in East Africa? (3) What is the overall proportion of multidrug-resistant (MDR) Staphylococcus aureus isolates from the HAE interface in East Africa? (4) What is the overall proportion of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) isolates from the HAE interface in East Africa.

The literature search was carried out independently by six researchers (MD, BA, YT, AS, MG and MZ). The online databases including PubMed, AJOL, SCOPUS, and M-libraries, Web of Sciences, HINARI and others were searched. Titles and abstracts were screened, followed by a full-text analysis of selected manuscripts. Seven authors (YD, SL, ABB, BAM, MZ, AS, and KG) selected eligible studies, independently. Disagreements were resolved via consensus by two authors (MD, YD). The investigators discussed the inclusion criteria in detail and depth before the screening process and held weekly reports on WhatsApp to discuss any doubts about their application to standardize their approach as much as possible and face-to-face meetings in case. We used Rayyan manage article screening and deduplication (available at www.rayyan.ai).

Study eligibility criteria

Inclusion

The inclusion criteria for this analysis specifically consisted of the following: (I) articles that presented a clear estimate of the proportion of S. aureus separately in animals and humans, and implemented within the same article and study year; (II) observational studies that demonstrated the prevalence of S. aureus isolates at the human-animal interface and/or work equipment in east African countries; (III) the human participants were dairy farm workers with frequent exposure to dairy animals, livestock owners and slaughterhouse workers, including butchers; veterinarians, milkers, all age with animal exposure from both sexes; (IV) The study included domestic ruminants commonly consumed as meat, such as goats, sheep, and cattle, as well as poultry. It also included dairy cows and animals selected for slaughter. All age groups and both sexes of animals were considered; (IV) Samples were collected from workers (e.g., hand swabs and nasal swabs), animals (both slaughter and dairy cattle), and farm equipment (including knives, milk tanks, and milking buckets).

Exclusion criteria

The following types of studies were not included in the analysis: those involving camels or other species, those with unclear or imprecise estimates of bacterial species across the affected host, review articles, hospital workers, hospital working equipment, duplicates, summary-only studies, qualitative studies, or knowledge, attitude, and practice (KAP) studies. Book chapters, case reports, editorials, short communications, opinions, or studies without original data. For studies that used only samples from dairies or slaughterhouses, the samples were excluded. This analysis also excluded articles that discussed patients who had no animal contact, those who had no animals, or those who had no animal exposures with S. aureus patients. Moreover, molecular epidemiological research, surveillance, systematic reviews/meta-analyses, and reviews were not included.

Data extraction

Data extraction was performed independently by four authors (MD, BAM, MZ, and K.G.). The following information was collected from the included studies: first author and year of publication, study period, study region, sample source, sample type, diagnostic method, overall examination results, number of positive events, S. aureus and MRSA prevalence, antimicrobial resistance patterns, and virulence genotypes. Primary and secondary outcomes were also recorded. A two-reviewer system was implemented to minimize bias: the initial data extraction was performed by two authors independently (MD and BAM), and the extracted data was subsequently verified by the other two authors (MZ and K.G.). Any disagreements arising during the critical appraisal process were resolved through discussion among all authors.

Outcomes

The primary outcomes included the pooled prevalence of S. aureus isolates and the proportion of those S. aureus isolates exhibiting antimicrobial resistance (AMR) within the HAE interface, assessed across all studies. The percentage of resistance for each antibiotic was calculated as the proportion of resistance (n), the total number of isolates (N). Secondary outcomes focused on the proportion of S. aureus isolates that were multidrug-resistant (MDR) and the proportion of S. aureus isolates that were methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA), across all studies.

Contextual definitions

Interfaces

refer to the specific points of interaction and contact where S. aureus, including its multidrug-resistant strains, is exchanged and transmitted between workers, livestock, and the surrounding environment. These interfaces could be the dynamic locations where bacteria, their resistance genes, and related factors come into contact, creating pathways for bacterial transmission and the spread of antimicrobial resistance. Understanding these interfaces is crucial for identifying risk factors and developing effective interventions for controlling S. aureus MDR and MRSA in this Study.

Environment is defined as all locations and items directly associated with agricultural practices involving livestock (including cattle, poultry, pigs, sheep, and goats) or the cultivation of crops linked to animal feed, extending beyond the farm itself to include abattoirs (slaughterhouses) and butcher's shops or processing facilities. This encompasses animal housing, manure management areas, pasture and grazing lands, water sources, feed and feed storage, general farm equipment, the farm workers' immediate environment, and the aforementioned processing environments. These interconnected settings represent potential reservoirs, sites of antimicrobial exposure, and transmission pathways for Staphylococcus aureus along the entire farm-to-consumer pathway.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is the ability of a bacterium to resist antimicrobial treatment, rendering it ineffective.

Methicillin resistance S. aureus (MRSA) is any strain of S. aureus that has developed or acquired multiple drug resistance to beta-lactam antimicrobial s in animals and humans.

Multidrug resistance (MDR) is the ability of S. aureus strains to resist the effects of multiple classes of antimicrobial s (at least three) that are structurally and functionally unrelated.

Assessment of study quality

In this review, two independent authors assessed methodological quality using the critical appraisal checklist of the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) used for studies reporting prevalence data [19]. The checklist is based on nine questions dealing with the quality of a study (Supplementary 2), These criteria included appropriateness of the sampling frame, use of a suitable sampling technique, adequate sample size, description of study subjects and setting, sufficient data analysis, use of valid methods for identifying conditions, use of valid measurements for all participants, use of appropriate statistical analysis, and an adequate response rate. However, the ninth question was excluded in our context (was the response rate adequate?). Authors, A.S and Y·D, employed “Yes =1,” “No,” or “Unclear =0” answers to evaluate the quality of the included articles. Discrepancies among the authors were resolved through discussion, and where necessary, by consultation with two authors (MD, YD). Consensus was reached in all cases. Studies with 7 or 8 “Yes” responses (>75 %) were categorized as high quality, indicating that these studies met most or all of the essential criteria and demonstrated strong methodological rigor. Studies were categorized into three quality levels. Those with 5 or 6 “Yes” responses (50–75 %) were classified as moderate quality, indicating they met several key criteria but had some methodological limitations. Studies with 4 or fewer “Yes” responses (<50 %) were deemed low quality, reflecting significant design or execution weaknesses that could limit the reliability of their findings. Only studies scoring ≥60 % were considered for final inclusion and analysis, effectively excluding all low-quality studies.

Data synthesis and statistical analysis

The random effects model, which employs the restricted maximum likelihood method (REML) to compute within- and between-study variability, was employed to estimate the aggregated prevalence and 95 % confidence intervals. This model was utilized to conduct a comprehensive meta-analysis (overall effect size (ES) or often represented as a percentage or proportion), assess heterogeneity, and determine the weight of each study. To account for expected between-study heterogeneity, proportions of resistance were combined across studies using random-effects models. For this purpose, we used mixed-effects logistic regression models per bacteria and antimicrobial, with a random intercept. These data were then subjected to proportional meta-analysis via the “metaprop” function of the ‘meta’ package version 4.1.3–0 in R statistical software [20].

Investigation of heterogeneity

In this meta-analysis, τ2 (representing between-study variance), Cochran's Q test (indicated by the p-value), and the inverse variance index (I2) were employed to assess the sources of heterogeneity across the studies. The I2 index, as described by Higgins and Thompson [21] was calculated to indicate low, moderate, and high levels of heterogeneity, corresponding to I2 values of <30 %, between 30 % and 60 %, and > 60 %, respectively. Notably, heterogeneity was deemed statistically significant if the I2 value was more than 30 %, and the Q test yielded a P value less than 0.10. The magnitude of study heterogeneity was evaluated through a forest plot diagram, which depicted the weights, effect magnitudes, and 95 % confidence intervals for between-study variability. A subgroup analysis was performed to investigate the prevalence of S. aureus, considering factors such as study period, study location, sampling unit (animals, humans, or others), and sample type/source.

Publication bias assessment and sensitivity analysis

Publication bias is an inevitable problem in systematic review and meta-analysis; it is well-known and one most important types of bias termed reporting bias, so it is also one of the main threats to the validity of meta-analysis. Publication bias is usually evaluated through funnel plot in which asymmetry may be assessed visually, and by using the Egger test. So Small study effects and publication bias were visualized using funnel plot diagrams and Egger's regression test [22]. Egger's regression test is used to test the funnel-plot symmetry, in which a regression model is built, using the standardized estimate of the effect size as a dependent variable and the inverse of the standard error as an independent variable. Hence, to assess publication bias, funnel plot and Egger's regression test were utilized.

A leave-one-out sensitivity analysis was performed to assess the influence of individual studies on the pooled effect size and overall heterogeneity. During this analysis, each study was iteratively excluded and recalculated.

Results

Literature search results

According to the PRISM 2020 flow chart (Fig. 1), an initial search identified 4748 articles across various electronic databases: PubMed (n = 10), SCOPUS (n = 1, 993), Web of Science (n = 356), AJOL (n = 4), HINARI (n = 14), M-libraries (n = 2356) and others (n = 13). After excluding 148 articles due to duplicates, 4, 600 articles remained. Of these, 3, 425 were removed based on a review of titles and abstracts. A total of 1, 175 articles were then sought for retrieval and 756 studies were not retrieved. Out of the 410 articles assessed for eligibility, 382 were excluded for various reasons. The minimum score for quality assessment was 62 % according to the JBI critical appraisal checklist used for studies reporting prevalence data. Thus, only 28 studies were considered for meta-analysis of the overall prevalence of S. aureus from animal-human-environment interface studies (Fig. 1 and Table 1).

Overview of the included Articles (n = 28) in the meta-analysis

Our analysis revealed a noticeable gap in research regarding S. aureus across East Africa. This scarcity likely stems from the fact that very few studies in the region comprehensively explore the interconnectedness of animal, human, and environmental factors in S. aureus transmission. In East Africa, we found relevant studies from Tanzania, Kenya, Ethiopia, Uganda, and Rwanda.

Ethiopia stood out as the primary contributor to the available literature, accounting for the majority of studies (n = 15; about 53 %). Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, and Rwanda contributed a much smaller proportion, with 4 (14 %), 4 (14 %), 3 (11 %), and 2 (8 %) studies, respectively. It is worth noting that some individual articles were used multiple times in our analysis because they reported data from different sampling units within the same study. All included studies employed a cross-sectional study design and various sampling units. For example, [23] investigated contamination in the meat supply chain by sampling from abattoir meat, butcher shop equipment, and the butchers themselves. In contrast, [24] looked at dairy settings, collecting samples from the environment (floor swabs), workers, and dairy cows. Overall, the studies tended to focus on dairy farms, abattoirs, and individuals who worked in these settings. Common samples included udder milk, swabs taken from hands, nasal swab, equipment, meat and meat swab, feces, and urine and blood samples. The review of 28 research studies focused on S. aureus diagnostics across various East African countries highlights significant trends and findings in public health (Fig. 1). Spanning from 2010 to 2024, these studies primarily investigated multidrug-resistant (MDR) organisms, with 20 studies dedicated to this topic and 10 specifically addressing MRSA. The majority of the studies in Ethiopia were conducted in the provinces of Oromia, Addis Ababa, and Mekelle. Other countries included in the research were Tanzania, Kenya, Uganda, and Rwanda, with investigations conducted in both urban and rural settings. The samples analyzed in these studies were from diverse sources, including udder or bulk milk, nasal and skin swabs, blood, urine, and environmental swabs.

Researchers collected samples from a wide range of hosts, including various animals like cows, sheep, swine, chickens, and dogs, as well as humans such as farmers, abattoir workers, and household members. This approach underscores the interconnectedness of human, animal, and environmental health, aligning with the one health concept. A combination of traditional diagnostic techniques like culture and biochemical tests as well as modern molecular methods like PCR and DNAse tests was employed. Some studies also utilized specialized techniques, such as Mannitol salt agar and Staphaurex, to enhance the identification of microbial pathogens.

The studies included in this review exhibited considerable variation in sample size. While some investigations analyzed fewer than 100 samples, others such as the study by [25] in Uganda, examined over 3000. This variation in sample size was mirrored by the wide range of reported positivity rates, likely reflecting differences in microbial pathogen prevalence across the studied populations. For example, Sonola [3] reported 413 positive cases out of 855 chicken cloacal swabs in Tanzania, whereas [26] found only 14 positive cases in 50 tank milk samples from Ethiopia. The variations in positivity rates underscore the significant presence of microbial pathogens and highlight urgent public health concerns, particularly regarding zoonotic and foodborne diseases and antimicrobial resistance (Table 1).

Pooled Proportion of S. aureus (Meta-analysis)

The Proportion of S. aureus extracted from the 28 eligible articles was subjected for meta-analysis. As a result, it was found that the overall random pooled proportion of S. aureus across studies was 19 % with the confidence interval ranging from 14 % to 25 %. Statistically significant heterogeneity was found between studies regarding S. aureus prevalence (I2 = 98 %, Q-test = 1521.65, p < 0.001). Despite the high heterogeneity, the studies contributed almost equally to the overall analysis, with individual study weights ranging narrowly from 3.1 % to 3.7 %(Fig. 2). This suggests each study was considered equally influential in the pooled estimate, with no single study disproportionately affecting the results.

Subgroup meta-analysis results

Given the considerable heterogeneity observed in the overall analysis, subgroup analyses were conducted to identify sources of variability across studies. These analyses considered potential moderators such as geographical location, sampling source/setting (e.g., abattoir, dairy farm), publication year, type of sample (e.g., udder cow's milk, swab, blood), and sampling unit (human, animal, environment).

Country-wise subgroup analysis revealed significant differences in S. aureus prevalence (Q = 30.28, p < 0.0001). Studies conducted in Ethiopia reported a prevalence of 0.19 (95 % CI: 0.12 to 0.27), while studies from Tanzania showed a higher prevalence of 0.26 (95 % CI: 0.14 to 0.43). Resistance of S. aureus in individual antimicrobials (interfaces)

The incidence of antimicrobial resistance, a paramount issue in public health, manifests significant discrepancies across various classes of antimicrobials, categories of animals and humans, as well as types of samples. In the case of chloramphenicol, a substantial degree of resistance was documented in animal nasal swabs (84 %), although this figure exhibited considerable variability across different studies [40,43,46,51]. Correspondingly, the level of resistance identified in animal milk was also considerable (28 %) and characterized by heterogeneity. In human samples, nasal swabs exhibited a high resistance rate (62 %) accompanied by significant heterogeneity, whereas hand swabs revealed a comparatively lower yet less variable resistance (32 %).

Concerning tetracycline, based on 10 studies [32,[35], [36], [37],39,40,42,43,46,51], the data indicate high pooled resistance levels in animal nasal swabs (89 %) and considerable resistance in milk (37 %), both showing significant heterogeneity. As for human samples, blood showed moderate resistance (36 %), nasal swabs demonstrated a high resistance rate (65 %), and hand swabs displayed high resistance (94 %). In terms of erythromycin, animal milk revealed moderate (31 %) resistance with significant heterogeneity; whereas nasal swabs showed lower (21.4 %) resistance without variability. As a contrast, human blood samples displayed low resistance (19 %) and heterogeneity. Hand swabs displayed moderate resistance (23 % with moderate variability), and nasal swabs showed moderate resistance (50 %) with significant heterogeneity.

With respect to gentamycin, animals displayed lower resistance in milk (16 %) characterized by significant heterogeneity, yet high resistance was evident in nasal swabs (75 %) accompanied by considerable variability. Human blood samples exhibited moderate resistance (28 %) without heterogeneity, while nasal swabs showed high resistance (58 %) characterized by significant heterogeneity. According to a single study, animal milk showed very low resistance to clindamycin (2 %) while nasal swabs showed moderate resistance (54 %). Even so, human nasal swabs showed moderate resistance (59 %) with significant heterogeneity.

Analyzing TMX (Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole) [32,35,40], animal nasal swabs exhibited high resistance (71 %) without significant variability, while milk demonstrated moderate resistance (29 %) with considerable heterogeneity. There was a low resistance level (54 %) in human blood swabs, whereas high resistance (47 %), which was heterogeneous, was found in nasal swabs, and lower resistance was found in hand swabs (18 %). Resistance to rifampicin was low in both animal nasal swabs (6.93 %) and milk (6.01 %), with moderate heterogeneity apparent in both samples. Human nasal swabs illustrated moderate resistance (40 %) with high heterogeneity.

Drawing upon a comprehensive set of studies [36,37,39,40,43,46,51], the pooled analysis revealed distinct differences in vancomycin resistance across sample types. Resistance was found to be moderately concerning in animal milk samples, standing at 33 % with low heterogeneity. This model acknowledges the likely variation in true MDR rates across different populations and settings, unlike the common effect model, which assumes homogeneity. The random effects model resulted in a higher pooled proportion of 39.55 % (95 % CI: 30.36 % - 49.54 %) with I2 = 93.8, compared to the common effect model's 32.38 %, and a wider confidence interval, reflecting the uncertainty introduced by the heterogeneity.

Methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA)

Table 6 represents reports on the individual prevalence of MRSA across various studies. Of the 28 articles reviewed, ten articles provided clear and consistent presentations of MRSA prevalence. The minimum MRSA proportion was observed in a study with only 0.5 % of isolates being MRSA, while the maximum was 100 %, indicating that all isolates in that particular study were MRSA. Most studies report moderate to low proportions of MRSA, with the majority falling below 30 %. The meta-analysis of ten studies estimating the pooled proportion of MRSA revealed a point estimate of 0.2856 (28.56 %) using the common effect model and 0.2763 (27.63 %) using the random effects model. While the point estimates are relatively similar, the confidence interval from the random effects model (0.1527 to 0.4471) is considerably wider than that of the common effect model (0.2661 to 0.3059), indicating that the random effects model is preferable. The use of the random effects model is justified by the assumption that the true proportion of MRSA varies across the study populations due to differences in population, study methods, geographic location, or other factors and the wide confidence interval highlights the large variation between studies.

Resistance and virulence features of S. aureus

Mzee et al. [29] identified both antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes. The study, used whole genome sequencing (NextSeq550), and Staphylococcus aureus isolates were screened for antimicrobial resistance and virulence genes. The antimicrobial resistant genes identified in this study were tet (K), blaZ, dfrG, erm(C), str, qacG and fosB encoding for tetracycline, penicillin, trimethoprim, macrolide, lincosamide, streptogramin B (MLSB), and multidrug efflux pumps respectively. Furthermore, mecA was also discovered in one of the 55 isolates included in the study. The study identified virulence genes including aur, hlgA, hlgB, hlgC, splA, splB, splE, edinB, edinC. Ogeto [32] conducted a study, and detected antimicrobial resistance genes. The study, employed conventional PCR assay to detect the expression of antimicrobial resistance genes from phenotypically antimicrobial resistance S. aureus isolates. All the three S. aureus isolates from milk that were phenotypically methicillin resistant did not express mecA or mecC genes while S. aureus isolates (n = 20) from human blood, 17 (85 %) expressed mecA gene and 3 (15 %) isolates did not express mecA nor mecC genes and 22.64 % expressed gyrA gene, 24.53 % expressed gyrB gene and 18.87 % expressed tetM gene by PCR. On the other hand, 4 (15.38 %) of the 26 isolates from raw cow milk expressed tetM gene and the three isolates of S. aureus that were phenotypically methicillin resistant did not express neither mecA nor mecC genes. Furthermore, multidrug resistance was observed among 19 (35.84 %) isolates of S. aureus from human blood. About 78.95 % of the multidrug resistant isolates from human blood were MRSA and 73.68 % of MDR-S. aureus harbored mecA, 63.16 % gyrA, 68.42 % gyrB and 26.32 % tetM genes. Results indicated that mecA gene was the predominant genetic determinant for methicillin resistance phenotypes, followed by gyrB, gyrA and tetM for the resistance of ciprofloxacin and tetracycline respectively, and depicted in Table 6. Overall, the study indicated an overall difference in resistance of S. aureus strains from humans to nearly all-antimicrobial classes as compared to S. aureus isolates from raw milk.

Evidences of genetic exchanges

Among the included studies, some studies have been carried out to establish the possibility of Staphylococcus aureus transfer from cows to humans genetically. For instance, a study found that spa-type t645 was present in S. aureus strains from both bovine and human origins. However, while the initial analysis may distinguish between bacterial species of the human and bovine samples, genotyping can reveal a high degree of genetic relatedness among the S. aureus strains from these hosts. Kateete et al. [36] observed this in Uganda and found that there was genetically similar S. aureus (spa-CC435, spa-type t645, which is equivalent to ST121) from humans and cattle although the isolates had different antimicrobial susceptibility profiles. Moreover, the study established that ST121 isolates whether from human sources in Dar es Salaam or from cows in Tanga are likely to have similar properties and may carry blaZ, dfrG, and erm(C) resistance genes. Multidrug resistance was also reported in a subset of S. aureus isolates; most of which belonged to clonal complexes CC152 and CC121. The presence of S. aureus lineages typical of one host (e.g. CC97 in cattle) in the other (e.g. humans) suggests that there is the potential for species to cross-transmit [29].

Discussion

Evidence of antimicrobial resistance within the one health framework is pivotal for establishing stewardship programs to combat resistance and ensure the prudent use of antimicrobials across human, animal, and environmental interfaces [[54], [55], [56]]. Antimicrobial resistance patterns at the interface between humans, animals, and the environment have been increasing in Africa. This highlights the importance of the current study in supporting the development and implementation of one health policies. In East Africa, Veterinary pharmaceutical disposal systems is at its infancy stage [57] and limited prudent antimicrobial use [58] have contributed to antimicrobial resistance across the human, animal, and environmental interfaces. Staphylococcus aureus, a prevalent gram-positive microorganism, acts as an indicator for this alarming trend. If drugs are not used and disposed of properly, resistance is likely to develop in samples isolated from animal products, the environment, and humans [59].

This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to synthesize and compare research findings on the interplay between human, animal, and environmental factors influencing antimicrobial resistance. The focus was on compiling data regarding S. aureus genes conferring antimicrobial resistance within this one health context. Notably, research did not aim to directly link resistant S. aureus strains across the animal-human-environment range in terms of epidemiology. Instead, the final objective was to offer a thorough summary that may guide one health policy initiatives in the future. The current study revealed that approximately 53 % articles from Ethiopia, with the remaining from Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, and Rwanda. This finding underscores the need for ongoing research in one health context in east African countries. Based on this meta-analysis, an overall random pooled proportion of S. aureus for east Africa was estimated to be 19 %, with a 95 % confidence interval ranging from 14 % to 25 %.This magnificent level of percentage demonstrated the urgent need to address the widespread use of the pathogen's antimicrobial resistance within the animal-human-environment interface. Implementing strict antimicrobial stewardship policies and promoting responsible practices are critical to mitigating the further development and spread of resistance in this interconnected system [60].

The subgroup analysis revealed a highest prevalence of S. aureus in Tanzania (26 %) among east Africa countries. This categorical variation suggests as there could be a potential differences in prescription patterns, expired drug disposal systems [57], veterinary stewardship programs, and enforcement of veterinary treatment practices in various clinical settings across different countries [58,61]. Additionally, a report from South Africa, based on two studies conducted in the same year, indicated substantial differences due to variations in sample types, collection methods, isolation techniques, and geographical locations, contributing to discrepancies and supporting the current meta-analysis [62,63]. Furthermore, the pooled prevalence of S. aureus in the subgroup analysis was highest in hand swabs (24 %), followed by milk samples (22 %). However, research from South Africa showed that the prevalence rates varied, with 20.4 % (102/500) of the samples being beef, 3.3 % (10/300) sheep, 14 % (14/100) pork, and 4 % (8/200) milk [63]. The current pooled proportion result was significantly elevated compared to prior results, notwithstanding the variance in sample types. This observed increase potentially indicates rapid dissemination of the microorganism through hand swabs and milk, representing a significant public health risk.

The rising issue of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) is stressed by the data presented, which reveals notable discrepancies in the prevalence of resistance across various classes of antimicrobials, distinct animal and human cohorts, and different sample types. Importantly, a substantial proportion of the documented resistance levels demonstrate statistically significant heterogeneity (indicated by a low p-value for I2), suggesting considerable variability among the studies contributing to this field. This intrinsic heterogeneity accentuates the context-specific nature of AMR, which is shaped by a complex interaction of local factors. For numerous combinations of antimicrobial agents and sample types in both animal and human populations were elevated resistance proportions. For example, resistance to chloramphenicol in animal nasal swabs and milk, alongside human nasal swabs, revealed substantial variability. This observation implies that determinants such as geographical location, animal husbandry methodologies, human sanitation standards, and varying patterns of antimicrobial utilization likely differ markedly across the studies included, thus influencing the resistance levels observed. Correspondingly, tetracycline resistance, which was markedly high in animal nasal swabs and moderately prevalent in animal milk, also exhibited significant heterogeneity. Elevated resistance to tetracycline in human nasal swabs further corroborated this pattern. Given the extensive utilization of tetracycline in both human and veterinary medicine globally, including in African contexts for the treatment of infections and as growth promoters in livestock [64], this heterogeneity likely reflects the disparate intensity and duration of its application across various environments.

Moreover, moderate resistance to erythromycin in animal milk displayed significant heterogeneity, as did lower resistance levels in human blood samples and moderate resistance in human nasal swabs. Since macrolides like erythromycin are essential for the treatment of respiratory and dermal infections, the observed variability in resistance may be associated with divergent prescribing practices and the prevalence of macrolide-resistant bacterial strains within particular populations and animal production systems. High resistance levels to gentamycin in animal nasal swabs and moderate resistance in animal milk, along with elevated resistance in human nasal swabs, all demonstrated significant heterogeneity. As aminoglycosides such as gentamycin are pivotal for managing severe bacterial infections, this variability may be linked to differences in infection control strategies and the selective pressure imposed by gentamycin usage across diverse healthcare and agricultural contexts. Resistance to TMX (Trimethoprim-Sulfamethoxazole), a commonly used and cost-effective antimicrobial, especially in resource-limited environments, exhibited significant heterogeneity in animal milk and high resistance in human nasal swabs. This variation could reflect differences in accessibility, guidelines for prescribing, and the prevalence of co-resistance mechanisms in varied contexts.

Furthermore, very high resistance to ciprofloxacillin in animal nasal swabs and elevated resistance in human nasal swabs both demonstrated significant heterogeneity. Since fluoroquinolones like ciprofloxacin are broad-spectrum antimicrobial s utilized for a range of infections, the observed variability in resistance may be associated with differing degrees of their application and the emergence and dissemination of resistance mechanisms, including plasmid-mediated resistance. Moderate resistance to oxacillin in both animal milk and nasal swabs also exhibited significant heterogeneity. Given that oxacillin is a penicillinase-resistant penicillin employed to treat Staphylococcus aureus infections, this variability likely reflects the diverse prevalence of methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) and other resistant staphylococcal strains across various populations and animal species.

Finally, the pronounced resistance to ampicillin in dairy products derived from animals exhibited considerable heterogeneity. As widely utilized broad-spectrum penicillin, this variability is likely indicative of the disparate levels of beta-lactamase enzyme production among bacterial populations situated in various environmental contexts.

These findings are especially troubling when considered within the framework of Africa and East Africa, regions that frequently experience a heightened prevalence of infectious diseases while grappling with deficiencies in diagnostic resources, healthcare infrastructure, and effective infection control methodologies [65]. The rampant and frequently unregulated application of antimicrobial s across both human and veterinary sectors in these locales further intensifies the situation [66]. In numerous African countries, antimicrobial s are accessible over the counter without the necessity of a prescription, which contributes to their inappropriate use and the consequent selection of resistant microbial strains [[67], [68], [69]]. Moreover, the employment of antimicrobial s as growth enhancers and for prophylactic measures within agricultural practices in these regions propels the development of resistance in animal populations, with the potential for transmission to humans via the food chain or through direct interaction [64]. The heterogeneity observed within Africa and East Africa likely mirrors the diverse socioeconomic conditions, healthcare accessibility, and agricultural methodologies prevalent across varying nations and communities. For instance, the patterns of antimicrobial use in livestock among nomadic pastoralist groups in East Africa may markedly differ from those observed in intensive agricultural systems. Likewise, disparities in antimicrobial consumption and sanitation practices between urban and rural demographics can significantly influence the prevalence of antimicrobial resistance (AMR).

On a global scale, these observations are consistent with the acknowledged escalation of AMR as a formidable threat to public health and food security [70,71]. The notable heterogeneity documented across studies worldwide highlights the localized characteristics of AMR determinants. Elements such as the execution and efficacy of antimicrobial stewardship programs, the integrity of sanitation and hygiene infrastructure, the strength of surveillance frameworks, and the interconnectedness facilitated by international travel and commerce all play integral roles in the intricate patterns of resistance observed. The elevated resistance to commonly prescribed antimicrobial s such as tetracycline, ampicillin, and TMP-SMX across both animal and human samples worldwide accentuates the pressing necessity for robust and context-specific strategies aimed at preserving the effectiveness of these critical therapeutic agents. Thus, the statistically significant heterogeneity noted in the prevalence of resistance to several pivotal antimicrobial s across a range of sample types and populations illustrates the complex nature of the AMR crisis.

The data shown brings to light a key health issue in Africa, particularly in East Africa: the growing problem of S. aureus resistance to antimicrobial s. The wide range of results across studies shows how complex this issue is pointing to differences in how antimicrobial s are used, healthcare systems, and environmental factors. The results show very high rates of resistance to common antimicrobial s like penicillin, ampicillin, and tetracycline. In developing countries, animal health professional often use and prescribe these antimicrobial s as first choices to treat bacterial infections in places with limited resources, and their decreasing effectiveness puts human, animal and the environment health issues at risk. The ongoing heavy use of this older antimicrobial s in many African countries makes it clear that we need programs to manage antimicrobial use better. Also, the high resistance to oxacillin and the presence of mecA genes show that methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) is a big problem.

It has been evidenced that Multidrug resistance (MDR) poses another urgent problem, with a pooled MDR proportion of nearly 39.55 % showing how resistant strains have spread. Based on 20 studies, the pooled prevalence of multidrug resistance was 39.55 % (95 % CI: 30.36 %–49.54 %) (Table 4).

A comprehensive review of bacterial infections in Ethiopia looking at it from a one health angle, studied six types of gram-positive bacteria and thirteen types of gram-negative bacteria. What's worth mentioning is that S. aureus showed up in 53 % of cases (95 %CI: 42–64 %) according to Tweldemedhin et al. [72]. The study identified Staphylococcus species and Enterobacteriaceae as common multidrug-resistant (MDR) bacteria across various sources. Comparable report was also investigated in the current study. The proportion of multi-drug resistant (MDR) S. aureus in clinical isolates has been reported to be notably high in Nepalese tertiary care hospital [73]. It emphasizes the need for an integrated approach to combat MDR bacteria, including policies, interventions, enhanced surveillance, stricter veterinary regulations, improved infection control, and awareness campaigns to reduce inappropriate antimicrobial use for better animal and human health outcomes [74,75]. This high rate reduces treatment choices leading to more morbidity and death. The difference in MDR rates between studies, from 3.1 % to 98.4 %, shows we need to watch local areas and take specific action. The high MDR rates in studies by Kungu et al. [37] and Geletu et al. [24] are worrying (Table 6).

Also, MRSA has a big presence, with a pooled estimate of 27.63 %. MRSA infections make health hurdle worse, keep people in hospitals longer, and cost more to treat. The MRSA rates vary a lot in different places, as shown by the wide range in the random effects model. This suggests that things like hospital cleanliness antimicrobial use, and spread in communities all play a part. Studies by Adugna et al. [23] and Kiiru and Kariuki [48] reporting 100 % and very high MRSA rates further show how bad this problem is in some areas. Recent WHO data revealed MRSA incidence rates exceeding 20 % in all regions, with some countries reporting rates as high as 80 % [76]. In Nepal, MRSA prevalence was reported to vary significantly across institutions, ranging from 26.1 % to 70.6 % [77]. These data underscore the importance of prudent antimicrobial use, continued surveillance of resistance patterns, and the exploration of new therapeutic approaches

The genetic basis of resistance is an indispensable factor in this growing challenge. Identifying resistance genes such as mecA, blaZ, tet(K), erm(C), and dfrG offers essential insights into the molecular mechanisms behind resistance, which can help in creating targeted interventions. The detection of virulence genes like pvl and tsst-1, alongside resistance genes as noted by Obanda et al. [44], suggests the possibility of highly virulent and resistant strains emerging. Additionally, the whole genome sequencing study conducted by Mzee et al. [29] provides a thorough analysis of the resistome and virulome of S. aureus, yielding valuable information for future research and intervention strategies (Table 6).

A One Health approach is essential for tackling AMR, as research on S. aureus at the intersections of human, animal, and environmental health highlights the connections among these areas. The detection of MRSA in both humans and animals, as reported by Kalayu et al. [39] and Kassa et al. [38], indicates a risk for zoonotic transmission. Various factors lead to elevated levels of AMR in Africa, such as inappropriate antimicrobial use stemming from over-prescription, self-medication, and the presence of substandard antimicrobial s. Fragile healthcare systems, marked by insufficient infection control measures, limited diagnostic resources, and poor sanitation, further fuel the spread of resistant infections. Moreover, agricultural practices that involve using antimicrobial s in animal feed for growth promotion also contribute to the emergence of resistant bacteria, which can then be passed on to humans. Environmental pollution from untreated sewage and industrial waste adds to the problem by introducing antimicrobial residues and resistant bacteria into the environment. The absence of strong surveillance systems makes it difficult to monitor AMR trends and carry out timely interventions, while poverty and overcrowding exacerbate the spread of infectious diseases and heighten the demand for antimicrobial s.

We need to take action on several key recommendations to address this crisis. Both human and animal health benefit from antimicrobial stewardship programs. The health care setting and community need to improve hygiene and sanitation to improve infection control practices. Monitoring trends in AMR and tracking the spread of resistant strains will be easier with strong surveillance networks in place. The human, animal, and environmental health sectors should collaborate to promote One Health. For innovative interventions to be developed in Africa, we need to invest in research. Rapid and accurate diagnostic tests will ensure that the right antimicrobial treatment is administered, while public education campaigns can raise awareness of the dangers of antimicrobial misuse. Last but not least, implementing and enforcing regulations to restrict antimicrobial use in agriculture is crucial to reducing the spread of resistant bacteria. A coordinated and sustained effort at the local, national, and global levels is necessary to address these complex challenges.

Limitations: This study focused on cattle, sheep, and goats, which are the predominant livestock species in East African pastoral systems and the main sources of meat, milk, and other animal products consumed by humans. As a result, less common livestock species such as camels were excluded. While this focus allowed for more robust and reliable estimates, it limits the generalizability of the findings to all livestock species in the region. Additionally, the exclusion of camels and other minor livestock may overlook potential reservoirs of zoonotic pathogens or antimicrobial resistance, highlighting the need for future studies to include these species to provide a more comprehensive One Health perspective.

Conclusion

According to this systematic review and meta-analysis, antimicrobial resistance is a significant problem in S. aureus at the HAE interface within the context of East Africa, as indicated by an overall prevalence of S. aureus 19 %. The pooled Prevalence were higher in Tanzania (26 %) than in Ethiopia (19 %) and Kenya (18 %). It was noted that antimicrobial resistance profiles were concerning, with 89.82 % of penicillin resistance, 59.96 % ampicillin resistance, and 44.73 % of tetracycline resistance detected. In addition, 39.55 % of the isolates were multidrug-resistant (MDR), while MRSA accounted for 27.63 %. These findings demonstrate that the public health risk posed by S. aureus would be escalating, and perhaps, be intensified by the excessive utilization of antimicrobial s and insufficient infection control measures. The rise burden of S.aureus resistance in East Africa necessitates immediate and sustained intervention. For mitigating this challenge a multidisciplinary framework that mingles surveillance, policy reform, public education, and innovative therapeutic approaches is imperative for curbing the spread of AMR. Effectively addressing this dilemma requires a unified endeavor at national, regional, and global scales to safeguard public health and preserve the efficacy of antimicrobial s in managing infections.
