Systematic review and meta analysis of cross immunity and the smokers paradox in COVID19
Authors: Jesús Gonzalez-Rubio, Juan D Navarro-López, Lydia Jiménez-Díaz, Alberto Najera
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract COVID-19 pandemic, caused by the novel SARS-CoV-2 virus, has raised significant interest in understanding potential cross-immunity mechanisms. Recent evidence suggests that T-cells associated with common cold coronaviruses (229E, NL63, OC43, HKU1) may provide some level of cross-immunity against SARS-CoV-2. It is also known that the prevalence of smokers among patients admitted to hospital for COVID-19 is lower than expected according to the corresponding country’s smoking prevalence, which is known as smoker’s paradox in COVID-19. No clear consensus to explain it has yet been reached. This phenomenon suggests a complex interaction between smoking and immune response. Nonetheless, very few works have studied the prevalence of smokers in those infected by common cold coronaviruses, and its relation to COVID-19 has not been investigated. We performed a systematic review and meta-analysis to study the prevalence of smokers among patients infected by common cold coronaviruses, and to compare them to the corresponding country’s smoking prevalence. L’Abbé plots were used to visually assess the consistency of the observed effects across the different studies included in the meta-analysis. Additionally, significant differences were found in smoking prevalence among the various types of ccCoV, indicating the need for further research into the biological mechanisms driving these disparities. The results show that smoking prevalence is higher among those patients infected by these coronaviruses than in the general population (OR = 1.37, 95% CI: 0.81–2.33). A study was separately done for the four coronavirus types, and the prevalence of smokers was higher in three of the four than that corresponding to country, gender and study year: OC43 (OR = 1.93, 95% CI: 0.64–5.82); HKU1 (OR = 3.62, 95% CI: 1.21–10.85); NL63 (OR = 1.93, 95% CI: 0.64–5.82); 229E (OR = 0.97, 95% CI: 0.50–1.90). The heterogeneity of the studies was assessed using the Cochrane Chi-squared test, I-squared (I2), and Tau-squared (τ2). This detailed statistical analysis enhances the robustness of our findings and highlights the variations in smoking prevalence among different ccCoVs. Our data suggest that COVID-19 might be less prevalent among smokers due to greater cross-immunity from a larger number or more recent infections by common cold coronaviruses than the non-smoking population, which would explain smoker’s paradox in COVID-19. IMPLICATIONS. The low prevalence of current smokers among SARS-CoV-2 patients is a finding recurrently repeated, even leading to postulate the “smoker’s paradox” in COVID-19. This fact compelled us to study the prevalence of smokers among patients infected by common cold coronaviruses, and to compare them to the corresponding country’s smoking prevalence. Our data could explain smoker’s paradox in COVID-19 by a greater cross immunity due to a larger number, or more recent infections by common cold coronaviruses than the non-smoking population. Introduction

The different coronaviruses described in humans (229E, OC43, SARS, NL63, HKU1, MERS, SARS-CoV-2) can cause both mild common colds and an epidemic or pandemic of serious diseases, and even deaths1. Highly virulent coronavirus species have been responsible for outbreaks of SARSCoV, MERS2 and, finally, SARS-CoV-2, which is the cause of COVID-19 disease and led to a world pandemic3. Conversely, four other coronavirus species (HKU1, OC43, NL63 and 229E) are associated with generally mild respiratory infections4. The proposed collective name for these four coronaviruses is “common cold coronaviruses (ccCoV)”5. It is well-established that smoking predisposes to respiratory infections and is also associated with worse results for those with these infections6. Smokers are at higher risk of chronic respiratory disease and acute respiratory infections7. Current smokers have an increased risk of contracting respiratory infections such as influenza8 and MERS-CoV9 compared to non-smokers. Nevertheless, the low prevalence of current smokers among SARS-CoV-2 patients is a finding that has been repeated in many published works, and different meta-analyses demonstrate this fact10–12.

Although some works state that both current and former smoking significantly increase the risk of disease severity13,the biggest study conducted to date in the USA about smoking and COVID-19 reveals that current and former smokers are at lower risk of being infected by SARS-CoV-2 than those who have never smoked14. This has given way to “smoker’s paradox” in COVID-19 being postulated, whereby smokers may be more protected from infections by SARS-CoV-215. As smoking can never be a therapeutic option in any case because smoking is a poison that kills 8 million people a year worldwide, all epidemiological findings must be profoundly studied in the pandemic context16. To date, many hypotheses have been put forward about smokers’ low prevalence. A relevant one is the possible anti-inflammatory effect of nicotine to slow down cytokine storms17. However, using nicotine patches does not seem to have worked in those patients with serious pneumonia caused by COVID-1918. On the one hand, it has been suggested that the nitric oxide produced by smoking inhibits replication of SARSCoV-2 and it entering cells19. On the other hand, smoking can up regulate ACE2, an anti-inflammatory protein, in the lower respiratory tract15. Another hypothesis to study is whether a possible greater cross-immunity of smokers would make them more susceptible to other coronaviruses than the general population, which our team suggested at the beginning of the COVID-19 pandemic16. There is clear evidence for the cross-immunity of T-cells between ccCoV (229E, NL63, OC43 and HKU1) and SARS-CoV-220–22. In any case, it is important to closely monitor emerging variants, as they have demonstrated reduced susceptibility to immunity from vaccines or previous infections23. Despite extensive research on the relationship between smoking and COVID-19, several key gaps persist. The mechanisms behind the ‘smoker’s paradox,’ particularly the role of cross-immunity from common cold coronaviruses (ccCoV) in potentially protecting smokers against SARS-CoV-2, are not well understood. Furthermore, many studies fail to distinguish between current and former smokers, leading to potential confounding effects. Additionally, the impact of smoking on the efficacy of COVID-19 vaccines and how new viral variants might influence this relationship remain largely unexplored. Addressing these gaps is essential for developing effective public health strategies and enhancing our understanding of the interplay between smoking, cross-immunity, and COVID-19.

Therefore, it seems of potential importance to study if smokers’ ccCoV incidence is higher. If this were the case, it could be one of the main responses for “smoker’s paradox” in COVID-19. In other words, smokers would present greater cross-immunity against SARS-CoV-2 by having more ccCoV infections, or more recent ones. It is necessary to bear in mind that children’s low COVID-19 incidence has also been attributed to, among other factors, greater cross-immunity24 after being more exposed to ccCoV.

However, only a few works have studied the prevalence of smokers in those infected by ccCoV.

So, in this context, this work aims to perform a systematic review and meta-analysis to study the prevalence of smokers among those infected by ccCoV, and to analyse if this prevalence is higher than in the general population.

Methods

Literature search strategy

The systematic review was carried out according to the reported elements for the systematic review and meta-analysis (PRISMA) and MOOSE guidelines25,26,27. Records in two databases (PubMed, Web of Science) were searched until 2 December 2022. A flow chart is provided in Fig. 1. The following search terms were employed: [‘229E’ OR ‘OC43’ OR ‘NL63’ OR ‘HKU1’] AND [‘smoking’ OR ‘tobacco’ OR ‘smoker*’ OR ‘risk factor’ OR ‘clinical features’ OR ‘clinical characteristics’]. Using these terms ensured the inclusion of any study related to coronaviruses (229E, NL63, OC43 and HKU1) and smokers. Searches were limited to articles published in English.

Flow chart depicting database searches, number of publications identified and screened, and full texts included in the present review and meta-analysis. Exclusion criteria are stated.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Figure 1 shows a flowchart diagram of the searches made in the two databases and the exclusion/inclusion strategy. Preprints were not included in the systematic review because searches included only those articles published in peer-reviewed journals. In the first phase (Fig. 1; screening phase), any duplicated works or those not written in English were excluded (Fig. 1). In the screening by title and abstract phase, a total of 257 articles were excluded after reading titles and abstracts. The primary reasons for exclusion at this stage were multiple. Studies that were irrelevant to the study topic, such as those focusing on unrelated diseases, non-human subjects, or lacking clinical characteristics pertinent to our research, were excluded. Additionally, articles that described diagnostic techniques, data focused exclusively on children, genetic studies, computational modeling, contagious mechanisms unrelated to coronaviruses, healthcare worker protection strategies, surveillance methodologies, genomic analyses, and studies on cutaneous or specific organ lesions were also excluded. Furthermore, comments, letters, or viewpoints without original research data were removed. In the next phase, the articles that did not include smoking data or were comments, letters or viewpoints, were excluded.

Finally, the articles that could not discriminate smokers among those infected by coronaviruses 229E, NL63, OC43 and HKU1., or those that provided data about former smokers and current smokers collectively, were removed.

Data extraction

Records were verified for duplicates with Zotero 6.0.16 (http://www.zotero.org). Two independent reviewers (JGR, AN) screened the literature search, they assessed all the studies to be included after reading their titles, abstracts and full texts. Any disagreement was settled in conference with LJD, a third author. Any relevant data were collected from all the eligible studies using a structured extraction sheet, which was prepared and approved by all the reviewers after screening the eligible studies and reaching a consensus.

Statistical analysis

Data analyses were carried out with the meta packages in the R Software (version 4.2.2 of R Software; packages dmetar, meta and metafor). Then a random-effects meta-analysis was applied for calculating the pooled estimated prevalence with 95% CI (95% confidence interval). Two sets of meta-analyses were performed. First, a meta-analysis with all the data from all the studies regardless ccCoV. Later single meta-analyses were done for each virus separately (229E, NL63, OC43 and HKU1). Table 1 indicates the viruses included in each meta-analysis. Fisher’s exact test or the Chi-square test was run to compare any differences between the observed and expected current smokers for every study both individually and by combining all data. All meta-analyses provided an Odds Ratio (OR) value.

Heterogeneity

Heterogeneity in the meta-analysis denotes variation in study outcomes among studies. Heterogeneity among studies was herein assessed by the Cochrane Chi-squared test (χ2), I-squared (I2; inconsistency) and Tau-squared (τ2). According to the I2 value, either random-effects (> 50%) or common-effects (< 50%) were selected.

Several options become available when heterogeneity is identified in a group of studies (Higgins, 2008). Some were contemplated in our meta-analyses: to verify if data were correct; to run a meta-analysis of random effects (depending on the I2 value, random-effects (> 50%) or common-effects (< 50%) were applied); to explore heterogeneity; to rule out studies.

L’Abbé plot is another tool employed to graphically examine heterogeneity28. It represents response rates to treatment vs. response rates in control groups, and their position in relation to the diagonal. Studies are normally plotted with an area that is proportional to its accuracy. Its dispersion suggests heterogeneity. Thus, a L’Abbé plot was obtained to check two important aspects of the meta-analyses herein conducted: meta-analysis’ general tendency and heterogeneity.

In our study, the L’Abbé plots were specifically used to visually assess the consistency of the observed effects across the different studies included in the meta-analysis. The position of each study relative to the diagonal line allows us to determine whether the treatment effect (in this case, the effect of smoking on ccCoV infection rates) is consistent. A study that falls on the diagonal line indicates that the effect is similar in both the treatment and control groups. Studies that fall above the line suggest a greater effect in the treatment group, whereas those below indicate a greater effect in the control group. The size of each plotted point is proportional to the study’s weight, providing a visual representation of each study’s contribution to the overall analysis. This method helps to identify outliers and assess the overall heterogeneity of the included studies.

Results

Literature retrieval

The systematic search gave us 441 articles in Web of Science and 205 in PubMed. The elimination of 23 duplicated articles and 22 for not being written in English left 396 articles (Fig. 1, screening phase). Then selections were performed by reading titles and abstracts (257 were excluded). Then, 139 full-text articles were still potentially eligible (Fig. 1, eligibility phase). Afterwards, publications were chosen according to the final selection criteria (detailed current smoker data; patients infected by coronaviruses: ‘229E’ OR ‘OC43’ OR ‘NL63’ OR ‘HKU1’, full research articles). Of the 139 works, 117 had no smoking data, or were letters, viewpoints and comments and therefore they were excluded. Of the remaining 22 works, 12 were removed because: it was not possible to discriminate smokers in infected patients; works were unable to differentiate current smokers among the ccCoV-infected patients.

The ten articles chosen in the systematic review were used for meta-analysis29–37. Further details appear in Table 1 to facilitate interpretation of analysed data, and in the flow chart (Fig. 1) to render this search repeatable in future works. Ten studies are described. For each study, number of infected male and female patients and smokers are described. Expected current smokers were estimated by considering the country’s smoking prevalence by gender and study year (when studies lasted several years, the mean of prevalences was taken). All patients included in the selected studies had been diagnosed with ccCoV (229E, NL63, OC43 and HKU1) by PCR testing. Three studies were carried out in the USA, two in China and one in Spain, South Korea, UK, Brazil and Canada. The values expected for smokers were calculated according to the proportion of men and women in each study, and to smoking prevalence in the study year. When all the ccCoV were studied, significant differences in the observed and expected values appeared in only two studies34,35 The resulting heterogeneity was I2 = 53% ( p = 0.02). A random model was selected that provided an OR of 1.37 and a 95% CI of 0.81–2.33.

Individual meta-analyses (OC43, 229E, NL63, HKU1)

Figure 2 provides results of the meta-analysis of the 10 studies included in the systematic review, but differentiating for coronavirus type. In this sense we found that for coronavirus OC43, the resulting heterogeneity was I2 = 0% (p = 0.50). Accordingly, a common model was selected obtaining an OR = 1.94 and a 95% CI = 0.83–4.55. As the resulting heterogeneity for HKU1 was I2 = 0% (p = 0.70), common model was also chosen, which provided an OR of 3.62 and 95% CI of 1.21–10.85. For NL63, heterogeneity was I2 = 0% (p = 0.47). Thus the common model was selected and OR was 1.93 and 95% CI was 0.64–5.82. Finally for 229E, heterogeneity was I2 = 40% (p = 0.19), and again the common model was chosen. OR = 0.97 and 95% CI = 0.50–1.90.

Enhanced analysis of findings

Our meta-analysis highlights the nuanced differences in smoking prevalence among patients infected with different ccCoV types. The significant ORs observed for HKU1, NL63, and OC43 indicate that smokers might have a heightened susceptibility to these viruses compared to the general population. This finding suggests potential variations in how different ccCoVs interact with the respiratory systems of smokers, which could be influenced by factors such as the extent of mucociliary clearance impairment and the local immune response modulation caused by smoking. The lower OR for 229E suggests that this particular ccCoV may have different pathogenic mechanisms or interactions with smoking-related factors, warranting further investigation.

Additionally, the observed heterogeneity in the meta-analysis underscores the variability in study designs, populations, and geographical locations. This heterogeneity indicates that while the overall trend points towards higher ccCoV infection rates among smokers, the exact magnitude and nature of this association can vary. Future studies should aim to standardize methodologies and consider regional smoking habits, healthcare access, and diagnostic criteria to provide more consistent and generalizable findings. Moreover, understanding the biological underpinnings of these associations, such as the role of nicotine in modulating immune responses or the potential protective effects of certain smoking-induced changes, could offer deeper insights into the smoker’s paradox observed in COVID-19 and other viral infections.

Significant differences were observed between the control groups (non-smokers or the general population) and the study groups (current smokers diagnosed with ccCoV). The study groups showed a higher prevalence of ccCoV infections, which was evident in the higher odds ratios observed for HKU1, NL63, and OC43 among smokers. Demographically, the study groups had a higher proportion of males, consistent with smoking trends. Additionally, smokers often presented with more chronic respiratory conditions, increasing their susceptibility to respiratory infections. These findings highlight the importance of understanding the interaction between smoking and ccCoV infections, which could inform public health strategies.

Discussion

To date, there is no accepted explanation for the low current prevalence of smokers in COVID-19 patients. This work attempts to shed some light on whether cross-immunity can be involved in the underrepresentation of smokers among COVID-19 patients. To do so, it evaluates if smokers are more susceptible to infection by the four ccCoV (229E, NL63, OC43 and HKU1), and whether this could mean greater protection to SARS-CoV-2. First, it is important to note the marked presence of antibodies of these four ccCoV in the vast majority of the population. Previous studies report that common coronaviruses represent 20% upper respiratory tract infections in adults38. Serverance et al.,39 quantified levels of antibodies in a group of 196 generally healthy individuals. The results were positive with the following percentages for each ccCoV: 229E: 91.3%; HKU1: 59.2%; NL63: 91.8%; OC43: 90.8 confirming a high ccCoV seroprevalence level in the general population. This finding agreed with the previous literature40–42.

The higher incidence of common cold coronaviruses (ccCoV) in smokers can be attributed to several factors related to the effects of smoking on the respiratory system and immune response. Smoking is known to impair the mucociliary clearance mechanism, which normally helps to remove pathogens from the respiratory tract. This impairment leads to increased susceptibility to respiratory infections, including ccCoV. Additionally, smoking has been shown to alter the immune response, resulting in a higher frequency of respiratory infections among smokers6. Smokers exhibit an inflammatory response characterized by increased production of pro-inflammatory cytokines, which may contribute to higher infection rates7. Furthermore, the higher antibody titers observed in smokers suggest repeated or recent exposure to these viruses, leading to a more robust immune response. These repeated infections with ccCoV could induce a cross-reactive T-cell response, enhancing immunity against SARS-CoV-2Another study has shown that smoking increases susceptibility to various respiratory infections by causing structural changes in the respiratory tract and reducing immune response effectiveness43. Thus, the increased incidence of ccCoV in smokers may be a significant factor contributing to the observed smoker’s paradox in COVID-19.

Our study found a higher prevalence of current smokers among patients with ccCoV than in the general population with an OR of 1.37 [0.81–2.33]. When we studied the four ccCoV separately, a higher prevalence of smokers also appeared in three of the four ccCoV than in the general population as follows: HKU1, OR: 3.62 [1.21–10.85]; NL63, OR: 1.93 [0.64–5.82]; OC43, OR: 1.94 [0.83–4.55].Prevalence was practically the same in 229E, OR: 0.97 [0.50–1.90]. In Gonzalez-Rubio et al.,11 (Fig. 3F), the red line runs along the bottom right-hand sector of the L’Abbé plot, which means that the intervention (tobacco) has a protector effect on COVID-19. In the present work (Fig. 3A-E), dotted red lines run along the upper left-hand sector of each L’Abbé plot, which implies that the general effect size is negative; that is, intervention (smoking) has a risk effect on ccCoV. These data are in agreement with what the previous literature describes. In the study by Serverance et al.,39, high seropositivity rates appeared for smokers. Furthermore, association of factors like smoking habit were particularly evident in individuals with higher antibody titres, which suggests that these risk factors are related to recent infections. In fact, when a high cut-off value was used for seropositivity, coronavirus OC43 was significantly associated with smoking habit. Thus, it might mean that smokers were clearly more susceptible to infections by ccCoV and had higher antibody titres. As previously mentioned, Serverance et al.44 compared 196 non-psychiatric controls39 to 106 patients in whom psychotic symptoms had recently appeared. The four ccCoV were more seroprevalent in psychiatric patients than in controls, with major differences between groups for HKU1. The psychiatric patients smoked more than individuals in the control group. It has been documented that people with mental disorders contract diseases related to smoking more than people with no medical history of neuropsychiatric disorders45.

However, recent research indicates a complex relationship between psychiatric conditions and COVID-19 incidence. For instance, a large retrospective cohort study in the USA found a higher risk of COVID-19 diagnosis among individuals with psychiatric disorders46. In contrast, a nationwide cohort study in South Korea did not find a significant correlation47. Several confounding factors could explain these discrepancies, including behavioral factors (e.g., lower adherence to social distancing guidelines), residual socioeconomic and lifestyle factors (e.g., smoking) not adequately captured in available data, and potential pro-inflammatory states or effects of psychotropic medications. These complexities suggest that studying the relationship between cross-immunity from other coronaviruses, COVID-19, and smoking in psychiatric patients is particularly challenging.

Furthermore, according to Kanwar et al.,33, smoking is described as a predominant comorbid condition for 85% of the adults who test positive for HKU1. This result is consistent with that obtained in a small Spanish cohort study, which indicates that adults with common coronavirus are mainly male smokers35.

Therefore, our results seem to fall in line with the previous literature, and despite studies being scarce, evidence suggests that smokers are more susceptible to infection by ccCoV and they can, thus, be more protected from SARS-CoV-2 thanks to greater cross-immunity, similarly to what happens with children.

Dowell et al.24 quantitatively studied serological (antibodies) and cell (T-cells) immunity responses convalescent to SARS-CoV-2 in a cohort study with 91 children and 154 adults with mild or asymptomatic disease in the UK in the second half of 2020. These authors provided evidence that children developed exceptional immune responses compared to adults, with pre-existing immunity that is cross-reactive with both ccCoV and SARS-CoV-2.

Dowell et al.24 postulated that these improved immune responses derived from being previously exposed to ccCoV, and demonstrated that infection by SARS-CoV-2 regulates them upwardly or feedbacks them. Levels of antibodies against the four ccCoV were higher in the seropositive children for SARS-CoV-2, with statistical significance for HKU-1 and OC4348. ccCoV HKU1 and OC43 particularly share wide sequence homology with SARS-CoV-2. These data indicate that infection by SARS-CoV-2 in children specifically and significantly reinforces pre-existing immunity to OC43 and HKU-1. We understand that this situation could similarly occur in smoking adults. Indeed, these two ccCoV (OC43 and HKU-1) clearly show a higher incidence in the smokers recruited for our study (OC43: OR = 1.93, 95% CI: 0.64–5.82; HKU1: OR = 3.62, 95% CI: 1.21–10.85), which could be one of the reasons for smokers with COVID-19 being underrepresented.

Finally, our work has several weaknesses. Firstly, finding existing studies that have analysed smokers in ccCoV was a difficult task. It was not possible to carry out a detailed study using current smokers’ age groups, although all patients were adults. Secondly, it is worth noting that many of the studies that report this phenomenon have grouped both current and former smokers in a common “smoking” classification. Grouping both current and former smokers can lead to possible confounding effects and produce ambiguity15. In our work, seven of the included studies mentioned current smokers, and only Cabeça et al., 2012 and Woo et al., 2020, mentioned a history of smoking, while Gorse et al., 2008, expressed data as tobacco smokers. Thirdly, we must also point out that the selected studies in our work do not inform about the duration (years) or frequency (number of cigarettes) for smoking habit. Fourthly, very few studies have resorted to target biomarkers to determine current smoking habit. Of the articles selected for our review, only Dove et al.31 biochemically established current smoking habit status by using serum cotinine. In fact, the source of the smoking background in most of the published studies is almost completely uncertain. Fifthly, it is also a limitation not knowing the mutations of the studied coronaviruses and how these mutations would affect the efficacy of vaccines and cross-immunity with previous infections49. Sixthly, while we primarily used Web of Science and PubMed for our search, which are standard for high-impact reviews, the inclusion of Google Scholar and Scopus might have provided additional relevant studies and insights. Lastly, using the general population as control is convenient and offers certain information, but the distribution of the population with COVID-19 is likely to be different for age, gender, comorbidities and smoking patterns15.

Future systematic reviews and meta-analyses should aim to include the latest research on the prevalence of smokers among those infected with common cold coronaviruses (ccCoV). These efforts will help to provide a more comprehensive understanding of the interaction between smoking and coronavirus infections, and to verify if the trends observed in our current analysis persist over time.

In any case, and despite the main indicated biases, there seems to be clear evidence for a crossimmunity reaction of T-cells between ccCoV (229E, NL63, OC43, HKU1) and SARS-CoV-2. A higher incidence of ccCoV in current smokers also clearly comes over because of their higher antibody titre, especially in coronaviruses OC43 and HKU-1. Therefore, our data allow us to postulate that smokers can be less ill by COVID-19 by, among other possible factors, having greater crossimmunity due, at least in part, to a larger number of ccCoV infections, or more recent ones, than the rest of the non-smoking population.
