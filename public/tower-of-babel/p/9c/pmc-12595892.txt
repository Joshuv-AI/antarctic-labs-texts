Neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging reveals microstructural damage in moyamoya disease: a study based on tract- and gray-based spatial statistics
Authors: Jia-Yan Shi, Shao-Peng Zhuang, Zi-Wei Cai, Shun-Fei Xu, Ying Tang, Hui-Wei Huang, Jian-Qi Li, Xian-Kun Tu, Hua-Jun Chen
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and purposeThis study aims to comprehensively assess microstructural abnormalities in both gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) in patients with moyamoya disease (MMD) using neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI). The analysis integrates GM–based and tract-based spatial statistics (GBSS and TBSS, respectively).MethodsDiffusion-weighted imaging was performed on 26 healthy controls and 15 patients with MMD. NODDI metrics—including the neurite density index (NDI), orientation dispersion index (ODI), and isotropic volume fraction (ISOVF)—as well as diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) parameters—fractional anisotropy and mean diffusivity (MD)—were estimated and compared using GBSS and TBSS approaches.ResultsThe analysis revealed significant microstructural alterations in both GM and WM among patients with MMD. In GM, reduced ODI was observed in multiple regions, including areas associated with the default mode network, executive control network, visual cortex, auditory cortex, sensorimotor cortex, and insula. In WM, decreased NDI and increased ISOVF were identified, predominantly in the corpus callosum, corona radiata, and bilateral frontal and parietal lobes. Although both DTI and NODDI metrics showed similar spatial distribution patterns of WM changes, the alterations detected by NODDI were more widespread. This suggests that NODDI may provide superior sensitivity for identifying microstructural changes associated with MMD.ConclusionThe integration of NODDI with GBSS and TBSS enhances the detection of cerebral microstructural alterations in MMD. These findings highlight the potential of NODDI-based metrics as valuable imaging biomarkers for improving diagnostic accuracy in MMD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12880-025-01994-6.Keywords: Moyamoya, Neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging, Global-based spatial statistics, Tract-based spatial statistics, Microstructure

Introduction

Moyamoya disease (MMD) is a chronic and progressive cerebrovascular disorder characterized by stenosis and occlusion of the circle of Willis, leading to the development of a dense network of collateral vessels [1]. These collateral vessels appear as a “puff of smoke” on angiographic imaging, placing patients with MMD at increased risk of both ischemic and hemorrhagic strokes [1]. Even in patients without overt brain lesions, cognitive impairments—including deficits in memory, attention, and executive functions—have been reported [2]. Such cognitive dysfunction is believed to arise, at least in part, from underlying cerebral structural damage [3]. However, these subtle structural alterations may be beyond the detection of conventional magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), underscoring the need for more sensitive imaging techniques.

Accumulating evidence from advanced diffusion MRI has demonstrated widespread gray matter (GM) and white matter (WM) abnormalities in MMD. Structural MRI studies have identified GM alterations in patients with MMD, including reduced volume, thickness, and density in cortical and subcortical regions such as the frontal lobe, thalamus, hippocampus, and cingulate cortex [3–5]. Additionally, a previous diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) study revealed decreased fractional anisotropy (FA) and increased radial diffusivity across major WM tracts, including the corpus callosum, posterior commissure, and superior longitudinal fasciculus, indicating compromised microstructural integrity and demyelination [3, 6]. Moreover, findings from diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI) have shown that patients with MMD exhibit decreased radial kurtosis—indicative of demyelination—and reduced axial kurtosis—reflecting axonal degeneration—in WM regions such as the corona radiata [7, 8]. These microstructural damages have been linked to impaired neurocognitive performance.

With advancements in diffusion MRI (dMRI), neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI) has gained prominence due to its multi-compartment modeling, which separates signals originating from intra-neurite water, extra-neurite water, and cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) [9]. This modeling framework allows for a more accurate interpretation of CSF-related interference compared to conventional DTI [9]. Unlike DTI and DKI, which indirectly characterize tissue microstructure through “signal representation” methods [10], NODDI provides a more direct and biophysically informed assessment and yields three key parameters: (1) the neurite density index (NDI); (2) the orientation dispersion index (ODI), which reflects the degree of coherence in neurite orientation; and (3) the isotropic volume fraction (ISOVF), representing the proportion of free water content [9]. Owing to these advantages, NODDI has been increasingly employed to investigate microstructural abnormalities in various neurological disorders, including Parkinson’s disease (PD) [11], mild cognitive impairment [12], and minimal hepatic encephalopathy (MHE) [13]. Notably, NODDI has substantially improved our understanding of the underlying mechanisms of these conditions [11–13].

Voxel-based analysis (VBA) is a well-established technique for analyzing structural brain images, relying on spatial smoothing to detect regional differences in tissue volume. However, VBA is particularly susceptible to partial volume effects (PVEs), especially in subcortical WM and sulcal regions [14]. In contrast, tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS) is a dMRI-based statistical approach that employs non-linear registration to accurately localize and quantify microstructural differences. TBSS reduces registration errors and biases and eliminates the need for arbitrary spatial smoothing by leveraging the anatomical consistency of major WM tracts [15]. TBSS has been instrumental in advancing research on various neuropsychological disorders—including Alzheimer’s disease (AD), autism spectrum disorder, and MHE—by revealing subtle microstructural abnormalities in WM bundles that may serve as sensitive imaging biomarkers [13, 15, 16]. Additionally, GM–based spatial statistics (GBSS), an extension of the TBSS framework, adapts this methodology to GM analysis. GBSS constructs a GM skeleton and projects diffusion metrics from the most representative local GM voxels onto this skeleton for group-level comparisons, thereby effectively reducing PVE-related errors from adjacent WM and CSF [17]. This technique has improved our understanding of GM involvement in neurological disorders such as AD and autism, facilitating the identification of GM abnormalities that may serve as more sensitive imaging biomarkers [14, 18].

The application of NODDI in MMD research remains limited. For instance, a previous study reported reduced NDI predominantly in the centrum semiovale and decreased ODI mainly in the cingulate cortex among moyamoya (MM) patients [19]. Notably, that study utilized VBA for analysis [19]. More recently, it has been shown that TBSS and GBSS can effectively leverage the NODDI model to provide unbiased and spatially accurate information on microstructural alterations in both WM and GM across various neuropsychological conditions, including AD and PD [11, 18, 20, 21]. In this context, the present study aimed to comprehensively investigate microstructural abnormalities in MMD by integrating NODDI with both TBSS and GBSS approaches—marking the first application of this combined framework in MMD research.

Materials and methods

Subjects

The study included 26 healthy controls (HC), and 15 patients diagnosed with MMD. The diagnosis of MMD was confirmed using cerebral digital subtraction angiography and magnetic resonance angiography. There were no significant differences between the two groups in terms of age, sex, or education level (Table 1). Exclusion criteria included (1) the presence of other neuropsychiatric disorders; (2) current use of psychotropic medications; (3) evident cortical or subcortical infarction or hemorrhage; (4) a history of revascularization surgery prior to participation; and (5) any contraindications to MRI.

MRI data acquisition

MRI data were acquired using a 3T scanner (Prisma; Siemens Medical Systems, Erlangen, Germany). dMRI was performed using a multi-shell acquisition protocol to enable the estimation of both DTI and NODDI parameters. A spin-echo echo-planar imaging sequence was employed with the following parameters: repetition time (TR) = 4200 ms; echo time (TE) = 72 ms; flip angle = 90°; number of slices = 72; field of view = 216 × 216 mm2; matrix size = 108 × 108; slice thickness = 2.0 mm with no gap; voxel size = 2 mm3 isotropic; b-values = 0, 1000, 2000, and 3000 s/mm2; and a slice acceleration factor of 2.

MRI data processing

The overall data processing pipeline consisted of three main components (Fig. 1): generation of diffusion-related metrics, creation of individual pseudo–T1-weighted images along with a study-specific pseudo–T1-weighted template, and analysis of microstructural alterations in both GM and WM.

Calculation of diffusion parameters

As shown in the left panel of Fig. 1, diffusion data were initially preprocessed using the FMRIB Software Library (FSL) to correct for eddy current–induced distortions and head motion artifacts [22; 23]. Following preprocessing, a diffusion tensor model was fitted to the corrected data using b-values of 0 and 1000 s/mm2, employing the weighted linear least-squares method implemented in the Diffusion Imaging in Python (DIPY) library (https://dipy.org/) [24; 25]. This model produced conventional DTI metrics, including FA and MD. In addition, a three-compartment tissue model based on NODDI was applied to the diffusion data [9]. This analysis was conducted using Accelerated Microstructure Imaging via Convex Optimization (AMICO) [26], yielding microstructural parameters such as the intracellular volume fraction of neurites (ICVF), ISOVF, and ODI. Within this framework, ISOVF reflects the fraction of CSF, while ICVF is interpreted as the NDI.

GBSS procedures

Microstructural changes in GM were evaluated using GBSS, following the pipeline available at https://github.com/arash-n/GBSS [27]. The processing workflow is illustrated in the middle panel of Fig. 1. A WM fraction map was generated by segmenting FA maps. The GM fraction map was then derived by subtracting the WM and CSF fraction maps from 1 (GM fraction = 1 - (WM fraction + CSF fraction). These three tissue fraction maps were combined, using their respective weights (pseudo-T1 = 0×CSF fraction + 1×GM fraction + 2×WM fraction), to construct pseudo–T1-weighted images. Subsequently, pseudo–T1-weighted images from all participants were used to create a study-specific pseudo–T1 template. Each subject’s pseudo–T1-weighted image was then registered to this template space using the Advanced Normalization Tools (http://stnava.github.io/ANTs) [28]. The same transformation parameters were applied to warp the GM fraction maps, along with the DTI and NODDI metrics, into the template space. All warped GM fraction maps were averaged and then skeletonized using the tbss_skeleton tool in FSL with GM fraction >0.65 in >75% of the subjects [29], as shown in purple in the top-right corner of Fig. 1. Finally, NODDI metrics, including NDI and ODI, were projected onto the GM skeleton.

TBSS procedure

WM alterations were assessed using TBSS within the study-specific template space, following a conventional statistical framework. As depicted in the bottom-right corner of Fig. 1, a mean FA image was generated by averaging all warped FA images. This mean FA image was then used to create a WM skeleton (shown in green), applying a threshold of 0.2 to define the core WM tracts. Subsequently, all warped DTI metrics (FA and mean diffusivity [MD]) and NODDI metrics (NDI, ODI, and ISOVF) were projected onto the mean FA skeleton for group-level comparison.

Statistical analysis

To investigate differences in cortical and WM microstructure between HC and patients with MMD, TBSS and GBSS analyses were applied to both DTI and NODDI metrics. Nonparametric permutation testing was performed using the Randomise tool in FSL, with 5000 iterations and threshold-free cluster enhancement. Significant clusters were identified based on a family-wise error (FWE)–corrected P value of less than 0.05. To reduce potential confounding effects, covariates including age, sex, and educational level were statistically controlled for in the analysis. For anatomical localization of significant findings, the John Hopkins University International Consortium of Brain Mapping DTI-81 White Matter Labels Atlas (JHU-ICBM-DTI-81 WM Labels Atlas), available in FSL (https://fsl.fmrib.ox.ac.uk/fsl/fslwiki/), and the Automated Anatomical Labeling version 3 (AAL-v3) atlas [30] were aligned to the study-specific template space.

Results

GBSS analysis revealed a significant reduction in ODI across widespread cortical regions in the MMD group compared to HC (FWE-corrected P < 0.05; Fig. 2). These reductions were predominantly observed in the frontal, parietal, occipital, temporal, and limbic lobes. Specifically, the decreased ODI was primarily localized to regions involved in higher-order cognitive processing, including the bilateral medial prefrontal gyrus, anterior and posterior cingulate cortex, precuneus, inferior parietal gyrus, middle and inferior frontal gyrus, superior parietal gyrus, and insula. Additionally, significant reductions were noted in the visual cortex (e.g., bilateral lingual gyrus, superior/middle/inferior occipital gyri, cuneus, and calcarine fissure and surrounding cortex), the auditory cortex (e.g., right superior and inferior temporal gyrus, and bilateral middle temporal gyrus), and the sensorimotor cortex (e.g., bilateral precentral and postcentral gyri, paracentral lobule, and supplementary motor area). No significant group differences were observed for NDI or ISOVF.

Compared to HC, patients with MMD demonstrated significantly reduced NDI across multiple WM regions, predominantly involving the corpus callosum, bilateral corona radiata, bilateral frontal and parietal lobes, as well as the left temporal and occipital lobes (FWE-corrected P < 0.05; Fig. 3). A significant reduction in FA was also observed in the MMD group, exhibiting a similar spatial distribution to that of NDI, albeit with a more limited extent (Fig. 4).

A significant increase in ISOVF was observed in the MMD group, involving widespread brain regions, primarily including the corpus callosum, bilateral corona radiata, bilateral frontal and parietal lobes, and the right temporal lobe (Fig. 5). Patients with MMD also exhibited a significant elevation in MD, with a largely overlapping spatial distribution (FWE-corrected P < 0.05; Fig. 6). Notably, ISOVF alterations were more prominent than those in MD, as evidenced by a broader extent of ISOVF changes detected under a more stringent statistical threshold (FWE-corrected P < 0.01; Supplementary Figs. 1 and 2).

Discussion

In this study, we employed GBSS and TBSS to assess GM and WM microstructural differences in patients with MMD. The key findings are as follows. (1) MM patients exhibited GM microstructural abnormalities, indicated by decreased ODI, in widespread regions, including areas associated with the default-mode network (DMN), executive control network (ECN), insula, visual cortex, auditory cortex, and sensorimotor cortex. (2) WM microstructural alterations, reflected by reduced NDI and increased ISOVF, were observed in several regions, primarily including the corpus callosum, corona radiata, and the frontal and parietal lobes. (3) Although alterations in DTI parameters (FA and MD) showed a similar spatial distribution, MM patients demonstrated more extensive changes in NODDI parameters, highlighting the greater sensitivity of NODDI in capturing microstructural alterations compared to DTI.

We observed reduced ODI in GM within the DMN, ECN, insula, and visual/auditory/sensorimotor cortex in MM patients, suggesting decreased dendritic complexity or arborization in these regions [31]. Consistent with our findings, previous studies have reported GM abnormalities in these regions, including macrostructural changes such as volume atrophy and cortical thinning in the ECN, insula, and visual/auditory/sensorimotor cortex, as well as microstructural alterations (evidenced by decreased ODI) in the DMN [3, 19, 32–34]. These structural abnormalities are likely responsible for various brain dysfunctions observed in MM patients [3, 19, 32–34]. Specifically, DMN-related regions are crucial for attention [35], the ECN supports executive function [36], and the insula serves as an integrative hub for sensory, emotional, and cognitive information [37]. Thus, cortical microstructural damage in these areas may contribute to cognitive impairments, such as attention and executive dysfunction, which are hallmark features in MM patients [33]. Additionally, the visual cortex plays a critical role in multisensory processing and influences behavioral outcomes [38], the auditory cortex is involved in speech sound decoding [39], and the sensorimotor cortex serves as the primary motor and sensory center [34]. Therefore, cortical microstructural impairment in these perceptual areas may be associated with perceptual dysfunctions observed in MM patients [33], however, such interpretations should be considered hypothesis-generating.

In WM, decreased NDI and increased ISOVF were observed in MM patients. The reduced NDI reflects a decline in axon density, while the elevated ISOVF suggests increased interstitial fluid within the WM [40]. These changes may result from adenosine 5’-triphosphate (ATP) depletion, a critical energy source for neuronal activity, caused by chronic ischemia-induced hypoxia and hypoglycemia in MMD [41]. The sustained ATP deficiency triggers excessive production of reactive oxygen species, which are harmful oxidative molecules that, at high concentrations, can be lethal to axons and contribute to ongoing oxidative stress, leading to progressive axonal damage [41]. Specifically, WM damage was observed primarily in the corpus callosum, corona radiata, and frontal and parietal lobes in MM patients. Consistent with our findings, previous diffusion MRI studies have reported decreased NDI and increased MD, which reflects myelin degeneration, in these WM regions in MM patients [7, 19]. It is well-established that the bilateral prefrontal and parietal lobes are connected via the corpus callosum [42], and both lobes are crucial for executive function [43]. Additionally, the corona radiata plays an essential role in attention [44]. Therefore, microstructural damage in these WM regions may provide a plausible structural basis for attention and executive dysfunctions in MM patients [33], but this remains tentative without direct cognitive assessment.

Although a similar spatial pattern was observed in the changes between FA and NDI, as well as between MD and ISOVF, MM patients exhibited more extensive alterations in NODDI measurements. This suggests that NODDI offers greater sensitivity than DTI in detecting WM microstructural impairments. The improved sensitivity of NODDI can be attributed to its advanced multi-compartment model, which allows for more precise quantification of neurite density and orientation [45]. Consistent with our findings, previous neuroimaging studies in ischemic and neurodegenerative disorders, such as stroke and AD/PD, have shown that NODDI outperforms DTI in detecting WM microstructural changes [46–48]. Therefore, it is not surprising that NODDI demonstrates superior diagnostic capability for WM abnormalities in MM patients.

Limitation

Several limitations should be acknowledged in this study. First, cognitive assessments (e.g., attention, memory, and executive functions) were not included, which means that potential functional implications of the observed microstructural alterations cannot be directly substantiated. Second, being cross-sectional, the present study could not determine whether the brain structural damage assessed by NODDI metrics was reversible. This will be addressed in future longitudinal studies, such as those following surgical revascularization. Third, the NODDI model did not explicitly account for compartment-specific T2 relaxation differences, which could influence the signal fraction of each compartment [49]. In future studies, NODDI modeling should incorporate advanced techniques, such as multi-time echo, to distinguish compartment-specific T2 relaxation and non-T2-weighted diffusion parameters [49]. Fourth, the modest sample size and sample heterogeneity may limit statistical performance. Larger cohorts and stratified analyses are planned in future studies. Finally, the preprocessing of diffusion data did not include specific correction for susceptibility-induced distortions, which might introduce minor geometric inaccuracies, despite the use of acquisition parameters (e.g., phase-encoding acceleration) designed to minimize them and rigorous visual quality checks.

Conclusion

Our findings provide a comprehensive insight into microstructural damage in both GM and WM in patients with MMD. The integration of NODDI with GBSS and TBSS offers enhanced sensitivity in detecting brain microstructural alterations compared to DTI, positioning these methods as potential ideal biomarkers to improve diagnostic performance in MMD.
