Stretchable multimodal deformation sensor with self-mode recognition by a single Hall sensor
Authors: Chong Zhang, Chengfeng Pan, Neng Xia, Haojin Yang, Lin Su, Xin Wang, Zhongyi Nie, Kedong Wu, Lingxiao Zhou, Mengdi Han, Carmel Majidi, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Stretchable sensor technologies capable of tracking multiple deformation modalities have attracted increasing attention due to their potential ability to improve the intelligence of soft robotics, stretchable electronics, and other soft-body systems. Existing multimodal deformation sensors typically require either complicated system integration and sensor layout or complex algorithmic techniques based on computational modeling and machine learning methods. Here, we report a stretchable multimodal deformation sensor that can measure and distinguish the magnitudes and modes of mechanical deformation (stretching, bending, twisting, and pressing) without reliance on complex computation or trained classification/regression models. This sensor relies on the layout of a stretchable magnetic film with a gradually varied magnetization profile and a 3-axis Hall sensor to decode the patterns of spatial change of magnetic flux strength under different deformation modes. We demonstrate the potential of this sensor in wearables and robotics by using it for neck motion monitoring, self-sensing electrical muscular stimulation, intelligent pneumatic gripper for objects classification, control of a stingray-inspired soft robot, and deformation recognition of an artificial elephant trunk.

Introduction

Sensors based on soft material systems possess intrinsically high mechanical compliance and have emerged as indispensable components for soft implantable or wearable electronics1–7, biomechanically compatible human-machine interfaces8–11, and soft robotic systems12–18. In biological systems, the natural somatosensory network employs diverse mechanoreceptors embedded in soft tissues to perceive and discriminate a wide range of mechanical stimuli with distinct deformation modalities, thereby enabling complex and adaptive interactions with the physical world19,20. Inspired by this capability, the long-standing objective of soft deformation sensors is to endow artificial systems with similar multi-dimensional sensing, i.e., detecting, differentiating, and decoding multiple modes of deformation to provide rich feedback for intelligent perception and actuation21.

A broad spectrum of soft sensors has been developed based on resistive22–25, piezoelectric11,26–28, capacitive29–31, inductive32–34, optical35–38, and magnetic transduction mechanisms39,40 (Fig. 1A). These devices have demonstrated the ability to monitor various deformation modes such as stretching, bending, twisting, and pressing. However, most existing soft sensors rely on monotonic changes in a single physical parameter (e.g., resistance, capacitance, inductance, light intensity) to represent multiple deformation modalities (Fig. 1B)23,35. This inherently limited degrees of freedom in the output signal lead to significant challenges in distinguishing deformation modes (Fig. 1B, and Supplementary Table S1). To overcome this limitation, two primary strategies have been pursued. The former integrates multiple sensing elements at predefined locations, allowing spatially distributed signals to be decoupled and interpreted for specific deformation modes36,41–44. The second leverages data-driven approaches, employing machine learning algorithms to classify or regress multi-modal deformations based on rich but high-dimensional datasets45–48. An emerging direction toward simplified yet multifunctional soft sensing involves designing material architectures and transduction mechanisms that intrinsically increase the degrees of freedom of the output signal. Instead of relying on multiple discrete sensors, recent studies have demonstrated single sensing nodes capable of multi-dimensional output responses49–53. Optical and magnetic transduction principles are particularly promising for this purpose, as they inherently encode multi-dimensional information: optical signals contain both intensity and chromaticity (color), while magnetic fields possess vectorial properties corresponding to three-dimensional flux components. For instance, Bai et al.49 realized elastomeric optical fibers with embedded chromatic patterns for multimodal deformation (stretching, bending, and pressing) sensing based on changes in both light intensity and color. Yan et al.50 developed a programed flexible magnetic film38 - Hall sensor system that distinguished normal and shear forces (pressing mode) through magnetic flux strength and direction. These demonstrations highlight the potential of multi-parameter sensing nodes but remain limited in their ability to comprehensively detect and distinguish all major soft-body deformation modalities, including stretching, bending, twisting, and pressing (Supplementary Table S1).

Here, we present a stretchable multimodal deformation sensor (SMDS) that enables simultaneous measurement and differentiation of all four fundamental deformation modalities: stretching, bending, twisting, and pressing (Fig. 1C–E). The SMDS integrates a large-area stretchable magnetic film, magnetized with a spatially varying flux vector profile (Fig. 1F, G), and a three-axis Hall sensor encapsulated within a soft elastomer matrix (Fig. 1C). This architecture yields both large deformation and tactile sensing capability, enabled by the multi-dimensional signal comprising magnetic flux components (Bx, By, Bz) that encode both the magnitude and direction of magnetic fields. Distinct deformation modalities induce combinations of magnetic flux changes along each axis, allowing direct and interpretable multimodal discrimination without the need for complex data processing. For comparison, conventional large-deformation sensors (Fig. 1A, and Supplementary Table S1) typically rely on a single physical variable as the output signal and exhibiting similar monotonic responses to different deformation modes (Fig. 1B left), making it difficult to distinguish among multiple deformation types at a given time. In contrast, the SMDS measures three independent variables, the magnetic flux intensities along the X-, Y-, and Z-axes (Fig. 1B right). These multi-dimensional signals display distinct response patterns corresponding to different deformation modalities, allowing straightforward recognition of deformation mode directly from the output signal. Furthermore, the SMDS achieves a combination of sensing and mechanical performance metrics including deformation modes (4), self-mode recognizability (yes), skin-like softness (Young’s modulus <300 kPa), flexibility (yes), and large stretchability (>300% strain) (Fig. 1D). We demonstrate the versatility of SMDS in diverse application scenarios encompassing biomedical engineering and soft robotics. Specifically, we employ the sensor for (1) monitoring human neck motions, (2) providing closed-loop feedback for electrical muscle stimulation, (3) recognizing fruits by a pneumatic gripper, (4) locomotion control of a stingray-inspired soft robot, and (5) detecting complex motions in a bionic elephant trunk. These demonstrations illustrate a framework of material-encoded multimodal perception, enabling intelligent, self-sensing soft systems with integrated mechanical awareness.

Results

Sensor design and working mechanism

Figure 1C illustrates the architecture of the SMDS, which comprises a stretchable magnetic film, a flexible printed circuit board (PCB) integrated with a three-axis Hall sensor, and a soft elastomer encapsulation bonding the components together. A representative magnetic film adopts a rectangular geometry (70 mm × 20 mm × 1 mm) and is fabricated from a neodymium-iron-boron (NdFeB) particle-silicone elastomer composite (see Methods section for details). We investigate the effect of particle size and ratio on magnetic flux intensity (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Figs. S1–3). The optimized magnetic film achieves a balance between magnetic flux density and mechanical compliance (Supplementary Fig. S4). It shows high stretchability >500%, low modulus <360 kPa, and low hysteresis (Supplementary Figs. S5, 6). The Hall sensor is mounted at the geometric center of the magnetic film to measure the magnetic flux density along the x, y, and z directions. To minimize mechanical mismatch between the rigid electronics and the compliant magnetic substrate, the flexible PCB is downsized to 5 mm × 5 mm × 2 mm (Supplementary Fig. S7). The Hall sensor is encapsulated using soft silicone adhesive, which mitigates stress concentrations during deformations (Supplementary Fig. S8) and yields great mechanical deformability (Fig. 1E).

A key step in fabricating the SMDS is the magnetization of the stretchable magnetic film to achieve a spatially varying magnetic flux vector profile, as shown in Fig. 1F (see magnetization setup in Supplementary Fig. S9). The magnetic flux vector at each point possesses both magnitude and direction, which are decomposed by the Hall sensor into three orthogonal components (Bx, By and Bz) (see the mapping setup in Supplementary Fig. S10). The measured distributions of Bx, By and Bz exhibit smooth spatial variations across the film (Fig. 1G), which endow the device with the capability to distinguish multiple deformation modes. Upon deformation, the magnetic flux vector (illustrated by a virtual magnetic needle in Fig. 2) undergoes mode-specific changes in strength and direction (Supplementary Movie 1). By monitoring variations in ΔBx, ΔBy and ΔBz, the magnitude and mode of the applied deformation can be accurately decoded (Supplementary Movie 2).Fig. 2Working mechanism of SMDS.Illustration of the working principle of multimodal self-mode recognition at different deformation modes of Stretching (A), Bending (B), Twisting (C), and Pressing (D). Changes of the magnetic field during stretching

As shown in Fig. 2A, uniaxial stretching alters both the strength and orientation of the magnetic flux vectors, driven by the reorientation of NdFeB particles embedded in the elastomer matrix under tensile strain. At the microscale, these particles behave as micromagnets whose magnetic moments align along the original magnetization direction in the relaxed state (Supplementary Fig. S11A). Under tension (Supplementary Fig. S11B), the microparticles rotate toward the x-axis, resulting in an increase in magnetic flux along x (ΔBx > 0) and a decrease along y (ΔBy < 0). The magnetic flux along z also increases (ΔBz > 0), consistent with previously reported micromagnet rotation models34.

Changes of the magnetic field during bending

The response of the SMDS to bending deformation is shown in Fig. 2B. Bending induces a spatial rearrangement of the NdFeB particles, thereby modifying the resultant magnetic flux at the Hall sensor position (Supplementary Fig. S12A). Specifically, flux vectors to the left of the sensing region (negative x-axis) rotate to reduce the local x-component, whereas those to the right (positive x-axis) enhance it. Due to the asymmetric initial flux distribution (Fig. 1F, G top), the overall x-component decreases under bending (ΔBx < 0). Along the z-axis, the opposing rotations of flux vectors on both sides of the sensing region reduce the resultant z-component (ΔBz < 0; Supplementary Fig. S12D). In contrast, the y-component increases (ΔBy > 0; Supplementary Fig. S12C), as bending-induced vector rotation enhances alignment along the y-direction near the sensor.

Changes of the magnetic field during twisting

The magnetic flux vector evolution under torsional deformation is presented in Fig. 2C. The mechanistic principle parallels that of bending, involving asymmetric rotation of flux vectors on opposite sides of the sensing region (Supplementary Fig. S13A). Twisting causes left-side (negative x) vectors to weaken and right-side (positive x) vectors to strengthen the local x-component, producing a net decrease in Bx (ΔBx < 0), albeit smaller than in bending. Corresponding spatial analyses along the y- and z-axes (Supplementary Fig. S13C, D) show ΔBy < 0 and ΔBz > 0, consistent with experimental results in Fig. 2C.

Changes of the magnetic field during pressing

When a compressive force is applied directly above the Hall sensor (Fig. 2D), all magnetic flux components show drastic decreases concurrently (ΔBx < 0, ΔBy < 0, ΔBz < 0), attributed to the local densification of the elastomer matrix and rotation of NdFeB micromagnets opposing the original magnetization direction, in agreement with the rotation model34.

Sensor characterization

During stretching, the x-axis flux component exhibits the largest signal variation. Accordingly, Mx = ΔBx/Bx0 was used to calibrate strain sensing. Figure 3C shows the tensile strain (ε) as a function of Mx, revealing a quadratic relationship (R² = 0.9972). For bending, twisting, and pressing, the z-axis component (Mz) showed the highest sensitivity. Figure 3D–F display the calibration curves of compressive force (F), bending angle (θb), and twisting angle (θt) versus Mz, all well fitted by second-order polynomial functions (R² = 0.9995, 0.9945, and 0.9968, respectively).We assume that the quadratic relationships originate from the non-linearity of the magnetic gradient, hyperelasticity of the elastomer matrix (stretching and pressing), and coordinate transformation (bending and twisting explained in Supplementary Note 2, 3). The sensitivity of the SMDS under different deformation modes is summarized in Supplementary Fig. S16, while response time data are presented in Supplementary Fig. S17. Deformation tests performed within 0.5 s demonstrate rapid response, with potential for faster operation at higher sampling rates50. The overall performance metrics are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. SMDS devices exhibited minimal hysteresis in all deformation modes, as evidenced by overlapping loading/unloading curves (Supplementary Fig. S18A–D). Long-term cyclic tests under stretching (0–100%), bending (0–120 °), and twisting (0-180 °) confirmed great repeatability over 3500 cycles (Supplementary Fig. S19A–C). Moreover, we also assessed the effects of magnetization direction. The stretchable films are magnetized to have different magnetic flux distribution by using the magnetization configurations shown in Fig. 3A. The differentiating pattern in Fig. 3B indicates same capability of differentiation. The calibration results in Fig. 3C–F suggest that we can tailor the sensitivity of SMDS for the practical application by changing the magnetization configuration.

Effects of thickness on the differentiation of deformations modes

To provide a general design guideline for tailored SMDS, we further study the effects of film thickness, geometry, and partial damage on multidimensional output patterns and their self-decouple capabilities. We investigated the influence of film thickness on SMDS performance (Fig. 4A and Supplementary Fig. S20). SMDSs with the thickness of 1 mm and 0.5 mm exbibit differentiation capability for different deformation modes (Fig. 4A). The SMDS with the thickness of 0.3 mm output same pattern for stretching and twisting modes. Effects of film geometry on the differentiation of deformations modes

We studied the effect of film geometry on SMDS performance (Supplementary Fig. S21). SMDSs with the shape of ellipse and triangle exbibit differentiation capability for different deformation modes (Fig. 4B–D). In particular, the SMDS with the shape of circle shows same pattern for stretching – twisting and bending – pressing modes due to the perfect symmetric shape (Fig. 4B). The differentiation capability of SMDS depends on the asymmetric distribution of magnetic flux on the magnetic film. Thus, the design of magnetic film should avoid perfect symmetric shape (i.e. circular shape).

Effects of partial damages on the differentiation of deformations modes

Last, we characterize the performance of SMDS with partial damage (Fig. 4E). In this experiment, we punched two circular holes on the magnetic film to test sensation ability of SMDS with is partially damaged (Supplementary Fig. S22B). Notably, SMDS still maintains the functionality of differentiation for multimodal deformations because the damaged magnetic film still maintains the asymmetric magnetic flux distribution (Supplementary Fig. S22A). Recalibration (Supplementary Fig. S22C–F) is required for the partially damaged SMDS, and it needs to be careful about the potential rupture during large deformation.

Biomedical applications

Human neck motions monitoring

To demonstrate the potential of the SMDS in wearable electronics and healthcare, we used it to monitor human neck motions. The sensor film with thickness of 1 mm is attached to the top pf splenius muscle by an adhesive medical polyurethane (PU) film (Fig. 5A), allowing better conformability to the skin. As shown in Fig. 5B, different neck motions generate distinguishable output patterns in all axes. When head turns left, pressing is dominant due to the large deformation and large pressure introduced by the PU film, which shows peak output in all three axes. When the head turns to right, bending accompanies twisting, which shows opposite output in Z axis. While nodding, bending is dominant, it shows less output in three axes, indicating small bending angles. Principal component analysis (PCA) further suggests that the distinguishable pattern of multidimensional output enables clear classifications of neck motions (Fig. 5C). This demonstrates benefits of multimodal sensing over single output sensors. Similarly, there are also other twist motions in human skin. In realistic scenarios, like opening bottle cups, and turning keys, surface of forearm twists during rotating hand. We attach SMDS to the surface of forearm (pronator) as shown in Supplementary Fig. S23A. Experiments in Fig. 23B show that SMDS can monitor slight deformations during these rotation behaviors. Closed-loop feedback for electrical muscle stimulation

To further highlight the potential of the SMDS in active human-machine interfacing, we developed a wearable self-sensing electrical muscle stimulation (EMS) patch that enables motion-synchronized muscle activation (Fig. 5D). The patch integrates sensing, actuation, and signal processing into a single stretchable platform composed of silicone elastomer, NdFeB particles, and liquid metal inclusions (Materials and Methods). As illustrated in Fig. 5D-inset, the system comprises three functional components: a magnetized SMDS mounted on the elbow for real-time joint monitoring, stretchable liquid metal leads providing electrical connectivity, and a pair of stimulating electrodes conformally attached around the biceps brachii. All conductive pathways were formed by mechanical sintering to achieve stable, low-resistance contacts. The addition of liquid metal shows negligible effect on the output pattern (Supplementary Fig. S25). The entire assembly was attached to the skin using a conformal adhesive film for reliable coupling during motion. The operation workflow of the EMS-assisted workout is shown in Fig. 5D-bottom. During exercise, the SMDS continuously measures the elbow bending angle (θbE) and transmits the data to the microcontroller, which drives the EMS pulse generator in open-loop control mode. As outlined in the logic diagram (Fig. 5F), EMS activation is triggered only when two conditions are satisfied: the bending angle θbE lies between 75° and 120°, and the elbow motion corresponds to flexion (lifting phase). A representative elbow bending profile for one full dumbbell lifting cycle is shown in Fig. 5G, accompanied by sequential snapshots (Fig.5E, from Supplementary Movie 3) that illustrate six characteristic motion states:(1) initial fully extended position, (2) EMS activation at the lower threshold during flexion, (3) deactivation at the upper threshold, (4) maximum flexion (end of lift), (5) extension phase without EMS activation, and (6) return to initial position. The activation of the EMS system is visualized by an LED indicator (red) synchronized with the stimulation pulse. The self-sensing EMS patch demonstrates motion-adaptive muscle stimulation, achieved through direct feedback from the integrated SMDS without external sensors or cameras. Together, these demonstrations underscore the versatility of the SMDS as a stretchable magnetic platform for deformation sensing and interactive control, capable of bridging human motion monitoring and adaptive actuation in next-generation wearable and rehabilitative electronics.

Locomotion control of a soft stingray-inspired swimmer

We further integrated the SMDSs with a soft stingray-inspired swimmer to monitor and control the deformation of pneumatic actuators. The soft swimmer, fabricated entirely from Ecoflex 30, consists of four pneumatically actuated fins (Fig. 6D). Compressed air was supplied through silicone tubing inserted into the tail of swimmer. Two SMDSs were attached to the dorsal fins and another two to the ventral fins for symmetric sensing. In the turning demonstration, one fin was held in a static bending configuration while the other was dynamically actuated to achieve directional locomotion. Maintaining the bent state of one fin required a pulse-width modulation (PWM) control of the solenoid valve regulating air flow to the corresponding actuator (Fig. 6D, bottom). Figure 6E shows the output signals from the SMDSs mounted on the static and dynamic fins. Under PWM regulation (Fig. 6F), the static fin gradually bent and held its deformed configuration (Fig. 6E, inset (2)), as reflected by an increase and subsequent stabilization of the SMDS output near a target threshold (Fig. 6E, top). The flapping fin, driven by alternating actuation of its two pneumatic chambers (Fig. 6E, insets (3) and (4)), was simultaneously monitored by the opposing SMDS (sensor ii, Fig. 6E, bottom). A total of sixteen flapping cycles were performed, during which the swimmer executed a controlled turn of 55° within 23 s (Fig. 6G and Supplementary Movie 5).

Detecting complex motions in a bionic elephant trunk

To demonstrate the capability of SMDS to differentiate complex deformations in soft robotics, we made a tendon driven artificial elephant trunk with five joints and four tendons (Methods), integrated the SMDS on the elephant trunk as shown in Fig. 6H. During stretching, Y axis shows opposite changes to X and Z axis. We can also tell the direction of twisting by the obvious patterns in multidimensional output (Fig. 6H). Similarly, we also perform remote control of a robot arm by tracking human arm motions (Supplementary Note 5 and Supplementary Fig. S26). These demonstrations collectively show the better performance of multimodal sensing over single output sensors in realistic scenarios. Simple fabrication and architecture allow easy integration of SMDS with various soft robots and enables interpretation of complex deformations in a straightforward way.

Discussion

Soft sensors capable of detecting multiple deformation modalities are essential for advancing soft robotics, wearable electronics, and human-machine interfaces. However, existing multimodal deformation sensors often rely on complex structural integration or intensive data processing based on computational modeling or machine learning. Here, we have developed a stretchable multimodal deformation sensor (SMDS) with a simple configuration, comprising a stretchable magnetic film integrated with a three-axis Hall sensor, that can quantitatively measure and distinguish stretching, bending, twisting, and compression without the need for complex computation. The SMDS achieves self-mode recognition through a spatially graded magnetization profile in the magnetic composite, which generates distinct magnetic flux signatures under different deformation modes. These signatures are directly decoded through the Hall sensor outputs along the x, y, and z axes, yielding interpretable multimodal sensing responses that follow clear quadratic relationships between magnetic strength and deformation parameters. We demonstrated the practicality of this material-sensor system in diverse applications, including posture monitoring during badminton motion, a self-sensing electrical muscle stimulation patch, bionic elephant trunk motion sensing, robotic arm teleoperation, and the closed-loop control of a soft stingray-inspired swimmer. These demonstrations highlight the potential of SMDSs for multifunctional integration across wearable and robotic platforms.

Future work may focus on developing theoretical models to further elucidate the sensing mechanisms, tailoring the magnetic film geometry for application-specific performance. Because of the limitations of experimental setups, future study on quantitively self-decoupling capabilities for combined deformation modes (Supplementary Figs. S27–29) would push the SMDS towards real-word applications, though PCA is sufficient to classify combined deformations. To enhance the general applicability, physical guided transfer study can be used to simplify the calibration process, which only requires several characterization points for each individual54. To further improve microstructural stability, encapsulating NdFeB particles with silicon dioxide forms covalent bond with surrounding silicone matrix. In addition, the sensor film is disposable and easy to fabricate, allowing general and straightforward applications in various scenarios. Overall, this work establishes a simple yet versatile material platform for multimodal deformation sensing. By combining structural minimalism with multidimensional sensing capability, the SMDS provides a pathway toward intelligent, self-sensing soft systems with mechanical awareness comparable to biological tissues, bridging the gap between soft materials, electronics, and machine intelligence.

Methods

Fabrication of stretchable magnetic film and sensor

The stretchable magnetic film is made from composite by uniformly dispersing neodymium (NdFeB) magnetic microparticles into silicone elastomer (Ecoflex 00-30, Smooth-on Inc.) with a weight ratio of 2:1. A planetary shear mixer (Thinky) was used to mix NdFeB and uncured elastomer at 2000 rpm for 60 seconds. A Thin film applicator was used to fabricate a thin film (1 mm) on a glass substrate. The film was then thermally cured in an oven under 80 °C for 60 min. The cured film was cut into desired shape and then was magnetized under a pulse magnetizer in a designed way (see the magnetization mode in Supplementary Fig. S9). The flexible PCB board with Hall sensor (MLX90393, Melexis) mounted was used to measure the magnetic flux in x, y, and z direction. The PCB board with hall sensor was bonded on surface of magnetic film and then encapsulated by using stretchable silicone glue.

Circuit design

The lightweight circuit, consisting of a low power consumption system on chip (SoC; DA14531, RENESAS) for data processing and wireless communication, a linear dropout regulator (LDO; NCP161BMX330TBG, onsemi) for power management, and a hall effect sensor (MLX90393SLW-ABA, Melexis). Moreover, the hall effect sensor connects with the processing circuit with serpentines, which increases the stretchability of the overall circuit without introducing additional components at the measurement point. The whole device is powered by a coin battery. Data is transmitter over a BLE app on the laptop and processed by a customized MATLAB script.

Sensor characterization

Mechanical property test

The stretchable magnetic film was cut into the dog-bone shape by a cutting die (ASTM D412) for tensile test. A universal material test machine (Instron 3400, Instron Inc) was used to conduct the tensile test at the strain rate of 20 mm/min.

Sensor calibration

A manual moving, and rotation platform was used to apply the mechanical deformation to the sensor including stretching, bending, twisting, and pressing. The ground truth of bending angle was extracted by the image process method from the photograph of SMDS under bending deformation. A portable forcemeter was used to measure the ground truth of compressive force. Arduino UNO was used to gain data from the Hall sensor through I2C communication protocol with sampling rate of 13.1 Hz. MATLAB 2022a was used to record and plot the data.

Demonstration setup

Self-sensing electrical muscular stimulation patch

The self-sensing electrical muscular stimulation patch was made from the composite of liquid metal, NdFeB microparticles and PDMS with the ratio of 4.1:2:1 by weight (Sylgard 184, Dow Corning), i.e. LM-SMDS. The patch was stencil printed through a paper mask which was cut by a CO2 laser cutter. The conductive paths and electrodes were mechanical sintering to be electrically conductive and two conductive fabric strips were used to build electrical connection to the pulse generator. Arduino UNO was used to collect data from SMDS, which was used to control the ON/OFF of pulse generator based on the bending angle of elbow. The pulse generator provided electrical pulse to the LM-EMS electrodes to stimulate the biceps of arm with the constant current of 7 mA, pulse width of 0.2 ms and pulse frequency of 20 Hz.

Robotic arm teleoperation

A commercialized six joints robotic arm (Dobot CR5, Shenzhen Yuejiang Technology Co., Ltd) was used in this demonstration. Three joints of robotic arm were enabled corresponding to the shoulder, elbow and wrist of human arm. Arduino Mega 2560 was used to collect data from SDMSs via I2C protocol. This data were sent to a computer in real-time through LABVIEW software. In where, the sensor data was decoded into operation commands to robotic arm. The command update frequency was set at 1 Hz due to the communication limitation between computer and robotic arm.

Control of stingray-inspired soft robot

The stingray-inspired soft robot was made of pristine Ecoflex-30. Four sets of pneumatic actuators were fabricated to form the fins. A membrane pump was used to provide air flow to the actuators. Solenoid valves were used to control the ON/OFF of air flow between the pump and actuators. Two SMDSs were glued at one side of fins and two other magnetic films were amounted at the opposite side to balance the deformation. Arduino Mega 2560 was used to collect data and execute PWM control.

Control of pneumatic gripper

The pneumatic gripper consists of three arms actuated by a single membrane pump. Uncured Ecoflex-30 was brushed on one side of SMDS and bonded to one gripper arm followed by a curing process under room temperature for two hours to ensure full integration. A manual switch regulates the air flow between the pump and actuators.

Fabrication and control of artificial elephant trunk

The Skeletons of the artificial elephant trunk consist of acrylic plates fabricated by laser cutting. Five skeletons are connected by four nitinol tendons (1 mm diameter) and a central hollow plastic tube (4 mm diameter) as the primary structural axis. Four springs are inserted between each two segments to provide restoration elasticity. An Ecoflex-30 film with a thickness of 1 mm are attached around acrylic skeletons as the skin of the elephant trunk. One SMDS is attached on the surface of the elephant trunk by Sil-epoxy. Various deformations are controlled by pulling different tendons. Stretching is achieved by pulling four tendons relative to the center skeleton. Bending is realized by pulling only one tendon while keeping others fixed. Twisting is performed by rotating the distal end acrylic plate.
