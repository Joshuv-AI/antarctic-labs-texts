A review of the iron requirements and its determination in commercial poultry
Authors: Natalie K. Morgan, Judith L. O'Keeffe, Mark J. Hackett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Iron (Fe) is an essential mineral for poultry, playing a critical role in oxygen transport and energy metabolism. Consequently, it is routinely supplemented into commercial poultry diets to safeguard against deficiencies that can negatively impact productive performance. Conversely, excessive dietary Fe can compromise performance by inducing oxidative stress, reducing absorption of other nutrients and negatively disrupting the gastrointestinal microbiota. The bioavailability, and thus absorption, of Fe is dictated by the form it is present in the feed, with haem, the organic form of Fe, being preferentially absorbed. Modern poultry diets, depending on location or context, may be devoid of animal proteins, and therefore without haem. Inorganic Fe sulphates have been the primary source of supplemental Fe in poultry diets, via premixes. These sources may provide Fe in the ferric state (Fe3+), which must first be converted to a ferrous state (Fe2+) for uptake, though it is more commonly supplied as ferrous sulphate monohydrate (Fe2+). The poor bioavailability of inorganic Fe has led to increased interest in alternative organic Fe sources, such Fe-amino acid chelates and Fe proteinates, with higher absorption efficiency at lower active inclusion levels. Iron absorption is also dictated by interactions with anti-nutrients, notably phytic acid and mineral antagonists. There is considerable variation in recommended supplemental Fe levels for poultry across published guidelines, feeding standards and scientific articles. For example, in broilers and broiler breeders recommended total Fe ranges from 20 to 110 mg/kg and in laying hens from 30 to 80 mg/kg. Advances in feed formulation, including use of organic sources of Fe, reduced concentration of antagonistic trace minerals and phytase supplementation, has enhanced bioavailability of Fe, indicating that commercial poultry may now be over-supplemented with Fe. Updated and context-specific Fe recommendations are essential for optimising poultry productivity. This review examines how to assess Fe utilisation in poultry, including methods for determining Fe status, proposes Fe recommendations for broilers, broiler breeders and laying hens based on outputs from research trials, and explores how Fe interacts with other nutrients and the microbiota.

Introduction

Iron (Fe) plays an integral role in numerous metabolic processes, such as oxygen transport, deoxyribonucleic acid synthesis, and electron transport (Shinde et al., 2011; Taschetto et al., 2017). It is also a component of haem-containing enzymes, such as catalase and glutathione peroxidase, and acts as a cofactor for numerous enzymes, including aconitase, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH) dehydrogenase and succinate dehydrogenase (McDermid and Loennerdal, 2012). Despite its abundance in diets, Fe utilisation is often limited in poultry, due to its poor bioavailability. Globally, there has been a rise in use of plant-based diets in the poultry industry (Vlaicu et al., 2024). The Fe in plant-based diets exists in the non-haem form, which is less efficiently absorbed compared to the haem form (Feijo et al., 2023), and is inhibited by anti-nutritional compounds such as phytic acid (Cowieson et al., 2006). Furthermore, the Fe in limestone and dicalcium phosphate is mainly in the ferric iron (Fe3+) state, which is less bioavailable compared to the ferrous iron (Fe2+) and haem state (Park et al., 2004). This is because, at intestinal pH, Fe3+ readily hydrolyses and precipitates as insoluble ferric hydroxides, reducing the amount of Fe remaining in solution for absorption (Piskin et al., 2022). Furthermore, Fe3+ has a comparatively higher affinity for dietary ligands such as phytates and polyphenols, resulting in the formation of stable complexes that render it chemically unavailable (Milman, 2020). Consequently, to ensure adequate Fe status, poultry diets are commonly supplemented with Fe, usually as inorganic sources such as ferrous sulphate (e.g., FeSO4·H2O), or increasingly organic chelated forms of Fe, including Fe-amino acid chelates (Fe-AA) (Bess et al., 2012) and Fe-proteinate (Fe-Pro) (Bai et al., 2021a). Inorganic Fe absorption requires Fe3+ solubilisation and reduction to Fe2+ and is constrained by pH and dietary inhibitors (Ems et al., 2017), while organic Fe forms are more stable, less reactive with antagonists and more readily absorbed (Byrne and Murphy, 2022). However, there is a lack of consensus about the optimal Fe concentration in commercial poultry diets, with substantial variation in recommendations observed between different sources (Table 1). Alongside ensuring the birds are receiving their minimum Fe requirements, there are environmental concerns regarding excessive usage of trace minerals, which has led to regulations that now limit the total Fe in animal feed; for example, the European Union has set an upper limit of 450 mg/kg Fe (Additives and Feed, 2016). Iron utilisation in poultry

Iron form

Iron occurs in three forms: Fe2+, Fe3+ and haem iron (Fe2+ chelated to haem). Haem Fe (organic form of Fe) is preferentially absorbed. Haem Fe is transported into enterocytes by haem transporter protein 1 (HCP1) (Conrad and Umbreit, 2002). Animal by-products are rich in haem Fe; the Fe from these sources is highly bioavailable, as it is bound into haemoglobin, myoglobin, ferritin, cytochromes, and Fe-containing enzymes (Tako and Glahn, 2011; Theil, 2004). However, modern poultry diets, depending on location and context, are sometimes formulated without animal proteins, and thus lack haem Fe, resulting in a reduced rate of Fe absorption.

The primary sites for Fe absorption in poultry are the duodenum and proximal jejunum, attributed to the specific morphological characteristics of the villi and high density of microvilli in these sections of the gastrointestinal tract (Sáiz et al., 1993). At physiological pH, non-haem Fe (inorganic form of Fe) is in the form of Fe3+ and must be converted to a Fe2+ to be absorbed. Non-haem Fe is transported into epithelial cells in the small intestine via a protein called divalent metal transporter 1 (DMT1), and then transported intracellularly through transferrin receptor (TFRC)-mediated endocytosis (Li et al., 2025). This is possible only after the Fe3+ has been reduced into Fe2+ by duodenal cytochrome b (Dcytb), a ferric reductase enzyme present at the luminal side of duodenal enterocytes (Frazer and Anderson, 2001; Zhang et al., 2017). Gastrointestinal pH dictates the oxidation state of Fe at the intestinal brush border, because the oxidative state of Fe is determined by the availability of oxygen. Soluble Fe2+ is expected to be dominant in the digesta exiting the gizzard, given it is highly acidic. The rapid transition to neutrality, induced by buffering in the duodenal loop, likely maintains the Fe3+ state (McKie et al., 2001). After it is absorbed into the enterocytes, Fe can then bind to ferroportin, a transmembrane transporter present in the membrane of avian intestinal epithelial cells (encoded by the ferroportin 1 [FPN1] gene), and is then transferred into the bloodstream (Tako and Glahn, 2011; Zhang et al., 2017). The rate at which Fe is utilised from the feed is dictated by how much Fe is stored in the body; Fe absorption is reduced if the system is saturated, even if the Fe provided is in the favourable forms of haem or Fe2+.

Traditionally, inorganic Fe sulphates have been the primary source of supplemental Fe in poultry diets. These are available as FeSO4·H2O and FeSO4·7H2O, with the monohydrate form being most used due to its comparatively higher solubility (Additives and Feed, 2016). However, given issues such as low bioavailability, high hygroscopicity and high excretion (Ma et al., 2016), there has been increased attention towards use of organic Fe sources (Sun et al., 2022a). Of particular interest are Fe-AA and Fe-Pro. Fe-amino acid chelates and Fe-Pro result from coordinate covalent bonds forming between amino acids or small peptides and an Fe ion from a soluble Fe salt, resulting in products with a mole ratio of 1 mol of Fe to 1 to 3 (preferably 2) moles of amino acids (Ashmead, 2001; Bai et al., 2021b). As demonstrated by Zhang et al. (2017), the bioavailability of these organic Fe sources is dictated by the chelation strength between Fe and their ligands, with a greater quotient of formation (Qr) resulting in increased intestinal Fe absorption. Examples of studies proving the benefits of Fe-AA and Fe-Pro over FeSO4 in broilers include Arnaudova-Matey et al. (2013) observing birds fed Fe bound to methionine (Fe-Met) were heavier at d 35 compared to those fed the same level of FeSO4, Bai et al. (2021a) detecting increased plasma Fe at d 21 in broilers fed Fe-Pro compared to FeSO4, and Shinde et al. (2011) finding higher Fe excretion at d 34 to 35 in birds fed FeSO4 compared to Fe-Met. In broiler breeders, Ebbing et al. (2019) observed Fe-AA resulted in more eggs per hen housed and higher fertility, and offspring with better growth and carcass characteristics compared to FeSO4. Also, in laying hens, Cao et al. (2022) found that egg production, egg weight and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were improved in birds fed Fe complexed with lysine and glutamic acid compared to FeSO4, and Sarlak et al. (2021) observed less abnormal eggs and higher eggshell calcium when feeding Fe complexed to glycine compared to FeSO4.

The advancement of nanotechnology has resulted in increased interest in Fe nanoparticles (size 100 nm or less) as a tool to enhance Fe absorption and bioavailability in poultry (Abd El-Ghany et al., 2021; Rehman et al., 2020; Saki et al., 2014). For example, in broilers, Basharat et al. (2023) observed that feeding 60 mg/kg Fe oxide nanoparticles (IONP) resulted in lower FCR compared to FeSO4, and Almeldin et al. (2024) observed 40 mg/kg IONP improved bird performance and reduced excreta ammonia. However, nanoparticles carry potential risks, as their high surface reactivity can induce oxidative stress through excessive reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation (Wang et al., 2024). Their small size may also permit tissue accumulation, exacerbating oxidative damage and inflammation over time (Michalak et al., 2022). Further research is warranted in IONP as a source of Fe, particularly in broiler breeders and laying hens.

Assessing Fe utilisation in poultry

Blood analysis

Traditional parameters used to assess Fe status in poultry are determination of ferritin, blood serum Fe, haematocrit (Ht) and haemoglobin (Hb) concentrations. Ferritin is an intracellular Fe reservoir protein that maintains Fe in a readily mobilised, and thus accessible, form (Orino and Watanabe, 2008). It contains an Fe core surrounded by an apoferritin subunit, which protects and stabilises the core, preventing excessive Fe release (Harrison and Arosio, 1996; Orino and Watanabe, 2008). Ferritin level is directly dictated by dietary Fe (Abdel-Rahman et al., 2022), making serum ferritin analysis the primary technique used to clinically evaluate Fe status. Bai et al. (2021b) and Feijo et al. (2023) validated that human serum enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (ELISA) can be used to detect chicken serum ferritin. Measured ferritin concentrations are species- and assay-dependent, and no validated reference range has yet been established for poultry ferritin. In terms of recommended levels, Feijo et al. (2024) indicated that 146 ng/mL is an adequate ferritin blood content to optimise broiler chicken growth. The optimum level for laying hens and broiler breeders has yet to be explicitly defined.

Haematocrit and Hb serve as indicators of how the hematopoietic system is functioning, which is dictated by Fe status; Ht reflects the volume of red blood cells (RBC) in blood, whereas Hb presents the oxygen-carrying capacity and Fe availability of blood (Billett, 1990). The suggested normal values of haematological indices for chickens are in the range of 2.6 to 3.5 × 106 cells/μL for RBC count, 7 to 13 g/dL for Hb and 22% to 35% for Ht, respectively (Bounous et al., 2000). Using Hb as a diagnostic marker of Fe status in chickens was established over 90 years ago; Elvehjem and Hart (1929) found that feeding Fe-deficient diets to White Leghorn chicks resulted in reduced Hb and anaemia, and proved that feeding ferrous sulphate or purified ferric chloride, but not ferric oxide, stimulated Hb synthesis. Moreover, Harmon (1936) identified relationships between Hb concentration and status of the bird, including proving Hb level reduced during egg production, was higher in high producing compared to low producing hens, and was highest at hatch and lowest at 2 weeks of age. Additionally, Waddell and Sell (1964) stated measurement of weight gain was a poor criterion for assessing Fe status compared to Hb concentration. Evidence of modern research using analysis of Hb concentration to assess Fe status includes Feijo et al. (2023), who observed dietary Fe level influenced Ht and Hb level of bird at age 7 to 21 d but not thereafter, and Aoyagi and Baker (1995), who found supplementation with 50 mg/kg Fe had no effect on Hb at d 21. Of note, ferritin reflects Fe stores, whereas Hb and Ht represent functional iron status. As a result, ferritin does not consistently correlate with Hb or Ht, limiting its utility for directly linking biochemical and haematological indicators of Fe status.

Medway and Kare (1959) established Ht as an indicator of blood and plasma volume, and then Hill and Matrone (1961) demonstrated that inadequate dietary Fe results in a decline in both Ht and Hb. Subsequently, Morck and Austic (1981) presented that Ht directly correlates with supplemented Fe, by feeding laying hens semi-purified diets supplemented with different levels of Fe (from 10 to 50 mg/kg) (Morck and Austic, 1981). In broiler breeder hens, it was found that both Ht and Hb increase when birds are fed Fe-supplemented diets after a period of Fe deficiency (Ebbing et al., 2019; Taschetto et al., 2017). However, Lin et al. (2020) found dietary Fe supplementation of 50 to 150 mg/kg had no impact on Ht level at any bird age. This indicates that both Hb and Ht should be analysed for hematologic profiling.

Tissue analysis

The liver is the main storage site for excess Fe, where it is stored in the form of ferritin (soluble, short-term Fe storage) and hemosiderin (insoluble, long-term Fe storage) (Sun et al., 2022a). Hemosiderin is an insoluble, aggregated product formed from ferritin degradation. While iron stored in ferritin is readily mobilised to meet metabolic demands, Fe sequestered in hemosiderin is released slowly and inefficiently, typically only during severe or prolonged Fe deficiency, and therefore represents a less bioavailable Fe reserve (Kasztura et al., 2022). The liver is also the primary site for production of proteins that maintain systemic Fe balance (Frazer and Anderson, 2001). Consequently, the liver is believed to be the most accurate tissue for assessing Fe status (Cao et al., 1996). The liver maintains Fe homeostasis, orchestrating systemic Fe balance by producing and secreting the peptide hormone hepcidin, which binds ferroportin (Liu et al., 2021; Nemeth and Ganz, 2021). During Fe deficiency, Fe is mobilised from hepatocytes to circulation, induced by hepcidin decreasing, allowing for Fe delivery to plasma via ferroportin, and resulting enhanced Fe absorption (D'Andrea et al., 2023). When the bird is overloaded with Fe, hepcidin is upregulated, resulting in degradation of ferroportin and decreased cellular Fe export (Ramey et al., 2009), and excess intracellular Fe is stored in hepatic ferritins (Tan et al., 2021). This signifies that measuring liver Fe concentration and analysing hepatocyte ferroportin and hepcidin expression, through measuring the proteins or gene expression by qPCR, provides an accurate indication of the Fe reserves in poultry. Divalent metal transporter 1 is also present in the liver, but is dispensable for hepatic Fe accumulation and Fe uptake (Wang and Knutson, 2013), suggesting there is little value in measuring it.

The spleen plays a critical role in Fe metabolism by acting as a blood filter. Splenic reticuloendothelial macrophages recycle Fe from old or damaged RBC through erythrophagocytosis, providing Fe for the synthesis of new RBC (Tan et al., 2021). Splenic Fe content can serve as an indicator of hepatic Fe status (Calvo et al., 1982; Lu et al., 2022). Strategies to assess bird Fe status in spleen samples include determining Fe deposits via histological analysis (Cork, 2000), quantifying total Fe or ferritin protein concentrations (Oshtrakh et al., 2006; Xue et al., 2017) as an indicator of Fe storage, or measuring gene expression of ferritin, FPN1, DMT1 and/or hepcidin (Tan et al., 2021) as an indicator of Fe import and export. Moreover, assessing these proteins and genes, along with TFRC, in the duodenum, provides valuable insight into Fe absorption across the intestinal epithelium, indicating if the intestine is retaining or releasing Fe (Bai et al., 2021a; Zoller et al., 2002). When Fe is deficient and hepcidin is low, expression of DMT1 and TFRC increases, importing Fe2+ into enterocytes. If Fe is overloaded and hepcidin is high, FPN1 expression is decreased, exporting Fe2+ into the bloodstream. An example of the value of this analysis is highlighted by Zhang et al. (2017), who investigated the effect of Fe source on Fe absorption and gene expression of DMT1 and FPN1 in ligated duodenal loops from broilers, identifying heightened Fe absorption when feeding Fe with a stronger chelation strength. The kidneys also play a crucial role in maintaining Fe homeostasis, including Fe reabsorption, expressing Fe-regulatory proteins and storage of Fe in ferritin and hemosiderin (Lu et al. (2022), and thus could also be used as a tissue for assessing Fe status. However, Cao et al. (1996) declared kidneys to be unsuccessful for Fe bioavailability comparisons.

Eggshell colour is dependent on the deposition of protoporphyrin-IX, which is a precursor of shell pigment that contains Fe (Samiullah et al., 2015). Protoporphyrin-IX deposition is partially Fe-dependent, increasing when Fe availability for haem synthesis is limited and when ferrochelatase activity is impaired (Sachar et al., 2016). Subsequently, there is a positive relationship between dietary Fe and eggshell colour; for example, Park et al. (2004) observed darker shells in eggs from laying hens fed diets supplemented with Fe-AA. This is important because darker eggshells is associated with improved shell strength (Gosler et al., 2005). Moreover, Ebbing et al. (2019) observed that when broiler breeders were fed diets fortified with Fe-AA the resultant increase in egg shell colour was correlated to significantly increased total hatch rate and progeny d 34 body weight and breast weight. This suggests that a detailed analysis of eggshell colour could serve as a useful and non-invasive method for evaluating Fe utilisation, although the effect of production stress on protoporphyrin-IX should be considered.

Methods for measuring Fe concentration in poultry diets and samples

The accurate measurement of Fe in biological systems is an important analytical capability. The are several analytical approaches to quantify total levels of Fe in biological samples, which can broadly be grouped into indirect colorimetric assays or direct elemental quantification. Analytical methods that can differentiate between different chemical forms of Fe (e.g., haem vs. non-haem Fe) and different Fe oxidation states (e.g., Fe2+ or Fe3+), collectively referred to as Fe speciation, are much sought after (Crichton and Boelaert, 2001).

Indirect colorimetric assays of total Fe

Indirect biochemical assays for Fe typically utilise the complexation of Fe ions with a specific chemical species (i.e., a ligand) to yield a colour product (i.e., a coloured Fe coordination complex); the intensity of the coloured product is proportional to Fe concentration of the sample, which is measured with a spectrophotometer, and quantified via comparison with calibration standards. Generally, each ligand is specific to only one oxidation state of Fe, or the ligand has substantially higher sensitivity to one oxidation state. Common examples of ligands that are used in colorimetric Fe assays include ferrozine and thiocyanate, which display preferential specificity to either Fe2+ (ferrozine) (Riemer et al., 2004; Tamarit et al., 2006) or Fe3+ (thiocyanate) (Riganakos and Veltsistas, 2003; Woods and Mellon, 1941). The ferrozine and thiocyanate assays inherently only detect soluble Fe, and thus protocols must incorporate appropriate cell lysis and acid digestion steps to ensure Fe is released from endogenous ligands into solution. To detect the total Fe pool, an appropriate reduction step must be employed to reduce Fe3+ to Fe2+ for the ferrozine assay, and likewise an oxidation step to oxidise Fe2+ to Fe3+ is required for the thiocyanate assay. Differential analysis (i.e., analysis of two samples, one before and one after either an oxidation or reduction step) can be applied in either the ferrozine (reduction step) or thiocyanate assay (oxidation step) to reveal the proportion of Fe2+ and Fe3+.

Colorimetric assays are advantageous as they are simple and do not require complex or expensive instrumentation for analysis. They do have limitations however, as they are unable to reveal the endogenous chemical form of Fe, as this information is lost during the cell lysis and acid digestion steps. In addition, the assays work on the assumption that 100% of the sample Fe is solubilised, and 100% of the Fe coordinates with the ferrozine or thiocyanate ligand. In reality, these assumptions may not be met, which can lead to underestimation of Fe content (Anastácio et al., 2008). Further, matrix effects and matrix difference between samples and calibration standards are an inherent limitation of any indirect colorimetric assay, which can result in under and over estimation of the Fe content.

Indirect colorimetric assays of haem and non-haem Fe

There is a suite of colorimetric assays that distinguish between non-haem and haem-Fe. Non-haem Fe can be detected by its reaction with bathophenanthroline, which forms a coloured complex that absorbs at 535 nm (Tangerås et al., 1980). Iron that is already coordinated within haem is bound too tightly to react with bathophenanthroline and is thus not detected. The reaction is specific for Fe2+, and therefore non-haem Fe3+ must first be reduced to Fe2+ for detection.

The haem-Fe assay (haemochromogen assay) takes advantage of the colorimetric reaction between haem-Fe and pyridine under alkaline and reducing conditions. Under alkaline and reducing conditions haem-Fe is reduced to Fe2+, and pyridine then coordinates to the Fe within the haem centre, producing a colour product that strongly absorbs at 557 nm (Barr and Guo, 2015; Hopp et al., 2020).

Direct spectroscopic and spectrometric quantification of total Fe

To overcome the limitations of colorimetric assays highlighted above, direct spectroscopic or spectrometric techniques can be used to quantify Fe. Such techniques include: atomic absorption spectrometry (AAS), micro plasma or inductively coupled plasma atomic emission spectrometry (MP-AES, ICP-AES) (Rahil-Khazen et al., 2002), inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (Barany et al., 1997), and X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF) (Geraki et al., 2002). Typically (except for XRF), samples are first acid digested to release the Fe into solution, and then the Fe is converted to single atoms or ions in the gas phase for AAS, MP-AES, ICP-AES, and ICP-MS analyses (ICP-MS analysis requires conversion of analyte to ions). These methods of direct spectroscopic and spectrometric Fe quantification typically offer superior detection limits over colorimetric assays and have long been considered the “gold standard” for elemental analysis. X-ray fluorescence spectrometry (XRF) is unique among the list of direct analytical methods for elemental quantification as it does not require acid digestion of the sample, as is capable of direct in situ elemental analysis.

Spectroscopic analysis of Fe speciation using X-ray absorption near edge structure (XANES) spectroscopy

Despite the wide-spread use of the aforementioned methods, none provide a detailed chemical picture of Fe speciation in biological samples. To this extent, XANES spectroscopy has emerged as a powerful tool for direct in situ characterisation of Fe speciation (and other metals) (Hackett et al., 2021; Pushie et al., 2014). The inherent advantages of XANES spectroscopy arise from the underlying photophysical properties of the technique–specifically, the absorption X-rays that promote (excite) a core electron from an Fe atom to an unoccupied molecular orbital (Penner-Hahn, 2005). Due to the proximity of core electrons to the nucleus of an atom, and the fact that the nuclear charge is unique to every individual element, the energy level of a core electron is highly specific to each element. While large differences in nuclear charge are observed between different elements of the periodic table, a smaller but similar effect is observed between different oxidation states of the same element (i.e., Fe3+ has a greater effective nuclear charge than Fe2+). Further, the energy of the unoccupied molecular orbitals is directly influenced by the nature through which the Fe has bonded to other molecules (ligands). For example, the energy of an unoccupied molecular orbital will be different depending on whether Fe is bonded to 4 or 6 ligands (i.e., a tetrahedral vs. octahedral coordination geometry). Similarly, the energy of the unoccupied molecular orbital is influenced by the specific atoms to which Fe forms bonds (i.e., a different orbital energy will be observed if Fe bonds to 4 sulphur or 4 oxygen atoms). Consequently, by measuring the specific energy required to excite a core electron to an unoccupied molecular orbital in an Fe atom, a substantial level of information is revealed regarding the oxidation state, number of bonds around the Fe atom (coordination number/geometry) and the specific atoms Fe is bound to (coordination environment). Lastly, due to the highly penetrating nature of X-rays, minimal or no sample preparation is needed, enabling XANES spectroscopy to make direct in situ measurement of Fe speciation across a range of biological samples.

Despite being well suited to study Fe speciation in situ, XANES spectroscopy is not without its limitations. Firstly, the measurement of a XANES spectrum requires a powerful and tuneable X-ray source, which at present, is only available at specialised particle accelerator facilities (synchrotron light sources) (Pushie et al., 2014). Due to the high demand to use synchrotron facilities across many scientific fields, access can be a prohibitive factor. Another limitation of XANES spectroscopy is that its inherent detection limits are not as good as bulk elemental techniques such as ICP-AES and ICP-MS. Lastly, the intense X-ray beam used in XANES spectroscopy can, in some circumstances, alter the endogenous metal speciation (a process called photodamage) (George et al., 2012). Consequently, it is recommended good practice to undertake a series of controlled experiments to assess the extent of photodamage occurring during XANES experiments, and measurements on dehydrated samples or at cryogenic temperatures are often preferred.

Ultimately, the choice of analytical measurement should be guided by careful consideration of the scientific aims, resources and time available. While XANES spectroscopy is one of the few methods capable of analysing Fe speciation in situ, the need to undertake such experiments at a synchrotron source can be prohibitive. If detecting differences in Fe concentration at ultra-trace levels is required (i.e., ppb or less), then ICP-AES or ICP-MS assays are likely to be the most suitable. For convenience and compatibility with high throughput, simple and accessible analysis, colorimetric methods are likely to provide the most suitable option.

Iron requirements in poultry diets

Recommended Fe requirements

Table 1 presents examples of current recommended Fe levels in poultry diets, derived from multiple sources. This table shows that the prescribed Fe level ranges greatly; for example, from 20 to 110 mg/kg in broilers. It may be that commercial poultry are receiving excessive levels of Fe. This was highlighted by Feijo et al. (2023), who stated there is sufficient Fe available in the diets, provided it is available for absorption, and Fe supplements are not required. This conclusion was based on lack of improvements in performance observed in the presence of supplemental Fe (Feijo et al., 2023). Several studies support this observation, as presented in Table 2, Table 3, Table 4. Some studies have observed reduced performance because of Fe supplementation. For example, Chen et al. (2025) observed reduced BW and daily gain with 1280 mg/kg Fe in broilers, attributed to poorer gut integrity, and Han et al. (2022) observed 800 mg/kg resulted in lower BW compared to lower level or no Fe supplement. This could be attributed to reduced feed intake; Cao et al. (1996) indicated that delaying feeding high Fe diets until at least 5 d of age can alleviate decreased feed intake associated with high Fe diets. Likewise, in laying hens, Paik et al. (2009) found feeding Fe-Met resulted in reduced egg production and poorer feed conversion compared to birds fed diets without supplemental Fe, and Park et al. (2004) observed lower FCR in hens fed the control diet not supplemented with Fe compared to those fed 300 mg/kg Fe-Met and 200 mg/kg Fe-AA (Availa-Fe-60). This illustrates the potential detrimental effects of feeding diets with excessive total Fe. According to Table 2, Table 3, the maximum total Fe should be 100 mg/kg in broiler and broiler breeder diets, based on bird performance, incidences of ascites and quantity of Fe excreted (Abbasi et al., 2015; Akbari et al., 2025; Akter et al., 2015; Aoyagi and Baker, 1995; Bess et al., 2012; Shinde PL et al., 2011; Vahl and Klooster, 1988). In laying hens, a maximum of 300 mg/kg Fe is recommended, if in inorganic form (Kim et al., 2023; Sarlak et al., 2025).

Conversely, there are numerous studies that have illustrated benefits from feeding high levels of dietary Fe. For example, in broilers, multiple studies observed that Fe supplements improved BW (Akbari et al., 2025; Aoyagi and Baker, 1995; Arnaudova-Matey et al., 2013; Han et al., 2022; Shinde PL et al., 2011; Vahl and Klooster, 1988) and breast meat quantity (Akbari et al., 2025; Behroozlak et al., 2021). Iron supplementation has also been shown to heighten egg production in both broiler breeders (Bess et al., 2012; Taschetto et al., 2017) and laying hens (Cao et al., 2022; Ramadan et al., 2010; Sarlak et al., 2021, 2025).

Table 2 illustrates that in broilers, in relation to bird performance, the optimum total dietary Fe level appears to be 60 to 80 mg/kg. This is based on examples such as Feijo et al. (2024) indicating 58 mg/kg total Fe was sufficient, observing no benefits from additional supplemental Fe, Almeldin et al. (2024) finding 66 mg/kg total Fe (40 mg/kg from nano-Fe oxide) resulted in optimum growth performance and meat qaulity, and Akbari et al. (2025), Akter et al. (2017) and Aoyagi and Baker (1995) presenting optimum BW gain (BWG) with total dietary Fe of 88 mg/kg (20 mg/kg from supplemental Fe), 60-50 mg/kg, and 76-86 mg/kg, respectively. Similarly, observations presented in Table 3 indicate that the optimum dietary Fe level for broiler breeders is 70 to 80 mg/kg; based on Gou et al. (2020) observing maximal hatching percentage with 72 mg/kg Fe, and Taschetto et al. (2017) identifying that 73.4 mg/kg resulted in improved hatched eggs per hen. In laying hens, as shown in Table 4, the optimum total dietary Fe level appears to be 120 to 130 mg/kg; based on Bertechini et al. (2000) observing no performance benefit from supplementing Fe into a diet with 127 mg/kg total Fe, Cao et al. (2022) finding benefits from feeding 120 to 135 mg/kg total Fe (45–60 mg/kg from supplemental Fe-AA), and Paik et al. (2009) observing poorer performance when supplementing Fe-Met to a diet with 121 mg/kg total Fe. Generally, inorganic Fe sources need to be supplied at higher levels compared to organic forms; for example, in laying hens, beneficial effects on egg production and weight were seen with 60 mg/kg Fe-AA or Fe-Pro (Cao et al., 2022; Revell et al., 2009; Sarlak et al., 2021; Xie et al., 2019), compared to 100 mg/kg or higher Fe required from FeSO4 sources (Kim et al., 2023; Park et al., 2004; Ramadan et al., 2010).

Iron requirements and absorption vary with bird age. This was illustrated by Mohanna and Nys (1998), who examined Fe retention in broilers over a 50-d period and observed Fe retention was highest during the first days of life, and then decreased rapidly until d 21, followed by little change thereafter. The effect of age on Fe retention was highly significant in this study (Mohanna and Nys, 1998). Similar findings were observed earlier in the 1960s, by Gruhn and Anke (1965), who found total body Fe content in female chicks peaked at between d 14 and 28 and then declined thereafter, and Davis et al. (1968), who observed that after d 21 Fe stored in the liver was being used for Hb formation in Fe-deficient chicks. Comparably in breeding birds, Fajmonova et al. (2004) found retention of Fe relative to weight gain was lowest at age d 38 and 28 in slow growing (hybrid Isa Brown) and fast growing (hybrid Ross 208) cockerels, respectively. Also, Morck and Austic (1981) found Ht, Hb and egg yolk Fe decreased from d 21 in breeding White Leghorn hens. There is a deficit of understanding about how Fe requirements vary with age in laying hens. However, it is established that the onset of egg laying is preceded by a decline in Hb, as Fe is redirected from blood plasma to the ovaries for follicle and egg development (Gou et al., 2020). Thus, it is important to provide ample Fe during this period. For example, Sarlak et al. (2025) observed that the rate of increase in abnormal eggs was three times higher in Fe-deficient birds compared to those receiving Fe supplementation. These findings indicate that Fe levels should be tailored to the specific dietary phase.

Iron interactions with other nutrients

Iron interaction with other minerals

Calcium and phosphorus

Mineral compounds added to diets, such as limestone and phosphates, may have high Fe content. Wilkinson (2023) reported Fe content of limestone samples ranged from 55 to 6800 mg/kg, and Bai et al. (2021b) reported a range from 100 to 200 mg/kg. This wide range in Fe content reflects inherent geological variability in the parent rock and depositional environment, including differing abundances of Fe-bearing minerals (e.g., clays, hematite), with further variation arising from quarrying, processing, and potential contamination during handling. Given this, when considering the total Fe content of poultry feed, Fe is generally not regarded as a ‘limiting nutrient’. For example, Feijo et al. (2024) states that broilers fed commercial corn-soybean meal diets, which contain approximately 58 mg/kg Fe, do not require supplemental Fe. However, the Fe in limestone and dicalcium is of low bioavailability because it is primarily present in the form of low-solubility oxides, in the Fe3+ state (Abbaspour et al., 2014; Sun et al., 2022b). Feijo et al. (2024) found that the Fe in limestone is in the form of approximately 62% Fe2+ and 38% Fe3+, whilst the Fe in dicalcium phosphate is 100% Fe3+, resulting in the Fe retained from limestone and dicalcium phosphate corresponding to just 1.9% of its total content. Subsequently, these authors observed a linear reduction in ileal digestible Fe in response to an increase in dietary Fe from commercial limestone and dicalcium phosphate, and calculated that 100 mg/kg Fe from limestone and dicalcium translates into an increase in ferritin of 7 ng/mL (Feijo et al., 2024).

High dietary Ca and P can reduce Fe absorption. This was illustrated by Waddell and Sell (1964) who observed that increasing dietary Ca level decreased RBC count and liver Fe concentration, and increasing dietary P decreased Hb, Ht, RBC count and retention of dietary Fe. Subsequently, Sell (1965) found increasing dietary Ca from 0.67% to 2% and P from 0.4% to 1.2% significantly reduced Fe absorption and liver Fe content. However, Shafey et al. (1991) found no significant effect of increasing dietary Ca and P content on Fe absorption, although did present that the heightened pH induced by Ca reduced the solubility and proportion of small mineral complexes, thus hindering Fe absorption.

Calcium can inhibit both haem and non-haem Fe absorption, either at the point of uptake into enterocytes (Ems et al., 2017) or during serosal transfer of Fe into the bloodstream (Hallberg et al., 1992). This could be a consequence of Ca acting as a non-competitive inhibitor of DMT1; Ca binds to a allosteric sites on DMT1, changing its shape, thus reducing its capacity as a Fe transporter (Cegarra et al., 2022; Shawki and Mackenzie, 2010). Another explanation could be that Ca and phosphate form insoluble complexes with Fe, inhibiting ability of non-haem Fe to be absorbed (Monsen and Cook, 1976); for example, in humans, Cook et al. (1991) found consumption of Ca-phosphate reduced Fe absorption by 62%. Furthermore, Ca influences the electrochemical environment of the intestinal lining (Hurwitz and Bar, 1968), which may inhibit ferric reductase enzyme activity, particularly Dcytb (Lynch, 2000), thus reducing the conversion of Fe3+ into Fe2+.

Manganese

Iron is adjacent to Mn in the periodic table, meaning they have similar chemical properties. Consequently, Mn and Fe interact with each other; both rely on DMT1 and FPN1 for absorption, and are transported in the blood by transferrin (Bai et al., 2008; Ye et al., 2017). Iron competes with Mn for these transport proteins, meaning that high levels of Fe can inhibit Mn absorption, and low levels of Fe can increase Mn uptake and alter Mn homeostasis,potentially resulting in neurotoxicity or deficiency symptoms (Bjørklund et al., 2017). This interaction was illustrated by Baker and Halpin (1991), who observed that administering a high level of Fe (5000 mg/kg) with supplemental Mn (1000 mg/kg) to chicks resulted in poorer weight gain and feed conversion compared to feeding a lower levels of Fe. Similarly, Wang et al. (2017) found that supplementing Fe, particularly at a high level (500 mg/kg), decreased Mn absorption and deposition in broilers, based on measured Mn concentration in the pancreas, duodenum mucosa and tibia. The authors attributed this observation to down-regulation of DMT1 and FPN1 in the duodenum (Wang et al., 2017). Furthermore, Bai et al. (2014) observed that dietary Mn supplementation reduced Fe concentration in the duodenum and liver of commercial hens, credited to Mn reducing duodenal Dcytb, DMT1 and FPN1 expression.

Zinc

Blalock and Hill (1988) observed Fe-deficient chicks are more susceptible to Zn toxicity, as evidenced by altered kidney and liver Zn and Fe concentrations, with Fe-supplemented birds demonstrating a greater capacity to sequester Zn via metallothionein compared with Fe-deficient animals. This is likely because in Fe-deficient birds the metal-binding protein metallothionein has reduced capacity to sequester Zn, thus disrupting Zn homeostasis (Richards et al., 2007). Moreover, Fe and Zn share some intestinal transporters, meaning more Zn may be absorbed in the absence of Fe, due to reduced competition (Kondaiah et al., 2019; Yu et al., 2017). However, Kordas and Stoltzfus (2004) noted that DMT1 is not the primary intestinal transporter of Zn, meaning DMT1 is not the site for this competition between Zn and Fe. High levels of Zn can induce Fe deficiency because Zn hinders ability of Fe to be absorbed and stimulates metallothionein expression. For example, Pimentel et al. (1992) and Stahl et al. (1989) found that birds fed high levels of Zn (1700–2000 mg/kg) presented reduced liver and tibia Fe concentration and packed cell volumes, despite no differences in Fe intake, and excreted more Fe. Furthermore, Zn can disrupt haem synthesis by competing with Fe and binding to the enzymes involved in haem production, such as ferrochelatase, or changing the structure of these enzymes; in this scenario, Zn is incorporated into protoporphyrin, the precursor to haem, instead of Fe (Chandrangsu and Helmann, 2016). There is insufficient evidence that excessive Fe promotes Zn deficiency.

Copper

Copper is essential for the function of the ferroxidase enzymes hephaestin and ceruloplasmin, which covert Fe2+ into Fe3+, enabling Fe to be transported via transferrin (Ems et al., 2017). Consequently, Cu deficiency can induce Fe deficiency (Lesson and Summers, 2005). This was illustrated by Hill and Matrone (1961) who observed Cu deficiency reduced RBC and heart cytochrome oxidase activity. The relationship between Cu and Fe was first reported by Al-Ubaidi and Sullivan (1963) in turkeys, whereby it was found that increasing Cu concentration in birds fed an Fe-deficient diet resulted in a decrease in body weight and Hb concentration. Also, Mcghee et al. (1965) found that feeding extremely different levels of Cu compared to Fe, or feeding high levels of both, had a negative impact on growth performance. This indicates that dietary Cu level directly influences Fe metabolism. Excessive Fe could antagonise the absorption of dietary Cu, by competing for binding to the extracellular domain of DMT1 (Lee et al., 2021), but this has yet to be confirmed in poultry.

Iron interaction with phytate

Phytate, or inositol hexaphosphate, are polyanionic molecule with the capacity to chelate positively charged cations (Humer et al., 2015). Iron in plant feedstuffs is highly complexed with phytate, which reduces the bioavailability of Fe (Cowieson et al., 2006); for example, the relative bioavailability of Fe in soybean meal was observed to be just 38.5% (Biehl et al., 1997). The prevalence of these Fe-phytate complexes is dictated by soil composition, climate and crop system. Supplemental phytase increases utilisation of phytate-bound Fe. For example, Feijo et al. (2023) fed broilers a high dose of phytase (1000 FYT) in combination with different levels of ferrous sulphate heptahydrate, and observed that phytase improved ileal Fe digestibility and bird performance, but had no impact on Fe intake or excretion, or Ht, Hb, ferritin or Fe contents in the liver. Based on this, these authors concluded that broilers fed corn-soy feeds with phytase did not require Fe supplementation to optimise performance (Feijo et al., 2023).

Nonetheless, phytate-Fe2+ and phytate-Fe3+ complexes that are phytase-resistant have been observed (Tang et al., 2006). For example, Maenz et al. (1999) established that Fe3+ is a potent inhibitor of phytate hydrolysis by phytase, regardless of pH. In this study, the inhibitory effects of Fe3+ decreased slightly as pH increased, but for Fe2+ an increase in pH resulted in inhibitory effects (Maenz et al., 1999). This is because trivalent cations form a tight intermolecular bond with phytate molecules, meaning they are not weakened in an acidic environment, and they are more resistant to hydrolysis compared to divalent cations (Marolt et al., 2015). Similarly, Akter et al. (2015) illustrated, using in vitro models, that phytate hydrolysis is reduced as the concentration of Fe increases; this was particularly evident at a higher pH of 6.5 compared to 2.5. Successively, Akter et al. (2017) demonstrated in broiler chickens that 100 mg/kg dietary Fe inhibited phytase activity, but 60 and 80 mg/kg dietary Fe did not. This indicates that in the presence of phytase, low levels of Fe are required. This was enforced by Dersjant-Li et al. (2025), who presented that a bacterial 6-phytase could replace the beneficial effect of supplementing Fe (along with Zn, Cu, and Mn) on growth performance in broilers, indicating Fe supplementation could be substantially lowered in diets containing phytase, reducing the likelihood of Fe excretion. This was also shown in broiler breeders by Abbasi et al. (2015), who recommended, based on broken-line analysis, that broiler breeder hens need 65 mg/kg supplemented Fe in the absence of phytase, but just 58 mg/kg in the presence of phytase.

Iron interaction with vitamins

At acidic pH, vitamin C reduces Fe3+ to Fe2+, through its role as an electron donor and cofactor for Dyctb (Du et al., 2012; Latunde-Dada et al., 2008). It also forms ascorbate-Fe3+ chelates, which prevent Fe3+ from precipitating as insoluble hydroxides or binding to phytate (Lynch and Cook, 1980). Consequently, vitamin C presence can result in Fe being maintained in the preferred reduced Fe2+ form for longer, and increases non-haem Fe being reduced to Fe2+ (Van Hieu et al., 2022), thus increasing Fe absorption. Vitamin C also functions as an antioxidant, donating electrons to neutralise free radicals and ROS, thus protecting against cellular damage (Traber and Stevens, 2011). This indicates that vitamin C can assist in maintaining RBC integrity, preventing their premature breakdown (haemolysis), protecting Hb and Fe-containing enzymes from damage (Obeagu, 2025).

Elgendey et al. (2022) observed that serum Fe concentration increased when broilers were fed diets supplemented with vitamin E and selenium. This can be attributed to vitamin E functioning as antioxidant, protecting RBC membranes and reducing damage to Hb by preventing oxidation of polyunsaturated fatty acids (Jilani and Iqbal, 2011), in a similar way to vitamin C. Vitamin E could also reduce oxidative stress induced by Fe overload; Fe overload leads to production of harmful free radicals, which can be neutralised by vitamin E (Gambaro et al., 2023).

Vitamin A is essential for mobilisation of Fe from ferritin in hepatocytes, facilitating Fe release from liver stores (Blaner et al., 2016). This was illustrated by Zhang et al. (2003) who observed that increasing supplemental vitamin A levels in broiler diets resulted in decreased Fe concentration in the liver and increased Fe concentration in the serum, indicating that vitamin A promotes mobilisation of Fe from liver to blood. These authors also found that Fe concentration in the duodenum and tibia and RBC count increased with dietary vitamin A level, indicating vitamin A also promotes Fe absorption and erythropoiesis (Zhang et al., 2003). Subsequently, Zhang et al. (2005) observed a complementary effect between Fe and vitamin A on improving Hb concentration and RBC count, BWG and serum catalase activity.

Vitamin B6 facilitates the conversion of Fe into haem by acting as a co-enzyme in the synthesis of δ-aminolevulinic acid (ALA), which is the first and rate limiting enzyme in the haem biosynthesis pathway (Hendawy et al., 2020; Imi and Shibata, 2021). Consequently, if vitamin B6 is deficient, the incorporation of Fe into haem may be impaired, resulting in reduced oxygen transport capacity. Vitamin B12 is essential for DNA synthesis and cell division, including erythroid precursor cells in the bone marrow (Kumar et al., 2023), meaning vitamin B12 deficiency can result in erythroblast apoptosis, and thus anaemia induced by ineffective erythropoiesis (Koury and Ponka, 2004). This is because erythroblast haem levels regulate uptake of Fe from transferrin; when haem levels are low, erythroblasts increase Fe uptake to produce more haem, and the opposite is true when haem levels are high (Ponka and Schulman, 1985).

Iron interaction with amino acids

Iron chemically binds to amino acids, forming chelates (Rahmouni et al., 2019); amino acids have two donor groups (amino and carboxyl) and can form stable, five-membered chelate rings with Fe atoms (Li et al., 2017). The side chains (R-groups) of amino acids participate in chelation and change the chemical environment of the donor groups (Byrne et al., 2021), dictating affinity for Fe. For example, Li et al. (2017) presented that glutamic and aspartic acid form chelates with Fe3+ at their oxygen-rich R groups, whereas arginine and asparagine form chelates with Fe2+ at their oxygen-rich R groups. Histidine residues in Hb and myoglobin bind directly to the Fe2+ atom of haem, via an imidazole group, holding the Fe in place, and can stabilise oxygen binding by forming hydrogen bonds with the oxygen molecule attached to haem (de Andrade Leite et al., 1999). Additionally, the sulphur groups of cysteine-containing amino acids bind to Fe and reduce Fe3+ to Fe2+ by donating electrons, increasing ability of Fe to be absorbed by DMT1; the thiol group in cysteine acts as a ligand to bind Fe (Bhattacharyya et al., 2013). Also, valine, leucine and phenylalanine create hydrophobic pockets around the haem group in Hb, protecting the haem group from oxidation by water (Liong et al., 2001); water can catalyse oxidation of Fe2+ to Fe3+, rendering the haem unable to bind to oxygen (Ems et al., 2017). Moreover, glycine plays an important role as a precursor for haem biosynthesis, in that the first step in haem synthesis involves condensation of glycine and succinyl-CoA to form ALA (Yien and Perfetto, 2022).

Amino acids are crucial for ferritin and transferrin synthesis; for example, transferrin is made up of approximately 700 amino acids (Ajayi et al., 2024). Therefore, low levels of amino acids can impair Fe distribution by reducing the quantity of transferrin available to transport Fe. However, even if amino acids are sufficient in the diet, without adequate Fe they cannot be effectively incorporated into Hb, leading to inefficient amino acid utilisation in Fe-deficient birds. The reduction in Hb production induced by Fe-deficiency reduces histidine utilisation (de Andrade Leite et al., 1999), resulting in increased histidine in the plasma, which can have negative impacts on zinc utilisation and cause cognitive impairment (Lackner et al., 2022). Moreover, many amino acid metabolising enzymes are Fe-dependent, such as phenylalanine hydroxylase (Lartey and Austic, 2008) and prolyl and lysyl hydroxylases (Salo and Myllyharju, 2021). Iron-sulphur enzymes may also be inactivated in the absence of Fe, negatively impacting energy metabolism by reducing ATP available for protein synthesis (Teh et al., 2024).

Presence of Fe allows for formation of Fe-amino acid complexes, which prevents Fe from binding to inhibitors such as phytate, making the Fe more bioavailable (Bess et al., 2012; Ebbing et al., 2019). However, excess Fe can result in production of hydroxyl radicals (Yan et al., 2022) which react with amino acids, resulting in their structure, and thus function, changing due to oxidation of side chains and fragmentation (Mujika et al., 2013). This oxidative stress causes protein hydrolysis and proteolytic degradation (Oke et al., 2024). To combat oxidative stress, sulphur amino acids may be diverted towards glutathione synthesis (Tesseraud et al., 2008), away from valuable protein synthesis and Fe-binding proteins. Also, excess Fe can accumulate in the liver, leading to ferroptosis, and resulting liver damage (Li et al., 2025), thus hindering amino acid catabolism by reducing transamination and deamination reactions.

Impact of Fe on the microbiota

Commensal and pathogenic bacteria in the gut acquire dietary Fe through 1) receptor-mediated transport from Fe-bound proteins such as haem and transferrin, 2) using ferric citrate transporters, and 3) through releasing siderophores (Pajarillo et al., 2021); siderophores are secreted macrocycles that sequestrate and solubilise Fe3+ (Xie et al., 2019). Iron-bound siderophores have a higher affinity for Fe compared to the hosts Fe-binding proteins (Hider and Kong, 2010). They can be transported across cell membranes by various transporters, with the assistance of TonB-dependent outer membrane siderophore receptors and ferric enterobactin siderophore ATP-binding cassette transporter systems (Hantke et al., 2003). Fe transporters, such as ferrous uptake systems and putative Fe transport systems are expressed in several gastrointestinal bacteria, and some of these bacteria produce haemophore proteins that transport haem via receptor-mediated uptake (Cartron et al., 2006; Krewulak and Vogel, 2008). Specific Bacillus strains can produce siderophores that sequester Fe, thereby limiting its availability to pathogens, such as Escherichia coli and Salmonella, depriving them of this key element for their development and virulence properties, and giving the Bacillus a competitive growth advantage (Ellermann and Arthur, 2017; Fgaier and Eberl, 2011; Harwood et al., 2018; Kramer et al., 2020; Sandvang et al., 2021). Further research is warranted to elucidate the mechanisms of action and interplay between dietary Fe and probiotics.

Iron toxicity, and the subsequent presence of unabsorbed Fe in the gut, can generate an environment that favours pathogenic bacteria with effective siderophore systems, increasing their colonisation (Yilmaz and Li, 2018). Excess Fe also promotes ROS generation in the gut, whereby Fe reacts with hydrogen peroxide to produce highly reactive hydroxyl radicals (Schieber and Chandel, 2014; Yan et al., 2022). The resulting oxidative stress can damage enterocytes and contributes towards shifting microbial balance towards pathogenic bacteria, impairing nutrient absorption (Li et al., 2024). This was illustrated by Gou et al. (2018) who observed that feeding an unrealistically high dose of Fe (1651 mg/kg) induced oxidative stress in the gastrointestinal tract of Chinese Yellow broilers, resulting in dilated endoplasmic reticulum, autophagosome formation of jejunal enterocytes and decreased villi, and significant changes in caeca microbiota composition. However, Yang et al. (2024) observed that feeding high Fe (500 mg/kg) favourably changed the relative bacterial abundance in the caeca in birds fed high-fat diets, stating that it reversed the microbiota imbalance induced by the high-fat diet, preventing excessive fat deposition in chickens. Further research is warranted into the relationship between dietary Fe and gastrointestinal microbiota composition.

Iron is an essential nutrient for the growth, survival and virulence of Salmonella (Tan et al., 2021), indicating that excessive dietary Fe may exacerbate prevalence of Salmonella. Salmonella secretes Fe-chelating molecules, such as the siderophore enterobactin, that acquire Fe in the gut (Jaswal and Behnsen, 2023; Spiga et al., 2023). The host combats this by secreting lipocalin-2 to sequester enterobactin (Jaswal and Behnsen, 2023; Xiao et al., 2017). However, Salmonella can produce salmochelin, a glycosylated forms of enterobactin, which cannot be bound by lipocalin-2, meaning salmonchelin can be used for bacterial Fe acquisition (Raffatellu et al., 2009). This host-pathogenic competition for Fe suggests that reducing the quantity of Fe in the hindgut, by lowering dietary Fe and/or improving absorption of Fe early in the gastrointestinal tract, could reduce the prevalence of Salmonella (Tan et al., 2021). It also indicates that use of highly bioavailable Fe requires further investigation, given microorganisms also readily utilise Fe, and thus improper use of these products could potentially increase Salmonella proliferation.

Limited Fe availability leads to competition between beneficial and pathogenic bacteria species for Fe, which can restrict the multiplication of beneficial species (Abbas et al., 2022). Consequently, Fe-deficiency modulates mucosal immunity by altering microbial profile, diversity and function (Parmanand et al., 2019; Zhu et al., 2024). The microbiota in Fe-deficient birds is less stable, and thus more vulnerable to colonisation by pathogenic bacteria species that have high-affinity Fe uptake systems (Noordine et al., 2024); pathogenic bacteria with efficient mechanisms to scavenge Fe, such as siderophores, can outcompete the commensal bacteria. Beneficial bacteria species, such as Lactobacillus, require Fe for metabolism, but lack strong Fe acquisition systems (Ellermann and Arthur, 2017). Consequently, in an Fe-deficient environment their population may decline, and the benefits they induce, such as maintaining gastrointestinal pH and short chain fatty acid (SCFA) production, may be reduced. Moreover, the general reduced host immunity induced by Fe-deficiency, caused by impaired Hb synthesis and oxygen transport, may also allow greater pathogen proliferation (Lin et al., 2020).

Conclusion

Both insufficient and excessive dietary Fe can have negative impacts on commercial poultry health and productivity. Based on research trial outputs, it is predicted optimal total dietary Fe is 60 to 80 mg/kg for broilers and broiler breeders and 120 to 130 mg/kg for laying hens. Inorganic Fe needs to be supplied at higher levels than organic sources. Iron requirement is dictated by the form of Fe (organic or inorganic) and diet composition, particularly prevalence of phytate. Inorganic Fe sources, although commonly used, have lower bioavailability than organic chelates. Broiler and broiler breeders appear to require approximately 40 to 60 mg/kg organic Fe compared to 60 to 80 mg/kg inorganic Fe, while laying hens require approximately 60 to 80 mg/kg organic Fe compared to more than 100 mg/kg inorganic Fe. It is hypothesised that, particularly if supplied as inorganic Fe, total dietary Fe should not exceed 100 mg/kg in broiler and broiler breeder diets and 300 mg/kg in laying hen diets. Further research is required to confirm and refine these values. There should be increased focus and research on organic Fe sources. Of particular importance is the impact of these organic sources on the gastrointestinal microbiota and absorption of other dietary nutrients.
