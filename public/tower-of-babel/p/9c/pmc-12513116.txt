Multicomponent-based analyses of ACS-patient-derived extracellular vesicles as likely tools for tailored interventional approaches
Authors: Saveria Femminò, Alessandro Sarcinella, Alberto Grosso, Francesca Mensitieri, Ovidio De Filippo, Francesco Bruno, Albino Coglianese, Marta Tapparo, Edoardo Pace, Sharad Kholia, Stefania Bruno, Rosa Ciullo, Fabrizio Buffolo, Giovanni Camussi, Gaetano Maria De Ferrari, Fabrizio Dal Piaz, Fabrizio D’Ascenzo, Maria Felice Brizzi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryReliable predictive biomarkers to reduce unnecessary coronary angiograms (CAGs) in non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and unstable angina (UA) patients displaying high-risk features are still lacking. Here, we show that profiling patient-derived circulating extracellular vesicles (EVs) can not only improve their risk stratification but also reduce unnecessary CAGs. Analysis of EVs and their miR cargo revealed that CD62p+EVs enriched in miR-130a-3p correlated with the absence of non-critical coronary artery disease (CAD). Proteomic analysis identified nine proteins differentially enriched in patients with or without critical-CAD (NO CAD), irrespective of their diagnosis. Multivariate analysis identified miR-130a-3p (odds ratio [OR]:0.35 [0.19–0.67]), phospholipid transfer protein (OR 0.96 [0.94–0.98]), and subunit beta of mitochondrial trifunctional enzyme (OR:0.96 [0.94–0.98]) as predictors of NO CAD. Furthermore, EV-miR-130a-3p enrichment predicted the absence of multivessel disease (OR:0.46 [0.23–0.90]). These findings establish EV profiling as a valuable tool for stratifying and optimizing the clinical management of patients with acute coronary syndrome.

Introduction

Acute coronary syndrome (ACS), defined as a sudden reduction in blood supply to the heart, includes ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (STEMI) and non-ST-segment elevation ACS, the latter comprising non-ST-segment elevation myocardial infarction (NSTEMI) and unstable angina (UA).1 Each year, more than 7 million individuals are diagnosed with ACS worldwide. Risk factors of ACS include age, male sex, smoking, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes, obesity, previous cardiovascular events, family history of coronary artery disease (CAD), renal dysfunction, and peripheral or carotid artery disease. The main cause of damage in ACS is the rupture of a lipid coronary plaque, followed by platelet-rich thrombus formation, which occurs in approximately 64% of ACS patients.2

UA and NSTEMI are closely related conditions, with improved differential diagnosis due to the introduction of high-sensitivity cardiac troponin (hs-cTn) T or I evaluation. Despite advances in treatments, both conditions continue to have high recurrence and mortality rates. Thus, recent international guidelines recommend a routine invasive strategy for both NSTEMI and UA patients displaying high-risk features.1 As a result, a large number of coronary angiograms (CAGs) are performed, but a considerable proportion of these patients are found to have non-critical-CAD (NO CAD).3 This highlights an ongoing challenge in accurately identifying patients with CAD who truly require invasive assessment.

Recently, circulating extracellular vesicles (EVs) have emerged as potential diagnostic/prognostic tools in different clinical settings, including cardiovascular diseases.4 EVs are small vesicles with a lipid bilayer membrane secreted by multiple cell types and are considered a novel paracrine and endocrine mechanism of cell-to-cell communication.5 Their cargo, which reflects the environmental conditions sensed by their cell of origin, consists of lipids, proteins, and RNAs, including micro RNAs (miRNAs). Due to the stability of miRNA and resistance to RNase degradation, many studies have focused on assessing EV-miRNA content to identify potential biomarkers for cardiac diseases. A systematic review comparing ACS and healthy individuals identified several free-circulating miRNAs that were either up- or downregulated.6 In particular, miR-126-3p, miR-130, miR-208a, miR-221-5p, miR-23a, miR-155, miR-133, and miR-134 were found to be differentially expressed in UA and NSTEMI patients.

The number and phenotype of circulating EVs have also been reported as predictors of specific cardiovascular disease6; however, their protein content has been poorly investigated.7 Profiling circulating EV protein content has gained particular interest due to the presence of two distinct external protein layers collectively referred to as the protein corona (PC), which reflect specific environmental conditions.8 Notably, we have previously shown that TnT is enriched in EVs collected from patients with NSTEMI and, more significantly, UA.4

This study was designed to address the unmet need for tailored interventions in patients with NSTEMI and UA. Its primary aim was to profile EVs recovered from these patients with the aim to identify predictor(s) of the absence of clinically relevant CAD (NO CAD). The study specifically focused on small EVs (sEVs), a term that has been consistently used throughout the text.

This approach aims to facilitate better clinical decision-making and avoid unnecessary invasive interventions. Particular attention was given to profiling EV phenotype, miRNA, and proteins.

Results

Clinical characteristics of patients

Critical CAD is observed in approximately 70%–90% of patients undergoing CAG for ACS, with variation depending on inclusion criteria and evolving definitions of ACS over time.9 Assuming a conservative estimation of 70% prevalence for critical CAD, we calculated that a minimum of 110 patients would be required to ensure sufficient power to evaluate the association of six clinical variables (age, gender, baseline ejection fraction, diabetes mellitus type 2, NSTEMI vs. UA, and chronic kidney disease) with the presence of atherosclerotic disease, along with several additional experimental variables of interest. This estimation is based on Peduzzi et al.,10 who recommended a minimum of 7–11 outcome events per independent variable in multivariable logistic regression analyses.

The baseline and procedural characteristics of patients included in the study are summarized in Table 1, Figure S1, and Tables S1 and S2, which compare those with CAD to those without. Among the 108 patients diagnosed with CAD, the majority were classified as NSTEMI (58.3%) rather than UA (41.7%), with a significant difference in the distribution of diagnoses (p = 0.045). In terms of coronary anatomy, single-vessel disease was more common in the CAD group (38.9%), compared to two-vessel (30.6%) and three-vessel disease (30.6%) (p < 0.001). These patients were more frequently treated with a single coronary stent (43.9%) (p < 0.001), highlighting the preferred procedural approach for their condition. Despite these differences, both groups (CAD and NO CAD) exhibited a significant burden of cardiovascular risk factors such as hypertension, dyslipidemia, and smoking. The prevalence of these factors did not differ significantly between the two groups, suggesting that CAD may not be solely influenced by the number of risk factors present but also by other factors, such as disease progression or the acute nature of the event. Furthermore, various biomarkers and clinical parameters, such as left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), creatinine levels, and the use of specific medications (e.g., ticagrelor and statins), were evaluated, revealing some noteworthy trends. While both groups shared common characteristics in terms of medical history and treatment strategies, CAD patients were more likely to require intensive pharmacologic management, including dual antiplatelet therapy (DAPT with ticagrelor; p < 0.001), statins (p = 0.006), and possibly anticoagulants (p = 0.42).

Overall, while CAD patients had some distinctive clinical features, particularly in terms of their acute presentation (e.g., higher hs-cTn), the underlying cardiovascular risk factor profile appeared similar across both groups, emphasizing the complex multifactorial nature of CAD, where traditional risk factors alone do not fully account for the development of critical disease.

As shown in Figure 1A, we first analyzed sEVs by TEM. Western blot (WB) analysis revealed the presence of the typical exosome markers CD63 and CD81 in all EV samples and the expression of GM130, a negative exosome marker, only in the cell lysate (Figure 1B). The expression of exosome markers was also evaluated using MACSPlex Exosome Kit, revealing the CD62p platelet marker as the most highly expressed (Figures 1C and 1D). Furthermore, we found that sEVs also expressed high levels of CD42, CD41b (platelet markers), and CD31 (endothelial marker) (Figure 1C), but not caveolin 3, a surrogate marker of cardiomyocyte-derived sEVs (Figure 1F). Individual NSTEMI and UA samples were also evaluated, with no significant differences being observed between the two groups in the analyses of CD62p, CD42, CD41b, and CD31 (Figure S2; Table S3). Of note, we found a correlation between the presence of NO CAD and sEVs expressing CD62p (Figure 1E) (p = 0.003).

miR-130a-3p EV cargo correlates with the presence of NO CAD

To identify predictors of critical CAD, we further evaluated the content of different sEV-miRNA. The selection of miRNAs was based on studies that analyzed circulating miRNAs in patients with ACS.6,11,12,13,14 Among them we selected eight miRNAs (miR-23a, miR-126-5p, miR-130a-3p, miR-133-5p, miR-134-5p, miR-155, miR-208-5p, and miR-221-5p) that have been previously shown to be more closely related to UA and NSTEMI.6,11,12 Although these miRs have been extensively investigated in CAD patients, previous research has not distinguished between those requiring intensive care and those who do not. We believe that selecting already validated biomarkers may help to reduce bias related to patient heterogeneity, including treatment differences, which could otherwise result in inconsistent or confounded outcomes. This study has been therefore performed to examine their potential role as predictive biomarkers for improving risk stratification, thereby reducing unnecessary coronary angiographies in high-risk CAD patients. By comparing their expression levels in patients enrolled in the present study with UA and NSTEMI, only miR126-5p and miR-130a-3p were found differentially expressed. Both miRNAs were enriched in sEVs from UA patients (p < 0.001 for both) (Figures 2H, 2L, and S3; Table S4). However, on comparing their expression in CAD vs. NO CAD patients, only miR-130a-3p was found to be highly enriched in sEVs isolated from NO CAD patients (2.4 [1.83–2.64] vs. 1.32 [1.08–2.25]; p < 0.001) (Figures 2I and 2M).

Proteomic analyses identified proteins involved in lipid transport and metabolism as potential biomarkers for the presence of NO CAD

In an effort to define putative protein biomarkers associated with the presence or absence of CAD, we carried out a proteomic analysis on sEVs isolated from patients with UA or NSTEMI, exhibiting CAD or NO CAD. sEV samples from each of the four resulting groups (named: UA-CAD, UA-NO CAD, NSTEMI-CAD, and NSTEMI-NO CAD) were subjected to a label-free shotgun proteomic investigation, according to previously published guidelines.15

For each sample group, we identified approximately 2,000 immunoglobulins and 490 non-immunoglobulin proteins. The high number of immunoglobulins is expected, since their presence has been reported in relation to the immunomodulatory effects of EVs.16 However, due to the inherent interindividual variability of immunoglobulins and the challenges in accurately identifying the mass spectrometry (MS)-based techniques, our study focused on proteins of non-immunoglobulin origin. Specifically, we identified 498 for UA-CAD, 485 for UA-NO CAD, 488 for NSTEMI-CAD, and 487 for NSTEMI-NO CAD, as detailed in Tables S5, S6, S7, and S8. Gene ontology analysis of these proteins indicated that the primary biological functions were similar across all four groups (Figure 3A). To evaluate whether the sEV proteome is influenced by the different pathological conditions of the various patients, we performed a PCA of the different samples. The resulting graph (Figure 3B) showed that all the UA-CAD samples were well grouped and distinct from the others, as were most of the UA-NO CAD samples. Conversely, NSTEMI-CAD and NSTEMI-NO CAD could not be clearly distinguished from each other by PCA. However, both groups were in a distinctly different region of the graph compared to the UA samples. To further investigate the sources of variability observed in the scores plot and to identify the molecular features driving the separation between experimental groups, we examined both the loadings and variance plots. The variance plot confirmed that PC1 and PC3 account for 15.1% and 3.1% of the total variance, respectively, indicating a modest but informative representation of the overall dataset structure. The cumulative variance explained by these components, approximately 18.2%, is consistent with typical values observed in complex biological datasets, where variation is often distributed across multiple dimensions. Analysis of the loadings plot (Figure S4) enabled us to explore the contribution of individual proteins to the selected components. Points located further from the origin along PC1 or PC3 represent proteins with a stronger influence in defining group differences in the score plot. Proteins showing opposing loadings along the same axis may contribute to inverse expression trends across samples.

A similar result also emerged from the heatmap summarizing the data obtained for all the analyzed samples (Figure 3C). Indeed, the proteomic profiles of samples within the same group were generally similar, with clear differences observed between the sEV proteomes of UA and NSTEMI patients. However, only subtle differences were observed between NSTEMI patients with CAD (NSTEMI-CAD) and NO CAD (NSTEMI-NO CAD) as well as between UA patients with CAD (UA-CAD) and NO CAD (UA-NO CAD), thus indicating that the proteomic profiles of sEVs are not easily differentiated based solely on CAD status in these patient subgroups.

The data obtained were subjected to statistical analysis, initially comparing UA-CAD patients with UA-NO CAD patients and NSTEMI-CAD patients with UA-NO CAD patients using the Student’s t test. Proteins were considered to be differentially expressed between samples when their ration had a p value of <0.05. To highlight the specific proteins with significantly different abundances in sEVs from patients with CAD versus NO CAD, we compared the proteomic data from the CAD and NO CAD groups. Given the clear proteomic differences observed between sEVs from UA and NSTEMI patients, we first compared UA-CAD to UA-NO CAD and NSTEMI-CAD to NSTEMI-NO CAD separately. Consistent with the comprehensive picture provided by the heatmap, the resulting volcano plots (Figures 3D and 3E) revealed that in both cases, only a limited number of proteins showed significant changes. Specifically, the abundance of 21 proteins was significantly different in the sEVs from UA-CAD compared to those of UA-NO CAD (Table S9), while 25 proteins showed differential abundance in sEVs from NSTEMI patients (Table S10). These proteins are involved in several different biological pathways, with most pathways differing between the two sample groups. However, in both cases, the biological pathways with the greatest number of proteins involved was lipid metabolism and transport (five for UA and five for NSTEMI) (Figures 3F and 3G). Nine proteins were differently abundant in CAD compared to NO CAD sEVs for both UA and NSTEMI patients (Table S11). Remarkably, among these, we identified four proteins involved in the lipid metabolism and transport pathway: basement-membrane-specific heparan sulfate proteoglycan core protein (HSPG2), apolipoprotein B (ApoB), phospholipid transfer protein (PLTP), and subunit beta of trifunctional enzyme mitochondrial (HADHB).

To refine our data analysis, we also compared the proteomic data from sEVs of CAD and NO CAD patients, regardless of UA or NSTEMI diagnosis (Figure 3H). This analysis led to the identification of 10 proteins whose abundance was significantly different between CAD and NO CAD samples (Table S12). Out of these, only three proteins were common when merging the results of the proteomic analyses from the UA and NSTEMI sample groups. Intriguingly, three out of the four were involved in the metabolism and transport of lipid pathways. Indeed, only ApoB did not emerge from this alternative data processing approach. miR-130a-3p enrichment in sEVs prevents endothelial cell activation and reduces the recruitment of inflammatory cells

To validate the biological relevance of the most promising biomarkers, we performed an in vitro assay aimed to recapitulate early events in plaque formation as well as disease progression leading to clinical manifestation. Specifically, we aimed to simulate endothelial activation and inflammatory cell recruitment, known as the initial step leading to CAD. First, we assessed whether sEVs enriched or depleted in miR-130a-3p could modulate the expression of ICAM1 (CD54)19 on endothelial cells. As shown in Figures 6A and 6B, sEVs enriched in miR-130a-3p (derived from NO CAD individuals) significantly downregulated CD54 expression, suggesting a suppression of endothelial activation. This inhibitory effect was reversed by transfection with a miR-130a-3p antagomir, confirming the specific involvement of miR-130a-3p in this event. Conversely, sEVs depleted of miR-130a-3p (from CAD patients) failed to suppress CD54 expression. Notably, transfection with a miR-130a-3p mimic restored the inhibitory effect, further supporting its functional role. Similar results were obtained upon the use of the miR-130a-3p antagomir or mimic (Figures 6A, 6B, and S5). In line with these findings, the adhesion of inflammatory cells to endothelial cells was reduced upon treatment with miR-130a-3p-enriched sEVs, while it increased in cells treated with miR-130a-3p-depleted sEVs (Figure 6C). Discussion

The present study is the first to focus on identifying biomarkers that can predict CAD in patients suffering from NSTEMI and UA.

Current international guidelines recommend using hs-cTn to rule out myocardial infarction (MI) due to its high negative predictive value.1 Additionally, hs-cTn has shown to exhibit a prognostic value, as patients with UA demonstrate lower rates of mortality, cardiovascular (CV) events, and CAD, requiring revascularization compared to NSTEMI.20 However, large registries have found that patients with UA still exhibit higher event rates compared to controls.21,22 Consequently, international guidelines advocate an invasive strategy during hospitalization for NSTE-ACS patients who show high-risk features or suspicion of UA.1 This recommendation has led to an increased number of CAGs in patients with NO CAD,23 emphasizing the need for biomarkers to identify such patients and avoid unnecessary procedures. EVs are present in all biological fluids.11 Their cellular origin and metabolic and environmental status dictate specific genomic and protein sorting. Notably, different EV subpopulations were initially found to predict outcomes in patients with MI.24 Over the past decade, several studies have reported the feasibility at using EVs in the clinical practice, highlighting the potential of EV multicomponent analyses for biomarker discovery.11,25,26

This study, performed on 146 ACS patients (78 NSTEMI and 68 UA), aimed to leverage sEV phenotyping, miRNA enrichment, and proteomics to identify predictive biomarkers and reduce unnecessary CAG in patients with NO CAD. Simple exosome isolation and purification methods that avoid time-consuming and costly processes should be prioritized to develop effective biomarker discovery tools to be applied in the clinical practice. In this study, we demonstrated for the first time that the charge-based precipitation protocol offers the rapid and cost-effective collection of all necessary information, theoretically facilitating its swift implementation for patient stratification and clinical decision-making.

Based on our previous observation that sEVs derived from UA patients are enriched in TnT,4 we first analyzed sEVs for TnT-EV-cargo in our patient cohort. Of note, we provide further evidence that sEVs are enriched in TnT regardless of UA or NSTEMI diagnosis. However, we did not observe a correlation between TnT levels and the occurrence of CAD, reinforcing the notion that TnT, whether free or EV-linked, cannot be considered a useful tool for stratifying patients requiring CAG. Instead, we identified TnT as part of the membrane-bound PC of circulating sEVs, which reflects the functional state of the tissue and may indicate a shared environmental backdrop in UA and NSTEMI.

Most studies investigating circulating EVs in ACS patients have reported elevated levels of platelet- or endothelial-cell-derived EVs, reflecting the canonical physiopathological events in ACS.27,28 CD62p is a platelet-specific surface marker.29 CD62p levels were found higher in CAD patients (MI, UA, and stable angina) compared to controls30 or UA and stable angina group.31 Tenaglia et al.32 also reported a significantly greater CD62p staining in atherectomy specimens from patients with UA compared to those from stable angina. It was also demonstrated that platelet-derived EV (PEV) levels were higher in epicardial coronary arteries compared to peripheral circulation, with a correlation observed between coronary PEV level and the severity of CAD, based on the SYNTAX score.33 Consistent with previous studies,26,27 we demonstrated that circulating sEVs reflect endothelial damage and platelet activation in the NSTEMI and UA setting. However, when considering the expression of different platelet and endothelial sEV markers, none were able to discriminate between NSTEMI and UA patients. Instead, we demonstrated that CD62p+EVs were correlated with the presence of NO CAD regardless of NSTEMI or UA diagnosis. This observation is consistent with previous data reporting low CD62p levels in patients with chronic CAD compared to those experiencing acute STEMI or stroke events.34

Unlike in cancer, the application of EV-miRNA cargo as diagnostic tool in cardiovascular diseases is still in the early stages of discovery. According to a recent review,35 miR-208a, miR-126, miR-150, miR-223, and miR-587 levels were significantly higher in ACS patients compared to controls. However, conflicting evidence exists regarding the protective role of miR-126 in terms of thrombus inhibition, reduction of endothelial damage, and migration of inflammatory cells in MI.36 In this study, we demonstrated that miR-126-5p is enriched in EVs recovered from UA patients, but its expression did not correlate with the occurrence of either CAD or NO CAD.

Platelets are recognized as a major source of both free and EV-enriched miRNAs,37 particularly miR-130 that is abundant in EVs.38 Consistently, we found that platelets are the most relevant parental sEV source, and miR-130a-3p is highly enriched in sEV derived from UA patients. More importantly, its levels correlate with the presence of NO CAD regardless of their diagnosis.

Apart from miR126, circulating levels of miR-130 have been reported to play a role as a potential predictor/biomarker in ACS. In patients with CAD, circulating levels of miR-130 were found to be significantly reduced compared to control.39 Furthermore, in another study, miR-130 levels were even lower in CAD patients with type 2 diabetes, suggesting it may serve as a potential independent predictor for CAD prevalence in patients with or without type 2 diabetes.40 Finally, miR-130 was also reported to be significantly upregulated in 80 AMI patients.41 Our study is the first to specifically analyze miR-130a-3p expression in sEVs obtained from UA and NSTEMI patients and to investigate its potential as a biomarker for the presence of NO CAD. Through multivariate analysis, we provide strong evidence that sEV-miR130-3p content serves as a significant predictor for the absence of both CAD and multivessel disease in this patient population.

This finding highlights the potential of sEV-miR-130a-3p as a non-invasive biomarker capable of distinguishing patients with NO CAD from those with more severe forms of the disease, thus offering a valuable tool for risk stratification and clinical decision-making in this patient cohort. Additionally, this miRNA may aid in guiding therapeutic approaches, reducing unnecessary invasive procedures such as CAG in patients with a low likelihood of CAD. Overall, our results underscore the relevance of sEV-derived miRNAs as diagnostic and prognostic markers, supporting their incorporation into future clinical practice for personalized management of ACS patients.

The potential of plasma proteomics to identify new putative biomarkers of various cardiovascular diseases has been widely reported.42,43 Recently, a targeted proteomic approach was found effective in refining the risk of recurrent cardiovascular events, suggesting that proteomic profiling may outperform traditional risk prediction scores to better discriminate individual recurrence rates.44 Furthermore, the use of untargeted proteomic for the analysis of circulating sEVs holds promise as a tool to develop innovative diagnostic strategies for different diseases.45,46

Indeed, the presence and/or the abundance of sEV protein content is multifaceted, influenced by several factors: (1) the expression levels in their cells of origin, (2) changes in secretion levels, and (3) the microenvironment of the releasing cells. This implies that recognizing a specific sEV protein cargo could provide more accurate predictions for the occurrence of specific pathological conditions.47,48 Proteomic analyses of sEVs from UA and NSTEMI patients revealed distinct grouping for UA patients with CAD or NO CAD. Conversely, PCA could not clearly distinguish NSTEMI patients assigned to the CAD or NO CAD group. Nevertheless, when comparing proteins differentially expressed in sEVs from patients with CAD versus NO CAD, our proteomic data provided the first evidence that PLTP and HADHB are enriched in EVs. Moreover, similar to miR-130a-3p and CD62p, these proteins can be proposed as putative predictors of the presence of NO CAD.

A substantial body of evidence has shown that plasma ApoB levels are influenced by the occurrence of CVD.49 Consistent with this, we found that ApoB levels in EVs correlate with the presence of CAD. While we acknowledge that the EV isolation procedure used in our study likely co-isolates LDL particles, the observed differences between the two group of patients, together with the low operation costs and time-efficiency offered by this protocol, supports its potential for broad application in clinical practice.

PLTP is implicated in transfer and exchange of phospholipids between lipoprotein particles and has been suggested to contribute in the atherosclerotic process.50 High PLTP activity has been proposed as an ACS risk factor,51 a predictor of CV events, and left ventricular dysfunction in CAD patients,52 as well as long-term all-cause mortality in patients with diabetes or suspected coronary disease.53 Recent studies have also shown that low PLTP activity is associated with higher HDL particle concentration, smaller HDL particle size, and higher TG concentration, but not with CAD risk.54 While we did not measure PLTP activity directly, we are the first to demonstrate its association with EV layers.55 Based on the Vroman’s effect,56 highly concentrated and small proteins can interact with the surface of EVs just after their release into circulation, thereby generating the EV-PC.8 In cancer, it has been shown that upon PC formation, EVs can be better taken up by immune cells and that immunoglobulins and complement protein enrichment mainly contribute to this process.16 Based on our observation, it can be speculated that the presence of high PLTP as a part of the PC on sEVs in NO CAD patients may play a role in a potential mechanism that facilitates its removal from the circulation, thereby preventing dangerous effects.

HADHB is the beta-subunit of the mitochondrial trifunctional protein (MTP), an enzymatic complex located in the inner mitochondrial membrane. It is responsible for the degradation of long-chain fatty acids and catalyzes the final step of beta-oxidation. Mutations in the HADHB gene lead to MTP deficiency, a rare autosomal recessive disorder affecting long-chain fatty acid oxidation. The phenotypes associated with total MTP deficiency also include early-onset cardiomyopathy and early death.57 However, HADHB can also act as an RNA-binding protein to post-transcriptionally regulate miRNA expression in post-ischemic neovascularization.58 Similar to HADHB, p53 and SMADs were also found to control pri- to pre-miRNA cleavage.59,60 Of note, bioinformatics network-pathway analyses identified p53 and SMAD pathways as among the enriched pathways. Therefore, based on the enrichment of HADHB in sEVs from patients with NO CAD, a potential role of HADHB sEV cargo in the posttranscriptional regulation of miRNAs that mitigate ischemia damage may be proposed. Bioinformatic pathway analyses also identified KEAP1-NRF2 pathway as a crucial factor distinguishing CAD and NO CAD patients. The KEAP1-NRF2 pathway is recognized as a key mechanism of protection against oxidative and chemical stresses.61 Moreover, its transcriptome has been implicated in providing protection against cardiovascular disease.62 Overall, these data not only strengthen our results but also support the notion that EV cargo can recapitulate specific pathological conditions, making it potentially exploitable for biomarker discovery.

To guarantee its clinical relevance, a biomarker must be mechanistically linked to CAD progression. Our functional studies showed that sEVs from NO CAD patients, unlike those from CAD patients, reduced ICAM1 expression and inflammatory cell recruitment, both key events in early atherogenesis, in plaque enlargement, CAD progression, and clinical manifestation. Among the molecular cargo identified, miR-130a-3p was recognized as a discriminator between CAD and NO CAD, particularly reflecting the severity of the underlying disease. Functional validation confirmed its specific role in modulating endothelial activation and inflammatory cell recruitment and possibly driving disease progression.

This study provides the first evidence that EV phenotyping and cargo analyses can represent a useful tool to investigate the occurrence of NO CAD in NSTE-ACS. Additionally, it provides a detailed characterization of EV-PC in UA and NSTEMI patients. Overall, the results of this study pave the way to further investigate sEV cargo, offering potential for a more tailored interventional approach, which is still regarded as an unmet need in NSTEMI and UA patients.

Limitations of the study

This study has several limitations. First, although the sample size is adequate for preliminary analyses, it may limit the generalizability of the findings, particularly given the heterogeneity in the clinical presentation of UA and NSTEMI patients. Second, the absence of external validation further restricts the extrapolation of the results to broader populations. Third, the lack of a control group consisting of healthy individuals or patients with other cardiovascular conditions makes it difficult to definitively assess the specificity of the identified biomarkers to NO CAD. The inclusion of such a group could have clarified whether these biomarkers are unique to the investigated conditions or reflect broader pathophysiological processes. However, the functional studies recapitulating early plaque formation and disease progression leading to clinical manifestation may support their predictive value. Fourth, the cross-sectional nature of the study design precludes the ability to assess causal relationships or monitor changes in biomarker levels over time. As a result, the role of these biomarkers in disease progression and prognosis remains unclear. Fifth, the study did not account for potential confounding factors such as variations in treatment regimens, comorbidities, or lifestyle factors, which could influence sEV composition and biomarker expression. Sixth, while the charge-based precipitation protocol used for sEV isolation was effective, further validation is needed to assess its robustness and applicability across different clinical settings, especially given the technical challenges and variability in sEV isolation methods. Lastly, while our focus on EV-miRNA and proteomic cargo provides valuable insights, the complexity of EV biology, including variations in their cellular origin and interactions with other molecular pathways, warrants more in-depth investigation. Despite these limitations, this study lays the foundation for further research into EV-based biomarkers in NO CAD and NSTE-ACS patients. Future studies with larger, more diverse cohorts, longitudinal designs, and the inclusion of control groups are necessary to validate and extend these findings.

Experimental model and study participant details

Study design

Critical CAD is observed in approximately 70% to 90% of patients undergoing CAG for ACS, with variation depending on inclusion criteria and evolving definitions of ACS over time.9 Considering a conservative estimate of 70% prevalence for critical CAD, we calculated that a minimum of 110 patients would be required to ensure sufficient power to evaluate the association of six clinical variables (age, gender, baseline ejection fraction, diabetes mellitus type 2, NSTEMI vs.UA, and chronic kidney disease) with the presence of atherosclerotic disease, along with several additional experimental variables of interest. This assessment is based on Peduzzi et al.,10 who recommended a minimum of 7 to 11 outcome events per independent variable in multivariable logistic regression analyses. From January 2019 to February 2024, a total of 146 patients diagnosed with UA (68) and NSTEMI (78) who underwent CAG were enrolled from the Cardiology Department of the University of Turin, “Città della Salute e della Scienza” Hospital. Inclusion criteria were: age>18 years, provision of written informed consent, UA and NSTEMI diagnosis, and admission for CAG. Patients’ gender and the age as well as their allocation to the specific experimental group are reported in the Tables. Patients diagnosed with STEMI were excluded. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics committee at the “Città della Salute e della Scienza” Hospital in Turin. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the principles of the Helsinki Declaration and all participants provided written informed consent (Clinical Trial number: NCT02195726). Diagnoses of UA, NSTEMI and STEMI were made by cardiologists according to latest ESC guidelines1 and fourth universal definition of myocardial infarction.63 Hs-cTnI, with a cut-off value of 34 ng/l, was used as marker of myocardial injury. Before CAG, blood samples were drawn from all the patients to perform laboratory analysis. For each patient, several variables were evaluated. CAD was defined as an obstructive coronary lesion with a stenosis degree greater than 75%, or greater than 50% with a positive functional test, at coronary angiography (including restenosis of a previous implanted stent). Clinical data from patients were used to stratify patients with or without CAD by independent operators. An additional operator performed the overall analysis.

Method details

EV purification from serum

After blood collection just before CAG, the serum was separated and subjected to precipitation, as previously described.64 Briefly, the precipitation solution consisting of 1mg protamine chloride/ml and 0.2g polyethylene glycol (PEG) in distilled water, was mixed with the sample at a ratio of 1:4 PEG-Protamin/sample and incubated overnight at 4°C. Post incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 1,500×g for 30 min, and the pellet was resuspended in sterile NaCl 0.9% and filtered with 0.22 μm filters to remove large vesicles. In preliminary experiments, Sephadex-100 column was used to remove lipoprotein contamination and no differences in their biological activity was detected. After separation, sEVs were stored at -80°C for further evaluation.

Cell cultures

Human Microvascular Endothelial Cells (HMEC) were sourced from the American Type Culture Collection and cultured in MCDB131 Medium, supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS), 10 ng/ml epidermal growth factor, 1 μg/ml hydrocortisone, 1% L-glutamine, and 1% streptomycin/penicillin at 37°C and 5% CO2.65 U937 cells were sourced the American Type Culture Collection and cultured in RPMI Medium supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% L-glutamine), 1% streptomycin/penicillin. at 37°C and 5% CO2. Both cell lines were tested for mycoplasma contamination.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

TEM was performed as described previously.64 TEM was performed by loading EVs resuspended in PBS on 200-mesh nickel formvar carbon-coated grids (Electron Microscopy Science, Hatfield, PA, USA) and left to adhere for 20 min. The grids were then fixed with 2.5% glutaraldehyde/2% sucrose solution, washed with distilled water, and then negatively stained with NanoVan (Nanoprobes, Yaphank). The sEVs were observed with a JEM 1400 electron microscope (Jeol, Tokyo, Japan).

Western blot analysis

sEVs were lysed and quantified by the Bradford method. Equal amounts of total protein extracts (30μg protein/sample) were separated by SDS-PAGE and electro-transferred to nitrocellulose membrane. Anti-CD63, anti-CD81, and anti-CD29 antibodies served as exosomal markers, while anti-GM130 served as a negative EV marker. Anti-ApoB and anti-HADHB antibodies were also used to validate the EV protein profile obtained via mass spectrometry. Post primary antibody labelling, the membrane was labelled with appropriate HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies. Proteins were detected with Clarity Western ECL substrate using the ChemiDoc Touch imaging system and quantified by densitometry using Imagelab analytic software. Original blots are included in the supplementary items as Data S1.

Flow cytometry (FACS) analysis

EV bulk population flow cytometry analysis was performed using the MACSPlex Exosome Kit (human, Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany), following the manufacturer’s protocol66 with CytoFLEX® Flow Cytometer. Single EV flow cytometric analysis was performed with CellStream system. EVs were detected using PE- and FITC-conjugated antibodies directed to CD81, CD9, Caveolin-3, Troponin. FITC and PE mouse non-immune Isotypic IgG served as controls. For PLTP, we used the unlabelled antibody anti-PLTP and the FITC-conjugated anti-Rabbit IgG (as secondary antibody. All antibodies were used according to the manufacturer's instructions provided in the datasheets.

Endothelial marker activation and co-culture system for endothelium-monocyte adhesion analysis

Human microvascular endothelial cells (HMECs; 1×105 cells/well) were seeded in 12-well plates and serum-starved for 16 hours. Cells were subsequently treated with sEVs (sEVs; 8 × 104 EVs/cell) derived from either CAD or NO CAD patients. To investigate the role of miR-130-3p, cells were transfected with a miR-130-3p inhibitor or mimic, along with their respective scrambled controls, at a final concentration of 20 pM, according with the manufacturer’s protocol.19 Transfections were carried out using Lipofectamine 2000 in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions. After 24 hours of treatment, cells were harvested and stained with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated anti-human CD54 antibody according to the manufacturer’s protocol. A FITC-conjugated mouse non-immune isotypic IgG served as staining control. Flow cytometric analysis was performed using a CellStream cytometer.

For co-culture experiments HMEC cells (7×104 cells/well) were stained with the green fluorescent membrane dye PKH67 and seeded in 24-well plates pre-coated with Matrigel (3 mg/ml). Following tube formation, cells were serum-starved and subsequently treated with sEVs (8×104 particles/cell) isolated from either CAD or NO CAD patients. To assess the specific role of miR-130a-3p, HMECs were transfected with either a miR-130a-3p inhibitor or mimic (as above), along with their respective scrambled controls, at a final concentration of 20 pM, according to previous data.19 Transfections were performed using Lipofectamine 2000. After 24 hours of treatment, U937 monocytes were labelled with the red fluorescent membrane dye PKH26 and added to the HMEC cultures at a 1:1 cell ratio. After a 2-hour incubation, non-adherent monocytes were gently washed off, and the remaining cells were analysed by fluorescence microscopy using the Leica THUNDER Imaging System.

Real-time PCR

Total RNA from sEVs was isolated using miRNeasy® Mini Kit and retro-transcribed for cDNA synthesis using miScript II RT Kit. The sEV expression of miR-126-3p, miR-130, miR-208a, miR-221-5p, miR-23a, miR-155-5p, miR-133 and miR-134 was detected using miScript SYBR® Green PCR kit with QuantStudio12KFlex (Applied Biosystem), according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. β-actin miScript PCR control served as a normalizer for miRNA expression.

Proteomic analysis

sEV samples from patients diagnosed as UA (10 CAD and 10 NO CAD) and NSTEMI (8 CAD and 8 NO CAD) were analysed by proteomics. Both biological and analytical replicates (n=3) were included for the analysis. sEV total protein content was assessed through Bradford assay and 10 μg of each sEV sample was digested using the In-Solution Tryptic Digestion and Guanidination Kit following the manufacturer’s protocol as in.67 Briefly, protein samples were incubated in 25 mM ammonium bicarbonate buffer in presence of 5 mM dithiothreitol (DTT) at 95°C for 5 minutes to obtain protein denaturation and cysteine reduction. Then cysteine residues were alkylated by adding 10 mM iodoacetamide to samples and incubating for 20 minutes in the dark at room temperature. Finally, proteins were subjected to tryptic digestion with 3 ng/μL Trypsin overnight at 37°C. The obtained peptide mixtures were analysed on an electrospray (ESI) Orbitrap Q-Exactive Mass Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific) coupled with a nanoUltimate300 UHPLC system (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Peptide separation was performed on a capillary EASY-Spray PepMap column 0.075mmÅ∼ 50 mm, 2μm, (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using aqueous 0.1% formic acid (A) and acetonitrile (ACN) containing 0.1% formic acid (B) as mobile phases and a linear gradient from 3 to 40% of B in 60 minutes and a 300 nL·min−1 flow rate. Mass spectra were acquired over an m/z range from 375 to 1500. MS and MS/MS data were analysed by Proteome Discoverer software and protein identifications were performed using the Homo sapiens reference proteome downloaded from UniProt Knowledgebase (UniProtKB; http://www.uniprot.org). A permutation-based False Discovery Rate (FDR) of 5% was applied to correct for multiple hypothesis testing. For relative quantification and abundance comparison among different samples, proteomic analyses were performed in duplicate on different sEV preparations, and each sample was run in duplicate in LC-MS/MS. Only the proteins identified in all the analyses were considered. Putative and cDNA derivative identifications were excluded. Statistical analyses of quantitative data were performed with Proteome Discoverer software using a two-sided unpaired t-test. One-on-one quantitative analyses were performed to compare samples from patients with UA-CAD and UA-NO CAD, NSTEMI-CAD and NSTEMI-NO CAD, and all CAD and NO CAD. Volcano plots for these comparisons were generated, with the x-axis representing the log2 values of the ratio of the mean area measured for each protein in the NO CAD patient samples to that measured in the CAD patient samples. The y-axis represented the -Log10 of the p-value calculated for each protein. PCA was performed by Proteome Discoverer software. Gene ontology analysis of identified non-immunoglobulins proteins was carried out using ShinyGO 0.80 (http://bioinformatics.sdstate.edu/go/) and STRING (https://string-db.org/) software. In both cases, an enrichment cut-off FDR of 5% was set, and only protein pathways comprising more than 10 proteins were considered.

Bioinformatic network-cluster analysis

Predicted and validated targets of miR-130a-3p used for bioinformatic analyses were retrieved from miRTarBase.68 We compared 9 proteins differentially expressed in CAD vs NO CAD regardless of their diagnosis and their interactors, with the validated targets of miR-130a-3p. The list of interaction proteins was retrieved from FunRich 3.1.3. The “Reactome 2022” pathways were obtained by testing shared proteins using Enrichr (retrieved in November 2024).69 The q-value was calculated with adjustment for multiple tests by Benjamini-Hochberg method. The pathway network and cluster analysis were designed with Gephi 0.9.2, showing significantly enriched pathways (adjusted p value<0.05 with at least 2 proteins per pathway). The nodes represent enriched pathways, and the node size is proportional to–log (q value) of each pathway. Nodes with no connections were excluded. Connection thickness is proportional to the number of overlapping genes, as calculated by Jaccard Index (Connections with Jaccard Index<0.4 were excluded). Modularity assessment was adopted for the development of unbiased clustering of pathways. For clarity, the names of the main pathways of each cluster are summarized in the Figure 5C.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Categorical variables are expressed as number and percentage. Continuous variables with normal distribution are expressed as mean±standard deviation (SD) whereas continuous variables without normal distribution as medians and interquartile range (IQR). Kolmogorov-Smirnoff and Shapiro-Wilk tests were used to evaluate Gaussian distribution. The following tests were used to evaluate differences among variables: student t-test for parametric continuous variables, Mann-Whitney U test for non-parametric continuous variables, Chi-square test for categorical variables and Fisher's Exact Test for 2x2 contingency tables. Binary logistic regression was used to assess the association with CAD, including all variables that showed a significant difference in univariate analysis or were considered of clinical relevance. To address the risk of type I error due to multiple comparisons, a Bonferroni correction was applied to evaluate the association of variables with critical CAD. Specifically, the significance threshold was adjusted to p<0.0125 p < 0.0125p<0.0125, accounting for four planned comparisons. The threshold for statistical significance was set at p<0.05. All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics 28.0.0.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA), and Graph Pad Prism version 8.2.1 (Graph Pad Software, San Diego, CA, USA).
