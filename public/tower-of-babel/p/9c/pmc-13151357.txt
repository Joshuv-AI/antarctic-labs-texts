Periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics combined with i-PRF for the treatment of gingival recession: a case report with 5-year follow-up
Authors: Haihong Wang, Juanjuan Zong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Orthodontic treatment can significantly impact periodontium, particularly in dentitions with a thin periodontal phenotype. Under such vulnerable anatomical conditions, orthodontic tooth movement may lead to iatrogenic complications, including the development or exacerbation of bony dehiscences and fenestrations, which can manifest as loss of periodontal support and gingival recession.Periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics offers the potential to reduce overall orthodontic treatment duration while enhancing hard tissue outcomes in the dentition. This case report presents the outcomes of Periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics combined with injectable platelet-rich fibrin in an adult patient with dental crowding, gingival recession, and severe alveolar bone loss. At the five-year follow-up, the patient demonstrated substantial improvements in both soft and hard tissue parameters.

Introduction

Patients with dental crowding often present with thin labial alveolar bone, a condition that increases susceptibility to iatrogenic complications during orthodontic tooth movement [1].In such anatomically vulnerable sites, tooth displacement may induce or exacerbate bony fenestrations or dehiscences, ultimately leading to loss of periodontal support and gingival recession [2].These sequelae not only compromise periodontal health but also pose significant challenges to safe and effective orthodontic treatment. In contrast to conventional corticotomy-assisted orthodontic therapy (CAOT), periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontic（PAOO） incorporates the application of bone grafting materials during surgical intervention [3]. This approach allows for selective augmentation of alveolar bone thickness and height in targeted regions. By enhancing the bony architecture, PAOO mitigates the risks of fenestration, dehiscence, and gingival recession associated with orthodontic movement, thereby promoting more stable and favorable periodontal outcomes.

PRF is characterized by a high concentration of platelets, leukocytes, and cytokines [4], such as transforming growth factor β1 and vascular endothelial growth factor, and exhibits osteoinductive, anti-inflammatory, anti-infective, and pain-inhibiting properties. By altering the centrifugation speed and time, injectable PRF (i-PRF), a liquid form of PRF, can be further obtained. Compared to other PRF formulations, i-PRF exhibits both higher and more uniformly distributed concentrations of platelets and leukocytes, which facilitate a sustained and controlled release of growth factors [5].I-PRF can be effectively combined with xenogeneic bone grafts, and its regenerative potential in oral tissues has resulted in its widespread clinical application in periodontology and implantology. Limited research is available on the incorporation of i-PRF in PAOO to achieve improved surgical outcomes and healing, and its application in patients with gingival recession has not been reported.

Case report

An 18-year-old female patient presented to the Department of Orthodontics at the Affiliated Stomatological Hospital of Nanchang University seeking treatment for dental crowding and aesthetic concerns. Physical examination: Slight facial asymmetry was observed, with the maxillary midline deviated 1.5 mm to the left, presenting a concave profile. Intraoral examination revealed moderate oral hygiene, severe Class III deep overbite, Angle Class II molar relationship, Class II crowding in the mandibular dentition, and severe gingival recession with root exposure in the lower anterior region (Fig. 1). Cone-beam computed tomography showed insufficient alveolar bone cover on the facial side of anterior incisors (Fig. 2). Cephalometric summary indicated an Angle Class II, skeletal Class III malocclusion, with SNA angle at 77.3, SNB angle at 77.7, and ANB angle at -0.4 (Fig. 3). The patient reported no relevant medical or family history, and previous clinical and radiographic examinations revealed no temporomandibular joint disorders.

Following interdisciplinary consultation between the orthodontic and periodontal teams, the treatment plan for this patient was determined as follows: initial orthodontic treatment with fixed straight wire appliances to correct the labial inclination of the maxillary anterior teeth, followed by periodontal intervention with PAOO surgery on the mandibular anterior teeth to improve the soft and hard tissue conditions, and finally, alignment and leveling of the dentition to relieve crowding, while further adjusting overbite, overjet, and molar relationships. In addition to the surgical intervention, periodontal management included oral hygiene instruction, professional mechanical plaque removal, and regular supportive periodontal maintenance. All treatment procedures were performed after obtaining the patient’s written informed consent.

Periodontally accelerated osteogenic orthodontics

Periodontal surgery was scheduled a year following the maxillary dentition’s orthodontic therapy. Pre-operative periodontal examination revealed satisfactory oral hygiene, mild erythema and edema of the buccal gingiva in the region of teeth 43–33, along with marked gingival recession.The gingival biotype was classified as thick, and the deep overbite had shown improvement compared to the initial presentation (Fig. 4). Pre-operative CBCT, similar to the pre-treatment CBCT, showed obvious buccal bone defects in the mandibular anterior region (Fig. 5).

We employed a modified “Pocket Technique” [6] based on the protocol described by Ma et al. elevating a rectangular full-thickness mucoperiosteal flap from teeth 43 to 33. A composite graft consisting of i-PRF mixed with xenogeneic bone material (Bio-Oss®) was applied to the alveolar region spanning teeth 33 to 43. The i-PRF and Bio-Oss particles were thoroughly blended for approximately ten minutes until a cohesive, gel-like consistency was achieved. This composite demonstrated enhanced plasticity compared with the dry granular graft material alone, improving intraoperative handling and contributing to graft stability. Wound closure was accomplished using interrupted and sling sutures (Fig. 6).

At the two-week postoperative follow-up for suture removal (Fig. 7A), separation between the buccal and lingual gingival tissues was noted, accompanied by partial exposure of the collagen membrane. Nevertheless, no signs of infection were observed throughout the postoperative period. Over time, the lingual gingiva progressively migrated and fused with the buccal gingiva, leading to complete wound closure. Concurrently, gingival healing progressed favorably, and the initially compromised gingival architecture gradually restored to a clinically healthy state (Fig. 7B-E). Upon completion of orthodontic treatment at the two-year postoperative follow-up, successful gingival morphological remodeling was achieved, and CBCT demonstrated increases in both the height and thickness of the buccal alveolar bone (Figs.7F, 8). At the five-year postoperative evaluation (Figs. 7 H, 9), both soft and hard tissue outcomes remained stable and well-maintained.

Discussion

Patients with dental crowding often present with thinner labial and buccal alveolar bone. In the presence of reduced bone volume, the availability of osteoblasts is limited, compromising the regenerative capacity of the labial alveolar bone. This can lead to progressive bone resorption, resulting in diminished alveolar height and an increased risk of fenestration or dehiscence defects [7, 8] Bone grafting materials, as an integral component of PAOO treatment, enhance both the thickness and vertical dimension of the alveolar bone in the surgical site. Osteoconductive or osteoinductive materials combined with resorbable collagen membranes promote the regeneration of adjacent soft and hard tissues, thereby transforming thin, vulnerable bone architecture into a more substantial and resilient form. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated that bone grafting materials can be utilized to correct existing bone dehiscence and gingival recession, while also mitigating the risk of iatrogenic defects during orthodontic tooth movement [9].

The most significant challenge in this case was the severe labial gingival recession in the mandibular anterior region, accompanied by markedly reduced alveolar bone height and a thin alveolar bone plate. Continued labial movement of the mandibular anterior teeth under these conditions would risk serious complications, including further attachment loss and even tooth dropout.Therefore, augmenting alveolar bone width and height via PAOO, particularly the labial/buccal bone width, proves especially beneficial in such circumstances. In our case, at 2 weeks postoperatively, we encountered a concerning issue of collagen membrane and bone graft material exposure (Fig. 7A). Although infection did not occur in the late stage and healing gradually progressed, this complication prompted considerable reflection on our part. We identified several contributing factors. First, the significant preoperative soft tissue deficiency (gingival recession) made complete coverage of the grafted surgical site difficult. Second, the relatively large volume of bone graft placed and insufficient intraoperative tension release may have resulted in excessive tension at the time of suturing, a consequence of overambitious surgical planning. Third, only interrupted and suspension sutures were employed without flap modification, which likely led to postoperative suture dehiscence and exposure. Finally, although patients were instructed to apply intermittent ice packs to the surgical site, take oral nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs, and rinse with compound chlorhexidine solution postoperatively, no follow-up was conducted to confirm patient compliance with these instructions.Currently, the modified incision designs for PAOO include the papilla preservation incision [10], the horizontal extended incision [11], and the pocket technique [6]. We believe that the “pocket technique” modified incision is most appropriate for this patient. What is required intraoperatively is more meticulous and thorough flap tension reduction, and the suturing technique at closure can draw upon the further modifications proposed by Ma [12]: positioning the collagen membrane slightly coronal to the gingival margin, applying interrupted relaxing sutures at the gingival margin to better protect the cervical bone graft and reduce displacement caused by gingival margin recession. In addition, the suturing technique was further refined so that the sutures pass not only through the buccal and lingual interdental papillae but also through the coronal border of the membrane, forming a stable circumferential support to ensure complete coverage of the graft. By 3 weeks post-surgery, we observed that the lingual gingiva appeared to slowly migrate toward the buccal side (Fig. 7 B).Eventually, it fused with the buccal gingival flap, closing the surgical site. With ongoing orthodontic treatment, the soft tissue morphology gradually recovered from an initially unaesthetic state to a normal appearance. We hypothesize that the creeping of the lingual gingiva and its fusion with the buccal side may be attributed to the normal height of the interdental alveolar bone, which supports the lingual gingiva in migrating toward the interproximal area and fusing with the descending buccal gingival flap, ultimately sealing the incision. This process may be attributed to the blood supply provided by the lingual alveolar bone, ensuring the survival of both soft and hard tissues. At the 1-year and 2-year follow-up visits, the gingiva in the surgical area exhibited redness and swelling (Fig. 7 E, F), attributable to the patient’s occasional neglect of oral hygiene and plaque control during orthodontic treatment. In response, we provided face-to-face instruction on toothbrushing technique, dental floss and water flosser use, supplemented by plaque-disclosing agents, and advised the patient to return for follow-up every 3 months along with regular periodontal maintenance therapy (Fig. 7 F shows after supragingival scaling). The importance of oral hygiene and plaque control was also strongly emphasized. At the 3-year and 5-year postoperative follow-up, the patient demonstrated satisfactory oral hygiene control; however, redness and swelling persisted in the marginal gingiva of the surgical area (Fig. 7 G, H). Upon reviewing the initial and follow-up photographs, we noted that the sites of persistent inflammation corresponded to those of the original gingival recession. We consider this condition to be the result of multiple contributing factors: the pre-existing soft tissue morphology unfavorable for plaque control, which predisposed the area to plaque retention and local irritation; the compromised gingival condition observed 2 weeks postoperatively; and the combined effects of buccal-lingual gingival separation and surgical trauma itself.In this regard, we should also reflect on the technical details of the surgical procedure, such as whether excessive force was applied during gingival flap fractionation. Furthermore, we will continue to monitor closely the postoperative changes in gingival status.

The etiology of buccal bone loss on lower incisors is multifaceted, involving a combination of inherent anatomical factors, deep bite [13], Chronic inflammatory periodontal disease, potential influence of orthodontic treatment, Low-level and long-lasting trauma, Occlusal trauma [14–16].While orthodontic treatment alone is rarely considered a primary cause of gingival recession, it can lead to situations that act as predisposing factors, especially when the buccal bone plate is very thin or presents with dehiscence [16, 17].Naturally occurring alveolar bone dehiscence and fenestration are common findings, particularly in the anterior region of Class III malocclusions [14].The presence of dehiscence or fenestration significantly increases the chances of gingival recession [18].In this patient, the labial gingival recession of the mandibular anterior teeth was attributed to pre-existing alveolar bone loss prior to treatment, as well as to the pretreatment malocclusion characterized by deep overbite and dental crowding (Fig. 2 demonstrates the deep bite).Occlusal trauma can induce vertical bone loss, leading to the formation of a V-shaped defect in the bone contour (dehiscence) over the affected root [15], nevertheless, similar patterns of alveolar bone loss and gingival recession have also been observed in untreated patients with severe crowding (> 5 mm), suggesting that malocclusion itself may predispose to unfavorable periodontal conditions [19].Consequently, prior to performing PAOO, a one-year orthodontic treatment was conducted to labially incline the upper anterior teeth, thereby improving the deep overbite relationship.For patients with anterior deep bite, mandibular anterior tooth intrusion represents a critical step in the management of excessive deep overbite [20].Anterior teeth intrusion refers to the process of pressing the teeth into the alveolar bone to reduce their exposure in the oral cavity.In clinical practice, temporary anchorage devices are predominantly used for this purpose; however, moderate external root resorption is frequently observed following their placement [21].Zadeh et al. [22]described the successful orthodontic treatment of an adult female presenting with borderline Class II Division 1 malocclusion, excessive protrusion, and deep bite using a combination of TADs and PAOO, which notably reduced treatment duration and achieved favorable alveolar bone expansion.Furthermore, Brugnami et al. [19]highlighted the benefits of combining ODO with temporary anchorage devices in cases requiring complex or en masse movements, suggesting that the synergistic effect of enhanced bone turnover and skeletal anchorage may broaden the scope of movements that can be predictably achieved without compromising periodontal support.

In the present study, i‑PRF was integrated with PAOO to enhance both hard and soft‑tissue regeneration. PRF is a three-dimensional fibrin scaffold obtained via centrifugation of autologous blood, which entraps platelets, leukocytes, and cytokines—including transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β1) and vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF).By adjusting centrifugation parameters, i-PRF can be prepared. Compared to other forms of PRF, i‑PRF demonstrates a higher and more homogeneous concentration of platelets and leukocytes, allowing a sustained and controlled release of growth factors. The use of i-PRF in bone grafting offers several advantages: (1) its fibrin network provides mechanical support, stabilizing and protecting the graft mixture; (2) the scaffold facilitates cellular migration, particularly of endothelial cells essential for angiogenesis, tissue formation, and graft integration; (3) the gradual degradation of fibrin enables prolonged release of platelet‑derived cytokines, which promotes healing; and (4) the high content of leukocytes and cytokines within i-PRF contributes to the modulation of inflammation and resistance to infection within the graft site [23–25].Xu et al. [26] also reported that in patients with thin gingival phenotype with localized gingival recession, the incorporation of PRF in PAOO resulted in mild postoperative reactions and allowed orthodontic treatment to be completed within one month. At the 6-month follow-up, increases in gingival height at the recession sites and notable gains in alveolar bone vertical dimension were documented. Muñoz et al. [27]demonstrated that the combined use of PRF in PAOO surgery reduced postoperative pain, inflammation, and infection, thereby lowering the requirement for analgesics and anti-inflammatory medications.Yashwant et al. [28]and the aforementioned studies consistently demonstrated that the use of PRF during PAOO procedures was associated with accelerated early wound healing.Shawky et al. [29]reported that in bone reconstruction surgery for patients with maxillary alveolar clefts, the combination of PRF with autologous bone grafting promoted significantly greater new bone formation compared to autologous bone grafting alone.Studies cited above consistently indicate that combining PRF with PAOO can reduce postoperative infection and achieve faster healing times, however, whether this combination yields greater new bone formation and sustained long-term outcomes warrants further investigation.In our case, the use of i-PRF may have contributed in several ways: stabilizing the bone graft material during the initial stage (i-PRF mixed with Bio-Oss bone granules forms a gel-like consistency), mitigating the risk of early exposure-related infection, and promoting wound healing.

PAOO has been a promising strategy for orthodontic treatment recently, the treatment appears particularly beneficial for patients with thin gingival biotype or more pronounced bone deficiencies [10].PAOO has been well documented to increase alveolar bone thickness [30, 31], moreover, surgical procedures for bone and gingiva augmentation frequently exhibit suboptimal long-term efficacy.Brugnami et al. [19]conducted a retrospective evaluation of long-term osteogenic stability over a mean follow-up period of 7 years, along with histological assessment of new bone formation and graft material integration following PAOO. Histologic examination showed newly formed lamellar and reticular bone. Bone marrow showed no inflammatory infiltration, and bone particles were still detectable but incorporated in the newly created bone.Most existing literature has evaluated the outcomes of alveolar bone augmentation using cone-beam computed tomography (CBCT), leaving the quality of the newly formed alveolar bone unclear.Currently, only a very limited number of studies have provided histological observations suggesting the possibility of new bone formation. Larger-scale studies and more comprehensive histological assessments are needed to validate these “osteogenic” findings.

Conclusion

For patients with high-risk anatomical features, including thin periodontal phenotype, gingival recession, or severe alveolar bone defects, PAOO may hold promise in improving periodontal tissue conditions and mitigating the potential adverse effects of orthodontic tooth movement, such as gingival recession and alveolar bone defects.
