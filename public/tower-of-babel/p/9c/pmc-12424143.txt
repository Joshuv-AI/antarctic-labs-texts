Global, regional and national epidemiology of acute myeloid leukemia (1990–2021): a statistical analysis of incidence, mortality, and DALYs
Authors: Binghuo Wu, Fei Song, Xue Han, Minyi Zhao, Meijia Huang, Wenwen Zhang, Tian Tan, Yanju Gong, Chenhui Cao, Songtao Cheng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study provides a comprehensive analysis of the global, regional, and national epidemiology of acute myeloid leukemia (AML) from 1990 to 2021, focusing on incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted life years (DALYs).MethodsUsing data from the Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study, we investigated the trends and patterns of AML across 204 countries and territories during 1990 to 2021. All statistical analyses and data visualizations were performed using R (version 4.4.2) and JD_GBDR (V2.36).ResultsThe global incidence of AML increased by 82.25% from 79.37 × 10³ cases in 1990 to 144.65 × 10³ cases in 2021, with significant variations across regions. AML-related deaths increased by 73.77% globally, from 74.92 × 10³ in 1990 to 130.19 × 10³ in 2021. DALYs rose by 23.70%, from 3342.91 × 10³ in 1990 to 4135.06 × 10³ in 2021.ConclusionsHigh SDI regions face higher incidence and mortality due to advanced diagnostics and aging, while low-middle SDI regions show rapid increases likely due to urbanization and lifestyle changes. Addressing these disparities requires improved healthcare infrastructure, early detection, and targeted interventions, particularly in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs).Keywords: Acute myeloid leukemia, global burden of disease study, incidence, mortality, disability-adjusted life years

Introduction

Acute myeloid leukemia (AML) is a highly complex and aggressive malignancy of the hematopoietic system [1]. It is characterized by the uncontrolled proliferation and impaired differentiation of myeloid progenitor cells [2]. AML predominantly affects adults and is associated with high mortality rates, making it one of the most difficult hematologic cancers to manage [3–5]. Over the past several decades, the global burden of AML has been on the rise, driven largely by an aging population and advancements in diagnostic techniques [6,7]. These trends underscore the pressing need for comprehensive epidemiological studies to inform healthcare strategies and shape public health policies. As the number of AML cases continues to increase worldwide, particularly among older individuals [8,9], it is essential to gain a deeper understanding of the primary risk factors and regional differences that contribute to the disease burden.

The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study serves as a crucial resource for evaluating the global impact of diseases such as AML [10,11]. According to the GBD data, AML incidence shows considerable variation across different regions [12,13], with higher rates reported in developed countries like the United States, Canada, and many European nations. This is primarily due to advanced healthcare infrastructure, more sophisticated diagnostic technologies, and a higher proportion of elderly populations in these regions. Conversely, while developing countries in Africa and Southeast Asia typically exhibit lower overall AML incidence, recent research suggests a steady increase in cases [14], driven by factors such as urbanization and lifestyle changes.

Age is a major risk factor for AML, with incidence rates rising notably in individuals over the age of 60 [15,16]. Research indicates that older adults, especially men, are at a higher risk of developing AML. As the global population continues to age, this demographic shift has significantly contributed to the growing burden of AML worldwide. Disability-adjusted life years (DALYs), which combine years of life lost (YLL) and years lived with disability (YLD), offer a comprehensive view of the impact of AML [12,17]. The DALYs rates for AML reflect a substantial burden, not only due to high mortality but also due to the long-term complications faced by survivors. Analyzing trends in DALYs, along with incidence and mortality, enables a more detailed understanding of the disease’s impact, guiding more informed public health strategies and resource distribution. While previous studies have explored this area [8,9], recent changes in the epidemiological trends of AML warrant further investigation.

This study aims to provide an updated, comprehensive analysis of AML’s epidemiology from 1990 to 2021, focusing on key metrics such as incidence, mortality, and DALYs rates. By analyzing global, regional, and national trends, we seek to identify the major factors influencing these trends, evaluate socioeconomic disparities, and inform better healthcare policy to mitigate AML’s impact globally.

Methods

Data source

The GBD study, spearheaded by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) at the University of Washington, is recognized as one of the most comprehensive and systematic efforts to monitor global health trends. Its primary mission is to evaluate the impact of diseases, injuries, and risk factors on population health worldwide [18]. The GBD framework facilitates cross-national and regional comparisons by using standardized metrics, namely incidence, mortality, and DALYs. DALYs are derived from the combination of YLL due to premature mortality and YLD [19]. The GBD study integrates data from a wide range of sources, including population censuses, household surveys, civil registration and vital statistics systems, disease registries, health care utilization records, air quality monitoring stations, satellite imagery, and disease notification systems. Cause-specific mortality rates and fractions were estimated using the Cause of Death Ensemble modeling framework (CODEm) combined with spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression to account for geographic and temporal variability. To ensure internal consistency, cause-specific death estimates were calibrated to match the all-cause mortality totals derived from the GBD’s demographic and mortality estimation system [20].

For this study, data regarding AML—specifically incidence, mortality, and DALYs—from 1990 to 2021 were retrieved from the GBD Results Tool (https://vizhub.healthdata.org/gbd-results/). The dataset spans 204 countries and territories [21] and was accessed on January 5, 2025. The analysis encompassed variables across different age groups (classified as under 5 years, 5–14 years, 15–49 years, 50–69 years, and above 70 years), sexes, and regions [8].

In addition, this study utilized the Sociodemographic Index (SDI), a composite indicator of development status [17]. SDI scores range from 0 (least developed) to 1 (most developed), incorporating metrics like income level, educational attainment, and fertility rates. Based on these scores, countries are categorized into five tiers: high, high-middle, middle, low-middle, and low SDI. This classification was used to explore how socio-economic differences correlate with the AML burden globally.

Methodology and statistical analysis

In this study, the R software package (version 4.4.2) and JD_GBDR (V2.36, Jingding Medical Technology Co., Ltd.) were used for the drawing of the figures. And the specific methods were reported in the literature [19]. We computed AML-related incidence, mortality, and DALY rates per 100,000 individuals, along with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI), using data provided by the GBD database. To evaluate temporal trends, we applied the Joinpoint regression model, which estimates annual percent change (APC) and its 95% CI for distinct time segments, offering insight into year-by-year fluctuations.

To assess long-term patterns from 1990 to 2021, the estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) and its 95% CI were calculated using a log-linear regression approach [22]. An EAPC with a lower CI limit greater than 0 was interpreted as a significant upward trend, while a value with an upper CI bound below 0 indicated a significant decline. We also employed fitted curve analyses to examine associations between AML burden metrics and SDI levels. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant throughout all analyses.

Results

The incidence and its change trend of AML

Comprehensive data analysis reveals that the global incidence of AML has significantly increased over the past 31 years, from 79.37 × 10³ (95% CI 62.81–99.43) in 1990 to 144.65 × 10³ (95% CI 126.24–164.85) in 2021, marking an 82.25% increase (EAPC = −0.03, 95% CI −0.12 to 0.06) (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure 1A). However, the global epidemiological trend of AML has been dynamic, with the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) initially rising and then falling. The maximum annual percent change (APC) was observed from 1990 to 2003, at 0.509% (95% CI 0.467%–0.551%), with the peak ASIR reaching 1.89 (95% CI 1.61–2.21) per 100,000 people in 2003. In contrast, the minimum APC was seen from 2018 to 2021, at −1.215% (95% CI −1.464% to −0.966%), with the lowest ASIR recorded in 2021 at 1.73 (95% CI 1.51–1.98) per 100,000 people (Supplementary Figure 1B). Males are more affected by AML than females, and this gap is widening, with the male-to-female ratio in ASIR being 1.98:1.62 in 1990 and 1.99:1.53 in 2021 (Table 1).

The incidence of AML in 1990/2021 and temporal trends.

ASIR: age-standardized incidence rate.

When analyzed by the SDI (Table 1 and Supplementary Figure 1A), it is evident that the high SDI region bore the highest AML burden until 2021, with incident cases rising from 27.83 × 10³ in 1990 to 56.65 × 10³ in 2021, and experiencing the most rapid increase over the past 31 years (EAPC = 0.41, 95% CI 0.25–0.56). Concurrently, the low-middle SDI region exhibited the second fastest growth in ASIR, increasing from 1.06 in 1990 to 1.17 in 2021 (EAPC = 0.34). When broken down by geographical zone (Table 1), Western Europe, High-income North America, and East Asia reported the highest number of incident cases (Western Europe: 11.79 × 10³ in 1990 and 24.64 × 10³ in 2021; High-income North America: 11.53 × 10³ in 1990 and 23.68 × 10³ in 2021; East Asia: 15.92 × 10³ in 1990 and 19.16 × 10³ in 2021). Among these, Australasia, Central Europe, and Western Europe showed the most rapid increases in ASIR (EAPC of Australasia: 0.95, 95% CI 0.68–1.22; EAPC of Central Europe: 0.92, 95% CI 0.77–1.07; EAPC of Western Europe: 0.87, 95% CI 0.74–1.01).

At the country or territory level, the USA, China, and India have consistently reported the highest number of AML incident cases. In 1990, these countries had 10.69 × 10³, 15.31 × 10³, and 5.29 × 10³ cases respectively (Supplementary Figure 2A and Supplementary Table 1). By 2021, the number of incident cases had risen to 21.53 × 10³ in the USA, 17.84 × 10³ in China, and 11.04 × 10³ in India (Supplementary Figure 1C and Supplementary Table 2). In 1990, Jordan, Monaco, and Fiji had the highest ASIR, with rates of 5.01, 4.50, and 4.34 per 100,000 people respectively (Supplementary Figure 2B and Supplementary Table 3). By 2021, the highest ASIR were observed in Monaco, Australia, and Fiji, with rates of 6.30, 4.92, and 4.15 per 100,000 people respectively (Supplementary Figure 1D and Supplementary Table 4). Mauritius, Jamaica, and Bulgaria experienced the most rapid increase in ASIR, with EAPC values of 5.12, 2.39, and 2.36 respectively during the period from 1990 to 2021 (Supplementary Table 5).

The death and its change trend of AML

Overall, the global number of AML-related deaths significantly increased from 74.92 × 10³ (95% CI 58.73–94.71) in 1990 to 130.19 × 10³ (95% CI 113.63–149.39) in 2021, marking a 73.77% increase (EAPC = −0.21, 95% CI −0.30 to −0.13) (Table 2 and Supplementary Figure 3A). The highest APC in age-standardized death rate (ASDR) was observed between 1997 and 2003, at 0.464% (95% CI 0.295%–0.633%), with the peak ASDR reaching 1.75 (95% CI 1.48–2.06) per 100,000 people in 2003. In contrast, the lowest APC was seen from 2018 to 2021, at −1.309% (95% CI −1.544% to −1.074%), with the lowest ASDR recorded in 2021 at 1.57 (95% CI 1.37–1.80) per 100,000 people (Supplementary Figure 3B). In both 1990 and 2021, AML-related deaths were higher among males than females (1990: 39.17 × 10³ in males and 35.74 × 10³ in females; 2021: 70.48 × 10³ in males and 59.71 × 10³ in females), mirroring the trend in incident cases (Table 2).

The death of AML in 1990/2021 and temporal trends.

ASDR: age-standardized death rate.

At the regional level, categorized by the SDI (Table 2 and Supplementary Figure 3A), high SDI regions recorded the highest number of AML-related deaths and the highest ASDR in both 1990 (24.62 × 10³ deaths, ASDR 2.33) and 2021 (47.19 × 10³ deaths, ASDR 2.31). Over the same period, the low-middle SDI region experienced the most rapid increase in ASDR, with an EAPC of 0.31 (95% CI 0.26–0.35). When broken down by geographical zone (Table 2), Western Europe, High-income North America, and East Asia also reported the highest number of AML-related deaths (Western Europe: 11.12 × 10³ in 1990 and 22.49 × 10³ in 2021; High-income North America: 9.68 × 10³ in 1990 and 18.37 × 10³ in 2021; East Asia: 15.43 × 10³ in 1990 and 16.51 × 10³ in 2021). Among these, Western Europe, Central Europe, and Andean Latin America saw the most rapid increases in ASDR, with EAPC values of 0.70 (95% CI 0.59–0.81), 0.69 (95% CI 0.54–0.84), and 0.66 (95% CI 0.53–0.80) respectively.

At the country or territory level, the USA, China, and India consistently had the highest number of AML-related deaths. In 1990, these countries reported 8.95 × 10³, 14.85 × 10³, and 5.25 × 10³ deaths respectively (Supplementary Figure 4A and Supplementary Table 6). By 2021, the number of deaths had increased to 16.65 × 10³ in the USA, 15.31 × 10³ in China, and 10.98 × 10³ in India (Supplementary Figure 3C and Supplementary Table 7). In 1990, Jordan, Fiji, and Monaco had the highest ASDR, with rates of 5.05, 4.48, and 4.08 per 100,000 people respectively (Supplementary Figure 4B and Supplementary Table 8). By 2021, the highest ASDR were observed in Monaco, Fiji, and Afghanistan, with rates of 5.34, 4.27, and 3.94 per 100,000 people respectively (Supplementary Figure 3D and Supplementary Table 9). Mauritius, Georgia, and Jamaica experienced the most rapid increase in ASDR, with EAPC values of 5.15, 2.40, and 2.35 respectively over the period from 1990 to 2021 (Supplementary Table 10).

Besides, we estimated the case fatality rate (CFR) by dividing the total number of AML-related deaths by the number of incident cases for each year from 1990 to 2021. The global CFR remained approximately 90% throughout the period (Supplementary Table 11), showing limited improvement despite advances in diagnosis and treatment.

The DALYs and its change trend of AML

Globally, DALYs attributable to AML increased from 3342.91 × 10³ (95% CI 2394.17–4709.12) in 1990 to 4135.06 × 10³ (95% CI 3446.49–4895.24) in 2021, marking a 23.70% increase (Table 3 and Supplementary Figure 5A). Contrary to the trends observed for incidence and mortality, the age-standardized DALYs rate showed a general downward trend over the years, decreasing from 65.55 (95% CI 48.34–88.77) in 1990 to 50.79 (95% CI 42.16–60.37) in 2021 (EAPC = −0.84, 95% CI −0.89 to −0.78) (Table 3 and Supplementary Figure 5B). In both 1990 and 2021, AML-related DALYs were higher among males than females (1990: males 1778.20 × 10³, EAPC = −0.77, 95% CI −0.82 to −0.72; females 1564.71 × 10³, EAPC = −0.90, 95% CI −0.97 to −0.84; 2021: males 2212.30 × 10³, females 1922.76 × 10³), mirroring the pattern seen in incident cases and death cases (Table 3).

At the regional level, categorized by the SDI (Table 3 and Supplementary Figure 5A), the middle SDI region reported the highest DALYs in both 1990 (1079.81 × 10³, 95% CI 697.19–1664.66) and 2021 (1194.87 × 10³, 95% CI 950.40–1478.13). Over the same period, the high-middle SDI region experienced the most rapid decrease in age-standardized DALYs rate, with an EAPC of −1.59 (95% CI −1.70 to −1.48). When broken down by geographical zone (Table 3), East Asia, South Asia, and Southeast Asia had the highest DALYs (East Asia: 880.70 × 10³ in 1990 and 586.47 × 10³ in 2021; South Asia: 396.57 × 10³ in 1990 and 619.34 × 10³ in 2021; Southeast Asia: 343.60 × 10³ in 1990 and 537.64 × 10³ in 2021). Among these, East Asia, Eastern Europe, and High-income Asia Pacific saw the most rapid decreases in age-standardized DALYs rate, with EAPC values of −2.68 (95% CI −2.94 to −2.43), −1.36 (95% CI −1.51 to −1.21), and −1.32 (95% CI −1.46 to −1.18) respectively.

At the country or territory level, China, India, and the USA reported the highest DALYs. In 1990, these countries had 851.95 × 10³, 283.63 × 10³, and 246.54 × 10³ DALYs respectively (Supplementary Figure 6A and Supplementary Table 12). By 2021, the number of DALYs had decreased to 548.56 × 10³ in China, 439.74 × 10³ in India, and 366.03 × 10³ in the USA (Supplementary Figure 5C and Supplementary Table 13). In 1990, Jordan, Fiji, and Türkiye had the highest age-standardized DALYs rates, with 185.22, 173.73, and 158.41 per 100,000 people respectively (Supplementary Figure 6B and Supplementary Table 14). By 2021, the highest rates were observed in Tokelau, Niue, and Fiji, with 204.88, 169.15, and 167.10 per 100,000 people respectively (Supplementary Figure 5D and Supplementary Table 15). Ukraine, China, and the Northern Mariana Islands experienced the most rapid decrease in age-standardized DALYs rates, with EAPC values of −3.01, −2.81, and −2.68 respectively over the period from 1990 to 2021 (Supplementary Table 16).

The correlation between SDI and AML’s incidence, mortality and DALYs

We initially calculated the correlation coefficient between the SDI and the EAPC values for the ASIR, ASDR, and age-standardized DALYs rate across 204 countries or territories. The findings indicated that there was no significant correlation between the SDI and the EAPC values for the ASIR (Figure 1A) or the ASDR (Figure 1B). However, a significant negative correlation was observed between the SDI and the EAPC values for the age-standardized DALYs rate (Figure 1C). Additionally, we examined the correlation between the SDI and the ASIR, ASDR, and age-standardized DALYs rate in 21 geographical zones. The results demonstrated that the ASIR (Figure 1D), ASDR (Figure 1E), and age-standardized DALYs rate (Figure 1F) were all significantly positively correlated with the SDI.

The AML’s incidence and age structure

We analyzed the incidence of AML and its rates across five distinct age groups: under 5 years, 5–14 years, 15–49 years, 50–69 years, and over 70 years, both globally and within regions categorized by the SDI. The analysis revealed that the majority of AML cases occurred in individuals aged over 50, for both sexes (Figure 2A). Furthermore, in all regions, the highest incidence rates were observed in the population aged over 70, followed by those in the 50–69 years age group (Figure 2B).

Discussion

The findings of this study highlight significant disparities in the burden of AML worldwide. Over the past three decades, the incidence of AML has generally increased, with developed countries reporting higher incidence rates due to better healthcare infrastructure and higher elderly populations [23,24]. The ASIR in regions like North America and Europe are significantly higher than in other parts of the world [25,26]. The elderly population in these regions faces higher risks due to age-associated factors. Meanwhile, low- and middle-income countries (LMICs) are also experiencing an increase in the incidence of AML. Although the absolute numbers remain lower in these regions, the EAPC in these regions, particularly in parts of Asia and Latin America, suggests an alarming trend, with some regions reporting a faster rise than others. It should be noted that access to diagnostic services, such as bone marrow aspiration and flow cytometry, varies significantly across countries and over time. In low-income regions, underdiagnosis in older patients may contribute to the lower incidence rates observed, especially among those aged >70.

Healthcare disparities continue to be a major obstacle in managing AML [27,28], especially in LMICs where access to advanced treatments, such as chemotherapy and targeted therapies, remains limited [29,30]. While survival rates for AML have improved in developed countries due to advancements in stem cell transplantation and targeted therapy [31–34], outcomes remain poor in resource-poor settings. In these regions, the lack of comprehensive healthcare systems, delays in diagnosis, and the absence of advanced treatments result in poor prognoses for AML patients. The rise in secondary AML, often a result of prior cancer treatments such as chemotherapy or radiation, further complicates outcomes, particularly in older adults [35,36]. AML survivors often face long-term complications, which contribute significantly to DALYs rates and underscores the need for long-term follow-up care and rehabilitative interventions. Although treatment data (e.g. chemotherapy and palliative care) are not available in the GBD database, it is plausible that access to AML therapy varies by region and contributes to differences in mortality and DALYs.

This study has several limitations. First, the GBD data are based on modeled estimates, especially in countries with limited cancer registry coverage. Second, patient-level clinical variables such as cytogenetics, treatment regimens, and comorbidities are not available, preventing stratified outcome analysis. Third, case fatality rate estimates are approximate, due to the use of population-level aggregated data rather than patient follow-up data.

Conclusion

The growing global burden of AML requires concerted efforts to improve prevention, early detection, and treatment strategies worldwide. Global health initiatives should focus on improving healthcare access in LMICs, and enhancing early diagnostic capabilities to reduce the incidence and mortality associated with AML. Collaborative efforts across countries, sectors, and scientific disciplines will be crucial in addressing the rising global burden of AML.
