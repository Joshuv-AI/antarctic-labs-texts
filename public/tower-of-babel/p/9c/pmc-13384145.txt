Changes in the indications for and outcomes of gastrostomy tube placement: an analysis of serial, prospective multi-site audits in Northern England
Authors: Alice Rose Weidner, Christopher G Mountford, David Oliver, Chris Wells, Jo Hulley, Emma Johns, Nick Thompson, on behalf of the Northern Nutrition Network
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveGastrostomy tubes placed either endoscopically (percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG)) or radiologically (radiologically inserted gastrostomy (RIG)) are established as standard methods for medium to long-term enteral feeding. There is a paucity of large-scale audit data to identify the main indications, complication rates and outcomes of patients having a PEG or RIG in the UK. The aim of our study was to determine changes in the use of and outcomes from gastrostomy tube placement in a large cohort of patients across the North East of England and North Cumbria.MethodsThe Northern Nutrition Network is a multidisciplinary network involving all hospitals in the North East of England and North Cumbria—an area with a population of 3.2 million in 2024. We conducted six 3-month cross-sectional prospective audits covering all hospitals where gastrostomies are placed between 2004 and 2022.ResultsA total of 889 procedures were performed during the six audit periods. Mean age has decreased from 71 years in 2004 to 63 years in 2022. Estimated incidence of gastrostomy placement in 2022 was 19.2 per 100 000. The most common indication in the first three audits was stroke; however, subsequently, this was replaced by dysphagia due to an ear-nose-throat/upper gastrointestinal malignancy. The use of prophylactic antibiotics increased from 83% to 93% during the 18-year period. 7-day mortality reduced from 2% in 2004 to 1% in 2022. Reduction was demonstrated in 30-day mortality rate from 9% in 2004 to 2.5% in 2022.ConclusionChanges in gastrostomy placement practice have occurred over time, with a change in the most common indication, a trend to use in younger patients, greater use of prophylactic antibiotics and a reduction in 7-day and 30-day mortality. Dementia no longer appears as an indication for the placement of a gastrostomy, in keeping with international guidelines.

Introduction

Percutaneous endoscopic gastrostomy (PEG) tube placement was introduced into clinical practice in 1980 by Ponsky and Gauderer as a safe and effective alternative to open surgical gastrostomy in paediatric patients with dysphagia.1 Radiologically inserted gastrostomy (RIG) was a technique developed later as an alternative method of insertion. Gastrostomy feeding is now established as a method to allow the provision of enteral feeding in patients who have functionally normal gastrointestinal (GI) tracts, but who cannot meet their nutritional needs because of inadequate oral intake.2 Gastrostomy tube feeding is to be considered when nutritional intake is likely to be inadequate for more than 4–6 weeks. Enteral feeding can prevent further weight loss and its associated physiological burden, as well as counteracting the deterioration in a patient’s quality of life as a result of their insufficient nutritional intake.3 4

Today, insertion of gastrostomy feeding tubes is a common therapeutic procedure performed on endoscopy and radiology lists regularly. Gastrostomy insertion is however an invasive procedure, which can be associated with complications and early mortality, thus emphasising the importance of careful consideration of the appropriateness of performing these procedures on an individual basis with appropriate Multi-disciplinary Team (MDT) discussion.5 6

The landscape for gastrostomy insertion is changing and reflects changes in the management of certain subgroups of patients. The 2021 survey of malnutrition and nutritional care in adults performed by the British Association of Parenteral and Enteral Nutrition showed that 40% of patients in hospital in the UK were at risk of malnutrition.7 Randomised controlled trials have shown benefits for gastrostomy feeding in patients following stroke (improved nutritional outcomes, higher likelihood of survival, earlier discharge) and the role of prophylactic pretherapeutic gastrostomy feeding in patients with head and neck tumours.8 9 Positive advances in clinical care, in combination with an ageing population at risk of malnutrition, inevitably broaden the parameters and scope of indications for gastrostomy insertion; however, this comes in combination with uncertainty around long-term outcomes.

A large study performed by the National Confidential Enquiry into Patient Outcome and Death (NCEPOD) did provide a snapshot in time when it reviewed mortality after gastrostomy insertion in a cohort of 16 648 patients between 1 April 2002 and 31 March 2003. This study showed there was an alarming association between gastrostomy insertion and early death, with 2% (14/692) of deaths occurring on the day of the procedure; 18% (126/692) of those dying did so between the first and third day after procedure. A total of 43% (296/692) of deaths occurred within 1 week and a further 26% (183/692) in the second week. Advisors reviewed cases from the NCEPOD study and gave an opinion as to whether gastrostomy insertion was appropriate for the patient and gastrostomy insertion was deemed to be futile in 19% of patients. This study highlighted the need for quality improvement, particularly the importance of decision-making with regard to appropriate patient selection.10

There is a relative lack of prospective data on the use and outcomes of patients having gastrostomy tube placement. The Northern Nutrition Network (NNN) is a multidisciplinary group within the North East of England which involves all acute hospitals and relevant disciplines and meets on a regular basis to promote standards of nutrition. Through this network, serial data relating to recurrent prospective audits about gastrostomy insertion across all sites have been collated, allowing for review and reflection on the quality of care delivered within the region. It is likely that clinical practice within this region reflects practice across the UK. In this study, we aim to outline changes in patient characteristics, indication for gastrostomy and outcome over time across a UK regional nutrition network of hospitals.

Methods

This study is based on a series of cross-sectional prospective audits which comprised review of PEG and RIG insertion during a 3-month audit cycle period across all hospital sites, separated by two to five yearly intervals. The first audit was performed in 2004, with five subsequent audits up to 2022. The hospitals participating in the study included tertiary referral centres, teaching hospitals and district general hospitals. All audits avoided July, August and December in case the workload during these months was affected by summer or public holidays.

In order to maintain consistency, an audit lead in each hospital identified all patients undergoing PEG/RIG insertion and completed the data for each gastrostomy insertion on a predetermined audit proforma. All data submitted during the study periods were then collated by the Newcastle team and presented at the NNN meeting.

The following variables were collected for each patient: hospital site, age, gender, date of procedure, grade of operator (consultant, trainee, nurse), grade of assistant (consultant, trainee, nurse), weight, body mass index (BMI), indication for procedure, conscious sedation dose (midazolam, fentanyl, pethidine, propofol) or use of general anaesthetic, type of tube, administration of prophylactic antibiotics, American Society of Anesthesiologists grade (1—normal, 2—mild systemic disease, 3—severe systemic disease, 4—severe systemic disease/threat to life), comorbidities, length of stay before procedure, length of stay after procedure, and 30-day mortality. Postprocedural complications were recorded, and where death occurred, the number of days after procedure was collected and whether death related to PEG/RIG. Later audits included a review of bloods performed within 1 week prior to PEG/RIG insertion (albumin, C-reactive protein, neutrophil, lymphocyte, haemoglobin, creatinine, prothrombin time). Pearson correlation coefficient was used to measure linear correlation between variables. Where data were missing, this is acknowledged within results and discussion.

As these audits collected standard patient information and no patient identifiable data were transferred outside of individual hospitals, formal ethical approval was not required. The audits were registered locally by the hospitals contributing to the audit, as per their Trust’s audit registration protocol. Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) checklist completion ensured that all relevant reporting requirements of the study were met (online supplemental file 1).

Results

Study population

All acute hospitals in the region took part in the serial audits, totalling 13 hospitals. A total of 889 patient procedures were included in our prospective study (see table 1). Gender split was only available for the 2016 and 2022 studies (2016=56 male/88 female with two undisclosed, 2022=101 male/49 female). The mean age was 66.6 years (see table 1). The current population covered by the North East and North Cumbria Integrated Care Board is 3.2 million (northeastnorthcumbria.nhs.uk), the area served by the NNN. Based on 2022 data collection over a 3-month period and regional population of 3.2 million, this equates to an annual rate of gastrostomy placement of 19.2 per 100 000. There was a negative correlation between the year of audit and the mean age of patients undergoing gastrostomy insertion, as demonstrated by a Pearson correlation coefficient of −0.82. Marginal differences were seen in the mean BMI (23.5 in 2016 vs 24 in 2022) and albumin levels (37 g/L in 2016 vs 40 g/L in 2022).

Indication for gastrostomy

In the first three audit cycles covering from 2004 to 2008, stroke was the most common indication for gastrostomy placement, with mechanical obstruction from ear-nose-throat (ENT) or upper GI cancer being the second most common indication. The great majority of these were ENT tumours. The audit cycles from 2011 to 2022 demonstrated a shift in the indications for gastrostomy insertion, with mechanical obstruction from ENT or upper GI cancer overtaking stroke as the most common indication for gastrostomy insertion, followed by stroke in second place. Dementia featured as an indication for PEG insertion in ≤5% of patients in each of the first three audit cycles (2004, 2006, 2008); thereafter, dementia did not feature at all as an indication for gastrostomy insertion (see figure 1).

Procedural care

There was a marked rise in the administration of prophylactic antibiotics, with 83% uptake in 2004, 89% in 2011 and 93% of procedures in 2022. 109 patients (75%) underwent gastrostomy insertion with conscious sedation in 2016 (average dose of midazolam of 2 mg, fentanyl administration was not recorded). In 2022, 62% (96/154) of patients received conscious sedation. In 40% (37/92) of patients who received midazolam, they did so in isolation. In 60% (55/92) of these cases, patients had midazolam in combination with fentanyl. A total of 91% (53/58) of patients receiving fentanyl had it in combination with midazolam, with 9% (5/58) of patients receiving fentanyl in isolation for the procedure. In one case, pethidine was used; three cases used propofol. In 2022, the average dose of midazolam used was 2 mg, and the average dose of fentanyl when given was 50 μg. 26 patients (18%) underwent a general anaesthetic for their gastrostomy insertion in 2016, compared with 15 (10%) patients in 2022. In patients who developed postprocedural chest infection in 2016, there were 15 out of 16 (94%) who received sedation for their procedure, with associated overall risk of postprocedural chest infection of 13.7% (15/109) in the sedated cohort, compared with 2.7% (1/37) in the unsedated cohort. In the cohort of patients receiving a general anaesthetic in 2022, 13% developed a postprocedural chest infection, with 4.8% of unsedated patients developing chest infection after the procedure.

Patient outcomes

Where documented, infection was the most common complication recorded, incorporating both chest and site infection, with 5% of cases in 2004, 7% in 2011 and 1.9% in 2022. Allowing for some fluctuation mortality has decreased overall. A 7-day mortality of 2% was seen in 2004, with a reduction to 1% in 2022 (see figure 2). With regard to the average patient age and gastrostomy insertion and 7-day mortality, the Pearson coefficient correlation was 0.79, indicating a strong correlation. The same was reflected in relation to the 30-day mortality with a Pearson coefficient correlation of 0.78.

Mortality associated with gastrostomy insertion.

Reduction was demonstrated in the 30-day mortality rate with 9% in 2004 vs 2.5% in 2022. The 30-day mortality in the 2016 audit was somewhat of an anomaly at 8% (12/146). Three of those 12 (25%) deaths were directly attributed to the gastrostomy procedure. This cohort was reviewed in relation to procedural complications and implications on mortality. The 30-day mortality rate in patients who had a gastrostomy complication was 18% (7/40) compared with 8.5% in the whole cohort, and 5% (5/106) in the cohort with no complications. Similar to the other audit years, the most common complication in 2016 was pneumonia/chest infection, occurring in 40% (16/40) of complications.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates a changing landscape in relation to gastrostomy insertion.

A shift in the indications for gastrostomy insertion with ENT/upper GI-associated obstruction overtaking stroke as the reason for insertion over our serial audits may reflect changes in guidance. The 2021 European Society of Gastrointestinal Endoscopy guidelines recommend early gastrostomy insertion in some patients with chronic degenerative disease or some types of malignancy if the patient has weight loss despite oral nutrition.11 This guidance may offer one explanation as to why mechanical obstruction from ENT/upper GI malignancies has overtaken stroke as the most common indication for gastrostomy—gastrostomy being used as a prophylactic measure in the pretherapeutic work-up in cancer care.

Our serial audits reflect the fact that increasing age and decreasing BMI are associated with increased risk of procedure-associated complications and mortality. In 2022, the Pearson coefficient correlations calculated in relation to age and 7-day and 30-day mortality indicated a strong correlation. Namely as the average age reduced, so did the 7-day mortality and the 30-day mortality (see table 1). Studies have shown that increased levels of albumin are associated with longer survival.12 It is evident from our serial studies that patient selection for gastrostomy insertion has progressed in such a way that biochemical markers in combination with age guide on appropriate patient selection.

Our study showed a lower number of patients with a diagnosis of dementia having a gastrostomy inserted, ≤5% in the first three audit cycles of 2004, 2006 and 2008, and none in later cycles, in comparison to the 18% of patients in the 2004 NCEPOD report. A clinical decision of withholding feeding is understandably very difficult; however, there is no evidence of improvement in mortality in patients with dementia who are gastrostomy fed. This is supported by the European Society for Clinical Nutrition and Metabolism guidelines for gastrostomy tube placement which state that its indication should not be a terminal measure in patients with a short life expectancy or advanced dementia.13 An absence of patients with dementia undergoing gastrostomy insertion from 2011 to 2022 demonstrates changes in patient selection considering futility.

The NCEPOD report indicated that 42% of patients had no antibiotic prophylaxis for their gastrostomy insertion. Our study shows an association between an increased uptake in the administration of prophylactic antibiotics and reduction in procedure-related complications, namely infection.

No increased risk of postprocedural chest infection was noted with time. The NCEPOD advisors expressed concerns that the use of sedation and local anaesthetic spray to the oropharynx may be implicated in pulmonary aspiration and postoperative respiratory complications. The serial audits do show that the rate of procedural complications has reduced on the whole.

Our prospective study demonstrates that gastrostomy-associated mortality has reduced between 2004 and 2022. A number of factors may have contributed to this, including better patient selection, supported by wider introduction of nutrition support teams, use of multidisciplinary discussion as well as increasing use of antibiotic prophylaxis.

Our study has several limitations. The most notable issue was inconsistency in data collection recording between study years, which meant that some data were not available across all study years. This included procedural sedation information which was only suitable for analysis in 2016 and 2022 datasets as well as consistent collection of data on grade of operator. We did not differentiate PEG from RIG in original data collection, limiting analysis by procedure type and highlighting the need to specify the procedure at the point of data collection in future studies. Procedure indication could have been refined further to differentiate ENT from upper GI tumours. While the vast majority of cases were ENT, including upper GI tumours within ‘obstructing tumour’ category limits interpretation. In addition, malnutrition and failure of function were not further interrogated at the point of data collection, limiting understanding of this group’s specific patient characteristics. Specifying indications related to gastrostomy insertion, such as venting, should also be ascertained to allow further review with regard to procedural and mortality outcomes. Lack of discreet data on PEG and RIG procedures highlights the potential for selection bias with regard to analysis of the broader study population.

Nevertheless, this study provides useful information from a large cross-sectional audit series on changing trends in gastrostomy insertion over time and assists units in planning services based on demand demonstrated over time. The study also emphasises the importance of patient selection and consideration given to morbidity and mortality risk associated with the procedure. Prospective data collection allows an appreciation for the variations in practice based on location as well as time.

Future studies should aim to differentiate between the two procedure types to ascertain more information on patient selection in relation to procedure type as well as the role of the MDT with regard to patient selection.
