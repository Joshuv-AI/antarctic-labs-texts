Resolution of chronic refractory migraine and development of burning mouth syndrome after focused ultrasound thalamotomy: illustrative case
Authors: Shachar Zion Shemesh, Paz Kelmer, Tsvia Fay-Karmon, Sharon Hassin-Baer, Lior Ungar
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUNDChronic migraine is a highly disabling neurological disorder. A subset of patients develop chronic daily headaches refractory to all standard therapies. MR-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) thalamotomy is an incisionless treatment for medication-refractory essential tremor (ET).OBSERVATIONSA 76-year-old woman with a 40-year history of chronic migraine with near-daily attacks, characterized predominantly by right-sided headaches and unresponsive to multiple preventive and acute treatments, underwent MRgFUS VIM thalamotomy for severe ET. After the procedure, her tremor resolved, and her chronic migraines were no longer reported during long-term follow-up. Shortly after the procedure, she developed burning mouth syndrome (BMS), characterized by bilateral oral burning dysesthesias with left-sided predominance involving the lips and oral mucosa. She was treated with gabapentin and amitriptyline, with partial symptomatic improvement. Postprocedure MRI confirmed the intended VIM lesion with slight medial extension toward the adjacent ventral posteromedial nucleus (VPM) of the thalamus, which relays trigeminal somatosensory input.LESSONSThis unique case illustrates that ablative neuromodulation of thalamic networks may be followed by marked improvement or remission of chronic refractory migraine, further supporting a possible role for the thalamus in migraine pathophysiology and pain transmission. Chronic migraine, defined as headache on ≥ 15 days per month for > 3 months with migrainous features on ≥ 8 days, is a prevalent and debilitating condition. Chronic migraine is a highly disabling disorder and a subset becomes refractory despite multiple preventive therapies, including those targeting calcitonin gene-related peptide (CGRP). Refractory chronic migraine represents a small but profoundly impaired group with persistent frequent attacks despite guideline-based care.1–6

Essential tremor (ET) was a comorbid condition in this patient, for which she sought treatment. MR-guided focused ultrasound (MRgFUS) thalamotomy is an FDA-approved, incisionless surgical approach for medication-refractory ET, targeting the ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) of the thalamus.3 The VIM is part of the ventral lateral thalamic relay for cerebellar outflow and is implicated in generating tremor oscillations.7–16 High-frequency focused ultrasound (FUS) energy produces a precise lesion in the VIM, alleviating tremor in the contralateral limbs. Common side effects of VIM thalamotomy include transient gait ataxia and sensory disturbances such as paresthesia or numbness in the face or distal extremities.16–19 Such effects are attributed to lesion proximity to adjacent structures (e.g., internal capsule laterally or the ventral posterior sensory nuclei medially). Although effects on migraine have not been reported in the ET literature, the thalamus is a key node in pain modulation. In particular, the ventral posterior nuclei are involved in processing nociceptive input from the trigeminocervical complex (TCC) and in thalamocortical pain transmission implicated in migraine, including phenomena such as trigeminal allodynia and photophobia. We present an illustrative case in which VIM thalamotomy for ET was followed by complete and unexpected remission of chronic refractory migraine. We describe the clinical course and imaging findings and discuss the implications for migraine mechanisms and potential risks of central neuromodulation.

Illustrative Case

A 76-year-old left-handed woman with a long-standing history of medically refractory chronic migraine and ET underwent MRgFUS thalamotomy. Her migraines began in her 30s and evolved into a chronic daily pattern over the last decade. Despite treatment at a specialized headache clinic, she experienced debilitating headaches virtually every day (mean ≥ 25 days/month). Attacks were predominantly right-sided unilateral throbbing headaches, occasionally perceived centrally, accompanied by photophobia, phonophobia, and nausea, consistent with migraine without aura. Multiple preventive classes of medication (beta-blockers, anticonvulsants, antidepressants, and other guideline-based agents), onabotulinumtoxinA, CGRP monoclonal antibody therapy, and interventional blocks had failed, with persistent near-daily disabling attacks. Overall, her migraine attacks remained intractable, causing significant functional impairment and poor quality of life. Notably, aside from hypertension, she had no other chronic medical issues. Her neurological examination between migraines was unremarkable.

In parallel, the patient had a 20-year history of progressive ET, predominantly affecting her left (dominant) hand. The tremor had gradually worsened to the point of interfering with eating, drinking, and writing. Standard tremor medications (propranolol and primidone) provided minimal relief. Given the medication-refractory nature of her tremor and its impact on daily activities, she was referred for MRgFUS thalamotomy. Preoperative MRI and high-resolution CT studies were performed for targeting and to ensure that there were no intracranial abnormalities (apart from age-related changes) that would contraindicate the procedure.

High-frequency ultrasound beams using the Insightec Exablate platform were stereotactically focused on the right thalamus, targeting the right VIM (anterior commissure–posterior commissure [AC-PC] distance 26.5 mm; target coordinates approximately 13 mm lateral to the midline, 6.6 mm anterior to the PC, and 1.7 mm superior to the AC-PC plane) to treat the contralateral left-hand tremor. Twelve gradual sonications were applied to create a discrete lesion in the right VIM, reaching temperatures above 55°C. Intraoperative clinical testing confirmed tremor arrest, and no new motor or sensory side effects were observed.

The patient tolerated the procedure well, aside from a transient headache during sonication that resolved within minutes after each sonication.

Following the thalamotomy, the patient experienced an immediate cessation of left-hand tremor, leading to significant improvements in daily manual functions and complete resolution of tremulous movements observed during clinical examination. Unexpectedly, within days following the procedure, the patient noted complete cessation of her usual daily migraine headaches. At follow-up visits over the ensuing 12 months, she remained entirely migraine free without any prophylactic medications.

Approximately 1 week after the procedure, the patient developed new oral sensory symptoms. She reported constant burning pain involving the lips and oral cavity, initially bilateral with clear left-sided predominance and later involving the tongue and hard palate, left greater than right. She also described intermittent dysgeusia with a bitter or metallic taste. Neurological examination demonstrated intact cranial nerve function and preserved facial sensation to pinprick and temperature, without objective sensory loss, although mechanical allodynia over the lips was present and more pronounced on the left. Oral examination revealed no mucosal or tongue abnormalities. Given the temporal association with thalamotomy and the normal oral examination, secondary burning mouth syndrome (BMS) was suspected. Gabapentin (up to 1800 mg/day) and amitriptyline (50 mg/day) were initiated, with marked symptomatic improvement. By 6 months, the patient reported an approximately 80% reduction in burning symptoms, although treatment was limited by dizziness. During this period, no recurrence of migraine-like headaches was reported.

Imaging Findings

MRI performed 24 hours after MRgFUS demonstrated the expected right thalamic VIM lesion with a small necrotic core and surrounding edema extending medially toward the adjacent sensory thalamus (ventral posteromedial nucleus [VPM]/ventral posterolateral nucleus [VPL]), without hemorrhage. Follow-up MRI at 3 months showed expected lesion maturation with resolution of perilesional edema and a small residual cavity (Figs. 1 and 2). Tractography findings were anatomically consistent with disruption of thalamic projections adjacent to the lesion, including pathways potentially relevant to trigeminal sensory processing (Fig. 3). Discussion

This case describes a remarkable and previously unreported clinical course in which MRgFUS thalamotomy targeting the VIM was followed by complete remission of a long-standing chronic migraine condition that had been refractory to conventional treatments. It was also followed by the development of a burning mouth pain syndrome temporally associated with imaging evidence of lesion extension toward the adjacent sensory thalamus (VPM).

Remission of Refractory Migraine After Thalamotomy

In this patient, chronic migraines ceased immediately following the thalamic lesion and remained absent throughout long-term follow-up. While causality cannot be established from a single observation, the close temporal association and anatomical proximity to sensory thalamic pathways support a mechanistic link. The likelihood of spontaneous migraine cessation in a 76-year-old is low, especially given her decades-long history of daily headaches. Although direct ablative targeting of thalamic projections for migraine has not been systematically studied, converging neuroimaging and neurophysiological data implicate the TCC–thalamocortical network in migraine generation, central sensitization, and pain persistence. In our case, a focused lesion in the sensory thalamus may have similarly disrupted the network required for migraine generation. We intentionally targeted the VIM (motor thalamus) for tremor, but the proximity of pain pathways in the region raises several mechanistic possibilities. The thalamus is a convergence point for trigeminothalamic tract input from the brainstem (spinal trigeminal nucleus) and ascending pain modulatory pathways. The VPM, in particular, relays facial and cranial sensory information (including nociceptive signals from meninges and craniovascular structures implicated in migraine) to the cortex.16 By creating a lesion that may have affected the VPM or adjacent sensory pathways, the procedure may have altered transmission of trigeminal pain signals to higher cortical pain centers. Specifically, we hypothesize disruption of thalamocortical projections originating from the TCC, which integrates nociceptive input from cranial and upper cervical structures and relays this information via the ventral posteromedial thalamus to cortical pain processing regions. This could have reduced the perception of migraine pain or altered propagation within migraine-related cortical networks. Additionally, beyond pure sensory relay, the thalamus is involved in the modulation and perception of pain intensity and in phenomena like allodynia. Thalamic nuclei (including the posterior thalamus) have increased activity during migraine attacks and are thought to contribute to central sensitization and the persistence of headache pain.13 A lesion in this region might modulate neuronal hyperexcitability or abnormal network oscillations associated with chronic migraine.

While broader network effects cannot be excluded, we propose that partial disruption of thalamocortical projections associated with the TCC, traversing or adjacent to the VPM, contributed to interruption of migraine-related nociceptive signaling.

BMS and Thalamic Injury

The main adverse outcome in this case was the development of BMS. BMS is typically an idiopathic pain condition seen in postmenopausal women, often attributed to neuropathic dysfunction of the trigeminal small fibers or central pain pathways.20–26 Patients experience burning pain of the tongue, lips, or oral mucosa with normal oral examinations. In primary BMS, evidence of peripheral trigeminal small-fiber neuropathy is common (e.g., reduced epithelial nerve fibers on tongue biopsy and abnormal trigeminal nerve quantitative sensory testing).27,28 Central mechanisms, including aberrant thalamic activity, have also been implicated; functional imaging shows altered thalamic and prefrontal activity in BMS patients.29 In our patient, the procedure may have resulted in partial dysfunction of central trigeminal sensory pathways at the level of the sensory thalamus. Such an “irritative” partial lesion may lead to hyperexcitability of central nociceptive circuits, producing positive sensory phenomena such as burning pain rather than complete sensory loss. Delayed sensory complications following MRgFUS thalamotomy, although uncommon, have been described. A plausible mechanism involves dysfunction of neurons within the zone of toxic perilesional edema surrounding the necrotic core. While most sensory adverse effects occur immediately or within hours of the procedure, some neurons may initially remain functionally viable but subsequently undergo delayed failure, apoptosis, or necrosis because of evolving edema and metabolic stress. This process may result in delayed-onset sensory phenomena, including dysesthesia or dysgeusia, days to weeks after treatment. Our patient’s symptomatology is essentially a focal thalamic pain syndrome limited to the oral region (trigeminal V2/V3 distribution). Partial or irritative injury to the VPM or its thalamocortical projections can produce positive sensory phenomena such as burning pain and allodynia while preserving basic modalities such as touch and pinprick. We hypothesize that the ultrasound lesion’s margins may have affected thalamocortical projections associated with the TCC, contributing to a central neuropathic pain syndrome localized to the oral region. The absence of complete numbness of the lower lip or tongue likely reflects partial involvement of sensory thalamic circuits rather than complete destruction of somatotopically organized trigeminal representations. Such dissociation between preserved primary sensation and painful dysesthesia is characteristic of central pain syndromes involving the thalamus.

It is notable that MRgFUS thalamotomy for tremor commonly causes transient paresthesias in the lips or fingertips contralateral to the lesion in a significant proportion of patients. In the pivotal trial, 38% of patients had numbness or tingling after VIM FUS thalamotomy. These are usually mild and often resolve over time.21 Our patient’s outcome can be viewed as an outlier on that spectrum as she developed a persistent and more painful form of sensory disturbance. At the 18-month follow-up, the patient reported a partial but clinically meaningful reduction in the intensity of oral burning symptoms under neuropathic pain treatment. Neuropathic pain treatment with gabapentin (up to 1800 mg/day) and amitriptyline (50 mg/day) resulted in marked symptomatic improvement (approximately 80%), albeit with dose-limiting dizziness. This outcome underscores the importance of precise targeting and dosimetry in thalamic procedures. Even a slight medial overshoot in lesion placement can affect the sensory thalamus and result in chronic neuropathic pain. Future cases of MRgFUS for any indication should carefully monitor for such symptoms, and MRI guidance should aim to confine lesions within target boundaries. If larger lesions are needed (e.g., for tremor recurrence), one might consider fractionating or shaping the lesion to avoid critical adjacent nuclei.

This observation supports a role for thalamic sensory networks in sustaining refractory migraine and highlights a potential mechanistic substrate warranting further investigation rather than a therapeutic recommendation.

The induction of BMS in our patient also provides a cautionary lesson. Creating a lesion in the sensory thalamus can itself produce a chronic pain syndrome, essentially swapping one pain disorder for another. While our patient found the trade-off worthwhile as the migraines had been far more debilitating than the oral burning, such an outcome might not be acceptable to others. BMS can significantly affect quality of life, especially when severe.5 Fortunately, our patient’s BMS symptoms have been moderate. In primary BMS, management is challenging, with only symptomatic treatments and modest success rates. In secondary BMS due to a defined lesion, treatments are similarly limited. Gabapentinoids and tricyclic antidepressants are commonly used for neuropathic pain and were partially effective in this case; topical clonazepam has also been reported as a symptomatic option in BMS. None are curative. This underscores that any consideration of thalamic ablation for migraine must be approached judiciously. It may be more prudent to target structures slightly removed from primary sensory relays (e.g., intralaminar nuclei or neural network nodes identified via functional imaging) to avoid such complications.

In summary, this case highlights a rare intersection of two challenging refractory conditions, chronic migraine and BMS, with a single intervention shedding light on both. The complete resolution of chronic migraine after VIM thalamotomy suggests that the trigeminal sensory thalamus is a critical hub in sustaining migraine headaches. The occurrence of BMS due to collateral damage of the VPM emphasizes the thalamus’s role in orofacial pain and the risk of central pain when sensory pathways are disrupted. Our findings encourage further research into thalamic modulation for migraine, while also urging caution. Any future therapeutic use of thalamotomy or deep brain stimulation for migraine must aim for maximal precision to avoid inducing new pain syndromes. Multidisciplinary care is warranted for patients who develop complications like BMS; in our case, close collaboration between neurology, neurosurgery, and orofacial pain specialists helped validate the diagnosis and monitor symptoms.

Observations

This illustrative case provides evidence that disrupting thalamic relay pathways (in this instance via MRgFUS lesioning of the VIM/VPM region) can lead to complete and sustained remission of long-standing, therapy-resistant chronic migraine. It underscores the thalamus as a key player in migraine pathophysiology and a potential therapeutic target in extreme cases.

While MRgFUS thalamotomy is effective for ET, practitioners should be vigilant for sensory side effects. Even mild thalamic lesion extension can produce sensory symptoms like numbness, tingling, or pain. In this case, inadvertent VPM involvement led to a central pain syndrome (BMS). This emphasizes meticulous planning to confine lesions; advanced neuroimaging or tractography may help delineate safe lesion boundaries for future cases.

The development of predominantly left-sided but ultimately bilateral burning mouth pain following a right-sided thalamic lesion is analogous to central poststroke pain seen after thalamic strokes, wherein patients experience burning dysesthesia in regions of lost sensory input. This reinforces that partial damage to sensory thalamic circuits can result in positive pain phenomena (central pain) rather than just sensory loss. Managing such pain is difficult, and prevention by precise targeting is paramount.

Lessons

Future Directions in Headache Management

This case opens considerations for research into thalamic or other central neuromodulation approaches for refractory headaches. Noninvasive or focal therapies (e.g., stereotactic lesions, deep brain stimulation, or FUS neuromodulation) targeting pain processing brain regions might be developed as last-resort options for patients with intractable chronic migraine. Any such approaches must balance potential benefits with the risk of adverse effects like those observed here. Robust clinical trials and ethical oversight will be needed before such interventions can be recommended.

Multidisciplinary Care and Patient Counseling

For patients undergoing functional neurosurgical procedures, especially those with chronic pain disorders, a multidisciplinary team should be involved. Preprocedure counseling should include discussion of possible unforeseen benefits (as in this case, migraine improvement) and risks (new sensory disturbances or pain). Postprocedure follow-up should actively assess for changes in headache frequency and new pain symptoms, referring to appropriate specialists (e.g., headache specialists, pain management, and oral medicine) as needed. In our case, the patient’s dramatic migraine relief greatly improved her quality of life despite the BMS, but this outcome and perspective may vary by individual. Comprehensive care and informed consent are essential.
