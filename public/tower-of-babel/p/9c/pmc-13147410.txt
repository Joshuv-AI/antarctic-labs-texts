From hepatitis A to acute liver failure in a resource-limited setting: a case report

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Hepatitis A virus (HAV) remains a major global cause of acute viral hepatitis, transmitted predominantly via the fecal-oral route through contaminated water and food. An estimated ∼1.5 million acute HAV cases occur annually worldwide, with the highest burden in settings of poor sanitation and limited access to safe water [1]. Most infections are self-limited, but a very small fraction (∼0.015% to 0.5%) progress to acute liver failure (ALF), particularly, among older adults or those with comorbidity [2,3].

Despite advances, key uncertainties persist regarding early, actionable predictors of deterioration from uncomplicated HAV to ALF and pragmatic care pathways when advanced diagnostics and therapies (e.g. thromboelastography, continuous renal replacement therapy, timely liver transplantation) are not readily available [4]. Studies implicate viral factors and host immune dysregulation, but clinical translation to setting-appropriate risk stratification remains fragmented [5]. Likewise, although guideline-based algorithms exist, their operationalization in health systems constrained by infrastructure, critical care capacity, and referral bottlenecks is insufficiently described [4].

Although most HAV infections are self-limited, the small subset that progresses to ALF requires rapid recognition and structured escalation of care [1]. ALF is conventionally defined by the development of hepatic encephalopathy and coagulopathy, usually an international normalized ratio (INR) ≥1.5, within 26 weeks of illness onset in a patient without preexisting cirrhosis or chronic liver disease [3]. In this context, reports from resource-limited settings are especially valuable because they help bridge the gap between diagnostic standards and real-world management when transplantation and advanced extracorporeal support are not readily available. We present the case of a previously healthy 19-year-old woman with acute HAV infection who initially presented with jaundice, hepatic encephalopathy, and mild INR elevation and who progressed to formally defined ALF within 72 hours. This report aims to highlight early warning signs of deterioration; describe a pragmatic supportive care pathway; and discuss the public health implications for referral systems, sanitation, and vaccination

Case description

A previously healthy 19-year-old woman presented with 20 days of epigastric pain, nausea, food-containing emesis, dark urine, jaundice, and altered mental status. She was hemodynamically stable and markedly icteric; neurologic examination showed intermittent incoherence consistent with hepatic encephalopathy. Initial laboratory tests showed prothrombin time (PT) 16 s, INR 1.23, total bilirubin 18.3 mg/dL (direct 8.4), aspartate aminotransferase 281 U/l, alanine aminotransferase 341 U/l, and creatinine 0.5 mg/dL. Serology was positive for anti-HAV IgM/IgG and negative for hepatitis B surface antigen and anti-hepatitis B core. Abdominal ultrasonography showed hepatomegaly.

Although she had encephalopathy at presentation, she did not yet meet formal criteria for ALF because the INR was 1.23. Within 72 hours, however, her condition deteriorated, with PT 26.2 s, INR 1.8, total bilirubin 28 mg/dL, aspartate aminotransferase 680 U/l, alanine aminotransferase 450 U/l, albumin 3.1 g/dL, ammonia 140 µg/dL, and persistent grade II encephalopathy, thereby fulfilling accepted criteria for HAV-associated ALF. She was referred to gastroenterology co-management. At that time, she had two adverse non-acetaminophen King’s College variables, although she did not meet full poor prognosis criteria; the Acute Liver Failure prognostic (ALFA) model score was −0.702, corresponding to an estimated 1-month probability of transplant or death of 20.9%.

Treatment included intravenous N-acetylcysteine using a non-acetaminophen protocol, a single empiric dose of vitamin K, neuroprotective measures, early enteral nutrition, conservative blood product use, and infection surveillance. Liver transplantation and advanced extracorporeal therapies were unavailable. After approximately 4 weeks of inpatient care, she was discharged in stable condition and remained clinically well on follow-up.

Discussion

The patient’s initial presentation, marked by acute hepatitis, hepatic encephalopathy, and mild INR elevation, represented a medical emergency requiring urgent specialist monitoring and dynamic reassessment. The early warning signs of progression included worsening INR, hyperbilirubinemia, hyperammonemia, and persistent grade II encephalopathy. These parameters align with guideline-endorsed triggers for urgent escalation, prognostication with King’s College criteria, and Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) [3]. In resource-constrained settings, timely recognition and structured escalation pathways are central to reducing preventable deterioration [4].

Liver injury in HAV is predominantly immune-mediated rather than directly cytopathic: severe phenotypes show expansion of clusters of differentiations 8⁺ cytotoxic T cells, heightened pro-inflammatory cytokines (e.g. interleukin [IL]-6, tumor necrosis factor-α, interferon-γ), and diminished regulatory T-cell activity, consistent with immune-amplified hepatocyte injury [[5], [6], [7]]. Although genotypes IA, IB, and IIIA predominate worldwide, consistent genotype–severity associations have not been established; distributions of IA vs IIIA appear similar across acute and fulminant cohorts, and genotyping alone should not guide risk prediction [6,7]. Some series report higher viral loads in fulminant cases, suggesting that antigen burden may intensify immune-mediated damage [7]. Emerging signals—including rare host immunogenetic susceptibilities (e.g. pathways modulating IL-18/IL-18BP or TIM-1–mediated bystander activation)—underscore the primacy of dysregulated innate–adaptive crosstalk, although these markers currently lack clinical utility for bedside stratification [6]. Most HAV infections are self-limited and recover with supportive care; therefore, escalation to organ support and advanced supportive measures should be reserved for patients who demonstrate dynamic clinical deterioration, worsening encephalopathy, renal dysfunction, or formal progression to ALF; advanced extracorporeal therapies such as continuous renal replacement therapy (CRRT) or plasma exchange are generally considered in patients with ongoing deterioration when available [3]; several elements require adaptation where advanced modalities are scarce. INR reflects severity but does not reliably predict bleeding due to rebalanced hemostasis; vitamin K is not a routine treatment for ALF-related coagulopathy. Rather, a single empiric dose may be reasonable when concomitant deficiency is suspected. In contrast, fresh frozen plasma should generally be reserved for active bleeding or urgent invasive procedures [3]. In grade ≥2 encephalopathy, CRRT effectively lowers ammonia levels. It is recommended even without renal failure, but limited availability often necessitates pragmatic measures—airway protection, frequent neuro checks, avoidance of sedatives, head elevation—and use of enteral ammonia-lowering strategies (e.g. lactulose) despite weaker evidence in ALF vs cirrhosis [8]. N-acetylcysteine should be framed as an adjunctive rather than universal intervention in non-acetaminophen ALF. The best available evidence suggests possible improvement in transplant-free survival when started in early encephalopathy stages (grades I-II) but not a universal survival benefit, and adverse effects such as nausea and vomiting may occur [9]. Corticosteroids should be restricted to scenarios with strong suspicion of autoimmune hepatitis; routine use in HAV-ALF is not supported [10]. Early hepatobiliary imaging and parallel documentation of transplant candidacy remain best practice, coupled with activation of referral pathways—even if transfer proves unfeasible [10].

At ALF diagnosis, the patient had clinically relevant prognostic risk, with two adverse King’s College variables and an ALFA score of −0.702 (estimated 1-month risk of transplant or death, 20.9%), supporting close monitoring and early referral despite not meeting full the King’s College criteria [11].

From a public health perspective, unsafe water, poor sanitation, and overcrowding may increase HAV exposure and delay care. This case suggests that, in selected patients, a resource-adapted bundle with early recognition, supportive management, and transplant-oriented referral can stabilize HAV-associated ALF while reinforcing prevention through vaccination, hygiene, and stronger referral systems

Conclusion

This case shows that early recognition and a resource-adapted supportive bundle may improve outcomes in HAV-associated ALF when transplantation is unavailable. It also highlights the importance of prevention and of reporting cases from low-resource settings to guide practical care pathways.
