Craniofacial extramedullary plasmacytoma with hard palate and bilateral cervical lymph node involvement: a diagnostic challenge and literature review
Authors: Xian Zhang, Junchun Hu, Dongdong Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundExtramedullary plasmacytoma (EMP) is a rare plasma cell tumor primarily affecting extramedullary sites without involving the bone marrow. While 80–90% of cases occur in the head and neck regions, craniofacial EMP involving the hard palate and spreading to bilateral cervical lymph nodes is exceptionally rare.Case presentationA 59-year-old female presented with a one-year history of a nasal mass on the right anterior side. Her medical history showed basal cell carcinoma and oropharyngeal tumors. Physical examination revealed a mulberry-like mass in the right nasal vestibule. Computed tomography (CT) of the paranasal sinus revealed a space-occupying lesion in the nasal cavity. The patient underwent surgical resection, with histopathological and immunohistochemical analysis confirming plasmacytoma. Bone marrow aspiration and biopsy showed no significant abnormalities. Subsequent positron emission tomography-computed tomography (PET-CT) identified involvement of bilateral cervical lymph nodes and the hard palate. This was confirmed by CT-guided fine-needle aspiration biopsy, with pathological results consistent with those of the nasal lesion. The final diagnosis was EMP involving the hard palate and bilateral cervical lymph nodes. The patient underwent four cycles of chemotherapy using the VCD regimen (bortezomib, cyclophosphamide, dexamethasone), followed by localized radiotherapy (36 Gy in 18 fractions). Currently, she shows no evidence of systemic involvement and is recovering well.ConclusionWe report a rare case of primary sinonasal EMP with involvement of the hard palate and bilateral cervical lymph nodes. Due to its non-specific clinical features, sinonasal EMP is often misdiagnosed as other head and neck malignancies. Pathological biopsy is crucial for definitive diagnosis. Introduction

Plasmacytoma is a malignant clonal proliferation of mature B-cells, classified by the World Health Organization (WHO) into two distinct entities: multiple myeloma (MM) and solitary plasmacytoma (SP). While MM manifests as a systemic malignancy with bone marrow involvement, SP presents as a localized tumor without evidence of disseminated disease. SP is further subcategorized into solitary bone plasmacytoma (SBP) for bone involvement and extramedullary plasmacytoma (EMP) for soft tissue lesions outside the skeletal system [1].

EMP is a rare entity, accounting for approximately 3% of all plasma cell neoplasms [2]. The established diagnostic criteria include: (1) a biopsy-confirmed isolated extramedullary mass of monoclonal plasma cells; (2) bone marrow plasma cell infiltration of less than 10%; and (3) the absence of systemic multiple myeloma (MM) features [3]. Although EMP shares cytological and immunophenotypic similarities with MM, the lack of significant bone marrow involvement (< 10% plasma cells) remains the key distinguishing factor.

The majority of EMP cases (≥ 85%) arise in the upper aerodigestive tract, with predilection for the nasal cavity/paranasal sinuses (37.9%), pharynx (32.8%), and larynx (13.7%) [4]. Less frequent sites include the gastrointestinal tract, lymph nodes, skin, and central nervous system, while involvement of adrenal glands, pancreas, or ovaries represents exceptional occurrences [5–10]. Herein, we report a diagnostically challenging case of EMP with triple-site involvement: nasal cavity, hard palate, and bilateral cervical lymph nodes. This unusual presentation underscores the potential for atypical dissemination patterns in EMP and highlights critical considerations for multidisciplinary management.

Case presentation

A 59-year-old woman presented with a one-year history of a newly discovered, sesame-sized, irregular mass in the right anterior nostril. Her main symptoms were a diminished sense of smell, intermittent nasal blockage, and headaches. She denied having rhinorrhea, epistaxis, fever, or other systemic symptoms. During the past six months, the mass significantly enlarged, obstructing the right anterior nostril and causing persistent right-sided nasal congestion. Her medical history included notable conditions such as facial basal cell carcinoma and oropharyngeal tumors. Physical examination revealed mild protrusion of the nasal dorsum on the right side. A mulberry-like, pale red neoplasm occupied the right anterior nasal cavity, obstructing the view of the deeper nasal passages. The left nasal mucosa was hyperemic, but the nasal septum showed no significant deviation, and the left inferior turbinate was not hypertrophic. No abnormal secretions were noted, and the paranasal sinus regions showed no tenderness.

Routine laboratory evaluations, including complete blood count, liver and renal function tests, serum electrolytes, coagulation profile, pre-transfusion screening, and β2-microglobulin levels, were within normal limits. Computed tomography (CT) of the paranasal sinuses demonstrated a well-defined space-occupying lesion measuring 26 × 21 × 17 mm3 in the right nasal cavity, which showed progressive and marked enhancement following contrast administration., suggesting a possible neoplastic process (Fig. 1A). In light of the patient’s history of basal cell carcinoma, the possibility of a metastatic lesion cannot be excluded. The patient underwent endoscopic resection of the right nasal vestibular mass under general anesthesia. Intraoperative findings revealed a firm, pale-red, mulberry-like neoplasm obstructing the right anterior naris and extending to the nasal limen, accompanied by cartilaginous hyperplasia of the nasal floor. The neoplasm was completely excised from its base, proceeding from anterior to posterior. Subsequent exploration confirmed that the surrounding nasal mucosa was free of invasion. Histopathological analysis of the resected tissue showed characteristics consistent with a lymphohematopoietic system tumor (Fig. 2A). Immunohistochemical (IHC) staining showed strong positivity for CD38, CD138, MUM1, and Bcl-2, focal positivity for kappa light chains, diffuse positivity for lambda light chains, and positivity for CD79a (Fig. 2B and E). The histological morphology and immunophenotypic profile strongly suggested a diagnosis of EMP. Subsequent PET-CT imaging showed hypermetabolic lesions in the nasopharynx, posterior nasal septum, hard palate, and bilateral cervical lymph nodes, indicating widespread extramedullary involvement (Fig. 1B and F). Biopsy of a cervical lymph node revealed reactive lymphoid hyperplasia with significant plasma cell proliferation (Fig. 2F). Immunohistochemical analysis confirmed positivity for MUM1, CD138, CD38, Bcl-2, CD117, IgG, and IgG4 in the lymph node, with a Ki-67 proliferation index of about 10% (Fig. 2G and J). Bone marrow cytology showed a plasma cell proportion of 2%, with no signs of bone marrow infiltration at this stage (Fig. 3A-B). The patient showed no clinical signs of systemic multiple myeloma, such as hypercalcemia, renal dysfunction, anemia, or bone pain (CRAB). Serum free light chain analysis and protein electrophoresis of serum and urine identified a monoclonal IgG component with slightly elevated lambda globulin levels at 17.33 g/L (normal: 7–16 g/L). The M protein concentration was 1.78 g/L, a level absent in healthy individuals (Fig. 3C-F, S1). The final diagnosis was nasal EMP with involvement of the hard palate and bilateral cervical lymph nodes. The presence of extensive regional disease and lymph node metastasis, which suggest a poorer prognosis and increased risk of systemic dissemination, prompted the adoption of an aggressive combined-modality strategy [11]. Consequently, induction systemic chemotherapy was administered prior to radiotherapy to facilitate cytoreduction and manage potential occult systemic disease. She received four cycles of VCD chemotherapy (bortezomib, cyclophosphamide, dexamethasone). Three months later, she was re-admitted for radiotherapy, targeting the nasal cavity, hard palate, and affected cervical lymph nodes. A total radiation dose of 36 Gy was administered in 18 fractions (Fig. 4A-D) [12]. Six months post-treatment, the patient’s condition remains stable, with no evidence of recurrence. Discussion

EMP is a rare pathological entity, accounting for approximately 3% of all plasma cell tumors. Epidemiologically, the disease demonstrates a strong predilection for the head and neck region, exhibits a clear male preponderance, and typically affects individuals in the sixth decade of life [11, 13]. This case report is notable for documenting an atypical presentation in a 59-year-old female. Moreover, the anatomical involvement is unprecedented; while over 80% of craniofacial EMP cases occur in sinonasal regions, most have been documented only in isolated case reports [14]. A comprehensive review of PubMed data from 2000 to 2025 identified cases of EMP occurring in the nasal cavity (Table 1) [15–30]. To our knowledge, this is the first report describing a concurrent primary manifestation in the nasal cavity, hard palate, and bilateral cervical lymph nodes, deviating from conventional dissemination patterns previously unreported in the literature.

Nasal cavity-located EMP often manifests with nonspecific symptoms like nasal congestion, epistaxis, headaches, or a localized mass, mainly due to tumor compression. The lack of specific clinical features often results in misdiagnosis with other head and neck malignancies like squamous cell carcinoma or lymphoma. Immunohistochemical confirmation showing CD138/CD38/MUM1 positivity and light chain restriction is essential, and PET-CT offers crucial staging information to detect atypical spread patterns, as shown by our patient’s unexpected hard palate and bilateral nodal involvement. Nonetheless, EMP is a diagnostically challenging entity requiring strict adherence to WHO criteria for precise classification. The core criterion includes an “isolated” or “solitary” extramedullary mass of monoclonal plasma cells, coupled with normal bone marrow (< 10% plasma cells) and no CRAB features. Our case, with its concurrent tri-site craniofacial manifestation, directly challenges conventional interpretations of a “solitary” lesion. This highlights a major diagnostic pitfall: the possible misclassification of extensive regional disease as systemic MM, leading to vastly different prognostic and therapeutic implications. The key distinction lies not only in the number of soft tissue sites but in the definitive absence of bone marrow involvement, emphasizing that multifocal extramedullary disease can still represent a localized entity distinct from MM.

The differential diagnosis of EMP necessitates the exclusion of plasmablastic lymphoma (PBL) and B-cell lymphomas with plasmacytic differentiation. Unlike EMP, which typically occurs in immunocompetent patients, PBL is frequently associated with immunodeficiency, EBV infection (~ 60% EBER-positivity), and MYC rearrangements. Immunophenotypically, EMP exhibits a neoplastic plasma cell profile (CD19-/CD45-/CD56+), whereas the plasmacytic components of Lymphoplasmacytic or Marginal Zone Lymphoma typically retain B-cell antigens (CD19+/CD45+) and lack CD56 expression [31]. Therefore, routine EBER testing and comprehensive immunophenotyping are essential for accurate classification.

Currently, there is no internationally standardized clinical staging system for EMP. Prognosis assessment and treatment decisions primarily reference the staging standards of MM, with individual adjustments based on the extent of local tumor invasion, systemic condition, and molecular markers [32]. EMP is classified into primary and secondary types. Primary EMP is less invasive with a relatively favorable prognosis, while secondary EMP progresses from MM or SBP, involving plasma cell infiltration into extramedullary tissues, leading to a poorer prognosis [33]. Notably, about 10% to 20% of EMP patients will progress to MM. Those with large tumor volumes (> 5 cm), postoperative recurrence, or genetic abnormalities face a high risk of transforming into MM.

Plasmacytoma is highly sensitive to radiotherapy (RT), with RT alone achieving excellent long-term local control rates ranging from 79% to 91% [11, 34]. Therefore, RT has traditionally been the primary treatment approach. For small, accessible, and well-localized EMP, surgical resection can be an appropriate treatment option. However, complete removal of tumors originating in the sinonasal tract is often challenging due to proximity to critical anatomical structures and potential for poor cosmetic outcomes. In cases of larger lesions or those in anatomically complex regions, complete surgical removal is difficult. A combination of radiotherapy and surgery can effectively shrink tumors while minimizing surgical trauma. According to the International Lymphoma Radiation Oncology Group, a dose of 30–35 Gy is recommended for lesions under 5 cm, and 35–40 Gy for tumors over 5 cm [34]. Furthermore, Studies show that patients receiving radiotherapy have a significantly lower risk of progressing to MM compared to those undergoing surgery alone, with better five-year disease-free survival (DFS) outcomes [12]. EMP has a relatively good overall prognosis. Studies indicate that tumor sizes over 5 cm and lymph node metastasis are linked to poorer overall survival [32]. Radiotherapy combined with short-term systemic treatment can better control local disease, preventing or delaying progression to MM [35, 36]. In our case, a sequential treatment approach was adopted. Four cycles of chemotherapy were first administered to address nodal disease, followed by consolidative radiotherapy to the primary and nodal sites.

This report, despite detailing a rare and instructive case, has several important limitations. Firstly, the inherent nature of a single-case study limits the generalizability of our findings to the broader EMP patient population. Secondly, specific aspects of our therapeutic approach require critical evaluation. The patient underwent four cycles of VCD chemotherapy; whether this duration and intensity are adequate for durable control in such a high-risk, multi-site presentation remains uncertain. Additionally, a major limitation in our post-treatment assessment is the absence of a follow-up PET-CT scan. As a result, although a favorable clinical response was observed, we cannot definitively ascertain if the residual lymphadenopathy has achieved complete metabolic response or if metabolically active but indolent disease persists. Thirdly, the relatively short follow-up period hinders a definitive evaluation of long-term outcomes. Despite these limitations, the case underscores critical areas for future research. There is an urgent need for prospective multicenter registries to gather data on rare EMP presentations to aid in developing evidence-based guidelines for this heterogeneous disease.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this case describes a rare and diagnostically challenging presentation of primary EMP with synchronous tri-site craniofacial lesions and bilateral cervical lymph node involvement. Our experience suggests that a combined-modality approach, including surgery, systemic chemotherapy, and consolidative radiotherapy, can effectively control disease in such high-risk, locally advanced cases. This report adds to the limited literature on atypical EMP and emphasizes the need for refined diagnostic criteria and individualized, risk-adapted therapeutic strategies for this heterogeneous disease.
