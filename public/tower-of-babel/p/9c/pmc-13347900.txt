Deep brain stimulation battery longevity: a cohort study comparing impact of anaesthesia modality and anatomic target
Authors: Noor Zaghlula, Kelsey Kinsman, Oliver Phillips, Jennifer Hong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDeep brain stimulation (DBS) treats various movement disorders, though routine battery replacement is required. Factors affecting battery life remain unclear; prior studies implicate programming parameters and device model. Advancements in intraoperative guidance enable DBS placement under general anaesthesia (GA) using direct targeting instead of conscious sedation (Monitored anesthesia care or MAC) using indirect targeting. This study examines the effect of DBS anaesthesia type on IPG battery longevity and other influencing factors.MethodsRetrospective chart review of 254 patients who underwent DBS placement between 2013 and 2023 at a tertiary care centre was completed under an IRB-approved protocol, 217 of whom met inclusion criteria. Using an accelerated failure time (AFT) model, battery longevity was assessed as a function of anaesthesia type, electrode location, stimulation parameters, diagnosis, demographics, Movement Disorder Society Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale part III score (MDS-UPDRS-III) and battery type.ResultsDBS implanted with MAC resulted in significantly reduced battery longevity, AFT coefficient=−0.149, 95% CI −0.27 to −0.03 (p<0.05) compared with GA. compared with globus pallidus interna, subthalamic nucleus implantation (AFT coefficient=0.223, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.35, p<0.05) and thalamic ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) implantation (AFT coefficient=0.134, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.26, p<0.05) were associated with significantly longer battery longevity. High amplitude settings significantly reduced battery longevity, AFT coefficient=−0.167, 95% CI −0.21 to −0.12 (p<0.05). Diagnosis, MDS-UPDRS-III score and battery model effects on IPG battery longevity were not statistically significant.ConclusionBattery longevity can be optimised by considering anaesthesia type and electrode location during implantation. Improved battery longevity reduces replacement frequency, associated complications and overall healthcare costs.

Introduction

Deep brain stimulation (DBS) is a well-established, highly effective neurosurgical intervention for the treatment of movement disorders, primarily Parkinson’s disease, essential tremor and dystonia. It is currently an avenue of active investigation for use in psychiatric conditions, such as treatment refractory obsessive–compulsive disorder.1 DBS systems consist of intracranial electrodes connected via a subcutaneous wire to an implantable pulse generator (IPG) in a patient’s chest wall. It is thought that DBS functions by disrupting pathological neuronal circuits via the use of constant or intermittent electric stimulation, thus altering the ionic microenvironment and neuronal signalling.2 DBS is a non-lesional intervention with the ability to titrate stimulation parameters to longitudinally optimise outcomes such as improvement in parkinsonism, dyskinesia and pill burden.3

While DBS has become the standard of care for patients with movement disorders that do not achieve adequate symptom control with medical management alone, a potential barrier to DBS placement is the finite battery life of the implanted pulse generator necessitating routine operative replacement. Though IPG replacement is a relatively short ambulatory surgical procedure, each battery replacement poses additional cost to the patient and comes with risks including infection and damage to existing hardware. Surgical site infection can necessitate explantation of the remaining hardware, leaving patients without control of their symptoms and requires subsequent neurosurgical intervention for reimplantation of leads and IPGs after resolution of the infection. Although rechargeable IPG batteries have been proposed to address this issue, available evidence suggests that rechargeable IPG batteries are costlier than non-rechargeable IPG batteries, pose practical challenges for patients with cognitive or physical impairments due to the requirement of frequent at-home charging (typically every 5–7 days for a median of 4.5 hours), necessitate additional training in device maintenance and increase caregiver burden.4 5

Understanding the clinical and patient factors that drive non-rechargeable IPG battery longevity is critical to optimising patient outcomes as the majority of DBS patients currently have non-rechargeable IPGs. Although there is a trend towards rechargeable IPG implantation, between 2009 and 2021, 86.71% of IPGs implanted in the USA and 82.48% of IPGs implanted in Europe were non-rechargeable.6 Furthermore, existing studies demonstrate that DBS systems are being implanted at progressively younger ages.7 The pivotal EARLY-STIM trial, which investigated earlier DBS implantation in Parkinson’s disease (defined as implantation within 4 years of Parkinson’s disease symptom onset and within 3 years of motor complications), demonstrated improvement in motor symptoms, psychological well-being and quality of life,7 prompting Food and Drug Administration approval for earlier device implantation and precipitating a shift in practice patterns to younger patients.68 From 2009 to 2021, the mean patient age at time of DBS implantation was 63.85 years in the USA and 56.63 years in Europe.68 Increasing IPG battery life for these patients could reduce the number of replacement surgeries over the lifetime of the device thereby reducing patient complications and healthcare system costs.

For non-rechargeable IPG batteries, battery life is influenced by stimulation mode (monopolar vs bipolar stimulation) and device model.9 10 Notably, recent battery longevity research has observed an average reduction in battery life between previous models (eg, Kinetra 4.3–6.5 years) to current models (eg, Activa PC 3–4.6 years) the causes of which are uncertain.11

Recent advances in neurosurgical guidance systems, specifically intraoperative MRI, have made it possible to implant DBS leads with MRI-guided visualisation of target brain structures under general anaesthesia (GA); often referred to as asleep DBS surgery. Conversely, awake DBS lead implantation occurs under conscious sedation using indirect targeting coordinates with microelectrode recording and intraoperative stimulation testing to guide lead placement. Because these approaches are fundamentally different surgical techniques for targeting, choosing the optimal procedure for each patient is a complex decision involving target selection, surgeon experience, institutional resources and patient preference. At our institution, DBS implantation was previously primarily performed under conscious sedation (monitored anesthesia care or MAC) with indirect targeting using a Leksell frame and intraoperative microelectrode recording and stimulation testing, until installation of an intraoperative MRI allowed for DBS implantation under GA with Clearpoint targeting system.

While many studies have investigated the relationship between awake versus asleep DBS placement with regard to symptom control and lead placement accuracy,1214 no studies to date have evaluated the relationship between awake versus asleep DBS surgery and IPG battery life.

Given the importance of maximising battery longevity to reduce cost and adverse effects of repeated IPG replacements, we sought to assess the effect of anaesthesia at time of lead implantation on battery longevity. As such, among patients with DBS lead implantation surgeries at our institution, anaesthesia type (ie, GA vs conscious sedation (MAC)) served as a proxy for target localisation method. Secondarily, we aimed to identify additional factors affecting battery longevity including lead target, diagnosis, disease severity (specifically in Parkinson’s disease), battery model and stimulation parameters.

Methods

Participant and study design

Following approval by the Dartmouth Hitchcock Medical Center (DHMC) Institutional Review Board, the DHMC electronic medical record (EMR) was queried retrospectively for all patients who underwent initial DBS lead implantation between 2013 and 2023. Eligible patients were identified using Current Procedural Terminology codes for implantation of cranial neurostimulators (61885, 61886 and 61888). Patients were excluded if battery replacement occurred for infection, hardware malfunction, epilepsy or psychiatric conditions, battery replacement occurred in 2022 or later, rechargeable batteries were implanted, patients passed away, paediatric patients or if insufficient data was found in the EMR. Inclusion and exclusion criteria are outlined in the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) chart (figure 1).

Clinical variables and outcomes assessment

Clinical data were obtained via retrospective chart review. Data collected included: demographics, indicating diagnosis, date of initial lead implantation, anaesthesia type at time of lead implantation, lead target(s), Movement Disorder Society-Unified Parkinson’s Disease Rating Scale Part III (MDS-UPDRS III)15 off score (if applicable to diagnosis), battery model, number of battery replacements, dates of battery replacements and stimulation parameters. All data was manually extracted via review of all relevant documentation in the EMR from neurology, neurosurgery and nursing providers.

Statistical analysis

Demographic information in each cohort was compared using χ² analysis for categorical variables and t-test for continuous variables.

The impact of anaesthesia type on battery longevity was analysed using a parametric accelerated failure time (AFT) model with log-logistic distribution and a gamma frailty term to account for unobserved heterogeneity among patients. Preliminary non-parametric analyses, including Kaplan-Meier and marginal hazard curves, were performed to assess the shape of the survival function and the suitability of different distributions. The Kaplan-Meier and marginal hazard curves confirmed a non-monotonic hazard function with an initial rise and subsequent decline in failure risk, indicating a log-logistic distribution. Parametric models with exponential, Weibull, log-normal and log-logistic distributions were compared using Akaike and Bayesian information criteria (AIC and BIC, respectively), confirming that the log-logistic specification was the best fit.

Following model selection, variables were individually tested for statistically significant impact on battery life. The variables significant at p<0.05 were carried forward into the multivariate model—these included anaesthesia type, electrode target and amplitude. Online supplemental table 1 illustrates iterations of the AFT model.

Given uneven distribution of electrode target and amplitude among the MAC and GA cohorts, concerns for multicollinearity were assessed using the test of variance inflation factor (VIF). VIF measures the degree to which these variables may overlap in their degree of impact on the primary outcome variable. A value of 1 indicates no overlap, 1–5 is considered mild implying a reliable model, while values between 5 and 10 are suggestive of significant multicollinearity and values >10 signify an unreliable model.

The VIF in the presented model was 1.22, indicating mild overlap, but no impact on model reliability.

For further reassurance, the model was rerun without the correlated variables in all possible combinations (online supplemental table 1). Our final model including all significant variables had the lowest AIC and BIC scores, confirming it as the most reliable model.

Therefore, the final AFT model analysing the effect of anaesthesia type on battery life included electrode target and stimulation setting as control variables. Additional covariates, including battery model and patient diagnosis, were initially considered but excluded from the final model because they were not statistically significant and worsened AIC/BIC fit. Lastly, a gamma-distributed frailty term was added to capture random effects associated with cluster-level unobserved heterogeneity.

In order to translate the AFT coefficients into easily understandable terms, time ratios were calculated as exp(β) and absolute differences in median battery survival were derived by applying these ratios to the baseline median survival estimated from the globus pallidus interna (GPi)/GA reference group.

Results

217 patients met inclusion criteria. Baseline demographics were similar between patients who underwent lead implantation under GA group (ie, asleep surgery) versus conscious sedation (MAC group, ie, awake surgery), with comparable mean ages and gender distribution (table 1). Parkinson’s disease severity as quantified by MDS-UPDRS -III off scores were similar between groups with mean MDS-UPDRS-III off scores of 40.642 (SD 12.55) in the GA group and 40.596 (SD 16.26) in the MAC group. The distribution of patients with Parkinson’s disease and dystonia were additionally similar between groups (table 1). A subthalamic nucleus (STN) lead target was statistically significantly more common in the GA cohort (56.5%) compared with the MAC cohort (16.4%), while ventral intermediate nucleus (VIM) and GPi lead targets were statistically significantly more common in the MAC cohort (29.1% and 54.5%, respectively) compared with the GA cohort (15.7% and 27.7%, respectively). Mean stimulation amplitude and pulse width were statistically significantly different between groups with mean amplitude of 2.996 mA (SD 1.1 mA) in the MAC group compared with 2.641 mA (SD 1 mA) in the GA group and mean pulse width of 72.75 μ s (SD 15.83 μ s) in the MAC group compared with 65.56 μ s (SD 14.81 μ s) in the GA group.

The effect of relevant variables on our primary outcome variable of IPG battery longevity was assessed using an AFT model as outlined in the methods. Results of the AFT model are illustrated in table 2. MAC was found to significantly negatively affect IPG battery longevity by a coefficient of −0.149, 95% CI −0.27 to −0.03 compared with GA. Lead placement in the STN and VIM significantly positively impacted battery longevity with AFT coefficients of 0.223, 95% CI 0.10 to 0.35 and 0.134, 95% CI 0.01 to 0.26, respectively, compared with GPi implantation. Higher amplitude settings significantly negatively impacted battery longevity with an AFT coefficient of −0.167, 95% CI 0.21 to −0.12. No other stimulation settings were found to significantly affect battery longevity in our cohort, pulse width AFT coefficient found to be −0.004, 95% CI −0.01 to 0 and frequency AFT coefficient found to be −0.001, 95% CI 0.00 to 0.00. Diagnosis of either Parkinson’s disease (AFT coefficient −0.193, 95% CI −0.44 to 0.05 or essential tremor (AFT coefficient −0.063, 95% CI −0.48 to 0.35) was not found to significantly impact battery longevity. Interestingly, stimulation mode (ie, monopolar vs bipolar) did not significantly impact battery longevity in our patient cohort (AFT coefficient 0.103, 95% CI −0.03 to 0.24). Additionally, no specific battery models were found to significantly impact battery longevity in our cohort. Represented models included Abbott Infinity 5, Abbott Infinity 7, Activa PC, Activa SC and Percept PC (table 2).

A covariate analysis was conducted given significant differences in distribution of patient demographic variables within MAC versus GA cohorts as well as observed statistically significant effects of electrode targeting and amplitude on battery longevity (online supplemental table 1). Without the inclusion of electrode target as a control variable, MAC statistically significantly decreases battery longevity with an AFT coefficient of −0.221, 95% CI −0.332 to −0.111, p value <0.001 and without inclusion of amplitude as a control variable the AFT coefficient was −0.199, 95% CI −0.329 to −0.070, p value=0.003.

With the inclusion of electrode targeting and amplitude as covariates the effect of MAC versus GA on battery life remained statistically significant, albeit attenuated, with an AFT coefficient of −0.149, 95% CI −0.265 to −0.033, p value=0.012 (table 2).

Discussion

DBS IPG battery life is a clinically relevant measure that impacts how patients are counselled regarding surgery and drives patient and provider surgical decision making. In our cohort of 217 patients, we compared DBS IPG battery longevity in patients who underwent DBS placement under GA compared with those who underwent DBS placement under conscious sedation (MAC) using a well-controlled AFT model. We have identified that IPG batteries in the GA cohort lasted approximately 5.79 months longer compared with the MAC cohort, a statistically significant difference. This significant difference was maintained after controlling for other factors known to impact battery life such as lead target16 and stimulation parameters.17 Variables that did not significantly impact battery longevity in our cohort (table 2) were excluded from the final model (ie, diagnosis5 and disease severity).18 This difference persisted after adjusting for known determinants of battery life, including lead target, stimulation parameters, diagnosis and disease severity, and constitutes a novel finding that could be used to inform preoperative counselling and surgical decision making.

In the analysed patient population, GA versus MAC anaesthesia served as a proxy for direct versus indirect targeting since MRI-guided DBS lead implantations were only performed in asleep cases and electrophysiological frame-based indirect targeting implantations were performed in awake cases. However, our data does not directly measure lead accuracy, thus it cannot be determined whether the effect of asleep versus awake surgery on battery longevity for our patients is due to improved targeting alone or an unknown effect of anaesthesia on subsequent stimulation efficacy.

With regard to target accuracy, prior research has demonstrated that clinical outcomes of direct targeting are not inferior to indirect targeting.19 Furthermore, Lahtinen et al described that patients with Parkinson’s disease who underwent STN DBS electrode placement had a reduced levodopa burden when direct targeting was used during lead implantation.13 They postulate that this decreased need for levodopa in the direct targeting group is due to improved target accuracy and thus improved symptom management.13 Additionally, Melo et al demonstrated improved accuracy in GPi lead placement with direct targeting which was attributed to the ability to account for individual anatomic variation when using direct targeting.20 A meta-analysis by Borges et al reported increased accuracy of GPi lead placement and decreased stimulation amplitude and pulse width in patients who underwent asleep (direct) versus awake (indirect) DBS lead placement.14 Indeed, previous work indicates that misplaced electrodes may necessitate greater volume of tissue activation thus increasing the total electrical energy delivered (TEED) and depleting batteries at a faster rate.10 21 In the context of prior evidence demonstrating superior targeting accuracy with direct methods and increased adverse outcomes with suboptimally placed electrodes, our findings indicate that asleep DBS placement may translate these advantages into meaningful gains in battery longevity.22

We additionally found improved IPG battery longevity when leads were placed in the STN or VIM compared with GPi. This finding is in line with previous findings of increased battery longevity in STN implantation compared with GPi implantation. This difference has been attributed to higher stimulation parameters in GPi-implanted patients and significant anatomic variation between these two targets, with the GPi roughly three times the size of STN, therefore presumably requiring higher charge density.9 22 Our findings of roughly 9.2 months increased battery longevity in STN placement compared with GPi placement are closely aligned with recent work by Yazdanian et al which has found median battery longevity in STN-targeted patients was 78.2 compared with 69.8 in GPi-targeted patients.9 For patients with Parkinson’s disease, multiple randomised controlled trials have demonstrated no clinically significant difference in motor symptom control between STN and GPi targets,112326 with some studies suggesting that electrode implantation in STN may lead to a reduction of Parkinson’s disease medications by up to 50%, a reduction not observed in GPi implantation.26 27 Therefore, appropriately selected patients could benefit from STN targeting with respect to reduced downstream battery replacements.28 29

Our findings suggest that in addition to appropriate patient selection and multidisciplinary shared decision making prior to DBS implantation, consideration of the target (STN vs GPi vs VIM) as well as the method of targeting (direct vs indirect) should be considered as it relates to IPG battery longevity. For example, in our cohort, a Parkinson’s disease patient who undergoes direct targeting of the STN can expect to have an IPG battery life that is on average 14.9 months longer than a comparable patient undergoing indirect targeting in the GPi.

Study limitations and future directions

This was a single-centre, retrospective chart review. At our centre, DBS placement over the study period was performed predominantly by two surgeons and, thus, external validity may pose a concern. The retrospective nature of this study impacted data analysis and participant number due to insufficient data recording in our EMR. Specifically, TEED could not be analysed as part of this study given rare routine recordings of impedance data for DBS patients in the EMR. Additionally, the retrospective design of this study does not allow for identification of causal relationships between battery longevity and DBS target localisation and anaesthesia type at time of implantation. Furthermore, while asleep versus awake DBS placement can be considered as proxies for target localisation, this study does not assess accuracy of lead placement explicitly. Analysed stimulation parameters are limited to those recorded in the clinic and do not capture alterations made by patients independently in the home environment, which could affect battery longevity.

Future work would ideally be prospective, expanded to multiple centres, allow for the collection of additional stimulation data, such as impedance, and assess target accuracy explicitly. Furthermore, it would be of interest to investigate financial implications of these results as they relate to optimisation of healthcare expenditures.
