Development and validation of a risk model for non-fatty chronic liver disease in middle-aged and older adults with metabolic syndrome
Authors: Xiumei Zhou, Xiaoxin Wu, Lingxiao Jin, Xuhong Zhao, Qiaozhen Zhang, Junfeng Yin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNon-fatty chronic liver disease (CLD) is a major global health burden. Individuals with metabolic syndrome (MetS) have a substantially increased risk of developing non-fatty CLD. This study aimed to develop and validate risk prediction models for non-fatty CLD among middle-aged and older adults with MetS.MethodsData were obtained from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). The dataset was randomly divided into a training set (70%) and a testing set (30%). Least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression with ten-fold cross-validation was applied for predictor selection. Two risk prediction models and nomograms were constructed to predict the 9-year risk of incident non-fatty CLD: a non-laboratory model and a laboratory-based model. Model performance was evaluated using receiver operating characteristic curves and the area under the curve (AUC), calibration curves, decision curve analysis (DCA), and clinical impact curves (CIC).ResultsAmong 4,748 participants, 253 developed non-fatty CLD during the approximately 9-year follow-up period, yielding a cumulative incidence of 5.33%. LASSO regression identified six non-laboratory predictors (male sex, dyslipidemia, heart disease, kidney disease, digestive system disease, and lack of piped water) in non-laboratory model and two additional laboratory indicators (low platelet count and high triglyceride level) in laboratory model. The non-laboratory model achieved AUCs of 0.762 (95% CI: 0.720–0.803) and 0.744 (95% CI: 0.680–0.808) in the training and testing sets, respectively, while the laboratory model showed improved discrimination with AUCs of 0.792 (95% CI: 0.754–0.830) and 0.769 (95% CI: 0.712–0.825). Calibration curves demonstrated good agreement between predicted and observed risks. DCA and CIC indicated that both models provided meaningful clinical net benefit across a wide range of risk thresholds.ConclusionBoth the non-laboratory and laboratory-based risk prediction models for 9-year risk of non-fatty CLD in individuals with MetS demonstrated good predictive performance. However, given the reliance on self-reported outcome data without objective confirmation, these findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Pending external validation, these models may eventually serve as practical tools for early risk stratification in this high-risk population.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12876-026-05129-1.Keywords: Nnon-fatty chronic liver disease, Metabolic syndrome, Nomogram, CHARLS

Introduction

Chronic liver disease (CLD) refers to a progressive dysfunction of liver function lasting more than six months, primarily including viral hepatitis, cholestatic liver diseases, autoimmune hepatitis and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease [1]. As a progressive disease, CLD can further progress to liver cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and even liver failure, ultimately leading to premature death [2]. Currently, CLD has been reported as the 11th leading cause of death worldwide, causing approximately 2 million deaths each year and accounting for about 4% of all deaths globally [3, 4]. In China, the burden of CLD is particularly substantial. It has been estimated that liver diseases affect approximately 300 million people, representing more than one-fifth of the population [5]. Given the substantial disease burden, identifying and intervening on modifiable risk factors is critical for early prevention and control.

Metabolic syndrome (MetS) is a pathologic condition characterized by metabolic abnormalities, including insulin resistance, abdominal obesity, hypertension, and hyperglycemia [6]. In recent decades, the prevalence of MetS has increased rapidly worldwide and has become an important public health concern [7]. Evidence from a recent meta-analysis indicated that MetS is significantly associated with an increased risk of liver-related events [8]. In addition, previous studies have also suggested that the synergistic effects of MetS components can promote oxidative stress and chronic inflammatory responses [9], thereby aggravating hepatocellular injury and accelerating the progression of liver fibrosis [10]. Beyond oxidative stress and inflammation, emerging evidence has revealed that MetS contributes to the progression and adverse outcomes of non-fatty CLD through multiple interconnected molecular pathways. Dysregulation of lipid metabolism, a hallmark of MetS, can exacerbate hepatic injury in adults with pre-existing non-fatty CLD by promoting systemic inflammation and metabolic dysfunction [11, 12]. Insulin resistance, a core feature of MetS, impairs insulin signaling and can aggravate pre-existing liver conditions, particularly chronic viral hepatitis, through increased free fatty acid flux and activation of pro-inflammatory cascades [13, 14]. Importantly, accumulating evidence has demonstrated that metabolic abnormalities associated with MetS accelerate liver fibrosis and increase the risk of cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma in patients with chronic hepatitis B or C, independent of viral load [15]. In this context, recent mechanistic updates have further emphasized the role of fatty acid receptor signaling in metabolic regulation [16] and epigenetic modifications [17] in liver disease pathogenesis. Collectively, these pathways establish a self-reinforcing vicious cycle between MetS and non-fatty CLD progression, underscoring the necessity of developing targeted risk prediction tools for this high-risk population. Furthermore, MetS has been shown to accelerate the progression of various CLD, including viral hepatitis and autoimmune liver disease, through mechanisms such as insulin resistance, chronic inflammation, and metabolic dysregulation [18]. These findings suggest that MetS may play an important role in the development and progression of CLD. Given the central role of the liver in metabolic regulation, an important question arises: should individuals with MetS pay greater attention to the risk of developing CLD than the general population? Moreover, it remains unclear whether the predictive performance of traditional CLD risk indicators differs in individuals with MetS.

However, most existing studies on CLD risk prediction have focused on the general population [19–21], and few have specifically targeted individuals with MetS, a group in which metabolic dysregulation can significantly modify the natural history of non-fatty CLD. Therefore, prediction tools specifically designed for this high-risk group are urgently needed but remain limited. In addition, middle-aged and older adults with MetS often face more complex health challenges [22]. Beyond metabolic abnormalities, they frequently present with multiple chronic conditions and persistent low-grade inflammation. Meanwhile, hepatic metabolic regulatory capacity gradually declines with aging [23], which may further increase susceptibility to CLD. Therefore, using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS), we aimed to develop and validate a risk prediction model for CLD among middle-aged and older adults with MetS. To improve clinical applicability, two models based on non-laboratory and incorporating laboratory indicators were constructed and evaluated for discrimination, calibration, and clinical utility analyses.

Methods

Study design and population

The data for this study were obtained from the CHARLS database (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/en) [24]. This database aims to provide a high-quality and nationally representative micro-database of Chinese residents aged 45 years and above. The project is a prospective cohort study that uses probability proportional to size (PPS) sampling. It started with a baseline survey in 2011, followed by face-to-face follow-ups every two years, with regular physical examinations and blood tests conducted between follow-ups. For the present study, we used the 2011 wave (June 2011 to March 2012) as the baseline and ascertained incident non-fatty CLD cases using data from the subsequent follow-up surveys, including the 2013 (wave 2), 2015 (wave 3), 2018 (wave 4), and 2020 (wave 5) waves. The follow-up period for outcome ascertainment thus extended from the 2011 baseline through the 2020 wave, with a total follow-up duration of approximately 9 years. The study covers 150 counties (districts) and 450 communities (villages) across 28 provinces (including municipalities and autonomous regions) in China. The CHARLS protocol was approved by the Peking University Institutional Review Board (IRB number IRB00001052-11014). All participants provided written informed consent before taking part in the study. This study is observational and our reporting follows the STROBE guidelines. The study was conducted in full accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.

This study used baseline and follow-up data from CHARLS 2011. After applying the following exclusion criteria, 4748 eligible participants were included: (1) participants under 45 years old (n = 648); (2) those without MetS at baseline (n = 11144); (3) those with CLD already present at baseline (n = 261); (4) participants with missing follow-up data (n = 184); (5) those whose CLD status could not be determined at the end of follow-up (n = 11); and (6) those with incomplete covariate data (n = 712). The study flow chart is shown in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Definition of MetS

MetS was defined according to the revised National Cholesterol Education Program Adult Treatment Panel III (ATP III) criteria for Asian populations [25]. According to this definition, participants were diagnosed with MetS if they met three or more of the following five criteria: (1) central obesity: waist circumference ≥ 90 cm in men or ≥ 80 cm in women; (2) elevated triglycerides (TG): ≥150 mg/dL (1.70 mmol/L) or receiving specific treatment for this lipid abnormality; (3) reduced high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C): <40 mg/dL (1.04 mmol/L) in men or < 50 mg/dL (1.29 mmol/L) in women, or receiving specific treatment for this lipid abnormality; (4) elevated blood pressure: systolic blood pressure ≥ 130 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 85 mmHg, or receiving antihypertensive therapy; and (5) elevated fasting plasma glucose (FPG): ≥100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L), or previously diagnosed with type 2 diabetes, or receiving antidiabetic medication. All anthropometric and biochemical indicators used to define MetS were derived from the standardized physical examinations and blood tests conducted by the CHARLS study at baseline [24].

CLD assessment

Trained investigators conducted face-to-face interviews using structured questionnaires to collect data on CLD cases [26]. Incident CLD during follow-up was defined as participants who reported no CLD at the 2011 baseline but reported a diagnosis of CLD in any of the subsequent follow-up surveys (2013, 2015, 2018, or 2020). The initial assessment of CLD was based on the question: “Have you ever been diagnosed with CLD (excluding fatty liver, tumors, and cancer) by a healthcare professional?” Therefore, the outcome of this study is specifically non-fatty CLD. Participants who reported only fatty liver disease without other types of CLD were not classified as CLD cases. This distinction is critical when interpreting our results, as the well-established mechanistic links between MetS and fatty liver disease are not directly addressed by our outcome definition. To reduce potential recall bias, participants were further asked: “Are you currently receiving any treatment for CLD or its related complications (such as traditional Chinese medicine, Western medicine, or other therapies)?” Based on the combined responses to these two questions, CLD status was classified as “yes” or “no”. It is important to acknowledge that the CHARLS questionnaire does not collect information on the specific etiology of CLD (e.g., viral hepatitis, alcoholic liver disease, autoimmune hepatitis, or other causes). Therefore, we are unable to report the etiologic composition of the outcome, and our analyses treat all non-fatty CLD cases as a single composite endpoint.

Candidate predictive variables

During the selection of potential predictor variables, we conducted an initial literature review to identify factors previously shown to be significant in predicting CLD [21, 27–29]. We identified six domains of potential predictors, including: (1) demographic and socioeconomic characteristics such as age, gender, marital status, education level, and living area; (2) medical history and health conditions covering hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, chronic lung disease, heart disease, stroke, kidney disease, digestive system diseases, arthritis or rheumatism, disability, and fall down; (3) lifestyle and behavioral factors including sleep time, smoking status, and drinking status; (4) anthropometric and composite indices such as body mass index (BMI), frailty index, triglyceride-glucose index (TyG), and its derivative index (TyG-BMI); (5) blood biochemical indicators involving complete blood cell parameters (e.g., white blood cell (WBC) count, platelet count, mean corpuscular volume (MCV), hemoglobin, hematocrit), glucose metabolism markers (blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HBA1c)), lipid profiles (total cholesterol (TC), TG, HDL-C, and low-density lipoprotein cholesterol(LDL-C)), renal function indicators (creatinine (Crea), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), uric acid (UA)), and inflammatory markers (C-reactive protein (CRP)); (6) other health-related factors including cognitive impairment, piped water, and circadian syndrome (CircS).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 23.0 and R 4.3.3. Normality of continuous variables was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test. Variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), while variables with a non-normal distribution are presented as median with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical variables as frequency (percentage). For group comparisons, independent samples t-test was used for normally distributed continuous variables, while the Mann–Whitney U test was applied for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test; Fisher’s exact test was used when expected cell counts were less than 5.

The prediction models were developed to estimate the risk of incident non-fatty CLD over the approximately 9-year follow-up period from the 2011 baseline to the 2020 survey wave. This time horizon was selected based on the availability of complete follow-up data and to provide a clinically meaningful window for risk stratification in this middle-aged and older adult population. To ensure analytical robustness, the dataset was randomly split into a training set (70%, n = 3323) and a testing set (30%, n = 1425). In the training set, least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO) logistic regression was applied for automated feature selection. The optimal penalty parameter λ was determined through ten-fold cross-validation, and the “λ.1se” rule was adopted to balance model simplicity and predictive accuracy. Subsequently, variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among the predictors selected by LASSO. We developed two types of risk prediction models: one based solely on non-laboratory indicators and another incorporating laboratory indicators. We chose logistic regression for primary model development because it directly estimates the absolute probability of incident CLD within a fixed time horizon (approximately 9 years), which is more clinically interpretable for risk stratification than hazard ratios from Cox models. Additionally, logistic regression combined with nomograms provides a straightforward, point-based risk estimation tool suitable for community and hospital settings. The interval-censored nature of the CHARLS biennial assessments makes exact event time estimation challenging, whereas logistic regression relies only on whether an event occurred during follow-up. To address competing risks, we conducted a complementary Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model as a sensitivity analysis to verify the robustness of our findings.

A three-part evaluation framework was employed to comprehensively assess model performance. First, discrimination was evaluated by constructing receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves and calculating the area under the curve (AUC) with its 95% confidence interval (CI) [30]. Second, calibration was assessed by plotting calibration curves to examine agreement between predicted and observed probabilities [31]. Third, decision curve analysis (DCA) and clinical impact curve (CIC) were used to quantify the net clinical benefit across a range of threshold probabilities [32]. In addition, classification-based metrics including sensitivity, specificity, Brier score, positive likelihood ratio (PLR), negative likelihood ratio (NLR), and diagnostic odds ratio (DOR) were derived to comprehensively evaluate the diagnostic performance of the models. To assess whether exclusion of participants with incomplete covariate data (n = 712) introduced selection bias, we applied the previously established models to these excluded participants and evaluated model performance using AUCs. To address the potential impact of undiagnosed CLD cases on model performance—a key concern given the self-reported nature of CLD diagnosis—we conducted a post-hoc sensitivity analysis. Specifically, we restricted the analysis to participants who reported at least one outpatient visit or hospitalization in the past year. The rationale was that individuals with recent healthcare contact would have a higher likelihood of receiving a formal CLD diagnosis if present, thereby reducing the proportion of undiagnosed cases in the non-CLD group. Given that death is a competing event for CLD in this middle-aged and older adult population (participants who die cannot subsequently develop CLD), we conducted a post-hoc competing risks analysis using the Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model. CLD was treated as the event of interest (cause = 1) and all-cause death as the competing event (cause = 2). The analysis was applied separately to the predictors of the non-laboratory model and the laboratory model. Subdistribution hazard ratios (SHRs) with 95% confidence intervals were calculated, and model discrimination was assessed using the C-index for competing risks. Finally, subgroup analyses were conducted separately for the two types of risk prediction models to further assess the stability of their predictive performance.

Results

Baseline characteristics

Among 17,708 participants identified in the 2011 CHARLS wave, 12,960 were excluded based on the exclusion criteria, resulting in a final sample of 4,748 participants. There were 253 CLD events recorded, corresponding to an incidence rate of 5.33%. In the overall cohort, the mean age was 60.04 years, 60.28% were female, 82.79% were married, 74.39% had primary education or less, and 63.40% lived in rural areas. The three most prevalent comorbidities were arthritis or rheumatism (41.01%), hypertension (39.26%), and digestive system disease (25.29%). The dataset was split in a 7:3 ratio into a training set (3,323 individuals) and a testing set (1,425 participants). Baseline characteristics did not differ significantly between the training and testing sets except for gender and LDL-C levels (Table 1). Of the 5,460 eligible participants, 712 (13.04%) were excluded due to incomplete covariate data. The proportion of missing data for each variable is summarized in Supplementary Table 1. As shown in Supplementary Table 2, excluded participants had higher burdens of metabolic abnormalities (hypertension, dyslipidemia, diabetes, heart disease, BMI, TyG, and TG), but CLD incidence did not differ significantly between the two groups (5.33% vs. 5.62%, P = 0.818).

Screening for predictors indicating CLD risk

Table 2 summarizes the baseline characteristics of participants in the training set. Univariate analysis identified 14 variables significantly associated with CLD incidence: gender, hypertension, dyslipidemia, chronic lung disease, heart disease, kidney disease, digestive system disease, arthritis or rheumatism, piped water, frailty index, MCV, platelet count, TG, and HDL-C (all P < 0.05). We then separately entered the 24 non-laboratory indicators and the full set of 41 indicators including laboratory measures into LASSO models for predictor selection. The selection process retained the following six important non-laboratory indicators: gender (LASSO coefficient = 0.007), dyslipidemia (LASSO coefficient = 0.653), heart disease (LASSO coefficient = 0.204), kidney disease (LASSO coefficient = 1.462), digestive system disease (LASSO coefficient = 0.507), and piped water (LASSO coefficient = 0.090) (Fig. 1A-B & Supplementary Table 3). The selection with laboratory indicators included all six of the above non-laboratory indicators plus platelet count and TG (Fig. 1C-D). The LASSO regression coefficients for the 8-item indicators are detailed in Supplementary Table 4. Subsequent multicollinearity diagnostics for the 6-indicator and 8-indicator sets showed that all VIF were below 5 and tolerance values were above 0.1, indicating no substantial multicollinearity issues (Supplementary Tables 5–6).

Models development

Forward stepwise selection was used to enter the 6-item and 8-item indicators into multivariable logistic regression models, constructing a non-laboratory model and a laboratory model. The non-laboratory model showed that male gender (OR = 1.640, 95% CI: 1.184–2.274), dyslipidemia (OR = 2.653, 95% CI: 1.823–3.815), heart disease (OR = 1.672, 95% CI: 1.136–2.425), kidney disease (OR = 5.039, 95% CI: 3.376–7.421), digestive system disease (OR = 2.403, 95% CI: 1.726–3.337), and lack of piped water (OR = 1.683, 95% CI: 1.218–2.328) were independently associated with CLD (Fig. 2A). A nomogram for the non-laboratory model based on these six variables is presented in Fig. 2B. The laboratory model similarly showed independent associations with CLD for male gender (OR = 1.543, 95% CI: 1.109–2.149), dyslipidemia (OR = 2.626, 95% CI: 1.790–3.808), heart disease (OR = 1.658, 95% CI: 1.119–2.419), kidney disease (OR = 5.179, 95% CI: 3.438–7.708), digestive system disease (OR = 2.425, 95% CI: 1.734–3.383), lack of piped water (OR = 1.736, 95% CI: 1.253–2.411), lower platelet count (OR = 0.994, 95% CI: 0.992–0.997), and higher TG level (OR = 1.002, 95% CI: 1.001–1.003) (Fig. 2C). The nomogram for the laboratory model based on these eight variables is shown in Fig. 2D.

We also conducted case analyses based on the non-laboratory model and the laboratory model separately. For example, in the non-laboratory model, a male patient with dyslipidemia, kidney disease, digestive system disease, and no access to piped water had a predicted CLD risk of 56.60%, which was well above the probability corresponding to the cutoff of 0.058 (Supplementary Fig. 2). In the laboratory model, a male patient with the same conditions—dyslipidemia, kidney disease, digestive system disease, no piped water, a platelet count of 237 × 10⁹/L, and a triglyceride level of 89.39 mg/dL—had a predicted CLD risk of 47.30%, also substantially higher than the probability corresponding to the cutoff of 0.060 (Supplementary Fig. 3).

Models evaluation

The AUC was calculated to evaluate the models’ ability to discriminate CLD in adults with MetS. The non-laboratory model had an AUC of 0.762 (95% CI: 0.720–0.803) in the training set and 0.744 (95% CI: 0.680–0.808) in the testing set. The laboratory model had an AUC of 0.792 (95% CI:0.754–0.830) in the training set and 0.769 (95% CI: 0.712–0.825) in the testing set. These results indicate that the nomograms have good discriminative ability and predictive value (Fig. 3).

Calibration of the prediction models was assessed using calibration curves. The calibration curves of the nomograms showed close agreement between predicted and observed risks (Fig. 4). The Brier scores for the non-laboratory model were 0.045 (95% CI: 0.039–0.051) in the training set and 0.050 (95% CI: 0.040–0.060) in the testing set. The Brier scores for the laboratory model were 0.045 (95% CI: 0.039–0.050) in the training set and 0.051 (95% CI: 0.041–0.060) in the testing set.

At threshold probabilities between 0.1 and 0.6, using the models to guide intervention yielded higher net benefit than treating all or no patients, highlighting the clinical value of the prediction models (Fig. 5). Sensitivity analysis

Applying the previously established models to the 712 excluded participants yielded AUCs of 0.751 (non-laboratory) and 0.776 (laboratory), comparable to the primary estimates (0.762 and 0.792), suggesting that exclusion of these participants did not substantially bias the results. In the sensitivity analysis restricted to participants with at least one outpatient visit or hospitalization in the past year (n = 1,278), the non-laboratory model achieved an AUC of 0.743 (95% CI: 0.686–0.800), and the laboratory model achieved an AUC of 0.761 (95% CI: 0.707–0.815). The corresponding ROC curves for this sensitivity analysis subsample are presented in Supplementary Fig. 4, which visually confirms the maintained discriminative ability of both models. Complete performance metrics are summarized in Supplementary Table 7. Compared with the original training set estimates, both models showed slightly lower AUCs. The laboratory model demonstrated increased sensitivity (0.720 vs. 0.682) but decreased specificity (0.737 vs. 0.816), reflecting a trade-off in diagnostic performance. The Brier scores increased to 0.061 for both models, indicating somewhat reduced calibration accuracy in this subsample. The diagnostic odds ratios decreased from 7.271 to 6.491 (non-laboratory model) and from 9.315 to 6.870 (laboratory model).

Competing risks analysis

To account for death as a competing event, we performed a Fine-Gray subdistribution hazards model. The results are summarized in Table 4. For the non-laboratory model, all six predictors remained statistically significant, with kidney disease showing the strongest association (SHR = 4.204, 95% CI: 2.897–6.101, P < 0.001), followed by dyslipidemia (SHR = 2.497, 95% CI: 1.768–3.525, P < 0.001) and digestive system disease (SHR = 2.192, 95% CI: 1.602–3.000, P < 0.001). The C-index for the non-laboratory model was 0.754 (95% CI: 0.711–0.797). For the laboratory model, the additional predictors—platelet count (SHR = 0.995, 95% CI: 0.992–0.997, P < 0.001) and triglycerides (SHR = 1.002, 95% CI: 1.001–1.003, P = 0.002)—remained significant. The C-index for the laboratory model was 0.778 (95% CI: 0.735–0.821). The cumulative incidence function (CIF) curves depicting the competing risks between non-fatty CLD and all-cause death are provided in Supplementary Fig. 5. Compared with the primary AUCs (0.762 for non-laboratory model and 0.792 for laboratory model), the C-indices from the competing risks analysis were modestly lower, suggesting that competition from death slightly attenuates model discrimination. However, the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of all predictor associations remained consistent with the primary analysis, confirming the robustness of our findings.

Subgroup analysis

To assess the stability of our models across different populations, we conducted subgroup analyses stratified by education level (below high school vs. high school and above), smoking status (yes vs. no), and drinking status (yes vs. no). The complete results are presented in Supplementary Table 8. In brief, both models demonstrated consistent discriminative performance across all subgroups. For the non-laboratory model, AUCs ranged from 0.676 to 0.771 across education subgroups, from 0.718 to 0.778 across smoking subgroups, and from 0.700 to 0.771 across drinking subgroups in the training set. For the laboratory model, the corresponding AUC ranges were 0.725–0.800, 0.754–0.818, and 0.720–0.804, respectively. All confidence intervals overlapped substantially, indicating no statistically significant differences between subgroups. These findings suggest that both models maintain stable predictive performance regardless of education level, smoking status, or drinking status.

Discussion

Previous studies have indicated that the adverse effects of MetS on hepatic function and overall metabolic status not only accelerate the progression of non-fatty CLD but may also create a self-reinforcing vicious cycle. Therefore, strengthening screening and implementing comprehensive intervention strategies for non-fatty CLD among individuals with MetS is critical. Based on data from CHARLS, we developed two risk prediction models for non-fatty CLD applicable among middle-aged and older adults with MetS. Both the non-laboratory and laboratory models showed good discrimination and predictive performance. These models may facilitate early identification of high-risk individuals and support timely interventions, thereby potentially improving long-term clinical outcomes. In interpreting these findings, it is important to recognize that MetS is likely to operate not as a direct cause of non-fatty CLD, but as a metabolic aggravating factor that accelerates disease progression in individuals with pre-existing liver disease of other etiologies, particularly chronic viral hepatitis. This conceptual reframing aligns our mechanistic interpretation with the likely disease spectrum in this Chinese middle-aged and older adult population.

Although numerous prediction models have been developed for the diagnosis of liver diseases, previous studies have largely focused on the general population [20] or on specific liver diseases, such as metabolic dysfunction-associated fatty liver disease (MAFLD) [33, 34]. In contrast, relatively few studies have specifically explored individuals with MetS, a population at particularly high risk for CLD. Given the substantial threat posed by MetS to liver health, developing risk prediction models tailored to this high-risk population is of considerable clinical importance. Nomograms provide intuitive visual tools for risk assessment by illustrating the relationship between predictors and the probability of disease occurrence. They also allow clinicians to perform rapid and individualized risk estimation based on different levels of risk factors [35, 36]. In this study, we therefore developed two risk prediction models and corresponding nomograms to estimate the risk of CLD among middle-aged and older adults with MetS. Furthermore, LASSO regression was applied for variable selection to identify the most relevant predictors while minimizing the risk of overfitting. Combined with multivariable logistic regression and multicollinearity diagnostics, this approach enhanced the robustness and stability of the prediction models.

To address screening needs across different clinical settings, we developed two risk prediction models for CLD in middle-aged and older adults with MetS. A non-laboratory model enables simple and scalable primary screening in community healthcare settings. In contrast, a laboratory-based model incorporating routine biochemical indicators provides improved predictive performance and is therefore more suitable for precise assessment in hospitals or health examination centers. Moreover, all candidate variables were easily obtainable clinical indicators, ensuring the practicality and scalability of the models [37]. Both models demonstrated good discrimination, calibration, and clinical net benefit, suggesting their complementary value in facilitating a continuum of care from community-based screening to hospital-based precision management.

In the model constructed using non-laboratory indicators, male, dyslipidemia, heart disease, kidney disease, digestive system diseases, and lack of piped water were identified as significant predictors of CLD in population with MetS. Previous studies have also reported that males have a higher risk of developing liver diseases, including MAFLD, compared with females [38, 39]. Although sex is a non-modifiable biological factor, it may serve as a clear screening indicator, suggesting that CLD screening should be prioritized in male adults with MetS. Notably, kidney disease showed a particularly strong association with CLD in this study, with OR of 5.039 (95% CI: 3.376–7.421). This finding is consistent with previous research [28], which also reported a bidirectional association between liver and kidney diseases and highlighted the concept of hepatorenal syndrome. Currently, growing evidences further supports the close interaction between the liver and kidneys [40]. In addition, Pérez-Escobar et al. proposed the concept of the “hepatic–renal–metabolic axis,” emphasizing that liver and kidney diseases should be considered as part of a shared metabolic–inflammatory continuum requiring integrated management [41]. Therefore, joint monitoring of liver and kidney function may be particularly important in the clinical management of adults with MetS. Another notable finding of this study was that lack of piped water was identified as an independent predictor and included in both prediction models (OR = 1.683 in the non-laboratory model and OR = 1.736 in the laboratory model), highlighting an important public health dimension. However, we acknowledge that this variable may also serve as a proxy for healthcare access and socioeconomic status, raising the possibility that the observed association partly reflects diagnostic opportunity bias rather than a purely biological effect. We have attempted to partially address this by adjusting for education, living area, and healthcare contact in our analyses, but residual confounding cannot be fully excluded. As a composite socio-environmental indicator, lack of piped water access may reflect multiple underlying vulnerabilities, including poor water quality, lower socioeconomic status, and inadequate sanitation infrastructure [42, 43]. In such settings, reliance on unregulated water sources may increase exposure to heavy metals and waterborne hepatitis viruses [44–46], which may collectively contribute to the development of CLD. These findings suggest that improving sanitation infrastructure and ensuring access to safe drinking water may play an important role in the primary prevention of CLD.

Beyond the liver-specific perspective, the risk factors identified in our study—dyslipidemia, elevated triglycerides, and kidney disease—should be interpreted within the broader cardiometabolic risk continuum. Individuals with MetS often exhibit clustering of interrelated abnormalities that simultaneously increase the risk of cardiovascular disease, type 2 diabetes, chronic kidney disease, and CLD [17]. Dyslipidemia and hypertriglyceridemia are not only established predictors of atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease but also key drivers of hepatic injury and fibrosis in various CLD [47]. The strong association between kidney disease and CLD observed in our study (OR ≈ 5.0) reflects the concept of a “hepatic-renal-metabolic axis,” wherein liver and kidney dysfunction reinforce each other through shared pathways such as systemic inflammation and oxidative stress [48]. As Wang et al. have synthesized, traditional cardiovascular risk factors and novel metabolic biomarkers jointly contribute to disease development across multiple organ systems, highlighting the need for integrated risk assessment beyond single-organ frameworks [49]. Furthermore, dietary factors may exacerbate metabolic steatotic liver disease and influence CLD progression in individuals with MetS [50]. Therefore, our findings support that CLD should be considered an integral component of the multisystem cardiometabolic disease continuum, warranting collaborative management across endocrinology, nephrology, cardiology, and hepatology.

Furthermore, we developed an optimized model incorporating laboratory indicators. Compared with the non-laboratory model, the optimized model additionally included higher TG level and low platelet count, which further improved its predictive performance (AUC = 0.769 in the testing set). Previous studies have also demonstrated that elevated TG levels are closely associated with the development and progression of CLD [51, 52], potentially through mechanisms such as systemic inflammation, insulin resistance, and metabolic dysfunction [53, 54]. Notably, our study identified a significant association between low platelet count and CLD. Previous evidence has also shown that low platelet count is associated with the progression of CLD [55]. Emerging evidence suggests that platelets play important roles in liver regeneration, immune regulation, and fibrosis progression [56, 57]. Reduced platelet counts may therefore impair hepatic repair and promote liver injury, potentially contributing to the development and progression of CLD.

The stable performance of our models across educational strata carries important clinical implications, particularly for the non-laboratory model intended for community-based screening. Education level often serves as a proxy for health literacy and socioeconomic status, which can influence disease awareness, healthcare-seeking behavior, and the ability to understand risk factors. Our finding that the non-laboratory model performed comparably well in participants with lower education (AUC = 0.771) and those with higher education (AUC = 0.729) suggests that its predictive accuracy is not diminished by lower health literacy. This is reassuring for community screening programs, where individuals with limited education may be less aware of CLD risk and less likely to seek timely medical evaluation. The consistent performance across smoking and drinking status subgroups further supports the robustness of both models. However, we acknowledge that the sample sizes of certain subgroups (e.g., current smokers) were relatively limited, and future studies with larger samples are needed to confirm these findings. Nevertheless, the observed stability across key sociodemographic and behavioral subgroups enhances the potential generalizability of our models in real-world clinical and community settings.

This study has several strengths. First, unlike most previous prediction models developed in the general population, our study specifically focused on individuals with MetS, a high-risk subgroup, thereby addressing the need for refined risk stratification in this population. Second, the analysis was based on the CHARLS dataset, which provides a large sample size, long follow-up period, and rigorously quality-controlled data, supporting the robustness and potential generalizability of our findings. Third, we developed two prediction models tailored to different clinical settings, enabling simplified community-based screening and more precise risk assessment in hospital settings.

Several limitations should also be considered. First, the diagnosis of CLD relied on self-reported information rather than imaging or histopathological confirmation, which may introduce recall bias or lead to underestimation of disease prevalence. Furthermore, because the CHARLS questionnaire specifically excludes fatty liver by self-report rather than by objective assessment, the ‘non-fatty’ boundary may not be pathologically clean. Advanced metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease with cirrhosis may be reported as ‘CLD’ whereas simple steatosis might be excluded, meaning that the dichotomy on which our outcome definition rests may not exist cleanly at the level of the self-reported data. We therefore acknowledge that our findings should be interpreted with this caveat in mind. To address potential undiagnosed CLD bias, we conducted a sensitivity analysis restricted to participants with recent healthcare contact (n = 1,278). Both models maintained acceptable discrimination, though with slightly lower AUCs than the original estimates. The modest performance decline may reflect greater clinical complexity in this subsample (e.g., more comorbidities), which could introduce noise. Notably, the laboratory model showed improved sensitivity but lower specificity, a trade-off not captured by AUC alone. These findings suggest that self-report bias impacts model performance in complex ways that may vary across subgroups. External validation using objective CLD assessments is needed. Additionally, self-reported CLD outcomes are subject not only to recall bias but also to differential diagnostic opportunities across subgroups. The “lack of piped water” variable, while valuable as a socio-environmental indicator, may also capture differences in healthcare access, health literacy, and socioeconomic status, factors that could influence the likelihood of receiving a formal CLD diagnosis. Consequently, the observed association between “lack of piped water” and CLD may partly reflect diagnostic and reporting biases rather than true disease risk. We partially addressed this by adjusting for education, living area, and healthcare contact, but residual confounding remains possible. In addition, recall bias may differ across subgroups. Rural participants (63.40% of our cohort) have lower healthcare access and health literacy than urban residents, potentially leading to higher rates of undiagnosed CLD and greater underreporting. Conversely, urban residents may have higher reported CLD prevalence due to greater diagnostic opportunities rather than higher true incidence. This differential misclassification could affect model generalizability across urban-rural settings. Second, the study population included only individuals aged ≥ 45 years, and the applicability of the models to younger populations with MetS requires further validation. Third, although multiple potential confounders were adjusted for, residual confounding from unmeasured factors, such as dietary patterns and medication use, cannot be completely excluded. Fourth, while we performed internal validation by splitting the dataset into training and testing sets, external validation in an independent cohort is currently absent. Internal validation alone cannot fully establish the generalizability of our models, as it does not account for potential differences in population characteristics, healthcare systems, or disease definitions across different settings. Therefore, our models should be considered exploratory at this stage, and we do not recommend clinical implementation without further external validation. To address this gap, we plan to externally validate our models in the following steps: (1) a separate cohort from the CHARLS 2015 or 2018 wave (different time period) to assess temporal stability; (2) an independent Chinese population-based cohort, such as the China Multi-Ethnic Cohort (CMEC) or a local hospital-based registry, to evaluate geographic generalizability; and (3) if data availability permits, international cohorts (e.g., NHANES in the United States or ELSA-Brasil) to test cross-cultural applicability. We acknowledge that these external validations require collaborative efforts and data sharing, which we are actively pursuing. Until such validations are completed, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating rather than practice-changing. Fifth, while we conducted a Fine-Gray competing risks analysis to account for death as a competing event, the C-indices (0.754 for the non-laboratory model and 0.778 for the laboratory model) were modestly lower than the primary AUCs (0.762 and 0.792, respectively). This suggests that competition from death slightly attenuates model discrimination, which is expected given the age of our cohort (mean 60 years). However, the direction, magnitude, and statistical significance of all predictor associations remained consistent with the primary analysis, supporting the robustness of our findings. Future studies with longer follow-up and larger numbers of competing events are needed to further validate these results. Sixth, our outcome definition specifically excludes fatty liver disease, as the CHARLS questionnaire asks about CLD diagnosis “excluding fatty liver, tumors, and cancer.” This is an important distinction because MetS is most strongly associated with non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD). Consequently, our study predicts non-fatty CLD rather than CLD in the broadest sense. Readers should interpret our findings with this caveat in mind.

Conclusion

In conclusion, we developed and validated two nomogram-based models for predicting non-fatty CLD risk among middle-aged and older adults with MetS. Both models demonstrated good predictive performance and potential clinical utility. Given the inherent limitations of self-reported outcome data, our findings should be considered exploratory and hypothesis-generating at this stage. Pending external validation in independent cohorts with objective outcome ascertainment, these models may provide practical tools for community-based screening and hospital-based risk assessment, may support early risk stratification and targeted screening strategies in high-risk populations. Future studies are needed to externally validate these models in other populations and to evaluate their potential application in real-world clinical settings.
