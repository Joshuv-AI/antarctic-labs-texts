Health behaviours as a mediator between health locus of control and cardiovascular risk in young adults with congenital heart defects
Authors: Renata Mroczkowska, Katarzyna Szwamel, Elżbieta Szlenk-Czyczerska, Roland Fiszer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractYoung adults with adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) often present modifiable cardiovascular risk factors. We examined whether health behaviours mediate the associations between health locus of control (HLoC) and cardiovascular risk. In 201 young adults with ACHD (18–31 years), we assessed HLoC using the Multidimensional Health Locus of Control (MHLC) scale, health behaviours using the Health Behaviour Inventory (HBI), and cardiovascular risk using the exploratory ASCRF index. We estimated standardised indirect effects (β_indirect) with 95% confidence intervals using Monte Carlo simulations. The mean ASCRF score was 2.78 ± 1.32, and unhealthy dietary habits (76.62%) and overweight/obesity (50.25%) were common. Internal HLoC was associated with a lower ASCRF, whereas chance HLoC was associated with a higher ASCRF; powerful others HLoC showed no total association. HBI fully mediated the I-HLoC–ASCRF association (β_indirect = − 0.09; 95% CI − 0.12 to − 0.06) and partially mediated the C-HLoC–ASCRF association (β_indirect = 0.06; 95% CI 0.04 to 0.08). For P-HLoC, HBI showed inconsistent mediation (suppression; β_indirect = − 0.07; 95% CI − 0.09 to − 0.05). Subscale analyses indicated domain-specific mediating pathways involving dietary habits, preventive behaviours, and health practices, but not positive mental attitude. Introduction

The World Health Organization (WHO) emphasises the critical role of a healthy lifestyle in preventing cardiovascular diseases and improving overall quality of life1,2. In young adults with adult congenital heart disease (ACHD), maintaining health poses a particular challenge, because clinical follow up must be complemented by consistent self care and sustained lifestyle modification3–5.

In Poland, the growing prevalence of obesity and persistently low levels of physical activity among young adults highlight the need for effective prevention. According to a WHO report from 2022, only 20% of young Poles meet the recommendations for physical activity3,6,7. Physical inactivity combined with unhealthy dietary habits and chronic stress promotes lifestyle related diseases, including diabetes and cardiovascular diseases8–11. Because early health risks consolidate later in life, prevention and health education should begin early and be continuous.

Cardiovascular risk is also linked to mental health. Depression and anxiety correlate with poorer cardiovascular health indicators and less favourable risk profiles12–15. Autopsy studies indicate that atherosclerotic changes may begin as early as adolescence, which strengthens the rationale for early prevention16. Patients with ACHD may be particularly vulnerable to acquired cardiovascular conditions because of physiological predispositions and long term consequences of surgical or interventional treatment17,18. Harris et al.12 demonstrated an increased burden of modifiable cardiovascular risk factors in adolescents and adults with congenital heart defects, highlighting the need for coordinated, multidisciplinary preventive interventions.

Early adulthood (approximately 20 to 30 years) is a period of substantial physical, emotional, and social changes and a critical window for consolidating health habits. This stage is often characterised by an accumulation of risks, such as an unbalanced diet, a sedentary lifestyle, psychological stress, and limited access to prevention, which may worsen long term wellbeing19. Evidence based preventive programmes developed in countries with mature public health systems may provide transferable strategies, while research on health behaviours helps explain discrepancies between self rated health and objective indicators19.

In this study, we assumed that systematic self care, adherence to recommendations, and participation in preventive programmes support improvements in health status and reductions in cardiovascular risk. Understanding patients’ beliefs about control over health and how these translate into daily behaviours is crucial for designing targeted educational interventions20,21.

An important psychological construct is health locus of control (Health Locus of Control, HLoC), which reflects whether an individual attributes health primarily to their own actions or to external forces. Individuals with a stronger internal locus of control (I-HLoC) more often engage in health promoting behaviours, including physical activity, a balanced diet, and adherence to recommendations, which may reduce cardiovascular risk22–26. In contrast, an external orientation, particularly chance locus of control (C-HLoC) and Powerful Others (P-HLoC), has been associated with less favourable health behaviours and poorer psychological wellbeing19,26,27. Higher I-HLoC has also been linked to lower engagement in risk behaviours, such as alcohol consumption, which confirms the relevance of this construct in cardiovascular prevention25,28.

Accordingly, we examined associations between the Multidimensional Health Locus of Control (MHLC) dimensions and the burden of cardiovascular risk measured with an exploratory composite index (ASCRF), with particular emphasis on health behaviours assessed using the Health Behaviour Inventory (HBI) as mediators. The aim was to identify domain specific behavioural pathways that may support health promotion and secondary prevention strategies in young adults with ACHD by incorporating profiles of beliefs and behaviours.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted the study in 2016–2017 at the Silesian Centre for Heart Diseases (SCCS) in Zabrze, Poland, among young adults with adult congenital heart disease (ACHD) treated at the Department of Congenital Heart Defects and Paediatric Cardiology and the affiliated outpatient cardiology clinic. Participation was voluntary. Participants received full information on the study aims, procedures, and confidentiality principles and provided written informed consent. To enhance response reliability, we ensured anonymity and data protection. The SCCS management approved the protocol; we conducted the study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the STROBE guidelines for reporting observational studies. We did not offer remuneration for participation.

Participants

We aimed to recruit a representative group of young adults with ACHD; however, estimating the required sample size was challenging because national registries were incomplete. Despite the Regulation of the Minister of Health of 5 December 2014 mandating registration of congenital heart defect cases, many individuals - particularly those with mild defects or defects not diagnosed in childhood - were not included in databases29. Therefore, we assumed that, during the analysed period, Poland recorded an average of 8 congenital heart defect cases per 1,000 live births30. Based on birth data (approximately 351,100 annually) reported by Szczygielski31 and long-term survival patterns depending on defect severity, with mortality reaching nearly 30% as described by Tennant et al.32, the size of the population eligible for follow-up in adulthood remained uncertain. Moreover, only about 8% of patients with ACHD remain under the care of specialised adult cardiology services33.

On the basis of these assumptions, we recruited 235 hospitalised or outpatient-treated patients at SCCS for initial screening; 201 individuals met the inclusion criteria and provided complete data.

More recent epidemiological estimates indicate that the prevalence of congenital heart defects in Poland is approximately 8–10 per 1,000 live births, and that over 90% of affected individuals survive to adulthood. It is estimated that 150,000–180,000 adults with ACHD live in Poland; however, only about 8% remain under the care of specialised tertiary referral centres34. In the absence of a national ACHD registry, we considered the final sample of 201 patients recruited at SCCS representative of the ACHD population treated in a referral centre and sufficient for mediation analyses. Future multicentre registry-based studies are needed to increase precision and external validity.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Inclusion criteria were: (1) age 18–31 years, (2) confirmed diagnosis of ACHD, and (3) ability to complete questionnaires independently. We excluded 34 patients because they did not consent, were older than 31 years at data collection, or had coexisting somatic or mental disorders (e.g., Down syndrome or intellectual disability) that precluded reliable questionnaire completion. The final sample comprised 201 participants with complete data for analyses.

Data sources and measurements

To improve data quality, we applied triangulation35 including: (1) medical record review, (2) clinical measurements (blood pressure and anthropometry), and (3) a standardised self-report survey.

1. Medical record review.

Medical records provided clinical information on defect type, age at diagnosis, history of surgical or interventional treatment, and current clinical status. SCCS hospital registries constituted the primary data source. Treating cardiologists verified data extracted from records; in case of discrepancies, we clarified them during recruitment with input from the patient or medical staff.

2. Clinical measurements.

We measured blood pressure (BP) and anthropometric parameters (height, body weight, waist and hip circumference). We performed procedures in accordance with WHO recommendations for anthropometry36 and the standardised BP measurement protocol described by Muntner et al.37. We conducted measurements in the morning.

We measured body weight using a calibrated medical scale (accuracy 0.1 kg), with participants wearing light clothing and no shoes36,38–40. We measured height and circumferences using a non-stretchable tape in line with WHO standards36 to characterise general and abdominal obesity.

We measured BP during hospitalisation under standardised clinical conditions. We took the first measurement on both arms to identify the arm with the higher value and then obtained two subsequent measurements on that arm. We performed the first analytical reading after 5–10 min of rest and the second after approximately 10 min; we used the arithmetic mean of these two readings for analyses. We defined arterial hypertension as SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg37. Because current recommendations typically advise at least three BP measurements, the two-reading protocol should be treated as a limitation related to measurement precision.

We calculated body mass index (BMI) and waist-to-hip ratio (WHR). We defined normal BMI as 18.5–24.9 kg/m², and normal WHR thresholds as < 0.85 for women and < 0.90 for men36,41. We interpreted values above these thresholds as abdominal obesity associated with increased risk of myocardial infarction, stroke, and diabetes42. In addition, we used waist circumference to identify individuals with the MONW (metabolically obese normal-weight) phenotype among participants with BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m², in line with current medical evidence43.

3. Assessment instruments.

We used validated self-report tools and author-developed measures under standardised conditions. Participants completed questionnaires independently in a quiet environment; we emphasised confidentiality to reduce social desirability effects and recall errors. We present additional results in the supplementary materials. We also conducted a sensitivity analysis assessing potential content overlap between the Health Behaviour Inventory (HBI) and the cardiovascular risk index (ASCRF); we report results in Supplementary Table 1.

Health Behaviour Inventory (HBI). The HBI by Zygfryd Juczyński comprises 24 items assessing four domains: Proper Eating Habits (PEH), Preventive Behaviours (PB), Health Practices (HP), and Positive Mental Attitude (PMA). Participants rated the frequency of behaviours over the past 12 months on a five-point scale (1 = almost never, 5 = almost always). The total score ranges from 24 to 120, with higher values indicating greater engagement in health-promoting behaviours. The Polish version shows high internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.85)44. In Supplementary Table 1, we assessed associations between HBI subscales and the total HBI score and ASCRF variants to evaluate potential content overlap.

Multidimensional Health Locus of Control (MHLC) Scale. The Polish adaptation by Zygfryd Juczyński based on the instrument by Wallston et al.45 measures three dimensions—Internal (I-HLoC), Powerful Others (P-HLoC), and Chance (C-HLoC)—using 18 items (six per dimension). Responses are provided on a six-point scale (1 = strongly disagree, 6 = strongly agree); higher scores indicate stronger beliefs corresponding to a given health control dimension.

Author-developed Questionnaire. An author-developed survey consisting of 30 closed-ended questions was used to collect demographic, environmental, and lifestyle data, including age, sex, education, socioeconomic status, family history of premature cardiovascular disease (CVD), and health-related habits (diet, physical activity, tobacco and alcohol use). This tool enabled contextual interpretation of psychological and behavioural findings.

Author-developed cardiovascular risk factor index (ASCRF). We developed the Author-developed Cardiovascular Risk Factors index (ASCRF) to quantify cumulative exposure to cardiovascular risk factors in young adults with congenital heart disease. We based the index on established frameworks, including World Health Organization (WHO) guidelines46, CANHEART recommendations47,48, and the American Heart Association Life’s Simple 7 framework49–52. ASCRF comprises eight dichotomous risk factors: male sex, family history of early-onset cardiovascular disease, low physical activity, unhealthy dietary habits, tobacco use, hypercholesterolaemia, arterial hypertension (SBP ≥ 140 mmHg and/or DBP ≥ 90 mmHg), and overweight/obesity (BMI ≥ 25 kg/m² and/or WHR ≥ 0.85 (women) / ≥0.90 (men))53. We coded behavioural components and family history dichotomously (0/1) according to CANHEART criteria47,48. Low physical activity indicated no activity or activity undertaken at most once per week. We defined unhealthy dietary habits as consuming fruit and vegetables not daily or not at all and/or consuming fast food, highly processed snacks, sweets, and/or alcohol 2–3 times per week. We coded family history as positive when myocardial infarction or stroke occurred in the father at < 55 years or in the mother at < 65 years and/or when parental hypertension was present. We coded each factor as 0/1 and assigned 1 point if at least one criterion within a given factor was met; we did not apply additional scoring of components. The total score equalled the sum of points (possible range: 0–8), with higher values indicating greater risk exposure.

For statistical analyses, we treated ASCRF as a continuous variable and did not apply categorisation thresholds. Because ASCRF is constructed as a sum of heterogeneous dichotomous factors, we interpreted it as an exploratory composite cardiovascular risk index rather than a psychometrically validated scale; we assessed internal consistency of the components using KR-20 (0.22). In sensitivity analyses, we used ASCRF variants and present them in Supplementary Table 1.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses included assessment of associations between variables and estimation of mediation models. We assessed distribution normality using the Shapiro–Wilk test54. We selected procedures based on distribution, measurement scale, and analytical aims. When normality assumptions were not met, we used the Mann–Whitney U test (comparisons of two independent groups) and the Kruskal–Wallis test (comparisons of three or more groups). We calculated correlations using Spearman’s rho for variables with non-normal distributions and Pearson’s correlation coefficient for variables approximating normality55. We analysed ASCRF as a continuous variable in accordance with its summative structure; we did not apply categorisation thresholds.

To assess potential content overlap between HBI and ASCRF, we decomposed ASCRF into a behavioural subscore (0–3: low physical activity, unhealthy dietary habits, tobacco smoking) and a non-behavioural subscore (0–5: male sex, family history, hypercholesterolaemia, hypertension, overweight/obesity). In addition, we used Pearson correlations and linear regression models to estimate shared variance between HBI subscales (PEH, PB, PMA, HP) and ASCRF variants (Supplementary Table 1).

We estimated indirect effects using the Monte Carlo method with Iman–Conover resampling that preserves correlations (1,000 iterations) to obtain stable confidence intervals56,57. Because of multiple testing across 12 primary mediation analyses, we controlled the false discovery rate (FDR) using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure; because Monte Carlo inference was reported as 95% confidence intervals (without p-values), we derived p-values for indirect effects from the Sobel test (using the a and b path parameters and their standard errors) and present them as q_BH in Supplementary Table 2.

Given the moderate sample size and the exploratory nature of analyses, we did not include covariates (age, sex, ACHD complexity) in the mediation models; we treated this as a limitation related to potential residual confounding. Despite applying FDR control, we interpreted mediation results cautiously as exploratory. Because we evaluated 12 primary indirect effects, we introduced Benjamini–Hochberg FDR control as a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table 2) and retained an exploratory interpretation. To improve readability, we moved all tables to the supplementary materials; we present full descriptive, correlational, and regression results in Supplementary Tables 3–10, and sensitivity analyses addressing content overlap in Supplementary Table 1. We present BH–FDR correction for indirect effects in Supplementary Table 2.

Mediation analysis

We based the mediation analysis on Hayes’ framework56, drawing on the classic approach by Baron and Kenny58. Figure 1 presents the conceptual scheme. Because data were cross-sectional, we interpreted indirect effects as statistical associational indirect effects, that is, a decomposition of interdependence between variables rather than evidence of a causal mechanism.

Conceptual mediation model illustrating the relationships between the independent variable (X), the mediator (M), and the dependent variable (Y). Path a represents the effect of X on M, path b represents the effect of M on Y, path c denotes the total effect of X on Y, and path c′ denotes the direct effect of X on Y after accounting for M.

The procedure comprised: (1) estimating the X–Y association (path c), (2) estimating X–M (path a) and M–Y (path b), and (3) estimating the X–Y association while controlling for M (path c′). We identified full mediation when path c′ became non-significant after including the mediator, and partial mediation when c′ attenuated but remained statistically significant. We used the Sobel test as a confirmatory method to assess the significance of indirect effects and to quantify the indirect effect (a×b), complementing the Monte Carlo estimation58,59. We reported indirect effects as β_indirect with 95% confidence intervals. We calculated descriptive statistics (means, standard deviations, medians, and percentages). All tests were two-sided, and we set the significance level at p < 0.05. We conducted analyses in Statistica 13.1 PL (StatSoft, Inc.)60, and we verified Sobel test results using an independent online calculator61.

Interpretation of effect size

We interpreted effect sizes according to Cohen’s conventional thresholds for standardised coefficients (approximately 0.10, 0.30, and 0.50 for small, medium, and large effects, respectively)62. Given the exploratory nature of the analyses and the number of tested indirect effects, we treated these thresholds as heuristic guidance. In addition, we applied Rosenthal’s descriptors63, the empirical recommendations by Fritz et al.64, and Kenny’s methodological framework for interpreting and evaluating mediation adequacy65.

Statistical rationale

We based the decision to use mediation on the theoretical assumption that health behaviours constitute an indirect pathway linking HLoC with cardiovascular risk in young adults with ACHD. Because some behavioural variables showed non-normal distributions and the sample size was moderate, we selected non-parametric procedures and Monte Carlo simulations to obtain robust estimates. We chose the Monte Carlo method with Iman–Conover resampling to obtain precise confidence intervals for indirect effects under these conditions57. We used the Sobel test as a confirmatory procedure58,59. Because we assessed 12 primary indirect effects, we interpreted results as exploratory.

Ethical considerations

We conducted the study in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. The Bioethics Committee of the Medical University of Silesia in Katowice, Poland, approved the protocol (KNW/0022/KB/159/15). All participants provided written informed consent before enrolment. We ensured confidentiality and anonymity at every stage, and processed data in accordance with applicable regulations and institutional standards.

Results

Characteristics of the study group

The study included 201 young adults with ACHD aged 18 to 31 years. Women accounted for 52.24% (n = 105; mean age 24.60 ± 3.46 years), and men for 47.76% (n = 96; mean age 23.10 ± 3.41 years). Unhealthy dietary habits were common (76.62%; n = 154), and 50.25% of participants met the criteria for overweight or obesity. A family history of cardiovascular disease was reported by 43.28% (n = 87), and 25.37% (n = 51) declared insufficient physical activity. Smoking (11.94%), hypercholesterolaemia (5.47%), and arterial hypertension (17.41%) were less frequent (Supplementary Table 3). Clinical characteristics, including defect type, timing of diagnosis, and defect correction method, are presented in Supplementary Table 4. Because of missing data, some clinical categories do not sum to 201; for correction method, data were available for n = 186 (missing: n = 15), and percentages were calculated for n = 186.

Descriptive statistics for the questionnaires and scales

The mean ASCRF score was 2.78 ± 1.32; the median was 3 (IQR: 2–4), and the observed range was 0–6 (Supplementary Table 5). The largest subgroup had two risk factors (28.86%), followed by three (26.37%) (Supplementary Table 3). We analysed ASCRF as a continuous outcome. The mean overall HBI score was 83.16 ± 12.94. The highest mean subscale scores were observed for PMA (3.61 ± 0.62), PB (3.58 ± 0.75), and HP (3.53 ± 0.64), and the lowest for PEH (3.14 ± 0.83) (Supplementary Table 5). Participants achieved higher I-HLoC scores (26.11 ± 4.97) than on the external dimensions (P-HLoC: 21.76 ± 5.68; C-HLoC: 19.32 ± 6.29) (Supplementary Table 5). In the mediation models, we entered the MHLC dimensions as continuous predictors.

Multidimensional health locus of control and cardiovascular risk: mediating role of health behaviours

First, we estimated indirect effects between the MHLC dimensions and cardiovascular risk (ASCRF) using the overall HBI index (Fig. 2).

Conceptual mediation model linking the MHLC dimensions with cardiovascular risk (ASCRF). Path a represents the effect of each MHLC dimension on the mediator, path b represents the effect of the mediator on cardiovascular risk, path c denotes the total effect, and path c′ denotes the direct effect after accounting for the mediator.

Before including HBI, I-HLoC was associated with lower risk (c = − 0.14; p < 0.001), and C-HLoC with higher risk (c = 0.20; p < 0.001), whereas P-HLoC showed no total effect (c = 0.01; p = 0.783). After including HBI, the direct effect for I-HLoC attenuated and became non significant (c′ = −0.06; p = 0.083), which corresponded to full mediation; for C-HLoC, the direct effect remained significant (c′ = 0.14; p < 0.001), which corresponded to partial mediation. For P-HLoC, the direct effect became significant after including HBI (c′ = 0.08; p < 0.05) despite a non significant total effect, which indicated inconsistent mediation (suppression).

All MHLC→HBI paths were significant (I-HLoC: a = 0.37; P-HLoC: a = 0.26; C-HLoC: a = − 0.26; all p < 0.001), and the HBI→ASCRF paths were negative and significant (b = − 0.23, − 0.27, and − 0.22; all p < 0.001). The standardised indirect effects (a×b) were small but directionally consistent: higher I-HLoC was associated with lower ASCRF through higher HBI (β_indirect = − 0.09; 95% CI [− 0.12; −0.06]); higher P-HLoC was associated with lower ASCRF through higher HBI (β_indirect = − 0.07; 95% CI [− 0.09; −0.05]); whereas higher C-HLoC was associated with higher ASCRF through lower HBI (β_indirect = 0.06; 95% CI [0.04; 0.08]) (Supplementary Table 6, Fig. 3).

Solid lines indicate the total effect (c), and dashed lines indicate the direct effect (c′); asterisks denote statistically significant paths. The pattern of effects was consistent with full mediation for I-HLoC and partial mediation for C-HLoC.

Detailed analysis of health behaviours

Next, we tested domain specific mediators (PEH, PB, PMA, HP) to identify behavioural targets (Supplementary Tables 7–10; Figs. 4, 5, 6 and 7). After applying the Benjamini–Hochberg correction (m = 12), 9 of 12 indirect effects remained statistically significant (Supplementary Table 2).

Proper eating habits (PEH). PEH fully mediated the association between I-HLoC and ASCRF (c = − 0.14; p < 0.001; c′ = −0.06; p = 0.078; β_indirect = − 0.09; 95% CI [− 0.12; −0.06]; Sobel Z = − 7.02; p < 0.001) and partially mediated the association between C-HLoC and ASCRF (c = 0.20; p < 0.001; c′ = 0.12; p < 0.001; β_indirect = 0.07; 95% CI [0.05; 0.10]; Sobel Z = − 6.08; p < 0.001). For P-HLoC, neither the total nor the direct effect was significant (c = 0.01; p = 0.783; c′ = 0.01; p = 0.824), and the indirect effect was null (β_indirect = 0.00; 95% CI [− 0.01; 0.02]) (Supplementary Table 7, Fig. 4).

Preventive behaviours (PB). PB showed patterns of partial mediation for I-HLoC and C-HLoC and an inconsistent (suppressive) pattern for P-HLoC. After including PB, the direct effects remained significant for I-HLoC (c′ = −0.09; p < 0.01), P-HLoC (c′ = 0.09; p < 0.01), and C-HLoC (c′ = 0.16; p < 0.001). The indirect effects were significant for all three dimensions: I-HLoC (β_indirect = − 0.05; 95% CI [− 0.07; −0.03]), P-HLoC (β_indirect = − 0.08; 95% CI [− 0.11; −0.06]), and C-HLoC (β_indirect = 0.04; 95% CI [0.02; 0.06]). Sobel tests confirmed mediation for I-HLoC and C-HLoC (Z = 4.52; p < 0.001; Z = 4.14; p < 0.001) (Supplementary Table 8, Fig. 5).

Positive mental attitude (PMA). PMA did not mediate the MHLC–ASCRF relationships. The direct effects remained essentially unchanged (I-HLoC: c′ = −0.14; p < 0.001; C-HLoC: c′ = 0.19; p < 0.001; P-HLoC: c′ = 0.03; p = 0.392), and the indirect effects were negligible and non significant (I-HLoC: β_indirect = − 0.01; 95% CI [− 0.03; 0.01]; P-HLoC: β_indirect = − 0.02; 95% CI [− 0.04; 0.00]; C-HLoC: β_indirect = 0.00; 95% CI [− 0.01; 0.00]). In accordance with the BH–FDR correction, the indirect effects for PMA did not remain statistically significant for I-HLoC and C-HLoC, whereas they remained significant for P-HLoC (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Table 9, Fig. 6).

Health practices (HP). HP showed small indirect effects. After including HP, the direct effects remained significant for I-HLoC (c′ = −0.11; p < 0.001) and C-HLoC (c′ = 0.18; p < 0.001), whereas the direct effect for P-HLoC was borderline (c′ = 0.06; p = 0.064). The indirect effects were significant for I-HLoC (β_indirect = − 0.04; 95% CI [− 0.05; −0.02]) and P-HLoC (β_indirect = − 0.05; 95% CI [− 0.06; −0.03]) and small for C-HLoC (β_indirect = 0.02; 95% CI [0.00; 0.03]). After BH–FDR correction, the indirect effect for C-HLoC in the model with HP remained statistically significant (Supplementary Table 2, Supplementary Table 10, Fig. 7).

Role of health behaviours as mediators

Across models, behavioural pathways differed by domain. Dietary behaviours (PEH) most consistently explained the association between I-HLoC and ASCRF and partially explained the association between C-HLoC and ASCRF, whereas PB and HP contributed smaller but statistically reliable indirect effects. For C-HLoC, PB and HP indicated an unfavourable pathway in which stronger chance beliefs were associated with lower engagement in preventive actions and routine health practices, which in turn related to higher ASCRF. For P-HLoC, indirect effects occurred in the global HBI model and in the PB and HP models despite weak or non significant total effects, and several models met criteria for inconsistent mediation (suppression). PMA did not act as a mediator. The findings identify diet, preventive actions, and routine health practices as primary behavioural targets in young adults with ACHD.

Discussion

The study suggests that health behaviours may constitute an important mediating pathway in the association between health locus of control (HLoC) and cardiovascular risk in young adults with ACHD66. More than half of participants had at least two coexisting risk factors, most commonly unhealthy dietary habits, overweight or obesity, and insufficient physical activity. The key finding was statistical full mediation for I-HLoC and partial mediation for C-HLoC, which supports a central contribution of lifestyle related behaviours alongside other determinants.

The analyses clarified how the three HLoC dimensions relate to behavioural domains and their links with ASCRF, which may support the design of tailored prevention strategies in this clinical group21. Higher I-HLoC was associated with a more favourable behavioural profile, particularly in diet related domains, whereas higher C-HLoC was associated with less favourable behavioural engagement. For P-HLoC, the total effect on ASCRF was weak or absent, yet indirect effects emerged in the global HBI model and in PB and HP, and the co occurrence of significant indirect effects with a change in the direct effect pattern supported interpretation as inconsistent mediation (suppression).

The absence or weakness of the P-HLoC total effect may have clinical relevance in the ACHD context. In a tertiary centre with structured follow up and close supervision, P-HLoC may not differentiate lifestyle change to the same extent as beliefs in internal agency or chance. P-HLoC may also reflect adherence to recommendations and engagement with care more strongly than the lifestyle components captured by ASCRF, which could weaken its contribution to the HLoC→behaviours→risk pathway. Future studies should therefore include direct indicators of adherence and healthcare utilisation.

According to Cohen’s thresholds, Rosenthal’s descriptors, and established recommendations for mediation analyses, the indirect effects were mostly small63–65. However, the pattern supports a behavioural mechanism for I-HLoC, where the direct association attenuated after accounting for behaviours, whereas partial mediation for C-HLoC suggests contributions from additional factors, such as psychological distress or environmental constraints. This interpretation remains consistent with reports linking internal control with healthier functioning67–69.

Dietary habits constituted the most consistent pathway. Higher I-HLoC corresponded to more favourable dietary patterns, whereas higher C-HLoC was associated with poorer dietary engagement, which may contribute to overweight or obesity and elevated blood pressure in ACHD17,67. This pattern aligns with evidence linking internal control with healthier diets and external orientations with less favourable choices70–74. The Food4Me study further shows that these relationships depend on context and may respond to needs tailored support74,75; in our cohort, PEH did not mediate the P-HLoC–ASCRF association, which further highlights domain specificity.

Compared with diet, PB and HP provided smaller but reliable behavioural links between HLoC and ASCRF. This hierarchy was most evident for I-HLoC and C-HLoC, whereas PB and HP appeared more relevant for P-HLoC. The broader prevention literature identifies diet quality as a key determinant of cardiometabolic and inflammatory burden and long term cardiovascular risk76,77. Contemporary European statements place nutrition at the centre of prevention and recommend integrating dietary counselling into multidisciplinary care and patient education, including in complex cardiac populations78. Evidence supports personalised approaches79 and the importance of ongoing education and monitoring80. Although the direction of findings remains consistent with earlier work67–69, cross country differences in health beliefs, self care norms, and programme availability may lead to heterogeneous effect sizes77,81,82.

Health practices, including physical activity, contributed additional, smaller indirect effects. Higher I-HLoC was associated with greater engagement in daily health practices, which related to a more favourable ASCRF profile83. Similar associations have been described in cardiology and other chronic conditions84–86. Future research should complement self report with objective measures (for example, accelerometry and wearable device metrics) to reduce common method variance and increase measurement precision.

Preventive behaviours also contributed to the indirect pathways. Consistent with studies linking internal control with adherence oriented routines and self regulation competences, stronger I-HLoC was associated with greater engagement in PB, whereas stronger C-HLoC was associated with lower participation in preventive actions87,88. Indirect effects in PB and related domains remained small and require cautious interpretation within an exploratory mediation framework62,63. System level factors, including access to e health solutions, may further modify these relationships, and digital education may particularly support individuals with lower I-HLoC89.

We did not model risk behaviours as a separate mediator. Although smoking and alcohol consumption remain important cardiovascular threats90,91, the interpretation was therefore limited to PB and the remaining HBI domains. Cheng’s meta analysis supports the stability of the association between internal control and health behaviours across contexts92. The relatively small indirect effects in our cohort may reflect young age, the characteristics of a tertiary centre, and limited availability of structured lifestyle support in Poland. From an implementation perspective, combining behavioural counselling with e health components may strengthen lifestyle modification; meta analytic evidence links higher eHealth literacy with greater engagement in health promoting behaviours93.

Overall, the results indicate domain specific behavioural pathways linking HLoC with ASCRF in young adults with ACHD. Diet constituted the strongest pathway, whereas PB and HP provided complementary contributions. I-HLoC was associated with a more favourable behavioural profile and a lower risk burden84,94, whereas C-HLoC was associated with less favourable behavioural engagement and partial mediation. P-HLoC did not differentiate ASCRF at the total effect level, yet domain specific indirect effects and suppression patterns suggest heterogeneous mechanisms. Programmes tailored to the patient profile may increase engagement75, and strengthening behavioural pathways may support reductions in cardiovascular risk68.

Limitations

The study has limitations related to both care organisation and the analytical design. We recruited participants from a single, highly specialised cardiology centre, which may limit the generalisability of findings to the broader ACHD population; during the data collection period in Poland, a nationwide network of ESC certified ACHD centres did not operate, which may have influenced referral pathways and the clinical profile of the cohort. We did not stratify participants by defect type or complexity, and subgroup analyses would have been underpowered; despite verification of clinical data in medical records, residual confounding cannot be excluded. We did not adjust the mediation models for covariates (age, sex, defect complexity) because of a moderate sample size and the exploratory aim of the analyses, which increases vulnerability to unmeasured confounding. We based sample size planning on approximate epidemiological data available at the design stage, which may have limited estimation precision; registry based and prospective recruitment and a priori power calculations are needed.

Most behavioural data relied on self report, which carries risks of recall bias and social desirability. Future studies should combine self report with objective measures, including accelerometry or wearable device metrics, dietary and biochemical markers, cardiometabolic profiling (for example, lipids and HbA1c), and standardised blood pressure measurement under ambulatory or home conditions. The small adjusted R² values (0.02–0.14) indicate that a substantial proportion of risk variance remains unexplained and may reflect unmeasured clinical, psychosocial, and socioeconomic determinants. We did not assess broader psychological factors (for example, anxiety, depressive symptoms, perceived stress) or socioeconomic characteristics (education, financial stability, employment), which may influence both beliefs and behaviours.

Because of the cross sectional nature of the data, causal inference is not possible. Interpretation should remain cautious, as we tested multiple indirect effects (12 primary mediation analyses); although we applied Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate control as a sensitivity analysis, we still interpret the mediation findings as exploratory. We analysed ASCRF as a continuous outcome, which facilitates mediation modelling, but alternative risk categorisation may yield different patterns. ASCRF should be interpreted as an exploratory composite cardiovascular risk index, rather than a psychometrically validated scale; we did not perform formal validation, and external validation is warranted. We assessed potential overlap between self reported behaviours and behavioural ASCRF components in a sensitivity analysis (Supplementary Table 1).

Blood pressure was based on two analytical readings obtained under hospital conditions; future studies should preferably use a three measurement protocol and/or home monitoring in line with current recommendations. In addition, because we collected data in 2016–2017, changes in ACHD care organisation and lifestyle patterns may limit the contemporary relevance of the findings, and replication in newer, multicentre cohorts is needed. Future studies may also consider the Life’s Essential 8 framework, which includes sleep and broader wellbeing72.

Conclusions

In this cohort of young adults with ACHD, health behaviours mediated associations between HLoC dimensions and cardiovascular risk burden (ASCRF). For I-HLoC, the pattern corresponded to full mediation, and Proper Eating Habits (PEH) constituted the most consistent indirect pathway. For C-HLoC, partial mediation suggested that behaviours contribute alongside other determinants. P-HLoC showed no significant total effect on ASCRF; however, significant indirect effects in the global HBI model and in PB and HP, together with suppression patterns, indicate heterogeneous pathways. Associations related to physical activity should be interpreted within the Health Practices domain. Because of the cross sectional, exploratory nature of the analyses and the lack of covariate adjustment, the findings require confirmation in longitudinal studies.
