Multi-target tracking for star sensor based on CenterTrack deep learning model
Authors: Jian Guan, Hui-Yan Cheng, Yan-Peng Wu, Cong Tian, Jing-Ya Qi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractDetecting and tracking non-cooperative targets in sequential star sensor images poses several challenges in rapid expanding space involving mega constellations, commercial space activities: poor real-time performance in target acquisition, inadequate generalization to different target speeds, and dependence on the star sensor’s attitude priors. To address these challenges, an improved multi-target tracking deep learning model based on the CenterTrack model is proposed in this paper. A sophisticated training and testing dataset that closely replicates real on-orbit star sensor images is constructed. By aggregating features across the entire sequence and enhancing the target identification ability from background noise, an improved tracking accuracy is realized. These improvements reduce false positive rates by approximately 60% and lower true target miss rates by 20% compared to the baseline original CenterTrack model. Furthermore, adjusting hyperparameters and optimizing the tracking algorithm reduces target ID switching frequency by approximately 50%. Compared to the traditional algorithm, the improved model can accurately capture newly appearing targets using only two frames, achieving a six-fold speed improvement. The generalization performance is significantly improved with respect to variations in target morphology and velocity, thus a higher target speed tolerance is achieved. The proposed model eliminates the requirement of external attitude priors, thereby enhancing its robustness, and shows significant potential for emerging on-orbit non-cooperative target tracking applications.Keywords: Deep learning, Star sensor, Multi-target tracking, Feature map, Attention mechanismSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

The on-orbit detection and tracking of distant non-cooperative targets, referred to as multi-target tracking (MOT), is one of the primary functions of modern high-sensitivity star sensors (hereafter referred to as “star sensors”). These sensors play a critical role in detecting satellites, small celestial bodies, and other space objects, while also serving as an essential component of early warning systems for potential threats. Due to their imaging characteristics, distant non-cooperative targets in star sensor images closely resemble background stars, typically appearing as Gaussian-diffused circular spots. This similarity makes it challenging to distinguish them based on morphological features alone. Additionally, these targets exhibit weak texture and structural characteristics, which limit the effectiveness of traditional contour-based tracking algorithms, such as model-based methods1 and shape-based approaches2. These conventional algorithms suffer from poor adaptability and high computational complexity, making them impractical for real-time on-orbit applications. Furthermore, variations in target velocity significantly impact their imaging morphology. High-speed targets exhibit motion blur, transitioning from point-like objects to elongated streaks. As targets traverse the star sensor’s field of view (FoV), factors such as solar illumination further affect their brightness and shape. Other algorithms that do not rely on prior information, such as optical flow techniques3, demonstrate poor adaptability to shape variations and are incapable of tracking fast-moving objects effectively. Similarly, particle filter-based methods4 and correlation filter-based methods5 exhibit low adaptability to target morphology and brightness variations, while also suffering from high computational costs and poor real-time performance. The mainstream on-orbit non-cooperative target acquisition algorithms (hereafter referred to as “traditional algorithms”) rely on the star sensor’s navigation star catalog and attitude prior data. The core principle of these algorithms consists of two main steps: The first step is extracting candidate star points from star sensor images. In current on-orbit applications, the centroiding method6 is commonly used, which employs background thresholding and connected component analysis to differentiate star points from the background. However, a significant limitation of this approach is its inability to distinguish two closely spaced star points on the image plane, as the connected component method may erroneously merge them into a single entity, leading to misidentification; The second step is non-cooperative target identification and tracking, where the extracted star points are analyzed to distinguish between non-cooperative targets (hereafter referred to as “targets”) and background stars. The extracted star points are initially used to determine the attitude of the star sensor. Based on this attitude data, the star catalog is further utilized to differentiate stars from targets. Once a target has been identified, traditional algorithms assume that distant non-cooperative targets exhibit approximately uniform linear motion across sequential star sensor images. Consequently, the algorithm predicts the target’s position in the subsequent frame based on its computed velocity. If a corresponding target is detected within a predefined neighborhood of this predicted position in the next frame, tracking is considered successful. The algorithm then updates the target’s latest position and velocity information and continues the prediction process for subsequent frames. The primary shortcomings of this algorithm can be summarized as follows: Firstly, in the star point extraction process, the method struggles to distinguish between closely spaced star points, leading to significant centroiding errors. When two target trajectories overlap, the algorithm may fail to track the correct targets. Additionally, target acquisition relies heavily on the star sensor’s ability to determine its attitude. Furthermore, the algorithm requires a minimum accumulation of 13 sequential star sensor images to compute the target’s velocity, imposing substantial latency. It also necessitates the establishment of a predefined velocity threshold for detectable targets, meaning any target exceeding this threshold cannot be captured; These constraints indicate that traditional algorithms are highly dependent on external conditions, lack real-time responsiveness, and do not generalize well to varying target velocities (i.e., all of which are critical challenges that require urgent resolution).

In recent years, deep learning (DL)-based MOT methods have been widely applied across various fields, with mainstream applications including vehicle tracking7, athlete tracking8, and radar/sonar-based target tracking. The core principle of DL in image processing is to utilize convolutional neural networks (CNNs) or encoder-decoder architectures9 for hierarchical multi-dimensional feature extraction, enabling the model to learn and capture rich spatial and semantic information within images and ultimately fit the desired tracking results. Fundamentally, DL-based methods rely only on pixel-level image data, thereby avoiding the limitations and bottlenecks encountered in traditional target acquisition algorithms. DL-based MOT methods can be broadly classified into two main categories. The first category consists of two-stage algorithms, where detection and tracking are performed as separate stages. Representative methods in this category include optimal transport-based simple online and real-time tracker (OC-SORT)10, adaptive appearance cue with joint detection and association network (AAC-JDAN)11, and FairMOT12, all of which are based on the DeepSort algorithm13. The second category consists of one-stage algorithms, which integrate detection and tracking into a single unified framework. Representative models in this category include CNN-based one-stage Siamese network models such as CenterTrack14 and SiamMOT15, which leverage CNNs to simultaneously estimate target positions and inter-frame displacement vectors to associate targets across frames. Additionally, there are Transformer16-based one-stage models that build upon the detection transformer (DETR) framework17 by incorporating specialized modules for target association, forming a tracking queue. Representative models in this category include multi-object tracking with transformers (MOTR)18, TransTrack19, and TransMOT20. However, the imaging characteristics of star sensor-captured star sensor images differ significantly from conventional MOT scenarios. Compared to human and vehicle tracking, where objects are visually distinguishable based on appearance, targets in star sensor images appear as point sources with minimal distinguishing features, making morphology-based differentiation infeasible. Additionally, compared to radar and sonar imaging, star sensor images contain a high density of targets with complex motion trajectories, further complicating detection and tracking tasks. Due to these unique characteristics, mainstream DL-based MOT methods may not be universally applicable to star sensor imagery; a detailed discussion on these challenges and potential adaptations will be presented in the following “Comparative analysis of mainstream DL-based MOT methods”. This study aims to overcome the limitations of traditional algorithms by employing DL-based methods for non-cooperative target acquisition and tracking using star sensors. Unlike conventional approaches, the proposed method does not rely on external conditions such as star catalogs or attitude priors data. Instead, it operates only on sequential image pixel data, enabling real-time target acquisition and enhanced generalization to varying target speeds and morphologies. To achieve this, CenterTrack was selected as the baseline model after a comparative analysis of mainstream DL-based MOT methods, as detailed in “Comparative analysis of mainstream DL-based MOT methods”. Further model enhancements were introduced to improve MOT performance metrics, as described in “The improvements of the proposed MOT DL model relative to CenterTrack” and “Model adjustment and tracking algorithm improvements”. Additionally, a customized training and testing dataset was designed and generated, with details provided in “Datasets”. The experimental results on the test dataset, along with a comparative evaluation against traditional algorithms et al., are presented in “Results” and “Discussion”, respectively.

Methodology and materials

Comparative analysis of mainstream DL-based MOT methods

The accuracy of target tracking in sequential star sensor images depends on the algorithm’s precision in trajectory prediction. Currently, DL models predict target trajectories using two main approaches. The first approach involves DL models autonomously learning and predicting trajectories while detecting targets, whereas the second approach applies DL models only for target detection while extracting Re-identification (ReID) features21, with trajectory prediction subsequently performed using traditional algorithms such as DeepSort. However, the latter method is not suitable for tracking small targets with complex motion trajectories and similar morphological features. The DeepSort algorithm operates by detecting targets in consecutive frames and matching them to update velocity estimates for continuous tracking. The matching process involves predicting the target’s position in the next frame using a Kalman filter, followed by associating the predicted positions with detected targets using the Hungarian matching algorithm. The Hungarian algorithm computes the optimal target assignments by considering both target distance and feature similarity (i.e., ReID). However, as previously discussed, the morphological similarity of background stars and non-cooperative targets limits the effect of feature-based distinction in matching accuracy. Instead, distance-based matching pairs targets by minimizing the sum of distances between predicted and detected targets, making it effective for large, slow-moving objects. For fast-moving small targets in sequential star sensor images, distance-based matching frequently results in incorrect associations (Fig. 2). Suppose at time T0, three newly detected targets appear, all initially assumed to have zero velocity. In the next frame (T1), each target moves such that Target 1 reaches the previous position of Target 2, Target 2 reaches the previous position of Target 3, and Target 3 reaches the previous position of Target 1 Under the Hungarian matching algorithm, T0’s Target 1 would be matched with T1’s Target 3, Target 2 with Target 1, and Target 3 with Target 2 Consequently, updating the Kalman filter would yield near-zero velocity estimates for all three targets, which does not reflect their actual motion (Fig. 2). Such errors compromise the Kalman filter updates, significantly impacting tracking accuracy. Therefore, for MOT in sequential star sensor images, the only feasible approach is to use DL models capable of autonomously learning target trajectories.

An example of Kalman filter updating error.

The CNN-based one-stage MOT model, represented by CenterTrack, extends center-point detection models to incorporate association tracking tasks. Compared to anchor box-based methods22 (e.g., YOLO), center-point detection is better suited for handling shape variations and occlusion problems. CenterTrack further enhances this by leveraging information from consecutive frames (i.e., using a Siamese network framework23, where a CNN autonomously learns to fit displacement vectors between frames, thereby achieving target association and tracking. CenterTrack has demonstrated state-of-the-art (SOTA) tracking performance on MOT2024 and other benchmark datasets. Additionally, CenterTrack is a lightweight model, offering advantages such as compact size, fast inference speed, and excellent real-time performance.

The Transformer-based one-stage MOT model, represented by MOTR, exhibits poor detection performance. The primary reason is that MOTR follows the DETR framework, which jointly optimizes detection and association tasks. However, the joint learning process creates conflicts between detection and association, resulting in suboptimal detection accuracy, as detailed in MOTRv225. In MOTRv2, an additional target detector was introduced to mitigate the conflict between detection and association learning, significantly improving detection performance. However, this modification increased model size and reduced inference speed. Experimental results indicate that the original MOTR model exhibits a false-negative rate exceeding 50% on the test dataset used in this paper, making it unsuitable for practical applications. Furthermore, its model size is 2 times larger than the CenterTrack model, while its inference speed is less than half of CenterTrack’s. These results highlight the general computational and real-time performance limitations of Transformer-based models, which tend to be computationally intensive and less efficient. Therefore, when applied to star sensor on-orbit embedded computing platforms, Transformer-based one-stage MOT models exhibit poor practicality due to high complexity, large spatial requirements, and suboptimal real-time performance.

The improvements of the proposed MOT DL model relative to CenterTrack

The performance of MOT algorithms is evaluated based on three key aspects: (1) Miss detection rate refers to instances where actual targets in the sequence are not detected; (2) false positive rate reports where detected targets do not actually exist in the sequence; (3) Target ID Switch (IDSW) occurs when a target in two consecutive frames is incorrectly assigned a different ID. If a target is temporarily lost for a few frames but retains its original ID, it is not considered an IDSW. Following the analysis and comparison in “Comparative analysis of mainstream DL-based MOT methods”, CenterTrack was selected as the baseline model for sequential star sensor image-based MOT. However, its original implementation does not yield optimal performance. To enhance its effectiveness, two major improvements were introduced.

ACF for Temporal Context: Inspired by memory-based networks such as RNNs26, this study introduces an Aggregating Cumulative Features (ACF) strategy to enhance the model’s ability to distinguish true targets from interference points, thereby reducing the false positive rate. The method involves storing the final feature map output by the CNN at each frame. A 2D convolution operation is then applied to this stored feature map based on its spatial dimensions, generating an output feature map that aligns in shape with the preprocessed feature maps (e.g., the feature map 1 in Fig. 3, derived from the subsequent frame). The processed stored feature map is then directly added to feature maps 1, 2, and 3 in Fig. 3, forming a composite input for training in the next frame. This operation is performed sequentially across all frames in the training image sequence, starting from the first frame. As a result, information from the entire sequence is propagated through the stored feature maps and incorporated into the computation of each successive frame. Since the original CenterTrack model only processes two consecutive frames at a time, it lacks a global understanding of the target’s feature variations across the entire sequence. By implementing ACF, the model gains awareness of long-term feature patterns, improving its ability to differentiate targets from background noise, such as interference light and noise points. Additionally, ACF reduces IDSWs by leveraging historical frames to improve the estimation of target displacement vectors, resulting in more accurate motion trajectory predictions. This is particularly beneficial for tracking fast-moving small targets, which are more prone to loss in the original CenterTrack model. A major cause of false positives in CenterTrack is its reliance on Centernet, a center-point detection network that generates Gaussian heatmaps to represent target positions. Since the Gaussian diffusion spots on the heatmap closely resemble star points and non-cooperative targets, the CNN can be misled during training, leading to false detections in locations where no actual targets exist. Although these false detections typically have low confidence scores, setting a detection threshold to reduce the miss detection rate inherently increases false positives. To mitigate this issue, a filtering mechanism was introduced: in sequential star sensor images, real targets (both stars and non-cooperative objects) appear in at least 5 frames, whereas false targets occur sporadically. A threshold was established where detected targets must persist for at least 3 consecutive frames to be considered valid. Targets failing to be consistently associated for 3 frames are excluded from the final tracking results. For detected targets, the estimated current frame’s center coordinates are combined with the predicted inter-frame displacement vector and compared with the center coordinates in the tracking queue. If a detected target fails to be associated in 3 consecutive frames, it is classified as a false detection. However, due to the randomness of false target displacement vector predictions in real star sensor images, this filtering approach is only partially effective. However, the introduction of ACF reduces the confidence scores of false targets, thereby effectively lowering the false positive rate.

2.Attention Mechanism for Improved Target Detection: Building on the ACF method, an attention mechanism was integrated into the feature extraction CNN to further reduce the miss detection rate. The original CenterTrack model exhibits low confidence scores for dim targets, leading to a higher probability of miss detections. This issue arises because dim targets are harder to distinguish from the background. By incorporating an attention mechanism, the CNN can better learn the contrast between target features and background during training, thereby improving the confidence scores for dim targets. The addition of a lightweight “plug-and-play” attention module allows the CNN to focus on the most relevant pixel information while suppressing irrelevant background noise. In this case, target pixels are emphasized as important, while background and noise pixels are de-emphasized. As a result, the model improves its ability to detect dim targets, reducing the miss detection rate at the same detection threshold and enhancing generalization to different target brightness levels. Furthermore, incorporating the attention mechanism improves the accuracy of inter-frame displacement vector estimation, thereby reducing IDSWs. Through a comparative analysis of mainstream plug-and-play attention mechanisms, including SE-Net27, convolutional block attention module (CBAM)28, and coordinate attention (CA)29, this study selects CA as the optimal choice. The primary reason for this selection is that, unlike SE-Net, CA preserves spatial information within feature maps, ensuring that critical positional details are retained. Furthermore, compared to CBAM, CA more effectively captures long-range spatial dependencies within feature maps, enhancing the model’s ability to recognize patterns across a broader spatial context. A more detailed theoretical explanation can be found in paper29. The integration of the CA attention module into CenterTrack is implemented as follows: the final feature map output from the feature extraction CNN is first processed through the CA module, ensuring that the spatial dimensions of the feature map remain unchanged. Subsequently, the refined feature map proceeds to the next stage of processing, allowing the model to leverage enhanced spatial information for improved target tracking performance. The final enhanced CenterTrack model architecture is depicted in Fig. 3.

Based on the number of stars and targets in the star sensor image, the maximum number of detected targets per frame is increased from 100 (default in the original model) to 300, ensuring a higher detection capacity to accommodate dense target distributions.

Improved Tracking Algorithm:

Improved Tracking Algorithm:

To reduce IDSWs, the original tracking algorithm is enhanced. The detection threshold must be optimized to minimize miss detection rates while avoiding an excessively low setting, which would increase false positive rates. Under normal conditions, an appropriately set detection threshold effectively differentiates targets (and stars) from the background. However, when two targets follow overlapping trajectories and appear in close proximity (though still distinguishable in feature maps), they interfere with each other may both obtain low confidence scores, potentially dropping below the detection threshold and leading to miss detections. When they are later detected again, they may be erroneously classified as new targets, causing IDSWs. To maintain tracking continuity when target confidence scores temporarily drop, a dual-threshold strategy inspired by ByteTrack30 is introduced. Two confidence thresholds are defined: low_thr (lower threshold for detection) and high_thr (higher threshold for registering new targets). Targets with confidence scores above low_thr are detected and associated with existing tracks in the tracking queue. If a detected target fails to match an existing track, it must have a confidence score above high_thr to be classified as a new target. The threshold that when targets with scores higher than can contribute to the heatmap for the next frame’s training is set between low_thr and high_thr. The original model employs a default detection threshold of 0.3. In this study, low_thr is set to 0.35, which remains close to the original setting, while high_thr is set to 0.65 based on extensive empirical evaluation, ensuring a sufficiently high confidence level (typically above 0.5) for new target registration. For brevity, detailed experimental procedures are not included here. The specific algorithm implementation can be found in paper30.

Simultaneously, modifications to the inter-frame association strategy are introduced. The association mechanism operates by summing the detected target’s estimated center coordinate with its predicted displacement vector and comparing it with the tracked target’s last known center coordinate. If the distance falls below a predefined threshold, the target is associated. However, miss detections are inevitable, and in the original model, when a target temporarily disappears, its last detected position is retained. While this method works for large, slow-moving targets, it is inadequate for fast-moving small targets. When such targets reappear after several frames, the stored position may be too far from their new location, preventing successful reassociation. To address this, a predictive update method is introduced: since distant non-cooperative targets and stars in star sensor images generally exhibit approximately uniform linear motion, missing targets in the tracking queue are updated using their last known displacement vectors. To mitigate estimation errors, displacement vectors are smoothed by averaging multiple past frame displacements, ensuring stable motion prediction. The implementation method is as follows: inter-frame displacement vectors are iteratively smoothed by averaging fitted vectors from consecutive frames for successfully tracked targets. Specifically, the fitted displacement vector of the current frame is averaged with that of the previous frame, and this averaged value is reassigned to the current frame. This procedure is repeated for each newly successfully tracked frame, ultimately yielding relatively stable and reliable displacement vectors.

Following the adjustments and improvements above all, the model’s IDSW rate significantly decreased by approximately 50%.

Datasets

Training dataset generation method

The on-orbit star sensor image simulation software adopted in this paper can simulate the actual imaging morphological characteristics of targets(including stars) with varying brightness and velocities in the star sensor image, based on the detector and lens characteristics of different star sensors. Using this software, a sequence of star sensor images is generated with a preset frame rate of 20 Hz, as specified in this study. A set of randomly generated targets is introduced, with each target assigned a random brightness (magnitude) within a specified range of 0 ~ 10, ensuring variability in visibility, and a random velocity within a range of 0 ~ 2°/s, simulating different motion speeds. The initial position of each target within the image is randomly determined, making the dataset diverse in spatial distribution, and every target is assigned a unique ID, serving as a ground truth (GT) label for training. In the first frame, 80 targets are generated, based on the study’s configuration. Each target moves at its assigned velocity across consecutive frames until it exits the star sensor’s field of view (FoV). Once a target leaves the FoV, a new target is randomly generated and introduced into the scene, ensuring that the number of targets per frame remains constant at 80. This dynamic dataset design allows the model to learn robust target tracking patterns, incorporating varied brightness levels, motion speeds, and trajectory behaviors to enhance tracking adaptability in real on-orbit applications.

Testing dataset generation method

The testing dataset consists of star sensor images in which each frame contains both targets and background stars. The target generation process follows the same methodology as in the training dataset. Based on real on-orbit conditions, the number of targets per frame typically does not exceed 15. Therefore, the testing dataset is configured to contain a maximum of 15 target/frame to maintain realism. The generation of background stars follows a different approach. Firstly, the motion speed of the star sensor is set to 0.06°/s, which is one of the most common on-orbit velocities. Each frame in the star sensor image has a corresponding optical axis direction, and using the current quaternion orientation, the navigation star catalog is queried to determine all visible stars within the sensor’s FoV. The projected positions of these stars are then computed, and the corresponding star coordinates are rendered in the image. In this study, approximately 120 background stars are included in each test frame, while each test sequence consists of 500 frames at a frame rate of 20 Hz, ensuring a sufficiently long sequence for robust model evaluation. Secondly, to assess the generalization capability of the DL model, the brightness (magnitude) range of targets in the testing dataset is extended to 0 ~ 12, and the target velocity range is increased to 0 ~ 3°/s.

In this study, both background stars and non-cooperative targets are classified under a single category referred to as “nops (Non-Background Objects or Planets)”. To distinguish background stars from non-cooperative targets in the test results, the analysis is based on motion trajectory consistency. Since all background stars exhibit uniform motion and make up the majority of detected “nops”, a histogram of displacement vectors is computed for all tracked “nops” in each frame. The dominant range of displacement vectors represents the motion of background stars, while outliers falling outside this range are classified as non-cooperative targets.

The on-orbit star sensor image simulation software generates target (including stars) images that closely approximate real on-orbit imaging conditions. The number of pixels traversed by the target between consecutive frames is then calculated by converting its angular velocity relative to the sensor’s field of view. This total energy is distributed across pixel intensity values following a Gaussian profile, generating distinct morphologies that reflect variations in target angular velocity. Results

where FN represents the total number of missed detections across the entire image sequence; FP represents the total number of false detections; IDSW represents the cumulative number of identity switches across all frames in the sequence; and GT represents the total number of actual targets in the entire image sequence. The MOTA metric ranges from a theoretical maximum of 1 down to negative infinity.

The model was trained using the previously described training dataset, with an actual dataset size of 3,500 star sensor images and a total of 100 training epochs. The trained model was evaluated on multiple test datasets generated using the aforementioned methodology. In most cases, a newly appearing target was associated with its first detected frame in the second frame and continuously tracked thereafter. In rare cases, new targets were not detected until they had appeared for more than three frames, but once detected, they were associated in the next frame and continuously tracked. The average miss detection rate (FN/GT) was approximately 15%, with an average miss detection rate only statistical targets (FN(Targets only)/GT(Targets only)) of 9%, and an FP/GT ratio of 6%. These detection error rates meet the required on-orbit target detection accuracy standards. The missed detections involved dimmer non-cooperative targets and background stars (magnitude ~ 9 or higher). The proportion of undetected dim stars relative to total stars was approximately 12%, which is relatively small and does not significantly impact the subsequent attitude priors task. The model also demonstrated good generalization to differences in target velocity between the test and training datasets. For targets moving at speeds above 2°/s, detection and tracking were mostly successful except for dim, low-magnitude objects. IDSW, frequent IDSWs occurred when target motion trajectories overlapped with stars or other targets(i.e., severe occlusion scenarios). Primary cause: When a target (or star) reappears after being undetected for several frames, the initial inter-frame displacement vector estimated by the DL-based model often exhibits large errors. Consequently, the predicted position based on this inaccurate vector deviates substantially from the extrapolated position in the tracking queue, leading to failed associations. Secondary cause: During target overlap events, distortions in morphological features can degrade the DL model’s detection accuracy. This may lead to significant errors in fitting the target’s center coordinates, further contributing to incorrect associations and IDSWs.

Quantitative analysis: The average IDSW/GT ratio reached approximately 58%, leading to an average MOTA score of 0.21. In scenarios characterized by severe occlusion and a high density of dim targets, reported MOTA values for DL-based methods typically fall between 0.1 and 0.3. For instance, in satellite video tracking of dim vehicles (e.g., the dataset of low-light images captured on-orbit by the BeiDou satellite), MOTA scores range from approximately 0.15 to 0.25. Similarly, urban traffic tracking under dense occlusions (e.g., MOT Challenge24 reports MOTA values between 0.2 and 0.35. The on-orbit application scenario addressed in this study shares operational similarities with the aforementioned cases; thus, a benchmark MOTA value in the range of 0.1 to 0.3 is appropriate. Elevated missed detection rates for dim targets and frequent IDSWs during severe occlusion (characterized by recurrent trajectory intersections) remain major challenges for current DL-based tracking models. Dataset Characteristics and Performance Analysis: The test dataset includes 81 targets (including stars) with magnitudes of 8.0 or dimmer, classified as dim targets. Among these, 23 were missed, accounting for 25% of all targets. The relatively high proportion of dim targets increases the difficulty for DL-based methods due to the low-SNR (signal-to-noise ratio). Dim targets often occupy only a few pixels and exhibit intensity values close to the background, making them hard to distinguish from the dominant background pixels. This imbalance causes the model to be biased toward background prediction, leading to frequent misclassification of dim targets as background.

Regarding high-velocity targets, 42 targets exhibited angular velocities exceeding 2°/s. Among them, only 4 experienced missed detections or tracking failures. The successful tracking of 38 out of 42 high-speed targets—including those beyond the training distribution—demonstrates the strong generalization capability of the proposed model in handling target velocity variations.

Discussion

Comparison with the traditional algorithm

To quantitatively compare the DL model with the traditional algorithm, the MOTA metric was computed for the traditional algorithm on the same test dataset. As previously described, the traditional algorithm processes stars and targets separately, excluding stars from target detection. Since the traditional method only retains the brightest stars for attitude priors and does not evaluate FN, IDSW, or tracking accuracy for stars, only the MOTA metric for target tracking is analyzed. For the traditional algorithm, the average FN/GT was approximately 65.3%, indicating a high proportion of undetected targets. The missed detections included targets with velocities outside the algorithm’s predefined threshold range, as well as dim targets within the defined velocity range. The high FN rate demonstrates the traditional algorithm’s limitation in detecting fast-moving and low-magnitude targets. In terms of IDSW occurrences, the traditional algorithm exhibited relatively more IDSWs for tracked targets. IDSWs occurred in cases where target motion trajectories overlapped. This issue arises because the centroid-based star extraction algorithm cannot distinguish two closely spaced target points, often treating them as a single merged object, leading to one target being missed entirely. Additionally, the centroiding process introduces positioning errors, causing displacement vector deviations for the second target. When the two targets separate again, either the missed detection or the deviation in displacement vectors can prevent successful re-association, leading to IDSWs. The average IDSW/GT (for targets only) was approximately 100%. FP/GT for the traditional algorithm was approximately 0.0%, showing almost no false detection due to the algorithm’s conservative target extraction criteria. Ultimately, the traditional algorithm achieved an average MOTA score of approximately − 0.653, which is much lower to the proposed MOT DL model. the proposed MOT DL model demonstrated superior target acquisition capability, detecting a higher percentage of non-cooperative targets than the traditional algorithm. However, its false positive rate was slightly higher. The overall MOTA score of the proposed MOT DL model is much higher than the traditional algorithm. To verify that the DL-based method exhibits superior robustness compared to the traditional algorithm, an additional test dataset was constructed using the same methodology as previously described. However, instead of 15 target/frame, the dataset was modified to include 80 target/frame, generating a 500-frame image sequence. Although such a high-density target scenario is unlikely to occur in real on-orbit conditions, it serves as a stress test to evaluate the robustness of both tracking methods under extreme conditions. In this high-target-density scenario, the traditional algorithm failed to determine attitude due to excessive target-induced interference, rendering it incapable of detecting or tracking non-cooperative targets. In contrast, the DL-based approach processed the test dataset, achieving an average FN/GT of approximately 7.3%, with the missed target proportion relative to total targets averaging 7.5%, an FP/GT of 7.5%, and an IDSW/GT of 82%. Compared to the low-target-density scenario, the DL model demonstrated a lower miss detection rate, while the false positive rate exhibited only a slight increase. However, due to the large number of targets, frequent trajectory overlaps between targets or between targets and stars led to a significant increase in IDSW. The average MOTA score for the DL model in this extreme case was 0.032. Despite the high IDSW rate, the DL approach still exhibited a clear robustness advantage over the traditional algorithm, which completely failed to capture non-cooperative targets under these conditions. This result highlights the DL model’s superior adaptability in handling complex and high-traffic on-orbit target tracking scenarios.

Conclusion

This study proposes a DL-based approach to address the on-orbit capture and tracking of non-cooperative targets using star sensors. Among mainstream MOT methods, the CenterTrack model was selected for its performance advantages and was further improved to enhance the MOTA metric. The training and testing dataset generation method designed in this study holds valuable reference significance for related research fields. Extensive experimental validation on simulated on-orbit star sensor image datasets demonstrated that the proposed DL approach significantly outperforms traditional algorithms in target capture capability, due to its strong generalization ability to varying target velocities and morphologies. Additionally, the proposed DL method does not rely on external factors such as star sensor navigation catalog information or attitude priors, offering higher robustness and real-time target capture performance, making it highly advantageous for on-orbit applications. Nevertheless, DL-based methods require enhancement in two critical aspects: 1. Detection Capability for Dim Targets: Despite progress, detecting low-SNR targets remains a significant challenge. Current optimization strategies include: Multi-scale feature fusion (e.g., cascading deep and shallow CNN features) to improve sensitivity across spatial resolutions; Data augmentation preprocessing, including GAN-based background uniformity correction, which has shown effectiveness in telescope-based observations of faint space debris; Multi-scale detail enhancement (e.g., PE-YOLO with Laplacian pyramid decomposition32; Emerging research focuses on: Cross-modal fusion, which incorporates modalities such as infrared imaging to compensate for limitations in visible-spectrum observations; Self-supervised learning, which leverages large volumes of unlabeled data to pretrain models and enhance generalization to unseen scenarios).

2. IDSW Mitigation: Compared to traditional tracking algorithms, DL-based methods still underperform in IDSW metrics. This is primarily due to: Limited robustness in inter-frame displacement vector estimation, Morphological feature degradation during occlusions. State-of-the-art solutions include: Attention mechanisms for robust and context-aware feature extraction (as applied in this study); Multi-feature matching fusion (e.g., FairMOT’s joint detection-tracking framework12; Cutting-edge approaches are exploring: Cross-modal tracking; Self-supervised Re-identification (ReID). Future Work: This study will continue to explore and integrate these advanced research directions to further improve tracking accuracy of non-cooperative space targets, particularly under challenging conditions (e.g., severe occlusion with dim targets).
