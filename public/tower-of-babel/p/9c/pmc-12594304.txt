A Comprehensive Analysis on the Impact of Hypothermic Machine Perfusion and its Influence on Delayed Graft Function
Authors: Zhihao Li, Mohammed Y. Mahgoub, Ahmed Zeen Alabedeen Alrifai, Byron Smith, Randi J. Ryan, Girish K. Mour, Caroline C. Jadlowiec, Naim Issa, Alexander R. Cortez, Samy Riad
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background.Delayed graft function (DGF) in deceased-donor kidney transplantation increases morbidity, prolongs hospitalization, and increases healthcare costs. Hypothermic machine perfusion (HMP) has emerged as a promising strategy to reduce DGF; however, large-scale real-world data remain limited.Methods.We performed a retrospective cohort analysis using the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (2014–2024), including adult dialysis-dependent recipients of deceased-donor kidneys preserved using either static cold storage (SCS) or HMP. The primary outcome was DGF, defined as dialysis requirement within 7 d posttransplant. A matched-pairs analysis was conducted using kidneys from the same donor.Results.The overall cohort included 59 859 recipients (46.8% SCS, 53.2% HMP), with 2208 matched pairs identified. In the overall cohort, HMP grafts had longer cold ischemia times and a higher kidney donor risk index. They were more frequently obtained from older and donation after circulatory death donors. Although the overall DGF incidence was higher with HMP (34.4% versus 30.9%, P < 0.001), matched-pairs analysis revealed significantly lower DGF rates with HMP (29.8% versus 36.1%, P < 0.001). Adjusted analyses identified HMP as protective against DGF (overall cohort odds ratio, 0.71; 95% confidence interval, 0.66-0.78; paired cohort OR, 0.68; 95% CI, 0.50-0.93). Additional modifiable risk factors include prolonged cold ischemia time, higher recipient body mass index, and longer dialysis duration.Conclusions.This real-world study demonstrates that HMP is associated with a significant reduction in DGF risk in kidney transplantation compared with SCS, supporting its broader implementation and targeted management of modifiable risk factors.

INTRODUCTION

Delayed graft function (DGF) affects approximately 30% of deceased-donor kidney transplants1,2 and is a frequent early complication caused by a number of donor and procurement-related factors.3 DGF has a significant impact on transplant outcomes, and concerns related to DGF likely contributes to organ nonutilization.4 For dialysis-dependent patients, DGF prolongs dialysis, extends hospital stays, increases rehospitalization rates, and raises healthcare costs.5 Although its link to long-term graft survival remains unclear, DGF is associated with adverse outcomes, including T cell–mediated rejection and graft failure.6 Given the increasing use of donation after circulatory death (DCD) kidneys,7 which are associated with a higher risk of DGF, it is important to address the financial implications and potential outcomes related to DGF. This highlights the need for effective strategies to prevent and manage DGF in kidney transplantation.

Several key risk factors for DGF have been identified, including donor-related factors (age, donation type [DCD versus donation after brain death (DBD)], standard versus expanded criteria, cause of death, and hemodynamic instability), graft-related factors (cold ischemia time, preservation method), and recipient-related factors (age, sex, body mass index [BMI], ethnicity, dialysis duration, diabetes, and HLA sensitization).8-10 Although predictive models for DGF are available,11 there remains a lack of effective therapeutic interventions to alter its course.

Hypothermic machine perfusion (HMP) has emerged as a strategy for reducing DGF and is being increasingly integrated into routine organ procurement practices. Although several clinical trials have demonstrated its benefits over static cold storage (SCS), these studies are limited by relatively small sample sizes and selective populations.12-14 In addition, there have been recent changes in kidney allocation including the implementation of Kidney Allocation System (KAS) and subsequently KAS250 in March 2021, which has impacted kidney use. Therefore, data from real-world experience that reflect the performance of HMP are limited and warrant evaluation.

To our knowledge, this is the most comprehensive study using the Scientific Registry of Transplant Recipients (SRTR) to evaluate the impact of HMP and the implementation of the KAS250 on DGF, providing insights into its potential impact on DGF incidence. A subgroup analysis of paired recipients, each receiving a kidney from the same donor, preserved either with SCS or HMP, enabled direct comparison controlling for donor-related factors. This study provided the most extensive real-world evidence to date on the role of HMP in reducing DGF.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Data Source

This study used data from the SRTR. The SRTR data system includes data on all donor, waitlisted candidates, and transplant recipients in the United States, submitted by the members of the Organ Procurement and Transplantation Network (OPTN). The Health Resources and Services Administration, US Department of Health and Human Services provides oversight to the activities of the OPTN and SRTR contractors.

Study Design

We conducted a retrospective analysis of SRTR data, including all adult (≥18 y) dialysis-dependent primary deceased kidney transplant recipients from 2014 to 2024. Recipients who received nonstandard induction therapy (ie, agents other than rabbit antithymocyte globulin [r-ATG], alemtuzumab, or interleukin-2 receptor antagonist, or their combinations), those on nontacrolimus/mycophenolate mofetil maintenance therapy, or those with missing data on maintenance, and those with missing or positive crossmatch results were excluded. The primary endpoint of this study was DGF, defined as the requirement for dialysis within the first 7 d posttransplant.

Definitions

SCS was defined as the static preservation of the kidney graft using a cold preservation solution without mechanical perfusion. HMP refers to the dynamic preservation of the graft using a machine perfusion device that maintains continuous circulation of a cold preservation solution. DGF was defined according to the OPTN criteria as the need for dialysis within the first week after transplantation. Primary nonfunction was defined as irreversible graft failure requiring permanent dialysis within 90 d after transplantation, without recovery of function. The Kidney Donor Risk Index (KDRI) was calculated using donor characteristics, including age, race, history of diabetes and hypertension, terminal serum creatinine, height, weight, hepatitis C serostatus, and cause of death, following the method established by the OPTN. The calculated panel-reactive antibody (cPRA) represents the proportion of organ donors against whom the recipient has pre-formed anti-HLA antibodies, expressed as a percentage. EraPost-KAS250 indicates transplants performed after the implementation of the updated KAS250 in March 2021, which was used to assess potential era effects on outcomes.

Statistical Analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as counts and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations. Group comparisons between the SCS and HMP cohorts were conducted using the chi-square test for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U test for continuous variables.

To identify independent predictors of DGF, a generalized linear mixed model with a binomial distribution and logit link was fitted. Fixed effects included donor, recipient, and transplant-related variables, while a random intercept for the donor’s Organ Procurement Organization center accounted for clustering.

Covariates included donor age, sex, race, donation type (DBD versus DCD), HMP, cold ischemia time, local allocation, KDRI, recipient age, sex, BMI, dialysis duration, end-stage renal disease etiology, induction therapy (r-ATG, alemtuzumab, or interleukin-2 receptor antagonist), steroid maintenance therapy, peak cPRA, number of HLA mismatches, vascular comorbidities (peripheral vascular, cerebrovascular, or coronary artery disease), donor cause of death, and transplant era (pre- versus post-KAS250). All covariates were included based on their clinical relevance.

The model was estimated to use maximum likelihood with Laplace approximation. Results are reported as odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs). All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.4.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). A two-tailed P value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

RESULTS

Study Population

A total of 112 761 primary dialysis-dependent recipients of deceased-donor kidney transplants were initially included. A total of 59 859 recipients were included in the analysis after excluding patients for incompatible induction therapy (n = 16 429), maintenance therapy (n = 18 208), and positive crossmatch. Among them, 28 041 (46.8%) received grafts preserved using SCS, whereas 31 818 (53.2%) received grafts preserved via HMP. Within this cohort, 2208 matched recipient pairs were identified as those who received kidneys from the same donor, with 1 graft preserved in SCS and the other using HMP (Figure 1). The use of HMP has steadily increased in the United States over the past decade (Figure 2).

Baseline Characteristics of Recipients and Donors in the Overall Population

Table 1 presents the baseline characteristics of the donors and recipients according to the preservation method. Grafts preserved with HMP were more frequently sourced from DCD donors (40.8% versus 13.2%) and came from older donors (median age: 41.7 versus 36.5 y), with higher BMIs, a greater prevalence of diabetes (43.2% versus 25.8%), and higher KDRI (median: 1.17 versus 1.12). These donors required less inotropic support (27.8% versus 37.1%, respectively). HMP graft recipients were older and more likely to have diabetic nephropathy (37.5% versus 31.2%) or vasculopathy (43.5% versus 33.3%). Immunologically, they had lower cPRA but more often had 4–6 HLA mismatches. HMP grafts were more often transplanted locally and had longer cold ischemia times (median: 21.0 versus 15.8 h). Minor differences in the immunosuppressive regimens were also noted.

Outcomes by Preservation Type in the Overall Population

In the univariable analysis of the overall population by preservation modality, DGF was more common in the HMP cohort (34.4% versus 30.9%, P < 0.001), while the rates of acute rejection at 6 and 12 mo were similar. No clinically meaningful differences were observed in hospital length of stay, 12-mo rehospitalization rates, or creatinine levels.

In multivariable analysis controlling for donor and recipient characteristics, HMP was independently associated with reduced odds of DGF (adjusted OR, 0.71; 95% CI, 0.66-0.78, P < 0.001). Risk factors included older donor age, male donor sex, longer cold ischemia time, higher KDRI, male recipient sex, higher recipient BMI, longer dialysis duration, use of alemtuzumab for induction, steroid-based maintenance therapy, and a higher number of HLA mismatches. Protective factors included Black or Hispanic recipient race, DBD, local organ transplantation, nondiabetic causes of kidney failure, and higher cPRA. The KAS250 policy did not significantly impact the risk of DGF (adjusted OR, 1.01; 95% CI, 0.91-1.13, P = 0.813).

Baseline Characteristics of Recipients and Donors in the Paired Population

To account for donor and allograft differences, we performed a paired analysis in which each SCS and HMP graft originated from the same donor (Table 2). Most donors (77.5%) were from DBD donors, with a median age of 39.0 y, 65.4% were male, 40% had diabetes, and 31.4% required inotropic support. The median KDRI scores were 1.11. Among the HMP grafts, right laterality was more frequent (53.3%), than the left laterality. They were more often transplanted locally (67.1% versus 60.6%) but had longer cold ischemia times (20.6 versus 15.3 h). The recipient characteristics differed only slightly between the groups and were not clinically meaningful. Compared with the SCS group, the HMP group had a higher proportion of male recipients (63.6% versus 59.8%, P = 0.028), more cases with diabetic nephropathy (35.6% versus 32.9%, P = 0.008), lower cPRA levels (24.7% versus 27.1%, P = 0.004), and more frequent HLA mismatches in category 4–6 (80.9% versus 77.3%, P < 0.001). The use of r-ATG was also slightly higher (70.7% versus 66.0%, P < 0.001).

Outcomes by Preservation Type in the Paired Population

In the paired population, the HMP group had a significantly lower DGF rate (29.8% versus 36.1%, P < 0.001) and a slightly lower primary nonfunction rate (2.1% versus 2.3%, P = 0.37). There were no significant differences between groups in creatinine concentration at 12 mo, 1-y graft survival (95.9% versus 96.0%, P = 0.93), or hospital length of stay (5.5 versus 5.6 d, P = 0.47; Table 3). Acute rejection episodes at 12 mo were less frequent in the HMP group, although not statistically significant (5.4% versus 7.0%, P = 0.083).

DISCUSSION

This study provided the most extensive real-world evidence supporting the protective effect of HMP in reducing the odds of DGF among primary dialysis-dependent kidney transplant recipients. Our study found that HMP significantly lowered the risk of DGF by 29% in the overall population and by 32% in a paired cohort analysis. In addition to HMP, other modifiable risk factors for DGF were identified, including prolonged cold ischemia time without HMP, higher recipient BMI, alemtuzumab induction, and longer dialysis duration. Notably, the implementation of the KAS250 policy was not associated with a change in DGF risk in this cohort. These real-world results complement existing evidence and highlight key targets for future strategies aimed at minimizing the risk of DGF.

HMP for kidney preservation was introduced in the 1980s along with the University of Wisconsin solution,15,16 and accumulating evidence has since increasingly supported its superiority over SCS, particularly in reducing DGF. A landmark international, paired, randomized study by Moers et al17 evaluated continuous HMP compared with SCS in 672 recipients from 336 deceased donors (87.5% DBD, 12.5% DCD). They reported DGF rates of 26.5% with SCS and 20.8% with HMP, resulting in an adjusted OR of 0.57 (95% CI, 0.36-0.88). In our study, we observed higher overall DGF rates (SCS: 36.1%, HMP: 29.8%) and a less pronounced protective effect for HMP (adjusted OR 0.68), than that observed by Moers et al. This variance in findings likely stems from the differences in the donor populations between our study and the donor population in the Moers study, as the latter included a higher percentage of DBD donors, younger donors, and shorter cold ischemia times compared with our cohort. In addition, the Moers study used substantially fewer covariates for adjustment in their model.

Further supporting the protective role of HMP against DGF, our analysis aligns with prior findings demonstrating its benefit in reducing the incidence of DGF. For instance, a clinical trial by Malinoski et al. showed that recipients of DBD kidneys preserved with HMP had significantly lower DGF rates (19%) compared with those preserved with SCS (30%), with an adjusted OR of 0.58 (95% CI, 0.46-0.74).14 Although this trial primarily compared hypothermic preservation methods, it used SCS as a control, which justifies its relevance to our discussion. In the context of DCD kidneys, Jochmans et al18 reported a reduction in DGF from 69.5% to 53.7% with HMP (adjusted OR, 0.43; 95% CI, 0.20-0.89). Conversely, Watson et al19 observed no significant difference in DGF for DCD kidneys, likely because of the use of end-ischemic perfusion at the transplant centers rather than continuous HMP. These findings are reinforced by 2 recent meta-analyses of randomized trials, which confirm that continuous HMP consistently reduces the risk of DGF, with pooled risk ratios of approximately 0.77 and 0.78 across both DCD and DBD kidneys.20,21 Importantly, these meta-analyses highlight that the protective effect is only evident with continuous HMP, as noncontinuous perfusion does not confer a significant benefit over SCS.

Accounting for the changes brought about by the KAS250 policy, which aimed to increase equitable distribution and optimize organ utilization, our analysis of the SRTR database provides real-world evidence from a large, paired cohort, further supporting the notion that HMP is associated with reduced DGF incidence, thereby corroborating the existing literature and emphasizing its potential clinical value. Cold ischemia time is largely influenced by allocation and logistics. The 2021 KAS250 policy introduced a circular allocation model prioritizing recipients within a 250-nautical-mile radius of the donor hospital. Early reports indicated increased cold ischemia times without a corresponding increase in DGF.22 Our analysis, which includes 2 additional years of data through 2024, supports this finding. A plausible explanation is that rising HMP utilization since the policy change has mitigated the expected DGF increase from longer cold ischemia times.

In addition to preservation methods, several modifiable risk factors offer opportunities for reducing the development of graft dysfunction. Recipient BMI, a surrogate for obesity, is a known risk factor for DGF.23 This underscores the importance of implementing targeted weight management strategies for transplant candidates, including structured lifestyle interventions, dietary support, bariatric surgery, and pharmacological therapies for patients with end-stage renal disease.24-26 Additionally, strategies to reduce the length of pretransplant dialysis are essential, as extended dialysis negatively impacts DGF and posttransplant outcomes.

Despite strong data supporting the use of HMP to reduce DGF risk across various donor types, obtaining access to HMP remains challenging for transplant centers because of associated cost and resources. Access to HMP varies across the United States with perfusion service largely either provided by organ procurement organization or transplant centers. Resources involved in supporting HMP access involve the initial expense of the perfusion equipment, along with disposable cost and the logistics and resources associated with its implementation and maintenance. Although HMP offers opportunities to improve outcomes and safely expand the donor pool while demonstrating cost-effectiveness through reduced overall costs and increased life-years,27 declining reimbursement rates create financial constraints that hinder transplant centers’ ability to invest in these technologies. To address these challenges, new full-service perfusion teams are being developed to provide comprehensive support and expertise. It is likely that this initiative will further expand to include kidney transplants in the near future.

A key strength of this study is the use of the large, well-curated SRTR dataset, which encompasses a decade of kidney transplants with a high proportion of HMP. Our statistical approach ensured a robust comparison between HMP and SCS by adjusting for key covariates and utilizing a paired subgroup design to minimize bias. Additionally, our analysis considered the KAS250 policy changes, which may extend cold ischemia times. This study also has several limitations. Despite robust methodology, the retrospective design inherently carries potential for selection and reporting biases. The lack of granularity in the SRTR dataset restricted the depth of analysis. For example, details regarding HMP protocols (oxygenation status, timing, or the type of machine utilized) were unavailable, yet these nuances could influence DGF outcomes. The SRTR dataset has limitations in capturing sequential HMP events and providing accurate estimates of total perfusion and ischemia times. As such, the current SRTR data treats the use of HMP as a binary event without accounting for the duration of use, specific perfusion parameters, or the device employed. Additionally, the study relies on the OPTN’s DGF definition (dialysis within the first week), which is limited by inconsistent clinical thresholds for posttransplant dialysis and lacks data on dialysis indication or duration. This also precludes the ability to distinguish between short- and long-term DGF, a distinction that is clinically meaningful but is currently not feasible within the constraints of the dataset.28 The introduction of normothermic regional perfusion (NRP) has reduced DGF rates but complicated the analyses as both NRP and HMP may be used in the same case.29 However, because of limitations of the SRTR dataset, we were unable to distinguish which DCD donors received NRP, making it difficult to account for its potential confounding effect on outcomes.

In conclusion, HMP has shown a protective effect against DGF in kidney transplants performed in the United States over the past decade, which was not affected by the changes brought by KAS250. This highlights the importance of technological advancements in expanding the donor pool while maintaining optimal recipient outcomes. Although HMP appears to improve short-term graft function by reducing DGF, its impact on long-term outcomes remains uncertain. Future progress in DGF research requires standardized methods and clinical endpoints for registry data collection and trial design to better evaluate DGF diagnosis and management. Furthermore, realizing the full potential of HMP necessitates overcoming significant implementation barriers, including logistical complexities, transportation challenges, and communication hurdles associated with increased organ offers, which can impede organ allocation efficiency. Although DGF is likely to remain a persistent challenge, continued progress in preservation technologies and allocation systems, along with addressing modifiable recipient factors identified in our study, offers a clear path toward reducing its incidence.
