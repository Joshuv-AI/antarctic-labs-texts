The Effect of the Presence and Absence of DNA Repair Genes on the Rate and Pattern of Mutation in Bacteria
Authors: Georgios Kalogiannis, Adam Eyre-Walker
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBacteria lose and gain repair genes as they evolve. Here, we investigate the consequences of gain and loss of 11 DNA repair genes across a broad range of bacteria. Using synonymous polymorphisms from bacteria and a set of 50 phylogenetically independent contrasts, we find no evidence that the presence or absence of these 11 genes affects either the overall level of diversity or the pattern of mutation. Using phylogenetic generalized linear squares yields a similar conclusion. It seems likely that the lack of an effect is due to variation in the genetic background and the environment which obscures any effects that the presence or absence of individual genes might have.

Significance

Bacteria have a large number of genes whose products maintain the integrity of their genome sequence. Despite the importance of these genes, they are frequently lost and gained during bacterial evolution. Here, we investigate the effects of gene presence and absence across bacteria and find no effects on the overall diversity or the pattern of mutation. It seems likely that this is due to variation at other genes and the environment obscuring any patterns that might be present.

Introduction

Most organisms have a wide variety of genes whose protein or RNA products maintain the integrity of the genome. Experimental deletion of one of these genes often leads to an increase in the mutation rate and a change in the spectrum of mutations. For example, if dnaQ is deleted from the genome of Escherichia coli the rate of mutation increases by three orders of magnitude and the spectrum of mutations becomes more biased toward transitions and AT→GC mutations (Niccum et al. 2018). A total of 11 genes have been experimentally deleted from E. coli and the effects of these deletions studied using mutation accumulation experiments (summarized by Sane et al. 2023). The deletion of most genes increases the mutation rate, with the exception of genes such as nfi and uvrA, which appear to have little effect when deleted in the laboratory (Foster et al. 2015). Many genes also alter the proportion of mutations that are transversions and affect the relative rates of GC→AT and AT→GC (note that throughout this paper, GC→AT refers to all mutations which decrease GC-content—i.e. G→A, G→T, C→A, and C→T) (Sane et al. 2023).

Surprisingly, given their importance in maintaining the genome, DNA repair genes appear to be frequently lost and gained from prokaryotic genomes. In their phylogenetic analysis of this process, Sane et al. (2023) inferred that 44 DNA repair enzymes have been gained a total of 731 times and lost 1,309 times across a phylogeny of 1,093 bacterial strains and species. Focusing on the 11 genes whose effects on the mutation rate and pattern have been experimentally investigated in E. coli, they found that there was an excess of cases in which the effect of one gene gain or loss was subsequently reversed by the gain or loss of another gene gain or loss along the phylogeny; for example, if they inferred that there was a gene loss that increased the proportion of transversions, they found an excess of instances in which a subsequent gene gain decreased the proportion of transversions (Sane et al. 2023). They hypothesized that this might allow an organism to access different suites of adaptive mutations through time.

Experimentally deleting repair genes in the laboratory generally leads to qualitatively similar effects in different bacterial species, although only two genes have been extensively studied—deletion of a repair gene followed by a mutation accumulation experiment has been studied in seven species for mutS and four species for mutL (Sane et al. 2023). Deletion of either gene leads to an increase in the mutation rate, but this can vary from 4-fold in Deinococcus radiodurans (Long et al. 2018) to several hundred-fold in most other bacteria (Lee et al. 2012; Dettman et al. 2016; Dillon et al. 2017; Long et al. 2018; Wei et al. 2018). Likewise, deletion of either gene dramatically increases the proportion of mutations that are transitions and GC→AT mutations in most species (summarized by Sane et al. 2023; supplementary table S13), though these effects vary. For example, the deletion of mutS reduces the proportion of mutations that are GC→AT by 50% in Vibrio fischeri (Dillon et al. 2017) but has no effect in Bacillus subtilis (Sung et al. 2015). Even amongst two Vibrio species, there is a quite substantial difference in the effect of deleting mutS (Dillon et al. 2017).

Results

How consistent are the effects of gene loss and gain in bacteria in their natural environment? We set out to answer this question using patterns of synonymous polymorphism at 4-fold degenerate sites. Although selection is known to act upon synonymous mutations, it is thought to be weak (Sharp et al. 2005; Hershberg and Petrov 2008), and hence, patterns of synonymous polymorphism are likely to reflect the rate and pattern of mutation. We considered three statistics: the number of polymorphisms per site, corrected for the number of strains that had been sampled using Watterson's correction (Watterson 1975); if diversity is neutral and population size is stationary, then dividing the number of polymorphic sites by Watterson's correction will yield the same value for all species with the same mutation rate and effective population size. We took the log10 value of the corrected diversity values because diversity varies across several orders of magnitude; furthermore, we expect the loss of a repair gene to increase the mutation rate multiplicatively (i.e. to increase the mutation rate by x-fold). In addition, following Sane et al. (2023), we considered two summaries of the mutation pattern: the proportion of mutations that are transversions (Tv bias) and the rate of GC→AT mutations relative to the sum of the rate of GC→AT and AT→GC rates, which they refer to as GC→AT bias; this is the predicted equilibrium AT content under a neutral model. For the latter analysis, we only considered species with at least three strains, inferring the direction of the mutation from its minor allele frequency. We considered the 11 DNA repair genes whose effects have been studied by deletion in E. coli. We obtained presence/absence data for the 11 genes from Sane et al. (2023). In total we had 290 species for which we knew the presence/absence of these genes and for which we had at least two strains; we also had 168 species for we had at least 3 strains. To control for phylogenetic effects, we constructed a series of phylogenetic contrasts using the phylogeny inferred by Sane et al. (2023) in which groups of related species differed with respect to presence or absence of one repair gene (Fig. 1). This yielded 50 contrasts across our genes varying from 15 for nfi to none for uvrA, mutY, and mutL (Table 1; Supplementary material). We also repeated the analysis using a phylogenetic generalized least squares model (PGLS) to try and maximize the power of the analysis, including all genes in the analysis except mutL because it is nearly perfectly coincident with mutS in its presence and absence, and uvrA and uvrD, because there are very few species which lack these genes in our dataset and the models fail to converge. We consider the log10 of the diversity, the log10 transversion-to-transition ratio and the log10 of the ratio of the GC→AT rate to the AT→GC rate; we consider these measures since we expect genes to act multiplicatively on the mutation rate and hence taking the log of these yields an additive model.

It might be expected that the loss of a DNA repair gene will lead to an increase in the rate of mutation and hence an increase in diversity. However, we find little evidence that species without a repair gene have higher log10 corrected synonymous diversity than those with the gene for all of the eight repair genes for which we have contrasts (Table 1, supplementary S1, Supplementary Material online). There is no significant difference if we consider the differences across all genes together (Table 1), or if we restrict out analysis to those genes which when deleted increase the mutation rate in E. coli by more than 10-fold (i.e. dnaQ, mutH, mutL, mutT, and uvrD). We also find no significant correlation between the effect of deleting the gene in E. coli and the effect we see across species (Spearman's correlation rs = 0.31, P = 0.46). If we repeat the analysis using PGLS, we find that diversity is significantly correlated to the presence or absence of three genes, but not in a consistent manner—diversity is negatively correlated with the presence of mutS and significantly positively correlated to presence of mutM and ung (all P < 0.001) (supplementary table S2, Supplementary Material online).

If we analyze the pattern of mutation, we find no significant effect of deleting repair genes from the genome on the proportion of mutations that are transversions except dnaQ which shows a significant effect (t-test, P = 0.041), but not if we take into account the number of tests we have performed (Table 2). We also find no significant effect if we combine data across genes according to the effect of deleting the genes in E. coli (i.e. we combine the Tv bias from species with a gene, for genes that increase the Tv bias when deleted from E. coli, e.g. mutM, with the Tv bias from species without the gene, for genes that decrease Tv bias when deleted from E. coli, e.g. dnaQ) (t-test P = 0.54) (Table 2). We also find no correlation between the ratio of Tv bias for absence/presence of the gene and the effect of deleting the gene in E. coli (r = −0.31, P = 0.46). No gene has significant effect on the transversion-to-transition ratio in a PGLS analysis (supplementary table S2, Supplementary Material online).

Discussion

We find little evidence that the presence versus absence of 11 DNA repair genes affects the rate or the pattern of mutation in bacteria. This is not due to a lack of statistical power in the simplest sense (i.e. ignoring all other sources of variation). If we simulate data using the observed number of 4-fold synonymous polymorphisms for each species and manipulate the rate and pattern of mutation (e.g. assume the Tv bias for the species with the gene is 0.5 and that without the gene is 0.5x), we find that we have very high power. For example, for the Tv bias analysis, we find that we would reject the null hypothesis of equal Tv bias in the species with and without the gene in at least 90% of simulated datasets even if the reduction in the Tv bias was 10%, for all genes except mutL, mutT, and uvrD. We have such strong power because we have 100s if not 1,000s of synonymous polymorphisms for each species (supplementary table S1, Supplementary Material online).

Given that we have good power, it is perhaps surprising that we see no effects of gene presence/absence, particularly given the dramatic effects we see when genes are deleted in E. coli and other species. For example, deletion of mutH increases the mutation rate by ∼100-fold and reduces the Tv bias to almost 0 (Foster et al. 2018), but we see no evidence of these strong effects in the species which lack mutH. There may be a number of related explanations. Many genes affect the pattern of mutation and although we have controlled for some of them in our analysis (in our contrasts only one of the 11 genes differs between taxa), we have not controlled for the majority. Hence, we might not see an effect of one particular gene, because changes have occurred at other loci. In fact, the loss or gain of a gene may precipitate compensatory changes (Sane et al. 2023)—i.e. if the loss of a repair gene increases the Tv bias this might be compensated for by the loss or gain of another gene. The effect of genetic background might be due to simple additive effects generating sufficient variation in the pattern and rate of mutation that it is difficult to discern the effects of a single gene; or it might be due to epistatic effects, in which the effect of the presence or absence of a gene depends upon the genetic background; for example, the deletion of mutS halves the proportion of mutations that are GC→AT in Vibrio fischeri but has no discernible effect in Bacillus subtilis (Sung et al. 2015).

The environment might also have a significant effect on the pattern of mutation. For example, some mutations in the dnaE gene in E. coli have larger effects on the mutation rate in rich compared to minimal media (Strauss et al. 2000). In short, the rate and pattern of mutation are quantitative traits with many genetic loci interacting with the environment. Hence, studying individual genes may have little predictive power.

It is also possible we have not observed an effect because of selection on synonymous codon use (Sharp et al. 2005; Hershberg and Petrov 2008) and variation in the pattern of mutation rate between species. Both of these are likely to add noise into our analysis and hence make it harder to discern any effect that exists. We may also have failed to detect an effect of individual genes because the current complement of genes is not a good guide to the expected mutation pattern. This disconnection might arise for several reasons. First, we typically only know the DNA repair genes from a single strain of a bacterium, which may or may not be a strain in our population genomic data. However, for many of our phylogenetic contrasts this is unlikely to be a problem since we typically have more than one species per contrast (Fig. 1), and it is likely that if two strains of related species have the same complement of genes then all, or most, strains of these bacteria have the same complement of genes. Second, it is possible that genetic or phenotypic mutators, generate most mutations along a lineage. For example, a mutator might arise either through mutation, or the spontaneous reduction in the expression of a repair enzyme, which generates many of the mutations we see, but these become unlinked from the mutator, in the case of a genetic change. Recently Garushyants et al. (2024) have noted that the mutation spectrum in E. coli is similar to that produced by mutants of the DNA polymerase II and III enzymes, suggesting that mutations in this species might have been generated by mutants or phenocopies of these gene mutants through evolutionary time.

The lack of effect of gene presence or absence on diversity might also be due the conflating effects of effective population size (Ne), since the diversity of neutral and weakly selected sites is expected to be proportional to the product of Ne and the mutation rate (u). Variation in Ne may therefore hide any effect on the mutation rate of gene presence/absence. Furthermore, there is evidence that the mutation is negatively correlated to Ne in bacteria (Lynch 2010; Lynch et al. 2016); this is thought to arise because selection to reduce the mutation rate is limited by genetic drift, so that species with small Ne have higher mutation rates. It is therefore possible that species with low Ne lose repair genes because of the effects of drift and that these or compensatory genes may be gained when Ne increases. There is evidence, for example, that bacterial endosymbionts lose many repair genes, which is consistent with their low Ne (Moran et al. 2008). The prediction from such a model is that neutral diversity should remain approximately constant through evolution as genes are lost and gained.

The DNA repair apparatus of the bacterial genome is a highly dynamic system with the gain and loss of many genes. Unfortunately, it is not currently possible to detect any consistent effects of gaining or losing particular genes, probably because the mutation rate and pattern are quantitative traits determined by many genes and the environment.

Materials and methods

We downloaded aligned orthologous gene sequences for bacterial species for which there were at least two sequenced strains from the ATGC database (Kristensen et al. 2017); in this database, sequences are clustered into inferred orthologous groups. We only considered core genes, i.e. those genes present in all strains of a particular species, since these are more likely to reflect the pattern of mutation in the species being studied.
