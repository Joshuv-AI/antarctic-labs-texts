To Evaluate Structured Competency-Based Curriculum for Health Programs among Community Medicine Postgraduates: Mixed Method Approach
Authors: Rashmi Kundapur, Anusha Rashmi, Kumar Guru Mishra, Gunvanti Rathod, Meely Panda, Vikas Bhatia, Shyamala Ravikoti
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Background:The postgraduate curriculum needs to be given a definite shape so as to make health programs more intensive and integrative. In the present scenario, most of the information is unclear and infrequent. A structured public health competency-based education is an important aspect to consider for integrative health care. Evaluate the structured competency-based curriculum (SCBC) prepared on health program matters.Methods:Mixed method model involving postgraduate students, faculties in community medicine departments of selected medical colleges and stakeholders from district administration. The SCBC was piloted and validated for use. The evaluation was then done based on pre- and posttest performance analysis, satisfaction survey, and questionnaires for reaction and learning components.Results:For a complete understanding of the program, students had to invest 52 hours of stipulated time. CBC assessment mean scores for a) problems in PHC and programmatic evaluation (78.9%) and b) identification of success and failure of programs (78.2%) were more than 75% mean score; whereas, assessment scores for topics on a) resource allocation and b) evaluation of program as write-up was 65% and 52%, respectively. As compared to the conventional method, the SCBC showed a critical evaluation score of 70% with a 0.001 significance level.Discussion and Conclusion:Students’ narratives were that they could understand as well as integrate them better on the field. There was enhanced satisfaction and encouragement. Structured curriculum will also help in making learning more student-centric and go a long way in boosting their creativity.

INTRODUCTION

The central government provides a broader framework and direction to all programs to be undertaken, while, medical education is part of the concurrent list. The Bhore Committee Report was prepared in 1946 with a detailed plan for the National Health Service with universal coverage.[12] None of the plans and policies evolved about health outcomes have ever given a definite shape to the postgraduate curriculum so as to make it more intensive and integrative. Public health specialists should transcend their efforts in order to contribute to health policy and reforms. For this, policy matters and updates should be thorough. The present scenario about educating health policy and programs is patchy and incomplete. Most textbooks provide an unclear broad framework; the progress and updates on health policy programs are hardly frequent in textbooks or journals, as the operational or implementation research is lacking. Thus, the student either keeps searching unsure sources for recent updates or relies on the empty framework.[2]

Bridging the gap in public health education is a mammoth task since public health as such is a vast domain, which veils invariable aspects of community needs and public health policies. Health policy and programs need constant updates and a common retrievable platform.[3] The knowledge transfer is broad range of activities for health research which can be converted into actual activities. Internationally, various terms are used interchangeably for this evidence-based health-care approach such as knowledge mobilization, research to action, dissemination, and implementation. Knowledge pillared on competencies will inculcate ethically sound application of knowledge.[4] Globally, integrative health care has become an important aspect and for this health promotion, there is the need for a structured public health competency-based education.[45]

It is always important to make sure that the graduate who comes out is competent enough to serve the needs of society. So, curriculum involving stakeholders and the students will make the learning a skill with maximum output. Some minor challenges are; post graduate learning is self-directed with no definite structure, depends on self-motivation and has no structured assessment. The graduates when take up job of programme manager are not sure about understanding and auditing of the program.

The structured curriculum has an orientation for objective-based assessment which is certified at various levels. They get a better perspective to employ these degree holders as the outcome is clear and competency is known before employment. Moreover, globally institutes are taking up residency programs and making them more formative rather than the previous time-bound methods spent on rotations. Focus needs to shift from time spent to abilities gained. India needs to take a major step toward achieving it and is nowhere near the start. The health policy and program matter for a public health specialist are more important since they form our basis to bring together the various dimensions toward an integrated learning approach. So, a validated SCBC was prepared by the investigator, in order to evaluate the health program based on immediate outcomes as compared to the existing one.

METHODOLOGY

A mixed model study including quantitative and qualitative methods was done and the respondents were postgraduates of community medicine in postgraduate teaching institutes (8 students). All faculties of the community medicine department were selected for evaluation and 50% of faculties from the other 5 medical colleges of the district were part of this taking it to a total of 31. Out of 10, 80% of stakeholders from district administration (thumb rule—8) were included. So, the total number of respondents was 41.

A structured competency-based curriculum (SCBC) with its time frame, teaching-learning methods, and assessment formats was created. This program was validated for construct and content using two external experts. Piloting is the scope of this educational research.

The curriculum with 4 subcompetency and 12 objectives were constructed for the total competency of health program and policy evaluation. It had multiple teaching-learning activities and evaluation patterns. All evaluations were formative and the competency was assessed in multiple formats. A 10-week program with a structured 8-week program and 2 weeks of pretest and post-test was designed. There were 29 activities and 26 evaluations of which 7 activities and 6 evaluations could not be done due to time constraints.

Pretesting and posttesting of the students were conducted regarding the policy and programs using a questionnaire which had multiple choice questions, single-line answers, long questions, and assessment through a critical review of the topic too. The pretesting and posttesting were on concept understanding of programs and policies, objectives, and achievements of those programs, implementation of the program involving financial implications, and the critical review of the outcome and measures to improvise the implementation (audit and gap analysis). One commonly applied health program (vector-borne disease control program) was given as a task to the students to learn by themselves, using the traditional method of self-directed learning. They were posted in health centers so that they would learn and practice the health program matters all by themselves. While another not so commonly used health program (National Blindness Control Program) had structured teaching-learning methods based on the objectives derived in line with (Medical Council of India) MCI guideline and gap analysis. The scoring of the pretest and posttest was compared with traditional self-directed learning. The narrative reflection from postgraduates along with feedback forms and Likert’s scale-based self-competency was introduced. Feedback from staff and stakeholders was taken along with focused group discussion among the faculty. Satisfaction level and completeness were asked with options of “yes” and “no.” The rating was considered full at 100%. Appropriate ethical permissions were taken from the IEC at the institution.

The modules consisted of work hours divided for facilitator based on simulated exercise preparation, evaluation of data submissions, evaluation of the group discussions conducted on the topic by the post-graduates, evaluation of the solved simulation exercise and lecture on analysis of the data. A total of 24-man hours was the stipulated time for the module from the facilitator side. Similarly, the module required the post-graduates to put in 52 hours of work input to gain the skills required for understanding the running of a particular program. It involved data collection, data analysis, group discussion, plan a write-up and presentation, posting in the program office, posting in PHC area and solving the simulation exercise. [Figure 1]

The structured program has inputs from both faculty and students. It was an adult learning program and hence it needed motivation of high level among students. So, after the introduction of this program, we had a narrativereflection and structured feedback. The answer was forunderstanding the reactions and learning. Therefore, forreactions, the questions were in the form of “yes and no”but for understanding, it had narrative reflection which was coded. Feedback had components to give out personal opinions above the yes and no. We analyzed using codes and the overall aspects of the feedback and narrative reflections were predicted. The objective assessment and practical approach gave the students more understanding of the program.

The satisfaction survey had questions on, how it was effective in assessing both reactions as well as learning. Reactions were mostly “yes” and “no,” and learning had components of qualitative writing which were coded and analyzed. The mean rank on completeness and happiness of learning was determined using a 1–5 rating scale. The completeness was 4.15 and happiness of learning was 4.64 among students. The faculty rated the completeness as 4.75. Stakeholders and faculty felt that the component of completeness was better as compared to students. The survey satisfaction speaks on the completeness of the curriculum that has multiple assessment methods and a time limit for each learning objective.

The overall outcome evaluation based on indicators as depicted in Table 4 shows the various evaluation questions against the indicators proposed to be achieved as well as the indicators actually achieved. The scores used were the various assessment tools (presentation score, rubric, debate score, single best answer scoring, and short answer scoring) used in Competency based medical education (CBME) and those scores were compared with traditional percentage of marks. There was 81% satisfaction among postgraduate students, who perceived the SCBC as better in comparison to the earlier self-directed learning. There was 95% satisfaction among faculty and stakeholders who could perceive this as better. The knowledge to assess and audit the health programs and policies improved among students and they scored 75% and above in the posttest checklist-based performance assessment. As regards the confidence in their competency, students scored themselves at 80%, whereas, stakeholders scored them at 85%.

DISCUSSION

CBC allows learners to acquire skills in the domains of knowledge, skills, and attitude.[6] A competency-based training ensures that learners demonstrate certain required skills in order to progress in various phases of training. The implementation of such a curriculum not only requires vigorous training method to be developed by trained faculty but also needs assessment tools to assess the trainees.[67] It was ensured in the current project that all needs for making a CBC with respect to skill acquisition about understanding and involvement in national programs are gained. With faculty being involved at each step of curriculum development to the development of assessment tools and evaluation, it was seen that the trainee would gain the required skills adequately based on a time frame to be followed. [Figure 1] The trainees’ activities for the specific programs assured that their involvement was complete and practical knowledge and skills required for understanding the running of a program were gained. Through the SCBC, the trainee is helped to reach milestone after milestone through the postgraduate program.[8] The current prepared module in the study ensured adequate gain of practical skills by the trainee as it involved field postings, postings in the program office, and data analysis which would give an idea of the actual working of the program.

As clearly observed in this study [Tables 2 and 3], the structured CBME curriculum proved to be an efficient tool in improving the scores of students. Though studies evaluating the same are scarce in the medical curriculum of India, it has been implemented widely in other countries. Klien-Collins R and Bayloe E deduced that the shift in focus toward competencies, rather than on seat time, allows the student to progress in the curriculum more efficiently.[9] The competency-based style of technical education has unique advantages of technicality and theory/practical integration. An additional strength includes the inculcation of previous life experiences for students.[10] A quasi-experimental study conducted by Zaker M et al. to evaluate the CBE model reflected higher cognitive and behavioral skills on cognitive and clinical skills of nursing students in Iran as was the result in another study by Kadam M among final year BSc Nursing students.[1112]

The narrative reflections of the students were analyzed using the thematic codes. We wanted to know how they felt about the components of the curriculum. We had reflective writing and feedback on the content. Reflective writing gave us the idea of learning, and feedback gave us the idea of reaction. Reactions of students and stakeholders along with faculty were taken; however, only reflective writing of students was considered. Most of the public health program had only passive learning and competency of monitoring and evaluation; whereas, global perspective was lacking. There was not any structure and whole components were considered. When students learn to exercise themselves in the field, it gives them immense confidence in their competency to carry out the program at all levels and in all regions. The task needs a simulated environment in order to develop a skill. We do not have internships after postgraduation, so this structured program when monitored during learning becomes efficient. The survey satisfaction among students as well as stakeholders also mentioned the same.

After reviewing the literature on the needs at various levels of postgraduate education, it was felt that a competency-based model of a two-year postgraduate course across all specialties, offsite training facility, and a criterion-based evaluation system would go a long way in dealing with some of the challenges of the existing system. The SCBC would help with career options, increasing the availability of teachers and specialists at the secondary and primary levels.[13] This was seen as part of the results on feedback and narrative reflections. The satisfaction score was 4.64, which was higher than expected and speaks on the happiness of learning in the structured program. Narrative reflection and feedback found that critical thinking and program evaluation were the learning parameters needed. Because of the complexity of our present-day public health system, there was a need for different types of data from different sources across the health system scenario.[14] Monitoring and evaluation structures, systems, and processes are supposed to be integrated throughout the tenure of public health programs.[15]

Though certain institutions and certain programs have structured credit hour-based learning they are for particular competencies only. The structure with competency and the inbuilt evaluation given in the feedback and narrative reflection in this study clearly mentions how the system approach made the postgraduates move to new learning domains and development of skills with minimal recourses. So the facilitator at the time of learning can give the onus to the learner with SCBC as the inbuilt evaluation determines the competency achieved at all levels. The satisfaction and feedback spoke on these aspects too. The complete outcome evaluation as depicted in Table 4 shows that there was an 81% perceived satisfaction score about the SCBC for health policy and program among the postgraduate students, which was 95% among the teachers—5% higher than expected. Students who were trained in the auditing of health policy and programs under the structured CBC could score 75% and higher in the checklist-based posttest performance assessment. As regards confidence in their competency, students scored themselves at 80%; whereas, stakeholders scored them at 85%.

CONCLUSIONS

The stakeholders were more satisfied than the learners. The achievement of the rubric is done as that fixed expectation is reached. Posttest score in structured curriculum was higher in critical and the confidence of students was high in this SCBC. As all knew where they had to reach, a very positive approach was started and students were happy putting themselves into the evaluation process. They scored considerably very high marks and felt competent as well. Survey satisfaction was above 4.5, which implied to have complete satisfaction and that was the major short-term achievement of this SCBC.
