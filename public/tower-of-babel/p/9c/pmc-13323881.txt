Red ginseng-mediated modulation of the NLRP3 Inflammasome in neuroinflammatory-related cognitive impairments
Authors: Min Yeong Lee, Se Jin Jeon, Hee Jin Kim, Mikyung Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The NLRP3 inflammasome is a key immune regulator involved in the pathophysiology of neuroinflammation and various neurodegenerative diseases. Recent studies have shown that both activation and inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome can influence disease progression and symptoms in models of cognitive impairment, including Alzheimer's disease (AD). Red ginseng (RG), a traditional medicinal plant, possesses anti-inflammatory properties and shows potential for modulating the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. This review highlights the effects of RG on the NLRP3 inflammasome, with a particular focus on its therapeutic potential in AD and related cognitive impairments. The structure and activation mechanism of the NLRP3 inflammasome are first described, followed by a discussion of its role in neurodegenerative diseases and neuroinflammation. We then explore how RG and its major components regulate the priming and activation phase of the NLRP3 inflammasome, and discuss their therapeutic potential based on findings from the neuroinflammatory-related cognitive impairment model. Furthermore, we identify supporting evidence for the application of the RG-NLRP3 mechanism in other central nervous system disorders (CNS) disorders, such as cerebral ischemia and vascular dementia. Overall, RG emerges as a promising therapeutic candidate for mitigating neuroinflammation and enhancing cognitive function in neuroinflammatory-related cognitive disorders through NLRP3 inflammasome regulation. Future studies using various neurodegenerative disease (NDD) models and clinical trials are necessary to further validate the therapeutic potential of the RG-NLRP3 pathway.

Introduction

With increasing life expectancy, the prevalence of neurological disorders, particularly neurodegenerative diseases (NDDs), is also rising. Cognitive impairment, characterized by deficits in memory, learning, and language, is a hallmark symptom of central nervous system (CNS) disorders, including dementia, Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD), and Huntington's disease (HD) [[1], [2], [3], [4]]. Among the mechanisms contributing to cognitive impairment, neuroinflammation has emerged as a key factor [9,10]. In particular, the nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptor family pyrin domain containing 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome pathway plays a central role in the innate immune response. [[11], [12], [13]]. The NLRP3 inflammasome, comprising the NLRP3 protein, apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), and pro-caspase-1, mediates the cleavage of pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their active forms, thereby promoting neuroinflammatory responses. An increasing number of studies report such inflammation that neuroinflammation contributes to neuronal damage, cognitive decline, and the progression of various neurodegenerative diseases and cerebrovascular disease [[14], [15], [16]].

Kamila et al. reviewed studies on neuroinflammatory cytokines in AD and proposed that anti-inflammatory interventions-particularly those targeting IL-1β and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α) pathways-could help mitigate symptoms of cognitive decline [14]. Notably, inhibition of the NLRP3 inflammasome has been shown to suppress microglial activation and IL-1β production, reduce amyloid plaque burden and tau phosphorylation, and prevent neuronal loss in the AD mouse model. AS the link between NLRP3 inflammasome activation and neuroinflammatory-related cognitive impairment becomes clearer, there is growing interest in identifying natural compounds capable of modulating this pathway.

Red ginseng (RG), a traditional medicinal plant widely used in East Asia, has been reported to possess anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective properties [[17], [18], [19], [20], [21], [22]]. Notably, its active components (ginsenosides) have been shown to have potential in regulating NLRP3 inflammasome activity across various pathological conditions, including neurodegenerative, metabolic diseases, and inflammatory disease [[23], [24], [25], [26]]. For example, RG has been shown to suppress NLRP3 activation by reducing levels of cleaved caspase-1, ASC, and IL-1β, leading to improvements in cognitive function [23]. Additionally, some studies have reported that non-saponin components such as Gintonin-Enriched Fraction (GEF), ginseng polysaccharides (GPS), and Maltol alleviate symptoms in various diseases by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome [[27], [28], [29], [30]]. Despite growing interest in the RG-NLRP3 axis, the molecular mechanisms underlying its effects remain incompletely understood. Therefore, this review aims to consolidate current findings and highlight the therapeutic potential of RG-mediated modulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome in neuroinflammatory-related cognitive impairment.

Method

This narrative review was conducted to summarize current evidence regarding the modulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome by RG and its components in neuroinflammatory-related cognitive impairment. Relevant literature was identified through searches of Google Scholar, PubMed, and Elsevier databases using combinations of the following keywords: “NLRP3 inflammasome,” “red ginseng,” “ginsenosides,” “cognitive impairment,” and “neuroinflammation.”

Studies were considered eligible if they (1) investigated RG, ginsenosides, or ginseng-derived compounds; (2) employed in vitro or in vivo models relevant to cognitive impairment or cognitive dysfunction; and (3) provided mechanistic evidence directly linking RG-mediated effects to modulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. Both neurodegenerative disease models (e.g., AD) and other experimental conditions associated with cognitive impairment were included.

Studies were excluded if they lacked cognitive or behavioral outcomes, did not assess NLRP3 inflammasome–related signaling, or focused solely on unrelated inflammatory pathways. Letters, commentaries, editorials, and studies without sufficient mechanistic relevance were also excluded. Based on these criteria, approximately 200 publications were initially screened, and 91 studies were selected for inclusion based on relevance, experimental rigor, and direct relevance to RG–NLRP3 inflammasome interactions.

Structure and activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome

Inflammasomes are multiprotein complexes that play pivotal roles in initiating and regulating inflammatory responses [31]. Structurally, they resemble a spike-like, round bundle composed of nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptors (NLRs), adaptor protein ASC, and pro-caspase-1 [32]. NLRs act as intracellular sensors that detect danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs) and pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs). Several NLR protein complexes, such as NLRP1, NLRP3, NLRP6, and NLRP12, share similar structural architecture but differ in their activation mechanisms.

NLRP proteins contain a pyrin domain (PYD), which facilitates interaction with the PYD of ASC. ASC is a key adaptor protein essential for inflammasome assembly and signaling. Although ASC lacks enzymatic activity, it serves as a critical linker within the inflammasome complex. Containing both a pyrin domain (PYD) and a caspase activation and recruitment domain (CARD), ASC interacts with NLRPs through PYD-PYD binding and with pro-caspase-1 via CARD-CARD binding. This assembly facilitates caspase-1 activation, which subsequently cleaves the pro-inflammatory cytokines pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their active forms, IL-1β and IL-18, that mediate inflammatory responses.

The NLRP3 inflammasome, in particular, plays a central role in initiating inflammation and promoting pyroptotic cell death [33]. It was first identified in the brain and acts as a key sensor of reactive oxygen species (ROS), ATP, and a variety of pathogens, thereby playing a crucial role in the innate immunity. Upon activation, NLRP3 recruits ASC and caspase-1, leading to the maturation and release of IL-1β and IL-18.

NLRP3 inflammasome activation occurs via two primary pathways: canonical and non-canonical [34]. The canonical pathway is TLR4 dependent, while the non-canonical pathway, functions independently of TLR4. The canonical pathway involves two sequential phases: priming and activation. The priming phase is initiated when pattern recognition receptors (PRRs), such as toll-like receptors (TLRs), recognize PAMPs or DAMPs [35]. This recognition triggers intracellular signaling, cascades primarily via the NF-κB pathway, resulting in upregulation of NLRP3, pro-IL-1β, and pro-IL-18 transcription. This ensures that sufficient components are available for subsequent inflammasome assembly and cytokine production.

Following priming, the activation phase is triggered by stimuli such as ionic flux, ROS generation, or lysosomal damage [36]. In this phase, activated NLRP3 oligomerizes and recruits ASC and pro-caspase-1, forming the active of NLRP3 inflammasome complex. Caspase-1, is subsequently activated and processes pro-IL-1β and pro-IL-18 into their active forms, initiating an inflammatory response.

ROS are key mediators of NLRP3 inflammasome activation [37]. ROS production is primarily driven by mitochondrial dysfunction in response to stimuli, such as ATP, pathogens, or cellular stress [38]. The accumulation of ROS near NLRP3 facilitates its oligomerization [39]. One important regulatory mechanism is the thioredoxin-interacting protein (TXNIP) pathway [40]. Under normal conditions, TXNIP binds to thioredoxin (Trx); however, oxidative stress induces TXNIP dissociation from Trx, enabling it to bind directly to NLRP3. This interaction enhances ASC recruitment and inflammasome formation.

Additionally, ROS contributes to ionic imbalance by priming potassium (K+) efflux [39,41]. The resulting drop in intercellular K+ concentration serves as a critical trigger for NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Roles of NLRP3 inflammasome in neuroinflammation and NDD

An increasing number of studies have reported that the NLRP3 inflammasome is closely associated with neurodegenerative diseases, which are marked by progressive structural and functional deterioration of neurons in the CNS.

AD is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder characterized by cognitive decline, neuronal loss, amyloid-β (Aβ) deposition, and tau hyperphosphorylation. Pathological proteins, particularly Aβ, primarily activate the NLRP3 inflammasome in microglial cells [42,43]. This activation leads to the release of IL-1β, induction of pyroptosis, further Aβ aggregation, and increased tau hyperphosphorylation, thereby promoting neuroinflammation and accelerating disease progression. Several studies have shown that NLRP3 inflammasome activity affects cognitive function [44,45]. For example, Heneka et al. demonstrated that deletion of NLRP3, or caspase-1, in an AD mouse model rescued long-term potentiation (LTP), and restored spatial and object recognition to wild-type (WT) levels [45].

Microglial NLRP3 inflammasome activation is recognized as a key contributor to the amyloid pathology of AD.

Aβ formation and aggregation exacerbate the progression of AD, while the NLRP3 inflammasome contributes to Aβ accumulation [13,[46], [47], [48]]. Regulation of the NLRP3 is thus considered a promising therapeutic strategy for AD. Injection of ASC specks into the intrahippocampus of APP/PSEN1 mice increased Aβ deposition, indicating that ASC aggregation accelerates amyloid pathology via NLRP3 inflammasome-mediated mechanisms [46]. Similarly, IL-1β expression, a downstream product of NLRP3 activation, was markedly elevated in microglia of AD model mice following Aβ stimulation [47]. In APP/PS1 mice, administration of the NLRP3 inflammasome inhibitor MCC950 attenuated Aβ accumulation and enhanced microglial Aβ phagocytosis [13].

Moreover, combined NLRP3 inflammation activation and a high-fat diet impaired memory and learning, increased mRNA expression of NLRP3, ASC, Caspase-1, and IL-1β, and elevated Aβ and hyperphosphorylated tau levels [48]. In the McGill-Thy1-APP-TG rat model, MCC950 restored LTP deficits [49]. These findings collectively support the role of NLRP3 inflammasome activation in AD pathology and direct contribution to progressive cognitive impairment through synaptic dysfunction and neuronal loss.

Tau pathology is another hallmark of AD. In Tau22 mice, an AD animal model, increased caspase-1 activity and elevated ASC and IL-1β levels were observed [50]. Deletion of ASC or NLRP3 reduced tau pathology and rescued spatial memory deficits. Aggregated tau also activates the NLRP3–ASC inflammasome in microglia, triggering a pro-inflammatory cascade that exacerbates neurodegeneration in AD [42]. These findings underscore the NLRP3 inflammasome as a central mediator of neuroinflammation in AD and reinforce its mechanistic link to cognitive decline.

Parkinson's disease (PD), another common neurodegenerative disorder, is characterized by the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons. The key pathogenic protein, alpha-synuclein (α-Syn), aggregates and activates the NLRP3 inflammasome, resulting in the release of IL-1β and IL-18 [51,52]. α-Syn activates the NF-κB pathway, contributing to chronic neuroinflammation. Furthermore, caspase-1 activation by NLRP3 inflammasome leads to α-Syn cleavage, promoting further aggregation and neuronal death. In lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and 6-hydroxydopamine (6-OHDA)-induced PD models, enhanced rotational behavior was accompanied by increased expression of NLRP3 inflammasome, pro-caspase-1, active caspase-1, and IL-1β. Treatment with the caspase-1 inhibitor Ac-YVAD-CMK ameliorated motor symptoms and reduced inflammasome-related proteins and IL-1β levels [44].

Pharmacological inhibition of NLRP3 inflammasome using MCC950 or OLT1177® in PD models reduced α-Syn aggregation, preserved dopaminergic neurons, and improved motor function [53,54]. In microglia, fibrillar α-Syn induced robust NLRP3 inflammasome activation, promoting extracellular IL-1β and ASC release without pyroptosis [53]. Administration of OLT1177® in MPTP-induced PD models resulted in reduced α-Syn and IL-1β levels [54]. Additionally, parkin loss has been shown to activate the NLRP3 inflammasome by increasing mitochondrial ROS production through the accumulation of the parkin-interacting substrate (PARIS) [55].

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a neurodegenerative disorder marked by motor neuron degeneration, leading to muscle weakness and atrophy. Mutant superoxide dismutase 1 (SOD1) is a key contributor to ALS. Elevated levels of NLRP3 and ASC have been detected in astrocytes of SOD1 mutant ALS mice [56]. In the microglia of these model mice, increased ROS, caspase-1 activity, and IL-1β expression have also been observed [57]. NLRP3 inflammasome plays a central role in regulating IL-1β release in response to mutant SOD1, as Nlrp3-deficient microglia failed to secrete IL-1β upon SOD1 stimulation [57]. SOD1 also promotes ROS and ATP production, both of which serve as critical signals for NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Red ginseng (RG) and regulation of NLRP3 inflammasome activity

RG is a traditional herbal medicine widely used in Asian countries and is well known for its anti-inflammatory and antioxidant properties. Its pharmacological activity is primarily attributed to ginsenosides-bioactive saponins such as Rg1, Rb1, Rg3, Rh1, and Rg5 [15,19,58,59].

Ginsenosides are categorized into two main types based on their chemical structure: panaxatriol and panaxadiol [15]. Rg1 and Rh1 belong to the panaxatriol group, while Rb1, Rg3, and Rg5 are classified as panaxadiol-type ginsenosides. Notably, during the steaming process used to produce RG. Rb1 is converted into Rg3, and Rg5 components are uniquely enriched in RG.

Ginsenosides influence both the priming and activation phase of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. During the priming phase, Rg1 and Rg3 exhibit anti-inflammatory effects by suppressing NF-κB signaling, thereby reducing the transcriptional upregulation of NLRP3 and its associated inflammatory mediators in a model of cancer and brain inflammation [60,61]. In the activation phase, Rg3 has been shown to inhibit ASC oligomerization and disrupt the NLRP3 ASC interaction, ultimately reducing IL-1β secretion in macrophages [62].

Additionally, Rb1 has demonstrated the ability to suppress mitochondrial ROS production and inhibit the TXNIP pathway, both contributors to adipocyte inflammasome activation [63]. Rb1 also downregulated the expression of NLRP3, pro-caspase-1, cleaved caspase-1, and IL-1β, thereby mitigating inflammasome-mediated inflammatory responses. Furthermore, Rg1 and Rh3, components of Korean Red Ginseng Extract (KRGE), were shown to attenuate IL-1β maturation and secretion induced by NLRP3 inflammasome activation [64].

Ginsenosides such as Compound K, Rb1, Rg1, and Re suppressed NLRP3 activation by reducing levels of cleaved caspase-1, ASC, and IL-1β, leading to improvements in cognitive function and neuroprotection [23,65,74]. Additionally, they help maintain redox homeostasis by activating antioxidant pathways, including the Nuclear factor erythroid-2-related factor 2 (Nrf2) signaling cascade, ultimately preserving neuronal integrity and synaptic function.

Compound K significantly improved cognitive deficits by inhibiting endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress and NLRP3 inflammasome activation, including TXNIP, ASC, cleaved caspase-1, and mature IL-1β in the hippocampus of mice [23]. In the senescence-accelerated mouse prone (SAMP8) model of age-related cognitive decline and AD, Rb1 and Rg1 treatment, respectively, for eight weeks improved performance in behavioral tests such as the Morris Water Maze (MWM), Novel Object Recognition Test (NORT), and Object Location Recognition (OLR) test by inhibiting the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway [65]. These ginsenosides also conferred neuroprotection by reducing hippocampal neuronal loss and suppressing the activation of astrocytes and microglia via downregulation of phosphorylated-NF-kB-p65 (p-NF-kB-p65). Rb1 notably suppressed the expression of the ASC-caspase-1 expression, TNF-α, Aβ, inducible nitric oxide synthase (iNOS), and the activation of microglia and astrocytes in the hippocampus.

Ginsenoside Re mitigated cognitive impairment in a mouse model of chronic restraint stress by downregulating key components of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, such as NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, IL-1β, and IL-18 [74]. Moreover, Re improved memory and cognitive performance by activating the Nrf2 antioxidant pathway and increasing brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression.

Another processed form of ginseng, Korean black ginseng extract (BGE), also ameliorated AD-like symptoms by suppressing the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway [66]. In 5xFAD mice, 18 weeks of BGE administration led to cognitive performance improvements in the Y-maze, MWM, and Passive Avoidance (PA) tests, which were associated with reduced expression of NLRP3, ASC, and IL-1β. Additionally, ginsenoside Rf was implicated in modulating caspase-1 activity and the AD pathology [67]. Rf enhanced spatial learning and memory in Aβ42-induced AD mouse models and attenuated p-tau and caspase-1 activation in Aβ-treated N2A cells.

The NLRP3 inflammasome in other CNS disorders

Vascular dementia (VaD), the second most common form of dementia after AD, is a major subtype resulting from cerebrovascular damage. It is primarily associated with cognitive decline caused by conditions such as cerebral infarction, cerebral hemorrhage, and small vessel diseases. 20(S)-Protopanaxadiol (PPD), a gut microbiota-derived metabolite of ginsenosides, has demonstrated neuroprotective effects in a rat model of VaD [72]. PPD improved both short and long-term memory performance in the Y-maze and MWM. It also attenuated ischemic damage in the cortex and hippocampal regions (CA1, CA3, and DG) and suppressed microglial activation. Notably, PPD downregulated mRNA and protein expression of NLRP3, ASC, IL-1β, gasdermin D (GSDMD), amyloid precursor protein (APP), and phosphorylated tau (p-Tau) in the hippocampus. Additionally, it modulated cytokine levels by decreasing IL-1β and IL-18 while increasing anti-inflammatory IL-10. These findings suggest that PPD confers neuroprotection in VaD by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome signaling and reducing markers such as p-tau and APP.

Cerebral ischemia, characterized by reduced blood flow to the brain, metabolic dysfunction, and cognitive impairment. Ginsenosides such as Rd, Rb1, Rg1, and Rg3 have shown neuroprotective effects in cerebral ischemia/reperfusion injury models by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation, reducing ROS production, and suppressing the release of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β [[68], [69], [70]]. Moreover, a combination of Panax ginseng and Ginkgo biloba extracts (CGGE) exhibited neuroprotective properties in an animal model of cerebral ischemia [71]. CGGE treatment improved neurological function, upregulated phosphorylated cAMP Response Element-Binding Protein (p-CREB), and downregulated the expression of NLRP3, ASC, and caspase-1, thereby inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation.

Another ginseng-derived compound, Notoginsenoside R1 (NGR1), has been shown to alleviate depression-like behaviors and memory impairment in mice with diabetic encephalopathy and reduce hippocampal damage [73]. NGR1 inhibited microglial activation and downregulated the expression of NLRP3, ASC, caspase-1, and IL-1β. Similarly, in HT22 hippocampal neurons, NGR1 decreased the expression of NLRP3 inflammasome components and reduced ROS levels, further supporting its anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effects (Table 1). Although the experimental models summarized in Table 1 encompass diverse disease contexts, all studies included were selected based on the presence of measurable cognitive impairment and its association with NLRP3 inflammasome–mediated neuroinflammation.

Taken together, these findings indicate that NLRP3 inflammasome activation represents a shared neuroinflammatory mechanism across diverse conditions associated with cognitive impairment. However, the pathogenic contribution of NLRP3 activation appears to vary depending on disease context. In AD, NLRP3 activation is closely linked to Aβ accumulation, tau pathology, and chronic microglial activation, thereby directly contributing to progressive cognitive decline. In VaD and cerebral ischemia, NLRP3 activation is more strongly associated with ischemia-induced neuronal injury, blood–brain barrier disruption, and secondary inflammatory cascades that indirectly impair cognitive function. Stress-related and metabolic encephalopathy models further suggest that NLRP3 may function as a mediator linking systemic stress or metabolic dysregulation to neuroinflammatory responses in the brain. Therefore, while NLRP3 inflammasome modulation represents a convergent therapeutic target, disease-specific mechanisms should be considered to avoid overgeneralization.

Beyond CNS disorder: border therapeutic potential of RG-mediated NLRP3 modulation

RG has demonstrated therapeutic effects across a wide range of diseases beyond CNS disorders. These effects are largely mediated through the regulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome signaling pathway.

Metabolic diseases, including type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM), obesity, and nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH), arise from dysregulation in energy production, storage, and utilization [75,76]. In patients with T2DM, elevated mRNA and protein levels of NLRP3, ASC, and proinflammatory cytokines have been reported, along with increased IL-1β maturation, IL-18 secretion, and caspase-1 activation [77]. Ginsenoside Rg1 has been shown to attenuate T2DM-induced renal inflammation and fibrosis by suppressing NLRP3 inflammasome expression and reducing ROS production [24].

In intestinal diseases, ginsenoside Rg2 modulates NLRP3 inflammasome activation and alleviates symptoms of ulcerative colitis in dextran sulfate sodium (DSS)-induced colitis models [78]. Furthermore, ginsenosides Rb and Rc, which are metabolized by intestinal microbiota into more active forms, increased their bioavailability and therapeutic efficacy in treating NLRP3-related diseases [79,80].

RG has also shown promise in cancer treatment [25,29]. Ginsenoside Rh3 and ginseng polysaccharide (GPS) have demonstrated anti-cancer effects in colon cancer [25]. Rh3 inhibited cancer cell proliferation by upregulating NLRP3, caspase-1, and IL-1β, thereby inducing pyroptosis. GPS promotes apoptosis through NLRP3 activation in colon cancer cells [29].

Red Ginseng Extract (RGE) also exhibits anti-inflammatory effects in skin diseases by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation [81,82]. In Staphylococcus aureus-infected human keratinocytes (HaCaT cells), RGE reduced IL-1β secretion and mitochondrial ROS (miROS) production, indicating suppression of the priming phase of NLRP3 activation [81]. Moreover, in UV-induced dermatitis models, RGE suppressed IL-1β secretion in both keratinocytes and macrophages, thereby attenuating the priming and activation phases of the NLRP3 inflammasome [82].

RG and its extracts also show therapeutic efficacy in liver diseases through modulation of the NLRP3 pathway [26,83]. Ginsenoside Rg1 ameliorated acute liver injury by downregulating NF-κB, NLRP3 inflammasome, caspase-1, caspase-3, IL-1β, and IL-18 expression [26]. In nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), NLRP3 activation contributes to hepatic inflammation and fibrosis, while its inhibition ameliorates the pathological features of nonalcoholic steatohepatitis (NASH) [84]. Panaxydol (PND), a ginseng-derived compound, has been shown to ameliorate NASH by inhibiting NLRP3 inflammasome activation and suppressing the release of IL-1β, IL-18, caspase-1 activation, and pyroptosis [83].

Future directions

Although increasing evidence supports the modulatory role of RG in NLRP3 inflammasome–mediated neuroinflammation, further studies are required to clarify its mechanisms in AD, as current data remain limited. Research on other NDD, such as PD and HD, is also scarce; therefore, employing diverse animal models—including AD transgenic, ischemic, vascular, and aging-related models—will be essential to determine whether RG's effects extend across neuroinflammatory-related cognitive impairments [16,66,[85], [86], [87]]. Comparative analyses of major ginsenosides (e.g., Rg1, Rb1, Rg3, and compound K) should help identify the most potent components in modulating NLRP3 activity [23,62,63,65]. If the protective effects of RG via NLRP3 inflammasome modulation are sufficiently demonstrated in neuroinflammatory-related cognitive impairments such as AD, future clinical investigations will be warranted to validate its therapeutic potential.

It should also be acknowledged that this review has certain limitations. Although multiple databases were searched, the list of keywords used in our strategy was relatively limited and may not have included all relevant studies related to NLRP3 inflammasome regulation or cognitive impairment. Therefore, some potentially relevant articles using alternative terminology or related pathways might have been missed.

Conclusion

The NLRP3 inflammasome has been increasingly identified as a central mediator in the development of neuroinflammatory-related cognitive impairment. Activation of the NLRP3 inflammasome triggers the cleavage of pro-caspase-1, resulting in the maturation of pro-inflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β and IL-18, which, in turn, promote neuroinflammation and cognitive impairment. Given this mechanistic insight, therapeutic strategies targeting the NLRP3 inflammasome have emerged as promising approaches for mitigating cognitive impairment.

RG and its components exert anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and neuroprotective effects in various disease models, including those associated with neuroinflammation, cognitive impairment, and metabolic dysfunction. These effects are largely attributed to the suppression of NLRP3 inflammasome activation, a key component in the immune response and a major contributor to inflammation. Although current evidence directly linking RG-mediated NLRP3 inhibition to therapeutic outcomes in neuroinflammatory-related cognitive impairment remains limited, findings from both in vitro and in vivo studies in other disease models suggest a consistent anti-inflammatory and neuroprotective effect. Given the involvement of NLRP3 inflammasome activation in the progression of cognitive impairment, it is plausible that RG may exert beneficial effects through this pathway.

Collectively, the findings reviewed in this study support the hypothesis that RG, through its modulation of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway, may serve as a potential therapeutic agent for neuroinflammatory-related cognitive impairment. Further research using various NDD animal models and clinical studies is required to delineate the precise molecular interactions between RG and NLRP3 inflammasome in the CNS, and the potential of RG as an effective treatment for NLRP3-driven AD and related cognitive impairment.
