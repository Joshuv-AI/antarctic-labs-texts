Randomized crossover trial comparing short-term hemodynamic and autonomic effects of e-cigarettes with and without nicotine and conventional cigarette smoking in apparently healthy individuals
Authors: Piotr Sobieraj, Paweł Dziliński, Maciej Siński, Sławomir Kujawski, Paweł Zalewski, Jacek Lewandowski
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AimsTo assess the short-term cardiovascular and autonomic nervous system effects of electronic nicotine delivery systems (ENDS), electronic non-nicotine delivery systems (ENNDS), and conventional cigarettes in apparently healthy adults.MethodsIn this randomized crossover study, 42 healthy volunteers aged 18–60 years with a history of conventional cigarette smoking completed three experimental sessions in random order, using ENDS, ENNDS, or conventional cigarettes. Hemodynamic and autonomic parameters—including heart rate (HR), systolic, diastolic, and mean blood pressure (SBP, DBP, MBP), stroke volume (SV), total peripheral resistance (TPR), and heart rate variability (HRV)—were continuously recorded on a beat-to-beat basis using a Task Force Monitor.ResultsENDS use induced acute changes: HR increased by 7.2 bpm (during intervention), SBP by 3.4 mmHg, DBP by 3.3 mmHg, and MBP by 3.7 mmHg. SV decreased, and HRV analysis indicated a shift towards sympathetic dominance. Conventional cigarettes caused similar, but more pronounced increases in SBP, DBP, and MBP during intervention (e.g., SBP 2.9 mmHg higher than ENDS). ENNDS resulted in smaller increases in HR (5.6 bpm less than ENDS), SBP (2.7 mmHg less), DBP (2.1 mmHg less), and MBP (2.6 mmHg less) compared to ENDS.ConclusionENDS and conventional cigarettes both induce acute cardiovascular stress, characterized by increased HR and BP, reduced SV, and sympathetic activation. The hemodynamic and autonomic effects of ENDS closely resemble those of conventional cigarettes. Although attenuated, ENNDS use was not physiologically neutral, raising concerns regarding its cardiovascular safety as an alternative to traditional smoking.Public trial registrationClinicalTrials.gov, NCT03037775.

Lay summary

This study examined how electronic cigarettes with nicotine, electronic cigarettes without nicotine, and traditional cigarettes affect the heart and blood vessels immediately after use in healthy adults.

Using electronic cigarettes with nicotine caused a rapid increase in heart rate and blood pressure and shifted the body toward a “stress” (sympathetic) nervous system response, producing effects very similar to those seen with traditional cigarette smoking.

Electronic cigarettes without nicotine caused smaller but still measurable changes in heart rate, blood pressure, and cardiovascular regulation, showing that they are not completely harmless to the heart and circulation.

Introduction

Tobacco use remains a major global health problem, with a prevalence of approximately 20% worldwide, similar to that observed in Europe and Poland [1] Interest in alternative products, particularly electronic nicotine delivery systems, which generate inhaled aerosols with (ENDS) or without nicotine (ENNDS), is growing [2] Although considered potentially less harmful than combustible cigarettes (CG), their cardiovascular safety remains uncertain, with emerging evidence suggesting increased risk compared to non-users [3]

ENDS and CG are known to acutely affect cardiovascular physiology, yet high-resolution data capturing beat-to-beat hemodynamic and autonomic responses remain limited [4]

This study aimed to assess the short-term cardiovascular and autonomic effects of vaping versus smoking in young healthy adults.

Methods

Design

The trial was designed as a randomized crossover study comparing the acute effects of an ENDS, an ENNDS, and CG in the group of 42 former CG healthy smokers aged 18–60 years. All participants provided written informed consent. The study was approved by the Bioethical Committee at the Medical University of Warsaw (No KB/126/2015) and registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT03037775).

Study endpoints

The primary endpoint was the change in heart rate (HR) from the pre-intervention baseline during the intervention. Secondary endpoints included changes in other recorded parameters. Additionally, the statistical analyses enabled comparisons of the intervention effects on HR and other parameters across combustible ENDS, cigarettes, and ENNDS.

Hemodynamic and autonomic parameters were assessed non-invasively using the Task Force Monitor (TFM; CNSystems Medizintechnik, Graz, Austria), enabling beat-to-beat analysis. Measured variables included HR, systolic (SBP), diastolic (DBP), mean blood pressure (MBP), pulse pressure (PP), cardiac output (CO), stroke volume (SV), and total peripheral resistance (TPR). Cardiac function indices included left ventricular ejection time (LVET), ejection rate (ER), contractility index (IC), acceleration index (ACI), and left ventricular work index (LWVI), end-diastolic volume index (EDI).

Autonomic function was evaluated using frequency-domain heart rate variability (HRV), including low- (LF) and high-frequency (HF) components and the LF/HF ratio.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables are presented as median with interquartile range. Categorical variables are presented as counts with percentages.

We used linear mixed-effects models to evaluate changes in cardiovascular parameters across study periods (baseline, intervention, and post-intervention) and interventions (ENDS, CG, and ENNDS).

Results

A total of 42 participants were enrolled, including 17 (40.5%) females. Median age was 28.3 (26.4–30.3) years, and median body mass index was 24.2 (20.7–26.2) kg/m2.

The summary of the results is presented in Fig. 1 Panel B.

HR and BP

ENDS increased HR by 7.2 bp.m. during the intervention (p < 0.001) and by 2.9 bp.m. post-intervention (p = 0.03). Changes with CG were similar both during (p = 0.272) and post-intervention (p = 0.798). ENNDS was associated with a smaller increase compared with ENDS by 5.6 bp.m. during (p = 0.004) and by 5.7 bp.m. post-intervention (p = 0.003).

ENDS increased SBP by 3.4 mm Hg during (p < 0.001) and by 4.2 mm Hg post-intervention (p < 0.001). CG increased SBP more than ENDS by 2.9 mm Hg during the intervention (p = 0.020), with no difference post-intervention (p = 0.794). ENNDS resulted in smaller increases compared with ENDS by 2.7 mm Hg during (p = 0.030) and by 4.1 mm Hg post-intervention (p = 0.001).

ENDS increased DBP by 3.3 mm Hg during (p < 0.001) and by 3.8 mm Hg post-intervention (p < 0.001). CG increased DBP more than ENDS by 2.8 mm Hg during (p = 0.009), with no difference post-intervention (p = 0.788). ENNDS showed smaller increases than ENDS by 2.1 mm Hg during (p = 0.045) and by 2.9 mm Hg post-intervention (p = 0.006).

ENDS increased MBP by 3.7 mm Hg during (p < 0.001) and by 4.4 mm Hg post-intervention (p < 0.001). CG increased MBP more than ENDS by 3.0 mm Hg during (p = 0.010), with similar post-intervention values (p = 0.934). ENNDS produced smaller increases than ENDS by 2.6 mm Hg during (p = 0.023) and by 3.7 mm Hg post-intervention (p = 0.002).

Heart contractility and peripheral resistance

ENDS decreased SV by 6.1 ml during (p = 0.001) and by 5.0 ml post-intervention (p = 0.008). Changes were similar for CG (p = 0.994; p = 0.988) and ENDS (p = 0.109; p = 0.622).

CO showed no change during or after ENDS use (p = 0.201, p = 0.601). Responses were similar for CG (p = 0.411; p = 0.833;) and ENNDS (p = 0.481; p = 0.055).

ENDS did not change TPR during (p = 0.435) but increased it post-intervention by 64.8 dyne·s/cm⁵ (p = 0.023). Changes were similar for CG (p = 0.887; p = 0.604) and ENNDS (p = 0.686; p = 0.466).

ENDS decreased EDI by 5.3 ml/m² during (p < 0.001) and by 4.5 ml/m² post-intervention (p = 0.002). Responses were similar for CG (p = 0.852; p = 0.914) and ENNDS (p = 0.060; p = 0.462).

ENDS did not change ACI during (p = 0.084) but decreased it post-intervention by 5.8 (100/s²) (p = 0.008). Similar effects were observed for CG (p = 0.897; p = 0.904) and ENNDS (p = 0.331; p = 0.996).

ENDS decreased IC by 4.0 (1000/s) during (p = 0.01) and by 3.6 (1000/s) post-intervention (p = 0.021). CG (p = 0.877; p = 0.836) and ENNDS (p = 0.194; p = 0.978) affected IC similarly to ENDS.

The increase of LVWI by 0.287 mmHg·l/(min·m²) during vaping ENDS was observed (p = 0.006) and without change post-intervention (p = 0.073). Responses were similar for CG (p = 0.053; p = 0.775). ENNDS did not differ during (p = 0.178) but decreased LVWI post-intervention by 0.433 mmHg·l/(min·m²) compared with ENDS (p = 0.003).

ENDS decreased LVET by 8.6 ms during (p < 0.001) and showed no difference post-intervention (p = 0.172). Responses were similar for CG (p = 0.842; p = 0.460). ENNDS showed greater decreases compared with ENDS by 7.4 ms during (p = 0.019) and by 6.75 ms post-intervention (p = 0.033).

Autonomic system

ENDS increased LFnu and decreased HFnu by 5.2 percentage points during (p = 0.005) and by 6.0 percentage points post-intervention (p = 0.001). Responses were similar for CG (p = 0.393; p = 0.450) and ENNDS (p = 0.449; p = 0.052).

ENDS increased LF by 389 ms² during (p = 0.006) with no change post-intervention (p = 0.408). CG decreased LF by 557 ms² compared with ENDS during (p = 0.006) and showed similar values post-intervention (p = 0.585). ENNDS showed similar changes during (p = 0.307) but increased LF post-intervention by 420 ms² compared with ENDS (p = 0.036).

ENDS did not change HF during (p = 0.445) but decreased it by 350 ms² post-intervention (p = 0.033). CG showed no differences compared with ENDS (p = 0.060; p = 0.788). ENNDS showed similar values during (p = 0.803) but increased HF post-intervention by 498 ms² compared with ENDS (p = 0.032).

ENDS did not change LF/HF (p = 0.304; p = 0.139). Similar effects were observed for CG (p = 0.313; p = 0.949) and ENNDS (p = 0.639; p = 0.299).

ENDS did not change BRRS (p = 0.972; p = 0.272). Responses were similar for CG (p = 0.148; p = 0.615) and ENNDS (p = 0.916; p = 0.869).

Discussion

In this study, ENDS use was associated with increases in HR, BP, vascular resistance, and sympathetic activity, accompanied by reductions in indices of cardiac contractility. These effects were largely comparable to those induced by CG, with only modestly greater pressor effects seen for CG. ENNDS confirmed attenuated but still measurable effects, indicating that vaping is not physiologically inert as reported previously [5]

Our findings extend prior literature by providing continuous, beat-to-beat assessment rather than intermittent measurements, which may underestimate peak physiological responses. Previous clinical and meta-analytic data have consistently shown increases in HR and blood pressure following both ENDS and cigarette use, with some suggesting a smaller effect size for ENDS [4] However, our results indicate that when assessed with high temporal resolution, the magnitude and pattern of cardiovascular responses to ENDS more closely resemble those of combustible cigarettes than previously appreciated. The observed shift toward sympathetic predominance and increased vascular resistance further support a shared autonomic and vascular mechanism underlying these effects.

Importantly, we also demonstrate a reduction in stroke volume with preserved cardiac output, likely mediated by compensatory tachycardia, consistent with historical observations in cigarette smoking [6] The concomitant shortening of LVET and increase in vascular load suggest an acute impairment in cardiac-vascular coupling.

This study has limitations, including a relatively small sample size and restriction to young, healthy, non-smoking individuals, limiting generalizability.

Conclusion

ENDS use induces acute cardiovascular stress with hemodynamic and autonomic effects closely resembling those of combustible cigarettes, while ENNDS are not without physiological impact.
