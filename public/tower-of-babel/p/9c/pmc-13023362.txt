Flexible statistical approaches for modeling nonlinear relationships in diabetes prediction using splines, Bayesian kernel regression and Bayesian regression trees
Authors: Thimani Dananjana Ranathungage, Harsha Blumer, Saman Muthukumarana
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractModeling nonlinear relationships is a fundamental challenge in statistical analysis, particularly when predictors exhibit complex and interacting effects on outcomes. This study compares three flexible methods for capturing such structures: restricted cubic spline regression (RCS), Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR), and Bayesian additive regression trees (BART). RCS enables explicit modeling of nonlinear associations via spline basis functions, BKMR leverages kernel functions within a Bayesian framework to capture nonlinear and non-additive effects, and BART provides a nonparametric ensemble approach that flexibly accommodates interactions and nonlinearities without prior specification. To demonstrate the utility of these methods, we apply them to the Pima Indians diabetes dataset, consisting of 768 observations of women at high risk of type II diabetes. After data preprocessing, including imputation and outlier handling, each method was fitted and evaluated using six performance measures. RCS and BKMR identified glucose, insulin, age, and skin thickness as significant predictors, while BART yielded the best predictive performance (AUC 97%). Predictor-response functions were used to enhance clinical interpretability. These findings illustrate that a methodology capable of capturing nonlinear effects can substantially improve prediction accuracy in epidemiological studies.Keywords: Nonlinear effects, Restricted cubic splines regression, Bayesian kernel machine regression, Bayesian additive regression trees

Introduction

Nonlinear relationships frequently appear in real-life data. Despite this, conventional parametric methods are often used in analyses, leading to potential biases in estimates and incorrect conclusions. Hence, our goal is to apply splines to capture potential nonlinear relationships in a regression setting, and use two robust Bayesian machine learning methods: Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR), and Bayesian additive regression trees (BART) for diabetes prediction and identification of important variables to enhance clinical understanding. Bayesian methods are probabilistic and generate robust predictions while effectively handling data uncertainty. We apply these three statistical methods in a health outcome-exposure (predictor) analysis to model possible nonlinear relationships that arise when modeling real data.

In today’s world, diabetes has become one of the main chronic diseases heavily influenced by lifestyle changes. This can result in a burden on healthcare emergency departments due to the severe complications associated with it, such as heart attacks, strokes, kidney failure, and leg and foot amputations. Accurate prediction of diabetes helps clinicians manage complications, thereby reducing medical costs and the burden on healthcare systems.

There are three types of diabetes, namely type I, type II, and gestational diabetes. Type I diabetes is more common among children, and in this condition, the pancreas makes little or no insulin, which is an inherent genetic condition. Type II diabetes occurs when the body does not use insulin properly. This condition appears among adults, and women face more serious consequences than men with type II diabetes [1]. Gestational diabetes is a condition that affects women during their pregnancy due to interference with insulin by pregnancy hormones. The Pima Indians diabetes database, which we used in this analysis, consists of adult women from a population with a high incidence rate of type II diabetes [2].

Studies of health data often evaluate the relationships between independent variables and a health outcome. Regression models are commonly used to determine these relationships. However, when nonlinear relationships are present, applying ordinary least squares or logistic regression can be problematic. The use of spline functions in the regression model is one approach to address this problem. A spline function transforms a nonlinear continuous predictor variable and can be applied to any regression model. In this study, we considered restricted cubic splines (RCS), a type of spline function that can flexibly capture complex nonlinear effects and is widely recommended for predictive modeling [3, 4]. For instance, a recent study on epidemiological methods [5] recommended RCS as a tool for improving model fit in the presence of nonlinear predictor-response associations while maintaining interpretability.

Bayesian kernel machine regression (BKMR) is another approach that has gained popularity recently due to its methodological advancement. In BKMR, a mixture of exposures is linked to the health outcome through a kernel function, enabling the model to capture complex, nonlinear, and non-additive relationships between the exposure components and the outcome. As a result, BKMR has emerged as a significant tool in the field, enabling users to model beyond isolated effects and capture more complex, simultaneous effects of exposures [6]. The BKMR model fitting employs Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC) within a Bayesian framework, and variable selection is facilitated via posterior inclusion probability (PIP). A recent study exploring age-and sex-specific determinants of diabetes used the hierarchical form of BKMR to determine the effects of individual risk factors on the development of diabetes and to quantify their joint associations [7]. Motivated by these studies, we used BKMR to model the simultaneous impact of health-related predictor variables on the diabetes outcome.

Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART), on the other hand, is an ensemble tree-based method that uses Bayesian modeling to fit the data. In tree-based models, the data is split into homogeneous subgroups to predict variations in outcomes. A single tree can easily capture nonlinearity and interactions, whereas a sum of trees ensures that additive components are well captured [8]. BART is considered a flexible approach and has been applied in a wide range of applications in recent literature due to its ability to capture nonlinearity and interactions without a prior specification. For instance, [9] used BART in an air quality-health outcome setting to model complex nonlinear and non-additive exposure-response surfaces. In another study on machine learning and Bayesian models for diabetes risk prediction, BART provided competitive performance measures [10]. Hence, BART was selected as a candidate model to explore the exposure-response relationship between diabetes and its predictors in our study.

In the diabetes prediction literature, the Pima Indians diabetes database has been extensively used, and the most commonly used algorithms in recent studies are decision trees [11–16], logistic regression [16, 17], naive Bayes [12–15], random forest [11, 14, 16, 18] and support vector machine (SVM) [13, 15, 16]. Among these studies, [17] achieved the highest accuracy of using logistic regression. In addition to these classical ML models, [12] used deep learning (DL) approaches on the Pima database and achieved an accuracy level of approximately . However, existing studies on the Pima dataset have primarily focused on achieving high classification accuracies using standard ML/DL algorithms, without examining the effects of predictors on the outcome.

Moreover, the development of diabetes involves complex, nonlinear relationships. For instance, the association between BMI and diabetes can behave nonlinearly. [19] used RCS regression on a cross-sectional study done in Northeastern China, and identified a significant nonlinear relationship between BMI and diabetes. Linear classification models, such as logistic regression, cannot capture these patterns, while black-box models (DL) capture them without revealing the functional form. Clinicians are more interested in methods that reveal these relationships rather than a model with high accuracy, as identifying underlying behaviours supports them in making informed decisions. This study addressed these gaps by systematically comparing BART, BKMR, and RCS regression for diabetes prediction in the Pima dataset, with emphasis on 1) capturing complex nonlinear relationships and interactions, 2) visualizing predictor-response functions, and 3) providing probabilistic predictions with uncertainty quantification. Hence, this comparative study facilitates diabetes prediction beyond classification accuracy by revealing predictor-response associations, supporting data-driven clinical decision-making.

The subsequent sections of this paper are organized as follows. We first explain the dataset, its variables, and data preprocessing steps. The next three subsections explain how we utilized the RCS, BKMR, and BART models, respectively, for our study. The results and discussion sections summarize the models’ outputs, interpretations, strengths and weaknesses of the utilized models, and finally conclude the study.

Methods

Dataset

Our study sample is a Kaggle database on ‘Pima Indians Diabetes’. Pima Indians are a tribe of people from Phoenix, Arizona, United States, who are a population at high risk for diabetes mellitus, both genetically and due to rapid changes in their culture and diet. The original database is from the National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases [2, 20]. The Kaggle database contains 768 observations and 9 variables, filtered from the original database. All patients in the current study sample are women aged 21 years or older [21].

The dataset consists of one outcome variable and eight predictor variables. The outcome variable indicates whether a study participant has diabetes or not (1 or 0), and the predictor variables include the number of pregnancies a participant has had, their BMI, insulin level, age, glucose level, skin thickness, diabetes pedigree function (DPF), and blood pressure (BP). [20] describes how the variables were measured or calculated. A comparative performance of BKMR, RCS, and BART was explored on these data to assess the importance of the variables in predicting diabetes status. A random seed was used to ensure the reproducibility of the results.

Data preprocessing

In data preprocessing, we imputed missing values coded with ‘0’s for the following columns: glucose, BP, skin thickness, insulin, and BMI using the median value of each variable. We examined the models with and without outliers and reported the outputs for both models. Outliers were detected and removed using the interquartile range (IQR) method. In addition, the variables were standardized using the z-score method to make the data more consistent, especially for variables such as insulin and glucose that have a wide spread. To assess the correlations among the variables, we calculated the Spearman correlation coefficient for the dataset with missing values imputed and outliers. Figure 1 shows the correlation heatmap, and no strong associations were observed in the data. An 80%-20% train-test split and a 10-fold cross-validation were employed for predictive modeling. Then we compared model performances on the test set using accuracy, precision, recall, specificity, F1-score, and the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC). Moreover, the individual and combined effects of predictors on the diabetes outcome were discussed.

Restricted cubic splines regression (RCS)

The spline function is a useful method for modeling nonlinear relationships between independent and dependent variables in a regression setting. In contrast to polynomial regression, which fits a single global polynomial such as quadratic or cubic throughout the range of the predictor, splines divide the predictor range into intervals through knots and fit piecewise polynomials that join smoothly at these knots [22]. In particular, RCS fits cubic polynomials within each interval and estimates the relationship between exposure and outcome separately within each interval. In RCS modeling, selecting the number of knots is more important than their placement in the variable space [24]. According to [23], the recommended number of knots is ideally between 3 and 5, though more than 5 knots may be used if the dataset is large and there is reason to believe the underlying relationships change rapidly. Based on these recommendations, in our RCS modeling procedure, we used a grid search () for , and 5 for the predictors age and BMI, which were nonlinearly related to the outcome. In addition, a fixed three-knot spline was used to model the nonlinear relationship between insulin and the outcome, as 49% of its data were imputed. Nonlinear associations of age, BMI, and insulin with the outcome were determined using an RCS model fitted with four knots on the training set, and assessed via Wald tests from ANOVA at the 5% level of significance. The placement of the knots was restricted to the quantiles recommended by [23] as it is a robust, data-driven method that ensures adequate observations fall within each interval.

The optimal knot combination for final modeling was determined by running a 10-fold cross-validated RCS model on the training set. The knot combination with the highest mean cross-validated AUC was chosen as the optimal combination. The R packages rms (version 8.1) [25] and rmsMD (version 1.0.1) [26] were used to fit the RCS models and visualize their outputs, respectively.

Bayesian additive regression trees (BART)

Finally, we applied the Bayesian additive regression trees (BART) model [30] to our diabetes data and evaluated its performance relative to the RCS and BKMR models. BART is a Bayesian nonparametric estimation method that uses a sum-of-trees model. Each tree in this ensemble is heavily penalized by the prior, making it a weak learner. These weak learners are then combined to form a sum-of-trees model, which can perform better than a complex model that requires alterations to perform well [31]. We applied BART to our diabetes dataset, as it can handle nonlinearity and interactions without explicitly defining them.

Discussion

In statistical predictive modeling, numerous studies have examined methods for handling nonlinear relationships that arise in real-world epidemiological data. This paper compares three such flexible nonparametric approaches for modeling the relationship between diabetes and eight clinical and demographic features in the Pima Indians diabetes dataset. Using restricted cubic spline regression, we identified significant nonlinear associations of age, insulin, BMI, and skin thickness with the outcome. Hence, we utilized three statistical models that effectively capture nonlinear and non-additive associations to model the Pima diabetes data. All three proposed approaches achieved more than 75% predictive performance and provided a robust performance across models with and without outliers. Insulin and glucose were identified as significant variables affecting diabetes outcome across all models. In addition, skin thickness and age had significant effects on diabetes as identified by the RCS and BKMR approaches. The BART and BKMR models showed almost the same order of variable importance. Blood pressure (BP) and diabetes pedigree function (DPF) were the least important predictors of diabetes. The study by [13], which used the same database identified factors such as glucose levels, BMI, age, and pregnancy as more important than skin thickness and blood pressure.

The methods we used in this paper have their own strengths and weaknesses. RCS models are easy to implement and interpret; however, in this approach, the researcher must choose the number of knots to model the nonlinear terms. This becomes computationally intensive in large datasets with multiple nonlinear predictors. In this study, we used mean cross-validated AUC to select 3-5 knots for the two predictors, age and BMI, and fixed the insulin knots at 3 due to the data limitation. BKMR is a relatively new and popular tool used in epidemiological studies because of its ability to model a mixture of complex, nonlinear and, non-additive associations via a kernel. One drawback of this approach is its high computational cost. However, it allowed us to examine different cross-sections of the kernel and to reveal the simultaneous effects of predictors on the diabetes outcome. For instance, at lower insulin levels ( and percentiles), only glucose showed a positive association with diabetes risk. In future work, alternative kernels may be explored for BKMR modeling in place of the default Gaussian kernel used in the current study.

Among the three approaches tested in this paper, BART provided the best performance measures, followed by BKMR. It was also computationally more efficient than BKMR. Partial dependence plots provide additional benefit for interpreting the effects of predictors on the response. In conclusion, when nonlinear predictor-response associations are present, more robust statistical methods can yield better predictions than conventional parametric methods.

As future work, we will extend these methods to administrative data obtained from the Manitoba Centre for Health Policy (MCHP) for the period from 2007 to 2025. Using databases such as hospital abstracts, medical claims, and emergency department records, we aim to examine predictor-response associations in real-life, high-dimensional data settings. These complex data may require accounting for hierarchical structures that leverage distributional summaries of individual-level covariates to predict group-level outcomes [37]. In addition, rather than focusing solely on smooth nonlinear effects of predictors on an outcome, one can model the network of relationships among features themselves using a probabilistic dependence structure [38].
