Anti‐Müllerian Hormone Levels Are Associated With Skeletal Maturity in Adolescent Girls: A Longitudinal Study
Authors: McKenzie L Ford, Audrey C Choh, Brandon Gonzalez, Steven R Lindheim, Frank Z Stanczyk, Lynda K McGinnis, Stefan A Czerwinski, Miryoung Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground and AimsThe role of anti‐Müllerian hormone (AMH), a potential marker of the hypothalamic–pituitary–ovarian axis, is not well established in adolescent females. Typical epidemiologic studies use secondary sexual characteristics or chronological age as predictors for AMH. Skeletal maturity, an indicator of bone development, however, has not been examined in association with AMH in adolescent females. This study aimed to examine patterns of change in AMH levels in relation to skeletal maturity in healthy adolescents from the Fels Longitudinal Study.MethodsA longitudinal study was conducted on 88 females (212 observations) between the ages of 8 and 18. AMH levels were analyzed using ELISA from stored frozen serum samples. Skeletal age from hand‐wrist radiographs, as well as demographics, anthropometrics, and cardiometabolic risk factors, were included in the analysis. In the stepwise linear mixed‐effects regression models, log‐transformed AMH (AMHlog) was regressed on relative skeletal age, the skeletal maturity indicator calculated as chronological age minus skeletal age. Chronological age modeled as a cubic function, adiposity measures, and cardiometabolic factors such as fasting glucose were included as covariates.ResultsSkeletal maturity significantly predicted lower AMHlog (β = −0.073, SE = 0.032, p = 0.02). Serum glucose levels were significantly associated with decreases in AMHlog (β = −0.008, SE = 0.004, p = 0.04). Chronological age was not significantly associated with AMHlog.ConclusionOur study showed a negative relationship between AMH and skeletal age relative to chronological age. This study highlights that AMH and skeletal maturity provide correlated information on growth and pubertal status in adolescent females.Keywords: adolescent girls, anti‐Müllerian hormone, longitudinal study, maturity, skeletal age

Introduction

The function of the anti‐Müllerian hormone (AMH) varies depending on the sex and life stage of an individual. During prenatal development, AMH is considered essential in the sex differentiation of a fetus and the absence of AMH plays a role in developing Müllerian ducts into female reproductive organs [1, 2]. To date, most AMH research has been conducted regarding fertility in adult women [2, 3, 4, 5], including the research on optimal AMH levels in appropriate gamete donor selection for assisted reproductive technology (ART) success [6, 7]. AMH, which is produced in the granulosa cells that surround the oocyte, is secreted during the preantral and small antral phases of follicular development and correlated with AMH‐secreting follicles, mainly antral secondary follicles. During aging, there is a decrease in production and serum levels of AMH [8]. In adolescent females, AMH research primarily focuses on polycystic ovarian syndrome (PCOS), where findings suggest that AMH levels are elevated in adolescents with PCOS [9, 10, 11]. Pediatric conditions of other menstrual and chromosomal conditions, such as oligomenorrhea and Turner syndrome, have also been researched to understand ovarian function better [12, 13, 14, 15].

Patterns of AMH in the context of growth and development in adolescent females have yet to be studied as well. We previously reported AMH to have a significant, nonlinear relationship with chronological age in relatively healthy female children between the ages of 8 and 18 [16]. Similarly, Lie Fong et al. [17] and Kelsey et al. [18] found AMH levels increase from infancy until adolescence, reach a peak at 15.8 years of age, plateau until 25 years of age, and then begin to decline gradually [17, 18]. These results suggest that childhood follicular dynamics may differ from that of adulthood. Furthermore, notable interindividual differences in AMH levels were reported across all chronological ages [17]. In contrast, Hagen et al. showed that intraindividual AMH levels only undergo minor fluctuations throughout childhood and adolescence [19]. Therefore, Hagen et al. suggested that a random AMH measurement would likely be representative for adolescent females and that AMH levels reflect the antral follicle counts in female children, similar to the relationship identified in adult females [19].

The role of AMH in growth and development is further complicated by other risk factors associated with reproductive health and adiposity. Ortega et al. reported that females who had early menarche have higher levels of AMH than those found in adult women with regular ovulatory cycles [20]. However, girls with anovulatory cycles, who tended to have higher AMH levels, were responsible for this trend. It remains unclear if AMH is the cause or consequence of anovulatory cycles [20]. Adiposity can also affect the nonlinear relationship of AMH with chronological age. During the pubertal transition, children with higher waist‐to‐height ratios had elevated AMH levels even after adjusting for chronological age [16].

These inconsistencies in the literature suggest that a better understanding of the role of AMH at different developmental stages, as opposed to chronological age, is still needed [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. One method to measure adolescent maturity is skeletal age, which assesses the developmental processes of bone [26]. As such, skeletal age is a tool that can be utilized as an alternative method for evaluating growth and maturity in children. Skeletal age determination is a relatively noninvasive method that typically relies on radiograph images [27] and is reported in years, similar to chronological age. Skeletal age is a relatively precise and reliable method for evaluating maturation [26, 28]. It has also been postulated that AMH, a transforming growth factor beta family member, may interact with bone metabolism during growth, which is partially captured in skeletal age assessment [25, 29].

While AMH levels have been monitored across chronological ages, their levels have yet to be examined in association with skeletal age. The aim of this study was to (1) examine the pattern of change in serial measurements of AMH by bone age and in chronological age and (2) to assess the influence of maturation on AMH levels while adjusting for factors associated with maturation in adolescent girls using serial data from the Fels Longitudinal Study. A better understanding of childhood AMH levels may prove useful in explaining or predicting AMH levels later in adulthood and, therefore, providing more insight on female fertility.

Methods

Study Design and Participants

This study was a retrospective secondary data analysis that utilized data from the Fels Longitudinal Study. The Fels Longitudinal Study originated in 1929 in Yellow Springs, Ohio, and continues to this day. It is the world's oldest continuous study of growth, development, and aging [30]. Fels Longitudinal Study participants are followed from enrollment, which is usually birth, until death or infirmity rendering their continued participation impossible [30].

The study participants comprised of female children who had serial measurements of AMH and had data for skeletal age. The data for this study were collected from 1990 through 2014. A total of 88 girls who were assessed at least once from 8 years of age to 18 years of age (total observations = 212) were included in the analysis. Due to the serial nature of the data collection method, there was not a standard number of observations per participant. Each participant was observed at least once but no more than six different occasions (range = 1–6 visits, median number of visits = 2). The average duration between the first and last visit was 4.67 years with a minimum of 1 year and a maximum of 9 years.

Participants' parents/guardians signed a written consent form and adolescents in the study provided verbal assent at each study visit. The Wright State University Institutional Review Board (IRB) approved all study protocols for the Fels Longitudinal Study. The Committee for the Protection of Human Subjects (CPHS) at the University of Texas Health Science Center in Houston (UTHealth Houston) approved the use of deidentified data and waived the obtainment of informed consent for the present study.

Measures

Anti‐Müllerian Hormone Assessment

Overnight fasting serum samples were collected from Fels Longitudinal Study participants by means of venipuncture following a minimum 8‐h fast and were stored at −80°C. The Reproductive Endocrine Research Laboratory at the University of Southern California, Los Angeles, California, conducted biomarker assays after the frozen, never‐thawed samples were shipped from the collection site (Wright State University) in Dayton, Ohio. The Ultrasensitive AMH enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit (Ansh Lab, Webster, TX) was used to measure AMH levels. More details about this method can be found in Smith et al. [16]. An assay sensitivity of 60 pg/mL was used, and the inter‐assay coefficient of variation was 9.7% at 1.6 ng/mL and 12.0% at 4.5 ng/mL [16].

Skeletal Age Assessment

Skeletal age was collected at each observation using the Fels method for the hand‐wrist. Briefly, the Fels method for hand‐wrist combines 85 grade maturity indicators and 13 epiphyseal to diaphyseal ratios for bones in the hand and wrist to create a single skeletal age estimate with a standard error [31, 32]. The chronological age and sex of an individual determine how many maturity indicators are evaluated. Although some indicators are assessed on a maximum five‐grade scale, most are either present or absent. The Fels method of skeletal aging is highly replicable with little inter‐assessor difference compared to other skeletal age assessment methods [28].

Our primary exposure of interest was relative skeletal age, which was created by subtracting a participant's skeletal age from their chronological age at the same point in time. Thus, positive numbers represent girls whose skeletons are less mature than their chronological age would suggest, suggesting some delayed or late somatic maturation, while negative numbers represent girls whose skeletons are more mature than their chronological age suggests, suggesting earlier somatic maturation [33, 34]. Using relative age enables us to avoid the dependency between bone age (BA) and chronological age. Despite this, we also modelled chronological age by itself and bone age by itself and examined the likelihood ratios for model fit and parsimony and to determine added effect of relative skeletal age compared to chronological age in predicting AMH levels. To illustrate the cross‐sectional association between AMH and maturation, we also grouped participants into three categories [34, 35]. If the relative skeletal age of a participant was > 1 standard deviation (SD) above the mean, the participant was considered to be late maturing. If the relative skeletal age of a participant was > 1 SD below the mean, females were considered to be early maturing at the first visit [34].

Covariates

Adiposity variables including body mass index (BMI) percentile, waist circumference, waist to height ratio, and total percent body fat from dual energy X‐ray absorptiometry [16] were measured at each study visit. BMI percentile was categorized using the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention's guidelines for defining childhood obesity where underweight was < 5th percentile, normal or healthy weight was ≥ 5th percentile but < 85th percentile, overweight was ≥ 85th percentile but < 95th percentile, and obese was ≥ 95th percentile.

Cardiometabolic risk factors have been known to be associated with growth and sexual maturation [36]. These variables included the homeostatic model assessment of insulin resistance (HOMA‐IR) index, glucose, insulin, systolic blood pressure (SBP), diastolic blood pressure (DBP), triglycerides, total cholesterol, high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL‐C), and low‐density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL‐C). Detailed information regarding the measurements of cardiometabolic risk factors can be found in Remsberg et al. [36] and Limon et al. [37].

Statistical Analysis

All statistical tests were conducted two‐sided and the results were considered significant at a p < 0.05. STATA (version 16) and SAS (version 9.4) were used for all data analyses.

As AMH levels were not normally distributed, the variable was natural log transformed for all analyses. One‐way ANOVA was used to compare the three maturing categories according to the relative skeletal age at participants' first visit, using mean log‐transformed AMH (AMHlog) values across the early, average, or late maturing categories. AMHlog observations were plotted against both chronological and skeletal age to examine patterns of variability over time by two timing variables.

Generalized linear mixed effect modeling was conducted utilizing a stepwise forward approach consisting of five statistical models. Model 0 included chronological age, chronological age2, and chronological age3. Model 1 included Model 0 parameters plus relative age. Model 2 included all variables from Model 1 with the addition of one adiposity variable from four adiposity variables (BMI percentile, waist circumference, waist to height ratio, or total % body fat). Model 3 included all variables from Model 2 with the addition of a significant cardiometabolic risk factor from the pool of cardiometabolic risk factors. A single cardiometabolic variable was included in the model to avoid multicollinearity. Model 4 included bone age, bone age2, and bone age3. All generalized linear mixed effect models included a random intercept and an unstructured covariance matrix adjusting for unbalanced serial correlated observations. The likelihood ratio test (LRT) was used to determine model parsimony and whether or not certain covariates were significant. All methods and analyses were performed in accordance with the Wright State University IRB and UTHealth Houston CPHS guidelines and regulations.

Results

Participants' Characteristics at the First Study Visit

A total of 212 serial observations from 88 adolescent girls (age range: 8–18 years) who had serial measurements of AMH levels, skeletal age, and covariates such as cardiometabolic risk factors were included for the present study. At the first visit, AMH levels ranged from 0.46 to 16.20 ng/mL, had a mean of 4.85 ng/mL, and a median of 3.65 ng/mL. Chronological age at the first visit ranged from 7.71 to 16.91 years with an average of 11.61 years and a SD of 2.75 years. Bone age at the first visit ranged from 7.05 to 18.00 years with an average of 12.19 years and a SD of 3.10 years. The average relative skeletal age at first visit was −0.57 years with a SD of 1.19 years and ranged from −3.37 to 3.01 years. Approximately 16% of participants were in early maturing group at the first visit, 69% of participants were in normal or average maturing group, and 15% of participants were in late maturing group. The majority of participants (72.7%) were of normal or healthy weight, 3.4% were underweight, 11.4% were overweight, and 12.5% were obese. To build onto Model 1 and account for adiposity characteristics, four adiposity variables were separately included in the second mixed model (Model 2). None of the adiposity variables (BMI percentile [p = 0.19], waist circumference [p = 0.92], waist to height ratio [p = 0.25], and total percent body fat [p = 0.18]) was significant at α = 0.05 level. Although none of the adiposity variables considered were significant, total % body fat was added into Model 2 as it had a higher −2LogL value (i.e., lowest p value) compared to the other adiposity variables at 248.8. Relative age had a β coefficient of −0.065 (SE = 0.032) and remained significant in the second model with a p value of 0.04 (Table 2). In Model 3, 15 additional cardiometabolic variables were each considered individually for the final mixed effect model (Model 3). Fasting glucose had a β coefficient of −0.008 (SE = 0.004) and was significant in the model with a p value of 0.04 and the model had a −2LogL of 247.3. Thus, glucose was incorporated into the final model (Table 2). None of the other cardiometabolic variables were significant or included in the final model. In this final model, relative age had a β coefficient of −0.073 (SE = 0.032) and was significant with a p value of 0.02 (Table 2). Finally, we modeled only bone age, bone age2, and bone age3 to compare against the Models 0 and 1 to determine which model had the best fit and was the most parsimonious.

The LRTs indicate that the relative age parameter is significant in Model 1 and that the addition of relative skeletal age improves significantly and parsimoniously the fit of Model 0 (Model 1). Overall, Model 4 was more parsimonious than Model 1. These results suggest that using bone age alone better explains AMH levels but it is more difficult to interpret measures of bone age. On the other hand, using chronological age and a measure of skeletal maturity (i.e., relative skeletal age) simultaneously is more easily interpreted, while model fit is not as bad as chronological age.

Discussion

Our study revealed nonlinear patterns for AMHlog by skeletal age and chronological age in female children and study results indicated that the relationship between skeletal age. AMHlog showed similar patterns to that of the previously reported chronological age and AMHlog [16]. In our previous study, we found a significant, nonlinear relationship between AMH levels and chronological age. These results indicate that AMH levels in children do not reflect full ovarian reserve [21].

AMH is expressed by granulosa cells of growing follicles beginning at the primary stage. While many consider AMH levels as an indicator of ovarian follicle reserve, it is only a measure of growing follicles [21, 22]. AMH is not produced by primordial follicles; high AMH inhibits primordial follicle activation [38]. Thus, AMH acts as negative feedback limiting the number of follicles entering the growing pool and protecting long‐term follicle reserves in adults. Our study, like others, found a steady increase in AMH levels indicating an increasing number of follicles entering the growing pool. Interestingly, our previous study identified a transient dip in AMH as adolescent girls approach puberty [16]. This indicates a possible prepubertal follicular wave occurs during human ovarian development, as it does in many other species [39]. Prepubertal hormone levels are not high enough to induce ovulation, therefore the growing follicles regress and AMH would decline. This decrease in AMH would allow for increased activation of primordial follicles, increased numbers of growing follicles with increased granulosa cell proliferation, and estrogen secretion followed by increased follicle‐stimulating hormone (FSH), all factors essential for puberty and entry into adult follicular cycling.

We found that peak AMHlog occurred near 16 years of chronological age, which was similar to previous studies [17, 18, 40]. We have compared our results to other studies that have reported AMH levels in females from birth into adulthood. Kelsey et al. [18] conducted a review of available literature and detected a decline in AMH levels during childhood similar to our results, although at a slightly earlier age (i.e., 8–12 years) [18]. The difference in age may be due to differences in study population. Lie Fong et al. [17] and Cui et al. [40] did not identify a significant dip in AMH levels during prepubertal development, although Cui et al. did mention an increase in fluctuation in AMH levels during the age range from 10 to 18 years [17, 40]. Most of these studies have been conducted on single samples from children at varying ages, thus single points of chronological age. Since our study resulted from repeated measures on individual girls as they aged, our model may be more sensitive to the relatively small waves of follicle growth and AMH levels.

Interestingly, our results demonstrated a different transitional pattern between AMH and skeletal age, than the pattern between AMH and chronological age. The pattern shown in Figure 2B indicates the relationships between AMH and maturation for participants who are biologically less mature than their chronological age. For this reason, it is possible that the true transition of AMHlog during puberty would more closely mirror the plot of AMHlog by skeletal age (Figure 2B). The generalized linear mixed effect model (Model 3, Table 2) confirmed the curvilinear association between relative skeletal age and AMHlog after controlling for all other variables. Our findings suggest a possible decrease in AMHlog levels in individuals who experience late maturation based on the tempo of growth categories at participants' first study visit. However, our cross‐sectional results did not show a statistically significant relationship between AMHlog and delayed skeletal maturation. These differences between the cross‐sectional and longitudinal analysis may be due to the increased power of the longitudinal study design, and its ability to incorporate interindividual variability in the analysis.

AMH levels provide valuable information on reproductive health in females and could be a useful biomarker in identifying suitable gamete donors and ensuing ART success [3, 6, 7]. As mentioned previously, AMH research in adolescents predominantly focuses on populations with preexisting conditions related to sexual maturity, hormones and fertility [9, 10, 11, 19, 41] and, though limited, research on AMH and growth and skeletal maturation has typically evaluated sexual characteristics [42, 43, 44]. Research regarding AMH in healthy adolescent populations using other maturational indicators is limited. Recent in vitro studies, however, provided some evidence of the regulatory role of AMH exposure on human bone remodeling mechanism, leading to a potential relationship between AMH and skeletal health [25, 29]. Our current study is the first to characterize the relationship between the role of AMH and skeletal age throughout the childhood of adolescent females. We further explored how AMH levels relate to skeletal maturity, a maturation indicator of somatic and skeletal growth rather than the maturation of secondary sex characteristics (e.g., breast development).

Regarding cardiometabolic risk factors, after holding all other variables constant, we found a one‐unit increase in glucose was associated with a decrease of 0.008 in AMHlog (1.01 ng/mL in AMH). That is, AMHlog had a significant, negative relationship with glucose. In a study using normal and overweight‐obese adolescent females, investigators found positive relationships between AMH and fasting glucose [45] while others found no correlation [40] or a negative relationship [46]. In a healthy, general population of adolescent females, cardiometabolic risk factors were not found to be associated with AMH levels [47]. We also found that there was no significant association between other metabolic factors including high body fat and AMH levels. Due to the inconsistencies regarding the association between cardiometabolic risk and AMH in the literature, particularly among healthy populations, it appears that much is still unknown about the true relationship between AMH and glucose.

A strength of this study included its longitudinal design, which allowed for the temporal analysis of AMH over several years. Also, along with increased precision and accuracy, the Fels method for the hand‐wrist is an appropriate skeletal age assessment method for this study sample of adolescent females in the United States because the skeletal ages from which the Fels method was created are comparable to that obtained from United States national surveys [30]. Additionally, the examination of a multitude of adiposity and cardiometabolic variables in growing children allowed us to incorporate these variables into the modeling and to account for biologically relevant relationships that have been identified in the literature with AMH levels. Furthermore, this study may serve as a foundation for further examination of AMH and skeletal maturation as the relationship between skeletal age and AHM levels has not yet been described.

The advantages of using skeletal age as an indication of maturation have been noted in many pediatric epidemiologic studies [26, 33, 34]. However, one limitation of using skeletal age as a growth indicator is that it may only be obtained up until hand ossification is completed. Once the skeletal age of a participant reaches 18 years it cannot exceed that age. A second limitation in this study is that the study sample included only 88 participants, despite the multiple observations available for approximately 60% of the participants. Third, we are unable to generalize the results of this study to a wider, more diverse population as the Fels Longitudinal Study consists of largely middle‐class, non‐Hispanic White participants.

In conclusion, this study is the first to describe the relationship between AMH and skeletal age. With clinical indicators, clinicians can provide information on either precocious or delayed puberty as well as endocrine abnormalities during puberty. As shown in studies of pre‐ and postmenopausal women in AMH and bone metabolism [48, 49], our study potentially expands the utility of skeletal age measurements to characterize endocrine and reproductive capacity. Conversely, the level of AMH, a sensitive measure of antral follicle counts and a preferred biomarker over FSH, could provide clinical perspectives on bone maturation during puberty. Future studies should longitudinally analyze the relationships of AMH with skeletal age and chronological age, as well as the interrelation between skeletal maturation or bone metabolism and AMH level in samples that are representative of the general population of healthy adolescent females.
