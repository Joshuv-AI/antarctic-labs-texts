Highly Efficient Methane Electrosynthesis Enabled by Precise, Multifaceted Interface Regulation
Authors: Weicong Xu, Xiaomin Xu, Chao Liu, Feifan Huang, Bin Wang, Peijun Li, Xiaofeng Ke, Xiangchen Kong, Qingwen Chen, Wulejiasi Aerxin, Anze Ning, Guangtong Hai, Huiyan Zhang, Tao Li, Rui Xiao, Zongping Shao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The electrocatalytic reduction of carbon dioxide (CO2) offers a pathway to transform greenhouse gas emissions into fuels and chemicals using renewable electricity. Among possible products, methane (CH4) is particularly attractive due to its high energy density and seamless integration with existing natural gas infrastructure, yet its electrosynthesis is hindered by demanding multi-electron kinetics and mass-transport constraints. Here, we report a four-channel copper tubular penetration electrode (TPE) with a honeycomb-like architecture that fundamentally reshapes the reaction environment for CO2 electroreduction. By precisely controlling electrode cross-sectional thickness, this multi-channel design regulates gas, electron, and electrolyte transport, stabilizing the three-phase interface required for efficient CH4 formation. The optimized TPE achieves a CH4 Faradaic efficiency of 87.5%, a half-cell energy efficiency of 43.46%, and stable operation for 100 hours. Combined experimental and theoretical analyses reveal that rational design of electrode architecture enables precise control over the CO2 adsorption configurations and reaction intermediates, thereby fine-tuning the reaction pathway toward CH4. These findings establish multi-channel TPEs as a powerful platform for efficient CO2 electromethanation.

Introduction

The electrocatalytic CO2 reduction reaction (eCO2RR) offers a promising route to transform CO2 into value-added fuels and chemicals using renewable electricity, supporting the transition toward a carbon-neutral economy1–3. Among various eCO2RR products, methane (CH4) is especially attractive because of its high gravimetric energy density (55.5 MJ kg−1), clean combustion, and dominant role in the global energy and chemical industries4. As the main component of natural gas, CH4 accounts for ~20-25% of global energy consumption and benefits from an established infrastructure for large-scale storage, transport, and utilization5. Electrochemical CO2-to-CH4 conversion, therefore, provides a direct pathway to decarbonize existing natural gas supplies while enabling long-term storage of intermittent renewable electricity. However, CH4 electrosynthesis requires a kinetically demanding eight-electron, proton-coupled reduction, making it difficult to achieve high selectivity, energy efficiency, and durability simultaneously6,7. These challenges place stringent demands not only on catalyst design but also on electrode architectures that can sustain efficient gas, electron, and electrolyte transport.

Recent advances in gas diffusion electrodes (GDEs), typically composed of high-activity catalysts, hydrophobic binders, and conductive additives, have accelerated progress toward practical eCO2RR technologies8–12. However, conventional plate-type GDEs rely on passive CO2 diffusion in the absence of a pressure gradient, which constrains gas transport under high-rate operation. Furthermore, the complex multi-component assembly of GDEs often compromises long-term stability: weak interfacial adhesion between the catalyst and substrate can lead to catalyst detachment and electrolyte intrusion into the gas diffusion layer, commonly referred to as flooding13. These interfacial and mass-transport limitations ultimately undermine catalytic selectivity and durability, posing major obstacles to efficient and sustained eCO2RR processes14,15. In this context, alternative electrode architectures that can actively regulate gas transport while maintaining structural integrity are highly desirable.

Tubular penetration electrodes (TPEs) have recently emerged as a promising alternative to conventional GDEs by incorporating an intrinsic pressure gradient that actively drives CO2 transport through a porous tubular framework16. The continuous, binder-free porous architecture of TPEs enables uniform gas distribution and promotes in situ CO2 pre-activation as the gas traverses the electrode cross-section16–18, thereby establishing a robust three-phase reaction interface that enhances both mass transfer and reaction kinetics. Importantly, the absence of hydrophobic binders and additives suppresses flooding and improves operational robustness relative to plate-type GDEs19–21. To date, however, reported TPE designs have been largely limited to single-channel configurations. CO2 electroreduction in these single-channel TPEs, based on silver19, tin22, and copper17,23,24, has predominantly yielded C1 products such as carbon monoxide (CO) or formate (HCOOH) with high efficiency, highlighting both the promise and current selectivity limitations of this electrode architecture.

Despite these advances, single-channel TPEs face inherent challenges in achieving the mass-transport environments required for deep CO2 electroreduction to highly reduced products such as CH4. Their relatively thick cross-sections (~100-300 μm) impose extended CO2 gas diffusion pathways, which limit local CO2 availability and hinder multi-electron, proton-coupled reduction processes17,22,24,25. In addition, fabrication protocols for TPEs remain insufficiently developed, often resulting in electrodes with poorly controlled cross-sectional thickness (CST), porosity, and wettability. Such structural variability strongly influences proton-electron transfer at the microscale and gas-liquid mass transport at the mesoscale, affecting CO2 diffusion, electrolyte penetration, intermediate stabilization, and ultimately product selectivity. As a result, precise control over cross-sectional geometry and interfacial properties emerges as a critical yet underexplored lever to steer reaction pathways toward deep-reduction products in TPE-based eCO2RR systems.

Herein, we report a structurally tunable four-channel copper (Cu) TPE featuring a honeycomb-like architecture designed to overcome the intrinsic mass-transport barriers of single-channel systems and enable deep CO2 electroreduction to CH4. The multi-channel design introduces parallel gas transport pathways and enables precise control over key microstructural parameters, most notably, CST, which can be systematically reduced to a wide range below 100 μm, thereby shortening CO2 diffusion lengths while maintaining structural integrity. This architecture establishes a robust and stable three-phase reaction environment that enhances coupled CO2 transport, electron transfer, and electrolyte penetration, conditions that are essential for kinetically demanding eight-electron CH4 formation (Fig. 1). Notably, the optimized four-channel TPE achieves highly efficient CO2-to-CH4 conversion, delivering a CH4 Faradaic efficiency (FE) of 87.5% at low overpotentials, a half-cell energy efficiency (CEE) of 43.46%, and a stable operation for 100 h. By correlating architectural parameters with electrochemical performance, and supported by in situ spectroscopy and theoretical modeling, this work reveals how rational electrode design can unlock efficient electromethanation and provides a generalizable blueprint for engineering advanced TPEs toward high-performance eCO2RR.Fig. 1Schematic illustration of the CO2 electroreduction reaction on structurally tunable four-channel Cu TPE.Schematic illustration of selectively modulating CO2 diffusion lengths and interfacial properties to steer the eCO2RR pathway.

Results

Electrode preparation and characterization

A four-channel Cu TPE was fabricated via a phase-inversion-assisted extrusion and sintering method using commercial Cu powder (Fig. 2A, B, and Supplementary Fig. 1). Cross-sectional scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of the green body confirmed the spherical morphology of the raw Cu powder (Supplementary Fig. 2), while those of the sintered TPE revealed a characteristic finger-like porous structure with an average CST of ~51.9 μm (Fig. 2C–G) (named as CST-51.9 for convenience). The well-fused porous network featured percolated microporous channels that promote efficient CO2 transport to the three-phase reaction interface (Fig. 2E). The time of gas diffusion could be further manipulated by thickness control, showing an inverse trend between gas permeation flux and the thickness of the diffusion layer26, validating our method for estimating the equivalent thickness of cross-section (Supplementary Fig. 3). High-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HR-TEM) image and the corresponding fast Fourier transform (FFT) pattern confirmed the presence of metallic Cu (Fig. 2H), in agreement with X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis (Fig. 2I). The green body displayed diffraction peaks corresponding to Cu, which were converted to CuO upon calcination and subsequently reduced back to metallic Cu under a hydrogen atmosphere. Interestingly, X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) revealed a surface dominated by Cu+ species (Fig. 2J), likely arising from partial oxidation upon air exposure27, without impacting the intrinsic structure or catalytic performance. Mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) confirmed that porosity remained relatively unchanged after binder removal upon calcination, but increased significantly following hydrogen reduction due to oxygen removal (Supplementary Fig. 4). Using the same fabrication technique with tailored synthesis conditions, a series of Cu TPEs with precise control over their average CST were prepared, abbreviated as CST-X, where X denotes the CST of the Cu TPE (Supplementary Table 1 and Supplementary Figs. 5–7). To fabricate the working electrode, the Cu TPE was inserted into a copper tube using conductive silver to establish electrical contact. CO2 electroreduction performance

The CO2 electroreduction was performed under ambient conditions in a near-neutral electrolyte (0.1 M KHCO3), using a testing system schematically illustrated in Supplementary Figs. 9, 10, where gas- and liquid-phase products were analyzed via online gas chromatography (GC) and offline 1H nuclear magnetic resonance (1H-NMR), respectively (Supplementary Figs. 11, 12). Based on linear sweep voltammetry (LSV), the electrolysis potential window was selected between −0.51 and −0.91 V vs. reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) (Supplementary Fig. 13). The product distributions for different Cu TPE electrodes as a function of applied potentials were summarized in Supplementary Fig. 14.

To elucidate the role of electrode structure in governing product selectivity, we systematically examined the effect of CST on eCO2RR performance. As shown in Fig. 3A, variations in CST led to pronounced changes in product distributions. Electrodes with relatively thin CSTs (36.1 ± 1.2 μm and 44.6 ± 0.4 μm) yielded mixed products, primarily CH4 and HCOOH. Strikingly, increasing the CST to an intermediate range (between 51.9 ± 1.1 μm and 82.1 ± 1.0 μm) markedly enhanced CH4 selectivity, with the 51.9 μm electrode achieving the highest CH4 FE of 87.5% ± 4.3%. To further probe thickness effects, a Cu TPE with ~100 μm CST was prepared and assessed for eCO2RR performance under identical conditions (Supplementary Figs. 15, 16), which generated CO as the dominant product (CO FE of 58.2% ± 1.4%), consistent with prior reports that overly thick electrodes (>100 μm) favor CO formation (see reported results compiled in Fig. 3A)17,22,24,25. In addition to product selectivity, CEE, full-cell energy efficiency (EE), and product formation rates were evaluated to assess the overall eCO2RR performance toward CH4. Achieving high half-cell EE requires simultaneously maximizing FE and minimizing overpotential, which remains challenging for multielectron CH4 formation. As shown in Fig. 3B, CEE for CO2-to-CH4 exhibited a strong potential dependence, reflecting the distinct overpotentials required for each electrode to achieve its maximum \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$${\mathrm{FE}}_{{\mathrm{CH}}_{4}}$$\end{document}FECH4. Among all tested electrodes, CST-51.9 delivered the highest CH4 CEE, reaching 43.46% at −0.91 V vs. RHE. The EE of CH4 products was further evaluated to provide a more realistic assessment of industrial applicability. The cell voltage was in the range of −2.9 to −2.1 V, and the derived CH4 EE of different TPEs showed an overall increasing trend (Supplementary Fig. 17). The CST-51.9 exhibited the highest CH4 EE, reaching 32.98% at −2.9 V. Consistently, the CH4 formation rate increased with increasingly negative potentials, with CST-51.9 achieving a maximum rate of 0.26 μmol s−1 cm−2 at −0.91 V vs. RHE (Fig. 3C). The CH4 partial current density at different potentials for TPEs was obtained based on the E-I curves (Supplementary Fig. 18 and Fig. 3D). The magnitude of the partial current density for a given product directly reflected its actual formation rate. A higher partial current density of the product corresponds to a faster product formation rate. As shown in Fig. 3D, the variation trend of the CH4 partial current density was consistent with that of the CH4 formation rate. Furthermore, long-term electrolysis demonstrated the robustness of this optimized architecture, as CST-51.9 sustained a stable current density of ~40 mA cm−2 and a high CH4 FE of ~87.5% over 100 h of continuous operation at −0.91 V vs. RHE (Fig. 3E). Post-reaction SEM, XRD, and XPS analyses revealed negligible changes in electrode morphology, crystal structure, or material composition (Supplementary Figs. 19–21), confirming fine structural stability. Notably, relative to recent CO2-to-CH4 electrolysis reports, the optimized multi-channel TPE (i.e., CST-51.9) achieves a compelling combination of FE, CEE, formation rate, and durability (Supplementary Table 2). Together, these results underscore the critical role of electrode architecture in steering CO2 electroreduction pathways toward methanation, with precise control over CST providing a rational design strategy for high-performance TPEs (Fig. 3F). In contrast, the CO2 non-penetration mode (Supplementary Fig. 22A), which lacks an epoxy seal, is limited by the sluggish CO2 mass transfer from the bubbled CO2 dissolving in the electrolyte to the electrode surface for initiating eCO2RR, akin to conventional planar electrodes. Interestingly, the CST-51.9 exhibits substantially enhanced eCO2RR performance in the CO2 penetration configuration (Supplementary Fig. 14C) relative to the non-penetration configuration (Supplementary Fig. 22B).

As shown in Fig. 4A, the CO2 concentration primarily exhibited a longitudinal, band-like distribution along the thin-walled area of the TPEs, rather than forming a uniform diffusion pattern over the entire circumference. This indicated that CO2 transport within the porous electrodes was nonuniform and displayed a distinct local preferential effect. Among these, the high-concentration CO2 area of the CST-51.9 formed the fastest and exhibited the greatest continuity. During the reaction, the electrolyte seeped along the thin-walled region toward the interior, approaching the CO2 gas-phase region. Therefore, for the CST-51.9 with a thinner wall, higher porosity, and larger pore size, the electrolyte could penetrate more easily and continuously, forming numerous gas-liquid-solid three-phase reaction interfaces that provided the local microenvironment required for deep CO2 hydrogenation (Fig. 4B). Based on the simulated local CO2 concentration (Fig. 4C-F and Supplementary Figs. 23–25), CST-51.9 exhibited the most favorable CO2 transport characteristics. When it was diffused along the thickness direction, the color decays most slowly, and most of the internal region remained orange-yellow. This indicated that CO2 had a stronger penetrating ability and lower local resistance within its porous network, thereby supplying sufficient CO2 to the gas-liquid-solid three-phase reaction interface and promoting the conversion of CO2 to CH4. As shown in Supplementary Figs. 26 and 27, the pressure distribution and CO2 volume-fraction results further demonstrated that the CST-51.9 exhibited the lowest overall CO2 transport resistance and better pore connectivity, which was conducive to maintaining a continuous and stable CO2 transport process. Considering the eCO2RR performance (Supplementary Fig. 14), we found that the CST-36.1 with the thinnest CST did not exhibit the best mass-transfer characteristics and optimal electrochemical performance. This suggests that eCO2RR performance was governed not just by the TPEs’ cross-sectional thickness for gas transport, but also potentially by their porosity. Indeed, variations in porosity are well known to alter the microscopic electron transfer rate, which in turn impacts the multi-proton-electron coupling processes during eCO2RR28. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) confirmed this correlation (Fig. 4G–I): as porosity increased (from CST-100 to CST-36.1 and further to CST-51.9), the electron transfer resistance gradually decreased, consistent with enhanced charge transport (Supplementary Fig. 28). Simultaneously, the increase in porosity was associated with a larger electrochemically active surface area (ECSA) (Supplementary Figs. 29 and 30), which improved reactant accessibility to active sites. Together, these effects demonstrate that higher porosity significantly facilitated electron transfer during eCO2RR.

Influence of interfacial wettability on H2O mass transport

Beyond facilitating electron transfer, porosity also modulated interfacial wettability, thereby exerting a critical influence on eCO2RR performance29–31. Contact angle measurements showed a decrease from 62.6° to 42.1° and to 22.2° with increasing porosity from 36.04% to 47.64% and to 59.30% for CST-100, CST-36.1, and CST-51.9, respectively (Fig. 5A–C), indicating enhanced hydrophilicity. This facilitated proton donor availability for eCO2RR, consistent with previous studies32. The LSV measurements under an Ar atmosphere reflected the pure hydrogen evolution reaction (HER) activity of the different TPEs. The HER current density increased with increasing wettability33, indicating a positive correlation between pure HER activity and the proton donor availability34(Supplementary Fig. 31). During eCO2RR, the primary proton donors were the adsorbed hydrogen intermediates (*H) derived from bulk H2O, and their surface coverage was strongly governed by water transport28. Although the direct detection of *H intermediates on Cu by spectroscopic methods such as in-situ attenuated total reflectance infrared (ATR-IR) spectroscopy was precluded by their intrinsically weak adsorption affinity, their interfacial coverage could be indirectly inferred from the local availability of water31,35–38. To probe this, confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was employed to visualize interfacial water distribution31. In the CLSM images, green fluorescent regions indicated the electrolyte phase, whereas dark areas corresponded to the solid electrode. The attenuation of fluorescence intensity at the liquid phase boundary indicates the reduction of available water. A linear z-axis fluorescence intensity scan (white arrows) across the cross-sectional image revealed that with increasing electrode porosity, the attenuation distance decreased from 14.03 to 8.03 μm (Fig. 5D–F), indicating an enhanced extent of water penetration and a higher effective interfacial water concentration. This trend correlated with increased hydrophilicity, reflecting improved H2O transport and consequently enhanced *H coverage. To further rationalize these observations, we propose a local geometric model of the electrodes (Fig. 5G–I). With increasing electrode porosity, the enhanced wettability allows the dynamic gas-liquid-solid three-phase interface to progressively penetrate radially into the porous cross-section. This penetration elevates the local *H coverage, thereby favoring deeper hydrogenation of eCO2RR intermediates and promoting CH4 formation. Understanding of the eCO2RR reaction pathways

To elucidate the influence of mass transfer on the eCO2RR reaction pathway, we employed in-situ ATR-IR to identify reaction intermediates at the dynamic three-phase reaction interface. The distinct intermediate species observed for each TPE system, CST-36.1, CST-51.9, and CST-100, provide critical insights into their respective reaction mechanisms (Fig. 6A–C). For CST-36.1, characteristic vibration bands were identified at ~1261 cm−1, ~1362 cm−1, ~1490 cm−1, and ~2074 cm−1, which were assigned to the key intermediates *COOH, HCOO*, *CHO, and *CO, respectively (Fig. 6A)39,40. The initial step involved the formation of *COOH, which subsequently underwent hydrogenation to yield the key atop-adsorbed *COatop intermediate. As the potential became increasingly negative, the *COatop peak at ~2074 cm−1 exhibited a red-shift to ~2069 cm−1, consistent with the Stark effect38. Subsequent protonation of *COatop led to the formation of *CHO, which was a crucial intermediate for CH4 production40. By comparison, the CST-51.9 TPE displayed a somewhat distinct set of intermediate peaks at ~1261 cm−1, ~1392 cm−1, ~1490 cm−1, and ~2055 cm−1, corresponding to *COOH, *OCH3, *CHO, and *CO, respectively (Fig. 6B)41,42. Similar to CST-36.1, the *COatop peak for CST-51.9 red-shifted from ~2055 cm−1 to ~2045 cm−1 at more negative potentials, indicating a stronger interaction between the *CO intermediate and the catalyst surface. This enhanced interaction facilitated further protonation, leading to the formation of *CHO and further multi-protonation coupling to generate *OCH3, another key intermediate for the formation of CH4. In the case of CST-100, a prominent peak was observed at ~1261 cm−1, corresponding to the *COOH intermediate (Fig. 6C). Interestingly, despite high CO production, no distinct *CO peak was detected. In addition, in-situ Raman characterization was conducted to explore the adsorption behaviours of different reaction intermediates over CST-36.1, CST-51.9, and CST-100 during eCO2RR (Supplementary Figs. 33–35). The results of in-situ Raman spectra were consistent with previously reported data44–46, revealing distinct vibrational bands characteristic of key reaction intermediates. The bands at around 290, 350, and 2090 cm−1 correspond to the frustrated rotation, Cu-CO stretching, and C-O stretching of linearly adsorbed *COL, respectively, which is generally considered a key intermediate for CO2 deep reduction to CH444. The intensity of Cu-COL and *COL over CST-100 was weaker than that of CST-36.1 and CST-51.9, indicating that *CO tends to desorb to form CO, which was consistent with the in-situ ATR-IR conclusion. The band at 1015-1020 cm−1 corresponds to HCO3−47. The band at 1060-1070 cm−1 corresponds to CO32−48. The relative intensity of CO32− and HCO3− reflected the local pH near the reaction interface. The band at 1386 cm−1 can be assigned to the C-O vibration of *COOH intermediate49,50. Additionally, the band at 1550 cm−1 was associated with the bending vibration (δ(H2O)) of interfacial water51,52.

We further employed density functional theory (DFT) calculations to systematically investigate how electrode structure governs the eCO2RR reaction pathway (Fig. 6D–F), specifically focusing on the formation of CO, HCOOH, and CH4. However, DFT calculations are atomic-scale simulations. Therefore, it is neither feasible nor physically meaningful to directly reproduce the actual micrometer-scale pore dimensions of the TPEs in a periodic DFT model. Instead, our focus was to control variables and construct simplified, physically motivated models that capture the dominant atomic-scale consequences of these structural differences. Our analysis focused on the free-energy changes of reaction intermediates for each product across the different TPEs. For the CST-36.1 electrode, the pathways to CO and CH4 shared three common intermediates: *CO2, *COOH, and *CO (Fig. 6D)53. A critical branching point occurred at the *CO intermediate, which could either desorb directly to form CO or undergo hydrogenation to form CH4. We found that the hydrogenation of *CO to *COH was more energetically favorable than its desorption, thereby promoting the formation of CH4. The rate-determining step (RDS) for CH4 production was the conversion of *COOH to *CO, with an energy barrier of 0.712 eV. Interestingly, the pathway to HCOOH proceeded through a distinct CO2 adsorption configuration, which significantly lowered the energy barrier for the *CO2 to HCOO* conversion from 1.030 to 0.872 eV, thereby promoting HCOOH formation (Supplementary Fig. 36). These findings demonstrated that different CO2 adsorption modes might lead to diverse reaction pathways, enabling the simultaneous production of CH4 and HCOOH, a result that aligned perfectly with our experimental observations.

On the CST-51.9 electrode (Fig. 6E), the RDS for HCOOH formation was the conversion of *CO2 to HCOO*, with an energy barrier of approximately 1.327 eV. In contrast, the RDS for both CH4 and CO production was the conversion of *CO2 to *COOH, with a lower energy barrier of about 1.047 eV. This suggests that the pathways to CH4 and CO were more energetically favorable. Further analysis revealed that the hydrogenation of *CO to *COH was energetically preferred over its desorption, while the subsequent hydrogenation steps of *COH to *C and *CH4 were exothermic, making the overall pathway to CH4 highly favorable. Interestingly, our in-situ ATR-IR results for CST-51.9 not only detected *COH but also identified *OCH3 as a key intermediate for CH4 and CH3OH generation54,55. DFT calculations revealed that the free energy for the hydrogenation of *COH to *CHOH was lower than that for the formation of *C (Supplementary Fig. 37), making the *CHOH pathway energetically more favorable and leading to the subsequent formation of *OCH3. The free energy for the hydrogenation of *OCH3 to form *CH4 was 0.156 eV, which was significantly lower than the free energy for the protonation of *OCH3 to *CH3OH (0.279 eV) (Supplementary Fig. 37). This result confirmed that in our specific reaction environment, the pathway to CH4 was thermodynamically more favorable than the pathway to CH3OH56. This computational finding was in agreement with our experimental observation that CH4 was the dominant C1 product while CH3OH was not detected. Furthermore, the energy barrier for the hydrogenation of *CO to *COH was lower than that for the dimerization of *CO, indicating that a higher concentration of *H inhibited C-C coupling to form multi-carbon products57 (Supplementary Fig. 38). For the CST-100 electrode, our calculations revealed that the energy barriers for the RDS for CO, HCOOH, and CH4 production were 0.688 eV (*COOH → *CO), 1.065 eV (*CO2 → HCOO*), and 0.783 eV (*CO → *COH), respectively (Fig. 6F). A comparative analysis of these energy barriers clearly indicated that the pathway for CO formation was the most energetically favorable on the CST-100 electrode.

The specific reaction pathway for the formation of different products was further clarified by combining in-situ ATR-IR and CLSM results to quantify the surface coverage of key intermediates, namely *CO and *H, as a function of the TPEs’ structural properties (Fig. 6G–I). This approach allowed us to directly correlate changes in intermediate coverage with observed product distributions. In-situ ATR-IR peak intensity of adsorbed species served as a proxy for their coverage, while the fluorescence attenuation distance in CLSM provided a quantitative measure of local H2O availability and, by extension, proton (*H) availability. Gas transport through the electrode’s porous structure is critical to the reaction pathway and can be described by different mechanisms based on the kinetic theory of gases, which is dependent on the ratio of the pore radius (rp) to the gas mean free path (λ) (see Methods for more details). The mean free path was the average distance a gas molecule travels between collisions, a value dependent on temperature, gas pressure, and molecular collision diameter. Based on this relationship, three primary transport regimes exist: Knudsen flow (rp/λ < 0.05), slip flow (0.05<rp/λ < 3), and viscous flow (rp/λ > 3)58.

For the CST-36.1 catalyst, which co-produces CH4 and HCOOH, the gas diffusion model operated in a slip flow regime (rp/λ = 1.61, see Supplementary Table 3 for more details), where molecular collisions with pore walls were more frequent than intermolecular collisions. This type of transport dynamics promoted multiple CO2 adsorption configurations on the active sites, thereby enabling distinct reaction pathways (Fig. 6G). The pathway to HCOOH was relatively direct, with CO2 being hydrogenated to the HCOO* intermediate before a final protonation step. In contrast, the pathway to CH4 was more complex, requiring multiple proton-electron transfers starting with the formation of the *COOH intermediate, followed by hydrogenation to *COH and subsequent steps to yield CH4. This slip flow transport mechanism provided a compelling explanation for the observed diversity in reaction products by facilitating varied reactant adsorption modes, which steered the reaction toward both HCOOH and CH4 formation.

In contrast, both the CST-51.9 and CST-100 catalysts operated in a viscous flow regime (rp/λ > 3), where diffusion was dominated by intermolecular collisions. This transport mode restricted the diversity of reactant adsorption configurations, favoring a single, dominant reaction pathway. For CST-51.9, this pathway led to high CH4 selectivity (Fig. 6H). The electrode structure facilitated rapid CO2 mass transfer and allowed the three-phase interface to penetrate deeply into the porous cross-section. This deep penetration enhanced the local H2O concentration, providing an abundant proton source (*H) that drove the multi-proton-electron coupling necessary for deep CO2 reduction. Critically, the generated *CO intermediate did not desorb but was instead further hydrogenated to a key *OCH3 intermediate, ultimately leading to CH4. For CST-100, the poor hydrophilicity confined the three-phase interface to the electrode surface (Fig. 6I). This limited access to H2O, along with the high resistance to mass transfer and electron transport within the porous structure, prevented the multi-proton-electron coupling required for CH4 formation. Consequently, the *CO intermediate readily desorbed from the surface, resulting in high CO selectivity.

Discussion

In summary, we have demonstrated that a structurally tunable four-channel Cu TPE enables precise regulation of mass transfer at the gas-liquid-solid three-phase interface, thereby creating environments that are singularly suited for deep CO2 electroreduction to CH4. Through rational electrode design, the CO2 adsorption configuration and types of key intermediates (*CO, HCOO*, and *OCH3) can be regulated to achieve selective switching among C1 products (CO, HCOOH, and CH4). The optimized configuration is thermodynamically favorable for the CO2 electroreduction to CH4, thereby delivering highly selective, energy-efficient, and durable CO2-to-CH4 conversion. This work establishes a clear structure-mass transfer-reactivity relationship for Cu TPEs and highlights multi-channel electrode structural engineering as a powerful and generalizable strategy for directing product selectivity in eCO2RR. More broadly, this work provides design principles for next-generation TPE architectures and underscores their potential for practical deployment of eCO2RR technologies within sustainable energy systems. While the present study focuses on fundamental electrode architecture and reaction interface engineering, reactor-level scale-up of TPEs, including flow-cell integration and stacked electrode configurations for high-current operation, represents an important future direction. We anticipate that the mechanistic insights and design principles established here will provide a foundation for advancing such scalable TPE-based reactor development.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and materials

Cu powder (99.9%, 1 μm), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, ≥ 99%), potassium bicarbonate (KHCO3, ≥ 99.5%), deuterium oxide (D2O, ≥ 99.9 atom%), and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO, ≥ 99.95%) were purchased from Aladdin. Polymeric methyl methacrylate (PMMA, injection molding grade) was purchased from Macklin. PEG-30 dipolyhydroxystearate (≥ 99%) was purchased from Croda. Reference electrode (Ag/AgCl) and counter electrode (Pt mesh) were purchased from Gaoss Union (Tianjin) Photoelectric Technology Co., Ltd. Electrolyte solutions were prepared using ultrapure water (18.2 MΩ).

Electrode preparation

The four-channel copper tubular penetration electrode (Cu TPE) was prepared via a combined phase-inversion/sintering process (Fig. S1). Briefly, a uniform slurry was prepared by planetary ball-milling Cu powder (70 wt%), N-methyl-2-pyrrolidone (NMP, 23 wt%), polymeric methyl methacrylate (PMMA, 6.5 wt%), and PEG-30 dipolyhydroxystearate (0.5 wt%) in a zirconia jar with ϕ 6 mm and ϕ 10 mm balls at 400 rpm for 24 h. Once cooled to room temperature (26 °C), the slurry was subjected to a vacuum of 1 mbar for two hours to purge any trapped air bubbles, resulting in a casting solution. This solution was then pushed through a spinning apparatus and molded in a water bath through a phase-inversion technique. Following the spinning process, the newly formed tubes were submerged in water for 24 h to fully remove the solvent. Subsequently, the tubes were stretched and dried at room temperature (26°C) for 36h, forming a green body. The green body was then cut into appropriate lengths and calcined in a muffle furnace at temperatures ranging from 350 to 550 °C, with a heating rate of 1 °C min−1, for 3 to 5 h to eliminate the PMMA. Once cooled naturally to room temperature (26 °C), the calcined samples were reduced in a flow of 5% H2 (balanced with argon) at 100 mL min−1 and 350 ~ 500 °C (heating rate 1 °C min−1) for 2 to 5 h to produce four-channel Cu TPEs under varied working conditions.

A 2 cm section of the Cu TPE was inserted into a standard copper tube, secured in place using conductive silver adhesive to ensure electrical connectivity. The exposed end of the Cu TPE tube, along with the junction between the Cu TPE and the commercial copper tube, was then sealed with gas-tight, non-conductive epoxy. After being cured at room temperature (26 °C) for 24 h, the functional Cu TPE was ready for use. Taking CST-51.9 as an example, the geometric area was calculated to be 1.45 cm2 (S = πDL = 3.14 × 0.231 × 2 ≈ 1.45 cm2, where S is the electrode area, D is the outer diameter of the Cu TPE, and L is the length of the Cu TPE). The mass of CST-51.9 is 0.189 ± 0.006 g.

Materials characterizations

The field-emission scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Scios 2 HiVac, Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to examine the microstructure of the Cu TPE at an operating voltage of 10 kV. Prior to imaging, samples were treated with gold spraying to ensure good electrical conductivity. Transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Tecnai G2 F20, Thermo Scientific, USA) was operated at an accelerating voltage of 120 kV to investigate the crystalline structure of Cu TPE. For TEM analysis, samples were dispersed in anhydrous ethanol, ultrasonicated for 20 min, drop-cast onto a molybdenum grid, and dried in an oven at 60 °C. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS, K-Alpha, Thermo Scientific, USA) was used to determine elemental composition and chemical valence state of the Cu TPE, with the C1s peak at 284.8 eV as the reference for calibration. To determine the crystal structure of Cu TPE, X-ray diffraction (XRD) data were recorded using a Bruker D8 Advance diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (40 kV, 30 mA) over a scanning range of 5° to 90° at a rate of 5° min−1. Confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) was operated on leica (stellaris 5). Firstly, the Rhodamine B was dissolved in 0.1 M KHCO3 to prepare a solution with a concentration of 1 mg ml−1. Then, the above solution (100 μL) was dropped onto a glass-bottomed dish. A piece of carbon paper (0.5 cm × 0.5 cm) was placed over the Rhodamine B droplet and the excess Rhodamine B solution was blotted off from the edges. Finally, the dish was placed under a laser confocal microscope for observation. Contact angle measurements were performed using a contact angle system (Biolin, Theta Flex, Sweden) at ambient temperature with 4 μL of water droplets as the probe liquid. Owing to the porous nature of the Cu TPEs, the deposited droplet did not stabilize immediately; therefore, the contact angle was recorded continuously from initial droplet–electrode contact until a stable value was reached. Mercury intrusion porosimetry (MIP) was carried out using a Micromeritics Autopore V 9605.

Electrochemical measurements

The CO2 electroreduction experiments were performed in an H-type cell separated by a Nafion 117 membrane. The dimensions of the Nafion 117 membrane is 6 cm × 6 cm, with a thickness of 183 μm. The Nafion 117 membrane was pretreated prior to use. First, the Nafion 117 membrane was treated in a 5 wt% H2O2 aqueous solution at 80 °C for 1 h to remove organic impurities within the membrane. Subsequently, the membrane was repeatedly rinsed with deionized water, followed by immersion in deionized water at 80 °C for 1 h to fully eliminate any residual H2O2. The membrane was further immersed in a 1.0 M H2SO4 solution at 80 °C for 1 h. Finally, the membrane was repeatedly rinsed with deionized water and then immersed in deionized water at 80 °C for 1 h to completely remove residual H2SO4 from the membrane. The 10 g of KHCO3 was dissolved in deionized water, and then the solution was diluted to 1 L to obtain 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte, which was stored in a sealed container. The electrolytes were all prepared immediately before use. The cathode contained CO2-saturated 0.1 M KHCO3 electrolyte (pH=6.86 ± 0.06). Electrochemical measurements were performed on a CHI660E workstation using a three-electrode setup. Ag/AgCl (in saturated KCl) was the reference electrode. A platinum plate was the counter electrode. Cu TPE was the working electrode. The cathode chamber was purged with CO2 for 30 minutes before testing.

Throughout all electrochemical tests, the CO2 inlet was maintained at 30 mL min−1. Although the feed rate remained constant at 30 mL min−1, the exit flow did not equal this value because hydrogen evolution and CO2 consumption altered the gas balance. Thus, the outlet flow rate of the electrolysis cell was measured using an automatic soap-bubble flowmeter. The reference electrode calibration was performed using a three-electrode system, with Ag/AgCl as the reference electrode, a Pt mesh as the working electrode, a Pt plate as the counter electrode, and 0.1 M KHCO3 as the electrolyte. Before the test, the chronoamperometry (CA) measurement was conducted to facilitate hydrogen adsorption over the Pt mesh surface, followed by purging with pure hydrogen gas to saturate 0.1 M KHCO3. During the measurement, hydrogen gas was continuously purged into the cathode compartment. In-situ attenuated total reflectance infrared (ATR-IR)

In-situ ATR-IR measurements were performed using a Ge prism as the ATR internal reflection element. Prior to measurement, the Cu TPE was ultrasonically cleaned with acetone, ethanol, and deionized water. A fine Cu wire was attached to one end of the Cu TPE for electrical contact, and the electrode body was partially sealed with non-conductive epoxy, leaving the probing region exposed. The electrode was mounted on a three-dimensional micromanipulator and gently approached the Ge prism until the outer thin-walled region made light, uniform contact with the prism, leaving only a very thin interfacial electrolyte film in the contact region. Thus, the ATR-IR signal probed the local interfacial region of the TPE adjacent to the Ge prism. A 0.1 M KHCO3 solution was used as the electrolyte. The Ag/AgCl reference electrode and Pt wire counter electrode were inserted into the electrochemical cell, and infrared spectra were collected at different applied potentials. During ATR-IR measurements, CO2 was continuously supplied through the TPE lumen to preserve the gas-fed configuration of the electrode. Chronoamperometric measurements were performed using a CHI660E electrochemical workstation, with potentials applied stepwise from −0.31 to −1.11 V vs. RHE. The ATR-IR spectra at OCP were also collected for comparative purposes.

In-situ Raman spectroscopy

In-situ Raman measurements were carried out using a Raman microscopy system (Horiba LabRAM Odyssey) with a 785 nm laser at room temperature (26 °C). The experiments were conducted in a three-electrode configuration with an Ag/AgCl electrode as the reference electrode and a platinum wire as the counter electrode in the anode chamber. Chronoamperometric measurements were performed using a CHI660E electrochemical workstation, with potentials applied stepwise from −0.31 to −1.11 V vs. RHE. The Raman spectra at OCP were also collected for comparative purposes.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations

All spin-polarized DFT calculations were performed using the Vienna ab initio simulation package (VASP) with the projector augmented wave (PAW) method59,60. Electron-ion interactions were described by standard PAW potentials, and a plane-wave basis set with a 400 eV cutoff energy was used to expand the wave functions. The Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation (GGA) was applied for exchange-correlation interactions61. Van der Waals effects were included via the DFT-D3BJ method62. Surface calculations were performed on the Cu(111) crystal plane with a slab thickness of 15 Å vacuum layer to eliminate spurious interactions. For CST-36.1, CST-51.9, and CST-100, idealized Cu confined-space models with different relative pore apertures were constructed to preserve the experimentally observed order of confinement among the electrodes. Different concentrations of adsorbed hydrogen intermediates (*H) were further introduced as atomistic descriptors to reflect the wettability-dependent local reaction environments. These DFT models were used to isolate the effects of confinement and wettability-related surface properties on the adsorption and conversion of key intermediates, rather than to reproduce the absolute micrometer-scale pore geometry of the TPEs. Brillouin-zone integrations used Monkhorst-Pack grids with a spacing of 0.04 Å−163. The electronic self-consistent loop converged at 10−5 eV, and atomic structures were optimized until residual forces fell below 0.03 eV Å−1. Transport mechanism

Assuming that pores in membranes consist of bundles of capillary tubes, gas transport through the capillary tubes can be described based on the kinetic theory of gases. There are three main ways for gas to move in capillaries: Knudsen flow, slip flow, and viscous flow. For capillaries of fixed size, we found that the principle by which gas molecules pass through the capillary depends on the relationship between the pore radius rp and the gas mean free path λ, following one of the methods mentioned earlier. Knudsen flow occurs when (rp/λ) <0.05, slip flow occurs in the range (rp/λ) = 0.05 to 3, and viscous flow occurs when (rp/ λ) > 363.
