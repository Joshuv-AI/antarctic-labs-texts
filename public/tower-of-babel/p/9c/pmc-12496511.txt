Rectal tuberculosis in a 48-years-old immuno-competent man: A rare case report
Authors: Bishal Budha, Anga Raj Dulal, Ram Chandra Bhandari, Neetika Paudel, Sadikshya Pandey
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT

Introduction

Tuberculosis (TB), an infectious disease caused by Mycobacterium tuberculosis, primarily affects the lungs but can also involve extra-pulmonary sites such as the lymph nodes, genitourinary tract, osteo-articular system, meninges, and digestive tract. Among these, gastrointestinal (GI) TB is relatively rare, with the ileocecal region being the most commonly affected segment [[1], [2], [3]]. Abdominal TB, particularly prevalent in individuals aged 25 to 45 years, may involve the GI tract through hematogenous spread, lymphatic dissemination, ingestion of bacilli, or direct extension from adjacent organs [4]. The disease often manifests as granuloma formation, mucosal ulceration, fibrosis, and scarring [4,5]. Rectal tuberculosis (TB) is an exceedingly rare form of GI TB, often presenting with nonspecific clinical features that mimic rectal malignancy, Crohn's and Inflammatory Bowel Disease (IBD) disease like recurrent fistula formation, ulceration, hemorrhage and chronic anal pain or discharge [6,7]. A high degree of clinical suspicion, along with microbiologic and histopathologic studies, is essential for confirmation [8]. Despite advancements in diagnostic imaging and endoscopic techniques, misdiagnosis remains possible, particularly in regions with a high TB burden.

Abdominal tuberculosis is higher in developing regions like Asia and Sub-Saharan Africa, while it is relatively rare in Western world. Nepal, a country with significant TB prevalence, reported an estimated 70,000 cases in 2022, with an incidence rate of 229 cases per 100,000 people. Rectal TB, although uncommon, represents a clinical and diagnostic challenge, especially in high-prevalence settings. We report a case of rectal tuberculosis in a 45-year-old male, highlighting the importance of considering this rare diagnosis in appropriate clinical contexts.

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE criteria [9].

Case presentation

A 48-year-old man presented to our Surgery OPD with the complaints of mass per rectum and per-rectal bleeding for the past one month, which was associated with streaks of blood in his stool, accompanied by pain during defecation and passage of hard stools. The patient reported a history of recurrent episodes of anal fissures since childhood and had been using Ayurveda medications for the past two years. He denied systemic symptoms such as unexplained weight loss, fever, night sweats, fatigue, appetite changes, or symptoms suggestive of pulmonary tuberculosis. His past medical history was unremarkable, with no history of diabetes mellitus, hypertension, cardiac diseases, bronchial asthma, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, or tuberculosis. The patient was a non-smoker, did not chew betel nut, and consumed alcohol occasionally.

On examination, he was anxious but was conscious and well oriented to time, place and person. There was no pallor, icterus, lymphadenopathy, clubbing, cyanosis, and edema. There was no palpable mass of digital rectal examination (DRE). His vitals were stable with recorded as pulse rate of 78 beats per minute, blood pressure of 120/80 mm of Hg, body temperature of 97 °F, respiratory rate of 18 breaths per minute and oxygen saturation of 96 % in room air. Systemic examinations, including abdominal, respiratory, cardiac, and nervous system assessments, revealed no abnormalities.

Laboratory investigations showed a hemoglobin level of 11.53 g/dL, hematocrit of 32.8 %, a total leukocyte count of 8910/mm3 with neutrophil predominance (70.03 %), and a platelet count of 261,300/mm3. Thyroid function tests were normal. Liver function tests showed SGOT (AST) at 16 U/L, SGPT (ALT) at 10 U/L, alkaline phosphatase (ALP) at 94 U/L, and lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) at 156 U/L — all within normal limits. The total bilirubin was 21 μmol/L and direct bilirubin was 3.1 μmol/L, both within the normal reference range. Serum albumin was also normal at 39 g/L. The erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) was 20 mm/h. The Mantoux test was negative, showing no induration. Acid-fast bacilli (AFB) were not detected in sputum samples from three consecutive days, and a chest X-ray was normal.

Pelvic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) revealed a relatively well-defined exo-phytic soft tissue lesion on the right lateral aspect of the mid-rectum, extending inferiorly below the anorectal junction (Fig. 1, Fig. 2). The imaging raised concerns about possible rectal carcinoma, prostate carcinoma, or metastasis. Colonoscopy showed ulcerations at the anal verge and a small rectal polyp. A biopsy of the lesion was performed and sent for histopathological examination.

CT image (axial section in 3D venous phase) showing hypo-dense area near the rectum (Figure A) indicative of abscess and compressed rectum (Figure B).

MRI (sagittal view with contrast) showing hypo-dense area with peripheral enhancement, which is typical of abscess.

Histopathological analysis revealed ulcerated rectal mucosa with granulation tissue, dense chronic inflammatory infiltrates, epithelioid granulomas, and a few giant cells (Fig. 3, Fig. 4) These findings were consistent with granulomatous proctitis, most likely of tuberculous origin. The patient was diagnosed with rectal tuberculosis.

HPE revealed granulomatous proctitis with mucosal ulceration most likely tuberculous origin.

Ziehl-Neelsen staining showing acid fast bacilli (pointed by white arrow) which are pink against blue background.

The differential diagnosis included rectal carcinoma, prostate carcinoma, Crohn's disease, and amoebiasis. Rectal carcinoma and prostate carcinoma were ruled out based on histopathology, which showed granulomatous inflammation without evidence of malignancy. Crohn's disease was excluded due to the absence of transmural inflammation or crypt architecture distortion, while amoebiasis was ruled out by the absence of trophozoites or cysts in stool and biopsy specimens.

The patient was started on anti-tubercular therapy (ATT) with isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, ethambutol, and pyridoxine supplementation. Supportive measures, including stool softeners and SITZ BATH, were also advised to aid healing and relieve symptoms. Stool softeners ease bowel movements by reducing straining, while SITZ baths enhance blood blow, reduce discomfort, and promote anal muscle relaxation.

At the one-month follow-up, the patient reported significant improvement in symptoms, including the resolution of per-rectal bleeding, pain during defecation, and hard stool passage. Liver function tests were within the normal range, and the patient tolerated ATT without adverse effects.

This case illustrates the importance of considering rectal tuberculosis in the differential diagnosis of anorectal lesions, particularly in regions where tuberculosis is prevalent. Timely diagnosis, supported by histopathology, and early initiation of ATT can lead to a favorable outcome in this rare but treatable condition.

Discussion

Among all tuberculosis cases, Extrapulmonary Tuberculosis (EPTB) accounts for 15 % of the cases. The sixth most frequent cause of it is abdominal TB. Any component of the digestive system, from the esophagus to the anal canal, can be affected by TB, however, with 64 % of cases, the ileocecal region is most frequently impacted by gastrointestinal TB due to its neutral pH, high lymphoid tissue density, prolonged fecal stasis, and absorptive transport systems that enable the absorption of ingested mycobacterium, followed by the jejunum and large intestine [10].

Rectal TB is extremely rare manifestation of extra-pulmonary tuberculosis (EPTB), accounting for less than 2 % of all abdominal TB cases and often coexists with pulmonary TB [11]. Rectal TB can arise through several mechanisms, including ingestion of contaminated food, swallowing of infected sputum, lympho-hematogenous spread from other infected sites, or direct extension from adjacent organs [12]. However, rectal TB, particularly in immunocompetent individuals, is an unusual presentation and poses significant diagnostic challenges.

Clinically, rectal TB may present with hematochezia, altered bowel habits, and abdominal pain, often accompanied by constitutional symptoms such as fever and weight loss. Complications such as anorectal abscesses, fistulas, and intestinal obstruction may occur if diagnosis and treatment are delayed. Puri et al. reported that 88 % of patients with rectal TB present with hematochezia, 75 % with constitutional symptoms, and 37 % with constipation [13]. In addition to being misdiagnosed as inflammatory bowel disease, solitary rectal ulcer syndrome, stercoral ulcer, and other granulomatous diseases, it typically mimics rectal cancer. Most of them have tight strictures within 10 cm of the anal margin, and during endoscopy, they resemble malignant rectal lesions, likewise, our patient had ulcers at anal verge with rectal polyp.

The diagnosis becomes difficult and challenging in the absence of the active or past history of Pulmonary tuberculosis, as was in our case. In developing countries, where loss to follow-up is a real issue, delay in prompt diagnosis may lead to significant morbidity and mortality of the patients [14]. In our patient, rectal TB was diagnosed without evidence of pulmonary TB, as suggested by negative acid-fast bacillus (AFB) staining in sputum and the absence of characteristic radiologic findings. This highlights the diagnostic complexity, particularly in individuals without a history of pulmonary TB. Imaging findings show that tuberculosis typically involves fewer than four segments with transverse ulcers, concentric wall thickening, preserved layers, and necrotic lymph nodes. Crohn's disease presents with longitudinal ulcers, skip lesions, cobblestone appearance, and the “comb sign.” [15,16] In contrast, malignancy usually has irregular, asymmetric wall thickening with loss of normal stratification and no necrotic lymph nodes. Endoscopy often reveals strictures or lesions resembling malignancy, emphasizing the need for histopathological (HPE) confirmation. Histopathological examination remains the gold standard for diagnosis. Features of TB include lymphoid aggregates, epithelioid granulomas with caseation, giant cells, and chronic inflammation. However, histopathological findings are characteristic in only 13–33 % of colonic TB cases, leading to potential delays in diagnosis [17]. At the same time, submucosal involvement is more common which limits the yield of HPE. A third of these patients have caseation, while 8 % to 48 % of patients have granulomas [18]. In our case, histopathology revealed granulation tissue, dense chronic inflammatory infiltrates, and epithelioid granulomas, which were diagnostic for TB and distinct from Crohn's disease. Gene XPERT has emerged as a valuable diagnostic tool due to its high sensitivity (95.7 %) and specificity (100 %) [19]. It has significantly reduced the reliance on laparotomies for tissue diagnosis. Despite these advancements, delayed diagnosis remains common, particularly in resource-limited settings where follow-up challenges exacerbate morbidity and mortality.

Treatment of gastrointestinal TB, including rectal TB, typically involves a standard anti-tuberculosis therapy (ATT) regimen. This consists of an intensive phase of isoniazid, rifampicin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol for two months, followed by a continuation phase of isoniazid and rifampicin for four months [20]. Surgery may be required for complications such as intestinal obstruction, perforation, or fistula formation, or when malignancy cannot be excluded [21]. In our patient, early diagnosis through endoscopic biopsy enabled prompt initiation of ATT, potentially preventing severe complications. Advances in diagnostic tools like colonoscopy and Gene XPERT have reduced the need for surgical interventions, but surgery remains a crucial option in cases with persistent obstruction, un-resolving stenosis after 3–6 months of therapy, or suspected coexisting malignancy.

Conclusion

Rectal tuberculosis, though rare, demands vigilance due to its mimicry of other conditions and diagnostic challenges. In regions with a high TB burden, early recognition through comprehensive clinical, microbiological, and histopathological evaluation is vital. Prompt diagnosis and treatment are pivotal in preventing complications and improving patient outcomes.
