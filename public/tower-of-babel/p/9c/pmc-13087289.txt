Functional near-infrared spectroscopy identifies neural biomarkers of burnout in active-duty Police officers
Authors: Wei-Yu Chen, Wen-Yu Wang, Yi-Hua Huang, Sanford P.C. Hsu, Yi-Min Wang, Ching-Po Lin, Chia-Wei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Burnout is a common phenomenon and appears to be comprehensive in all walks of life. In recent years, the World Health Organization (WHO) has included it in the International Classification of Diseases. The growing discussion by international organizations shows increasing public concern about the impact. In addition, more related studies have pointed out that it has caused a significant economic burden on the world. At this stage, the judgment of burnout is still vague and can only rely on the subjective judgment of the relevant scales, but it is still difficult to be a reliable indicator. An objective and effective burnout assessment system is crucial. This study chose the police as the starting point. Using functional near-infrared spectroscopy (fNIRS) to measure changes in hemoglobin concentration in the prefrontal lobe during 33 active police officers performing the mental arithmetic task and the verbal fluency task. We extracted the features after processing the signals. After that, the group was marked with the relevant scale score and then imported into the support vector machine algorithm. Finally, a model with a training accuracy rate of 91.3% and a test accuracy rate of 90.0% was obtained, demonstrating that the burnout assessment system using fNIRS combined with machine learning is promising.

Introduction

In recent years, burnout has become a widespread phenomenon, prevalent in various industries and positively correlated with occupational stress1. In the Netherlands, the welfare costs for employees suffering from burnout-related illnesses amount to as much as 1.7 billion euros annually2, while the global economic burden exceeds 300 billion U.S. dollars3. This issue has increasingly garnered public attention in recent years. The European Agency for Safety and Health at Work (EU-OSHA) has repeatedly emphasized it as a critical concern for ‘healthy workplaces’4,5. Moreover, the WHO designated it as the theme for World Mental Health Day in 2017. In 2019, burnout was included in the 11th Revision of the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11), where it was defined as a syndrome resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. Its characteristics include feelings of energy depletion or exhaustion; negative emotions related to one’s work and reduced professional efficacy. Furthermore, the Third European Survey of Enterprises on New and Emerging Risks (ESENER-3), conducted in the same year, underscored the adverse effects of occupational stress and highlighted the importance of occupational health in preventing work-related stress and other psychosocial risks6.

Burnout arises from excessive workload, job stress, role exhaustion, and organizational issues7–10. Symptoms manifest physiologically (e.g., fatigue, insomnia), emotionally (e.g., irritability, depression), and behaviorally (e.g., cynicism, withdrawal)11. Burnout severity is categorized as Mild, Moderate, or Severe12.

This study adopts the Copenhagen Burnout Inventory (CBI)13, frequently used in Taiwan14,15, which defines burnout as physical and psychological exhaustion across three subscales: personal burnout (CBI_1, 5 items), measures overall burnout.

due to personal, social, and health-related factors; work-related burnout (CBI_2, 5 items), assesses work-induced burnout, distinguishing it from personal stressors; client-related burnout (CBI_3, 6 items), evaluates burnout from client interactions, including patients, students, or customers. To better align with work cultures in Taiwan and Japan, the Ministry of Labor’s Occupational Safety and Health Research Institute revised the CBI16, adding a fourth subscale, “Overcommitment” (CBI_4, 5 items), which is based on Siegrist’s Effort-Reward Imbalance Model17. This scale measures excessive work dedication but is distinct from burnout itself.

The revised CBI has been validated in numerous studies13,18–20. Its construct and criterion validity have been confirmed, and it remains a cost-effective, publicly available alternative to the MBI.

Police officers face high stress due to threats like terrorism, gun violence, and public scrutiny, contributing to physical and mental health issues, including cardiovascular disease, PTSD, and burnout21–24. Trauma exposure increases suicide risk, with 20% of British officers showing PTSD-like symptoms25.

Burnout affects both individuals and organizations, leading to reduced performance and increased use of force26,27. Dutch studies link burnout to violent attitudes in police28. The 24-hour shift system worsens stress by disrupting sleep and physiological rhythms29,30. High burnout rates are reported among police in the UK, Mexico, and Spain, where 11% meet burnout thresholds31–33.

This study assesses police occupational stress and burnout through blood oxygen analysis via fNIRS for an objective evaluation.

Research on burnout and physiological characteristics has examined cortisol levels, immune responses, metabolism, and brain activity. Mommersteeg et al. found increased anti-inflammatory cytokine Interleukin-10 (IL-10) production in the burnout group following lipopolysaccharide stimulation, based on saliva endocrine and immune analyses34. In terms of brain activity assessment, various neuroimaging techniques have been utilized in burnout research. Among these techniques, fNIRS has emerged as a particularly valuable method. fNIRS combines several advantages over other neuroimaging modalities, including no radiation exposure, lower system cost, real-time measurement capability, high portability, and superior resistance to motion artifacts and electrical noise35.

Previous studies have demonstrated that fNIRS can effectively measure occupational stress and burnout-related brain activity36–43. Research has indicated altered activation patterns in the prefrontal cortex regions among individuals experiencing burnout compared to control groups. These findings suggest that fNIRS can detect meaningful neurophysiological correlates of burnout. This study used fNIRS as the core technology to explore the relationship between burnout and physiological characteristics.

Correlation

Correlation between scales

Correlation analysis revealed strong positive relationships between burnout subscales, with personal and work-related burnout (ρ = 0.86), work-related and client-related burnout (ρ = 0.78), and personal and client-related burnout (ρ = 0.75) all significantly correlated. This indicates that police officers’ daily burnout is closely linked to work-related and client-related burnout. Additionally, anxiety exhibited stronger correlations with burnout subscales than depression, contrasting with prior research44, which found burnout more strongly correlated with depression than anxiety. Figure 2 presents the results of the best-performing features selected using VFT as the stimulus and CBI_2 for grouping. The selected features are the normalized task-phase activation value of ∆[HHb] in the right prefrontal cortex (Ch3_HHb_Task_Act_N, p-value: 0.0002) and the task-phase activation value of oxygenated hemoglobin concentration in the right prefrontal cortex (Ch3_HbO2_Task_Act, p-value: 0.00062). To clarify the relationship between these two features, their correlation coefficient was calculated, yielding a result of 0.22, indicating a low correlation. This suggests that the physiological information carried by these two features is relatively independent. However, when computing the standardized scores (Z-score) for each feature and correlating them with CBI_2 scores, the correlation between the normalized task-phase activation value of ∆[HHb] and CBI_2 was 0.31, while the correlation between the task-phase activation value of oxygenated hemoglobin concentration and CBI_2 was 0.48. Since neither correlation exceeds an absolute value of 0.5, this indicates that the fNIRS-derived blood oxygenation features do not have a direct linear or simple nonlinear relationship with the scales used in this study. This suggests that using a single feature alone may not yield effective evaluations.

Additionally, it can be observed that the model’s decision boundary effectively separates high-risk and low-risk groups without signs of overfitting. The training accuracy of the model is 91.3%, while the test accuracy is 90.0%. Given that the low-risk group outnumbers the high-risk group, a naïve baseline model that blindly classifies all data points as low-risk would achieve an accuracy of 69.7%. The significantly higher accuracy of this model demonstrates its predictive capability.

Model validation

The model’s classification performance and generalization ability were evaluated using a confusion matrix and leave-one-out cross-validation. Figure 3 presents the confusion matrix, which illustrates the actual labels of the test dataset samples versus the predicted labels generated by the model. In subfigure (a), which shows the unnormalized confusion matrix, it can be observe that all three high-risk individuals in the test dataset were correctly classified, while among the seven low-risk individuals, only one was misclassified.

Checking the CBI_2 subscale score of the misclassified case reveals a score of 50, which is exactly on the group classification threshold. This suggests that their physiological features do not strongly lean toward either group, leading to the misclassification by the model. Subfigure (b) presents the normalized confusion matrix, where normalization makes it easier to observe the model’s sensitivity, specificity, and other performance metrics. The model’s sensitivity is 100%, while its specificity is 85.7%, resulting in an F1-score of 85.7%. Additionally, the positive predictive value (PPV) and negative predictive value (NPV) are 75% and 100%, respectively. Moreover, the model’s leave-one-out cross-validation accuracy is 82.6%, further confirming the model’s stability.

To eliminate the potential bias introduced when splitting the dataset into training and test sets, the model was retrained multiple times by adjusting the random state parameter (which shuffles the data) and then re-evaluated. The average accuracy was calculated to be 91.3%, with a standard deviation of 3.1%, indicating that the model does not exhibit bias toward specific data samples.

Currently, the model’s specificity is relatively lower. While the 100% sensitivity ensures that high-risk individuals are not misclassified as low-risk, there is still room for improvement in overall performance. One potential reason for this is the imbalance in sample distribution—the number of high-risk individuals is significantly lower than that of low-risk individuals. As a result, the model may lack sufficient detailed information on the blood oxygen characteristics of the high-risk group.

Discussion

SVM results discussion

In the model trained using VFT stimulation and CBI_2 grouping labels, if the x-value (Ch3_HHb_Task_Act_N) is fixed in Fig. 2(a), the y-value (Ch3_HbO2_Task_Act) for the high-risk group is generally lower than that of the low-risk group. In other words, when stimulated by VFT, the maximum change in oxygenated hemoglobin concentration in the right prefrontal cortex of the high-risk group is smaller than that of the low-risk group. The smaller change in oxygenated hemoglobin concentration may be linked to burnout-related alterations in brain function, such as reduced vasodilation or slower blood flow. Conversely, if the y-value is fixed, it can be observed that the x-value for the high-risk group is also lower than that of the low-risk group. This indicates that under VFT stimulation, the high-risk group exhibits a smaller maximum change in ∆[HHb] in the right prefrontal cortex. The smaller change in ∆[HHb] could indicate reduced brain activity due to burnout-related factors like slower blood flow or altered oxygen consumption. Based on these observations, it is hypothesized that compared to the low-risk group, the high-risk group may experience higher tissue oxygen consumption or slower blood flow in the right hemisphere during the VFT test.

Overall, the greater blood oxygen signal changes in the low-risk group compared to the high-risk group during VFT stimulation suggest that the high-risk group may exhibit relatively lower brain activation. This finding may be related to prior fMRI research, which has shown that individuals experiencing high stress have significantly reduced gray matter volume in the dorsolateral prefrontal cortex and anterior cingulate cortex45. Regarding models trained with different stimuli and different subscale grouping labels, when using MAT as the stimulus, the models trained with CBI_2 and HADS_A grouping could not be constructed as two-dimensional machine learning models. This is because, during feature selection, either the feature combinations were insufficient (less than one valid pair after removing highly correlated features) or the number of selected.

features were less than two. For the remaining models, those using MAT stimulation or those not using CBI_2 for grouping had significantly lower training accuracy compared to the model using VFT stimulation and CBI_2 grouping. Most of these models had an accuracy below 70%, indicating poor evaluation performance. The relatively lower testing and validation accuracy further suggests that these models have poor generalization ability.

The model demonstrated strong evaluation performance and generalization ability. To validate feature selection, task-rest phase differences were examined, revealing significant differences between high-risk and low-risk groups in right prefrontal cortex features. Notably, the low-risk group exhibited stronger left hemispheric activation, indicating lateralization. The findings support the hypothesis that the high-risk group’s lack of lateralization (p-value = 0.059) reflects compensatory mechanisms for cognitive decline due to burnout. This aligns with previous research suggesting weaker activation in older adults or Alzheimer’s disease46,47. Under MAT stimulation, the low-risk group also showed left hemispheric lateralization, consistent with prior NIRS and MEG studies in similar populations48-51. The burnout levels in police officers were minimally influenced by demographic variables, but personal and work-related burnout were highly correlated, particularly with anxiety. fNIRS data during VFT revealed that the high-risk group exhibited lower hemoglobin changes in the right prefrontal cortex, suggesting higher tissue oxygen consumption or slower blood flow, as shown in Fig. 4. VFT stimulation yielded better classification performance than MAT, with CBI_2 yielding the best results. The low-risk group demonstrated consistent left hemispheric lateralization in both VFT and MAT, corroborating previous studies on VFT stimulation and heart rate variability46,48,49. In conclusion, the study highlights significant differences in right prefrontal cortex oxygenation between high- and low-risk groups, with superior classification performance using VFT stimulation, further supporting the role of fNIRS in assessing burnout in high-stress populations46–51.

Limitations

This investigation was conducted as a pilot study with a small sample size (N = 33) recruited from a single police district, which may limit the external validity of the findings. The machine-learning performance reported here should be interpreted as exploratory; small-sample analyses can yield optimistic estimates, particularly in pilot settings. Future studies will prospectively pre-register the analysis plan, collect a larger multi-site cohort, and adopt leakage-controlled validation frameworks such as nested cross-validation. Additionally, several potentially relevant covariates, including years of service, rank, and shift patterns, were not systematically recorded in this pilot phase. These factors will be prioritized in subsequent investigations to further clarify their influence on prefrontal hemodynamic features.

Methods

Headgear system

This study utilized a custom-developed continuous wave near-infrared spectroscopy (CWNIRS) device to measure hemodynamic changes in the prefrontal cortex. The system features an optode array with four measurement channels positioned at the F7, Fp1, Fp2, and F8 locations according to the international 10–20 EEG electrode placement system, as shown in Fig. 5. The device is designed with an adjustable head-mounted form factor to ensure proper positioning and minimal interference during data collection.

Experimental design

This study employed fNIRS to analyze cerebral oxygenation dynamics during cognitive tasks, including a Verbal Fluency Task (VFT) and a Mental Arithmetic Task (MAT). After preprocessing, extracted features were classified using machine learning, and reliability was assessed using cross-validation.

VFT

VFT evaluates frontal cortex function, including working memory and executive control52,53, is widely employed in neuropsy- chological assessments and cognitive research54,55. This study adheres to the protocol of Chou et al.56, ensuring a controlled participants are instructed to maintain a stable posture and facial expression. The test comprises three phases: a 30-second rest period, a 60-second task period, and a 70-second recovery period. The task phase includes three 20-second sub-tasks, each drawing a prompt from distinct question banks. Participants generate as many words as possible based on the given syllables, with auditory cues marking phase transitions.

We utilized a support vector machine (SVM) as a common baseline classifier for this pilot investigation. To prioritize generalization and facilitate future benchmarking, we used an RBF kernel with fixed hyperparameters set to standard defaults (C = 1.0 and gamma = 1/n_features). We deliberately avoided exhaustive hyperparameter tuning to reduce overfitting risk in this small pilot dataset.

After labeling each data point, data from the 33 participants were split into a training set and an independent test set at a 7:3 ratio for model development and evaluation. Stratified sampling was used to maintain a balanced distribution of high- and low-risk participants across splits.

MAT

MAT is a cognitive stress test linked to working memory and executive function57,58. It elicits significant neural activation, particularly in the frontal and parietal lobes50,59. This study follows the framework of Betz et al.60. The test comprises a 30-second rest phase, a task phase featuring 45 arithmetic problems (each lasting 6 s), and a 70-second recovery phase. Participants solve addition or subtraction problems on a tablet, receiving real-time feedback.

Participant recruitment and use of questionnaires

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of National Chiao Tung University (Approval No. NCTUREC109111E). Participants were active-duty police officers aged 20–60, excluding those with major psychiatric or neurological disorders, brain injuries, or substance dependencies. Prior to fNIRS measurements, participants completed the CBI and the Hospital Anxiety and Depression Scale (HADS)44,61. The CBI employed a Likert scale to assess burnout, while HADS evaluated depression and anxiety through a 14-item questionnaire. All methods were carried out in accordance with relevant guidelines and regulations, and informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their inclusion in the study.

Participants were recruited from the Zhongzheng Second Police District of the Taipei City Police Department, and data collection was conducted at multiple precincts within the district. Participants were subgrouped into high-risk (H) and low-risk (L) groups based on subscale cut-off scores from the CBI and HADS (Table 5). Consistent with the study rationale, CBI_3 (not directly relevant to police work) and CBI_4 (measuring work over-engagement rather than burnout) were excluded from model training.

Signal processing

The wearable fNIRS system measures ∆[HbO2], ∆[HHb], and calculates ∆HbT to analyze vascular responses. Due to susceptibility to physiological noise and motion artifacts, signals undergo filtering and segmentation before feature extraction.

Signal filtering and segmentation

To mitigate various noises, appropriate filtering techniques are applied. Motion artifacts, a primary concern in fNIRS measurements, are corrected using an IMU-based approach. After filtering, the data is segmented into three phases for analysis: Resting, Task, and Recovery. Figure 6 demonstrates the effectiveness of the signal processing approach, showing data before and after processing during a semantic test.

Feature extraction

Channels Ch2 and Ch3, corresponding to the prefrontal cortex, are analyzed through key features: the Stage Mean Difference, which quantifies blood oxygenation changes between Rest, Task, and Recovery phases; the Transition Slope, measuring the rate of change in oxygenation during phase transitions; Task Activation, capturing peak blood oxygen response during stimulation; and Stage Standard Deviation, evaluating variability within each phase. The Transition Slope is calculated using the 8-second blood oxygen data before entering the new phase. This is because changes in blood oxygen are typically faster during the initial response to a new stimulus. The 8-second interval is based on research by S.Coyle et al. (2004)62, which indicated that fNIRS hemodynamic responses typically reach the maximum amplitude 5 to 8 s after stimulus onset, making 8 s an appropriate cutoff. In addition to the standard deviation, all four categories of features mentioned above also included normalized values computed by scaling the blood oxygen signals to the range [-1, 1]. The normalized features represent each participant’s feature values relative to the signal’s extrema and enable comparison of feature trends across participants. In total, 90 features were calculated. The feature extraction process is illustrated in Fig. 7.

Feature selection

Feature selection optimizes model performance by reducing redundancy. The Shapiro-Wilk test determines normality, guiding the choice between t-tests and Mann-Whitney U tests. rank correlation assesses feature dependencies63. Features demonstrating significant differences are ranked based on p-values for support vector machine (SVM) model training.
