Association of circadian-metabolic syndrome with incident chronic liver disease in middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults: a prospective cohort study on mediation by cardiovascular disease
Authors: Xikun Yang, Jiangping Ma, Linghao Xu, Hui Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThis study aimed to investigate the association between circadian-metabolic syndrome (CircS) and chronic liver disease (CLD) among middle-aged and elderly Chinese people, and to evaluate the mediating role of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in this relationship.MethodsThe study included data from 6082 Chinese individuals from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS). None of them had CLD at the time of the baseline survey (2011), and multifactorial COX regression models were used to analyse the association between CircS and new-onset CLD. In addition, the mediating effect of CVD between CircS and CLD was analysed.ResultsA total of 588 (9.67%) of 6082 participants developed CLD during follow-up. The hazard ratio (HR) for new-onset CLD in participants diagnosed with CircS compared with those without CircS was 1.33 (95% CI 1.12–1.57).The risk of new-onset CLD increased 1.14-fold (95% CI 1.08–1.20) for each additional component of the CircS component. The CVD was found to mediate the association between CircS and incident CLD compared with participants without CircS, proportions mediated were 20.6%. For each additional component of the CircS component, the CVD was found to mediate the association between CircS and incident CLD, proportions mediated were 10.40%.ConclusionCircS is associated with an increased risk of incident CLD in the middle-aged and elderly Chinese population. CVD may play a certain mediating role in the relationship between CircS and CLD.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40001-025-03329-1.

Introduction

In the past two decades, key clock genes in mammals have been discovered and proven to be interconnected in transcriptional and translational feedback loops [1]. In the recent years, researchers have begun to uncover a close connection between the circadian clock system and diseases as well as individual physiological states [2]. The term “circadian rhythm syndrome (CircS)” has been employed to denote that from cells to organisms, an adaptive evolutionary process occurs, resulting in periodic fluctuations in their life activities in response to the diurnal fluctuations of environmental conditions [1]. CircS is regarded as a novel risk cluster based on reduced sleep duration, abdominal obesity, depression, hypertension, dyslipidemia, and hyperglycemia [3]. The recent studies have found that CircS can be utilized for risk prediction of cardiovascular diseases and is more significant than metabolic syndrome [4]; CircS also plays a crucial role in the occurrence and development of various diseases such as cognitive impairment [5], psoriasis [6], stroke [7], and chronic diarrhea [8].

Previous studies defined circadian rhythm syndrome as the five components of metabolic syndrome plus short sleep duration (< 6 h/day) and depression (assessed by CES-D score ≥ 10). Meeting at least four components constituted CircS. However, participants co-presenting with hypertension, obesity, hyperglycaemia, and hyperlipidaemia yet lacking reduced sleep duration or depressive symptoms were still classified as CircS, which proved misleading. Consequently, in our study, we renamed circadian rhythm syndrome as circadian-metabolic syndrome (CircS).

Chronic liver disease (CLD) poses a serious threat to the health of people worldwide, and identifying the risk factors for CLD is of utmost importance for its prevention. In China, patients with CLD account for more than 20% of the total population [9]. Liver diseases cause approximately 2 million deaths annually, accounting for 4% of the total number of deaths [10] Multiple factors are closely associated with CLD, mainly viral infections and metabolic disorders. A recent study investigating the relationship between circadian rhythm disruption and non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) in murine models has revealed that chronic circadian rhythm dysfunction may independently function as a significant carcinogenic factor in human liver cells [11]. Concurrently, the hepatitis C virus has been demonstrated to disrupt the rhythmic gene expression within hepatocytes, thereby impacting the liver’s physiological metabolism and subsequently contributing to the development of CLD and hepatocellular carcinoma. Mounting evidence suggests a close relationship between alterations in circadian rhythms and liver diseases.

Consequently, CircS may represent a novel category of risk factors for liver diseases. Nevertheless, to the best of our knowledge, the relationship between CircS and CLD has not been empirically established in the literature. The aim of this study was to investigate this relationship using data from the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) and to explore the mediating effect of cardiovascular disease (CVD) in it.

Methods

Study design and population

Study design and population

Study design and population

The China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) is an ongoing longitudinal survey with national representativeness targeting adults aged 45 years and above. It aims to investigate information including sociodemographic characteristics, health status, medical conditions, family structure, and health-related behaviors. The baseline survey of CHARLS was initiated in 2011, followed by follow-up surveys every 2–3 years. Currently, CHARLS has generated five waves of survey data in 2011, 2013, 2015, 2018, and 2020. Blood test data in CHARLS were collected in 2011 and 2015. Detailed data can be obtained from the CHARLS official website (http://charls.pku.edu.cn/). The Biomedical Ethics Committee of Peking University approved the CHARLS research project (IRB00001052—11015). All participants provided informed consent and agreed to share their data.

We utilized the five waves of CHARLS data to select eligible participants based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. In this study, we first excluded individuals lacking diagnostic information for CircS and CVD at baseline (2011), as well as those with CLD. Subsequently, we excluded those lacking CLD diagnostic information in the four follow-up surveys. Furthermore, we excluded individuals with insufficient covariate data. Finally, 6082 subjects (2735 males and 3347 females) were included in this study. 2.Assessment of CircS and CVD

Assessment of CircS and CVD

Flowchart of participant inclusion and exclusion

Venous blood samples were promptly received by the Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention in Beijing within 2 weeks after leaving the CDC station. These samples were immediately stored and frozen at −20 °C prior to delivery. After necessary analyses in the laboratory of China Medical University, they were transferred to a deep freezer and maintained at −80 °C. In the clinical laboratory of Youanmen, Capital Medical University, the concentrations of biomarkers such as C-reactive protein (CRP), total high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), triglycerides (TG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and uric acid (UA) were measured using the enzyme colorimetric method [12].

Blood pressure was measured three times by trained nurses using a sphygmomanometer, and the average value was calculated. Hypertension was defined as systolic blood pressure ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic blood pressure ≥ 90 mmHg or the use of antihypertensive medications.

The Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression Scale (CES-D) (10-item version) was employed to assess depressive symptoms. Depression was defined as a CES-D score ≥ 10 points [13].

Anthropometric measurements were conducted by trained staff. Body weight was measured without shoes to an accuracy of 0.1 kg. Height was measured using a vertical stadiometer to an accuracy of 0.1 cm. Waist circumference was measured at the umbilical level to an accuracy of 0.1 cm. Central obesity was defined as a waist circumference ≥ 85 cm in men and ≥ 80 cm in women.

Sleep duration was evaluated through personal self-report. A sleep duration of less than 6 h was classified as reduced sleep duration.

The concept of CircS was initially derived from metabolic syndrome and was assessed based on the following seven components: reduced sleep duration, depression, central obesity, elevated triglycerides (≥ 150 mg/dl or drug treatment for TG elevation), reduced high-density lipoprotein (HDL < 40 mg/dl in men; HDL < 50 mg/dl in women; or drug treatment for HDL-C reduction), hypertension, and elevated fasting blood glucose (≥ 100 mg/dl or drug treatment for hyperglycemia). Participants with four or more components were diagnosed with CircS.

In addition, during the baseline survey, participants were asked whether they had been diagnosed with stroke or heart disease (including heart attack, coronary heart disease, angina pectoris, congestive heart failure, or other cardiac problems) by a doctor. A positive response to the above question was defined as having CVD.3.Outcome indicator (CLD)

3.Outcome indicator (CLD)

Outcome indicator (CLD)

The outcome event was the occurrence of CLD during the follow-up period. CLD was defined based on a positive response (“yes”) to the question in the health status and function questionnaire: “Have you been diagnosed with liver disease (excluding fatty liver, cancer, and tumors) by a doctor?” This definition, which relies on physician diagnosis and excludes specific conditions, is consistent with the approach used in prior studies utilizing the CHARLS dataset [14].4.Covariates

4.Covariates

Covariates

Interviewers collected and recorded sociodemographic information, health-related behaviors, and medical information through questionnaires. Selected sociodemographic characteristics included age, gender (male/female), education level (primary school or below/junior high school to high school/undergraduate or above), marital status (married/unmarried), and place of residence (rural/urban). The categories “married with spouse present” and “married but not living with spouse” were classified as married, while “separated,” “divorced,” “widowed,” and “never married” were classified as unmarried. Health-related behaviors included smoking status (yes/no), drinking status (yes/no), and afternoon napping (yes/no). Medical information included chronic kidney disease, cancer, hyperuricemia, and body mass index (BMI). Hyperuricemia was defined as serum uric acid > 420 μmol/L in men and > 360 μmol/L in women [15]. BMI was calculated by dividing body weight (kg) by the square of height (m). LDL and CRP were adjusted among the blood test indicators.5.Statistical analysis

5.Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis

Continuous normally distributed variables were described using means and standard deviations. Non-normally distributed continuous variables were described using medians and interquartile ranges. Categorical variables were described using frequencies and percentages. Independent sample t tests, rank sum tests, or chi-square tests were used to compare baseline differences between groups.

We first employed the Kaplan–Meier curve to describe the trend of CLD incidence over time in participants with and without CircS and used the log-rank test to analyze the differences in CLD occurrence between the two groups. Subsequently, the COX proportional hazards model was used to estimate the hazard ratios (HRs) and 95% confidence intervals (95%CIs) of the association between CircS (continuous/categorical) and the occurrence of CLD. The details for the time-to-event data: (1) Definition of Event and Censoring: The event was defined as the first occurrence of physician-diagnosed CLD during the follow-up period, as identified through the question: “Have you been diagnosed with liver disease by a doctor?” in any follow-up survey (2013, 2015, 2018, or 2020). Participants were censored at the date of the last follow-up survey in which they provided health information, at the time of death (if occurred during follow-up), or at the end of the study period (2020), whichever came first; (2)Time Zero: The start of follow-up (time zero) for all participants was defined as the date of the baseline interview in 2011. (3) Follow-up Time: The total person-years of follow-up were calculated from the baseline interview until the occurrence of the event, censoring, or the end of the study. We will also report the median follow-up time along with the interquartile range (IQR) for the entire cohort in the Results section to provide a clear summary of the follow-up duration; (4) Survival Time: Survival time (i.e., time-to-event) was calculated in years from the baseline (2011) until the occurrence of CLD for those who developed the event, or until the censoring time for those who did not. Four models were estimated: in Model 1, no covariates were adjusted; in Model 2, age, gender, place of residence, marital status, and education level were adjusted; in Model 3, smoking status, drinking status, and afternoon napping habits were further adjusted on the basis of the covariates in Model 2; in Model 4, kidney disease history, cancer history, hyperuricemia, and CRP were further adjusted on the basis of the covariates in Model 3.

Next, we evaluated the indirect association mediated by CVD between CircS (continuous/categorical) and the CLD and the direct association not mediated by CVD. Using the CMAverse package in R language, we calculated the total effect (TE), natural indirect effect (NIE), and natural direct effect (NDE) of CircS on the incidence of CLD. We constructed two regression models: one was a multivariate logistic regression model with CVD as the dependent variable and CircS (continuous/categorical) and confounding factors as independent variables; the other was a multivariate COX regression model with CLD as the outcome variable and CircS (continuous/categorical), CVD, and confounding factors as independent variables. NDE represents the direct impact of CircS on CLD, independent of CVD, while NIE represents the proportion of the influence of CircS on CLD affected by its relationship with CVD over time. To describe the magnitude of the mediating effect, we measured the proportion of the association mediated by CVD by calculating NIE divided by the sum of NDE and NIE.

Finally, subgroup analyses, interaction analyses, and sensitivity analyses were performed. The sensitivity analyses included: (1) analyzing after excluding obese patients (BMI > 28 kg/m2); (2) conducting mediating analysis using the second wave of CVD data (2013); (3) calculating the E-value; (4) performing analysis using the logistic regression model.

The above statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 25.0 and R language 4.4.4.

Discussion

In our prospective cohort study based on the national middle-aged and elderly population, we innovatively discovered that CircS is associated with the incidence of CLD. During the subsequent nine-year follow-up study, CircS demonstrated a significant correlation with the incidence of liver disease. Furthermore, our analysis revealed a substantial mediating effect of CVD on the association between CircS and the development of CLD, with the mediating effect accounting for 20.6% of the relationship after adjustments in the multivariate model (Model 4). These findings highlight the importance of circadian rhythm health in the prevention of CLD and suggest that integrated cardiometabolic management may be beneficial in high-risk populations.

Our study adds to a growing body of evidence linking circadian disruption to liver disease. Prior research examining the relationship between circadian rhythms and CLD has predominantly concentrated on specific behaviors, such as irregular sleep duration or reduced sleep time. For instence, A comprehensive prospective cohort study utilizing data from the UK Biobank identified that inadequate sleep duration, recurrent insomnia, snoring, and habitual daytime sleepiness were associated with a 19% to 37% elevated risk of newly diagnosed severe Metabolic Associated Steatotic Liver Disease (MASLD) [16]. Similarly, Wang [17] et al. investigated 22,807 participants in China and found a significant correlation between going to bed late and an increased prevalence of NAFLD. Furthermore, although individual components of CircS (e.g., depression, dyslipidemia, obesity) have been independently linked to liver pathologies, to the best of our knowledge, prospective evidence regarding the holistic cluster of CircS in relation to incident CLD remains limited. This study extends prior work by demonstrating that CircS, as a composite entity, is longitudinally associated with an elevated risk of developing CLD. On this basis, it was further confirmed that CircS, as a comprehensive indicator for assessing circadian rhythm disorders, is a more powerful and comprehensive predictor of CLD than any single sleep or metabolic abnormality.

Notably, CircS may offer superior predictive value compared to traditional metabolic syndrome (MetS). Shi et al. demonstrated that CircS was a better predictor of cardiovascular events than MetS in Chinese adults [4], suggesting that the inclusion of non-metabolic components such as sleep deprivation and depression provides a more holistic risk profile. Our study supports this notion in the context of liver disease, underscoring the need for a paradigm shift from MetS to CircS in chronic disease risk assessment.

Research into biological mechanisms may elucidate the relationship between CircS and CLD. The circadian rhythm system, which is prevalent among mammals, is organized as a hierarchical multi-oscillator structure. The central clock, situated in the suprachiasmatic nucleus (SCN) of the brain, is synchronized by light signals detected by the retina. These signals are transmitted via the retinohypothalamic tract to coordinate subsidiary oscillators in peripheral tissues [18]. The circadian rhythm plays a crucial role in the regulation of various hepatic functions, primarily through a transcription-translation feedback loop that orchestrates the diurnal oscillations of its components and numerous target genes via transcriptional and post-transcriptional mechanisms. This process is essential for maintaining liver homeostasis, with BMAL1 and REV-ERBα/β serving as central regulatory factors. Through transcriptomic analysis of hepatic stellate cells (HSCs) isolated from the livers of mice during the quiescent and active phases of the circadian rhythm, researchers observed an upregulation of pro-fibrotic-related genes and a downregulation of anti-fibrotic-related genes in the HSCs of mice exhibiting circadian rhythm disorders. In addition, typical clock genes such as clock, Bmal1, Cry1, Cry2, Per1, and Per2 exhibit periodic expression and/or activity within cells, driving cell-autonomous circadian rhythms [18]. Bu et al. [19] found that a large number of genes in the livers of Per1/Per2 double-knockout mice showed weakened circadian rhythm fluctuations, making them more sensitive to a high-fat diet and more prone to developing lipid metabolism disorders and NAFLD induced by a high-fat diet.

Moreover, an analysis of the components of CircS can be utilized to elucidate the phenomenon of an elevated risk of CLD within the CircS population. A substantial cross-sectional study revealed that individuals with severe depression exhibited a 1.6–2.2-fold increased likelihood of developing non-alcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD) compared to those without major depressive disorder (MDD) [20]. Pathological analysis of liver tissues from patients with NAFLD revealed a dose-dependent association between the severity of depressive symptoms and the extent of hepatocyte swelling. In comparison to non-depressed individuals, subclinical MDD patients exhibited a 2.1-fold increase in the likelihood of hepatocyte swelling, while clinical MDD patients demonstrated a 3.6-fold increase [21]. In the hepatic system, surplus free fatty acids and carbohydrates undergo conversion into triglycerides, which are subsequently encapsulated within very low-density lipoproteins (VLDLs), secreted into the bloodstream, and transported to adipocytes for storage. Furthermore, elevated blood glucose levels stimulate the release of insulin, which suppresses lipolysis [22], and facilitates lipogenesis by activating the synthesis of malonyl-CoA. Over time, the accumulation of excessive adipose tissue, particularly in the abdominal region, results in the release of inflammatory mediators, including tumor necrosis factor (TNF), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and other pro-inflammatory markers [23]. The interplay between inflammation and pathological metabolic alterations establishes a self-perpetuating cycle, ultimately contributing to the development of CLD.

A novel finding of our study is the mediating role of CVD in the CircS–CLD relationship, which is biologically plausible. Studies have proven that individuals with CircS are associated with a higher risk of CVD [24]. In addition to metabolic, infectious, and autoimmune conditions, heart failure and vascular diseases are also causes of CLD [25]. In clinical practice, elevated plasma liver function test concentrations are common in CVD patients [26]. Cardiogenic liver diseases, such as hepatic steatosis and cirrhosis, are usually caused by heart failure [27]. Yildiz et al. [28] found a positive correlation between brain natriuretic peptide (BNP) and the severity of decompensated non-alcoholic cirrhosis. Cesari et al. [29] found that an increased left atrial diameter was an independent risk factor affecting the survival of cirrhotic patients. Ultrasound indices reflecting cardiac function and BNP are also of great significance for assessing the risk of death from cirrhosis. Our mediation analysis suggests that managing cardiovascular health may partially mitigate the risk of CLD in individuals with circadian rhythm disorders.

In this study, the baseline survey results showed that individuals in the CircS group tended to be older, female, living in rural areas, divorced/separated/widowed, less likely to drink and smoke, with a lower education level. They also had a higher prevalence of depression, reduced sleep duration. However, in the subgroup analysis, marriage and smoking had a more significant relationship with the incidence of CLD. This indicates that divorce or separation and smoking cessation can reduce the risk of CLD. A prospective cohort study using CHARLS demonstrated that an increase in depressive symptoms was associated with a higher risk of CLD [30]. We thus hypothesize that modifications in marital life could mitigate specific stressors, including family disputes, familial obligations, or unhealthy relationships, thereby diminishing stress and depression and consequently reducing the risk of CLD. Numerous clinical studies have demonstrated that smoking adversely affects the incidence and severity of NAFLD, the progression of liver fibrosis, the development of hepatocellular carcinoma, and the prognosis of patients with advanced liver disease [31]. Consequently, smoking cessation may partially mitigate the risk of CLD and decelerate its progression. It is posited that the interplay of these potential factors contributes to the incidence and advancement of CLD. These findings highlight the complex interplay between circadian biology, behavior, and social determinants of health in the pathogenesis of CLD.

Utilizing data from CHARLS, we implemented a nationally representative prospective cohort study in China, encompassing a substantial sample of the Chinese population. After adjusting for numerous confounding variables, we employed COX regression analysis to examine the association between CircS and CLD. Comprehensive subgroup analyses, sensitivity analyses, and mediation analyses were conducted to provide a robust evaluation of the relationship between CircS and the risk of CLD. Nevertheless, our study is subject to several significant limitations. Firstly, the assessment of sleep information and CLD was conducted through retrospective self-reporting, which may introduce information bias. Secondly, the findings of this study are primarily applicable to middle-aged and elderly populations in China, potentially limiting their generalizability to other age groups within China and to populations in different countries. Third, the substantial exclusion of participants warrants careful consideration. Although these exclusions were necessary due to missing data on CircS/CVD diagnosis or incomplete follow-up for CLD, they may introduce selection bias. Specifically, baseline exclusions (e.g., lack of CircS/CVD data) might disproportionately affect vulnerable subgroups (e.g., rural elderly with limited healthcare access), potentially underestimating CircS prevalence and its association with CLD. Loss to follow-up (n = 754) could lead to informative censoring if participants who dropped out systematically differed in CLD risk factors (e.g., severe CircS patients being more likely to discontinue due to health deterioration). This might bias survival estimates (e.g., Kaplan–Meier curves) and attenuate hazard ratios in Cox models. To mitigate these concerns, we conducted sensitivity analyses: (1) E-value analysis (Additional File: Table S4) indicated that unmeasured confounders would need strong associations (HR > 1.99) with both CircS and CLD to fully explain our results, supporting robustness despite potential selection bias. (2) Replication of mediation analysis using the 2013 CVD data (wave 2) yielded consistent results (Additional File: Table S3), reducing concerns about follow-up attrition affecting mediation conclusions. Nevertheless, future studies should prioritize minimizing missing data through proactive follow-up strategies (e.g., multiple contact methods) and employ advanced statistical methods (e.g., multiple imputation or inverse probability weighting) to address informative censoring. Fourth, the diagnosis of CLD was based solely on self-reported physician diagnoses, which may introduce misclassification bias. Participants might underreport CLD due to lack of awareness, stigma, or recall bias, or overreport due to misunderstanding of the question. Such non-differential misclassification would likely attenuate the observed associations toward the null, suggesting that our hazard ratios may be conservative estimates of the true effect. Future studies should incorporate serological tests (e.g., ALT, AST), imaging, or medical record linkage to improve diagnostic accuracy and minimize misclassification.

Conclusion

In this large, prospective cohort study, we found that CircS was significantly associated with an elevated risk of incident CLD among middle-aged and elderly Chinese adults. Notably, this association was partly mediated by CVD, which accounted for approximately one-fifth of the total effect. These findings suggest that CircS, as a holistic cluster of circadian-related risk factors, is a novel and significant predictor for CLD development, independent of traditional risk factors. The mediating role of CVD highlights the importance of an integrated cardiometabolic management approach for preventing CLD in individuals with circadian rhythm disruptions. However, the findings should be interpreted in the context of several limitations, including the use of self-reported CLD diagnoses and potential selection bias due to missing data. Future research incorporating objective measures of liver function and longer follow-up is warranted to confirm our findings and elucidate the underlying biological mechanisms. Despite these limitations, our study underscores the importance of addressing circadian rhythm health in the primary prevention of chronic liver diseases.
