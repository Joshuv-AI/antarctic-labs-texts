Genetic contributions to mitochondrial dysfunction in amyotrophic lateral sclerosis etiology
Authors: Nikki D Russell, Jonathan M Downie, Mark B Bromberg, Stefan M Pulst, Lynn B Jorde
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryAmyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a fatal neurodegenerative disease with multiple genetic causes. Given the strong evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction in ALS, this study aimed to identify genetic contributors to ALS by focusing on genes involved in mitochondrial function. Whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing data from 1,034 individuals with ALS were analyzed using two distinct computational tools, which ranked candidate genes based on functional relevance to ALS. POLG, the sole mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) polymerase, emerged as a top candidate gene. RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) analysis revealed that among genes upregulated in samples with a POLG variant, there was an enrichment for mitochondrial pathways, including translation, localization, and mitophagy. It also revealed variants in POLG and SOD1, a well-known ALS gene, to be the most enriched in samples with expression profiles of mitochondrial-related genes that differed most from those of unaffected control subjects. POLG variant carriers also exhibited an increased burden of mitochondrial genome variants, a pattern shared by carriers of variants in other genes involved in mtDNA maintenance. Additionally, POLG variant carriers had elevated mtDNA copy number (mtDNA-CN), similar to carriers of variants in mitophagy-related genes, suggesting impaired mitophagy. Together, these findings implicate POLG as an ALS-associated gene and link mtDNA maintenance defects, altered expression of mitochondrial-related pathways, and impaired mitophagy to the ALS etiology.Keywords: ALS, POLG, mitochondrial dysfunction, disease genetics

Introduction

Amyotrophic lateral sclerosis (ALS) is a rare, rapidly fatal neurodegenerative disease that is caused by the death of motor neurons in the motor cortex and spinal cord.1,2 It is the most prevalent adult-onset motor neuron disease, with a lifetime risk of approximately one in 400 (1 in 347 for males and 1 in 436 for females).3 The limited current FDA-approved therapies provide only modest benefit for most patients, with greater efficacy limited to rare ALS subtypes.4,5

While there is a lack of consensus in the field, the preferred definition for familial ALS (FALS) is that one or more first- or second-degree relatives are also diagnosed with ALS. Affected individuals lacking this family history are considered to have sporadic ALS (SALS).6 FALS accounts for 5%–10% of ALS cases.7,8 The heritability estimates from twin studies for FALS and SALS are 60%–86% and 38%–78%, respectively (in twin pairs with SALS, there is no other family history of the condition).9,10 Despite the high heritability of ALS, 68% of familial cases and only 11%–17% of sporadic cases can be attributed to known genetic variants.11,12 FALS and SALS are clinically indistinguishable, and causal variants in several genes (e.g., superoxide dismutase 1 [SOD1] and C9ORF72) are found in both FALS and SALS cases.12 There is also a significant polygenic component in ALS, and some affected individuals have variants in multiple known ALS-associated genes.13 The genetic risk for patients with SALS appears to be a combination of rare variants with a variable effect size and common variants with a small effect size.13,14

The first known ALS-associated gene, SOD1, was discovered in 1993.15 SOD1 encodes a mitochondrial antioxidant enzyme that is essential for maintaining mitochondrial health and function. Since then, numerous other ALS-associated genes with mitochondrial roles have been identified, including CHCHD10, FUS, and OPTN.16,17,18 The discovery of these genes, along with extensive evidence of mitochondrial dysfunction in patients with ALS and ALS animal models, underscores the critical role of mitochondrial health in ALS etiology.19

In an effort to uncover genetic contributors to the missing heritability of ALS, we analyzed rare genetic variation and gene burden in 1,034 individuals with ALS. To interpret and prioritize the millions of variants in the genomes of individuals with ALS, we applied two complementary computational tools to identify disrupted genes and rank potentially disease-causing variants. Our analysis implicates POLG, the nuclear-encoded sole mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) polymerase, in ALS. Given the established role of mitochondrial dysfunction in ALS, POLG may represent a critical link between mitochondrial impairment and ALS etiology. We also extended our investigation beyond POLG to other mitochondrial-related pathways of mitophagy and mtDNA maintenance. These findings further our understanding of ALS pathogenesis and highlight genes and pathways with strong evidence linking them to ALS etiology.

Material and methods

Acquisition of ALS samples

To minimize heterogeneity and false positive results, only samples of European descent were used. To be considered of European descent, we required the sample to have at least 80% European ancestry, estimated by Somalier v.0.2.15.20

ALS samples were obtained from three different cohorts and were whole-genome sequenced unless otherwise noted. The first cohort comprises 140 samples collected at the University of Utah School of Medicine. These patients provided written consent to be included in genetic studies. Further details about this cohort have been described previously.12,21 Of these 140 samples, 132 were of European descent and were included. 64 were whole-exome sequenced, and 68 were whole-genome sequenced.

Samples in the second cohort were obtained from the organization Target ALS (https://www.targetals.org/). Genomic data were available for 231 ALS samples and 31 unaffected control samples. Of the 231 ALS samples, 206 were of European descent and selected for this study.

Samples from the third cohort were obtained from the Answer ALS Foundation Program, “Answer ALS” (https://www.answerals.org/).22 The data portal contained 774 ALS samples and 99 control samples with genomic data. Of the 774 ALS samples, 696 were of European descent and selected for this study. These last two cohorts were made available after submission of a data use agreement.

Most of the samples in the subset selected from Answer ALS, Target ALS, and the University of Utah would be categorized as SALS because there was no reported family history. However, in our ALS samples, 11% had a family history (116/1,034), and of those samples, only 3% had a known ALS-causing variant identified in the family history (32/1,034). We included all samples; however, to ensure the inclusion of FALS samples did not bias our results, we repeated the analysis using only the 918 SALS samples. As detailed later, their inclusion had no significant impact.

We obtained 729 control samples from 4 different cohorts. As above, Somalier was used to determine ancestry, and samples of European descent were selected. 525 unrelated samples were obtained from the 1000 Genomes Project.23 96 control subjects were taken from the Utah Longevity Cohort, selected on the basis of their good health and long life span.24 In addition, 15 control samples were obtained from Target ALS, and 93 were obtained from Answer ALS.

VAAST analysis to identify candidate genes

We used VAAST2 (Variant Annotations, Analysis, and Search Tool, v.2.2.0)25,26 to identify genes that had an excess of rare, deleterious variants in individuals with ALS compared to control subjects. VAAST2 is a probabilistic disease-gene framework that combines amino acid substitution data with aggregative scoring to prioritize variants.26 ALS was modeled with autosomal-dominant inheritance (heterozygous state), incomplete penetrance, and a minor-allele frequency (MAF) < 0.001 (European MAF). MAF was chosen based on previous studies using similar cutoffs.27,28,29 Significance was determined by permutation testing using 500,000 permutations. Further confirmation of the deleteriousness of a variant was done using the prediction tools CADD, REVEL, PolyPhen, Provean, and MetaSVM.30,31,32,33,34 Only three control individuals carried rare, deleterious POLG variants.

We then used Phevor (v.2) to re-rank genes identified by VAAST2.35 Using Human Phenotype Ontology (HPO) terms, Phevor integrates gene function, disease characteristics, and phenotype data to better prioritize disease-relevant genes. We applied Phevor twice with different sets of HPO terms. The first analysis used ALS-specific terms: ALS (HP: 0007354), abnormal motor neuron morphology (HP: 0002450), motor neuron atrophy (HP: 0007373), and frontotemporal dementia (HP: 0002145). The second analysis used broader, ALS-relevant terms, such as dysphagia (HP: 0002015), generalized muscle weakness (HP: 0003324), and paralysis (HP: 0003470). This was done to better capture additional ALS-associated genes that may not be directly linked to the previous ALS-specific HPO terms. Full lists of terms are included in Table S1.

VAAST2 and Phevor together produced a ranked list of candidate genes. Because our goal was to identify previously unreported ALS genes, we focused on the Phevor results using the broader HPO terms. Results from both analyses are shown in Table S1. POLG received the highest Phevor score in the broader-term analysis and ranked eighth in the ALS-specific analysis. To assess the potential influence of familial cases, we repeated the broader-term Phevor analysis using SALS samples alone. POLG ranked first in both analyses, indicating that inclusion of FALS cases did not affect the results. For this reason, and because classification based on family history may be unreliable, all subsequent analyses were performed using the combined SALS and FALS cohorts.

vIQ analysis

We also used vIQ v.2, the academic version of the tool GEM.36 vIQ is an AI-based variant prioritization framework that integrates multiple genomic and clinical database annotations and employs Bayesian methods to score and rank genes and variants.36 We assumed a dominant model of disease inheritance, incomplete penetrance, and the same MAF cutoff of 0.001. We used a vIQ/GEM score (Bayes factor) cutoff of >0.60. Previous work shows that this vIQ score yields a 95% probability that the causative gene is included among the top 4 candidate genes.36 This cutoff resulted in 4,452 variants (21.4%) to be included for consideration out of the 20,792 variants originally scored above 0 by vIQ. Unlike VAAST2, vIQ evaluates individual samples and trios using gnomAD variant frequencies as a reference. Because gnomAD variant frequencies are incorporated as control data, vIQ was not applied to our individual control samples.

Gene expression analysis

Whole-transcriptome “bulk” RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) data for ALS samples were obtained from Target ALS and Answer ALS. Altogether, 1,348 samples were derived from 16 different tissues from 560 individuals with ALS, and 120 control samples were derived from blood.

We obtained FASTQ files from a total of 967 Target ALS samples derived from 15 different tissues: induced pluripotent stem (iPS) cell lines, motor neuron cell lines, cerebellum, choroid, frontal cortex, lateral motor cortex, medial motor cortex, motor cortex (unspecified), occipital cortex, temporal cortex, sensory cortex, liver, lumbar spinal cord, thoracic spinal cord, and cervical spinal cord. Further details regarding the RNA extraction and processing pipeline are available on the Target ALS website (https://www.targetals.org/). The 967 samples corresponded to 179 individuals with ALS who also had genomic data and met the ancestry criteria described above.

All RNA samples from Answer ALS were derived from blood samples. Further details regarding the extraction method, library preparation, and RNA processing can be found on the Answer ALS website (https://www.answerals.org/). Of the 458 ALS samples for which transcriptomic data were available, 381 corresponded to samples that had genomic data and met the criteria described above and so were used in our expression analysis.

Control expression data were generated from RNA-seq of 120 blood samples from CEPH (Centre d'Etude du Polymorphisme Humain; Human Polymorphism Study Center) participants.37 These samples are of European descent and unrelated.

To measure gene expression levels, we first aligned FASTQ files to the Build38 human genome using Hisat2.38 FeatureCounts was used for transcript quantification, and differential expression analysis and count normalization were carried out with the default DESeq2 method.39,40 RNA expression data from different tissue sources were pooled, and tissue type was included as a covariate in the DESeq2 analysis to control for tissue-specific expression. Subsequent analyses did not reveal tissue-specific enrichment, indicating that this adjustment was effective. Because of our focus on POLG variants, the groups compared were individuals with ALS with and without a POLG variant. We specifically focused on MANE select transcripts, single representative transcripts for each human protein-coding gene that are most representative of the biology at that locus.41 Significantly differentially expressed genes were first filtered on an adjusted p value (p-adj value) < 0.05 (Benjamini-Hochberg correction) and a more stringent filter of p-adj < 0.005. Tissues not clearly relevant to ALS (e.g., liver, blood, or cell lines) and any tissues with very low sample count, such as the sensory cortex (n = 2), were omitted from analysis. The tissues that were analyzed were the frontal cortex, medial motor cortex, lateral motor cortex, motor cortex (unspecified), sensory cortex, occipital cortex, temporal cortex, cerebellum, choroid, cervical spinal cord, thoracic spinal cord, and lumbar spinal cord. The gene list analysis tool from the PANTHER (protein annotation through evolutionary relationship) classification system (https://pantherdb.org/) was used in GO enrichment analysis of significantly differentially expressed genes.42

We next compared all individuals with ALS to control samples by obtaining transcript per million (TPM) values for genes. TPM values were obtained from Salmon v.1.10, a bias-aware quantifier of transcript expression.43 Salmon generates TPM values directly from FASTQ files, skipping the alignment step. To evaluate expression patterns of nuclear and mitochondrial genes involved in mitochondrial function, principal-component analysis was applied using the prcomp command of the R statistical package (v.4.1.2). The gene set (1,135 genes) was obtained from MitoCarta3.0.44 Paired-end FASTQ file mapping was done on a decoy-aware GRCh38 transcriptome, following Salmon documentation. Expression data from all tissues were pooled. To account for tissue-specific expression effects, a linear model was fitted for each gene with tissue source as a covariate, and residuals from these models were extracted to obtain tissue-adjusted expression values. Genes with zero variance or no expression across all samples were excluded.

Mitochondrial genome variant quantification

Homoplasmic mitochondrial variants are present in all (or nearly all) copies of the mitochondrial genome. Heteroplasmic mitochondrial variants are present in only some mitochondria, most likely from errors in mitochondrial replication, oxidative stress, or other environmental insults, and these variants increase in frequency with age.45 We therefore confined our analysis to heteroplasmic variants.

To quantify the average number of heteroplasmic variants in the mitochondrial genome, we used MitoHPC,46 a bioinformatics pipeline for evaluating mtDNA variants. The GATK Mutect2 tool was used for somatic variant calling.47,48 MitoHPC first generates a unique reference for each sample using its own mtDNA consensus sequence generated from homoplasmic sites and major-allele heteroplasmic sites called by Mutect2. Then, heteroplasmic variants are called by remapping reads against the sample’s unique mtDNA reference sequence. This method greatly reduces the number of false positives and is better for calling individuals who are from mitochondrial haplogroups more distant from the reference mitochondrial genome, such as haplogroup L. Due to a lack of sufficient read depth of mtDNA, and despite sufficient read depth of nuclear-encoded DNA, mtDNA variants could not be quantified for Utah samples. These samples are thus excluded from analysis of mitochondrial variant quantification. We included variants with heteroplasmy levels as low as 3%, based on benchmarks and thresholds recommended in the documentation of the MitoHPC pipeline.

We used a one-way ANOVA test to compare the average number of mitochondrial genome variants in control samples, ALS samples without a POLG variant, and ALS samples with a POLG variant. Post hoc comparisons were conducted using Tukey’s Honestly Significant Difference (HSD) test to adjust for multiple comparisons. We also performed a two-way ANOVA test with cohort as the second factor to determine whether a cohort had a significant impact on the average number of mtDNA variants. Given there was no difference between the p-adj value when not including cohort as a factor and including cohort as a factor (one-way ANOVA: p-adj < 0.03; two-way ANOVA: p-adj < 0.03), we concluded that a one-way ANOVA was sufficient. We performed an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) including age of onset as a covariate; the p-adj values remained unchanged, demonstrating that age of onset did not contribute to the observed difference.

Mitochondrial genome copy-number quantification

To estimate mitochondrial DNA copy number (mtDNA-CN), we used the depth command in SAMtools v.1.9, which computes sequencing depth at each position across the genome.49 Assuming no difference in sequencing efficiency between the nuclear DNA and mtDNA, the average sequencing depth should be proportional to DNA copy number for both autosomal DNA and mtDNA.

Results

Variant identification and gene prioritization

We first applied VAAST2, followed by Phevor, on our 1,034 ALS samples to score and identify potential causative variants and genes.25,26,35 Phevor ranks genes based on the HPO terms provided. For the preliminary run of VAAST2 and Phevor, we entered ALS-specific HPO terms. Many top candidates in this analysis are ALS-associated genes, such as DNM2 and CYFIP2, validating the accuracy of these tools52,53 (Table S1). However, our goal was to identify candidate ALS genes that may not have previously been directly linked to ALS. Accordingly, the next VAAST2/Phevor analysis included HPO terms that more broadly define ALS-relevant phenotypes (Table S1). The top-ranked gene using the new HPO terms was POLG, which was ranked eighth in the previous ALS-specific analyses (Tables S1 and S2). To determine the influence of including FALS samples, we repeated the VAAST2/Phevor analysis using broadly defined HPO terms, focusing solely on individuals with SALS (n = 920). POLG was again ranked first, demonstrating that family-history-based classification did not influence gene prioritization. Therefore, all subsequent analyses were performed using the combined FALS and SALS cohorts.

We also used vIQ to score and rank potential causative variants and genes.36 Like VAAST2, vIQ ranked known ALS genes highly (Table S3). In contrast to VAAST2, vIQ analyzes each sample individually without aggregate scoring and identifies and prioritizes potentially causative variants and genes independent of their overall frequency in our cohort. VAAST2’s aggregative scoring approach is less effective at detecting causal genes when locus heterogeneity is high, whereas vIQ is unaffected by this limitation.36 A total of 21 POLG variants corresponding to 20 affected individuals had a log10-scaled Bayes factor score above 0 from vIQ, which indicates support of the hypothesis.36 In fact, 20 of those 21 variants met the >0.6 threshold, indicating substantial support, with variants scoring as high as 1.475 and averaging 1.03. For reference, the average vIQ score for TARDBP was 2.5 (n = 10 variants), and for SOD1, it was 2.1 (n = 27 variants).

POLG variants

Among the 1,034 individuals with ALS, we identified 17 distinct rare (MAF < 0.001), nonsynonymous, predicted-deleterious POLG variants in 25 (Table S2). In comparison, we found only 3 rare, deleterious POLG variants in our control cohort of 729 individuals, yielding a significant odds ratio (OR) of 6.05 (p < 0.002, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 1.74–19.79). A nominal p level was used here because POLG was selected based on prior genome-wide analyses. A replication cohort (Project Mine) also produced a significant OR for POLG, albeit less so than in our cohort (OR = 2.24, p < 0.02, 95% CI = 1.16–4.36).54 While the OR is smaller in the replication cohort, it is still similar to some other known ALS genes with a more moderate effect size described in previous studies, such as SPG11 (OR = 2.88) and CHCHD10 (OR = 1.51).55,56,57

The 25 samples were distributed equally across cohorts, indicating no overrepresentation within any one cohort (Table 1). The variants were distributed throughout the gene without clustering in any specific domain (Figure 1). None of the affected individuals had an expansion in C9ORF72 or ATXN2, and only one of the 25 samples had a variant in the known ALS gene SOD1. While the SOD1 variant could be the sole driver of ALS etiology in this individual, we elected to include this sample given the complementary roles of POLG and SOD1 in mitochondrial function, with the assumption that they may both be contributing to ALS etiology through mitochondrial dysfunction.

Mitochondrial-related gene expression

To determine the effect of POLG variants on gene expression patterns, we performed differential expression analysis of RNA-seq data and compared ALS samples with a POLG variant to ALS samples without. 3,103 genes were significantly downregulated in ALS samples with a POLG variant (p-adj < 0.05). Enrichment analysis of these genes showed no significant enrichment for any mitochondria-related categories (Table S4). However, analysis of the 1,729 genes significantly upregulated in ALS samples with a POLG variant revealed significant enrichment of 34 mitochondria-related categories (p-adj < 0.05). Some of these categories included mitochondrion organization (GO: 0007005, p-adj < 2.25 × 10−9), mitochondrial fission (GO: 0000266, p-adj < 0.015), mitochondrial translation (GO: 0032543, p-adj < 1.64 × 10−8), mitophagy (GO: 0000423, p-adj < 1.96 × 10−3), and others listed in Table S4.

With a more stringent p-adj of <0.005, 1,536 genes were downregulated and 540 genes were upregulated in samples with a POLG variant compared to ALS samples without. As in the previous analysis, no mitochondrial pathways were enriched among the downregulated genes (Table S4). While not as numerous, 6 mitochondrial pathways were enriched in the upregulated genes, including mitochondrial translation (GO: 0032543, p-adj < 1.13 × 10−6) and mitochondrial gene expression (GO: 0140053, p-adj < 1.27 × 10−7) (Table S4). An analysis including only ALS-relevant tissues, such as the frontal cortex and spinal cord, resulted in similar enrichment of mitochondrial pathways in samples with a POLG variant (Table S4). The enrichment of mitochondria-related pathways among genes upregulated in ALS samples harboring POLG variants suggests increased involvement of mitochondrial processes in these affected individuals compared with other ALS samples.

We hypothesized that ALS samples with mitochondrial gene expression profiles most different from control subjects might harbor genetic variants that significantly impact mitochondrial function. To investigate this, we compared expression profiles of ALS samples to control samples, specifically focusing on mitochondrial genes encoded by both nuclear and mitochondrial genomes (1,135 genes). The PCA plot showed a clear case-control separation (Figure 2).

We selected the top 300 out of 1,348 RNA samples with the greatest deviation from control subjects based on PC1 values. This group included samples that differed more than one standard deviation (24.8) in a negative direction from the ALS sample centroid (PC1 = −3.97). The total number of RNA samples exceeded the number of individuals with ALS because some affected individuals had multiple RNA samples derived from different tissues, with tissue source controlled by including it as a covariate.

We then assessed vIQ scores in these 300 samples (Table S3). After excluding CFTR due to the high frequency of variants of this gene in Northern Europeans and a non-significant enrichment (OR = 0.69, p > 0.9), one of the two genes most represented in the top 300 samples was POLG. In fact, the highest PC1 value, indicating the most distinct mitochondrial gene expression profile relative to control subjects, was a sample containing a highly ranked vIQ variant (vIQ score = 1.065) within POLG. This sample originated from the frontal cortex, a brain region that includes the motor cortex, which controls voluntary muscle movements and is relevant to, and affected in, ALS.58,59 Overall, nine of the top 300 samples harbored a POLG variant. Comparing the frequency of POLG in all samples with the frequency of POLG in the top 300 samples, this was a very significant enrichment (OR = 5.41, p < 0.0015). Inclusion of affected individuals with a POLG variant highly ranked by VAAST resulted in 4 more affected individuals being included in the top 300.

The second most highly represented gene was SOD1, with variants seen in 9 samples. SOD1 has known effects on mitochondrial function and is a well-established ALS-causing gene.60,61,62 The fact that POLG ranks equally with SOD1 in this analysis strengthens the case for POLG’s involvement in ALS, acting through widespread transcriptional changes affecting mitochondrial health and function.

Mitochondrial genome variant quantification

Due to the role of POLG in mtDNA replication, we examined the number of mitochondrial genome variants in individuals harboring a POLG variant. While there was no significant difference between control subjects and individuals with ALS without a POLG variant (p-adj > 0.99), we found that individuals with ALS with a POLG variant had a significantly higher average number of mitochondrial genome variants compared to individuals with ALS without a POLG variant (p-adj < 0.029) (Figure 3; Table S5). Accumulation of mtDNA variants has previously been linked to ALS, suggesting a potential mechanism by which POLG variants may contribute to ALS etiology.63,64 Individuals with ALS with a known monogenic cause exhibited the lowest average mtDNA variant burden (23.84 variants), followed by the overall ALS cohort (25.49 variants), whereas POLG-associated cases showed the highest burden (31.62 variants). This pattern suggests that POLG-associated ALS results at least in part from increased mitochondrial genomic instability.

Additionally, one of the pathways significantly enriched in the genes upregulated in affected individuals with a POLG variant was mitochondrial gene expression (p-adj < 3.94 × 10−8). Many of the genes in this category are mitochondrial ribosomal subunits, mitochondrial RNA-processing factors, or mitochondrial translation initiation factors. This is another indicator that the integrity of mtDNA in samples with a POLG variant is compromised.

Given POLG’s role in mitochondrial function, we extended our analysis to the other 17 genes that are essential for mtDNA maintenance.65,66 vIQ and VAAST2 identified 34 additional rare, deleterious variants in 12 of these genes. As in POLG, the average number of mtDNA variants was significantly higher than in individuals with ALS with no variants in these mtDNA maintenance genes (p-adj < 0.037) (Figure 4). mtDNA-CN is not increased in these 34 samples, indicating that this result is not simply due to higher mtDNA levels (Figure S1).

Further investigation of individuals with ALS carrying a variant in a mtDNA maintenance gene, including POLG, revealed a notable trend relating to the reported site of symptom onset. Bulbar-onset ALS occurs when initial symptoms manifest in the neck or face, typically presenting as slurred speech, difficulty chewing, and weakness or twitching in the muscles of the face, jaw, and throat. Bulbar onset accounts for only approximately 27% of focal-onset cases, with nearly three-quarters classified as limb onset.67,68 In our dataset, bulbar onset was reported in 223 of 920 individuals with focal-onset ALS (24%), closely matching the expected distribution. However, among the affected individuals with a mtDNA maintenance gene variant, including POLG, and a reported focal site of onset (n = 50), 23 had bulbar onset (46%) (Figure 5). This is significantly more than expected based on the expected distribution (p < 0.0015), where approximately 12 bulbar-onset cases are expected.

Mitochondrial DNA copy number

We found that ALS samples had a significantly higher average mtDNA-CN compared to control subjects (Figure 6; Table S6), consistent with previous reports.69,70,71 Some POLG variants are known to cause mtDNA-CN depletion syndromes, some of which were identified in our ALS cohort.72 Despite this, individuals with ALS carrying POLG variants exhibited, on average, higher mtDNA-CN than other individuals with ALS (Figure 6). While not significant (p-adj > 0.26), we observed a strong cohort-specific effect on mtDNA-CN values, potentially due to sequencing platform differences. To account for this, we included cohort as a factor in the ANOVA test comparing mtDNA-CN between different groups. After accounting for cohort, samples with a POLG variant had significantly higher mtDNA-CN than other ALS samples (p-adj < 0.038) (Figure 6). This finding is notable given the established association between elevated mtDNA-CN and ALS and POLG with mtDNA-CN depletion. These results suggest that, in the context of ALS, POLG variants may exert effects distinct from those observed in other POLG-related disorders.

Aberrant mtDNA-CN levels have been associated with impaired mitophagy, mitochondrial health, and neurodegeneration.73,74,75 As shown earlier, genes upregulated in samples with a POLG variant were significantly enriched for genes involved in autophagy of the mitochondrion (p-adj < 1.47 × 10−3). Several of these mitophagy genes with expression levels upregulated by a mutated POLG have already been associated with ALS, such as BECN1 and ATG5.76,77 Recent studies have also suggested POLG may directly activate mitophagy.78 Accordingly, we generated a list of genes that are related to mitophagy and harbor highly ranked vIQ variants (Table S3). This allowed us to study mitophagy genes, such as MFN2 and PRKN, each of which could contribute to ALS etiology, that individually lacked a sufficient number of high-ranking variants for statistical analysis but have a more discernible effect when combined.79,80 We found that samples with a high-ranking vIQ variant in a mitophagy gene had significantly higher mtDNA-CN than the remainder of the ALS samples (p-adj < 0.003) (Figure 7). This effect is even stronger when including the sample cohort as a factor in the ANOVA test (p-adj < 4.7 × 10−6) (Figure 7).

Variants in these genes and in POLG may contribute to ALS by reducing the cell’s ability to recycle mitochondria, leading to excess mtDNA-CN, which is a hallmark of ALS cases.71 The mitophagy genes had no significant increase in mitochondrial genome variants (p-adj > 0.88) (Figure S2), unlike samples with a POLG variant. Mitophagy genes and POLG may both contribute to ALS etiology through impaired mitophagy and a subsequent increase in mtDNA-CN but are distinct in that POLG variants seem to contribute to mitochondrial dysfunction through both impaired mitophagy and an increase in mitochondrial genome variants.

Discussion

POLG’s role as the sole mtDNA polymerase makes it essential for maintaining mtDNA integrity.72 Pathogenic POLG variants have been associated with mitochondrial depletion and deletion syndromes, often manifesting as neurodegenerative or neuromuscular disorders such as progressive external ophthalmoplegia (PEO) and ataxia.72 However, POLG has not previously been associated with ALS. Our findings indicate that POLG may contribute to the ALS etiology through mitochondrial dysfunction.

POLG emerged as a top candidate in our VAAST2/Phevor analysis. This, combined with its critical role in mitochondrial health, prompted further investigation. RNA-seq analysis revealed that ALS samples with POLG variants had significant upregulation of genes involved in mitochondrial pathways—including translation, localization, and mitophagy—relative to ALS samples without POLG variants. While mitochondrial dysregulation is a recognized hallmark of ALS,19 our data suggest that POLG variant carriers may exhibit a more severe or distinct mitochondrial phenotype.

ALS samples with POLG variants had significantly more mitochondrial genome variants, consistent with the hypothesis that mutant POLG introduces replication errors. Prior work suggests that it is not necessarily the location of mtDNA variants that drives disease but rather the cumulative burden of variant load within affected tissues.81,82,83 This aligns with ALS being a late-onset disease, where mtDNA variants may accumulate over time and contribute to neurodegeneration. This pattern was also seen in carriers of variants in other mtDNA maintenance genes, suggesting a broader role for mtDNA instability in ALS and highlighting other targets for future study.

Unexpectedly, POLG variant carriers showed elevated mtDNA-CN, in contrast to POLG’s known association with mtDNA depletion syndromes.72 This, together with mitophagy gene upregulation in the same samples, suggests impaired mitochondrial turnover. A similar pattern was seen in carriers of mitophagy gene variants, highlighting the role of disrupted mitochondrial turnover in ALS etiology.

While it remains unclear whether POLG variants alone are sufficient to cause ALS, the convergence of increased mtDNA variants, altered mitochondrial gene expression, and elevated mtDNA-CN supports a model in which POLG contributes to ALS through disrupted mitochondrial dynamics—particularly via impaired mitophagy, translation, and the upregulation of mitochondria-related genes.

Mouse models support this hypothesis. While POLG knockout is embryonically lethal, heterozygous expression of a proofreading-deficient POLG allele results in mitochondrial dysfunction and premature aging, supporting our hypothesis of a dominant mechanism of POLG variants in ALS etiology.84,85,86 More relevantly, a muscle-specific POLG variant induces mitochondrial dysfunction and progressive muscle degeneration, a phenotype observed in SOD1 ALS models and reflective of skeletal muscle atrophy, a hallmark feature of ALS.81,87,88 Expression analysis from the muscle-specific POLG models revealed upregulation of neurodegeneration-related and mitophagy pathways.81 This study highlights the pathogenic potential of POLG variants within neuromuscular tissues and implies that POLG dysfunction may contribute to ALS through cell-specific effects in muscle and motor neurons.

Our results also suggest tissue-specific effects. Individuals with ALS with mtDNA maintenance gene variants, including POLG, were significantly enriched for bulbar-onset ALS. The genes responsible for mtDNA maintenance are implicated in a wide range of diseases that can be grouped together under the umbrella term of mtDNA maintenance defects (MDMDs).65 Many of these disorders involve cranial neuromuscular symptoms, such as ophthalmoparesis (weakness of the extrinsic eye muscles), ptosis (drooping eyelids), dysphagia (difficulty swallowing), dysarthria (slurred speech), and general facial weakness.65,89 Chronic PEO (CPEO), the most common manifestation of MDMDs,90 is associated with variants in 13 of the 18 genes we examined.65 Although extraocular motor neurons are classically spared in ALS, MDMDs demonstrate that defects in mtDNA maintenance can selectively impact certain cranial motor neuron populations. Given that ALS symptoms often progress to adjacent anatomical regions, it is possible that genes affecting cranial motor systems in one disorder could also contribute to bulbar dysfunction in ALS, reflecting a shared but non-identical susceptibility of cranial motor neurons to mitochondrial dysfunction.68

While there are limitations to our study, including the inability to detect ultra-rare variants due to sample size, we believe the identification of POLG as a potential ALS gene opens several avenues for future research. In addition to POLG, genes involved in mtDNA maintenance had increased mitochondrial genome variants, implying impaired mtDNA health. Genes involved in mitophagy were shown to have increased mtDNA-CN, implying impaired mitophagy. Mitophagy genes did not show an increase in mtDNA variants, and mtDNA maintenance genes did not show an increase in mtDNA-CN, indicating that these genes function in distinct pathways to impact mitochondrial health. Notably, POLG variant carriers showed an increase in both measurements. This, paired with the expression data, implicates POLG in multiple aspects of mitochondrial dysfunction. Further studies could explore the specific mechanisms by which POLG variants might lead to ALS, including the impact on mitochondrial dynamics, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation. These results contribute to our understanding of ALS genetics and may offer potential therapeutic avenues targeting mitochondrial health, with the hope of improving clinical outcomes for patients with ALS in the future.
