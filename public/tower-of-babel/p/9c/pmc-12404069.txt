A New Species and Eight New Records of Ascomycetes from Freshwater Niches in Korea
Authors: Thuong T. T. Nguyen, Gregory Bonito, Hyang Burm Lee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractFreshwater ascomycetes are fungi that play an important role in the decomposition of submerged plant debris in freshwater habitats. At present, approximately 3000 species of ascomycetes have been described from freshwater habitats. However, the number of fungi in freshwater habitats in South Korea is small, and many species are still completely unknown. During investigations of freshwater ascomycetes in South Korea, three Dothideomycetes strains and seven Sordariomycetes strains were collected. Based on morphological and multigene phylogenetic analyses, one new species of Neopestalotiopsis was delimited: Neopestalotiopsis koreensis. In addition, Chaetocapnodium philippinense, Curvularia plantarum, Engyodontium rectidentatum, Fusarium arcuatisporum, Microdochium yunnanense, Septoriella verrucispora, Stephanonectria chromolaenae, and Trichoderma amoenum, are reported for the first time from South Korea. Herein, detailed descriptions and illustrations of the morphological characteristics of these species are provided.

Introduction

Freshwater ascomycetes are fungi that play an important role in the decomposition of organic materials, including submerged plant debris, in freshwater habitats [1–4]. They grow as saprotrophs on submerged or partially submerged plant substrates in aquatic habitats [5]. Freshwater ascomycetes are categorized into 13 classes (Arthoniomycetes, Candelariomycetes, Coniocybomycetes, Dothideomycetes, Eurotiomycetes, Laboulbeniomycetes, Lecanoromycetes, Leotiomycetes, Lichinomycetes, Orbiliomycetes, Pezizo­mycetes, Saccharomycetes, and Sordariomycetes), 1018 genera and 2968 species [6]. Most of these fungi belong to the classes Sordariomycetes (823 species and 298 genera) and Dothideomycetes (677 species and 229 genera) [6–8].

Dothideomycetes species are among the first reported fungi in freshwater environments [9]. They are characterized by asci with two wall layers (bitunicate asci) and often with fissitunicate dehiscence [10]. Calabon et al. [6] listed 18 orders, 75 families, 229 genera, and 677 species of Dothideomycetes in freshwater habitats. Most freshwater Dothideomycetes species belong to the order Pleosporales (391 species), followed by Tubeufiales (145 species) [6].

Sordariomycetes is one of the largest classes of freshwater ascomycetes and are characterized by perithecial ascomata and inoperculate unitunicate asci [11,12]. This class is currently classified into 40 orders, 85 families, 298 genera, and 823 species [6,7]. Among the 40 orders, Chaetosphaeriales has the highest number of species (149 species), followed by Hypocreales (80 species) [6].

Dothideomycetes and Sordariomycetes are known for the production of bioactive compounds [13–15]. Anguillosporal, a new antibacterial and antifungal metabolite, has been isolated from the freshwater fungus Anguillospora longissima [16]. In a previous study, two new metabolites isolated from the freshwater fungus Delitschia corticola (YMF 1.01111) were effective against three fungal pathogen, Alternaria, Sclerotium and Fusarium species, and three pathogenic bacterial species, Bacillus cereus, Brevibacillus laterosporus, and Staphylococcus aureus [17]. Moreover, six novel compounds and three known compounds obtained from the freshwater fungus Chaetomium sp. YMF 1.02105 exhibited antimicrobial activity against S. aureus (ATCC 6538) in standard disk assays [18]. Although recent studies have revealed new species and new records of freshwater ascomycetes in South Korea [19–27], significant knowledge gaps exist in our understanding of these fungi in this region.

This study aimed to expand our understanding of the diversity and distribution of freshwater ascomycetes in South Korea and to illustrate and describe a novel species, Neopestalotiopsis koreensis, and eight new records, Chaetocapnodium philippinense, Curvularia plantarum, Engyodontium rectidentatum, Fusarium arcuatisporum, Microdochium yunnanense, Stephanonectria chromolaenae, Septoriella verrucispora, and Trichoderma amoenum.

Materials and methods

Sampling, isolation, and morphology

Freshwater samples were collected from different regions of South Korea including Chungcheongnam-do, Jeollabuk-do, Jeollanam-do, Gyeonggi-do, and Gyeongsangbuk-do (Supplemental Table S1). The samples were placed in sterile plastic bottles for transport to the laboratory and stored at 4 °C. The samples were processed according to the method described previously by Nguyen et al. [22] and Lee et al. [28]. Briefly, 100-µL aliquot was dispensed onto potato dextrose agar (PDA, Difco, Detroit, MI), and corn meal agar (CMA, Difco, Detroit, MI) amended with neomycin (50 mg/L). The plates were incubated at 25 °C for 5–14 d. The pure cultures were deposited in the Environmental Microbiology Laboratory Fungarium at Chonnam National University, Gwangju, South Korea and also in the Culture Collection of the Nakdonggang National Institute of Biological Resources (NNIBR), Sangju, South Korea. Type specimens were deposited at the Chonnam National University (CNUFC) Fungarium, Gwangju, South Korea, as inactive dried cultures.

For morphological analysis, the strains were grown in triplicate on PDA, CMA, and oatmeal agar (OA, MB Cell, Seoul, South Korea) and incubated at 25 °C in the dark for 7–14 d. Microstructures were observed under an Olympus BX53 light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) after mounting fragments of the fungal mycelia in 60% lactic acid.

DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and sequencing

Genomic DNA was extracted from 7-d-old fungal cultures grown on PDA at 25 °C by using the SolgTM Genomic DNA Preparation Kit (Solgent Co. Ltd., Daejeon, South Korea), according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Six loci were amplified and sequenced: the internal transcribed spacer (ITS) region using the primers V9G/ITS4 [29,30], nuclear large subunit (LSU) rDNA using the primers LR0R/LR5 [31], the RNA polymerase II second largest subunit (RPB2) gene using the primers RPB2-5F/RPB2-7cR and RPB2-5F2/RPB2-7cR [32,33], a fragment of the translation elongation factor 1-α (TEF-1α) gene using the primers EF1/EF2 and EF1-983F/EF-2218R [34,35], the glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) gene using the primers gpd1/gpd2 [36], and β-tubulin (BenA) gene using the primers T1/T22 and Tub2Fd/Tub4Rd [37,38]. The amplification conditions were as reported by Lee et al. [28] and Nguyen et al. [39]. The PCR products were purified using an Accuprep PCR Purification Kit (Bioneer). DNA sequencing of the PCR products was performed at Macrogen (Daejeon, South Korea) using the above-mentioned PCR primers. The newly obtained sequences are deposited in the GenBank database (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/genbank/).

Phylogenetic analyses

The obtained sequences were compared with similar fungal sequences retrieved from the National Center for Biotechnology Information (NCBI) database using the Basic Local Alignment Search Tool (BLAST). The sequences were aligned using MAFFT version 7 (https://mafft.cbrc.jp/align ment/server) [40] for each molecular marker. The alignments were concatenated, and maximum likelihood (ML) analysis was performed using the RAxML-HPC2 program on XSEDE (version 8.2.8) through the CIPRES Science Gateway (https://www.phylo.org) [41,42] by employing the generalized time-reversible (GTR) model and applying the partitioning option, which estimates the gamma shape parameter and the proportion of invariable sites for every gene separately. Branch support (BS) for ML analysis was determined using 1,000 bootstrap replicates. Trees were visualized using FigTree version 1.4.3. [43] with BS support values higher than 70% in branches.

Results

Phylogeny

Phylogenetic analysis of Chaetocapnodium, Septoriella, and Engyodontium based on ITS, LSU and TEF-1α loci revealed that the isolates CNUFC UrW2-5, CNUFC GRW-22, and CNUFC NYT-H6 clustered with C. philippinense, S. verrucispora, and E. rectidentatum, respectively (Figures 1, 3, and 5). Analysis of the combined dataset of ITS, GAPDH, and TEF-1α sequences revealed that CNUFC GHW2-11 was nested with Curvularia plantarum CGMCC 3.19342 (ex-type) and USJCC-0033 (Figure 2). The combined phylogenetic analysis of ITS, BenA and TEF-1α sequences reveal that CNUFC YNW1 and CNUFC YNW2 strains forms a distinct, well-supported clade separate from other species of Neopestalotiopsis, and is closely related to Neo. clavispora and Neo. cavernicola (Figure 4). The combined phylogenetic analysis of ITS and RPB2 sequences revealed that CNUFC HPW-20 and CNUFC DBSW-32 clustered within the same clade as Fusarium arcuatisporum and Trichoderma amoenum, respectively (Figures 6 and 9). Moreover, the combined analysis of ITS, LSU, and RPB2 sequences found that CNUFC CT7 was close to Microdochium yunnanense SAUCC1011 (ex-type), SAUCC1012 and SAUCC1015 (Figure 7). In the combined analysis of ITS and LSU rDNA sequences, CNUFC HBA-19 clustered with Stephanonectria chromolaenae (Figure 8).

Discussion

Freshwater ascomycete fungi are important decomposers of submerged organic materials, including woody and herbaceous substrates. They are well-studied in Australia, China, Japan, Thailand and North America [5,6]; however, less attention has been paid to these fungi in South Korea. In this study, we isolated fungi from freshwater environments and characterized them morphologically and with multi-gene phylogenetic analysis. We show that our isolates belong to the classes Dothideomycetes and Sordariomycetes and we describe one novel species, Neo. koreensis sp. nov., and provide eight new records for South Korea: C. philippinense, Cu. plantarum, E. rectidentatum, F. arcuatisporum, M. yunnanense, St. verrucispora, St. chromolaenae, and T. amoenum.

In the class Dothideomycetes, Pleosporales is the largest freshwater order consisting of 391 freshwater species [6]. Two species reported here (Cu. plantarum and S. verrucispora) belong to the order Pleosporales. Another species (C. philippinense) belongs to the order Capnodiales.

The genus Chaetocapnodium (Capnodiales, Capnodiaceae) was introduced by Liu et al. [44], with C. siamensis Hongsanan & K.D. Hyde as type species. This species was originally isolated from the leaves of an unidentified plant. Most members of this genus have been found on host plants [59,60]. At present, the genus consists of 10 species [61]. Chaetocapnodium philippinense was first reported on the leaves of palm (Arecaceae) in China [44]. Our strain was isolated from a freshwater sample in South Korea, thus providing a new host record of C. philippinense.

The genus Curvularia (Pleosporales, Pleosporaceae) was described by Boedjin in 1933 [62], with C. lunata Boedjin as type species. This genus currently consists of 234 described species [61]. Members of this genus have been detected on various hosts and geographical locations worldwide. Curvularia plantarum was first reported on Saccharum officinarum in China [45] and has since been recorded on other hosts in Sri Lanka and Thailand [46–48]. In China, it has also been implicated as the causal agent of rice spikelet rot and leaf spot disease in sweet potato (Ipomoea batatas) [49,50] while our strain, CNUFC GHW2-11, was isolated from a freshwater sample in South Korea. The morphological characteristics of CNUFC GHW2-11 are similar to the original description provided by Raza et al. [45]. However, we noted a slight difference in the size of conidia between our specimen and the description provided by Raza et al. [45].

The genus Septoriella (Pleosporales, Phaeosphaeriaceae) was initially described by Oudemans in 1889 [63], with S. phragmitis Oudem serving as the type species [64]. This genus is characterized by mucoid, appendage-bearing, pale brown, multiseptate conidia formed on holoblastic conidiogenous cells [64,65]. Members of this genus are commonly associated with various host plants, including grasses, cereals and other herbaceous plants [64–66]. At present, Septoriella consists of 51 species [61]. Wanasinghe and Maharachchikumbura [67] introduced S. verrucispora comb. nov. to accommodate the taxon previously described as Hydeopsis verrucispora. In this study, our strain (CNUFC GRW-22) was identified as S. verrucispora based on the combined phylogenetic analysis of ITS, LSU and TEF-1α sequences. Septoriella verrucispora was reported from dead culms of an herbaceous plant in China [51]. The occurrence of S. verrucispora in South Korea is interesting because this species was previously detected only in China.

In the class Sordariomycetes, Hypocreales is the largest freshwater order consisting of 80 species [6,7]. Four species reported in this study (E. rectidentatum, F. arcuatisporum, St. chromolaenae and T. amoenum) belong to the order Hypocreales. Another species (M. yunnanense) and (Neo. koreensis) belong to Xylariales and Amphisphaeriales, respectively.

The genus Engyodontium (Hypocreales, Cordycipitaceae) was first reported by de Hoog in 1978 [68]. It included two species: E. album (Limber) de Hoog and E. parvisporum (Petch) de Hoog. After several taxonomic changes, the genus currently contains three species: E. geniculatum, E. parvisporum, and E. rectidentatum [61]. So far, no members of this genus have been detected in South Korea. Species of Engyodontium are characterized by cobweb-like colonies and hyaline, globose to subglobose conidia. They have been isolated from soil and arthropod cadavers, as well as from opportunistic cutaneous and subcutaneous infections in animals, including humans [69]. Gams et al. [52] introduced the species E. rectidentatum. This species is widely distributed and has been collected from air over a sugarcane field, buried keratinous substance and soil in India [52], as well as from soil in Japan [52]. More recently, it has been collected from citrus orchard pests in Iran [53] and tadpole-containing freshwater in South Korea in this study. Our strain CNUFC NYT-H6 exhibits morphological similarities to E. rectidentatum CBS 64174 by having ovoid to fusiform conidia. However, E. rectidentatum CBS 64174 has longer conidia than our strain [52].

The genus Fusarium (Hypocreales, Nectriaceae) was introduced by Link in 1809 [70], with F. sambucinum Brondeau as the type species. Members of this genus are commonly found on plants and plant-derived materials, as well as in air, water, and soil [71]. The genus includes more than 330 species [72]. Fusarium arcuatisporum was originally described by Wang et al. [54]. It was isolated from pollen of Brassica campestris, Oryza species and a leaf of Nelumbo nucifera in China [54]. Our new strain, F. arcuatisporum CNUFC HPW-20, was isolated from a freshwater sample. Fusarium arcuatisporum CNUFC HPW-20 has smaller conidia [27–38 × 3.5–4.5(–5) μm] compared to those of the ex-type strain (29–49.5 × 4–6 μm) [54].

The genus Microdochium (Xylariales, Microdochiaceae) was introduced by Sydow in 1924 [73]. The type species M. phragmitis Sydow was found on the leaves of Phragmites australis. Members of this genus are commonly reported as plant pathogens or saprotrophs and are frequently isolated from some diseased plant hosts [55]. At present, 63 Microdochium species are listed in the Index Fungorum [61]. These species are characterized by coelomycetous asexual morphs that produce polyblastic, sympodial, or annellidic conidiogenous cells with hyaline conidia. Microdochium yunnanense was introduced by Huang et al. [55] based on a specimen collected from the leaves of Indocalamus longiauritus in China [55]. Our strain exhibits morphological similarities with the ex-type strain M. yunnanense SAUCC1011; however, it has larger conidia [55]. Additionally, chlamydospores are present in our strain but not in the type strain.

The genus Neopestalotiopsis (Amphisphaeriales, Pestalotiopsidaceae) was introduced by Maharachchikumbura et al. [74], with Neo. protearum (Crous & L. Swart) Maharachch., K.D. Hyde & Crous as type species. The genus was introduced to accommodate pestalotiopsis-like taxa, which are characterized by varicolored median cells and indistinct conidiophores [74]. The genus currently comprises 127 species [61]. Members of this genus commonly occur as endophytes, plant pathogens, or saprotrophs [74–77]. The proposed a new species, Neo. koreensis is phylogenetically related to Neo. clavispora and Neo. cavernicola (Figure 4). Neopestalotiopsis koreensis differs from Neo. clavispora and Neo. cavernicola by producing shorter conidia [16–21.5(–22.5) × 5–7 μm vs. 20–24 × 6–8 μm, and 16–21.5(–22.5) × 5–7 μm vs. 10–30 × 5–10 μm] and shorter apical appendages [(4–)7–21.5 μm vs. 22–32 μm, and (4–)7–21.5 μm vs. 5–30 μm) [78,79]. Furthermore, Neo. koreensis produces 2–3 tubular apical appendages, while Neo. cavernicola has 1–4 tubular apical appendages [79]. Neopestalotiopsis clavispora was collected from dead leaves of Magnolia sp., decaying wood, and fallen leaves of Quercus rubra [74,78], while Neo. cavernicola was collected from inner cave rock surfaces in China [79]. In contrast, our new strain was collected from freshwater habitat in South Korea. This is the first report of a new Neopestalotiopsis species from freshwater habitats, expanding our understanding of the ecological distribution of this genus.

The genus Stephanonectria (Hypocreales, Bionectriaceae) was introduced by Schroers et al. [80], with St. keithii as the type species. Members of this genus are characterized by brown, smooth to rough, minutely papillate perithecia, surrounded by a crown-like ostiolum [80]. The genus currently contains three described species: St. chromolaenae, St. ellipsoidea, and St. keithii [61]. Stephanonectria chromolaenae was introduced by Perera et al. [56] based on specimens collected from the dead stem of Chromolaena odorata in Thailand. Our collections of St. chromolaenae from South Korea matched the description provided by Perera et al. [56]; however, they differ in having smaller conidia. This is the first record of this genus from a freshwater habitat.

The genus Trichoderma was first introduced by Persoon in 1794 [81] and is characterized by fast-growing, irregularly branched conidiophores, solitary flask-shaped phialides. These species are abundant on decaying wood and in soil [82,83]. Among these, T. harzianum, T. atroviride, and T. asperellum can be used for the biological control of plant pathogenic fungi. Trichoderma amoenum was first reported from soil in China by Zheng et al. [58]. To our knowledge, this is the first report of T. amoenum from South Korea. It is also the first report of this species from a freshwater sample worldwide.

The discovery of these species has revealed the abundant fungal diversity in freshwater in South Korea and has further enriched the number of freshwater ascomycetes known in South Korea. More scientific studies in freshwater habitats are expected in the future.
