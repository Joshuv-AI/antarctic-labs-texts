Salivary opiorphin as a potential non-invasive biomarker for dental anxiety
Authors: Teena Sheethal Dsouza, Prathiksha D Kudva, Prajna Bhandary, Ashma Dorothy Monteiro, Kelvin Peter Pais
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDental anxiety remains a major challenge in clinical dentistry, often preventing patients from seeking necessary treatment and compromising oral health outcomes. Although psychological scales such as Corah’s Dental Anxiety Scale are widely used to assess anxiety, objective biochemical markers could provide valuable complementary information. Opiorphin, an endogenous peptide known for its analgesic and antidepressant-like effects, has recently emerged as a potential biomarker for stress and pain regulation. However, the role of Opiorphin in dental anxiety remains largely unexplored; therefore, to the best of our knowledge, the present investigation represents an initial effort to address this gap in the literature. This study aimed to estimate salivary opiorphin levels in patients undergoing routine dental checkups and examine their correlation with anxiety levels using Corah’s Dental Anxiety Scale. Additionally, it sought to evaluate the relationship between salivary opiorphin and the established dental anxiety biomarker, salivary alpha-amylase.MethodsFifty-six adult participants aged 20–60 years who reported for routine dental checkups without associated pain, systemic illness, or recent analgesic use were enrolled. Background

Dental anxiety encompasses not only the perception of intraoperative pain but also the psychological distress associated with anticipation and the perceived quality of the clinician-patient relationship. Historically, dental anxiety has been recognized as a prevalent global concern; it was identified by Agras et al. as the fifth most common manifestation of anxiety [1]. Current epidemiological data suggest a prevalence between 12% and 15%, with significantly higher rates reported among female and younger populations [2, 3]. Notably, subclinical anxiety levels often remain undetected in routine dental practice, as patients may employ internal coping mechanisms or exhibit avoidant behaviors rather than explicit verbal expression. Such avoidant patterns frequently result in the postponement of essential care, thereby escalating the requirement for emergency interventions. Research indicates that approximately 49% of adults seeking emergency dental treatment experience significant dental anxiety [4]. These emergency visits, often necessitated by severe odontogenic pain, typically involve complex or invasive procedures that can further exacerbate pre-existing negative perceptions of dental treatment [5].

Effective clinical management requires both the accurate identification of anxious patients and the implementation of appropriate behavioral or pharmacological interventions. Clinicians must rely on diagnostic intuition and observational expertise to detect subtle behavioral cues, which can exhibit considerable inter-individual variability [6]. The primary objective of the practitioner is the attenuation of fear to foster long-term positive motivation for future care [6]. However, distinguishing persistent dental anxiety from a patient’s baseline temperament remains a diagnostic challenge, particularly when observable behavioral changes are not apparent in response to specific clinical stressors [7].

Given these diagnostic complexities, biochemical markers offer an objective means of quantifying the physiological stress response. Cortisol and alpha-amylase are established indicators of hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal (HPA) axis activity and sympathetic nervous system activation, respectively [8]. As these molecules are present in measurable concentrations in oral fluids, they can be quantified through salivary sampling [9]. Saliva collection is a non-invasive, hygienic, and cost-effective diagnostic medium that minimizes additional procedural stress for the patient [10]. While salivary alpha-amylase (sAA) has emerged as a prominent marker for SNS activity, its definitive association with dental anxiety remains equivocal. For instance, AlMaummar et al. demonstrated that while sAA showed a weak correlation with generalized anxiety, it was effective in identifying dental phobia in pediatric populations [11]. Consequently, there is a clear need to identify more consistent salivary biomarkers for the objective assessment of dental anxiety.

In this context, salivary opiorphin has gained significant attention regarding its role in pain and stress modulation. As an endogenous human peptide, opiorphin regulates the bioavailability of enkephalins and exhibits potent analgesic and antidepressant-like properties through the modulation of endogenous opioid-dependent pathways [12]. Previous investigations have demonstrated that salivary opiorphin levels increase in response to inflammation-mediated dental pain, showing a positive correlation with perceived pain intensity [12–14]. Despite these findings, the potential psychological modulatory effects of opiorphin in humans remain largely unexplored. Human salivary opiorphin concentrations range from 2.8 to 25.9 ng/mL, representing the highest concentrations found among body fluids [15].

To the best of our knowledge, the concentration of opiorphin in individuals experiencing dental anxiety has not yet been investigated. Therefore, the present study represents an initial effort to evaluate salivary opiorphin as a potential biomarker in this context. The primary objectives of this investigation were to quantify salivary opiorphin levels in patients undergoing routine dental examinations and to assess their correlation with anxiety levels as measured by Corah’s Dental Anxiety Scale. Furthermore, the study sought to examine the relationship between salivary opiorphin and the established SNS biomarker, salivary alpha-amylase.

Method

The preliminary investigations regarding salivary opiorphin had already been performed. The establishment of salivary opiorphin as a biomarker for detecting anxiety levels in patients was confirmed in the current study. Ethical clearance for the study was obtained from the Institutional Ethical Committee (Ref. No. ETHICS/ABSMIDS/646/2025), AB Shetty Memorial Institute of Dental Sciences, Nitte DU. The research was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki as revised in 2013 and in compliance with applicable national and institutional guidelines for research involving human participants. Informed consent to participate was obtained from all of the participants in the study.

Sample size calculation: To detect a clinically important difference of at least 3ng/mL with 80% power and 5% level of significance, a minimum of 56 participants were recruited for the study. Informed consent was obtained from each participant.

Inclusion CriteriaPatients aged 20–60 years who reported to the Department of Conservative Dentistry and Endodontics for a routine dental check-up were included in the study.Exclusion CriteriaChronic health conditions: Individuals with ongoing medical problems that could affect saliva composition or anxiety levels.Medications affecting saliva or anxiety: Those taking drugs known to influence saliva production (e.g., antihistamines) or anxiety levels (e.g., antidepressants).Oral health concerns: Individuals with dry mouth, salivary gland dysfunction, recent pulpal pain, periodontal disease, or recent medication use for these conditions (within 24 h).Hormonal fluctuations: Pregnant women, those on oral contraceptives, and women currently undergoing menstruation were excluded to minimize the potential hormonal influences on saliva composition and anxiety levels.Medication use: Patients using inhaled steroids were excluded.

The recruited participants were briefed about the study and informed consent was obtained. Participants were initially instructed to rinse their mouths thoroughly with an alcohol-free mouthwash to minimize the risk of sample contamination. Following this, unstimulated whole saliva was collected using the spitting method, wherein participants were asked to allow saliva to accumulate in the mouth and then gently spit into a sterile collection tube (Tarsons Centrifuge tube 15mL) over a defined period. The collected samples were immediately transferred to the laboratory, where they were centrifuged to separate the clear supernatant from cellular debris and other particulate matter. The resulting supernatant was carefully aliquoted and stored at − 80 °C until further biochemical analysis to ensure the stability of the analytes.

A brief structured questionnaire was administered to obtain each participant’s relevant medical and dental history, including information about any systemic conditions or medications that could influence salivary composition. A thorough clinical examination of the oral cavity was conducted to assess overall oral health status and ensure participants met the inclusion criteria. To assess dental anxiety, Corah’s Dental Anxiety Scale (DAS) [16] was employed, which provided a quantitative measure and categorical classification of anxiety levels among participants.

Biomarker analysis

The salivary opiorphin concentration was quantified using a commercially available enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) kit specifically designed for human opiorphin detection (ELK9764, Human OPI ELISA Kit). Similarly, the activity of salivary alpha-amylase was measured using a Human AMY1 (amylase alpha 1, salivary) ELISA kit (ELK1995) (Fig. 1). All assays were performed in accordance with the manufacturer’s instructions, and the optical density was recorded using a microplate reader to determine biomarker concentrations.

Discussion

The Dental Anxiety Scale (DAS) questionnaire serves as a subjective tool for assessing dental anxiety. However, its reliability may be limited in patients who are unable to provide consistent responses. Consequently, research has focused on identifying objective biomarkers that can accurately and conveniently reflect both psychological and biological stress, offering a valuable alternative to traditional self-report measures [17].

Saliva serves as a valuable, non-invasive diagnostic fluid for detecting biomarkers of orofacial and systemic conditions, including pain. Its ease of collection and established research history make it a convenient and reliable medium for clinical assessment [18]. Among the most commonly studied salivary biomarkers of stress are cortisol, lysozyme, salivary alpha-amylase (sAA), and chromogranin A (CgA). However, these markers present certain limitations, including variability due to individual physiological differences and external factors such as time of day, diet, and stressor type. Moreover, several of these biomarkers, particularly cortisol and sAA, exhibit pronounced circadian rhythms, making the timing of sample collection a critical factor that can affect result reliability [19]. These drawbacks highlight the need for exploring alternative biomarkers with more stable expression and stronger correlation to psychological stress.

A pioneering study by Wisner and Rougeot et al. in was the first to identify Opiorphin as a natural human peptide that inhibits enkephalin-degrading zinc ectopeptidases, thereby enhancing the body’s endogenous opioid signalling [15]. By demonstrating that Opiorphin produces morphine-like analgesic effects without direct receptor activation, the research opened a groundbreaking avenue for developing safer, physiology-based pain therapies. Opiorphin is a naturally occurring and potent bioactive endogenous pentapeptide with the amino acid sequence Gln-Arg-Phe-Ser-Arg [15, 20]. Both in vitro and in vivo studies have demonstrated that opiorphin binds to and inhibits the enzymes human aminopeptidase and human neprilysin, thereby modulating pain perception and stress-related biochemical pathways [21]. In orofacial research, numerous studies have investigated variations in salivary opiorphin levels under different physiological and pathological conditions associated with stress and pain. Its potential as a biomarker has been explored in several conditions, including Temporomandibular Disorders, Burning Mouth Syndrome, Oral Potentially Malignant Disorders, and dental pain entities such as Symptomatic Irreversible Pulpitis and Symptomatic Apical Periodontitis [22–26].

Beyond its analgesic properties, opiorphin exhibits several pharmacological effects, including antidepressant, anti-inflammatory, anticancer, and erectile function–enhancing activities [20]. In this study, we explored the potential antidepressant effect of opiorphin by measuring its salivary levels in individuals without any existing systemic or oral diseases. This served as a baseline investigation and represents the first study to examine salivary opiorphin in this specific context.

Javelot et al. reported that peripheral administration of opiorphin produced an antidepressive and anxiolytic-like effect, mediated selectively through µ-opioid receptor-dependent pathways, without affecting locomotor activity in non-habituated mice [27]. Similarly, Yang et al. found that central administration of opiorphin elicited anti-depressive effects by indirectly activating both µ- and δ-opioid receptors in mice. These findings suggested that opiorphin may modulate anxiety-related behaviors through opioid receptor–mediated mechanisms while maintaining normal motor function [28].

The present study found a strong and statistically significant positive correlation between dental anxiety scores and salivary opiorphin levels, indicating that opiorphin secretion increases with rising anxiety levels. This suggested that opiorphin may play a role in the physiological stress response associated with dental anxiety, potentially reflecting its involvement in both pain modulation and emotional regulation pathways.

The positive association between anxiety and elevated opiorphin levels may be explained through the framework of stress-induced analgesia (SIA). Opiorphin acts by inhibiting the breakdown of endogenous enkephalins, thereby intensifying opioid receptor signaling [15]. In high-anxiety states, the activation of the body’s stress-response systems often triggers a temporary reduction in pain sensitivity as an adaptive survival mechanism [29]. Our findings suggest that elevated opiorphin may serve as a biochemical mediator in this process, where acute psychological distress prompts an up-regulation of opiorphin to preserve endogenous opioid stability and dampen potential pain perception during periods of high arousal.

Conversely, no significant association was observed between salivary opiorphin and alpha-amylase levels, implying that these biomarkers may represent distinct aspects of the stress response with opiorphin being more closely linked to emotional or psychological stress [20], while alpha-amylase reflects sympathetic nervous system activation [8]. These findings highlighted the potential of opiorphin as a novel, non-invasive biomarker for assessing dental anxiety, warranting further investigation in larger and more diverse populations.

Limitations of the study

Although the study provides valuable preliminary insights, certain aspects could be improved in future research. As previously noted, sample collection was not restricted to a specific time of day. This is a critical limitation for salivary alpha-amylase (sAA), which follows a strong diurnal rhythm (typically rising steadily throughout the day). This circadian variation likely introduced significant physiological ‘noise’ that was independent of the participants’ anxiety levels. Such variance may have masked the correlation between sAA and anxiety, explaining the null findings. To minimize these confounding effects, future studies should standardize collection within a narrow time window.

In addition, gender- and age-related differences were not specifically analyzed, though the study sample was representative of the general adult population. Future studies could benefit from standardized sampling times and subgroup analyses to further strengthen the understanding of salivary opiorphin dynamics concerning anxiety.

Finally, this study was not prospectively registered in a clinical trials database. While registration was not mandatory given the observational, non-interventional design of the research, we acknowledge that pre-registration is an emerging best practice for enhancing transparency and reducing reporting bias in human subject research.

Conclusion and future perspectives

The current study revealed a strong and statistically significant positive correlation between salivary opiorphin levels and dental anxiety. This indicates that opiorphin secretion increases in proportion to the intensity of anxiety. In contrast, no significant association was observed between opiorphin and salivary alpha-amylase, suggesting that these biomarkers reflect distinct aspects of the stress response. These findings underscore the potential of salivary opiorphin as a new, non-invasive biomarker for assessing dental anxiety, providing an objective complement to traditional psychological scales.

Future research should aim to validate these results in larger and more diverse populations and examine the responsiveness of opiorphin across varying levels of dental and general anxiety. Longitudinal and interventional studies may further elucidate whether salivary opiorphin can serve as a predictive marker for stress-related disorders or treatment outcomes. Additionally, standardizing sampling protocols and evaluating diurnal variations would enhance the reliability of opiorphin-based assessments in clinical settings.
