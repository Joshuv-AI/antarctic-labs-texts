Premature Ventricular Contractions as a Result of Subacute Myocarditis in a Professional Football Player
Authors: Charlotte Pirquet, Daniel Underberg, Brian Osler, Derek Schwabe-Warf, David Shipon

History of Presentation

A 33-year-old male professional football player was referred to the cardiologist by his team physician for clearance to play in the setting of premature ventricular contractions (PVCs) on electrocardiogram (ECG) as well as atypical chest pain. His chest pain was fleeting, sharp, and not reproducible. He had a few episodes of lightheadedness and palpitations with physical activity; no syncope, shortness of breath, or recent illness. His physical examination was unrevealing, including no murmurs, jugular venous distension, or edema. He smoked marijuana socially and did not use stimulants.

•This case highlights the importance of shared decision-making among the patient, sports cardiologist, team physician, and medical staff in return-to-play recommendations for athletes with arrhythmias and suspected myocarditis, especially when real-world return-to-play decisions often do not align with diagnostic pathways.

Past Medical History

His only previous diagnosis was PVCs. They were first diagnosed in 2019 with a reportedly negative work-up, though the records were unavailable. On chart review, he tested positive for Epstein-Barr virus for a recovered infection. His family history was significant for coronary artery disease in his father, diagnosed in his 70s. There was no family history of sudden cardiac death or cardiomyopathy.

Differential Diagnosis

The differential diagnosis for atypical chest pain and PVCs on ECG in an athlete includes hypertrophic cardiomyopathy, myocarditis, acute/chronic sarcoidosis, arrhythmogenic cardiomyopathy (ACM), coronary artery disease, anomalous coronary artery disease, electrolyte disturbances, and stimulant use.

Investigations

Laboratory testing was significant for high-sensitivity troponin of 8, N-terminal pro–B-type natriuretic peptide <36, cardiac C-reactive protein of 0.60, negative anticardiolipin antibodies, normal serum metanephrines, negative antinuclear antibodies, normal IL-6, normal erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and negative Lyme antibodies. ECG showed small Q waves in leads II, III, and aVF that did not meet criteria for positivity; fragmented R waves in leads I, aVL, and III; and occasional PVCs without high-risk features (Figure 1). Exercise echocardiogram stress testing was negative for ischemia or infarction and showed occasional PVCs and a decreased PVC burden at peak heart rate. There was >40 ms between the peak of the T wave and PVCs (Figures 2 and 3). A Holter monitor was worn for 18 hours and showed 4% PVCs, occasional ventricular triplets, and no PVCs on T waves. Transthoracic echocardiography showed normal biventricular size, function, and wall thickness and no segmental wall motion abnormalities; normal global longitudinal strain of −21.9%; and no valvular abnormalities.

Given his concerning cardiac symptoms and known arrhythmia, cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) was ordered to rule out structural changes not seen on transthoracic echocardiography. CMR showed late gadolinium enhancement (LGE) throughout the anterolateral and inferolateral walls, with corresponding mild regional hypokinesis, probable basal left ventricular myocardial edema, and mildly reduced left ventricular ejection fraction (48%) (Figure 4). Because these findings suggested an infiltrative or inflammatory process, a cardiac positron emission tomography (PET) scan was obtained. It showed no evidence of active myocardial inflammation, helping to rule out active sarcoidosis, and suggested a diagnosis of subacute myocarditis; however, chronic sarcoidosis could not be fully ruled out (Figure 5). His laboratory findings, particularly the negative C-reactive protein, troponin, IL-6, and B-type natriuretic peptide, indicated there was no active inflammation in the areas of LGE on CMR. With LGE and mild edema on CMR and negative PET scan, his findings were consistent with subacute myocarditis likely due to remote Epstein-Barr virus infection.

Management

The patient was initiated on guideline-directed medical therapy (GDMT) with metoprolol, losartan, and dapagliflozin.

Outcome and Follow-Up

There was thorough discussion with the patient and his team physician about the risks of PVCs during high-intensity exercise. Through shared decision-making (SDM), the patient continued to play after the reassuring stress test, with CMR pending. When the CMR results were known, he was started on GDMT. Discussion noted that myocarditis was the most likely etiology but that ACM, chronic sarcoidosis, and an early idiopathic cardiomyopathy could not be ruled out. Although high-intensity exercise is a common trigger for inflammation in ACM, the stress ECG findings and playing without cardiac symptoms for several years with known PVCs made ACM less likely. Genetic testing was offered, but the patient deferred to subsequent follow-up. He could have had healed myocarditis, but LGE suggested subacute was more likely. Early idiopathic cardiomyopathy could not be ruled out with the available data. It was concluded the most likely diagnosis was subacute myocarditis due to a remote viral infection, with persistent edema from continuing to play. After a collaborative discussion led by his team sports cardiologist in partnership with the team physician and medical staff, the patient elected to rest for 2 to 3 months on GDMT and follow up with repeat CMR and genetic testing. The patient played without any complications, retired at the end of the season, and has not yet had follow up.

Discussion

This case underscores the complexity of return-to-play (RTP) decisions in elite athletes with myocarditis and ventricular arrhythmias, particularly when the diagnosis is probable but not definitive. Given the presence of LGE on CMR, this patient is in a higher risk category, however, his PET scan and laboratory tests ruled out active inflammation. Current guidelines advise against RTP for at least 3 to 6 months and until there is resolution of LGE on CMR for patients with myocarditis.1 There is no research comparing outcomes between athletes with subacute and acute myocarditis.

The guidelines do not specify by sport, and they emphasize tailoring the recommendations to the individual's clinical recovery and objective testing. Notably, the guidelines acknowledge the gap in literature and are largely determined by expert deliberation.1 As highlighted in a recent American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology statement on RTP for athletes with cardiovascular abnormalities, RTP decisions should not rely solely on rigid timelines but instead incorporate individualized risk stratification, athlete preferences, and an SDM framework.2 This approach acknowledges the professional and psychological impact of prolonged restriction from participation in sports while balancing risk factors.

In examining this patient's risk profile, the literature has identified high-risk features on CMR. A meta-analysis of 11 studies found that the presence of LGE on baseline CMR was associated with an increased risk of all-cause mortality, cardiac mortality, and major adverse cardiac events (MACE) in acute myocarditis.3 A left ventricular subepicardial/midmyocardial stria pattern has been shown to be associated with life-threatening arrhythmias and sudden cardiac death, which was not present in this patient.4 Additionally, a <50% decrease in LGE or increased LGE on follow-up CMR is associated with poor prognosis.5 Although he had edema on CMR and did not yet have a repeat study, his PET scan and laboratory work-up were negative for signs of active inflammation.

Another approach to examining this patient's risk is with his ECG findings. Fragmented R waves are associated with LGE. The combination of any ECG abnormality and LGE on CMR is associated with increased risk of MACE.6 Fragmented QRS complexes have been shown to have poor sensitivity and high specificity (63%-96%) for myocardial scar, with uncertain prognostic implications.7 Polymorphic and irregular ventricular arrhythmias are associated with active myocarditis and increased risk of fatal arrhythmias whereas monomorphic ventricular arrhythmias, such as in this patient, are associated with healed myocarditis and a consequent lower risk of fatal arrhythmias.8 The patient's PVCs decreased at maximum heart rate on exercise stress testing, suggesting his PVCs are not related to structural heart disease.9 He had T waves >40 ms from the QRS and 4% PVC burden on Holter monitoring, which are low risk for arrhythmogenicity and cardiomyopathy, respectively. Although he had fragmented R waves and LGE on CMR, putting him at increased risk of arrhythmias, his stress test and PVC characteristics were reassuring.

Genetic testing is indicated in this patient to complete risk stratification. Genetic testing was recommended to the patient, as some variants, such as LMNA, DSP, FLNC, and TMEM43, have increased risk of ventricular arrhythmias and sudden death.10 His negative family history, playing for several years without complication, and imaging findings nonspecific for an inherited cardiomyopathy made an inherited cardiomyopathy less likely, but it still needed to be ruled out to complete the evaluation. Through SDM, the patient elected to defer genetic testing to subsequent follow-up.

Overall, this patient is at increased risk of MACE from his ECG abnormalities and CMR findings of myocardial edema, whereas his exercise stress test results, PET scan, laboratory work-up, and PVC features were associated with a good prognosis. In this patient, arrhythmogenicity was thoroughly evaluated and findings were reassuring. The absence of contraindications for RTP, limited evidence guiding management, and an uncertain diagnosis underscore the role of risk stratification and SDM. For professional athletes, RTP decisions must integrate clinical risk, imaging, electrophysiologic findings, and the broader context of career and quality of life. This case highlights how contemporary guidelines and emerging literature support a nuanced, patient-centered approach, emphasizing the role of the sports cardiologist in navigating indeterminate pathology.

Conclusions

The choice to return to play in athletes is a complex decision integrating clinical findings, imaging, electrophysiology, and the patient's career and quality of life. Because the guidelines are based on limited evidence, SDM plays a more central role in managing athletes with suspected myocarditis, especially when the diagnosis is not confirmed. Further research is needed to investigate more precise RTP recommendations and prognostication of ECG and CMR findings.
