Expanded nursing roles for person-centred care for people with cognitive impairment in hospitals: developing a complex intervention using a multi-method-approach
Authors: Marcelina Roos, Verena von der Lühe, Sophie Peter, Mareike Löbberding, Nadine Scholten, Sascha Köpke, Martin Nikolaus Dichter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundPeople with cognitive impairment face several challenges during hospitalisation. Insufficient attention to person-centred care often means that individual needs are not recognised, which can lead to complications and adverse outcomes such as falls and a decrease in the nutritional status. To promote person-centred care on hospital wards, nurses acting as change agents possessing both clinical expertise and leadership skills are a key factor. We aimed to develop a complex person-centred care intervention for people with cognitive impairment delivered by Expanded Practice Nurses in acute care hospitals and to derive tailored implementation strategies.MethodsThis study used a multi-method-design. We performed two systematic reviews aiming to synthesise existing evidence on advanced nursing roles and factors influencing role implementation. To explore the setting and target groups, we conducted two surveys on the scope of practice of Expanded Practice Nurses and on attitudes of health care professionals towards advanced nursing roles in Germany. The results guided two expert workshops to model the intervention and prioritise implementation strategies using participatory principles. We created a logic model to depict underlying processes and contextual factors of the developed complex intervention.ResultsFourteen intervention components form the complex intervention and reflect the tasks of the Expanded Practice Nurse within the interprofessional team. We defined nine components directly related to patients, five system-related tasks to promote person-centred care at an organisational level, and described opportunities for adapting the role to different contexts. Implementation strategies target qualifying the Expanded Practice Nurse, engaging the interprofessional team and people with cognitive impairment, as well as familiarising with local relationships and structures.ConclusionsThe comprehensive intervention development results in a detailed role description as well as implementation strategies allowing adaptation to different acute care contexts, especially in countries in an early stage of development of Advanced Practice Nursing. The developed nursing role is a key to promote person-centred care in the interprofessional team. By developing a person-centred culture on the wards, individual needs of people with cognitive impairment can be identified and addressed in time, which can lead to a reduction in complications and an improved hospital experience for patients.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12913-026-14685-x.Keywords: Acute care, Advanced practice nursing, Advanced practice nurses, Complex intervention, Delirium, Dementia, Hospitals, Multi-method, Neurocognitive disorders, Nurse's role

Background

About 40% of older people admitted to an acute care hospital show cognitive decline, with dementia and delirium being the most common causes [1]. Research has demonstrated that cognitive impairment during hospitalisation is associated with adverse outcomes. After hospital admission, people with cognitive impairment enter an environment in which task-orientated processes and priorities are often in contrast to their personal needs as demonstrated by unnecessary bed moves, inadequate pain management, inappropriate catheterisation, and poor communication [2]. Research investigating the perspective of people with cognitive impairment shows that their need for care and treatment, recognition as a person, and meaningful interactions is often only partially met [3–5]. They are disrupted from their routines and seek to comply with hospital procedures, having difficulties structuring their day and feeling bored and frustrated [3–5]. Relatives perceive the person with cognitive impairment as particularly vulnerable during the stay and fear that the person will not be able to express their needs or that these will not be heard [3]. Relatives therefore have an increased need for information and communication [3–5] but find it difficult to reach the responsible staff and sometimes feel disruptive and disregarded in seeking contact [3, 5]. Healthcare professionals themselves report a lack of knowledge, skills, and resources to sufficiently address the needs of people with cognitive impairment, leading to staff frustration, distress, and dissatisfaction with current care [6]. Additionally, negative attitudes towards people with cognitive impairment are common among hospital staff [7]. Evidence shows that the failure to recognise and address the specific needs of people with cognitive impairment results in an increased prevalence of behavioural and psychological symptoms, such as agitation and aggression, as well as functional and further cognitive decline. In addition, there is an increased risk of adverse events and complications during hospitalisation, such as malnutrition, pressure ulcers, falls, and fractures [2]. As a result, people with cognitive impairment have significantly longer hospital stays [2], higher rates of hospital readmission [8], and an increased risk of death compared with people without cognitive impairment [2].

In the last decades, the concept of person-centred care has received increased attention and is now considered as a key in improving the care of people with cognitive impairment in acute care [9]. Person-centred care represents different approaches and practices in which the person is placed at the centre of care. There are several definitions, frameworks, and terminologies that are continuously being revised and refined [10]. A recent scoping review explored the conceptualisation of ‘centredness’ and highlighted nine core elements with ‘sharing responsibility’, ‘therapeutic relationship’, and ‘patient as a person’ being the most common [11]. Additionally, Mohr et al. [12] identified nine key categories of person-centred interventions for people with dementia in a systematic review. Intervention components included ‘social contact’, ‘physical activities’, ‘cognitive training’, ‘sensory enhancement’, ‘daily living assistance’, ‘life history oriented emotional support’, ‘training and support for (professional) caregivers’, ‘environmental adjustments’, and ‘care organisation’. Person-centred care is considered as a core component of healthcare professionals’ skills and recommended in several national and international strategies and guidelines [13–15]. Evidence suggests that person-centred care contributes to improved patient and healthcare professional experiences and care quality as well as reduced healthcare costs [9]. However, previous research highlights a lack of studies on person-centred care interventions for people with cognitive impairment in the hospital setting [12]. Existing studies, conducted across various healthcare contexts, report several challenges in implementing person-centred care, leading to a gap between theory and practice [16, 17]. Findings suggest that cultural change at system level is required [9, 18] and that the presence of so-called ‘change agents’ is a key factor when successfully introducing person-centred care [19]. Change agents act as mentors and role models in daily practice. They are characterised by an organisational authority and clinical expertise in a specific area of care [19]. There is a large overlap between the change agent role and the core competencies of Advanced Practice Nurses [20], making the introduction of nurses in advanced roles a promising strategy for implementing person-centred care.

In many countries, Advanced Practice Nurses have been evolved in response to growing needs and complexity in health care [21]. The International Council of Nurses defines an Advanced Practice Nurse as either a generalist or specialised nurse who has acquired, through additional graduate education at a minimum of a master’s degree, an expert knowledge base, skills for complex decision-making, and clinical competencies [22]. Evidence shows that the care provided by Advanced Practice Nurses is associated with significant improvements in patient outcomes across various patient populations and healthcare settings. In addition, studies demonstrate that Advanced Practice Nursing contributes to innovation and improvement at organisational and system levels while being cost-effective [23–25]. Despite the value of Advanced Practice Nurses to health systems, the development of advanced nursing roles varies across the globe. While Advanced Practice Nurses are common and play fundamental roles in countries such as England and the USA, several other countries are in the early stages of role development [26, 27]. One of these countries is Germany [28], where different initiatives have been launched to promote the introduction of Advanced Practice Nurses (§ 63 3c Social Care Act V; § 64d Social Care Act V [29]). However, these efforts face a variety of legislative, professional, and structural barriers in the German health system [30]. Currently, bachelor-qualified Expanded Practice Nurses with clearly defined areas of responsibility are increasingly deployed as an intermediate stage in the transition to Advanced Practice Nursing [28, 31].

With the ENROLE-acute (Expanded Nursing ROLEs for person-centred care for people with cognitive impairment in ACUTE care) project, we focus on the development and evaluation of a complex person-centred care intervention for people with cognitive impairment in acute care by introducing Expanded Practice Nurses (ISRCTN81391868; date of registration: 12.06.2023) [32].

In this article, we describe the development process of the complex intervention based on theoretical frameworks. A special focus is on how the results of the individual sub-studies have driven the development process rather than presenting detailed results of the sub-studies. In doing so, we draw on the current recommendations for developing complex interventions and their reporting [33, 34].

Methods

Overall study design

The study integrates both existing evidence and stakeholders’ perspectives in a multi-method design using participatory principles. As a theoretical basis, we followed the Medical Research Council’s guidance for developing and evaluating complex interventions [34, 35]. The guidance proposes the phases of ‘intervention development or identification’, ‘feasibility’, ‘evaluation’, and ‘implementation’ as well as core elements to be considered in all phases of the process. This paper addresses key actions of the intervention development phase, focusing on the following core elements: ‘consideration of context’, ‘development of programme theory’, ‘stakeholder engagement’, ‘identification of uncertainties’, and ‘refinement of the intervention’. We did not address the element ‘economic considerations’ systematically in this phase. We also applied the PEPPA framework (participatory, evidence-based, patient-focused process for Advanced Practice Nursing role development, implementation, an evaluation), focussing on steps one to six: (1) ‘define patient population and describe current model of care’, (2) ‘identify stakeholders and recruit participants’, (3) ‘determine need for a new model of care’, (4) ‘identify priority problems and goals to improve model of care’, (5) ‘define new model of care and Advanced Practice Nurse role’, and (6) ‘plan implementation strategies’ [36]. This paper adheres to guidance for reporting intervention development studies in health research (GUIDED, see Supplementary material Table 1) [33].

Initially, we formed a project advisory board consisting of representatives from all relevant stakeholder groups, who advised us on both methods and content. The advisory board consisted of 15 members: stakeholders with knowledge of local structures and external experts with knowledge of the research topic. Members represented the fields of nursing management, nursing development, medicine (psychiatry, neurology, and geriatrics), Advanced Practice Nursing, person-centred care, representatives of people with cognitive impairment, as well as nursing research. The board met three-monthly to discuss the status of the project and next steps.

The intervention development process included three phases. First, we identified evidence by conducting systematic literature reviews on intervention components and factors influencing implementation. Second, we surveyed target groups to gain a comprehensive understanding of the setting and the perspectives of those involved. Third, we used this newly gained knowledge to model the intervention, to derive implementation strategies, and to develop a logic model as a visualisation of the programme theory. For this, we went through iterative cycles in expert workshops and within the research team.

Identifying evidence

Systematic review

Our aim was to systematically review empirical evidence on advanced nursing roles and tasks performed by nurses caring for people with cognitive impairment and their relatives in acute care hospitals. Therefore, we conducted a systematic review (Prospero: CRD42021265157) applying Cochrane methods [37]. We developed a search strategy for each database and searched MEDLINE via PubMed, CINAHL via EBSCOhost, Cochrane Library, ALOIS, Web of Science, and LIVIVO without time restrictions. Additionally, we used Web of Science for citation tracking. Qualitative and quantitative empirical studies published in peer-reviewed journals in English or German were included. Two independent reviewers performed screening and full-text review as well as quality assessment with the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool [38]. For data extraction, we developed standardised tables on study and intervention characteristics [37, 39]. To synthesise the data, we used Hamric and Hanson’s model of Advanced Practice Nursing [20] and clustered tasks in a structured process using the MAXQDA software version 22.0.0 [40]. One reviewer conducted data extraction and data synthesis, which were checked by a second reviewer.

Scoping review

To identify barriers and facilitators to the successful implementation of advanced nursing roles in acute care, we conducted a scoping review of international literature using the Arksey and O’Malley framework [41]. We systematically searched five databases (CINAHL via EBSCO host, Cochrane Library, LIVIVO, MEDLINE via PubMed, and Web of Science) without time limit. In addition, we conducted a forward and backward citation tracking via reference lists and Web of Science. We included empirical studies in German or English, published in peer-reviewed journals, that explicitly report factors influencing the implementation process of advanced nursing roles. Two researchers independently screened identified records in Covidence (www.covidence.org) and assessed methodological quality using the Mixed Methods Appraisal Tool [38]. We extracted and summarised data in tables and carried out a narrative synthesis following the Centre for Reviews and Dissemination’s Guidance for Undertaking Reviews in Health Care [42]. We focused on main study characteristics as well as core elements of advanced nursing roles and categorised barriers and facilitators in MAXQDA version 22.0.0 [40] using the Yorkshire Contributory Framework [43]. Data extraction and analysis were performed by one reviewer and checked by another. We prospectively registered the scoping review in Open Science Framework (registration number: 10.17605/OSF.IO/BQ2T3).

Exploring setting and target groups

Survey I

Our purpose was to gain a better understanding of the development of Advanced Practice Nursing in acute care in Germany. Therefore, we aimed to describe the scope of practice of Expanded Practice Nurses, factors of role development, as well as intra- and interprofessional collaboration in a cross-sectional study using a paper-and-pencil survey. We included Expanded Practice Nurses according to the definition of the Nursing Council of New Zealand [44], who worked in an acute hospital in Germany in direct patient care. We used convenience sampling and comprehensive recruitment strategies to identify and invite potential participants. The questionnaire was composed of three questionnaires on scope of practice of nurses in advanced roles and pretested. The final questionnaire included 79 closed and open-ended items in six domains: ‘role performance’, ‘competence’, ‘role clarity’, ‘type and frequency of performed activities’, ‘intra- and interprofessional collaboration’, and ‘sociodemographic and professional characteristics’ [28]. We conducted primarily descriptive analysis of quantitative data using SPSS version 28.0 [45] and aggregated answers to open-ended questions in a structured process according to Kuckartz and Rädiker [46] using the MAXQDA software version 22.0.0 [40].

Survey II

To investigate the attitudes of intra- and interprofessional team members as a pivotal factor in developing and implementing advanced nursing roles, we conducted a multi-centre cross-sectional study using a paper-and-pencil questionnaire. In a convenience sample of hospitals in Germany, we included all nurses and physicians from selected wards experienced in working with Expanded Practice Nurses. We developed and tested the questionnaire in an iterative process based on Lynn’s methodology [47]. The final questionnaire contained 94 closed and open-ended questions in the domains ‘general perceptions’, ‘knowledge and role clarity’, ‘accepted activities and responsibilities’, ‘qualification requirements’, ‘impact’, ‘perceived professional competence’, ‘collaboration’, ‘barriers and facilitators’, and ‘sociodemographic and professional characteristics’ and will be published elsewhere. We analysed closed questions primarily by means of descriptive statistics using the SPSS software version 28.0 [45] and open-ended questions by a content analysis-based method [46] using the MAXQDA software version 22.0.0 [40].

Modelling intervention components and implementation strategies

Workshops

To model intervention components and implementation strategies, we conducted two one-day online expert workshops based on our findings in the reviews and surveys. The workshop setup was guided by respective studies on the development of complex interventions [48, 49] and is detailed in Supplementary material Table 2. A senior researcher experienced in conducting intervention development workshops led both workshops. For virtual visualisation and collaboration, we used the Mural tool (www.mural.co), an interactive online whiteboard. Potential participants were representatives of people with cognitive impairment and their relatives as well as experts from clinical practice, management, and research with experience in the care of people with cognitive impairment, Advanced Practice Nursing, and complex interventions. For recruitment, we used professional contacts and networks and invited participants through personal request. We audio-recorded and documented both workshops in written form.

The workshops followed a stepwise modelling process. The aim of the first workshop was to develop a common understanding of the project and current care practices for people with cognitive impairment among participants. First, the principal investigator introduced the project and relevant topics, focusing on ‘people with cognitive impairment’, ‘person-centred care’, and ‘Advanced Nursing Practice’, as well as the workshop objectives based on a comprehensive presentation. In the next step, participants developed potential care pathways of people with cognitive impairment in hospitals in small groups using a case example. Results served as a basis for a moderated plenary discussion focusing on targeted approaches to optimise care for people with cognitive impairment and considering person-centred care principles. After the first day, we provided participants with a summary of the workshop findings and invited them to take part in an online survey based on the systematic review results. In the online survey, participants rated and prioritised potential tasks of an Expanded Practice Nurse role aimed at improving the care of people with cognitive impairment by fostering a person-centred culture on hospital wards.

The second workshop took place 14 days after the first. The aims were to discuss the online survey results on the Expanded Practice Nurse role and to identify influencing factors and strategies for successful role implementation. In the first phase, we presented results of the online survey and asked participants to comment on the findings in a moderated plenary discussion to gain a deeper understanding. The second phase based on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR) [50] and the Expert Recommendations for Implementing Change (ERIC) [51]. Both frameworks support the systematic identification of barriers and facilitators as well as tailored strategies for implementing change. After introducing the CFIR by means of a presentation and handout, participants discussed barriers and facilitators in the context of the project in a moderated plenary discussion. Based on the discussion, participants independently selected and rated the CFIR factors according to their relevance for the introduction of the Expanded Practice Nurse role on a structured evaluation sheet. Afterwards, we presented and discussed the results in the group.

After the workshops, we used the online survey results on potential tasks and the related participants’ discussion to develop a first draft of the Expanded Practice Nurse role by selecting most important tasks within the research team and the project advisory board. For role implementation, we compared influencing factors identified by the workshop participants and results of the scoping review on barriers and facilitators. After discussing the comparison in the research team, we entered factors with the highest ranking into an online matching tool (https://cfirguide.org/tools/), which provided recommendations for implementation strategies based on ERIC. We selected the strategies with high ratings and adapted them within the research team and the project advisory board in an iterative way.

Logic model

A logic model represents one core element of complex interventions and graphically depicts the assumed causal pathways between intervention components, mechanisms of change, and outcomes as well as their interaction with the context [34, 35]. It serves to demonstrate how the intervention works and under what circumstances it produces outcomes. In addition, a logic model supports a common understanding of the people involved in the development phase and serves as a guidance to organise empirical data for the subsequent process evaluation [34]. We drew on established literature to design the model [52, 53]. Developing a logic model is a non-linear process that requires several rounds of testing and refinement throughout the development phase [35]. We designed a first draft at the beginning of the project, which we then discussed and adapted within the research team. In further revision rounds, we sequentially incorporated the results of our sub-studies, perspectives of our project advisory board, and feedback from nursing researchers experienced in developing programme theories of complex interventions involving Expanded Practice Nurses into the logic model.

Ethical considerations

We received ethical approval by the Ethics Committee of the German Society of Nursing Science (number: 21–022). As our sub-studies involved participants from various organisations, we decided to obtain approval from a nationally established committee whose ethical review is recognised by multiple institutions. Participation in the surveys and expert workshops was voluntary. All participants had the opportunity to ask questions and gave their written informed consent. Data protection followed the requirements of the General Data Protection Regulation.

Identifying evidence

Systematic review

Our search resulted in 48 studies. We found activities described in all six competence domains of Hamric and Hanson’s Advanced Practice Nursing model [20], with a focus on the central competence ‘direct clinical practice’ as well as ‘guidance and coaching’. In ‘direct clinical practice’, we found tasks related to screening and assessment suggesting that Expanded Practice Nurses play a key role in leading, conducting, and supporting screening and assessment regarding cognitive impairment (e.g. delirium) and other relevant areas for this patient group (e.g. individual preferences). In addition, Expanded Practice Nurses accompany people with cognitive impairment and their relatives in the care process, develop goals and care plans together, and engage to create meaningful relationships. ‘Coaching and guidance’ includes providing information and counselling to patients and their families, such as the development of action plans. Expanded Practice Nurses also provide training for members of the interprofessional team, for which they develop and deliver formal and informal training on various aspects relating to people with cognitive impairment. ‘Consultation’ includes arranging consultations by other professionals to ensure comprehensive care and conducting consultations for staff. In the area of ‘leadership’, the Expanded Practice Nurse acts as a leader on clinical and system level within the hospital. This includes coordinating interdisciplinary rounds and volunteers, developing guidelines or ward specific action plans, and monitoring staff performance. The Expanded Practice Nurse serves as a resource and role model for staff and relatives in the interaction with people with cognitive impairment. The included studies describe interprofessional ‘collaboration’ with various professional groups within and outside the hospital. Tasks relating to ‘ethical decision-making’ are described least frequently. Expanded Practice Nurses carry out Advance Care Planning and support relatives in ethical decisions. As part of ‘research’ tasks, they observe changes in practice, participate in research projects at different levels, and support other nurses in accessing evidence-based knowledge.

Implications for intervention development

All identified activities served as a basis for potential tasks that form the Expanded Practice Nurse role during the expert workshops.

Scoping review

To address the context, we focussed on results of the four studies from Germany for the intervention development. The studies assessed the perspectives of Expanded Practice Nurses (n = 3) as well as patients and other healthcare professionals (n = 1). We found barriers and facilitators which need to be considered for the successful implementation of advanced nursing roles in the three domains ‘situational factors’, ‘local working conditions’, and ‘latent/organisational factors’ of the Yorkshire Contributory Factors Framework [43]. Barriers and facilitators referred most frequently to team factors. These include the extent to which the interdisciplinary team, especially physicians and other nurses, recognise and accept the Expanded Practice Nurse role, have a clear role understanding, and are confident of its positive impact. Another influencing factor is the extent to which the team considers the advanced nursing role’s clinical focus and the initiation of change to be relevant. Barriers related most frequently to the management of staff and staffing levels. Studies reported factors such as a lack of extra time to pursue the advanced nursing role, leading to a lack of time resources and flexibility to handle daily work and meet role expectations. Facilitators were most frequently patient-related. These include patients and relatives recognising the Expanded Practice Nurse role and expressing their positive attitudes towards the role to members of the interdisciplinary team.

The results of the international studies identified will help us to further strengthen our results and derive generalisable conclusions on the implementation of advanced nursing roles.

Implications for intervention development

The factors identified supported the development of implementation strategies after the second expert workshop.

Exploring setting and target groups

Survey I

Of 108 questionnaires sent out, we received 84 back (response rate 77.7%). The participants were on average 41.7 (± 10.5) years old, mostly female (n = 67, 79.8%), and qualified at Master’s level (n = 53, 63.1%). They had 18.1 (± 10.3) years of experience in the nursing profession and 5.0 (± 5.1) years in the Expanded Practice Nurse role.

Participants performed activities in all five Advanced Practice Nursing domains of Hamric and Hanson’s model that we queried. The most common activities were ‘direct clinical practice’ and ‘coaching and guidance’. Participants tended to take on a ‘leadership’ role at the clinical level, e.g. coordinating complex cases or acting as a role model, more often than at the system level, e.g. leading case conferences. ‘Research’ activities were reported more frequently in implementing and utilising research results than in conducting and publishing research. The results show that the participants take on tasks for which they were overqualified in some cases and which do not do justice to their formal qualifications. Participants rated factors of role development such as role clarity, role security, perceived competence, and autonomy of action as overall positive or advanced, which can be interpreted as facilitating in the execution of their role. Existing uncertainty, for example with regard to role clarity, shows a need for continuous reflection on one’s own role and clarification of the role within the institution. Participants perceived intra- and interprofessional collaboration with other nurses and physicians as positive. Participants valued trustful exchange with other Expanded Practice Nurses for their own role development.

Implications for intervention development

We integrated all domains of Hamric and Hanson’s Advanced Practice Nursing model into the new role, specifically focussing on selected domains. We set the focus on ‘direct clinical practice’ as core of the role, stressing ‘clinical leadership’ and ‘guidance and coaching’ within the ward and defining a reduced ‘research’ component. We sought to provide opportunities for exchange with other Expanded Practice Nurses and for individual role reflection. To promote role clarity within the hospital, we worked closely with the nursing management, the nursing development unit, and ward managers in the project at an early stage. Finally, we planned to carefully monitor factors of role development during the intervention phase.

Survey II

We focussed on the results from one large university hospital in the western part of Germany for the intervention development. 59 nurses participated in the study. Despite extensive recruitment efforts, we were unable to recruit physicians. On average, nurses were 39.7 (± 12.5) years old, mostly female (n = 39, 67.2%), and had 16 (± 12.3) years of professional experience. All participants had completed at least three years of vocational training and 10.9% (n = 6) held an academic degree. Most nurses (n = 23, 41.8%) stated that Expanded Practice Nurses are responsible for but not based on their ward.

Overall, the attitudes towards Expanded Practice Nurses were highly positive. Most nurses appreciated the introduction of advanced nursing roles and rated them as a valuable resource for patients and their own work. Participants considered almost all the proposed Expanded Practice Nurses activities in the domains of Hamric and Hanson’s model to be appropriate. Only ratings of activities traditionally allocated to physicians’ responsibilities in the German healthcare system, such as ordering imaging and expert consultations and prescribing medication, showed moderate agreement. At the same time, nurses supported a dynamic role development with an expansion of Expanded Practice Nurses activities with increasing professional experience and qualification. In contrast, the ratings of the success of the implementation of Expanded Practice Nurses were mixed. Respondents noted that Expanded Practice Nurse roles and responsibilities are not clearly defined and that they have limited knowledge of the related activities.

To further strengthen our results and derive generalisable conclusions on attitudes towards advanced nursing roles, we are currently conducting our survey in other hospitals in Germany.

Implications for intervention development

We concluded to focus on achieving role understanding by informing both team leaders and team members as concretely and practically as possible about the new role and demonstrating the link to their daily practice. To promote team proximity, we allocated the new Expanded Practice Nurses role to a ward as a permanent team member and prepared the Expanded Practice Nurses in advance on how to explain their role to others and involve team members in change processes. In addition, we reflected on the defined intervention components based on the acceptability rating. Where the results indicated less acceptability, e.g. for initiating expert consultations, we developed specific implementation strategies in the form of intensified information. Finally, we enabled a dynamic role development with the possibility to expand activities with increasing professional experience and qualification by adding optional components and a low-threshold definition of selected Expanded Practice Nurses’ tasks.

Modelling intervention components and implementation strategies

Workshops

A total of 11 experts participated in the workshops, representing the perspectives of nursing development (n = 2), nursing management (n = 2), nursing science (n = 2), nurses in advanced roles (n = 4), and people with cognitive impairment (n = 1). Due to scheduling conflicts, we were unable to recruit physicians. Participants were 55.3 (± 6.2) years old and mostly female (n = 8, 72.7%).

Description of current care practices and opportunities for improvement (workshop 1): The workshop participants’ discussion resulted in ten categories: (1) a shared basic understanding for the care of people with cognitive impairment, (2) people with cognitive impairments’ involvement in care processes, (3) relatives’ involvement in care processes, (4) addressing the care of people with cognitive impairment in the interprofessional team, (5) standards for screening and assessment, (6) conduct of appropriate measures, (7) provision of specialised services tailored to individual needs, (8) organisation of care structures, (9) shaping the environment, and (10) structured discharge planning. The clusters served as a common basis for the rating on possible intervention components in workshop 2.

Rating and prioritisation of potential tasks of an Expanded Practice Nurse (after workshop 1 and workshop 2)

The online survey results indicated that the experts rated most Expanded Practice Nurse tasks extracted from the literature as relevant, with priorities emerging during the subsequent discussion.

In terms of ‘direct clinical practice’, workshop participants prioritised the development of care plans and relationship building with patients and relatives. In addition, they described a high relevance for conducting tailored interventions to address needs. However, participants considered it unrealistic for the Expanded Practice Nurse to personally conduct every intervention and therefore described delegating and promoting the implementation of tailored interventions in daily practice as key tasks. According to the online rating, screening and assessment were less of a priority. Participants argued that screening is a team task and that the Expanded Practice Nurses should only conduct a comprehensive assessment when a need is indicated. Tasks traditionally associated with physicians’ responsibilities, such as reviewing and administering medication or checking laboratory tests, had a lower priority.

Concerning ‘guidance and coaching’, participants rated providing expert advice, offering training for patients, relatives, and colleagues, and being informed about existing training programmes as a high priority.

In terms of ‘consultation’, workshop participants described both conducting consultations by the Expanded Practice Nurse and initiating consultations by other experts as important tasks.

In terms of ‘leadership’, participants rated being a resource and role model as a major priority. In addition, they described working with procedural instructions as important on condition that the need exists. Participants considered monitoring the implementation of measures as relevant, in contrast to the general monitoring of colleagues’ work. According to the participants, coordination tasks were less relevant, as these are often time-consuming and do not necessarily have to be conducted by nurses in advanced roles.

Participants also rated ‘collaboration’ with other people involved in care as well as the scientific team as a major priority. They described the need for the Expanded Practice Nurse having a mediating role. In contrast, participants stated collaboration with primary care not as a priority when introducing the new role.

For ‘ethical decision making’, the rating resulted in a high priority in supporting relatives and a lower relevance in dealing with conflicts within the team.

Overall, participants rated ‘research’ as less important than the other core competencies. One priority was the monitoring and evaluation of change in clinical practice. However, transferring evidence-based knowledge and supporting research projects were less important. Participants explained that basics for evidence-based practice, such as colleagues’ interest and knowledge, are often still lacking.

Selection and prioritisation of influencing factors and strategies for role implementation (workshop 2 and after workshop 2)

We used the highest rated construct in each of the five CFIR domains and the ten highest-rated expert recommendations derived. The comparison with the influencing factors identified in the scoping review did not lead to any changes. However, the scoping review results informed the subsequent development of the expert recommendations for deriving implementation strategies. The constructs and recommendations are illustrated in Supplementary material Table 3.

Implications for intervention development

We concluded that the Expanded Practice Nurse role needs to combine both patient- and system-related activities. While patient-related activities relate to the care of individual patients, system-related activities are located at an organisational level. The categories (workshop 1) and the online survey results (after workshop 1 and workshop 2) served as a first draft for relevant Expanded Practice Nurse tasks at both levels. According to participants’ comments, we focused on tasks specifically addressing the care of our target group rather than general challenges in care. Finally, we concluded that there are tasks relating to the Expanded Practice Nurse’s responsibility, but also activities relating to the team. In terms of implementation, we used the expert recommendations (after workshop 2) as a basis for developing a first draft of tailored implementation strategies.

Logic model

Figure 2 illustrates the logic model with the four main elements ‘intervention’, ‘implementation’, ‘mechanisms of change’, and ‘outcomes’, that are embedded in the ‘context’ [52]. The mechanisms of change are intermediate steps that we believe are crucial to achieving the defined primary and secondary outcomes. The context contains influencing factors that are not directly addressed by the intervention, organised in the micro, meso, and macro level system.

Logic model (Supplementary material Fig. 2)

Overall, the logic model illustrates our assumption that by promoting person-centred care through an Expanded Practice Nurse, the individual needs of people with cognitive impairment are recognised and addressed. This leads to a reduction or avoidance of adverse events and subsequently to a decrease in the length of hospital stay. To test our assumptions, we will examine initial effects, processes, and preliminary cost outcomes in a future feasibility study.

Intervention components

We defined 14 intervention components that reflect the tasks of Expanded Practice Nurses and members of the interprofessional team. The components are illustrated in Fig. 3, where the tasks are based on Hamric and Hanson’s Advanced Practice Nursing model [20].

Intervention components (Supplementary material Fig. 3)

We integrated all domains in the role to enable sustainable further development of the role beyond the project. Supplementary material Table 4 provides a detailed description and examples of the individual components. Components include mandatory core components (no. 1–12) and adaptable peripheral components (no. 13–14). All intervention components are designed with flexibility from the outset. For example, the expanded nursing assessment component offers a variety of instruments for identifying and screening cognitive impairment or individual needs. This gives Expanded Practice Nurses the opportunity to select the most suitable tools for their specific context, allowing them to tailor the assessment to meet the unique needs of the ward. While most components are patient-related (no. 1–9), Expanded Practice Nurses also take on tasks at a system level, which indirectly influence the care of people with cognitive impairment (no. 10–14). We adapted the components to the context of a university hospital, for example by integrating the existing electronic system for the screening and expanded nursing assessment components.

In addition, we defined basic conditions for the Expanded Practice Nurse role to ensure the degree and quality of implementation. The profile requires a nurse that is qualified to at least level six of the European Qualifications Framework (Bachelor’s level or equivalent) [55]. Expanded Practice Nurses should have strong communication and interaction skills and be enthusiastic about person-centred care for people with cognitive impairment. In addition, we expected Expanded Practice Nurses to have good English language skills. We assumed that Expanded Practice Nurses must possess a minimum position of 75% and be released from the ward routine for two working days per month to facilitate role execution. For each intervention ward, we planned to provide two Expanded Practice Nurses.

Implementation strategies

For further information on the implementation strategies, please see Supplementary material Table 5. After discussing the suitability and realisation of the ERIC expert recommendations within the research team and project advisory board, we decided to add two implementation strategies (‘Expanded Practice Nurses activities’ and ‘interprofessional team members activities’). We assume that both are necessary so that the newly developed intervention components reach their target groups (either the interprofessional team or the people with cognitive impairment and their relatives). Subsequently, we clustered all strategies into four groups according to their target level: ‘Expanded Practice Nurse’, ‘interprofessional team’, ‘people with cognitive impairment and relatives’, and ‘relations and structures’.

The implementation at Expanded Practice Nurse level includes a 200-hour training programme, a 10-day internship in a hospital abroad with established expanded practice models for people with cognitive impairment, and fortnightly two-hour coaching sessions.

The implementation at the interprofessional team level contains a half-hour recruitment meeting with the ward manager, a one-hour kick-off meeting with the interprofessional team, and the provision of information material. Moreover, the conduct of Expanded Practice Nurse activities addressing the interprofessional team, such as providing knowledge to colleagues, ensures that the intervention components reach interprofessional team members.

Similarly, implementation at the level of people with cognitive impairment and their relatives includes activities of the Expanded Practice Nurses, such as conducting an expanded nursing assessment, and the interprofessional team, such as planning, conducting, and evaluating person-centred care interventions.

At the relations and structures level, we integrated system-related implementation strategies. These include the involvement of and ongoing exchange with local stakeholders and external experts including people with cognitive impairment, their relatives, and representatives. In addition, we review hospital controlling data on the prevalence of people with cognitive impairment and their length of hospital stay and conduct a structured 5-day work shadowing on hospital wards with a high prevalence. Finally, we prevent disadvantaging the participating wards by providing financial compensation for training and extra time for Expanded Practice Nurse activities.

Discussion

This paper describes the methodological steps, theoretical basis, sub-studies, and their implications for the development of a complex intervention, suggesting an evidence-based Expanded Practice Nurse role and respective tasks to foster a person-centred care culture for people with cognitive impairment on hospital wards.

Our study addresses two identified gaps in previous research: Firstly, the lack of person-centred interventions for people with cognitive impairment in acute care and secondly, challenges in the implementation of person-centred care [12, 16, 17]. Person-centredness is evident in various facets of the role. On one hand, tasks, such as ‘planning and evaluating person-centred interventions’ or ‘training for staff’, reflect the categories of person-centred interventions identified by Mohr et al. [12]. Additional tasks, such as ‘monitoring’ or ‘conceptual work’, address organisational change within the interprofessional team. On the other hand, the theoretical basis for describing the Expanded Practice Nurse role, the Hamric and Hanson model [20], follows the person-centred care approach by placing patients and their relatives at the centre. By addressing these gaps, our intervention has the potential to provide person-centred care for people with cognitive impairment in acute care resulting in a reduction of complications and length of hospital stay as well as improved experiences for all involved.

The developed role aligns with the scope of practice outlined in international literature for nurses in advanced roles caring for people with cognitive impairment. Given the relatively low development of Advanced Practice Nurses in Germany [30], this new role represents a significant expansion of the scope of practice of a registered nurse, requiring more in-depth competencies. Firstly, the role requires specialist knowledge and clinical skills, for example to be able to carry out an expanded assessment with regard to cognition or to be able to counsel relatives on specific questions. This is also reflected in recommendations on the skill-grade-mix of nurses in Germany [56]. In addition, the intervention components require an increased level of clinical leadership as well as organisational and communication skills, for example by the Expanded Practice Nurses taking on an advisory and guiding role for colleagues in planning and evaluating person-centred care interventions or designing and introducing an interprofessional case conference. In this context, the role also adheres to national guidance [56] and furthermore illustrates its potential development towards the Advanced Practice Nurse level in leading case conferences. Finally, all system-related intervention components represent an expansion of the scope of practice compared to the clinically orientated role of a registered nurse. Furthermore, the Expanded Practice Nurse role is flexible and offers opportunities for ongoing growth through experience and additional qualifications. We achieved this by initially delineating the level of each intervention component. Following the completion of a Master’s programme, additional tasks can be integrated into existing components, or peripheral components can be designated as core components, and vice versa.

Next to role development, we identified influencing factors and derived various tailored implementation strategies using well-established implementation frameworks. Hereby we followed recommendations to focus not only on intervention components during intervention development, but also on the successful implementation into practice [57]. Our implementation strategies target three key areas: the qualification of the Expanded Practice Nurses, the engagement of the interprofessional team and people with cognitive impairment, and the familiarisation with local relationships and structures. By addressing these different levels, we aim to ensure that the newly developed intervention components reach all relevant target groups. Recent findings suggest that the combination of knowledge transfer, stakeholder involvement and support, and familiarisation with local structures are promising strategies for implementing the new advanced nursing role [58].

At the end of intervention development, we were able to describe the procedures, responsibilities, materials, and dose of each intervention component and implementation strategy in detail. We integrated both into the hospital’s existing systems and processes. The practical realisation, for example of new processes and tools in existing systems, are central questions that will be addressed in a future feasibility study determining initial effects, processes, and costs leading to potential refinement of the intervention.

Comprehensive intervention development using a multi-method design acknowledges the current state of research and includes the perspectives of all parties involved. Our systematic approach and its transparent reporting facilitate comprehensibility and replicability of the developed intervention. This allows the role and related implementation strategies to be adapted to different contexts and countries, especially those in the early development phase of Advanced Practice Nursing, and its effectiveness to be evaluated as recommended [35].

Limitations and strengths

This study has limitations that need to be considered. First, we were not able to include the physician perspective in the survey and expert workshops. However, we integrated feedback from the medical members of the advisory board to model the intervention and implementation strategies. Additionally, the patient perspective was only represented by one workshop participant. However, this person is experienced in engaging in participatory research projects. We assume that this and our efforts to create an open and trusting atmosphere during the workshops allowed all participants to express themselves without restriction. Second, for the workshops, we drew on interim results from the scoping review and the survey on attitudes of healthcare professionals towards advanced nursing roles. However, it should be noted that the results were available in full with regard to the context relevant to the project and that our research questions were therefore sufficiently answered. Third, with regard to the core elements of the Medical Research Council’s framework, we did not systematically include economic considerations as part of the intervention development. However, the advisory board discussed economic factors regarding implementation, for example in relation to exemption granted to Expanded Practice Nurses for the realisation of intervention components. In the subsequent feasibility study, we will conduct an economic evaluation of direct healthcare costs.

Conclusions

While person-centred care for people with cognitive impairment is known to improve outcomes and is advocated by international guidelines, its implementation in acute care hospitals remains limited. To promote person-centred care, nurses acting as change agents possessing both clinical expertise and leadership skills are a key factor. Through our project, we not only propose a conceptual realisation of an Expanded Practice Nurse role for person-centred care of people with cognitive impairment but also provide guidance for its implementation. Additionally, our logic model encompasses assumptions on mechanisms of change, relevant outcomes, and contextual factors of the role. We drew upon a diverse range of methodological and theoretical foundations, allowing us to address the complexity of care, varying responsibilities, and lack of regulations on task distribution. The forthcoming feasibility study will result in further guidance for future developments.
