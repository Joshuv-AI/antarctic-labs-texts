BEnefit of HYpnosis on Pain during stitches in an Emergency Room: a study protocol using a randomised controlled trial – the BE-HYPER protocol
Authors: Léa Pérard, Maëlys Clinchamps, Frédéric Dutheil, Sarah De Saint-Vincent, Camille Lucchini-Roche, Jeannot Schmidt, Farès Moustafa, Julien S Baker, Bruno Pereira, Jean-Baptiste Bouillon-Minois
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionWounds are one of the most common causes of emergency department visits. Their care often involves stitches, a potentially painful and stressful procedure. Pain and stress management is usually based on local anaesthesia, which is not always optimal. Medical hypnosis may offer a non-pharmacological alternative. Heart rate variability is a pain-free, easy-to-use and objective biomarker of stress. This study aims to assess the benefit of hypnosis on stress and pain during stitching using a randomised controlled trial.Methods and analysisThis is a single-centre, randomised controlled trial in an emergency department in Clermont-Ferrand. Patients consulting for suturable wounds will be randomised into two groups: with or without medical hypnosis. Measurements will include questionnaires, heart rate variability, physical activity levels and blood biomarkers. The primary outcome is heart rate variability during the procedure. Data will be analysed with intention-to-treat and per-protocol approaches, with p<0.05 considered statistically significant.Ethics and disseminationThe study has received approval from the Ethics Committee Sud-Méditerranée III, France, and is registered on ClinicalTrials.gov. Written informed consent will be obtained from all participants. Results will be published in a peer-reviewed journal.Trial registration numberNCT06497712.

Introduction

According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (USA) in 2019, lacerations accounted for 8.2% of all emergency department (ED) visits1 and 13% in 2013 according to the French Society of Emergency Medicine.2 The most common way to treat laceration is stitches.3 However, this therapeutic remains painful and stressful for the patient.4 Stress can be defined as any threat to an organism’s homeostasis.5 Stress assessment can be subjective and objective. The Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) was initially developed to assess pain.6 Although controversial, VAS is still considered the gold standard to assess pain levels, but also the efficacy of pain management.68 More recently, VAS was validated to evaluate stress.9 VAS is attractive because it is simple to implement, easy to understand and time efficient. However, VAS remains a subjective assessment of pain. Heart-rate variability (HRV)10 and blood markers such as norepinephrine, epinephrine, cortisol, dopamine, dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA), leptin, ghrelin, tumour necrosis factor alpha (TNF-alpha) are objective measurements and are impacted by pain and stress.1118 Measuring HRV is noninvasive, easy to use and reproducible.19 Linked to the autonomous nervous system, HRV reflects the cardiovascular response to regulatory impulses affecting heart rhythm.20 Stress among patients with laceration has been widely studied. Hypnosis offers a valuable approach to improve stress management.21

Hypnosis uses various communication tools to enhance patient adherence and improve the process. According to Erickson, several types of hypnosis can be distinguished. Conversational hypnosis does not require any schema or hypnotic induction protocol. It relies on conversational techniques using various methods such as focusing attention on a point, using metaphors, analogies and paradoxes. It can also draw on the patient’s internal resources (eg, previous successes). Indirect suggestions can be made to induce behavioural change in the patient. This type of hypnosis can be used to create a calm atmosphere and a trusting relationship with the patient to improve care.22 23 Formal hypnosis is a more protocolised type of medical hypnosis. Its aim is to induce a hypnotic trance where therapeutic action can take place. After an initial approach with the patient (pretalk), the patient must go through an induction stage (based on sensorially) leading to dissociation (a modified state of consciousness/trance), followed by a stage of return to the baseline status.24

The effectiveness of hypnosis to decrease pain and anxiety has been demonstrated, leading to a significant reduction in drug requirements and improving the comfort of these patients.25 According to the French Society of Anesthesiology and Intensive Care Medicine, all patients do not have the same ability to receive hypnosis under the induction of a third party. Different scales—such as the Stanford Hypnotic Scale26—distinguish in the general population 25% of highly hypnotisable individuals and 25% of poorly hypnotisable individuals. However, if patients do not have the same capacities to respond to hypnotic inductions, motivation can modify these aptitudes during the treatment.27 Hypnosis seems interesting in pain management, particularly for acute pain.28 However, these studies are most often based on subjective primary outcome measures. Hypnosis in emergency medicine has been particularly studied in children, especially during care pain induced.29 It reduces the doses of analgesics used.30 Therefore, hypnosis seems valuable as an analgesic technique in managing acute pain in emergency medicine.31 32

However, no study covered a holistic approach by measuring concomitantly perception of pain and most biomarkers, in an interventional study aiming at decreasing pain and stress among patients in the ED. The main objective of this study will be to examine changes in pain and stress. More specifically, we want to assess the impact of medical hypnosis among patients that need stitches using HRV—an objective biomarker of pain. Secondary objectives will be to study (1) the impact using subjective analysis with VAS (2) objective blood biomarkers (3) the factors influencing the benefits of the intervention (sociodemographic and lifestyle) and the impact of medical hypnosis on emergency healthcare workers that perform stitches (4).

Methods and analysis

Study design

This study is a randomised controlled trial investigator-initiated, monocentric (ED at Clermont-Ferrand University Hospital), prospective, single-blind, parallel-group randomised clinical. We will randomly assign patients to one of two groups: with hypnosis versus without hypnosis. The procedures (stitches) performed in the study will be done in a classic way for wound care in the ED. However, we will add HRV monitoring and blood sampling, which are not routinely performed. We will not use psychotherapy or cognitive-behavioural therapies. Instead, we will employ medical hypnosis as a tool to alter patients’ critical consciousness in order to modulate pain and stress induced by stitches. The study is registered in ClinicalTrials.gov under the identification number NCT06497712 after the French Ethics Committee’s approval.

Study design.

Participants

The study will be proposed to every patient who consults the ED of the University Hospital of Clermont-Ferrand. Participants will be included in this study if they meet all of the following criteria: age up to 18 years old, presenting to the ED for a stitchable wound, being able to provide informed consent to participate in the research (online supplemental material 1). The participants will not be included if they are not affiliated to a health insurance, being part of a legally protected population (minors, guardianship, curatorship, deprived of freedoms, safeguard of justice), pregnant women, breastfeeding women, if they have a medical history of cardiac rhythm disorders (atrial fibrillation, pacemaker), endocrine or psychiatric disorder, do not speak French, hearing impairment, wounds located on the eyelids, nose, ears or mouth, surgical wound, intake of toxic substances, the use of nitrous oxide (NO), brain injury with a Glasgow Coma Scale <15 or if they refuse to participate. Other concomitant care is permitted during the trial

Patient and public involvement

Patients and members of the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Randomisation

Patients will be randomly assigned to one of two groups in a 1:1 ratio using permuted block randomisation, with block sizes varying to ensure unpredictability. The randomisation list was generated by the team’s biostatistician and will be managed and updated through the RedCap electronic Case Report Form (eCRF) software.

Due to the nature of the intervention, full blinding is not feasible. Participants in the hypnosis arm, as well as the clinicians delivering the intervention, will be aware that a hypnotic induction is being administered. However, participants will not be informed of the study hypotheses or group comparisons, and outcome assessors and data analysts will remain blinded to treatment allocation. This partial blinding strategy is intended to limit bias in outcome assessment.

Study intervention

The practice of medical hypnosis will be conducted by a physician or a nurse who has already undergone training in medical hypnosis. The session will proceed as follows. (1) As soon as the patient is settled, the hypnotic process can begin with the implementation of conversational hypnosis. We will introduce suggestions, use metaphors to build trust with the patient and proceed to medical hypnosis. (2) Prior to the beginning of stitches, the patient will be offered a hypnotic induction. The hypnotic induction is the initial phase of the hypnosis process. Its goal is to bring the patient to a state of dissociation: the hypnotic trance. Induction is usually achieved through progressive relaxation, sensory focusing and a process of confusion. (3) After the hypnotic induction, there follows a phase of deepening or maintenance of the hypnotic trance for the duration of the medical procedure. This phase aims to maintain the state of dissociation through suggestions, ratification and sensory confusion. (4) Finally, at the end of the stitches, comes the phase of reassociation, aiming to gradually ground the patient back into the present time and place. Patients will be monitored throughout their management in the ED, which typically lasts a couple of hours. There is no standardised operation to maintain hypnosis due to the diversity of putative responses.

Both the intervention and control groups will receive the gold standard of care, which includes thorough wound cleaning, debridement as needed, local anaesthesia administered with lidocaine and appropriate suturing.

Outcomes

Main outcome

The main outcome is HRV during intervention when the patient is in the ED. A Bittium Faros will record HRV parameters. It is a heart rate transmitter holter simply positioned on the chest, with a putative 48 hours recording time, a beat per minute within a 25–240 range. The aim will be to demonstrate that this cost-effective and easy-to-use device is sufficient to detect stress. The HRV data will be examined according to the recommendations of the European Society of Cardiology and the North American Society (Task Force). HRV will be explored in time and frequency domains. The methodology developed by our team will also be applied. Both time and frequency domains will be used to investigate HRV. Data will be analysed using Kubios HRV Software. We will apply a very low artefact correction. Multiple parameters will be examined in the time domain, including R-R intervals and their SD, the ratio of adjacent N-N intervals differing by more than 50 ms to the total number of N-N intervals (pNN50), and the square root of the average squared difference between successive R-R intervals (rMSSD). The parameters pNN50 and rMSSD are associated with high-frequency (HF) power, which in turn reflects parasympathetic activity. Spectral domain analysis will investigate HF (0.15–0.4 Hz) and low-frequency (0.04–0.15 Hz) power. HF power primarily represents parasympathetic (vagal) activity directed towards the sinus node, while low-frequency (LF) power serves as an indicator of both parasympathetic and sympathetic functions. Normalised units (nu), which represent their relative values in relation to the total power minus the very LF component, will be employed to assess LF and HF power components. Therefore, LFnu and HFnu are considered to reflect the most accurate measurements of sympathetic and parasympathetic activity, respectively. The LF/HF ratio, which indicates the balance between sympathetic and vagal influences, will also be calculated.

Secondary outcomes

Blood samples will be collected at the beginning and end of the session by an emergency nurse or an emergency physician for a total of 21 mL twice. This volume (42 mL) represents about 0.7% of the volume of a 71 kg person, that is, far below the maximum volume of blood allowed to be sampled, which is 2.5% of the total volume of blood, that is, 142 mL for a person of 71 kg. Centrifugation, aliquoting and conservation of plasma and serum will be achieved at the ‘Institut de Médecine du Travail, Faculté de Médecine, 28 place Henri Dunant, 63000 Clermont-Ferrand’. The following biomarkers will also be measured at the Institut de Médecine du Travail, using ELISA kits. We plan to measure cortisol, DHEA, DHEA-S, ghrelin, leptin, TNF-alpha, epinephrine, norepinephrine, dopamine.

Participants will have to complete a short questionnaire at the beginning and at the end of the session using RedCap. This questionnaire is composed of VAS assessing stress and pain, on a horizontal, non-calibrated line of 100 mm, ranging from very low (0) to very high (100). It will also ask about sociodemographic variables, lifestyle habits, clinical measurements (height, weight), procedure (duration of procedure, number of stitches, wound location, length of stay in the ED) (table 1).

Data collected

The data relating to the protocol will be collected in an e-CRF. Study data will be collected and managed using REDCap electronic data capture tools hosted at CHU de Clermont-Ferrand. All data are anonymised. We estimated that the data recruitment period would take 1 year. The first participant was enrolled on 15 May 2025.

Sample size calculation

This study aims to demonstrate an improved HRV in the intervention group, in comparison with the control group. According to previous data,33 it seems reasonable to estimate that effect size for the primary analysis will be around 0.5 for HRV parameters (eg, log(LF/HF), rMSSD, SDNN, pNN50). To highlight such a clinically relevant difference, 85 participants per randomised group will be needed for a two-sided type I error at 5%, a statistical power at 90%. Finally, it is proposed to include 180 to consider non-available data.

Statistical analysis

All analyses will be performed according to a predefined statistical analysis plan, using the Stata software V.15.0 (StataCorp) before the breaking of the randomisation code, according to International Conference on Harmonisation-Good Clinical Practice guidelines. All analyses will be detailed in a separate statistical analysis plan.

The primary analysis will be conducted in the intention-to-treat (ITT) population being all randomised patients except those who withdraw consent for data use. We will perform a per-protocol analysis on the primary outcome. The criteria for including patients in the ITT and in the per-protocol populations, respectively, are provided in the statistical analysis plan.

Baseline variables will be reported as numbers and percentages for categorical variables and medians with IQRs for continuous variables. As according to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) 2010 statement, group differences in baseline variables will not be compared using significance testing unless specifically requested by peer reviewers.

In order to evaluate the impact of hypnosis during stitches among ED’s patients presenting for laceration assessed by HRV, the primary endpoint will be compared between groups by the Student’s t-test, or the non-parametric Mann-Whitney test if assumptions of t-test are not met. The normality will be studied using Shapiro-Wilk test. The homoscedasticity will be analysed with Fisher-Snedecor test. The results will be expressed using effect size and 95% CI.

Adjusted analyses will be performed using multiple linear models to provide effect estimates derived from multivariate regression models with HRV parameters as the dependent variable. The normality of residuals from the model will be analysed as aforementioned. If appropriate, a logarithmic transformation will be applied for the dependent variable. We will incorporate adjustment for baseline covariates in the ITT population with anticipated relationships with the primary outcome. Results will be presented as regression coefficients, effect sizes and 95% CIs. Particular attention will be paid to multicollinearity using the Farrar-Glauber test and variance inflation factor. Significance of the intervention will be assessed based on p values and effect sizes with 95% CIs calculated from this regression.

The secondary analyses aiming to evaluate the impact of hypnosis during stitches on VAS and blood biomarkers will be conducted similarly to those proposed for the primary endpoint.

Dichotomous secondary endpoints (such as pain categorised according to thresholds reported in literature, blood biomarkers categorised according to their statistical distribution and data reported in literature) will be analysed using χ² or Fisher’s exact tests. The numbers at risk in each treatment group and the number (and proportion) of events will be reported, as well as the equivalent absolute risk difference and relative risk ratio (95% CI).

Planned subgroup (eg, according to sex, age, procedure duration, previous hypnosis experiment) analyses will be performed irrespective of whether there is evidence of a treatment effect. Subgroup analyses will assess heterogeneity of the effect on the primary outcome measure in prespecified subgroups of patients. According to usual recommendations, the interactions between group randomisation and subgroups will be evaluated in regression models. The primary outcome will be evaluated in the subgroup analyses without adjustments for multiplicity.

We do not expect missing data on the primary outcome measure and a complete case analysis will be performed. We will use an eCRF with a pragmatic design and incentive strategies to maximise complete registration in order to minimise the occurrence of missing data. For secondary endpoints, if less than 5% of the data are missing, a complete case analysis without input of missing values will be performed. If the frequency of missing data is >5%, an additional analysis will be performed using the multiple imputation method (Stata command mi). We will use outcomes and the most important baseline characteristics in the multiple imputation. The exact variables to be used to estimate the missing values will be outlined in the detailed statistical analysis plan; if multiple imputation is used, then the primary result of the trial will be based on the imputed data.

A two-sided p value of less than 0.05 will be considered for statistical significance of all analyses. Because of the potential for type 1 error due to multiple comparisons, findings from analyses of secondary endpoints will be interpreted as exploratory.

Data availability statement

Investigators will make available the documents and personal data strictly necessary in accordance with laws and regulations (Articles L.1121-3 and R.5121-13 of the Code of Public Health). No Data Monitoring Committee (DMC) is planned for this trial. This decision is based on the following considerations: the intervention under investigation—medical hypnosis—is a non-invasive, non-pharmacological technique with minimal associated risks, this is single-centre and investigator-initiated, without involvement of any commercial sponsor, no interim analyses or early stopping rules are planned, the trial does not involve vulnerable populations or life-threatening conditions, data collection procedures and outcome assessments are clearly defined, and adherence will be monitored by the study team. However, the study team will remain vigilant throughout the trial and will report any unexpected adverse events or protocol deviations to the appropriate ethical oversight bodies, in accordance with Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Data that underlie the results reported in this protocol will be made available on reasonable request to the corresponding author. Data will be shared with researchers who provide a methodologically sound proposal for use in achieving the goals of the approved proposal. Proposals should be directed to LP. To gain access, data requestors will need to sign a data access agreement.

Ethics and dissemination

The study protocol has been approved by the Ethics Committee Sud-Méditerranée III, University Hospital, Nîmes, FRANCE. The study is registered in ClinicalTrials.gov under the identification number NCT06497712 after the French Ethics Committee’s approval. The Ethics Committee initially approved the study on 16 May 2024 and subsequently approved an amendment on 15 January 2025. All patients will be informed about the details of the study and sign written informed consent before enrolment in the study with the investigator. Data anonymisation is ensured via REDCap starting at the time of participant enrolment. The results will be reported according to the Standard Protocol Items: Recommendations for Interventional Trials (SPIRIT) guidelines. Results from this study will be published in a peer-reviewed journal.

Discussion

The expected result is a benefit of hypnosis on pain during stitches performed in the ED, assessed by HRV, VAS and blood biomarkers.

Wounding is one of the most common causes of ED visits. Currently, its management requires local anaesthetic but also potentially NO. NO is a strong greenhouse gas that contributes to global warming (298 more than carbon dioxide) due to its positive radiative forcing effect.34 Hypnosis appears as an interesting alternative. Indeed, it is affordable, easy to perform with no or few side effects. Regarding the main outcome, we expect an increase in parasympathetic activity and a decrease in sympathetic activity in the intervention group compared to the control group. For the secondary outcome measures, significant variations in stress biomarkers and patient satisfaction (assessed by VAS) on the questionnaire are anticipated. Reducing pain and stress could improve the patient experience and reduce the need for analgesics. The literature has already shown the benefits of hypnosis for certain indications, but there are still few studies on treatment-induced pain in adults with the implementation of medical hypnosis.

The BE-HYPER study is a randomised controlled trial that will be implemented at the University Hospital of Clermont-Ferrand. This methodology has a unique strength that could prove the impact of the hypnosis by controlling other confounding factors. The use of HRV appears as another additional strength by providing objective results, and is also reproducible for further studies. If the results are positive, we will be able to expand the practice of hypnosis for more emergency healthcare workers in our ED, but also in other ED units. Thus, we expect a future benefit for many patients within the next years.

Potential limitations

We can note some study limitations. First, this study is the first study assessing medical hypnosis for stitchable wounds using HRV monitoring among adult ED patients. All our expectations are based on previous data such as paediatrics or emergency healthcare workers’ studies. So, it seems possible that our sample size is not strong enough or the intervention protocol is not perfect. However, we based our sample size on a very small one, that is, 42 children, and we expect 180 patients.33 Furthermore, to our knowledge and to date, there is no standardised protocol for hypnosis regarding ED patients. Second, we do not use the suggestibility hypnosis scale. Only selected receptive patients could increase significantly from our results. However, these scales are time-consuming which is not possible in an ED. Furthermore, we want to assess the benefit of hypnosis among every patient consulting in the ED to generalise our results. Lastly, this study is monocentric, precluding the generalisability of our results.
