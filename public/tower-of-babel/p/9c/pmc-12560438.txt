Management of meningitis in critical obstetric patients: a case report
Authors: Fildza Sasri Peddyandhari, Dita Aditianingsih, Hazia Hanifa Bilqis
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPregnancy is considered a relative immunology-compromising condition that increases the risk of severe infections, including meningitis. Pregnant women and fetuses who have invasive bacterial infections are far more likely to suffer substantial complications. Meningitis in critically ill pregnant women is a rare yet life-threatening condition that can develop rapidly, affecting both maternal and fetal well-being. Delayed recognition can lead to higher morbidity and mortality. We hereby present three cases of bacterial meningitis in pregnant Indonesian women referred to the Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital Intensive Care Unit. This case report aims to analyze and compare the management of meningitis in critically ill obstetric patients.Case presentationAll patients were Indonesian women aged 19, 27, and 29 years old; one patient was primigravida and two were multigravida, with gestational age of presentation ranging from 24 weeks to 30 weeks. One patient had early termination of pregnancy, one continued with the pregnancy, and one experienced intrauterine fetal death.ConclusionOur cases demonstrate the importance of resuscitation and therapeutic strategies on the basis of the stabilization of the mother’s acute condition and the importance of a multidisciplinary approach.

Background

Managing critically ill obstetric patients is challenging because of their altered physiology and the additional consideration of fetal well-being [1]. The most common conditions in pregnant women requiring transfer to the intensive care unit (ICU) are hypertensive disorders, pulmonary insufficiency, and obstetric hemorrhage [2, 3]. Maternal sepsis is also one of the conditions in which the mother may need to be transferred to the ICU. It is quite infrequent, with the incidence of septic shock in pregnancy at 2.3 cases per 100,000 maternities [4]. Meningitis is an inflammation of the meninges. It is a rare disease in pregnant women that can progress into a rapid emergency condition that can lead to a life-threatening situation. The estimated incidence of community-acquired bacterial meningitis is 2.6–6 per 100,000 adults per year in developed countries, with Streptococcus pneumoniae and Neisseria meningitidis as the leading causes [5]. The most frequent causes of meningitis in pregnancy were tuberculous meningitis, S. pneumoniae, and herpes virus meningitis [5, 6].

The most common signs and symptoms of meningitis are headache, seizure, altered mental status, vomiting, positive meningeal signs, and fever [7]. The classical triad is fever, nuchal rigidity, and altered mental status. Neurological complications are the leading cause of death in younger meningitis patients, while systemic complications are the most common cause of death in the elderly patients with meningitis [8]. An altered mental status or cranial nerve dysfunction may compromise the airway and cause respiratory failure, requiring critical care and intervention. Once meningitis is diagnosed, the patient must be given immediate treatment, such as antibiotics or antifungals, as well as adjuvant therapy such as dexamethasone. Moreover, it is not uncommon that intensive care unit patients have other diseases or comorbidities that may worsen and complicate the condition. This condition affects both maternal and fetal well-being. Delayed recognition can cause higher morbidity and mortality.

In the case of a critically obstetric patient, a fetomaternal conflict may arise where saving the maternal life to preserve the maternal health might override the beneficence to the fetus. In this scenario, the prime importance is the safety of the mother [1, 9]. A prudent decision to terminate pregnancy must be well thought out, hence a well-collaborated multidisciplinary approach involving the intensivists, obstetricians, neonatologists, neurologist, internal medicine physicians, and nurses has to be made to improve maternal outcome [10].

Therefore, this case report provides information about management of meningitis in critically ill obstetric patients admitted to the intensive care unit (ICU) of our hospital.

Case presentation

The first case was a 29-year-old Indonesian woman, multigravida, referred to the Cipto Mangunkusumo Intensive Care Unit with chief complaint of headache 1 day prior to admission to the hospital. The pain numeric rating scale was 7/10. The throbbing headache was felt on top of the head and was exacerbated by a fever. The headache subsided with paracetamol and rest. The patient felt more comfortable in a quiet place.

On arrival, the patient was conscious with a Glasgow coma scale of 15 and oxygen saturation of 98% with a non-rebreathing mask at 10 L per minute. Cardiac auscultation heart sounds revealed irregular rhythm with a pansystolic murmur at the pulmonal valve. Echocardiography showed severe pulmonary stenosis. The patient had New York Heart Association functional classification (NYHA FC) III–IV. There were clubbed fingers and nuchal rigidity was positive. Blood examination revealed leukocytosis of 1221 × 109/L. Obstetric ultrasonography revealed intrauterine growth restriction. Cerebrospinal fluid indicated bacterial infection and tested negative for polymerase chain reaction (PCR) tuberculosis. Cerebral fluid analysis for leukocytes and protein was elevated and glucose levels were decreased, indicating bacterial meningitis. Cerebral fluid culture showed no growth of microorganisms. The patient was immediately given vancomycin (500 mg every 6 hours, intravenous) and meropenem (2 g every 8 hours, intravenous) for 10 days and dexamethasone (20 mg every 24 hours, intravenous) to treat meningitis.

On day 5 in the ICU, the patient’s condition worsened; her level of consciousness deteriorated (Glasgow coma scale E3M5V2), and she had sudden apnea. Obstetric Doppler ultrasonography revealed cerebral blood-flow redistribution of the fetus. Head computed tomography (CT) scan revealed diffuse brain swelling and hydrocephalus (Fig. 1). The patient was then intubated, and scheduled for an emergency cesarean section to terminate the pregnancy. The baby was born alive at 31 weeks. External ventricular drainage (EVD) was also inserted to alleviate the hydrocephalus; 2 days following the caesarean delivery, the patient’s condition improved and she was already extubated. The second patient was a 27-year-old Indonesian woman, primigravida, referred to our ICU with a chief complaint of seizures 3 days prior to admission with a high fever. The patient once experienced a seizure while she was sleeping; the preictal was unknown. During the seizure, the patient’s eye and head were slightly moving to the left, along with her left hand and left foot also convulsing; the patient was unconscious during the seizure, which lasted for 2 minutes, and 3 hours after the seizure, the patient had an altered mental status and was initially treated in the ICU in another hospital before she was referred to the ICU of Cipto Mangunkusumo Hospital.

During physical examination in the ICU, the patient was sedated and intubated with a Glasgow coma scale of E2M4VT. Oxygen saturation was 93–100% on a ventilator with FiO2 40%. There were clubbed fingers, as well as an increase in physiological reflex on the right side, positive nuchal rigidity, and positive Babinski reflex on the right side. Peripheral blood laboratory revealed leukocytosis of 1706 × 109/L. Head CT scan revealed diffuse brain swelling (Fig. 2). Cerebrospinal fluid analysis for leukocytes and protein was elevated and glucose levels were decreased, indicating bacterial meningitis. The cerebrospinal fluid culture showed no growth of microorganisms. The patient received vancomycin (500 mg every 6 hours, intravenous), meropenem (2 g every 8 hours, intravenous) for empirical therapy for 14 days and also dexamethasone (20 g every 24 hours, intravenous). The patient stayed in the ICU for 34 days. The patient’s condition deteriorated throughout the second week of treatment; she had altered mental status to E1M3VT. After a few days, the intracranial infection condition resolved. The fetus was in good condition, and there were no indications of termination of pregnancy, although due to prolonged hospitalization the patient also had ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP) and had to be tracheostomized to hasten ventilator weaning, and had a complication of pneumothorax after tracheostomy. After 3 days on a chest tube, on day 32, the patient was able to be on spontaneous breathing trial (SBT), and on day 34 she was discharged from the ICU.

The third case was a 19-year-old Indonesian woman, multigravida, referred to our ICU with chief complaint of loss of consciousness 1 week before admission. For 1 week prior to admission, the patient had been unable to communicate or respond to inquiries clearly. This was followed by involuntary movement of the right arm and drooping of the right eyelid. There had been a fever 2 weeks prior to admission and a headache 6 months prior to admission.

During the physical examination, the patient was sedated with a Glasgow coma scale of 9 T. Blood pressure was 185/80 mmHg, with an irregular heart rate of 92 beats per minute, and saturation was 99% on a ventilator with FiO2 40%. Pupils were anisocoric (2 mm/6 mm, slow reactive/nonreactive). Nuchal rigidity was positive, with left eye’s nervus III paresis, ophthalmoparesis of the left eye, bilateral proptosis, and bilateral positive Babinski reflexes. On the extremities, there were clubbed fingers and left-sided hemiparesis. Obstetric examinations were normal.

Laboratory results indicated leukocytosis of 1074 × 109/L. Cerebrospinal fluid analysis for leukocytes and protein were elevated, and glucose levels were decreased, indicating bacterial meningitis. The cerebrospinal fluid test revealed negative acid-fast bacilli, and the cerebrospinal fluid culture revealed no growth of microorganisms. Head CT scan revealed hydrocephalus and basal enhancement (Fig. 3). The patient was treated for meningoencephalitis tuberculosis (METB) with antituberculosis medication, including isoniazid (300 mg every 24 hours, orally), rifampicin (450 mg every 24 hours, orally), ethambutol (1000 mg every 24 hours, orally), and meropenem (2 g every 8 hours, intravenous), as well as adjunctive therapy with dexamethasone (20 mg every 24 hours, intravenous). The patient additionally had a ventriculoperitoneal (VP) shunt to treat the hydrocephalus. On day 12, the patient had drug-induced liver injury (DILI). Her hepatic enzyme levels rose more than tenfold, from aspartate aminotransferase (AST) 74 U/L and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) 80 U/L to AST 1580 U/L and ALT 1698 U/L due to the antituberculosis regimen. Isoniazid and rifampicin were stopped on day 13 due to elevated liver enzymes, and were switched for a modified antituberculosis medication, which includes levofloxacin (750 mg every 24 hours, intravenous), streptomycin (1 g every 24 hours, intravenous) and ethambutol (1 g every 24 hours, orally). On day 16, the patient’s condition improved, and she was discharged to the ward. However, on the next day (day 17), the patient was readmitted to the ICU due to a seizure and septic shock. The patient’s condition deteriorated, and the fetus was identified as IUFD on obstetric ultrasonography. This was later suspected to have caused sepsis due to intraabdominal infection due to the IUFD. The fetus was spontaneously delivered and evacuated on day 23 of hospitalization. Rifampicin was reintroduced on day 34, and isoniazid and pyrazinamide were reintroduced on day 38. In the following days after the spontaneous delivery, the level of consciousness remained compromised and the seizure progressed to an epileptic state. The intensivist consented to elevate the antituberculosis drug dose for the meningitis. The patient’s condition improved in the following days. Unfortunately, the patient was left with sequelae, including left-sided hemiparesis. Discussion

The Confidential Enquiry into Maternal and Child Health in the United Kingdom issued certain recommendations aimed at improving child health and reducing maternal mortality, highlighting the importance of early recognition and management of severely ill pregnant women [11, 12]. Throughout pregnancy, both the placenta and fetus release immunosuppressive cytokines. A reduction in circulating B cells is particularly prominent during the third trimester, revealing “physiological” B cell lymphopenia due to the effect of elevated estrogen on lymphopoiesis [13]. Pregnancy is also associated with changes in B cell subsets, specifically lower numbers of innate B-1 cells during pregnancy, delivery, and the early postpartum period. The majority of “innate” immunoglobulin (Ig)M antibodies, which play a protective function in the early stages of infection, are produced by B-1 cells [14]. The function of B cells also declines as pregnancy advances, resulting in a lack of responsiveness to mitogens and infectious agents, potentially raising the risk of infection, including meningitis [11].

In our cases, the average age at presentation was 25 years old, which is younger than the average age reported in previous studies [5]. In this study, all of our patients presented with the classical triad of meningitis: fever, positive nuchal rigidity, and altered mental status. While the site of origin of infection in meningitis is mostly caused by otolaryngological infection, in all of our cases, the site of origin of infection was unknown [6]. All of our patients’ brain CT scans indicated inflammation, suggesting meningitis, with one patient showing basal enhancement, which is characteristic of tuberculous meningitis [15]. The most common time of onset of meningitis in pregnancy in our cases was in the second trimester, which does not align with previous studies reporting that the most common onset was in the third trimester [5, 6, 16].

Previous studies showed that nonpregnant patients with meningitis frequently had positive human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) status [17]. All of our patients tested negative for HIV. This was similar to a previous report on meningitis in pregnancy, where most patients also tested negative for HIV [5, 18]. One patient had a possible immunocompromised condition due to autoimmune disease, with a weakly positive ANA test.

For our first patient, previous studies showed that pulmonary stenosis alone can be well tolerated during pregnancy and is rarely a significant obstacle to a successful pregnancy [19, 20]. The sudden apnea due to worsening of the pulmonary stenosis in our first patient had an unknown cause. However, it was thought to be due to the physiological changes during pregnancy in the third trimester, which has the most significant changes (e.g., blood volume, blood pressure, and heart rate) compared with other trimesters [21]. It was also thought to be worsened by the septic condition of the patient, which affected her overall hemodynamic stability. Balloon valvuloplasty was not possible because of the septic/infectious condition of the patient. A multidisciplinary team of intensivists, obstetricians, neurologists, and internists decided on immediate cesarean delivery. Immediate cesarean delivery has been shown to improve outcomes for both the fetus and the mother due to enhanced thoracic compliance and relief of the inferior vena cava [22].

Although cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) culture is considered the gold standard for diagnosing meningitis, all of our patients had negative culture results. This outcome may be due to prior antimicrobial therapy from the previous hospital. In addition to the negative culture results, our case showed the importance of administering early, broad-spectrum antibiotic regimens that are consistent with current evidence-based practice [23, 24]. This was proven by an improvement in the condition of the first and second patients. Current standard therapy for bacterial meningitis also includes adjunctive dexamethasone [25].

Unfortunately, our third patient experienced DILI when her hepatic enzyme levels rose more than tenfold due to the antituberculosis regimen and had to switch to another antituberculosis regimen. This was thought to be the cause of the worsening condition due to inadequate tuberculosis treatment of the mother, which led to septic shock and disseminated intravascular coagulation (DIC), thus causing IUFD [26].

A previous study stated that a tuberculosis regimen of isoniazid, rifampicin, and ethambutol appears to be safe for the fetus [27]. However, the second-line drug, streptomycin, is teratogenic to the fetus, which can cause birth defects, specifically ototoxicity. We did not find any study identifying streptomycin as a direct cause of IUFD, but it could still be a contributing factor to the fetus’s worsening condition, in addition to the septic and DIC states of the mother [27].

A previous study showed that miscarriage was more common in the first trimester [5]. This did not align with our case, as our patient who experienced a miscarriage was in the second trimester. Our cases showed that miscarriage was more likely in mothers who experienced unstable hemodynamics, highlighting the importance of maternal resuscitation, as optimal stabilization of the fetus depends on adequate maternal resuscitation [28]. A previous study also stated that miscarriage was more frequent in pregnancies with active TB compared with those without TB, which is similar to our current study [29].

The treatment of meningitis must be individualized. In this case, all of our patients had other underlying diseases as well as complications during ICU care, making treatment more complicated. We found a variety of outcomes for both the fetus and the mother in our cases. However, on the basis of the improvement in the patients’ clinical conditions, prompt diagnosis and treatment appeared to have successfully overcome the meningitis.

Conclusion

Meningitis during pregnancy is rare, but many may lead to critical illness. This study emphasizes clinical presentation and successful resuscitation and therapeutic strategies on the basis of the stabilization of the mothers’ acute condition, as well as the importance of physical examination and serological and radiological results for accurate diagnosis and prompt treatment. The use of antibiotics, along with surgery when necessary, can enhance patient outcomes. This study highlights the importance of a multidisciplinary team, including intensivists, obstetricians, neurologists, and internists, in managing maternal and fetal health.
