Association of stress hyperglycemia ratio with the incidence of delirium in critically ill patients: a retrospective cohort study with exploratory machine-learning analyses
Authors: Yuzeng Xu, Xiaoyong Zhu, Wenying Lu, Shengjie Yao, Lifeng Ni
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDelirium is a prevalent and severe complication among critically ill patients. The Stress Hyperglycemia Ratio (SHR), reflecting acute illness-induced hyperglycemia relative to chronic glycemic status, has emerged as a robust prognostic marker. However, its relationship with delirium remains not fully understood. This study aimed to evaluate the association between admission SHR and delirium incidence in critically ill patients.MethodsThis large-scale retrospective cohort study utilized data from the MIMIC-IV database, including 7,744 adult patients with a first-time ICU admission. The primary outcome was the incidence of delirium. We employed multivariable logistic regression and restricted cubic splines (RCS) to assess the relationship between SHR and delirium. A Boruta-based feature selection procedure and multiple machine-learning algorithms were additionally applied as exploratory analyses to examine the relative importance of SHR.ResultsThe overall incidence of delirium was 26.0%. A significant, nonlinear J-shaped relationship was identified between SHR and delirium risk (P for nonlinearity = 0.005). After adjusting for multiple confounders, restricted cubic spline visualization and piecewise regression suggested a potential inflection at SHR 0.94 (OR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.69–2.06; P < 0.001). In exploratory machine-learning analyses, SHR was consistently retained as an important feature, and the association was robust across subgroup analyses.ConclusionAdmission SHR was independently and non-linearly associated with delirium in critically ill patients. The observed inflection point (SHR 0.94) requires validation in an external independent cohort. SHR may complement existing delirium risk assessment methods, pending external validation.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12883-026-04795-8.

Introduction

Delirium, a common form of acute brain dysfunction in critically ill patients, has an incidence rate of up to 20% and significantly increases the risk of mortality, prolonged hospitalization, and long-term cognitive impairment [1, 2]. Therefore, effective tools for identifying risk factors are urgently needed.

The pathophysiology of delirium is complex, involving multiple processes such as neuroinflammation, neurotransmitter imbalance, oxidative stress, and blood-brain barrier disruption [3]. Acute hyperglycemia may induce or exacerbate delirium by impairing the central nervous system through these pathways [4, 5]. Recent studies have indicated that perioperative acute hyperglycemia may be associated with an increased risk of postoperative delirium (POD) or postoperative cognitive dysfunction (POCD) [6]. Another study also confirmed that postoperative hyperglycemia is linked to an increased risk of delirium after non-cardiac surgery [7].

However, admission blood glucose levels, as a risk indicator, have significant limitations because they cannot differentiate between acute stress responses and underlying chronic diabetes. The Stress Hyperglycemia Ratio (SHR), which adjusts the admission blood glucose level for glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c), more accurately reflects the relative increase in blood glucose caused by acute illness [8]. Previous studies have demonstrated that SHR is a stronger predictor of prognosis than a single glucose measurement in various critical conditions, including myocardial infarction, stroke, and sepsis [9–11].

Although the neurotoxic mechanisms of stress hyperglycemia and its association with adverse outcomes have gained attention, studies specifically evaluating the predictive value of SHR for the risk of delirium in critically ill patients remain scarce. Therefore, this study conducted a large-scale retrospective cohort study. We hypothesized that a higher SHR is an independent risk factor for the development of delirium in critically ill patients after adjusting for other confounding factors. This study aims to provide new, evidence-based insights into the risk assessment and management of delirium in critically ill patients.

Methods

Data source

The data were extracted from MIMIC-IV v3.1 (Medical Information Mart for Intensive Care IV, version 3.1), an electronic health record database containing data from over 50,000 patients admitted to the ICU at Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center (BIDMC) in Boston, Massachusetts, from 2008 to 2022. The database employs de-identification protocols to ensure data privacy. The Institutional Review Board of BIDMC granted a waiver of informed consent and approved the sharing of research resources. The author (Lifeng Ni) obtained access to this database (certification number: 65512516).

Ethical considerations

The study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Zhejiang Xiaoshan Hospital (Approval No. 2025010). All procedures were performed in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Data collection

Structured Query Language (SQL) in Navicat Premium 17 (Version 17.0) was utilized to extract or calculate the subsequent values. Data on patient characteristics, encompassing age, sex, and tobacco use, were gathered. Comorbidities were identified using the International Classification of Diseases (ICD-9 or ICD-10) codes, including myocardial infarction (MI), congestive heart failure (CHF), peripheral vascular disease (PVD), cerebrovascular disease (CVD), chronic pulmonary disease (CPD), traumatic brain injury (TBI), renal disease, diabetes, rheumatic disease, liver disease, and malignant cancer. Baseline vital indicators, encompassing heart rate, mean arterial pressure, and body temperature, were documented. Laboratory tests conducted on the first day of intensive care unit (ICU) admission were collected, encompassing potassium, creatinine, hemoglobin, calcium, alanine aminotransferase (ALT), albumin, white blood cell count (WBC), sodium, blood glucose, glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), and blood urea nitrogen (BUN). Moreover, specific scoring systems, like the Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA), Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and Simplified Acute Physiology Score II (SAPS II), were obtained.

Additionally, information regarding the utilization of dexmedetomidine, midazolam, insulin, propofol, mechanical ventilation, and the delivery of vasoactive drugs was collected. The use of corticosteroids was also retrieved.

Outcome

The primary outcome was incident delirium during ICU stay, defined as any positive CAM-ICU assessment after ICU admission. Because CAM-ICU evaluations are routinely conducted only when RASS ≥ − 3, patients with no documented CAM-ICU assessments (often due to deep sedation or mechanical ventilation) were excluded; secondary outcomes included ICU length of stay, hospital length of stay, and 30-day ICU mortality.

SHR and delirium

The stress hyperglycemia index (SHR) is defined as the ratio derived from the following formula: SHR = blood glucose upon admission (mg/dL) ÷ [28.7 × HbA1c (%) − 46.7] [12].

Delirium was assessed using the Confusion Assessment Method for the Intensive Care Unit (CAM-ICU). According to the standard assessment workflow, CAM-ICU evaluation was performed only when the Richmond Agitation–Sedation Scale (RASS) score was ≥ − 3. CAM-ICU contains the following four features: feature 1: acute change or disease fluctuation; feature 2: attention disorder; feature 3: consciousness disorder; and feature 4: thinking disorder. When the patient meets the following two feature combinations, feature 1 + feature 2 + feature 3 and feature 1 + feature 2 + feature 4, the CAM-ICU positive diagnosis can be considered as delirium [13].

Missing data

Variables with missing data proportions exceeding 15%, such as height (31.8%), weight (19.1%), ALT (54.7%), and albumin (71.9%), were excluded from the analysis (Table S1). To handle missing data, we used multiple imputation with the R package “mice.” Five imputed datasets were generated to balance bias reduction with maximal retention of statistical power.

Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were presented as mean ± standard deviation or median (interquartile range), depending on their distribution normality. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages). For intergroup comparisons among the four cohorts, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for continuous variables, while the chi-square test was applied for categorical variables. Non-normally distributed data were analyzed using the Kruskal-Wallis test.

Univariate and multivariate binary logistic regression models were employed to examine the association between the SHR and delirium incidence. Confounding variables were selected based on clinical relevance and the significance of covariates identified in univariate analyses. To assess multicollinearity, the variance inflation factor (VIF) method was used, with a VIF threshold of ≥ 5 indicating multicollinearity. To transparently demonstrate the robustness of the association between SHR and delirium and to minimize the risk of overadjustment, we employed a hierarchical, stepwise adjustment approach, sequentially adding blocks of covariates—from demographic factors to comorbidities and vital signs —then to laboratory indices, treatments, and severity scores. Three sequential models were constructed: Model 1 (adjusted for age, gender, and tobacco), Model 2 (adjusted for Model 1 + HR, MBP, temperature, MI, PVD, CVD, CPD, CHF, diabetes, renal disease, TBI, liver disease, and malignant cancer), and Model 3 (adjusted for Model 2 + creatinine, BUN, WBC, MV, use of vasoactive drugs, insulin, dexmedetomidine, midazolam, propofol, corticosteroids, CCI, SOFA, and SAPSII). SHR was categorized into quartiles (Q1–Q4), with Q1 serving as the reference group to evaluate dose-response relationships while controlling for confounders.

To explore potential nonlinear associations between SHR and delirium risk, restricted cubic splines (RCS) with three knots were applied. The covariates we adjusted include age, gender, HR, MBP, temperature, MI, PVD, CVD, CPD, CHF, diabetes, renal disease, TBI, liver disease, malignant cancer, creatinine, BUN, WBC, MV, and the use of vasoactive drugs, insulin, dexmedetomidine, midazolam, propofol, corticosteroids, CCI, SOFA, and SAPSII. Additionally, piecewise linear regression analysis was conducted to quantitatively identify critical thresholds (inflection points) in the SHR-delirium relationship. The optimal inflection point was determined using likelihood ratio tests by comparing models with different breakpoint positions. The resulting breakpoint should be regarded as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, and no definitive clinical decision threshold is inferred; further validation in independent external cohorts is warranted. All analyses were adjusted for relevant covariates, and effect estimates were reported as adjusted odds ratios (OR) with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CI). To enhance the robustness of our findings, we conducted subgroup analyses and performed interaction tests to thoroughly investigate variable relationships.

To identify robust predictors of delirium in critically ill patients, a Boruta feature selection algorithm based on Random Forest was employed. This method iteratively generates randomly permuted shadow features and compares the distributions of importance between the original and shadow features. The probability that a feature significantly outperforms the shadow features is assessed via a binomial test. After multiple iterations, predictors whose importance is significantly higher than that of the shadow features (two-sided test, P < 0.01) are retained as robust variables [14].

Selected risk-associated factor variables were incorporated into the machine learning workflow. The dataset was randomly split into training and validation sets at a 70:30 ratio. Model tuning was performed on the training set using cross-validation, with key hyperparameters optimized based on classification performance. The filtered predictors were used to train seventeen benchmark test algorithm models: Logistic Regression, K Neighbors Classifier, Naive Bayes, Decision Tree Classifier, SVM-Linear Kernel, Gaussian Process Classifier, MLP Classifier, Ridge Classifier, Random Forest Classifier, Ada Boost Classifier, Gradient Boosting Classifier, Linear Discriminant Analysis, Extra Trees Classifier, Light Gradient Boosting Classifier, Cat Boost Classifier, Dummy Classifier, Extreme Gradient Boosting to explore delirium risk in critically ill patients.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.2; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria; http://www.R-project.org) and a free statistical analysis platform (version 2.1; Beijing, China; http://www.clinicalscientists.cn/freestatistics). P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of study individuals

This study comprised 7,744 patients after thorough screening according to established inclusion and exclusion criteria (Fig. 1). The total prevalence of delirium among these subjects was 26%. The mean age of the recruited participants was 67.0 years, with 63.1% identifying as male (Table 1). Based on the quartile values of the SHR index recorded at admission, participants were classified into four groups: Q1 (0.16–0.84), Q2 (0.84–1.03), Q3 (1.03–1.21), and Q4 (1.21–9.80). Table 1 indicates that patients in the Q4 group exhibited markedly elevated values for heart rate, mean arterial pressure, temperature, potassium levels, BUN, WBC, CCI, and SAPS II, as well as higher rates of ventilation and renal replacement therapy. The Q4 group exhibited significantly higher comorbidity burdens, especially renal illness, liver disease, malignant cancer, MI, and CHF. This cohort demonstrated the highest 30-day mortality rate and the longest ICU admission duration.

Following the revisions, the OR diminished but remained statistically significant in Model 2 (OR = 1.82, 95% CI: 1.61–2.06; P < 0.001) and Model 3 (OR = 1.68, 95% CI: 1.47–1.92; P < 0.001). The association between SHR and the incidence of delirium in critically ill patients

The restricted cubic spline regression analysis, when fully adjusted for confounding variables, indicated that SHR exhibited a nonlinear association with the risk of delirium (P for nonlinearity = 0.005) (Fig. 2). A positive association was observed, with the curve appearing to become steeper at an SHR of 0.94 (OR = 1.88, 95% CI: 1.69–2.06; P < 0.001). This inflection point was derived from spline visualization and piecewise regression and should be interpreted as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating finding rather than a definitive clinical decision point, pending external validation. Below this threshold, the estimated dose-response curve approximated a horizontal line, indicating that the relationship between SHR and delirium was not statistically significant (P = 0.14) (Table S3). Figure S1 shows the performance of SHR and laboratory data. The area under the SHR curve is higher than that of blood glucose and HbA1c.

Discussion

In this large-scale, controlled, retrospective study based on the MIMIC-IV database, our findings indicate a J-shaped, nonlinear relationship between the SHR and the occurrence of delirium in critically ill patients (P for nonlinearity < 0.05). Specifically, restricted cubic spline visualization and piecewise regression suggested a potential inflection at SHR 0.94, after which the slope appeared steeper; this threshold should be interpreted as an exploratory, hypothesis-generating finding rather than a definitive clinical decision point and requires validation in independent external cohorts. Furthermore, subgroup analyses stratified by age, sex, diabetes, dexmedetomidine use, and CCI score revealed no significant interactions among the subgroups. These findings have important implications for current strategies in the management of delirium in critically ill patients.

Previous studies have demonstrated an association between perioperative hyperglycemia and postoperative delirium (POD). A blood glucose level exceeding 8.5 mmol/L during the postoperative period has been identified as a predictor of delirium following hip fracture surgery [15]. Preoperative hyperglycemia constitutes a risk factor for POD in elderly non-diabetic patients undergoing total hip arthroplasty [16]. Another study revealed that among hospitalized elderly patients with HbA1c ≥ 6.5%, the SHR at admission was correlated with increased delirium risk [17]. These findings are consistent with our research results. SHR more precisely represents acute stress-induced hyperglycemia by accounting for chronic glycemic regulation [18, 19]. Increased SHR indicates excessive hyperglycemic responses to stress, presumably reflecting significant disturbances in metabolic homeostasis and inflammatory pathways during critical illness [20].

This imbalance may facilitate delirium via interconnected mechanisms. First, severe stress hyperglycemia exacerbates systemic inflammation by increasing proinflammatory cytokines, including IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α [21, 22]. In critical illness, these cytokines readily cross the compromised blood-brain barrier, activating microglia and triggering neuroinflammation, neuronal dysfunction, and neurotransmitter imbalances, all of which are associated with the pathophysiology of delirium [23, 24]. Second, direct neuronal toxicity: Prolonged hyperglycemia induces neuronal and glial injury through advanced glycation end products (AGEs), mitochondrial malfunction, and apoptosis [25–27]. Thirdly, Endothelial Dysfunction and Oxidative Stress: Acute hyperglycemia disrupts cerebral autoregulation, enhances blood-brain barrier permeability, and promotes the entry of neurotoxic substances, worsening neuronal damage and cognitive deficits [28–30]. Fourthly, Klotho-mediated cognitive effects: Hyperglycemia partially affects cognition through diminished klotho levels [31].

In this context, the SHR should be interpreted primarily as a risk-associated biomarker rather than a stand-alone clinical decision tool. Importantly, because SHR is closely linked to acute physiological derangements in critical illness, it may to some extent reflect overall disease severity. Although the association persisted even after adjustment, causality cannot be inferred from this retrospective observational study. In the absence of defined glycemic management guidelines in intensive care [32, 33], SHR captures the relative magnitude of acute stress-induced hyperglycemia in relation to an individual’s chronic glycemic status. Our findings suggest that elevated SHR may help identify patients at increased risk of delirium. However, it should be viewed as a complementary marker that augments, rather than replaces, established delirium risk assessment strategies. Integrating SHR with clinical evaluation and existing scoring systems may offer incremental value for early risk stratification.

The Boruta algorithm, which identifies the most relevant features for target classification by simulating randomness, has gained significant attention as a widely adopted feature selection method [34]. In this study, the Boruta algorithm’s feature selection results indicated that the SHR is important (located in the “green zone” with a high Z-score). Boruta analysis demonstrated that SHR plays a critical role in achieving the research objectives and is closely associated with the target variable, thereby facilitating the classification of delirium incidence in critically ill patients. However, it should be noted that this does not imply SHR is a definitive determinant. The reasons are as follows: First, although Boruta is a robust feature selection tool, its results may be influenced by feature correlations. Therefore, even if SHR is highly important in the model, it may not be the most critical determinant. Second, multivariable logistic regression analysis confirmed that higher SHR levels are significantly associated with delirium incidence in critically ill patients, consistent with the Boruta algorithm identifying SHR as an important feature. This consistency supports the practical value of SHR in predicting delirium incidence. Furthermore, classification models constructed by incorporating the screened variables into multiple machine learning algorithms all demonstrated favorable performance. In summary, this study’s findings suggest that the SHR may serve as an important biomarker or influencing factor in the incidence of delirium in critically ill patients.

Our work extends the delirium literature by moving beyond absolute glucose measurements and demonstrating a nonlinear, J-shaped association between admission SHR and delirium in a large critical-care cohort. The quantified, hypothesis-generating inflection point (SHR 0.94) provides an interpretable framework for future validation and may help define risk escalation zones. Importantly, the consistent retention of SHR in Boruta feature selection and its stability across subgroup analyses support SHR as a robust, complementary risk-associated biomarker rather than a model-specific artifact.

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, due to the retrospective and single-center design, causal relationships between the SHR and delirium cannot be established. Residual confounding from unmeasured variables may persist despite comprehensive multivariable adjustment. Second, HbA1c is not a routine test in many ICU settings; in our cohort, it was available for only 18.5% of eligible ICU patients, limiting the bedside applicability and generalizability of SHR-based association analysis. Furthermore, HbA1c testing is often preferentially conducted in patients with suspected dysglycemia or established diabetes, which may introduce selection bias related to ordering practices.

Additionally, the identification of delirium was dependent on routine CAM-ICU documentation and required patients to be sufficiently arousable (RASS ≥ − 3). Hypoactive delirium may be inadequately detected in patients who are deeply sedated or mechanically ventilated, resulting in outcome misclassification bias that could lead to an underestimation of the observed associations. Fourth, only baseline SHR was evaluated; dynamic changes in SHR during ICU stay and long-term neurocognitive outcomes were not assessed. Therefore, prospective, multicenter studies are warranted to validate and extend these findings.

Conclusion

Admission SHR was independently and non-linearly associated with delirium in critically ill patients. The observed inflection point (SHR 0.94) requires validation in an external independent cohort. SHR may complement existing delirium risk assessment methods, pending external validation.
