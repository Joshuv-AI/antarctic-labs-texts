Clinical analysis of supplemental maxillary lateral incisors: a retrospective cross-sectional study
Authors: Shan Meng, Jing Cai, Fenfang Qiu, Lihong Shu, Guangzheng Han, Zhenghui Xiong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Statement of problemSupplemental teeth are supernumerary replicas of normal teeth. They often involve the maxillary lateral incisors (MxLI), affecting both function and aesthetics of anterior dentition.PurposeTo investigate the prevalence of supplemental MxLI through panoramic radiographs, analysing distribution patterns and clinical attributes.MethodsWe retrieved panoramic radiographs of 146,538 patients treated at Suzhou Stomatological Hospital between January 2014 and December 2024. All instances of supplemental MxLI were identified and recorded for statistical analysis (SPSS v24.0).ResultsThe overall rate of supplemental MxLI was 0.205%(300/146538), with male patients accounting for the preponderance (208 vs. 92; p < 0.001). Left and right sides were similarly involved, displaying 146 and 167 supplemental teeth, respectively (p > 0.05). Most supplemental teeth were unilateral (287 [95.67%] and 13 [4.33%]patients have bilateral supplemental teeth; p < 0.01), with 72.67% occurring in mixed dentition. Some (5.67%) were fused with regular MxLI. However, the vast majority (91.00%) were accompanied by tooth torsion, dentition crowding, midline disorder, disturbed eruption of adjacent teeth, or other abnormalities (i.e., supernumerary teeth at other sites, congenitally missing teeth, microdontia of MxLI, tooth developmental delay, and more).ConclusionSupplemental MxLI are rarely seen in clinics, occurring at a low rate (0.205%). There is no apparent right or left predilection, but most are unilateral occurrences. On occasion, they may be fused with regular MxLI, and a majority are accompanied by other dental developmental abnormalities. Supplemental MxLI constitute a special variety of supernumerary teeth, creating a number of aesthetic and functional problems. Panoramic radiographs enable early detection of developmental abnormalities during permanent tooth eruption so that reasonable treatment plans may be devised.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00784-025-06696-5.

Introduction

Supernumerary teeth are dental anomalies characterized by an excessive number of teeth relative to normal dentition [1]. Supernumerary teeth, which are eumorphic to adjacent teeth at the end of a tooth series and well aligned in the arch are known as supplementary teeth [2]. In the primary dentition, most supernumerary teeth are supplemental, with a very low prevalence of 0.3–0.8% [3]. In the permanent dentition, the incidence of supplemental supernumerary teeth ranges from 0.1% to 3.8% [4–6], and the most commonly affected tooth is the MxLI [7]. Given the paucity of published cases, we analyzed 300 patients diagnosed with supplemental MxLI to identify and document their clinical characteristics. By organizing relevant case data and verifying diagnostic and therapeutic efficacy, this study aims to provide references for establishing standardized clinical pathways, promote the standardized development of diagnosis and treatment in orthodontics and pediatric dentistry, and ultimately improve patients’ oral health quality and reduce disease burden.

Materials and methods

Patient population

Study candidates (N = 146,538) were 3–18-year-old children and adolescents who underwent panoramic radiography at our hospital between January 2014 and December 2024, presenting for routine examinations, dental disease treatment, orthodontic care, or other dental services. This age range was chosen to cover the full critical period of dental development—from the completion of primary dentition to the near-complete eruption of permanent dentition. We retrieved radiographs of 75,797 males and 70,741 females (male-to-female ratio: 1.07:1), among whom 300 were diagnosed with supplemental teeth. Eligible subjects met three criteria: (1) presence of supplemental MxLI; (2) ability to undergo oral and panoramic radiographic examinations; (3) clear digital panoramic radiographs without anatomic distortion. Exclusion criteria included: (1) blurred digital imaging, tooth germ obscurity, or unclear/overlapping structural anatomy; or (2) systemic abnormality, ectodermal dysplasia syndrome, cleft lip or palate, Down’s syndrome, or other prohibitive ailment.

Research methods

Panoramic radiographs (assessed under standardized lighting, screen brightness, and resolution) were jointly interpreted by two in-hospital pediatric stomatologists with > 5 years of experience each. Interexaminer reliability was tested using 100 radiographs, resulting in a kappa value of 0.82. The diagnosis of a supplemental MxLI hinges on two core pillars: (1) panoramic radiography as the primary imaging tool to localize the tooth and assess its relationship with adjacent structures; and (2) confirmation of the supplemental tooth’s morphological similarity to neighboring teeth (a key feature distinguishing it from other supernumerary teeth).

Patient confidentiality was maintained via unique protocol and case numbers from database records. A total of 300 supplemental tooth cases were enrolled, with concurrent documentation of other dental developmental abnormalities (e.g., supernumerary teeth, congenital absence). Diagnoses adhered to the standards outlined in Pediatric Stomatology (5th Edition) [8].

Statistical analysis

The qualitative data we collected were expressed as n (%), using Chi-square test to assess differences in supplemental tooth distribution according to sex and laterality (right vs. left; one or both sides). All computations were driven by standard software (SPSS v24.0; IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA).

Discussion

Clinical manifestations of supplemental teeth

The prevalence of permanent dentition supplemental teeth varies significantly across populations. In Asians, reported rates are 2.7–3.4% [4]; in Caucasians, the range is broader (0.1–3.8%) [5]; notably, Indians have a lower prevalence (1.2%), while Nigerians and Turks show higher rates (2.14–9.06%) [9–11]. A South Indian study reported a male-to-female ratio of 1.49:0.85, with the posterior maxillary region being the most commonly affected (77.8%) [12]. Though supernumerary canines and lateral incisors are rare (2.8%) [13], supplemental teeth in the permanent dentition most often involve the MxLI [14]. These cross-population differences suggest ethnic or regional factors may influence the occurrence of this dental anomaly.

In our 10-year hospital-based study, the prevalence of supplemental MxLI was 0.205%—relatively low compared to the population-level prevalence of permanent supplemental teeth noted above. Consistent with prior findings highlighting male predominance in supplemental tooth occurrence [15–17], our data showed a male-to-female ratio of ~ 2.26:1 (208 males vs. 92 females) for supplemental MxLI, which is higher than the 1.2:1 ratio reported in past studies (1954–2023) involving 11 MxLI cases [2, 7, 18, 19]. While our analysis revealed no significant laterality bias (146 left vs. 167 right cases), supplemental MxLI was predominantly unilateral (287 cases) rather than bilateral (13 cases)—a finding that differs from earlier case reports.

Causes of supernumerary teeth

At present, there is no clear consensus on the etiology of supernumerary teeth, with four prevailing theories summarized as follows:Tooth germ dichotomy theory: The tooth bud splits into two parts (equal or differentin size), producing two teeth (either of equal size, or one normal and onedysmorphic) [20].Dental lamina hyperactivity theory: Supernumerary teeth originate from independentlocal hyperactivity of the dental lamina, which is more widely accepted [21].Atavism theory: It refers to the occasional biological “regression” where anindividual exhibits morphological structures of its species’ ancestors [22].Genetic theory: Supernumerary teeth are associated with specific syndromes, such ascleidocranial dysplasia, Trichorhinophalangeal syndrome type I, and Rubinstein-Taybi syndrome, among 8 syndromes with relatively high incidence of this anomaly [23].

For the morphologically similar supplemental MxLI in our cohort, their origin aligns better with the tooth germ dichotomy theory, while the widely accepted dental lamina hyperactivity theory also offers a potential etiological perspective.

Supplemental teeth with other abnormalities

In our cohort, 23 patients with supplemental MxLI were also accompanied by 24 deciduous supplemental teeth, which were morphologically similar to adjacent MxLI. These small deciduous supplemental teeth often erupted normally and aligned neatly (Figs. 7, 8 and 11), while crowding and obstruction (as in Fig. 16) were rarely observed.

While our focus was on the clinical manifestations of permanent MxLI, we also identified supplemental teeth at other permanent incisor sites during the study: 2 in maxillary central incisors and 1 in mandibular lateral incisors. Fusion between supplemental and regular permanent teeth is rare (Figs. 9 and 10). Supplemental teeth are deemed a distinct variety of supernumerary teeth. Six of our patients displayed supernumerary teeth of maxillary midline areas (all of which were conical), including 1 with inverted growth nd 5 with of positive growth, as shown in Fig. 11. Unilateral supplemental teeth and maxillary supernumerary teeth in the midline region(Figs. 5), bilateral supplemental teeth (Figs. 2, 3 and 4), other position permanent and MxLI supplemental teeth(Figs. 9 and 10), as well as separately shown deciduous and inherited permanent supplemental teeth (Figs. 7, 8 and 11, and 16), belong to the same individual and were not part of a developmental disorder. In contrast, multiple supernumerary teeth are typical of dysplastic syndromes such as cleidocranial dysostosis, Gardner’s syndrome, and cleft lip/palate [24, 25].

Among our patients, 24 had 29 additional congenital permanent tooth agenesis cases. Affected sites included mandibular lateral incisors (14; Fig. 3), mandibular second premolars (7; Fig. 12), mandibular central incisors (3), maxillary first premolars (2), MxLI (2; Fig. 13), and 1 maxillary canine (Fig. 14). One patient also had a congenitally missing deciduous tooth (Fig. 16). The coexistence of supernumerary and congenitally missing teeth is a rare dental anomaly with unknown etiology. Nirmala et al. [26] argued that this concurrence arises from disrupted differentiation, migration, and proliferation of neural crest cells, caused by epithelial-mesenchymal cell interaction abnormalities during odontogenesis initiation.

There was one of our patients with permanent tooth fusion and one with deciduous tooth fusion. Ultimately, fused and supernumerary teeth occurred simultaneously in the same patient. Literature reports describe fusion of supernumerary teeth with mandibular molars (second/third) or maxillary central incisors (distal supernumerary teeth) [27–29]. In our two patients, however, supplemental teeth were located in the maxilla, while fused teeth were in the mandible (Figs. 15 and 16).

Bilge et al. [30] have reported a 7.87% (37/470) incidence of microdontia, and according to Gokkaya et al. [31], the incidence of microdontia in patients with congenitally missing teeth is 5.6% (10/177). There were 912 MxLI in our cohort, with microdontia (Figs. 4 and 10, and 13) occurring at a rate of 11.62% (106/912). This rate slightly exceeds those cited elsewhere in the literature.

Delayed tooth development (DTD) refers to delayed tooth germ development caused by local or systemic factors. In this study, only two cases of DTD were observed in the examined MxLI (Fig. 17)—defined as the same tooth in the same age group showing at least a two-stage lag in Nolla staging, attributed to local or systemic factors [32–34]. In our prior study, we found that individual tooth developmental delays most commonly involved mandibular second premolars, followed by MxLI and maxillary second premolars [35].

A single instance of triple teeth (3 left upper lateral incisors, no fusion) surfaced within our cohort and is illustrated in Fig. 18. Elsewhere in the literature, three fused deciduous teeth have been reported [36, 37], and there is one case report of triple permanent teeth involving left maxillary central and lateral incisors and one supernumerary tooth fused [38].

Critical consideration should be given to whether and when to extract supplemental teeth. If these teeth are well-aligned in the dentition (Figs. 1 and 13), observation alone may be chosen. In the absence of clinical symptoms, no treatment is needed for fused supplemental teeth and lateral incisors—though pit-and-fissure sealants or preventive restorations can be used at fusion lines. Treatment is only indicated for cases with caries, periodontal disease, crowding, or aesthetic concerns, and optimal management depends on fusion degree, patient age, and overall periodontal health.

Treatment of fused teeth typically requires multidisciplinary input [39]. When crowding causes supplemental tooth torsion, selecting which tooth to extract can be challenging due to their structural similarity—key considerations include crown shape, root length, and tooth development, with priority given to teeth with normal crown shape, slender root contours, and no obvious displacement [40]. Additionally, the need for subsequent orthodontic treatment must be determined. One must also decide whether orthodontics are needed thereafter. Timing of extraction is most critical: supplemental teeth usually erupt during the deciduous or mixed dentition stages. Once erupted, extraction plans should be carefully designed to minimize surgical trauma.

Summary

In conclusion, the prevalence of supplemental MxLI is very low, and it may be associated with various concurrent anomalies. Dentists should conduct thorough clinical and radiographic oral examinations before confirming a diagnosis and formulating a treatment plan. For practitioners, radiographic imaging is essential to complement clinical examinations—helping identify patients’ dental developmental anomalies, as well as pathological conditions of the hard/soft tissues, jaws, and related structures. Comprehensive interdisciplinary management is imperative for treating such cases.
