Transcranial ultrasonography via sonolucent implants: a review
Authors: Marcio Yuri Ferreira, Alleh Nogueira, Artur Shlifer, Vadim Zhigin, Faith Singh, Tamika Wong, Olivia Albers, Sana Bokil, Brianna Suffren, Raphael Bertani, David Langer, Netanel Ben-Shalom
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Transcranial ultrasound (TCUS) imaging techniques via sonolucent implants have demonstrated clinical utility across a variety of settings and are increasingly being adopted in neurosurgical and neurocritical care practices worldwide. This review appraises the use of ultrasound through sonolucent implants in clinical contexts, providing a critical evaluation of its diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic potential. We discuss its application in selected areas such as ventricular size assessment and hydrocephalus monitoring, postoperative surveillance for fluid collections and hemorrhage, bypass patency assessment in cerebrovascular surgery, and functional brain imaging. Emerging interventional use of this technique as a platform for focused ultrasound therapies is also explored. Future directions for research and clinical integration are discussed. Technical challenges and inherent limitations of ultrasound data acquired through sonolucent implants — including device-specific considerations, imaging artifacts, and the absence of standardized protocols — are critically addressed, alongside a forward-looking discussion of research priorities and barriers to clinical adoption.

Introduction

Sonolucent cranial implants refer to a range of bone defect replacements (including prostheses and burr hole covers) made from materials that allow ultrasound penetration. Clinicians have historically avoided ultrasound for intracranial assessment due to poor penetration through bone and standard reconstructive materials [13]. Designed for point-of-care ultrasonographic monitoring of intracranial structures, sonolucent implants facilitate an alternative to conventional imaging methods — computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging [13]. Neither conventional approach qualifies as a point-of-care assessment, necessitating inpatient transport or separate outpatient appointments [13]. Sonolucent cranial implants span two principal clinical categories. The first comprises burr hole covers — small, disc-shaped prostheses inserted at the time of a standard neurosurgical procedure (e.g., ventriculostomy or shunt placement) in patients with an otherwise intact calvarium. The second comprises cranioplasty implants — larger reconstructive prostheses used in patients who have previously undergone decompressive craniectomy and subsequently require skull restoration. Although both device types are fabricated from sonolucent materials and share the goal of enabling transcranial ultrasonographic access, the clinical indications, implant dimensions, insonation angles, and anticipated imaging targets differ substantially between settings and should not be conflated. This review builds upon and extends prior appraisals of sonolucent cranioplasty, incorporating more recent evidence and offering a structured clinical framework that addresses diagnostic, prognostic, and therapeutic applications in an integrated manner. Where prior reviews have focused primarily on technical feasibility, we additionally appraise the clinical context, patient selection considerations, image acquisition parameters, and future research priorities relevant to routine neurosurgical practice.

Computed tomography exposes patients to ionizing radiation, posing cumulative risks from frequent use or intermittent use sustained over time [24]. Although radiation-free, magnetic resonance imaging is more costly, logistically challenging, less accessible, and contraindicated or prone to artifacts in patients with metallic implants [13, 27]. Compared with such alternatives, ultrasonography may reduce costs and logistical complexity, permits both anatomical and physiological assessment, enables continuous real-time monitoring without additional financial or operational burden, and allows more rapid evaluation [13, 15]. While especially valuable in neurocritical care, these capabilities may also offer greater clinical and economic advantages than conventional imaging in noncritical patients with cranial implants [13, 15]. Beyond its diagnostic role in intracranial monitoring, focused ultrasound via sonolucent implants is increasingly regarded to guide interventions with enhanced efficacy, safety, and seamlessness — particularly in functional neurosurgery and neuro-oncology [1, 5, 12, 13, 15, 16]. Herein, we present a critical, evidence-based review of transcranial ultrasonography through sonolucent implants, placing this emerging modality in context for the practicing clinician. We discuss the feasibility, reliability, safety, and clinical applications; insights for optimal performance; and future directions to enhance evidence, training and education, clinical indications, and practical implementation. The images reported in this study are real cases, and every patient signed informed consent and authorized the use of the images for research and publications.

Clinical usefulness

Feasibility and reliability

Converging evidence from in vitro, cadaveric, and in vivo studies supports the technical feasibility and clinical safety of ultrasonographic imaging through sonolucent cranial materials — including polyolefin, polymethylmethacrylate (PMMA), and polyetheretherketone (PEEK) — with image quality sufficient for gross assessment of intracranial structures, particularly ventricular caliber in patients with hydrocephalus or cerebrospinal fluid disorders [2, 10, 15, 17, 19]. In vitro, cadaveric, and in vivo studies consistently demonstrate that sonolucent implants transmit ultrasound energy with minimal attenuation and spatial distortion, supporting their proper use as acoustic windows [2, 10, 15, 17, 19].

Data on reliability remains limited, as early published studies have primarily focused on feasibility and safety endpoints [2, 10, 15, 17, 19]. Given that sonolucent materials have been shown to transmit ultrasound energy with minimal attenuation and spatial distortion — compared to native bone [17], through which ultrasound has demonstrated good intra-observer and interobserver agreement [21, 28] — it is reasonable to expect adequate reliability when ultrasound scans through sonolucent implants are performed by trained operators. To support proper validation, future studies should systematically assess and report reliability metrics.

A fundamental question underlying the clinical adoption of sonolucent cranial implants is whether the frequency and severity of early postoperative intracranial complications justify the deliberate selection of a sonolucent material over standard alloplastic alternatives. Based on published cranioplasty series, the incidence of epidural hematoma approximates 2–4%, cerebrospinal fluid leak 1–4%, and shunt malfunction in hydrocephalus patients is a recognized ongoing risk. These complications are most likely to manifest in the early postoperative period — precisely when point-of-care ultrasonographic surveillance would offer the greatest incremental value over scheduled outpatient imaging. The ability to perform bedside transcranial ultrasound without ionizing radiation, patient transport, or scheduling delays may represent a meaningful clinical advantage in this window, particularly in neurocritical care settings. That said, the incremental benefit of a sonolucent implant over native transcranial windows (when present), or over standard CT-based surveillance algorithms, has not been formally quantified in comparative studies. Future research should directly compare monitoring outcomes between patients receiving sonolucent versus standard implants, and should assess whether sonolucent implant–enabled surveillance reduces time to diagnosis, rates of delayed complication, or need for reoperation. Until such evidence is available, the decision to select a sonolucent implant should be individualized and informed by the anticipated monitoring need, expected complication risk, and the availability of alternative imaging resources.

Safety and tolerability

The safety profile of sonolucent cranial implants parallels that of traditional alloplastic implants. An increased risk of infection or revision specifically attributable to the sonolucent properties of the implant compared with standard materials has not been identified [6]. Relatively infrequent complications have been documented. Subcutaneous swelling or bulging associated with resorbable plates has been reported, usually with a benign and self-limiting course [3, 6, 10, 11, 14, 19, 23]. While cosmetic outcomes and patient satisfaction are generally favorable, chronic palpability or visibility may be more evident in regions with thin overlying soft tissue or among pediatric patients [3, 6, 10, 11, 14, 19, 23]. Long-term data are scarce, underscoring the need for larger, multicenter studies to elucidate rare or delayed complications and to confirm safety across diverse patient populations.

Most reported adverse events include wound infection (1–4%), delayed scalp healing or wound revision (2–3%), epidural hematoma (2–4%), cerebrospinal fluid leak (1–4%), and new-onset seizures (1–4%) [11, 19, 23]. Chronic swelling, implant visibility, or palpability (particularly with resorbable or bioresorbable implants) has also been reported, with pooled incidence rates ranging from 7 to 21%, depending on implant type and patient population [3, 14]. Rarer complications such as implant exposure, sinus formation, and the need for reoperation or implant removal due to infection or tumor recurrence have been reported, but appear to occur in fewer than 1% to 4% of cases [3, 19].

Diagnostic applications

Preliminary studies suggest that sonolucent cranioplasty materials provide effective acoustic windows for both qualitative and quantitative assessment of midline structures, ventricular anatomy, and superficial cortical surfaces, with measurements demonstrating strong concordance with computed tomography [2, 6, 10, 15, 17–19, 25]. Although early clinical and preclinical studies support these applications, much of the existing literature is limited by restricted sample sizes, qualitative endpoints, and a lack of systematic validation across diverse neurological conditions. The most consistent data support use in the evaluation of hydrocephalus and cerebrospinal fluid disorders, as well as assessment of bypass graft patency. More emergent applications, including functional imaging and broader neurodiagnostic use, show promise but remain less well validated. Some potential indications warrant consideration:

Therapeutic applications

Cranial ultrasound–guided interventions have been limited by the acoustic impedance of the native calvarium. Sonolucent cranial implants provide a critical opportunity to overcome this barrier. Therapeutic applications of sonolucent transcranial ultrasonography focus primarily on enabling or enhancing focused ultrasound interventions. It should be noted that high-intensity transcranial focused ultrasound systems designed for clinical use — such as transcranial MR-guided focused ultrasound (tcMRgFUS) platforms — achieve both blood–brain barrier disruption and thermal ablation through the intact skull, without requiring a sonolucent implant. These systems employ large multi-element phased arrays and sophisticated skull-correction algorithms to compensate for bone-mediated acoustic aberration. The role of sonolucent implants in the focused ultrasound context is therefore distinct: they are most relevant for lower-intensity or more portable applications in which a defined, artifact-free acoustic window facilitates targeting, monitoring, or repeated access — particularly in resource-limited or point-of-care settings. Future comparative studies should clarify which clinical scenarios are best served by implant-based versus transcranial approaches. Most studied therapeutic uses of focused ultrasound include:

Blood–brain barrier disruption for enhanced drug delivery [1, 5, 12, 16] — Focused ultrasound–mediated, transient, and localized blood–brain barrier opening is the most advanced therapeutic application, with clinical trials demonstrating feasibility and safety in patients with primary and secondary brain tumors. This approach improves delivery of chemotherapeutic agents, monoclonal antibodies, and other treatments that typically have limited central nervous system penetration. Most clinical data focus on high-grade gliomas, including glioblastoma and anaplastic astrocytoma, though blood–brain barrier disruption has also been explored in brain metastases from systemic cancers. Tumor characteristics such as location (superficial versus deep), size, and proximity to eloquent cortex influence both the technical feasibility and safety of focused ultrasound–mediated blood–brain barrier opening. Sonolucent implants may enhance point-of-care ultrasound visualization, improving assessment and procedural guidance. Moreover, focused ultrasound–facilitated blood–brain barrier opening may promote the release of tumor-derived biomarkers (such as circulating tumor DNA) into the peripheral circulation, potentially improving diagnosis and monitoring. Prognostic applications

Prognostic applications of ultrasound imaging through sonolucent cranial implants remain largely investigational, with only preliminary qualitative data reported. Further studies are warranted to validate optimal biomarkers derived from this approach and to determine their prognostic value. Several features make sonolucent transcranial ultrasound a potentially valuable tool for prognosis. Preliminary data demonstrate high feasibility and safety for serial imaging, strong correlation with computed tomography findings, and the capacity to provide actionable information at the bedside. Implant-related complication rates are low, and this technique may even reduce the need for repeated costly or radiation-exposing imaging studies. Image acquisition and interpretation

Choice of region of interest

Selection of the region of interest for sonolucent cranial implants should be informed by clinical and technical factors to optimize transcranial ultrasonography [6, 19]. Foremost is the anticipated need for serial imaging of specific intracranial structures or vascular territories [6, 19]. For example, in patients undergoing extracranial–intracranial bypass, the implant is typically positioned over the vascular anastomosis site (such as the pterional region for superficial temporal artery–middle cerebral artery bypass or the parietooccipital region for occipital artery–middle cerebral artery bypass) to facilitate direct visualization and monitoring of graft patency. Additional considerations include the underlying pathology, relevant anatomical targets for surveillance, and the feasibility of implant placement regarding surgical access and cosmetic outcome [6, 19].

Implant size and cranial position critically determine the insonation angle, depth of penetration, and the spectrum of intracranial structures accessible for evaluation. Frontal or temporal placements generally optimize visualization of the anterior ventricular system, corpus callosum, and midline structures, while parietal or parieto-occipital placements are preferred for posterior vascular territory assessment (e.g., occipital artery–MCA bypass surveillance). Burr hole covers, by virtue of their smaller aperture (~ 14–20 mm diameter), provide a limited but focused acoustic window, whereas larger cranioplasty implants permit broader field-of-view imaging with greater angular flexibility.

Minimizing implant site curvature is advisable to reduce sonographic artifacts and maintain image quality [6, 19]. The presence of hardware or materials such as plating systems and dural sealants near the implant can also introduce artifacts and should be avoided when clinically appropriate and feasible [6, 19]. The implant thickness and material composition should be chosen to optimize sonolucency and minimize ultrasound attenuation; however, comparative data evaluating these properties across different materials remain limited. Patient preferences and cosmetic outcomes are especially important in elective procedures, with favorable aesthetic results expected when site selection is carefully planned [6, 19].

Choice of assessment timing

Optimal timing for transcranial ultrasound through sonolucent implants has not been established and awaits proper investigation. Imaging should be individualized based on the patient’s clinical status and specific indications. The sonolucent properties of materials generally allow for high-quality imaging once the patient is clinically stable [2, 10]. Early postoperative ultrasound is both feasible and safe, with timing primarily guided by clinical need rather than implant integration or healing [2, 10]. Current evidence does not indicate a required waiting period before imaging; instead, it is driven by clinical considerations such as assessment of postoperative complications, monitoring of ventricular size, or surveillance for fluid collections. Clinicians should be aware that early postoperative conditions, such as fluid accumulation, residual air, and tissue swelling, can temporarily reduce image quality; As these changes subside and the tissue integrates with the implant, acoustic coupling improves, resulting in clearer ultrasound images [19].

Image acquisition and interpretation

Transducer selection for transcranial ultrasonography through sonolucent implants should be guided by both the size of the acoustic window and the clinical target. For burr hole covers, a low-frequency phased-array (sector) probe (2–5 MHz) is typically employed, analogous to conventional transcranial Doppler settings, as the small aperture necessitates a footprint compatible with the cover diameter. For larger cranioplasty implants, either a phased-array or a linear transducer may be appropriate depending on the depth of the target structure; higher-frequency linear probes (7–15 MHz) may provide superior resolution for superficial structures, while lower frequencies are preferred for deep targets such as the third ventricle or deep perforators. Regardless of probe type, standard “knobology” precautions include: (1) applying liberal amounts of acoustic coupling gel to eliminate air gaps between the probe and the implant surface; (2) optimizing depth, focal zone, and gain prior to each examination; (3) using tissue harmonic imaging when available to reduce artifact; and (4) documenting probe type, frequency, depth, and gain settings to enable reproducibility and cross-operator comparison. Standardized documentation of these parameters is particularly important for serial monitoring and multicenter research.

Factors that may affect image assessment

Clinical factors influencing the assessment and interpretation of transcranial ultrasound through sonolucent implants remain poorly characterized. In contrast, several technical variables have been demonstrated to affect image quality. Acoustic properties of implant materials likely impact ultrasound transmission, though comparative data are lacking. Thicker implants may attenuate the ultrasound signal more than thinner ones, diminishing image quality and signal-to-noise ratio [17, 18]. Nonetheless, attenuation remains substantially lower than that of native cranial bone, and spatial distortion is minimal when using well-designed implants [17, 18].

Implant curvature can introduce artifacts or distortions; highly curved prostheses may cause beam refraction or defocusing, resulting in peripheral image artifacts or reduced resolution [6, 19]. Artifacts may also arise from the implant itself, underlying hardware such as plating systems, pneumocephalus, or dural sealants, complicating image interpretation [19]. The type of dural substitute used beneath the implant likely differentially affects ultrasound transmission: benchtop models suggest that substitutes including DuraGen (Integra LifeSciences, Princeton, NJ, USA) and Durepair (Integra LifeSciences, Princeton, NJ, USA) have minimal impact on image quality, whereas others, such as DuraMatrix-Onlay (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) and DuraMatrix Suturable (Stryker, Kalamazoo, MI, USA) [6].

Transducer frequency influences resolution and penetration depth. Higher-frequency probes offer superior spatial resolution but are more susceptible to attenuation, even though sonolucent materials, whereas lower-frequency probes achieve greater penetration at the cost of resolution [19]. Selection of the transducer should be tailored to the clinical context and implant properties [19]. Optimal probe positioning, application of acoustic gel, and adjustment of imaging parameters are critical to maximize image quality and reduce artifacts [19].

Future directions

Challenges and timeframe to clinical adoption

Several barriers limit the widespread clinical adoption of sonolucent cranial implants for transcranial ultrasound. Variability in imaging parameters, qualitative rather than quantitative reporting, and heterogeneity in implant materials and surgical adjuncts contribute to inconsistent image quality and interpretation across centers. Challenges include material and design limitations, interference from surgical materials, imaging artifacts, and lack of standardized imaging protocols. Most studies to date have focused on qualitative findings, highlighting the need for systematic collection of quantitative data to validate and standardize imaging approaches. Successful clinical integration will require operator training to recognize the unique imaging characteristics and potential artifacts of sonolucent implants. Promising early data is limited by small samples, heterogeneity, and short follow-up, slowing adoption. Experts estimate that widespread implementation may require 5 to 10 years [13].

The need for standardization, training, and credentialing

Standardized protocols for transcranial ultrasound through sonolucent cranial implants are critical to enhance clinical adoption and utility. Defining optimal ultrasound settings, transducer selection, and procedural steps can reduce variability and improve image quality. This is critical given that implant properties and surgical materials affect acoustic transmission and artifact formation. Protocols should also guide artifact management, patient positioning, and compatible dural substitute selection. Experience from other ultrasound applications highlights the importance of standardization to ensure safety, efficacy, and consistent results across centers. Because insonation perspectives are inherently constrained by implant geometry and placement, direct comparison of quantitative measurements across patients — or across centers — requires careful documentation and standardization of implant location, size, and orientation. This variability represents a significant methodological challenge for multicenter studies and underscores the need for consensus reporting standards. Protocols would also enable systematic collection of quantitative data necessary for validation, quality control, regulatory approval, and large-scale studies. Such efforts are vital to develop evidence-based guidelines and enable integration into routine neurosurgical and critical care practice.

Expanding indications

The most robust evidence for diagnostic use of transcranial ultrasonography via sonolucent cranial implants pertains to hydrocephalus, cerebrospinal fluid disorders and bypass patency assessment. In contrast, evidence supporting functional imaging and broader neurodiagnostic applications is even more preliminary and requires further validation. Therapeutic applications have been primarily explored in high-grade gliomas, especially glioblastoma, with early-phase studies extending to brain metastases and other primary central nervous system tumors. The feasibility and safety of these interventions depend heavily on tumor characteristics, including location, size, vascularity, and proximity to eloquent brain regions. Future clinical trials are expected to clarify and expand the clinical role of sonolucent implant–facilitated ultrasound.

To validate clinical applications for diagnosis, intervention, and ultrasound-derived prognostic markers, several critical steps are required. First, larger prospective studies are needed to systematically acquire quantitative ultrasound measurements (such as ventricular dimensions, parenchymal metrics, and hemodynamic parameters) across diverse patient populations and clinical contexts. These data should be directly compared with gold-standard imaging modalities to confirm accuracy, reproducibility, and inter-operator reliability. Preliminary findings show promising correlations in ventricular size monitoring among patients with cranioplasty but are limited by small cohorts and narrow clinical indications.

Second, multicenter trials using standardized imaging protocols are essential to evaluate the generalizability of quantitative ultrasound markers and to establish clinically meaningful thresholds for diagnosis, treatment, or prognosis. For instance, in the setting of blood–brain barrier disruption, quantitative algorithms correlating imaging changes with local acoustic pressure have been developed and validated against qualitative clinical grading, but further validation in larger, heterogeneous cohorts is necessary to determine prognostic relevance for outcomes such as tumor control or neurological function.

Third, longitudinal studies should assess how temporal changes in qualitative and quantitative ultrasound markers relate to clinical outcomes, including neurological recovery, shunt function, and tumor recurrence. These efforts will help clarify the prognostic value of ultrasound markers and support their integration into clinical decision-making. Artificial intelligence

Artificial intelligence (AI) is already being widely utilized to segment magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computed tomography (CT) images across a diverse range of clinical applications, thereby improving diagnostic accuracy, workflow efficiency, and treatment planning. Additionally, AI-based segmentation methods are increasingly being applied to ultrasound images for various diagnostic purposes [4, 8]. We believe that implementing AI-driven segmentation techniques for transcranial ultrasound through sonolucent implants holds significant promise, with the potential to unlock new possibilities and maximize the clinical value of TCUS in the coming decades. At our department, we are actively investigating and developing such software, and ongoing studies will report these findings in the years ahead. Bridging gaps in evidence

Sonolucent cranial implants offer a promising avenue for transcranial ultrasound, yet several important gaps hinder thorough evaluation. Standardized reporting of procedural details and outcomes is urgently needed to enable meaningful comparisons across studies and accelerate the translation of research into practice. Most current data derive from highly selected cohorts, limiting validation in more diverse populations and clinical contexts. Although feasibility and safety are better documented for applications such as ventricular measurement in hydrocephalus and cerebrospinal fluid disorders, comprehensive assessments in other indications remain scarce.

The safety profile of sonolucent implants appears comparable to that of other alloplastic cranioplasty materials, with no unique adverse events clearly attributable to sonolucency. Nonetheless, long-term safety data are limited, warranting multicenter studies to characterize rare or delayed complications and confirm safety across broader populations.

Many studies are underpowered and lack the methodological rigor and follow-up duration necessary to identify rare or delayed complications. Crucially, there is little data addressing how implant size, location, or material properties affect imaging quality and safety.

High-quality, prospectively collected data on patient-reported outcomes and long-term safety are notably absent. The influence of patient-specific factors such as age, comorbidities, and complex clinical conditions remains poorly understood. Moreover, there is a dearth of studies in high-risk or special populations (including emergency cases, patients with obesity, or immunocompromised individuals) further limiting the generalizability of existing findings.

While transcranial ultrasound through native bone windows demonstrates high feasibility and strong concordance with computed tomography or magnetic resonance imaging for measures such as third ventricle diameter, these results cannot be directly applied to imaging through sonolucent implants due to differences in acoustic properties and potential artifacts. A key deficit is the absence of published data on intra-observer and interobserver agreements for sonolucent implant–based ultrasound. Future investigations must systematically assess and report reliability metrics to validate this modality adequately.

Conclusions

The growing use of transcranial ultrasound through sonolucent implants in both research and clinical settings suggests that this technique is to become a standard component of neuroimaging and neurosurgical practice. Continued training, education, and quality assurance efforts may help mitigate technical challenges and limitations, improving the accuracy and value of image acquisition and interpretation. As outlined in this review, diagnostic, therapeutic and prognostic insights continue to emerge as the technology is applied across diverse patient populations and neurological conditions. Future studies will yield the foundation for standardized protocols, enabling faster and smoother widespread adoption of routine transcranial ultrasound in Neurosurgery. The integration of artificial intelligence for image segmentation and interpretation is promising and warrants investigation.
