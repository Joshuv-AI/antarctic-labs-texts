A novel pathogenic mutation of DDX58 causes Singleton-Merten syndrome
Authors: Siming Peng, Jinbo Wang, Yusha Wang, Yiying Dou, Wei Wang, Yuhao Yao, Yongguo Yu, Qing Zhou, Xiaomin Yu, Hongmei Song
Published: 2025

DDX58 (DExD/H-box helicase 58) encodes retinoic acid-inducible gene I (RIG-I), a crucial cytosolic dsRNA sensor that recognizes dsRNA from infected viruses and activates the type I interferon (IFN-I) pathway [1]. In the absence of viral dsRNA, the caspase activation and recruitment domains (CARDs) of RIG-I interact with the DEAD helicase domain, which leads RIG-I to an autoinhibited conformation. During viral infection, the C-terminal repressor domain recognizes and binds target RNA, releasing CARDs to interact with mitochondrial antiviral signaling protein (MAVS) [2]. Antiviral signaling is transmitted to TBK1/IKKε and IKKα/β by MAVS, triggering activation of transcriptional factors IRF3/IRF7 and NF-κB, respectively. This leads to the secretion of IFN-I and pro-inflammatory cytokines [3]. IFN-I further activates signaling pathways that induce IFN-stimulated genes (ISGs), establishing an antiviral state in host cells [2,4]. Several DDX58 variants have been reported in Singleton-Merten syndrome (C268F, E373A, E510V, and Q517H) and lupus nephritis (R109C) [5].

Singleton-Merten syndrome (SMS) is an autosomal dominant multisystemic disorder characterized by aortic and valvular calcification, glaucoma, dental dysplasia, psoriasis, skeletal abnormalities, and other variable phenotypes [6]. Pathogenic variants in DDX58 or IFIH1(interferon-induced with helicase C domain 1), another viral dsRNA sensor, leading to aberrant activation of type I interferon signaling, have been associated with SMS [7]. Despite an apparent pathogenesis, the genetic background of SMS and details regarding its phenotypic heterogeneity remain largely unknown.

In this study, we identified a novel de novo variant in DDX58 (p.D550Y) in a patient with SMS. The D550Y mutation disrupts RIG-I autoinhibitory conformation, which leads to RIG-I activation and upregulation of IFN-I signaling. PBMCs from the patient showed elevated IFN signatures. JAK inhibitor therapy suppressed the IFN signal and partially relieved clinical symptoms in the patient.

The patient, a 4-year-old girl, was delivered by cesarean section following an assisted reproductive pregnancy (Fig. 1a). She presented with partial lipodystrophy, predominantly affecting the facial and extremity regions (Fig. 1b upper right). Dysmorphic facial features were noted, including a flattened nasal bridge, atrophy of the nasal alae, and pinna hypoplasia (Fig. 1b upper left). She had mild to severe flexion contractures at the elbow, knee and proximal interphalangeal joints. Her skin was dry and hard, especially the extremities and digits, and pigmentations were present on the lower extremities (Fig. 1b lower left). Additional findings included nail hypoplasia and anonychia, ulceration with eschar formation on the left heel, and autoamputation of the distal phalanx of the right second toe (Fig. 1b lower right). Laboratory investigations revealed elevated erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), IgE and thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) with normal C-reactive protein (CRP) (Table S2). Other laboratory features of the proband are summarized in Tables S2 and S3. Radiographic evaluation of the hands demonstrated acro-osteolysis. Cranial computed tomography (CT) imaging showed dense calcification in the frontal lobe and bilateral basal ganglia (Fig. 1c). Skin biopsy revealed absent hair follicles and reduced eccrine sweat gland density. Given the early onset of the disease and the severe clinical course of the patient, we performed whole-genome sequencing on the family and found that the patient had a de novo variant p.Asp550Tyr (D550Y) in DDX58 (Fig. 1a and S1a). D550 in DDX58 is highly conserved across species (Fig. S1b-c). This variant was not present in public databases or Chinese cohorts (Fig. S1d). Multiple predictive models predicted it to be deleterious (Fig. S1d). To further explore the impact of the DDX58 D550Y mutation on the activation of the RIG-I downstream signaling pathways, we transfected 293T cells with RIG-I-WT and D550Y plasmids and assessed the transcriptional activities of IFN-β, NF-κB and ISRE promoters. Compared with RIG-I-WT-overexpressing cells, RIG-I-D550Y-transfected cells exhibited significantly higher luciferase activity, with or without poly(I:C) stimulation, an extracellular dsRNA analog (Fig. 1d). Similar results were also observed when RIG-I-D550Y was compared with the previously reported pathogenic variants (Fig. S2a-b) [5]. Additionally, RNA sequencing and the gene set enrichment analysis (GSEA) demonstrated enhanced activation of IFN-I and NF-κB signaling pathways in RIG-I-D550Y-transfected cells compared with RIG-I-WT-transfected cells (Fig. 1e and S2c). Activation of IFN-I signaling was confirmed by the 28-gene IFN score analysis (Fig. S2d). Western blot analysis revealed increased phosphorylation of IRF3 and p65 in RIG-I-D550Y-transfected cells compared with RIG-I-WT-transfected cells, regardless of poly(I:C) treatment (Fig. 1f, Fig. S2e). Additional findings included enhanced IRF3 dimerization and elevated phosphorylation of STAT1 and STAT2 were also observed in RIG-I-D550Y-transfected cells, with or without poly(I:C) stimulation (Fig. 1f). Moreover, qPCR assay further confirmed the elevated expression of ISGs in RIG-I-D550Y-transfected cells, irrespective of poly(I:C) stimulation (Fig. S2f). To exclude the influence of endogenous RIG-I, we further transfected plasmids carrying DDX58 mutation into RIG-I knockout (RIG-I KO) cells and performed luciferase reporter gene assays, western blot and qPCR analysis. Consistently, RIG-I with the D550Y mutation activated the IFN and NF-κB signaling pathways more potently than wild-type RIG-I, with or without poly(I:C) stimulation (Fig. S3a-c). In summary, these results suggest that the DDX58 D550Y variant constitutively activates the IFN-I pathway in HEK293T cells.

Next, the molecular mechanism underlying constitutive RIG-I activation by the D550Y mutation was investigated. The D550 site is located in the DEAD helicase domain in the spatial conformation. To test the effect of the D550Y mutation on the intramolecular interactions of the RIG-I, we constructed RIG-I protein truncations lacking the two N-terminal CARDs and N-terminal CARDs (Fig. S4a). The R109C variant, known to disrupt intramolecular interactions, was used as a positive control [5]. Although structural modeling based on duck RIG-I indicated that D550 is not located at the interface between the DEAD helicase domain and the CARDs domain (PDB accession 4a2w), the disrupted interaction between D550Y ΔCARD and WT CARDs was demonstrated by co-immunoprecipitation (Fig. 1g, Fig. S4b). Attenuated interaction between CARDs and the DEAD helicase domain of D550Y mutation may lead to the exposure of CARDs and release from the auto-repressed state, resulting in RIG-I signaling activation. Posttranslational modifications of RIG-I are important for controlling its activation, and dephosphorylation of the RIG-I CARD domain is essential for RIG-I activation [8]. Western blot analysis showed greater dephosphorylation of the CARD domain at Thr170 in HEK293T cells transfected with RIG-I-D550Y compared with those transfected with RIG-I-WT (Fig. 1h, Fig. S4c). Liberated CARDs are further modified by the K63-linked ubiquitination at K172, which is essential for RIG-I oligomerization and adaptor protein MAVS binding [4]. Significantly higher K63-linked ubiquitination of RIG-I-D550Y was observed (Fig. S4d), and RIG-I oligomerization in the D550Y mutation was also enhanced (Fig. 1i, Fig. S4e-f). Furthermore, co-immunoprecipitation assays demonstrated increased interaction between RIG-I-D550Y and MAVS (Fig. 1j). In brief, these data suggest that the D550Y mutation disrupts the autoinhibition conformation of RIG-I, leading to RIG-I-MAVS-mediated IFN signaling activation.

Given the robust IFN signature in this patient driven by the DDX58 D550Y mutant, JAK inhibitor therapy was initiated. Ruxolitinib (2.5mg twice daily) initially improved nystagmus, lipodystrophy, and foot ulcers. The dosage of ruxolitinib was increased to 5 mg twice daily due to the recurrence of her ocular abnormalities and gangrene in the toes one month later. Subsequently, tocilizumab (160 mg, 12 mg/kg, q2w) was added, and ruxolitinib was changed to upadacitinib (7.5mg qd) because of her vessel inflammation and foot ulcer deterioration. Consistent with in vitro results, the patient’s PBMCs showed enhanced IFN signaling prior to treatment detected by RNA sequencing and qPCR (Fig. 1k and S5a), which was further confirmed by a 28-gene IFN score (Fig. S5b). Furthermore, flow cytometry revealed elevated basal phosphorylation of STAT1 in the patient’s PBMCs compared to controls (Fig. S5c). Following JAK inhibitor treatment, IFN-I signaling was suppressed, as indicated by decreased IFN-I signaling, reduced ISG expression, and a lower IFN score (Fig. 1k and S5a-b). Phosphorylation levels of STAT1 in treated PBMCs were also comparable to those in healthy controls (Fig. S5c). Clinically, the patient exhibited improvements in lipodystrophy (Fig. 1l, left), palmar xerosis (Fig. 1l, middle), and foot ulceration (Fig. 1l, right). These results support the efficacy of JAK inhibitor therapy in patients with SMS caused by the DDX58 D550Y variant.

As cytosolic RNA sensor, RIG-I and MDA5 play critical roles in recognizing viral invasion and promoting type I interferon activation, and RIG-I gain of function (GOF) variants lead to sustained activation of the type I interferon pathways in patients with SMS and lupus nephritis, while clinical phenotypes of patients with IFIH1 variants include SMS, Aicardi-Goutières syndrome and systemic lupus erythematosus [9,10]. The disease spectrum of DDX58 and IFIH1 variants overlaps with those reported in this study, suggesting the DDX58 D550Y mutation-driven diseases belong to the spectrum of diseases with aberrant activation of dsRNA sensors (Table S1). In this study, JAK inhibitors reduced the patient's elevated IFN signature, demonstrating targeted therapy benefits for Singleton-Merten syndrome. Future studies with larger patient populations are needed to define the long-term efficacy of targeting IFN-I signaling by JAK inhibitors or Anifrolumab in DDX58 mutation-driven diseases.
