Anterior cingulate cortex neuron subtypes differentially regulate seizures
Authors: Ziqian Yan, Ting Tang, Kaishan Wang, Bin Fu, Zesheng Li, Mingshan Liu, Hongyi Huang, Yanfeng Yang, Qiang Zheng, Yangmin Zheng, Yongzhi Shan, Yumin Luo, Guoguang Zhao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to investigate the regulatory roles of distinct neuronal subtypes within the anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) in acute seizures and to identify cell type‐specific mechanisms underlying seizure modulation in this region.MethodsAcute seizure models were established in mice via pentylenetetrazol injection. In vivo fiber photometry and miniscope calcium imaging were employed to monitor neuronal calcium activity, and multichannel electroencephalography was used to record brain electrical signals simultaneously. Subsequently, bidirectional chemogenetic and optogenetic manipulations were performed on calcium/calmodulin‐dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) excitatory neurons and vesicular γ‐aminobutyric acid (GABA) transporter (vGAT)‐expressing GABAergic interneurons, as well as parvalbumin (PV) and somatostatin (SST) interneuron subpopulations.ResultsCalcium recordings demonstrated that both excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the ACC exhibited significant, temporally coordinated hyperactivity during acute seizures. Inhibition of CaMKII neurons significantly reduced seizure severity, whereas their activation induced spontaneous seizurelike activity. Enhancing GABAergic interneuron activity significantly decreased seizure frequency and severity. Notably, inhibition of GABAergic interneurons resulted in markedly more severe seizures. Further examination of interneuron subtypes revealed functional heterogeneity; activation of SST interneurons effectively suppressed seizures, whereas PV activation did not produce significant antiseizure effects. Critically, inhibition of either PV or SST interneurons triggered spontaneous seizurelike activity, indicating that both subtypes are necessary for maintaining network stability.SignificanceThis study elucidates the differential roles of ACC neuronal populations in acute seizure dynamics. Activating GABAergic interneurons or inhibiting CaMKII‐positive neurons effectively suppresses seizure activity. Among interneuron subtypes, SST activation reduces seizures, whereas PV activation does not. However, inhibiting either subtype triggers spontaneous epileptiform activity, indicating both are indispensable and play complementary roles in maintaining network homeostasis. These findings reveal the microcircuit mechanisms by which the ACC modulates acute seizures, highlighting that cortical stability relies on both the classical excitation–inhibition balance and the cooperative interplay of multiple interneuron subtypes.Keywords: CaMKII neurons, excitation–inhibition balance, GABAergic interneurons, somatostatin interneurons

INTRODUCTION

Epilepsy is a chronic central nervous system (CNS) disorder arising from abnormal, excessive, and synchronized neuronal discharges, clinically manifesting as recurrent unprovoked seizures. 1 Current epidemiological estimates indicate that approximately 70 million individuals worldwide are affected by epilepsy. 2 The etiology of this condition is highly heterogeneous, encompassing genetic, structural, metabolic, infectious, and immune etiologies. 3 Although a variety of antiepileptic drugs are widely used in clinical practice, approximately one third of patients still manifest drug‐resistant epilepsy, 4 , 5 which accounts for substantial disability‐adjusted life‐year reduction and elevated premature mortality risks, thereby imposing a considerable global disease burden. 6

Normal CNS function is critically dependent upon the dynamic equilibrium between excitatory and inhibitory synaptic transmission. A shift in the excitation/inhibition (E/I) balance toward hyperexcitability represents a fundamental mechanism underlying epileptogenesis and seizure generalization. 7 Prior investigations have elucidated the cellular substrates governing this balance within discrete brain regions. For instance, cholinergic projections from the septum to the hippocampus can suppress epileptic activity through recruitment of somatostatin (SST)‐expressing interneurons, 8 and optogenetic activation of the thalamic reticular nucleus attenuates limbic seizures through modulation of midline thalamic output. 9 Therefore, elucidating the network substrates of E/I imbalance at the microcircuit level and identifying cell type‐specific targets capable of modulating this equilibrium may provide insights for developing antiseizure strategies.

However, the precise circuit mechanisms underlying E/I imbalance remain incompletely understood, particularly with regard to specific cortical regions and neuronal subtypes. Although hippocampal circuits have been extensively investigated, emerging evidence indicates that cortical structures play a crucial yet underappreciated role in seizure generation and propagation. The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), functioning as a central hub of the limbic system, is extensively implicated in higher order brain functions including emotion regulation, cognitive control, and pain processing. 10 Importantly, ACC is widely connected with key epileptogenic regions, suggesting its role in seizure regulation. Clinical evidence further supports its involvement in the seizure network. Neuroimaging studies have revealed significant gray matter volume reduction in the ACC of patients with genetic generalized epilepsy. 11 Furthermore, functional magnetic resonance imaging and intracranial electroencephalographic (EEG) data indicate that network activity within the ACC and medial prefrontal cortex is strongly correlated with impaired consciousness during seizures. 12 At the molecular level, aberrant expression of glutamate ionotropic receptor kainate type subunit 1 in ACC projection neurons can induce epileptiform activity. 13 Although the ACC is implicated in epilepsy and its comorbidities, 14 the specific contribution of local E/I dynamics remains elusive. In particular, it is unknown how the interplay between excitatory and inhibitory neurons within these microcircuits 15 modulates seizures.

In this study, using a pentylenetetrazol (PTZ)‐induced acute seizure model in mice, we integrated behavioral analysis, in vivo fiber photometry calcium imaging, EEG, and chemogenetic and optogenetic approaches to examine the roles of ACC excitatory and inhibitory neurons in acute seizure regulation, and further dissected the contributions of γ‐aminobutyric acidergic (GABAergic) interneuron subtypes. This study aims to reveal the ACC microcircuit mechanism underlying acute seizure regulation and provide new evidence for understanding the role of cortical nodes in the seizure network.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Animals

Male wild‐type C57BL/6J mice and transgenic mice, including CaMKII‐Cre (C57BL/6J‐Tg[Camk2a‐Cre]), Vgat‐Cre (C57BL/6J‐Slc32a1 em[Cre] ), SST‐Cre (C57BL/6J‐Sst em[Cre] ), parvalbumin (PV)‐Cre (C57BL/6J‐Pvalb em[Cre] ), and glutamate decarboxylase 67 (Gad67)‐GFP (C57BL/6J‐Tg[Gad1‐EGFP]) mice (6–8 weeks old, 22–24 g) were purchased from Genepax Biotechnology Co. and housed at the Animal Center of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University. All transgenic lines were maintained as heterozygous breeding colonies. Animals were housed in a specific pathogen‐free facility under a 12‐h light/dark cycle (lights on from 8:00 to 20:00), with temperature at 22 ± 2°C and humidity at 40%–60%. All experimental procedures were approved by the animal ethics committee of Xuanwu Hospital (approval number: XW‐20230605‐1) and performed in accordance with the National Institutes of Health guidelines.

Acute seizure model and behavioral assessment

PTZ (Sigma‐Aldrich) was dissolved in .9% saline as a 50 mg/mL stock (stored at −20°C in the dark) and diluted to 2 mg/mL on the day of the experiment. Acute seizures were induced by intraperitoneal PTZ injection (60 mg/kg). Immediately after injection, mice were placed in an observation box, and behavior was recorded by a video camera for 30 min.

EEG recording and analysis

Recording electrode screws were fixed to the skull above the ACC, and reference and ground electrodes were implanted over the cerebellum. EEG signals were acquired at 2000 Hz using a multichannel acquisition system (Neuracle), with simultaneous video recording of behavior. Raw signals were band‐pass filtered (1–100 Hz) and notch filtered at 50 Hz. Power spectral density was calculated using Welch's method, with frequency bands as follows: delta (1–4 Hz), theta (4–8 Hz), alpha (8–12 Hz), beta (12–30 Hz), and gamma (30–100 Hz). During behavioral scoring and EEG analysis, the experimenter was blinded to the experimental conditions.

Stereotaxic surgery

Under pentobarbital sodium anesthesia (50 mg/kg ip), mice were head‐fixed in a stereotaxic instrument (RWD Life Science), and body temperature was maintained at 37–38°C using a heating pad. A glass micropipette was used to inject 180 nL of virus into the ACC (coordinates relative to bregma: anteroposterior +.8 mm, mediolateral ±.3 mm, dorsoventral [DV] −1.75 mm) at a rate of 35 nL/min, with the pipette left in place for 10 min postinjection. For optogenetics and fiber photometry experiments, a ceramic ferrule optical fiber cannula (diameter = 200 μm, numerical aperture = .37) was implanted with the tip at DV −1.55 mm; for miniscope imaging experiments, a gradient‐index (GRIN) lens was implanted. All implants were secured with dental cement. Mice were allowed to recover for 3–4 weeks to ensure sufficient viral expression. Adeno‐associated virus (AAV) vectors were obtained from BrainCase with titers ≥ 1 × 1012 vg/mL, including the following: rAAV2/9‐hSyn‐DIO‐GCaMP6s, rAAV2/9‐hSyn‐DIO‐hM3D(Gq)‐mCherry, rAAV2/9‐hSyn‐DIO‐hM4D(Gi)‐mCherry, rAAV2/9‐hSyn‐DIO‐mCherry, rAAV2/9‐EF1α‐DIO‐eNpHR3.0‐mCherry, rAAV2/9‐EF1α‐DIO‐ChR2(H134R)‐mCherry, and rAAV‐EF1α‐DIO‐taCasp3‐T2A‐TEVp‐P2A‐EGFP.

In vivo fiber photometry

Population calcium signals were acquired with a fiber photometry system (Thinkertech). A 470‐nm light‐emitting diode (LED) was used to excite GCaMP6s. The two excitation lights were delivered interleaved at 40 Hz, and fluorescence signals were detected by a photomultiplier tube at 40 Hz. Data were processed with MATLAB. The fluorescence change was calculated as ΔF/F0 = (F − F0)/F0 × 100%. Metrics included mean ΔF/F0 and the area under the curve (AUC) during seizures.

In vivo miniscope calcium imaging

A head‐mounted microscope system (nVista HD, Inscopix) was used to monitor single‐cell activity of ACC neurons. Two to three weeks after viral expression, a GRIN lens (1.0 mm) was implanted and secured with dental cement. After 2–3 weeks of recovery and fluorescence stabilization, a baseplate was attached. The internal 475‐nm LED served as the excitation source, with a sampling rate of 20 Hz. Image data were preprocessed with Inscopix Data Processing Software, downsampled to 10 Hz, and neurons were extracted with the principal component analysis–independent component analysis algorithm.

Chemogenetic manipulation

Clozapine‐N‐oxide (CNO; Tocris) was dissolved in .9% saline to prepare a .5‐mg/mL working solution. Mice received an intraperitoneal injection of CNO (5 mg/kg) 1 h prior to PTZ injection.

Optogenetic manipulation

Laser stimulation was generated by a laser source (Newdoon) and delivered via a patch cord connected to the implanted fiber cannula. Laser stimulation commenced 5 min prior to PTZ injection and continued until the end of the observation period. For inhibitory manipulation (eNpHR3.0), 594 nm yellow light (10 mW at fiber tip, continuous mode) was used. For excitatory manipulation Channelrhodopsin‐2 (ChR2), 473 nm blue light (8 mW at fiber tip, pulsed mode, 20 Hz, 5‐ms pulse width) was used. Control mice received light stimulation with identical parameters.

Immunofluorescence staining

Sixty minutes after PTZ injection, mice were anesthetized and transcardially perfused with saline followed by 4% paraformaldehyde. Brains were harvested and postfixed at 4°C overnight. Coronal sections (30 μm) were obtained with a cryostat. Sections were permeabilized with .3% Triton X‐100, blocked with 3% donkey serum, and incubated with primary antibodies at 4°C overnight. Primary antibodies were as follows: guinea pig anti‐c‐Fos (1:1000; Synaptic Systems), rabbit anti‐c‐Fos (1:500; Abcam), rabbit anti‐NeuN (1:500; Abcam), rabbit anti‐CaMKII (1:200; Abcam), rabbit anti‐Gad67 (1:200; Invitrogen), guinea pig anti‐PV (1:200; Synaptic Systems), and mouse anti‐SST (1:50; GeneTex). Sections were incubated with Alexa Fluor‐conjugated secondary antibodies (1:500; Invitrogen) for 2 h at room temperature and mounted with 4,6‐diamidino‐2‐phenylindole mounting medium. Fluorescence images were acquired with a confocal microscope (Leica).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses and plotting were performed using GraphPad Prism 9.0. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. The Shapiro–Wilk test and F‐test were used to evaluate normality and homogeneity of variance. The two groups were compared using the unpaired t‐test, Welch t‐test, or Mann–Whitney U‐test. Two‐way analysis of variance was used for multigroup comparisons, followed by Bonferroni or Tukey post hoc tests; p < .05 was considered statistically significant. Given the highly exploratory nature of this study, no formal a priori power analysis was conducted prior to the experiments. The sample size was determined based on comparable studies in the field, effect sizes observed in pilot experiments, and principles of animal ethics.

RESULTS

PTZ induces robust behavioral and electrophysiological seizures in mice

We evaluated PTZ‐induced acute seizures using behavioral scoring and EEG recordings in freely moving mice (Figure 1A). Following intraperitoneal injection of PTZ, mice exhibited characteristic seizure behaviors as assessed using the modified Racine scale (Figure 1B). Behaviors progressed from facial twitching to limb clonus to generalized tonic–clonic seizures; stages I–III were classified as focal and IV–VI as generalized seizures (GSs). The PTZ group exhibited marked cortical hypersynchronous discharges characterized by repetitive high‐amplitude spike–wave complexes and polyspike bursts (Figure 1C). Power spectral density analysis showed that absolute power across the δ, θ, α, β, and γ frequency bands was significantly elevated in the PTZ group compared to controls (Figure 1D). Behavioral assessment confirmed significantly elevated seizure stages in the PTZ group (Figure 1E), with every PTZ‐treated mouse experiencing at least one GS episode (Figure 1F). Following PTZ injection, mice exhibited EEG seizure onset at 2.62 ± .40 min and generalized seizures at 3.79 ± .65 min (Figure 1G,H). Together, these data confirm that PTZ effectively triggers cortical hypersynchronous discharges and seizure behaviors in mice.

Widespread activation of excitatory and inhibitory neurons in the ACC during seizures

Immunofluorescence revealed a significant increase in c‐Fos‐positive cells in the ACC following PTZ‐induced seizures compared with saline controls (Figure 2A,B). The colocalization rate of c‐Fos with calcium/calmodulin‐dependent protein kinase II (CaMKII) neurons was significantly higher in the PTZ group than in controls (Figure 2C,D). Inhibitory neurons were also significantly activated, as shown by a markedly higher colocalization rate of c‐Fos with Gad67 in the PTZ group (Figure 2E,F). PV and SST neurons comprised 43.8% and 38.3% of Gad67‐positive cells, respectively (Figure S1A,B), and both subtypes showed significantly elevated c‐Fos induction after PTZ injection (Figure S2A–D). These findings indicate that PTZ‐induced seizures trigger widespread neuronal activation in the ACC.

ACC CaMKII neurons bidirectionally regulate seizurelike activity

To track the population dynamics of ACC excitatory neurons during seizures, we used fiber photometry to record calcium signals from CaMKII neurons (Figure 3A). Coimmunolabeling confirmed that 97.14% ± .85% of enhanced green fluorescent protein (EGFP)‐expressing cells were CaMKII‐positive, and 97.16% ± 1.48% of CaMKII‐positive cells expressed EGFP (Figure 3B). Heatmap visualization revealed notably enhanced calcium transients during PTZ‐induced seizures (Figure 3C). Signals surged within 0–.5 s of onset then dropped below baseline by .5–1 s (Figure 3D,E). We performed in vivo calcium imaging using head‐mounted miniaturized microscopes in freely behaving mice (Figure 3F). Whereas most neurons were relatively quiescent at baseline, PTZ‐induced seizures elicited synchronized, high‐amplitude calcium responses across the imaging field (Figure 3G). Representative single‐neuron traces confirmed substantial increases in both frequency and amplitude of calcium transients, with multiple neurons displaying near‐synchronous activity within the seizure window (Figure 3H). To quantify this observed synchronization, we performed pairwise correlation analysis across all recorded neurons. The synchrony index, calculated as the mean pairwise Pearson correlation coefficient, revealed a significant elevation during the ictal period relative to baseline (Figure S3A). Temporal analysis showed that synchrony peaked during the tonic–clonic phase and gradually declined following seizure termination, paralleling the behavioral and electrographic seizure progression (Figure S3B).

To test causality, we bilaterally injected Cre‐dependent hM4D(Gi) vectors into the ACC of CaMKII‐Cre mice (Figure 3I–K). Coimmunolabeling confirmed that 96.70% ± 1.05% of mCherry‐expressing cells were CaMKII‐positive, and 96.04% ± 2.18% of CaMKII‐positive cells expressed mCherry (Figure 3J). Time–frequency EEG analysis showed that, whereas mCherry controls displayed high‐amplitude spike–wave complexes and broadband power increases after PTZ injection, chemogenetic inhibition markedly attenuated these pathological bursts (Figure 3L). Behavioral analysis confirmed that the hM4D(Gi) group showed reduced seizure stages (Figure 3M), fewer GS episodes (Figure 3N), and prolonged latencies to EEG seizure onset and GS (Figure 3O,P). Optogenetic inhibition via eNpHR3.0 produced similar antiseizure effects (Figure S3C–H).

Conversely, to test whether activation of ACC CaMKII neurons is sufficient to induce seizurelike activity in the absence of PTZ, we expressed hM3D(Gq) in this population (Figure 3Q). EEG recordings showed that CNO administration triggered paroxysmal spike discharges, and time–frequency analysis revealed intermittent high‐frequency power elevations coinciding with clonic behaviors (Figure 3R). Compared with mCherry controls, hM3D(Gq)‐expressing mice exhibited spontaneous seizures of varying severity (Figure 3S), more frequent GSs (Figure 3T), and shorter latencies to EEG seizure onset and GS (Figure 3U,V). This proepileptic effect was further validated by combined CNO and PTZ administration (Figure S3I–N), which significantly worsened seizure severity. Selective ablation of ACC CaMKII neurons using taCasp3 (Figure S4A–C) also reduced seizure stages (Figure S4D), decreased GS frequency (Figure S4E), and prolonged seizure latencies (Figure S4F,G). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that ACC CaMKII neurons bidirectionally modulate seizure susceptibility and severity.

ACC GABAergic neurons bidirectionally regulate seizurelike activity

We monitored ACC GABAergic neuron activity during seizures using fiber photometry in Vgat‐Cre mice (Figure 4A). Coimmunolabeling confirmed that 89.68% ± 5.20% of EGFP‐expressing cells were Vgat‐positive, and 87.26% ± 4.36% of Vgat‐positive cells expressed EGFP (Figure 4B). Recordings revealed significantly elevated calcium signals during PTZ‐induced seizures (Figure 4C,D), with a marked increase in AUC during the ictal phase (Figure 4E). To examine whether activation of ACC GABAergic neurons is sufficient to suppress PTZ‐induced acute seizure activity, we bilaterally injected Cre‐dependent hM3D(Gq) vectors into the ACC of Vgat‐Cre mice (Figure 4F–H). Coimmunolabeling confirmed that 81.40% ± 6.89% of mCherry‐expressing cells were Vgat‐positive, and 91.85% ± 4.12% of Vgat‐positive cells expressed mCherry. Following CNO activation, the hM3D(Gq) group showed significantly reduced PTZ‐induced discharge amplitudes and suppressed power across all frequency bands compared to controls (Figure 4I). Behavioral quantification indicated that GABAergic neuron activation significantly reduced both maximum seizure stage and GS frequency (Figure 4J,K), while substantially prolonging latencies to EEG seizure onset and GS (Figure 4L,M). Real‐time optogenetic activation using Channelrhodopsin‐2 (ChR2) during PTZ challenge produced consistent antiseizure effects (Figure S5A,B), including reduced seizure stages (Figure S5C), fewer GS episodes (Figure S5D), and prolonged latencies to EEG seizure onset (Figure S5E) and GS (Figure S5F).

To investigate the impact of silencing GABAergic neurons on seizure susceptibility, we selectively inhibited ACC Vgat neurons using hM4D(Gi) (Figure 4N). Following CNO administration alone, the EEG rapidly shifted into continuous high‐voltage tonic discharges, with spectrograms showing high‐intensity power bursts across all bands (Figure 4O). Compared to mCherry controls, hM4D(Gi)‐expressing mice exhibited spontaneous seizures (Figure 4P), increased GS frequency (Figure 4Q), and markedly reduced latencies to EEG seizure onset and GS (Figure 4R,S). This proepileptic effect was further validated under combined CNO and PTZ challenge (Figure S5G,H). Inhibiting GABAergic neurons significantly exacerbated seizure severity, with elevated maximum seizure stages (Figure S5I) and reduced latencies to EEG seizure onset (Figure S5K) and GS (Figure S5L), although total GS number was not significantly different (Figure S5J). These findings demonstrate that ACC GABAergic neurons exert bidirectional modulatory effects on acute seizure susceptibility and severity.

Activation of SST interneurons, but not PV interneurons, effectively attenuates seizures

Given the antiseizure effects of global GABAergic activation, we next investigated the specific contributions of PV and SST interneurons to acute seizure regulation within the ACC (Figure 5A,B). We first expressed hM3D(Gq) bilaterally in the ACC of PV‐Cre mice. Coimmunolabeling confirmed that 81.39% ± .71% of mCherry‐expressing cells were PV‐positive, and 84.17% ± 4.64% of PV‐positive cells expressed mCherry (Figure 5C). Following CNO activation, the hM3D(Gq) group still exhibited high‐amplitude continuous spike–wave discharges and power enhancement, comparable to mCherry controls, despite a trend toward attenuation (Figure 5E). Although PV activation showed a numerical trend toward seizure suppression, differences in maximum seizure stage, GS frequency, and seizure onset latencies were not statistically significant (Figure 5G–J).

When we applied the same strategy to activate SST interneurons in SST‐Cre mice, coimmunolabeling confirmed that 82.58% ± 4.61% of mCherry‐expressing cells were SST‐positive (specificity), and 82.91 ± 3.81% of SST‐positive cells expressed mCherry (selectivity; Figure 5D). In contrast to the PV activation results, the SST‐hM3D(Gq) group showed reduced amplitudes, with spectrograms revealing attenuation of high‐frequency power (Figure 5F). Activating SST interneurons reduced maximum seizure stage (Figure 5K) and GS frequency (Figure 5L), while prolonging EEG seizure onset (Figure 5M) and GS latency (Figure 5N). Activation of SST interneurons is sufficient to suppress hyperexcitability; however, under PTZ‐induced disinhibition, activation of PV interneurons failed to produce comparable antiseizure effects.

Basal activity of both SST and PV neurons is essential for maintaining ACC network homeostasis

To investigate the roles of these subtypes, we performed selective chemogenetic inhibition by injecting Cre‐dependent hM4D(Gi) into the ACC of PV‐Cre and SST‐Cre mice (Figure 6A,B). Under CNO conditions, inhibiting either PV or SST neurons induced paroxysmal spike–wave and epileptiform discharges with high‐power fluctuations (Figure 6C,D). Compared to controls, PV and SST inhibition each significantly elevated seizure severity and reduced EEG seizure onset latency (Figure 6E–H). When combined with PTZ stimulation (Figure S6A,B), inhibition of neither PV nor SST interneurons significantly changed the maximum seizure grade or total GS frequency (Figure S6C,D,G,H). However, both procedures significantly reduced the time to onset of EEG seizures and the latency of GS (Figure S6E–J). Thus, despite differing effects upon activation, both interneuron populations are critically required to prevent network hyperexcitability and coregulate cortical inhibitory tone.

DISCUSSION

Generalized seizures arise from aberrant reconfiguration across large‐scale brain networks. 1 , 16 The anterior cingulate cortex is extensively connected with limbic structures, thalamic nuclei, and neocortical regions. 12 , 17 Despite this anatomical prominence, the specific gating mechanisms by which the ACC participates in seizure propagation have remained poorly understood. Here, we employed multimodal recordings coupled with cell type‐specific manipulations to systematically dissect the contributions of distinct ACC neuronal populations to acute seizure activity in a PTZ‐induced mouse model. Our results demonstrate opposing functional roles for CaMKII neurons and GABAergic interneurons. Suppressing CaMKII neuronal activity or enhancing GABAergic transmission mitigated PTZ‐induced seizures, whereas the converse manipulations provoked epileptiform activity in otherwise naïve mice. Furthermore, we revealed functional heterogeneity within GABAergic subtypes; chemogenetic activation of SST‐positive interneurons produced a significant anticonvulsant effect, whereas activation of PV‐positive interneurons did not yield a significant effect; however, inhibition of either subtype exacerbated seizure severity. These data establish that the stability of the ACC network does not rely on a simple linear excitation–inhibition balance, but rather on an asymmetric, subtype‐specific regulatory mechanism in which SST and PV interneurons exert distinct yet complementary roles.

By specifically inhibiting CaMKII neurons, we significantly reduced PTZ‐induced acute seizures. This aligns with the classical hypothesis that attenuating major projection neuron output limits seizure propagation. 1 , 18 Similarly, specific activation of Vgat‐positive GABAergic neurons resulted in marked suppression of seizure stage and duration. Enhanced inhibition raises the threshold for paroxysmal depolarizing shifts by increasing shunting inhibition and hyperpolarizing currents. 7 , 19 This bidirectional validation indicates that either directly weakening excitatory drive or enhancing inhibitory tone exerts antiseizure effects by remodeling the E/I setpoint of the ACC. It should be noted that, because PTZ induces seizures via systemic disinhibition rather than through focal initiation, this study demonstrates that ACC neuronal activity participates in modulating the expression of acute seizures but does not identify the ACC as a primary seizure onset zone.

Notably, we uncovered a marked asymmetry in this seizure activity triggered by different manipulations. GABAergic inhibition exhibited shorter latencies and more severe tonic phases compared to those induced by direct CaMKII neuronal activation. This disparity likely reflects differential engagement of homeostatic feedback circuits. Under baseline conditions, cortical networks rely heavily on negative feedback to maintain stability. 20 When excitatory output is artificially elevated, local microcircuits rapidly mobilize compensatory inhibition to partially offset the excitatory perturbation. 21 By contrast, targeted ablation of GABAergic function dismantles the core feedback architecture and leaves networks profoundly disinhibited, allowing even physiological inputs to trigger runaway excitation. 7 , 22 This network‐level asymmetry is further supported biophysically by the single‐cell calcium dynamics we observed. Unlike CaMKII neurons, which exhibit a pronounced postseizure calcium undershoot (Figure 3D), GABAergic neurons do not show suppression below baseline following the seizure‐associated rise in calcium signals (Figure 4D). In excitatory neurons, the postseizure calcium trough may reflect the summation of multiple inhibitory processes, including activity‐dependent hyperpolarization mediated by small‐conductance (SK) and large‐conductance (BK) calcium‐activated potassium channels, 23 hyperpolarizing currents generated by Na+/K+‐adenosine triphosphatase (ATPase) activation, 24 firing suppression mediated by adenosine via A1 receptors, 25 and GABAergic inhibition provided by persistently active interneurons in the late seizure phase. 26 In contrast, GABAergic neurons recover to baseline levels more rapidly, possibly reflecting the enriched mitochondrial distribution characteristic of GABAergic interneurons, which provides enhanced capacity for both ATP‐dependent calcium extrusion and direct mitochondrial calcium sequestration. 27

Regarding distinct interneuron subtypes, chemogenetic activation of SST neurons significantly suppressed seizures, whereas comparable activation of PV interneurons did not produce a significant antiseizure effect. However, in the absence of PTZ, inhibiting either PV or SST interneurons alone was sufficient to trigger spontaneous epileptiform activity. Together, these findings support a synergistic model in which PV and SST interneurons cooperatively regulate ACC network excitability through complementary inhibitory functions, consistent with their distinct computational roles of division versus subtraction in cortical circuits. 28 Several factors may underlie the differential effects of SST and PV neuron activation. First, these interneuron subtypes differ in synaptic targeting; SST neurons primarily innervate distal dendrites of pyramidal neurons, whereas PV neurons mainly target the soma and axon initial segment. 29 , 30 Under the intense and spatially widespread excitatory drive induced by systemic PTZ, dendritic inhibition by SST neurons may more effectively limit excitatory input integration and thereby raise the threshold for spike generation. Second, SST neurons exhibit weak frequency adaptation, and their output synapses have short‐term facilitation properties, 31 whereas PV interneurons are susceptible to depolarization block under extreme pathological conditions. 32 , 33 Furthermore, high loads of postsynaptic chloride influx can overload KCC2 cotransporters and cause a depolarizing shift in the GABA reversal potential. 34 , 35 Third, in networks strongly driven by PTZ, profound somatic saturation might mask PV neuron activation's antiseizure efficacy via a ceiling effect. Functionally, PV‐mediated perisomatic inhibition restricts initial pyramidal cells bursting, 34 , 36 whereas SST‐mediated dendritic inhibition limits downstream excitation spread. 37 Thus, our activation results likely reflect differing baseline recruitment thresholds during acute global disinhibition, rather than proving SST neurons are uniquely superior in controlling seizures.

Our findings reinforce that cooperative interneuron networks are central to maintaining homeostasis, as inhibiting GABAergic cells drove more severe acute epileptiform activity than activating excitatory neurons. Although selectively activating SST neurons suppressed PTZ‐evoked seizures, both PV and SST populations are required to regulate cortical excitability. Consequently, modulating these subtypes provides rationale for exploring ACC circuits in broader neuromodulation research. 38 , 39

Several limitations remain. Crucially, the acute PTZ model relies on systemic disinhibition and does not mimic spontaneous focal seizures or chronic epileptogenesis. 40 , 41 Additionally, although various ACC populations participate in modulating these acute events, our findings do not establish the region as a primary seizure initiation hub. Delineating the precise functional hierarchy of the ACC will require future chronic epilepsy models paired with simultaneous multiregion recordings.

CONCLUSIONS

Our study demonstrates that seizures can be suppressed by either activating GABAergic interneurons or inhibiting CaMKII neurons in the ACC. Although activating SST rather than PV interneurons sufficiently inhibits seizures, inhibiting either subtype triggers epileptiform discharges, suggesting both contribute to seizure control through complementary roles. These results reveal cell type‐specific contributions of ACC neurons to acute seizure regulation.
