Autoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy with full-length spinal cord lesions: a case report and literature review
Authors: Mengyi Huang, Qunbo Jia, Yushu Ouyang, Xiaoteng Feng, Ronghui Ju
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAutoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy (GFAP-A) is an autoimmune inflammatory disease of the central nervous system triggered by GFAP. Currently, the diagnosis is mainly based on the detection of autoantibodies that recognize GFAP in the cerebrospinal fluid. Its imaging manifestations are complex and variable. It is extremely necessary to enhance the understanding of this disease among radiologists and reduce the damage caused by invasive procedures.Case presentationWe report a case of a 39-year-old female patient with lesions throughout the spinal cord. The patient presented with recurrent fever without obvious cause, and no obvious lesions were found in the lung CT examination. Two months later, she developed weakness of both lower limbs with neck and back pain. Contrast-enhanced MRI of the cervical and thoracic spinal cord demonstrated diffuse intramedullary punctate-to-linear and patchy enhancement, with associated leptomeningeal enhancement. GFAP-IgG was subsequently detected in the cerebrospinal fluid, confirming the diagnosis of GFAP-A. In cases where CSF antibody test results are negative or weakly positive, a diagnosis of GFAP-A can still be established when characteristic imaging manifestations are consistent with clinical symptoms. Background

Autoimmune glial fibrillary acidic protein astrocytopathy (GFAP-A) is a rare central nervous system autoimmune inflammatory disorder caused by GFAP. First reported in 2016, it has garnered significant academic attention since then [1]. The etiology and pathogenesis of GFAP-A remain unclear, but may be associated with concurrent autoimmune diseases, tumors, or prior infections [2].

Predominantly affecting individuals aged 40–50 years, with slightly higher prevalence in males [2], GFAP-A typically presents with symptoms including fever, headache, motor dysfunction, visual disturbances, psychiatric abnormalities, cognitive decline, autonomic dysfunction, and peripheral neuropathy. Severe neurological deterioration may occur in some patients, potentially manifesting as coma and respiratory failure. Disease-specific antibodies (GFAP-IgG) play a crucial role in pathogenesis and serve as key diagnostic markers. However, up to 45% of patients exhibit positive results for other autoantibodies in serum and cerebrospinal fluid, complicating diagnosis [2]. Imaging features are therefore essential for clinical evaluation. MRI findings often reveal abnormal signals in the brain and/or spinal cord, with cerebral lesions predominantly located periventricular regions showing perpendicular to lateral ventricles radiating enhancement, while diffuse restriction is extremely rare. Long et al. reported that approximately 68.8% of patients present with longitudinally extensive spinal cord lesions, occasionally involving the thoracic spinal cord but sparing the lumbar region [3].

We present a case of GFAP-A with extensive spinal cord lesions and intracranial manifestations. Following treatment, the lesion extent was significantly reduced. Additionally, we conducted a literature review on GFAP-A to discuss its etiology, pathogenesis, imaging features, and differential diagnoses, providing reliable evidence for clinical and radiological diagnosis.

Case report

A female, 39-year-old patient was admitted to hospital after suffering from fever for 6 months, bilateral lower extremity weakness accompanied by neck and back pain for over 4 months, and aggravation of these symptoms for 20 days. In early January 2024, the patient experienced recurrent fever without obvious cause, with body temperature ranging from 38 °C to 39 °C. After taking paracetamol, the fever subsided, but it then recurred. She was treated at the local hospital and underwent pulmonary computed tomography and other examinations, but no abnormalities were found. In February 2024, she experienced bilateral lower extremity weakness, unbearable back pain, and an inability to walk, and was admitted to hospital. She underwent enhanced cervical and thoracic magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) examinations, which showed a pattern consistent with LETM (Longitudinally extensive transverse myelitis) in the cervical and thoracic spinal cord, with patchy and linear enhancement on T1 weighted images after contrast. After hormone pulse therapy, the symptoms improved significantly. In early June 2024, the symptoms worsened again, and the patient also presented with “talking in her sleep and speech confusion”. She was admitted to hospital on June 27th.

On post-admission physical examination, the patient was drowsy and sometimes gave irrelevant answers to questions. Muscle strength in the left lower limb was grade 3, and in the right lower limb was grade 2. The Babinski sign was positive, and the Kernig sign was negative. Laboratory tests showed the following: tumor markers screening and the rheumatism items showed no abnormalities, both ANCA and tuberculosis antibody detection returned negative results. The anti-β2-glycoprotein 1 antibody IgM level was 25.00 AU/mL; varicella-zoster virus antibody IgG was positive. Cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) analysis following lumbar puncture revealed: white blood cell count 12 × 10⁶/L (lymphocytes 97.0%, neutrophils 3.0%), glucose level of 1.93 mmol/L, chloride of 126 mmol/L, total protein 1.76 g/L, and immunoglobulin IgG level of 195.0 mg/L. CSF oligoclonal protein electrophoresis: CSF immunoglobulin IgG was 178.0 mg/L, suggesting an immunoinflammatory response. Cell-based assay (CBA) showed CSF GFAP IgG of 1:3.2 (Moderately weakly positive), while no GFAP antibody was detected in serum. Antibodies against aquaporin-4 (AQP4), myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein (MOG), and myelin basic protein (MBP) were negative in both serum and CSF. MRI examination showed periventricular hyperintensities on T2 weighted images, slightly high FLAIR signal, no abnormalities on diffusion weighted imaging(DWI), and linear perivascular enhancement on T1 weighted images after contrast, a patter similar to LETM in the spinal cord, with diffuse multifocal intramedullary enhancement (Fig. 1).

MRI of the Brain (a-d) and Spinal Cord (e-h) Before Immunotherapy. a Axial T2-weighted images, b T2 fluid-attenuated inversion recovery (FLAIR) images showed hyperintensity lesions in periventricular. c, d Axial T1-weighted gadolinium-enhanced images showed periventricular radial linear enhancement extending from the ventricles, suggesting perivascular enhancement. e-g Sagittal T2-weighted images showed longitudinally extensive hyperintensity in spinal cord. f, h Sagittal T1-weighted gadolinium-enhanced images showed diffuse punctate enhancement within spinal cord and leptomeningeal enhancement

Combined with the patient’s antibody test, clinical symptoms, and imaging findings, the diagnosis was GFAP-A. After high-dose methylprednisolone pulse therapy (1000 mg for 5 days) with gradual tapering, the patient’s symptoms did not improve by day 8, presenting with involuntary tremors of the limbs and face, gait instability, marked recent memory decline, and reduced attention. Considering additional immunoglobulin therapy for 5 days. Following tapering of prednisone to 120 mg, it was switched to oral prednisone. Concurrently, mycophenolate mofetil immunosuppressive therapy was added. Subsequently, the patient showed notable improvement in attention, memory, and limb tremors, and was discharged. Four months later, because of fever accompanied by tremors in both hands and numbness in both lower limbs, the patient was again examined in hospital. The clinical symptoms had improved compared to the previous episode. Head and spinal cord whole-tissue enhanced MRI scanning showed that the overall lesion range was significantly reduced (Fig. 2). Discussion and conclusions

As a rare autoimmune inflammatory disease of central nervous system, the etiology of GFAP-A is still unclear and may involve concurrent autoimmune diseases, tumors, or preceding infections. A meta-analysis of 681 patients by Hagbohm et al. showed 98 patients to have tumors, among which ovarian teratoma was the most common [2]. However, these data do not confirm a causal relationship between tumors and GFAP-A; the association may be a paraneoplastic immune response caused by tumors. Approximately 45% of patients had a preceding viral state, mainly upper respiratory tract infections and gastrointestinal infections, indicating that pathogen infection may trigger immune responses. Li et al. speculated that EBV may directly invade the nervous system to induce vasculitic reactions, or that EBV may have common antigens with the nervous system to induce lymphocytes to produce pathogenic antibodies [4]. Some non-neurological autoantibodies, such as rheumatoid factor, anti-SSA, anti-nuclear, and anti-PM-Scl antibodies have also been reported [3]. Moreover, many studies have found that patients with GFAP-A have overlapping syndromes, in which NMDAR-IgG, AQP4-IgG and MOG-IgG are present [5, 6].Our patient presented with repeated fever at their first hospital visit. Laboratory tests showed the presence of antibodies against varicella-zoster virus IgG (+), while tumor markers and other autoantibody tests were all negative. Therefore, viral infection causing the onset of GFAP-A in this patient cannot be ruled out.

GFAP plays a significant role in regulating the morphology and movement of astrocytes, the formation of the blood-brain barrier, and the regulation of synaptic function. However, since GFAP is an intracellular protein lacking surface antigens, the pathophysiological pathways of the disease remain unclear. Studies have found [7] that anti-GFAP antibodies do not exert direct pathogenic effects by binding to cell surface antigens. However, in animal model studies [8], researchers detected anti-GFAP antibodies in the serum of mice, indicating that GFAP-specific T cells may be involved in the pathogenesis of GFAP-A.

In limited biopsy studies [3, 5, 9], significant inflammatory cell infiltration has been observed in the meninges and perivascular regions, including CD3+, CD4+, CD8 + T cells, CD20 + B cells, and CD138 + plasma cells. Since CD8 + T cells mainly infiltrate near neurons and astrocytes, they may exert toxic effects on neuronal cells [5]. In contrast, CD4 + T cells were scattered, and their pathogenic role remains unclear. Furthermore, recent research on the pathological mechanisms of anti-GFAP antibodies [10] has identified two major phenotypes in GFAP-A patients: one predominantly lymphocytic and the other granulomatous. Although no loss or reduction in GFAP immunostaining, fragmentation, or phagocytosis of astrocytes was evident, upregulation of MHC class I on astrocytes and the presence of granzyme- and perforin-expressing CD8 + T cells in close apposition to astrocytes were observed, along with complement C4d deposition on astrocytes. This suggests that anti-GFAP antibodies themselves may not be directly pathogenic but rather indicate an inflammatory response within the central nervous system.

Additionally, a genetic association study [11] evaluated the relationship between eight classical HLA loci and autoimmune GFAP astrocytopathy. The study found that HLA-A*3303 and HLA-DPB1*05:01 were significantly associated with autoimmune GFAP astrocytopathy, with A*3303 linked to increased disease susceptibility. Given the essential role of HLA class I molecules in the priming and activation of CD8 + T cells, this association provides supporting evidence for the notion that autoimmune GFAP astrocytopathy is likely mediated by autoreactive CD8 + T cells. Moreover, the HLA-A*3303 allele was also associated with prolonged hospital stay and elevated blood glucose levels in patients.

GFAP-A can affect both children and adults, with the main onset age ranging from 40 to 50 years old. Males slightly outnumber females [2]. The most common clinical manifestations include fever, headache, movement disorders, visual abnormalities, mental and behavioral abnormalities, cognitive decline, autonomic nerve dysfunction, and peripheral neuropathy. A small number of patients have severe deterioration of their neurological status and may present with coma and respiratory failure. Deng et al. reported eight area postrema syndrome (APS) patients who were positive for GFAP-IgG [12] and who mainly presented with hiccups, nausea, and vomiting. They suggested that APS might be an early manifestation of GFAP-A, but not an isolated clinical manifestation. Recent reports have shown that serum potassium ≤ 3.9 mmol/L is independent risk factors for the diagnosis of GFAP-A [13]. GFAP-IgG is detected in the CSF in most cases, however, up to 45% of patients are positive for other autoantibodies, making the diagnosis of GFAP-A more challenging [2].

GFAP-A can involve the brain and/or spinal cord. The diagnostic value of MRI of the brain and spinal cord is much higher than that of computed tomography and is the preferred examination. 74% of patients have abnormal manifestations on cranial MRI, with hyperintensities on T2 and FLAIR weighted images [2]. These lesions are mainly distributed around the ventricles, with abnormally high signals in the deep white matter being less common. Lesions can also involve the cerebellum, brainstem, and skull [14]. DWI is usually normal, but a few cases show restricted diffusion [15]. Spinal cord lesions mostly involve the cervical and thoracic spinal cord, and the conus medullaris and cauda equina can also be involved. Spinal cord lesions are widely distributed longitudinally, although approximately 10% of patients have smaller spinal lesions of less than three vertebral levels [16]. Enhancement patterns on MRI may provide an important diagnostic clue in GFAP astrocytopathy. In the brain, the most characteristic pattern is periventricular radial linear enhancement (PVRL); leptomeningeal enhancement and other meningeal/ependymal or punctate patterns have also been reported [2, 7, 10, 17]. In the spinal cord, gadolinium-enhanced images may show linear pericentral (central canal), leptomeningeal, punctate, or patchy intramedullary enhancement, often in association with longitudinally extensive myelitis [2, 7]. Importantly, these enhancement patterns are not entirely specific; similar linear periventricular radial enhancement has been described in other inflammatory or infiltrative disorders (e.g., neurosarcoidosis and certain vascular/infiltrative conditions) [18, 19].

The MRI findings of GFAP-A–such as PVRL, leptomeningeal/brainstem surface enhancement, and longitudinally extensive central T2 hyperintensity in the spinal cord–are closely associated with its underlying pathology. PVRL correlates with inflammation in the perivascular (Virchow-Robin) spaces, where biopsy reveals abundant inflammatory cell infiltration (e.g., CD20 + B cells and CD138 + plasma cells) without vascular wall involvement(Here, we emphasize the perivascular spaces around the penetrating vessels within the brain parenchyma) [10]. This imaging-pathology correlation relates to the regional distribution of GFAP isoforms [17, 20]: GFAPα, the major isoform in the CNS, is predominantly expressed in perivascular white matter, while GFAPδ/ε is mainly located in the meninges and subependymal regions in adults and shows high steroid responsiveness. The former, together with astrocyte activation, microglial activation, and inflammatory infiltration, contributes to blood-brain barrier disruption and PVRL enhancement. The latter explains leptomeningeal enhancement and its rapid resolution after treatment [3].

Furthermore, the extensive longitudinal T2 hyperintensity in the spinal cord corresponds to GFAP expression in areas such as the central gray matter, reflecting astrocytic immune injury and inflammatory response. The enhancement pattern in the spinal cord (e.g., patchy or pial enhancement) is similar to that in the brain and also indicates perivascular inflammation and astrocyte activation [20].

GFAP-A exhibits diverse manifestations, with some patients testing negative for GFAP-IgG. Certain critical clinical and imaging (e.g., MRI) findings may aid in the differential diagnosis of GFAP-A. Diseases that need to be differentiated from GFAP-A include: neuromyelitis optica spectrum disorder (NMOSD), myelin oligodendrocyte glycoprotein antibody disease (MOGAD), and multiple sclerosis (MS).

NMOSD: NMOSD is closely related to AQP4-IgG [21]. Optical neuritis, transverse myelitis, and area postrema syndrome are characteristic of the disease. The brain lesions of NMOSD are distributed in the last area of the medulla oblongata, the periventricular region, hypothalamus, and subcortical or deep white matter, with relatively blurred margins. Spinal cord MRI can present with LETM, which is characterized by an extensive involvement of the spinal cord, with hyperintensity on T2-WI traversing at least 3 vertebral body segments in length. There is a predominance of the central cord as more than 70% of the lesion resides within the central grey matter and more than 50% of spinal cord lesions occupy more than half of the spinal cross-section [22]. In the earlier phases, “bright spot-like” enhancement on enhanced scanning is considered a relatively unique imaging sign [23, 24].

MOGAD: MOGAD clinical manifestations include optic neurites, transverse myelitis, and acute disseminated enceph-alomyelitis. The brain lesions of MOGAD patients are large [25] and scattered. Short segment lesions in the lower segment of the spinal cord, conus involvement, and “H” sign are representative imaging features of MOGAD myelitis. An important feature is that 50–80% of cases have complete regression of lesions after acute onset [26, 27].

MS: MS predominantly affects individuals aged 20 to 40, with a higher prevalence in females. The clinical manifestations depend on the varying degrees of CNS structural involvement. Optic neuritis as well as Uhthoff’s and Lhermitte’s phenomenon are characteristic manifestations [22]. Dawson’s sign is a common brain MRI sign [28] and spinal cord lesions are often short, usually not exceeding three spinal cord segments, and the characteristic enhancement pattern which is described as “open ring”. Furthermore, regarding optical nerve involvement in MS is typically unilateral, anterior, and involves a short segment of the nerve [22].

At present, the most widely used regimens during the acute stage are high-dose steroid hormone pulse therapy, intravenous infusion of gamma globulin and plasma exchange. A meta-analysis shows that most patients respond well to immunotherapy and methylprednisolone pulse therapy. Only a small number of patients have undergone plasma exchange and intravenous immunoglobulin infusion, among whom 88% have a good response and achieved complete or partial remission [2]. However, some patients have a poor response to treatment and experience recurrence or have varying degrees of functional disorders, and even die. During a 19 month follow-up, up to 28% of patients experienced recurrence, usually during the period of gradual steroid dose reduction [7]. This emphasizes the necessity of monitoring and considering maintenance therapy. For patients with a tendency to relapse, immunosuppressants, such as rituximab and azathioprine, can be added. Patients with overlapping AQP4 antibodies can have poor responses to first-line immunotherapy and patients with AQP4 antibodies and spinal cord changes are more prone to recurrence [7]. Hormone therapy combined with immunomodulators is recommended for these patients.

Admittedly, this study has certain limitations. This report describes a single patient; therefore, the frequency and specificity of the observed enhancement pattern cannot be established, nor can it be used as a standalone diagnostic sign. Similar intramedullary and leptomeningeal enhancement patterns may occur in other inflammatory, infectious, or infiltrative myelopathies, and interpretation should rely on the overall clinicoradiologic context together with CSF GFAP-IgG testing. In addition, the follow-up duration was limited, and longer longitudinal imaging is needed to better characterize evolution, treatment response, and the risk of relapse.

In summary, this case highlights that GFAP-A can present with longitudinally extensive spinal cord involvement accompanied by intramedullary punctate-to-linear/patchy enhancement and leptomeningeal enhancement, together with the characteristic radial periventricular enhancement in the brain. Based on literature and clinical experience, we propose that certain spinal MRI features (such as LETM and PVRL) along with infectious meningitis-like symptoms (headache, fever) and CSF alterations (suggesting an infectious profile) may serve as indicators for testing GFAP-IgG. We recommend routine GFAP antibody screening in patients presenting with the aforementioned suspicious findings. In cases where CSF antibody test results are negative or weakly positive, a diagnosis of GFAP-A can still be established when characteristic imaging manifestations are consistent with clinical symptoms. And follow-up MRI is recommended given the risk of relapse during steroid tapering.
