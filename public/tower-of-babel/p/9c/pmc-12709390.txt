OsmoFold: A high-throughput tool for predicting the impact of osmolytes on protein structure
Authors: Vincent Nicholson, Sujina Maharjan, Shahar Sukenik, Thomas C. Boothby
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Small molecules can affect a protein’s fold and stability. Physical chemistry conceptualizes this effect using Tanford’s transfer model, which describes the change in free energy of a protein upon transfer from water to a solution containing some concentration of a small molecule. Using additive transfer free energies of individual amino acids, one can apply the transfer model to quantitatively predict how small molecules affect protein folding equilibria. However, no open-source framework currently exists for applying this method at scale. Here, we present OsmoFold, an open-source suite of tools designed to act as a user-friendly, high-throughput option for predicting the impact of osmolytes on protein folding. OsmoFold is available as a Google Colab notebook, requiring no programming experience and only a protein structure file as input, and as a Python package for the analysis of large data sets and seamless integration into bioinformatic pipelines. To showcase OsmoFold’s versatility, we demonstrate its ability to recapitulate results from published experimental data. In addition, we show OsmoFold’s utility in predicting ideal excipients for biologic pharmaceuticals using an in vitro study of human blood clotting factor VIII. Our results reaffirm the utility of this framework for predicting the effect of osmolytes on protein stability. At the same time, the accompanying tool makes this framework more accessible and versatile than before. In doing so, OsmoFold has the potential to drive advancements in protein stability research, improving biologic formulation strategies and therapeutic development.

Significance

Small molecules can act as protein stabilizers, but predicting the magnitude and nature of their impact on a protein’s fold has traditionally required specialized knowledge and tools. OsmoFold bridges this gap by making quantitative predictions of how small molecules impact protein stability accessible to a broad range of researchers. Built using established physical chemistry theory, OsmoFold includes a Google Colab notebook that requires no programming skills and a Python package for high-throughput integration into computational pipelines. These tools allow users to easily simulate the impact of different osmolytes on any protein structure. We believe that OsmoFold will enable researchers across biophysics, pharmaceutical sciences, and biotechnology to utilize these predictions, facilitating faster and more accurate studies of protein–small-molecule interactions.

Introduction

For the last 50 years, an important paradigm in biochemistry has been the ability of osmolytes, organic small molecules that are compatible with other biomolecules even at high concentrations, to regulate protein folding, dynamics, and self-assembly (1,2,3). In cells, osmolytes are enriched during water stress, where they have been observed to be essential for maintaining proteostasis (4,5,6,7,8). In vitro, they alter the thermodynamics of protein folding and can either stabilize or destabilize folded proteins (9). The ability of osmolytes to stabilize biomolecules has led to their widespread use as stabilizing excipients to prevent the degradation of proteins and biologic pharmaceuticals in vitro (10,11,12,13,14,15).

In physical biochemistry, the transfer model has been used to quantify the effect of cosolutes, including osmolytes, on protein folding (3,16). The transfer model describes the change in free energy of a protein with a fixed conformation upon transfer from water to a solution containing a given cosolute (ΔGtr). This free energy is determined by the interactions between the solute and the surface chemistry of the protein. As a result, the ΔGtr for a given protein-osmolyte combination changes along with that protein’s conformation. Thus, an osmolyte can stabilize the native state of a given protein if the transfer free energy (TFE) of the native state (ΔGtrF) is lower than the TFE of the denatured state (ΔGtrU) (3,9). This is frequently observed with osmolytes such as trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), trehalose, and sucrose (17,18). The opposite effect, destabilization, is also possible (19). If ΔGtrF is greater than ΔGtrU for a given protein-osmolyte combination, the denatured state will be stabilized relative to the native one. This is commonly observed with osmolytes such as urea and guanidinium hydrochloride (20,21).

In the early 2000s, Matthew Auton and Wayne D. Bolen pioneered the determination of additive TFE values (9,16,19,22). In brief, if one measures the ΔGtr of each amino acid side group and of the peptide backbone in the presence of a given osmolyte, the ΔGtr of an entire protein can be determined by summing these values and multiplying by the relative accessible surface area (RASA) of each chemical group (16). Although simple, this technique allows one to calculate ΔΔGtr values, which quantify the difference in TFE between two conformational states of a protein. This value signifies whether a given osmolyte will favor the presence of one structure over another (16,22), and it has been highly successful in recapitulating experimental data (16,22).

Auton and Bolen developed a web server for automatically performing these calculations for a given protein of interest (16). Although this has proven to be a useful tool, the implementation is limiting in four key ways. First, the list of osmolytes available through the web server cannot be modified by the end user. Second, the tool only allows for TFE calculations using monomeric protein chains, meaning that one cannot use it to calculate the effect of osmolytes on protein quaternary structure. Third, the tool has low throughput, allowing only one structure to be analyzed at a time. Finally, the reliance on a web server means the tool cannot be integrated into existing pipelines.

Here, we present OsmoFold, a suite of tools designed to enable easy and high-throughput calculation of ΔΔGtr values using Auton and Bolen’s method. OsmoFold is available in two forms, offering different capabilities and levels of accessibility. The first form, a Google Colab notebook, enables users to calculate ΔΔGtr values using uploaded Protein Data Bank (PDB) files from any source and requires no programming knowledge. The second form, a Python package, enables the high-throughput computation of ΔΔGtr values with a high degree of customizability.

We benchmark OsmoFold through several case studies involving in vitro protein unfolding and protein dimerization. These case studies demonstrate that ΔΔGtr predictions from OsmoFold closely align with previously published experimental data. In one example, we use OsmoFold to accurately recapitulate the effect of osmolytes on the folding equilibrium of a well-characterized peptide, SH3 (23). In another, we use OsmoFold to predict the effect of osmolytes on the dimerization of a small protein domain, GB1 (23). Finally, we use OsmoFold to predict the effect of osmolytes on proteins undergoing thermal stress in E. coli cell lysate, demonstrating that OsmoFold is effective in complex, mixed solutions (24).

We then apply OsmoFold practically to calculate ΔΔGtr values for a variety of protein biologics. We find that, although several osmolytes perform well across the board, there are protein-to-protein differences in which osmolytes act as stabilizers versus destabilizers. We affirm the accuracy of these predictions using human blood clotting factor VIII (FVIII), a common biologic that is used to treat hemophilia (25,26). Using empirical studies of FVIII blood clotting capacity, we showed that OsmoFold’s predicted ΔΔGtr values for different osmolytes correlate with their ability to protect FVIII from thermal stress while in solution.

The results presented here reaffirm the utility of this method for predicting the energetics of osmolyte-induced protein conformational changes (16). At the same time, the accompanying tools make this method far more accessible, customizable, and high throughput for labs that seek to use it. We believe that OsmoFold will empower researchers across biophysics, pharmaceutical sciences, and biotechnology to utilize ΔΔGtr predictions, facilitating more accurate studies of protein-osmolyte interactions and protein folding and accelerating the identification of optimal excipient-biologic pairs.

Materials and methods

Analysis of data from Rydeen et al.

Rydeen et al. (2018) collected data on two proteins in the presence of several osmolytes, including each of the nine included in OsmoFold (23). We obtained experimental values for the ΔΔGtrF→U of SH3 with each osmolyte and the ΔΔGtrD→M of GB1 with each osmolyte, as well as uncertainty in the measurements, from the publication’s supplemental files.

To generate OsmoFold predictions for the ΔΔGtrF→U of SH3, we generated OsmoFold predictions for each conformer of an empirical NMR structure of SH3 from the PDB (PDB: 2AZS) and took the mean value (27). To generate predictions for the ΔΔGtrD→M of GB1, we generated OsmoFold predictions for each conformer of a solution NMR structure of the dimeric form of the GB1 A34F mutant (PDB: 2RMM) and took the mean value. This allowed us to calculate the ΔGtr of the dimer, ΔGtrD. The ΔGtr of the monomer, ΔGtrM, was calculated by deleting chain B from the aforementioned structures and inputting the resulting monomeric structure into OsmoFold. The ΔΔGtrD→M was then determined with the following equation:ΔΔGtrD→M=2(ΔGtrM)−ΔGtrD

Analysis of data from Pepelnjak et al.

In order to systematically compare these data to the predictions produced by OsmoFold, we had to filter the Pepelnjak data set in two ways. First, we compared the Limited Proteolysis Coupled to Mass Spectrometry data for each osmolyte and identified proteins that appeared in datasets for at least four of the six osmolytes. We then filtered this list further by eliminating proteins that had only four appearances in the data and that also appeared in the glucose data set. This was done because our inability to predict the ΔΔGtrF→U of proteins with glucose would leave us with only three data points, thus making it impossible to build a meaningful correlation.

The remaining list of 338 proteins (see File S4, Correlations_Final.csv) then needed PDB files to be run through OsmoFold. Because it was infeasible to manually find structures for each of these proteins, we used the AlphaFold database (https://alphafold.ebi.ac.uk) to download a vetted AlphaFold prediction for each protein. Chainsaw (28) was used to separate the proteins into individual folded domains. ΔΔGtrF→U values were calculated for each folded domain, and then the ΔΔGtrF→U of the entire protein was given as a sum of these domains. Any amino acids that were not included in a folded domain were included in the final prediction by assuming their RASA = 1. We then correlated the resulting ΔΔGtrF→U values with the stabilization scores published in Pepelnjak et al. (2024).

FVIII clotting assay

FVIII clotting was measured in accordance with the method put forth in Packebush et al. (2023) (26). Briefly, 5 μL of FVIII was added to 50 μL of FVIII-deficient blood plasma. 50 μL of APTT-XL reagent was added to the mixture, which was then incubated at 37°C for 3 min. After incubation, 50 μL of CaCl2, warmed to 42°C, was added. FVIII function was determined using 50% clotting time (aPTT). To determine aPTT, OD was measured at 405 nm, every 15 s over a 10-min period using a plate reader (Tecan Spark Cyto 600).

FVIII thermal stress

To evaluate the protective effect of osmolytes on FVIII during thermal stress, 5 μL of 10 mg/mL FVIII was mixed with 5 μL of 1 M osmolytes (thus making a solution of 5 mg/mL FVIII and 500 mM osmolyte) and incubated at 56°C for 1 h. For positive controls, identical solutions were stored at 4°C for 1 h. For negative controls, a solution of 500 mM osmolyte and 0 mg/mL FVIII was prepared. After incubation, control and test samples were tested for aPTT using the FVIII clotting assay outlined above. Percent protection was calculated using the following equation:CN−CECN−CP

Here, CN is the measured clotting time of the negative control, CE is the measured clotting time of the experimental sample, and CP is the measured clotting time of the positive control. Experiments were repeated three times with three technical replicates each, for an n of nine.

Monte Carlo correlation error simulations

To generate the correlation statistics in Figs. 2 and 5, custom code was written to test a large variety of correlations with simulated error. Two normal distributions were created for each datapoint, one using the x-axis mean and standard deviation, and the other using the y-axis mean and standard deviation. By generating random values from the resulting normal distributions, we could simulate the effect of random error on each point in both the x and y direction (OsmoFold predictions inherently do not come with an approximation of error, and therefore, error was only simulated for experimental data. The exception is Fig. 2, in which multiple conformers of the same protein were used to produce a standard deviation for the OsmoFold predictions). Using this system on each of our correlation plots, we performed 100,000 error simulations, each having a unique Pearson correlation coefficient, R. We can use this list of R-values to calculate Rsd. The code used to execute this method is available in the supporting material (File S3) and is well annotated. This is identical to the method used in Nicholson et al. (2025a) (30).

Calculating ΔΔGtrF→U and ΔΔGtrD→M using the Auton and Bolen web server

The “Protein M-Value Calculation” web server, located at http://best.bio.jhu.edu/mvalue/, performs predictions of single PDBs uploaded via a short form and returns results in plain text. We used this web server to re-create several of the analyses above (with minor differences) for comparison with OsmoFold.

For SH3, we used the first conformer from the same solution NMR structure as above (PDB: 2AZS) and performed predictions in both OsmoFold and the web server. In the results returned by the web server, ΔΔGtrF→U is referred to as “m-value”. Separate predictions are provided for the ΔΔGtrF→U of the lower-bound solvent accessibility of the unfolded state, the upper bound, and the mean. As in OsmoFold, the mean solvent accessibility was assumed to be most representative. Both predictions were then multiplied by 0.4 to reflect the concentrations used by Rydeen et al. (2018).

For GB1, we again use the first conformer of a solution NMR structure (PDB: 2RMM) of the GB1 dimer and delete one chain to get the structure of the monomer. These structures were then run through both OsmoFold and the web server. The web server returns mean predictions for the ΔGtr of the native state of these structures, which are then used in the above equation to calculate ΔΔGtrD→M. These values were again multiplied by 0.4.

Finally, for FVIII, we use the same structure as before (PDB: 3CDZ). The web server returns an error when computing ΔΔGtrF→U values for the full structure, so it was separated into heavy and light chains for the purposes of this comparison. For both chains, OsmoFold and the web server were used to compute a ΔΔGtrF→U value, which we then multiplied by 0.5 to reflect the concentration in the FVIII experiments. The heavy and light chain values were then summed to give a final prediction of the ΔΔGtrF→U of the full structure.

Results

OsmoFold: An overview

Since Auton and Bolen published the first web server for predicting ΔΔGtr values for protein-osmolyte combinations, several tools have been developed to enable more complex, high-throughput protein structural analysis (16,31,32,33). Building on this foundation, this work aims to adapt existing ΔΔGtr prediction methods into a more high-throughput tool: OsmoFold.

The core equation of OsmoFold is an implementation of Auton and Bolen’s method for using TFEs to calculate the ΔGtr for a given protein-osmolyte system.ΔGtr=∑iαigi+gbb∑jαj

Here, i is the numerical index for each sidechain in the linear protein chain, whereas j is the index for each backbone unit. The parameters αi and αj denote the RASA of each chemical group, in which 0 represents complete exclusion from the solvent, and 1 represents the maximum possible solvent accessibility of the given residue. Finally, gi represents the experimentally determined TFE for each R group with a given osmolyte, and gbb represents the same for each backbone unit (3,16,34).

In the present implementation, we use nine sets of additive TFEs directly from Auton and Bolen (9,22) (Fig. S1). Solvent-accessible surface areas are calculated using MDTraj (35), a computational tool for protein structural analysis. These values are scaled to RASAs by dividing by the theoretical maximum surface area of the residue (16) (Fig. S1). Experimentally derived additive TFEs are available for nine osmolytes at the time of our preparation of this manuscript: trehalose, trimethylamine N-oxide (TMAO), sarcosine, sucrose, sorbitol, proline, betaine, glycerol, and urea (19,22,36). For trehalose, multiple sets of values were available from Auton (22) and independently from Hong et al. (36). In addition, OsmoFold will accept a dictionary of custom TFE values as input. This means that users can apply OsmoFold’s method using novel or proprietary TFE values, as well as those predicted computationally.

Calculating ΔGtr with this method enables a complex analysis of the effect of osmolytes on protein structural changes (Fig. 1). For example, computing the difference between the ΔGtr of a folded protein (ΔGtrF) and its unfolded state (ΔGtrU) yields ΔΔGtrF→U, a prediction for the effect of an osmolyte on the folding-unfolding equilibrium of the protein (Fig. 1 A). We mirror Auton and Bolen’s approach for approximating the solvent-accessible surface area of unfolded proteins by using the mean of upper- and lower-bound solvent-accessible surface area from Creamer et al. (16,37,38). OsmoFold Colab

OsmoFold Colab (https://tinyurl.com/osmofold-colab) is a user-friendly tool for calculating the ΔGtr of protein structures in the presence of various osmolytes. Users can upload PDB files from any source and choose analyses from a drop-down menu to perform calculations on protein folding and unfolding, conformational changes, homodimerization, and heterodimerization (Figs. 1 and S2 A). OsmoFold Colab allows users to calculate osmolyte effects on protein structural changes without requiring prior knowledge of programming or underlying equations for calculating ΔGtr values. The resulting ΔΔGtr values are then displayed using a simple Matplotlib script, with detailed raw outputs being stored as a CSV file (Fig. S2 B).

OsmoFold Local

In contrast to OsmoFold Colab, OsmoFold Local is a Python package designed for high-throughput applications, such as analyzing predicted structures of entire proteomes or the output of molecular dynamics simulations. The code is publicly available on GitHub (https://github.com/vnchlsn/OsmoFold), featuring comprehensive documentation and active issue tracking. In addition, extensive unit tests have been written for each function.

OsmoFold Local provides three main user-facing functions:

protein_unfolded_dG(): Calculates the ΔGtr of a completely unfolded protein in 1 M osmolyte, assuming uniform disorder. The results scale by osmolyte concentration (e.g., ΔGtr at 0.5M is half the value at 1 M).

protein_folded_dG(): Computes the ΔGtr of a folded protein structure in 1 M osmolyte using RASA values derived from a PDB input. Results also scale with osmolyte concentration.

protein_ddg_folding(): Takes the difference between protein_folded_dG() and protein_unfolded_dG() to predict ΔΔGtrF→U for a given protein, based on its folded and unfolded states.

OsmoFold Local can process PDB files from any source both individually or in batches using parallel processing, which enables one to calculate the ΔGtr of whole proteomes or for a structural ensemble of a single protein. A far more thorough breakdown of each function, its inputs, and its outputs can be found in OsmoFold’s documentation, available online at ReadTheDocs (https://osmofold.readthedocs.io).

We next present several use cases where OsmoFold Local has been applied to support experimental data on protein-osmolyte interactions. These examples highlight the versatility of the tool in practical applications.

OsmoFold predicts the osmolyte-induced stabilization and destabilization of SH3

SH3 is a highly conserved peptide found in the N-terminal domain of many signaling proteins (Fig. 2 A) (39). This 59-residue, beta-sheet-rich globular motif is frequently used as a model for protein folding studies (39,40). Rydeen et al. (2018) investigated the effects of osmolytes and small molecules on protein folding, using SH3 as a model system. They quantified the folded-to-unfolded equilibrium of SH3 in various solution conditions using 19F NMR, reporting the results as ΔΔGtrF→U values (23). These values capture the influence of osmolytes on the free energy difference between folded and unfolded states (23).

Using OsmoFold’s protein_ddg_folding() function and a solution NMR structure containing 10 conformers of SH3 (PDB: 2AZS) (40), we predicted the ΔΔGtrF→U of each conformer in the presence of several of the osmolytes studied in Rydeen et al. (2018) and report the mean for each osmolyte (Fig. 2 B). We then scaled these values by 0.4 to match the 400 mM concentration used in their study. The resulting predicted ΔΔGtrF→U values strongly correlate with the experimental values, with an R-value of 0.9542 (Fig. 2 B). Using a Monte Carlo-based error analysis (30), we utilize the standard deviations reported by Rydeen et al. (2018) to compute the standard deviation in this R-value, which we calculate to be 0.02 (Fig. 2 B).

Although our predicted ΔΔGtrF→U values strongly correlate with data from Rydeen et al. (2018), the absolute magnitudes differ slightly from a compensation line (y=x). This suggests that OsmoFold is successful at predicting the stabilizing effect of osmolytes in a relative sense (i.e., whether one osmolyte will be more or less stabilizing than another) but less successful at predicting the absolute magnitude of said stabilization. In their methods paper on this approach, Auton and Bolen find a similar deviation from the compensation line, which they attribute to the activity coefficients of individual amino acids in the presence of each osmolyte not being known, making it impossible to account for activity in these predictions (16). Notably, urea differs most from the compensation line in both their work and ours, which is consistent with this notion (16). Another potential confounding variable is the solution condition in which the experimental ΔΔGtrF→U values were determined. In determining their additive TFEs, Auton and Bolen used tripeptides in pure water with 1 M of each osmolyte (9). In determining the ΔΔGtrF→U of SH3, Rydeen et al. (2018) used a buffered solution of 50 mM HEPES, 50 mM citrate, and 50 mM bis-tris-propane at pH 7.4 (23).

Taken together, these results demonstrate that OsmoFold strongly predicts the relative effect of osmolytes on proteins with simple folded-to-unfolded transitions.

OsmoFold predicts the osmolyte-induced association and dissociation of GB1

In addition to SH3, Rydeen et al. (2018) examine the effect of osmolytes on the dimer-to-monomer transition (D→M) of GB1. GB1 is the B1 domain of the streptococcal immunoglobulin-binding protein G (41) (Fig. 2 C). Wild-type GB1 is predominantly monomeric, but a point mutation (A34F) greatly increases the stability of the homodimeric state, resulting in a monomer-dimer equilibrium (42). Using 19F NMR, the authors quantify the effect of osmolyte on the dimer-to-monomer transition, ΔΔGtrD→M (23). Similar to their result with SH3, Rydeen et al. (2018) observe that osmolytes have differential effects on this conformational change. Several osmolytes (TMAO, trehalose, sarcosine, and betaine) stabilize the dimer, whereas others (sorbitol, sucrose, urea, glycerol) stabilize the monomer (23).

Although TFEs have traditionally been used to predict conformational changes primarily in monomeric proteins (16), they have occasionally been used to predict the stability of proteins in the dimeric state (43,44). We therefore wondered if OsmoFold could successfully recapitulate Rydeen et al.’s data on GB1 dimerization. Mirroring our approach with SH3, we calculated the ΔGtr of several conformers of a GB1 A34F dimer and monomer (PDB: 2RMM, Fig. 2 D) and took the mean. We then predicted the effect of osmolytes on the dimer-to-monomer transition with the following equation:ΔΔGtrD→M=2(ΔGtrM)−ΔGtrD

Like before, we scaled these values by 0.4 to match the 400 mM concentration used in the experimental study. The resulting predictions correlated with the data from Rydeen et al. (2018), with an R-value of 0.6697 (standard deviation = 0.06). This result contains clear outliers. Namely, sucrose and sorbitol were predicted by OsmoFold to strongly stabilize the dimer, but Rydeen et al. (2018) found that they actually weakly destabilize it.

In comparison with the data on SH3, we find a trendline that is much further from the y=x compensation line, which suggests that the systematic error in the magnitude of ΔΔGtr varies on a solution-by-solution (or perhaps protein-by-protein) basis.

Taken together, these results suggest the utility of OsmoFold in predicting the relative effects of osmolytes on changes in protein quaternary structure, albeit with lower accuracy than simple protein folding/unfolding predictions.

OsmoFold predicts osmolyte thermal stabilization of the E. coli proteome

Although OsmoFold can predict the ΔΔGtr of osmolytes with single structures, these calculations involve very few proteins and therefore do not explicitly require a high-throughput tool. We therefore wondered if OsmoFold could recapitulate the effect of osmolytes on a data set of several hundred proteins.

In 2024, Pepelnjak et al. measured the ability of several osmolytes to prevent the thermal denaturation of proteins in E. coli lysate (24). Using limited proteolysis coupled to mass spectrometry, they determined which proteins in the E. coli proteome underwent denaturation at different temperatures and which of those proteins were protected by osmolytes (24,45,46). From these data, they generate a “stabilization score” for each osmolyte with each protein detected by mass spectrometry. In this context, the stabilization score simply represents an osmolyte’s ability to affect a protein’s denaturation temperature. If the osmolyte raises the temperature required for denaturation (thereby stabilizing the protein), it will have a positive stabilization score. If an osmolyte lowers the requisite temperature, it will have a negative stabilization score (24).

Of the six osmolytes tested in this study (trehalose, TMAO, betaine, proline, glycerol, and glucose), all but glucose had empirical TFEs published in the literature and were therefore available in OsmoFold (24). Thus, we wondered if empirical stabilization scores of different protein-osmolyte combinations correlated with the predicted ΔΔGtrF→U values from OsmoFold. (2024) data, isolated the folded domains of each structure using Chainsaw (28) (see materials and methods), and calculated the ΔΔGtrF→U of each one for each of the five osmolytes (Fig. 3). Pepelnjak et al. use 1 M solutions of each osmolyte except for trehalose, where 500 mM was used. OsmoFold can inform the selection of stabilizing excipients for biologic pharmaceuticals

Protein biologics are protein-based pharmaceuticals made by or derived from living organisms. They are widely used in the treatment of disease and are often highly unstable (47,48,49), making their storage and transport challenging, particularly in regions with limited or unreliable cold-storage infrastructure (47,50). To mitigate this instability, stabilizing excipients, including osmolytes, are commonly used as stabilizers (12,51,52).

Our benchmarks revealed that OsmoFold was most effective when applied to single proteins in vitro. In addition, its predictions are more accurate for the relative effect of different osmolytes than they are for the absolute magnitude of osmolyte stabilization (Fig. 2). Given this, a good application for OsmoFold is to identify effective stabilizing excipients for a variety of protein biologics.

Each of these biologics is typically formulated alongside stabilizing excipients, with five of them using at least one osmolyte from OsmoFold’s list of nine (Table 2). Although the overall trend was clear, two osmolytes, urea and betaine, were outliers. Urea, despite its negative ΔΔGtrF→U and well-known destabilizing properties, was functionally indistinct from more neutral osmolytes. This is likely due to a floor effect in our assay, since protection cannot be measured below 0%. More interestingly, betaine showed unexpectedly high protection despite a neutral ΔΔGtrF→U, suggesting potential specific interactions not captured by the additive model.

By using ΔΔGtrF→U predictions to identify optimal stabilizers, research groups and pharmaceutical companies may streamline the process of identifying excipients for different drugs and, in doing so, conserve resources in research and development.

OsmoFold exhibits similar accuracy to related methods while providing improvements in throughput and customizability

OsmoFold expands upon currently available tools, such as Auton and Bolen’s web server, by offering a more customizable and versatile implementation of existing methods. Where the web server can only accept single pdb files as input and requires filling out a form for each prediction, OsmoFold is open source, can process large numbers of structures in parallel, and accepts a wide variety of inputs. In addition to Auton and Bolen’s web server, there are several other methods and tools for predicting the ΔΔGtrF→U of a protein and a given osmolyte, often relying on different underlying theory (72,73,74). To our knowledge, none of these tools match OsmoFold in throughput and often require one to perform preliminary analyses, such as molecular dynamics simulations.

For example, a recent preprint, Pereira and Martínez (2025), uses coarse-grained simulations of the denatured state of folded proteins, combined with all-atom simulations of the osmolyte’s interaction with the resulting structures (74). Kirkwood-Buff solvation theory is then used to calculate a lower and upper bound for the ΔΔGtrF→U value. This method is low throughput (as individual computationally intensive simulations need to be performed for each protein-osmolyte combination), so it was not feasible to repeat our benchmarks using their method. However, the authors do make direct calculations of the ΔΔGtrF→U of SH3 in the presence of TMAO and urea in their preprint. For TMAO, they predict a ΔΔGtrF→U between 130 and 500 cal/mol, and for urea, they predict a ΔΔGtrF→U between −150 and −560 cal/mol. In both cases, these bounds capture the experimental values from Rydeen et al. (450 ± 20 and −310 ± 20 cal/mol, respectively) and OsmoFold’s predictions (390 ± 30 and −460 ± 18 cal/mol, respectively). Furthermore, using an established web server, ProtSA (75,76), to predict the ensemble of the unfolded state did not significantly affect OsmoFold’s predictions (Fig. S6).

Taken together, these results suggest that OsmoFold offers similar predictive accuracy to established methods while offering improvements in throughput, customization, and ease of use.

Discussion

Over the past two decades, advances in our understanding of protein–small-molecule interactions have enabled researchers to quantitatively predict the effects of osmolytes on protein folding equilibria. However, no tool has previously existed to apply this method at scale. Here, we introduce OsmoFold, a Python package and accompanying Colab notebook designed to increase the accessibility and throughput of ΔΔGtr predictions. Our results demonstrate that OsmoFold is both user-friendly and high throughput, making it suitable for a broad range of research applications.

We show that OsmoFold accurately reproduces experimental data in both ideal conditions, such as predicting the ΔΔGtrF→U of SH3, and more complex systems, such as lysate-based thermal protection assays. Additionally, we apply OsmoFold to the in vitro stabilization of FVIII by various osmolytes, finding that those with higher predicted ΔΔGtrF→U values are generally more effective at preserving protein function during thermal stress. Together, these findings highlight the utility of ΔΔGtr predictions, increase their accessibility to the scientific community, and suggest that they may accelerate excipient discovery in the development of protein biologics.

OsmoFold demonstrates the versatility of ΔΔGtr predictions

A principal goal of this work has been to predict ΔΔGtr values in a variety of biological contexts. In the past, this method has first and foremost been employed to predict the impact of osmolytes on protein folding-unfolding equilibria (16,19). Less often, the method has been used to predict the impact of osmolytes on the transition between two folded protein conformations, such as between the “open” and “closed” conformation of E. coli HSP90 (77). By evaluating OsmoFold in both proven and novel contexts, we gain a clearer understanding of the method’s capabilities and boundaries.

As has been repeatedly demonstrated in literature, the method is most effective when applied to monomeric proteins in vitro with distinct folding-unfolding equilibria (16). When predicting the ΔΔGtrF→U for SH3, OsmoFold closely matches experimental values, achieving an R-value of 0.9542.

However, predictive accuracy declines in more complex systems. For example, when applied to the GB1 peptide, which undergoes a monomer-dimer transition, OsmoFold yields a lower R-value of 0.6697. There are several possible reasons for this observation. Most prominently, the dimerization of GB1 involves only a small number of residues, which results in very small ΔGtrM and ΔGtrD values relative to the typical margin of error. This issue is compounded by the fact that the experimental data were collected in the presence of several salts, which have an unaccounted for effect on the TFEs of each individual osmolyte. These findings suggest that accurate predictions of dimerization equilibria require larger protein interfaces and minimal interference from additional solutes.

Finally, OsmoFold was generally effective in recapitulating the whole-proteome data from Pepelnjak et al. (2024). As expected, protein-osmolyte combinations with a high ΔΔGtrF→U had significantly higher stabilization scores than ones with a low ΔΔGtrF→U. The correlation was by far the most compelling when examining the results protein by protein, with ∼83% of proteins producing a correlation with an R-value greater than 0. We feel that this supports the use of OsmoFold in chemically heterogeneous environments, provided that predictions are interpreted with caution. One should also consider the experimental design used to produce empirical data. For example, Pepelnjak et al. (2024) used known concentrations of osmolytes added to the media of their E. coli. However, cellular concentrations of these osmolytes were not quantified, nor were the effects of these additions of levels of natural osmolytes determined. This could account in part for discrepancies between some OsmoFold predictions with the empirical data. In addition, it is worth noting that stabilization score, as calculated in Pepelnjak et al., could simply be an imperfect analog ΔΔGtrF→U of folded proteins in solution with osmolytes.

Overall, our series of benchmarks for OsmoFold’s ability to recapitulate experimental data has resulted in two key findings. First, OsmoFold is ideally used with single proteins in vitro in the presence of a single osmolyte and few to no other cosolutes, confirming findings from Auton and Bolen and others (16). Second, while its predictive power diminishes in multicomponent or crowded systems, OsmoFold still captures meaningful biological trends and can be used to generate hypotheses for further validation.

OsmoFold can accelerate the selection of suitable stabilizing excipients for protein biologics

Using the temperature-sensitive biologic pharmaceutical FVIII, we demonstrated that OsmoFold was successful at identifying optimal excipients. Although the functional data presented here are limited in scope, they suggest that OsmoFold may be useful in identifying excipients for a variety of other biologics. The predictions shown in Fig. 4, when cross-referenced with Table 2, suggest that several biologic pharmaceuticals may be using suboptimal stabilizing excipients. For example, current formulations of FVIII use sucrose as an excipient, but both OsmoFold and empirical experiments show that sarcosine provides ∼30% more protection to FVIII than sucrose under thermal stress. By providing useful predictions, OsmoFold may be used to narrow lists of candidate excipients for new biologics, accelerating the research and development of new biologic pharmaceuticals while reducing costs.

A major potential application for this approach is in the storage of biologic pharmaceuticals outside of the cold chain. Currently, many biologics are reliant on the cold chain for shipment to hospitals and pharmacies (47). This severely limits the ability of countries and regions with unreliable electricity to access these medicines, leading to a global divide in healthcare access and outcomes (47). However, biologics that are preserved in the dry state (either through desiccation or lyophilization) or stabilized by excipients in solution can be safely shipped at ambient temperatures (7,47). Thus, OsmoFold may aid researchers in developing formulations for biologics that allow for storage outside the cold chain, increasing global health outcomes.

Although the correlation between ΔΔGtrF→U and protective capacity is significant overall (Fig. 5 D), two notable outliers merited discussion. First, urea, which OsmoFold correctly predicts to destabilize FVIII, has a measured protective capacity of ∼0%, which is comparable to more neutral osmolytes like proline and glycerol. This likely reflects a floor effect in the assay; protection cannot be measured below zero, and osmolytes that destabilize proteins thus converge on a similar functional outcome with ones that have no effect.

The second and more intriguing outlier is betaine. Despite having a predicted ΔΔGtrF→U comparable to glycerol, it offers substantially greater protection. This discrepancy could arise from specific interactions between betaine and FVIII, which are not captured by additive TFE models. Indeed, specific binding can dramatically enhance an osmolyte’s stabilizing effect (24). Notably, Roth et al. (2012) found that betaine significantly improves FVIII secretion in CHO cells compared with other osmolytes, including trehalose and sorbitol, suggesting that FVIII and betaine may interact directly (78).

Although excluding betaine from the analysis increases the R-value of the correlation, we refrain from doing so without direct evidence of a binding interaction. For this reason, we present the data with all osmolytes included (Fig. 5 D) and interpret the correlation conservatively. Future studies using techniques like NMR or isothermal titration calorimetry could further clarify the mechanistic basis for betaine’s effect on FVIII stabilization.

Limitations and directions for future improvement of OsmoFold

Although OsmoFold offers compelling predictive performance in both simple and complex systems, its accuracy is still constrained by a few key technical limitations. Addressing these could further broaden its utility and precision.

As described above, one cannot account for the role of activity in these predictions without reliable activity coefficients for each amino acid in both water (γi,w) and in 1 M of each osmolyte (γi,os). γi,os has not been experimentally determined for most amino acid–osmolyte combinations, meaning activity is unaccounted for in OsmoFold’s final predictions (16). Experimental determination of these activity coefficients would likely meaningfully improve predictions but would be labor intensive (79,80). Alternatively, simulations have been increasingly successful at determining these activity coefficients in silico (79), and future versions of OsmoFold could incorporate computationally derived activity coefficients where empirical data are lacking.

A second limitation lies in how OsmoFold approximates solvent-accessible surface area for the denatured state. Although the generalized mean values from Creamer et al. (1997) have proved to be effective (16), it is likely that a sequence-based prediction of solvent accessibility in the unfolded state would be more effective. We see little difference in the two methods for SH3 (Fig. S6), but the magnitude of the difference is likely to vary protein to protein. Disordered proteins exhibit a dynamic ensemble of conformations whose average solvent accessibility is highly sequence specific, and growing evidence suggests this may also hold for the denatured states of folded proteins (76,81,82). Thus, incorporating sequence-based predictions of denatured solvent-accessible surface area could improve OsmoFold’s accuracy. Other pipelines for predicting ΔΔGtrF→U values, such as that of Pereira and Martínez (2025), have incorporated physics-based simulations to better sample the conformational ensemble of specific proteins (74).

Although tools like ProtSA can predict the conformational ensemble of denatured proteins (75), there is no open-source implementation of these methods that can be integrated into OsmoFold. Should one become available, it could be readily integrated. In lieu of this, users may run ProtSA predictions separately and use them with OsmoFold to get a ΔΔGtr that better approximates the denatured chain. Alternatively, molecular dynamics simulations, like those used in Pereira and Martínez (2025), can be used to generate unfolded conformers (74). OsmoFold’s modular architecture enables such user-defined inputs to be incorporated with minimal effort.

In sum, although OsmoFold is a functional and high-throughput implementation of existing thermodynamic models, its accuracy remains constrained by gaps in experimental data and by assumptions about unfolded-state structure. However, these limitations are not insurmountable. Advances in experimental measurement, sequence-based solvent-accessible surface area prediction, and open-source ensemble modeling tools offer clear paths for improving the predictive accuracy of this method.

Conclusion

Osmolytes play diverse roles in biology, from enabling organisms to survive abiotic stress to maintaining proteostasis during small environmental perturbations. In medicine and biotechnology, osmolytes are widely used as protein stabilizers. Despite their importance, predicting and quantifying osmolyte effects has remained challenging and inaccessible to many researchers. OsmoFold addresses this gap, representing a significant advancement in making ΔΔGtr predictions accessible and scalable for researchers across disciplines.

Our series of benchmarks demonstrates that although OsmoFold performs best with monomeric proteins in vitro, it can still provide valuable insights in more complex biological contexts. Furthermore, the successful application of OsmoFold to identify optimal excipients for FVIII suggests promising applications in biopharmaceutical development, potentially reducing costs and accelerating research. Through this, OsmoFold may help identify formulations that enable the storage of biologics outside the cold chain, ultimately expanding access to life-saving medicines globally.
