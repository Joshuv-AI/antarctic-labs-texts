Exercise habits and swallowing-related symptoms in older adults: a cross-sectional and a single-arm pre–post intervention study
Authors: Misa Nakamura, Kenta Aoki, Tatsunori Murakami, Mitsumasa Hida, Koichi Shimano, Masakazu Imaoka
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundIn a super-aged society, extending healthy life expectancy is a critical challenge. Impaired swallowing function especially impacts upon quality of life of older adults and it is known to increase the risk of conditions such as aspiration pneumonia. We investigated the relationship between exercise habits and swallowing-related symptoms. To clarify the relationship with oral function, we examined the exercise habits of community-dwelling older adults, with particular attention to swallowing-related symptoms, through a cross-sectional study. Further, we sought to clarify the change of swallowing-related symptoms before and after an exercise program in groups with different exercise habits.MethodsIn Study I, a cross-sectional study conducted in September 2024, we analyzed the exercise habits and swallowing-related symptoms of 308 participants. Study II was a single-arm pre–post intervention study conducted between January and March 2025 after registration in November 2024. We compared the swallowing-related symptoms of 67 participants before and after an exercise intervention (60 min, once weekly, for 10 weeks). The primary outcome was the score from the Seirei swallowing questionnaire (low representing fewer/milder swallowing symptoms, high representing more/more severe swallowing symptoms), and secondary outcome was dysphagia if any item on the questionnaire was marked ‘A’ (severe/frequent). We categorized participants into low exercise habit (≤ 1 day/week) and high exercise habit ≥ 2 days/ week) groups based on their habits.ResultsIn Study I, multivariable analysis showed that higher exercise frequency was independently associated with a lower swallowing score. Consistently, a significant linear trend was observed across increasing exercise frequency categories. In Study II, a significant decrease in swallowing scores was observed in the low exercise habit group after the intervention. However, two-way repeated measures analysis of variance revealed a significant main effect of Time, but the Time × Group interaction was not significant. When the presence or absence of dysphagia was examined before and after the intervention, a significant decrease in dysphagia was observed in the low exercise habit group.ConclusionWe observed an association between exercise habits and swallowing-related symptoms in community-dwelling older adults. We also observed a reduction in swallowing-related symptoms in our participants over the course of a 10-week exercise program.Trial registrationUniversity Hospital Medical Information Network Centre (UMIN) (registration number: UMIN000056010).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12877-026-07766-3.

Background

With the advent of a super-aged society, it is imperative to extend healthy life expectancy. Intervention before the onset of care dependency is therefore crucial. This necessitates comprehensive prevention actions with attention to nutrition (diet, oral function), physical activity (exercise, etc.), and social participation (employment, leisure activities, etc.) [1]. For many older people, declining oral function leads to physical frailty, dependency on care, and increased mortality risk [2, 3]. Among oral functions, swallowing is essential for daily living. Dysphagia not only reduces quality of life, it is strongly associated with aspiration pneumonia in older adults, and this can lead to prolonged hospital stays, worsening of other diseases, and increased mortality risk [4]. Preventing dysphagia is therefore a critical challenge in improving the health of older adults. ‘Oral frailty’ refers to a state where there is overlap of multiple ‘minor declines’ in various oral functions, and this increases the risk of oral functional decline [5]. However, this remains a condition that is amenable to improvement. Among community-dwelling older individuals in Japan, for example, both oral frailty/oral function decline and dysphagia are observed at relatively high frequencies. Oral frailty reportedly affects approximately 22–30% of people [6, 7], and dysphagia affects around 20% [8]. Among community-dwelling independent older individuals, the presence of dysphagia has been shown to be associated with disease clusters such as tooth loss, oral dryness, Care Dependency Scale scores, malnutrition, dementia, neurological disorders, and cerebrovascular disease/hemiplegia [9, 10].

Evidence is accumulating on the relationship between physical/motor function (grip strength, standing up) and oral function [11–13]. Previous studies have reported associations between performance on the standing up test and oral diadochokinesis [11]. Furthermore, grip strength, single-leg stance, and the Timed Up & Go test have also been associated with tongue movement and oral diadochokinesis [12]. There are also reported associations between grip strength, walking speed, and occlusal force, tongue-lip movement, masticatory function, and swallowing function [13]. A strong relationship is thus suggested to exist between motor function and oral function. Meanwhile, habitual exercise has been shown to be effective in maintaining physical ability in older adults [14]. However, many studies use walking ability (speed, Timed Up & Go time, walking speed) as a proxy indicator for ‘habit’ [15–16], and there are few reports on the relationship between genuine ‘exercise habit’ and oral function [17–18].

In this study, we focus on the exercise habits of community-dwelling older adults, to clarify the relationship with oral function, particularly swallowing-related symptoms, through a cross-sectional study, and then further to clarify the change of swallowing-related symptoms before and after an exercise program.

Methods study design

The first part of this investigation is a cross-sectional study (Study I) that focuses upon the exercise habits of community-dwelling older adults. The second part, a single-arm pre–post intervention study (Study II), aims to elucidate the exercise habits of community-dwelling older adults, to clarify the relationship with oral function, particularly swallowing-related symptoms, and further to clarify any change of swallowing-related symptoms before and after an exercise program in groups with different exercise habits. This study thus includes both an observational and a pre–post intervention component. The observational component is reported in accordance with the Standardized Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) statement [19]. For the pre–post intervention component, participant flow, eligibility, enrollment, completion, and attrition were documented to ensure transparent reporting (Fig. 1).

Setting

This study was conducted as two investigations in Kaizuka City, Japan. Participants were recruited from the public via leaflets about a community health check program. All participants underwent a health check jointly organized by the Kaizuka City Elderly Welfare Division and Osaka Kawasaki Rehabilitation University. Study I recruited participants in July 2024 after approval by the ethics committee (see ‘Ethical Statement’ below). Participants underwent measurements and completed questionnaires at the community center where the health check was held in September 2024. Study II was approved by the institutional ethics committee and prospectively registered in University Hospital Medical Information Network Centre (UMIN) (registration number: UMIN000056010) on November 2024, prior to the commencement of participant recruitment in December 2024. It involved an exercise program conducted between January and March 2025. Participants attended exercise classes at the community hall, undertaking 60 min of exercise once per week for 10 weeks. The program consisted of five components: a warm-up (5 min), an aerobic exercise session (20 min), rest (5 min), whole-body muscle strength training (25 min), and then a cool-down period (5 min). The program was conducted in a group format (approximately 30 people per group) and based on a standardized manual. Sessions were conducted face-to-face, with instructors providing demonstrations and individual feedback. Instructors were two physical therapists, each of whom had over 5 years of clinical experience. Participants’ attendance rates were recorded. They underwent oral and physical function measurements and they completed questionnaires one week prior to the start of the exercise intervention and then again one week after its completion.

Selection criteria

The inclusion criteria for this study were Japanese nationals aged ≥ 60 years and living independently at home. We excluded participants with pacemakers and those with insufficient or incomplete data. Additionally, for Study II, we excluded individuals from the analysis who attended five or fewer of the 10 exercise classes. Consequently, the study population was intended to be a generally healthy group with motivation to monitor their physical condition through participation in health check-ups.

Participants

In the cross-sectional study (Study I), all participants underwent the health check-up. After exclusions (see Results), we analyzed 308 participants. The subsequent longitudinal study (Study II) comprised participants who had taken part in Study I and attended the exercise program. After exclusions, the analysis cohort comprised 67 participants (see Results).

Ethical statement

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, having received approval from the Osaka Kawasaki Rehabilitation University Ethics Committee (Study I: OKRU-RA0081, Study II: OKRU-RA0101). Written consent was obtained from all participants prior to the commencement of the study.

Outcome measures

All variables were assessed by independent specialists within the research team, including physiotherapists, occupational therapists, speech-language-hearing therapists, and dental hygienists, under the supervision of the principal investigator (M.N.). Sociodemographic variables, body composition variables, and medical history variables were treated as covariates, while exercise habit variables and swallowing scores were considered independent and dependent variables, respectively.

Dependent variables

Swallowing-related symptoms

Participants’ swallowing-related symptoms was assessed using the Seirei swallowing screening questionnaire [20]. It comprises 15 items that reflect history of pneumonia, nutritional status, oral function, pharyngeal function, esophageal function, and epiglottic function. It is one of the widely used swallowing screening tools in Japan, and is characterized by its suitability for community-based geriatric studies and clinical settings [21]. Each question is answered on a three-point scale, with ‘A’ representing severe symptoms (4 points), ‘B’ representing mild symptoms (1 point), and ‘C’ representing no symptoms (0 points). Low scores represent fewer/milder swallowing symptoms, high scores represent more/more severe swallowing symptoms. The presence of dysphagia is suspected if any response is rated ‘A’ [20], although there is no definitive cut-off value for decreased swallowing function. The primary outcome of this study was the total score of the Seirei swallowing screening questionnaire (swallowing score), and the secondary outcome was the occurrence of dysphagia. This tool assesses self-reported swallowing-related symptoms and does not directly measure physiological swallowing performance.

Physical function

Grip strength was measured using a grip dynamometer (T.K.K. 5401, Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Niigata, Japan), following a previously reported method [22]. Gait speed was expressed as the average speed from five trials of a 2.4 m normal walk, starting without acceleration and ending without deceleration [22].

Body composition

Body composition parameters (body mass index, skeletal muscle mass index, and percentage body fat) were measured using a bioelectrical impedance analysis device (InBody 270; InBody, Tokyo, Japan) at 20 and 1000 kHz, following a previously reported method [22].

Socio-demographic status

Information on sex, age, and years of education (continuous variable) were derived from the self-answered questionnaire.

Exercise habits and smoking habits

Participants responded to the question ‘Do you exercise regularly?’ with one of the following options: ‘Daily’ (category: 1), ‘5–6 days per week’ (category: 2), ‘2–4 days per week’ (category: 3), ‘1 day per week’ (category: 4), or ‘none’ (category: 5). Smoking habits were reported as ‘never smoked’, ‘smoked in the past but have now stopped’, or ‘current smoker’.

Depression and medical history

Depressive status was assessed using the GDS-15 (continuous variable) [23], a community-based scale used for depression screening in general older populations. We collected information on past medical history using a proprietary self-administered questionnaire, categorizing the presence or absence of stroke, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, diabetes mellitus, osteoporosis, and osteoarthritis into two categories.

Oral function (Study II only)

Intraoral moisture levels were measured using an oral moisture meter (Moisture Checker Mucus, Life Co., Ltd., Saitama, Japan). This device measures the moisture content within the lingual epithelium using the bioelectrical impedance analysis method. With the participant relaxed, a dental hygienist vertically applied the sensor to the mid-dorsum of the tongue, approximately 1 cm from the tip, applying consistent pressure for approximately 2 s. This was repeated three times, with the median value recorded as the moisture content [24].

For oral diadochokinesis, subjects were instructed to rapidly repeat the sounds /pa/, /ta/, and /ka/ in sequence for 5 s each, performing each sound once. Counting was performed using a digital measurement device (KENKOU-KUN®; Takei Scientific Instruments Co., Ltd., Niigata, Japan), which was developed for automatic measurement [25].

Statistical analysis

The primary outcome was the swallowing score, and secondary outcome was dysphagia. For numerical variables, normality of distribution and homogeneity of variance were tested prior to intergroup comparisons. Student’s t-test was used when both groups satisfied the assumptions of normal distribution and homogeneity of variance, and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was used when data were non-normally distributed. The chi-square test was used for between-group comparisons of categorical data. For changes in paired continuous data, a paired t-test was used if normality was confirmed, and the Wilcoxon signed-rank test was used if normality was not confirmed. To examine changes in swallowing scores over time and differences between groups, a two-way repeated measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed, with time (pre- and post-intervention) as the within-subject factor and group (low vs. high exercise habit) as the between-subject factor. To account for baseline differences, an analysis of covariance (ANCOVA) was conducted with the post-intervention swallowing score as the dependent variable, group (low vs. high exercise habit) as the fixed factor, and the baseline (pre-intervention) swallowing score as a covariate. McNemar’s test was used to assess whether there was a change in category for the same subject before and after intervention.

In Study I, exercise frequency was categorized into five groups: daily (category: 1), 5–6 days/week (category: 2), 2–4 days/week (category: 3), 1 day/week (category: 4) and none (category: 5). For dichotomized analyses, participants were classified into a lower exercise habit group (≤ 1 day/week) and a higher exercise habit group (≥ 2 days/week). Multivariable linear regression analyses were conducted to examine the association between exercise frequency and swallowing score. Exercise frequency was analyzed both as a dichotomous variable (higher vs. lower habit) and as an ordinal variable (1–5 categories) to assess a potential dose–response relationship. For the ordinal analysis, exercise frequency was coded from 1 (daily) to 5 (none), such that higher values indicate lower exercise frequency. This coding was used when the variable was treated as continuous in the regression models. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and linear regression analysis (Supplementary Material 1). Age, sex, and depressive symptoms (GDS-15) were included as potential confounders. Depressive symptoms were included as a covariate because depression may be associated with both exercise habits and swallowing function, and it may influence self-reported swallowing assessments. Gait speed was not included in a sensitivity analysis because it may lie on the causal pathway between exercise frequency and swallowing function. To assess a linear trend across exercise frequency categories, ordinal values (1–5) were assigned to each category and entered as a continuous variable in the multivariable linear regression model adjusted for age, sex, and GDS-15. Two-tailed p values and 95% confidence intervals (CI) are presented. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP 19 (SAS Institute, Cary, NC). The two-way ANOVA and ANCOVA methods were performed with SPSS statistics software (version 26; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Dose–response relationship between exercise frequency and swallowing score

When exercise frequency was treated as an ordinal variable (1–5), we could observe a significant dose–response relationship. Each one-category increase in exercise frequency was associated with increase in swallowing score (B per category = 0.51, 95% CI = 0.19 to 0.84, p for trend = 0.002), (B per category = 0.61, 95% CI = 0.28 to 0.94, p for trend = < 0.001), and (B per category = 0.41, 95% CI 0.07 to 0.73, p for trend = 0.018), unadjusted (Crude), adjusted by age and sex (Model 1), and age, sex, GDS-15 score (Model 2) (Table 2). A positive regression coefficient therefore indicates that lower exercise frequency is associated with poorer swallowing scores.

Study II

Based on predefined exclusion criteria, we excluded data for the following subjects: interruption due to illness (n = 2), absence from measurement sessions (n = 13), pacemaker wearers (n = 1), attendance at exercise classes ≤ 5 times (n = 2), and individuals with insufficient or incomplete information (n = 2). Following exclusions, data from 67 individuals were ultimately analyzed and categorized at pre-intervention into ‘high exercise habits’ (≥ 2 days/week, n = 35) and ‘low exercise habits’ (≤ 1 day/week, n = 32). The flowchart is shown in Fig. 1. The average attendance rate was 91.3%, and the median number of attendances was nine times out of 10. This high adherence supports the feasibility of the intervention program.

We compared baseline measurements between the high exercise habit and low exercise habit groups. For oral diadochokinesis/pa, the low exercise habit group had lower values than the high exercise group (5.86 vs. 6.18, respectively; p = 0.040). No significant differences were observed for other measurements (Supplementary Material 2).

Comparison of changes in swallowing-related scores, and other oral functions by exercise habit group

We compared the change in amounts before and after the intervention for each measurement between the two exercise habit groups. Wilcoxon signed-rank-tests indicated a significant reduction in swallowing scores in the low exercise habit group (pre: 4.25 [SD = 4.91] vs. post: 2.66 [SD = 3.59], p = 0.011), whereas no significant pre–post difference was observed in the high exercise habit group (pre: 3.91 [SD = 4.77] vs. post: 3.71 [SD = 5.08], p = 0.987) (Table 3). Between-group differences in the change from baseline to post-intervention were assessed using the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, revealing a statistically significant difference (p = 0.028). Two-way (Time × Group) ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Time (F = 4.87, p = 0.031), but the Time × Group interaction was not significant (F = 2.94, p = 0.091). ANCOVA adjusting for baseline swallowing scores showed no significant group effect was observed (F = 3.14, p = 0.081) (Table 3). Changes in tongue moisture from pre- to post-intervention were observed in the low exercise habit group (p = 0.007); however, the change from pre- to post-intervention did not significantly differ between the two groups. Two-way ANOVA revealed a significant main effect of Time (F = 6.87, p = 0.011), but the Time × Group interaction was not significant (F = 2.21, p = 0.142). ANCOVA, adjusting for baseline tongue moisture values, showed no significant group effect (F = 0.18, p = 0.670) (Table 3). No differences were observed between the two groups in other oral function measurements (Table 3). Change amounts in other measurements are shown in Supplementary Material 3.

Pre-and post-intervention comparison of the number of participants with dysphagia

Focusing on dysphagia, we analyzed the change in the presence or absence of dysphagia before and after the intervention. In the high exercise habit group, four participants deteriorated from absence of dysphagia at pre-intervention to dysphagia at post-intervention, while one participant improved from having dysphagia to absence of dysphagia. In the low exercise habit group, no participants deteriorated from absence at pre-intervention to dysphagia at post-intervention, while four improved from dysphagia to absence. In the low exercise habit group, the McNemar’s test for paired samples showed significantly decreased numbers with dysphagia at post-intervention (p = 0.046) (Table 4).

Discussion

In Study I, an analysis of questionnaire responses from community-dwelling older adults revealed a relationship between swallowing scores and exercise habits. Exercise habits in relation to oral function have not been widely reported, but one study divided exercise habits into daily physical activity level and leisure-time physical activity and investigated the relationship between these levels of exercise and the risk of dysphagia. An inverse correlation was shown between leisure-time exercise level and the risk of dysphagia, which indicates that higher levels of leisure-time physical activity were associated with a lower risk of dysphagia [18]. Another study reported that intense recreational activities were associated with a reduced risk of needing oral care in older adults [17]. These reports strongly support the results of this study.

This exercise survey inquired about regular weekly exercise or sports activities, showing that 162 participants (52.6%) exercised two or more days per week, while 146 participants (47.4%) barely exercised at all. The proportion of Japanese people that exercise two or more days per week is reportedly 48.9% in people their 60s and 62.8% in people in their 70s [26], and our findings are thought to be broadly in line with average trends.

The attenuation of the association after adjustment for GDS-15 suggests that depression may partially explain the relationship between exercise habits and swallowing function. However, the association remained statistically significant, which indicates that exercise habits may be independently associated with swallowing-related scores beyond the effect of depressive symptoms.

In Study II, a significant change over time was observed but no significant group × time interaction was found, indicating that the magnitude of change did not significantly differ between groups. Although a significant reduction was observed within the low exercise habit group, this finding should be interpreted with caution. The absence of a significant group effect in the ANCOVA, after adjusting for baseline values, suggests that the observed change in the low exercise habit group may be partially explained by poorer baseline values and regression to the mean rather than a true differential effect of the intervention. This study design did not include a non-intervention group, so it was not possible to elucidate causal inferences regarding the intervention effect or the aforementioned possibilities.

Factors associated with swallowing function in older people include tooth count, dry mouth, poor oral hygiene, oral diadochokinesis, and maximum tongue pressure [27–29]. In this study, we observed a significant increase in oral moisture in the group with low exercise habits, which may have contributed in part to the observed improvement in dry mouth, a factor related to swallowing function. Oral dryness in older people leads to reduced chewing efficiency, diminished digestive initiation due to decreased secretion of enzymes such as amylase in saliva, reduced speech clarity, and conditions like candidiasis, which is a risk factor for aspiration pneumonia [30].

Numerous reports have documented increased salivary volume and improved oral dryness in older people following oral exercise programs, oral exercise combined with whole-body exercise, or combined programs [31–34]. However, to the best of our knowledge, there are no reports concerning whole-body exercise programs alone. This study indicates that whole-body exercise may enhance oral moisture, albeit limited to the group with low exercise habits. However, no significant increase in oral moisture was observed in the group with high exercise habits. This supports findings in younger individuals that the effect on salivary volume varies depending on exercise intensity and content [35]. Furthermore, from the perspective of oral mucosal ‘moisture’, not only salivary volume, but salivary components (mucin, proteins, viscosity) are also influential. Exercise reportedly increases mucin MUC5B in younger individuals [35]. Although we did not measure salivary components, the low exercise habit group may have had increased moisturization due to the occurrence of such changes. However, these mechanisms remain speculative, as we did not directly measure salivary composition this time.

Study limitations

There are several notable limitations in association with this study. Regarding study design, this study combined a cross-sectional and a single-arm pre–post intervention study, which limited the ability to establish causality. Especially, Study II employed a single-arm pre–post design without a non-intervention control group, and this restricts the ability to establish causal relationships and to distinguish intervention effects from potential confounding factors. Secondly, participants were recruited from voluntary health check-ups and they may represent an especially healthy and health-conscious population, which may limit generalizability. Thirdly, the intervention period in this study was short: the exercise intervention was conducted over just 10 weeks, which prevents confirmation of long-term effects. Future studies should increase the number of participants and conduct long-term follow-up surveys to examine changes in results due to sustained exercise habits. Fourth, the swallowing index was a self-reported questionnaire, so the change of swallowing-related scores may reflect perception, mood, or response bias after participating in the social program. Future analysis will require the incorporation of objective swallowing function assessments.

Conclusion

We observed an association between exercise habits and swallowing-related symptoms in community-dwelling older adults. Participation in a 10-week exercise program was associated with modest reduction in swallowing-related symptoms in a group of participants who otherwise did little exercise. However, we found no significant differences between participants with higher versus lower baseline exercise habits after adjusting for baseline swallowing scores. These findings suggest that establishing regular exercise may be beneficial for maintaining swallowing-related symptoms, but differential effects based on prior exercise habits were not supported.
