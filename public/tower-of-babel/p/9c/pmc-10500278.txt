A selective ER-phagy exerts neuroprotective effects via modulation of α-synuclein clearance in parkinsonian models
Authors: Dong Yeol Kim, Jin Young Shin, Ji Eun Lee, Ha Na Kim, Seok Jong Chung, Han Soo Yoo, Sang Jin Kim, Hwa Jin Cho, Eun-Jae Lee, Soo Jeong Nam, Seong Heon Kim, Jaewon Jang, Seung Eun Lee, Phil Hyu Lee
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
SignificanceThe endoplasmic reticulum (ER) is selectively degraded by ER-phagy to maintain cell homeostasis. α-Synuclein accumulates in the ER, causing ER stress that contribute to neurodegeneration in Parkinson’s disease, but the role of ER-phagy in α-synuclein modulation is largely unknown. Here, we investigated the mechanisms by which ER-phagy selectively recognizes α-synuclein for degradation in the ER. We found that ER-phagy played an important role in the degradation of α-synuclein and recovery of ER function through interaction with FAM134B, where calnexin is required for the selective FAM134B-mediated α-synuclein clearance via ER-phagy. These data suggest that ER-phagy represents a specific ER clearance mechanism for the degradation of α-synuclein.

PD, a multifactorial neurodegenerative disorder that affects the motor, cognitive, and autonomic system, is characterized pathologically by the progressive loss of dopaminergic neurons in the SN and the presence of Lewy bodies and proteinaceous cytoplasmic inclusions of aggregated α‐synuclein (1, 2). α‐synuclein consists of 140 amino acids and is found as an unfolded cytoplasmic protein in neuronal synaptic terminals and cytoplasm (3). Although the exact pathogenesis of PD is still unknown, an imbalance between misfolded α-synuclein accumulation and the activity of cellular clearing systems plays a major role in the neurodegeneration of extensive brain areas. Autophagy is an intracellular degradation pathway, which allows the degradation of different cytoplasmic components by the lysosome (4). Ample evidence has demonstrated that damaged organelles and aggregated proteins are selectively degraded through autophagy (5, 6).

Selective autophagy targets are dysfunctional organelles, such as mitochondria, peroxisomes, lysosomes, and the endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and ubiquitinated protein aggregates or pathogens (7–16). Various types of selective autophagy are important for maintaining cell homeostasis, and their disruption is associated with different human diseases (17–19). To date, studies on the autophagic modulation of unfolded α-synuclein have mainly focused on the role of macroautophagy or mitophagy in PD. Autophagic degradation of ER fragments, which is termed ER-phagy, is a type of selective autophagy (20–22). To date, several mammalian membrane-anchored or transmembrane receptors for the ER-phagy have been identified (20, 23–26). Among them, FAM134B, RTN3L, and ATL3 are important for the degradation of ER sheets and tubules, respectively (20, 25, 27). CCPG1 expression is induced during ER stress and activates the degradation of ER membranes and proteins (26), whereas SEC62 mediates the removal of excess ER membranes and proteins during the ER stress recovery phase (24).

In cellular and animal models of PD, several studies have shown that toxicity due to the overexpression of human wild-type or A53T mutant α-synuclein is correlated with ER stress and activation of the unfolded protein response (UPR), the ER quality control systems (28–33). In addition, overexpressed α-synuclein is accumulated in the ER, causing chronic ER stress that contributes to neurodegeneration in α-synucleinopathies (30, 34). Therefore, the removal of α-synuclein accumulating in the ER along with modulation of chronic ER stress is an important strategy for PD treatment. In this study, we aimed to uncover the mechanisms by which autophagy selectively recognizes and modulates α-synuclein for degradation in the ER using α-synuclein-overexpression cellular and animal models. We identified that ER-phagy would represent a specific ER clearance mechanism for the degradation of α-synuclein accumulated in the ER. Moreover, we found that the enhancement of ER-phagy could exert neuroprotective effects on dopaminergic neurons and recover motor performance in parkinsonian models.

Results

α-Synuclein Overexpression in the ER Leads to ER Dysfunction and Cell Death.

We obtained human brain tissues from three normal individuals and five patients with pathologically confirmed PD by immunostaining and western blotting (SI Appendix, Fig. S1 A–D). Using an ER marker, we confirmed that α-synuclein was accumulated in the ER of the PD patients and the α-synuclein transgenic (TG) mice by immunostaining and ER fractionation (Fig. 1 A and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 A and B). Additionally, we observed that phosphorylated α-synuclein was present in the ER of the PD patients and the α-synuclein TG mice (Fig. 1 B and C and SI Appendix, Fig. S2 B and C).

The levels of ER stress markers of ATF4, p-eIF2α, and CHOP, were significantly elevated in the PD patients compared to healthy controls (Fig. 1D) and the ER stress markers, additionally including P-IRE1 and GRP94 were increased in the α-synuclein TG mice than control mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S2E). These results showed that α-synuclein accumulates in the ER, leading to excessive ER stress. Therefore, we created a construct, cloning α-synuclein into a vector containing the ER retention signal sequence (ER-α-syn) to examine whether α-synuclein overexpresses in the ER would cause ER dysfunction. We observed that ER-α-syn was expressed in approximately 60 to 70% of the cells, where α-synuclein was exclusively accumulated in the ER (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A) and the expression of α-synuclein was increased in the ER-α-syn cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S3B). The MTS assay showed that cell viability was significantly decreased in the ER-α-syn group compared to the control group (Fig. 1E).

The levels of ER stress markers were significantly elevated in the ER-α-syn group compared to the control group (Fig. 1F and SI Appendix, Fig. S3D). Morphological analysis revealed that α-synuclein overexpression in the ER induced the increase of ER expansion (Fig. 1G and SI Appendix, Fig. S3E) and peripheral ER content (Fig. 1H). These data show that α-synuclein accumulation in the ER leads to ER dysfunction and cell death. Next, we constructed an ER retention model using neural epidermal growth factor-like like 2 (ER-NELL2) to determine whether α-synuclein-induced cell death and ER dysfunction was caused by ER overload or α-synuclein-specific. We confirmed that NELL2 was exclusively accumulated in the ER (SI Appendix, Fig. S4A) and NELL2 was markedly expressed in the ER-NELL2 cells (SI Appendix, Fig. S4B). No changes in cell death, ER stress markers, and ER volume were observed in the NELL2 overexpressing group (SI Appendix, Fig. S4 C–E).

ER-Phagy Attenuates α-Synuclein-Induced ER Dysfunction.

To examine the involvement of ER-phagy in the PD conditions, the expression levels of ER-phagy receptors, such as FAM134B, CCPG1, and SEC62, were measured in the SN of PD patients and the α-synuclein TG mice. The levels of FAM134B and CCPG1 were decreased in the PD patients and the α-synuclein TG mice compared to the control individuals and mice. However, the expression of SEC62 did not differ between the groups (Fig. 2A and SI Appendix, Fig. S2D). These results suggested that ER-phagy was decreased in the SN region of the PD patient brain and the α-synuclein TG mice, and particularly prominent reduction in FAM134B levels could play an important role in the α-synuclein pathology. Hence, the FAM134B ER-phagy receptor was transfected into the ER-α-syn cells to evaluate whether ER-phagy would recover α-synuclein-induced-ER dysfunction and cell viability. Evaluating various incubation times, the recovery of ER dysfunction was most prominent at 48 h after ER-α-syn and FAM134B transfection; therefore, a 48-h period was selected as the standard incubation time. The MTS assay showed that FAM134B transfection in the ER-α-syn cells (ER-α-syn /FAM134B) led to increased cell viability compared to the ER-α-syn group (Fig. 2B and SI Appendix, Fig. S5A). In addition, the expression and levels of intracellular α-synuclein were significantly decreased in the ER-α-syn/FAM134B group compared to the ER-α-syn group (Fig. 2 C and D and SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). The levels of the ER stress markers were significantly increased in the ER-α-syn group, while they were decreased in the ER-α-syn/FAM134B group (Fig. 2E and SI Appendix, Fig. S5C). Finally, autophagy markers, such as LAMP1 and LC3 II, were significantly increased in the ER-α-syn/FAM134B group compared to the ER-α-syn group (Fig. 2F and SI Appendix, Fig. S5D). Immunostaining and ultrastructural analysis showed a striking ER expansion in the cell periphery in the ER-α-syn group, whereas this trait was markedly restored in the ER-α-syn/FAM134B group (Fig. 2 G and H). The immunofluorescence staining showed that FAM134B overexpression led to an increased colocalization of the LC3 puncta with α-synuclein and FAM134B than the ER-α-syn group (Fig. 2I). Similarly, FAM134B overexpression increased colocalization of LAMP1 spot with the α-synuclein and FAM134B (Fig. 2J). When treating the cells with autophagy inhibitors, such as bafilomycin A1 and chloroquine, the levels of intracellular α-synuclein were increased in the ER-α-syn/FAM134B group (Fig. 2K). Moreover, we examined whether ER-phagy would modulate α-synuclein-induced-ER dysfunction and cell viability in wild type α-synuclein (WT-α-syn) overexpressing cells. The FAM134B transfection in the WT-α-syn cells led to increased cell viability, decreased intracellular α-synuclein, decreased ER stress markers, and increased autophagy markers compared to the WT-α-syn group (SI Appendix, Fig. S6 A–D). These results indicated that the reduction of α-synuclein in the ER through FAM134B occurs through autophagy.

Inhibition of ER-Phagy Accentuates α-Synuclein-Induced ER Dysfunction.

FAM134B siRNA was used to determine whether endogenous FAM134B suppression specifically inhibited ER-phagy-mediated α-synuclein modulation and protective effects. After the assessment of FAM134B siRNA efficiency (SI Appendix, Fig. S7A), inhibition of FAM134B expression in ER-α-syn cells (ER-α-syn/siFAM134B) resulted in a more prominent reduction of cell viability than that of the ER-α-syn group (Fig. 3A). Specifically, FAM134B inhibition in the ER-α-syn cells led to a decreased expression of endogenous FAM134B, which was accompanied by increased levels of intracellular α-synuclein (Fig. 3 B and C). In addition, the expression of ER stress and apoptosis markers tended to increase in the ER-α-syn/siFAM134B group compared to the ER-α-syn group (Fig. 3 D and F). However, there was no significant change in the levels of autophagy markers between the ER-α-syn/siFAM134B group and the ER-α-syn group (Fig. 3E). FAM134B inhibition decreased LC3 puncta and LAMP1 spot colocalized with the α-synuclein compared to the ER-α-syn group (Fig. 3 G and H). These results suggest that FAM134B, an ER-phagy receptor, plays an important role in the degradation of α-synuclein and recovery of ER function.

Calnexin Is Required for α-Synuclein Degradation through FAM134B-Mediated ER-Phagy.

Since FAM134B does not have an ER luminal domain, the α-synuclein destined to degradation must be selectively recognized by the ER quality control machinery. Therefore, we investigated the involvement of ER chaperones in the degradation of α-synuclein through ER-phagy. Based on a published list of α-synuclein and ER chaperone interactors (28, 30, 35), we silenced various ER chaperone genes including calnexin, calreticulin, BiP, ERDJ3, and GRP94, through siRNA (SI Appendix, Fig. S7B). The silencing of calnexin most effectively inhibited the degradation of α-synuclein in the ER-α-syn/FAM134B cells, whereas that of other ER chaperones, such as calreticulin, BiP, ERDJ3, and GRP94, did not sufficiently inhibit α-synuclein degradation (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). These results suggested that calnexin is required to select the α-synuclein destined to ER-phagy. Therefore, we identified interaction of α-synuclein, calnexin, and FAM134B in the ER by immunoprecipitation analysis (Fig. 4A). Next, calnexin siRNA was used to examine whether calnexin suppression could specifically counteract the effect of ER-phagy-mediated α-synuclein modulation. The MTS assay showed that calnexin inhibition significantly decreased cell viability in ER-α-syn/FAM134B cells, with a resulting viability comparable to that observed in ER-α-syn cells (Fig. 4B). In addition, calnexin inhibition in the ER-α-syn/FAM134B cells (siCal) resulted in increased levels of intracellular α-synuclein compared to that in the ER-α-syn/FAM134B group, with no changes in the expression of FAM134 (Fig. 4 C and D). The expression of ER stress markers was significantly increased in the siCal group compared to the ER-α-syn/FAM134B group (Fig. 4F). However, the levels of autophagy markers in the siCal group were still high, similar those of the ER-α-syn/FAM134B group (Fig. 4E). These results indicated that the interaction of FAM134B with α-synuclein is mediated by calnexin and that the selective, FAM134B-mediated degradation of α-synuclein is dependent on the binding of α-synuclein to calnexin.

ER-Phagy Enhancement Has a Protective Effect on Dopaminergic Neurons in the ER-α-Syn Mouse Model.

To study the role of ER-phagy on dopaminergic neurons in animal models, we constructed adeno-associated virus (AAV) vectors to overexpress α-synuclein in the ER (ER-α-syn virus) and FAM134B (FAM134B virus). We confirmed that about 70% of percent TH-positive neurons were expressing α-synuclein in the ER-α-syn virus–injected mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S9A) and that α-synuclein and FAM134B were expressed in the PDI-positive ER of the TH-positive neurons in mice injected with the two viruses (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 B and C). Similar to the cell model, to determine whether α-synuclein-induced cell death and ER dysfunction were due to ER overload or α-synuclein-specific, we constructed AAV vectors to overexpress NELL2 in the ER (ER-NELL2). NELL2 was expressed in the ER of the TH-positive neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 A–C) in ER-NELL2 virus–injected mice. No changes in TH-positive cell death, motor behavior, and ER stress markers were observed in ER-NELL2 virus–injected mice (SI Appendix, Fig. S10 D–H). Mice injected with ER-α-syn viruses in the SN had significantly decreased number of TH-positive neurons in SN region (Fig. 5A) and DARPP 32-positive neurons in the striatum (ST) (Fig. 5B) compared to the control mice. Western blotting also confirmed that the expression of TH and DARPP 32 was significantly decreased in the SN and ST, respectively (Fig. 5C). The analysis of the behavioral performance revealed that the animals receiving ER-α-syn virus injection in the SN exhibited a reduced latency to fall in the rotarod test (Fig. 5D) and increased latency to descend in the pole test (Fig. 5E) compared to the control animals. However, FAM134B virus injection in ER-α-syn virus–injected mice significantly increased the number of TH- and DARPP 32-positive neurons and their protein expression in the SN and ST, respectively (Fig. 5 A–C). Moreover, impaired motor coordination and balance were restored in the rotarod and the pole tests. (Fig. 5 D and E). In addition, ER-α-syn virus–injected mice presented a significant increase in intracellular α-synuclein levels (Fig. 5 F and G), apoptosis markers (Fig. 5H), and ER stress markers (Fig. 5I) compared to the control mice. In contrast, FAM134B virus injection in ER-α-syn virus–injected mice significantly decreased the intracellular α-synuclein levels and apoptosis and ER stress markers (Fig. 5 F–I). Also, FAM134B overexpression in ER-α-syn virus–injected mice increased the expression of autophagy markers in the SN (Fig. 5J). Ultrastructural analyses showed that the dopaminergic neurons in the ER-α-syn virus–injected mice exhibited a striking ER expansions, whereas FAM134B overexpression restored ER morphology in the ER-α-syn virus–injected mice (Fig. 5K).

ER-Phagy Inhibition Leads to Loss of Neuroprotection on Dopaminergic Neurons in the ER-α-Syn Mouse Model.

To study the consequences of endogenous FAM134B loss in in vivo, we constructed a FAM134B knockdown shRNA-mCherry (shFAM134B) virus. After the shFAM134B viruses were injected directly into the SN of ER-α-syn virus–injected mice, we confirmed that α-synuclein and FAM134B were expressed in the TH-positive neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S9 D and E). shFAM134B virus injection in the ER-α-syn virus–injected mice induced a significant decrease in the number of TH-positive neurons in the SN (Fig. 6A) and DARPP 32-positive neurons in the ST (Fig. 6B) compared to the mice injected only with the ER-α-syn viruses. The expression of TH and DARPP 32 was also decreased in the SN and ST of shFAM134B virus–injected mice, respectively (Fig. 6C). However, the number and expression of TH- and DARPP 32-positive neurons in the only shFAM134B group were comparable compared to the control group. Behavioral analysis revealed that shFAM134B virus injection in the ER-α-syn virus–injected mice tended to decrease the latency to fall in the rotarod test and increase the latency to descend in the pole test compared to the only ER-α-syn virus–injected mice (Fig. 6 D and E). In addition, shFAM134B virus injection in the ER-α-syn virus–injected mice increased the intracellular α-synuclein levels, apoptosis, and ER stress markers compared to the only ER-α-syn virus–injected mice (Fig. 6 F–I). However, the expression of autophagy markers did not differ between the groups (Fig. 6J). Ultrastructural analyses showed that shFAM134B virus injection in the ER-α-syn virus–injected mice was accompanied by more severe ER expansions in the dopaminergic neurons compared to the ER-α-syn virus–injected mice (Fig. 6K). These in vivo results indicated that FAM134B is important for α-synuclein degradation and recovery of ER dysfunction in α-synuclein overexpressing mice.

ER-Phagy Enhancement Has a Protective Effect on Dopaminergic Neurons in the WT α-Syn Mouse Model.

To explore the role of ER-phagy on universal parkinsonian animal model, we constructed AAV vectors to overexpress WT-α-syn and FAM134B. After these viruses were injected directly into the SN of mice, we confirmed that α-synuclein and FAM134B were expressed in the PDI-positive ER of the TH-positive dopaminergic neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 A–C). Mice injected with WT-α-syn viruses in the SN had significantly decreased number of TH-positive neurons (SI Appendix, Fig. S11D) and TH expression (SI Appendix, Fig. S11G) in the SN region compared to the control mice. The behavioral analysis revealed that the mice receiving WT-α-syn virus injection exhibited a reduced latency to fall on the rotarod test and increased latency to descend on the pole test compared to the control mice (SI Appendix, Fig.S11 E and F). However, FAM134B virus injection in WT-α-syn virus–injected mice significantly increased the number of TH-positive neurons and their protein expression in the SN (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 D and G), which was accompanied by the restoration of impaired motor coordination and balance (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 E and F). In addition, WT-α-syn virus–injected mice exhibited a significant increase in the expression of α-synuclein (SI Appendix, Fig. S11H) and ER stress markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S11I) compared to the control mice, whereas FAM134B virus injection in WT-α-syn virus–injected mice significantly decreased the intracellular α-synuclein levels and ER stress markers (SI Appendix, Fig. S11 H and I).

Discussion

This study investigated the mechanisms by which autophagy selectively recognizes α-synuclein for degradation in the ER. The major findings in this study are as follows: 1) FAM134B-mediated ER-phagy plays an important role in the degradation of α-synuclein and recovery of ER function; 2) calnexin is required for α-synuclein degradation through FAM134B-mediated ER-phagy; and 3) ER-α-syn virus injection in the SN allows to obtain a PD animal model, and the enhancement of ER-phagy has a protective effect on the dopaminergic neurons in the ER-α-syn animal model. These data suggest that ER-phagy represents a specific ER clearance mechanism for the degradation of α-synuclein accumulated in the ER.

The ER has many important intracellular functions, such as storage of calcium, protein synthesis, and protein folding capacity. ER stress is induced by the accumulation of misfolded proteins, changes in the ER lumen ion conditions, and various cell stress factors (36). ER stress triggers the activation of the UPR, which is a prosurvival response that reduces unfolded protein accumulation and restores ER function. However, when protein aggregation is constant and excessive, stress cannot be resolved and the ensuing signaling switches from prosurvival to proapoptotic. In this state, ER stress–induced apoptosis proceeds through an increase in C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) expression, activated by the UPRs (37). When misfolded proteins accumulate in the ER and ER stress occurs, proteins in the ER are degraded through two different pathways of ER-associated degradation (ERAD) and autophagy (38). ERAD delivers the proteins for degradation from the ER to the cytoplasmic proteasome. In autophagy, the cargo is delivered for degradation to the lysosome. ER stress–induced macroautophagy can be nonselective or selective. Many studies had been conducted on ER-phagy, and several receptors involved in ER-phagy have been identified (20, 23–26). However, research on the association between ER-phagy and the accumulation of misfolded proteins in neurodegenerative proteinopathies is still lacking.

In this study, as expected, α-synuclein was accumulated in the ER of the PD patients and α-synuclein TG mice. The ER-α-syn viral model also led to ER stress and morphological alterations, with increased cell death. Here, we have identified the ER-phagy receptor FAM134B as a key mediator of α-synuclein degradation in the ER. A previous study reported that FAM134B, as an ER-phagy receptor, could mediate the turnover of the ER via autophagy to maintain ER homeostasis (20). In this study, we found that FAM134B overexpression in ER-α-syn cells increased the expression of autophagy markers, decreased the levels of intracellular α-synuclein, and restored ER stress and ER morphology. As a result, FAM134B delivery to ER-α-syn cells significantly increased cell viability. Moreover, when suppressing endogenous FAM134B in the ER-α-syn cells, ER stress increased, ER morphology was more severely altered, and intracellular α-synuclein levels were higher, which was followed by a more severe reduction of cell viability. These results showed that FAM134B plays an important role in the degradation of α-synuclein and recovery of ER function. In patients with PD, it is known that cytosolic α-synuclein accumulates in the ER and accumulated α-synuclein causes ER stress and eventually leads to cell death (39). Therefore, our data suggested that FAM134B-mediated ER-phagy could function as an ER quality control pathway for α-synuclein clearance in the ER in α-synucleinopathies. However, recent studies have reported ER protein degradation through other ER-phagy receptors, such as SEC62, CCPG1, TEX264, RTN3L, and ATL3 (23–26). Since the present study does not cover the relationship between the other ER-phagy receptors and α-synuclein, further research on the role of other ER-phagy receptors in α-synuclein clearance and its related ER stress is needed.

Moreover, we have demonstrated that the ER chaperone calnexin is a main player in α-synuclein degradation via ER-phagy. It has been reported that calnexin binds to the α-synuclein and DJ-1 complex, but direct interaction between α-synuclein and calnexin is unknown (35). In this study, we identified an interaction between α-synuclein and calnexin in the ER by immunoprecipitation analysis. Moreover, using calnexin siRNA, we confirmed that calnexin suppression specifically counteracted the effect of ER-phagy-mediated α-synuclein modulation. Calnexin is an ER chaperone that assists the folding of proteins in the ER (40) and interacts with FAM134B (41). Thus, it is plausible that the interaction of α-synuclein, calnexin, and FAM134B might represent a substrate for ER-phagy–mediated degradation. Regarding other ER chaperons, it is currently unknown whether additional ER chaperones, such as calreticulin, BiP, GRP94, and ERDJ3, are associated with α-synuclein degradation by ER-phagy. The current silencing results of other ER chaperones suggest that calreticulin and BiP may also be partially involved in the ER-phagy-mediated degradation of α-synuclein. However, calnexin suppression in the ER-α-syn/FAM134B system did not reduce the intracellular α-synuclein levels despite autophagy enhancement, indicating that calnexin plays a main role in the selective FAM134B-mediated degradation of α-synuclein via ER-phagy.

Interestingly, we found that ER-α-syn virus injection in the SN resulted in marked loss of dopaminergic neurons in the midbrain, which was accompanied by parkinsonian motor deficits on behavioral analyses. This result suggested that the α-synuclein overexpression in the ER of the SN neurons would be a PD animal model, mimicking the motor aspects of the PD. Similar to results of in vitro experiments, ultrastructural analyses showed that FAM134B delivery to ER-α-syn virus–injected mice increased the expression of autophagy markers and decreased α-synuclein levels in the SN while restoring ER morphology. Consequently, FAM134B overexpression in the ER-α-syn virus–injected mice significantly attenuated the loss of dopaminergic neurons in the SN and recovered the impaired motor performance of the mice. In addition, when suppressing endogenous FAM134B, ER morphology was severely altered, and ER stress and intracellular α-synuclein levels were increased, followed by more severe dopaminergic neuronal loss and motor deficits. More importantly, we found that FAM134B overexpression significantly decreased dopaminergic neural loss as well as the intracellular α-synuclein levels and ER stress markers in the WT-α-syn virus–injected mouse model. These data further supported the notion that the modulation of ER-phagy might be important for α-synuclein degradation and recovery of ER dysfunction in ER-α-syn virus–injected mice and conventionally used PD model, suggesting the potential of ER-phagy as a therapeutic target for α-synuclein modulation in the PD.

In conclusion, the present study demonstrated that FAM134B-mediated ER-phagy plays an important role in α-synuclein degradation and that ER-phagy enhancement has a protective effect on the dopaminergic neurons of the parkinsonian animal models. Our data suggest that ER-phagy enhancement, focusing on α-synuclein clearance, might be a promising therapeutic strategy for PD.

Materials and Methods

Cell Culture.

A549 cells were obtained from the Korean Cell Line Bank (Seoul, South Korea). They were maintained in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM; HyClone) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; HyClone) and an antibiotic mixture of penicillin and streptomycin (100 U/mL; HyClone). For the in vitro experiments, the A549 cells were plated at a density of 2.0 × 105 cells/well and 1.0 × 104 cells/well (Corning). Following transfection of A549 cells for 24 h, bafilomycin A1 (50 nM, Sigma-Aldrich) and chloroquine (100 nM, Sigma-Aldrich) were treated for 24 h to inhibit autophagy.

Human Brain Tissues.

The research resources (human brain tissue) were provided by the Korea Brain Bank Network (KBBN) operated through the National Brain Bank Project funded by the Ministry of Science and ICT of South Korea. Human brain tissues used for research purposes were approved by the institutional review board of the Institutional Bioethics Committees, Yonsei University Hospital (4–2020–0341).

Normal brains included one female and two males with no significant Lewy body pathology (median age of 58.3 y), and PD human brains included two females and three males (median age of 74.4 y).

Plasmid Transfection.

For transfection, an ER-α-syn, an FAM134B, an ER-NELL2, and empty vector were used. The ER-α-syn, ER-NELL2, and empty vector contained an ER targeting sequence and an ER retention sequence. The A549 cells were maintained in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and an antibiotic mixture. Then, they were then plated 24 h before transfection and grown to 70 to 80% confluence. The cells were transfected using jetPRIME (Polyplus-transfection) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. To knock down FAM134B, calnexin, calreticulin, BiP, ERDJ3, and GRP94 in A549 cells, an FAM134B, calnexin, calreticulin, BiP, ERDJ3, and GRP94 small-interfering RNA (siRNA) constructs (Bioneer) were purchased and tested for knockdown efficiency. The transfection of siRNA into the A549 cells was performed with Lipofectamine RNAi max (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s protocol.

Cell Viability.

Cell viability was assessed using a 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-5-(3-carboxy methoxyphenyl)-2-(4-sulfophenyl)-2H-tetazolium inner salt (MTS, Promega Corp.) assay, which is based on a tetrazolium compound, MTS, and an electron acceptor agent, phenazine methosulfate (PMS, Promega Corp.). Briefly, the cells were seeded into a 24-well plate at a density of 5 × 104 cells/well in 200 μL of medium, stabilized for growth, and then transfected with the α-synuclein ER retention vector and FAM134B vectors. After 48 h, 20 μL of MTS/PMS mixture solution was added to the culture medium, and the cells were further incubated for 1 to 4 h at 37 °C. Finally, the absorbance was measured at 490 nm using a spectrophotometer (42).

Western Blotting.

An equal amount of total protein (10 μg) was resolved by 10% or 15% sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and then transferred to a nitrocellulose membrane (Amersham Pharmacia Biotech). The membranes were blocked with 5% nonfat milk in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) containing 0.05% Tween-20 (PBST) for 1 h at room temperature. After a briefly washing step with PBST, the blots were incubated overnight at 4 °C with specific antibodies: anti-LC3 (1:1,000, Sigma), anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH, 1:1,000, Sigma), anti-p-IRE1 (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-IRE1 (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-ATF4 (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-p-eIF2α (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-eIF2α (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-LAMP1 (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-DARPP32 (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-α-synuclein (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-p-α-synuclein (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-Calnexin (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-CCPG1(1:1,000, Abcam), anti-SEC62 (1:1,000, Abcam), anti-GRP94 (1:1,000, Santa Cruz), anti-β-actin (1:1,000, Santa Cruz), anti-CHOP (1:1,000, Proteintech), anti-FAM134B (1:1,000, Proteintech), anti-NELL2 (1:1,000, AbFrontier), anti-caspase 3 (1:1,000, Cell signaling), and anti-cleaved caspase 3 (1:1,000, Cell Signaling). The blots were then incubated with horseradish peroxidase-conjugated anti-rat, anti-rabbit, and anti-mouse antibodies (1:5,000, Santa Cruz). Immunoreactive proteins were detected using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) western blotting detection system.

α-Synuclein Measurement.

The amount of α-synuclein was measured using a sandwich ELISA kit (AnaSpec). The standards included in the kit and 10 mL of each diluted sample were added to microtiter plates precoated with antibodies specifically recognizing α-synuclein. After overnight incubation at 4 °C and washing, a detection antibody, linked to an enzyme, was applied. After incubation and washing, 350 μL of wash solutions was added to each well. Then, the plate was inverted and dried without moisture. The substrate was added and incubated for 15 min at 37 °C, and then, the reaction was stopped with a stop solution. The color reaction was measured with an automatic ELISA microplate reader (BioTek Instruments) at a set wavelength of 450 nm. The Bio-Rad software was used to create standard curves and calculate the concentration of the samples.

Immunocytochemistry and Immunohistochemistry.

A549 cells and brain sections were rinsed twice with PBST for 5 min at room temperature and then blocked with 0.5% bovine serum albumin in 1× PBST. After blocking, the samples were incubated overnight at 4 °C with anti-TH (1:1,000, Sigma), anti-DARPP32 (1:100, Abcam), anti-LC3 (1:100, Sigma), anti-p-α-synuclein (1:100, Abcam), anti-LAMP1 (1:100, Abcam), and anti-protein disulfide isomerase (PDI, 1:100, Invitrogen) primary antibodies. After overnight incubation, the samples were rinsed three times with 1× PBST (10 min per rinse). Immunofluorescence labeling was performed by incubating the cells and the brains with mouse anti-IgG Alexa Fluor (Invitrogen) and rabbit anti-IgG Alexa Fluor (Invitrogen). The cell nuclei were counterstained with DAPI (Invitrogen). The anti-DARPP32 and anti-TH antibodies were detected with 0.05% DAB staining (Vector Laboratories). The immunofluorescence was analyzed using confocal microscopy on a Zeiss LSM 780 confocal imaging system (Zeiss), whereas the DAB-stained samples were analyzed using bright-field microscopy.

ER-Tracker Blue Loading.

ER-Tracker Blue was prepared and stored in accordance with the manufacturer’s manual. The cells were seeded on 16-mm glass coverslips and incubated with an ER tracker to a final concentration of 1 μM for the final 15 min of treatment at 37 °C in 5% CO2. The cells were washed once with PBS prior to paraformaldehyde fixation and then washed again before being mounted on Dako fluorescent mounting medium onto glass slides. The measurement of ER area was performed using Image J as follows. The borders of each cell were drawn using the bright field picture, and then, the ER area was calculated and displayed as a fraction of the total cell area.

Cell Sorting.

For cell sorting, FACSAria™ II (BD Bioscience) was used. The samples were sorted at a concentration of 1 × 107 cells/mL with a sample pressure of 60 psi, nozzle measuring 70 μm, and a maximum event rate of 20,000 events per second (eps). GFP-positive, mCherry-positive, and GFP/mCherry-double-positive cells were collected.

Electron Microscopy.

For electron microscopy (EM) analysis, cocultured A549 cells and brain tissues were fixed with 2% glutaraldehyde–paraformaldehyde in 0.1 M PB (pH 7.4) for 12 h and then washed two times for 30 min in 0.1 M PB. They were postfixed with 1% OsO4 dissolved in 0.1 M PB for 2 h, dehydrated in an ascending gradual series (50 to 100%) of ethanol, and then infiltrated with propylene oxide. The specimens were embedded using a Poly/Bed 812 kit (Polysciences). After pure fresh resin embedding and polymerization at 65 °C in an electron microscope oven (TD-700, DOSAKA) for 24 h, thick sections (200 nm) were initially cut and stained with toluidine blue for light microscopy. In addition, thin sections (80 nm) were cut and double stained with 3% uranyl acetate and lead citrate for contrast staining. The sections were cut using an ultramicrotome (Leica EM UC7, Leica Microsystems). All of the sections were observed by transmission electron microscopy (JEM-1011, JEOL) at an acceleration voltage of 80 kV (43).

Immunoprecipitation Assay.

Antibodies against α-synuclein and calnexin antibodies were added to 1 mg of the cell lysates and incubated for 4 h at 4 °C. Protein G agarose beads (Invitrogen) were added and incubated overnight at 4 °C. Then, the lysates were spun at 3,000 rpm, and the pelleted protein G agarose was washed five times with lysis buffer. The pellet was resuspended with 30 μL 1× SDS sample buffer and subjected to western blot with α‐synuclein, calnexin, and FAM134B antibody.

Animal Experiments.

All procedures were performed in accordance with the Laboratory Animals Welfare Act, the Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals, and the Guidelines and Policies for Rodent Experiments provided by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (Seoul, South Korea) of the Yonsei University Health System. Transgenic B6; C3-Tg (Prnp-SNCA*A53T) 83Vle/J mice, expressing A53T human α-synuclein, were originally obtained from the Jackson Laboratory (JAX004479, USA). The hemizygous A53T mice with a mixed C57BL/6J × B6 background provided the transgenic and nontransgenic litter mates for the study. DNA was purified from the tail using a DNA extraction kit with genotype identification (5′-TGTAGGCTCCAAAACCAAGG-3′; 5′-TGTCAGGATCC ACAGGCATA-3′). The α-synuclein TG mice used in the experiment were 20 mo old. Male C57BL/6 J mice aged 7 wk (Orient Bio, Seongnam, Gyeonggi-do, South Korea) were acclimated in a climate-controlled room with a constant 12 h light/dark cycle for 1 wk before the initiation of virus administration. Briefly, the mice were anesthetized with isoflurane (Baxter), and the viruses were slowly injected into the SN (−3.1 mm posterior to bregma, ±1.2 mm lateral to midline, and −4.3 mm ventral to the brain surface) using a stainless steel (26-gauge) injection needle connected to a 10 μL microsyringe (Hamilton). The needle was left in place for 10 min before being slowly withdrawn (44). The ER-α‐syn mice were randomly divided into four groups: a) vector-only (control); b) AAV-ER-α‐synuclein; and c) AAV-ER-α‐synuclein with AAV-FAM134B; and d) AAV-ER-α‐synuclein with AAV-shFAM134B. All of the mice were killed 2 mo postoperatively. The WT-α‐syn mice were randomly divided into three groups: a) vector-only (control); b) AAV-WT-α‐syn; and c) AAV-WT-α‐syn with AAV-FAM134B. In addition, to determine the effect of nonspecific ER overload on neuronal toxicity, we constructed AVV vectors to overexpress NELL2 in the ER.

Subcellular Fractionation.

Brain tissues were homogenized in a homogenization buffer (0.85% NaCl and 10 mM Tricine–NaOH, pH 7.4). They were then disrupted using a homogenizer and repeated with passages through a 30-gauge syringe needle. The homogenates were centrifuged at 1,500 g for 10 min at 4 °C before the postnuclear supernatant fractionation. A gradient stock solution of 30% iodixanol was prepared by diluting OptiPrep (Sigma-Aldrich) density gradient medium (60% w/v solution of iodixanol in water) in the homogenization buffer in a 2:1 ratio. Linear continuous gradients of 2.5 to 30% iodixanol were formed from a discontinuous gradient by sequentially over-layering each of 30% (0.5 mL), 20% (0.5 mL), 17.5% (0.5 mL), 15% (2 mL), 12.5% (0.5 mL), 10% (2 mL), 7.5% (2 mL), 5% (2 mL), and 2.5% (1 mL) iodixanol. The resuspended lysate preparation (1 mL) was loaded on top of the gradient and centrifuged in a Beckman SW 41 rotor at 200,000 g for 2.5 h at 4 °C. After centrifugation, sequential fractions of 1 mL were collected from the top of the gradient. All operations were performed at 4 °C. The subcellular markers (calnexin for the ER and β-actin as a cytosolic marker) were analyzed in each gradient fraction.

Administration of AAV-ER-α‐Syn, AAV-FAM134B, AAV-shFAM134B, AAV-ER-NELL2, and AAV-WT-α-Syn through Stereotaxic Surgery.

For AAV-based shRNA expression, pSicoR-shRNA (#11579, Addgene) vectors with XbaI/HindIII-containing enzymatic site were inserted into the pAAV-minCMV-mCherry (#27970, Addgene) vector by the same enzymatic site. The minimal CMV promoter in these vectors was replaced with a human cytomegalovirus (CMV) immediate early enhancer and promoter containing β-globin intron by infusion cloning (Clontech, In-Fusion® HD Cloning Kit). The control shRNA and human FAM134B or mouse FAM134B shRNA was inserted into the HpaI-XhoI restriction enzyme sites of pSicoR vector and verified by sequencing. For overexpression of human WT-α‐synuclein, ER-α‐synuclein, and ER-NELL2, pAAV-CMV-EGFP (#67634, Addgene) vectors were inserted into the human WT-α‐synuclein, ER-α‐synuclein, and ER-NELL2 by infusion cloning. The viral vectors were pseudotyped, where the transgene of interest was flanked by inverted terminal repeats of the AAV2 packaged in an AAV-DJ capsid. AAV-DJ was engineered via DNA family shuffling technology which created a hybrid capsid from eight standard AAV serotypes. The plasmids for rAAV vector production included the construct for the AAV-DJ-serotype, the AAV vector transfer plasmid, a plasmid encoding rep and serotype-specific cap, and a plasmid encoding adenoviral helper sequences. These vectors (human WT-α‐synuclein, human ER-α‐synuclein, human FAM134B, mouse FAM134B shRNA, or human ER-NELL2 vectors) were thereafter purified by iodixanol gradients by the Korea Institute of Science and Technology (KIST) Virus Facility. The viruses were produced by the KIST (Seoul, South Korea). The animals were injected with 4 μL of AAV2-ER-α‐syn, AAV2-FAM134B, AAV2-shFAM134B, AAV2-WT-α-syn, and AAV2-ER-NELL2 (approximately 1 × 1013 genome copies per milliliter) into the right and left SN at a flow rate of 0.3 μL/min. In the ER-α‐syn/FAM134B, ER-α‐syn/shFAM134B, and WT-α-syn/FAM134B models, two types of viruses were mixed in a 1:1 ratio and injected into the SN.

Rotarod Test.

For motor performance, the mice were tested on the rotarod apparatus (MED Associates). Mice were habituated to the device for 15 min 1 d prior to the start of the training session. During the training trials, the mice were trained to run on the rotarod (at 20 rpm) for 10 min without falling, twice a day for 3 consecutive days prior to the virus injection. During the test trials, the mice were placed on the rotarod with increasing speed, from 5 rpm to 40 rpm, and placed on the rotarod at 30 rpm (cutoff time of 700 s maximum). The latency time until the mice fell off the rotarod was recorded (45).

Pole Test.

Each mouse was placed on the top of a vertical wooden pole (1 cm in diameter, 50 cm in height) with rough surface. The day before testing, the mice were habituated to the apparatus by placing them at the top of the pole and allowing them to descend five times. The time it took for each mouse to reach the bottom of the pole and place all paws on the floor was recorded. A default value of 120 s was recorded if the mouse was unable to rotate completely downward, fell off, or slipped down the pole (45). All behavioral tests were performed in the same order and the same time of day in all mice. All mouse groups were randomized and performed blind to the experimenter.

Statistical Analyses.

Differences between two experimental group means and multiple-experimental group means were evaluated by the Student t test and one-way ANOVA followed by the Bonferroni post hoc test, respectively. Differences with P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.
