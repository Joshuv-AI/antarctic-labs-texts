Safety and efficacy of remote ischaemic preconditioning in diabetic kidney disease: a pilot randomised, parallel-arm, sham-controlled trial protocol
Authors: Lanjing Wang, Linpei Jia, Shuangfeng Huang, Changhong Ren, Yao Yu, Yan Wang, Wenbo Zhao, Xunming Ji, Sijie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDiabetic kidney disease (DKD), characterised by rapid progression and poor prognosis, accounts for approximately 50% of all end-stage renal disease. Remote ischaemic preconditioning (RIP) can reduce the impact of subsequent fatal ischaemic events through brief episodes of ischaemia remote from a target organ. Therefore, we aim to determine whether RIP can slow the renal failure progression in patients with DKD.Methods and analysisThis is a pilot randomised, parallel-arm, sham-controlled trial of 60 participants with stable DKD (chronic kidney disease stages 3–4). Participants will be randomly assigned to the intervention or control group undergoing one daily RIP or sham RIP for 3 months, with a subsequent 6-month follow-up period. The primary study outcome is the incidence of end-stage kidney disease. The secondary outcomes include changes in biomarkers of chronic kidney disease progression, cardiovascular outcomes, rates of renal failure requiring dialysis or transplantation, tolerability rates and adverse event incidence.Ethics and disseminationThis study has been approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital of Capital Medical University (no. Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) is a growing epidemic affecting 10% of the population worldwide. Significantly, diabetic kidney disease (DKD) is the main cause of CKD and affects approximately 40% of patients with diabetes.13 Approximately 10% of patients with early-stage CKD and approximately half of patients with advanced-stage CKD suffer progression to renal failure and require dialysis or transplantation to survive.4 5 Moreover, DKD progresses particularly rapidly and has a poor prognosis, accounting for almost 50% of end-stage renal disease (ESRD) cases.1 2 6 Dialysis, in particular, is a burdensome therapy associated with poor patient outcomes and high societal and economic costs. Strategies to prevent progression to renal failure focus on exquisite blood pressure control, renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system inhibition for proteinuria DKD and glycaemic control with the use of sodium-glucose cotransporter-2 (SGLT-2) inhibitors in patients with diabetes.7 8 Even so, despite the optimisation of these parameters, many high-risk patients with DKD will progress to renal failure. Recurrent ischaemic damage to the failing and fibrotic kidney appears to be one of the final common pathways of progressive kidney damage in late-stage DKD, irrespective of the original cause of kidney disease.9 10 Specific strategies to alter this pathway in DKD have not yet been developed. In this context, it is crucial to seek novel pharmaceutical or non-pharmaceutical approaches to optimise the treatment of DKD.

As DKD progresses, the worsening renal interstitial fibrosis exacerbates tissue ischaemia and hypoxia, ultimately culminating in ESRD.11 12 This pathogenic cascade suggests that targeted interventions to attenuate renal fibrosis could potentially modify the natural history of DKD. Our previous work has established that abdominal aortic occlusion induces renal injury, while hypoxic conditioning can effectively reverse this damage.13 Building on these findings, we hypothesise that remote ischaemic preconditioning (RIP) may confer therapeutic benefits in DKD by mitigating the fibrotic-ischaemic progression.

Ischaemic conditioning occurs when a transient episode of ischaemia reduces the effect of a subsequent larger ischaemic insult. Similar levels of protection can be achieved by RIP. RIP is a non-invasive physical therapy that induces remote vital organs to adapt to ischaemia through repeated, short-term ischaemia‒reperfusion training on non-vital organs such as limbs, thereby improving their tolerance to ischaemic injury and enabling them to withstand subsequent fatal ischaemic events.14 Clinical studies using RIP have demonstrated protection against ischaemic target renal damage in a variety of acute and chronic clinical settings.15 16 In the renal setting, RIP performed in patients undergoing dialysis is known to abrogate brain, heart and liver ischaemia occurring during haemodialysis treatments.17 RIP may play a role in reducing the incidence of cardiac surgery-associated acute kidney injury (AKI).18 However, whether RIP can improve the renal function of patients with DKD is unclear and is worthy of further study.

Our overarching hypothesis is that RIP, performed in patients with DKD, could delay progression to renal failure by abrogating progressive ischaemic damage in the failing kidney. The present proposal is a pilot study addressing this hypothesis and is aimed at generating proof of concept and feasibility data on the benefits of RIP in patients with DKD.

Randomisation and blinding

Participants will be randomised to the intervention or control group (1:1) by a computer-generated program. Randomisation will be stratified by CKD stage (G4: eGFR 15–30; G3: eGFR 30–60 mL/min/1.73 m²), with a fixed block size of 4 to ensure balanced allocation between treatment groups. A biostatistician who is not directly involved in the study and who is blinded to patient allocation will be responsible for the computerised randomisation procedures. Prior to recruitment, a list of randomised treatment assignments will be generated, and participants will be assigned to the intervention or control arm as they are recruited based on this list. Participants will be assigned to the randomisation sequence after finishing their run-in phase.

Participants will not be informed of the pressure values applied during the procedure to ensure the blinding. Blinding setting has been described in our previous study.19 The outcome assessors/analysts, principal investigators and coinvestigators will be blinded for the study. However, since all participants will receive active RIP treatment during the run-in phase, the research coordinator will not be blinded for this study.

Participant recruitment/screening

The research coordinator will first work with someone within the participant’s circle of care (such as a nurse or a physician) to pre-identify potential eligible participants. The nephrologists will use the inclusion/exclusion criteria for each potential patient. This individual will inform the participant that they may be eligible to participate in a research study and ask for the patient’s permission for the research coordinator to contact them to discuss the study in detail.

After obtaining verbal permission to approach, potential participants will be given more detailed information on the study and the informed consent form (ICF). Participants will have sufficient time to decide if they want to participate and will be given the opportunity to ask questions and discuss further with their family or primary care physician. A signed ICF will be obtained from each participant prior to any study-mandated procedures. Participants who meet the screening criteria and provide ICF will be randomly assigned to either the intervention group or the control group.

Device setting and training

The research coordinator will demonstrate one full inflation/deflation cycle training on the RIP device. An enrolment log and participant identification code list will be kept to record the patients who became participants in the study. The participants will be identified by this unique participant identification code in any study-related form or database. The participant’s name and any other identifying details will not be included in any trial-related forms or databases.

Sample size

The pilot randomised controlled trial (RCT) is determined to recruit an adequate number of participants to evaluate the feasibility of the trial design. As suggested by the literature, a sample size of 12–30 participants per group is reasonable for the pilot nature.2022 For the purposes of this pilot feasibility/proof of concept study, we will enrol 60 participants and randomly assign them to the experimental group (RIP) and control group (sham RIP). Data on outcomes and biomarkers obtained from this pilot sample will inform sample calculations for a future larger RCT.

Treatment groups

Intervention

Participants randomised to the intervention group will perform one daily session for 3 months consisting of five consecutive cycles of simultaneous bilateral arm cuff inflation to 200 mm Hg (eg, above systolic pressure) for 5 min, followed by 5 min of deflation (rest). Participants will be instructed to perform the daily RIP intervention at a fixed, self-selected time throughout the study period. The specific chosen time was recorded to ensure consistency in application timing for each individual participant. Participants will be taught to self-administer the RIP. The RIP device (IPC-906D) is an easy-to-use, automated device developed specifically for this purpose (figure 2).

Remote ischaemic preconditioning device.

In addition to receiving the RIP device, participants in the intervention group will continue their scheduled visits as per standard of care (once a month to once every few months).

Control/sham

Participants randomised to the control arm will use the device as described above; except in the control group, inflation will reach 60 mm Hg, which is less than the diastolic pressure (therefore, no ischaemia). Patients will also receive usual care, which is DKD follow-up care based on prescheduled sessions with their healthcare team.

Due to the low risk of the intervention, a formal data safety monitoring board will not be established. The research coordinator will be available to participants to be contacted as needed, and the coordinator will contact participants during the study period on a weekly basis to inquire about problems or concerns. Follow-up evaluations through structured interviews will be conducted to systematically document patient-reported tolerability and AEs. Risk

Patients will experience tight squeezing of their upper arm that may be mildly uncomfortable; they may also experience mild numbness, and once the cuff is deflated, they may experience a blood flow rush through the arm, which may cause the skin to turn red and tingle.

Blood samples will be taken as per the study’s requirements. For most people, needle punctures for blood draws do not cause any problems. Participants might feel pain or be light-headed from the blood drawn. Participants may have the following at the site of the needle stick when blood is drawn: temporary discomfort (bruising, redness, bleeding at the injection site) or infection (rarely).

Statistical analysis

The clinical characteristics of the cohort will be described using standard summary statistics. We will report the median percentage adherence as well as the proportion of patients with >80% adherence in each treatment group (active vs sham). We will explore intergroup differences in biomarker levels using GLMing accounting for repeated measures.

To evaluate potential effect modification by CKD severity, we will conduct predefined subgroup analyses stratified by CKD stage (G4: eGFR 15–30; G3: eGFR 30–60 mL/min/1.73 m²). In addition, to address potential confounding effects of concomitant medications, we will perform subgroup analyses stratified by medication use.

The data will be organised and analysed using SPSS software V.26.0 (SPSS, Inc, Chicago, Illinois, USA). Quantitative data will be expressed as the mean±SD, while qualitative data will be expressed as the frequency (percentage). Correlation analysis of quantitative data will be performed via Pearson correlation analysis or Spearman correlation analysis. Differences between two groups will be compared by using t tests or Mann-Whitney U tests. The baseline and follow-up data will be compared using paired t tests. One-way analysis of variance will be used to compare multiple groups, and analysis of covariance will be used to compare multiple factors and multiple groups. p<0.05 will be considered to indicate statistical significance. During the statistical process, outliers will be eliminated based on the principle of means±3 SD. For the analysis of secondary outcomes involving various biomarkers, we will implement false discovery rate correction to address multiple comparisons.

Discussion

This study will be the first randomised clinical trial to address the effect of RIP on the safety and efficacy of delaying progression to renal failure in DKD patients. RIP has been previously investigated in patients with AKI, but its impact on patients with CKD is currently unclear.26 In particular, because diabetes is a major cause of chronic renal failure, our research is necessary.

RIP stimulates endogenous protective mechanisms against ischaemia and hypoxia through repeated, transient and reversible ischaemia and hypoxia stimulation of the upper limb to reduce damage to vital organs caused by subsequent severe ischaemia and hypoxia events.27 28 In recent years, the protective effects of RIP on the brain and heart have been consistently proven, and the renoprotective effects of RIP have received increasing attention.19 29 Existing clinical studies have confirmed that RIC intervention before percutaneous coronary intervention can significantly reduce the incidence of contrast-induced AKI.183032 At the same time, for patients after cardiac surgery, RIP can also reduce the occurrence of postoperative AKI.18 32 RIP has also been studied in patients who underwent partial nephrectomy, although there is insufficient clinical evidence to prove the positive role of RIP.33 34 Animal experiments have also been carried out to explore the mechanism of the renoprotective effect of RIP. G1 cell cycle arrest mediated by activation of the NF-κB-dependent Sema5B pathway was shown to be associated with the effect of RIP on AKI.35 In addition, RIP attenuates renal fibrosis by inhibiting the TGF-ß1/p-Smad2/3 signalling pathway.36 However, at present, most relevant studies have focused on the prevention of acute renal dysfunction by RIP, while the therapeutic effect of RIP in patients with CKD is still unclear. Therefore, we aim to recruit participants with DKD (stages G3–G4) to explore the therapeutic effect of RIP on this population.

At present, there is no consensus on establishing effective renal protection through the ischaemic phase, number of cycles or interval between each cycle. Meersch et al evaluated four different RIP doses (3×5 min, 3×7 min, 3×10 min or 3×5 min + 2 × 10 min). They confirmed that RIP significantly reduced the incidence of AKI, regardless of the dose used, and suggested that RIP might have dose-dependent renal protective effects.37 In our study, we will apply a well-established protocol used in previous research: 5 min of ischaemia and 5 min of reperfusion for five consecutive cycles.38 In addition, according to our previous experience in RIP-related trials, bilateral upper limb compression therapy is safe and well tolerated by participants. The RIP device used in this study is independently developed by our research team, featuring a fully standardised and automated treatment protocol. Furthermore, our research team has extensive experience in RIP clinical trials, as demonstrated by our prior publications in high-impact journals, and we maintain rigorous operator training and quality control measures to ensure reproducibility.19 38 This study is conducted as a pilot, parallel-arm, sham-controlled trial, providing preliminary data for planning a subsequent large-scale trial.
