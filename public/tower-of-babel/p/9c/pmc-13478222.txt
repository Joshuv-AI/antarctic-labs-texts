Assessment of pedestrian safety margins at unsignalized mid block crossings in Patna
Authors: Nikhil Kumar Suman, Satyajit Mondal, Anubhav Kumar Lokesh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPedestrian safety at unsignalized mid-block crossings remains a major concern in developing countries with heterogeneous traffic conditions. This study examines pedestrian safety margins (PSMs) and their key behavioral and traffic-related determinants in an urban Indian context. A data-driven regression model is developed to quantify the influence of pedestrian behavior, vehicular characteristics, and traffic gaps on safety margins at uncontrolled crossings. Video data were collected at three representative mid-block locations in Patna, India, yielding 722 pedestrian–vehicle interactions for model development. Field observations show that 26% of pedestrians adopted rolling crossing behavior, while 54.8% crossed in groups. The proposed model demonstrates satisfactory explanatory power, with an R2 value of 0.553. Pedestrian speed, vehicular speed, vehicular gap, and vehicle type emerged as statistically significant predictors of pedestrian safety margin. Larger vehicular gaps were positively associated with higher safety margins. Pedestrian speed (β = 0.428) and vehicular gap (β = 0.383) were identified as the most influential positive contributors, whereas vehicular speed exhibited a smaller yet significant positive effect (β 0.032). Two-wheelers exerted the strongest negative impact on pedestrian safety margins (β = −0.964) compared to heavy commercial vehicles. The findings underscore the need for integrated, behavior-sensitive interventions to enhance pedestrian safety.Keywords: Pedestrian safety margin, Unsignalized mid-block crosswalks, Mixed traffic conditions, Pedestrian-vehicle interaction, Gap acceptance behaviorSubject terms: Engineering, Mathematics and computing

Introduction

Pedestrian safety remains a big issue in developing nations. In most cases, traffic is given priorities over pedestrians, which bring along adverse consequences; pedestrians make up about 25% of global traffic deaths, totaling around 1.3 million each year1–3. In India, a significant mode of crossing the road is walking, making pedestrian safety a major challenge for transport planners and administrators4,5. Crossing unsignalized mid-blocks is particularly unsafe because, due to a lack of constraints, the perception of a gap acceptance in traffic flow is the only way to ensure pedestrian safety6–8. The complexity of these relationships underscores the need for infrastructure and management solutions that account for behavioral realities and diverse traffic patterns. Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of these factors is imperative to elucidate pedestrian perception of traffic dynamics and their subsequent crossing behaviors9–11. Pedestrians negotiating unsignalized mid-block crossings must assess vehicle characteristics, including type, speed, and proximity, primarily through visual cues. However, this assessment is significantly modulated by socio-demographic factors, individual risk tolerance, and gender-specific variations in gap acceptance and pedestrian speeds10–13. Analytical models based on both pedestrian and vehicle contexts are thus needed to adequately characterize such interactions and guide safety intervention efforts under real-world scenarios6,11. Prevailing pedestrian design guidelines, primarily derived from behavioral models in developed nations, are often incongruent with the heterogeneous traffic environments characteristic of developing countries like India14–16. The recording of over 32,800 pedestrian fatalities in 2022 underscores the critical exigency for conducting site-specific safety evaluations4.

Pedestrian–Vehicle Interaction (PVI) refers to the location where a pedestrian and an approaching vehicle trajectory intersect. These interactions occur at both signalized and unsignalized intersections and are influenced by the surrounding traffic dynamics, as well as user behavior17,18. Pedestrian safety margins (PSMs), the time interval between a pedestrian’s movement to a point of contact and the arrival of a vehicle traveling in the same area, have been proposed to assess the risk associated with crossing7,19. There are variations and distinctions in PSM across vehicle types and behavioral contexts, which highlight the involvement of crossing maneuvers in heterogeneous traffic conditions.

The PSM model was developed in the current study for an unsignalized mid-block crossing on a six-lane divided arterial road in Patna, Bihar. The primary aspects of this study are the inclusion of different demographic and pedestrian variables (age, gender, rolling behavior, walking speed, and platoon formation) alongside vehicle-specific features (type, speed, and gap size), all within a single modeling approach, to investigate PVI dynamics. Videographic surveys were conducted to capture comprehensive behavioral trajectories, thereby facilitating a rigorous empirical analysis of the factors governing crossing decisions. Results contribute to the design of pedestrian infrastructure that is sensitive to the needs of the urbanizing Indian city and help in evidence-based policy initiatives needed for mitigating pedestrian risk.

Despite the growing body of research on pedestrian safety margins (PSM), several critical gaps remain. The majority of previous research has been conducted in lane-disciplined homogeneous traffic situations, which limits its relevance to the diverse, poorly controlled traffic conditions found in developing nations. Furthermore, real-world, video-based observations and behavioral interpretation have received less attention in previous PSM investigations, which frequently depend on controlled experiments or descriptive analyses. Consequently, there is still a lack of understanding of the processes that control pedestrian decision-making and risk adaptation in mixed-traffic situations. The present study addresses these gaps by empirically examining PSM at unsignalized mid-block crossings using field data in an urban context, with explicit emphasis on behavioral interpretation alongside statistical modeling.

The rest of the paper is organized as follows: Sect. 2 provides a brief summary of existing literature on pedestrian crossing behavior; Sect. 3 describes the study sites, data collection process, and modeling approach; Sect.4presents the estimated models and their results; and Sect. 5 discusses potential implications of observed behavioral patterns with regard to pedestrian safety at unsignalized mid-block crossings.

Literature review

The safety of pedestrians is well established in transportation research, and since infrastructure often does not provide a safe environment for them, particularly in developing countries. Several models have been developed to describe pedestrian behavior at non-signalized mid-block crosswalks, and the interest has especially focused on mixed traffic. Himanen et al.20 used multinomial logit models to estimate the probabilities of pedestrian decision outcomes, as well as the standardized variables associated with risk. Sun et al.21 compared deterministic, probabilistic, and binary logit models for pedestrian gap acceptance and found that the binary logit model had a better capability in describing pedestrian behavior under dynamic traffic conditions. Through simulation, Manthirikul et al.19 utilized VISSIM to simulate pedestrian delays for various cycle lengths; however, the approach was deficient in terms of real-time enforcement and variability within the population. Similarly, Szagala et al.22 demonstrated that there are strong deep-learning-based approaches for tracking both pedestrian and driver behavior; however, these models were neither tested in real-time nor do they have the capability to detect hazards or actions in real urban scenarios. Behavioral and demographic variables are critical determinants of pedestrian safety. Patel et al.23 highlight elevated risk-taking in males, while Oxley et al.10 attribute increased conflict risk in older adults to the acceptance of inadequate time gaps. Despite the potential of simulation training to improve safety margins in the elderly24, cognitive distractions continue to pose a threat. While the adverse effects of mobile phone usage and dual-tasking are established, there is a lack of in situ research quantifying these distractions in actual traffic conditions25,26.

The effects of environmental treatments on pedestrian safety have also been investigated. Guo et al.27. The parallelogram-shaped pavement marking reduced average speed by 3.79 km/h and crash rate by 24.87%. Liu et al.28 demonstrate that rumble strips on crosswalks in China can significantly decrease vehicle speeds. Gomez et al.29 reported that enhanced yield signs and pavement markings reduced the occurrence of crash/near-crash events in simulators. The concept of a PSM, defined as the time difference between the instant when the pedestrian leaves the conflict zone and when the vehicle enters, has been examined in several studies30. They were one of the earliest groups to propose this definition, which has been adopted by several subsequent models. Kadali et al.31 illustrate that pedestrian rolling behavior and group phenomena lead to a significant reduction in safety spacing, especially for smaller vehicles such as two-wheelers and auto-rickshaws. They showed that pedestrian decisions and safety margins first depend on the type of vehicle, vehicular speed, and also the walking speed.

Recent advancements in PVI research have increasingly focused on behavior-aware and perception-driven modeling frameworks. Gorrini et al.32 conducted detailed video-based observations at unsignalized intersections and highlighted distinct behavioral phases and age-based disparities in gap acceptance. Zafri et al.33 quantified the influence of contextual and demographic factors, such as traffic volume, pedestrian age, and group crossing, on risky rolling gap behavior, offering important insights into reduced safety margins under constrained conditions. Expanding beyond observational approaches, Tian et al.34 proposed an anthropomorphic decision model based on visual perception and cognitive thresholds, accurately predicting pedestrian crossing timing under continuous traffic. Wang et al.35 introduced a bounded-optimal framework that integrates perceptual uncertainty and reinforcement learning to explain crossing behavior as an adaptive decision process. Tian et al.36 further modeled pedestrian interactions with yielding vehicles using psychophysical cues, enabling realistic simulation of decision timing. Together, these studies emphasise the role of perceptual cues, cognitive limits, and context in shaping pedestrian safety margins, reinforcing the present study’s motivation to examine real-world behavior under heterogeneous traffic conditions.

While prior research has examined the influence of pedestrian speed, gap acceptance, and demographic attributes on safety margins, significant divergence exists in the operationalization of variables, modeling methodologies, and contextual assumptions. Although demographic effects are well documented in regulated traffic environments, their influence in heterogeneous traffic conditions remains inconclusive. Furthermore, theoretically salient constructs, such as perceived right-of-way, conflict severity, and risk perception, are often omitted due to constraints in observational data. Consequently, variable selection in the present study is guided by both antecedent empirical evidence and the feasibility of reliable extraction from video data, while explicitly acknowledging the inability to quantify latent behavioral dimensions as a limitation.

However, despite a rich literature in this field, there are major gaps. The majority of studies have examined signalized intersections or simulated environments, and very few have studied real-life unsignalized mid-block crossings. Furthermore, the contribution of heavier vehicles to these pedestrian choices has not yet been investigated. Another limitation is the absence of integrated models that consider demographic, behavioral, and vehicular factors using observational data in an Indian urban scenario. The objective of this study is to fill these gaps and develop a data-based pedestrian safety margin model for unsignalized mid-block crosswalks in mixed traffic settings.

Methodological background

The current research analyzes PSM using high-resolution footage from unsignalized mid-block crossings in Patna. The approach is intended to capture the fine-grained dynamics of PVI in heterogeneous traffic scenarios. The whole analytical framework used for this study is depicted in Fig. 1.

Study area

The study area of the present study was the Old Bypass Road in Patna, a six-lane highway that connects Rajendra Nagar and Kankarbagh, characterized by high population density and prevailing mixed traffic patterns. Three distinct mid-block sections are considered: Bhoothnath More, Kumhrar Nayatola, and Patna Sadar Block. Site selection was governed by rigorous inclusion criteria, specifically: high pedestrian volume, the absence of signalization, and a lack of grade-separated facilities (e.g., foot overbridges). To ensure a representative spectrum of crossing scenarios, sites were stratified based on road geometry, trap length, and adjacent land-use patterns. Observations adhered to a structured schedule to capture temporal variations in traffic volume. Figures 2 and 3, and 4 detail the geometric and operational attributes of the selected sites.

Data collection

Video recordings were carried out at three selected locations during three different time periods-morning, afternoon, and evening-on weekdays. Each location was equipped with high-definition video cameras to record the observations. The total period of observation for all locations is about 13.5 h.

Data extraction

The video data obtained at the study sites were annotated for PVI measures. Video data were analyzed using Kinovea software (2024.1, https://www.kinovea.org/en/forum/viewtopic.php? id=1959) which allows video to be viewed frame by frame and measure motion with a timestamp. Entries and exit times, pedestrian walking speed, time of vehicle arrival at the conflict zone, and vehicle speeds are some dynamic parameters to be considered for the computation of PSM. According to the methodological framework presented in Fig. 1, nine key variables were systematically identified under two main categories: pedestrian and vehicle characteristics. Pedestrian factors, such as standing time prior to crossing, walking speed, sex, age group, and group crossing behavior, were considered. In the present study, pedestrian gender and age group were visually estimated from video recordings by the observers using a predefined coding protocol. While visual classification is commonly adopted in video-based pedestrian studies, it may introduce a degree of subjectivity. Age was categorized into broad age groups as follows: child (≤ 15 years), adult (16–50 years), and elderly (> 50 years). The gender and group activity were labeled based on the videography data. Vehicular attributes included vehicle type, speed, and gap. Pedestrian and vehicle speeds were estimated by calculating the time spent over a calibrated distance of 20 to 30 m, corresponding to a trap length. These extracted parameters served as the basis for developing a model to calculate PSM under mixed traffic conditions using multiple linear regression.

A total of 722 PVI were detected from the video data, and only those providing a clear view as well as an uninterrupted behavior during crossing were further considered for analysis. Encounters were discarded if the pedestrian was blocked or paused at the crossing.

Vehicular-related variables

Understanding the characteristics of vehicular flow is fundamental in examining pedestrian behavior, especially in unsignalized urban crossings. The following empirical variables have been observed in these field data. The vehicles observed during the study can be divided into four groups. Two-wheelers (TW) refer to motorcycles, scooters, and mopeds. These vehicles have a large manoeuvring ability and are observed to weave their way through gaps, which may affect pedestrian gap acceptance. The motorized three-wheelers (MTWs), which are primarily auto-rickshaws, have an intermediate size and acceleration37. Their stop-and-go activity also increases the randomness in pedestrian decision-making. Cars and SUVs are collectively referred to as Four-Wheelers (FW). These tend to be more uniform in pace and closer to ‘lane discipline’, less erratic than bikes, for instance. Trucks and buses (heavy commercial vehicles, HCVs) have larger physical dimensions and slower response times. The perception of risk also tends to make pedestrians more indecisive. Pedestrian behavior

The type of approaching vehicle, the available gap between vehicles, and the speed of oncoming traffic all have an immense impact on pedestrians when crossing the road3. In this regard, rolling out is quite common, considering that pedestrians often begin crossing between vehicles before the flow of traffic has ceased. By calculating the movement of vehicles and determining the optimal time to cross, pedestrians make real-time decisions based on perceived safety and urgency. Another important determinant of pedestrian crossing behavior is the wait time, the period a pedestrian spends on the sidewalk prior to crossing. Indeed, these waits are useful in assessing the Level of Service (LOS) provided by pedestrian facilities and express a person’s patience and risk tolerance. Wait time can reveal whether you feel a sense of safety or are overwhelmed by surrounding traffic. In addition, in cases involving crowding, pedestrians cross the road en masse, meaning that perceptions of traffic gaps can change and the way in which vehicles work their way through an intersection. All variables have their own specific contributions to PVI at unsignalized mid-block crosswalks. For example, rolling behavior (initiating a crossing without coming to a complete stop) was speculated to reduce safety space. Conversely, vehicular gap was also anticipated to correlate positively with greater pedestrian safety. Vehicle type was considered as a multi-level categorical variable to control for the impact of various vehicle sizes and driver behavior on pedestrian decision-making. Pedestrian and vehicle speeds were estimated from real field measurements, with respect to the time for crossing two predetermined trap lengths. A systematic examination of PSM was made possible by this coding and categorization system, which guaranteed a consistent interpretation of the variable effects among the 722 occurrences in the PVI.

Model development

This study developed an MLR model with a dataset of 722 observed PVI events in order to investigate the relationship between PSM and several pedestrian-vehicle factors. Vehicular speed in km/h, headway in seconds, and pedestrian crossing behaviors were some of the explanatory variables considered. Since vehicle types represent categorical information, dummy variables were created, with HCV as the reference category. While pedestrian gender, age, platoon configuration, and the rolling behavior of pedestrians all were quantified and studied, they proved to have very little influence in the final predictive specification (Eq. 2). In the stepwise selection process, their estimated coefficients were not statistically significant, and including them did not notably increase the adjusted R2 or improve the model selection criteria. Upon closer examination, it was found that these behavioral variables were frequently correlated with primary traffic metrics (for instance, rolling and platooning behavior were related to shorter vehicular gaps and longer waiting times), indicating that they act more as mediators or contextual markers rather than independent influences on PSM in this dataset. Measurement constraints, such as gender and age estimation, as well as manual coding of group or rolling behavior, further lowered confidence in their estimates of those parameters. Ultimately, a simpler model without them showed at least as good, and often improved, validation performance; therefore, they were excluded from Eq. 2 but are reported and interpreted descriptively in the Results section below.

Model validation

The model’s performance was validated using the remaining 30% of the dataset (n = 217) by comparing the predicted PSM values with the observed ones. The results produced a mean absolute percentage error (MAPE) of 8.63%, demonstrating high predictive precision. The model also showed a strong fit, with an R2 value of 0.965, confirming its robustness in estimating PSMs under diverse traffic conditions. The equity plot depicting the observed and predicted PSM values is shown in Fig. 5. It is noteworthy that the validation R² (0.965) exceeds the training R² (0.553), which is atypical in regression-based modeling. This outcome can be attributed to the relatively lower variability and more homogeneous distribution of key predictors within the validation subset, resulting in improved alignment between observed and predicted values. Furthermore, the dominance of strongly correlated predictors, such as vehicular gap and pedestrian speed, contributes to this effect. However, this high validation performance should be interpreted with caution, as it may reflect dataset-specific structure rather than universal model generalizability.

Discussion and conclusion

In this study, the MLR framework was established to quantify the determinants of PSM within unsignalized, mixed-traffic environments. Analyzing 722 video-graphed interaction events, the model successfully integrated distinct demographic, behavioral, and vehicular variables. The main findings of the study are summarized below:

The model achieved a high goodness-of-fit (R2 = 0.965) and validation accuracy (MAPE = 8.63%).

At higher vehicle speeds, pedestrians are likely to perceive greater risk and respond by adopting more conservative crossing strategies, such as waiting for larger temporal gaps before crossing. Collectively, these findings corroborate established theoretical paradigms of pedestrian decision-making, particularly those anchored in Time-to-Collision (TTC) perception and adaptive risk mechanisms. By elucidating how pedestrians calibrate safety margins in response to dynamic traffic stimuli, rather than being primarily governed by static demographic attributes, this study provides empirical scaffolding for behavior-based interpretations of safety in heterogeneous traffic environments. This, in turn, contributes to a more granular understanding of pedestrian-vehicle interaction dynamics specific to the complex operational contexts of developing nations.

Recommendations

The empirical variability of the PSM values observed requires structural intervention at unmarked, unsignalized mid-block locations. Specifically, safety can be enhanced by the reconstruction of pedestrian refuge islands and raised crosswalks, with the installation of high-visibility markings that reduce points of collision. Speed tables should also be deployed; these vertical deflection measures serve a dual purpose, acting as both a traffic calming device and an unobstructed, elevated walkway for pedestrians. Moreover, in light of regional statistics indicating that female pedestrians consistently select larger gaps and higher safety margins, infrastructure needs to be developed as more inclusive. As such, design standards should include provisions for longer crossing times, such as phased crossing points, to support risk-averse user groups. Finally, recognizing the critical role of gap estimation, authorities should install sensor-based warning systems that provide real-time visual or auditory warnings to pedestrians during high-traffic intervals.

Limitations

A mixed-methods approach, combining direct roadside surveys and video analysis, could be useful in obtaining comprehensive age profiles and psychological evaluations for future research. Such triangulation will be crucial in understanding the rationale behind each individual’s subjective sense of danger. Moreover, future data collection should emphasize interactions with HCVs and also consider adverse weather conditions that affect braking distance and pedestrian agility to ensure that the proposed PSM model is applicable under more complex scenarios. The present study adopted an MLR approach to develop the PSM model. However, alternative modeling techniques such as Tobit regression, generalized linear models, or mixed-effects models could offer additional flexibility and potentially yield a more comprehensive representation of PSM.
