Knowledge of human papilloma virus and vaccine hesitancy among health sciences students in Türkiye
Authors: Eda Unal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vaccine hesitancy among health sciences students has become a growing public health concern, particularly regarding the human papillomavirus (HPV), the most prevalent sexually transmitted infection globally. Aim: This study examines the association between health sciences students’ knowledge of HPV and their hesitancy toward vaccination. A cross-sectional design was employed among health sciences faculty students (n = 331) at a university in Türkiye between January and April 2025. Data were collected via an online questionnaire comprising sociodemographic items, the Vaccine Hesitancy Scale, and the Human Papillomavirus Knowledge Scale. Descriptive statistics and multiple linear regression analyses were conducted. Of the participants, 82% were female and 89% were nursing students. Only 7% had received the HPV vaccine, while 47% expressed willingness to be vaccinated. Higher HPV knowledge scores, including general HPV knowledge, HPV test knowledge, HPV vaccine knowledge, and HPV vaccine accessibility, significantly predicted lower vaccine hesitancy (p < 0.001). Students’ vaccine hesitancy is associated with their knowledge and awareness of HPV. Integrating targeted educational interventions on HPV and vaccination into nursing and health sciences curricula may help reduce vaccine hesitancy and promote preventive health behaviours among future professionals.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-62858-9.

Introduction

Human papillomavirus (HPV) is the most prevalent sexually transmitted infection worldwide, affecting the skin, genital mucosa, and oropharyngeal region1,2. Most sexually active individuals are infected with HPV at least once in their lifetime, often without presenting symptoms2. High-risk HPV genotypes are responsible for approximately 91% of cervical cancers, including 89% of anal cancers in males, 93% in females, 63% of penile cancers, 75% of vaginal cancers, 69% of vulvar cancers, and 72% of oropharyngeal cancers in males and 63% in females3. Therefore, vaccination against HPV is recommended to prevent HPV-related diseases and cancers1.

Currently, six HPV vaccines are available globally, providing protection primarily against high-risk HPV types 16 and 18, which cause cervical cancer4. The US Advisory Committee on Immunization Practices recommends that HPV vaccination begin routinely at age 9, ideally between the ages of 11 and 125. In line with the World Health Organization (WHO)’s global cervical cancer elimination strategy, the goal is to achieve 90% vaccination coverage among girls aged 15 years and older by 20302. Many high-income countries have achieved substantial progress through national immunisation programs offering the HPV vaccine free of charge and reduced disease prevalence6. Conversely, in developing countries such as Türkiye, HPV vaccination is not included in the national immunisation schedule and is administered privately on a paid basis7. In our country, cervical cancer screening is included in our National Cancer Screening Program, and healthy women aged 30–65 are screened every 5 years with an HPV-DNA test. Although vaccination is highly recommended, the HPV vaccine is not yet integreted into the national vaccination program8.

Evidence shows that HPV vaccination programs have led to marked reductions in HPV infection prevalence and related cancers3. Nevertheless, vaccination coverage remains suboptimal in low- and middle-income countries, including Türkiye1. Vaccination not only confers individual protection but also contributes to herd immunity, reducing disease transmission and mortality9. Despite its proven efficacy, increasing vaccine hesitancy has become a global public health challenge10. The WHO identifies vaccine hesitancy, defined as delay, reluctance, or refusal to vaccinate despite availability of services, as one of the top ten threats to global health11,12.

HPV vaccine hesitancy is shaped by multifactorial determinants, including vaccine cost, accessibility, trust in healthcare providers, perceived safety, and sociocultural beliefs13. A systematic review in Türkiye revealed that although healthcare workers generally hold positive attitudes towards HPV vaccination, actual vaccination rates remain low due to high cost, lack of inclusion in national programs, insufficient knowledge, and limited professional recommendations14. Similarly, studies among health sciences students in Türkiye have reported low levels of HPV knowledge and vaccination uptake, with improved knowledge being associated with more favourable attitudes towards vaccination15. Another study found a negative correlation between HPV knowledge level and HPV vaccine hesitancy16.

Healthcare professionals, particularly nurses, play a critical role in promoting vaccine acceptance, dispelling misconceptions, and guiding evidence-based preventive practices17,18. Their knowledge, personal vaccination behaviour, and attitudes strongly influence public vaccine confidence and uptake19. Understanding vaccine hesitancy among health sciences students, who represent the future frontline of healthcare, is therefore essential to strengthening community health protection. This study aimed to examine the relationship between the knowledge levels of health sciences students regarding HPV and their vaccine hesitancy.

Methods

Study design

This cross-sectional study aimed to examine the relationship between nursing and health sciences students’ knowledge of Human Papilloma Virus and their vaccine hesitancy. A cross-sectional design was chosen to provide a snapshot of current attitudes and knowledge levels during a single academic semester.

Sampling and participants

The study population comprised 1.075 undergraduate students enrolled in nursing, nutrition and dietetics, and physiotherapy and rehabilitation programs at a public university in western Türkiye. Using SurveyMonkey sample size calculation (95% CI, 5% margin of error), the required sample was 284. A total of 339 students participated, and after excluding eight incomplete responses, 331 were included (response rate 30.7%). Participants were recruited using convenience sampling and included if they were actively enrolled and volunteered to participate.

Data collection procedure

Data were collected via an online survey administered using Google Forms distributed through institutional WhatsApp groups between January and April 2025. The first page explained the purpose, confidentiality, and voluntary nature of participation. Participants could submit the form only once, ensuring anonymity and data integrity.

Instruments

Sociodemographic variables (age, gender, department, parental education) and HPV vaccination status were collected. The Sociodemographic questions included age, gender, department, class, parental education level, immunisation status for the Human Papilloma Virus vaccine, belief in the HPV vaccine, and willingness to receive of getting the HPV vaccine20.

Two validated Turkish scales were used.

The Vaccine Hesitancy Scale Long Form (VHS-LF) (Kılınçarslan et al., 2020; Cronbach’s α = 0.933 in this study), 21-items and 4 sub-dimensions, 5-point Likert scale assessing vaccine hesitancy and opposition21. The VHS-LF scale is calculated by scoring as follows: 1 point for “strongly disagree,” 2 points for “disagree,” 3 points for “partially agree,” 4 points for “agree,” and 5 points for “strongly agree.” The subdimension “benefits and protective value of the vaccine” consists of five items, all of which are reverse-coded. The second subdimension, “anti-vaccination,” is scored on six items; the third subdimension, " solutions to avoid vaccination,” and the fourth subdimension, “legitimisation of vaccine hesitancy,” are also scored. High scores indicate a high level of an individual’s anti-vaccination attitude. In the original validation study, the Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale was reported as 0.90521.HPV Knowledge Scale (Waller et al., 2013; Turkish adaptation by Bozkurt & Özdemir, 2023; α = 0.86 in this study), a 33-item scale measuring general and vaccine-specific HPV knowledge22,23. The scale consists of four sub-dimensions: general HPV knowledge, HPV testing knowledge, HPV vaccine knowledge, and HPV vaccine accessibility. In scoring the scale, 1 point is awarded for each correct answer, and 0 points are given for incorrect answers and “I don’t know” statements. The total score obtained from the scale ranges from 0 to 33, and a higher score indicates a higher level of knowledge about HPV screening tests and the HPV vaccine. The Cronbach’s alpha coefficient for the scale is reported as 0.9623.

Data analysis

Analyses were conducted using R (version 4.5.0). Descriptive statistics (mean ± SD, frequencies, and percentages) were computed. The base stats package was used to construct linear regression models, and the lm.beta package was used to compute standardized regression coefficients (β).

Prior to conducting regression analyses, all relevant assumptions were evaluated. Linearity was assessed through scatter plots, which indicated approximately linear relationships between predictors and outcomes. Normality of residuals was examined using histograms and P–P plots, which suggested that the assumption was largely satisfied. Homoscedasticity was evaluated via plots of standardized residuals against predicted values, revealing no systematic patterns. The Durbin–Watson statistic ranged between 1.5 and 2.5 across models, indicating independence of residuals.

Multicollinearity was examined using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF). Given the presence of categorical predictors, adjusted GVIF values were interpreted. Across all models, adjusted VIF values ranged from 1.02 to 1.57, well below conservative thresholds (VIF < 5). Corresponding tolerance values ranged between 0.40 and 0.98, exceeding the recommended minimum of 0.10. These findings indicate no evidence of problematic multicollinearity, despite conceptual overlap among HPV-related knowledge variables.

To further evaluate potential shared variance or suppression effects, zero-order correlations were calculated for all predictors and outcome variables. Correlation patterns were consistent with regression results. For example, perceived HPV vaccine accessibility showed a strong negative zero-order correlation with vaccine benefit perception (r = − 0.73), comparable to its standardized regression coefficient (β = −0.63). In the overall model, the zero-order correlation (r = − 0.74) exceeded the standardized coefficient (β = −0.34), suggesting variance partitioning rather than statistical suppression. These findings indicate that large standardized coefficients reflect substantive associations rather than multicollinearity or suppression artifacts.

Because all predictors and outcomes were derived from self-report measures collected at a single time point, common method variance (CMV) was additionally examined. A single-factor exploratory factor analysis including all composite scale scores was conducted. The first unrotated factor accounted for 58% of the total variance. Although this value exceeds the conventional 50% threshold, the included variables represent conceptually distinct constructs (HPV-related knowledge and vaccine hesitancy dimensions) that are theoretically expected to correlate. Therefore, the observed shared variance likely reflects substantive conceptual overlap rather than solely method bias. Nevertheless, findings should be interpreted with caution given the cross-sectional and self-report nature of the data.

Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Overall, the diagnostic evaluations support the adequacy of the regression models and the robustness of the reported findings.

Ethical approval

Ethical approval for this study was conducted by the Ethics Committee of the Clinical Research at Bursa Uludag University (Approval number: 2024-09, 27 November 2024). The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki. All participants were informed about the purpose of the study, assured of confidentiality, and provided written consent prior to participation. Participation was voluntary, and respondents could withdraw at any time without consequence.

Results

Among the students, 83% reported believing in the HPV vaccine, 7% had received the HPV vaccine, and 47% stated that they were willing to be vaccinated (Table 1).

Predictors of benefit and protective value of vaccine

A multiple regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of the “Benefit and Protective Value of Vaccine” subscale scores (Table 2). The overall model was statistically significant, F(15, 314) = 28.06, p < 0.001, and explained a substantial proportion of the variance (R² = 0.57; adjusted R² = 0.55).

HPV vaccine accessibility was negatively associated with perceived benefits and protective value of vaccination (β = −0.63, p < 0.001). Similarly, HPV vaccine knowledge was also a significant negative predictor (β = –0.17, p = 0.005), suggesting that higher knowledge levels were associated with lower benefit/protection scores on this subscale.

Additionally, father’s education at the undergraduate level was associated with significantly lower scores compared to those whose fathers had a primary-school or lower education (β = –0.13, p = 0.026). Other predictors, including gender, grade level, age, and the remaining parental education categories (secondary and high school), general HPV knowledge, HPV testing knowledge), were not statistically significant (p > 0.05).

Predictors of anti-vaccination

A multiple linear regression analysis indicated that the model predicting Anti-Vaccination scores was statistically significant, F(15, 314) = 31.91, p < 0.001, explaining a high proportion of variance (R² = 0.60; adjusted R² = 0.59).

General HPV knowledge (β = –0.31, p < 0 0.001), HPV testing knowledge (β = –0.24, p < 0.001), HPV vaccine knowledge (β = –0.25, p < 0 0.001), and HPV vaccine accessibility (β = –0.15, p = 0 0.004) were associated with lower levels of anti-vaccinations.

Additionally, third grade students showed significantly higher scores than first grade students (β = 0.13, p = 0 0.010). Other variables (gender, grade [2nd and 4th year], parental education [secondary school, high school], and age) were not significant (p > 0 0.05). (Fig. 2).

Predictors of solutions to avoid vaccination

The regression model predicting Predictors of Solutions to Avoid Vaccination was also significant, F(15, 314) = 19.98, p < 0.001, accounting for a moderate proportion of variance (R² = 0.49; adjusted R² = 0.46). General HPV knowledge (β = –0.30, p < 0.001), HPV testing knowledge (β = –0.24, p < 0 0.001), HPV vaccine knowledge (β = –0.17, p = 0 0.009), and HPV vaccine accessibility (β = –0.13, p = 0.021) were negatively associated with as students’ endorsement of reasons for anti-vaccination. No significant effects were found for gender, grade level, parental education, and age (p > 0.05). (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This study explored the relationship between health sciences students’ knowledge of HPV and their vaccine hesitancy, highlighting the multidimensional links between knowledge domains and attitudinal subscales. The overall vaccination rate (7%) observed among students is consistent with previous research from Türkiye (2–9%)17,24,25, and remains far lower than international rates (0.3% in China vs. 94% in Norway)6. This gap likely reflects structural and cultural barriers, including the absence of the HPV vaccine from the national immunisation program, cost-related obstacles, and sociocultural or religious considerations26.

A systematic analysis identified factors such as low awareness and knowledge about HPV and the vaccine, the cost of the vaccine, lack of information, access difficulties, low income, educational level, and age as barriers to vaccination14,27. In addition, another important reason behind the refusal of the HPV vaccine may have been that students, due to the misconception that they are not at risk if they are not currently sexually active, perceive the risk of this infection as low, which prevents the vaccine from being taken seriously28.In the study conducted in India, nursing students cited being sexually inactive, lack of information, and the vaccine’s accessibility as reasons for not getting the HPV vaccine28. In addition to these findings, contemporary studies demonstrate that cultural factors heavily influence HPV vaccine acceptance17,29. Recent analyses of media coverage in Türkiye indicate that associating the vaccine directly with sexuality and conservative values—particularly parents viewing the vaccine as an endorsement or encouragement of early sexual activity—negatively impacts vaccine acceptance29. Furthermore, deep-rooted stigma, discrimination, and anxieties related to sexual activity have been shown to systematically reduce institutional and individual acceptability of HPV vaccines17.

In the current study, the students’ HPV knowledge score was (17.56 ± 6.78). The level of knowledge about general HPV, HPV testing, and HPV vaccines, as well as the accessibility of HPV vaccines, was found to be moderate. Akalın (2022) found similar results in a study conducted with nursing and medical students17. Studies conducted in Türkiye15 and Jordan30 have found that students’ knowledge levels about HPV were low. These results indicate that HPV-related content is insufficiently embedded in undergraduate curricula. Educational exposure during university has been shown to shape vaccine acceptance, with structured modules and faculty-led discussions positively influencing attitudes30,31.

In this study, nearly half of participants (47%) reported willingness to receive the HPV vaccine, while another 47% remained undecided, and 6% (n = 21) stated they did not want to get it. Similarly, in Alper Yıldız’s 2021 study, 48.4% of participants indicated that they were undecided about getting the HPV vaccine32. A study conducted in Saudi Arabia also found that 40.9% of health sciences students were willing to get vaccinated33. Being health sciences students may have influenced the students’ willingness to get vaccinated. Compared to the general population, medical students are supposed to be more knowledgeable, according to Farsi et al. (2021)34. Social media may have influenced students’ perceptions of HPV vaccine safety30. Prior systematic reviews confirm that cost, perceived safety, and access to reliable information are consistent predictors of vaccine acceptance35.

The present study demonstrated that vaccine hesitancy was moderate (mean score: 41.92 ± 11.29), and higher overall HPV knowledge was inversely associated with vaccine hesitancy. This inverse relationship was consistent across all knowledge domains, including general HPV knowledge, HPV testing knowledge, HPV vaccine knowledge, and HPV vaccine accessibility. Similarly, a study conducted with participants aged 18 and over across Türkiye found moderate vaccine hesitancy36. In this study, health sciences students gaining awareness about HPV and vaccine knowledge during their education may have contributed to slightly lower vaccine hesitancy. In the Saudi Arabian study, the lack of sufficient information about the vaccine was among the reasons for the 59.1% HPV vaccine hesitancy rate among health sciences students33.

The current study confirmed the relationship between students’ overall HPV knowledge level and vaccine hesitancy, and showed that as HPV knowledge level increased, students’ vaccine hesitancy decreased. In particular, increases in the students’ HPV knowledge scores, comprising general HPV knowledge, HPV testing knowledge, HPV vaccination knowledge, and HPV vaccine accessibility knowledge, led to a decrease in overall vaccine hesitancy. To prevent vaccine hesitancy related to HPV among health sciences students who shape the health of society, providing both quality HPV education and supporting students with information about their insufficient HPV knowledge could be more effective. This is because healthcare personnel across all disciplines have unique and complementary roles in combating vaccine hesitancy37. Motivational interviewing training specific to immunisation provided to healthcare professionals can increase their knowledge, attitudes, and effective communication about vaccination, promote vaccination in the community, and improve community vaccination decisions18.

This study is important because, unlike previous studies in the literature, it shows the subdimensions of the vaccine hesitancy scale, the HPV knowledge scale, and the associations between these subdimensions. According to this study, the subscales of the HPV scale, including general HPV knowledge, HPV testing knowledge, HPV vaccine knowledge, and HPV vaccine accessibility, are all related to the subscales of vaccine hesitancy, such as the benefits and protectiveness of vaccines, anti-vaccinations, solutions to avoid vaccination, and legitimisation of vaccine hesitancy. This result shows that information, awareness-raising, and accessibility-based interventions can be effective in combating vaccine hesitancy. In this study, HPV vaccine knowledge, HPV vaccine accessibility, which are sub-dimensions of the HPV scale, and fathers’ education level, were found to be related to the sub-dimensions of the vaccine hesitancy scale, specifically the perceived benefits and effectiveness of the vaccines. HPV vaccine knowledge, knowledge on the accessibility of the HPV vaccine, and the father’s education level decreased as these factors increased. A study found that age, education, and income level did not affect negative beliefs about vaccines12. A low maternal educational level has been shown to negatively influence HPV knowledge14, and HPV vaccine hesitancy has been reported to be higher among men than women33. Given the finding of the present study that vaccine hesitancy decreased with an increasing paternal education level, we propose that educational programs tailored to parental—particularly paternal—education levels may play an effective role in reducing HPV vaccine hesitancy.

This study has proven that as students’ knowledge of HPV, HPV testing, HPV vaccination, and the accessibility of the HPV vaccine increases, negative beliefs about the vaccine decrease. Negative beliefs about vaccines were found to be higher among 3rd-grade students compared to 1st-grade students. Similar to this study, it was found that although first-grade students had a low level of knowledge about the HPV vaccine, they exhibited a positive attitude towards it. It was stated that this outcome was due to the students’ limited exposure to health education at the beginning of their studies and that their attitudes could change depending on clinical experience as their health knowledge increased28. In contrast, Akalın found in her 2022 study that medical and nursing students had higher levels of HPV knowledge compared to other students in the fifth and sixth grades.

In a study comparing HPV vaccine awareness across medical schools, medical students were found to have higher levels of awareness33. One study found that midwifery students showed more vaccine hesitancy compared to nursing and paediatric nursing students9. This study found no difference between nursing, nutrition-dietetics, and physiotherapy and rehabilitation students. This may be due to the sample of study. This trend can generally be attributed to differences in educational experience, professional identity, and access to scientific information9. These differences may lead to different interpretations of messages regarding vaccination within the community.

In this study, as the students’ scores for general HPV knowledge, HPV testing knowledge, HPV vaccine knowledge, and HPV vaccine accessibility increased, the students’ solutions to avoid vaccination decreased. Providing students with accurate information about the HPV vaccine can improve the rationale for the HPV vaccine. It has been stated that increasing knowledge about the HPV vaccine can significantly improve the attitudes of nursing students in Spain towards HPV vaccines38. In this study, as students’ general HPV knowledge, HPV testing knowledge, and HPV vaccine knowledge increased, and as their grade level rose (from 1 to 2), their legitimisation of vaccines for vaccine hesitancy decreased. It is stated that current educational interventions targeting healthcare workers can reduce vaccine hesitancy36. In his 2023 study, Ergün determined that students’ prior knowledge of HPV had an impact on their understanding of HPV information, general HPV screening tests, general HPV vaccination, and the current HPV vaccination program15. A study conducted with female university students in China showed that vaccine hesitancy is a risk factor for their intention to get the HPV vaccine13.

Strengths and limitations

This research has a few limitations. First, the fact that the study’s sample includes health sciences students who are knowledgeable about HPV could lead to selection bias. Secondly, the smaller number of nutrition and dietetics and physiotherapy and rehabilitation students in the study sample compared to nursing students led to heterogeneous results. Thirdly, the self-report nature of the survey and scales may have led to bias in the students’ results. Fourthly, the generalisability of this study is limited because it only included students from a single university in western Türkiye. Therefore, it can expand the sample size and the scope of the study population to ensure homogeneity for future research.

The strength of this study is the detailed demonstration of the relationship between the HPV knowledge scale, its sub-dimensions and sociodemographic variables on students’ total anti-vaccination score and sub-dimension scores. This is quite important for planning the education needed to address students’ HPV vaccine hesitancy.

Conclusion

This study confirmed that HPV-related knowledge scores were associated with the total anti-vaccination scores and sub-dimension scores of health sciences students. General HPV knowledge, HPV testing knowledge, HPV vaccine knowledge, and the accessibility of the HPV vaccine all were associated the four sub-dimensions (“Benefits and Protection of Vaccines”, " “Anti-vaccination”, " Solutions to Avoid Vaccination”, and " Legitimization of vaccine hesitancy”).

Based on these findings, updating and structuring the curriculum and supporting it with quality education to address the HPV knowledge gaps that cause vaccine hesitancy regarding the HPV vaccine among health sciences students will empower them. This result shows that information, awareness-raising, and accessibility-based interventions can be effective in combating vaccine hesitancy. Despite these findings, different samples are needed to demonstrate the relationship between HPV knowledge and vaccine hesitancy among health sciences students and the impact of sociodemographic factors influencing them.
