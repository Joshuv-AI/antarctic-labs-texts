Music and Affective Prosody after Surgical Removal of the Right Arcuate Fasciculus: A Case Study
Authors: Joanna Sierpowska, Jennifer Grau‐Sánchez, Aleksi J Sihvonen, Neus Ramos‐Escobar, Angels Camins, Andreu Gabarrós, Daniela Sammler, Teppo Särkämö, Antoni Rodríguez‐Fornells
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTThe right hemisphere of the human brain plays an important role in music processing, with lateralized functions for pitch, meter, and melody recognition among other features. However, the relationship between white matter and music processing is relatively little explored. We report a case study of a 50‐year‐old musician with a right parieto‐insular brain tumor who underwent resective surgery. Post‐surgical diffusion tensor imaging revealed that the integrity of the right frontal aslant tract and the ventral pathway of the patient was preserved, whereas the right dorsal pathway (arcuate fasciculus and portions of parietal and insular cortex) was largely removed. A comprehensive neuropsychological evaluation revealed that the patient did not present language, receptive prosody, or cognitive deficits after the tumor removal, with the exception of a selective impairment in recognizing the emotion of fear in prosody. Moreover, the patient showed no signs of amusia, and did not report any problem with musical activities or music ‐related emotional processing. The only subjective report concerned rhythmic abilities. This case study suggests that the right dorsal stream does not subserve musical and receptive affective prosody processing, contributing to the ongoing debate of white matter correlates of these cognitive functions.Keywords: amusia, aprosodia, brain tumor, language, music, receptive affective prosody, right arcuate fasciculus

Introduction

The right hemisphere plays an important role in speech prosody and music processing, with lateralized functions for pitch, meter, and melody recognition among other features [1−3]. While the left hemisphere shows a high specialization for language, the right one is thought to contribute more to the perception and production of speech prosody [4−6]. Music processing abilities are distributed bilaterally [7, 8], but the right hemisphere seems to play a major role, as demonstrated by the manifestation of acquired amusia in patients with right hemisphere damage. In language, prosodic acoustic features such as pitch variations and temporal information allow communicating emotions and support conveying the intended linguistic meaning [9]. Speech‐related prosodic information extracted from pitch and quasi‐rhythmic timing variation is processed along dorsal and ventral pathways in the right hemisphere. These pathways convey information across fronto‐temporo‐parietal perisylvian networks and parallel the language streams in the left hemisphere [1, 10]. The dorsal stream connects posterior superior temporal regions with inferior frontal and premotor areas through the arcuate fasciculus and the superior longitudinal fasciculus. This stream is proposed to be involved in the processing of the temporal aspects of auditory stimuli and in audio‐motor transformations [11]. The ventral stream, which is suggested to be related to more abstract knowledge of prosody, includes posterior and anterior temporal regions that are connected to inferior frontal and occipital areas through the middle longitudinal, uncinate, and inferior fronto‐occipital fasciculi (IFOF) [1, 12]. The disruption of these pathways may result in aprosodia, which is the inability to process linguistic prosody (e.g., problems of perceiving stress in words or impaired discrimination of sentence intonations) and affective prosody (e.g., perceiving and producing emotions via speech). Deficits in linguistic prosody have been associated with damage to the left hemisphere, whereas affective aprosodia is mainly presented in patients with right hemispheric lesions, negatively affecting interpersonal communication [6, 13−18].

Regarding music processing, the right hemisphere also shows an advantage with lateralization for the processing of the spectrotemporal resolution and emotion recognition in music. The right hemisphere perceives and integrates general contour, specific interval sizes, or local duration ratios of tunes as well as the temporal features that compose rhythms [19]. Similar to language, these acoustic features allow for the processing of pitch and rhythm and the recognition of different emotions expressed in melodies.

Amusia is a perceptual deficit characterized by the inability to process the basic auditory features of music, affecting primarily the perception of pitch in a melodic context but which can also disrupt the processing of rhythm, timbre, memory, and emotion recognition in music [20]. Thus, individuals with amusia have impaired abilities to perceive, process, and interpret music. Amusia can be present in patients with lesions in the right hemisphere, especially when these lesions involve right fronto‐temporal regions and the dorsal and ventral pathways [21−23]. Importantly, a relatively recent study provided evidence about the critical role of the right IFOF in amusia [24].

Whereas the relationship between right hemisphere damage and the concurrence of aprosodia and amusia seems clear, it is still unknown if there are different outcomes associated with lesions selectively affecting the dorsal or ventral pathway or even the degree of overlap between lesions affecting both streams. Given that both prosody and music processing exploit similar auditory cues in speech and music (pitch and temporal information), it is reasonable to expect that both processes share similar neural networks. Indeed, neuropsychological overlap between coinciding deficits in prosody and music processing have been reported [25−28]. Moreover, subtle deficits in linguistic and affective prosody have been observed in people with congenital amusia [29, 30].

In this article, we present a single case study of a musician who completely preserved his prosodic and musical skills after the removal of a massive right parieto‐insular malignant brain tumor. We explored the precise localization and extent of the post‐surgical cavity and its subjacent white matter organization, and compared these results with the up‐to‐date findings in the field.

Materials and Methods

Clinical Information

We met patient CM in the context of a routine neuropsychological evaluation before surgery for the removal of a right parieto‐insular high‐grade glioma (anaplastic astrocytoma; Figure 1, upper row). CM was a 50‐year‐old experienced musician who completed higher education (18 years of education, including philological studies). Before surgery, he worked full‐time as a librarian and was an active professional choir singer, devoting many hours to rehearsals and concerts. His musical history includes singing lessons from the age of 18 and piano and guitar practice for 10 and 5 years before the surgery, respectively. The patient's first language was Spanish, and the Edinburgh Handedness Inventory revealed that his manual dominance was predominantly right [31]. Hearing and vision were normal both before and after surgery, and there were no clinical indicators suggesting visuospatial deficits during CM's neurological or neuropsychological examinations.

Lesion visualization. Structural T1 images showing the right parieto‐insular high‐grade glioma of the patient before the surgery (upper row) and the extent and location of the post‐surgical cavity (bottom row).

Before surgery, the patient was assessed with a comprehensive battery of language and cognitive tasks screening for possible intraoperative electrical stimulation (ESM) procedures in the awake brain surgery (see Table 1). CM's scores revealed no significant preoperative deficits, making him a good candidate for awake intraoperative procedures. He did not report complaints about his musical activities or apparent signs of amusia, and he presented an outstandingly good level of collaboration, all of which made him a good candidate for awake surgery. However, after taking into account the size of the lesion and that the tumor was of high grade, the patient was assigned for an asleep surgery. At this point, the patient's presurgical scores confirmed a left‐hemispheric lateralization for language (thus within the preserved hemisphere), but the surgical removal plan put his musical skills in peril. Hence, the patient was referred for a post‐surgical follow‐up neuropsychological evaluation that included the assessment of language and cognitive functions and a personalized protocol to evaluate musical abilities. CM was assessed 3 months after the surgery at the neurology department of the Bellvitge Academic Hospital (L'Hospitalet de Llobregat, Spain). The language and cognitive assessment were carried out and informed by a senior neuropsychologist. The experimental task of emotional recognition in music and receptive affective prosody was carried out by J.S., and the music structured interview and Montreal Battery of Evaluation of Amusia (MBEA) by J.G.‐S. (all after surgery).

CM signed an informed consent form to participate in a study investigating language and cognitive deficits in patients presenting with expansive brain lesions. The study was carried out in line with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Clinical Research Ethics Committee of Bellvitge Academic Hospital (L'Hospitalet de Llobregat, Spain). Given that this is a case study, the patient's data generated and/or analyzed during the current study are not shared.

Neuropsychological Evaluation

Language and Cognition

The post‐surgical neuropsychological evaluation took place 3 months after surgery. It included standardized tests and tasks to evaluate language, receptive affective prosody, and cognitive functions, as well as music perception. Verbal fluency was assessed by asking the patient to retrieve as many words within a minute following a semantic and phonological cue [32]. Naming was evaluated with the Boston Naming Test [33], and phonological processing was assessed with a task that consisted of the repetition of words and nonwords [34]. Language comprehension was evaluated with the Token Test [35], and the digit span subtest from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale (WAIS‐IV) to assess short‐term memory (Digit Span Forward) and working memory (Digit Span Backward) [36]. All of these tasks were also administered pre‐surgically.

Music and Receptive Affective Prosody

In order to evaluate receptive affective prosody, a prosody task composed of 84 auditory items was adapted to Spanish from the original work by Castro and Lima [37]. To create a database in Spanish, we first translated the original Portuguese task and obtained 16 real language sentences. The original sentences in the Portuguese task were syntactically simple and composed of high‐frequency words with no emotional content (as assessed by three independent judges). Importantly, all the sentences were of neutral semantic content, like in: “el cuadro está en la pared” (the painting is on the wall). Relying on the language similarity, we did not additionally control for frequency and emotionality in Spanish. The mean length of the original Portuguese sentences was 8 syllables (SD 1.3, range: 6–11), and the mean length of the translated sentences was of 8.19 syllables (SD 1.05, range: 6–10). Subsequently, we transfigured these sentences to generate 16 pseudosentences following the syntax, morphology, and phonotactics of Spanish by replacing one to three phonemes of the content words (nouns, adjectives, and verbs carrying salient semantic information). In the same way as in the original task, vowels were replaced by vowels and consonants by consonants, and we also kept the syllabic structure and stress of the original word. For example, the “el cuadro está en la pared” was transformed into “El duepro está en la narén.” Once this corpus was created, we recorded the sentences in an audio booth using a female voice of a person with experience in theater acting. This voice‐over artist read each of the 16 short sentences with neutral messages expressing different types of emotions through prosody (fear, sadness, joy, neutral, anger, surprise, and disgust), from which we selected a final 84 auditory items to cover all the emotions. Auditory stimuli were presented using the E‐Prime software (Psychology Software Tools; Sharpsburg, PA), and the written labels of the different emotions appeared on a computer screen. The participant was asked to identify the emotion expressed in each sentence and select the correct emotional label using the computer keyboard. The evaluation of music perception abilities included an assessment of amusia, emotion recognition in music, and musical memory.

The screening for amusia was done using the MBEA [38], which is a widely used standardized test composed of six subscales evaluating different musical components: scale, contour, interval, rhythm, meter, and memory. The test comprises 30 novel musical phrases that are manipulated with regard to the different music components. In four subtests, 30 melody pairs are presented to the patient, who is asked to judge whether they are the same or not. In the other subtests, the patient had to indicate whether melodies were a waltz or a march (meter), or whether they had been presented before or not (memory).

Emotion recognition in music was assessed using a task composed of 56 auditory items of musical excerpts extracted from the original database by Peretz and colleagues [39]. The patient was asked to identify the emotion expressed in the excerpt (fear, sadness, joy, or neutral), obtaining one point for each correct response.

There was no feedback at the end of any of the trials of any of the neuropsychological, prosody, and music testing tasks.

For the purpose of this study, and to obtain a normative z‐score in the Spanish population, both the receptive affective prosody and MBAE tests were validated in a group of five age‐matched control participants who were male choir singers from Catalonia (mean age 49.4 years).

Recognition of familiar songs was assessed using the modified Famous Melodies Test, including classical, popular, and folk melodies that are well‐suited for Western European culture [40]. In this task, 38 instrumental pieces were presented, containing either well‐known or novel instrumental songs. The patient was asked to identify whether the song was familiar to him or if he had heard it for the first time. Each correct response scored one point.

Additionally, we used a structured interview based on the Barcelona Music Reward Questionnaire (BMRQ, Spanish version) [41], with questions regarding both the post‐surgery and pre‐surgery time periods (in a retrospective manner) to assess the possible postsurgical changes in the patient's enjoyment of music. The BMRQ assesses sensitivity to music reward, capturing the capacity to experience pleasure from music listening and related activities. The BMRQ has been translated and validated into different languages, including Spanish, and is widely used to measure music reward sensitivity (see also Ref. [42]).

Brain Imaging Assessment

Before surgery, a magnetic resonance imaging evaluation protocol was used for diagnosis and intraoperative anatomy‐driven navigation. In the post‐surgery evaluation, T1‐weighted images were acquired to visualize the lesion and the post‐surgical cavity. In addition, high‐resolution diffusion‐weighted images (DWIs) were acquired to dissect the major language‐related white matter tracts. The acquisition details, software implemented, and the guidelines for the in vivo dissections are described in our previous publications [34, 43, 44]. Additionally, the exact area of the post‐surgical cavity was delineated using the MRIcron software [45] and normalized to the standard MNI space. This allowed for the comparison of the patient's lesion with previous voxel‐based lesion‐symptom mapping (VLSM) results of 77 patients with right‐hemispheric strokes and their MBEA scores [22].

For a precise definition of brain tumor and post‐surgical cavity locations, we compared the T1‐weighted brain scans with the Harvard‐Oxford cortical structural atlas [46], as well as with the Juelich Histological atlas [47], which allowed for the division of the insula (both atlases were implemented in the FSLeyes visualization tool [48]). The T1‐weighted brain image was first reoriented to standard, skull stripped, and then aligned linearly (FLIRT) and warped nonlinearly (FNIRT) to a standard template. Once transformed, we compared the T1 image with brain structures, as they are defined in the atlas.

Results

Neuropsychological Results

During the neuropsychological evaluation appointments (both before and after surgery), CM presented with good fluency of spontaneous speech. The patient did not report language or cognitive complaints, and he explained that he was autonomous in the activities of daily living and participating in leisure and community activities. After surgery, he returned to choir singing sessions, but he expressed that he needed to pay more attention and make efforts to be able to adequately follow the rhythm of the pieces sung. The results of the neuropsychological evaluation are presented in Table 1. Both the pre‐ and post‐surgical evaluation of language and cognition revealed a satisfactory level in verbal fluency tasks, intact naming, flawless phonological processing and comprehension, and good working memory. In the postsurgical evaluation and regarding the emotion recognition in prosody, CM showed an isolated impairment in recognizing the emotion of fear (Z = −2.67), while he had no difficulty in adequately identifying any other kind of emotion.

The evaluation of musical abilities revealed that CM did not present with any impairment in this regard. In this sense, the results from the MBEA showed overall intact musical perception. Despite the patient's subjective report of augmented effort needed to follow the rhythms during choir sessions, he obtained high scores in the rhythm and meter subscales. Moreover, CM was able to perfectly recognize emotions in musical pieces, regardless of the specific kind of emotion. Finally, CM presented an excellent level of musical memory, as indicated by his scores in the subscale assessing memory from the MBEA and the Famous Melodies Test. While comparing CM's post‐surgical enjoyment of music with the pre‐surgical state (retrospective, as assessed with BMRQ), he acknowledged that he was listening to more music before surgery. The most significant change was related to how much money the patient was spending on music (the facet of music exploration and information seeking in the musical seeking subscale), which changed from a considerable amount to not much. As he commented, he had so many recordings that he had no lifetime left to listen to all of what he collected over the years. As an example of what he meant, he mentioned the writer Eduardo Galeano (who at the time of the interview had recently passed away) and his reflection on how he planned how many books he could read (Galeano) in regard to how many years of life he had left. Importantly, this postsurgical change in musical seeking resulted in lowering patient's overall BMRQ score from 67 to 63 points (with a score of 65 being the cutoff to consider anhedonia), which, although a small, can be interpreted as a clinically relevant shift.

All other dimensions touched upon in this interview—the considerable enjoyment and feeling of relaxation, comfort, emotion (although rarely accompanied by tears), or even chills while listening to music, the pleasure while playing with other people, or feeling a special connection with people he shares his favorite music with, or participates in concerts with (as well as with the artist), or music‐related information seeking—remained the same before and after surgery. Interestingly, the patient reported that he usually does not move to the music and generally has a low feeling of needing to move while listening to music (even his favorite one), although he usually does feel the need for humming his favorite songs, and these were the same before and after surgery (for scores, see Table S1). The patient reported that he can easily perceive if someone is singing in dissonance and out of tune in the same way as before surgery, and that he does not need to hear the lyrics to recognize a familiar melody (also the same as before surgery). Additionally, to the formal evaluation of the patient's abilities, we could also review a video recorded 5 years after surgery, where the patient was singing and his performance was accurate. Moreover, it is important to note that after surgery, the patient was autonomous in his daily self‐care and actively involved in the community (e.g., participating in the choir rehearsals).

Brain Imaging Results

Post‐surgical structural imaging revealed that, in addition to the significant parieto‐insular structural loss, the patient presented a considerable level of post‐surgical tissue replacement as the mass effect disappeared (Figure 1, lower row). In consequence of this, the impact of the lesion on its vicinal tissues significantly diminished. For comparison, Figure 1 also shows the right parieto‐insular lesion provoked by the tumor before the surgery (upper row). The brain tumor mainly infiltrated the parietal (the postcentral, supramarginal, and angular gyri and the parietal operculum and portions of the central opercular cortex) and insular cortex (both lateral granular areas 1 and 2), but its limits also touched small portions of the frontal cortex (precentral gyrus), as well as the Heschl's gyri, planum temporale, and the superior portion of the lateral occipital cortex. Given the extension of the lesion into the two main lobes, we labeled it as parieto‐insular. The tumor caused a midline shift of brain structures, which remitted after surgery, and the presence of vasogenic edema around the tumor was scarce before surgery and further reduced postsurgically. The post‐surgical cavity included virtually the same areas as the tumor. Although the majority of the tumoral mass was removed, its residual portion was left affecting the insula (the lateral granular areas 1 and 2).

Additionally, DTI in vivo dissections revealed that after surgery, the right arcuate fasciculus was completely removed with the tumoral mass, as this tract could not be reconstructed (Figure 2). Nevertheless, the frontal aslant tract and the bundles inferior to the Sylvian fissure (uncinate fasciculus, inferior longitudinal fasciculus, and IFOF) preserved their integrity.

Discussion

In this study, we reported the case of CM, a musician diagnosed with a right parieto‐insular high‐grade glioma with virtually perfect receptive affective prosody and music perception skills after the complete surgical removal of extensive areas of the fronto‐parieto‐insular cortex and right dorsal pathways (including arcuate fasciculus). This study provides new evidence about the relationship between right white matter circuitry and receptive affective prosody and music perception.

Sustained prosody and music perception abilities despite the complete removal of the right dorsal stream in CM suggest that the integrity of right dorsal white matter may not be a necessary condition to support affective receptive prosody and music perception. A model by Schirmer and Kotz [49] proposes that emotional recognition in spoken language is a multistep process with individual subprocesses that are hierarchically organized along the ventral stream. The lower step would be the acoustic analysis mediated by the primary and secondary auditory cortices of both hemispheres. This bilateral primary sensory processing is followed by the extraction of emotional meaning from acoustic cues, a process where the right ventral stream plays a major role. Finally, the emotional meaning is available for high‐order cognitive functions such as explicit evaluative judgments and involves the inferior frontal gyrus and orbitofrontal cortex in the right hemisphere. Subcortical structures such as the putamen, amygdala, and parahippocampal gyrus have also been associated with emotional processing [50−52]. For example, auditory information is projected from the thalamus to the amygdala and from here to cortical regions, enabling rapid and rough emotional analysis of relevant acoustic information [53]. This model provides a more comprehensive view of the regions involved in the subprocesses related to prosody, where the right ventral pathway is an intermediate step involved in extracting the emotional meaning. This is in line with evidence derived from studies in neurological patients that highlight the critical role of the right ventral stream structures in deficits related to prosody [18, 28, 54].

Therefore, CM's excellent prosodic abilities are in line with the literature, arguing that the functions sustained by the right dorsal stream might not be essential for emotional recognition. The dorsal stream is thought to be involved in the processing of temporal aspects of speech rhythm processing (see Ref. [55] for a review), but the primary and secondary auditory cortices of both hemispheres also process the temporal dimensions of auditory cues at a lower level [49]. Interestingly, CM's isolated impairment in fear recognition in prosody resembles results by Sheppard and colleagues [54], who showed a similar selective impairment (following strokes affecting the right hemisphere and amygdala). Moreover, Sheppard and colleagues’ results further confirmed that the impairment of emotional prosody identification was associated with lesions affecting the ventral stream. Previously, the difficulty in recognizing fear in musical excerpts was observed by Gosselin and colleagues in patients after unilateral temporal lobe excision [56]. In that study, the authors observed that this specific impairment followed excisions including the vicinity near the amygdala, which in our patient was spared. Future studies may aim at disentangling if emotional recognition of fear in music and prosody share neural correlates, since CM correctly performed the task of fear recognition in music in contrast to the recognition of fear in prosody (see also Refs. [10, 57, and 58]). Importantly, not only have white matter structures been related to prosody, but also the right portions of the premotor cortex, inferior frontal gyrus, and superior temporal cortex [1]. The results of our study confirm the possible involvement in prosody (as well as prosodic perception) of all of the aforementioned cortical areas, as these were spared in the tumoral lesion and resection. However, our results challenge the findings from a case study by Nicholson and colleagues [59], who showed that a lesion in the right fronto‐parietal cortex was associated with impaired prosodic perception, as our lesion involved large portions of the parietal cortex.

With regard to music processing, Sihvonen and colleagues [24] have recently shown that damage to the right IFOF is the strongest predictor for post‐stroke amusia. This tract was preserved in CM, who did not show any sign of amusia. We also observed that the patient's lesion did not overlap with the lesions of 77 stroke patients who presented amusia in a previous study by Sihvonen and collaborators [22]. The surgical cavity in our patient involved parietal cortices located superiorly compared to Sihvonen and collaborators’ VLSM findings. Additionally, the lesions presented in this group of amusic stroke patients comprised subcortical structures. The evidence coming from individuals with congenital amusia suggests that malfunction of the dorsal stream is involved in this condition [60, 61]. However, these findings require further research, as Chen and colleagues observed that these relationships may vary depending on the neuroimaging methodology applied [62].

More importantly, a recent study [28] investigated for the first time the overlap of lesions resulting in both aprosodia and amusia in a large sample of subacute stroke patients. These authors showed that both acquired aprosodia (linguistic and affective) and acquired amusia (pitch and rhythm) were associated with significantly overlapping lesion patterns in the right frontal and basal ganglia regions. Importantly, supporting the present findings, Sihvonen and colleagues also observed that the right IFOF was the strongest predictor for both aprosodia and amusia [28]. Therefore, this study and the present one provide crucial information about the role of the right ventral stream in sustaining these functions. Additionally, the same authors found no evidence for dorsal stream involvement (see also Ref. [54]) in prosody perception.

Previously, a limited number of studies were conducted in patients with brain tumors, and using intraoperative ESM tends to converge with the idea that disruption of music production and singing is associated with right fronto‐temporal regions (which in CM were virtually spared). In this sense, ESM applied to the anteroposterior pars opercularis of the right inferior frontal gyrus elicited a spontaneous switch from a speaking to a singing mode in a single case patient [63]. Katlowitz and colleagues [64] also reported a case of a professional male vocalist who showed that ESM applied to the right posterior superior temporal gyrus suppressed singing but not speaking. Similar findings involving right superior temporal regions in music production were reported by Garcea and collaborators [65] using ESM in a musician who had a tumor in the right temporal lobe (see similar results using transcranial direct current stimulation [66]). Interestingly, in this sense, we can deduce that CM's tumoral lesion as well as the postsurgical cavity are only mildly impacting the temporal regions, as well as the pars opercularis of the right inferior frontal gyrus, which further supports the hypothesis that these areas can be involved in singing. Disentangling musical processing from emotional musical processing, as well as further ESM studies involving the inferior frontal gyrus and the right temporal lobe in brain tumor patients, could be an interesting avenue for future research.

An important aspect to consider is that CM had unilateral damage, and the intact regions of the left hemisphere may contribute to compensating for the damage to the right arcuate fasciculus. Despite the right hemisphere playing a major role in affective prosody and music processing, neuroimaging studies show bilateral activations and suggest that the left hemisphere subsystems support these functions [17, 67]. For instance, the left inferior frontal gyrus is proposed to integrate prosodic information with linguistic information [49], and several regions of the left hemisphere are involved in interval and rhythm identification in music [8]. These data provide evidence of a clear crosstalk between hemispheres regarding music processing, which may also suggest the potential emergence of compensatory mechanisms after injury [68]. In relation to linguistic prosody, the interhemispheric interaction (and left‐hemispheric) contribution is even clearer [69, 70]. However, a recent study using VLSM and deterministic DTI tractography in stroke patients did not reveal clear associations between left‐hemispheric structures and linguistic and affective prosody [28].

Functional plastic changes following tumoral lesions are common and include reorganization within injured areas and reshaping via recruitment of adjacent functional areas and contralateral homologous regions [71]. Better functional compensation is associated with slowly growing lesions. The tumor of this patient was of high grade, meaning that the lesion expanded relatively fast in time. This might rule out the explanation that prosody and music perception are maintained because of a rapid functional reorganization involving intact regions [72] and implied that the preserved abilities in the patient are not just a matter of reorganization/plasticity but may indeed suggest that the right dorsal stream is not substantially involved in prosody/music perception. However, in some cases, high‐grade gliomas may be detected after a long period of less malignant tumor (e.g., WHO grade II) growth, which would in turn support the plasticity hypothesis.

Finally, it is worth mentioning that our patient did not report language or cognitive complaints, which is in agreement with what we observed during the neuropsychological evaluation. However, the patient reported that he needed to put more effort to follow the rhythms during choir sessions (see also their results in the BMRQ scale). This subtle deficit in rhythm processing could be associated with the lesion in the right dorsal stream, as this stream has been previously associated with amusia [24], also being implicated in rhythm perception in music [62, 73] and short‐term music learning [74]. It is also important to note that the evaluation carried out in our patient might not have been sensitive enough to detect more subtle music processing deficits, especially in an experienced musician. One possibility is that other tasks covering the broad spectrum of music processing abilities could have revealed errors related to the patient's complaints.

In conclusion, this study describes the case of an experienced musician with a brain tumor infiltrating the right dorsal stream with preserved prosody and music perception skills, as well as continuing singing practice after tumor surgery. The evidence provided here may support models of musical processing, which involve mainly the ventral processing stream in this regard.

Limitations of the Study

In the first place, CM's evaluation of music and prosody was only performed post‐surgery, and our knowledge with regard to his presurgical performance comes from a self‐report. However, we can assume that it was virtually perfect (as reported) before surgery, as these abilities were preserved after surgery. Also, there were no subjective complaints regarding the patient's abilities before surgery.

Second, the assessment protocol was mainly focused on receptive abilities in music and prosody, which limits our conclusions concerning music or prosody production to what we know from a self‐report. Also, given that the rhythmic and metric subtests of the MBEA are not a measure of musical beat processing per se [75], we cannot discern if CM presented with beat‐perception deficits. If encountering patients with a neural profile like CM, we would complement their assessment with the Beat Alignment Test [76]. Another limitation is that we could neither consider the effect of CM's extensive musical training on the premorbid brain organization, nor its role in brain reorganization after surgery. Another limitation is the small size of our control sample (N = 5). It was important for us to compare our patient's results to a specific group: male choir singers age‐matched to our patient, but even though we expanded our search beyond the metropolitan area of Barcelona, these were the only eligible participants we were able to enroll.

Conclusion

The present study contributes to the ongoing debate on how the white matter structure of the right hemisphere is involved in music processing. Our case report presents evidence that the removal of the right dorsal stream, mainly the arcuate fasciculus, was not associated with deficits in language, receptive affective prosody, cognition, or musical processing. Overall, our results suggest a possible independence between music processing and the right dorsal stream, which may help in therapeutic decisions such as brain tumor surgery while preserving the quality of life of the patient.
