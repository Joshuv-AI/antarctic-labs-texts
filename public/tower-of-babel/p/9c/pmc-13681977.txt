Prevalence and predictors of undernutrition among preschool children (3 to 5 years) in Nigerian semi-urban community: conventional anthropometric indices versus composite index of anthropometric failure approach
Authors: Ifeoma Christiana Afiaenyi, Florence Ifeyinwa Nwoga
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAlthough numerous studies have been conducted on the predictors of undernutrition among children in Nigeria, none of them used composite index of anthropometric failure approach (CIAF). CIAF estimates accurately the actual burden and magnitude of undernutrition in children. Therefore, this study utilized both the conventional and CIAF approaches to determine the prevalence and predictors of undernutrition among preschool children in a semi-urban Nigerian community.MethodsCross sectional survey design was adopted. Multistage sampling technique was used to select 492 children used for the study. Data was collected, between July and August 2025, through questionnaires and anthropometric measurements. Data obtained were analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics, version 21.0. Chi square was used to establish relationships among categorical variables. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression analysis were used to determine factors associated with undernutrition and significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe forms of malnutrition among the participants included stunting (26.2%), underweight (12.2%), wasting (7.7%) and overweight/obesity (25.4%). Using the CIAF approach, 67.9% had no failure at all, 1.0% experienced only wasting, 4.9% were wasted and underweight, 1.8% had all the 3 forms of undernutrition, 5.5% were stunted and underweight and 18.9% had stunting as a single manifest. Maternal educational level and monthly income, toilet facility used, and having been sick within 3 months prior to the study were significant predictors of stunting, underweight and wasting among the study population. Age of the children, maternal educational level and monthly income, number of under-five children in the household, dietary diversity score, type of toilet facility used, source of drinking water and recent illness were significant predictors of CIAF. Stunting index had the highest value (0.82).ConclusionsChildren affected by undernutrition using the CIAF approach was more than that observed using the conventional approach. Government should, therefore, integrate the CIAF as a routine screening instrument at both primary healthcare facilities and community-level nutrition surveillance programmes, given its capacity to capture the magnitude and severity of malnutrition within the same individual.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s40795-026-01432-y.

Introduction

Several studies around the world have shown that pre-school children are especially vulnerable to poor dietary practices and malnutrition due to their high nutrient needs and dependency on adult caregivers for proper feeding [1–5]. Undernutrition contributes to mortality in children and daily adjusted life years later in adulthood. Despite extensive interventions given by the government and other state actors on nutrition, the prevalence of undernutrition remains alarmingly high especially in resource poor setting.

Stunting represents a chronic undernutrition that affects not only the physical growth and development of under five children but also cognitive development thereby endangering their chances of survival later in life [6]. According to Nigerian Demographic and Health Survey (NDHS) [7], the stunting prevalence in Nigeria is 37% with even higher percentages being reported in remote communities in Nigeria [8]. The long-term consequences of stunting include increased risk of developing chronic diseases later in life and reduced productivity with consequent poverty amongst others.

Wasting, a manifestation of acute malnutrition, is characterized by low weight-for-height (WHZ < -2 SD) [9]. Wasting indicates acute undernutrition and is often caused by recent food shortages or acute illnesses [10]. It is a strong predictor of mortality among children under five years of age. Globally, an estimated 45 million children suffer from wasting, with the highest burden found in Asia and Africa [11]. Wasting compromises immunity and results in delayed physical and mental development. In sub–Saharan Africa, wasting affects about 6.2% of children [12]. NDHS [7] reported a wasting prevalence of 7% in Nigeria. It has been reported elsewhere [13] that Nigeria ranks second in the highest prevalence of stunted children globally and has a higher-than-the-world average child wasting prevalence. Staggering rates of malnutrition indices (26.8% to 30.6% stunting, 10.5% to 35.6% wasting and 23.0% to 31.2% underweight) have also been reported in urban areas of Nigeria [8]. A child is considered underweight if his weight-for-height Z score is < -2SD from the median [9] and could be a composite extraction of both stunting and wasting. An underweight prevalence of 39.8% has been reported in Enugu, Southeast Nigeria [14].

Despite the substantial body of research documenting the prevalence of malnutrition among under-five and school-aged children in Nigeria, a critical methodological gap persists: none of these studies have employed CIAF as an analytical framework. This represents a significant limitation as conventional approaches that assess stunting, wasting, and underweight as discrete, mutually exclusive categories are inherently unable to capture the coexistence of multiple forms of anthropometric failure within the same child [4, 15, 16] – a phenomenon increasingly recognized as both common and consequential in undernourished populations. Consequently, the true burden of child malnutrition in Nigeria may be substantially undereported by existing single-indicator approaches. CIAF reveals the magnitude of overlapping anthropometric failure and captures the unduplicated total burden of undernutrition in a population [15]. To build upon the already existing conventional approaches, Mandal and Bose [17] proposed three complimentary indices namely; the stunting index, wasting index, and underweight index – designed to elucidate the relative contribution of each anthropometric dimension to overall anthropometric failure as captured by CIAF. These indices provide deeper analytical insight into the composite burden, yet they remain conspicuously absent from the Nigerian Child malnutrition literature.

Given the persistent high burden of undernutrition in Nigeria, there is a compelling need to move beyond conventional indicator-based assessments toward a more comprehensive analytical approach. This study, therefore, applies the CIAF, together with the three indices described by Mandal and Bose [17], to determine the genuine magnitude and severity of undernutrition and its associated predictors among under-five children in Nsukka, a Nigerian semi-urban community.

Methods

Study design

Cross sectional survey study design was used in this study. The study adopted both descriptive and analytical cross-sectional designs. The descriptive cross sectional survey design was used to illustrate the basic characteristic and to determine the prevalence of undernutrition while analytical cross sectional survey design was used to establish relationships among variables using Chi square.

Study setting

The study was conducted in Nsukka Local Government Area (LGA) of Enugu State, Southeast Nigeria between July and September 2025. Nsukka LGA is one of the urban and rural mix LGAs in Enugu North Senatorial District. It has numerous communities with populations engaged primarily in farming, petty trading, and civil service. The area has several primary health care facilities, nursery and primary schools, both private and public, which serve children under five years of age.

Study participants

A single population formula (4P (1 – P)/W2) (Cochran’s) was used to obtain the sample size for the study. We used the stunting prevalence of 21.4% reported among preschool children in Enugu urban [18] at 95% confidence interval. The minimum sample size obtained was multiplied by a design effect of 1.7 and 10% non-response rate was added to obtain a total of 502 children. These children were drawn from different nursery and primary schools using multi-stage sampling technique. Stage 1, out of the 24 registered primary schools with early childcare centers in Nsukka urban, 8 most densely populated schools were purposively selected. Stage 2, proportionate sampling was used to select the number of children in each school based on the school population. Stage 3, class register was used to ascertain the number of children within the age range for the study and systematic random sampling was used to select the eligible children from each school.

Inclusion criteria included children aged 3 to 5 years, children who were apparently healthy at the time of the study, children who agreed to have their anthropometric measurements taken, and those children whose parent(s) gave their consent to participate in the study. Children who had prominent bowlegs were excluded from the study because the condition may have altered the actual height of those affected.

Data collection methods

Questionnaire

A set of relevant questions derived from the literature and practical experience were compiled together in a questionnaire used for the study. The questionnaire was validated, and it had a reliability index of 0.87. This was used to obtain information about the children’s sociodemographic characteristics, dietary habit and environmental and health factors associated with malnutrition. Interviewer administered method of questionnaire administration were used for the parents who were not literate enough to fill the questionnaire independently.

Anthropometry

The heights of the children were measured using a microtoise height meter rule. Each child was asked to remove shoes, caps, or head coverings. The child was made to stand upright against the stadiometer, heels together, arms at sides, legs straight, shoulders level, and looking straight ahead. The back of the head, buttocks, and heels were made to touch the meter rule. The headpiece was gently lowered to rest on the top of the child’s head and the reading was taken and recorded to the nearest 0.1 cm. The weight was measured with a Hanson bathroom scale with a capacity of 120 kg. Each child was weighed without shoes and in light clothing. Each child was asked to stand in the center of the scale platform, looking straight ahead and standing still. The weight was recorded to the nearest 0.1 kg. The weighing scale was constantly adjusted to zero after each measurement to ensure accuracy.

The birth dates of the children were obtained from their parents/class register, and these were used to calculate their ages in months. The weight and height values were related to their ages and compared to WHO [9] child growth standards for height-for-age, weight-for-age, and weight-for-height. A child is considered stunted, wasted, or underweight if the z scores for height-for-age, weight-for-age, and weight-for-height was less than minus two standard deviations below the WHO median of standards for age and sex. Both the conventional anthropometric indices (stunting, wasting and underweight) and composite index of anthropometric failure (CIAF) (A = no failure, B = wasting only, C = wasting and underweight, D = wasting, stunting and underweight, E = stunting and underweight, F = stunting only, Y = underweight only) were used to assess undernutrition among the children. Additionally, the importance of each of the conventional indices in relation to CIAF was calculated using Stunting Index (SI) (stunting/CIAF), Wasting Index (WI) (wasting/CIAF) and Underweight Index (UI) (underweight/CIAF).

To checkmate intra-observer variability, each trained assistant took two measurements for each child, and the average of the two values was used for the analysis.

Statistical analysis

Data were collected, cleaned, and analyzed using IBM SPSS statistics, version 21.0. Chi square test was carried out to compare the basic characteristics and prevalence of malnutrition among the children according to sex. Results were presented as frequencies and percentages. Bivariate and multivariate logistic regression was used to ascertain the relationship among variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Background characteristics of the participants

Out of the 502 questionnaires shared to participants’ parents, 10 (2%) were incompletely filled and hence were excluded as the analyses were done using complete case analysis. Therefore, the response rate for this study was 98% (492). The basic characteristics of the children are presented in Table 1. The study recorded more female (54.7%) than male (45.3%) participants. A good number (56.3%) of the mothers had only secondary education or less. Majority (89.8%) of the mothers were working and a little above half (55.5%) earned up to ₦50,000:00 and above. More than half (53.2%) of the children were not exclusively breastfed. Majority (97.6%) of the children had access to treated drinking water and 18.3% of the children had been reportedly sick within the past 3 months prior to this study.

Background characteristics of the children according to sex

Prevalence of malnutrition among the preschool children

The prevalence of malnutrition using both the conventional method and CIAF is presented in Table 2. Judging from the conventional anthropometric indices, the number of stunted children were comparable (p > 0.05) between males (25.1%) and females (27.1%). More females (9.3%) than males (5.8%) were wasted. High prevalence of overweight/obesity (25.4%) was observed in this study. From the CIAF standard, more females (35.3%) than males (28.3%) have one or more anthropometric failure. Wasting was significantly (p < 0.05) higher in females (9.3%) than in males (5.8%). Coexistence of stunting and underweight were found more in females (5.9%) than in males (4.9%) but this is not significant (p > 0.05). A slight male preponderance (2.7%) was observed in the manifestation of the three forms of undernutrition (p > 0.05). Stunting index had the highest value (0.82), followed by underweight index (0.38) and wasting index (0.24).

Predictors of undernutrition among the children using the conventional method

Several factors were associated with stunting in this study (Table 3). The result of the bivariate and multivariate analysis showed that children born to mothers without tertiary education (AOR = 2.05, 95% CI 1.34 to 3.14, p = 0.001), children whose mothers earned less than ₦50,000:00 monthly (AOR = 2.51, 95% CI 1.66 to 3.80, p = 0.000), those that used pit toilet / open defecation (AOR = 2.07, 95% CI 1.38 to 3.12, p = 0.001) those who were reportedly sick three months prior to this study (AOR = 2.39, 95% CI 1.46 to 3.90, p = 0.001) had their risks of being stunted significantly increased. Discussion

Prevalence of malnutrition among the preschool children

The overall prevalence of stunting recorded in this study was 26.1% with females being slightly more stunted than the males. This value is lower than the national prevalence of stunting (36.1) in Nigeria reported elsewhere [19]. Another researcher [20] reported a stunting prevalence of 37.5% among preschoolers in Enugu state. However, a lower prevalence of stunting was reported in Anambra state [21]. In Ekholuenetaleet et al. [19], the study consisted of all the children in Nigeria including those in Northwest where undernutrition was terribly high, and this could have resulted to the high prevalence they observed. Didia et al. [21] sampled children from both urban and rural early childcare centers and this could be responsible for the higher prevalence recorded in their study. This aligns with other findings that prevalence of undernutrition is higher in rural areas compared to urban areas [18, 22, 23]. The pattern of females being more stunted than males was contrary to the findings of other researchers [18, 24, 25]. The discrepancy could be attributed to the different cultural practices where in some communities, male children were valued more than females. Stunting has been implicated in serious health consequence including poor cognitive development, increased risk of metabolic diseases in adulthood and a pernicious cycle of multigenerational malnutrition [26] since the development of short stature of the girl child in adulthood has been reported to be associated with obstetric complications and poor pregnancy outcomes [27, 28].

The prevalence of underweight found in this study was higher than those reported elsewhere [29] but lower than 17.2% reported earlier [18] in Enugu Urban. In the latter, the study was carried out at the heart of the state capital where things are quite expensive relative to our study setting. The increased cost of food items could predispose some households to food insecurity with a resultant malnutrition among the children. Underweight has been described as an aftermath of previous and recent weight loss or failure to gain weight [30] which is largely due to insufficient food intake. The overall prevalence of wasting in this study was 7.7% with slight female preponderance. This is in line the reports of earlier researchers [25, 31, 32]. Others, however, reported insignificant increase in the prevalence rate of wasting in males than in females [33, 34]. Wasting is a consequence of acute undernutrition and it’s usually caused by recent food shortages or acute illnesses. High prevalence of overweight/obesity found in this study is in tandem with other studies [8, 10, 35]. Childhood and obesity predispose children at greater risk of early onset of non-communicable diseases, and this reduces their overall quality of life.

Predictors of undernutrition according to the conventional approach

Low maternal educational level and monthly income, use of pit toilet/open defecation, and having been sick within three months prior to this study were significant predictors of stunting, underweight and wasting among the study population. That children born to mothers with tertiary education are less likely to experience stunting has been affirmed by earlier researchers [36, 37]. Higher education exposes mothers to greater awareness of improved infant feeding and care practices which have essential roles in children’s overall wellbeing. Women’s financial capability is inversely related to the incidence of all the indicators of undernutrition assessed. It has been reported elsewhere [38] that women are better care takers of children and children from female headed [39] households were better nourished than their counterparts from male headed households of the same income level. Other researchers had earlier affirmed that richer households were less likely to have malnourished children [40–42]. This stresses the need for women empowerment so that they could provide sufficient food for their household especially the children to guarantee optimal growth and development.

Use of pit toilet and practice of open defecation had more than two-fold increase in the odds of being stunted, wasted or underweight. This aligns with other previous reports from Benin [43], Ghana [44] and Ethiopia [45]. However, other authors [46] recorded insignificant associations between toilet facility used and undernutrition. Open defecation exposes the children to infection which further depletes their body nutrient stores thereby increasing their risks of being malnourished. Recent illness was found to be a significant predictor of undernutrition in this study. Obasohan et al. [47] stated that children who had diarrhoea two weeks before their survey were more likely to be poorly nourished than children who did not have the illnesses. These reports were supported by previous studies [48, 49].

Furthermore, female gender, children below the age of four, working mothers and low dietary diversity score significantly increased the odds of incidence of underweight among the studied children. Earlier researchers [50, 51] reported that boys had better anthropometric status than girls both in terms of stunting and underweight noting that some communities value their male children than the females. This culture is obtainable in this study setting. This results to undue privileges being given to the male children in the form of having a bigger share of meals/meats relative to their female counterparts. Children who were between the ages of 48 to 60 months had their odds of being underweight reduced by 70% compared to the children below the age of 48 months. This is similar to previous report [49] which stated that severe stunting was more amongst 24 to 35 months old than any other group in their study participants. However, the finding is contrary to the reports of other researchers [52, 3]. Possible reason for our finding could be that children of the latter age range have not fully transitioned to the family diet and sometimes, their complementary foods are inadequate and at this age, majority of them have been weaned off from breastfeeding. Older children (4 years and above) are capable of independent feeding and so does not solely depend on the mothers who may be engaged in other activities to keep the family going for feeding. Low dietary diversity score significantly increased the likelihood of underweight among the study population, a finding in agreement with the reports of previous authors [53–55]. Dietary diversity is a significant determinant of diet quality and micronutrient density in children [56]. Children from low-income household are at greater risk of low dietary diversity as their caregivers provide what they can afford and not the children need thereby leading to insufficient intake of the necessary nutrients needed for growth and development.

Surprisingly, children born to working mothers had more likelihood of developing all the three forms of undernutrition, though the association was only significant for underweight. Obasohan et al. 2024 stated that children of working-class mothers have increased odds (AOR = 1.14, 95% CI 1.10–1.29) of being poorly nourished compared to children whose mothers do not work. The reason for this could be that working mother breastfeed their babies for shorter duration to resume their office duties at the appropriate time [57] and these babies were left at the mercy of day care centers, babysitters/nannies who may not know much about the importance of adequate nutrition in infancy [58, 59] thereby increasing their chances of being undernourished. Another possible explanation is that maternal employment limits the time available for optimal childcare practices, such as exclusive breastfeeding and appropriate complementary feeding. In the absence of substitute caregiving, children may experience inadequate feeding and increased exposure to infections which collectively contribute to undernutrition despite anticipated income improvements from the mothers’ employment.

Predictors of CIAF

Significant predictors of CIAF observed in this study include younger age (below 48 months), low maternal educational level and monthly income, having up to three under-five children in the household, low dietary diversity score, use of pit toilet/open defecation, drinking of untreated water and recent illness. High maternal education has consistently been shown to significantly reduce the probability of undernutrition in children, a finding consistent with earlier reports [3, 60–62, 52]. Educated mothers are more knowledgeable, have greater confidence, employment opportunities, and resource control which beget favourable outcome in child feeding practices, health seeking behaviour and overall wellbeing of the children [63, 64].

Increased maternal income reduced the odds of manifesting anthropometric failure in this study. This aligns with previous studies [3, 62, 65]. Although these studies looked at the entire household income/wealth index, it has been noted earlier [39] that children of female headed households fared better nutritionally. Financially empowered women have access to diverse types of foods which provides the children the needed nutrients for optimal growth and development. Wealthy families also have better living conditions and quality health care which facilitates proper growth and reduce undernutrition in children [66]. Use of pit toilet/open defecation as a determinant of anthropometric failure has earlier been discussed [43–45]. Undernutrition due to poor water, sanitation, and hygiene (WaSH) practices has been linked to their role in the incidence of diarrhoea which prevents nutrient absorption.

Unexpectedly, children born into the household with more than 5 persons had 49% reduced risk of manifesting any anthropometric failure than their counterparts. Previous studies had reported contrary findings [67–69]. However, Bras and Mandemakers [50] reported in their findings that children born to larger families were taller and heavier on the average relative to other children born into households with fewer number of persons. Reason for our finding could be that larger family sizes often include grandparents who take pleasure in taking care of their grandchildren including feeding them. This could have resulted in a better nutritional outcome.

Stunting index had the highest value contributing 82% of the total burden of anthropometric failure among the children. This aligns with the findings of earlier researchers around the globe who reported stunting as the highest form of malnutrition in India [70] in Northeast Ethiopia [71] and Uganda [72]. The onset of stunting usually begins unnoticed and often emanates from chronic undernutrition during the critical first 1000 days of life [73] making it difficult to correct. Consequently, greater proportion of the children that experienced any failure at all are exposed to a myriad of nutritional and metabolic abnormalities associated with stunting.

Limitations of the study

The study was done in Nsukka, a semi-urban Nigerian community, and selection was done at the eight registered and mostly populated primary schools with early childcare. Consequently, this is not a true representation of all the semi-urban communities in Nigeria. We also acknowledge that there might be recall bias in 24 h dietary recall by the mothers. Although cross sectional studies do not establish causality, the study highlights the factors associated with undernutrition among the study participants which align with the findings of previous empirical studies.

Conclusion

This study unveiled that the CIAF approach identified more children with undernutrition than the conventional anthropometric indices. CIAF revealed the severity of undernutrition by exposing the children affected by multiple forms of undernutrition. Factors such as low maternal educational level and monthly income, having up to three under-five children in the household, low dietary diversity score, use of pit toilet/open defecation, drinking of untreated water and recent illness have been identified as strong predictors of CIAF among the study participants. Governments are advised to integrate the CIAF as a routine screening instrument at both primary healthcare facilities and community-level nutrition surveillance programmes, given its capacity to capture the magnitude and severity of malnutrition within the same child. Furthermore, concerted efforts in the form of nutrition education, women empowerment, improved environmental condition and access to quality health care services are advocated to curb the menace of anthropometric failure in children.
