Phosphomannomutase 1 restrains adipose thermogenic programming via inosine signaling
Authors: Yafen Ye, Rongrong Xu, Tianwen Liu, Yingying Su, Xuhong Lu, Jingjing Sun, Jiarui Zhao, Yan Lu, Ying Yang, Junfeng Han
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesPhosphomannomutase 1 (PMM1) is an evolutionarily conserved metabolic enzyme classically linked to mannose metabolism, but its physiological role in adipose tissue remains unknown. This study aimed to define the function of PMM1 in thermogenic regulation and systemic metabolism.MethodsAn unbiased, integrative transcriptomic analysis of human subcutaneous adipose tissue was performed to relate PMM1 expression to clinical measures. Functional studies in mice and in primary murine and human adipocytes with PMM1 loss- and gain-of-function were conducted to investigate PMM1's role in the thermogenic program, assessed by metabolic phenotyping, RNA sequencing, targeted metabolomics, and signaling assays.ResultsPMM1 expression was inversely associated with obesity-related anthropometric and biochemical measures and was markedly induced by thermogenic stimulation. Adipocyte-specific Pmm1 knockdown promoted adipose thermogenic remodeling, increased energy expenditure, and protected mice from diet-induced obesity and insulin resistance. Mechanistically, PMM1 deficiency rerouted glucose metabolism into the pentose phosphate pathway, increasing inosine monophosphate and extracellular inosine. The elevated inosine engaged adenosine A2A/A2B receptors, activated the PKA–CREB signaling cascade, and enhanced a thermogenic program in both murine and human adipocytes. Pharmacologic blockade of A2A/A2B receptors or PKA abrogated these effects, confirming the requirement of inosine signaling. In contrast, PMM1 overexpression in primary adipocytes blunted thermogenic activation.ConclusionsPMM1 functions as a key metabolic brake on the adipose thermogenic program by limiting inosine production and downstream A2A/A2B–PKA signaling. These findings reveal a previously unrecognized PMM1–inosine regulatory pathway linking purine metabolism to energy homeostasis and identify PMM1 as a potential therapeutic target for obesity and related metabolic disorders.

Introduction

Obesity is a major public health crisis underlying metabolic disorders such as type 2 diabetes, and it arises from chronic energy surplus stored in white adipose tissue (WAT) [1,2]. By contrast, thermogenic fat, brown and beige adipocytes, dissipate excess energy as heat via Uncoupling Protein 1 (UCP1) [3,4]. In adults, metabolically active thermogenic fat depots can still be detected, and individuals in whom these depots are more active tend to have lower adiposity and a healthier metabolic profile [[5], [6], [7], [8]]. Consequently, activating thermogenic fat to increase energy expenditure is a promising anti-obesity strategy [4,9]. However, obesity impairs beige fat recruitment and function, underscoring the need for interventions that can restore thermogenic capacity in adipose tissue [10].

Thermogenic activation triggers broad metabolic reprogramming in adipose tissue, increasing flux through glycolysis, the pentose phosphate pathway (PPP), and the TCA cycle [[11], [12], [13], [14]]. Proteomic analyses confirm coordinated changes in the abundance of metabolic enzymes [15], and perturbing several of these pathways can markedly influence thermogenesis [16,17]. However, the roles of many metabolic enzymes in adipose thermogenesis remain poorly defined. Phosphomannomutase 1 (PMM1) catalyzes the interconversion of mannose-6-phosphate and mannose-1-phosphate in vitro [18,19]; however, in vivo this reaction is predominantly catalyzed by its paralog PMM2 to produce GDP-mannose for N-glycosylation [20]. Consistently, mutations in PMM2 cause congenital glycosylation disorders, which PMM1 cannot compensate for [21,22]. Moreover, Pmm1-deficient mice are phenotypically normal under standard laboratory conditions [22]. Notably, PMM1 also exhibits an inosine monophosphate (IMP)-dependent glucose-1,6-bisphosphatase activity that is thought to provide an alternative route into glycolysis and may transiently rescue glycolytic flux under stress conditions, such as brain ischemia [23,24]. This non-canonical function suggests that PMM1 may serve as a regulator of central carbon metabolism beyond its canonical role in mannose pathways. However, PMM1's expression pattern and physiological role in adipose tissue have not been systematically examined, and whether it contributes to thermogenic adaptation remains unknown.

Glucose metabolism can promote purine metabolism by supplying ribose precursors required for nucleotide synthesis [25]. Purine metabolites serve not only as biosynthetic building blocks but also as signaling molecules that bind to receptors and modulate cellular functions [26]. Inosine is a purine nucleoside generated by adenosine deamination or by 5′-nucleotidase-mediated dephosphorylation of IMP. Although long considered relatively inert, inosine is now recognized as bioactive, capable of modulating cellular metabolism, and stress adaptation [27,28]. In adipose tissue, inosine activates A2A and A2B adenosine receptors, elevating cAMP–PKA signaling and promoting thermogenesis. Furthermore, blocking inosine uptake by knocking down ENT1 increases extracellular inosine, thereby enhancing thermogenesis [29]. Despite this emerging role of inosine as a thermogenic signal, the upstream metabolic determinants that constrain inosine availability in adipocytes remain poorly defined. In particular, it is unclear whether metabolic enzymes outside canonical purine biosynthetic pathways, including PMM1, can indirectly regulate inosine-dependent signaling by shaping intracellular metabolic states.

This study identifies PMM1 as a previously unrecognized regulator of adipose thermogenic remodeling. We first performed an unbiased analysis of human subcutaneous adipose tissue and found that adipose PMM1 expression inversely correlates with adiposity and metabolic risk in humans. Functional experiments demonstrated that Pmm1 is enriched in mature adipocytes and acts as a negative regulator of the thermogenic program. Mechanistically, knockdown of Pmm1 results in a metabolic shift that elevates extracellular inosine levels, thereby activating A2A/A2B receptor-mediated PKA–CREB pathway and upregulating thermogenic gene expression in mouse and human adipocytes. Collectively, our findings reveal a novel physiological role for PMM1 beyond glycosylation, linking purine metabolism to the regulation of adipose thermogenic programming.

Materials and methods

Data acquisition and analysis

The GSE70353, GSE129153, GSE92405, and GSE199063 datasets were downloaded from the Gene Expression Omnibus (GEO; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/geo/). GSE70353 was processed in R for probe annotation and data cleaning. To mitigate low-expression noise, the 5,000 genes with the highest median absolute deviation were selected for weighted gene co-expression network analysis (WGCNA) using the “WGCNA” R package with clinical phenotypes. Based on BMI, samples were stratified into obese (BMI ≥30) and non-obese (BMI <30) and encoded as 1 or 0. The Boruta algorithm identified 50 “confirmed” candidate genes within the significant co-expression modules. Importance scores were visualized with the “ggplot2” R package.

Human subjects

Eighteen adult subjects were recruited between January and December 2015, including 7 obese subjects (BMI 34–56 kg/m2) who underwent bariatric surgery at Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital and 11 normal-weight subjects (BMI 18.5–24.9 kg/m2) who underwent laparoscopic cholecystectomy at Shanghai Seventh People's Hospital. Subjects with diabetes, liver dysfunction, coronary heart disease, kidney disease, cancer, or other chronic diseases were excluded. Biopsies of abdominal SAT were collected from the incision site near the umbilicus. Biopsies were immediately rinsed with saline to remove blood, snap-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C. Frozen samples were then sent to Shanghai Biotechnology Corporation for RNA extraction and sequencing. The study was approved by the ethics committees of Shanghai Sixth People's Hospital affiliated to Shanghai Jiao Tong University School of Medicine and Shanghai Seventh People's Hospital. All subjects provided written informed consent.

snRNA-seq data analysis

Single-nucleus RNA-seq (snRNA-seq) datasets E-MTAB-8562 and E-MTAB-8564 were obtained from ArrayExpress. The “Seurat” and “Harmony” R packages were used for data processing and batch correction. PMM1 expression was visualized with the “scCustomize” and “Nebulosa” R packages. Thermogenesis scores were computed on adipocyte subsets with the “AUCell” R package using “WP_THERMOGENESIS.v2024.1.Hs” gene set and plotted with the “ggplot2” R package.

Animals

All animal procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC). Male C57BL/6J mice were sourced from GemPharmatech Co., Ltd (Nanjing, China) and randomly assigned to experimental groups. Mice were housed in groups of 3–5 per cage under specific pathogen-free (SPF) conditions (22–24 °C, 50–60% humidity, 12-hour light/dark cycle) with free access to food and water. Beginning at 8 weeks of age, a subset of mice was switched from chow to a high-fat diet (HFD; Research Diets, D12492).

Local delivery of AAV

Under anesthesia, 7-week-old mice received multi-site iWAT or BAT injections of recombinant AAV8 vectors (2 × 1010 vg/mouse; GeneChem, China). The vector backbone comprised an Adipoq promoter-EGFP-miR155-WPRE-SV40 polyA cassette expressing either shPmm1 (target: 5′-GGCTCTGACTACTCTAAGA-3′) or a scrambled control (AAV-Con). Mice were analyzed 4 weeks post-injection.

Biodistribution of AAV vectors

Genomic DNA was extracted from mouse tissues using a DNA Isolation Mini Kit (Vazyme, DC102). AAV vector genomes were quantified by TaqMan qPCR (Thermo Fisher Scientific, 4444556) targeting the ITR sequence (Forward: 5′-GGAACCCCTAGTGATGGAGTT-3′; Reverse: 5′-CGGCCTCAGTGAGCGA-3′; Probe: 5′-FAM-CACTCCCTCTCTGCGCGCTCG-BHQ1-3′). Copy numbers were determined using a 10-fold standard curve ranging from 2.38 × 101 to 2.38 × 106 copies per reaction and normalized to gDNA input. The lower limit of quantification (LLOQ) was 23.8 copies/ng gDNA; samples with undetermined Ct values were assigned half the LLOQ for visualization.

Cold exposure

Prior to cold exposure, mice were acclimated at thermoneutrality (30 °C) for 14 d and single-housed with free access to food and water. For mice receiving iWAT injection, animals were then exposed to 4 °C for 72 h. Core temperature was measured rectally at baseline, hourly for the first 6 h, and again at 72 h. For mice receiving BAT injection, animals were exposed to 4 °C for 4 h, and rectal temperature was measured at baseline and hourly thereafter. Surface temperature was monitored at the indicated time points by infrared thermography using a thermal imaging camera (HIKMICRO).

Metabolic cage

Energy expenditure (RER, VCO2, VO2, Heat) and locomotor activity were measured using a high-definition multiplexed respirometry system (Sable Systems, USA). Mice were acclimated in metabolic cages for 24 h. Chow-fed mice were then recorded for 24 h. HFD-fed mice were first recorded for 24 h to obtain basal measurements, received an intraperitoneal injection of CL-316,243 (1 mg/kg, Sigma C5976) at 24 h, and were monitored for an additional 24 h.

Glucose tolerance and insulin tolerance test

For glucose tolerance test (GTT), mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for 10 weeks and fasted 12–16 h were injected intraperitoneally with glucose (1 g/kg). For insulin tolerance test (ITT), mice fed a high-fat diet (HFD) for 11 weeks and fasted for 6–8 h were injected with insulin (1.5 U/kg). Blood glucose was measured from the tail vein at 0, 15, 30, 60, 90, and 120 min post injection. To avoid order effects, blood sampling was alternated between AAV-Con and AAV-shPmm1 mice at each time point.

Histological, immunohistochemistry, and fluorescence imaging

Adipose and liver tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (Servicebio, G1101), paraffin-embedded, sectioned, and stained with H&E using standard procedures. For immunohistochemistry, tissue sections were blocked and incubated with rabbit anti-UCP1 (Abcam, ab10983, 1:200) or anti-tyrosine hydroxylase (TH) antibody (Sigma, AB152, 1:1000), followed by HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody. For fluorescence imaging, fresh tissues were cryosectioned to visualize EGFP fluorescence and subsequently counterstained with DAPI. Slides were scanned using a PANNORAMIC digital slide scanner (2DHISTECH). Three non-overlapping fields were analyzed per mouse using Fiji software (ImageJ), and values were averaged per mouse (n = 3 mice per group).

Cell culture

Stromal vascular fractions (SVFs) were isolated from iWAT or BAT of 6–8-week-old C57BL/6J mice as previously described [30]. For cell differentiation, SVFs were cultured to contact inhibition, then induced for 2 days with induction medium (DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 0.5 mM IBMX, 1 μM dexamethasone, 5 μM rosiglitazone, 1 μM insulin, and 50 nM T3), then maintained for 4–6 days in DMEM containing 10% FBS, 5 μM rosiglitazone, 50 nM T3, and 1 μM insulin. For human primary adipocytes, SVFs were obtained from SAT of patients undergoing suction-assisted lipectomy and isolated as previously described [31]. To differentiate SVFs into beige adipocytes, cells were induced for 4 days with an induction medium composed of 1 μM insulin, 0.5 mM IBMX, 1 μM dexamethasone, 10 μM rosiglitazone, and 200 nM T3, then maintained until day 21 in a maintenance medium containing 1 μM insulin, 10 μM rosiglitazone, and 200 nM T3. Medium was changed every other day. Where indicated, adipocytes were treated with 50 μM inosine, 10 μM H89, 1 μM ZM241385, or 150 nM PSB603, control cells received vehicle.

Virus infection and siRNA transfection

Lentiviruses were produced in HEK293T cells by co-transfection with psPAX2, pMD2.G, and pLKO.1-puro (control) or pLKO.1-shPmm1, following standard procedures [29]. Recombinant adenoviruses were generated by GeneChem. Mouse primary adipocytes were infected with viruses on day 3 of differentiation. Human primary adipocytes (day 19 of differentiation) were transfected with siRNAs against PMM1 (GenePharma) using Lipofectamine RNAiMAX reagent (Invitrogen, 13778150).

Oxygen consumption rate measurement

Mitochondrial respiration was measured in differentiated adipocytes using a Seahorse XF96 analyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA) following the manufacturer's instructions. Oligomycin (2 μM), FCCP (1.5 μM), and rotenone/antimycin A (1 μM each) (Agilent, 103015-100) were injected sequentially. For human adipocytes, oligomycin and FCCP were applied at 5 μM. Oxygen consumption rate (OCR) was normalized to cellular protein per well.

RNA extraction, qRT-PCR, and RNA-seq

Total RNA was extracted with TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, 15596018) and reverse-transcribed with the PrimeScript RT kit (Takara, RR047B). qRT-PCR was performed using SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme, Q511-02). Primer sequences are listed in Table S1. For RNA-seq, libraries were prepared and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (Sino TechGenomics, China). Reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome (GRCm38.102) with Hisat2 and analyzed for differential expression using edgeR (criteria: p < 0.05 and |fold change| > 1.5, FPKM >1 in at least one group). KEGG enrichment was performed on upregulated genes.

Western blotting

Cells and tissues were lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors (Roche, 04693132001, 04906837001). Equal protein amounts were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, blocked with 5% non-fat milk, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against PMM1 (Proteintech, 11252-2-AP), UCP1 (Abcam, ab10983), phospho-PKA substrate (CST, 9621), phospho-CREB (CST, 9198), total CREB (CST, 9197), HSP90 (CST, 4877), and TUBULIN (Sigma, T6199). After incubation with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (CST, 7074; 7076), protein bands were visualized with ECL reagent (Millipore, WBKLS0500) and imaged on the ChemiDoc Touch Imaging System (Bio-Rad).

Mitochondrial DNA quantification and MitoTracker staining

Genomic DNA was extracted with the FastPure Cell DNA Isolation kit (Vazyme, DC102-01). mtDNA content was determined by qRT-PCR as the ratio of mtRNR1 or mtND1 to RBM15. For mitochondrial staining, cells were incubated with MitoTracker (150 nM, 15 min; Invitrogen, M7512) and then imaged using a fluorescence microscope (Nikon, Japan). Mean fluorescence intensity was quantified using Fiji software (ImageJ).

Metabolomics and extracellular inosine (UHPLC/UPLC–MS/MS)

Differentiated adipocytes (∼1 × 106 cells) were quenched on dry ice and extracted on the dish with ice-cold methanol, followed by the addition of water containing stable-isotope internal standards and an acetonitrile rinse. Pooled extracts were centrifuged, dried under nitrogen, and reconstituted in aqueous acetonitrile. Metabolites were separated on an ACQUITY BEH Amide column (2.1 × 100 mm, 1.7 μm) using an Agilent 1290 Infinity II UHPLC and detected on a 6470A triple-quadrupole mass spectrometer operated in negative-mode ESI with MRM. Data were processed in MassHunter with manual peak verification, and absolute/relative quantification was based on external calibration and analyte-to-internal-standard peak-area ratios. Analyses were performed by ProfLeader Biotech (Shanghai, China).

For extracellular inosine, culture supernatants were snap-frozen at −80 °C, thawed on ice, deproteinated with solvent containing isotopically labeled internal standards, and analyzed by LC–MS/MS on an amide HILIC UPLC coupled to a SCIEX Triple Quad 6500 Plus QTRAP in MRM mode. Absolute quantification used external calibration with internal-standard normalization. This assay was performed by Metabo-Profile Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). Pathway enrichment was performed on MetaboAnalyst 6.0 (https://new.metaboanalyst.ca/MetaboAnalyst/).

Statistical analysis

For comparisons between two groups, unpaired two-tailed Student's t-tests were used. Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for paired data. One-way ANOVA with Dunnett's post hoc test was applied for multiple-group comparisons versus one control and two-way ANOVA with Bonferroni correction for two-factor experiments. Metabolic cage data (VO2, VCO2, Heat) were analyzed by ANCOVA with body weight as a covariate. Analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism 10. Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was defined as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, and ∗∗∗p < 0.001. ns, not significant.

Results

An unbiased screen identifies and validates PMM1 as a novel regulator of human obesity and adipose tissue thermogenesis

To identify novel regulators of human obesity, we performed WGCNA on a public SAT transcriptomic dataset with extensive clinical phenotyping [32]. This identified two gene modules significantly correlated with adiposity (BMI, waist-to-hip ratio, waist circumference), the inflammatory marker IL-1ra, insulin resistance (HOMA-IR, fasting insulin), and dyslipidemia (Figure 1A). The Boruta algorithm was used to identify key genes within these modules, yielding 50 “confirmed” candidates (Figure 1B). To prioritize genes with potential adipocyte-intrinsic roles, we intersected these candidates with differentially expressed genes (DEGs) from primary human adipocytes following forskolin (FSK) treatment, yielding 13 final genes (Figure 1C). Among these, PMM1, an enzyme not previously well characterized in adipocytes, emerged as a notable candidate. We then validated PMM1 in our RNA-sequencing dataset and observed a significant negative correlation between PMM1 expression in SAT and BMI (Figure 1D). This finding was confirmed in the public dataset, where PMM1 negatively correlated with adiposity and insulin resistance metrics (Figure 1E), and further validated in two additional independent cohorts. In monozygotic twin pairs discordant for obesity, the obese twin consistently exhibited lower PMM1 expression (Figure 1F). Furthermore, in a longitudinal cohort, PMM1 expression in obese individuals was restored to lean-control levels following bariatric surgery (Figure 1G). In primary human adipocytes, PMM1 expression was also reduced by TNF-α (Figure 1H), an inflammatory cytokine elevated in obesity. To further explore the potential role of PMM1 at both the physiological tissue level and adipocyte-autonomous level, we performed pre-ranked Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) on RNA-seq datasets from whole SAT and isolated mature adipocytes, respectively. In both datasets, ranking genes by their correlation with PMM1 showed positive enrichment of thermogenic and metabolic pathways, including oxidative phosphorylation, thermogenesis, the TCA cycle, and carbon metabolism (Figure 1I). Consistently, PMM1 expression positively correlated with thermogenic markers CIDEA, ELOVL3, and TMEM26 (Figure 1J–L), linking it to the adipocyte thermogenic program. To further evaluate PMM1 in adipocyte thermogenesis, we analyzed a snRNA-seq dataset of human deep-neck supraclavicular BAT [33], a thermogenic adipose depot. PMM1 was predominantly enriched in mature adipocytes marked by ADIPOQ, PLIN1, and CIDEA, particularly those with higher thermogenic scores (Fig. S2A–H).

Pmm1 expression is adaptively regulated in adipose tissue under HFD and thermogenic conditions

To characterize the expression pattern and adaptive regulation of Pmm1 in mice, we profiled its expression across major metabolic tissues, finding it most abundant in BAT (Fig. S3A and B). We then focused on BAT and iWAT, the two principal murine thermogenic adipose depots, and fractionated them into stromal vascular fraction (SVF) and mature adipocyte fraction (MAF). Pmm1 expression was markedly enriched in the MAF of both depots (Figure 1M and Fig. S3C). HFD feeding reduced Pmm1 mRNA and protein in both iWAT and BAT (Figure 1N, O and Fig. S3D and E), and isolated adipocytes from the iWAT of HFD-fed mice showed a progressive decline in Pmm1 expression over time (Figure 1P). In contrast, thermogenic activation via β3-adrenergic stimulation (CL-316,243) or cold exposure induced Pmm1 expression in parallel with Ucp1 upregulation (Figure 1Q–S and Fig. S3F–H). In iWAT, cold exposure elevated Pmm1 mRNA as early as 24 h and by more than 10-fold after 7 days, which was also confirmed at the protein level (Figure 1R,S). BAT exhibited a similar but modest induction of Pmm1 (Fig. S3F–H), a trend further supported by a public snRNA-seq dataset [33] showing elevated expression in mature brown adipocytes during cold exposure and a reduction at thermoneutrality (Fig. S3I). Collectively, these results establish Pmm1 as a metabolic regulator coordinating adipose adaptations to nutrient excess and thermogenic stimulation.

Adipocyte-specific Pmm1 knockdown in iWAT promotes browning and increases energy expenditure

To investigate the in vivo function of Pmm1, we knocked down Pmm1 specifically in iWAT using an adipocyte-targeted AAV (Figure 2A and Fig. S4A). Biodistribution analysis confirmed marked vector enrichment in the injected iWAT, with limited dissemination to BAT, eWAT, and liver (Fig. S4B). Despite the leakage, EGFP signal and Pmm1 knockdown remained restricted to iWAT (Fig. S4C–E), and MAF-specific Pmm1 knockdown supported adipocyte-selective targeting (Figure 2B,C). This knockdown did not affect body weight (Fig. S5A), food intake (Fig. S5B) or hypothalamic appetite-regulating gene expression (Fig. S5C). Metabolic cage analysis at room temperature showed similar physical activity and RER (Fig. S5D–F) between groups. Nevertheless, AAV-shPmm1 mice exhibited significantly increased VCO2 and VO2 (Figure 2D,E), with a trend towards increased heat production (Figure 2F). Gene and protein expression analysis confirmed effective Pmm1 knockdown in iWAT without affecting the adipocyte differentiation marker Fabp4, while thermogenic genes Ucp1, Cidea, Cox8b, and Cox7a1 were significantly upregulated, and UCP1 protein was also markedly increased (Figure 2G,H). In addition, Pmm2 expression remained unchanged, indicating no compensatory response (Figure 2G). Pmm1 knockdown did not alter relative fat-pad or liver weights (Fig. S5G). Gross appearance and histology of iWAT, BAT, eWAT, and liver were examined (Fig. S5H and I). In iWAT, H&E staining showed smaller adipocytes with a leftward shift in adipocyte area distribution, and UCP1 immunohistochemistry showed an increased UCP1-positive area in AAV-shPmm1 mice (Figure 2I). To evaluate cold adaptation, another cohort was acclimated to thermoneutrality for 14 d and then exposed to 4 °C. During the first 6 h of cold exposure, AAV-shPmm1 mice maintained higher core temperature than controls (Figure 2J), and this difference persisted at 72 h (Figure 2K), consistent with sustained iWAT browning. Despite improved cold tolerance, body and tissue weights remained comparable between groups (Fig. S5J and K). Gross appearance and histology of iWAT, BAT, eWAT, and liver were further evaluated (Fig. S5L–N). Histologically, cold-challenged iWAT from AAV-shPmm1 mice displayed enhanced browning, characterized by more multilocular adipocytes, a decreased lipid area fraction, and an increased UCP1-positive area (Figure 2L), while the TH-positive area was comparable (Fig. S5M). In contrast, BAT, eWAT, and the liver remained unchanged (Fig. S5N). Consistently, iWAT from AAV-shPmm1 mice showed no change in Adipoq or Fabp4, but robust induction of the thermogenic genes Ucp1, Cidea, Elovl3, and Pgc-1α, along with further UCP1 protein accumulation (Figure 2M,N). We also generated a BAT-localized, adipocyte-specific Pmm1 knockdown mouse by AAV delivery (Fig. S6A and B). While Pmm1 knockdown in BAT modestly elevated interscapular surface temperature and thermogenic markers at room temperature (Fig. S6C–H), no significant differences were observed following thermoneutral acclimation and acute cold challenge (Fig. S6I–N). Taken together, these results establish Pmm1 as a negative regulator of the adipose thermogenic program, particularly in iWAT browning.

Adipocyte-specific Pmm1 knockdown in iWAT is sufficient to protect against diet-induced obesity and metabolic dysfunction

To evaluate whether the metabolic benefits of Pmm1 knockdown protect against diet-induced obesity, we injected AAV-shPmm1 or control into iWAT of 7-week-old mice. During 12 weeks of HFD, shPmm1 mice gained body weight more slowly and remained significantly leaner than controls despite comparable food intake (Figure 3A,B). Consistently, the mRNA expression of key hypothalamic appetite-regulating genes, including Pomc, Agrp, Npy, and Mc4r, showed no significant differences (Figure 3C). Indirect calorimetry under HFD conditions showed no differences in physical activity or RER between groups (Figure 3D–F), consistent with observations under CD conditions. Notably, VO2, VCO2, and Heat were moderately higher at baseline in shPmm1 mice and further increased after CL-316,243 stimulation, all of which were independent of body weight (Figure 3G–L).

Glucose tolerance and insulin sensitivity were also improved in shPmm1 mice, with lower blood glucose levels and reduced curve area (AUC) during GTT and ITT (Figure 3M−P). Body composition showed reduced relative iWAT mass, while BAT, eWAT, and liver weights were unchanged (Figure 3Q). Gross examination revealed smaller iWAT depots and a more reddish liver in the knockdown group (Figure 3R). Histological analysis revealed smaller adipocytes in iWAT, evidenced by a leftward shift in the adipocyte area distribution, with decreased lipid accumulation in the liver (Figure 3S). These findings demonstrate that iWAT-specific knockdown of Pmm1 protects against HFD-induced obesity and improves metabolic homeostasis.

Seahorse analysis further showed that Pmm1-knockdown beige adipocytes exhibited a significantly elevated maximal respiration capacity and proton leak, with no change in basal respiration (Figure 4C,D). RNA-seq revealed upregulation of mitochondria-related genes (Figure 4E), and immunoblotting confirmed elevated electron transport chain proteins ATP5A, UQCRC2, and MT–CO1 (Figure 4F). To further assess the metabolic shift, we conducted targeted LC-MS/MS. As Pmm1 acts upstream in GDP-mannose biosynthesis, we first measured GDP-mannose, a key substrate for mannosylation. Neither GDP-mannose abundance nor the expression of N-linked glycosylation genes changed (Fig. S8C and D), arguing against altered glycosylation as the cause of thermogenesis. Instead, broader metabolite profiling revealed 13 significantly altered metabolites among 34 detected, indicating non-canonical metabolic rewiring with enrichment pointing to TCA cycle and PPP (Figure 5C,D). Notably, ribose-5-phosphate (R5P), a PPP-derived purine precursor, was significantly increased, and IMP, the first branch–point metabolite in de novo purine synthesis, was also elevated (Figure 5E,F). However, the levels of its canonical downstream products, AMP and GMP, remained unchanged (Figure 5G, H). In parallel, secreted inosine increased by over 10-fold (Figure 5I), which can be generated from IMP hydrolysis. These findings suggest that Pmm1 knockdown increases IMP that is subsequently diverted toward inosine production.

Extracellular inosine has been shown to promote thermogenesis via A2A/A2B–PKA signaling in adipocytes [29]. Since Pmm1 knockdown elevated inosine secretion, we examined whether inosine could recapitulate the thermogenic response in our beige adipocyte model. Exogenous inosine treatment upregulated thermogenic genes such as Ucp1, Cidea, Cox8b, Pgc-1α, Elovl3 (Figure 5J) and enhanced p-PKA substrates and p-CREB, increasing UCP1 protein (Figure 5K).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that Pmm1 deficiency reprograms beige adipocytes toward increased inosine production and secretion, thereby linking purine metabolism to thermogenic activation.

Discussion

In this study, we identified Pmm1 as a critical regulator of adipose tissue thermogenic programming and energy homeostasis. Pmm1 expression is dynamically regulated by metabolic state, decreasing in obesity but increasing with thermogenic stimulation, and exhibits an inverse correlation with adverse metabolic parameters. Notably, adipocyte-specific Pmm1 knockdown enhanced the thermogenic program, particularly iWAT browning, and energy expenditure, protecting mice against diet-induced obesity and metabolic dysfunction. Mechanistically, Pmm1 deficiency reprogrammed adipocyte metabolism to elevate extracellular inosine, which engaged adenosine A2A/A2B receptors to activate the PKA–CREB signaling cascade, driving thermogenic remodeling in both murine and human adipocytes. Collectively, our study uncovers a previously unappreciated role for Pmm1 beyond its canonical glycosylation function, linking it to the regulation of adipose thermogenic regulation via inosine signaling.

While BMI is a primary metric for defining obesity, mounting evidence indicates that BMI alone does not adequately reflect obesity-related metabolic risk. Accordingly, comprehensive assessments employ additional measures such as waist circumference and waist-to-hip ratio, alongside biochemical markers like triglycerides [[34], [35], [36]]. We combined WGCNA and the Boruta algorithm [37,38] to identify genes in human SAT whose expression correlates with diverse anthropometric and biochemical parameters. WGCNA revealed two gene modules strongly associated with the obesity-related measures, and the Boruta further refined these to 50 “confirmed” candidate genes. To identify adipocyte-intrinsic regulators, we intersected these 50 genes with DEGs from primary human adipocytes stimulated with FSK. FSK activates the cAMP–PKA signaling pathway [39], enabling us to examine cell-autonomous transcriptional responses under defined stimulation. This approach yielded a set of candidate genes with known or putative roles in adipocyte biology, among which were previously implicated in thermogenic regulation, such as Cidea and Slit2-C. Cidea-deficient mice display elevated core body temperature and metabolic rate upon cold exposure and are resistant to diet-induced obesity [40]. Likewise, the Slit2-C activates PKA signaling in adipocytes, promoting thermogenesis and improving glucose homeostasis [41]. The presence of these known factors in our results supports the credibility of our approach. Importantly, the screen also uncovered novel candidates, most prominently Pmm1, a metabolic enzyme not previously implicated in adipose thermogenesis.

Global Pmm1-knockout mice are viable and exhibit no overt phenotype based on developmental, histological, and neurological assessments [22]. Therefore, the function of Pmm1 in specific tissues or metabolic processes has remained unclear. Our findings uncover a distinct role of Pmm1 in adipose thermogenic program regulation. Pmm1 knockdown in adipocytes significantly enhanced thermogenic gene expression and mitochondrial respiration. In vivo, iWAT-specific Pmm1 knockdown elevated energy expenditure even at ambient temperature. Pmm1 expression is markedly induced by thermogenic stimulation, which we interpret as a compensatory “brake”, a notion further supported by its delayed increase relative to Ucp1 during ISO stimulation. This feedback inhibition model resembles that of other thermogenic suppressors such as PWWP2B and a cold-induced truncated AC3, such thermogenic rheostats are thought to prevent excessive energy dissipation and stabilize systemic energy balance [42,43]. In this light, the reduced PMM1 expression observed in obesity is a correlative observation and may reflect adaptive remodeling in metabolically stressed adipose tissue. Notably, we observed no compensatory upregulation in Pmm2 in Pmm1-deficient adipocytes, and Pmm2 knockdown did not affect thermogenic gene expression or mitochondrial respiration, suggesting that Pmm1 exerts a specific, non-redundant role in thermogenic regulation. This specificity may reflect evolutionary divergence: Pmm2 is more conserved, closely related to yeast homolog Sec53, whereas Pmm1 has evolved more rapidly, possibly acquiring a unique metabolic function [21,44].

Extending our observations of Pmm1's specific role, we investigated how its loss promotes the thermogenic program. Indeed, transcriptomic profiling of Pmm1-deficient adipocytes revealed the enrichment of “AMPK signaling”, which we subsequently confirmed to be dispensable for this thermogenic response. The AMPK signature may serve as a secondary consequence of intracellular metabolic perturbations rather than a direct mechanistic driver as previously reported [45,46]. This observation, alongside the central carbon metabolism pathway, encompassing glycolysis, the TCA cycle, and the PPP, collectively points to a broader cellular metabolic rewiring. Such rewiring may also be reflected in the enrichment of the glucagon signaling pathway, which includes multiple metabolic nodes involved in carbohydrate metabolism [47,48].

Thermogenic fat activation involves broad metabolic changes, including marked PPP upregulation to generate NADPH via its oxidative branch and support lipogenesis [14]. The non-oxidative PPP has also been linked to thermogenesis, as deletion of transketolase (Tkt), a key enzyme of this branch, causes accumulation of non-oxidative intermediates and increases energy expenditure [49]. Notably, the non-oxidative PPP produces R5P, a precursor for de novo purine nucleotide synthesis [25]. Tracer studies have shown that glucose-derived carbons enter IMP through PPP-derived R5P [50]. However, whether the PPP's contribution to thermogenesis is mediated through purine metabolites remains unknown. In our study, Pmm1 knockdown in beige adipocytes increased sedoheptulose 7-phosphate (S7P) and erythrose 4-phosphate (E4P) and R5P, accompanied by elevated IMP and extracellular inosine, suggesting a shift toward PPP-derived purine synthesis and consequent inosine production. Consistent with this, Tkt knockout led to R5P accumulation and elevated inosine, linking perturbation of the non-oxidative PPP to increased purine output [51]. Although Pmm1 is traditionally associated with mannose metabolism, evidence indicates that compared to Pmm2, it has a broader substrate range and effector sensitivity, including responsiveness to hexose-1,6-bisphosphates [52]. For instance, under transient cerebral ischemia Pmm1 switches to a glucose-1,6-bisphosphatase, converting glucose-1,6-bisphosphate to glucose-6-phosphate and thereby sustaining glycolysis [24]. This tissue-dependent activity suggests that Pmm1 can regulate glucose metabolism and respond to energetic stress in specific tissues. Additionally, given that glucose-1,6-bisphosphate serves as a cofactor for several metabolic enzymes [53], Pmm1, by shaping cellular glucose-1,6-bisphosphate level, might indirectly modulate PPP flux and inosine production. Importantly, Pmm1 knockdown did not alter GDP-mannose or N-glycosylation related gene expression. Consistently, Pmm1 knockout mice and cells overexpressing Pmm1 exhibit no detectable glycosylation alterations [22,54]. This indicates that Pmm1's thermogenic effects are glycosylation-independent, extending its role beyond glycosylation to metabolic regulation via the PPP and purine nucleotide turnover.

Recent studies have shown that nucleosides can serve as both alternative carbon sources and signaling molecules that modulate cellular functions, including thermogenesis [29,55,56]. Adenosine, released upon sympathetic stimulation, activates thermogenic adipocytes via A2A receptor-mediated lipolysis and thermogenic recruitment [55]. Similarly, inosine acts through A2A/A2B–cAMP–PKA signaling to promote browning and energy expenditure [29], while guanosine enhances thermogenesis by activating the KEAP1–NRF2 antioxidant pathway [56]. Consistent with this, we found that the Pmm1-knockdown thermogenic program was not further enhanced by exogenous inosine and was blocked by A2A/A2B antagonism or H89, supporting mediation through inosine–A2A/A2B–PKA signaling. Furthermore, although Pmm1 was induced by isoproterenol, Pmm1 knockdown showed no additive thermogenic effect with isoproterenol and remained effective in β-less adipocytes, suggesting that the thermogenic program triggered by Pmm1 loss does not require sustained canonical β-adrenergic input. Consistently, unchanged TH staining in iWAT argues against overt sympathetic remodeling as a driver of the thermogenic phenotype. Notably, IMP increases during thermogenesis, and pharmacological inhibition of IMP dehydrogenase further augments this accumulation and promotes adipose thermogenesis [57]. However, phosphorylated purine nucleotides such as IMP have poor membrane permeability, so the surplus IMP is likely dephosphorylated and released as inosine, which can accumulate extracellularly and act in an autocrine or paracrine manner [58,59]. In our study, Pmm1 knockdown increased intracellular IMP but produced a greater rise in extracellular inosine. Therefore, inosine, rather than IMP, mediates the thermogenic effects of Pmm1 loss.

In summary, Pmm1 is a key negative regulator of the adipose thermogenic program, acting through a previously unrecognized Pmm1–inosine–A2A/A2B–PKA–CREB signaling pathway that controls thermogenic activation in murine and human adipocytes. These findings broaden the current understanding of purine metabolite-mediated energy regulation and highlight Pmm1 as a potential therapeutic target for obesity and related metabolic disorders. Future studies should investigate the precise mechanisms by which Pmm1 regulates inosine production and explore its roles in other metabolically active tissues.
