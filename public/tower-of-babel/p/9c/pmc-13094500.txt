Multilevel geographic determinants of smoking and smokeless tobacco use among young men in India: evidence for targeted addiction prevention policies

Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Smoking, Smokeless tobacco use, Multilevel analysis, Geographic variation, Variance partitioning, Youth health policy India

Introduction

Tobacco use represents one of the world's leading preventable causes of morbidity and mortality, with particularly devastating consequences in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs). Tobacco use has been identified as the fifth largest risk factor for Years of Life Lost (YLL) and Disability Adjusted Life Years (DALY), globally in 2021 (Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME)., 2024). The burden disproportionately affects LMICs, where nearly 3600 people die daily in India alone due to tobacco-related causes (Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME), 2024).

Studying adolescent tobacco use in LMICs is critically important for several compelling reasons. First, tobacco use is predominantly initiated during adolescence, with most people starting before age 21, making early prevention strategies essential (John et al., 2021). Second, earlier initiation significantly increases mortality risk, beyond numerous health consequences, including cancer, heart disease, and respiratory illnesses (WHO, 2024). Third, adult smoking rates generally reflect adolescent smoking rates, making youth tobacco use an important predictor of future population-level tobacco burden (International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS) and ICF, 2021).

The situation in LMICs is particularly concerning due to lax tobacco control measures, unenforced smoking bans, and widespread access to cheap cigarettes (Cunha et al., 2020). In India, 39.1% of men and 4.1% of women aged 15–49 years reported tobacco use in 2021 (6). While consumption rates are highest among men aged 35 years and above (exceeding 50%), younger populations show troubling patterns, with 14.3% of men aged 15–19 years and 38.6% of those aged 20–34 years using tobacco products (Carrión-Valero et al., 2023).

The economic burden attributed to tobacco consumption falls heavily on men, who account for 91% of tobacco-related economic costs in India (Rodopman Arman, 2023). These costs are distributed between direct (22%) and indirect (78%) expenditures, with smoking contributing 74% and smokeless tobacco 26% of the total burden (Rodopman Arman, 2023). Despite global efforts through the WHO Framework Convention on Tobacco Control, tobacco use persists at alarming rates globally, with significantly higher prevalence among men (35.5%) compared to women (7.9%) (Rodopman Arman, 2023).

Young people typically initiate tobacco use due to curiosity, peer pressure, stress, and targeted marketing strategies (Goel et al., 2024; P. K. Singh et al., 2022). The addictive nature of nicotine creates dependency patterns with profound impacts on neural health (Pouranik et al., 2021, United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2022). Studies have documented high exposure to pro-tobacco advertisements among adolescents in LMICs, with exposure to multiple advertising sources significantly associated with increased odds of cigarette smoking (Naciones Unidas [UN], 2024). Tobacco companies strategically target youth through attractive packaging, flavouring, aggressive marketing, celebrity endorsements, and product placement (Charan Konanki and Dasaratharamaiah, 2020, Chirisa et al., 2022, United Nations Population Fund., 2023).

Despite the importance of adolescent tobacco use in LMICs, research about the association between community-level compliance with tobacco control policies and youth tobacco use is largely unavailable but critically needed to inform policy enforcement and development (Giovenco et al., 2021). While prior studies in LMICs have explored adolescent tobacco use, most have either focused on individual-level factors or single-level geographical units (Pouranik et al., 2021, United Nations Department of Economic and Social Affairs, 2022). Few, if any, have systematically partitioned variance across multiple administrative levels—and none have contrasted smoked versus smokeless tobacco to inform product-specific control strategies. Our study addresses this critical gap.

This multi-level variation is especially relevant in the context of governmental policies and programs implemented at various administrative levels (states, districts, PSUs), which interact with and potentially reinforce geographical disparities (P. K. Singh, Singh, et al., 2021). Hierarchical policies are the basic strategy in the Indian administrative system, where macro-level policies are formulated for the states, and micro-level planning directly deals with the PSU or ward level policies. India's youthful demographic profile raises an alarming concern about the early onset of tobacco use among the youth population at an adolescent age., with almost 255 million young people aged 15–24 years (approximately 17% of the total population of India), creates two faces of challenge (i) to prohibit the new users, and (ii) to reduce the percentage of existing tobacco users (Pandey, 2017). A larger youth proportion would be beneficial in the long run-economic development and demographic dividend, with an open window of opportunity, till 2041, where, working-age population is exceeding over dependent population (Jawad & Power, 2016). However, this potential economic advantage can only be realized by addressing preventable health burdens like tobacco use among youth through the multi-level approach-based policies for macro to micro levels.

Major objectives of the study are: to estimate the level of smoking, SLT, and any tobacco use among young men between 15–24 years of age in India, and to estimate the multi-level attributions in the smoked, SLT, and any tobacco use analysis at state-level geographical variations (PSU and districts).

Methods

Data and study population

This study utilized publicly available data from the men's file of the fifth round of the National Family Health Survey (NFHS-5) conducted during 2019–21. The NFHS is conducted by the International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS), Mumbai, under the guidance of the Ministry of Health and Family Welfare, Government of India. (Charan Konanki and Dasaratharamaiah, 2020, Chirisa et al., 2022).

Using a two-stage stratified probability-based sampling design, NFHS-5 collected information on de facto male samples from 28 states and 8 union territories (UTs). The survey provides comprehensive information on socioeconomic characteristics, health behaviors, and tobacco use patterns among men aged 15–54 years. For this study, we selected 31,070 men aged 15–24 years from 25,155 households, representing 8526 PSUs, 707 districts, 28 states, and 8 UTs.

To capture the hierarchical nature of socioeconomic and geographical variations in tobacco use, we considered four geographic levels: (i) state, (ii) district, (iii) PSU, and (iv) household. This approach reflects India's federal administrative structure, where the central government addresses national interests while state administrations handle regional matters, with districts and PSUs serving as crucial implementation units for various policies and programs (Giovenco et al., 2021; P. K. Singh, Singh, et al., 2021). PSUs represent natural community-level units: in rural areas, PSUs are villages (revenue villages or gram panchayats), while in urban areas, PSUs are Census Enumeration Blocks (CEBs)—small geographic units typically containing 125–150 households that represent neighbourhoods with relatively homogeneous socioeconomic characteristics. These PSU definitions are particularly relevant for understanding tobacco use patterns, as villages and urban neighbourhoods constitute the immediate community environment where young men are exposed to tobacco retail outlets, peer networks, social norms regarding tobacco use, and local enforcement (or lack thereof) of tobacco control regulations. In the second stage, households within each PSU were randomly selected.

Outcome variables

The main outcome variables were: (i) smoked tobacco use, (ii) smokeless tobacco (SLT) use, and (iii) any type of tobacco use in the 24 h preceding the survey. All variables were coded as binary, with '1′ indicating 'current users of tobacco' and '0′ indicating 'non-users of tobacco'.

Smoked tobacco use included consumption of cigarettes, bidis (hand-rolled tobacco wrapped in tendu leaves), and other forms of combustible tobacco like cigars, pipes, and hookahs. SLT use encompassed products such as gutkha/paan masala with tobacco, khaini (dried tobacco leaves consumed with slaked lime), paan with tobacco, other chewing tobacco, snuff, and other smokeless variants. Both categories represent common tobacco consumption patterns in India, with regional variations in preferences (Jawad and Power, 2016, Pandey, 2017).

Covariates

To ensure robust modelling, we adjusted for key background characteristics: (i) age groups (15–19 and 20–24 years), (ii) educational status (no education, primary, secondary, and higher level), (iii) wealth status (poorest, poorer, middle, richer, and richest quintiles), and (iv) place of residence (rural/urban).

Statistical analyses

The data were organized in a four-level hierarchical structure: individuals/households (level 1) nested within PSUs (level 2), which were nested within districts (level 3), which in turn were nested within states/UTs (level 4) (Jain et al., 2021, Kim et al., 2016). To account for this complex sampling design and capture heterogeneities in tobacco use patterns, we employed four-level random intercept logistic models.

Model specifications

Null models were analysed with four-level random intercepts to establish baseline geographic variation in tobacco use without controlling for individual characteristics. The adjusted full models were analysed by incorporating individual factors (age, education, wealth status, residence) were incorporated to identify residual unobserved heterogeneities. The proportional change in variance at each level was estimated by comparing geographic variance between null and full models.

For robustness, we conducted stratified analyses across states and UTs, excluding 2 states (Goa and Sikkim) and 6 UTs (Andaman and Nicobar Islands, Chandigarh, Dadra and Nagar Haveli and Daman and Diu, Ladakh, Lakshadweep, Puducherry) with fewer than six districts. This allowed us to assess the attribution of district and PSU-level factors in tobacco use disparities across different regions. All analyses were performed using Stata version 16 with the xtmelogit command (StataCorp, 2021).

Ethics statement

This study utilized de-identified, anonymized secondary data from NFHS-5, which received ethical approval from the ICF Institutional Review Board and relevant Indian authorities prior to data collection. All participants provided informed consent during the original survey. The publicly available dataset is completely anonymized with no possibility of individual identification (3). The DHS program explicitly permits secondary analysis for research purposes. As this study involved only secondary analysis of pre-approved, anonymized data—with no human subject contact, no new data collection, and no participant risk—it qualifies for exemption from additional institutional review under standard research ethics guidelines.

Table 1 presents the prevalence of smoked, smokeless, and any tobacco use among young men (15–24 years) across India by individual characteristics. Educational disparities were pronounced, with primary-educated young men showing the highest consumption of smoked tobacco (26.4%), SLT (37.9%), and any tobacco (50.7%), followed by those with no education. Smoked tobacco use was highest in Meghalaya (46.7%), Mizoram (41.2%), West Bengal (34.9%), Arunachal Pradesh (27.9%), and Sikkim (25.2%). Partitioning of variations at different geographic levels

Table 3 presents variance estimates and VPCs for tobacco use at the PSU, district, and the state levels. In the null model for smoked tobacco, PSU-level factors accounted for the largest proportion of variance (55.1%), followed by state-level (32.7%) and district-level factors (12.1%). For SLT use, state-level factors contributed the most (59.8%), followed by PSU-level (28.0%) and district-level factors (12.2%). Any tobacco use variance followed a similar pattern to smoked tobacco, with PSU (46.3%), state (37.5%), and district (16.3%) level factors accounting for decreasing proportions.

Discussion

This study provides novel insights into the geographical attribution of tobacco use variations among young men in India, with important policy implications for tobacco control in LMICs. We found that nearly one-fourth of young Indian men consume some form of tobacco, with higher prevalence among those aged 20–24 years, less educated individuals, economically disadvantaged groups, and rural residents. These findings align with previous research identifying socioeconomic and demographic risk factors for tobacco use (Nongrum et al., 2024; A. Singh et al., 2020, Welding et al., 2021).

Our study's unique contribution lies in quantifying the relative importance of different administrative levels in explaining tobacco use variations. After adjusting for individual characteristics, we found that local-level (PSU) geographical factors account for the largest proportion of variance in smoked tobacco use (58.2%), while state-level factors dominate for SLT use (53.1%), particularly in rural areas. However, the state-specific variations highlighted the need for geographically tailored intervention strategies that account for the relative importance of different administrative levels in shaping tobacco use behaviors. These findings suggest distinct spatial dynamics for different tobacco products, necessitating tailored intervention approaches.

The strong PSU-level effects on smoking behaviors likely reflect local availability, social norms, and retail environments. Previous research has documented how tobacco companies strategically locate retail outlets near schools, colleges, workplaces, and commercial areas to target young consumers (Dwivedi and Satish, 2014, Jawad et al., 2015, Patel et al., 2012). The affordability of single-unit sales (instead of full packs) makes cigarettes and bidis accessible to price-sensitive youth (Nandi et al., 2015, Nazar et al., 2021). These local marketing and accessibility factors may explain why PSU-level variations are particularly pronounced for smoked tobacco. In this regard the tobacco free village and the tobacco free educational institution campaigns of the government should also focus on compliance with ban on loose sale of tobacco products in line with the mandates under Article 11 of the WHO FCTC and Section 7 of the Cigarette and Other Tobacco Products Act.

Conversely, the dominance of state-level factors in SLT use patterns likely reflects the influence of agricultural practices, cultural traditions, and state-specific policies. SLT products often depend on raw tobacco production, which varies with geographical features like climate, soil conditions, and rainfall (Singh et al., 2020, Singh et al., 2020, Singh et al., 2021c). Regional traditions also shape consumption patterns, with certain SLT products having deep cultural roots in specific communities (Agrawal et al., 2023). Additionally, tobacco control policies and enforcement vary significantly across states, which may contribute to these state-level effects (Kumari et al., 2024, Singh et al., 2021a). Focused effort for STL control should be introduced by the governments in the high SLT burden states and districts under the National Tobacco Control Programme.

The rural–urban differences in variance attribution patterns highlight the need for setting specific strategies. The stronger influence of PSU-level factors in urban areas (62.0% for smoked tobacco) compared to rural areas (51.0%) suggests that neighbourhood-level interventions targeting retail environments might be especially effective in urban settings. Conversely, the pronounced state-level effects for rural SLT use (56.7%) indicate that broader policy measures and cultural interventions might be more appropriate in rural contexts. To this effect, the tobacco free village equivalent for urban PSUs should be urgently introduced by the local, state and national governments to address rising smoking in urban PSUs and the tobacco free village campaign should focus more on SLT control initiatives to address the high SLT use in rural areas.

Implications for tobacco control policy and practice

Our findings have substantial implications for tobacco control policies in India and other LMICs with similar administrative structures. For smoked tobacco control, short-term and long-term actions are recommended:

Enhanced local retail regulations

Local authorities should implement and strictly enforce regulations on tobacco retail density, particularly around educational institutions. The high attribution of PSU-level factors (58.2%) indicates that such local interventions could significantly impact smoking prevalence, especially in north-eastern states where rates exceed 40%.

School-based prevention programs

Educational institutions represent critical venues for prevention. Tobacco free educational institutions campaign should be effectively implemented with full enforcement of 4, 6 of COTPA in line with Article 16 of the WHO FCTC. PSU-focused interventions should target schools and vocational training centres, in particular declaring the surroundings of such institutions as no tobacco vending zone, to protect youth from tobacco use initiation. School curriculum to include harms of tobacco use.

Community-based enforcement mechanisms

With PSU-level factors accounting for most variance in smoking behaviour, community participation in monitoring and enforcing tobacco control laws should be prioritized. This is particularly important in urban areas where PSU-level attribution reaches 62.0%. Village and block level coordination committees for tobacco control should be formed under the National Tobacco Control Programme for effective implementation of the tobacco control laws at PSU levels.

For SLT control, short-term and long-term policies are warranted, particularly at the state-level:

1.State-specific production and distribution policies: States with high SLT prevalence, particularly Arunachal Pradesh (37.6%), Manipur (31.4%), and Nagaland (29.3%), should implement comprehensive policies including mandatory licensing for addressing production, distribution, supply, sale and other compliances with the eixtsing laws to address the region-specific SLT products.

These geographically tailored interventions are especially important in the context of adolescent tobacco use in LMICs. Early tobacco initiation establishes addictive behaviors that persist into adulthood and predict future population-level tobacco use attributable burden. By addressing the specific geographical determinants of different tobacco products, LMICs can more effectively reduce tobacco's substantial health and economic burden among their youth.

Strength and limitations

This study benefits from a large, nationally representative sample of 31,070 adolescents and the use of rigorous multilevel modeling, enabling a detailed assessment of geographic variation in youth tobacco use. Separate analyses of smoked and smokeless tobacco, examination of four policy-relevant levels, and urban–rural stratification enhance the policy relevance of the findings. Use of recent NFHS data from 2019 to 2021 further strengthens the study’s contemporary relevance.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. The cross-sectional design limits causal inference, as associations may be influenced by unmeasured confounding; longitudinal studies following youth cohorts and policy changes would provide stronger causal evidence. Tobacco use may be underreported due to social desirability bias, which could affect variance attribution if stigma varies systematically across geographic contexts. The NFHS does not directly capture key contextual determinants, including retail density, enforcement quality, community norms, industry marketing, cessation service availability, or program implementation. Consequently, while variance partitioning identifies where variation occurs, it cannot explain the underlying drivers. Residual confounding from unmeasured individual and contextual factors remains possible, and cross-level interactions were not fully explored. Finally, findings reflect the 2019–2021 period and may not capture more recent policy and market changes. Future research should emphasize longitudinal designs, linkage with independent geographic data, and analyses across multiple NFHS rounds.

Conclusions

Our study provides compelling evidence that different geographical levels play distinct roles in shaping tobacco use patterns among young men in India. The predominant influence of PSU-level factors on smoking behaviour (58.2%) and state-level factors on SLT use (53.1%) demonstrates that effective tobacco control requires tailored approaches at different administrative levels.

These findings are particularly significant in the context of adolescent tobacco use in India and other LMICs, where tobacco control efforts face numerous challenges, including limited resources, differential policy enforcement, and aggressive industry marketing targeting youth. Our results suggest that addressing tobacco use among young adults—who represent both the current and future burden of tobacco-related health and economic costs—requires geographically nuanced interventions that recognize the relative importance of local versus state-level factors for different tobacco products.

The geographical determinants identified in this study provide a roadmap for developing more effective, context-specific tobacco control interventions that can significantly impact youth tobacco use in resource-limited settings. By implementing multi-level tobacco control strategies — such as restricting the proximity of retail tobacco outlets to schools and educational institutions, enforcing vendor-free buffer zones, and strengthening point-of-sale regulations through micro-level, place-based interventions — policymakers can disrupt the environmental cues and easy accessibility that drive tobacco initiation among adolescents. Concurrently, target the states and districts with higher tobacco use through macro-level policies, particularly in the high burden states. By using the multiple-level approach, the policy-maker could be able to save the youth from addiction and harness their true potential of the demographic dividend and achieve the Sustainable Development Goals.
