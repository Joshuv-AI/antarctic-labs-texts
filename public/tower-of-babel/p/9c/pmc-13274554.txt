Comparison of DermaGenius and In‐House qPCR Methods for Detection of Recalcitrant Dermatophyte Infections: A Practical Approach for Dermatologists and Mycologists
Authors: Murat Durdu, Engin Kaplan, Oğuzhan Bingöl, Nevzat Ünal, Mehmet Emin Demircili, Bram Spruijtenburg, Carolina Melchior do Prado, Eelco F J Meijer, Macit Ilkit
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackgroundChronic and recalcitrant dermatophytosis has become increasingly problematic in dermatology practice, largely due to emerging antifungal resistance, affecting not only terbinafine but also azoles. Among the causative agents, Trichophyton indotineae has emerged as a key pathogen associated with treatment failure, extensive disease, and frequent relapse, even in immunocompetent patients.ObjectiveThis study aimed to evaluate and compare two rapid molecular diagnostic approaches: A commercial multiplex qPCR assay (DermaGenius Resistance Multiplex PCR, DermaGenius RMP) and a T. indotineae‐specific in‐house qPCR assay, focusing on their practical value for early detection of recalcitrant dermatophyte infections and terbinafine resistance in routine dermatology practice.MethodsSixty‐four dermatophyte isolates obtained from patients with chronic or treatment‐resistant dermatophytosis in Türkiye wereanalysed. Species identification and detection of squalene epoxidase (SQLE) gene mutations associated with terbinafine resistance were performed using DermaGenius RMP and the in‐house T. indotineae‐specific qPCR assay, with confirmatory ITS/SQLE sequencing and CLSI microbroth dilution antifungal susceptibility testing.ResultsOverall concordance between the two qPCR methods was 93.8% (κ = 0.74, z = 5.99, p < 0.001) for T. indotineae identification. Most recalcitrant cases were caused by T. indotineae, and SQLE mutations were detected in > 90% of these isolates, consistent with the observed clinical resistance. DermaGenius RMP enabled rapid, standardised detection of terbinafine resistance–associated mutations suitable for routine diagnostics, whereas the in‐house qPCR provided highly specific identification of T. indotineae. Notably, 10 T. indotineae isolates harboured the SQLE F397L substitution despite terbinafine susceptibility.ConclusionsRapid molecular diagnostics, particularly qPCR‐based assays, provide actionable information early in the course of infection. In this study, both assays showed comparable performance for T. indotineae detection. Prompt identification of T. indotineae and associated resistance mutations may help avoid ineffective antifungal therapy, reduce chronicity, and support rational treatment decisions in routine clinical practice.Keywords: antifungal resistance, dermatophytes, multiplex qPCR, squalene epoxidase, terbinafine, trichophyton indotineae

Introduction

In the ongoing fight against microorganisms, therapeutic strategies are evolving as pathogens adapt and change [1]. Over the past decade, dermatologists worldwide have increasingly encountered chronic, recurrent, and treatment‐refractory dermatophytosis, a scenario that differs markedly from the traditionally benign and readily treatable nature of these infections. This problem has now been reported in 50 countries across six continents, emerging as a substantial global public health threat [2, 3, 4, 5].

The rising incidence of chronic and recalcitrant dermatophytosis has increased the frequency of difficult‐to‐treat cases, particularly given the limited number of available antifungal agents. Notably, Trichophyton indotineae, a lineage within the Trichophyton mentagrophytes complex, can cause extensive and recalcitrant infection even in immunocompetent individuals, unlike classical dermatophytosis. Lesions associated with T. indotineae are typically characterised by prominent inflammation, intense pruritus, and frequent post‐inflammatory hyperpigmentation after clinical resolution [6, 7, 8]. As T. indotineae is often associated with resistance to antifungal agents, including terbinafine, these infections represent a distinct clinical concern. Collectively, these features have increased the need for accurate and rapid diagnostic methods and have prompted a re‐evaluation of effective treatment strategies.

From a practical dermatology perspective, the central challenge is no longer limited to diagnosing dermatophytosis but also includes early identification of resistant pathogens that require alternative therapeutic approaches [9]. In this context, rapid molecular diagnostics have become clinically indispensable. Quantitative real‐time PCR (qPCR) assays offer several advantages for daily dermatology practice, including (i) rapid turnaround time, enabling early therapeutic adjustment; (ii) species‐level discrimination, which is critical for identifying T. indotineae; and (iii) detection of resistance‐associated SQLE mutations, allowing prediction of terbinafine treatment failure before clinical relapse [10, 11]. Commercial multiplex systems such as DermaGenius Resistance Multiplex PCR (RMP) provide standardised, user‐friendly platforms suitable for routine laboratories, enabling dermatophyte detection and identification of key SQLE mutations linked to terbinafine resistance [11, 12].

Here, we compare DermaGenius RMP with an in‐house T. indotineae‐specific qPCR assay and correlate molecular findings with clinical and antifungal susceptibility data from patients in Türkiye to illustrate how rapid diagnostics can directly improve patient management.

Materials and Methods

Isolates

A total of 64 dermatophyte isolates from patients with chronic or treatment‐resistant dermatophytosis across various cities in Türkiye between April 2024 and December 2024 were included in this study. Dermatophytes were subcultured on Sabouraud glucose agar (SGA; Merck, Darmstadt, Germany) supplemented with chloramphenicol (100 μg/mL; Sigma‐Aldrich, Steinheim, Germany) and gentamicin (50 μg/mL; Sigma‐Aldrich) and incubated at 28°C for 14 days. Trichophyton indotineae (CBS 149165) and T. tonsurans (DMSZ 12285) were used as reference strains.

DNA Extraction

DNA was extracted using a cetrimonium bromide‐based method [11] and the PathoNostics Extraction kit PN‐502 (PathoNostics B.V., Maastricht, The Netherlands), according to the manufacturer's protocol.

Species Identification

Molecular identification of Trichophyton species and/or analysis of the squalene epoxidase (SQLE) gene from the 64 isolates was performed using the DermaGenius Resistance Multiplex real‐time PCR kit PN‐303 (DermaGenius RMP; PathoNostics B.V., Maastricht, The Netherlands), our previously described T. indotineae‐specific in‐house qPCR (Tindo‐qPCR) [11], and sequencing of the ITS and SQLE regions when confirmation was required. Species identification and SQLE analysis targeting L393 and F397 in the DermaGenius RMP were performed according to the manufacturer's instructions. Detection of an SQLE L393/F397 mutation together with a T. interdigitale/T. mentagrophytes signal was interpreted as presumptive T. mentagrophytes ITS genotype VIII (T. indotineae) [13]. All analyses were performed on a Rotor‐Gene Q instrument (QIAGEN, Hilden, Germany), with each isolate tested in duplicate.

For ITS/SQLE amplification and the in‐house Tindo‐qPCR assay, DreamTaq DNA polymerase (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) and BlasTaq 2× qPCR Master Mix (ABM, Vancouver, Canada) were used [11], respectively, following the manufacturers' instructions. Amplification was performed on a CFX96 Touch Real‐Time PCR Detection System (Bio‐Rad, Hercules, CA, USA). To identify non‐Trichophyton species, resolve discrepancies, and clarify dual‐species identifications reported by DermaGenius RMP (e.g., T. rubrum /T. soudanense), ITS sequencing was performed using ITS1 and ITS4 primers [14].

Detection of SQLE Mutations

Partial SQLE amplification and sequencing spanning amino acid positions 393 and 397 were performed using in‐house primers (forward: CCTCAGACCTGTCACGAATAGG; reverse: CTTTGAAGTTCGGCAAATACG). ITS and SQLE amplicons were submitted to Macrogen Europe B.V. for Sanger sequencing. Species identification was performed by BLAST analysis (https://blast.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/Blast.cgi), and SQLE variants were identified by comparing sequences with reference SQLE sequences (GenBank accession numbers: Trichophyton interdigitale: OM313312.1; Trichophyton rubrum: MG587093.2) using MEGA X [15].

Resistance Investigation

In vitro antifungal susceptibility testing (AFST) against seven agents was performed according to Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) M38, 3rd edition [16]. Isolates were subcultured on SGA at 28°C for 7 days before testing. Inoculum density was adjusted using a spectrophotometer to 65%–70% transmission at 530 nm. Microtiter plates were incubated at 30°C for 96 h, and minimum inhibitory concentrations (MICs) were read visually. For amphotericin B, itraconazole, voriconazole, posaconazole, and isavuconazole, the MIC was defined as complete growth inhibition. For fluconazole, the MIC was defined as 50% growth reduction relative to the growth control, and for terbinafine as 80% growth reduction. The tentative cutoff value of 0.25 μg/mL, based on previous studies indicating that isolates with this MIC value or higher belong to non‐wild type distribution and may harbour resistance‐associated SQLE mutations, was applied [17].

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi version 2.6.44. Agreement between DermaGenius RMP and in‐house qPCR was evaluated using Cohen's kappa coefficient. The statistical level of significance for all tests was considered as 0.05.

Results

Species Identification

DermaGenius RMP identified 56 isolates (87.5%) as T. interdigitale/T. mentagrophytes, 4 (6.2%) as T. rubrum /T. soudanense, 2 (3.1%) as T. tonsurans, and 1 isolate each (1.5%) as T. schoenleinii/T. quinckeanum and a non‐Trichophyton species. The Tindo‐qPCR assay yielded positive (T. indotineae) results for 54 isolates (84.3%). Comparison of isolate‐level results between DermaGenius RMP and Tindo‐qPCR showed discrepancies for 4 isolates (6.25%; isolates 1310, 1312, 1330, and 1393) (Table 1). Agreement between DermaGenius RMP and in‐house qPCR was high, with an overall concordance rate of 93.8%. Cohen's kappa coefficient demonstrated substantial agreement between the two methods (κ = 0.74, z = 5.99, p < 0.001). Based on ITS sequencing, including resolution of dual identifications reported by DermaGenius RMP (e.g., T. rubrum /T. soudanense), 2 isolates (3.1%; 1310 and 1312) were found to be misidentified by the DermaGenius RMP, and 2 (3.1%; 1330 and 1393) were misidentified by Tindo‐qPCR. In addition, all isolates reported as T. rubrum /T. soudanense and non‐Trichophyton by DermaGenius RMP were identified by ITS sequencing as T. rubrum and Microsporum canis, respectively. Overall concordance between DermaGenius RMP and Tindo‐qPCR was 93.8% (60/64), comprising correct identification of T. indotineae and consistent negativity among non‐T. indotineae isolates. DermaGenius RMP detected substitutions at positions 393 and/or 397 in 54 of 61 analysed isolates (88.5%), whereas partial SQLE sequencing detected substitutions in 55 of 59 isolates (93.2%) (Table 1 and Table 2).

Detection of SQLE Mutations

Sequencing identified L393F (1/59, 1.7%), L393S (1/59, 1.7%), and predominantly F397L (53/59, 89.8%). Among the 54 mutant isolates analysed by DermaGenius RMP, sequencing confirmed substitutions at position 393 in 2 isolates (3.7%) and at position 397 in 50 isolates (92.5%). With the exception of 1 isolate ( T. rubrum , isolate 1357), all mutant isolates identified by DermaGenius RMP and SQLE sequencing were T. indotineae. Two mutant isolates (3.7%) identified by DermaGenius RMP carried substitutions at position 393 (L393F and L393S). Among the analysed non‐T. indotineae isolates, 4/5 (80%) were wild‐type at positions 393 and 397, whereas only 1 T. indotineae isolate (1.7%; isolate 1397) was wild‐type at these positions. A per‐isolate mutation comparison for amino acid positions 393 and 397 could not be performed for 6 (9%) of the strains due to unavailable data from either DermaGenius RMP or SQLE sequencing (Table 2).

In addition to substitutions at positions 393 and 397, sequencing of the partial SQLE region (amino acids 320–454) identified Y414H, A448T, and W450T in 8 (13.5%), 8 (13.5%), and 6 (10.1%) of 59 isolates, respectively (Table 2). The W450T substitution was identified for the first time in the SQLE gene of T. indotineae in this study. Dual and triple substitution patterns were also observed, including F397L + Y414H, F397L + A448T, F397L + W450T, and F397L + Y414H + W450T, detected in 8 (13.5%), 7 (11.8%), 6 (10.1%), and 1 (1.6%; isolate 1366) isolates, respectively.

In a per‐isolate comparison of SQLE profiles for isolates assessed by both methods (n = 57), all but 1 isolate (1.7%; T. rubrum , isolate 1454) showed concordant identification of substitutions at positions 393 and 397 between DermaGenius RMP and SQLE sequencing (Table 2).

AFST

AFST using CLSI microbroth dilution was performed for all 56 T. indotineae isolates against seven antifungal agents and compared with SQLE mutation status (Tables 2 and 3). Based on MIC50, posaconazole showed the highest overall in vitro activity, followed by amphotericin B. For terbinafine, 44 isolates had elevated MICs (≥ 0.25 μg/mL), all of which carried substitutions at the L393 or F397 hotspot in SQLE. Notably, 10 isolates had low terbinafine MICs (≤ 0.016 μg/mL) despite harbouring SQLE F397L. Only 1 isolate lacked SQLE substitutions and also showed a low terbinafine MIC (0.016 μg/mL) (Figure 1; Tables 2 and 3). The overview and key results of the study are illustrated in Figure 2.

Discussion

The increasing burden of chronic and recalcitrant dermatophytosis has transformed what was once a relatively straightforward clinical entity into a complex diagnostic and therapeutic challenge for dermatologists. Our findings indicate that addressing this challenge requires not only appropriate antifungal selection but also early, resistance‐oriented molecular diagnostics that can guide treatment decisions before clinical failure occurs. In this study, concordance between DermaGenius RMP and Tindo‐qPCR for molecular identification of the 64‐dermatophyte isolate collection was high (93.8%), with four discrepant results (6.2%) resolved by ITS sequencing. Despite the substantial agreement observed (κ = 0.74), several limitations should be considered. Kappa statistics may be influenced by the distribution of categories, particularly in datasets with a high prevalence of a single outcome, as seen in our cohort, where T. indotineae predominated. This imbalance may have affected the magnitude of the kappa value, potentially underestimating the true level of agreement. We also observed strong agreement between DermaGenius RMP and partial SQLE sequencing for the detection of resistance‐associated SQLE mutations at amino acid positions 393 and 397, which were observed almost exclusively in T. indotineae isolates. The consistent wild‐type profile among non‐T. indotineae isolates and the near‐complete concordance of mutation calls between methods, with the exception of a single isolate, support the diagnostic utility of DermaGenius RMP for reliable identification of T. indotineae and associated SQLE resistance mutations.

In the SQLE hotspot analysis, the predominance of the F397L is consistent with global reports. Additional substitutions, including Y414H, A448T, and W450T, may also contribute to reduced antifungal susceptibility [18]. To the best of our knowledge, the W450T mutation has not been reported previously by recent reviews or genomic investigations, making its effect on terbinafine MICs unknown [19, 20]. Although terbinafine‐susceptible isolates are typically wild type in the SQLE hotspot region, 10 isolates in this study had low MICs despite harbouring the F397L substitution [21]. This uncommon discordance suggests that the genetic background of these isolates may mitigate the effect of F397L on terbinafine susceptibility [18]. Future investigations, supported by whole‐genome sequencing data and transcriptomic analyses, are needed to test this hypothesis. Although SQLE hotspot mutations, such as those targeted by DermaGenius RMP, are generally strong predictors of phenotypic resistance, our findings support incorporating timely AFST to guide therapeutic choices when molecular and phenotypic results diverge. In addition, the high itraconazole MICs observed here further constrain treatment options, although the association with clinical outcomes remains to be defined.

The diagnostic strategy evaluated here supports a shift from reactive to proactive management of dermatophytosis. Incorporating rapid molecular diagnostics into routine practice may improve initial treatment selection, reduce chronicity, and limit ongoing selection for resistant strains [22]. Our results support the use of standardised multiplex qPCR assays as first‐line tools, complemented by targeted in‐house assays, for contemporary management of recalcitrant dermatophyte infections. In this context, qPCR‐based methods enable rapid detection of both the etiologic agent and resistance‐associated mutations. Importantly, commercial and in‐house qPCR approaches should be considered complementary components of a clinically driven diagnostic algorithm rather than competing alternatives.

Nevertheless, some limitations of the study should be considered. First, the study population comprised patients with chronic or treatment‐resistant dermatophytosis from a single centre in Türkiye; therefore, the high prevalence of T. indotineae and SQLE mutations may not reflect patterns in other geographic regions. Second, DermaGenius RMP was evaluated using cultured isolates rather than direct clinical specimens; thus, the operational benefit of direct‐from‐sample testing and its effect on real‐world turnaround time could not be assessed. Third, we did not compare the performance of multiple commercial assays, which would be preferable when comparing direct clinical samples. Fourth, the functional significance and clinical impact of SQLE substitutions outside positions 393 and 397 remain uncertain and require further investigation, including genetic and experimental validation. Despite these limitations, the study provides clinically relevant evidence supporting integration of rapid molecular diagnostics into routine practice for early identification of recalcitrant dermatophyte infections and antifungal resistance.

In conclusion, our findings support the following practical recommendations for dermatologists and mycologists: (i) do not wait for repeated treatment failure before requesting molecular testing in patients with chronic dermatophytosis; (ii) use DermaGenius RMP early in patients with suspected recalcitrant disease, particularly when direct‐sample testing is available, to rapidly identify resistance‐associated mutations; (iii) reserve in‐house qPCR and sequencing for confirmatory testing, complex cases, or epidemiological investigations; and (iv) integrate molecular results into treatment planning rather than treating them as purely academic findings.
