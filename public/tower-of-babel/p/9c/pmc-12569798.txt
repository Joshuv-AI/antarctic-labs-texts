SOGIE-diverse inclusivity: An analysis of 2SLGBTQ+ representation on Ontario, Canada public health unit websites
Authors: Tin D. Vo, Todd A. Coleman, Cameron McKenzie, Kyran Ferrier, Grace Henry, Alik Sarian, Corey Bernard, Ruth Cameron, Charlie Davis, Daniel Grace, LLana James, Nick Mulé
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesThe objectives of this study were to: 1) examine how often sexual orientation, gender identity and expression (SOGIE)-diverse terms appeared on websites of public health units (PHUs) in Ontario, Canada, and 2) determine the contexts within which these terms appeared on these PHU websites.Study designThis study employed a summative content analysis of 34 Ontario PHU websites.MethodsThe study involved compiling a list of SOGIE-diverse terms, searched PHU websites using their integrated search systems, and retrieved webpages where relevant search terms were found. The data analysis included enumerating how often the SOGIE-diverse terms appeared and summarizing the contexts within which the terminology appeared on the 34 PHU websites.ResultsAcross these websites, terms commonly found were variations of “LGBT,” “sexual/sexuality,” and identity terms, including “bisexual,” “gay,” “queer,” and “lesbian.” SOGIE-diverse communities were reflected in two broad categories: sexual health and infectious diseases; and mental health, SOGIE-diversity discrimination, and resources.ConclusionsIn this website review, most PHUs offer some relevant information about and for SDCs; however, they also lacked in specific, SOGIE-relevant ways. While sexual health and mental health are pertinent concerns, this myopic view of relevant health concerns for SOGIE-diverse communities excludes holistic health conceptualizations and lacks nuanced understanding of SDCs’ rich lived experiences. The implications of this work are to ensure that PHUs have mutual understanding of SOGIE-diversity related terms, and how they represent SDCs via their websites and the information and services offered for these communities. Ontario PHUs, especially through public-facing platforms such as websites, have a unique opportunity to educate both SDCs and the broader public. Clear, consistent, and collaborative communication can minimize inequitable health outcomes for these communities.

Introduction

For over half a century, global human rights policies and laws supporting SOGIE-diverse communities1 (SDCs) have advanced significantly, including in Canada, improving protections, visibility, and health outcomes. Legal reforms began in 1969 with Canadian government reforms to the Criminal Code to decriminalize homosexuality, with more recent protections such as marriage equality, in 2005 [1].

SDCs face disproportional levels of adverse health outcomes compared to heterosexual/cisgender counterparts, across mental, sexual, and physical health, and in healthcare access and utilization [2,3], similar to other countries, necessitating for health equity frameworks to address SDCs’ needs. Moreover, intersectionality [4] must be considered within these frameworks, as diverse SDCs, in addition to heterosexism and cisnormativity, face other systems of oppression, such as racism and ableism, that put them at disproportionate risk of health concerns [3].

In Canada, provinces are responsible for delivering healthcare, including public health services. At the time of analysis, Ontario's 34 public health units (PHUs) provided services ranging from health promotion, sexual health, mental health, to healthy growth and development [5]. PHUs are one of the points of contact for individuals seeking health information or care, placing PHUs in a unique position to prevent adverse health outcomes in SDCs. We recognize that some SDCs may seek health information from SDC-focused community-based health organizations in Ontario, many of which refer individuals to their local PHUs for more information.

This content analysis examined how often and in what contexts SOGIE-diverse terms appeared on PHU websites. Our research questions were: 1) How frequently are SOGIE-diverse issues addressed on Ontario PHU websites? and 2) In what contexts do they appear?

Methods

SOGIE-diverse content from 34 Ontario PHU websites was collected between October 2023 and March 2024 (one for each Ontario PHU during the time of data collection). Authors compiled a list of SOGIE-diverse terms to assess inclusion, ranging from broader terms such as “sex” and “gender” to specific SOGIE-diverse terms such as “queer” and “Two-Spirit”, with variations also considered (e.g., “non-binary” and “nonbinary”). Using Google search engines, three authors searched each term on each PHU website, recording results into a MS Excel spreadsheet for contextual descriptions. See Supplementary Material.

Frequency in which terms appeared on 34 PHU websites was summarized. Two researchers independently reviewed occurrences of terms and met to discuss observations across the data compiled in the MS Excel spreadsheet. After conferring, the two researchers collaboratively organized each mention within the spreadsheet into two broad categories: sexual health and infectious diseases; and mental health, supports, and community resources. Under each category, contexts under which the terms appeared were described within the spreadsheet. The researchers further determined themes emerging from reviewing each category and synthesized the findings.

Results

Frequency of SOGIE diverse terms on Ontario PHU websites

Across the 34 PHU websites, terms commonly found were variations of “LGBT” (76.47 %), “sexual/sexuality” (76.47 %), and identity terms, including “bisexual” (73.35 %), “gay” (67.65 %), “transgender/transsexual” (67.65 %), “queer” (67.65 %), and “lesbian” (61.76 %). Least used terms included specific terms such as “trans woman/women” (5.88 %) and “trans man/men” (17.65 %). Data for frequencies and search terms are provided in Supplementary Material.

Sexual health and infectious diseases. Thirty PHUs (88.2 %) referenced SDCs via sexual health and infectious diseases. Five sub-themes emerged: sexual health clinic information; HIV/STI resources and services; definitions of gender and sexual orientation and related health inequities; social determinants of health; and rates of STIs and other transmissible infections. The two most common were sexual health clinic information (15 PHUs; 44.1 %) and HIV/STI resources and services (15 PHUs; 44.1 %). External resources included the Gay Men's Sexual Health Alliance, Ontario HIV Treatment Network, CATIE, and Sexfluent.

Seven PHUs (20.6 %) described gender and sexual orientation and health inequities due to systemic discrimination (e.g., homophobia, transphobia), putting SDCs at greater risk for sexual health concerns (e.g., HIV, STIs). Interestingly, 6 PHUs (17.6 %) named gender and sexual orientation as social determinants of health, but did not elaborate on how or why.

Mental health, SOGIE-diversity discrimination, and resources. Thirty-one PHUs (91.2 %) referenced SDCs in contexts of mental health and discrimination. Five sub-themes emerged: local/regional events and supports for SDCs, regional/provincial/national resources and services, explanations of health inequities linked to SOGIE-diversity systemic oppression, definitions on SOGIE-diversity, and recognition of SDCs mental health outcomes.

Local/regional events and supports included SOGIE-diverse organizations, pride groups and events, support groups, community health centres with SOGIE-diverse programming (e.g., gender-affirming care), positive space and other SOGIE-diversity inclusion training, HIV/AIDS service organizations, sexual violence support centres, and inclusive counselling. Regional/provincial/national resources and services were dedicated to SOGIE-diverse youth, adults, parents of SOGIE-diverse youth, and allies. Resources included understanding SOGIE-diversity, mental health support and resources, and ways to support and work with SDCs.

The third most common subtheme, among 19 PHUs (55.9 %), addressed mental health inequities faced by SDCs due systemic oppression (e.g., heterosexism, transphobia). Topics included need for safe spaces for SDCs to counteract systemic oppression faced in mainstream society, strategies for allies and healthcare providers to address systemic oppression (e.g., antidiscrimination policies) and create welcoming spaces for SDCs, and resources for educators and service providers to support SDCs, such as how to address cyberbullying, or supporting SOGIE-diverse older adults.

Discussion

Ontario PHU websites varied in their representation of SDCs and showed noticeable inconsistencies. First, while most (but not all) used some SOGIE-specific terms (e.g., bisexual, gay, queer, and lesbian), many omitted others such as trans men/women, nonbinary, and cisgender, contributing to erasure of subgroups prominently affected by public health matter (e.g., normatively centering cisgender identities, excluding trans experiences).

Secondly, SDCs tended to be represented under two broad categories: 1) sexual health and infectious diseases; and 3) mental health, SOGIE-diversity discrimination, and resources. Nearly a quarter of PHU websites listed SOGIE-diverse mental health and community resources, but did not provide contextual information to elaborate why such resources would be helpful. Further, SDCs were often described in relation to sexual health and infectious diseases, linking their identities to health risks tied to sexual health/behaviours, implying that sexual health is the main health concern. While sexual health and mental health are pertinent concerns, this narrow focus overlooks unique SOGIE-diverse experiences in other health domains, including chronic diseases [6], cancer screening [7], healthy nutrition [8], and reproductive health and family planning [9]. While PHU websites should have clear health information for populations at risk of public health concerns, this myopic view of relevant health concerns for SDCs excludes holistic health conceptualizations and lacks nuanced understanding of the diversity of SDCs’ rich lived experiences.

Some PHUs referenced specific sexual behaviours that may warrant regular STI testing, however, they did not regularly identify particular subgroups within SDCs who might especially benefit from routine testing. Similarly, mpox vaccine eligibility criteria originally described specific sexual behaviours, with some specific identities listed; a lack of specificity (e.g., language excluding transgender women) in language can be exclusionary, and potential clients may not see themselves represented in these criteria to warrant concern or need for mpox vaccination. While some PHU websites stated their sexual health clinics were welcoming of all people no matter their gender, sexual orientation, race, etc., a lack of specificity in identities fails to recognize how historical systemic oppression (e.g., heterosexism, transphobia) might impact individuals’ (mis)trust of the healthcare system, access to services, and sexual risk taking and decision-making, potentially exacerbating their risk of STI transmission. Additionally, when referencing SDCs at risk of STIs and other infectious diseases, PHUs primarily referred to gay, bisexual, and queer men who have sex with men as the main subgroups. The lack of discussing subgroups within SDCs risks implying SDCs as monolithic.

In this review, most PHUs offered some relevant information about and for SDCs; however, they lacked in several important ways. Implications of this work are to ensure that PHUs have mutual understanding of SOGIE-diversity terms, and how they represent SDCs via their websites and the information and services offered to them. PHU websites often serve as a first point of contact for SDCs and community agencies seeking health information, support, and resources. Comprehensive descriptions about how certain identities/lived experiences and behaviours lead to specific health outcomes would be helpful to include on PHU websites to ensure appropriate and effective engagement with public health services.

Due to societal discrimination, SDCs have been early adopters of the internet [10]. Ontario PHUs, as local health authorities, are instrumental sources for health information. Website language and content play an important role in information provision. Erasure (i.e., exclusion of information about SDCs), inconsistent terminology, and, when present, a predominant focus on sexual and (to a lesser degree) mental health issues, ignores unique SOGIE-diverse health concerns, potentially leading to persistent health disparities [11]. Suggestions to enhance holistic inclusion of SOGIE-diverse needs on PHU websites are: 1) adopting standardized inclusive terminology and definitions (with regular audits to ensure newer terms are added); 2) expanding health topic coverage to comprehensively include additional issues relevant to SDCs such as chronic conditions and reproductive health; 3) collaborating with local SDCs and organizations to ensure comprehensive coverage of relevant information; and 4) creating clear pathways to educational resources, local services, and support.

While gender, and more debatably, sexual orientation, are widely recognized in public health frameworks and scholarship as social determinants of health [12,13], they are often addressed vaguely, without fully addressing systemic oppression and inequities tied to them. Ontario PHUs have a unique opportunity, especially through public-facing platforms (e.g., websites), to educate both SDCs and the wider public. Clear, consistent, and collaborative communication can effectively minimize inequitable health outcomes.

Ethics approval

As information was gleaned from public facing websites, research ethics board approval was not required for this analysis.
