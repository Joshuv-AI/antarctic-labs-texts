Structure–Function Correlations Using Artificial Intelligence–Extracted Retinal Layer Attenuation Measurements in OCT in Eyes with Geographic Atrophy
Authors: Kenta Yoshida, Miao Zhang, Adam Pely, Verena Steffen, Simon S Gao, Usha Chakravarthy, Christina Rabe, Sascha Fauser, Daniela Ferrara, Catherine Cukras
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo investigate the structure–function relationships between retinal layer attenuation on OCT and a range of functional assessments in patients with geographic atrophy (GA).DesignRetrospective analysis of 2 phase III clinical trials.SubjectsOne thousand five hundred and nineteen subjects with GA who were enrolled in phase III studies investigating Lampalizumab: Chroma (NCT02247479) and Spectri (NCT02247531).MethodsStructural retinal features were assessed by artificial intelligence (AI)-based automated segmentation algorithms on OCT images to calculate metrics for outer retinal layer attenuations: loss for external limiting membrane (ELM), ellipsoid zone (EZ), and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE). Functional measures included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), low-luminance visual acuity (LLVA), and reading speed collected in all patients. Microperimetry was collected in a subset of patients.Main Outcome MeasuresCorrelations (Pearson r) between the OCT layer loss metrics with visual function measures.ResultsCross-sectional analysis did not show any notable correlations between loss areas of each layer for the full OCT scan range and BCVA but did show correlations with LLVA (EZ loss area r = –0.39). After spatially defining regions using the ETDRS circles, notable correlations were observed between BCVA and ELM loss area within the central 1 mm diameter circle area (r = –0.46) and for LLVA with the central 3 mm diameter circle even after exclusion of the central 1 mm diameter region. Microperimetry outcomes correlated with OCT layer losses; the number of scotomatous points correlated equally well with total macular ELM and EZ losses (r = 0.67), mean macular sensitivity had the highest correlations with EZ loss areas, and perilesional sensitivity or responding sensitivity showed the highest correlation with the difference in EZ and RPE loss area (r = –0.50, r = –0.48).ConclusionsThe current analyses using AI-based OCT layer losses provide new insights into the spatial dependency of the relationship between anatomical and functional changes in GA. The differential associations noted between BCVA and LLVA with central and paracentral macular pathology, respectively, suggest alternative pathways for diverse aspects of visual function and help inform the design of anatomical and functional assessments for clinical trials in GA.Financial Disclosure(s)Proprietary or commercial disclosure may be found in the Footnotes and Disclosures at the end of this article.Keywords: Geographic atrophy, AMD, OCT, BCVA, Microperimetry

Age-related macular degeneration is a leading cause of vision loss in patients over 50 years.1 The late stages of the disease, particularly geographic atrophy (GA), contribute significantly to the visual disability. Historical definitions of the appearance of GA have been rooted in the delineation of areas of retinal pigment epithelium (RPE) loss. In color fundus photographs, this has focused on the lack of pigmentation from the absence of RPE2,3 and on fundus autofluorescence (FAF), which images the presence of lipofuscin in the RPE, thus providing better contrast to delineate where the RPE is absent and the surrounding, apparently preserved retina.4 Using the area of hypoautofluorescence on FAF imaging has been the mainstay of the outcome measures in GA trials, and the growth rate of GA area has been used as a primary outcome for clinical trials in this indication. However, the growth rates established using these measures only reflect the rates of RPE loss and not the other aspects of the neurosensory retina.

Histopathologically, GA is not only seen to correlate to areas with RPE loss but also with photoreceptor loss and external limiting membrane (ELM) loss as well as to changes in the choriocapillaris.5 The correlates of these changes in histopathologic study can be readily seen with the evolution of OCT, which has become a mainstay of retinal imaging.6 Changes to the outer retina in eyes with GA have been long observed to extend beyond the areas of RPE loss, which has been described as the junctional zone of atrophy, representing the region between atrophic areas and more normal retina7,8 with notable range of changes to the photoreceptor ellipsoid zone (EZ) band. The ability to document aspects of the retinal abnormalities in GA beyond RPE loss have evolved with OCT imaging, as well as the ability to quantify those pathological changes leveraging artificial intelligence (AI)-based image segmentation and analysis. For example, recent analyses of clinical studies of GA have highlighted the potential of using AI-based retinal layer segmentation of OCT images, where measures of the EZ layer, have revealed a larger treatment effect that was detectable at earlier timepoints, in comparison to the change in GA area measured with FAF imaging.9, 10, 11, 12

The goal of clinical trials in GA has focused on assessing for differences in structural changes to reveal evidence of efficacy. However, demonstration of visual function benefit is also critical to reveal the real benefit of an intervention to patients. The key measure of visual function in ophthalmology trials has been historically based in visual acuity, but this measure is especially challenging in GA as it is highly dependent on where the GA is localized spatially in the macula. Studies of impact on central best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) have shown a slow but definite decline over 2 years for better seeing eyes (20/50 or better) with 40% losing 3 lines of visual acuity or more and an average of 22 letters of visual acuity loss over 5 years across all eyes with GA.13

Correlating structural changes in GA area with visual acuity loss in the shorter term remains particularly challenging.14 Efforts have been conducted with measures of anatomic atrophy defined by atrophy area measured on color fundus photographs or hypoautofluorescence on FAF, which are thought to represent areas of RPE and photoreceptor loss but which may not reflect the totality of retinal abnormalities in GA, as recently demonstrated by OCT assessment.15 Additional functional tests such as microperimetry can assess the retinal sensitivity in specific regions of the macula, allowing for functional testing outside of the fovea and with spatial knowledge of the area being tested. Analyses using microperimetry have demonstrated better anatomical/functional correlation in GA.7,16, 17, 18 Machine learning approaches using paired structural information from OCT with retinal sensitivities obtained with microperimetry have shown that retinal function can be reasonably estimated from a combination of structural OCT features.19,20

Herein, we present data collected for the lampalizumab program in patients with GA, including both FAF images and OCT volumes, as well as visual function assessments including BCVA, low-luminance visual acuity (LLVA), and microperimetry, with the objective to explore anatomical/functional correlations in GA leveraging AI-based OCT segmentation.

Methods

Clinical Trial Data

Data from 2 phase III clinical trials for lampalizumab in patients with GA were used in the study; Chroma [NCT02247479] and Spectri [NCT02247531]. The trials adhered to the Declaration of Helsinki and were Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act compliant. Protocols were approved by the institutional review board at each study site before the trials started. All patients provided written informed consent for future medical research and analyses. Detailed information of the clinical study design including patient eligibility have been previously reported.18,21 Key inclusion and exclusion criteria included participants aged ≥50 years with bilateral GA secondary to age-related macular degeneration without choroidal neovascularization. Geographic atrophy lesions had sizes ranging from 2.54 to 17.78 mm2 (1–7 disc areas) and a BCVA letter score of ≥49 (Snellen equivalent, 20/100 or better).21 Briefly, BCVA and LLVA were assessed on ETDRS chart at specified visits including study day 1 (baseline), week 48, and week 96. Low luminance deficit was calculated as the difference between the BCVA and LLVA measures. Fundus autofluorescence and OCT images of both eyes were obtained at screening and specified visits. Only images collected on SPECTRALIS HRA-OCT2 devices (Heidelberg Engineering) were included in this study. Fundus autofluorescence images were evaluated for GA lesion area at the Doheny Image Reading Center. Reading performance was assessed using the MNRead acuity cards,22 and has been previously described (Wu et al, manuscript under review) with each card consisting of 19 sentences and each sentence having a total of 60 characters across 10 words. Testing in the study eye was performed monocularly with the appropriate near refractive correction, occluding the nontested eye, and starting with the sentence with the largest print size. Multiple measures of reading performance were calculated including the mean reading speed of the 10 largest print sizes, previously termed the Reading Accessibility Index (ACC).23 Patient-reported outcome with the 25-item National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire (VFQ-25), whose 25 items comprise 11 subscales on different aspects of vision-related functioning and quality of life and 1 item on general health, as described previously,24 was evaluated in this study. Microperimetry assessments were performed only for the study eye at screening, baseline, and at specified visits including week 48 and week 96 using the 10-2 pattern on the MP-1 mesopic microperimeter (NAVIS software version 1.7.1; Nidek). Microperimetry assessments were performed only for patients enrolled at selected sites and who were willing and eligible to participate in this substudy. Details of the microperimetry assessments have been described previously18 but briefly, mean sensitivity, number of absolute scotomatous points, perilesional sensitivity (average of points adjacent to absolute scotomatous points), and responding sensitivity (average of all nonscotomatous points; >0 dB at baseline) were calculated for all eyes participating in the microperimetry substudy.

In the current study, only data from the study eyes were evaluated.

OCT Volume Segmentation and Calculation of the Retinal Layer Loss Area

OCT volumes (20 × 20 degree scan, 1024 A-scans per B-scan, 496 pixels per A-scan and 49 B-scans per volume) were processed with an internally developed automated segmentation model named EyeNotate, which employs a DeepLabv3+ architecture.25 Briefly, the model was initially trained on a dataset comprising annotations made by experts on 6718 OCT B-scans from 189 subjects with intermediate age-related macular degeneration and GA (no images from Chroma and Spectri were used for the training). The model segments 7 retinal layers and drusen. Based on the layer segmentation, en face thickness maps for individual layers were generated (Fig 1). Representative examples of layer segmentations are shown in Supplementary Figure S1 (available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). Using the generated en face maps, loss area for the following 3 retinal layers was calculated. The layer loss was defined as the thickness of the layers less than the thresholds: ELM+myoid zone measured from anterior aspect of the ELM reflectivity band to anterior aspect of the EZ band (below referred to as ELM loss, <10 μm), EZ+interdigitation zone measured from the anterior aspect of the EZ band to the anterior aspect of the RPE band (below referred to as EZ loss, <15 μm), and RPE measured from the anterior aspect of the RPE band to the posterior aspect of the RPE band (below referred to as RPE loss <10 μm). Within an eye, the EZ loss area–RPE loss area was also examined. In addition to the loss areas for the entire en face maps, loss areas were also calculated for the area within certain distances from the center of the OCT scan (ranges 0.25–3 mm radius, Fig 1). In this study, only the OCT volumes obtained at the screening visit were segmented.

The model specifically identified areas including external limiting membrane (ELM), ellipsoid zone (EZ), and retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), and en face thickness maps for individual layers were generated. GCC = ganglion cell complex; ILM = internal limiting membrane; INL = inner nuclear layer; IPL = inner plexiform layer; IZ = interdigitation zone; MZ = myoid zone; ONL = outer nuclear layer; OPL = outer plexiform layer; RNFL = retinal nerve fiber layer.

The model performance was evaluated by comparing Intersection over Union metrics between human–human and model–human in a holdout set of 31 B-scans with RPE loss lesion >30 μm, which showed reasonable performance (mean Intersection over Union for RPE/ELM/EZ loss region of 0.83∼0.93 for human–human and 0.75∼0.78 for model–human). Note that the human–human Intersection over Union is overoptimistic because human #2 reviewed human #1 results during annotation. The performance was also evaluated for EZ and RPE loss. Two expert retina specialists (including C.C.) annotated 4 equally spaced B-scans each for 14 OCT volumes for EZ and RPE loss, along with EyeNotate, and DICE score were calculated for en face layer loss. Mean DICE scores for layer loss were 0.9 and 0.88 (EZ and RPE) between 2 human experts, while those between human experts and EyeNotate were 0.87 and 0.80∼0.83.

Correlation Analysis Between Imaging-Based Measures and Visual Functions

Cross-sectional analysis was performed for evaluating the correlations between imaging-based measures and visual functions at the baseline visits of the clinical trials of interest. Correlations between imaging-based metrics (GA lesion area measured on FAF by the reading center, and retinal layers’ loss area measured on OCT with AI-based segmentation as defined earlier) and functional measures (BCVA, LLVA, and microperimetry outcomes) were assessed with Pearson correlation coefficient (r). For microperimetry, the following metrics were evaluated as described previously in detail:18 mean macular sensitivity, number of absolute scotomatous points, perilesional sensitivity, and responding sensitivity.

Spatial Analyses

To investigate the spatial correlation between functional measures and certain areas of the retina, we also included analyses using circles of various sizes centered at the center of the OCT volume. These ranged from a circle of 0.25 mm radius, 0.5 mm radius (the central subfield of the ETDRS grid), 1 mm radius, 1.5 mm radius (the inner circle of the ETDRS grid), 3 mm radius (the outer circle of the ETDRS grid), and a ring shape consisting of an area between a circle of 0.5 mm radius and 1.5 mm radius (Supplementary Figure S2, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org). The fraction of each area occupied by the retinal layer loss was then used for analyses correlating with visual function measures.

Results

Description of Eyes Analyzed

Geographic atrophy area measurements on FAF by the reading center were available for 2175 patients from the 2 trials. Our analysis included 1773 patients who had OCT volumes with the SPECTRALIS system for segmentation, and ≥1 functional metric of BCVA, LLVA, ACC, or microperimetry available. Among them, 733 participants had data for reading speed, 893 participants for VFQ-25, and 176 participants for baseline microperimetry outcomes available. See Supplementary Table S1, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org, for descriptive statistics.

AI-Based Retinal Layer Loss on OCT Compared to Reading Center-Based GA Area on FAF

Artificial intelligence-based measures of RPE loss were generated for all baseline OCT volumes and compared to the reading center-based GA areas on FAF. Figure 2A illustrates the high agreement observed between these measures with a Pearson correlation coefficient r = 0.90 (see Supplementary Figure S3, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org, for Bland–Altman plot). Within an eye, we compared the area of EZ loss compared to the area of RPE loss. In all but 1 case, the area of EZ loss was larger than the area of RPE loss. We note that there was a large range for the difference between EZ loss area and RPE loss area in study eyes. Many eyes had EZ loss only slightly larger than the area of RPE loss, whereas others had areas of EZ loss that were 2-3 times that of the RPE loss (1.44 ± 0.41, mean ± standard deviation). The correlation matrix in Figure 2C summarizes the intraocular correlations between the OCT-based segmentations of different retina layers as well as for the GA area based on FAF measures.

Correlations Between Retinal Layer Loss Area on OCT and Visual Function

Measures of retinal abnormalities were compared to BCVA measures for study eyes. There was low correlation between BCVA and GA area on FAF, as well as between BCVA and any of the retinal layer loss areas on OCT (RPE loss, EZ loss, and ELM loss) (Fig 3). Weak to moderate correlations were found between LLVA and the FAF or OCT area metrics, with the highest correlations observed between LLVA and EZ loss area (r = –0.39), while lower correlations were observed with RPE loss area on OCT or GA area on FAF (–0.23 or –0.28). Reading speed ACC had similar low levels of correlations with GA area on FAF, ELM, and RPE (–0.21 to –0.19). The National Eye Institute VFQ-25 scores also had low correlations (total EZ loss area, r = –0.22).

Solid blue lines and shaded ribbons represent linear regression lines and 95% confidence intervals. (B) Pearson correlation coefficient. ACC = Reading Accessibility Index; BCVA = best-corrected visual acuity; ELM = external limiting membrane; EZ = ellipsoid zone; FAF = fundus autofluorescence; GA = geographic atrophy; LLD = low luminance deficit; LLVA = low-luminance visual acuity; RPE = retinal pigment epithelium; VFQ-25 = 25-item National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire.

As BCVA is driven by a local, central function within the macular area, we investigated the correlations between the GA area on FAF and retinal layer loss on OCT within specific areas of the retina. When retinal layer loss was analyzed by macular subfield rather than total area, distinct correlations with visual function emerged. The correlations between BCVA and retinal layer loss areas demonstrate a spatial dependency based on the distance from the center of the OCT scan (Fig 4). For BCVA, higher correlations were seen with the loss area within the regions closest to the center of the OCT scan. For loss areas within 0.5 mm diameter, ELM and RPE loss area showed moderate correlation (r = –0.49 and –0.43) followed by correlation with EZ loss area (r = –0.36). In contrast, higher correlations were revealed between LLVA and retina layer loss areas within more eccentric regions from the center, with the highest correlations observed with regions within 3 mm diameter circle with EZ area loss showing the highest negative correlation (r = –0.45), while ELM loss and RPE area loss showed r of –0.38, and –0.23, respectively. The correlations in the wider regions were maintained even after excluding the loss area in the center region (1 mm diameter); similar r was observed for the loss areas calculated within a ring between the 1 mm and 3 mm diameter subfields. We explored whether this relationship was dependent on the central visual acuity of the eyes by analyzing this by visual acuity quartiles. A similar spatial relationship for LLVA was observed with all visual acuity quartiles (Supplementary Figure S4, available at www.ophthalmologyscience.org).

Correlations between BCVA, LLVA, or LLD, and the OCT-based layer loss areas in the center region of the OCT scan for (A) ELM, (B) EZ, and (C) RPE. Numbers represent Pearson correlation coefficient. ACC = Reading Accessibility Index; BCVA = best-corrected visual acuity; ELM = external limiting membrane; EZ = ellipsoid zone; LLD = low luminance deficit; LLVA = low-luminance visual acuity; RPE = retinal pigment epithelium; VFQ-25 = 25-item National Eye Institute Visual Function Questionnaire.

Reading speed also showed higher correlations when analyzed with respect to the distance to the center of the OCT scan. Reading speed ACC was most correlated with the ELM loss area in a central 2 mm diameter circle (r = –0.51) and with EZ loss area in the central 1 mm diameter circle (r = –0.47). Larger circles or circles excluding the centermost area had much lower correlations. Analyzing the relationship of the National Eye Institute VFQ-25 score with macular subfields did not reveal any additional correlations.

Correlations Between OCT Layer Loss Area and Microperimetry Metrics

Correlations between microperimetry outcomes and GA area on FAF or retinal layer loss on OCT were evaluated in 176 eyes (Fig 5). We examined the similar OCT metrics as in the aforementioned acuity correlations, but also included the metric of the difference of the EZ loss area and the RPE loss area. This difference is small in lesions where the EZ loss is similar to the RPE loss and positive where the EZ loss area is greater than the RPE loss area. The number of scotomatous points detected in microperimetry correlated with the GA area measured on FAF, with the area of ELM loss, and with the area of EZ loss (r = 0.67∼0.69), followed by RPE loss (r = 0.57) on OCT. The mean macular sensitivity also demonstrated negative correlation with the area of EZ loss (r = –0.58) on OCT. The perilesional sensitivity representing the average of the values that are next to scotomatous points, demonstrated the highest negative correlation to the difference between the area of the EZ loss and RPE loss (EZ-RPE, r = –0.5). When the EZ loss to RPE loss difference was small (i.e. the EZ loss ends near where the RPE loss ends) the perilesional sensitivity was high, whereas when the EZ loss exceeded the RPE loss significantly, the perilesional sensitivity was lower. The average sensitivity of all nonscotomatous points (mean responding sensitivity) demonstrated correlation with the area of EZ loss (r = –0.45) and with the EZ area minus RPE loss difference (r = –0.48).

Discussion

In this analysis of a historical dataset from 2 large clinical trials with robust measurements of anatomical and functional parameters in eyes with GA, we show important associations of clinical relevance and value. First, AI-based OCT segmentation of RPE loss and hypoautofluorescence area on FAF showed a high correlation. Second, we demonstrate the spatial dependency of BCVA and LLVA functional measures to different regions of EZ and RPE loss. Best-corrected visual acuity correlated with the smallest central area, whereas LLVA correlated best with the area from 0.5 to 1.5 mm from the fovea. Third, a key measure of importance to patients notably reading performance correlated with central layer integrity. Fourth, on microperimetry, the number of scotomatous points correlated well with the area of EZ layer loss. Fifth, perilesional sensitivity on microperimetry had the highest negative correlation to the difference between EZ and RPE loss area. These findings are of high importance as they can inform the conduct and design of future studies and trials of GA.

Investigating structure and function correlations is central to understanding the repercussions of retinal diseases and for focusing the design of clinical trial endpoints, which are essential for demonstrating efficacy of an intervention and impact to patients. While progress is being made in the development of interventions for GA in slowing structural changes as measured by GA growth on FAF,26,27 far less progress has been made in demonstrating functional efficacy.28 Given the nature of the relatively slow degenerative process of GA, and the commonly observed foveal sparing in the earlier manifestation of the disease, it is understandable that the pragmatic and commonly used measures of function pose challenges in capturing the visual decline experienced by patients with GA. As the goal of having an intervention that is meaningful to patients and their providers, and thereby acceptable also to regulators, a better understanding as to the functional consequences of structural correlates is necessary. Then, with a better understanding of the relationship between retinal structural changes and function, this will add to the ability to focus assessments to demonstrate the functional impact of the disease.

The large number of eyes in this study that were analyzed to investigate the anatomical/functional correlation in GA highlight the importance of spatial analysis for visual function interpretation. As previously demonstrated, there is little if any relationship between GA area on FAF and BCVA.24 Given that BCVA metrics are driven by the fixation location, the impact of the GA lesion on BCVA is highly dependent on its spatial configuration, rather than its total area. Our analysis that investigated the contribution of areas closest to the center of the fovea reveals that the amount of retinal structural changes in the centermost area, in our case within 0.25 mm radius of the fovea (here, assessed at the center of the OCT scan), was most correlated to BCVA. Interestingly, measures of RPE integrity, either assessed by RPE loss on OCT or even GA area on FAF, were most correlated with BCVA, more than EZ loss.

In contrast, our spatial and structural analysis of LLVA revealed that the structural feature that was most associated with this functional measure was EZ loss on OCT and the spatial area that was most highly correlated was in the 3 mm diameter subfield. Our findings suggest that areas of the retina remote to the fovea were drivers of the correlation, rather than the fovea itself. We further confirmed this interpretation by conducting the analysis of the 3 mm diameter area subtracting out the central 1 mm foveal circle (resulting in a ring of radius 0.5 mm to 1.5 mm) which maintained the strength of the correlation. We tested whether this relationship with more eccentric areas of the fovea was dependent on the BCVA of the cohort by conducting subgroup analyses of BCVA quartiles, but the relationship held similarly for all BCVA levels.

While the cellular drivers for LLVA have yet to be determined, several hypotheses have been proposed. The LLVA test is a discrimination task conducted under mesopic conditions, and therefore has been thought, by some, to be a test of central–cone function.29 However, our results demonstrating a higher correlation of a circle with a radius 1.5 mm than radius 0.25 mm does not support this. Another hypothesis has been that higher-order cone–cone interactions driven by surrounding mechanisms of the horizontal and amacrine cells would support a less focused area of correlation. However, correlations between EZ and LLVA were similar within 3 mm diameter, even after excluding the central 1 mm diameter (Fig 4B, comparing between the third and the last columns from the right), suggesting that the central 0.5 mm radius circle did not contribute to the correlation. This argues against a central cone-centered origin and instead supports a contribution from an area outside the central fovea which likely represents a need for rods in this response and rod–cone interactions.30,31

This novel analysis provides insight into the structural drivers of both BCVA and LLVA, in terms of cell layers as well as in terms of spatial distribution. The work from Curcio and collaborators has generated a histologic mapping of the cellular population at different eccentricities from the fovea, demonstrating that photoreceptor subtype and RPE geometry varies greatly with distance from the fovea, and it thereby follows that retina function will vary greatly based on this anatomy of the retina and the impact of pathologic changes.32 While BCVA has been recognized to be a functional test that reflects cone density and packing in the foveal area, the cellular drivers of LLVA have not been well elucidated. Our finding that LLVA is most highly correlated with photoreceptor EZ presence in the region between 0.5 mm and 1.5 mm from the fovea is novel and does point to a functional response generated from a mixed rod–cone source. These results are also consistent with the results of Balakas et al20 who used a different approach to interrogate the structure–function relationship in eyes with GA. Their work used a random forest model using OCT segmentations as inputs to develop a model to predict BCVA and LLVA. While the approach and datasets were different, they similarly concluded that for LLVA, the photoreceptor layer was most important in the prediction of LLVA with evidence that a region more eccentric from the fovea was involved. They also found that OCT-determined RPE loss measures occurring in the fovea was the feature that was most important in the random forest model for predicting BCVA.

Reading speed was best correlated with central areas of the macula; it was notably different from BCVA, as it had best correlation with larger central circles (1-2 mm diameter) while larger circles of 3 mm or 6 mm diameter had less strong correlations. These findings highlight the dependency between reading speed with regions with high-density cones but which also spans an area larger than the fovea and thus consistent with a minimal required reading rectangle.33 As reading is a particularly important task in terms of patient reported outcomes, this association is of value when analyzing results of trials which incorporate this functional measure in its portfolio of tests.

Our analyses show that microperimetry functional measures and OCT-based correlations have clear spatial interdependency and indicate the spatial regions that are driving the function in focused macular areas. These illustrate the utility of understanding not only the geometry of RPE loss, whether measured by FAF or OCT, but of needing to take into account the area of EZ loss as well. With a better understanding of these relationships, an argument could be made on using certain surrogate structural markers.

Conclusions

In summary, the current study has characterized the anatomical and functional relationships in GA, based on the correlations observed between detailed measures of retinal morphology and a comprehensive portfolio of functional measures in a robust clinical trial dataset. These data have the potential to facilitate prespecification of analysis plans which could highlight areas of the retina most likely to be affected by functional change over time. Our next steps will be to investigate if baseline structural features correlate with anatomically based GA growth rates and with changes in function over 1 and 2 years. These analyses could highlight the population of eyes that would most demonstrate a functional benefit in interventions targeting the slowing of GA growth and illustrate the benefit to the patients.

Scatter plots for correlations between BCVA, LLVA, or LLD, and the OCT-based layer loss areas in the center region of the OCT scan for (A) ELM, (B) EZ, and (C) RPE. BCVA = best-corrected visual acuity; LLVA = low-luminance visual acuity; LLD = low luminance deficit; ELM = external limiting membrane; RPE = retinal pigment epithelium; EZ = ellipsoid zone.
