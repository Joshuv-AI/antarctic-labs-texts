Comparative Evaluation of Caries Removal Efficacy of Ceramic Bur, Polymer Smart Bur, and Conventional Tungsten Carbide Bur in Primary Molars: A Clinical Study
Authors: L Krishnaveni, Jayanta K Dash, Ratna R Baliarsingh, Prayas Ray, Monika Khwairakpam, Sarita Das
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractAim and backgroundIn pediatric dentistry, successful dental care involves both the proper execution of the procedure and the development of a positive approach to dental visits. Recent advances in conservative dentistry have introduced self-limiting methods for caries removal, designed to reduce pain and discomfort while enhancing patient comfort. The present in vivo study aimed to compare the efficiency of caries removal, the time required, and pain perception associated with ceramic burs, polymer smart burs, and conventional tungsten carbide burs in primary molars.MethodsA total of 90 primary molars from 30 children (21 boys and 9 girls) aged between 5 and 9 years (mean age 6.77 ± 1.36) were included. Caries excavation was carried out using tungsten carbide burs (group I), polymer burs (group II), and ceramic burs (group III). The parameters assessed were the efficacy of caries removal, time required for excavation, and pain perception, and comparisons were made among the three groups.ResultsPolymer burs required significantly more time for caries excavation than both tungsten carbide and ceramic burs. Caries removal efficacy was highest with tungsten carbide burs, followed by ceramic burs, while polymer burs showed the lowest efficacy, with statistically significant differences between groups. Pain perception was lower with ceramic and polymer burs compared to tungsten carbide burs.ConclusionCeramic burs are more efficient than polymer burs in minimally invasive caries excavation.Clinical significanceMinimally invasive dentistry in pediatric patients is clinically significant as it focuses on early prevention, preserves tooth structure, minimizes discomfort and trauma, and supports long-term oral health through less invasive, more child-friendly techniques. Introduction

Conventional caries excavation methods employ hand excavators in combination with high- and low-speed handpieces fitted with stainless steel, carbide, or diamond burs. These instruments are highly effective and allow for the rapid removal of decayed enamel and dentin.1 However, such approaches have notable limitations, including excessive loss of healthy tooth structure, a lack of conservatism, and the generation of pressure and thermal effects on the pulp. Additional concerns such as vibration, noise, pain, and the frequent need for local anesthesia often contribute to negative treatment experiences, particularly fear and anxiety among pediatric patients.2

Minimal invasive dentistry emphasizes removing only the infected and irreversibly damaged dentin while conserving the surrounding tooth structure that has the potential for remineralization. Within minimally invasive dentistry, self-limiting caries excavation techniques have been introduced to ensure a more controlled and standardized approach to dentin removal.3

The polymer bur, a disposable self-limiting instrument, was first introduced by Boston in 2003. Its design principle is based on the difference in hardness between various dental tissues. Manufactured from medical-grade polyether ketone-ketone, the bur possesses a Knoop hardness value of 50 and features straight, shovel-shaped cutting edges.4 Its mechanism of action involves a plowing motion, whereby the blades initially compress the carious dentin locally and then displace the softened material along the surface of the underlying sound dentin. The process continues until the blades fracture at the surface level, dislodging the loosened debris, which is then brought to the surface. When in contact with healthy dentin, the bur's cutting capacity is markedly reduced, resulting in abrasion rather than cutting of sound or minimally affected tissue. Due to this property, polymer burs are considered “dentin-safe” and “self-limiting.” This technique provides a conservative, patient-friendly option for the removal of dentinal caries and can help encourage positive patient behavior during restorative procedures in pediatric dentistry.5,6

One of the notable innovations in minimally invasive dentistry is the introduction of alumina-based ceramic burs reinforced with stabilized zirconia (CeraBur, K1SM, Komet). The design of these burs allows selective excavation of diseased tissue while conserving sound tooth structure. Used in a slow-speed handpiece operating between 1,000 and 1,500 rpm, ceramic burs provide smooth operation, enhanced tactile sensitivity, high cutting efficiency, and corrosion resistance.3,7,8 Hence, this study was designed to compare caries removal efficacy, the time required, and the pain perception by using ceramic bur, polymer smart bur, and conventional tungsten carbide bur in primary molars.

Materials and Methods

Before commencing the study, the research protocol received approval from the Institutional Ethics Committee of SCB Dental College and Hospital (Approval No: 105/2021). The study participants were recruited from the Outpatient Department of Pediatric and Preventive Dentistry at SCB Dental College and Hospital, Cuttack. Written informed consent was obtained from parents or guardians after they were provided with a comprehensive explanation of the study procedures.

A total of 30 children (21 males and 9 females), aged between 5 and 9 years (mean age: 6.77 ± 1.36 years), were enrolled. Each child presented with at least three carious primary maxillary or mandibular molars, yielding a total of 90 teeth for the study. These teeth were randomly allocated into three groups of 30 each (Fig. 1) using a computer-generated randomization sequence. All clinical procedures were performed in a single appointment by the same operator to maintain consistency.

Clinical Procedure

For all cases, topical anesthesia (Lidocaine Topical Aerosol USP 15% w/w) was applied to the gingiva surrounding the selected tooth. Isolation was achieved with a rubber dam (Coltene Rubber Dam Kit Hygenic) to enhance visibility and efficiency.

Caries removal was then carried out using a polymer smart bur (SS White SmartBurs II) in a slow-speed handpiece operating at 5,000–10,000 rpm. Parents were instructed to report to the Department of Pedodontics if any adverse events were observed after treatment.

Assessment Procedure

For all groups, completion of caries removal was confirmed visually and tactically—absence of soft dentin and smooth passage of a dental explorer without catches or tug-back. Additionally, a caries detector dye (Reveal, Prevest DenPro, USA) was applied for 10 seconds using an applicator tip, rinsed off, and the outcome graded on a 0–5 scale by a blinded investigator using the criteria proposed by Ericson et al.9

The duration of caries removal was recorded by an assistant using a stopwatch, beginning at the start of cavity preparation and ending once the infected dentin was completely excavated. Pain perception during the procedure was assessed for each group using the face, legs, activity, cry, and consolability (FLACC) scale10 by an examiner.

Statistical Analysis

All collected data were entered into a Microsoft Excel worksheet and analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS; IBM Corp., Released 2020, IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, Version 27.0, Armonk, New York, USA). Descriptive statistics, including the mean and standard deviation, were calculated for continuous variables, while frequencies and proportions were determined for categorical variables. Fisher's exact test was employed to compare caries removal efficacy across study groups. The differences in working time between groups were analyzed using the Kruskal–Wallis H test, followed by pairwise comparisons with the Mann–Whitney U test and Bonferroni's correction. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses.

Discussion

The minimally invasive approach in dentistry emphasizes conserving natural tooth structure by promoting remineralization, limiting the removal of decayed tissue, and restoring only when necessary. This method is considered less aggressive, focusing on preserving healthy areas of the tooth and supporting its inherent capacity for remineralization.7 In primary teeth, however, achieving a balance between removing both mineralized and demineralized tissues while maintaining pulp vitality remains a challenge. The idea of self-limiting and painless caries removal has gained considerable importance among dental professionals and researchers, especially in pediatric dentistry. Multiple strategies are being investigated to advance this concept, yet identifying an ideal biologically sound and conservative technique for caries removal continues to be a matter of debate.11

Visual and tactile examination is the most common method for evaluating caries removal, but it relies heavily on the operator's judgment and experience, making it prone to inaccuracies. Caries detector dyes were introduced to enhance the precision of caries removal by differentiating between the outer, irreversibly denatured infected dentin and the inner, reversibly affected dentin. These dyes specifically highlight degraded collagen fibers, making carious areas more identifiable. Dyes with higher molecular weight limit penetration into porous tissues, thereby reducing the chances of overstaining and unnecessary removal of sound dentin.12 However, research has shown that the absence of staining does not necessarily indicate complete bacterial elimination. It is now well established that the staining mechanism of these dyes is associated with the organic framework of demineralized dentin, and not with bacterial localization.13,14 In the present study, the extracted teeth could not be sacrificed for advanced techniques, such as confocal laser scanning microscopy to confirm thorough caries removal. Therefore, a combination of the available evaluation methods was adopted.

Standard caries removal techniques employ hand excavators along with rotary instruments, such as stainless steel, carbide, and diamond burs, which are operated using both high- and low-speed handpieces. Their superior stiffness and hardness make them more durable and resistant to wear during cutting. However, this increased hardness also makes them more brittle, leading to a higher risk of chipping or fracturing.15 Carbide burs may produce heat, pressure, vibrations, and noise during use, which can cause discomfort or pain and often necessitate local anesthesia. Such factors are particularly concerning in children, as they tend to heighten anxiety and reduce overall treatment comfort.16

Ceramic burs are designed to facilitate controlled excavation by enabling clinicians to differentiate between carious and sound dentin through tactile feedback. Their cutting efficiency is optimized for soft dentin, while the narrow neck design improves clinical visibility. In addition, these burs are biocompatible and resistant to corrosion, offering smooth operation during use. Studies have also indicated that their functional lifespan is significantly longer than that of conventional tungsten carbide burs. The white working surface of the ceramic bur provides greater visual contrast with the darker shade of carious dentin, thereby enhancing detection and removal compared to traditional carbide instruments.3,8

Polymer-based smart burs represent a modern innovation aimed at the selective removal of carious dentin. They are engineered to eliminate only the softened, infected tissue and gradually lose their cutting efficiency once they contact sound or partially demineralized dentin.17 When reaching healthy dentin, the blades bend and deform, thereby reducing their cutting capacity and providing distinct tactile feedback to the operator. Unlike conventional rotary burs, which remove dentin through chip formation by orthogonal cutting, polymer burs rely on mechanisms, such as plastic deformation and shearing.18 One significant advantage of polymer burs is that they can be single-use, reducing the risk of cross-infection, unlike conventional diamond burs.19 Nevertheless, certain limitations exist: they demand precise technique, are relatively costly, and are prone to damage if used against enamel or intact dentin. Moreover, the thicker smear layer produced during their use can interfere with adhesive penetration, potentially compromising the bonding performance of restorative materials, such as glass ionomers.20

The duration of caries removal plays a crucial role in pediatric dentistry, as prolonged procedures can negatively influence a child's cooperation and behavior. In this study, the time required for excavation with polymer burs was considerably longer than that observed with tungsten carbide and ceramic burs, which is consistent with findings from earlier research.21–26 The extended working time associated with polymer burs may be explained by their lower material hardness, the slower rotational speed at which they function, the defined cutting trajectory, and the frequent need for replacement once deformation occurs upon contacting sound dentin.27 In the present study, the mean time recorded for ceramic burs was 246.43 ± 68.76 seconds, and a statistically significant difference was noted when compared with polymer burs and conventional burs. Furthermore, ceramic burs demonstrated shorter excavation times compared with other minimally invasive techniques, such as hand excavation and chemomechanical caries removal. This efficiency can be attributed to their composition of alumina–yttria ceramic, which provides high wear resistance, superior cutting ability, and sufficient hardness, thereby enabling more rapid caries removal.8,28

In the present investigation, the highest caries removal efficacy was achieved with tungsten carbide burs, followed by ceramic burs, while polymer burs demonstrated the lowest efficacy, with a statistically significant difference noted among the groups. Within the ceramic bur group, complete caries removal was observed in 70% of the cases, aligning with previous reports.21,29 According to Dammaschke et al., ceramic burs left a greater amount of residual caries on the cavity floor compared with tungsten carbide burs, although this difference was not statistically significant.3 Similarly, Neves Ade et al. reported a greater presence of unprotected collagen after excavation with ceramic burs, indicating that residual carious dentin may persist following preparation. Clinically, ceramic burs offer enhanced tactile sensitivity compared with air rotor instruments, allowing smooth removal of softened dentin. Once sound dentin is reached, the operator perceives resistance and vibration, serving as an indicator to cease excavation.30

In the current study, polymer burs demonstrated the lowest caries removal efficacy, with complete caries elimination achieved in only 43.3% of teeth. These findings are consistent with earlier reports comparing polymer burs to conventional burs.24,26,31 The results suggest that polymer burs are significantly less effective in removing dentinal caries than both ceramic and tungsten carbide burs in primary molars. This reduced efficiency may be explained by their self-limiting property, as the cutting blades deform upon encountering sound dentin, thereby limiting further excavation. Repeatedly checking the cutting flutes and frequently replacing the bur during treatment not only extends the duration of the procedure but also risks surpassing the patient's tolerance and compliance thresholds. This is a significant challenge in providing pediatric dental care.32

Minimally invasive dentistry has introduced self-limiting excavation methods that aim to reduce pain and discomfort, thereby creating a more child-friendly treatment experience.33 Allen et al. demonstrated that limiting dentin removal to the outer infected layer, while maintaining the odontoblastic reaction zone, allows caries excavation to be performed without requiring local anesthesia.4

The use of ceramic and polymer (smart) burs for dentin caries excavation caused less discomfort compared to conventional carbide burs. In particular, ceramic burs were associated with reduced pain, likely because they operate at low rotational speeds. This technique minimizes the number of dentinal tubules cut, which in turn reduces pain sensation compared to conventional carbide burs and air rotor instruments. These observations are consistent with the results reported by Matar and Salma.29 The increased discomfort with high-speed instruments can be attributed to the vibrations produced, the heightened sensitivity of vital dentin, and the rise in temperature at the cutting surface. An increase in intrapulpal temperature can trigger inflammatory changes within the pulp tissue, which in turn amplifies the patient's pain perception.34

The study was limited by a small sample size and a lack of standardization in lesion size. Other minimally invasive techniques were not included for comparison, and microbial analysis was not performed. Furthermore, the lack of follow-up prevented assessment of the long-term success of the restorations and the development of secondary caries.

Clinical Significance

Minimally invasive dentistry in pediatric patients is clinically significant as it focuses on early prevention, preserves tooth structure, minimizes discomfort and trauma, and supports long-term oral health through less invasive, more child-friendly techniques. This study provides evidence that the ceramic bur is an effective, minimally invasive tool for caries excavation.
