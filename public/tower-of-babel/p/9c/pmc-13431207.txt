Intraocular pressure and optic disc cupping in school-aged children: An epidemiological study in Taiwan
Authors: Hsiao-Lien Chang, Yen-Ching Lin, Yun Hsia, Jehn-Yu Huang, Bo-I Kuo, Tsing-Hong Wang, Chao-Wen Lin, Yao-Lin Liu, Tzu-Hsun Tsai, I-Jong Wang, Chien-Chia Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PURPOSE:The purpose of the study was to investigate intraocular pressure (IOP), cup-to-disc ratio (CDR), and ocular biometrics in school-aged children in Taiwan and to evaluate the factors associated with IOP and CDR.MATERIALS AND METHODS:This nationwide school-based study prospectively enrolled children aged 6–18 years. Cycloplegic autorefraction, A-scan ultrasonography, IOP measurement with a rebound tonometer, and CDR assessment with indirect ophthalmoscopy were performed. High myopia (HM) was defined as spherical equivalent (SE) <−5.00 diopters or axial length (AL) ≥26 mm. Linear mixed-effects models evaluated the associations between ocular parameters, age, and sex and identified factors associated with IOP and CDR.RESULTS:We enrolled 6264 eyes from 3132 children (1435 girls and 1697 boys; mean age 12.1 ± 3.4 years; mean IOP 17.2 ± 2.6 mmHg). The median CDR was 0.3 (range: 0.1–0.9); 10.8% of eyes had a CDR >0.4, and 1.8% had a CDR >0.6. The overall prevalence of HM was 8.0%, increasing to 25.4% at age 17. AL, anterior and vitreous chamber depth, IOP, and CDR all increased with age (all P < 0.001, except IOP, P = 0.006), whereas lens thickness and SE decreased (P < 0.001). AL was independently associated with IOP (β = 0.118, P = 0.010) and CDR (β = 0.006, P < 0.001). Among adolescents aged 15–18 years, HM eyes had significantly greater IOP than non-HM eyes (18.0 ± 3.2 vs. 17.2 ± 2.7 mmHg, P < 0.001). In HM eyes, greater IOP correlated with larger CDR (β = 0.005, P = 0.020).CONCLUSION:In school-aged children, IOP and CDR showed a slight increase with age. AL was an important determinant of IOP and CDR. HM may amplify the interaction among IOP, CDR, and AL.

Introduction

Refraction and ocular structures undergo dynamic changes throughout childhood and adolescence and may vary across ethnic groups. Prior studies have demonstrated age-related trends between ages 4 and 18, such as decreasing lens thickness (LT), deepening anterior chamber depth (ACD), and elongating axial length (AL).[12] However, changes in intraocular pressure (IOP) during this period remain controversial, with reports of increasing,[34] decreasing,[5] or with a variable pattern with age.[678] Similarly, the cup-to-disc ratio (CDR) varies widely among adult individuals and is a key parameter in glaucoma assessment.[9] The distribution of CDR and associated factors in children has been investigated.[910] However, the relationship between age, axial elongation, and CDR is complex.[911121314] This complexity may be attributed to myopic eyes, which typically have larger optic discs and cupping, thereby further complicating the associations among age, axial elongation, and CDR.[14]

Given the increasing global prevalence of myopia, particularly the high rates observed in Asian populations,[15] and its impact on ocular anatomy, establishing normative data for IOP and CDR in children is essential. Such data are valuable for assessing glaucoma risk in pediatric populations. However, these normative data remain lacking in Taiwan. To address this gap, the present study aims to provide age-specific reference values for IOP, CDR, and ocular biometrics in a large cohort of school-aged children in Taiwan.

Materials and Methods

Study population

This study was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of National Taiwan University Hospital (Rec: 201510078RINB) and conducted between 2015 December and 2018 December in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Informed consent was obtained from all participants and their parents or legal guardians.

Enrollment

School-aged children between 6 and 18 years old were enrolled from elementary schools, junior high schools, vocational schools, and senior high schools across Taiwan. There were three levels of urbanization, including metropolitan precincts, provincial cities, and other rural areas. Nationwide sampling yielded schools across different regions of Taiwan, and their locations and urbanization levels are summarized in Supplemental Table 1. Students were randomly selected based on sex and academic year to ensure a representative distribution. Participants using orthokeratology lenses were excluded due to the inability to obtain accurate refraction measurements. Participants were stratified into three age groups (6–11, 12–14, and 15–18 years) based mainly on school stages in Taiwan (elementary, junior high, and senior high school), which also broadly reflect developmental stages from childhood through early to late adolescence.

Ophthalmic examinations

A comprehensive ocular examination was conducted by ophthalmologists in a cross-sectional design. Anterior segment structures were evaluated using slit-lamp biomicroscopy. The posterior pole was examined through a dilated fundus examination using an indirect ophthalmoscope, and the horizontal and vertical CDR were recorded. The average CDR was calculated by averaging of horizontal and vertical CDR. Cycloplegia was induced using Mydrin-P (tropicamide 0.5% and phenylephrine hydrochloride 0.5%, Santen), instilled at least three times. Cycloplegic autorefraction was performed 30 minutes after the first instillation using a Topcon KR-800S autorefractor. Each eye was measured at least three times. Spherical and cylindrical values were recorded, and spherical equivalent (SE) was calculated as the spherical value plus half of the cylindrical value. High myopia (HM) was defined as SE <−5.00 diopters or AL ≥26 mm. Ocular biometry, including AL, ACD, vitreous chamber depth (VCD), and LT, was measured using A-scan ultrasonography (Accutome). IOP was measured using ICare PRO rebound tonometer (ICare Finland Oy, Helsinki, Finland); each eye was measured at least six times, and the average value was recorded.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.0.3; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria). Descriptive statistics were presented as mean ± standard deviation for continuous variables and as frequency and percentage for categorical variables. Because measurements from both eyes of the same subject were included, linear mixed-effects models were employed to account for within-subject correlation by incorporating subject-specific random effects. These models were used to evaluate the association between age and ocular biometric parameters, to compare biometric measurements between boys and girls, and to compare IOP between the HM and non–HM groups. Variables with P < 0.1 in the univariable analyses were included in the multivariable model. The final model was determined using backward selection. A two-tailed P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 6264 eyes from 3132 children (1697 boys and 1435 girls) were included in the analysis. The mean age of the participants was 12.1 ± 3.4 years. The age distribution of all participants is summarized in Table 1. More than 200 children were enrolled for each year of age, except for those aged 15 and 18. Ocular biometric parameters, IOP, and CDR across different ages are presented in Table 1. The LT decreased from 3.76 ± 0.67 mm at age 6 to 3.58 ± 0.28 mm at age 18, whereas the ACD increased from 3.38 ± 0.37 mm at age 6 to 3.68 ± 0.33 mm at age 13, and the VCD showed a continuous increase from 15.51 ± 1.01 mm at age 6 to 17.98 ± 1.17 mm at age 18. Regarding refraction, spherical power declined from + 0.74 ± 1.04 diopters (D) at age 6 to –2.90 ± 2.34 D at age 18. Cylindrical power slightly increased in magnitude from –0.62 ± 0.70 D at age 6 to –1.16 ± 0.99 D at age 18. SE decreased from + 0.05 ± 0.59 D to –2.03 ± 1.34 D over the same period [Figure 1a]. Significant axial elongation was observed, with AL increasing from 22.65 ± 0.81 mm at age 6 to 25.09 ± 1.24 mm at age 18 [Figure 1b]. The IOP initially increased up to age 9, then reaching a minimum of 16.95 ± 2.23 mmHg at age 11, and then increased to a peak of 17.53 ± 3.08 mmHg at age 17 [Figure 1c]. The CDR ranged from 0.27 (ages 7 and 9) to 0.33 (age 14) [Figure 1d]. Linear mixed model analysis showed that LT, SE, spherical power, and cylindrical power were significantly negatively correlated with age (all P < 0.001), whereas ACD, VCD, AL, IOP, and CDR were positively correlated with age (all P < 0.001, except for IOP, P = 0.006).

Given that AL is a key determinant, we assessed the prevalence of HM across different age groups [Table 4]. The prevalence increased markedly from 3.8% at age 11 to 7.2% at age 12 and from 16.8% at age 16 to 25.4% at age 17. We further compared IOP between highly myopic and non-highly myopic eyes across age groups [Table 5]. In the 6–11 and 12–14 age groups, there was no statistically significant difference in IOP between the two groups. However, in the 15–18-year age group, highly myopic eyes had significantly higher IOP than non-highly myopic eyes (18.0 ± 3.2 vs. 17.2 ± 2.7 mmHg, P < 0.001). We also examined the proportion of eyes with enlarged cupping across age groups. The prevalence of eyes with CDR >0.4 ranged from 2% to 17% across ages, whereas the prevalence of CDR >0.6 remained low (1%–3%) [Table 6]. CDR was significantly larger in highly myopic eyes than non-highly myopic eyes (0.32 ± 0.13 vs. 0.30 ± 0.13, P = 0.040). Moreover, among highly myopic eyes, higher IOP was significantly associated with larger CDR (β = 0.005, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.0008–0.009, P = 0.020; linear mixed model). In contrast, no such association was observed in non-highly myopic eyes (β = –0.0001, 95% CI: –0.001–0.001, P = 0.764).

Discussion

In this large, school-based cohort of Taiwanese children aged 6–18 years, we established normative data for IOP, CDR, and ocular biometry, and examined their associations with age, sex, and refractive status. IOP and CDR increased with age and longer AXL. It is worth noting that highly myopic eyes in older adolescents were associated with higher IOP, which in turn was linked to larger CDR.

Population-based IOP values in children of different ethnicities have been reported to range from 11.5 to 17.6 mmHg.[34567816171819] Various factors associated with IOP have been identified, including sex, age, outdoor activity, AL, maternal myopia, central corneal thickness (CCT), ACD, corneal diameter, and blood pressure.[346171819] In the present study, we also found a positive association between AL and IOP. However, each 1-mm increase in AL corresponded to only a 0.12 mmHg increase in IOP, which may be clinically irrelevant. Among children aged 6–14 years, the prevalence of HM was low, and the IOP differences between high and non-high myopes were minimal. The difference in IOP between high myopes and non-high myopes reached statistical significance only in the late adolescent group, where the prevalence of HM was 19%. This finding may not be generalizable to younger age groups, in which the prevalence of HM is very low and statistical power is limited. Previous studies have also reported higher IOP in myopic children,[2021] although some have found associations between hyperopia and elevated IOP as well.[22] One hypothesis posits that higher IOP may lead to axial elongation by increasing scleral stress, stimulating collagen remodeling, and inducing ischemia.[23] However, accumulating evidence suggests that elevated IOP occurs after the onset of myopia rather than being a causal factor.[2425] In one longitudinal study, lower baseline IOP was observed in children with progressing myopia, possibly being attributed to increased scleral compliance.[25] Other studies have found no association between IOP and refractive status or myopia progression.[826] Thus, the relationship between IOP and myopia remains inconclusive and warrants further investigation.

The relationship between age and IOP in children is complex, with previous studies reporting increasing,[34] decreasing,[5] or variable age-related patterns.[678] Jiang et al. described an M-shaped pattern in a cohort of 6364 children aged 4–18 years. They observed an increase in IOP before age 10 years, followed by a decline during early adolescence, stabilization through late adolescence, and a further decline at age 18 years.[6] Our findings are generally consistent with this pattern. The upward trend in IOP among children under 10 years of age has been consistently observed across several studies,[7819] and adult-level IOP is reached around age 12 years.[4622] In contrast to the relatively consistent pattern observed in younger children, IOP trends during adolescence appear more variable. One possible explanation is related to changes in accommodation, which decrease before the age of 10.[27] Greater accommodation has been associated with lower IOP in healthy individuals.[28] Therefore, the decline in accommodation in children might contribute to the observed decrease in IOP.

Enlarged optic disc cupping may reflect early glaucomatous changes or represent a physiological variant. Normative data on CDR in children have been reported, with average values ranging from 0.2 to 0.4.[101229] However, data from Asian populations remain limited. In our study, the average CDR ranged from 0.28 to 0.33 across different age groups, which is comparable to existing literature. Notably, 29% of children in our cohort had a CDR >0.3, which is substantially higher than the 10% reported in a UK pediatric population.[12] However, refractive status was not reported in that UK study, limiting the ability to attribute differences in CDR to the higher prevalence of myopia among Asian children. In line with this finding, another study also reported that East Asian children had larger CDR and smaller neuroretinal rim area than European White children.[30] Several factors have been associated with enlarged cupping, including family history,[31] prematurity, low birth weight, ethnicity,[32] low body mass index, malnutrition, or physiological variants.[12] In our cohort, myopic eyes with greater ACD, VCD, and AL, as well as male sex, were associated with larger CDR. However, in multivariable analysis, only longer AL remained significantly associated with increased CDR, suggesting that axial elongation is a key determinant. Myopic eyes often possess larger optic discs, which may account for increased physiological cupping.[3033] Age has also been shown to correlate positively with CDR. Previous studies reported an annual CDR increase of 0.0075 in full-term children and 0.0160 in preterm children.[11] In our cohort, the estimated annual increase in CDR was 0.004. Consistent with prior literature, we found no significant association between IOP and CDR in the overall cohort. However, among highly myopic eyes, greater IOP was significantly associated with larger CDR. This suggests that in eyes with axial elongation, the optic nerve head may be more vulnerable to IOP-related stress, potentially elevating the risk of future glaucomatous damage.[34]

This study has several limitations. First, IOP was measured using a rebound tonometer rather than Goldmann applanation tonometry, as the latter is often difficult for young children to tolerate and cooperate with. Consequently, most studies investigating IOP in children have also utilized non-contact or rebound tonometry. Second, IOP was measured only once, despite its known short- and long-term fluctuations, which may be more pronounced in less cooperative children. A single time-point measurement may not fully reflect IOP variability. Nevertheless, given the large sample size of our cohort, the data remain representative of the overall IOP distribution in Taiwanese children. Third, CCT data were available for only a small subset of participants (418 eyes), with a mean CCT of 572 ± 33.2 µm. This limited sample size may reduce the generalizability of CCT-related findings and preclude adjustment for CCT in the analysis of IOP, despite its known influence on tonometric measurements. Finally, CDR was assessed by ophthalmologists using indirect ophthalmoscopy, which is inherently subjective and may be subject to both interobserver and intraobserver variability. Furthermore, detailed measurements of other optic disc parameters, such as disc size, rim area, and cup volume, were not available in this study. Future studies utilizing objective imaging modalities such as optical coherence tomography and fundus photography are warranted to validate and expand upon our findings.

Conclusion

We established normative data of IOP, CDR, and ocular biometric parameters in a large, school-based cohort of Taiwanese children aged 6–18 years. Both IOP and CDR demonstrated age-related increases in this Taiwanese population; however, AL emerged as the key determinant influencing both parameters. A significant association between higher IOP and larger CDR was observed only in highly myopic eyes, suggesting that axial elongation may enhance the susceptibility of the optic nerve head to IOP-related stress. Future studies should adopt a longitudinal design to clarify the temporal relationship between axial elongation, IOP changes, and the evolution of optic disc morphology.
