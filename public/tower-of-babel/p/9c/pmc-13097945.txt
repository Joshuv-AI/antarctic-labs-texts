Comprehensive analysis of spinal sagittal balance, spinal bone mineral density, and pulmonary function in severe ankylosing spondylitis using quantitative computed tomography
Authors: Jianlin Li, Zhengkai Zhao, Qiuyi Cai, Yi Li, Yang Xie, Yan Wang, Jin Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundAnkylosing spondylitis (AS) is frequently complicated by osteoporosis and progressive sagittal malalignment and may be accompanied by respiratory limitation. However, in radiographically severe AS, the cross-sectional relationships among sagittal alignment, spinal volumetric bone mineral density (vBMD), and imaging-derived pulmonary metrics remain incompletely characterised. We investigated these associations using quantitative computed tomography (QCT) and CT-based lung volumetry.MethodsThis retrospective, cross-sectional study included 135 patients with radiographically severe AS (modified New York sacroiliitis grade 3–4) evaluated at a single center between 2021 and 2023. Lumbar trabecular vBMD was measured via QCT, sagittal alignment parameters were assessed on standing radiographs, and pulmonary metrics (TLVin, TLVex, ΔTLV) were derived from paired inspiratory/expiratory chest CT scans. Correlations were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR). Multivariable linear regression examined associations with sagittal vertical axis (SVA) and pelvic tilt (PT), while multivariable logistic regression was employed to identify independent predictors of osteoporosis (defined as vBMD < 80 mg/cm3).ResultsSpinal vBMD and SVA differed significantly between radiographic grades (grade 3 vs 4). In FDR-adjusted correlations, vBMD was inversely associated with age (ρ = −0.58, q < 0.001) and BMI (ρ = −0.28, q = 0.007). Height showed robust associations with alignment (PT: ρ = −0.37, q < 0.001; TK: ρ = −0.39, q < 0.001; SS: ρ = 0.34, q < 0.001) and with TLVin (ρ = 0.33, q < 0.001), while BMI was inversely associated with TLVex (ρ = −0.27, q = 0.011). In prespecified multivariable models, the SVA model explained modest variance (R2 = 0.119) with no independent covariate at p < 0.05; PT was independently associated with height (B = −0.33°/cm, p = 0.001; R2 = 0.248). In the prespecified osteoporosis model, age (OR per 10 years 5.37), grade 4 (OR 4.35), and smoking history (OR 3.23) were independently associated with osteoporosis; discrimination within the derivation cohort was good (AUC 0.874). CT-volumetry showed high reproducibility (intra-/inter-observer ICCs 0.95–0.99).ConclusionIn radiographically severe AS, spinal vBMD is strongly influenced by age and radiographic severity. The identification of a high-risk osteoporosis profile, which is characterized by advanced age, grade 4 sacroiliitis, and smoking history, supports targeted bone health surveillance in these subgroups. While CT-derived lung volumetry is technically reproducible, its cross-sectional associations are predominantly anthropometric. Future prospective studies incorporating longitudinal data and standard spirometry are warranted to clarify the clinical utility of these imaging biomarkers.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12891-026-09655-7.

Introduction

Ankylosing spondylitis (AS) is a chronic inflammatory disease that primarily affects the axial skeleton. It results in progressive spinal stiffness and fusion, which significantly impacts quality of life, particularly among younger populations [1]. In addition to its characteristic spinal involvement, AS presents systemic risks that critically influence bone health and pulmonary function [2].

The spinal sagittal balance, which refers to spinal alignment from a lateral perspective, is crucial in AS. It ensures appropriate posture, equal weight distribution, and minimizes spinal stress [3]. Alterations in sagittal balance in AS patients can affect spinal mechanics and posture, thereby influencing vertebral and joint loads [4]. This biomechanical alteration may directly affect spinal volumetric bone mineral density (vBMD), a critical factor in disease prognosis, as excessive bone loss in the spinal vertebrae can lead to severe outcomes, such as vertebral fractures. Therefore, precise assessment of spinal vBMD is essential for monitoring disease progression and managing patient care [5–7]. Furthermore, changes in sagittal balance in AS patients can also contribute to respiratory problems due to spinal rigidity and chest wall involvement, potentially affecting pulmonary function [8].

Recent advancements in imaging, particularly Quantitative Computed Tomography (QCT), have significantly enhanced our understanding of AS and its assessment. QCT involves calibrating the CT scanner using calcium hydroxyapatite phantoms to represent various BMDs, enabling precise conversion of Hounsfield units into accurate BMD values [9]. Furthermore, QCT is superior to dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry (DXA) in differentiating trabecular and cortical bone, offering a three-dimensional view less affected by confounding factors such as spinal degeneration [10]. Additionally, it facilitates non-invasive pulmonary function evaluation due to its ability to capture detailed images of the lungs and thoracic cavity. Although spirometry remains the conventional method for pulmonary function assessment, QCT can evaluate pulmonary function without active patient participation [11], which is particularly advantageous for AS patients with severe spinal stiffness or pain who may encounter difficulties with traditional respiratory tests.

Currently, QCT has been utilized by several researchers to investigate BMD changes among AS patients. For example, Wu et al. discovered a significant association between lower trabecular BMD in the hip and disease progression duration and the extent of hip involvement in AS patients. Furthermore, they found that BMD changes were related to hip functional status [12]. QCT has been extensively used to assess pulmonary function in obstructive pulmonary diseases. For example, Xie et al. demonstrated that quantitative evaluation of ventilation and perfusion abnormalities using ventilation/perfusion single-photon emission computed tomography (V/P SPECT) can be an objective measure for assessing disease severity and lung function loss to guide localized treatments [13]. In a sagittal balance study, Guo et al. found that AS patients with moderate and severe hip involvement exhibited a similar spinosacral angle and lumbar lordosis; however, those with severe hip involvement had a lower pelvic femoral angle, pelvic tilt, higher sacral slope, and sagittal vertical axis [14].

Despite BMD’s clinical significance, few studies have examined the interrelationships among spinal sagittal balance, Spinal vBMD, and pulmonary function, particularly in severe AS cases, where disease manifestations are heightened. Therefore, in our study, we aimed to use QCT to explore the associations between spinal sagittal balance, Spinal vBMD, and pulmonary function in severe AS cases characterized by pronounced and potentially debilitating clinical effects to gain a more comprehensive understanding of the systemic implications of this disease.

Methods

Study population

This retrospective cross-sectional study enrolled patients diagnosed with AS from The Third People's Hospital of Chengdu, Sichuan, China, between 2021 and 2023. All participants underwent CT-based assessment of the sacroiliac joints, with severe AS defined by the presence of grade III or IV sacroiliitis according to the modified New York (mNY) criteria [15]. Eligibility required that patients were aged ≥18 years, possessed a confirmed AS diagnosis fulfilling the mNY radiographic criteria, and had complete QCT and chest CT datasets available for analysis. Candidates were excluded if they presented with: (1) concurrent systemic inflammatory diseases or major comorbidities (e.g., severe cardiovascular, renal, or neurological disease) potentially confounding bone metabolism or pulmonary metrics; (2) a history of spinal surgery or major trauma; (3) conditions substantially impairing pulmonary function; or (4) suboptimal image quality. Of the 185 patients initially screened, 50 were excluded due to incomplete laboratory or imaging records or fulfillment of the exclusion criteria, resulting in a final analytical cohort of 135 patients. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of The Third People’s Hospital of Chengdu, and written informed consent was obtained from all participants before enrolment.

Collection of anthropometric and clinical data

Demographic data, including age and sex, were recorded. Standardized protocols were followed to measure height and weight, and body mass index (BMI) was also calculated. Additionally, smoking, drinking, and family history were recorded. Blood samples were collected after an overnight fasting period to analyze relevant factors, including C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), calcium, and phosphate.

Radiographic assessment

The radiographic assessments were conducted by two highly experienced radiologists (Gao and Zhao), each possessing more than fifteen years of professional experience. Any discrepancies were resolved through consensus. The patients underwent anteroposterior and lateral spinal radiographs using DR (Siemens Healthineers GmbH, Forchheim, Germany). The spinal sagittal balance parameters including thoracic kyphosis (TK: the angle between the superior end plate of T5 and the inferior end plate of T12), lumbar lordosis (LL: the angle between the inferior end plate of T12 and the superior end plate of S1), cervical lordosis (CL: the angle between the inferior end plate of C2 and the inferior end plate of C7), C7 sagittal vertical axis (SVA), sacrofemoral distance (SFD), pelvic incidence (PI), pelvic tilt (PT), and sacral slope (SS), were measured [16].

The CT scans were performed using a 64-multidetector CT system (SOMATOM Definition AS, Siemens Healthineers, Germany), while the Spinal vBMD was measured using QCT Pro software (Mindways Software). The region of interest (ROI) was carefully delineated on the trabecular bone of the vertebral body, carefully avoiding cortical bone and the basivertebral venous plexus to ensure an accurate bone density representation. The AS severity was assessed on CT images based on the grading system outlined in the modified New York criteria for AS, as follows: grade 0: normal; grade 1: suspicious changes (some indistinctness observed on the joint boundaries); grade 2: minimal abnormality (localized areas with erosion or sclerosis, exhibiting no alteration in the joint width, observed within small regions); grade 3: unequivocal abnormality (moderate or advanced sacroiliitis with erosions, evidence of sclerosis, widening, narrowing, or partial ankylosis); and grade 4: severe abnormality (complete ankylosis observed) [17]. CT-based pulmonary function assessment

The pulmonary function was assessed using CT-based quantitative lung volumetry derived from paired inspiratory and expiratory chest CT scans. Briefly, inspiratory and expiratory series were acquired with patients in the supine position. Participants were instructed to perform a breath-hold at full inspiration for the inspiratory acquisition and at end-expiration for the expiratory acquisition; studies with obvious motion artefact were reviewed and excluded or reprocessed when necessary. Thin-section volumetric reconstructions suitable for quantitative analysis were used, consistent with vendor-recommended conditions for lung and lobar segmentation workflows. Quantitative analysis was performed using the Philips IntelliSpace Portal workstation (version 12.0) with the lung analysis workflow. The software automatically extracted the tracheobronchial tree and performed lung and lobar segmentation (right upper, right middle, right lower, left upper, and left lower lobes), followed by manual correction when required. Lung volumes were computed from the segmented lung mask at the inspiratory and expiratory phases, yielding the inspiratory total lung volume (TLVin) and expiratory total lung volume (TLVex). The total lung volume change (∆TLV) was defined as TLVin − TLVex (Fig. 2). To assess measurement reproducibility, CT-derived lung volumetry was re-analysed in a subset of 30 cases. Two readers (Li and Zhao) independently performed the lung segmentation and volume extraction using the same workflow and were blinded to clinical data. For intra-observer reproducibility, Reader 1 repeated the measurements after an interval of ≥1 week. Agreement for TLVin, TLVex, and ΔTLV was quantified using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC; absolute agreement), and Bland–Altman analysis was used to estimate bias (mean difference) and 95% limits of agreement [19].

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all tests. Continuous variables were reported as means ± standard deviation (SD), while categorical variables were presented as percentages. The Kolmogorov–Smirnov test was used to assess normality. Intergroup comparisons were conducted using independent-samples t-tests or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) for continuous variables, and the chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical variables. Pearson correlation was used for normally distributed variables, whereas Spearman's rank correlation was used for non-normally distributed variables. For the correlation matrix, p-values were adjusted for multiple testing using the Benjamini–Hochberg false discovery rate (FDR) procedure [20], and FDR-adjusted q values are reported.

Multivariable linear regression was used to examine cross-sectional associations between sagittal alignment parameters (SVA and PT) and prespecified clinically relevant covariates. SVA and PT were selected a priori as primary sagittal alignment outcomes because they are widely used markers of global sagittal alignment (SVA) and pelvic compensatory retroversion (PT), and they are core sagittal modifiers in established spinopelvic assessment frameworks [21–23]. To reduce overfitting and avoid data-driven variable selection, covariates were defined a priori and included age, sex, height, BMI, spinal volumetric BMD, radiographic grade (grade 4 vs 3), CT-derived TLVin, and CRP. Regression coefficients with 95% confidence intervals were reported, and multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factors (VIF). To identify independent predictors of osteoporosis, binary logistic regression was performed with osteoporosis, defined as lumbar trabecular BMD <80 mg/cm3 according to standard american college of radiology QCT cut-off values, as the dependent variable [24]. Univariate logistic regression was first conducted to screen potential associations. In multivariable analysis, we fitted an a priori clinically informed model including age, sex, BMI, smoking history, CRP, and radiographic grade (grade 4 vs grade 3) [5, 25]. Model discrimination was assessed using the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve and the area under the ROC curve (AUC).

For study size, no formal a priori sample size calculation was performed because this was a retrospective cross-sectional study; the study size was determined by the number of eligible patients with complete QCT and chest CT data during the study period. In accordance with STROBE recommendations, we report how the study size was derived. To reduce overfitting, we used prespecified, clinically relevant covariate sets for both multivariable linear regression and multivariable logistic regression. The final analytic cohort comprised 135 patients, including 89 osteoporosis cases and 46 non-osteoporosis cases. For the multivariable logistic regression model (six predictors), this corresponds to approximately 14.8 events per predictor (89/6) and 7.7 non-events per predictor (46/6), supporting a parsimonious model specification [26].

Reproducibility of CT-derived lung volumetry

CT-derived lung volumetry demonstrated good reproducibility. Intra-observer agreement was high for TLVin (ICC 0.981), TLVex (ICC 0.972), and ΔTLV (ICC 0.957). Inter-observer agreement was similarly high (TLVin ICC 0.989, TLVex ICC 0.968, and ΔTLV ICC 0.949). Bland–Altman analysis demonstrated a minimal mean bias, along with the corresponding 95% limits of agreement (Supplementary Table 1).

Spinal vBMD

Spinal vBMD showed a strong inverse association with age (Spearman ρ = −0.58, q < 0.001), indicating lower Spinal vBMD with increasing age. Spinal vBMD was also inversely correlated with BMI (ρ = −0.28, q = 0.007). In addition, Spinal vBMD demonstrated a modest inverse correlation with PT (ρ = −0.23, q = 0.038). By contrast, several weaker correlations observed at nominal significance, such as Spinal vBMD with height (ρ = 0.22), TLVin (ρ = 0.18), and with SFD/PI (ρ ≈ −0.17 to −0.19), did not remain significant after FDR correction (all q > 0.05) and should therefore be interpreted cautiously.

CT-derived lung volumetry

For CT-derived lung volumetry, height was positively correlated with TLVin (ρ = 0.35, q < 0.001). BMI was inversely correlated with TLVex (ρ = −0.27, q = 0.011). Other nominal associations (e.g., age with TLVin; BMI with TLVin; height with TLVex or ∆TLV) were small in magnitude and did not survive FDR correction (all q > 0.05).

Sagittal alignment parameters

Height demonstrated robust associations with several sagittal alignment parameters, including inverse correlations with PT (ρ = −0.37, q < 0.001) and TK (ρ = −0.39, q < 0.001), and a positive correlation with sacral slope (SS) (ρ = 0.34, q < 0.001). Other weak correlations involving SVA, SFD, CL, and relationships between alignment parameters and lung volumetry (e.g., PT with TLVin; LL with ∆TLV) were of small effect size (|ρ| ≈ 0.20) and were not statistically significant after FDR correction (q > 0.05).

Discussion

This study utilized QCT to investigate the interplay between spinal sagittal balance, vBMD, and CT-derived lung volumetry in patients with severe AS. By integrating these three distinct imaging modalities, we characterized cross-sectional associations within a cohort defined by advanced radiographic disease. The combined imaging approach is clinically relevant because osteoporosis and sagittal imbalance are established complications of advanced AS, and thoracic cage involvement may contribute to respiratory limitation [5, 27].

We observed a robust inverse association between spinal vBMD and age, alongside a moderate inverse association with BMI following FDR correction. These findings are consistent with prior reports that low BMD is common in AS and tends to worsen with advancing age and cumulative disease burden. Klingberg et al. previously emphasized the high prevalence of bone loss in AS, noting that spinal assessment via conventional DXA is often confounded by syndesmophyte formation [5]. In this context, QCT-based assessment provides a distinct advantage by specifically targeting trabecular bone, thereby remaining largely unaffected by the "bone bridging" that artificially inflates areal BMD measurements.

Importantly, radiographic severity was linked to lower Spinal vBMD. Patients with grade 4 sacroiliitis had lower Spinal vBMD than grade 3. This finding aligns with the concept that advanced structural disease and restricted mobility exacerbate systemic bone loss. It also supports QCT-specific evidence suggesting that spinal ankylosis and reduced mechanical loading are associated with lower measured trabecular density [28]. Collectively, these results reinforce the necessity of bone health monitoring in severe AS, where the risks of both fragility fractures and deformity-related morbidity are clinically substantial.

To enhance interpretability and minimize data-driven bias, we employed an a priori multivariable logistic regression model incorporating clinically established covariates. After adjustment, age, grade 4 sacroiliitis, and smoking history were identified as independent predictors of osteoporosis. This pattern is consistent with observational cohorts, such as the Córdoba AxSpA Task force, Registry and Outcomes (CASTRO study, which identified age and radiographic severity as key correlates of low hip BMD [25]. The association with smoking is also biologically plausible and consistent with broader osteoporosis literature and inflammatory joint disease reviews describing smoking as a risk factor for bone loss and fractures [29, 30]. Interestingly, the lack of an independent association between CRP and osteoporosis may be attributed to the cross-sectional nature of this single measurement, which may not adequately capture the cumulative inflammatory burden or the influence of heterogeneous treatment exposures. Furthermore, while BMI correlated with bone health at the univariate level, it was not an independent predictor in the multivariable model. The relationship between adiposity and bone health remains complex and may be influenced by skeletal site and mechanical loading [31]. In severe AS, this is further complicated by "pseudo-obesity," where kyphosis-related loss of standing height inflates BMI measurements, potentially obscuring its true metabolic relationship with bone density.

Beyond these metabolic and anthropometric considerations, the progressive structural transformation of the spine itself plays a critical role in the clinical profile of the disease. Severe AS is frequently accompanied by progressive sagittal malalignment and compensatory pelvic retroversion, and prior AS studies have linked spinopelvic parameters to clinical outcomes and quality of life. In our cohort, grade 4 sacroiliitis was associated with higher SVA compared to grade 3, confirming that advanced structural disease correlates with more pronounced global sagittal imbalance, which is consistent with a previous study [32]. In FDR-adjusted correlation analyses, height exhibited robust associations with PT, TK, and SS. These findings are unsurprising because anthropometry contributes to spinopelvic morphology and alignment, and SVA/PT are widely regarded as key descriptors of global sagittal alignment and pelvic compensation in established spinopelvic frameworks [33].

We further developed a priori multivariable linear regression models for SVA and PT to identify potential drivers of imbalance. The SVA model explained only a modest proportion of variance (R2≈0.12), with no covariates retaining independent associations. This suggests that sagittal imbalance in severe AS is likely governed by unmeasured factors such as the specific pattern of spinal ankylosis, hip involvement, or paraspinal muscle compensation. Conversely, the PT model explained a higher degree of variance (R2≈0.25) and remained independently associated with height. These findings should be interpreted as cross-sectional associations rather than predictions and underscore the multifactorial nature of alignment in advanced AS.

While these sagittal and pelvic parameters define the global posture of the patient, the structural alterations of the axial skeleton also extend to the thoracic compartment, potentially impacting pulmonary capacity. Respiratory impairment in AS is typically attributed to restricted chest wall expansion and costovertebral ankylosis [2]. Quantitative CT lung volumetry is a promising imaging-derived marker, and prior work demonstrates correlations between paired inspiratory–expiratory CT indices and standard ventilatory function measures [11]. In AS specifically, CT-based studies have reported reductions in lung volume and altered thoracic morphology among patients with pulmonary function impairment [18]. In our study, the most robust associations with lung volume were anthropometric: height correlated positively with TLVin, while BMI correlated inversely with TLVex. While nominal alignment-lung correlations were observed, they did not withstand the FDR correction. This suggests that in this specific cohort, CT-derived lung volumes are dominated by body habitus rather than sagittal alignment parameters. This may contrast with other spinal deformity cohorts, such as those with severe idiopathic kyphosis, where curvature magnitude more directly influences pulmonary capacity [34]. In addition, to test the strength of our pulmonary imaging approach and its reproducibility, we demonstrated high intra- and inter-observer reliability for TLVin, TLVex, and ΔTLV (ICCs ~0.95–0.99 with Bland–Altman agreement), supporting the technical stability of the segmentation workflow. This supports the feasibility of incorporating CT-volumetry in future AS studies, although standard spirometry was not uniformly available in our cohort, precluding direct validation against FVC/FEV1/TLC. Future prospective studies incorporating same-day spirometry will be important to confirm clinical interpretability and to determine whether CT-derived volumetry adds incremental value beyond routine physiological assessment.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective cross-sectional design precludes causal inference or the evaluation of temporal relationships between deformity and bone loss. Additionally, the study cohort was enriched for advanced radiographic disease because most patients presenting to our institution during the study period had marked structural involvement; therefore, individuals with mild radiographic sacroiliitis (mNY grades 1–2) were not included. This may limit generalisability across the full AS spectrum and may not capture patterns present in earlier disease stages. Besides, standard pulmonary function testing (e.g., spirometry and plethysmography) was not routinely available for all participants; accordingly, we used CT-based lung volumetry (TLVin, TLVex, and ∆TLV) as an imaging-derived surrogate, which may limit direct clinical interpretability. Finally, the absence of a detailed medication history and exact disease duration may introduce residual confounding.

To address these limitations, future studies should prioritise longitudinal, preferably prospective designs and include patients across the full range of radiographic stages. Standardised collection of spirometry and comprehensive clinical data, especially disease duration and detailed pharmacological exposure (agent, dose, and duration), will enable appropriately adjusted modelling and more clinically meaningful subgroup analyses, thereby clarifying the evolution and clinical significance of these imaging biomarkers across disease stages.

Conclusions

In this retrospective cross-sectional study of radiographically severe AS, QCT-derived Spinal vBMD and spinopelvic alignment differed meaningfully by radiographic sacroiliitis grade, and Spinal vBMD showed a strong age-related decline. Using a clinically prespecified multivariable model, older age, grade 4 sacroiliitis, and smoking history were independently associated with osteoporosis, identifying a readily actionable high-risk profile for targeted bone health screening and preventive management in advanced disease. CT-derived lung volumetry was technically reproducible, supporting its feasibility as an imaging-based pulmonary metric; however, in this cohort, its associations were predominantly anthropometric and did not demonstrate robust independent relationships with sagittal alignment after multiple-testing correction. Collectively, these findings highlight the value of integrated quantitative imaging for phenotyping advanced AS and support intensified osteoporosis surveillance, particularly in older patients with advanced radiographic disease and smokers, while underscoring the need for prospective longitudinal studies incorporating standard spirometry, treatment history, and disease duration to clarify temporal relationships and clinical utility.
