Global Burden of Sickle Cell Disease: Adequacy of Pain Control as Evidenced by Frequency of Oral Pain Medication Use, Health Outcomes, and Emotional Well‐Being Across Diverse Populations
Authors: Akshat Jain, Lisa Roberts, Safiye Sahin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionSickle cell disease (SCD) is a chronic genetic disorder causing severe pain, anemia, and systemic complications that negatively affect patients’ physical and mental well‐being. The aim of this global cross‐sectional study was to evaluate the relationship between oral pain medication (OPM) use frequency and health outcomes, emotional well‐being, social participation, and satisfaction with care among people with SCD.MethodData were collected via an online survey across five continents from February to September 2024.ResultsA total of 123 participants were divided into two groups based on OPM use frequency: weekly or more (n = 62) and once or twice a month (n = 61). Participants in OPM weekly group reported poorer self‐rated health, shortness of breath, more frequent hospitalizations, and missed school days due to SCD crises compared to the monthly group (p < 0.05). However, use of treatments such as hydroxyurea and preventive screenings like transcranial Doppler was similar across groups. Emotional well‐being also significantly differed in the weekly group, with lower happiness levels, difficulty in emotional regulation, and reduced social participation (p < 0.01). Satisfaction with medical care was significantly lower among weekly OPM users (p < 0.001).ConclusionThese findings suggest that higher OPM use is associated with poorer physical, emotional, and social outcomes. The results highlight the importance of comprehensive and multidisciplinary care approaches that may support the overall well‐being of individuals with SCD and utilize OPM as a relevant clinical metric to assess disease burden in SCD.Clinical TrialThe authors have confirmed clinical trial registration is not needed for this submission.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a genetic blood disorder characterized by chronic anemia, periodic episodes of pain, and a myriad of other complications, significantly impacting the quality of life of affected individuals [1, 2, 3]. It is particularly prevalent in regions with high rates of malaria, including Sub‐Saharan Africa, India, and the Middle East, although it also affects Hispanic and other populations in Europe, the Americas, and Australasia due to global migration patterns [3]. The management of SCD requires comprehensive healthcare support, including access to specialized medical care, patient education, and community support systems [4, 5].

In the management of SCD, pain management is a critical aspect of treatment to maintain the well‐being of patients with SCD [6]. However, the biology of SCD pain is multifactorial and cannot be attributed to simple nociception. Studies suggest that neuroimmune interactions, central sensitization, and neuropathic mechanisms play a critical role in both acute and chronic pain among this population [7, 8]. In addition, pharmacogenetic variation can influence the way patients metabolize opioids and other analgesics, which in turn affects both efficacy and risk of side effects [9]. These overlapping biological and epigenetic factors contribute to substantial heterogeneity in patients’ pain experiences and responses to therapy. As a result, many patients continue to live with poorly controlled pain despite currently available disease‐modifying agents. Beyond biology, global disparities further complicate management. While patients in high‐resource settings may have access to a range of oral narcotics and adjuvant therapies, in many low‐ and middle‐income countries, such medications are not reliably available, leaving patients with limited options for pain relief. This global inequity underscores the need to contextualize pain management strategies within both biological and structural frameworks and standardize OPM use as a clinical trial endpoint for future therapies in development, in addition to frequency of vaso‐occlusive events (VOE).

A hallmark symptom of SCD, pain indicates the frequency and severity of VOE, especially pain crisis, which requires the use of oral pain medications (OPMs) [10]. Therefore, the frequency of OPM can be considered an indicator of overall health status among this population. Current studies show that pain related to SCD is linked with higher rates of hospitalization [11], increased complications [12], and negative health outcomes, such as depression and anxiety [1].

In addition to pain management, other disease management strategies such as hydroxyurea therapy, blood transfusions, surveillance (e.g., transcranial Doppler screening), and preventive care (e.g., immunizations) also play a crucial role in managing disease progression and complications [13, 14, 15]. However, access to these treatments can be inconsistent and is often influenced by socioeconomic factors, the availability of specialized care, and general healthcare infrastructure [2, 16, 17, 18]. Although some studies report the benefits of hydroxyurea therapy on pain management in SCD [19], there is still a need to explore the relationship between OPM use and treatment of SCD globally.

The frequency and severity of pain also significantly impact emotional well‐being among people with SCD [20, 21, 22]. More frequent and intense pain episodes are often associated with greater emotional distress, including feelings of frustration, sadness, and helplessness, which can increase the risk of depression and anxiety [1, 21, 22, 23]. This isolation can be worsened if others do not understand their condition or if societal stigma influences how they are treated in social settings [24, 25].

Furthermore, individuals experiencing frequent or severe pain may perceive unmet needs in pain management, and/or dissatisfaction with the care they receive. If pain is inadequately managed, or if healthcare providers fail to address their concerns, individuals are likely to report lower satisfaction with medical care [20, 26]. A well‐coordinated pain management plan, individualized to an SCD patient with empathetically engaged healthcare team, can greatly improve satisfaction with care [26]. However, empathy and responsiveness alone may not be sufficient for patients experiencing very high levels of pain, particularly when oral medications are inadequate to control symptoms. These patients often have more severe disease and consequently poorer health outcomes, diminished quality of life, and greater emotional distress. This emphasizes the importance of integrating biological, pharmacological, and psychosocial approaches to achieve meaningful improvements in care. In this way, effective pain management is critical for physical well‐being, emotional health, and social functioning for individuals living with SCD.

While previous research has highlighted the relationship between pain and quality of life among people with SCD [21, 23, 27, 28, 29], there remains a significant gap in understanding how frequency of OPM use is associated with broader health outcomes, including physical health, emotional well‐being, social participation, and satisfaction with care. In addition, existing studies relied on single‐country samples. This study aimed to collect data from individuals in 11 countries across six continents, which helps us look at global patterns in a more consistent way. Therefore, the objective of this research is to examine differences in demographic characteristics, physical and emotional well‐being, social participation, and satisfaction with medical care between two groups globally: those who require OPM once or twice a month and those who require OPM once a week or more.

Methods

Design and Setting

The cross‐sectional design took place from February to September 2024, across five continents: Africa, Asia, America, Europe, and Australia. Sickle cell clinics in each of these regions were provided the link for the digital survey.

Sample

Inclusion criteria required being diagnosed with SCD. There was no age, gender, and geographic area restriction. Potential participants who were not able to read English or have a proxy available to complete the survey for them were excluded.

An adequate sample size for this study was computed by power analysis to detect statistically significant differences between the two OPM frequency groups. We used G*Power software (3.1.9.7) with a medium effect size (w = 0.35), a significance level of 0.05, and a desired power (1−β) of 0.80. Based on these calculations, adequate sample size should be at least 105.

Data Collection Process

Data collection was conducted using a convenience sampling method from February to June 2024. For this, participants were recruited online by patient advocacy networking and social media groups related to SCD. To increase the response rate, periodic reminders were sent to the participants through email or social media to fill in the online, anonymous survey. All respondents 18 years of age or older were informed that participating in the survey constituted giving their informed consent. For minors, the survey responses came from either a parent or a healthcare provider on the patient's behalf to appropriately reflect the child's status. Healthcare providers could also answer on behalf of adult participants who did not speak English. All data were collected anonymously and stored in a secure password‐protected database that only the research team had access to, protecting participant confidentiality. All participants were informed that they could omit responses to specific questions or withdraw from participation at any point in the research without any consequences.

Data Tool

The data tool is provided in the Supporting Information. Demographic data collected in the survey include age, categorical questions on gender, income, country of residence, and SCD genotype. For healthcare access, the survey included questions related to having private insurance or reliance on government healthcare, coverage of SCD‐related expenses, and access to medical resources, which were all categorical variables.

Given the truly global nature and scope of this study addressing sociocultural barriers, income inequality, access to care, and geographies, we designed questionnaires, adopting elements from quality of life and health‐related quality of life (HRQoL) measurement tools to capture a holistic view of the experiences of individuals with SCD. Specifically, items were adopted or adapted from the following scales.

Short Form Health Survey‐36 (SF‐36)

This scale is a self‐reported measure of overall health and quality of life [30]. It includes eight domains of health and is designed to determine health status in various populations. Questions adapted and adopted for the current study include “My overall physical health is,” “How often do you feel happy?” and “Do you find it challenging to manage your emotions?”

Pediatric Quality of Life Inventory (Peds QL)

This tool is used for children and adolescents and includes questions about physical, emotional, social, and school functioning [31]. Questions such as “How often do you or your child suffering from sickle cell disease have to take pain medicine by mouth?” and “How often did you or do you miss school due to sickle cell‐related complications?” are reflective of this tool.

Adult Sickle Cell Quality of Life Measurement Information System (ASCQ‐Me)

This scale was developed to assess physical, mental, and social well‐being of adults with SCD, and includes items related to pain episodes, social and emotional impact, and disease management [32]. Questions adapted for the current study include “Have you ever had to have your joint replaced due to damage to bones from sickle cell disease?” and “Do you feel that sickle cell disease affects your happiness on a daily basis?”

World Health Organization Quality of Life (WHOQOL)

This tool covers broad aspects of quality of life, including physical health, psychological health, level of independence, social relationships, and environment [33]. The current study questions related to access to healthcare, financial burden, and social support align with the domains covered by WHOQOL.

EuroQol 5‐Dimension (EQ‐5D)

This tool includes dimensions such as mobility, self‐care, usual activities, pain/discomfort, and anxiety/depression [34]. Questions in the current study, such as “Do you experience any of the following symptoms?” and “Have you ever been on medicines for depression/mood changes/anxiety due to sickle cell disease?” are reflective of EQ‐5D dimensions.

In the survey, general health status was evaluated with a Likert‐type scale (poor/fair vs. good/very good). Specific questions pertaining to symptoms and complications, such as pain, fatigue, jaundice, infections, frequency of hospitalization, and joint replacement due to damage caused by SCD, were included as categorical variables. Treatment status, including the use of hydroxyurea, folic acid, blood transfusions, penicillin for children below 5 years, and transcranial Doppler testing, was elicited with categorical questions.

Then, OPM use frequency was assessed by asking participants, “How often do you/your child suffering from sickle cell disease have to take pain medicine by mouth?” Response options ranged from “Every day” to “Rarely,” capturing the variation in pain experiences among individuals with SCD. For the purposes of analysis, responses were categorized into two distinct groups based on frequency of OPM use: those who reported using OPM “at least once a week” (including the options “Every day,” “More than 3 times a week,” “1–3 times a week,” and “Once a week”), and those who reported using OPM “once or twice a month.” This grouping allowed for meaningful comparison between individuals with higher versus lower pain management needs.

Emotional well‐being was assessed using questions on self‐reported happiness (sometimes/rarely vs. always/often), emotional regulation (yes/no), and the daily impact of SCD on happiness (yes/no/unsure). Participation in social activities was also reported via a Likert‐type scale (rarely/never, sometimes, and always/often). Other psychosocial factors, such as use of mental health medication, availability of mental health services, perceived support from teachers/classmates, and if they ever had to miss school because of complications related to SCD, were categorical variables. Finally, satisfaction with medical care was measured with a Likert‐type scale (very dissatisfied/ dissatisfied, neutral, and satisfied/very satisfied).

Statistical Methods

Data were analyzed using SPSS (version 29). Descriptive statistics including frequencies and percentages for categorical variables were calculated. Chi‐square tests were applied to evaluate the differences in categorical variables such as sample characteristics, health status, symptoms, treatment types, and emotional well‐being based on OPM frequency. Spearman's rank correlation analyses were run to test the relationships among the variables. The level of significance considered was p < 0.05. Missing data were less than 5% and were handled with the approach of pairwise deletion.

Among all participants, 63.6% completed the survey for themselves (self‐reported status), parent‐reported status was 29.8%, and proxy‐reported status was 6.6% (completed by a healthcare provider on behalf of the participant). The mean age was 21.93 years (SD = 12.19), with 67.5% of the sample ≥ 18 years. The majority (76.9%) of the participants were from African countries. The participants’ income levels reported were 31.9% low, 46.2% low‐middle, and 21.8% upper‐middle income. Most participants (62.6%) were diagnosed with SCD between 1 month and 10 years of age, and 56.6% had the SS genotype. Most of the sample (62.5%) reported that both parents had SCD, 75.2% did not have insurance coverage for all SCD‐related costs, and 64.8% depended on government healthcare.

A significant difference was found in the income level between the two groups (p = 0.002). Of those who required OPM at least once a week, 44.3% were classified as having low incomes, while for the group requiring OPM once or twice a month, the figure was 19.0%. For all other demographic characteristics, no significant differences were observed (p > 0.05).

Other symptoms included anemia, avascular necrosis, back pain, constipation, mental health issues, circulatory problems, eye problems, gallstones, memory loss, heart palpitations, vomiting, and organ failure.

The overall self‐reported health status was significantly different between the two groups (p < 0.001); of participants needing OPM at least once a week, most (73.8%) rated their health as poor or fair, versus 26.2% needing OPM once or twice a month. Similarly, 57.4% of the participants needing OPM at weekly basis noted that they experienced shortness of breath compared to only 35.1% of those needing OPM at monthly basis (p = 0.015). Also, there was a significant difference in the number of hospitalizations per year for crisis between groups, where 46.8% of the weekly group had four or more hospitalizations compared to 20.0% in the monthly group (p = 0.002). By the same instance, the number of school days missed due to SCD complications was significantly higher in the weekly group. Of them, 51.7% often or always missed school, while in the monthly group this was only 25.0% (p = 0.002). The other health and treatment characteristics did not show any significant differences between two groups (p > 0.05).

Emotional Well‐Being, Social Support, and Satisfaction by OPM Use Frequency

Table 3 illustrates the emotional well‐being, social support, and satisfaction with medical care for all participants. Overall, 60.3% of participants reported feeling happy sometimes or rarely, while 39.7% reported feeling happy always or often. In terms of emotional regulation, 52.1% reported they could not regulate their emotions easily, whereas 35.5% reported no difficulties, and 12.4% were uncertain. Regarding participation in social activities, 41.8% reported they participated rarely, 37.7% sometimes, and 20.5% always or often. A total of 56.2% indicated that their happiness was affected by SCD on a daily basis. Last, 11.5% of the participants reported being on medication for their mental health, and 17.4% reported access to mental health services. Of the respondents, 44.1% perceived support from teachers or classmates, while 55.9% did not. In addition, regarding satisfaction with medical care, 27.0% reported dissatisfaction or strong dissatisfaction, whereas 38.5% noted neutral satisfaction, and for 34.4% it was satisfactory or very satisfactory.

When we compared these variables by OPM use frequency, significant differences were observed between the groups. In the weekly OPM group, more participants responded that they felt happy always or often, compared with the monthly group, at 27.4% versus 52.5% (p = 0.005). Discussion

This research study analyzed the results of a global survey conducted among patients suffering from SCD in 11 countries with the highest global sickle cell prevalence spread over six continents. Based on results, this study highlights several health and treatment characteristics, emotional well‐being, social support, and satisfaction with medical care, which are significantly different among individuals with SCD according to the frequency of their OPM use.

First, participants requiring OPM at least once a week or more reported poorer health and more frequent shortness of breath, along with more hospitalizations due to crises, than participants who required OPM once or twice a month. These results are consistent with earlier studies indicating that higher severity and frequency of pain are associated with greater disease burden and frequent healthcare use [28, 35]. Indeed, the weekly OPM group reported significantly more missed school days, indicating daily functioning and educational opportunities were significantly disrupted by frequent pain crises [23, 29]. The findings point to the need for improved disease management aimed at reducing painful episodes, particularly among high‐frequency OPM users.

Furthermore, it is important to consider the role of chronic pain, neuropathic pain, and opioid‐induced hyperalgesia in understanding the patterns of OPM use observed in this study. Individuals with SCD who frequently use opioids may experience heightened pain sensitivity due to opioid‐induced hyperalgesia, which can contribute to a cycle of increasing pain and higher medication use [36]. Chronic pain itself, along with episodes of exacerbation, significantly impacts physical functioning, emotional well‐being, and social participation [37]. Recognizing these complexities underscores the need for long‐term, multidisciplinary strategies in managing pain for individuals with SCD, including careful opioid management, non‐opioid pain strategies, and integration of psychosocial support individualized to the SCD patient.

Additionally, in our study, SCD‐specific treatments and preventive measures, including the use of hydroxyurea and screening by transcranial Doppler, did not differ significantly between the two OPM groups. This reflects that pain frequency is not only affected by treatment adherence, but is also affected by other factors related to genetic variability, the presence of comorbidities, or access to healthcare services [38]. These findings mirror a general low rate of preventative measures, such as transcranial Doppler screening at 16.5% in our study. Another study also investigated the prevalence of transcranial Doppler screenings among children with SCD aged 2–16 years in North Carolina Medicaid from 2016 to 2019 and found that only 39.5% to 40.1% of children underwent annual TCD screenings [39]. In line with the existing literature, our results on transcranial Doppler test indicate unmet needs for comprehensive care to reduce disease progression and complications [6, 13].

Emotional well‐being and social participation were also lower in the group of the weekly OPM users. For instance, they reported lower levels of happiness, more daily impact of SCD on mood, and less social engagement compared to their monthly OPM counterparts. These results are in line with the literature reporting poor mental health and a lack of social connectedness due to chronic pain and stigma [25, 40, 41, 42]. Disparities in satisfaction with medical care further support addressing patient concerns and ensuring empathetic, patient‐centered care in the setting of frequent pain episodes [26].

Importantly, access to mental health services and the perceived support from teachers and classmates did not differ significantly between groups. The overall low access to mental health services—17.4% in our study—illustrates the insufficient integration of mental health support in routine SCD care. Providing resources to address emotional regulation and decrease the psychosocial impact of pain may improve quality of life among individuals with SCD, especially those experiencing pain frequently [1, 43].

Lastly, the findings of the present study show how frequency of OPM use is related to health and psychosocial factors in individuals with SCD across a wide variety of settings and cultural contexts. These significant group differences contribute to the existing literature by suggesting that more frequent OPM use may reflect challenges in physical and emotional well‐being, social functioning, and satisfaction with care [21, 44]. Overall, our study demonstrates that monitoring frequency of OPM use can illuminate unmet needs and opportunities to alleviate suffering. While causal relationships cannot be established due to the cross‐sectional design, our results underscore the need to investigate how comprehensive and multidisciplinary approaches might support improved outcomes and quality of life for this population.

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first truly global OPM frequency and quality of life survey that analyzes these unique endpoints. By understanding these differences, this study contributes to the literature on the overall well‐being of people with SCD. The inclusion of a global sample also provides a unique opportunity to understand the broader challenges faced by people with SCD worldwide.

Nonetheless, the results should be considered in light of the limitations of the study. The sample size did not allow for subgroup analysis, which would have provided a more nuance in results specific to setting. Despite the small sample size, it is a diverse sample, yet the results show the universality of SCD pain affecting overall quality of life. Other limitations include the relatively short period of data collection and language limitations. Had the survey been available in multiple languages, additional participants may have been recruited, not only enlarging the sample size but ensuring even broader representation. Another limitation of this study is that OPM use was measured only by frequency and did not distinguish between opioid and non‐opioid analgesics, nor did it capture dosage or intensity of use. This relatively broad categorization may have obscured important differences in pain management practices across settings. More detailed assessments of medication utilization, such as those used in other global SCD studies (e.g., CaSIRE and SWAY), would allow for more nuanced insights into prescribing patterns, treatment adequacy, and patient experiences. Future research should incorporate these distinctions to better elucidate global similarities and differences in pain management. The last limitation is the potential for selection bias. Participants may be more engaged with advocacy groups or connected to formal healthcare systems, such as SCD clinics. This could mean that the sample includes individuals who are more informed, receive more consistent care, or are managing more severe disease, compared to the general SCD population. These factors may also differ by country, given variations in healthcare access and clinical availability. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the generalizability of the findings.

Conclusion

OPM frequency is an important metric associated with quality‐of‐life surveys performed to date, analyzing these and providing unique insight into SCD and psychosocial endpoints. Understanding these correlations provides an opportunity to target interventions to improve the overall well‐being of people with SCD. This global sample also provides a unique perspective on the challenges faced by this population worldwide.
