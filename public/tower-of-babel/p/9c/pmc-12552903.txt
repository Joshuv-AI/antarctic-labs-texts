Effects of maternal late-pregnancy and early-infancy exposures to respiratory syncytial virus circulating intensity on the risk for respiratory syncytial virus hospitalisation in the first two years of life: a comparative retrospective cohort study between Scotland and Singapore
Authors: Shihao Ma, Bingbing Cong, Chee Fu Yung, Harish Nair, You Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundAlthough it is recognised that young infants could be protected against respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) infections by maternal RSV antibodies, such protective effects are not well assessed at the population level and are confounded by varied exposures to RSV circulation in the community during early infancy. In this study, we aimed to disentangle the role of late-pregnancy and early-infancy exposures to RSV circulation in determining RSV hospitalisation risk in offspring by explicitly accounting for the background exposure to RSV circulation.MethodsIn this comparative retrospective cohort study, we leveraged RSV surveillance and hospitalisation data from Scotland and Singapore and constructed retrospective cohorts by birth months, with each birth month cohort as the observation unit. For each birth cohort, the outcome of interest was laboratory-confirmed RSV hospitalisation until the age of two years; the exposures of interest were RSV community incidence rates during the late-pregnancy period (defined as three months before birth) and during the early-infancy period (defined as three months after birth). In Scotland, we included hospital records from children born between Jan 1, 2017 and Nov 30, 2022 from the national health database available with Public Health Scotland. A quasi-Poisson model was used to assess the role of late-pregnancy and early-infancy exposures to RSV circulation in determining the risk for RSV hospitalisation for the age of 0–<6 months, 6–<12 months and 12–<24 months, separately, while accounting for the background RSV incidence rate in the community obtained from surveillance dashboard concerning viral respiratory diseases by Public Health Scotland. The analysis above was subsequently repeated in Singapore with medical data from children born between April 1, 2005 and June 30, 2015 at KK Women's and Children's Hospital. A random-effect meta-analysis was conducted to synthesise the incidence rate ratio (IRR) derived from the regression model above across the two countries.FindingsA total of 13,838 hospitalisations from 71 birth month cohorts and 8475 hospitalisations from 123 birth month cohorts were included in Scotland and Singapore, respectively. Increase in early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation consistently increased the risk for RSV hospitalisation in the first six months in Scotland and Singapore (pooled IRR: 2.47, per 10/100,000 increase in the RSV incidence rate in the community; 95% CI: 2.23–2.74). In the second year of life, there was borderline significance for the early-infancy RSV exposure to RSV circulation in Scotland and Singapore (pooled IRR: 1.15, 95% CI: 1.01–1.31). While increase in late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation was consistently associated with decreased risk for RSV hospitalisation for the first three months of life in Scotland and Singapore (pooled IRR: 0.56, 95% CI: 0.46–0.69), for the subsequent three months (i.e., 3–<6 months), the decreased risk persisted only in Scotland (IRR: 0.19, 95% CI: 0.10–0.36) and not in Singapore (IRR: 1.03, 95% CI: 0.83–1.28). For both countries, there was no association between late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation and RSV hospitalisation beyond six months of life.InterpretationBy differentiating the effect of maternal late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation from early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation, this study evaluates the effect of maternal RSV exposure against RSV severe disease at the population level. While this study used population-level maternal late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation as a proxy for maternal RSV antibody levels, the observed shorter duration of protection associated with late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation in tropical (Singapore) versus temperate (Scotland) regions indicates that the potential role of local RSV circulation patterns on this protective effect warrants further consideration. Our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and more serological studies based on antibody responses are needed to evaluate the effect of late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation on RSV hospitalisation in regions with different circulation patterns.FundingNone.

Research in context

Evidence before this study

We searched PubMed from database inception to May 17, 2025, for studies that reported the association between maternal respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) antibody level (or exposure to RSV as a proxy) and RSV diseases in infants using the search terms “(RSV OR respiratory syncytial virus) AND (Maternal OR Prenatal OR Pregnan∗) AND (hospitali∗ OR Bronchiolitis OR Pneumonia OR lower respiratory) AND (child∗ OR pediatric OR paediatric OR infant∗)”. No language restriction was applied. Existing serological studies with modest sample size (from 131 to 1418) suggest that maternally derived RSV neutralizing antibodies could provide protection against RSV hospitalisation in young infants; however, mixed results were observed regarding the effect size and duration, partly due to the lack of adjustment for the varied level of RSV exposure among infants.

Added value of this study

In this population-level retrospective cohort study, we disentangled the effect of maternal late-pregnancy and offspring early-infancy exposures to RSV circulation in the community on RSV-associated hospitalisation risk in the first two years of life by leveraging community RSV circulation and hospitalisation data from Scotland and Singapore, representing temperate and tropical regions respectively. While in both countries, increased early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation was found to increase the risk for RSV hospitalisation in the first six months of life and the second year of life to a lesser extent, there were notable discrepancies in the role of late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation between the two countries — protective effect of increased late-pregnancy exposure was observed for the first six months of life in Scotland but only for the first three months of life in Singapore, suggesting that RSV seasonality patterns might affect the duration of protection from maternal RSV exposure against offspring RSV hospitalisation.

Implications of all the available evidence

Local RSV seasonality patterns may influence the protective effect of maternal RSV exposure. As the use of maternal late-pregnancy exposure to community RSV circulation as a proxy for maternal antibody levels is associated with potential uncertainties and biases, our findings should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating and highlight the need for further serological investigation into the duration of protection of RSV maternally derived antibodies in regions with different RSV circulating patterns.

Introduction

Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is a leading cause of acute lower respiratory infections (ALRI) in infants and young children.1,2 Over 60% of RSV-associated hospitalisation and mortality occur during the first year of life.3 Recently, novel RSV prophylaxis products that provide passive immunisation for infants, namely a long-acting monoclonal antibody and a maternal vaccine, have received licensure and are being used in national immunisation programmes in high and upper middle income countries.4

Previous studies showed that the risk for RSV diseases during infancy varied substantially by birth months relative to local RSV season; those born before the peak month of RSV epidemics had the highest risk, largely owing to high-level of exposure to RSV during early infancy.5, 6, 7 Moreover, maternal RSV antibody could also have played a role8, 9, 10; mothers of infants born shortly before onset of RSV season were likely to have low level of antibody from antenatal exposures, which translates to elevated risk for their babies.

However, it is challenging to disentangle the role of maternal exposure to RSV during the third trimester of pregnancy and the offspring's exposure during the early infancy period in determining the risk for severe RSV disease. In temperate countries, RSV exhibits distinct seasonal circulation11; mother–infant pair is mostly expected to have either high late-pregnancy exposure followed by low early-infancy exposure, or low late-pregnancy exposure followed by high early-infancy exposure; high late-pregnancy exposure followed by high early-infancy exposure is uncommon due to the relatively short duration of the RSV season. Since the onset of the COVID-19 pandemic, unprecedented periods of persistent low RSV circulation and persistent high RSV circulation were observed in Scotland. Therefore, this disruption of RSV seasonality provided a unique opportunity to disentangle the effects of both exposures. Moreover, it remains unclear whether these effects persist in regions with year-round RSV circulation, such as Singapore, a country lying on the equator.12

In this retrospective cohort study, we aimed to disentangle the effects of maternal late-pregnancy and early-infancy exposures to RSV circulation on the risk of severe RSV disease during the first two years of life at the population level by leveraging hospitalisation and surveillance data in Scotland with seasonal RSV circulation compared to Singapore with year-round RSV circulation.

Methods

Study design and data sources

Retrospective birth cohort analysis in Scotland

We conducted a retrospective birth cohort analysis of children born in Scotland between January 1, 2017, and November 30, 2022 using national data of new births (National Records of Scotland13), hospitalisation (Scottish Morbidity Record, SMR14) and surveillance of laboratory test results (Electronic Communication of Surveillance in Scotland, ECOSS15). We considered an individual birth cohort as those who were born in the same month of a calendar year (e.g., January 2017). The study was conducted at the individual birth-cohort level as children in each birth cohort shared the same history of exposure to RSV circulation in the community. Specifically, there were two exposures of interest: late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation in the community, defined as the average weekly RSV incidence rate among individuals of all ages in the community (available from the Scottish surveillance dashboard16) over three months before births; and early-infancy exposure, defined as the average all-age RSV incidence rate in the community during three months after births. The outcome of interest was age-specific and age-cumulative incidence rate of RSV-associated hospitalisation in the first two years of life; RSV-associated hospitalisation was defined as hospitalisation with the following ICD-10 codes anywhere in the clinical diagnosis field indicating RSV infection/bronchiolitis (J12.1, J20.5, J21.0, and B97.4), or hospitalisation with laboratory-confirmed RSV infection using polymerase chain reaction (PCR) (Fig. 1).Fig. 1Schematic figure of study design. Schematic figure of study design. RSV: Respiratory syncytial virus.

A data-driven method previously used to determine influenza epidemics, namely the moving epidemic method,17 was applied to define high RSV circulation intensity for the Scottish RSV surveillance data, which determined that an RSV incidence rate of 1.7/100,000 was the threshold for signalling start of RSV epidemic. Using this cut-off, all birth cohorts were split into four groups based on the late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation and early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation: high late-pregnancy exposure and high early-infancy exposure (H-H group), low late-pregnancy exposure and high early-infancy exposure (L-H group), high late-pregnancy exposure and low early-infancy exposure (H-L group), and low late-pregnancy exposure and low early-infancy exposure (L-L group).

Comparative analysis in Singapore

To verify the observed association of late-pregnancy and early-infancy exposure to circulating RSV with RSV-associated hospitalisation risk in children in Scotland, we chose a tropical country with a year-round RSV circulating pattern as comparison. Similar to above, a retrospective birth cohort analysis of children born in Singapore between April 1, 2005, and June 30, 2015 using national data of new births was used.18 As described elsewhere,19 records of all admissions with laboratory confirmation of RSV infection among children up to the age of two years or until the end of 2015 were extracted from KK Women's and Children's Hospital-KK Hospital (the single public healthcare specialist women's and children's hospital in Singapore). In KK Hospital, all children admitted with respiratory symptoms are routinely tested for respiratory viruses using direct immunofluorescence assay (DFA) during the study period.

Statistical analysis

For Scotland, we compared age-specific and cumulative RSV-associated hospitalisation rate (per 100,000 live births) for each chronological month of age until two years of life across the four exposure groups, namely the H-H, H-L, L-H, and L-L groups. Furthermore, a quasi-Poisson model was constructed to assess the role of late-pregnancy and early-infancy exposures to RSV circulation in determining RSV hospitalisation risk. The dependent variable was number of RSV-associated hospitalisations (with the number of live-births as an offset) in one of the three age groups: 0–<6 months, 6–<12 months and 12–<24 months. The independent variables were the late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation, early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation, and the RSV background exposure beyond the early-infancy period corresponding to the age group in the model (that is, for example, when the outcome of interest was RSV hospitalisation during 6–<12 months of life, the background exposure was the average RSV incidence in the community during the same period), all of which were included as continuous variables in the model; the effect size was presented as the risk per 10/100,000 increase in the RSV incidence rate in the community. Detailed statistical model formulae are available in Appendix p 3. Subgroup analysis was conducted to stratify 0–<6 months into 0–<3 months and 3–<6 months to examine whether any statistical significance observed in the age group of 0–<6 months persisted in each of the two subgroups. An ad-hoc analysis was also conducted to determine the role of late-pregnancy and early-infancy exposures to RSV circulation among infants aged 4–6 months, separately by each month of age. We further explored each month during the late-pregnancy period, i.e., the first, second and third month before birth.

For Singapore, the same quasi-Poisson model as above was used to assess the effect of late-pregnancy and early-infancy exposures to RSV circulation on RSV hospitalisation risk. As no data from RSV surveillance in the community were available, data on RSV detection among hospitalised cases were used as a proxy for intensity of exposure to RSV circulation in the community; specifically, it was defined as the ratio of the monthly number of RSV-associated hospitalisations to the maximum monthly hospitalisation number observed during the study period, taking the range of 0–1. For ensuring comparability, the RSV exposure proxy in Singapore was rescaled for matching with the RSV exposure measure (i.e., RSV incidence rate in the community) in Scotland by assuming that the effect of early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation was the same between Scotland and Singapore in infants aged less than 6 months. Of note, the rescaling did not change statistical significance but rather adjust the magnitude of effect sizes, enabling cross-country comparisons. Sensitivity analysis separating study period into the two periods, 2005–2009 and 2010–2015, was conducted to examine the time-specific robustness of the model.

For the quasi-Poisson model, the incidence rate ratio (IRR) was derived by exponentiating the regression coefficient for each independent variable as the effect size measure. We further conducted synthesis of RR by a random-effect meta-analysis across the two countries using the metafor R package.20

All data analyses were performed in R software (version 4.2.0). This study was conducted and reported in accordance with the REporting of studies Conducted using Observational Routinely-collected health Data (RECORD) Statement. Ethical approvals with waiver of informed consent were obtained from the NHS Scotland Health and Social Care Public Interest and Privacy Panel (Project No: 2223-0019), and from the Singhealth Centralised Institutional Review Board (Ref: 2017/2223). The study used anonymised and aggregated data.

Results

In Scotland, prior to the COVID-19 pandemic, RSV epidemics usually occurred during the autumn and winter months, starting in October and ending in February of the following year; during the COVID-19 pandemic, circulation of RSV was persistently low during the 2020–21 autumn and winter season; and there was an out-of-season RSV resurgence in the summer of 2022, which extended to February of 2023 (Appendix p 7). A total of 71 individual birth month cohorts in Scotland were included from January 2017 to November 2022, in which 13,838 RSV hospitalisations occurred. In Singapore, RSV exhibited near year-round circulation from January 2005 to December 2015; during this period, 8475 RSV hospitalised cases were recorded in a total 123 individual birth month cohorts (Appendix p 7). The study populations of the two countries were similar in terms of maternal age, prematurity and sex ratio. Singapore had a higher low birth weight and congenital heart disease (10 per 1000 live births) and Down syndrome (3 per 1000 live births), while Scotland had a higher infant mortality rate (less than 3 per 1000 live births) (Appendix p 5). It should be noted that these differences are likely due to the different guidelines and recommendations and study duration between the two countries (Appendix p 5).

In Scotland, based on the RSV circulation strength in the community during late-pregnancy and early-infancy periods, the birth cohorts were divided into four exposure to RSV circulation groups: H-H group (6 cohorts); L-H group (15 cohorts); H-L group (15 cohorts); and L-L group (35 cohorts) (Appendix p 4, 6). In the first year of life, the cumulative RSV hospitalisation rate was highest in the L-H group (6603/100,000, 95% CI: 6411–6799), followed by the H-H group (3300/100,000, 95% CI: 3078–3533) and the L-L group (2885/100,000, 95% CI: 2799–2972), and was lowest in the H-L group (2230/100,000, 95% CI: 2114–2350). In the second year of life, the differences in the RSV hospitalisation rate among the four groups persisted but narrowed, with lowest hospitalisation rate (903/100,000, 95% CI: 829–982) in the H-L group and highest rate (1392/100,000, 95% CI: 1302–1486) in the L-H group (Fig. 2, Appendix p 7).Fig. 2The age-specific and cumulative RSV-associated hospitalisation rates (per 100,000 live births) by age in months through the first two years of life by the four exposure groups of cohorts in Scotland. Results from regression analysis showed that increase in early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation consistently increased the risk for RSV hospitalisation in the first six months in Scotland and Singapore (pooled IRR: 2.47, per 10/100,000 increase in the RSV incidence rate; 95% CI: 2.23–2.74). For infants aged 6–<12 months, an increase in early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation was found to decrease the risk for RSV hospitalisation in Scotland (IRR: 0.68, 0.49–0.97), while no significant association was observed in Singapore (IRR: 0.96, 0.82–1.13), although the difference between the countries was not statistically significant (p = 0.081). In the second year of life, there was borderline significance for the early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation in Scotland and Singapore; the pooled IRR was 1.15 (95% CI: 1.01–1.31), suggesting a slight increase in the risk of RSV hospitalisation (Fig. 3). In Scotland, late-pregnancy exposure to increased RSV circulation in the community was associated with decreased RSV hospitalisation risk during the first six months of life (IRR: 0.42, 95% CI: 0.31–0.57) (Fig. 3); subgroup analysis showed that such association persisted at the age of 0–<3 months (IRR: 0.50, 95% CI: 0.36–0.70) and 3–<6 months (IRR: 0.19, 95% CI: 0.10–0.36); further ad-hoc analysis confirmed that the association held for each month of age in 3–<6 months age band (Fig. 4, Appendix p 9). By contrast, we observed a borderline statistically non-significant association between late-pregnancy exposure to increased RSV circulation with RSV hospitalisation risk during the first six months of life in Singapore (IRR: 0.85, 95% CI: 0.72–1.002) (Fig. 3). Further stratification by age suggested that increased late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation was associated with decreased RSV hospitalisation risk at the age of 0–<3 months (IRR: 0.60, 95% CI: 0.47–0.76) but not for 3–<6 months (IRR: 1.03, 95% CI: 0.83–1.28). In both Scotland and Singapore, exploratory analysis that stratified the late-pregnancy period into the first, second and third month before birth yielded findings consistent with the main analysis for the age of 0–<6 months as well as 0–<3 months and 3–<6 months (Fig. 4, Appendix p 10–11). Discussion

In this comparative retrospective cohort study, we disentangled the effect of late-pregnancy and early-infancy exposures to RSV circulation on RSV-associated hospitalisation risk in the first two years of life by leveraging data from two countries (Scotland and Singapore) with different RSV epidemiology. In both countries, increased early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation in the community was found to increase the risk for RSV hospitalisation in the first six months of life and the second year of life to a lesser extent. However, there were important discrepancies in the role of late-pregnancy exposure to increased RSV circulation between the two countries — protective effect of increased late-pregnancy exposure to increased RSV circulation was observed for the first six months of life in Scotland but only for the first three months of life in Singapore. These findings prompt further serological investigation into the protection provided by maternal RSV exposure in settings similar to Singapore where RSV circulation is found year-round.

As expected, early-infancy exposure to increased level of RSV circulation was associated with increased risk for RSV hospitalisation during the first six months of life, regardless of local RSV seasonality patterns; the increased risk persisted in the second year of life albeit to a lesser extent as infants experiencing RSV season during early infancy would be relatively younger when the second RSV season occurred in the following year. Within the first six months of life, we showed that the association between early-infancy RSV exposure to increased RSV circulation and hospitalisation risk was statistically significant only for the age of 0–<3 months but not 3–<6 months of age, reflecting the decreased risk of RSV severe diseases over increased age as immune and respiratory systems matures; interestingly, we also observed a protective effect of increased early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation for RSV hospitalisation at the age of 6–<12 months in Scotland (after accounting for the exposure to RSV circulation).

In Scotland, we showed that increase in late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation was associated with a 58.0% reduction in the risk of RSV hospitalisation in young infants aged 0–<6 months (the reduction persisted in both 0–<3 months and 3–<6 months). The above findings were consistent with the Maternal Immunization Study for Safety and Efficacy (MATISSE) phase-3 trial results, which demonstrated an efficacy of 56.8% against RSV hospitalisation within 180 days after birth.21 The protective effect of maternal late-pregnancy exposure to increased RSV circulation in Singapore could only be observed in the first three months (i.e., no protective effects at 3–<6 months). This shortened duration of protection could be explained by the year-round RSV circulation patterns in Singapore. Moreover, our findings suggest that the protective effect of maternal late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation remains limited compared to the high risk of early-infancy exposure to increased RSV circulation, underscoring the importance of immunisation.

We acknowledge several limitations in our study. First, we focused on the infant population as a whole and did not stratify our study cohorts further by high-risk factors, such as prematurity, congenital heart diseases, and broncho-pulmonary dysplasia; nonetheless, as the occurrence of these conditions were likely to be independent of month of birth, we did not anticipate main findings to be biased. Second, as the unit for analysis was birth-month cohort (i.e., population level) rather than individual child, our findings might not be generalisable to the individual level. It is worth noting that our study used late-pregnancy exposure to RSV circulation in the community as a proxy for maternal RSV antibody level at the population level; therefore, the study findings by no means suggest that maternal RSV infection during late pregnancy could protect infants against RSV hospitalisation. Third, due to the absence of national RSV surveillance data in Singapore, we used data on RSV detection in hospitalised young children as a proxy for maternal exposure level during late pregnancy; nonetheless, it should be noted that standardised testing process at the only public healthcare specialist paediatric hospital in Singapore have been widely used to generate temporal trends of RSV circulation in previous publications.22 Although the study populations from both countries were similar in some demographic characteristics, they differed in other risk factors, such as the prevalence of low birth weight, congenital heart disease, and Down syndrome. These differences are likely due to variations in clinical guidelines and recommendations and non-overlapping study periods between the two countries. Additionally, RSV-associated hospitalisations in Singapore were identified by DFA, which had lower sensitivity compared to molecular methods such as PCR and might have resulted in under-detection of RSV cases; nonetheless, as our analysis focused on relative trends across cohorts with different levels of exposure to RSV circulation rather than absolute hospitalisation rates, the potential impact of the detection bias was considered minimal. Finally, as an ecological study, our analysis cannot establish causality between late-pregnancy exposure to increased RSV circulation and infant risk of severe RSV infection. While we adjusted for background RSV circulation, we acknowledge that co-circulating pathogens or the COVID-19 pandemic related changes in transmission patterns might have influenced our findings; other residual confounding might present, such as variations in health-care services and hospital admission criteria over time and between the two countries. Future serological studies measuring neutralising antibody titres in cord blood could provide more direct evidence of immune protection and help validate our findings; our findings should therefore be interpreted as hypothesis-generating.

In summary, this study disentangles the effects of late-pregnancy and early-infancy exposure to RSV circulation, quantifying the population-level protection conferred by maternal exposure to RSV circulation. Although the differential durability of this protection between temperate and tropical regions suggests that local RSV circulation patterns may influence the longevity of maternally derived antibodies due to natural infection, these findings are based on using maternal exposure to RSV circulation as a proxy for antibody levels and should therefore be interpreted with caution due to potential biases and uncertainties. More serological studies based on birth cohorts are needed to investigate the duration of protection conferred by natural-infection-induced antibodies in regions with different RSV epidemiology.
