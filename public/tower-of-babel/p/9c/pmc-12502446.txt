Sustainable valorization of fish head wastes into nitrogen-based nutrients: a comparative life cycle assessment
Authors: Somyong Lee, Soeon Ahn, Jin Ho Zong, Chulhong Oh, Sangwon Suh, Dong Soo Hwang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe production of nitrogen-based nutrients is a major contributor to climate change, with industrial ammonia synthesis releasing over 500 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalents annually—more than 1.2% of global anthropogenic greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. This is largely due to the fossil fuel–intensive Haber–Bosch process, which remains the dominant pathway for reactive nitrogen production in global food and feed systems. However, emerging alternatives such as marine biological by-products offer a fundamentally different nitrogen sourcing pathway. In this study, we conducted a comparative life cycle assessment (LCA) of greenhouse gas emissions for peptones derived from marine fish head waste (cutlassfish, salmon, tuna) and conventional land-based sources (soybean, skimmed milk). Marine-derived peptones exhibited substantially lower greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) —9, 4.5, and 2.6 kg CO₂-eq, respectively—compared to 22 kg for soybean and 64 kg for skimmed milk. When weighted by global production volumes, the average greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) were 5.8 kg CO₂-eq for marine-based peptones and 49.6 kg CO₂-eq for land-based peptones—an 88% reduction. Scenario-based analysis showed that marine systems, due to their centralized and energy-intensive nature, achieved up to 90.7% additional reductions in greenhouse gas emissions under renewable electricity substitution. To ensure methodological robustness, we applied the A–B comparison framework—which reduces attributional bias when comparing systems with differing co-product structures—and found that marine-based peptones consistently outperformed land-based alternatives across all simulation runs. These findings highlight the potential of fish-processing waste valorization as a low-impact nitrogen source and underscore its strategic role in developing climate-resilient biotechnologies and sustainable protein supply chains.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13036-025-00560-6.Keywords: Peptone, Life cycle assessment (LCA), Marine biomass, Sustainable nitrogen sources, Circular bioeconomy

Introduction

Ammonia production is a major industrial source of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, releasing nearly 500 million tonnes of CO₂-equivalents annually, over 1.2% of total anthropogenic emissions, primarily due to its reliance on the fossil fuel–intensive Haber–Bosch process [1, 2]. Despite this environmental burden, the process remains indispensable to modern agriculture, with approximately 85% of ammonia used in fertilizer manufacturing and nearly half of the global population relying on food produced using synthetic nitrogen inputs. This dependency underscores the critical role of ammonia in sustaining global food systems but also highlights the urgent need for alternative nitrogen sources that are less carbon intensive. Transitioning toward such alternatives requires exploring nutrient production pathways that reduce fossil fuel use while supporting circular bioeconomy strategies and enabling carbon-neutral nitrogen provisioning [1, 2].

The Haber–Bosch process contributes approximately 120 teragrams (Tg) of fixed nitrogen annually through fossil fuel–based synthesis [3]. In contrast, marine biological nitrogen fixation, carried out by free-living diazotrophs and cyanobacteria, contributes an estimated 71 to 196 Tg N per year—without requiring any external energy input [4]. This striking contrast underscores the potential for a strategic shift in nitrogen nutrient management: by increasing reliance on naturally occurring marine nitrogen fixation and reducing dependence on energy-intensive synthetic fertilizers, we can significantly mitigate environmental impacts. Greater use of natural nitrogen sources may also enhance nitrogen cycle efficiency and reduce ecological harm.

While numerous studies have evaluated the environmental implications of nitrogen fertilizer production—particularly focusing on the carbon-intensive Haber–Bosch process [1, 2]—relatively few have explored the life cycle performance of nitrogen-containing nutrients derived from marine byproducts. Recent advances in enzymatic hydrolysis have enabled the extraction of peptones from fish-processing residues, such as heads and bones, offering a promising valorization pathway for otherwise discarded biomass [5–7]. However, most existing studies have concentrated on process optimization or nutrient functionality, with limited application of cradle-to-gate life cycle assessment (LCA) to quantify environmental impacts. Furthermore, previous LCA studies on peptone production often relied on proxy data or failed to incorporate comparative uncertainty analysis, which may obscure the significance of environmental trade-offs. Recent methodological advancements now emphasize the importance of subtractive modeling (A–B difference approaches) and Monte Carlo simulation to assess uncertainty propagation and ensure statistically meaningful comparisons across alternative nutrient sources [8–12].

In response, recent studies have explored the use of marine-derived peptones produced from fish processing by-products—particularly from cutlassfish, salmon, and tuna heads—as a more sustainable alternative [5, 7, 13]. These marine peptones have shown potential to reduce greenhouse gas emissions and resource use, offering a sustainable alternative without compromising microbial performance.

To address this gap, the present study investigates the feasibility and environmental performance of marine-derived peptones produced via enzymatic hydrolysis of fish head waste specifically from cutlassfish (Trichiurus lepturus), chum salmon (Oncorhynchus keta), and yellowfin tuna (Thunnus albacares) in comparison with conventional land-based peptones derived from soybean and skimmed milk. A cradle-to-gate life cycle assessment (LCA) was conducted to quantify environmental impacts across four key indicators: global warming potential (GWP100), fossil fuel depletion, water use, and land occupation [14]. In addition to LCA, the functional properties of the marine-based peptones were evaluated to assess their suitability for microbial fermentation and other biotechnological applications [5–7].

This comparative approach offers a comprehensive perspective on the sustainability trade-offs between marine- and land-based nitrogen sources, using peptone as a representative nitrogen-containing nutrient. The findings aim to inform the development of more resource-efficient and lower-impact alternatives to conventional nutrient inputs, while supporting broader efforts to reduce environmental footprints and promote circular bioeconomy strategies [13].

Materials and methods

Experimental and raw materials

This study builds upon previous experimental findings to evaluate the environmental performance of peptone production from marine-based byproducts. The selected raw materials include the heads of chum salmon (Oncorhynchus keta), yellowfin tuna (Thunnus albacares), and cutlassfish (Trichiurus lepturus), chosen for their high global capture volumes and the substantial proportion of head mass relative to total body weight. According to the FAO and NPAFC Capture Production Statistics, recent annual global catches were approximately 1.0 million tonnes for salmon (including chum, pink, sockeye, and other species), 1.35 million tonnes for yellowfin tuna—a species commonly used in peptone production—and 1.23 million tonnes for cutlassfish [15–17]. Based on species-specific head-to-body mass ratios—approximately 16% for salmon, 13% for yellowfin tuna, and 19% for cutlassfish—these production volumes yield estimated head waste quantities of 160,000 t, 182,000 t, and 228,000 t, respectively.

Rather than using fresh biomass, this study utilizes post-processed fish heads typically discarded during seafood processing. These heads were hydrolyzed enzymatically to produce high-value peptones. Primary experimental data used to construct the mass and energy balances were obtained from Marasinghe’s work on cutlassfish head hydrolysis [13], which employed a continuous enzymatic process using Protamex (proteases from Bacillus sp.). In addition, unpublished experimental data for chum salmon and yellowfin tuna head hydrolysis were directly performed by Dr. Chulhong Oh (one of the corresponding authors), where other commercial proteases were also applied. Although these details were not fully described in the initial submission because the LCA modeling applied the generic ‘enzymes’ dataset from ecoinvent (no protease-specific LCI was available), we now explicitly specify the enzymes used for transparency. enabling species-specific modeling without the need for proxy extrapolation. The datasets included detailed material flows, enzyme and water usage, and energy requirements for each species. Secondary background data for upstream processes, such as enzyme production and electricity generation, were sourced from the ecoinvent 3.9.1 database [18].

Peptone production process

Marine-based peptones were produced using a standardized enzymatic hydrolysis process comprising four main steps: pretreatment, enzymatic hydrolysis, centrifugation, and drying. Operating parameters—such as steaming duration, enzyme dosage, and water-to-substrate ratio—were optimized for each species based on their tissue characteristics [5–7, 19]. A schematic overview of the process is provided in Fig. 1. Comprehensive mass and energy flow data for each unit operation of fish-based peptones (cutlassfish, salmon, tuna) are summarized in the Supporting Information (SI, Table S1a). Stepwise inventories for land-based peptones (soybean and skimmed milk) are provided in SI Table S1b. A comparative summary of inputs and outputs for both marine- and land-based systems, normalized to the functional unit of 1 kg freeze-dried peptone, is presented in SI Table S2. Although minor species-specific process optimizations (e.g., enzyme dosage, hydrolysis time) were implemented, the core hydrolysis system and unit operations were standardized across all scenarios to ensure fair and consistent comparison between feedstocks. This harmonized approach enables the differences in greenhouse gas emissions to be attributed primarily to the raw material origin rather than process variability.

Process flowchart for peptone production

Although detailed LCA inventory data for marine peptone production were experimentally derived, corresponding process parameters for soybean- and skimmed milk–based peptones were compiled from published patents and literature sources [20–23]. These include process steps such as alkali extraction, enzymatic hydrolysis, and drying for soybean peptone, and enzyme-assisted hydrolysis with ultrafiltration for skimmed milk peptone. All mass and energy input data were normalized to 1 kg of peptone to enable consistent comparison across nutrient sources. Thermal treatment steps indicated as ‘heat’ in Fig. 1 were in practice supplied by electrically powered devices. Accordingly, the associated energy demand was already accounted for under electricity consumption, and no separate heat flow is reported in SI Table S1.

Life cycle assessment (LCA)

A cradle-to-gate life cycle assessment was conducted to quantify and compare the environmental impacts of marine- and land-based peptone production. The assessment followed the ISO 14,040 and ISO 14,044 standards, covering the four key phases: goal and scope definition, life cycle inventory (LCI), life cycle impact assessment (LCIA), and interpretation.

Modeling was carried out in OpenLCA (version 1.11.0), using the ecoinvent 3.9.1 database to ensure consistency and transparency in background processes. The system boundary encompassed all operations from raw material acquisition to the production of 1 kg of dried peptone, excluding downstream use and end-of-life stages. For marine-based systems, a cut-off approach was applied, treating fish head waste as burden-free at the point of entry into the system in line with ISO 14,044 cut-off criteria. For land-based systems (soybean and skimmed milk), allocation was likewise not required, because the input flows used in our model (e.g., soybean, skimmed milk) were defined directly as the amounts necessary to produce 1 kg of peptone, rather than as shares of broader co-product systems. To assess robustness, we additionally performed a sensitivity analysis in which fish heads were treated as low-value co-products and allocated using an economic value-based approach, with results reported in the SI Table S4a-b.

All inputs, including water, enzyme, and energy usage, were normalized to the functional unit. Residual outputs such as bones and skin were excluded from the main product stream but documented in the inventory as “fish residues” for potential valorization.

Impact assessment methodology

The environmental impact assessment was conducted using OpenLCA and incorporated two distinct methods. Global warming potential over a 100-year time horizon (GWP100) was calculated based on the IPCC 2021 characterization factors and is summarized in Table 1. All other impact categories—including terrestrial ecotoxicity, fossil resource scarcity, water consumption, and human noncarcinogenic toxicity—were assessed using the ReCiPe 2016 Midpoint (H) method. This combined approach was chosen to ensure that climate change results remain fully consistent with international reporting frameworks (e.g., UNFCCC inventories) by using the most recent IPCC factors (AR6, 2021), while maintaining methodological coherence across multiple environmental categories through ReCiPe 2016. Results for these additional indicators are reported in the Supporting Information (SI, Figure S1). The use of midpoint-level indicators allows for consistent comparisons and provides decision-relevant insights into the relative sustainability of marine-derived and land-based peptone production systems.

Results

Comparative greenhouse gas emissions analysis of marine- and land-based peptones

To evaluate the climate change impact of peptone production from different nitrogen sources, we assessed the Global Warming Potential over a 100-year horizon (GWP100), using IPCC 2021 characterization factors. Table 1 presents the values of greenhouse gas emissions for two conventional land-based sources—skimmed milk and soybean—and three marine-based alternatives derived from cutlassfish, salmon, and tuna head waste.

As shown in Table 1, the values of greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) for land-based peptones were substantially higher than those of marine-based alternatives. Skimmed milk-based peptone exhibited the highest impact at 64 kg CO₂-eq per kilogram of product, followed by soybean at 22 kg CO₂-eq. In contrast, marine-based peptones showed considerably lower climate impacts, ranging from 2.6 kg CO₂-eq for tuna, 4.5 kg CO₂-eq for salmon, to 9.0 kg CO₂-eq for cutlassfish. The particularly low greenhouse gas emissions of tuna-based peptone can be explained by its higher protein yield per unit of raw material, which reduces the amount of head waste required, and its lower drying energy demand compared to salmon and cutlassfish. Contribution analysis confirmed that raw material efficiency and energy intensity were the dominant drivers of this difference (Fig. 2).

Contribution analysis of variance in greenhouse gas emissions in land-based and marine-based peptone production systems. (A) Process-level contributions from five standardized unit operations: raw material preparation, protein hydrolysis, enzyme inactivation, centrifugation, and freeze-drying. (B) Input-level contributions from electricity, water, and materials input (including enzymes, chemical reagents, and feedstock)

To provide a fair and representative comparison, we recalculated weighted average values of greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) for each category. For marine-based peptones, weighting was based on global wild capture production and head yield fractions across the relevant species: approximately 1.2 million tonnes of cutlassfish (19% head), 1.4 million tonnes of yellowfin tuna (13%), and 1.0 million tonnes of salmon with a 16% head yield [15–17]. These figures yield estimated head waste quantities of 228,000 t, 182,000 t, and 160,000 t, respectively—corresponding to weight fractions of 43%, 34%, and 23%. Based on these proportions, the weighted average greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) for marine-based peptones was calculated to be 5.80 kg CO₂-eq.

For land-based peptones, weighting was applied based on global raw material production volumes. In 2021, global skimmed milk powder production exceeded 5 million tonnes, while soybean protein concentrate production was approximately 2.7 million tonnes [20, 24]. Based on these quantities, a weight of 65% was assigned to skimmed milk and 35% to soybean. This yielded a weighted value of greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) of 49.6 kg CO₂-eq for land-based peptones.

These results confirm the substantial environmental advantage of marine-based peptone production per functional unit. As shown in Fig. 3, this weighted comparison demonstrates that marine-based peptones offer a markedly lower greenhouse gas emissions impact (GWP100), highlighting the environmental potential of valorizing fish head waste.

The weighting approach applied here ensures methodological consistency by incorporating global production volumes and realistic head yield fractions for each species, thereby avoiding skewed interpretations caused by extreme values of individual feedstocks. All inventory data were sourced from the ecoinvent v3.9 database, and marine-based process models were constructed based on experimental protocols developed in our previous study [13], with further refinements introduced in this work. Identical enzymatic hydrolysis conditions were applied across all scenarios to ensure comparability. Model outputs from OpenLCA were cross-checked using different IPCC characterization factors (AR5 vs. AR6). As shown in the sensitivity analysis (Figure S2), absolute values varied slightly between the two versions, but the relative differences between marine- and land-based systems remained consistent, confirming the reproducibility of the results.

Uncertainty and difference distribution approach

To ensure that the observed reduction in climate impact is not only based on mean values but is also statistically valid under input variability, we employed a two-tiered Monte Carlo framework that combines independent uncertainty propagation and paired distributional comparison.

First, the five individual peptone feedstocks were aggregated into two weighted average systems, land-based (skimmed milk and soybean) and marine-based (cutlassfish, salmon, and tuna), using global production volumes as weighting factors. For each aggregated system, 1,000 Monte Carlo simulations were conducted to propagate uncertainty from upstream parameters such as electricity usage, enzyme input, and water consumption.

All parameters were modeled using log-normal distributions with conservative geometric standard deviations (e.g., ± 20% or greater), which are appropriate for LCA since input parameters typically follow multiplicative and skewed distributions. Setting a geometric standard deviation of 1.2 reflects a wide but plausible uncertainty range, intentionally designed to stress-test the comparison by allowing for significant variability in both directions. Despite this conservative uncertainty configuration, the resulting distribution of greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) for marine-based peptones remained substantially narrower and more stable (e.g., ± 6.7 kg CO₂-eq for tuna) than that of land-based peptones (e.g., ± 17.6 kg CO₂-eq for skimmed milk), indicating greater robustness of marine-based systems under variable conditions.

However, comparing the two output distributions separately can obscure shared variances and interdependencies arising from process similarities. Therefore, to directly assess whether marine-based peptones offer a statistically meaningful advantage, we applied a distribution-of-differences framework following Suh and Heijungs [9–12, 19]. For each of the 1,000 iterations, we simultaneously sampled input values for both systems and calculated the paired difference in greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) (𝑎i − 𝑏i), where 𝑎 and 𝑏 represent the land-based and marine-based values, respectively, under the same parameter set. This pairing removes confounding effects and captures only the systematic performance difference between systems.

As shown in Fig. 4, the entire distribution of differences lies above the zero baseline, with a median advantage in greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) of approximately 37.5 kg CO₂-eq and an interquartile range spanning from about 25 to 52 kg CO₂-eq. Importantly, no iteration resulted in a negative difference, meaning that in none of the 1,000 simulated scenarios did marine-based peptones exhibit higher climate impacts than land-based ones. This outcome confirms the statistical dominance of marine-based systems even under wide-ranging uncertainty, and supports the robustness of marine byproduct valorization as a climate-preferable pathway for nitrogen-based nutrient production.

Comparative contribution analysis

To better understand the sources of uncertainty in greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) results, we conducted a contribution analysis at both the process stage and materials input level. This analysis identifies which parts of the production system contribute most to the variance observed in Monte Carlo simulations (Fig. 2).

At the process level (Fig. 2A), both marine-based and land-based peptone systems showed freeze-drying as the dominant contributor to variance in greenhouse gas emissions, accounting for 39% and 32% of the total variance, respectively. This highlights the energy-intensive nature of the drying process, particularly in marine-based systems where water content and stabilization needs are higher. Other process stages such as centrifugation and enzyme inactivation also contributed notably to overall variance in both systems, though with slightly more even distribution in the land-based case.

At the input level (Fig. 2B), a distinct divergence emerges. In marine-based systems, electricity use overwhelmingly dominated the uncertainty, contributing 96% of total variance in greenhouse gas emissions. In contrast, the variance in land-based systems was more broadly distributed across electricity (65%), water (12%), and material inputs (23%). This indicates that while land-based peptone production involves a greater diversity of input sensitivities, reflecting the complexity of processing agricultural feedstocks such as soybean and skimmed milk, marine-based systems are primarily influenced by energy-related factors.

These findings not only illustrate differences in uncertainty structure but also point toward clear environmental improvement strategies. Specifically, the energy-concentrated variance in marine-based systems suggests a high responsiveness to energy source transitions. When electricity is sourced from renewable options, as explored in the subsequent scenario analysis, marine-based systems demonstrate a marked reduction in greenhouse gas emissions, far greater than the marginal improvements seen in land-based systems. This sensitivity implies that decarbonization of electricity supply has a disproportionately large benefit in marine-based pathways.

Overall, marine-based peptone production exhibits a more streamlined and energy-driven uncertainty profile. Its strong dependence on electricity not only simplifies variance attribution but also enables more impactful mitigation through renewable energy integration. In contrast, land-based systems, while generally more complex in material flows, present a broader and less concentrated target for intervention. These insights reinforce the strategic value of marine byproduct valorization in building low-carbon, climate-resilient nitrogen nutrient systems.

Scenario-based sensitivity integration

To assess the impact of potential improvements in peptone production, we developed and evaluated three scenario-based modifications and their effects on greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100). These scenarios were applied to both marine-based and land-based systems using production-weighted average values. A summary of the greenhouse gas emissions results (GWP100) and percentage reductions under each scenario is presented in Table 2.

Scenario A (100% renewable electricity) led to the most dramatic reduction in greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100). The marine-based systems experienced a 90.7% decrease, dropping from 5.8 to 0.5 kg CO₂-eq, while land-based systems showed a 46.6% reduction, from 49.6 to 26.5 kg CO₂-eq. This result aligns with previous contribution analyses, which identified electricity—especially from freeze-drying—as the dominant contributor to climate-related impact. The large margin of improvement illustrates the transformative potential of transitioning to renewable electricity sources, particularly for energy-intensive marine-based systems. In Scenario A, all electricity inputs were substituted with the ecoinvent dataset ‘electricity, from renewable energy sources, at medium voltage’. According to the dataset metadata, this mix consists of 91% hydropower, 5% solar, and 4% wind.

Scenario B (40% reduction in drying energy use) simulated the replacement of freeze-drying with a more efficient drying method, such as vacuum belt drying. This led to a 17.6% reduction in greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) for marine-based (to 4.8 kg CO₂-eq) and a 14.3% reduction in land-based systems (to 42.5 kg CO₂-eq). While less impactful than Scenario A, this scenario shows that improving energy efficiency in critical process stages—especially drying—can yield meaningful emissions reductions.

Scenario C (10% improvement in hydrolysis efficiency) targeted upstream biochemical processes. By increasing protein yield and reducing energy intensity per output, this modification reduced greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) by 22.1% in marine-based systems (to 4.5 kg CO₂-eq) and by 15.0% in land-based systems (to 42.2 kg CO₂-eq). Though more modest, this scenario highlights the relevance of optimizing enzyme-based processing for climate impact mitigation. The choice of these scenario parameters was based on literature evidence. Scenario B reflects documented energy savings achievable through advanced drying technologies such as vacuum belt drying and modular optimization frameworks [25]. Scenario C was motivated by enzymatic optimization studies that have reported measurable improvements in protein yield and hydrolysis efficiency when valorizing fish by-products [26].

Among the three, Scenario A produced the largest environmental benefit, with greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) in marine-based systems reduced by over 90%, nearly double the reduction observed in land-based systems. This outcome confirms the findings from variance-based contribution analysis, which showed electricity alone driving over 90% of marine system emissions. The marine-based process, being highly centralized around energy-intensive steps, responded strongly to a single change in energy sources. In contrast, the land-based system exhibited more distributed environmental burdens across multiple inputs and processes, limiting the effectiveness of isolated interventions. These results suggest that marine-based peptone production is not only lower in baseline emissions but also more responsive to decarbonization strategies, especially when supported by renewable energy and energy-efficient technologies. Beyond climate change impacts (GWP100), the scenario analysis was extended to fossil resource scarcity, terrestrial ecotoxicity, and human non-carcinogenic toxicity to evaluate potential impact shifting. Results (Table S3) showed consistent trends across all impact categories, with no evidence of burden shifting between scenarios. Renewable electricity in Scenario A was assumed for all electricity-consuming processes, including hydrolysis, enzyme inactivation, centrifugation, and freeze-drying.

Discussion

Environmental and resource efficiency of marine-based peptones

While fish byproducts offer a sustainable nitrogen source, their industrial adoption has been limited by inconsistent data and underreporting, especially in developing regions. However, increasing emphasis on carbon neutrality has renewed interest in valorizing these byproducts. Our results demonstrate that extracting peptones from fish heads yields significantly lower greenhouse gas emissions compared to conventional land-based sources such as soybean and skimmed milk. Species-specific compositional analysis revealed that cutlassfish heads contain approximately 17.2% muscle and 27.6% bone, while salmon heads contain up to 21.6% muscle and 16.7% oil [5, 27]. These components can be further processed into high-value derivatives such as hydroxyapatite, chondroitin sulfate, and omega-3 fatty acids. Importantly, partial protein recovery from crushed bone fractions can increase total peptone yield. It is estimated that up to 90% of the head mass can be valorized, with the remaining 10% potentially used as low-impact fertilizer or feed, thereby offsetting additional emissions. A more precise estimation of this residual fraction—using proximate analysis and lipid profiling—is essential to improve system boundaries in life cycle assessment (LCA). Broader valorization of fish waste, beyond head tissues, could therefore enhance carbon mitigation outcomes through maximum resource recovery and minimized disposal burdens. A methodological limitation of this study lies in the assumption that fish head waste enters the system as a burden-free input, consistent with the ISO 14,044 cut-off approach. Although this reflects current best practice and is widely applied in LCA standards, we acknowledge that fish heads can also be valorized into low-value co-products. To address this, we performed a sensitivity analysis using economic allocation (SI, Table S4a-b). Importantly, this approach ensures a fair comparison between marine- and land-based systems: for land-based raw materials (soybean, skimmed milk), allocation was not applied because they were modeled as inputs dedicated to peptone production only, rather than co-products of other processing chains. Thus, both marine- and land-based systems were assessed under consistent and comparable boundary conditions.

Functional suitability for biotechnological applications

Peptone quality was also evaluated based on molecular size and amino acid composition. Cutlassfish-derived peptides exhibited the lowest average molecular weight (~ 609 Da), indicating high solubility and rapid uptake, which are beneficial for microbial fermentation. In contrast, salmon and tuna peptones contained higher levels of essential amino acids, including lysine and leucine—important for recombinant protein synthesis and cell culture. These characteristics suggest that species-specific selection of marine peptones can support tailored applications: salmon and tuna for high-structural-integrity uses such as therapeutic protein production, and cutlassfish for microbial platforms requiring rapid metabolism and nutrient efficiency [5–7]. In addition, amino acid composition analysis (Table S5) showed that marine peptones are nutritionally comparable to soybean and skimmed milk peptones, with source specific differences in profiles. Nevertheless, these differences do not undermine functional equivalence, since both marine- and land-based peptones fulfill the same role as assimilable nitrogen sources for microbial growth. Importantly, published evidence demonstrates that fish-derived peptones can even support superior microbial performance compared with soybean-, or skimmed milk-based peptones [28–30]. This confirms that, despite compositional variations, marine peptones provide robust nitrogen sources and may even offer advantages in microbial cultivation [31–33].

Integrated assessment and decarbonization pathways

A scenario-based analysis was conducted to explore decarbonization opportunities. Among the three scenarios, substituting conventional electricity with 100% renewable electricity (Scenario A) resulted in the largest reduction in greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100): a 90.7% decrease for marine-based peptones (from 5.8 to 0.5 kg CO₂-eq) and a 46.6% reduction for land-based peptones (from 49.6 to 26.5 kg CO₂-eq). This aligns with contribution analysis findings that electricity, especially for freeze-drying, accounts for over 90% of the variance in greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) in marine systems. Scenario B (a 40% reduction in drying energy use) and Scenario C (a 10% improvement in hydrolysis efficiency) showed moderate reductions but reinforced that targeted interventions can yield significant climate benefits. Marine-based systems were consistently more responsive, due to their centralized, energy-intensive processes [34]. In contrast, land-based systems had more distributed environmental burdens across multiple inputs and stages. This indicates that marine-based peptone production is not only lower in baseline emissions but also more adaptable to decarbonization strategies. Thus, when paired with clean energy integration and process optimization, marine-based peptones offer a compelling low-carbon alternative for biotechnology, food, and pharmaceutical industries [35–37].

Conclusions

This study demonstrated that fish head by-products—specifically from cutlassfish (CFH), salmon (SH), and tuna (TH)—can serve as environmentally sustainable alternatives to conventional nitrogen sources such as skimmed milk (SM) and soybean (SB) for peptone production. Through a comprehensive life cycle assessment (LCA), we evaluated and compared marine-based and land-based peptone systems in terms of greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100), fossil resource use, and water consumption. It should be noted that, while terrestrial ecotoxicity and human non-carcinogenic toxicity were calculated in the LCIA, they were not emphasized in the main text because they are less directly relevant to food-related bioprocessing and resource use. To ensure transparency, the full results are reported in the Supplementary Information (Figure S1; Table S3).

Using a production-weighted A–B difference approach, marine-based peptones showed a significantly lower carbon footprint, with a baseline average of greenhouse gas emission (GWP100) at 5.8 kg CO₂-eq compared to 49.6 kg CO₂-eq for land-based systems—an 88% reduction. Among marine species, tuna-derived peptone exhibited the lowest greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) at 2.7 kg CO₂-eq, followed by salmon (4.0) and cutlassfish (4.8). These reductions were consistent across additional scenario analyses, where substituting renewable electricity (Scenario A) reduced greenhouse gas emissions (GWP100) by 90.7% in marine systems and 46.6% in land-based systems. Process-focused improvements, such as energy-efficient drying (Scenario B) and enhanced hydrolysis (Scenario C), also contributed to meaningful emission reductions, especially in energy-intensive marine systems.

Overall, the findings reinforce the suitability of marine-based peptones as low-carbon nitrogen sources. Marine systems not only have lower baseline emissions but also respond more effectively to decarbonization strategies due to their centralized energy demands. These insights offer compelling life cycle evidence that valorizing marine by-products into peptones represents not only an environmentally advantageous alternative, but also a strategic pathway toward climate-resilient biotechnology and sustainable protein production.
