Nanoparticle carriers in the treatment of intracellular bacterial infections: Current approaches and challenges
Authors: Zheng Lu, Bin He, Zhixin Lei, Lijun Wu, Dongyang Ye, Dan Xu, Shanqin Chen, Xiaotian Zhang, Ziqi Weng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathogenic bacterial intracellular infection by Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Staphylococcus aureus is a major complication to treatment because of resistance to immune actions and the ability to withstand traditional antibiotics. Failure of existing therapies is usually due to inefficient intracellular penetration of drugs and inappropriate targeting of subcellular sites. We discuss the nanoparticle drug delivery systems (NDDSs) here that enhance intracellular delivery of antibiotics using customized carrier materials, targeted internalization processes and stimuli-responsive release of antibiotics. These systems enhance specificity of pathogen targeting and efficacy of drugs and overcome barriers of microenvironment. We also address translational challenges such as toxicity, heterogeneity of infection, scalability of manufacturing, and scanty clinical data. Future trends include versatile hybrid vehicles, combination with immunomodulators or gene-editing applications and AI-enhanced personalized nanomedicine. The potential to tackle pathogen-specific issues with nanotechnology advances can provide possible solutions to the treatment of persistent intracellular infections and antibiotic resistance, which are the prospects with NDDSs.Keywords: Nanoparticle drug delivery systems, Intracellular bacterial infections, Stimuli-responsive nanocarriers, Antibiotic resistance, Biomimetic nanoparticles

Introduction

The intracellular bacterial infections are extremely harmful to the health of humans and animals, with resultant chronic disease, treatment failures, and escalating cases of antimicrobial resistance (Fu and Harrison, 2021). Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Salmonella enterica, Staphylococcus aureus, and Listeria monocytogenes have also acquired the ability to enter host cells and establish a micro-reservoir, which is to a large extent immune to normal immune systems (Bai et al., 2023; Bussi and Gutierrez, 2019; Crowley et al., 2016; Shahid et al., 2021). Once internalized, these bacteria take advantage of the host pathways to escape being killed in the phagolysosomes, control immune signaling, and replicate in nutrient-rich niches, causing infections that are difficult to cure and prone to relapse (Fredlund and Enninga, 2014; Petit and Lebreton, 2022).

Consequently, conventional antibiotics are often ineffective against intracellular pathogens. This ineffectiveness is primarily due to poor membrane permeability, which limits the accumulation of antibiotics within host cells, and the hostile microenvironment of acidic or enzyme-rich phagolysosomes, which can further reduce antibiotic efficacy (Carryn et al., 2003; Tulkens, 1991). Additionally, antibiotics may accumulate in subcellular compartments that do not coincide with the bacterial niche, leaving intracellular bacteria unexposed to therapeutic concentrations (Baquero et al., 2023). As a result, prolonged high-dose antibiotic therapy is frequently required, which can promote the development of resistance and lead to recurrent infections (Jambhrunkar et al., 2023).

Poor membrane permeability is not the only reason why the conventional antibiotics have limited efficacy against intracellular and respiratory bacterial infections. Multifactorial resistance mechanisms are common in Gram-negative pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa, which is a combination of decreased outer-membrane permeability, active efflux systems, antibiotic-inactivating enzymes, target-site alteration, and adaptive stress responses (Adamiak et al., 2025; Giovagnorio et al., 2023). Specifically, P. aeruginosa exhibits high levels of intrinsic and acquired resistance due to low-permeability outer-membrane structure, deregulation or acquisition of β-lactamases, porin modifications that decrease intracellular accumulation of antibiotics. These processes have a synergistic effect and give rise to multidrug-resistant phenotypes that are hard to eliminate using free-drug formulations alone (Kürkçü et al., 2024; Nazir et al., 2025).

Besides classical determinants of resistance, biofilm formation, persister-cell populations, and horizontal transfer of resistance-related genetic elements make the treatment more difficult. Biofilms limit the diffusion of antibiotics, form a heterogeneous metabolism, and provide a tolerant microenvironment where slow-growing or dormant subpopulations survive treatment and reseede the recurrent infection (Fernández-Billón et al., 2023). P. aeruginosa also possesses a high level of genomic plasticity, which allows it to acquire and transmit accessory genes, which may help it adapt and resist antimicrobials (Chen et al., 2023). Such characteristics are particularly applicable in the case of lower respiratory tract infections, in which persistent infection niches of devices or chronic conditions prefer the survival of bacteria. Hence, nanoparticle-based drug-delivery systems are appealing not only due to their potential to enhance cellular uptake, but also due to their potential to circumvent loss mediated by efflux, enhance local drug concentration, enhance penetration into biofilms and infected cells, and complement combination strategies to overcome complex resistance phenotypes (Dangla-Pélissier et al., 2021; Johnston et al., 2023; Mahmoud et al., 2025).

Different other strategies such as antimicrobial peptides, vaccines, probiotics, immune potentiators, and plant products have been suggested to address these limitations (Millet and Maertens, 2011). They can however, not be used clinically due to reasons like low stability, low efficacy, the development of resistance, or high cost (Tucker et al., 2021).

To overcome these unsolved problems, there has been the emergence of nanoparticle drug delivery systems (NDDSs) as multifunctional platforms (Abed and Couvreur, 2014). They are especially suitable when it comes to the treatment of intracellular bacterial infections due to their distinct characteristics such as increased stability, regulated drug release, augmented cellular permeability, and targeted delivery (Hosseini et al., 2022; Subramaniam et al., 2021).

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a significant opportunistic Gram-negative pathogen that is involved in lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) especially in hospitalized and critically ill patients. It has a particularly high clinical implication in intensive care units (ICUs), where it is often linked to severe pneumonia and ventilator-associated pneumonia (VAP). A number of studies have indicated that P. aeruginosa is one of the pathogens that are frequently isolated in lower respiratory tract specimens. Indicatively, in a retrospective study of 1171 bacterial isolates of hospitalized patients with LRTIs, P. aeruginosa was identified in 16.2% of the isolates, indicating its significant role in the pathogenicity of the diseases (Guzek et al., 2015). Likewise, P. aeruginosa was reported as one of the major pathogens recovered in the sputum samples of patients with LRTIs (Uzoamaka et al., 2017), and it was reported that P. aeruginosa was mainly isolated in patients with LRTIs in the ICU, with the overall prevalence of 34.5% among the cases (Yang et al., 2021). These results highlight the need to appreciate the significance of P. aeruginosa as a significant etiological agent in LRTIs, particularly in hospital-acquired infections and intensive care units.

Therapeutic control of LRTIs related to P. aeruginosa is still difficult due to its high potential of resistance, genetic heterogeneity, and often being associated with multidrug resistance (MDR). MDR strains were found in ICU patients (23.5%) with P. aeruginosa pneumonia, and previous exposure to anti-pseudomonal antibiotics and nursing home residence were found to be independent risk factors (Trinh et al., 2017). In another study it was shown that there was a great deal of inter-strain variation and high resistance rates with 46.8% of isolates being amikacin resistant and most of them containing resistance genes including blaTEM and blaOXA (Yang et al., 2021). Furthermore, microbiome-based patient analysis of patients with P. aeruginosa VAP revealed that the microbial composition of the lower respiratory tract of patients with P. aeruginosa infection was significantly different compared to non-infected intubated patients, indicating that the P. aeruginosa infection is associated with complex microbial dysbiosis and not simple mono-pathogen colonization (Qi et al., 2018). All these observations add to the fact that P. aeruginosa does not just cause LRTIs, but is also a high-risk pathogen whose survival, adaptation, and resistance to antimicrobials complicate treatment particularly, justifying the use of advanced targeted delivery systems, like nanoparticle-based therapies.

This review is critical analysis of therapeutic options of intracellular bacterial infections using NDDSs. We pay attention to the choice of carrier materials and its relevance to the infection, intracellular internalization strategies, and the design of stimulus-sensitive drug delivery systems to be used in intracellular microenvironments. Besides literature review, we also point out challenges facing pathogen-specific, translational and emerging opportunities. This review will help to inform the rational design and clinical translation of NDDSs as next-generation anti-infective platforms by combining these views.

Carrier materials for NDDSs

Success in NDDSs in the treatment of intracellular bacterial infections heavily relies on the type of carrier material used (Howard et al., 2014). Although a variety of nanocarrier platforms have been created to treat cancer and other illnesses, few have shown apparent promise in the context of antibacterial therapy (Yao et al., 2020). These carriers may be generally categorized into inorganic, organic, and hybrid/biomimetic systems.

Inorganic carriers

Metallic nanoparticles (e.g., silver, gold, gallium, copper) and metal–organic frameworks (MOFs) exhibit intrinsic antibacterial properties in addition to serving as drug carriers (Salouti and Ahangari, 2014). For example, Ga2+/Cu2+-MOFs have shown synergistic activity against methicillin-resistant S. aureus (MRSA) by releasing both antibiotics and antibacterial ions (Wang and Clapper, 2022; Ye et al., 2023). Similarly, mesoporous silica and iron oxide nanoparticles facilitate high drug loading and controlled release (Ahmed et al., 2022; Kumar et al., 2023), while carbon nanotubes enhance phagocytic uptake and can damage bacterial membranes directly (Hassani et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2018). Ag@ADMH, and other silver nanoparticle-loaded hydrogels, provide sustained MRSA antibacterial performances and, at the same time, regulate the inflammatory and regeneration processes of the wounds (Chen et al., 2024; Masimen et al., 2022). However, their translation is limited by concerns about long-term toxicity, biodegradability, and off-target tissue accumulation.

Recent advances illustrate how careful selection of framework chemistry and composite design can mitigate some of these concerns while preserving strong antibacterial activity (Hasan et al., 2021). Polymer systems such as ZIF-8 incorporated into poly(ε-caprolactone) or PVDF membranes generate reactive oxygen species under light exposure and eradicate Gram-negative bacteria, yet display low cytotoxicity toward mammalian cells and good mechanical stability (Moradialvand et al., 2024). Other polymer-encapsulated MOFs exploit humidity-responsive or self-cleaning surfaces to resist biofouling, with some Cu-BTC–based fabrics showing negligible toxicity in invertebrate models (Hasan et al., 2021). These examples highlight how engineering MOFs as hybrid coatings or membranes can decouple antibacterial performance from extensive metal-ion leaching and support their use as NDDS carriers against microbial infections.

Organic carriers

Organic nanocarriers remain the most widely studied due to their biocompatibility and flexibility in drug loading.

•Liposomes: Nanoparticles in liposomes also increase the penetration rate of antibiotics into biofilms (Makhlouf et al., 2023), and the antibacterial efficacy of liposomal nanoparticles are superior to those of free drugs, and can also be used in combinations to counter resistance and relapse (Panthi et al., 2024). Microfluidics-based liposomes provide a high level of size control, better reproducibility, and higher therapeutic efficacy, which is a scalable solution to the shortcomings of standard liposome production (Marrazzo et al., 2021; Naghib and Mohammad-Jafari, 2024).Beyond conventional antibiotic-loaded vesicles, recent antibacterial formulations emphasize multifunctional lipid and polymeric systems that address biofilms and resistant strains (Faghihkhorasani et al., 2023). In particular, nanoencapsulation of essential oils and other hydrophobic antimicrobials in PLGA or lipid-based carriers enhances their stability and allows targeted action against biofilm-forming species and respiratory pathogens such as Haemophilus influenzae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, and Streptococcus pneumoniae (Moradialvand et al., 2024). These findings support the broader use of organic NDDSs as platforms for both conventional antibiotics and non-traditional antibacterial agents.

Hybrid and biomimetic carriers

To overcome the limitations of individual systems, hybrid carriers such as polymer–inorganic composites and biomimetic nanocarriers have been developed (Liao et al., 2020). MOFs, which are covered with lipids or polymers enhance the stability of drugs and targeting (Makowski et al., 2019). The better results of immune evasion and targeting to the infection site are provided by biomimetic platforms, such as exosome-coated nanoparticles and macrophage membrane-derived carriers (Li et al., 2025). These systems are highly promising when it comes to translational applications, but scalability and reproducibility remain the present challenges (Wang et al., 2023).

Metal-organic framework (MOF) based NDDSs

As antibacterial NDDSs, metal-organic frameworks (MOFs) have become increasingly popular due to their high surface area, which is highly tunable, as well as, modular metal-ligand chemistry (Maranescu and Visa, 2022). In addition to acting as passive reservoirs of antibiotics, most MOFs have intrinsic antimicrobial properties due to the release of metal-ions, the generation of ROS or contact-killing interfaces. Recent research indicates that ZIF-type and zirconium-based MOFs can be loaded with fluoroquinolones and or other antibiotics and can be designed to release their cargo in acidic or enzyme-rich microenvironmental conditions when exposed to infections, maximizing the concentration of their cargo locally but minimizing systemic exposure (Hasan et al., 2021). MOFs are also being incorporated into polymer matrices or textiles to produce mixed-matrix membranes and coatings that retain their structural integrity, exhibit low mammalian cytotoxicity and are active against Escherichia coli and other pathogens (Moradialvand et al., 2024). Although these have been described, the long-term fate of MOF fragments, the potential buildup of metal nodes in tissues, and the robust and scalable synthesis protocols are all significant factors to consider regarding the translation of MOFs into antibacterial NDDSs (Liu et al., 2024).

These systems show strong promise for translational applications, though scalability and reproducibility are current hurdles.

Among the various carrier types, liposomes and polymer-based systems are closest to clinical translation due to their well-established safety profiles and flexibility for functionalization (Reinsalu et al., 2024). Biomimetic carriers represent a promising next-generation approach because of their high targeting efficiency and immune evasion capabilities, although large-scale production methods are still under development (Han et al., 2024). Inorganic nanocarriers, while effective in vitro, require further long-term safety validation before clinical use.

Targeted internalization strategies

To be efficient in eradicating intracellular pathogens, NDDSs have to enter host cells and arrive at the bacterial niche. The internalization route has a great impact on biodistribution, drug release, and therapeutic efficacy. The internalization may be by non-specific physicochemical interactions, receptor-mediated process, or biomimetic process (Ying Yang & Xiaojing Wang, 2024). Notably, various approaches are more appropriate in case the target pathogen can live in vacuoles (e.g., Mycobacterium tuberculosis, Salmonella), or in the cytosol (e.g., Listeria monocytogenes, Shigella).

Non-receptor-mediated uptake

Passive uptake is strongly influenced by physicochemical properties of the nanoparticle, such as particle size, surface charge, and morphology (He et al., 2010). Nanoparticles smaller than 200 nm are typically internalized via clathrin-mediated or caveolae-mediated endocytosis, while cationic carriers enhance uptake through electrostatic interactions with negatively charged cell membranes (Zhang et al., 2009). Rod-shaped or high-aspect-ratio particles are internalized more efficiently than spherical ones. Although non-specific uptake is cost-effective and broadly applicable, its lack of precision and potential for off-target accumulation limit its clinical utility (Wang et al., 2022).

Immuno-evasive homologous targeting: Cell membrane-coated nanoparticles (e.g., macrophage or bacterial membranes) also provide immunity clearance protection. A novel antibiotic delivery system, mannosylated exosomes (MExos) with lysostaphin (MExoL) and vancomycin (MExoV), to intracellularly kill MRSA patients displayed rapid organ accumulation in vivo and allowed lowering vancomycin dosage to reduce toxicity and resistance (Fig. 2B) (Jia et al., 2022a, Jia et al., 2022b).

Such systems are especially compatible with pathogens that exploit phagocytic uptake pathways and are particularly relevant for targeting vacuole-associated bacteria; however, their production methods must be standardized and reproducible to enable clinical translation.

In bacteria that can be vacuole-residing (M. tuberculosis, Salmonella), receptor-mediated and biomimetic methods provide an improved means of targeting using natural uptake pathways (Crowley et al., 2016; Luan et al., 2025a, Luan et al., 2025b). Conversely, non-specific uptake and stimuli-responsive release is more suitable in the case of cytosolic pathogens (Listeria, Shigella) (Babaei et al., 2017; Shahid et al., 2021). Therefore, to develop effective NDDSs, it is crucial to implement the internalization strategy depending on the pathogen lifestyle.

Stimuli-responsive drug release

One of the key advantages of NDDSs is their ability to release therapeutic agents in response to specific microenvironmental signals within infected host cells. Stimuli-responsive systems can trigger antibiotic release at the site of infection, thereby increasing efficacy and reducing systemic toxicity. Relevant triggers for intracellular bacterial infections include pH, reactive oxygen species (ROS), host or bacterial enzymes, and pathogen-derived virulence factors (Gao et al., 2011).

pH-responsive systems

Phagosomes and phagolysosomes, where many intracellular bacteria such as Mycobacterium tuberculosis and Salmonella reside, are characterized by acidic conditions (pH ∼4.5–5.0). NDDSs can be engineered with acid-sensitive linkages (e.g., hydrazone, acetal bonds) or pH-sensitive polymers that destabilize in acidic environments, resulting in targeted drug release within infected compartments (Tang et al., 2011). pH-based triggering is one of the most validated and clinically translatable methods, as demonstrated by the delivery of rifampicin to phagolysosomal bacteria using liposomes containing pH-sensitive fusogenic lipids (Gao et al., 2010). MOFs also provide an inherently pH-responsive scaffold for antibiotic delivery. ZIF-8 frameworks loaded with ciprofloxacin, for example, show minimal drug leakage at physiological pH but rapidly release their cargo under mildly acidic conditions mimicking infected tissues and intracellular compartments, leading to efficient bacterial killing while limiting systemic exposure (Hasan et al., 2021). When combined with polymer coatings, these MOF systems can further tailor release profiles and improve biocompatibility, offering a complementary approach to classic polymeric pH-sensitive carriers in NDDS design (Moradialvand et al., 2024).

ROS-responsive systems

Phagosomal oxidative bursts produce abundant levels of ROS in bacterial clearance. NDDSs incorporating ROS-sensitive linkers (thioketal, aryl boronic esters) deactivate under the influence of oxidative stress to release encapsulated antibiotics (Zhang et al., 2015). The ROS-responsive micelles have shown an increased clearance of S. aureus in macrophages (Qiao et al., 2021). ROS levels are, however, dependent on infection stage and host immune status, which can lower uniformity among patient populations (Zhang et al., 2015). Certain MOF–polymer composites also exert antibacterial effects through in situ ROS generation rather than solely through cargo release. Zinc- and iron-based MOFs embedded in polymer matrices can act as photodynamic agents under visible or LED light, producing ROS at the material–bacteria interface and achieving several-log reductions in viable counts (Hasan et al., 2021). Such “carrier-as-therapeutic” designs blur the line between drug and delivery system and may be particularly useful where high local oxidative stress is desirable but systemic ROS exposure must remain low (Moradialvand et al., 2024).

Enzyme-responsive systems

•Host enzyme-responsive carriers: Enzymes like cathepsin B and phospholipases, which are highly found in lysosomes, can be used to induce the degradation of nanoparticles. Cathepsin B- cleavable polymeric carriers have been used to increase the intracellular release of fluoroquinolones (de la Rica et al., 2012).

Virulence factor–responsive NDDSs

A new technology uses bacterial toxins as release agents. Nanocarriers that are α-hemolysin-receptive based on S. aureus or typhoid toxin-receptive based on Salmonella rupture when exposed to the infection site and release antibiotics (Ruschig and Marschall, 2023). This scheme gives it outstanding pathogen specificity, but must be designed with care so as not to release prematurely into the systemic circulation (Landa et al., 2025).

Among the triggers, the pH-responsive systems are the best proven and most translatable to the clinic because of their predictable and reproducible nature, highly pathogen-specific and highly innovative designs (Gao et al., 2010). There is added specificity with ROS- and host enzyme-responsive carriers, but these carriers can vary based on host immunity (Pradhan et al., 2021; Zhang et al., 2015). Multi-stimulus-responsive platforms capable of integrating robustness and specificity could be useful in the future (Jahanbekam et al., 2025).

Challenges in translating NDDSs to clinics

Despite impressive preclinical results, the clinical translation of NDDSs for intracellular bacterial infections remains limited. Unlike oncology, where several nanomedicines have reached the market, antibacterial NDDSs face unique biological and regulatory barriers.

Biocompatibility and toxicity concerns

Long-term safety of nanocarriers remains a significant challenge. Carbon-based and metallic systems can accumulate in tissues, potentially causing oxidative stress and inflammation (Wu et al., 2022). Even polymeric and lipid carriers, which are generally considered biocompatible, may elicit immune responses or cause unforeseen off-target effects at higher doses (Elmowafy and Al-Sanea, 2021). Most toxicity studies have been conducted in rodents, making it difficult to predict human safety profiles (Sharma et al., 2021).

Heterogeneity of intracellular infections

Bacterial pathogens have diverse intracellular survival mechanisms that can only be addressed using NDDS approaches and not using a single size fits all solution (Osman et al., 2022). As an illustration, M. tuberculosis and Salmonella would remain in acidic phagolysosomes (pH 4.5–5.0), and, as such, pH-responsive carriers, including MOFs or acid-labile liposomes, would be the best choice, whereas Listeria and Shigella would escape into the neutral cytosol (pH 7.2), and, in such cases, a carrier with cytosolic delivery abilities, such as cell-penetrable peptide-conjugated nanoparticles (Duan et al., 2017; Pires et al., 2023). This pathogen-specific localization has a direct effect on the carrier selection: cationic liposomes increase phagolysosomal targeting of M. tuberculosis, whereas cytosol-targeting dendrimers are more effective (Listeria) (Sharma et al., 2018). Moreover, the differences in immune status, comorbidity, and microbiota composition between patients make them even more complex in predicting treatment and choosing NDDS (Ezike et al., 2023a, Ezike et al., 2023b).

Large-scale synthesis and reproducibility

Massive NDDS manufacturing (laboratory to manufacturing) introduces issues of consistency in particle dimensions, surface functionalization and batch to batch change (De Jong and Borm, 2008). Systems that are bone-like, such as exosomes and nanoparticles coated with membranes, have exceptionally high barriers since they use biological sources, which limit reproducibility and scalability (Alshawwa et al., 2022a, Alshawwa et al., 2022b). Such manufacturing challenges are particularly evident to more sophisticated inorganic materials like MOFs and carbon nanomaterials. Membranes, fibers, or coatings with potent antibacterial activity can be produced using scalable methods based on the sequential dipping, one-pot method, electrospinning, and ultrasonication to produce membranes, fibers, or coatings (Moradialvand et al., 2024). Each method introduces variability in crystal morphology, defect density, and metal–ligand composition that may alter drug-release profiles and safety (Afzal et al., 2022). Similarly, surface functionalization of carbon nanotubes or graphene, which is essential for dispersibility and targeting, can be difficult to standardize across large batches. Addressing these formulation-specific sources of variability is critical if complex inorganic NDDSs are to progress beyond proof-of-concept studies (Gao et al., 2011).

Regulatory and cost barriers

The regulatory mechanisms of nanomedicines are underdeveloped, particularly in the infection-related indications. Issues with the definition of critical quality attributes, production, and normalization of toxicity assays are a problem that halts clinical immunization (Milewska et al., 2021). Furthermore, complex NDDSs tend to be more expensive than conventional antibiotics and consequently are not readily adopted in low-resource settings where bacterial infections are most prevalent (Mittal et al., 2019).

Lack of clinical trial data

Although several NDDSs have entered clinical trials for cancer and inflammatory diseases, only a limited number have been evaluated for bacterial infections. The lack of robust clinical data hampers confidence in their therapeutic potential for these indications. This gap highlights the need for increased investment, strategic collaborations, and the development of infection models that more accurately reflect human disease (Manzoor et al., 2012; Mittal et al., 2019).

Pulmonary and LRTI-specific translational barriers

Although nanoparticulate antibiotic systems have a good preclinical potential, its clinical translation has been hampered by a number of practical and biological challenges. One of the biggest challenges is that formulations which are optimized on a laboratory scale do not necessarily maintain the same physicochemical characteristics when the formulation is scaled up to industrial scale, and thus, batch-to-batch variability in particle size, drug loading, release profile and storage stability occurs (Alshawwa et al., 2022a, Alshawwa et al., 2022b). Inhaled systems to be used in LRTIs, translation is even more complicated by the necessity to maintain aerosol performance, deep-lung deposition and formulation integrity during nebulization or dry-powder processing (Islam and Reid, 2024). Moreover, nanoparticles have to bypass mucus entrapment, surfactant interactions, alveolar macrophage clearance, and heterogeneous microenvironment of infection, which can decrease effective drug delivery to infected locations, once they have been transported to the lung (Huang et al., 2021; Sabuj and Islam, 2021). Recent reviews thus point at the fact that in vitro success may not be a predictive of in vivo or clinical performance of pulmonary nanomedicines.

There are also certain examples of these translational limitations. One of the latest nanoformulations in the pulmonary system is liposomal inhaled antibiotics but even these systems have significant limitations, such as formulation instability during aerosolization, lack of penetration into dense mucus or biofilm-rich areas, and lack of predictability of reproducible deposition across patients with varied lung pathologies (Bassetti et al., 2020). Polymeric nanoparticles can enhance sustained release and intracellular delivery but their translation is frequently hampered by the issues of residual solvents, toxicity of the polymer, sterilization, and long-term biocompatibility (Cipolla et al., 2016). Similarly, although nano-in-microparticle and dry-powder inhalation systems have the potential to enhance aerodynamic behavior, they must be engineered with a high level of control of particle engineering, moisture sensitivity, redispersion and manufacturability (Huang et al., 2026; Patel et al., 2026). All of this suggests that the primary problem is not just to prove the efficacy of antibacterials, but to develop a formulation that is stable, scalable, safe, device-compatible, and clinically reproducible in realistic pulmonary conditions. Thus, the development of nanoparticulate antibiotics in the future to treat LRTIs will rely on the combination of antimicrobial efficacy and formulation strength, compatibility with inhalers, toxicological and regulatory safety, at the earliest developmental stages.

The most important needs are infection-specific development strategies, routine safety testing, and investment on the international level, because although the pipeline of antibacterial NDDSs is not as extensive as that of oncology (Wei et al., 2012). It will be a collaborative process between the material scientists, microbiologists, clinicians, and regulatory bodies to close the scientific knowledge gap between the bench and bedside (Ezike et al., 2023a, Ezike et al., 2023b).

Future directions

The next generation of NDDSs for intracellular bacterial infections must move beyond proof-of-concept studies and focus on clinically relevant designs that facilitate therapeutic translation. Several promising avenues exist to advance these platforms toward clinical application.

Integration of biomimetic and stimuli-responsive strategies

With intracellular infections, single-function nanocarriers in most cases do not overcome the various barriers. Precise targeting and controlled release can be achieved using hybrid systems, which consist of biomimetic coating (e.g., macrophage membranes, exosomes) and a stimuli-responsive trigger (e.g., pH, ROS, bacterial enzymes) (Gao et al., 2023). These types of multifunctional NDDSs would be able to avoid immune clearance, reside in infected tissues, and release antibiotics only in the niches occupied by pathogens (Ezike et al., 2023a, Ezike et al., 2023b).

Examples of hybrid (biomimetic + stimuli-responsive) systems for intracellular bacterial infections include: (i) macrophage-membrane–coated, D-alanine–functionalized gold nanoparticles (MM@DAu NPs) that exploit membrane camouflage for homing to infected phagocytes and use near-infrared photothermal activation to precisely ablate intracellular bacteria via a cascade-targeting design; this platform significantly improved intracellular killing in bacteria-infected macrophages (Xiong et al., 2024). (ii) bacteria-activated macrophage-membrane–coated, ROS-responsive antibiotic nanoparticles, where membranes from pathogen-stimulated macrophages confer infection-site tropism and a reactive‑oxygen-species trigger enables on-demand antibiotic release inside infected cells, enhancing eradication of intracellular S. aureus (Luo et al., 2024). (iii) mannose-decorated, glutathione-responsive antibiotic nanocarriers that target mannose receptors (CD206) on macrophages hosting intracellular pathogens and employ the high-GSH intracellular milieu to unlock/accelerate drug release, boosting anti-intracellular MRSA activity (Peng et al., 2022).

NDDSs combined with immunomodulators or gene-editing tools

Bacterial persistence is not the only way that intracellular infections are maintained, but also through host immunity impairment. Immunomodulators that are carried by NDDSs (cytokines or Toll-like receptor agonists) have the potential to restore host defense mechanisms (Chauhan et al., 2021; Dai et al., 2022). Also, new approaches based on CRISPR-Cas systems with nanoparticle delivery have the possibility of selectively silencing bacterial resistance genes or virulence factors within host cells (Saw et al., 2022). Such combinations are potent strategies for defeating multidrug resistance (Pradhan et al., 2021).

(iv) Lipid–polymer hybrid NPs co-delivering an antibiotic and an immunomodulator—intracellular MRSA-targeted particles that load chrysomycin A with interferon-γ (IFN-γ) use cascade targeting to infected macrophages while IFN-γ boosts macrophage antimicrobial activity, together yielding superior clearance of intracellular MRSA (Cai et al., 2025). (v) Host-directed, immunoactive NPs for TB—biomimetic curdlan-PLGA nanoparticles (curdlan is a Dectin-1 agonist) reprogram infected macrophages by inducing autophagy and modulating innate responses, thereby reducing intracellular Mycobacterium tuberculosis in cells and in a mouse model (Bekale et al., 2025).

Artificial intelligence–assisted NDDS design

Machine learning and AI can be used to design NDDSs faster by making rational predictions of the optimal carrier properties, drug-nanoparticle interactions, and host-pathogen interactions (Aundhia et al., 2025; Vora et al., 2023). Using the AI-informed screening, it might be the most efficient to find the optimal formulations of various intracellular pathogens, and time-saving in terms of development timelines (Yacoub et al., 2022). Artificial Intelligence–Assisted NDDS Design for Intracellular Bacterial Infections: AI now accelerates three stages of NDDS development. First, payload discovery/optimization: machine-learning models mine metagenomes and proteomes to yield vast catalogs of antimicrobial peptides (AMPs) with in vivo activity, providing rich cargo options for macrophage-targeted, pH/ROS/GSH-responsive carriers against intracellular S. aureus and M. tuberculosis (Santos-Júnior et al., 2024). Second, formulation and delivery design: multiscale and data-driven models (e.g., DeepONet margination simulators; PBPK+QSAR/ML frameworks) predict how NP size/shape/charge, surface ligand density (e.g., mannose/peptides), and material class affect biodistribution and intracellular exposure in phagocytes, enabling in silico selection of designs with higher macrophage uptake and endo-lysosomal release (Kulkarni et al., 2025). Third, inverse design/personalization: AI reviews and toolkits now support ranking of smart, stimuli-responsive nanocarriers and tailoring formulations to host/pathogen features, pointing toward subtype- and patient-specific NDDS for hard-to-eradicate intracellular infections (Noury et al., 2025).

Personalized nanomedicine for intracellular infections

The heterogeneity of infections within the cells of patients requires precision medicine. Personally designed NDDSs with the help of AI could achieve optimal therapeutic outcomes at minimal toxicity levels based on the immune status of the affected person, the microbiota composition of the individual, and the genetic makeup (MENON). This implies that the possibility of patient-centered nanomedicine to treat infections such as tuberculosis or drug-resistant staphylococcal disease (Li et al., 2022), which may not go away, may be opened by point-of-care diagnostics and AI-based NDDS selection (Alghamdi et al., 2022).

Precision is further improved by surface-engineering rules (ligand orientation/density, charge/hydrophobicity, PEGylation) to tune organ/cell targeting and intracellular exposure, and by cell-membrane cloaks (e.g., macrophage membranes) that home to inflamed, bacteria-harboring phagocytes (Zheng et al., 2025). For tuberculosis and other intracellular infections, integrating host-directed therapy (HDT) payloads (autophagy inducers, immunomodulators) into macrophage-targeted NDDS supports phenotype-specific regimens that can shorten treatment and reduce tissue injury (Cubillos-Angulo et al., 2022). Recent infection-responsive, macrophage-targeted platforms demonstrate on-demand antibiotic release and intracellular killing, illustrating how design can match a patient's microenvironmental cues (Dai et al., 2024). Looking ahead, multi-omics frameworks in TB and other infections provide the blueprint for patient-tailored NDDS selection (payload + targeting ligand + trigger), operationalizing precision nanotherapy against hard-to-eradicate intracellular pathogens (Guha et al., 2024).

Emerging carbon- and graphene-based antibacterial platforms

Carbon nanotubes and graphene-derived materials provide an additional set of tools for next-generation antibacterial NDDSs. Functionalized nanotubes can act both as high-capacity antibiotic carriers and as active antimicrobials via membrane disruption, while surface-engineered graphene oxide enables photothermal and photodynamic therapies that can be switched “on” at the infection site using near-infrared light (Kumar et al., 2019; Xin et al., 2019). Recent graphene-polymer hybrids have achieved spatiotemporally controlled bacterial killing and accelerated wound healing in vivo, underscoring the potential of 2D materials to complement traditional metallic and polymeric nanoparticles (Liu et al., 2020). Future work will need to balance their potent antibacterial mechanisms with careful control of oxidation state, flake size, and surface chemistry to minimize off-target toxicity and ensure safe elimination from the body (Mei et al., 2020).

Future directions for LRTI treatment

Future nanoparticulate antibiotic studies must go beyond traditional nanoencapsulation to multifunctional and disease-adaptive systems that are more reflective of the pathological complexity of lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs) (AlQurashi et al., 2025). Recent developments indicate that inhalable nanocarriers, which have been designed not only to load drugs, but also to deposit in the deep lung, exhibit a long pulmonary retention, mucus and biofilm penetration, and intracellular delivery to infected macrophages and epithelial cells are the most promising platforms (Mondal et al., 2024). Recent trends include increased focus on lipid-based inhalable nanoformulations, polymeric nanoparticles to deliver drugs over time, and nano-in-microparticle dry-powder systems that have the potential to enhance aerosol behavior and local delivery of antibiotics to the lung (León-Buitimea et al., 2020).

These advances are especially applicable to chronic infections due to multidrug resistant pathogens like Pseudomonas aeruginosa, where failure to treat is mediated by biofilm formation, low antibiotic penetration and intracellular persistence (Yang et al., 2021). The other direction of importance in the future is the creation of stimuli-responsive, combination, and theranostic nanoplatforms. In recent literature, there is evidence of systems responding to infection-related signals like pH, enzymes or inflammatory microenvironment to release drugs locally, enhancing site specificity and reducing systemic toxicity (Nazir et al., 2025). Increasing interest is also in co-delivery approaches, such as antibiotics in combination with antimicrobial peptides, adjuvants, or anti-biofilm agents to conquer multidrug resistance and maximize bacterial killing (Vassallo et al., 2020). It is also emerging that nanomaterials can be used to aid integrated diagnostic-therapeutic (“theranostic”) strategies, such as being able to image infections and treat them at the same time, though these are still mostly preclinical (Ahmed et al., 2012). Together, these developments suggest that future developments in this area will rely on creating nanoparticulate systems that are antibacterial, but also pulmonary barrier-adaptive, biologically responsive and clinically translatable.

In the future, NDDS will involve the implementation of patient-specific multifunctional platforms rather than single-function carriers (Aslam et al., 2022). Filling the gaps in the field of translational research and unleashing the potential of nanomedicine in the fight against intracellular bacterial infection through the inclusion of continuous progress in biomaterials, immunology, and computational science is a realistic way to overcome the existing bottlenecks (Afzal et al., 2022).

Conclusions

To conclude, nanoparticle-based drug delivery systems (NDDSs) are one of the promising approaches to improving the treatment of intracellular bacterial infections through increasing the antibiotic stability, controlled release, uptake by the cell, and site-specific delivery. Nevertheless, these infections have therapeutic issues that go beyond the problem of limited drug permeability and are heavily dependent on complex resistance mechanisms, such as efflux activity, enzymatic degradation, target modification, biofilm formation and bacterial persistence. Such obstacles are especially applicable in lower respiratory tract infections (LRTIs), where such pathogens as Pseudomonas aeruginosa play a significant role in the disease burden, multidrug resistance, and adverse clinical outcomes. Recent developments suggest that the field is shifting toward the more advanced benefits of nanocarriers to more advanced nanoparticulate antibiotic systems that are specific to infectious microenvironment. Specifically, inhalable liposomal, polymeric and nano-in-microparticle formulations have become appealing to LRTIs due to their potential to enhance pulmonary deposition, mucus penetration, intracellular delivery, and biofilm and resistant bacterial population resistance. However, despite the promising results of numerous studies, greater rigorous analysis is required to differentiate between those platforms that simply enhance the performance of formulations and those that have quantitative decreases in MIC, intracellular bacterial burden, or biofilm viability. Meanwhile, there are still significant barriers to translationally that restrict clinical application. These are formulation instability in storage or aerosolization, manufacturing and scale-up issues, variability in deposition in diseased lungs, safety of carrier materials and the continuing disparity between preclinical efficacy and reproducible clinical benefit. Thus, the development in the future will not only be based on the design of more sophisticated nanocarriers, but also a combination of antibacterial activity with scalability, biocompatibility, regulatory viability, and disease-targeted activity. In general, NDDSs are a very useful platform in anti-infective therapy, the future success of which will be based on the rational and evidence-based design of clinically translatable systems that directly respond to the biological and therapeutic complexity of intracellular infections and LRTIs.
