Low lymphoma macrophage infiltration predicts poor outcomes for R-CHOP– but not Pola-R-CHP–treated patients with DLBCL
Authors: Franck Morschhauser, Georg Lenz, Alex F Herrera, Christopher R Flowers, Marek Trněný, John M Burke, Jing-Zhou Hou, Philipp B Staber, Eliza A Hawkes, Koji Izutsu, Steven Le Gouill, David Belada, Alessandra Tucci, Mark Yan, Will Harris, Kai Lu, Christopher R Bolen, Jamie Hirata, Calvin Lee, Yanwen Jiang, Fabrice Jardin, Katerina Hatzi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Low M1 infiltration is the main immune-related negative prognostic factor for progression-free survival in anti-CD20 + CHOP–treated patients.•Pola-R-CHP activity appears largely independent of M1 macrophage enrichment or overall immune cell infiltration.

Introduction

Dissecting cellular heterogeneity in diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL) and elucidating its role in disease biology and clinical treatment outcomes has been the focus of active investigation. The lymphoma microenvironment is shaped by various factors, including mutations, age, immune status, and disease stage.1, 2, 3 The diversity and plasticity of these ecosystems contribute to treatment outcomes through mechanisms that span both tumor-supportive and immunosuppressive roles. Nonmalignant cells can alter clinical trajectories by mediating effector functions, creating physical or metabolic barriers to drug penetration, or contributing to a tumor-promoting niche that fosters cancer cell survival and proliferation or enables immune evasion.4

Successful therapeutic strategies in DLBCL harness the tumor microenvironment to exert their activity. For instance, anti-CD20 antibodies rely on antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity and antibody-dependent cellular phagocytosis (ADCP), whereby immune effector cells, such as natural killer cells and phagocytes, are recruited via Fcγ receptor engagement to mediate their tumor cytotoxic effect.5 Given the current ubiquitous use of CD20-targeted chemoimmunotherapy as standard frontline therapy for DLBCL, recent efforts have aimed to fully characterize the effect of the tumor microenvironment in this therapeutic setting and to describe transcriptome-based lymphoma microenvironment subtypes or ecosystems.6, 7, 8, 9 Nonetheless, how the lymphoma microenvironment shapes clinical outcomes in the context of novel immunotherapies, including T-cell–directed therapeutic strategies or targeted antibody-drug conjugate (ADC)–containing regimens, is likely to be distinct, and elucidating its role may lead to improved treatment strategies and therapeutic interventions.

In this study, we aimed to define the key lymphoma microenvironment factors associated with clinical outcomes in patients treated with anti-CD20 (rituximab or obinutuzumab) plus CHOP (cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone; anti-CD20 + CHOP), and to understand how treatment with a modified, ADC-containing chemoimmunotherapy regimen alters the influence of the pretreatment immune landscape on clinical outcome. We leveraged 3 large phase 3 clinical studies of patients with previously untreated DLBCL,10, 11, 12 including 2 involving anti-CD20 + CHOP regimens (NCT01287741, NCT00486759)10,11 and 1 testing an ADC-containing regimen, the POLARIX study (NCT03274492),12 which demonstrated prolonged progression-free survival (PFS) with Pola-R-CHP (polatuzumab vedotin plus rituximab, cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, and prednisone) compared with R-CHOP (rituximab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone). Linking comprehensive lymphoma immune microenvironment profiles to clinical outcomes highlighted the relevance of lymphoma macrophages in anti-CD20 + CHOP treatment outcomes. Therefore, we focused on the role and function of lymphoma macrophages, the significance of which remains unresolved because previous studies yielded contradictory results that varied depending on the patient cohort and study design.13, 14, 15, 16 Finally, using experimental models and clinically informed, physiologically relevant treatment doses, we delineated the relative contribution of polatuzumab vedotin effector functions to its cytotoxic efficacy.

Methods

Clinical studies and gene expression analysis

This analysis leveraged bulk RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) performed on pretreatment formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) lymphoma biopsies collected from treatment-naïve patients with DLBCL, who were enrolled across 3 clinical studies.10, 11, 12 All study protocols were approved by the institutional review board or ethics committee at each participating center, in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. In the GOYA study (NCT01287741),10 the activity of G-CHOP (obinutuzumab plus cyclophosphamide, doxorubicin, vincristine, and prednisone) was compared with R-CHOP, showing equivalent clinical efficacy between both the arms. In the POLARIX study,12 the clinical efficacy of Pola-R-CHP was compared with R-CHOP (data cutoff, 15 June 2022). The gene expression profiles of pretreatment tumor FFPE tissue samples were obtained using the RNA Access assay. RNA-seq reads were aligned to the human reference genome (GRCh38.p10) using the Genomic Short-read Nucleotide Alignment Program, allowing for a maximum of 2 mismatches per 75-base sequence. Transcript annotation was based on the GENCODE gene database (GENCODE release 27). To quantify gene expression levels, the number of reads mapping unambiguously to the exons of each gene was calculated. We also generated RNA-seq profiles from the R-CHOP control arm of the MAIN study (NCT00486759).11 The resulting transcriptomes were analyzed using the xCell17 and quanTIseq18 algorithms.19 Macrophage signatures used by xCell are described in the study by Aran et al17. Enrichment scores for monocytes/macrophages (Mono/Mac) S1-3 and Mono/Mac1-6 signatures for each patient were estimated by calculating the average standardized expression in transcripts per million for genes within each signature. Assessments of the cell-of-origin (COO) subtype were performed by NanoString for the GOYA and POLARIX studies. For the MAIN study, the gneSeqCOO package was used to estimate COO from bulk RNA-seq data.20

IHC

CD68 immunohistochemistry (IHC) in the GOYA study was performed as previously described.21 Tissue microarrays constructed using duplicate 0.6 mm cores from diagnostic pretreatment FFPE tissue were stained with CD68 clone PG-M1 (Dako, Agilent Technologies, Cheshire, United Kingdom; dilution, 1:250). Intratumoral CD68+ macrophages showing granular cytoplasmic staining were assessed in a standard area containing predominantly tumor cells within each tissue microarray core.21

ADCP assay

Human monocyte-derived macrophages (MDMs) were generated by magnetic purification (Miltenyi Biotec; catalog no. 130-117-337) of CD14+CD16− cells from peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs). Cells were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum, 5% human serum, and 100 IU/mL penicillin/streptomycin, supplemented with macrophage colony-stimulating factor (50 ng/mL) for 7 days at 37°C with 5% CO2. Carboxyfluorescein diacetate succinimidyl ester (CFSE)–labeled BJAB target cells were incubated with MDMs (1 MDM:3 BJAB cells) and treated with 10 μg/mL anti-CD20 (rituximab), anti-CD79b (unconjugated polatuzumab, 114 ng/mL), or monomethyl auristatin E (MMAE)–conjugated anti-CD79b (polatuzumab vedotin) for 4 hours at 37°C. Drug dosage was based on clinical pharmacology estimates corresponding to physiologically relevant doses in patients. Cells were subsequently stained with anti-human CD11b (BioLegend; clone, ICRF44; catalog no. 301350; lot number, B388635) and SYTOX Blue (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog no. S34857) and assessed by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, Fortessa X-20). Macrophage ADCP was determined by calculating the proportion of CFSE+CD11b+ cells among live differentiated CD11b+ cells (SYTOX Blue−CD11b cells).

Lymphoma cocultures

BJAB, SUDHL16, SUDLH4, or SUDLH6 target cells were cocultured with human PBMCs (1:1 ratio) in RPMI 1640 medium containing 10% fetal bovine serum and 100 IU/mL penicillin/streptomycin in the presence of anti-CD20 (rituximab, 100 ng/mL), anti-CD79b (unconjugated polatuzumab, 114 ng/mL), or MMAE-conjugated anti-CD79b (polatuzumab vedotin, 114 ng/mL) for 48 hours. Cells were subsequently stained with CD19 (BioLegend; clone, HIB19; catalog no. 302219; lot number, B378286), CD14 (BD Biosciences; clone, MϕP9; catalog no. 563561; lot number, 3156277), and LIVE/DEAD Fixable Near-IR (Thermo Fisher Scientific; catalog no. L34976; lot number, 2729850) and assessed by flow cytometry (BD Biosciences, Fortessa X-20). Lymphoma cytotoxicity was determined based on the percentage of CD19 cell survival compared with untreated conditions.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using R programming language (version 4.1.1) or GraphPad software (version 10.1.10). Using the xCell and quanTIseq algorithms, each cell type was stratified into high- vs low-immune infiltration groups by median expression and correlated with investigator-assessed PFS and overall survival (OS). For the MAIN study, only R-CHOP–treated patients were included, whereas for the GOYA study, G-CHOP– and R-CHOP–treated patients were pooled because there was no evidence of a treatment effect. For the POLARIX study, immune cell fractions were estimated at baseline across all patients; however, R-CHOP– and Pola-R-CHP–treated patients were analyzed as distinct cohorts when associations with time-to-event outcomes were assessed because of the known treatment effect. Hazard ratios (HRs) across all studies were adjusted for key prognostic factors, including age (≤60 vs >60 years), COO (activated B-cell [ABC], germinal center B-cell [GCB], unclassified, or unknown), and International Prognostic Index (IPI; ≤2 vs >2). HRs in the GOYA study were additionally adjusted for treatment arm (R-CHOP vs G-CHOP). No adjustments for multiple comparisons were performed.

Results

Low baseline M1 macrophage signature is a consistent poor prognostic indicator in patients with DLBCL treated with CD20-targeted chemoimmunotherapy

The GOYA clinical study

To understand which lymphoma microenvironment components are most closely correlated with anti-CD20 + CHOP treatment outcomes, we generated immune cell fraction profiles from the GOYA study10 using xCell17 and quanTIseq.18 Baseline patient characteristics of the RNA-seq–evaluable population (n = 539) was consistent with the characteristics of the overall study population (supplemental Table 1). Consistent with previous studies,6,7 the resulting immune infiltration profiles revealed extensive heterogeneity among patients with DLBCL (Figure 1A). Immune fraction quantification indicated a predominant presence of B cells, consistent with their tumor cell origin. M1 macrophage enrichment had the highest median estimated immune signature captured, followed by CD8 T-cell enrichment (Figure 1B). Other immune signatures, such as regulatory T cells and M2 macrophages, were also captured at lower levels (Figure 1B). Across the immune cell fraction estimates generated by both algorithms, enrichment of classically activated M1 macrophages was consistently among the most strongly associated with a lower risk of disease progression in patients treated with anti-CD20 chemoimmunotherapy (R/G-CHOP; Figure 1C). Patients with above-median M1 macrophage enrichment had significantly longer PFS outcomes compared with patients with below-median M1 macrophage levels (HR, 0.67; 95% confidence interval [CI], 0.49-0.91; P < .05; Figure 1C-D). Enrichment of alternatively activated M2 macrophages was not significantly associated with PFS outcomes in the GOYA study (Figure 1C). Marker gene analysis of the lymphoma immune contexture supported these results and independently validated that M1 macrophage enrichment was strongly associated with PFS in the GOYA study (HR, 0.70; 95% CI, 0.51-0.95; P < .05; Figure 1C; supplemental Figure 1A). Similar to PFS, deconvolution analysis showed that M1 macrophage scores were strongly associated with OS. Patients with high M1 macrophage enrichment showed significantly improved outcomes compared with patients with low M1 macrophage enrichment (HR, 0.61; 95% CI, 0.41-0.89; P < .05; Figure 1D; supplemental Figure 1B).

The MAIN clinical study

Examining a smaller cohort of R-CHOP–treated patients in an independent clinical study10 (the MAIN study, n = 75; supplemental Table 1) also showed that M1 macrophage enrichment was associated with a trend toward improved PFS outcomes (HR, 0.44; 95% CI, 0.17-1.16; P < .1; Figure 1E) and was strongly associated with OS (HR, 0.25; 95% CI, 0.06-0.95; P < .05; Figure 1E), despite sample size constraints. Similar results were obtained using the marker gene approach (supplemental Figure 1C-D).

The POLARIX clinical study

To independently validate these findings, we performed additional lymphoma microenvironment analyses in the ongoing POLARIX study.12 Using the same transcriptomics-based immune microenvironment analyses, we confirmed our previous findings from the GOYA and MAIN studies in the POLARIX R-CHOP arm (n = 334; supplemental Table 2). M1 macrophage and overall macrophage infiltration represented the primary immune-related positive prognostic factors for PFS in this independent cohort (Figure 1F). Patients with high M1 macrophage enrichment showed significantly superior PFS by both quanTIseq (HR, 0.60; 95% CI, 0.41-0.88; Figure 1F-G) and xCell (HR, 0.57; 95% CI, 0.39-0.84; P < .01; Figure 1F; supplemental Figure 1E) compared with patients with low M1 macrophage enrichment. Similarly, OS was significantly longer in patients with high M1 macrophage enrichment treated with R-CHOP in the POLARIX study (HR, 0.51; 95% CI, 0.29-0.90; Figure 1G; supplemental Figure 1F). To strengthen our conclusions, we modeled quanTIseq and xCell M1 macrophage scores as continuous variables by fitting Cox proportional hazards models adjusted for age, IPI (as ≤2 vs ˃2), and COO. When modeled as continuous variables, the M1 signature scores were associated with PFS and OS in the GOYA and POLARIX studies (supplemental Table 3).

Taken together, immune microenvironment analyses of 3 large phase 3 studies showed that lymphoma-infiltrating macrophages, resembling a classically activated M1 polarization phenotype, were associated with better PFS and OS in patients with DLBCL treated with R/G-CHOP. These analyses were adjusted for COO, age, and IPI score, indicating that M1 macrophage enrichment has independent predictive value.

Macrophage subset enrichment rather than overall macrophage enrichment is linked to improved R-CHOP outcomes in DLBCL

To better understand the clinical and pathobiological characteristics of patients with high vs low M1 macrophage enrichment, we correlated our transcriptome-based lymphoma M1 infiltration estimates with various clinical and biomarker features. In both the GOYA and POLARIX studies, for which COO was available, patients with GCB-DLBCL had significantly higher M1 macrophage enrichment compared with those with ABC-DLBCL (P < .001, as estimated by quanTIseq; Figure 2A). However, patients with low M1 macrophage enrichment, stratified by the median, included patients with both ABC-DLBCL and GCB-DLBCL in both studies, suggesting that this patient population was not predominated by a specific COO (Figure 2B). M1 macrophage enrichment, as estimated by xCell, did not significantly differ depending on COO (supplemental Figure 2A). Consistent with our findings, patients with dark-zone (DZ) gene expression signature-positive DLBCL, who represent a fraction of patients with GCB-DLBCL and present with inferior outcomes, were predominantly patients with low M1 macrophage enrichment, which may reflect an overall depleted tumor microenvironment.22,23 Mapping patients with high vs low M1 macrophage enrichment to various LymphGen DLBCL subtypes, which have distinct outcomes and therapeutic vulnerabilities,24 showed that more patients with low M1 macrophage enrichment were in the MCD, EZB, and A53 subsets (Figure 2B). No significant difference was observed in baseline M1 macrophage levels according to IPI score or age (supplemental Figure 2B-E). Given the availability of tumor macrophage staining from biopsies obtained in the GOYA study,18 we investigated how M1 macrophage infiltration scores correlated with total macrophage infiltration, as defined by CD68+ IHC. We identified 222 patients with both transcriptomic profiles and CD68 IHC staining available from matched lymphoma biopsies. Overall, M1 signature scores derived from either algorithm correlated with CD68+ IHC levels in tumor tissue (Figure 2C; supplemental Figure 2F). Nonetheless, classification of patients as macrophage-high or -low based on the median CD68 staining level failed to show a significant difference in PFS outcomes (Figure 2D). Interrogation using Cox regression models with different quartile cutoffs, or evaluation of CD68 as a continuous predictor, did not show a significant relationship between high CD68+ cell levels and improved outcomes. This raises the possibility that specific macrophage subsets, rather than overall macrophage infiltration, may be accountable for the observed effect on CD20-targeted chemoimmunotherapy outcomes.

To better understand the macrophage subsets associated with improved anti-CD20 + CHOP–driven responses, we correlated our quanTIseq and xCell M1 macrophage scores with signatures derived from a spatial transcriptomic analysis of defined CD68+ macrophage subsets from reactive lymph nodes and lymphoma tissues.25 M1 macrophage scores from both the algorithms strongly correlated with the spatially derived DLBCL macrophage signatures (MacroSig6) as well as with MacroSig4, derived from DZ macrophages (Figure 2E). A parallel correlation of quanTIseq and xCell M1 macrophage scores with signatures of 3 DLBCL Mono/Mac cell states derived from EcoTyper7 indicated a strong correlation with Mono/Mac S3, followed by Mono/Mac S2 (Figure 2E).

Overall, these results suggest that anti-CD20 antibodies may leverage specific subsets of lymphoma-infiltrating macrophages, which may include DZ macrophages or Mono/Mac S2/S3 macrophages, that can also contain immunosuppressive or tumor-promoting properties.

Pola-R-CHP responses appear uncoupled from engagement of functional lymphoma macrophages that facilitate rituximab activity

To investigate the relationship between the pretreatment lymphoma microenvironment and therapeutic outcomes with Pola-R-CHP, we used transcriptomics-based microenvironment profiles from patients treated with Pola-R-CHP in the POLARIX study (n = 331). In contrast to anti-CD20 + CHOP outcomes (Figure 1C-G), M1 macrophage infiltration was not associated with PFS in the Pola-R-CHP arm when quantified by either quanTIseq (HR, 0.90; 95% CI, 0.58-1.38; Figure 3A) or xCell (HR, 0.95; 95% CI, 0.62-1.46; Figure 3A; supplemental Figure 3A). M1 macrophage infiltration levels were, however, comparable between treatment arms (supplemental Figure 3B). Despite the enrichment of other immune infiltrates linked to either improved or poor survival outcomes in the R-CHOP arm (Figure 1C-F), overall, microenvironment signatures in the Pola-R-CHP arm were not associated with treatment outcomes, suggesting that Pola-R-CHP activity may be less dependent on lymphoma immune infiltration (Figure 3A; supplemental Figure 4). The only exception was the apparent association between above-median mast cell enrichment, estimated by xCell, and poor Pola-R-CHP outcomes. Considering the low mast cell enrichment scores in lymphoma, future studies will determine the relevance of this observation. Patients treated with Pola-R-CHP also had similar OS outcomes regardless of high or low M1 macrophage enrichment (HR, 0.76; 95% CI, 0.42-1.36; Figure 3C). Pola-R-CHP treatment improved survival outcomes in patients with low M1 macrophage infiltration levels compared with R-CHOP (HR, 0.59; 95% CI, 0.4-0.86; P = .006; Figure 3D). Moreover, a significantly higher proportion of patients with DLBCL with low M1 macrophage enrichment progressed within 24 months after R-CHOP treatment initiation compared with patients with high M1 macrophage enrichment (P < .001; Figure 3E). By contrast, all patients treated with Pola-R-CHP remained in remission at rates similar to those of patients with high M1 macrophage enrichment treated with R-CHOP, regardless of M1 signature enrichment (Figure 3E). Furthermore, no difference in PFS was observed among patients treated with Pola-R-CHP regardless of tumor-associated macrophage signature enrichment (supplemental Figure 3C).

In summary, these results suggest that the addition of polatuzumab vedotin to the treatment regimen appears to alter the effect of the preexisting lymphoma microenvironment, rendering preexisting lymphoma macrophage prevalence negligible with respect to treatment outcomes.

Polatuzumab vedotin activity is largely driven by MMAE-mediated cytotoxicity despite ADCP competency

To understand why M1 macrophage enrichment was not associated with therapeutic outcomes in patients treated with Pola-R-CHP, we examined whether polatuzumab vedotin could mediate macrophage-dependent phagocytosis using ADCP assays. These experiments showed that polatuzumab can mediate ADCP in the presence of differentiated macrophages, regardless of MMAE conjugation, and at levels similar to those of rituximab (Figure 4A-B). Using lymphoma cell lines, we examined the relative contribution of ADCP to polatuzumab vedotin cytotoxic activity by coculturing lymphoma cells with donor-derived PBMCs in the presence of anti-CD20, anti-CD79b, MMAE-conjugated anti-CD79b, or combination treatments, dosed at clinically informed and physiologically relevant antibody concentrations. Of note, macrophage abundance in these lymphoma cocultures roughly reflects macrophage infiltration levels typically observed in DLBCL tumors (∼10% as indicated by CD68+ IHC). Polatuzumab vedotin showed significantly higher cytotoxic activity compared with unconjugated anti-CD79b antibody treatment (P = .0009) or with single-agent rituximab treatment (P = .014; Figure 4C). Moreover, anti-CD20 combined with MMAE-conjugated anti-CD79b treatment resulted in significantly higher cytotoxicity compared with anti-CD20 plus unconjugated anti-CD79b treatment (Figure 4C; supplemental Figure 5).

Taken together, these results suggest that although polatuzumab vedotin is ADCP-competent, its activity is largely driven by MMAE payload delivery in lymphoma tissue. The relative contribution of lymphoma macrophages to its mechanism of action appears to be less pronounced.

Discussion

The immune microenvironment has emerged as an important determinant of the biological and clinical characteristics of lymphoma, as well as treatment trajectories. Given the cellular heterogeneity and plasticity of DLBCL, recent studies have focused on elucidating cross talk between the tumor microenvironment and lymphoma, including efforts to classify lymphoma based on microenvironment composition and functional states.6,7,26 To understand how immune cell infiltrates may alter therapeutic outcomes in the context of CD20-targeted chemoimmunotherapy treatments compared with a recently approved ADC-containing chemoimmunotherapy regimen (Pola-R-CHP), we estimated the relative contribution of the lymphoma microenvironment to DLBCL treatment outcomes. Recent insights into determinants of polatuzumab vedotin antilymphoma activity, based on functional genomic studies, revealed that CD79b epitope glycosylation may hinder epitope accessibility and reduce treatment efficacy, whereas genetic loss of KLHL6, a recurrent mutation in DLBCL, may lead to increased sensitivity because of improved epitope availability.27 Limited knowledge exists regarding how the addition of this therapeutic ADC, with an alternative mechanism of action, may alter therapeutic responses and the dynamic interplay between lymphoma cells and their highly heterogeneous microenvironment.

In this post hoc analysis, we initially performed an unbiased lymphoma microenvironment investigation of the most influential determinants of therapeutic outcome after adjusting for known prognostic factors (eg, COO). Leveraging collective transcriptomic data from 1279 patients treated across 3 phase 3 frontline DLBCL clinical studies, and using 2 independent trained algorithms, we found that low M1 macrophage enrichment was a consistent negative prognostic indicator in patients treated with anti-CD20 + CHOP but, interestingly, not in patients treated with Pola-R-CHP, even after adjusting for known prognostic factors. Reports investigating the effect of macrophages on lymphoma outcomes have yielded contradictory results, possibly because of threshold effects or small population size, with some reports suggesting that macrophages are positive prognostic indicators and others highlighting a negative association with outcomes.13 Work focused on PD-L1 expression in DLBCL also showed a positive relationship between anti-CD20 chemoimmunotherapy outcomes and macrophage signature enrichment in the GOYA and MAIN studies.28 Our conclusions are based on a post hoc analysis of independent clinical studies that spanned almost 2 decades and varied in terms of patient eligibility, underscoring the consistency of these findings.

Among various immune cell subsets, our analysis included macrophage subset annotation distinguishing 2 antagonistic types of macrophage polarization and activation. M1 macrophages are considered classically activated and are typically induced by proinflammatory lipopolysaccharide and interferon-γ, whereas M2 macrophages, known as alternatively activated macrophages, are typically induced by anti-inflammatory cytokines such as interleukin-4 and interleukin-13.29 Our results suggested that M1-like macrophages are relatively abundant in lymphoma tissue and may play distinct roles in lymphoma biology and therapeutic responses, whereas M2-like signatures were less abundant. Although this conventional M1/M2 macrophage dichotomous classification may begin to explain the relationship between macrophage subsets and clinical outcomes in DLBCL, it does not sufficiently reflect the exact nature and function of the lymphoma-associated macrophages that directly influence anti-CD20 chemoimmunotherapy activity. Therefore, we attempted to better understand the macrophage phenotype associated with the observed positive prognostic effect. Macrophages are extremely plastic and constantly adapt to microenvironment signals; delineating their context-specific roles and functional diversity remains challenging and is the subject of active investigation.30 Along these lines, macrophage activation, including that of intratumoral macrophages, is determined by spatiotemporal factors, leading to a spectrum of phenotypes and functional outcomes.31 A machine-learning approach dissecting cellular heterogeneity in DLBCL (EcoTyper)7 showed that Mono/Mac existed in 3 cellular states within DLBCL tumors. Another study showed that lymphoma macrophages are enriched in inflammatory- and mesenchymal-type DLBCL, but are scarce in depleted or Germinal Center (GC)-like lymphomas.6 More recently, 3 LymphoMAP archetypes (FMAC, LN, and TEX) were described, each containing different profiles of macrophage subsets.9 Consistent with the observed lymphoma macrophage diversity and plasticity, our work suggests that, rather than overall intratumoral macrophages, a specific transcriptionally defined subpopulation of intratumoral macrophages may better define which macrophages facilitate anti-CD20 treatment responses. Leveraging a recently published comprehensive spatial transcriptomic data set25 that included various spatially defined macrophages from healthy lymph nodes and lymphoma tissues, including interfollicular, DZ, and light zone macrophages, we found that our xCell and quanTIseq M1 macrophage scores were highly correlated with DLBCL-derived macrophages, as well as with DZ macrophages. It is possible that anti-CD20 antibody treatment may also leverage incipient tumor-promoting macrophages to achieve therapeutic responses. This is in line with limited evidence from CHOP-treated patients, in whom studies have shown that macrophage enrichment was associated with poor outcomes.16,32 Similarly, correlation with the EcoTyper-derived Mono/Mac cell states indicated a strong correlation with Mono/Mac S3, followed by Mono/Mac S2, suggesting that these M1-like prognostic scores possibly reflect a subset of lymphoma infiltrating-macrophages rather than their overall infiltration. Additional analyses leveraging single-cell resolution lymphoma data sets combined with in vivo experiments using tailored murine models could further elucidate the role of these lymphoma macrophages in chemoimmunotherapy outcomes.

Finally, our analysis showed a lack of association between Pola-R-CHP treatment outcomes and M1 macrophage enrichment, indicating that patients with DLBCL with low levels of M1 infiltration may benefit from treatment with Pola-R-CHP compared with R-CHOP.12,33 Although we showed that polatuzumab vedotin can mediate lymphoma phagocytosis in vitro, our experiments suggested that its efficacy is largely independent on lymphoma phagocytosis, because effector engagement alone had negligible effects at clinically relevant doses. Of note, although enrichment of various microenvironment components in CD20-targeted chemoimmunotherapy was associated with treatment outcomes, significant association between immune infiltrates with Pola-R-CHP outcomes were largely absent. This raises the possibility that the addition of a distinct therapeutic modality such as an ADC, designed for direct payload delivery to tumor cells, may alter the effect of the preexisting tumor microenvironment on treatment outcomes. Although CD20-targeted chemoimmunotherapy may rely on the presence of specific functional immune effectors for its clinical activity, Pola-R-CHP may achieve therapeutic responses in patients lacking a preexisting favorable immune contexture, thereby at least partially uncoupling treatment outcomes from the lymphoma microenvironment. Although the present study focused on the lymphoma microenvironment before treatment, we cannot rule out the possibility that polatuzumab vedotin treatment may trigger immunogenic cell death, thereby inducing activation of T cells, dendritic cells, or other immune cells that could enhance its therapeutic efficacy. A limitation of the present study is its inability to uncouple the effects of chemotherapy from those of the therapeutic antibodies. As the clinical use of Pola-R-CHP in DLBCL treatment expands and more chemotherapy-free regimens become available (such as combinations with T-cell–engaging bispecific antibodies), future studies should further elucidate how the immune microenvironment may influence patient responses. Spatially resolved multiomics profiling of clinical patient biopsies before and during the course of Pola-R-CHP treatment, as well as applied machine learning approaches investigating lymphoma microenvironment subtypes, will be needed to fully delineate any additional intrinsic or microenvironment determinants to treatment responses with Pola-R-CHP.
