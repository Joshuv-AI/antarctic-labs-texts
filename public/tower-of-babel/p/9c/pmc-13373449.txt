Potential Treatment of Steroid‐Resistant Alopecia Areata Using Allogeneic Exosomes Derived From Adipose Tissue Mesenchymal Stromal Cells: A Case Report
Authors: Masoumeh Kianpisheh, Mohsen Piroozhashemi, Shabnam Feshangchian‐Atashbar, Ali Ghamari, Alireza Shoae‐Hassani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTA 31‐year‐old man with severe, corticosteroid‐resistant alopecia areata presented with substantial scalp hair loss (Severity of Alopecia Tool [SALT] score: 60%) despite a 1‐year regimen of topical corticosteroids, 5% minoxidil, and oral prednisolone. He was treated with allogeneic exosomes derived from adipose tissue mesenchymal stromal cells (ADMSCs‐Exo) at 3‐week intervals over 12 weeks (four sessions; 1012 particles each). A cessation of hair shedding was noted after the initial session, followed by progressive regrowth that led to near‐complete hair restoration by Week 12. Treatment was accompanied by an increase in anti‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐10) and a reduction of IFN‐γ, TNF‐α, and IL‐17 toward baseline levels. No adverse effects were reported during active treatment or throughout the 18‐week posttreatment follow‐up period. While limited to a single patient, this case suggests that ADMSC‐derived exosomes warrant further investigation as a potential cell‐free therapeutic option for refractory alopecia areata.Keywords: adipose derived mesenchymal stem cell, alopecia areata, autoimmune diseases, exosome, hair loss

Key Clinical Message

ADMSC‐derived exosomes achieved full hair regrowth and normalized inflammatory cytokines in refractory alopecia areata, providing a safe, cell‐free treatment that avoids the systemic side effects commonly seen with prolonged corticosteroid use.

Introduction

Alopecia areata (AA) is the predominant immune‐mediated reason for hair loss globally. The primary reason is thought to be autoimmune assaults on hair follicles (HFs) instigated by CD8+ T cells, and the interferon (IFN)‐γ is significant in initiating the collapse of HF immune privilege and impairing HF stem cells [1, 2]. The typical treatment choices for AA primarily consist of nonspecific oral or systemically broad immunosuppressants, alongside the frequent use of intralesional and topical steroids as first‐line therapies [3]. In cases where these treatments prove ineffective, subsequent treatment options may involve the use of JAK inhibitors like Tofacitinib, which are commonly utilized in the management of autoimmune disorders [4]. However, tofacitinib was reported safe in low dosages, but some patients exhibit dyspepsia, nausea, headache, and even anemia with 10 mg daily tofacitinib at a 3‐month follow‐up [5].

Some studies have demonstrated the promise of ADMSC therapies in promoting substantial hair regrowth in patients with AA [6, 7]. In contrast to stem cells, recent studies suggest that mesenchymal stromal cell‐derived exosomes may exert immunomodulatory and regenerative effects through paracrine mechanisms while avoiding direct engraftment of living cells. Exosomes, which are 30–150 nm extracellular vesicles responsible for facilitating intercellular communications, are distinguished by a phospholipid bilayer containing specific surface markers and cargos that influence cell signaling and gene expression, such as cytokines, growth factors, and regulatory microRNAs (miRs) [8, 9]. Exosomes are generated through the endosomal pathway and are secreted into the extracellular environment, where they participate in tissue homeostasis and immune regulation [9].

While conventional immunosuppressive modalities and JAK inhibitors target systemic immune signaling, they fail to restore local tissue homeostasis within the HF microenvironment. Emerging paradigm shifts in regenerative dermatology highlight that ADMSCs‐Exo exhibit an inherently enriched proteomic and miR cargo such as miR‐122‐5p and miR‐181a‐5p that specifically suppress the hyperactive T‐cell signaling cascades unique to AA pathogenesis [10]. Furthermore, ADMSCs can be harvested in high yields via minimally invasive lipoaspiration, yielding exosomes with significantly greater immunomodulatory and antiapoptotic potencies on dermal papilla cells (DPCs) compared to bone marrow or umbilical cord counterparts for their deployment in steroid‐refractory autoimmune alopecia.

In this report, we present the case of a young adult male with nonresponsive AA who was treated with ADMSCs‐Exosome.

Case History/Examination

A 31‐year‐old male visited the dermatology clinic reporting patchy hair loss over the past two years. After being diagnosed with AA, he had previously undergone treatment with oral and topical steroids. He was prescribed 50 mg oral prednisolone every other day and instructed to apply 5% minoxidil topically twice daily for the last 12 months. Unfortunately, none of these treatments yielded positive results. The patient exhibited significant hair loss. Based on his symptoms and medical history, nonresponsive AA was suspected. His paraclinical results, which encompassed complete blood counts, liver and kidney function tests, lipid profile, HIV, HBS ag, HBC ab, and HCV, as well as TSH, testosterone, iron, TIBC, and ANA, all fell within normal ranges.

Methods (Differential Diagnosis, Investigations, and Treatment)

Using a handheld dermatoscope (×10 magnification), the following findings were documented: black dots, exclamation mark hairs, broken hair shafts, and yellow dots. Scarring, scaling, and erythema were absent.

Investigations

Initial laboratory and diagnostic evaluations were performed to exclude systemic or autoimmune causes of hair loss and to establish the patient's eligibility for ADMSCs‐Exo therapy. The investigations revealed complete blood count, liver and kidney function tests, lipid profile, viral screening, thyroid function, autoimmune screening, hormonal and iron level studies. In addition, the patient's peripheral blood cytokines were quantified using a multiplex cytokine detection kit (Biotechne; R&D Systems, USA). Baseline measurements revealed elevated pro‐inflammatory cytokines (IFN‐γ, TNF‐α, IL‐17) and reduced anti‐inflammatory cytokines (IL‐4, IL‐6, IL‐10), consistent with active immune‐mediated AA. Dermatologic examination and dermatoscopy supported the diagnosis of AA, showing well‐demarcated patches of nonscarring hair loss without scaling, erythema, or signs of fungal infection.

Treatment

The patient received allogeneic ADMSCs‐Exo prepared at the Stem Cell and Regenerative Medicine Research Center, Iran University of Medical Sciences. Characterization included nanoparticle tracking analysis, electron microscopy, and expression of exosomal markers (CD63, CD81) as described previously by our team [10, 11]. The administration protocol was as follows: four intradermal injection sessions with 3‐week intervals (total 12 weeks) and the dose of 1012 exosome particles per session. Intradermal micro‐droplet injections distributed evenly across the alopecic scalp using a 30‐gauge needle. The total volume of injected exosomes was 3 mL per session with no premedication. The administration parameters were established based on escalating safety and efficacy profiles documented in recent clinical trials evaluating MSC‐derived extracellular vesicles for nonscarring alopecia. A dose of particles ensures an optimal, nonsaturating concentration of exosomal signaling molecules capable of saturating the target cell surface receptors across a SALT score scalp surface area. The 3‐week treatment interval was selected to match the dynamic pharmacodynamic window of exosomal miR cargo inside the tissue. This allows sufficient time for the localized suppression of local T‐cell infiltrates and the continuous activation of the Wnt/β‐catenin pathway in resting hair follicle stem cells (HFSCs) without triggering local microenvironment congestion or inflammatory mechanical micro‐trauma from over‐frequent injections. Conclusion and Results (Outcome and Follow‐Up)

Hair loss ceased and new hair grew back after the first treatment. His hair fully recovered following four rounds of ADMSCs‐Exo therapy (SALT 0%) (Figure 2). Our results showed that after ADMSCs‐Exo injections the levels of IL‐4, IL‐6, and IL‐10 increased significantly, while the IFN‐γ, tumor necrosis factor‐α (TNF‐α), and IL‐17 decreased and were placed within the normal range (Figure 3). No adverse effects were seen during the treatment or after 18 weeks of follow‐up.

Discussion

This clinical case demonstrates that exosomes derived from adipose‐derived MSCs have the potential to treat severe, steroid‐resistant AA. Exosomes from adipose‐derived MSCs have been shown to modulate inflammatory and oxidative pathways [12] and exert sustained immunomodulatory effects. Given that AA is driven by inflammation and collapse of HF immune privilege [13], and that immunosuppressants often provide limited benefits and may cause adverse reactions [14], ADMSCs‐Exo represent a less toxic alternative to agents such as tofacitinib. Also, prolonged systemic corticosteroid therapy, particularly at moderate to high doses, is associated with several well‐recognized adverse effects that may limit its long‐term use in patients with AA. Chronic corticosteroid exposure can contribute to metabolic complications such as weight gain, hyperglycemia, insulin resistance, dyslipidemia, and increased risk of prediabetes or diabetes mellitus. In addition, musculoskeletal adverse effects including osteoporosis, muscle wasting, and impaired collagen synthesis may occur with extended treatment duration. Other potential complications include hypertension, increased susceptibility to infection, adrenal suppression, and psychological disturbances. These limitations highlight the need for alternative therapeutic approaches with improved safety profiles, particularly, in patients with refractory disease requiring prolonged treatment [15].

Some studies have reported that platelet rich plasma therapy (PRP) could improve severe AA [16], but other studies show no improvement for patients with severe AA after PRP therapy [17]. Because PRP is an autologous product and various variables are involved in its production at individual treatment centers, outcomes typically vary. Furthermore, the variability in platelet counts among individuals can lead to inconsistent outcomes, as their concentration may not always yield optimal results. Additionally, factors such as the use of anticoagulant drugs and other variables affecting platelets might contribute to the potentially limited benefits of PRP. In contrast, exosomes offer a standardized, cell‐free therapeutic product.

In this patient, anti‐inflammatory cytokines increased and pro‐inflammatory cytokines normalized following ADMSCs‐Exo therapy (Figure 3), supporting an immunomodulatory role for exosomes in AA. To our knowledge, this is among the first reports documenting cytokine normalization following exosome therapy in AA. Limitations include the single‐patient design and lack of long‐term follow‐up. Larger controlled studies are needed to define optimal dosing, durability, and predictors of response.

The dual clinical success‐arresting active hair shedding while simultaneously driving follicular regeneration points to a multi‐targeted mechanism of action inherent to ADMSCs‐Exo cargo. Pathologically, AA is characterized by the collapse of HF immune privilege mediated by CD8 + NKG2D+ T cells and elevated local IFN‐γ expression [18, 19]. Our observation of peripheral cytokine normalization reflects a systemic‐to‐local modulation: exosomal cargos deliver specific regulatory miRs that disrupt the JAK–STAT signaling pathway down‐stream of the IFN‐γ receptor, effectively muting the autoimmune cascade. Concurrently, the surface markers and enclosed growth factors (such as VEGF and bFGF) shift the perifollicular macroenvironment from a Th1/Th17‐driven pro‐inflammatory status toward a Th2‐mediated anti‐inflammatory state [20], as verified by the parallel elevations in IL‐4 and IL‐10. This local immune suppression allows the exosome cargo to directly activate dormant HFSCs via the upregulation of the Wnt/β‐catenin and PI3K/Akt pro‐survival pathways, accelerating the structural transition of injured follicles from the inactive telogen phase back into the active anagen phase.

Our clinical outcomes align with and expand upon emerging literature using extracellular vesicles for hair restoration [20, 21]. While the vast majority of current clinical trials evaluate ADMSCs‐Exo solely within the context of nonautoimmune androgenetic alopecia (AGA), literature addressing refractory autoimmune AA remains highly scarce. In a recent study by Nobari and colleagues, two female patients with severe, chronic refractory AA were treated with a single session of intradermal adipose‐derived exosomes, achieving a partial reduction in SALT scores (Case 1: 79.6–59.6; Case 2: 70.2–58.6) over a 3‐month follow‐up [22]. In contrast, our multisession regimen (four doses of particles at 3‐week intervals) achieved complete therapeutic resolution (SALT 0%) with absolute safety over an extended 18‐week monitoring window. This suggests that a repetitive dosing protocol may be required to permanently suppress established autoimmune memory cells within the HF bulb, positioning this case report as a foundational template for future randomized controlled clinical trials.

Conclusion

ADMSC‐derived exosomes associated with complete and sustained hair regrowth in a patient with severe, refractory AA and restored inflammatory cytokine balance without adverse effects. This cell‐free therapy represents a promising future option for managing difficult AA cases.
