A yeast-based oral vaccine platform reveals antigen-dependent protection against Eimeria tenella
Authors: Wenqi Han, Lang Chen, Lu Wang, Qiping Zhao, Shunhai Zhu, Yu Yu, Jiayu Bai, Hongyu Han, Lili Hao, Hui Dong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The development of effective oral mucosal vaccines against intracellular enteric pathogens remains a formidable hurdle, demanding innovative platforms that combine targeted antigen delivery with the induction of potent local immunity. Avian coccidiosis, caused by Eimeria parasites, exemplifies this need, as its control is hampered by drug resistance and limitations of live vaccines. Here, we engineered a novel oral vaccine platform based on Saccharomyces cerevisiae surface display to deliver defined Eimeria tenella rhoptry antigens (EtRON2, its variants EtRON2L1-1 and EtRON2L1-2, and EtROP17). Recombinant yeast strains demonstrated efficient surface antigen localization. In vivo trials in chickens showed that the live yeast vaccine expressing the core invasion antigen EtRON2 conferred the highest level of protection among all tested candidates, characterized by a high anticoccidial index (ACI = 172), a 54% reduction in oocyst shedding, minimized weight loss, and a systemic Th1/Th17-polarized response suggestive of mucosal T-cell recruitment. Protection was dependent on both antigen identity and carrier viability; inactivated yeast or other antigens provided only partial efficacy. Our findings reveal a critical triad for successful mucosal vaccination: functional centrality of the antigen, viability of the delivery vehicle, and induction of a polarized adaptive immune profile. This study establishes a versatile and safe yeast-based oral vaccine platform and provides a generalizable design framework for next-generation mucosal vaccines against intracellular pathogens.

Introduction

The development of effective vaccines against intracellular enteric pathogens remains a formidable challenge in both human and veterinary medicine (Kaufmann, 2007). These pathogens, which include protozoa like Cryptosporidium and Eimeria, as well as invasive bacteria such as Salmonella and Listeria, share a common need to invade and replicate within host cells, often exploiting the intestinal mucosa as the primary portal of entry (Duchet-Suchaux et al., 1991; Wallach et al., 1995). Protecting against such infections requires vaccine strategies that can induce robust mucosal immunity—particularly secretory IgA and tissue-resident T cells—at the site of invasion (Mestecky et al., 1999; Holmgren and Czerkinsky, 2005). However, conventional parenteral vaccines often fail to elicit strong gut mucosal responses, and oral subunit vaccines face hurdles such as antigen stability, efficient delivery to gut-associated lymphoid tissue (GALT), and the lack of suitable adjuvants (Neutra and Kozlowski, 2006; Lycke, 2012).

Poultry coccidiosis, an enteric disease caused by Eimeria parasites, inflicts staggering economic losses on global agriculture, primarily through impaired growth and mortality in chickens (Chapman, 2014; Blake et al., 2020). Eimeria tenella, among the most pathogenic species, targets the ceca, causing hemorrhagic lesions and severe morbidity (Blake et al., 2020). Current control remains reliant on two flawed pillars: prophylactic anticoccidial drugs, whose efficacy is eroded by rapidly evolving resistance and residue concerns (Chapman et al., 2010; Peek and Landman, 2011), and live attenuated vaccines, which are hampered by production constraints, cost, and the risk of virulence reversion (Williams, 2002; Shirley et al., 2005). Recombinant subunit vaccines offer a safer profile, but their development has been stymied by the fundamental obstacles of antigen degradation and poor mucosal uptake. This results in a failure to effectively prime and engage GALT (Blake et al., 2017), the very inductive site required for protective gut immunity. Consequently, avian coccidiosis presents a pressing and specific need for novel, cost-effective oral vaccine platforms capable of inducing robust mucosal immunity.

Effective vaccine design requires precision targeting of antigens essential for the parasite lifecycle. For apicomplexan parasites like E. tenella, invasion is a critical vulnerability. Proteins secreted from apical organelles, particularly the rhoptry, mediate host cell attachment and invasion (Dubois and Soldati-Favre, 2019). A conserved, central event is the formation of the moving junction via the interaction between rhoptry neck protein 2 (RON2) and apical membrane antigen 1 (AMA1) (Tyler and Boothroyd, 2011). Building on this universal mechanism, our foundational work has systematically deconstructed the moving junction complex in E. tenella. We identified and characterized a family of EtRON2 variants (EtRON2L1-1, EtRON2L1-2, EtRON2L2), demonstrating their stage-specific expression, surface exposure during invasion, and—critically—that antibodies against them potently inhibit sporozoite invasion in vitro (Liu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025). Furthermore, we mapped specific interaction networks between these EtRON2 variants and their partner EtAMAs, cementing their functional role in invasion (Liu et al., 2024). Based on these mechanistic insights, EtRON2 variants emerge as high-value vaccine targets. Additionally, EtROP17, a rhoptry bulb protein with predicted B-cell epitopes, has been identified as a promising candidate from prior screening (Liu et al., 2022). However, a decisive translational gap persists: the capacity of these mechanistically grounded antigens to confer in vivo protection, especially via the physiologically relevant oral route, remains untested.

To bridge this gap, the choice of delivery platform is paramount. Recent strategies, such as using engineered, invasive Lactobacillus plantarum to deliver DNA vaccines, show promise but introduce complexities of host cell entry and variable efficiency (Zhang et al., 2020). We posited that a whole, recombinant yeast system offers a superior alternative for oral subunit vaccination. Saccharomyces cerevisiae, a GRAS (Generally Recognized As Safe) organism, is an ideal vector: its eukaryotic machinery ensures proper antigen folding; its cell wall β-glucans are potent innate immune adjuvants that engage receptors like Dectin-1 to drive Th1-polarized responses (Brown and Gordon, 2001; Levitz, 2010); and it supports high-density, low-cost fermentation. Critically, the Aga2p-based surface display system stably tethers antigens exteriorly, shielding them from gut degradation and promoting sustained mucosal exposure (Boder and Wittrup, 1997). This platform has been validated for other pathogens but has not been leveraged to deliver the mechanistically grounded E. tenella rhoptry antigens identified in our prior work.

Therefore, we hypothesized that oral vaccination with S. cerevisiae surface-displaying the rhoptry antigens EtRON2 (along with its variants EtRON2L1-1 and EtRON2L1-2) and EtROP17 would elicit protective immunity against E. tenella challenge. To test this, we engineered and characterized the corresponding recombinant yeast strains, produced live and heat-inactivated vaccine formulations, and conducted a comprehensive in vivo evaluation in chickens. Protection was assessed through parasite burden, intestinal pathology, weight gain, the anticoccidial index (ACI), and antigen-specific humoral and cellular immune responses. Our work not only translates mechanistic insights into a practical vaccine strategy against avian coccidiosis but also provides a generalizable framework for the rational design of oral mucosal vaccines against other intracellular enteric pathogens.

Materials and methods

Animals, parasites and vectors

The Shanghai strain of E. tenella (Resource No. CAAS21111611) was maintained in our laboratory. One-day-old Sanhuang chickens were obtained from the Shanghai Minyou Poultry Breeding Specialized Cooperative and raised in a coccidia-free environment. No anticoccidial drugs were administered in their feed or drinking water throughout the study. Prior to experimental infection, fecal samples were microscopically examined to verify the absence of Eimeria infection. All animal procedures were conducted in strict accordance with the guidelines approved by the Animal Ethics and Welfare Committee of Shanghai Veterinary Research Institute (Permit No.: SHVRI-SZ-20240417-3). The S. cerevisiae strain EBY100 and the yeast expression vector pYD1 were purchased from Beijing Kulabo Technology Co. Ltd.

Gene cloning and plasmid construction

Sporulated oocysts of E. tenella were collected and purified as previously described (Eckert et al., 1995). Total RNA was extracted from sporulated oocysts using the TRIzol Reagent (Invitrogen, Carlsbad, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA integrity was assessed by agarose gel electrophoresis, and concentration was quantified using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The qualified total RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using M-MLV reverse transcriptase (Invitrogen).

Based on the predicted transmembrane structures of EtRON2 (ETH_00012760), EtRON2L1-1 (ETH_00013625), EtRON2L1-2 (ETH_00030325), and EtROP17 (ETH_00005190), DNA fragments encoding their predicted extracellular regions were selected for primer design, with all primers incorporating appropriate restriction enzyme sites (Table 1). These fragments were subsequently amplified by PCR using the synthesized cDNAs as templates. Each PCR product was digested with the corresponding restriction enzymes (New England Biolabs, Ipswich, MA, USA) and cloned into the linearized pYD1 vector using T4 DNA ligase (New England Biolabs) to generate four distinct recombinant plasmids: pYD1-EtRON2, pYD1-EtRON2L1-1, pYD1-EtRON2L1-2, and pYD1-EtROP17. Each plasmid was then transformed into E. coli DH5α competent cells (TaKaRa, Dalian, China). Expression and identification of recombinant yeast strains

Preparation of competent Saccharomyces cerevisiae EBY100. Competent cells of S. cerevisiae EBY100 were prepared using the Super Yeast Competent Cell Preparation and Transformation Kit (SK2401, Solarbio, Beijing, China). The process began by streaking the frozen glycerol stock onto a YPDA agar plate (Coolaber, Beijing, China) using the four-quadrant method, followed by incubation at 30°C for 72 h. A well-isolated single colony was then selected and inoculated into 3 mL of liquid YPDA medium (Coolaber) for overnight culture at 30°C with shaking at 200 r/min. This pre-culture was subsequently transferred to 50 mL of fresh YPDA medium in a flask and grown until the OD600 reached 0.4–0.5. Cells were harvested by centrifugation at 5000 r/min for 5 minutes, and the pellet was washed with 10 mL of Y1 solution. Finally, the cells were resuspended in 1 mL of Y2 solution, aliquoted into 50 μL portions in sterile cryotubes, and stored at -80°C for future use.

Yeast transformation and screening of positive clones. The recombinant plasmids pYD1-EtRON2, pYD1-EtRON2L1-1, pYD1-EtRON2L1-2, and pYD1-EtROP17 were individually transformed into competent yeast cells, with the empty pYD1 vector transformed in parallel as a negative control. For each transformation, 5–10 μL of plasmid DNA (approximately 200 ng/μL) was mixed with 350 μL of Y3 solution, and the total volume was adjusted to 360 μL with sterile ddH₂O. The mixture was combined with 50 μL of competent cells and gently resuspended by pipetting. Following incubation at 30°C for 60 minutes with intermittent mixing every 10 minutes, the cells were collected by brief centrifugation. The pellet was resuspended in 400 μL of sterile deionized water and spread onto SD/Trp⁻ selection plates (Coolaber), which were subsequently incubated at 30°C for 2–4 days.

To screen for positive clones, randomly selected yeast colonies were inoculated into YNB-CAA liquid medium (Coolaber) containing 2% glucose (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA) and cultured at 30°C with shaking at 250 r/min for 13 hours. Genomic DNA was extracted from the yeast cultures using a commercial yeast genomic DNA extraction kit (Tiangen Biotech, Beijing, China) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The extracted genomic DNA was then used as a template for PCR amplification using primers listed in Table 1 to confirm the identity of positive clones.

Induction and identification of target protein expression. Positive clones selected from the transformation plates were inoculated into YNB-CAA medium supplemented with 2% glucose and cultured at 30°C with shaking at 250 r/min until the OD600 reached 2.0–5.0. For target protein induction, the cells were transferred to YNB-CAA medium containing 2% galactose (Sigma-Aldrich), with the initial OD600 adjusted to approximately 1.0. The induction culture was then maintained at 20°C with continuous shaking at 250 r/min for 72 hours. During this period, cell growth was monitored by measuring the OD600 at 0, 12, 24, 36, 48, 60, and 72 hours to construct growth curves.

Following induction, 1 mL samples from each culture were collected for protein analysis. Cells were pelleted by centrifugation and resuspended in 40 μL of PBS. Then, 10 μL of 5 × SDS loading buffer (Beyotime, Shanghai, China) was added, and the samples were boiled at 100°C for 12 minutes. Western blot analysis was performed using standard procedures. The protein samples were separated by SDS-PAGE and electrophoretically transferred onto PVDF membranes (Millipore, Billerica, MA, USA). After blocking with 5% skim milk, the membranes were incubated with rabbit polyclonal antibodies against EtRON2, EtRON2L1-1, EtRON2L1-2, or EtROP17 (generated in-house; antibody specificity was validated as previously described at 1:200 dilution in 5% skim milk) (Liu et al., 2024; Chen et al., 2024; Tang et al., 2025). Horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (Abcam, Cambridge, UK) at 1:10000 dilution was used as the secondary antibody. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) detection system (Beyotime).

Analysis of surface display efficiency in recombinant yeast. Yeast cells from 72-hour induced cultures were processed to evaluate surface display efficiency. For each strain, 200 µL of culture was collected and centrifuged at 4500 r/min for 4 minutes. After discarding the supernatant, the cell pellets were washed twice with 1 × PBS. Owing to the surface-localized nature of the target proteins, no permeabilization treatment was performed. The cells were then resuspended in 500 µL PBS and washed three additional times under identical centrifugation conditions. Samples were subsequently incubated for 1 hour with 500 µL of rabbit antisera specific to EtRON2, EtRON2L1-1, EtRON2L1-2, or EtROP17 (each diluted 1:200 in PBS), with gentle mixing throughout the incubation. Following washing, cells were incubated with 200 µL of FITC-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG (H+L) (Abcam) (1:10000 dilution) for 45 minutes in the dark with periodic agitation.

For immunofluorescence observation, 4 µL of the stained cell suspension was mounted on glass slides and examined under a fluorescence microscope. For quantitative assessment, the remaining cell suspensions (approximately 196 µL after immunofluorescence sampling) from EBY100/pYD1-EtRON2, EBY100/pYD1-EtRON2L1-1, EBY100/pYD1-EtRON2L1-2, EBY100/pYD1-EtROP17, and EBY100/pYD1 (negative control) were analyzed by flow cytometry. Surface display efficiency was calculated as the percentage of fluorescent cells detected for each recombinant strain.

Preparation of recombinant yeast vaccines

Live recombinant yeast vaccines. The recombinant yeast strains EBY100/pYD1-EtRON2, EBY100/pYD1-EtRON2L1-1, EBY100/pYD1-EtRON2L1-2, EBY100/pYD1-EtROP17, along with the empty vector control EBY100/pYD1, were cultured and induced for surface expression as previously described. Briefly, induction was carried out in YNB-CAA medium supplemented with 2% galactose at 20°C for 72 hours with continuous shaking. Post-induction, the cells were centrifuged at 4°C and 4000 r/min for 5 minutes. After supernatant removal, the cell pellets were washed three times with sterile 1 × PBS to remove residual medium components. The pellets were then resuspended in sterile PBS, and yeast cells were quantified using a hemocytometer. The cell suspension was adjusted to a concentration of 5 × 108 cells/mL, preparing it for immediate oral immunization as a live yeast vaccine. Chickens were orally immunized six times with 1 × 108 cells per dose (in 200 µL).

Inactivated recombinant yeast vaccines. For the preparation of inactivated vaccines, the aliquoted live yeast cells of each recombinant strain (approximately 1 × 1010 cells in total) were centrifuged and washed as described above. The washed pellets were resuspended in 10 mL of sterile PBS and subjected to thermal inactivation at 95°C for 10 min (Soutter et al., 2022). To confirm successful inactivation, 50 µL of the heat-treated suspension was streaked onto selective SD/Trp⁻ agar plates. The plates were incubated at 30°C for 2–4 days. The absence of microbial growth confirmed the complete inactivation of the yeast cells (detection limit < 1 CFU per plate). The inactivated yeast vaccines were stored at 4°C until use.

Immunization and challenge infection

A total of 240 one-day-old chicks were randomly allocated into 12 experimental groups, with each group consisting of 2 replicates of 10 chickens. The grouping design was as follows: EBY100/pYD1-EtRON2 (Live and Inactivated), EBY100/pYD1-EtRON2L1-1 (Live and Inactivated), EBY100/pYD1-EtRON2L1-2 (Live and Inactivated), EBY100/pYD1-EtROP17 (Live and Inactivated), EBY100/pYD1 empty vector (Live and Inactivated), Unimmunized-challenged, and Unimmunized-unchallenged.

All groups were housed separately in coccidia-free facilities. The ten vaccination groups received oral administrations of 1 × 108 cells of either live or inactivated recombinant yeast resuspended in 200 µL PBS via gavage on days 3, 7, 10, 13, 16, and 19. The two unimmunized control groups were given an equivalent volume of PBS on the same schedule. At 21 days of age, all chickens except those in the unimmunized-unchallenged group were orally challenged with 10,000 freshly sporulated E. tenella oocysts. This challenge dose was predetermined in pilot studies to reliably induce patent infection and measurable intestinal pathology in unvaccinated controls (Tang et al., 2025).

Evaluation of protective efficacy

Body weight was measured individually on days 21 (pre-challenge), and 29 (8 days post-challenge). The relative body weight gain was calculated by comparing the average weight gain of each experimental group to that of the healthy control group (unimmunized-unchallenged).

Fecal samples were collected daily from 6 to 8 days post-challenge. Oocysts per gram (OPG) of feces were quantified using the McMaster technique with three technical replicates per group, following established protocols (Eckert et al., 1995). Total oocyst output per group was calculated as: (Mean OPG) × (Total fecal sample weight). Mean lesion scores were evaluated on day 8 post-challenge according to standard criteria (Johnson and Reid, 1970). The anticoccidial index (ACI), calculated as (Survival Rate + Relative Weight Gain Rate) − (Lesion Score + Oocyst Score), was evaluated for each group based on the established criteria (De Pablos et al., 2010), with the following interpretative scale: ACI < 120 indicates no protection, 120–160 denotes partial protection, and > 160 corresponds to strong protection.

Detection of antibodies and immune cell markers

On day 21 post-hatch, immediately before challenge, blood and cecal tissues were collected from four randomly selected chickens per replicate (8 chickens per group total). Serum was obtained by allowing blood to clot at room temperature for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 3000 × g for 10 min at 4°C, and stored at –20°C. For mucosal analysis, cecal tissues were opened, rinsed with ice-cold PBS, and the mucosal layer was scraped off. The scrapings were weighed, homogenized in PBS (1:5, w/v) by vortexing for 1 min, and centrifuged at 5000 × g for 5 min at 4°C. The supernatant was aliquoted and stored at –80°C for secretory IgA (sIgA) measurement.

Antigen-specific serum IgY was quantified by indirect ELISA. Briefly, 96-well plates were coated with recombinant antigen (2 µg/well) in coating buffer at 4°C for 16–18 h. After washing, plates were blocked with 1% BSA in PBS for 1 h at 37°C. Following another wash, diluted serum samples were added and incubated for 2 h at 37°C. Bound IgY was detected using HRP-conjugated rabbit anti-chicken IgY (1:10,000 dilution; Abcam) incubated for 1 h at 37°C, followed by TMB substrate development. The reaction was stopped with 2 M H₂SO₄, and the absorbance was measured at 450 nm. Secretory IgA in cecal supernatants was measured using a similar indirect ELISA protocol. Cecal supernatants were added to antigen-coated plates, and bound sIgA was detected using HRP-conjugated goat anti-chicken IgA (1:5,000 dilution; Abcam). Serum levels of CD4 and CD8 markers and cytokines (IFN-γ, IL-6, IL-17, IL-18) were quantified using commercial chicken-specific ELISA kits (Cusabio, Wuhan, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Data analysis

All experimental data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS Statistics version 22.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and GraphPad Prism (version 9.0; GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA, USA). For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) was performed followed by Tukey's honestly significant difference post hoc test. Comparisons between two groups were analyzed using Student's t-test. Differences were considered statistically significant at P < 0.05.

Vaccine protective efficacy

To comprehensively evaluate the protective efficacy of the yeast-based vaccines, we assessed three critical parameters following challenge infection: clinical outcome (body weight gain), parasitological burden (oocyst shedding), and pathological severity (intestinal lesions). Additionally, an integrated Anticoccidial Index (ACI) was calculated to provide a holistic view of protection.

Recombinant yeast vaccination mitigates coccidiosis-induced growth impairment. As shown in Fig. 3A, all groups immunized with either live or inactivated S. cerevisiae (including empty vector controls) gained significantly more weight post-challenge than the unvaccinated infection control (P < 0.05), indicating a baseline protective effect of the yeast vehicle. Beyond this baseline, the magnitude of weight gain improvement depended on both the expressed antigen and the formulation (Fig. 3B, C). Among live yeast vaccines, expression of EtRON2L1-2, EtROP17, or EtRON2 conferred superior weight gain compared to the live empty-vector control. In contrast, the protective effect of inactivated yeast vaccines was more restricted, with only EtRON2L1-1 and EtRON2L1-2 outperforming their empty-vector control. Discussion

This study establishes and evaluates an oral mucosal vaccine platform based on yeast surface display and delineates a decisive triad of factors governing its efficacy against the enteric protozoan Eimeria tenella: antigen centrality, carrier viability, and immune polarization. These findings extend beyond avian coccidiosis, offering a potentially generalizable design framework for vaccines targeting intracellular enteric pathogens, a category that remains challenging due to mucosal tropism and obligate intracellular replication (Kaufmann, 2007). We synthesize our results through the lens of these three interconnected determinants.

First, antigen selection is paramount, with protective efficacy directly correlating with its functional indispensability in the invasion process—a principle broadly applicable to apicomplexan parasites (Doolan et al., 2003; Sette and Rappuoli, 2010). In our study, the live yeast vaccine expressing EtRON2, the core component of the conserved moving junction (Besteiro et al., 2011), achieved the highest anticoccidial index (ACI of 172). This superior efficacy likely stems from antibody-mediated neutralization of a spatially exposed, invasion-essential complex (Burns et al., 1989) and from targeting a later, conserved step in invasion that may constitute a more vulnerable chokepoint (Carruthers and Boothroyd, 2007). The convergence of high protection for EtRON2 delivered via two distinct platforms (yeast and Lactobacillus) (Zhang et al., 2024) underscores its exceptional immunogenicity. In contrast, the partial protection conferred by EtRON2L1-2 and EtROP17 suggests more ancillary roles. This empirically defined “antigen hierarchy” underscores and refines a fundamental tenet for mucosal vaccine design: functional necessity and spatial accessibility during host-cell entry are key predictors of target value (Moyle and Toth, 2013).

Second, the viability of the delivery vehicle critically and antigen-dependently shapes the immune response quality and protection outcome (Sinani and Şenel, 2025). The improved weight gain in empty-vector groups indicates a baseline effect of the yeast carrier, probably through β-glucan-mediated innate immune stimulation and modulation of the intestinal microenvironment. Thus, antigen-specific protection should be interpreted relative to the empty-vector control. For EtRON2, the live yeast carrier was significantly more protective than its inactivated counterpart. Beyond presenting natively folded antigen, live yeast offers inherent adjuvant properties; its cell wall β-glucans engage receptors like Dectin-1 to drive Th1/Th17-polarized responses (Brown and Gordon, 2001; Levitz, 2010). We propose that the metabolic activity and gut persistence of live yeast may mimic prolonged antigen exposure, potentially engaging autophagy pathways (Dietrich et al., 2003; Pieters, 2008) to promote cell-mediated immunity, akin to the priming effect of live attenuated vaccines (Plotkin et al., 2018). The specific induction of a Th1/Th17 cytokine profile alongside a reduction in serum CD4 and CD8 levels in the EtRON2-live group may reflect an effective effector T-cell generation and mucosal homing (Mora and von Andrian, 2008; Khader et al., 2009), although mucosal T-cell infiltration was not directly examined. The reduced efficacy of inactivated formulations may partly reflect heat-induced disruption of the native conformation of surface-displayed antigens, although this possibility was not directly examined in the present study. Notably, the equal efficacy of live and inactivated formulations for EtRON2L1-2 suggests that the advantage of live carriers is antigen-dependent, highlighting the necessity for antigen-specific formulation optimization rather than assuming a universal benefit of live vaccines—a critical consideration for developing vaccines against diverse enteric pathogens.

Third, optimal protection correlates with the induction of a specific, integrated mucosal immune phenotype. The live EtRON2 vaccine uniquely elicited a systemic Th1/Th17 bias coupled with evidence of T-cell recruitment to mucosal sites—a response profile crucial for defense against intracellular parasites (Lillehoj and Lillehoj, 2000) and aligned with the goals of mucosal vaccination (Holmgren and Czerkinsky, 2005). In contrast, the inactivated EtRON2 vaccine stimulated peripheral T-cell expansion without concomitant Th1 polarization, resulting in a “decoupled” and less protective response. This distinction emphasizes that for intracellular enteric infections, stimulating immunity is insufficient; it must be appropriately polarized and directed to the mucosal frontier. In addition, formulation effects on humoral immunity were also antigen-specific: live EtROP17 specifically induced mucosal sIgA, whereas inactivated EtRON2 uniquely elicited serum IgY. The mechanistic basis for this difference remains unclear but may involve antigen-specific differences in release kinetics from live versus inactivated yeast, epithelial uptake, or epitope accessibility, warranting further investigation. Notably, the unchanged IL-18 levels suggest that the yeast platform does not activate the inflammasome pathway, which may be favorable for safety by avoiding excessive inflammation.

Interestingly, the rank order of surface display efficiency did not correlate with protective efficacy. EtRON2L1-2 showed the highest display efficiency (59.63%) but only partial protection (ACI = 140), while EtRON2 with moderate display efficiency (50.69%) conferred the strongest protection (ACI = 172). This observation further supports the antigen hierarchy discussed above: antigen identity and functional relevance during invasion—rather than display quantity per se—are the primary determinants of protective efficacy, and the Aga2p display system serves primarily as a delivery platform rather than a quantitative driver of protection.

The superior and integrated immune phenotype elicited by our live yeast platform provides a clear mechanistic rationale for its efficacy and facilitates comparison with alternative technologies. While multi-epitope DNA vaccines often confer only moderate protection (Chen et al., 2023), and highly invasive bacterial vectors pose inherent safety concerns (Deretic et al., 2013), our live yeast-EtRON2 formulation achieves an optimal balance of inherent biosafety (GRAS status), oral practicality, and potent mucosa-tropic immunogenicity. The level of protection (ACI up to 172) rivals that achieved by more complex regimens using invasive vectors with cytokine adjuvants (Zhang et al., 2020), yet our platform is fundamentally simpler and safer (van der Hoek et al., 2019). Consequently, within the landscape of coccidiosis control—where options are limited by drug resistance, suboptimal efficacy of subunit vaccines (Blake et al., 2015), and safety concerns of live-attenuated or invasive vectors (Deretic et al., 2013; Chapman, 2014)—this yeast-based strategy represents a strategically advantageous advance. Future translational development should focus on optimizing the immunization regimen, exploring multivalent combinations (e.g., EtRON2 with EtROP17) to broaden protection (Shirley et al., 2005), and evaluating long-term immunity and cross-species protection to fully assess field potential (Williams, 2002).

In conclusion, by deconstructing vaccine success into the essential triad of antigen centrality, carrier viability, and immune polarization, our work provides not only a promising candidate but also a blueprint for the rational design of mucosal vaccines. We synthesize our empirical findings into a testable “antigen-centrality–carrier-viability–immune-polarization” framework. This triad offers a generalizable guide for the rational design of next-generation oral vaccines against intracellular enteric pathogens, presenting a potentially useful strategy to overcome persistent bottlenecks in disease control. Nevertheless, several limitations warrant mention: antibody responses were reported as OD450 absorbance values without endpoint titers or functional neutralization assays; surface display efficiency was assessed as the percentage of FITC-positive cells rather than antigen molecules per cell, limiting quantitative comparisons among antigens; the impact of heat inactivation on antigen conformation was not assessed; and the sample size was small (8 birds/ group) with no examination of long-term immunity. These represent important directions for future investigation.
