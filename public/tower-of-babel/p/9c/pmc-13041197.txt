Exogenous resources drive convergent successional processes within soil microbial food web in two soil types
Authors: Yu-Jia Cai, Yuhai Chang, Jessica Louise Ray, Hong J Di, Ju-Pei Shen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSoil microorganisms mediate critical ecosystem processes, including nutrient cycling and climate regulation. However, the extent to which their functional resilience and microbial food web dynamics respond uniformly to organic amendment across two land-use types remain poorly understood.ResultsIn this study, we conducted a 30-day microcosm experiment to investigate how exogenous resources addition restructures the assembly of primary microbial functional groups (bacteria, fungi, and protists) in urine-amended soils under contrasting land-use regimes: intensively managed maize fields and natural woodlands. Results showed that straw addition consistently reduced both OTU richness and Shannon diversity of bacteria (by 8% and15%, respectively), fungi (26% and 21%), and protists (21% and 29%) in both soil types, yet enhanced cross-domain microbial interactions. Co-occurrence network analysis revealed variation in trophic interaction based on network parameters: maize soils fostered bacteria-dominated networks, whereas fungal hubs dominated in woodland systems. Deterministic processes predominantly govern bacterial community assembly, contrasting with the stochastic dominance observed in fungal and protist communities. Notably, both land use regimes showed consistent decreases in α-diversity, increased network complexity, and shifts toward similar assembly processes despite initial differences in community structure.ConclusionOur findings demonstrate that organic amendments can override land-use legacies in shaping microbial community dynamics, highlighting the integrated impact of availability and biotic interactions in driving soil microbial food web dynamics.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s40793-026-00872-0.Keywords: Protists, Straw addition, Soil microbial food web, Bottom-up regulation, Land use

Introduction

Soil microbial-food webs consisting of bacteria, fungi, archaea, viruses and protists, play crucial roles in various ecosystem processes such as carbon sequestration and contaminant removal [1]. Advances in culture-independent DNA metabarcoding technology has significantly enhanced our understanding of microbial community structure and function [2, 3]. Microbial community assembly and interactions within and across domains, however, remain less understood. Numerous studies have highlighted that microbial community diversity and structure are key determinants of ecosystem function and nutrient availability [4, 5]. Continuing research has furthermore revealed that microbial interactions such as grazing, play a fundamental role in maintaining ecosystem function [6]. In terrestrial ecosystems, a stable diverse microbial food web fosters resistance and resilience against environmental stressors like nutrient addition or land management practices [7–9]. Thus, understanding the intricate interactions and trophic structures within stable microbiomes are of paramount importance for mitigating the impacts of environmental disturbances.

Straw returning to soil is a common amendment to improve soil properties such as bulk density, pH and organic carbon, all of which can regulate microbial biomass and activity [10–13]. For example, straw addition can change the network interaction and assembly processes of microbial communities [14]. Furthermore, the imbalance in soil elements due to the introduction of straw would reshape soil microbial food webs [15, 16]. Consequently, bacterial-, fungal- and protists-based soil food webs may exhibit divergent response to environmental perturbations by triggering trophic cascades through either top-down (predation by protists regulating bacterial and fungal communities) or bottom-up (resource availability shaping microbial communities from lower to higher trophic levels) pathways [5]. For instance, organic material utilized by one specie may be a metabolic by-product of another [17, 18]. Specifically, nutrient availability shapes microbial assemblages, influencing the resources accessible to higher trophic protists through bottom-up regulation [19, 20]. In contrast, protists function as top microbiome predators in top-down regulation, preying on bacteria and fungi in a selective or non-selective manner [21]. There is increasing evidence highlighting the role of protists in response to disturbance, such as fertilization [12]. However, little is known regarding the selective grazing preference of protists in soils rich in organic matter.

The interactions within microbial food webs are closely related to assembly processes of microbial communities [14, 22, 23]. Deterministic and stochastic processes are two types of ecological processes that control the assembly of soil microbial communities [24]. The deterministic process is a non-random process governed by biotic and abiotic factors, while a stochastic process is governed by random events [25]. These assembly mechanisms have been well-addressed for diverse terrestrial habitats, such as successional soils, crop lands [26, 27]. It has been widely accepted that fungal-based microbial food webs are more prevalent in forest soils, while bacterial-dominated food webs typically emerge in intensively farmed soils [21, 28]. These variations in soil microbial dominance reflect their long-term adaptions to resource availability and assembly process [29]. However, how the assembly processes of microbial community drive the co-occurrence network within microbial food webs remains unclear.

In general, the microbial heterogeneity and co-occurrence networks reveal distinct responses to changes in resource availability. To explore the response of different microbial communities to resource pulse event, i.e. straw addition, we carried out microcosm experiments using two contrasting soil types obtained from a maize field and an adjacent woodland as described previously [30]. Urine was included as a nitrogen complement to simulate realistic agricultural conditions where carbon and nitrogen inputs are often coupled. This approach prevents nitrogen limitation during straw decomposition, thereby enabling a clearer investigation of how resource availability shapes microbial food-web dynamics beyond carbon addition alone. We hypothesized that (1) straw addition would create a selective environment favoring fast-growing taxa, thereby reducing the α-diversity of all three microbial groups; and (2) straw addition, as a major perturbation introducing homogeneous resources, would initially enhance deterministic selection by altering soil properties and trophic interactions. This study aims to elucidate the multi-trophic structure and regulation of soil microbiomes, highlighting the significance of protists in microbial ecology. By examining microbial responses across trophic levels, we seek to better understand how resource inputs, such as straw, shape microbial community dynamics and their ecological functions in distinct land use types.

Materials and methods

Preparations of soils, maize straw, and urine for microcosms

Soil sampling site and collection procedures were detailed as previously [30]. Briefly, soil samples were obtained from intensively managed maize field (MF) and its adjacent woodland (WL) in October 2018 in Yanqing district, Beijing, China. After removing stones and plant debris, the soil was homogenized, air-dried, and sieved through a 2-mm mesh. The air-dried soil was stored at room temperature for subsequent microcosm experiment. The soil is Fluvo-aquic (Inceptisol according to USDA classification) with a sandy loam texture. Basic soil properties are listed as follows: soil organic matter (SOM) 16.2 and 11.3 g kg− 1, total nitrogen (TN) 1.0 and 0.6 g kg− 1, total phosphorus (TP) 0.6 and 0.9 g kg− 1 dry soil, total potassium (TK) 6.6 and 7.4 g kg− 1, ammonium (NH4+-N) 11.6 and 12.5 mg kg− 1, and nitrate (NO3−-N) 64.6 and 7.1 mg kg− 1, respectively.

Fresh maize straw was air-dried at 50 °C, mechanically chopped into 2–3 cm fragments, and homogenized by grinding through a 180 μm sieve. The maize straw contained 421 mg kg− 1 TC, 11 mg kg− 1 TN (with a C/N ratio of 38.6), 0.85 g kg− 1 TP, and 7.63 g kg− 1 TK (dry weight). Given the high C/N ratio of straw, urine was added as N complement. Urine collection was carried out from a maize-fed adult pig for 7 days, mixed well, filtered to remove suspended particles, and subsequently stored at -80 °C until use. The pig urine had a TN concentration of 46.6 g L− 1 and a pH greater than 9.0.

Soil microcosms set-up

MF and WL soils were subjected to a 7-day preincubation in darkness at 25 °C, with soil moisture maintained at 20% (w/w) to reactivate microbial activity prior to experiment treatments. For microcosm construction, 96 g of preincubated soil (equivalent to 80 g dry soil) was packed into a 250 mL-glass flask and adjusted to a bulk density of 1.3 g cm−3. Following preincubation, filtered swine urine (final concentration: 100 mg N kg−1 dry soil) was added to each flask to simulate urine-amended conditions. All the flasks were divided into two treatment groups: urine-only (denoted as U) and urine co-amended with maize straw substrate (denoted as US). The maize straw was applied at 0.6% (w/w), corresponding to a field application rate of 10 t ha−1 (to a soil depth of 7.5 cm) [31]. The microcosms finally consisted of four treatments: MF-U, MF-US, WL-U and WL-US, each with 12 replicates (3 replicates × 4 time points). Destructive sampling was conducted at days 0, 3, 8, and 30. All these microcosms were sealed with a breathable sealing membrane to prevent the exogeneous microbes and maintain the aerobic condition, and then incubated in the dark at 25 °C for 30 days with soil moisture periodically adjusted to 25% (w/w) through sterile water replenishment. Soil samples were collected destructively and kept at -20 °C for microbial community analysis.

Soil DNA extraction, PCR and high-throughput sequencing

Soil DNA was extracted using the Soil DNA Isolation Kit (DNeasy Power Soil Kit, QIAGEN, Germany) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The quality and concentration of extracted DNA were assessed using a NanoDrop ND-1000 Spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) at 260 and 280 nm wavelengths.

In this study, bacterial 16 S rRNA gene V4 region, fungal ITS2 region and protist 18 S rRNA gene V4 region were amplified using the following primer sets: 515 F/806R [32], ITS7/ITS4 [33] and TAReuk454F/TAReukR [34], respectively. The PCR reaction mixture and sequencing processes were carried out as described previously [30]. Briefly, the PCR reaction mixture contained 4 µL of 5 × FastPfu Buffer, 2 µL of 2.5 mM dNTPs, 0.8 µL of each primer (5 µM), 0.4 µL of FastPfu Polymerase, and 10 ~ 100 ng of total DNA in ABI GeneAmp 9700 thermal cycler. Negative control was applied during the amplification without no DNA template. Positive control was carried out using the plasmid with the correct insert clones of each target gene as described previously [30]. Sequencing was performed on the Illumina MiSeq platform using a paired-end protocol (2 × 250 bp and 2 × 300 bp) at Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China.

Bioinformatic analysis

All raw sequences were processed in QIIME 1.9.1 (Quantitative Insight into Microbial Ecology) pipeline [35]. After filtering chimeras, UPARSE was applied to bin the sequences into OTUs at the 97% sequence identity level [36]. Representative sequences of bacteria, fungi and protist were compared against the SILVA (release 132), UNITE (ver 8) reference alignment and Protist Ribosomal Reference (PR2) database (ver 4.5) using the RDP (bacteria) and BLAST (fungi and protist) classifier, respectively. In total, 4,109,886 bacterial, 3,608,828 fungal, and 4,034,845 protistan reads were obtained from 48 samples. For bacterial sequences, all OTUs classified as chloroplasts or mitochondria were removed from the dataset prior to downstream ecological analysis. For fungal sequences, Plantae and ambiguous taxa were excluded. For protist sequence analysis, Rhodophyta, Streptophyta, Fungi, Opisthokonta_X, Merazoa and other ambiguous taxa were all removed [12]. Subsequently, the three microbial sequences were randomly rarefied to 32,000, 25,000, and 15,000 sequences per sample, resulting in 13,605 bacteria, 2040 fungi, and 2461 protist OTUs, respectively. Protist sequences were further classified at the genus level into three functional groups: consumers, parasites, and phototrophs as described previously [1]. In addition, the alignments were then used to build maximum-likelihood trees with FastTree [37]. Sequence reads were deposited into the NCBI Sequence Read Archive (SRA) database with the project accession number PRJNA1082242.

Microbial co-occurrence network analysis

We used the rarefied and filtered OTU table to conduct the co-occurrence network analysis using the CoNet in Cytoscape 3.9.1 [38]. Specifically, OTUs with a relative abundance greater than 0.1% with occurrence in at least 3 samples were included in the analysis. The co-occurrence networks were calculated independently for both the treatment groups and land-use type. As a result, an average of 1080, 239 and 313 OTUs for bacteria, fungi and protists were included in each network computing. Each network computation involved five correlation measures (Spearman correlation, Pearson correlation, Bray-Curtis distance, Kullback-Leibler distance, and mutual information) and a bootstrapping approach with 1000 iterations. To identify clusters within a network, ClusterViz, a plugin in Cytoscape, was employed. Network topological parameters were subsequently computed using the Network Analyzer tool in Cytoscape.

Soil chemical parameters measurement

The measurement of soil organic matter (SOM) has been addressed using as previously mentioned in previous studies [39]. After passing through a 100-mesh sieve, soil samples were analyzed for total nitrogen (TN) using an elemental analyzer (Vario EL III, Elementar). Ammonium nitrogen (NH4+-N) and nitrate nitrogen (NO3−-N) were extracted with 1 M KCl solution and quantified using a continuous flow analyzer (AA3, SEAL, Germany).

Statistical analysis

Microbial α-diversity indices, including OTU richness and Shannon diversity, were calculated from the rarefied OTU table using the “vegan” package in the R statistical computing environment [40]. A Bray-Curtis dissimilarity matrix was generated to assess the variations in microbial community structure. The results were visualized via nonmetric multidimensional scaling (NMDS) using the “vegan” package. The effects of straw, land types and incubation time on microbial community structure were assessed using PerMANOVA (permutations = 999) through “adonis” and “anosim” functions in the “vegan” package.

Genus-level differential analysis was conducted to identify the discriminatory taxa using DESeq2 package [41]. BetaMNTD (beta-mean nearest taxon distance) and betaNTI (beta-nearest taxon index) were employed to examine the soil microbial community assembly processes. BetaNTI, calculated based on the deviation between the observed betaMNTD and the mean of the null distribution [24, 42], was used to infer whether stochastic or deterministic processes dominate the community assembly using the “picante” [43] and “NST” packages [44]. If |betaNTI| < 2, the variance in microbial community composition is mainly caused by stochastic processes, otherwise, deterministic processes shape the microbial community [24]. Differentially abundant OTUs for straw addition were distinguished from the non-straw-amended group using the generalized linear model approach in the BioConductor “EdgeR” package [45]. Random forest analysis was applied to identify the best abiotic and biotic factors for the variation of protistan community diversity using the “randomForest” package in R (v.4.2.3).

Construction of structural equation modeling (SEM)

To explore how straw incorporation affects microbial diversity, a structural equation model (SEM) was constructed using AMOS 23.0 (AMOS Development Corporation). The model was developed based on the work of Wu et al. [46]. Model fit was evaluated based on the following indices: χ2/df (0 ≤ χ2/df ≤ 3, 0.05 ≤ P ≤ 1.00), normed fit index (NFI > 0.9), comparative fit index (CFI > 0.9), goodness-of-fit index (GFI > 0.9), and root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA ≤ 0.05). Microbial α-diversity index used in the SEM was the min-max normalization the average of richness and Shannon index values of bacteria, fungi and protists [47].

Results

Dynamics of soil properties over the incubation period

Soil properties exhibited distinct temporal patterns and varied significantly between treatments and land-use types (Fig. S1). Soil SOM and TN remained relatively stable, except for a sharp decrease detected in MF-U soil within the first 3 days. Additionally, a rapid decreasing shift of NH4+-N was observed over the incubation period following amendment for all the treatments. In contrast, soil NO3−-N remained consistently low in woodland, while it exhibited a U-shaped trend in maize land soils over the incubation period. Across land use types, soil NO3−-N, SOM and TN all displayed a strong land-use legacy effect, with significantly higher values recorded in maize field than in woodland (P < 0.05).

Exogenous resources alter soil microbial diversity and structure

In general, significant variations were observed in soil microbial α-diversity (i.e. OTU richness and Shannon diversity), with bacteria exhibiting the highest diversity, followed by protists and fungi. Specifically, the three microbial functional groups displayed a similar α diversity pattern across land uses and treatments (Fig. 1). Straw addition significantly reduced both OTU richness and Shannon diversity of bacteria (by 8% and 15%, respectively), fungi (26% and 21%), and protists (21% and 29%) across both land uses. Furthermore, maize field soil exhibited significantly higher bacterial-, fungal- and protistan OTU richness compared to woodland soil. In contrast, bacterial Shannon diversity was significantly higher in maize field soil than in woodland soil, whereas an opposite trend was observed for fungal Shannon diversity between the two land uses (Fig. 1).

NMDS analysis revealed distinct sample clusters based on straw addition and land use types (Fig. 2). Intra-domain phylogenetic heterogeneity was notable, with higher dissimilarity observed in fungal and protistan groups (Fig. 2A). Substantial variations in microbial communities were observed for both soil types and sampling times. Specifically, changes in bacterial composition were evident at the first sampling point (day 3), while more pronounced shifts in fungal and protistan communities became apparent later in the incubation (Fig. 2). Particularly, a clear successional trend in microbial community structure was evident over the incubation period. Except for the bacterial community, the initial fungal and protistan microbial community structures at day 0 clustered more closely with microcosms without straw addition. Responses of specific microbial taxa to exogenous resources

Straw addition also influenced the taxonomic composition of bacteria, fungi and protists, with significant variations observed at both phylum and genus levels across land uses and treatments (Figs. 3 and S2, S3, S4). At the phylum level, Proteobacteria, Acidobacteria, Chloroflexi, Actinobacteria, and Bacteroidetes were the most abundant bacterial phyla, with their relative abundances gradually increasing throughout the incubation period. In contrast, Firmicutes declined sharply from 40% (day 0) to only 6% (day 30) in the straw-amended treatment (Fig. 3A). For fungi, Ascomycota and Basidiomycota were the most dominant phyla. Specifically, the fungal class Agaricomycetes showed a similar declining pattern to Firmicutes, while Sordariomycetes (phylum Ascomycota) increased with straw addition (Fig. 3B). Protistan communities were dominated by the superphyla Rhizaria, Stramenopiles, Amoebozoa and Alveolata. The relative abundance of Ciliophora increased over incubation in the straw-amended treatment, while Apicomplexa, Ochrophyta and Chlorophyta showed the reverse trend (Fig. 3C). We further found that the protistan communities were dominated by the consumer functional group with their relative abundances gradually increasing throughout the incubation period (from 55.7% to 61.6% to 83.8% and 76.8%, respectively) following straw addition for maize field and woodland (Fig. S5).

However, straw amendment exhibited three distinct microbial response types: (i) ubiquitously enhanced taxa, (ii) woodland-depressed taxa, and (iii) maize-depressed taxa, reflecting land-use-driven specialist (Figs. We further employed DESeq2 analysis to identify differentially abundant taxa (|log2 Fold change| > 2, -log10 P value > 2) for woodland and maize land soils (Fig. 4). The total number of differentially abundant microbial genera in woodland was much higher than that of the maize land soil, suggesting a more pronounced response to straw incorporation in the former. A larger number of differentially abundant genera were identified for bacteria, followed by protist and fungi, for both soil types, indicating a more pronounced impact of specific taxa within the bacterial community. Moreover, we observed significant overlaps in differentially abundant taxa between two land uses after straw addition (Figs. S6A, C, E). A set of 145 bacterial genera were enriched in both land uses, predominantly consisting of Proteobacteria, with 37.0% Alphaproteobacteria, 27.2% Gammaproteobacteria, 22.2% Betaproteobacteria and 13.6% Deltaproteobacteria. Conversely, the response of fungi and protists differed in the two soil types, where a larger number of fungal and protistan genera were enriched in the maize field (Figs. S4C, D, E, F). The number of the enriched fungal and protist taxa shared for both soil types with straw addition were comparable (Figs. S4D and F). Half of the fungal genera belonged to Sordariomycetes class, while the protistan genera mainly consisted of Lobosa, Cercoza, Ciliophora and Stramenopiles_X. Furthermore, there were 18 fungal and 2 protist genera displaying a contrasting response to straw addition, enriched in the maize field but depleted in the woodland (Figs. 4E, F, G, and H).

Exogenous resources enhance microbial community interaction

We performed null models to assess whether the addition of straw significantly altered the processes governing microbial community assembly (Fig. 5). Interestingly, betaNTI values for bacteria were higher than 2 for both soils, while those for fungi and protists were mostly distributed between − 2 and 2 (Fig. 5A), indicating remarkably different community assembly processes for three microbial functional groups (Fig. 5B). The bacterial community assembly was primarily governed by deterministic processes in both land uses, while fungi and protists assemblages were influenced by both deterministic and stochastic processes (Fig. 5B). The consistently high βNTI values ( > + 2) for bacterial communities indicate that heterogeneous selection was the predominant deterministic process governing bacterial assembly in both land-use systems. Notably, our null model analysis revealed that straw addition increased the relative contribution of stochastic processes to the assembly of all three microbial groups, suggesting potential increases in ecological drift due to rapid population turnover and dispersal limitation. Subsequently, we inferred a co-occurrence network that encompassed all three microbial groups for each land use, aiming to gain deeper insights into the impact of straw addition on inter- and intra-domain interactions (Fig. 6). Straw addition significantly altered the overall structure of the microbial interaction networks. As shown in Table 1, the network scale (total nodes) and connection density (average degree) increased in both land uses, indicating a general enhancement of network complexity. Notably, the total number of connections (edges) surged by 269% in the maize field following straw addition, whereas it remained relatively stable in the woodland soil. Furthermore, a systematic shift occurred in the domain composition of the networks: the proportion of bacterial nodes increased under both land-use types, while the proportion of fungal nodes consistently decreased (Table 1). This supports our conclusion that straw resource input reinforces the central role of bacteria within the network. Comparison of the nodes within the networks, we observed that 21.4% and 21.8% of nodes were shared between straw and non-straw soils in maize fields and woodlands, respectively, while only 2.9% of the nodes were common across all networks, primarily comprising bacteria (Fig. 7A). Straw addition not only increased overall network connectivity but also significantly increased the proportion of cross-domain edges, particularly those linking bacteria with fungi and protists (Fig. 7B).

Identify main factors driving microbial diversity

We employed random forest modeling and SEM to investigate how straw addition influences soil microbial diversity. Random forest modeling indicates that soil NH4+-N, SOM as the most important predictors of protistan α-diversity (Fig. S7A), followed by bacterial and fungal α-diversity. The SEM results further clarified that straw addition did not exhibit a significant direct effect on soil NH4+-N and SOM, while it exerted a strong direct negative effect on the α-diversity of all three microbial domains (Fig. S7B). In contrast, SOM had a consistent positive direct effect on microbial α-diversity. The final model demonstrated a good fit to the data (χ²/df = 2.53, P = 0.11; CFI = 0.99; RMSEA = 0.05), explaining 81% of the variance in protistan α-diversity.

Discussion

Heterogenous features of microbiome in two soil types

Our findings indicated that the maize field soil hosts a more diverse community of bacteria, fungi, and protists (Fig. 1), underscoring the impact of land use types on protists to a similar extent as observed for bacteria and fungi [48]. The intensively managed maize soil has received annual manure amendments containing readily available organic C and N, which would support microbial growth [39]. The continuous input of organic manure favors the proliferation of fast-growing bacteria [49, 50], thus allowing bacteria to dominate in the microbial food web in agricultural ecosystem (Fig. 6). In contrast, although the woodland soil had previously been used for maize cultivation until approximately 10 years ago, the microbial communities are subjected only to natural perturbations with minimal anthropogenic influence [30]. Nutrients are limited or sequestered by mineralization, causing the re-establishment of the woodland biome. These processes promote the prevalence of slow-growing fungi [51, 52]. In this study, we observed that fungi acted as key connectors in the woodland soil microbial co-occurrence network indicating an established fungal dominated food web.

Generally, bacterial-dominated soil food webs tend to be more resilient, but less resistant to extra nutrient addition, whereas fungal-dominated systems demonstrate inverse functional traits [53]. Notably, the functional partitioning of bacteria, fungi and protists exhibited pronounced divergence in these two soil types. Straw addition was associated with reduced prominence of fungi as key connectors in the woodland microbial network, and co-presence interactions predominate over mutual exclusions in both soil types, although their ratio varied. Furthermore, the addition of straw increased the intra-domain microbial interactions, particularly for the bacterial community with the elevated abundance of Polyangiaceae and Lysobacter, which are mostly bacterivorous groups [6]. The intricate dynamics between these two types of microbial food webs emphasize the significance of land management strategies in maintaining resilient and fertile soil ecosystem [54]. The enhanced cross-domain network, especially the increased bacteria-protist links, as evidence of a strengthened trophic cascade triggered by the bottom-up resource pulse.

Similar community successional responses were detected for bacteria, fungi and protists in response to straw incorporation (Figs. 1 and 2): the bacterial community responded more rapidly (within hours of treatment initiation) to straw addition, followed by fungi and protists (day 3). This phenomenon might be attributed to the high surface-to-volume ratio, high abundance and short generation times of bacteria [55]. These traits suggest that nutrient availability drives bottom-up regulatory effect on soil microbial food webs [56, 57]. The addition of straw combined with porcine urine as nutrient source supplies a substantial amount of bioavailable nutrients, exerting a strong selective pressure on soil microorganisms [11, 14, 49]. When straw is added to the soils, the labile components initially stimulate the growth of some bacterial groups such as Proteobacteria, Bacteroidetes, Bacillales and Actinobacteria [58–60]. Subsequently, fungi, particularly Sordariomycetes and Dothideomycetes, secrete enzymes capable of decomposing the recalcitrant organic matter in the amended straw, thereby facilitating the conversion of complex organic substances into more bioavailable nutrients for other microorganisms [28, 61]. Furthermore, the proliferation of bacteria and fungi triggers the predatory activities of protists, including Lobosa, Cercozoa, Ciliophora and Stramenopiles_X [62–64]. The rapid increase in specific microbial taxa is further corroborated by a concurrent decrease in α-diversity and an intensification of microbial interspecies interactions (Figs. 1 and 5).

Cross-domain microbial interactions in response to exogenous resource inputs

The significant increase in network edges, average degree, and clustering coefficient after straw addition were detected (Table 1), indicating more complex potential interaction networks. Such enhanced microbial interactions suggest a potential intensification of microbial associations, which may reflect enhanced opportunities for both cooperative and competitive processes to acquire resources from extra inputs or microbial biomass. These interactions, including cooperation and predation, occur among bacteria, fungi, and protists, contributing to the composition and regulation of microbial food webs [21]. Previous studies have addressed the impact of straw addition on bacterial and fungal assemblages, revealing significant changes in community composition and co-occurrence patterns [65, 66]. Similar effects on protists assemblages were also detected in our study. Protists are ubiquitous in soils and represent an immensely diverse unicellular eukaryotes, including phototrophic or heterotrophic types. In this study, the functional analysis of protistan communities could link to their selective grazing in different soil types. Following straw addition, we observed a pronounced and consistent shift towards consumer-dominated protistan assemblages in both soils (Fig. S5). This dramatic increase strongly indicates a selective grazing preference by protists for the bacterial and fungal prey that proliferated under the resource-rich conditions by straw amendment. These findings suggest that exogenous organic matter acts as a bottom-up controller, first stimulating prey populations, which in turn fuels the growth of specific protistan grazers [65]. Consequently, protists likely play a pivotal and distinct regulatory role in the soil microbiome, beyond the more commonly studied bacterial and fungal dynamics, by selectively consuming certain microbial taxa and thereby modulating the overall community structure, functional potential, and carbon turnover processes. Future research employing functional classification is essential to disentangle the distinct roles of different protistan trophic guilds in mediating trophic regulation following organic amendment. While our findings further confirm previous research that protists are sensitive to special resource pulse events [12]. The proliferation of bacteria and fungi provides increased prey resources for heterotrophic protists, while potentially creating competitive conditions for phototrophic protists [5, 19], though the latter requires further investigation.

In this study, we found a prominent role of deterministic processes in structuring bacterial communities, while fungi and protists exhibited a greater influence from stochastic processes (Fig. 5). Null model analysis was used to reveal the relative influence of deterministic and stochastic processes within our controlled experimental system. While microcosms simplify spatial complexity, they are a valid tool for isolating and understanding the effect of a specific perturbation (resource addition) on community assembly rules. The strong deterministic signal, particularly heterogeneous selection (βNTI > + 2), likely reflects bacteria rapid growth rates and high niche specialization to immediate abiotic gradients (e.g., NH4+-N, pH, labile carbon) created by straw decomposition. Their community composition is thus predictably filtered by the altered soil environment. The strong deterministic filtering by the resource pulse (evidenced by βNTI) reduced diversity by selecting for a subset of fast-responding, resource-adapted taxa. This newly formed, functionally streamlined consortium subsequently engaged in intensive and diverse interactions to process the complex substrate, leading to increased network connectivity and cross-domain links observed in the co-occurrence analysis. Thus, the diversity loss and complexity increase are interpreted as two facets of the same successional process, community reorganization towards a more specialized and tightly interconnected state. It is important to note that the stochastic component inferred from these null model analyses represents the residual variation not explained by the measured environmental factors and deterministic selection. This stochastic fraction can encompass true random processes (e.g., ecological drift, probabilistic dispersal) as well as the effects of unmeasured biotic interactions, which may be complex and context-dependent.

Deterministic process generally plays an important role in the initial stages of microbial colonization [67]. Previous studies have emphasized that deterministic processes dominate in shaping microbial communities, while stochastic processes contribute significantly to historical contingency, ecological drift and dispersal [68]. Contrasting to our hypothesis, we found the contribution of stochastic processes increased in all three groups with the addition of straw, possibly due to the proliferation or decline of specific taxa [69], suggesting an increased community turnover in response to the straw addition. The rapid deterministic shift in bacteria is linked to the early enrichment of specific bacterial taxa (Fig. 4). For example, the addition of straw resulted in enrichment of Sphingomonadaceae, Xanthomonadaceae, and Streptomycetaceae in both soil types. These taxa are known to be involved in the degradation of complex compounds as well as N fixation [69, 70]. The abrupt increase in nutrient availability may have stimulated microbial motility and/or displacement [68, 71–73], thereby promoting intra- and inter-domain microbial interactions among bacteria, fungi and protists [26, 74]. Furthermore, the large, pulsed resource input may temporarily overwhelm existing competitive hierarchies and niche partitions. This creates a transient state of high resource availability where random colonization, death, or drift events (ecological drift) gain relative importance over deterministic selection, thereby elevating the stochastic signature in community assembly. The observed patterns in microbial community dynamics, particularly the shifts within different trophic groups, highlight the significance of inter-kingdom microbial interactions in response to disturbances in ecosystems. Additionally, we acknowledge that a 30-day microcosm incubation captures primarily the initial phase of microbial succession. However, such short-term experiments are well-established in soil microbial ecology for isolating the immediate effects of resource pulses on community structure and interaction networks [75]. They allow for controlled investigation of mechanistic responses before longer-term field factors (e.g., seasonal dynamics, macrofauna) introduce additional variability.

Conclusion

This study revealed that soil microbial food web exhibited a remarkable similarity in response to the addition of exogenous resources. Nutrient enrichment resulting from straw incorporation led to a decrease in α-diversity but a considerable enhancement in the multitrophic complexity of the emerging microbial co-occurrence networks. The rapid proliferation of bacteria upon straw addition initiated a cascade effect on co-occurring fungal and protistan assemblages, indicating a bottom-up regulation of the soil microbial community. Bacterial community assembly processes were dominated by deterministic processes, while fungal and protistan community assembly were dominated by stochastic processes. These findings underscore the dual role of exogenous resources in modulating microbial diversity and interaction networks, with implications for soil nutrient cycling under varying land-management practices. It is essential to note that this study characterizes a short-term response, and further research is needed to determine whether these responses to nutrient input persist within the microbial community and ultimately impact soil ecosystem functions.
