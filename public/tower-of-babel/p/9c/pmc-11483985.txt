The effect of pulmonary hypertension and right ventricular dysfunction on early mortality and morbidity in patients undergoing mitral valve replacement
Authors: Faruk Gencoglu, Mehmet Ali Yesiltas, Ozan Koyuncu, Ali Murat Mert
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionThe indications for concominant tricuspid valve surgery in patients undergoing mitral valve surgery for rheumatic reasons are limited. The aim of our study was to investigate the effects of severe pulmonary hypertension and low TAPSE values on early-term mortality and morbidity in patients undergoing mitral valve replacement.MethodsThe data of all patients who underwent mitral valve replacement between January 2013 and August 2020 were examined retrospectively. Patients were divided into 2 groups according to pulmonary artery pressure (PAP ≥ 50 and PAP < 50). The group with PAP > 50 was then divided into 2 subgroups according to TAPSE (1.5 ≥ or < 1.5) values. The early-term mortality and morbidity rates of these groups were compared.ResultsSeventy-nine patients who underwent mitral valve replacement were included in the study. Fifty-four (68%) of them were female, and 25 (32%) were male. During the preoperative period, the TAPSE was 16.8 ± 3.0 mm, and the PAP was 52.1 ± 14.1 mmHg. There were 53 patients with PAP > 50 and 26 patients with PAP < 50. In the PAP > 50 group, the rates of tricuspid regurgitation (p < 0.001), blood transfusion (p < 0.001), intensive care unit stay (p < 0.001), need for CPAP (p = 0.043), reintubation (p = 0.048), acute renal failure (p = 0.028), and mortality (p = 0.026) were found to be significantly different.ConclusionIn conclusion, we believe that in patients with mitral valve pathology, early referral for surgical intervention, before the pulmonary pressures significantly increase and right ventricular function deteriorates, can enhance survival outcomes.Keywords: Mitral valve surgery, Mitral valve replacement, Pulmonary hypertension, Right ventricular dysfunction

Introduction

Mitral stenosis may occur because of rheumatic heart disease, although its incidence is lower in developed countries. Commissural adhesion, thickened leaflets and fibrotic, shortened, calcified chordae tendineae constitute the morphology of the disease [1]. The rare causes of MS include degenerative, congenital (i.e., parachute valve), annular calcification, and malignant carcinoid syndrome [1–3]. A history of rheumatic fever presents in 50–60% of patients, and women are affected 2–3 times more often than men. MS may occur after 10–30 years of a rheumatic fever episode. As the incidence of RHD decreases, the incidence of MS also decreases. The cause of acute rheumatic fever is beta-hemolytic streptococci, but the mechanism of this fever is still unclear. Streptococcal antigens cross-react with human tissue and may produce an immunological response. Rheumatic heart disease affects the endocardium, myocardium, and pericardium to varying degrees [2, 3]. Mitral regurgitation (MR) is classified as primary (degenerative or organic) or secondary (functional or ischemic). Primary MR is caused by abnormalities in the leaflets, chordae tendineae, papillary muscles and annulus [4–6]. MR is the second most common reason for procedures requiring valve surgery in Europe after aortic stenosis [7]. The most common cause of MR is degeneration. The degenerative type is characterized by fibroelastic deficiency, and Barlow’s disease is characterized by myxomatous degeneration. Other causes of primary MR are infective endocarditis, mitral annular calcification (MAC), rheumatic heart disease, connective tissue disease and congenital malformations [8].

The diagnosis of tricuspid valve disease and indications for concomitant surgery in patients with rheumatic etiology who underwent mitral valve replacement are quite limited in the literature. Tricuspid valve pathology accompanying degenerative or functional mitral valve pathologies is often secondary (functional). However, in the literature, 20–30% organic tricuspid valve involvement is also mentioned simultaneously with rheumatic mitral valve disease [4–7]. Tricuspid valve pathologies that develop following rheumatic mitral valve surgery lead to alterations in right heart function. Tricuspid valve regurgitation is directly associated with increased mortality [5]. The mitral valve should be considered a complex functional whole consisting of the left atrium, left ventricle, chordae tendinea, papillary muscles, annulus and leaflets. A defect in any of these components can cause reverse flow from the left ventricle to the atrium during systole [7–10].

While current guidelines and publications on degenerative and functional valve pathologies are more certain, there is an evidence gap to address this gap in research on rheumatic pathologies. In our study, we aimed to observe early results of severe pulmonary hypertension and low TAPSE values in patients who underwent mitral valve replacement.

Methods

A total of 1382 open heart surgeries performed between 01.01.2013 and 01.08.2020 were retrospectively examined. A total of 381 of these patients underwent mitral valve surgery. The records of 79 patients who met the study criteria were scanned. The inclusion criteria for the studies are shown in Table 1. Patient groups excluded from the study were not included due to their potential to increase early mortality and morbidity, which could negatively impact the results. The average age was 60.09 ± 12.7 years. Fifty-four (68%) of them were female, and 25 (32%) were male. Echocardiography results before discharge were evaluated. Postoperative data (intubation time, drainage, rhythm changes, etc.) were examined.

The patients included in the study were divided into 2 groups according to their pulmonary artery pressure (PAP > 50 and PAP < 50). Patients with PAP > 50 were subsequently divided into 2 subgroups according to their TAPSE values (TAPSE < 1.5 and TAPSE > 1.5). The early-term mortality and morbidity rates of these groups were compared. During the preoperative period, the TAPSE was 16.8 ± 3.0 mm, and the PASB was 52.1 ± 14.1 mmHg.

Echo evaluation

A Philips EpiQ-7 device was used for echocardiographic evaluation. Systolic PAP was calculated by adding the right atrial pressure to 4 times the square of the peak velocity of the tricuspid regurgitation flow on the apical 4-chamber or right ventricular outflow, as in the Bernoulli equation. (systolic PAP = [4 x Vmax2] + right atrial pressure). The tricuspid annular plane systolic excursion (TAPSE) is a parameter used to evaluate right ventricular systolic function. A TAPSE < 15 mm indicates right ventricular systolic dysfunction. It has been shown that apical displacement of the tricuspid annulus in systole in PAH patients is correlated with right ventricular systolic function and has prognostic significance [11, 12].

Surgical procedure

All patients underwent surgery under general anesthesia. After skin sterilization and sterile draping, the heart was accessed by opening the pericardium following standard median sternotomy. Following systemic heparinization, aorta-bicaval cannulation was performed, and cardiopulmonary bypass was established. An antegrade cardioplegia cannula was placed through the aortic root. By applying a cross clamp, the heart was arrested with antegrade isothermal potassium blood cardioplegia and protected for intermittent periods. The mitral valve was accessed by performing a left atriotomy. After confirmation of rheumatic morphology on valve examination, the valve tissue was resected, and the mechanical or biological mitral valve was implanted in the anatomical position. During implantation, Teflon-pregnant supported single sutures were used. After controlling the leaflet motions, the air was evacuated, and the left atriotomy was closed. In cases where the tricuspid valve was affected, annuloplasty (St Jude Medical, Inc., St Paul, MN, USA) was performed after right atriotomy in some cases with Teflon-pregnant supported sutures or individual sutures. Following tricuspid valve repair, air evacuation followed by primary closure of the right atriotomy was performed. After declamping, warming and monitoring adequate cardiac contractility, cardiopulmonary bypass was discontinued. The cannulas were removed, bleeding control was achieved, and drainage tubes were placed in the mediastinum and, when necessary, in the thoracic cavities. The sternum was approached with wire, the other layers were closed in accordance with the anatomical plan, and the operation was terminated.

Postoperative management

All patients were followed in intensive care after surgery. Patients transferred with mechanical ventilation and rhythm, arterial and central venous pressure, and oxygen saturation were monitored. Arterial blood gas monitoring was performed at regular intervals. Patients whose intensive care follow-up was completed were transferred to the ward and mobilized. The drainage tubes and catheters were removed. The patients were discharged following a period of clinical recovery.

Statistical analysis

The SPSS 22.0 package was used in the study. Normality analysis of the data was performed with the Kolmogorov Smirov-Shapiro initial test. In groups of three, normally distributed continuous variables were evaluated with one-way ANOVA, and nonnormally distributed data were evaluated with the Kruskal‒Wallis test. Categorical data were compared with the chi-square test. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression analyses were performed to evaluate the combined endpoints and parameters that independently predicted patient death. After performing univariate analysis, a multiple regression model was created based on the results obtained. A P value < 0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

When the postoperative ECHO data of the patients were examined, there was a limited decrease in TAPSE in both groups. However, no significant improvement was observed in the postoperative TAPSE of the group with PAP > 50, which was lower than that of the preoperative group with PAP < 50. This showed that the impaired right ventricular functions did not recover after surgery. A significant decrease in pulmonary artery pressure was observed after surgery in both groups. TAPSE values need to be re-evaluated in the long term.

The blood transfusion requirement was found to be significantly greater in the group with PAP > 50 (p < 0.001). We believe that high venous system pressure due to high pulmonary pressure and increased venous drainage cause more blood transfusions. In the same group, the intensive care unit (ICU) stay (p < 0.001) was longer. Low lung capacity, the need for CPAP, and poor condition are among the reasons for longer ICU stays. CPAP (p = 0.043) need was found to be significantly greater in the group with PAP > 50. Patients in the higher PAP group were older, in poor condition, and had low lung capacity due to high pulmonary pressure. Although there was no mortality in the PAP < 50 group, mortality (16.9%, p = 0.026) was detected in the PAP > 50 group. The most important causes of mortality were reintubation (15.1%, p = 0.048) and pneumonia and sepsis, which are correlated with prolonged intubation. In addition, the increased need for inotropes in these patients after intubation and acute renal failure (24.5%, p = 0.028) are other reasons. Discussion

In recent years, patients with mitral valve pathology have been treated more effectively, and thus, a decrease in postoperative mortality and morbidity has been observed. The severity of mitral valve pathology and the duration of exposure to this pathology maintain their importance as the main determining factors in the development of pulmonary hypertension and pulmonary dysfunction, which are among the leading causes of mortality and morbidity. Secondary pulmonary hypertension is frequently seen as a complication of mitral valve diseases and significantly affects postoperative prognosis [13]. In patients with mitral valve disease, secondary hypertrophic changes occur in pulmonary arterioles due to long-term high pulmonary vascular resistance. The airway resistance increases, and the diffusion capacity decreases. Blood flow and ventilation shift to the upper zones of the lungs, and the ventilation time is prolonged. This respiratory condition that occurs in patients with mitral valve disease is defined as “mitral lung” [14]. In the study of Lafçı et al., the left atrium, pulmonary artery pressure, and right ventricle were significantly greater in the COPD group, and the right atrium was found to be significantly smaller. The pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) decreased significantly after COPD attack. The number of attacks within a year was negatively correlated with the ejection fraction (EF) measured during the postattack period [15]. Ohno et al. reported that in patients with mitral valve disease who did not develop pulmonary hypertension or did not have tricuspid regurgitation with the development of pulmonary hypertension, there was a significant improvement in respiratory function with valve surgery, and there was no improvement in respiratory function with mitral valve surgery in patients with tricuspid regurgitation and pulmonary hypertension [16]. In the study conducted by Benfari et al., functional tricuspid insufficiency was common in a large cohort of patients with heart failure with reduced ejection fraction. It was independently associated with pulmonary hypertension, atrial fibrillation, and severe heart failure. Long-term, more advanced functional tricuspid regurgitation is associated with significantly worse survival, regardless of baseline characteristics [17]. In our study, although there was no statistically significant difference in patients with high pulmonary pressure, extubation hours were greater in patients with PAP > 50, the need for CPAP was greater, and reintubation rates were greater. In general, our patients with PAP > 50 had very poor preoperative conditions and weak muscle strength. These patients also experienced problems with mobilization in the postoperative period. The susceptibility of these patients to atelectasis and secondary infection was greater. Postoperative survival raises the issue of timing. Deciding surgery at an early stage when patients are symptomatic before secondary changes in the pulmonary bed progress might be more beneficial. Whether the secondary hypertrophic changes in the lungs resulting from increased pulmonary pressures regress in the postoperative period is a subject for further investigation. Large-scale studies are needed to evaluate early and late complications in patients with elevated pulmonary pressures.

In the meta-analysis conducted by Wang et al. evaluating the clinical outcomes of patients with tricuspid regurgitation, it was shown that moderate and severe tricuspid regurgitation were associated with increased mortality, independent of pulmonary pressure, LVEF and RV dysfunction. It has been predicted that the severity of tricuspid regurgitation has a gradual relationship with mortality [18]. This meta-analysis supports our study and shows that right ventricular function is at the forefront in the relationship between mitral valve disease and mortality. This highlights the necessity for large-scale prospective studies to determine the relationship between mitral valve pathologies and pulmonary pressures in relation to mortality.

The TAPSE is decreased in patients with increased pulmonary artery pressure [19]. In a study of 63 patients conducted by Forfia et al., mortality increased significantly in pulmonary hypertension patients whose TAPSE decreased to less than 1.5 cm. In our study, high pulmonary hypertension was found to be an important parameter in terms of mortality and morbidity, but when we divided the patients with high pulmonary pressures into subgroups according to TAPSE values, no statistically significant difference was found in terms of mortality or morbidity. As a result of our clinical experience, TAPSE values (< 1.5 mm) are high in patients with low postoperative inotrope needs, ICU stays are long, and the possibility of other complications and mortality rates is high. It is believed that due to the secondary changes in the lungs caused by low TAPSE values and elevated pulmonary pressures, these patients exhibit reduced tolerance in the early postoperative period following extubation, owing to decreased lung capacity, making them more susceptible to postoperative complications and mobilization difficulties. Further large-scale studies are needed to better understand this phenomenon.

In the study conducted by Gammie et al., among patients who underwent mitral valve surgery, a lower 2-year mortality rate was observed in those who underwent tricuspid annuloplasty than in those who underwent isolated mitral valve surgery. This showed that progression was slower in severe tricuspid regurgitation patients. In another study, it was found that although there were no anatomical changes during the acute exacerbation of COPD, pulmonary artery pressure (PAP) increased during the exacerbation period and decreased afterward; similarly, TAPSE decreased during the exacerbation and increased after the exacerbation [20]. In our study, TR regressed in the early period after annuloplasty, and a parallel partial regression occurred in pulmonary pressure. These long-term results are the subject of another study.

According to a study by Guazzi et al., secondary pulmonary hypertension is a common consequence of chronic left atrial hypertension caused by myocardial and valve disease. These processes activate anatomical and functional changes in the pulmonary vascular bed, ultimately leading to RV dysfunction. These changes are clearly associated with increased morbidity and mortality mediated by effects on multiple organ systems [21]. In our study, high pulmonary pressure was clearly associated with mortality.

The emergence of right heart failure increases the operative mortality from 5 to 11%, and the mortality of patients during follow-up increases from 8 to 22% [22]. However, right heart failure symptoms before mitral valve surgery are very rare. Cardiac output is expected to increase in response to exercise after mitral valve surgery. Cardiac output cannot be increased in response to exercise in patients with ongoing TR [16]. In our study, the early mortality rate of high pulmonary pressure mitral valve surgery was 16%. Late-term mortality rate research is the subject of another study. In our clinic, it has been observed that the damage to the pulmonary bed and the resulting increase in pulmonary resistance in the preoperative period, although slightly regressed in the early and mid-postoperative periods, does not return to the desired levels. Further large-scale studies examining long-term outcomes are necessary to better understand this condition.

Conclusion

In our study, the relationships between pulmonary artery pressure and postoperative mortality and morbidity and between pulmonary artery pressure and tapse values measured before mitral valve replacement were evaluated. Blood transfusion, length of ICU stay, need for ventilation, reintubation rate, acute renal failure, and mortality rate were found to be significantly greater. In conclusion, it has been determined that patients should be referred for surgery earlier, considering other comorbid factors, before reaching high pulmonary pressures and low TAPSE values. However, large-scale studies are needed to support this finding.
