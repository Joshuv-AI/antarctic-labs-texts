Aberrant recovery of timescale-aligned amplitude balance links to symptoms and cognition in schizophrenia
Authors: Sir-Lord Wiafe, Spencer Kinsey, Najme Soleimani, Raymond O Nsafoa, Nigar Khasayeva, Amritha Harikumar, Robyn Miller, Vince D Calhoun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractSchizophrenia involves well-documented disruptions of large-scale brain coordination, but how strongly networks engage one another, and how that engagement recovers when disrupted, remains poorly understood. We identify amplitude proportionality, the maintenance and recovery of matched engagement strength between networks, as a core dimension of coordination, largely obscured by intrinsic timescale differences. We developed a timescale-aligned, time-resolved amplitude framework that isolates amplitude differences from temporal misalignments, applied to a multi-site schizophrenia cohort (160 controls, 151 patients) with test-retest validation in the Human Connectome Project (n = 827). Patients with schizophrenia showed greater amplitude imbalance, especially during fast fluctuations, along with more frequent re-entry into unbalanced states and slower recovery to stable amplitude coordination. The imbalance carried symptom-specific signatures: cerebellar amplitude abnormalities scaled with positive symptoms, while cognitive control and default-mode imbalance scaled with negative symptoms. Slower recovery was also associated with poorer reasoning and problem-solving. These findings identify aberrant amplitude proportionality and impaired recovery to magnitude balance as a core dynamic feature of schizophrenia, with circuit-resolved signatures mapping onto distinct symptom dimensions, providing individual-level metrics as candidate biomarkers and hypothesis-generating targets for future neuromodulation studies.Subject terms: Neuroscience, Schizophrenia

Introduction

Schizophrenia is marked by profound disruptions in large-scale brain coordination, closely tied to symptoms and cognitive deficits [1, 2]. Several influential frameworks have been advanced to explain these abnormalities, including theories of dysconnectivity [3, 4], excitation–inhibition imbalance [5, 6], predictive coding failures [7], and departures from criticality [8]. Each highlights important aspects of network dysfunction, but all largely overlook a fundamental question: when networks coordinate, do they engage each other with proportionate strength, and can they recover that proportionality when it breaks down?

This question matters clinically because the strength of neural engagement, not only its timing, carries functional information. In prior work, amplitude-related blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) measures have been associated with glucose utilization [9, 10], local synaptic and neuronal activity [11, 12], and trait-like stability across individuals [13], supporting the view that signal amplitude contains biologically meaningful information. Here, however, we focus specifically on the relative size of BOLD fluctuations in independent component analysis (ICA) derived network time courses and therefore interpret amplitude as a systems-level measure of proportional engagement between networks. In schizophrenia, where large-scale connectivity dynamics are known to be disrupted [14], whether networks maintain proportionate amplitude coordination and recover from imbalances has never been directly tested. This is a meaningful gap because failures of proportional engagement would not only reflect illness expression but would plausibly produce circuit-specific imbalances related to distinct symptom dimensions, and recovery deficits scaling with cognitive impairment, since tasks such as working memory, attention, and flexible reasoning rely on rapid, well-regulated coordination across networks [15].

The reason this dimension has gone unmeasured is largely methodological: brain networks operate on different internal clocks [16, 17]. Prior work has shown that unimodal regions operate on a faster timescale [16, 17], transmodal networks operate on a slower timescale [16, 17], and neurovascular coupling introduces additional lags and distortions [18]. These temporal mismatches mask amplitude relationships when timing is not accounted for first, because two networks engaged in the same operation may appear either similar or different in strength due to timing alone. Prior approaches have been either amplitude-invariant or timescale-blind, leaving this dimension unmeasured [19–25]. Both families of methods, therefore, underreport amplitude disparities when networks operate on mismatched timescales, leaving a fundamental dimension of brain coordination hidden in plain sight.

We propose timescale normalization as the systems principle that addresses this. We hypothesize that in a healthy brain, networks compensate for their different intrinsic speeds so that their BOLD amplitudes remain proportionate despite timing mismatches, producing relatively homogeneous amplitude disparities and rapid recovery after momentary disruptions [26]. In schizophrenia, multiple lines of evidence point to disruptions in timing-dependent processes, including predictive coding [7], excitation–inhibition balance [5, 6], and long-range integration [2]. These abnormalities should weaken timescale normalization specifically at the level of amplitude coordination, producing more heterogeneous disparities between networks and slower stabilization after perturbations. We further hypothesized that such failures would be most pronounced during faster BOLD fluctuations, where timing variability is greatest and coordination demands are highest [27], and that the resulting imbalances would map onto specific symptom dimensions and cognitive deficits rather than reflecting a uniform, diagnosis-level disruption.

To probe this, we used dynamic time warping (DTW), a method that corrects for temporal distortions between signals while preserving the size of their fluctuations [27]. In fMRI, DTW has demonstrated greater sensitivity to motor function, robustness to noise, superior test-retest reliability, reduced dependence on global signal regression, and improved detection of group differences, including sex effects and clinical diagnoses such as schizophrenia and autism [28–32]. Building on this foundation, we introduced a time-resolved approach that captures moment-to-moment amplitude disparities between brain networks after correcting for temporal misalignment, capturing the relative proportional balance of network activity as it evolves dynamically in time.

Applied to a multi-site schizophrenia cohort (160 controls, 151 patients) with test-retest validation in the Human Connectome Project (n = 827), the framework revealed a mechanistic picture of disrupted amplitude coordination. Patients showed widespread amplitude imbalance across network pairs, most pronounced during faster BOLD fluctuations, and entered unbalanced configurations more frequently while recovering to balance more slowly. Critically, the imbalance was circuit-resolved and symptom-specific: cerebellar amplitude disparities were associated with positive symptom severity, while imbalances within cognitive control and default-mode networks related to negative symptoms. Recovery dynamics were associated with reasoning and problem-solving ability. Together, these findings position aberrant amplitude proportionality and impaired recovery to balance as a core dynamic feature of schizophrenia, with circuit-resolved signatures mapping onto distinct symptom dimensions and cognitive deficits. Beyond schizophrenia, the framework offers a generalizable tool for linking amplitude coordination dynamics to clinical outcomes, with individual-level metrics carrying candidate biomarker and neuromodulation potential across psychiatric conditions where network coordination is implicated.

Results

Establishing DTW as a timescale-corrected measure of network amplitude disparity

Brain networks operate on different internal clocks, meaning raw signal comparisons conflate timing differences with genuine amplitude disparities. We addressed this using standard DTW and normalized dynamic time warping (nDTW), defined as the DTW distance scaled by the alignment-path length, which allows comparability across region pairs and has shown higher sensitivity to group differences [30]. Controlled simulations confirmed that nDTW behaves as required. DTW successfully aligned temporally warped signals (Fig. 1a), and both DTW and nDTW remained stable under phase perturbations while correlation declined, confirming insensitivity to timing alone (Fig. 1b). When amplitude was perturbed instead, DTW and nDTW increased with the degree of modulation while correlation remained relatively flat, demonstrating selective sensitivity to amplitude disparities that correlation misses entirely (Fig. 1b). Sensitivity and bandwidth analyses confirmed robust performance across signal parameters with γ > 1 showing strongest sensitivity to amplitude disparities (Fig. 1c,d). Together, these simulations establish nDTW as a reliable measure of inter-network amplitude disparity under realistic neuroimaging conditions. As shown in Fig. 1e, low nDTW values reflect convergence, where networks fluctuate with proportionate strength, while high values reflect divergence, where one network consistently outpaces the other. This convergence-divergence axis, capturing whether networks engage each other with matched magnitude, is the core quantity applied to fMRI data throughout this study. Figure 1e is intended only to illustrate how nDTW distinguishes convergence from divergence in representative network pairs; all group-level and whole-brain inferences are based on the full pairwise network matrices shown in subsequent analyses.

Whole-brain nDTW reveals widespread amplitude divergence with symptom-specific circuit signatures in schizophrenia

The intrinsic brain networks identified using the NeuroMark pipeline were categorized into seven functional domains: subcortical (SC), auditory (Aud), sensorimotor (SM), visual (Vis), cognitive control (CC), default mode (DM), and cerebellum (Cb). To ensure our framework captured clinically relevant amplitude dynamics, we extended analyses to broader frequency bands encompassing faster BOLD fluctuations (slow-5, slow-4, and slow-3; F1: 0.01–0.198 Hz) alongside the conventional range (F2: 0.01–0.15 Hz), and examined how varying γ affects both reliability and sensitivity to group differences.

Test-retest reliability improved with higher γ values across both frequency ranges, with γ≥1.5 demonstrating significantly higher reliability than correlation (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, FDR-corrected p<0.05; Fig. 2a). Because physiological noise correction in the fMRI data reduces within-subject variability [33], this improved reliability at higher γ values likely reflects reduced sensitivity to physiological noise. Sensitivity analyses showed that γ=1.5 and γ=1.25 achieved the greatest ability to detect group differences across both frequency bands, outperforming other γ values and correlation (Fig. 2b). Since γ=1.25 showed significantly lower test-retest reliability than the correlation benchmark in the F1 range, γ=1.5 was selected as the optimal parameter. Critically, nDTW at γ=1.5 consistently detected more group differences in the broader F1 frequency range than in F2 across all γ values, whereas correlation showed no such advantage, demonstrating that nDTW is uniquely sensitive to clinically relevant amplitude imbalances during faster BOLD fluctuations.

Patients with schizophrenia showed greater amplitude divergence from controls across multiple network pairs, with controls exhibiting significantly greater convergence across several networks (FDR-corrected, Fig. 2c). Effect sizes were substantial (Cohen’s d range 0.19–0.89, median = 0.49), underscoring the practical clinical significance of these amplitude disparities. Critically, the divergence was not uniform but carried distinct symptom-specific circuit signatures. Higher positive symptom severity was associated with more homogeneous, balanced disparity between cerebellar networks and the default mode, cognitive control, auditory, visual, and subcortical networks, a pattern suggestive of aberrant amplitude homogenization rather than simple divergence. Higher negative symptom severity was associated with greater divergence between cognitive control, default mode, and visual networks, reflecting a failure of proportional engagement among the brain’s highest-order coordination systems.

Faster BOLD fluctuations selectively amplify clinically relevant amplitude imbalances in schizophrenia

Expanding the frequency range from F2 to F1 had strikingly divergent effects in the two groups. Controls exhibited significantly lower amplitude disparity in F1 compared to F2, reflecting enhanced network convergence at faster fluctuations, whereas patients with schizophrenia showed significantly higher disparity in F1 than F2, reflecting amplified divergence (FDR-corrected; Fig. 3a). This diverging pattern, where faster fluctuations pull healthy and schizophrenia brains in opposite directions, directly increases sensitivity to group differences and suggests that faster BOLD dynamics place coordination demands that healthy brains meet but schizophrenia brains cannot.

Effect sizes were modest overall (median Cohen’s d = 0.18 for controls and 0.15 for schizophrenia), yet faster frequencies consistently amplified distinctions between groups across multiple network pairs (Fig. 3a). Upset plot analysis identified 914 significant pairs across both frequency ranges, with 196 unique to F1 and only 10 unique to F2, underscoring the substantially greater clinical sensitivity of the broader frequency range (Fig. 3b). Among pairs significant in both ranges, F1 showed higher practical relevance, with unique F1 pairs displaying Cohen’s d > 0.2.

To confirm these F1-specific effects reflect genuine amplitude processes rather than noise, surrogate analyses using 1,000 phase-randomized datasets within the 0.15–0.198 Hz range confirmed that several pairs remained significant, establishing that faster fluctuation effects are statistically robust and reflect non-stationary amplitude processes (Fig. 3c). Critically, symptom associations were exclusive to F1 (Fig. 3d). Positive PANSS scores correlated with amplitude disparities linking the cerebellum to subcortical, default mode, cognitive control, auditory, and visual networks, while negative PANSS scores were primarily associated with cognitive control and default mode connections. These associations were absent in F2, confirming that faster BOLD fluctuations are not merely more sensitive statistically but are specifically where clinically relevant symptom signal resides.

Brain amplitude coordination dynamics unfold through three recurring states with an obligatory intermediary

Time-resolved nDTW revealed that amplitude coordination dynamics across all participants organized into three recurring states: a convergent state (state 1), in which networks fluctuate with proportionate strength; a divergent state (state 3), in which networks consistently outpace one another in magnitude; and a mixed state (state 2), showing neither strong convergence nor divergence (Fig. 4a). Three clusters were identified as optimal by elbow plot analysis of within-cluster variance (Fig. 4c), and all subsequent analyses used this three-state solution.

Centroids were normalized so positive values represent convergence and negative values represent divergence. Convergent and divergent states show the top 5% of the strongest connections, while the mixed state shows the top 2.5% most convergent and 2.5% most divergent connections. c Elbow plot of within-sum of squares (WSS). The first inflection point in the derivative of WSS indicated three optimal clusters. d State transition probabilities between the three states. e Stationary distribution of the Markov chain, showing that long-run dynamics are shaped primarily by the mixed and convergent states. f Mixing time of the dynamics, with convergence rate further characterized by the spectral gap and entropy rate. g Perturbation of state transitions and effects on the spectral gap, showing changes in convergence rate. h Perturbation of state transitions and effects on the entropy rate.

The neuroanatomical profile of each state was distinct (Fig. 4b). The convergent state was dominated by sensory networks, with the most proportionate pairs (top 5%) drawn predominantly from visual, sensorimotor, and auditory networks. The divergent state was characterized by cerebellar network pairs, with the cerebellum featuring prominently among the most disproportionate pairs (top 5%). The mixed state combined both dynamics, with the top 2.5% most convergent and most divergent pairs coexisting, reflecting a flexible intermediate configuration.

A Markov chain model of state transitions revealed a striking organizational feature: no direct transitions occurred between the convergent and divergent states (Fig. 4d). All shifts between these states must pass through the mixed state, establishing it as an obligatory intermediary in amplitude coordination dynamics. Self-retention probabilities were highest for the mixed state (0.9021), followed by the convergent state (0.8485) and the divergent state (0.8133), reflecting the relative stability of each configuration. The stationary distribution confirmed the dominance of the mixed state (57.36%), with the convergent state accounting for 37.21% and the divergent state for only 5.42% of dynamics (Fig. 4e), highlighting that the brain spends most of its time in intermediate or proportionate coordination.

Recovery dynamics were quantified using two complementary metrics (Fig. 4f). The entropy rate was 36.6%, indicating a system biased toward order rather than randomness, with meaningful structure in how states succeed one another. The spectral gap was 0.1851, corresponding to a mixing time of 36 timesteps (72 seconds, TR = 2 seconds), reflecting how long the system takes to return to its stationary distribution after perturbation. This mixing time was derived from the farthest point in the Markov chain state space, specifically the fully divergent configuration [0, 0, 1], providing a conservative estimate of recovery duration under worst-case conditions (Fig. 4e).

Exploratory perturbation analyses examined how transition probabilities shape entropy and recovery rate (Fig. 4g-h). Increasing self-retention in any state slowed both convergence rate and entropy, while adding transitions from the mixed state to other states enhanced both. Notably, increasing transition probability from the mixed to the divergent state extended trajectories away from the stationary distribution, slowing return to stability. These analyses reveal that higher entropy does not universally accelerate recovery; its effect depends critically on which specific transitions are altered, underscoring the importance of transition structure rather than overall randomness in governing coordination dynamics.

Schizophrenia is marked by prolonged off-balance states and impaired recovery to amplitude coordination

Patients with schizophrenia spent significantly more time in mixed and divergent configurations, while controls predominantly occupied the convergent state, revealing a fundamental difference in how the two groups navigate amplitude coordination dynamics. Patients showed longer mean dwell times in both the mixed and divergent states, while controls spent more time in the convergent state (Fig. 5a). Occupancy rates mirrored this pattern, with patients showing higher mixed and divergent occupancy and controls showing greater convergent occupancy (Fig. 5b).

Transition probability analyses revealed the mechanistic basis of this imbalance (Fig. 5c). Controls were more likely to transition from the mixed state toward convergence, reflecting a regulatory pull toward proportionate network engagement. Patients, by contrast, more often transitioned from convergent to mixed and from mixed to divergent states, and showed higher self-retention in both mixed and divergent configurations, reflecting a system that trends away from balance rather than toward it. The stationary distribution confirmed these tendencies at the population level, with schizophrenia dynamics more strongly shaped by mixed and divergent states and controls more strongly shaped by the convergent state (Fig. 5d).

Critically, controls exhibited a significantly higher spectral gap, reflecting a faster rate of return to the stationary distribution after perturbation (Fig. 5e). Starting from the fully divergent configuration and using a tolerance of 10−3, controls had a shorter mixing time, indicating quicker recovery to a stable amplitude-balanced configuration. Strikingly, entropy rates did not differ significantly between groups, establishing that the recovery advantage in controls is not explained by overall system randomness but by transition structure specifically. This dissociation between entropy and spectral gap is mechanistically important: schizophrenia does not simply produce noisier dynamics, it produces dynamics whose transition architecture resists return to balance.

Amplitude coordination state dynamics relate to cognition, with reorganized relationships in schizophrenia

Amplitude coordination dynamics predicted cognitive performance in both groups, but the relationship between brain states and cognition was fundamentally reorganized in schizophrenia relative to controls, revealing that the same dynamic architecture serves different cognitive functions depending on illness status (Fig. 5h).

In controls, working memory was positively associated with transitions from convergent to mixed states and negatively with convergent self-retention and mixed-to-divergent transitions, suggesting that flexible entry into the mixed state supports memory performance. Verbal learning showed the opposite pattern, with positive associations with transitions from mixed to convergent states and negative associations with convergent-to-mixed transitions, indicating that return to and maintenance of the convergent state facilitates structured memory encoding. Reasoning and problem-solving were positively associated with the spectral gap, directly linking faster recovery to amplitude balance with higher cognitive ability and connecting individual differences in recovery dynamics to real-world cognitive outcomes.

In schizophrenia, the cognitive roles of these states were reorganized. Reasoning and problem-solving showed a reversed pattern relative to controls: negative associations with mixed-to-convergent transitions but positive associations with self-retention in the mixed state, suggesting that patients rely on prolonged intermediate coordination rather than convergent stability for higher-order cognition. Attention and vigilance were positively associated with transitions from divergent to mixed states and negatively with self-retention in the divergent state, suggesting that escaping persistent divergence is necessary for sustained attentional function in patients. Together, these findings reveal that schizophrenia does not simply reduce cognitive performance but reorganizes the dynamic coordination patterns that support it, with patients depending on states that healthy brains use only transiently and suffering cognitively when trapped in fully divergent configurations.

Discussion

Large-scale brain coordination in schizophrenia has been studied extensively through the lens of connectivity and synchrony, yet a fundamental dimension of that coordination has remained unmeasured: whether networks engage one another with proportionate signal strength, and whether they recover that proportionality when it is disrupted. The challenge has been methodological, as networks operate on different intrinsic timescales, which conflates timing differences with genuine amplitude disparities when raw signals are compared. By correcting for these mismatches and tracking amplitude coordination dynamically, the present study provides evidence that network engagement strength and its recovery can be quantified in the healthy brain and how this coordination is systematically disrupted in schizophrenia. These findings extend existing accounts of dysconnectivity [3, 4], excitation-inhibition imbalance [5, 6], and predictive coding failure [7] by opening a previously inaccessible dimension of coordination breakdown, one grounded in the relative strength of network engagement rather than its timing alone.

A central contribution of this work was the demonstration that clinically relevant amplitude imbalances in schizophrenia emerge specifically when faster BOLD fluctuations are included in the analysis. Conventional resting-state fMRI studies typically restrict analyses to the 0.01–0.15 Hz range, and higher-frequency BOLD signals have often been dismissed as a physiological artifact [34]. Yet a growing body of work has identified frequencies above this range as carrying meaningful neural variability [35–37], and our findings advance this literature by showing that these faster fluctuations are not merely interpretable but specifically where amplitude imbalances in schizophrenia become detectable. Group differences emerged exclusively when the broader 0.01–0.198 (F1) range was included, and a phase-randomized null model confirmed that these effects reflect non-stationary amplitude processes rather than noise. Notably, controls and patients diverged in opposite directions at faster fluctuations, with controls showing enhanced convergence and patients showing amplified divergence, suggesting that faster BOLD dynamics impose coordination demands that healthy brains meet but schizophrenia brains cannot. This pattern is consistent with the broader literature on schizophrenia as a disorder of rapid coordination processes [2, 5, 6], and it carries practical implications for biomarker development: amplitude imbalances visible only in faster frequency ranges may have been systematically missed in prior studies that restricted analyses to conventional bands.

Within this broader frequency range, static analyses of timescale-corrected amplitude disparities revealed widespread divergence between schizophrenia and control brains across multiple network pairs, with effect sizes ranging from small to large (Cohen’s d 0.19–0.89, median 0.49) and underscoring the practical clinical magnitude of these disparities. This aligns with prior reports of altered physiological and metabolic processes in schizophrenia [38, 39], and extends them by showing that the disruption manifests as a failure of proportional engagement between networks rather than as a uniform reduction or increase in activity. Critically, this divergence was not uniform but organized along symptom-specific circuit lines. Positive symptom severity was associated with altered cerebellar interactions, particularly between cerebellar networks and auditory, default mode, cognitive control, subcortical, and visual systems. The cerebellum is a recognized predictive hub [40] and central to the cognitive dysmetria framework, which attributes schizophrenia in part to miscalibrated coordination among prefrontal, thalamic, and cerebellar regions [41]. Our findings extend this framework by showing that cerebellar amplitude imbalance scales quantitatively with positive symptom severity at the individual level. The direction of this association, with lower divergence corresponding to more severe symptoms, suggests a pattern of aberrant amplitude homogenization rather than simple divergence, consistent with maladaptive or compensatory cerebellar engagement in psychosis [42].

Negative symptom severity, by contrast, was associated with greater amplitude imbalance among cognitive control and default mode networks, consistent with evidence that motivational, affective, and goal-directed deficits in schizophrenia arise from dysfunction within higher-order processing systems [43]. Prior work has connected impaired physiology and metabolism to negative symptoms [44, 45], and recent evidence suggests that prefrontal stimulation can reduce negative symptom severity through metabolic modulation [46]. Our findings complement this literature by providing a timing-invariant, amplitude-based marker that scales with symptom dimension, offering a quantitative readout of the proportional engagement failures within higher-order systems that relates to negative symptom expression. Together with the cerebellar findings, these circuit-specific signatures indicate that amplitude proportionality does not break down uniformly in schizophrenia but along pathways that map onto its distinct clinical dimensions, providing a quantitative bridge between network-level coordination dysfunction and the symptom dimensions clinicians routinely assess.

Beyond static disparities, time-resolved analyses revealed that amplitude coordination dynamics organize into three recurring states: a convergent state characterized by lower amplitude disparity, a divergent state characterized by higher disparity, and a mixed state representing intermediate dynamics. A striking organizational feature emerged from the Markov chain analysis: no direct transitions occurred between the convergent and divergent states, with all shifts between these poles passing through the mixed state. This establishes the mixed state as an obligatory intermediary in amplitude coordination dynamics, a structural constraint with implications for how the brain navigates between coordination extremes. The stationary distribution further showed that the mixed state dominated system dynamics, accounting for 57% of activity, with the convergent state at 37% and the divergent state at only 5%. This distribution suggests that the healthy brain spends the vast majority of its time in either intermediate or proportionate coordination, only briefly visiting the divergent regime, with the mixed state functioning as the regulatory hub through which all reconfigurations must pass.

These dynamic features resonate with brain criticality theory, which posits that the brain operates near a critical point between order and disorder to maintain both stability and flexibility [47]. The dominance of the mixed state, combined with a moderate entropy rate of 36.6%, supports the idea that brain coordination dynamics balance these competing demands, with a slight bias toward order while remaining within the zone of criticality [48]. Critical entropy has been proposed as an indicator of brain criticality [49], and the values observed here are consistent with a system that preserves the adaptability necessary for flexible cognition. The obligatory passage through the mixed state may itself be a structural expression of this critical regime, providing a regulated route between coordination extremes rather than allowing abrupt jumps that would destabilize the system. We interpret these connections cautiously as consistent with criticality theory rather than as a direct test of it, but the framework offers a principled basis for understanding why proportional engagement and its smooth recovery may be fundamental to healthy brain function and why their disruption in schizophrenia carries cognitive and clinical consequences.

A central dynamic finding was that schizophrenia involves a specific impairment in the brain’s capacity to recover proportional engagement after departures from balance. The spectral gap, which quantifies how quickly the system returns to its stationary distribution after perturbation, was significantly reduced in patients, with longer mixing times indicating slower stabilization to a coordinated configuration. Critically, this recovery deficit was not accompanied by differences in entropy rate, establishing that the impairment does not reflect overall noisier dynamics in schizophrenia but a specific failure in the transition architecture that supports return to balance. State-space analysis revealed that trajectories from the divergent state required the longest path to reach stability, and patients showed an excess of mixed-to-divergent transitions alongside elevated self-retention in both mixed and divergent configurations, together explaining why their dynamics resist return to convergence. This dissociation between entropy and spectral gap is mechanistically important: schizophrenia does not simply produce noisier coordination dynamics, it produces dynamics whose transition architecture resists return to balance. The pattern is consistent with an impaired homeostatic regulation of network coordination, in which the regulatory pull back toward proportionate engagement is weakened, leaving patients trapped in unbalanced configurations longer and recovering less efficiently. This recovery deficit also highlights that high entropy alone does not guarantee effective information integration or cognitive performance [50].

The dynamic architecture revealed here was not merely associated with diagnosis but predicted cognitive performance differently in healthy and schizophrenic brains, indicating that the same coordination states serve fundamentally different cognitive functions depending on illness status. In controls, working memory was supported by transitions from convergence into the mixed state, consistent with the view that flexible amplitude balance enables higher-order cognitive functions [51]. Verbal learning, by contrast, depended on the opposite pattern, with positive associations to mixed-to-convergent transitions and negative associations to extended retention in the mixed state, indicating that tasks requiring structured encoding rely on the stability of the convergent regime rather than the flexibility of the mixed one [52]. Reasoning and problem-solving were positively associated with the spectral gap, directly linking faster recovery to amplitude balance with higher cognitive ability and connecting individual differences in recovery dynamics to real-world cognitive outcomes. In schizophrenia, these relationships were reorganized. Reasoning and problem-solving showed a reversed pattern relative to controls, depending on prolonged retention in the mixed state and negatively related to mixed-to-convergent transitions, suggesting that patients rely on extended intermediate coordination rather than convergent stability for higher-order cognition. Attention and vigilance were positively associated with transitions from divergent to mixed states and negatively with self-retention in the divergent state, indicating that escaping persistent divergence is necessary for sustained attentional function in patients. Together, these findings reveal that schizophrenia does not simply reduce cognitive performance but reorganizes the dynamic coordination patterns that support it, with patients depending on states that healthy brains use only transiently and suffering cognitively when trapped in fully divergent configurations.

The framework presented here carries several translational implications for psychiatric research and clinical practice. First, the individual-level metrics derived from amplitude coordination dynamics, particularly recovery measures such as the spectral gap and circuit-specific imbalance scores, offer candidate biomarkers for stratifying patients along symptom dimensions rather than relying on categorical diagnosis alone. The cerebellar amplitude signature for positive symptoms and the cognitive control and default mode signature for negative symptoms provide quantitative individual-level markers that could complement existing clinical assessments and support symptom-dimension-based subtyping in future research. Second, the circuit-specific nature of these signatures nominates targeted hypotheses for neuromodulation: cerebellar circuits for patients with predominantly positive symptom presentations and prefrontal-default mode circuits for those with predominantly negative symptom presentations, aligning with the growing personalized neuromodulation literature in psychiatry. Third, the framework opens a path toward monitoring brain dynamics through approaches that directly capture regional signal strength, such as photobiomodulation, which modulates cerebral blood flow and could, in principle, be guided by amplitude coordination measures (Supplementary Material). We frame this last possibility cautiously, as a clean and biologically plausible avenue for modulating amplitude coordination rather than as a validated therapeutic claim. More broadly, by establishing amplitude proportionality and its recovery as a measurable, individual-level dimension of brain coordination, this framework offers a generalizable tool for linking dynamic brain function to clinical outcomes across psychiatric conditions where coordination breakdown is implicated.

Although this framework provides a robust approach for quantifying timescale-corrected amplitude coordination dynamics, several considerations should be noted. First, BOLD amplitude is inherently a relative measure influenced by preprocessing choices, ICA scaling, and spatial heterogeneity in signal intensity; our metric therefore reflects relative differences in signal strength rather than absolute physiological quantities, and caution is warranted when linking these disparities to underlying neurobiology. Second, although our sample sizes (HCP: 827; fBIRN: 311) support stable estimation of dynamic metrics, studies with smaller cohorts may need to place greater emphasis on the static amplitude-disparity measure, which is less sensitive to sample size than transition-based quantities such as spectral gap or entropy rate. These considerations notwithstanding, the framework is methodologically grounded through simulations and test-retest analyses and offers a foundation on which future work can build.

In summary, our study establishes aberrant amplitude proportionality and impaired recovery to balance as a core, previously unmeasured dimension of schizophrenia, with circuit-specific symptom signatures and cognitive consequences that map onto the disorder’s distinct clinical dimensions. By correcting for the brain’s varying intrinsic timescales, our framework reveals a putative regulatory failure in how networks engage one another with proportionate strength and how they return to coordinated function after departures from balance. Beyond schizophrenia, this approach offers a generalizable lens for examining how dynamic coordination breakdown contributes to cognitive and clinical outcomes across psychiatric and neurological conditions, grounded in a measurable principle of brain function previously underexplored.

Dynamic time warping as a measure of amplitude disparity

Dynamic time warping is a widely used signal processing algorithm that aligns signals with nonlinear temporal deformations by applying adaptive “elastic” transformations to achieve a meaningful similarity measure [53, 54]. When temporal deformations complicate phase or timing relationships, it becomes logical to isolate these aspects and concentrate solely on the amplitude of the signals. DTW tackles this by resolving temporal dissimilarities, optimally aligning signal pairs to minimize a distance metric and bring them to a shared temporal scale, leaving amplitude as the primary source of disparity.

The algorithm computes a distance cost matrix that captures the cumulative cost of aligning pairwise elements of the sequences and identifies the optimal warping function to adjust their time axes, minimizing a distance metric. To enhance computational efficiency and ensure meaningful alignments, particularly when guided by domain knowledge, a window size constraint is applied to limit the maximum allowable warping between indices in the time series [54].

Allowable window size for fMRI application

The two main challenges of DTW are its computational cost, with a time complexity of O(M×N), and the issue of singularities [54], where a single time point in one signal is unnaturally matched to a large portion of the other signal. Both challenges are mitigated by constraining the alignment to a window size that limits the maximum window of allowable alignment [56]. Given the focus on temporal deformations such as leads/lags and nonlinear dynamics like stretching and shrinking, selecting an appropriate window size to capture relevant temporal deformations in fMRI data is crucial. In our previous study using the proposed warp elasticity approach, we demonstrated that stretching and shrinking dynamics could introduce anti-correlations [32]. Considering that anti-correlations may also arise from lead/lag relationships, it is intuitive to choose a window size that spans the length of the signal required to produce maximum anti-correlations (−1). This approach has also been validated as optimal in prior fMRI studies [29]. The longest wavelength yielding an anti-correlation of −1 corresponds to the lowest frequency component (0.01Hz), which equates to a 100-second window. Achieving an anti-correlation of −1, requires a time shift of half the wavelength (50 seconds). For the DTW algorithm to capture relevant temporal deformations in fMRI data, two 50-second windows are sufficient to account for deformations in both directions, resulting in a total window length of 100 seconds [29], corresponding to the low-cutoff frequency of 0.01Hz. However, since the filter design for the data, commonly Butterworth, is typically nonideal, we instead recommend selecting a window size based on the spectral characteristics of the activity signal, determined by the −3db point of the low-frequency cutoff of the fMRI signal [21, 31, 32, 57]. Resting-state fMRI data

The first dataset used is from the Function Biomedical Informatics Research Network (fBIRN) study, including resting fMRI data collected from schizophrenia patients and controls [59]. Scans were collected at a repetition time (TR) of 2 seconds. The study led to a total of 160 controls with an average age of 37.04 ± 10.86 years, ranging from 19 to 59 years. Among these, 45 were female, and 115 were male. Additionally, there were 151 patients diagnosed with schizophrenia, with an average age of 38.77 ± 11.63 years, ranging from 18 to 62 years. In this group, 36 were female, and 115 were male. The controls and patients with schizophrenia were meticulously matched in terms of age, gender distribution, and mean framewise displacement during scans (age: p = 0.18; gender: p = 0.39; mean framewise displacement: p = 0.97). Patients were in a clinically stable condition during the time of their scans. The fBIRN dataset is used in all the clinical analyses performed in this study.

The second dataset used in our study is the resting-state fMRI dataset collected from 827 subjects via the Human Connectome Project (HCP) database [60, 61]. We utilized both session scans acquired using a Siemens Skyra 3 T scanner with a multiband accelerated, gradient-echo echo-planar imaging (EPI) sequence. The scanning parameters were set to a TR of 0.72 s, 72 slices, an echo time (TE) of 58 ms, and a flip angle of 90°. A voxel size of 2 × 2 × 2 mm was used to acquire 1200 time points of fMRI data for each subject. The HCP dataset was used for our test-retest analysis.

Resting-state fMRI pre-processing

fMRI data requires extensive preprocessing to correct for various sources of noise and artifacts before analysis. The preprocessing steps commonly applied in fMRI studies include slice timing correction, realignment, spatial normalization, and spatial smoothing [62]. Following typical preprocessing [63], we implemented the NeuroMark pipeline, a fully automated spatially constrained ICA on the preprocessed fMRI data [64]. Using the neuromark_fMRI_1.0 template (available in GIFT at http://trendscenter.org/software/gift or http://trendscenter.org/data), we generated 53 intrinsic connectivity networks (ICNs) for each subject. These ICNs are grouped into brain functional domains, including subcortical, auditory, sensorimotor, visual, cognitive control, default mode, and cerebellum.

Resting-state fMRI post-processing

To enhance the quality of these ICNs, we implemented detrending and despiking methods to eliminate drifts, sudden fluctuations, and significant artifacts that may have persisted after the initial preprocessing stages. The time series of the ICNs were bandpass filtered within two frequency ranges: F1 (0.01-0.198 Hz) and F2(0.01-0.15 Hz). An infinite impulse response filter was designed using the butter function in MATLAB and applied via the filtfilt function to ensure zero phase shifts and preserve phase information. The optimal filter order was estimated using the Buttord function in MATLAB, which returns the lowest order of the Butterworth filter with no more than 3 dB ripple in the passband and at least 30 dB of attenuation in the stopband. Finally, we performed z-scoring on the ICNs. The MATLAB version used was MATLAB R2022a for all steps of the analysis.

Test-retest reliability

Typically, test-retest reliability is evaluated using the intra-class correlation coefficient (ICC). However, the ICC relies on a linear parametric ANOVA model that assumes separability and additivity [65]. This makes ICC unsuitable for DTW applications, as DTW generates a distance metric based on nonlinear warping, where pairwise distances are interdependent [29, 30] and thus lack decomposable variance components. We utilize the HCP dataset, which includes two sessions and a large sample size of 827 subjects, facilitating robust test-retest analysis.

Group difference and clinical association analyses

We employed generalized linear models for both group difference analyses and associations with clinical scores. All GLM analyses controlled for sex, age, site, and mean frame displacement. For clinical association analyses conducted within the schizophrenia group, the chlorpromazine-equivalent (CPZ) antipsychotic dose was included as a covariate. P-values were adjusted using the false discovery rate method, with significance set at a threshold of 0.05. For group difference analyses and computerized multiphasic interactive neurocognitive system scores, a normal distribution was assumed, whereas a Poisson distribution was applied for PANSS score associations due to the positive integer and skewed nature of the fBIRN dataset’s PANSS scores. Effect sizes were calculated using Cohen’s d.

Group difference sensitivity test

To evaluate the sensitivity of various gamma values of nDTW and correlation in distinguishing between controls and individuals with schizophrenia, we conducted McNemar’s sensitivity tests comparing each gamma value and correlation against each other. Following the GLM group difference analyses, we counted the number of significant brain network pairs (FDR-corrected p < 0.05) for each gamma value (nDTW) and for correlation. We then compared the unique and shared significant pairs between each gamma and correlation using McNemar’s test, with p-values adjusted via FDR correction across all comparisons.

These sensitivity tests were performed separately for each frequency range, F1 and F2. Additionally, we compared F1 and F2 across all gamma values and correlation to assess how transitioning from the lower frequency range F2 to the broader F1 range affects group difference sensitivity.

Validation of high fMRI frequency results

High-frequency fMRI signals are vulnerable to various sources of noise and artifacts, including physiological factors like respiratory and cardiac activity, as well as non-neuronal influences such as head motion [66]. Comprehensive preprocessing steps followed by our use of ICA help mitigate these concerns. ICA effectively separates independent components, allowing us to identify and remove artifact-related components from the data. Additionally, we included head motion parameters as covariates in our GLM analyses to ensure that detected group differences in high-frequency signals are not attributable to head motion effects.

We conducted several additional analyses to further validate our findings within the F1 frequency range.

Statistical effects on groups with the inclusion of fast BOLD fluctuations

We aimed to evaluate the impact of transitioning from the F2 to the broader F1 frequency range on both control and schizophrenia groups. Since F1 and F2 comprise the same dataset differentiated only by frequency range, a paired sample t-test was appropriate for this analysis. To validate the use of the paired t-test, we first assessed the normality of nDTW scores for each group between F1 and F2 using the Shapiro-Wilk test, ensuring that t-values were close to 1.

Subsequently, we conducted paired t-tests separately for the control and schizophrenia groups to examine the effects of transitioning from F2 to F1, with p-values adjusted using the FDR correction. Effect sizes for these comparisons were calculated using Cohen’s d. Additionally, to highlight the differential impact of transitioning from F2 to F1 between groups, we computed the differences in Cohen’s d effect sizes between controls and schizophrenia. This comparison aimed to determine whether the transition to the broader F1 frequency range enhances or diminishes group-specific distinctions.

Furthermore, we identified statistical group differences between schizophrenia and controls that were present in F1 but not in F2 by analyzing the shared and unique significant brain network pairs from our GLM analysis (see Section 4.8). We also highlighted their corresponding effect sizes, demonstrating that significant group differences are more pronounced in the F1 frequency range.

Amplitude imbalance dynamics

Brain state estimation

In time-resolved fMRI studies, k-means clustering is frequently utilized to identify recurring brain states within the temporal dynamics of the data [68]. The DTW algorithm inherently anchors the first and last time points during alignment, potentially introducing bias by disproportionately influencing brain states at the temporal extremes. To mitigate this bias, we excluded the first and last 5 time points from the time-resolved DTW metrics prior to clustering. This exclusion reduces alignment-induced artifacts, enabling the clustering algorithm to more accurately capture intrinsic transitions between brain states.

We concatenated the time-resolved DTW metrics from all subjects in the fBIRN dataset into a single feature matrix, where each time point from every subject serves as an individual sample and each brain network pair constitutes a distinct feature. We implemented the k-means algorithm with cluster numbers ranging from 1 to 10, setting the maximum number of iterations to 10,000 and using 20 random initializations to ensure robust convergence. The city-block (Manhattan) distance metric was selected for its demonstrated robustness in handling high-dimensional data [69]. To determine the optimal number of clusters, we generated an elbow plot based on the within-cluster sum of squares (WSS). The optimal cluster number was identified as 3, corresponding to the first significant inflection point in the WSS curve’s first derivative.

Markov chain & stationary distribution

We quantified the transitions between brain states by counting the number of times each subject moved from one state to another and normalizing these counts across each state to construct individual transition probability matrices. This represents the probability of a subject transitioning from one state to another and further provides insights into the Markov chain of state dynamics. To gain a comprehensive understanding of the general transition patterns independent of specific groups, such as patients and controls, we aggregate the probability transitions across all subjects in the fBIRN dataset.

Furthermore, the stationary distribution of the Markov chain represents the long-term behavior of the system, indicating the proportion of time the system spends in each state once it has converged to equilibrium [70]. This distribution serves as an indication of a “stable point” when analyzing systems from the perspective of their Markov chains rather than traditional state-space representations. For the stationary distribution to be unique and ensure convergence from any initial state, the Markov chain must be ergodic [71]. A Markov chain is considered ergodic if it is irreducible (it is possible to get from any state to any other state, aperiodic (the system does not cycle in a fixed pattern), and positive recurrent (the expected return time to each state is finite).

Impact of transition probabilities on Markov chain convergence and entropy rate

To evaluate how specific transitions within the Markov chain influence the convergence and entropy rates of the dynamics, we systematically perturbed the aggregated transition matrix. This process involved incrementally increasing the probability of individual transition cells and subsequently renormalizing the corresponding rows to ensure that each row’s probabilities sum to one. Consequently, the targeted transition’s probability increased while the probabilities of other transitions within the same row decreased.

For each perturbed transition, we recalculated both the spectral gap and the entropy rate to determine their percentage relative changes compared to the original values. This perturbation procedure was applied to every transition cell across 10,000 random samples, with perturbation magnitudes ranging from 10−1 to 10−3 on a logarithmic scale to ensure uniform sampling. To maintain comparability between transitions, each sample employed a single perturbation magnitude uniformly across all transition cells.

Time-resolved DTW group difference: schizophrenia vs controls

To assess the clinical relevance of the time-resolved DTW measure, we conducted group difference analyses on several derived metrics: state transitions, stationary distribution, spectral gap, mixing time, and entropy rate (see Section 3.11). Unlike the aggregated approach in Section 3.11, these metrics were calculated individually for each subject. To ensure the uniqueness of these results, we excluded subjects with non-ergodic state transitions.

Additionally, we included mean dwell time and occupancy rate in our group comparisons. Mean dwell time quantifies the average duration a subject remains in a particular state once entered [72], reflecting the persistence of states within the Markov chain. Occupancy rate represents the percentage of time a subject spends in each state [72], indicating the relative prevalence of states during the observation period.

Using these subject-specific metrics, we distinguished schizophrenia patients from control subjects and performed group difference analyses employing a GLM, as described in Section 3.8. FDR correction is applied to each metric across all three identified states.

CMINDS score association
