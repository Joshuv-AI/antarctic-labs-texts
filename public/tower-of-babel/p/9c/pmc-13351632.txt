Social Determinants of Health Are Associated With Increased Risk of Preoperative Methicillin-Resistant Staphylococcus aureus Colonization in Patients Undergoing Total Joint Arthroplasty
Authors: Muhammad Hamza Ilyas, Akin Adio, Diego Gonzalez-Morgado, Joseph A Abboud, Hafiz F Kassam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundSocial determinants of health (SDOH) are associated with disparities in health outcomes and increased vulnerability to infectious diseases. This study evaluated the association between SDOH and methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) colonization in patients undergoing primary total hip arthroplasty (THA), total knee arthroplasty (TKA), or total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA).MethodsA retrospective cohort study was conducted using the United States TriNetX Research Network. Adults aged 18 years or older who underwent primary THA, TKA, or TSA were identified. Propensity score matching was performed using demographic variables and comorbidities. MRSA colonization rates were compared using risk ratios (RRs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), and subanalyses were conducted by joint type and age group. Cox regression evaluated independent risk predictors.ResultsSDOH were associated with a significantly higher risk of preoperative MRSA colonization across all joint types, including THA (2.2% vs 0.8%; RR, 2.74; 95% CI, 1.85–4.04), TKA (2.4% vs 1.1%; RR, 2.16; 95% CI, 1.60–2.92), and TSA (3.0% vs 1.6%; RR, 1.95; 95% CI, 1.16–3.29) (all P ≤ .01). In Cox proportional hazards models, increased hazard of preoperative MRSA colonization was observed with male sex, obesity, and prior surgical history (hazard ratio range, 1.14–2.84). Additional comorbidities, including alcohol abuse, psychoactive substance abuse, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease, and immune disorders, were also associated with increased hazard over time.ConclusionsSocial disadvantage was associated with an increased risk of MRSA colonization among patients undergoing primary total joint arthroplasties. These findings highlight the need for increased SDOH documentation and incorporation into preoperative risk stratification and infection prevention strategies.Level of EvidenceIIIKeywords: Social determinants of health, MRSA colonization, Total joint arthroplasty

Introduction

Periprosthetic joint infection (PJI) remains one of the most serious complications with substantial clinical and economic consequences [1,2]. Preoperative methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus (MRSA) colonization is a well-established risk factor for surgical site infections (SSIs) and PJI [3]. PJI caused by resistant organisms like MRSA incurs higher costs due to the need for prolonged antibiotic therapies and the increased complexity of revision surgeries [4,5].

Given the rising global demand for total joint arthroplasties (TJAs) [6], identifying MRSA colonization and preventing PJI is a priority for healthcare systems [4], underscoring the necessity of implementing cost-effective preoperative screening and decolonization strategies [7]. While MRSA screening protocols and decolonization have proven effective in reducing SSI rates [7], the success and cost-effectiveness of these programs rely on accurately identifying patient populations at high risk for MRSA colonization [8]. This requires a comprehensive understanding of the social determinants of health (SDOH) including demographic, clinical, and social risk factors that contribute to MRSA colonization prevalence [9].

SDOH are the social conditions in which individuals are born, live, and work, encompassing factors such as environment, geographic location, neighborhood characteristics, access to healthcare, nutrition, and socioeconomic status[10]. The U.S. Department of Health categorizes SDOH into 5 domains: economic stability, education, healthcare access and quality, social and community context, and neighborhood and built environment. These factors collectively influence health, well-being and clinical outcomes [10,11]. It is widely recognized that social deprivation is linked to health disparities, resulting in a higher incidence of chronic diseases, comorbidities, and generally poorer health [12,13]. This suggests that social deprivation may be associated with increased susceptibility to MRSA colonization. Despite the relationship between SDOH and health disparities [14,15], literature on the relationship between SDOH and the prevalence of preoperative MRSA colonization in TJA patients remains limited.

Therefore, the objective of this study was to investigate the association between SDOH and preoperative MRSA colonization. This study addressed the following questions: First, is SDOH independently associated with an increased risk of preoperative MRSA colonization in patients undergoing primary total hip, knee, or shoulder arthroplasty? Second, which demographic and clinical variables are associated with increased hazard of preoperative MRSA colonization over time in this population? These aims sought to clarify the role of SDOH and patient-related risk factors in identifying individuals at increased risk for MRSA colonization prior to primary TJAs.

Materials and methods

Data source

Data for this study were obtained from the TriNetX US Research Network, a federated electronic health record platform that includes more than 105 million patients across 70 participating healthcare organizations. The network provides access to deidentified, longitudinal clinical data including diagnoses, procedures, medications, and laboratory results [16]. All contributing organizations map data into a common model through syntactic and semantic harmonization performed by TriNetX. Each patient is assigned a unique encrypted identifier, allowing clinical encounters to be tracked over time while preserving anonymity. Because all data are fully deidentified and aggregated, this study was exempt from institutional review board oversight and written informed consent was not required.

Cohort selection

The TriNetX US Research Network was queried to identify adults who underwent primary TJAs between 2005 and 2024. SDOH were identified using International Classification of Diseases (ICD)-10 Z-diagnostic codes (Supplementary Table 1). These codes document a patient’s social and economic circumstances, including education barriers, occupational strain, housing instability, financial hardship, and psychosocial challenges. The American Hospital Association Coding Clinic issued guidance in February 2018 that permits any licensed clinician involved in a patient’s care to document these social determinants using these codes [17]. This guidance was also approved by the ICD-10-Clinical Modification (CM) Cooperating Parties and incorporated into the ICD-10-CM Official Guidelines for Coding and Reporting, which supports the validity of using them [18]. Preoperative MRSA colonization was identified using documented diagnosis codes, including ICD-10 code Z22.322. This code has been used in prior TriNetX arthroplasty studies and has demonstrated consistent associations with postoperative outcomes, supporting their validity for identifying MRSA colonization [19]. Cohorts were constructed using sequential query groups. First, patients with documented Z55–Z65 codes were assigned to the SDOH cohort. Second, within each deprivation cohort, patients were required to have a documented primary arthroplasty procedure, identified using Current Procedural Terminology codes for primary total hip arthroplasty (THA), total knee arthroplasty (TKA), or total shoulder arthroplasty (TSA) (Supplementary Table 1). Finally, MRSA diagnoses documented after the index arthroplasty were excluded to ensure that only preoperative MRSA status was analyzed. The primary analysis compared patients with documented SDOH to those without documentation. Cox proportional hazards regression was then performed within each arthroplasty cohort to identify independent demographic and clinical predictors of preoperative MRSA colonization over time.

Statistical analysis

Propensity scores were generated for patients with and without documented SDOH using logistic regression incorporating demographic variables and comorbidities listed in Table 1, Table 2, Table 3. Matching was performed in a 1:1 ratio using greedy nearest-neighbor matching with a caliper of 0.1 pooled standard deviations. After matching, baseline characteristics were reevaluated to confirm adequate covariate balance prior to all outcome comparisons. Preoperative MRSA colonization rates were compared using risk ratios with 95 percent confidence intervals. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used depending on cell size thresholds, and statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P value less than 0.05.

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis

Separate joint-specific cox proportional hazards models were constructed to accommodate heterogeneity in preoperative observation intervals and to evaluate time-to-event risk rather than cumulative incidence alone. Covariates were selected based on clinical relevance and prior literature and included age, sex, diabetes mellitus, chronic kidney disease, tobacco use, alcohol abuse, psychoactive substance abuse, chronic obstructive pulmonary disease (COPD), immune disorders, any prior surgical history, prior skin infections, and obesity (body mass index [BMI] ≥30 kg/m2) [[20], [21], [22], [23], [24]]. Hazard ratios with corresponding 95% confidence intervals were reported, and statistical significance was defined as a 2-sided P value < .05. All analyses were completed within the TriNetX Analytics environment, which integrates Java, R, and Python-based statistical packages.

Results

Overall cohort demographics

Primary total hip arthroplasty

Before propensity score matching (PSM), patients with documented SDOH undergoing primary THA differed significantly from those without documented SDOH across multiple baseline characteristics. Deprived patients were older (61.1 ± 12.7 vs 58.7 ± 12.1 years, P < .001) and more frequently female (63.0% vs 53.7%, P < .001). They also demonstrated substantially higher rates of diabetes mellitus (21.6% vs 0.7%, P < .001), chronic kidney disease (9.9% vs 0.2%, P < .001), heart failure (8.8% vs 0.1%, P < .001), liver disease (13.7% vs 0.2%, P < .001), alcohol abuse (8.6% vs 0.1%, P < .001), and COPD (12.6% vs 0.2%, P < .001). After 1:1 PSM, (4140 patients per cohort), all covariates were well balanced, with postmatching standardized mean differences (SMDs) < 0.10 for all covariates (Table 1).

Primary total knee arthroplasty

In the unmatched TKA cohorts, deprived patients were older than nondeprived patients (62.9 ± 10.7 vs 60.4 ± 10.3 years, P < .001) and more frequently female (71.1% vs 59.3%, P < .001). The deprived cohort also exhibited markedly higher prevalences of diabetes mellitus (27.5% vs 1.0%, P < .001), chronic kidney disease (10.7% vs 0.2%, P < .001), heart failure (8.6% vs 0.1%, P < .001), liver disease (14.0% vs 0.3%, P < .001), alcohol abuse (5.5% vs 0.1%, P < .001), and COPD (11.1% vs 0.2%, P < .001). Following PSM (5583 patients per cohort), baseline characteristics were well balanced, with all postmatching SMDs <0.10 (Table 2).

Primary total shoulder arthroplasty

Prior to matching, socially deprived TSA patients were significantly older (63.2 ± 10.2 vs 60.0 ± 11.1 years, P < .001) and more often female (67.9% vs 56.3%, P < .001) compared with nondeprived patients. Higher prevalences of diabetes mellitus (27.8% vs 0.8%, P < .001), chronic kidney disease (12.0% vs 0.2%, P < .001), heart failure (10.3% vs 0.1%, P < .001), liver disease (14.2% vs 0.2%, P < .001), alcohol abuse (8.2% vs 0.1%, P < .001), and COPD (16.3% vs 0.2%, P < .001) were observed in the deprived cohort. After PSM (1350 patients per cohort), all baseline variables achieved adequate balance, with postmatching SMDs <0.10 for demographic variables, comorbidities, and BMI (Table 3).

Primary outcomes

Among patients undergoing THA, MRSA colonization occurred more frequently in the SDOH cohort (2.2% vs 0.8%), corresponding to a 2.74-fold increased risk (RR, 2.735; 95% CI, 1.851–4.042; P < .001). A similar pattern was observed in TKA, where SDOH-coded patients demonstrated higher MRSA rates than nondeprived patients (2.4% vs 1.1%) and a significantly elevated risk (RR, 2.164; 95% CI, 1.601–2.924; P < .001). Similarly, in TSA, MRSA was likewise more prevalent among SDOH patients (3.0% vs 1.6%), representing an approximately twofold increased risk (RR, 1.952; 95% CI, 1.160–3.286; P = .010) (Table 4, Fig. 1).

Cox proportional hazards regression analysis

Total hip arthroplasty

In the multivariable Cox proportional hazards analysis of patients undergoing primary THA, male sex was independently associated with an increased hazard of preoperative MRSA colonization (hazard ratio [HR], 1.14; 95% CI, 1.08–1.21; P < .0001), whereas increasing age was associated with a modest reduction in risk (HR 0.99 per year; 95% CI, 0.99–1.00; P < .0001). Alcohol abuse was independently associated with higher hazard of MRSA colonization (HR, 1.50; 95% CI, 1.07–2.12; P = .020). Any prior surgery demonstrated the strongest association with MRSA colonization (HR, 2.84; 95% CI, 2.43–3.33; P < .0001). Obesity (BMI ≥30 kg/m2) was also independently associated with increased hazard (HR, 1.28; 95% CI, 1.05–1.56; P = .016). Other evaluated covariates were not independently associated with MRSA colonization after adjustment (Table 5, Fig. 2).

Total knee arthroplasty

Among patients undergoing primary TKA, male sex remained independently associated with increased hazard of preoperative MRSA colonization (HR, 1.20; 95% CI, 1.14–1.27; P < .0001), while increasing age was associated with lower risk (HR, 0.99 per year; 95% CI, 0.99–1.00; P = .0003). Psychoactive substance abuse (HR, 2.10; 95% CI, 1.11–4.00; P = .024) and COPD (HR, 1.88; 95% CI, 1.41–2.51; P < .0001) were independently associated with MRSA colonization. Discussion

Preoperative MRSA colonization remains a modifiable risk factor for adverse outcomes following TJAs, yet efficient identification of patients at increased risk remains challenging. In this study, SDOH were associated with higher rates of MRSA colonization prior to primary hip, knee, and shoulder arthroplasty, even after adjustment for measured demographic and clinical factors. These findings suggest that SDOH may provide additional information relevant to preoperative risk stratification and highlight an opportunity to refine targeted screening and decolonization strategies within routine perioperative arthroplasty care.

On cox regression analysis, independent predictors of preoperative MRSA colonization over time included male sex, alcohol abuse, obesity, diabetes, COPD, psychoactive substance use, a history of prior skin infection, and a history of any prior surgery. Male sex has been associated with increased vulnerability to MRSA colonization, with prior evidence demonstrating nearly a twofold higher likelihood of nasal carriage in men compared with women [25]. Alcohol abuse has been repeatedly implicated as a strong determinant of MRSA acquisition in hospital-exposed individuals, with one study reporting nearly a tenfold increase in colonization risk among those with documented alcohol misuse [26]. Chronic medical comorbidities, including diabetes, COPD, and heart failure, have likewise been associated with heightened susceptibility to colonization due to impaired host defenses and frequent healthcare contact, with prior studies demonstrating significant associations for both chronic illness burden [27]. Prior skin or soft tissue infections and HIV also emerged as an established risk factor, exhibiting more than threefold higher odds of nasal MRSA colonization [28]. Additionally, atopic dermatitis has been identified as a significant contributor to MRSA colonization, with dermatologic surveillance studies demonstrating markedly increased carriage rates among patients with chronic eczematous skin disease [29]. Similarly, individuals with injection-drug use histories are more likely to experience unstable housing and dense interpersonal contact networks, conditions previously shown to sustain community-associated MRSA spread [26].

Increasing age was consistently associated with a modest but statistically significant reduction in the hazard of preoperative MRSA colonization across arthroplasty cohorts. Prior evidence demonstrated that MRSA transmission in younger populations was driven predominantly by community- and household-level exposures concentrated in deprived environments [30]. Similarly, other studies identified the presence of young children in the household and crowded living arrangements as major determinants of colonization [31,32]. These findings suggest that the elevated MRSA risk observed among younger adults likely reflects increased community-based exposure.

Our findings are also consistent with prior non-arthroplasty studies demonstrating a link between social deprivation and increased colonization or infection with antimicrobial-resistant organisms [[33], [34], [35]]. Low income and recent immigration have all been linked to higher community acquired-MRSA colonization risk, likely due to increased person-to-person contact and challenges in maintaining adequate hygiene and sanitation [14,36]. Individuals experiencing homelessness or housing instability, especially those using shelters or public showers, have significantly higher odds of MRSA nasal colonization, reflecting both environmental exposure and reduced access to personal hygiene facilities [37,38]. Incarceration and residence in communities with high jail admission rates also drive MRSA disparities, both directly and indirectly, by increasing exposure risk and disrupting social networks [39]. In summary, the combination of restricted healthcare access and increased contact with high-risk environments observed in deprived settings has been associated with higher MRSA colonization burdens.

Mitigating MRSA-related complications in TJA begins with understanding the clinical consequences of preoperative colonization and the effectiveness of targeted decolonization protocols. Preoperative MRSA colonization has been shown to adversely affect arthroplasty outcomes, including longer hospitalization, higher readmission rates, and increased septic and aseptic revision risk, reinforcing the clinical value of identifying colonized patients before surgery [5]. S aureus nasal screening with targeted decolonization protocols using mupirocin and chlorhexidine has also been demonstrated to significantly reduce colonization rates and lower the risk of both total SSI and MRSA-specific SSI following TJAs [40].

Future work should explore whether tailored perioperative pathways can mitigate this disparity and improve postoperative infectious outcomes. More deprived groups may benefit from specific targeted interventions delivered perioperatively, as this period provides a critical window of opportunity to implement strategies that directly address underlying health inequalities [41]. Such approaches may include targeted optimization of comorbid disease, enhanced postdischarge surveillance, and more intensive follow-up in patients with poor baseline health status, who consistently demonstrate the strongest risk effects [41].

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, this analysis was retrospective and relied on administrative electronic health record data, which are subject to selection bias, residual confounding, and potential misclassification. Second, SDOH were identified using ICD-10 Z codes. While these codes are endorsed by national coding guidelines, they depend on clinician documentation and may undercapture true deprivation, particularly in patients with limited healthcare engagement. The use of ICD-10 coding to identify MRSA colonization may be subject to misclassification, as coding does not distinguish between persistent colonization and prior treated infection and may not fully capture true carrier status. Third, the use of ICD-10 Z-codes to identify SDOH is dependent on clinician documentation and may undercapture the true prevalence of social deprivation. Fourth, participation in the TriNetX Research Network is voluntary and may overrepresent larger, research-oriented healthcare systems, which could limit generalizability to smaller or resource-limited institutions. Fifth, follow-up was limited to encounters recorded within participating healthcare organizations, and MRSA diagnoses documented outside the network may not have been captured. Finally, our study demonstrated associations rather than causation, and the observed relationships should be interpreted accordingly. Despite these limitations, this study represents one of the first to demonstrate an association between social disadvantage and preoperative MRSA colonization in patients undergoing total joint arthroplasty. Future studies incorporating healthcare exposure variables, including recent hospitalization, are warranted to refine risk prediction and inform targeted perioperative interventions.

Conclusions

Social disadvantage was associated with increased rates of preoperative MRSA colonization among patients undergoing TJAs. Incorporation of SDOH information into preoperative assessment may enhance risk stratification and help identify patients who could benefit from selective MRSA screening and targeted decolonization strategies. As arthroplasty care continues to shift toward outpatient and ambulatory settings, improved recognition and documentation of SDOH may represent a pragmatic approach to optimizing perioperative infection prevention efforts.
