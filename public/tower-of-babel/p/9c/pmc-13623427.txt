A case of dupilumab-induced psoriatic alopecia
Authors: Julian Pearce, Zoe Jane Avila
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDupilumab is a human monoclonal antibody, targeting the alpha subunit of the interleukin (IL)-4 receptor, shared by both IL-4 and IL-13 cytokines. Paradoxical reactions have been reported with its use, including psoriatic manifestations, but reports of associated psoriatic alopecia are sparse. We report a case of a 29-year-old man with severe atopic dermatitis from childhood who developed a psoriasiform rash and inflammatory alopecia affecting the scalp 7 weeks after the initiation of dupilumab. Histological features from a scalp biopsy were in keeping with psoriatic alopecia, including overlapping psoriasiform and spongiotic features, and the presence of peribulbar, deeper inflammation with eosinophils and plasma cells. Dupilumab was stopped and the scalp treated topically with steroids and ketoconazole shampoo. The patient was commenced on abrocitinib and within 6 months he had full regrowth of hair and his eczema was well controlled. Psoriatic alopecia has rarely been reported as a paradoxical reaction and can mimic the more common presentation of paradoxical alopecia areata. Histology is helpful in distinguishing these presentations, and cessation of the medication often leads to resolution. Paradoxical psoriatic manifestations are more common with tumour necrosis factor-α inhibitors, and a proposed mechanism for this reaction is a shift between the T helper (Th) 2 pathway to the Th1/Th17 pathway. Clinicians should be aware of this paradoxical reaction when prescribing dupilumab, and consider switching therapy to an alternative agent with a different mechanism of action should they encounter it.

Dupilumab is a human monoclonal antibody, targeting the alpha subunit of the interleukin (IL)-4 receptor, shared by the IL-4 and IL-13 cytokines, which are secreted by CD4+ T-lymphocytes.1 It is licensed for the treatment of moderate-to-­severe atopic dermatitis in children from the age of 6 months, and adults. Psoriatic paradoxical reactions have previously been reported; however, paradoxical psoriatic alopecia is rare, and has distinguishing clinical and histopathological features to aid diagnosis. This report describes a case of paradoxical psoriatic alopecia secondary to dupilumab and reviews the literature associated with this entity, including key diagnostic features and discussion of management options.

Case report

The patient was a 29-year-old man with a background of severe atopic dermatitis who developed dupilumab-induced psoriatic alopecia. He had a history of childhood eczema, which worsened in adulthood, complicated by recurrent secondary bacterial infections. Previous treatments included topical corticosteroids and courses of oral prednisolone. In 2023 he developed erythroderma secondary to uncontrolled eczema requiring hospital admission. Ciclosporin 2.5 mg kg–1 over 8 months led to a degree of disease stabilization over 8 months but associated hypertension. After counselling, dupilumab was selected as the next therapy.

His other medical background included asthma, allergic rhinitis, hay fever and multiple nut allergies. He had bilateral keratoconus, which was managed surgically a year prior to dupilumab initiation. His ophthalmology team agreed dupilumab was appropriate to use. He had no personal or family history of alopecia or autoimmune disease.

Dupilumab was initiated in June 2024. Seven weeks after starting treatment the patient developed a widespread, asymptomatic, scaling, psoriasiform rash and an inflamed itchy scalp associated with hair loss. He had not used any new products on his skin or scalp, and he had not taken any other recently prescribed or over-the-counter medication. At review, there were widespread, erythematous, scaling patches of varying sizes over his limbs and trunk (Figure 1a, b). Alopecia affected the mid-scalp and vertex with associated erythema, crust and scale (Figure 1c, d). Trichoscopy demonstrated erythema, crusts, focal haemorrhage and yellow dots with no evidence of scarring (Figure 1e, f).

Results

Mycology samples from the trunk and scalp were negative for fungi. Bacterial swabs from the scalp showed heavy growth of fully sensitive Staphylococcus aureus, Pseudomonas species and fully sensitive beta haemolytic Streptococcus group B. Punch biopsies (4 mm) were taken from a scaling macule on the right thigh (one) and the left parietal scalp (two: one horizontally sectioned and the other bisected; half for vertical haematoxylin and eosin, half for immunofluorescence). Dupilumab was suspected to have caused paradoxical psoriasis and psoriatic alopecia, and was stopped. The patient was treated with ketoconazole shampoo twice weekly, dermovate scalp application twice weekly, eumovate ointment daily to the trunk and limbs for 2 weeks, then twice weekly ongoing, with 50:50 white soft paraffin to the trunk and limbs.

Histopathology from the right thigh demonstrated a mixed pattern psoriasiform dermatitis with confluent para­keratosis, variable loss of the granular cell layer, psoriasiform hyperplasia with suprapapillary thinning, as well as spongiosis of the epidermis and colloid body formation (Figure 2a). A prominent perivascular, interstitial and perifollicular predominantly lymphocytic inflammatory infiltrate was seen in the superficial dermis, and to a mild degree, the deep dermis, with histiocytes, plasma cells and occasional eosinophils (Figure 2b).

A patchy interface reaction is present with apoptotic keratinocytes at dermoepidermal junction (up pointing arrow). Original magnification ×10. Histopathology from the scalp demonstrated striking telogen shift (telogen count: 67%) (Figure 3a). The interfollicular epithelium demonstrated psoriatic changes including confluent parakeratosis with loss of the granular cell layer, psoriasiform hyperplasia with suprapapillary thinning and intraepithelial neutrophilic pustules, as well as mild spongiosis with lymphocyte exocytosis (Figure 3b). An acute folliculitis with bacterial colonies in follicular ostia was also noted. The total number of hair follicles was preserved, with some bulbs in the subcutis replaced by stele. Atrophy and loss of sebaceous lobules was seen with basaloid remnants remaining (Figure 3c). A prominently lymphocytic perivascular and perifollicular inflammatory infiltrate was present with plasma cells and eosinophils; most pronounced superficially at the infundibular and isthmic level. More focal, conspicuous deep plasma cells and eosinophils were also visualized, including in a peribulbar arrangement (Figure 3d), with a lack of peribulbar lymphocytic inflammation around anagen hair follicles. Special stains (diastase periodic acid–Schiff and Alcian Blue periodic acid–Schiff) for fungi, immuno­histochemistry for Treponema and immunofluorescent studies were negative.

The features were consistent with dupilumab-associated psoriasiform dermatitis and psoriatic alopecia after clinical and histopathological correlation.

Ten days after the cessation of dupilumab the patient presented suberythrodermic and was treated with a reducing course of oral prednisolone. Within 6 weeks of stopping dupilumab and recommencing topical treatments, the psoriasiform plaques and scalp inflammation resolved. After counselling and screening investigations, abrocitinib 100 mg once daily was subsequently initiated. Twelve weeks after initiation, his eczema has significantly improved. Six months after dupilumab was stopped the patient’s hair had fully regrown (Figure 4).

Discussion

Paradoxical reactions represent de novo or worsening of an underlying immune-mediated disease.2 Paradoxical psoriatic manifestations are mostly reported with tumour necrosis factor-α inhibitors.3 Classical alopecia areata is the most common paradoxical hair loss form with dupilumab, typically occurring within 6 months of initiation with a median time interval of 6.5 weeks.4 There are also reports of dupilumab improving pre-existing alopecia areata.5 Paradoxical psoriatic alopecia is a rare phenomenon. A proposed mechanism is an IL-4 blockade induced shift between the T helper (Th) 2 pathway (associated with atopic dermatitis) to the Th1/Th17 pathway (associated with psoriasis), in part due to increased interferon-γ expression.2 IL-4 and IL-13 expression has also been found to be positively associated with the development of sebaceous glands, probably explaining the dramatic sebaceous gland atrophy in paradoxical psoriatic alopecia.1,6

Su and Zeng’s scoping review found between 1.8% and 3.3% of patients treated with dupilumab had a drug-­associated psoriasis/psoriatic manifestation and de novo psoriasis was more common than a drug-induced flare of pre-existing disease.7 Scalp lesions occurred in 31%, although the frequency of alopecia was not reported. Two different patterns of dupilumab-associated alopecia were described: classic alopecia areata and psoriatic alopecia plaques; the latter, as in our case, was associated clinically with erythema and scale.

A diagnosis of paradoxical psoriatic alopecia can be made on clinical grounds. Where histology is undertaken, features distinguishing this from classic psoriatic alopecia include overlapping psoriasiform and spongiotic features, a greater degree of inflammation and the presence of deep inflammation, especially with eosinophils and/or plasma cells.6 All of these features were present in our patient. Alopecia areata can be excluded as epidermal psoriasiform changes, brisk superficial inflammatory infiltrates, peribulbar plasma cells and sebaceous gland atrophy are not permissible histological features,6 along with no peribulbar lymphocytic inflammation seen around anagen hair follicles.

Management of dupilumab-induced paradoxical psoriatic lesions is broadly similar to that of classical psoriasis. Withdrawal of the drug is often successful in improving paradoxical disease.7,8 Switching therapy may be necessary for control of the underlying atopic dermatitis. Alternative therapies include tralokinumab, an anti-IL-13 monoclonal antibody, but which has conflicting reports of both causing de novo paradoxical psoriasis,9 flaring pre-existing psoriasis10 and improving dupilumab-induced psoriasis when used as a second-line agent.11 Janus kinase inhibitors provide an alternative mechanism of action to control atopic dermatitis and may theoretically reduce the risk of an associated paradoxical reaction. Baricitinib 4 mg daily and upadacitinib 30 mg daily have both been reported to successfully treat dupilumab-associated psoriasis.12

This case contributes to the limited existing literature of drug-induced paradoxical psoriatic alopecia. The diagnosis can be made on clinical grounds; however, histopathology is often helpful in confirming the diagnosis, with specific histopathological features as described. Clinicians should be aware of this entity when prescribing dupilumab and consider switching therapy to a drug with a different mechanism of action should this paradoxical reaction occur.
