Microglial Ccl4-Ccr5 signaling links systemic inflammation to synaptic loss and memory deficits in chronic kidney disease
Authors: Yaochen Cao, Jingyun Wang, Xitong Li, Xin Chen, Jinsheng Xiong, Tianyao Wang, Hongming Sun, Ziqiang Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChronic kidney disease (CKD) is increasingly associated with cognitive impairment, yet the molecular pathways connecting systemic inflammation to synaptic dysfunction remain unclear. This study investigates the role of the Ccl4-Ccr5 axis in microglia-mediated synaptic phagocytosis during CKD-associated cognitive decline.MethodsA 5/6 nephrectomy rat model was established in rats to induce CKD, validated by renal function markers. Cognitive performance was evaluated through spatial, contextual, and recognition memory tests. Hippocampal cellular alterations were analyzed using single-cell RNA sequencing and immunofluorescence for microglial activation and synaptic density. The functional roles of Ccr5 were assessed via in vivo and in vitro CRISPR-mediated knockdown or overexpression, while Ccl4-Ccr5 interactions were confirmed by co-immunoprecipitation.ResultsCKD rats showed significant cognitive deficits, increased hippocampal microgliosis, and synaptic loss. Ccr5 knockdown reduced microglial synaptic engulfment, restored synaptic plasticity, and improved memory, whereas Ccr5 overexpression enhanced phagocytic activity.ConclusionsThese findings demonstrate that Ccl4-Ccr5 signaling drives pathological synaptic elimination by microglia, linking systemic inflammation to cognitive decline in CKD. Targeting this pathway may provide a therapeutic approach to preserve neuronal function.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12974-026-03852-w.Keywords: Chronic kidney disease, Cognitive impairment, Microglia, Synaptic phagocytosis, Ccl4-Ccr5 axis, Neuroinflammation

Introduction

Chronic kidney disease (CKD) affects approximately 8–16% of adults worldwide [1] and is characterized by progressive renal dysfunction and reduced glomerular filtration rate [2, 3]. Impaired renal clearance leads to the systemic accumulation of uremic toxins, which contributes to multiple complications, including cardiovascular disease and cognitive impairment [4, 5]. Cognitive deficits in CKD patients, particularly in memory and executive function, are increasingly associated with blood-brain barrier (BBB) disruption, uremic toxin accumulation, and chronic systemic inflammation [6, 7]. Elucidating the neurobiological mechanisms that link CKD to cognitive dysfunction is essential for improving patient outcomes.

Microglia, the resident immune cells of the central nervous system (CNS), are essential for maintaining neural homeostasis through immune surveillance and phagocytosis of cellular debris [8]. Under pathological conditions, excessive microglial activation can drive neuroinflammation and synaptic dysfunction [9, 10]. In CKD, uremic toxins and chronic inflammation compromise BBB integrity, allowing peripheral inflammatory signals to infiltrate the CNS and activate microglia. This activation drives M1 polarization and the secretion of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β and TNF-α [7]. Moreover, non-canonical inflammasome activation, including caspase-11-dependent pathways, further exacerbates microglial dysfunction [10]. Beyond their immune roles, microglia are critical regulators of synaptic remodeling and plasticity [11]. Dysregulated microglial phagocytosis has been implicated in pathological synapse loss in neurodegenerative disorders, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD) [12]. Together, these findings highlight microglial dysfunction as a key contributor to CKD-associated cognitive impairment and a promising therapeutic target.

The Ccl4-Ccr5 axis, widely studied for its role in immune cell trafficking and HIV entry [13, 14], has recently emerged as an important regulator of neuroinflammatory processes. Binding of Ccl4 to Ccr5 promotes immune cell migration; however, under pathological conditions, aberrant activation of this pathway may enhance microglial phagocytic activity and compromise synaptic integrity [15, 16]. Despite these findings, its role in CKD-related cognitive impairment remains poorly understood.

We hypothesize that aberrant activation of the Ccl4–Ccr5 axis drives microglia-mediated synaptic elimination in CKD, thereby contributing to cognitive decline. To test this hypothesis, we employed a 5/6 nephrectomy–induced CKD rat model combined with single-cell RNA sequencing, immunofluorescence, and confocal microscopy to characterize Ccr5 expression in microglia and its association with synaptic phagocytosis. In vitro models involving Ccr5 knockdown and overexpression were also employed to validate its functional role. This study aims to elucidate how Ccl4-Ccr5 signaling modulates microglial behavior and to identify potential therapeutic targets for improving neurocognitive outcomes in CKD.

Materials and methods

Animal research ethics statement

All animal experiments were conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Institutional Animal Experiment Ethics Committee and were approved prior to study initiation. A group size of n = 6 rats was selected based on preliminary data demonstrating sufficient power to distinguish biological variation from technical noise, and in accordance with previously published studies reporting reliable phenotypic detection with 5–8 animals per group (JASN 2023; 34:110–121). All efforts were made to minimize animal suffering and to reduce animal use to the minimum necessary. Animal care, handling, and experimental procedures strictly adhered to internationally recognized standards for laboratory animal welfare, and humane endpoints were observed throughout the study.

Animals and establishment of the CKD model

Male Wistar rats (6–7 weeks old, 200–250 g; Beijing Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd., No. 102) were housed in a specific pathogen–free (SPF) facility under controlled conditions (24 ± 1 °C; 12 h light/12 h dark cycle) with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee (approval no. HYLL-2023-439). All euthanasia and tissue collection procedures were performed in accordance with institutional animal care guidelines and current ethical recommendations to minimize pain and distress.

The 5/6 nephrectomy (5/6 Nx) model of CKD was established according to a previously published protocol [17]. Briefly, at week 2, the upper and lower poles of the left kidney were surgically resected, followed by complete removal of the right kidney at week 4, resulting in ablation of approximately 5/6 of total renal mass while preserving residual renal function. Sham-operated rats underwent identical surgical exposure at the same time points without renal tissue removal. All animals were anesthetized with isoflurane inhalation, and anesthesia was maintained using a VetEquip vaporizer. After surgery, the abdominal incision was sutured layer by layer, disinfected locally, and coated with antibiotic ointment to prevent infection. Rats were provided with adequate fresh food and water postoperatively. Recovery was monitored daily, including assessments of food and water intake, body weight, activity levels, and changes in excreta.

Behavioral assessments were conducted at predefined time points. Electrophysiological recordings, including long-term potentiation (LTP) measurements, were performed at week 16 following completion of behavioral testing to ensure that behavioral outcomes and synaptic plasticity were evaluated under comparable physiological conditions. To avoid potential confounding effects of repeated interventions, independent cohorts of animals were used for each experimental purpose. Specifically, animals subjected to behavioral testing were not used for electrophysiological recordings or brain tissue collection. Separate cohorts were allocated for each experimental module, including immunofluorescence, Western blotting, and quantitative PCR analyses.

At week 16, rats were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane, and euthanasia was performed by exsanguination under deep anesthesia. Blood samples were collected from the inferior vena cava and tail vein, and 24-hour urine samples were obtained before terminal tissue collection. Kidneys were weighed, fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, and processed for histological evaluation. Brain tissues were rapidly harvested for single-cell RNA sequencing [17].

For assessment of neuronal proliferation, 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU; 120 mg/kg, intraperitoneal) was administered twice daily for 7 consecutive days beginning on day 5 post-modeling. Rats were sacrificed 8 weeks later, and brain tissues were collected for histological analysis.

Behavioral tests

All behavioral experiments were conducted at weeks 14–15 after model establishment and were performed during the light phase (09:00–15:00) in the same testing room. Ambient temperature was maintained at 24 ± 1 °C, and noise and external disturbances were minimized. Rats were transferred to the behavioral laboratory in their home cages and allowed to acclimate for 30 min prior to testing. Animals were then individually moved to each apparatus using a transparent holding cage to minimize handling-related stress. All apparatuses were cleaned with 70% ethanol between trials to eliminate olfactory cues. Behavioral activity was recorded using an overhead video-tracking system and analyzed in a blinded manner with automated software unless otherwise specified.

Object recognition test (ORT)

The ORT was used to assess recognition memory, which depends on the coordinated activity of the cerebral cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala, with particular involvement of the lateral cortex and ventral hippocampus [18].

Open-field habituation

Open-field habituation

One day prior to ORT testing, rats underwent habituation in the testing arena to minimize novelty-induced stress. The apparatus consisted of an opaque gray square box (60 × 60 × 40 cm) equipped with an overhead video camera. Rats were gently placed in the center of the empty arena and allowed to freely explore for 10 min under moderate illumination (30–50 lx). No objects were present, and no behavioral data were recorded during this session. Following habituation, rats were returned to their home cages.

2.ORT procedure

ORT procedure

The ORT was performed at week 14 and lasted for two consecutive days, including a training phase and a test phase.

Training phase

Two identical objects (object A and object A′) were positioned symmetrically within the arena. Rats were placed in the center of the arena and allowed to explore freely for 8 min under moderate lighting (30–50 lx). Object exploration was defined as nose orientation toward an object at a distance of < 2 cm or direct physical contact; climbing on or passing by an object without active investigation was not considered exploration. After training, rats were returned to their home cages.

Test phase

Three hours after the training phase, one of the familiar objects was replaced with a novel object (B), while the remaining familiar object (A) was left in the same location. Rats were reintroduced into the arena and allowed to freely explore for 8 min. Exploration time directed toward the novel and familiar objects was recorded.

The recognition index was calculated as follows: Recognition index (%) = [time spent exploring the novel object/total exploration time of both objects] × 100. A higher recognition index indicates better recognition memory performance.

Object location test (OLT)

The OLT was used to assess spatial recognition memory, which primarily depends on dorsal hippocampal function [18]. The test evaluates the ability of animals to detect changes in object location within a familiar environment. The OLT was conducted at week 14, with an interval of at least 24 h after completion of the ORT, using the same square arena.

Training phase

Two identical objects (C and C′) were placed symmetrically within the arena. Rats were placed in the center and allowed to freely explore for 8 min under moderate illumination (30–50 lx), enabling familiarization with both objects and their spatial positions.

Test phase

Three hours after training, one object (C′) was relocated to a novel position while the other object (C) remained in its original location, with all other environmental conditions unchanged. Rats were allowed to explore the arena for 8 min, and exploration times for the relocated and stationary objects were recorded.

The recognition index was calculated as follows: Recognition index (%) = [time spent exploring the object in the novel location/total exploration time of both objects] × 100. A higher recognition index indicates improved spatial recognition memory.

Y maze test

The Y-maze test was used to assess spontaneous alternation behavior, an index of short-term and working memory that primarily reflects prefrontal cortex–hippocampal circuit function [19]. This paradigm is also widely applied to evaluate cognitive flexibility and executive function. Testing was conducted at week 14 using a single-session spontaneous alternation protocol.

The Y-maze consisted of three opaque arms arranged at 120° angles (40 cm long × 10 cm wide × 15–20 cm high) and was illuminated with moderate white light (30–50 lx). Rats were placed in the central area of the maze and allowed to freely explore for 8 min. An arm entry was defined as the placement of all four paws into an arm. The sequence and total number of arm entries were recorded. Spontaneous alternation was defined as consecutive entries into three different arms (e.g., ABC, BCA, or CAB). The percentage of spontaneous alternation was calculated as:

Spontaneous alternation (%) = [number of actual alternations/(total arm entries − 2)] × 100.

Higher spontaneous alternation values indicate better spatial working memory and cognitive flexibility.

Barnes maze test

The Barnes maze test was used to assess spatial learning and long-term memory in rodents and is highly dependent on hippocampal function [20]. This non-aversive paradigm evaluates the ability of animals to use distal visual cues for spatial navigation and is well-suited for examining memory consolidation following long-term interventions. Testing was conducted at week 15 over 5 consecutive days.

The apparatus consisted of a circular platform (100–120 cm in diameter) with 20 evenly spaced holes (10 cm in diameter) along the perimeter, one of which led to an escape box (target hole). The platform was illuminated with bright white light (400–600 lx) to motivate escape behavior, and fixed distal visual cues were positioned around the testing room. For all trials, rats were placed in the center of the platform at the start of each trial.

Day 1: habituation phase

Rats were placed near the target hole and gently guided into the escape box, where they remained for approximately 2 min. This procedure was repeated twice to allow familiarization with the escape box.

Days 2–4: acquisition training phase

Each rat underwent three training trials per day with inter-trial intervals of 15–20 min. Trials lasted up to 180 s. Rats that located the target hole were allowed to remain in the escape box for 1 min. If the target was not located within 180 s, rats were gently guided to the escape box and allowed to remain there for 1 min. Escape latency, number of errors (non-target hole investigations), and movement trajectories were recorded.

Day 5: probe test

The escape box was removed, and rats were allowed to explore the platform freely for 90 s under identical lighting conditions. Time spent in the target quadrant, time spent in the vicinity of the target hole, and the number of target hole investigations were recorded as indices of spatial memory retention.

Contextual fear conditioning

The CFC test was used to assess associative memory linking a specific context with an aversive stimulus and integrates hippocampal, amygdalar, and prefrontal cortical function [21]. This paradigm is well-suited for probing interactions between contextual memory and emotional processing. Testing was conducted at week 15.

Experiments were performed in a standard fear-conditioning chamber equipped with a metal grid floor and an internal video camera for behavioral recording. Chamber walls were transparent or opaque as appropriate. All procedures were carried out under red light conditions (10–20 lx) to minimize environmental stress. Rats were transferred from the waiting cage to the chamber before each session.

Day 1: context habituation

Rats were allowed to freely explore the chamber for 5 min in the absence of auditory cues or foot shocks to habituate to the context. Baseline locomotor activity was recorded.

Day 2: conditioning (training) phase

Rats were placed in the chamber and allowed to explore for 2 min, followed by three tone–shock pairings. Each auditory cue (2 kHz, ~ 80 dB, 30 s) co-terminated with a foot shock (0.5–0.7 mA, 2 s) delivered during the final 2 s of the tone. Inter-trial intervals were 60–90 s. Animals remained in the chamber for an additional 1–2 min after the final pairing before session termination. Freezing behavior was recorded throughout.

Day 3: contextual fear memory test (context test)

Rats were returned to the same chamber for 5 min without tone or shock presentation. Freezing behavior was quantified as an index of contextual fear memory. Freezing was defined as complete immobility except for respiration lasting ≥ 1 s and was scored using automated analysis software or by a blinded observer. Contextual fear memory was expressed as the percentage of total time spent freezing.

If required by the experimental design, cue-dependent fear memory and extinction could be assessed over subsequent days by modifying contextual features (e.g., chamber odor, wall inserts, and lighting) and presenting the auditory cue in the absence of shock.

In vivo field excitatory postsynaptic potentials (fEPSPs) recording

Field EPSPs were recorded from the hippocampal CA1 and dentate gyrus (DG) regions following established protocols [22]. Rats were anesthetized and positioned in a stereotaxic frame, and body temperature was maintained at 37 ± 0.5 °C using a feedback-controlled heating system (Harvard Instruments). After skull exposure, craniotomies were performed for electrode placement. Bipolar stimulation electrodes were inserted at 4.0 mm posterior, 3.0 mm lateral to Bregma for CA1, and 7.4 mm posterior, 3.0 mm lateral for DG. Monopolar recording electrodes were placed at 3.5 mm posterior, 2.0 mm lateral to Bregma, targeting the CA1 stratum radiatum or DG granule cell layer. Electrode depth was adjusted to optimize signal quality. Stimulation was delivered at 60% of the maximal evoked response using a digital-to-analog converter (1322 A, Axon Instruments) and stimulus isolation unit (Getting Instruments). Signals were amplified with a differential amplifier (p55a) and analyzed using WinLTP software. A stable baseline was recorded for 30 min using single-pulse stimulation (one pulse every 20 s). LTP was induced in the CA1 region using high-frequency stimulation (HFS; four trains of 50 pulses at 100 Hz with 15 s inter-train intervals) and in the DG using theta-pattern stimulation (sTPS; four trains of 10 pulses at 400 Hz with 200 ms intra-train intervals and 15 s inter-train intervals).

Brain slice electrophysiology

Brain slice electrophysiological recordings were performed at week 16 following completion of behavioral testing, ensuring that synaptic plasticity measurements reflected observed behavioral phenotypes under comparable physiological conditions [23]. Rats were transcardially perfused with ice-cold, oxygenated NMDG-based artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing (in mM): 93 NMDG, 93 HCl, 2.5 KCl, 1.25 NaH2PO4, 10 MgSO4·7H2O, 30 NaHCO3, 25 glucose, 20 HEPES, 5 sodium ascorbate, 3 sodium pyruvate, and 2 thiourea, continuously bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2. Brains were rapidly extracted and immersed in the same solution. Coronal brain slices (300 μm) were prepared using a vibrating blade microtome (VT1200 S, Leica, Germany) and incubated at 32 °C in oxygenated NMDG aCSF for 10–15 min. Slices were then transferred to room-temperature standard aCSF (126 mM NaCl, 2.5 mM KCl, 1.25 mM NaH2PO4, 2 mM MgSO4·7H2O, 10 mM glucose, 26 mM NaHCO3, 2 mM CaCl2) for ≥ 1 h. All reagents were sourced from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA).

Slices were placed in a recording chamber and continuously perfused with oxygenated aCSF (2–3 mL/min) at 28 °C. Neurons were visualized using an Olympus BX61W1 microscope equipped with infrared DIC optics. Whole-cell patch-clamp recordings were obtained using borosilicate glass pipettes (3–4 MΩ; Sutter Instrument) filled with an internal solution containing (in mM): 133 potassium gluconate, 18 NaCl, 0.6 EGTA, 10 HEPES, 0.3 Na₃GTP, and 2 MgATP (pH 7.2, 280 mOsm).

Action potentials were evoked by step current injections (0 to + 360 pA in 20 pA increments, 400 ms duration). Paired-pulse ratio (PPR) was assessed using two stimuli separated by 50 ms and calculated as the ratio of the second to the first response amplitude. Signals were recorded using a MultiClamp 700B amplifier and Digidata 1550B system, filtered at 2 kHz, and sampled at 10 kHz. Cells with series resistance exceeding 30 MΩ were excluded. Data were analyzed offline using Clampfit 10.6 software (Molecular Devices).

Serum and urine analysis

Serum and urinary creatinine levels were measured using commercial assay kits (E-BC-K188-M, Elabscience, Wuhan, China). Urinary microalbumin concentrations were quantified using ELISA kits (E-EL-R0025, Elabscience). Creatinine levels were determined by enzymatic colorimetric methods, and blood urea nitrogen (BUN; E-BC-K183-M) was measured using an automated biochemical analyzer (Hitachi High-Tech, Tokyo, Japan). The urine albumin-to-creatinine ratio (UACR) was calculated from urinary microalbumin and creatinine values as an index of renal injury [24].

Renal morphology

Kidneys were sectioned (4 μm) and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and Masson’s trichrome to evaluate histological changes. Inflammation, tubular dilation, and cast formation were scored at 200× magnification across randomly selected fields (25 × 25 μm) using a SDi-quantitative 5-point scale (1: ≤10% affected area; 5: ≥76%). Fibrosis was assessed from 10 non-overlapping fields in Masson-stained sections. Fibrotic area (blue staining) was quantified using ImageJ software. All scoring was performed independently by two blinded investigators [25].

Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) (exploratory study)

scRNA-seq was performed as an exploratory analysis according to a previously published protocol [17]. Cell dissociation: Two rats were randomly selected from the CKD model group and the sham group after renal function assessment, and hippocampal tissues were collected. Tissues were washed with Hank’s Balanced Salt Solution (HBSS) to remove blood and debris, minced into small fragments, and enzymatically digested in a trypsin-containing dissociation solution at 37 °C for 15 min with intermittent gentle agitation. The resulting cell suspension was filtered through a 40-µm cell strainer to remove undissociated tissue, and red blood cells were eliminated using an RBC lysis buffer. After centrifugation, cell pellets were resuspended for downstream processing. Preparation of single-cell suspensions: Cell concentration was adjusted to approximately 1,000 cells/µL. Cell number, viability, and purity were assessed using Trypan Blue staining with a hemocytometer or an automated cell counter. Only suspensions with > 90% viable cells were used to ensure sequencing quality. Library preparation and sequencing: Single-cell barcoding and gel bead-in-emulsion (GEM) generation were performed on the 10x Genomics Chromium Controller platform. cDNA synthesis, amplification, and library construction were conducted according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Libraries were sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform using 150-bp paired-end reads. Raw sequencing data were processed using Cell Ranger software (10x Genomics) for initial quality control, read alignment, and generation of gene expression matrices. Downstream analyses were conducted using the Seurat package. Cells were filtered based on the following criteria: number of detected genes (nFeature_RNA) > 200, total UMI counts (nCount_RNA) < 50,000, and mitochondrial gene content (percent.mt) < 15%. Data were normalized, and the top 2,000 highly variable genes were selected for principal component analysis (PCA). The first 17 principal components were chosen for downstream analyses based on the ElbowPlot method. Cell clustering was performed using Seurat’s FindClusters function with a resolution of 0.4, followed by nonlinear dimensionality reduction using t-distributed stochastic neighbor embedding (t-SNE). Marker genes for each cluster were identified using Seurat, and cell types were annotated based on established lineage-specific markers in combination with the CellMarker database (http://xteam.xbio.top/CellMarker/). Pathway enrichment analyses were conducted using the “ClusterGVis” package.

Intercellular communication analysis

Intercellular communication analysis was performed using the CellChat framework according to a previously published protocol [17]. To enable direct comparison of ligand–receptor–mediated communication between experimental groups, CellChat objects were constructed independently for the CKD and sham datasets and subsequently merged. The built-in comparison framework of the CellChat package was then applied to visualize group-wise differences in communication from “source cell populations → target cell populations.” Specifically, the communication networks of the two groups were merged into a single CellChat object, and the direction of comparison was fixed by cell group indexing. Microglia-associated cell populations were designated as the source (sources.use = 4), while neuron-related cell populations were defined as the targets (targets.use = 5:11). Communication probabilities for each ligand–receptor pair were calculated under a unified ligand–receptor database and identical network inference parameters to ensure comparability across groups. In the resulting bubble plots, bubble size represents the relative magnitude of the communication probability, while the color gradient indicates the strength of the interaction. All figures were rendered using ggplot2 (v3.4.4) and exported in vector format to meet journal-quality figure preparation standards.

Cell culture and lentiviral infection

Primary rat microglia (#R1900, ScienCell, CA, USA) were cultured in microglia medium (#1901, ScienCell). HEK293T cells (CRL-3216, ATCC) were maintained in RPMI 1640 supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS; #11875119, GIBCO) at 37 °C in 5% CO2.

For lentiviral production, HEK293T cells were transfected with pLVTHM vectors encoding control (oe-NC, sh-NC), overexpression (oe-Ccr5), or knockdown (sh-Ccr5-1, sh-Ccr5-2) constructs using Lipofectamine 3000 (L3000015, Invitrogen). After 48 h, viral supernatants were collected, filtered through 0.45 μm membranes (Millipore), and stored at −80 °C. Microglia were transduced at appropriate multiplicities of infection (MOI) [26]. shRNA sequences were as follows: Ccr5-shRNA-1 (NM_001190236.1): 5’-TCGATTCCAACCTATATCTATGA-3’; Ccr5-shRNA-2: 5’-ATCTTTATGAGATCTCAAAAAGA-3’; sh-NC: 5’-GATCCTCGCTTACCGATTCAG-3’.

Stereotaxic adeno-associated virus (AAV) injection and experimental grouping

Stereotaxic AAV injection experiments were performed according to previously published procedures [27]. AAV vectors expressing Ccr5-shRNA or control shRNA (sh-NC) were constructed by Hanbio Biotechnology (Shanghai, China) using the pHBAAV-U6-MCS-CMV-EGFP vector backbone (AAV021; 1 × 1013 vg/mL). Additional AAV constructs were used to label neuronal and synaptic components, including Synaptophysin-mCherry-EGFP (presynaptic vesicles), GAD67-Synaptophysin-mCherry-EGFP (inhibitory synapses), PSD95-mCherry-EGFP (postsynaptic excitatory sites), and Gephyrin-mCherry-EGFP (inhibitory postsynaptic markers), all at 1 × 1013 vg/mL. Rats were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital and stereotaxically injected with 5 µL of AAV into the lateral ventricle (coordinates: posterior 0.9 mm, mediolateral 1.4 mm, dorsoventral 3.8 mm) at a rate of 0.5 µL/min using an RWD microinjection pump. Two weeks after injection, Ccr5 knockdown efficiency was confirmed by RT-qPCR and Western blot, followed by CKD model induction.

Oral administration of the CSF1R inhibitor PLX5622 (PLX; HY-114153; MedChemExpress, Shanghai, China) was achieved by premixing PLX5622 into AIN-76 A standard chow at a concentration of 1200 ppm. Control chow consisted of the same AIN-76 A formulation without PLX5622 [28]. Male Wistar rats aged 5–6 weeks were allowed to acclimate for 1 week, after which they were given ad libitum access to chow containing PLX5622 or control chow starting 14 days before the first nephrectomy surgery and continuing uninterrupted until sacrifice at week 16. No changes to the dietary regimen were made throughout the experimental period.

According to different experimental objectives, animals were randomly assigned to groups in separate batches, with n = 6 rats per group. Independent cohorts were used for different experimental modules to avoid confounding effects caused by multiple interventions. The experimental groups were organized as follows: (1) Baseline CKD phenotype groups: sham and CKD, used to evaluate the effects of 5/6 nephrectomy on renal function, behavior, and electrophysiological properties. (2) PLX5622 treatment groups: sham, sham + PLX, CKD, and CKD + PLX, used to assess the impact of long-term microglial depletion on CKD-associated cognitive function and synaptic plasticity. (3) Ccr5 intervention control groups: sham + sh-NC and sham + sh-Ccr5, used to examine the basal effects of Ccr5 knockdown under physiological conditions. (4) CKD + Ccr5 intervention groups: CKD + sh-NC and CKD + sh-Ccr5, used to evaluate the effects of Ccr5 knockdown in the CKD context on behavioral performance, synaptic plasticity, and microglia-mediated synaptic phagocytosis.

Microglia isolation from CKD rat brains

Rats from the CKD + sh-NC and CKD + sh-Ccl4 groups were deeply anesthetized and transcardially perfused with 10 mL of ice-cold PBS to remove circulating blood. The hippocampus, identified by its characteristic C-shaped structure surrounding the dentate gyrus [29], was rapidly dissected on ice. The homogenate was suspended in Syn-PER Synaptic Protein Extraction Reagent (87793, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and centrifuged at 1200×g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain the total homogenate fraction (THF) [30]. The THF was subsequently centrifuged at 15,000×g for 20 min at 4 °C, and the resulting supernatant was collected for downstream analysis.

For single-cell isolation, hippocampal tissues were dissociated using the Neural Tissue Dissociation Kit (130-093-231, Miltenyi Biotec, Germany). A Percoll gradient (17089109, Cytiva, USA) was applied to remove myelin. The resulting cell pellet was resuspended in 5% MACS buffer. Microglia were isolated via magnetic-activated cell sorting (MACS) using CD11b/c MicroBeads (130-105-643, Miltenyi Biotec) for immunomagnetic labeling and enrichment [31].

Synaptosome isolation and pHrodo labeling

Synaptosome isolation and pHrodo labeling were performed according to a previously published protocol [32] to evaluate the phagocytic capacity of microglia toward synaptosomes with different Ccl4 levels. After completion of behavioral testing, rats from the CKD + shNC and CKD + shCcl4 groups were subjected to transcardial perfusion with 10 mL of ice-cold PBS to remove blood. Brains were rapidly removed, and the hippocampus—identified by its characteristic C- or S-shaped structure surrounding the dentate gyrus with well-defined lamination—was carefully dissected and kept on ice. Hippocampal tissue was homogenized at a ratio of 1 g tissue per 10 mL Syn-PER™ Synaptic Protein Extraction Reagent (Cat. No. 87793, Thermo Fisher Scientific) supplemented with protease inhibitors. Homogenization was performed on ice using a Dounce homogenizer with approximately 10 gentle strokes. The homogenate was centrifuged at 4 °C and 1,200 g for 10 min. The pellet was discarded, and the supernatant was collected as the total homogenate fraction (THF). The THF was then centrifuged at 4 °C and 15,000 g for 20 min. After discarding the supernatant, the pellet was gently resuspended in a small volume of Syn-PER lysis buffer to obtain the crude synaptosome fraction. Prior to the phagocytosis assay, synaptosomes were labeled with the pH-sensitive dye pHrodo™ Red Avidin (P35362, Invitrogen). A working solution was prepared according to the manufacturer’s instructions, and synaptosomes containing equal amounts of protein were incubated with pHrodo Red Avidin for 45 min at room temperature in the dark. After incubation, the synaptosomes were washed twice with PBS by centrifugation at 4 °C and 12,000 g for 5 min each. The supernatant was discarded, and the labeled synaptosomes were resuspended in 200 µL of prewarmed culture medium for subsequent use. Primary microglia were isolated from Ccr5shNC and Ccr5sh CKD rats and seeded at a density of 2 × 104 cells per well. After cell attachment and recovery, pHrodo-labeled synaptosomes were added to the cultures at an approximate volume ratio of 1:100 (synaptosome suspension to culture medium). The cultures were gently mixed and incubated at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO₂ for the designated time period. The reaction was then terminated, followed by cell fixation, immunostaining, and confocal imaging to quantitatively assess microglial phagocytosis of synaptosomes with different Ccl4 levels.

Fluoro-Jade C (FJC) staining

FJC staining was performed using a commercial detection kit (TR-160-FJ, Biosensis, Wuhan, China) to assess neurodegeneration, as FJC selectively labels degenerating neurons [33]. The hippocampus, characterized by its C- or S-shaped structure surrounding the dentate gyrus with well-defined lamination, was selected for analysis. At the end of the experiment, the average number of FJC-positive cells per square millimeter (mm²) was recorded and quantified. Image processing and quantitative analysis were performed using ImageJ software.

Western blot analysis

Extracellular vesicle (EV) and cellular proteins were lysed in RIPA buffer (25 mM Tris, 150 mM NaCl, 0.1% SDS, 0.5% sodium deoxycholate, 1% Triton X-100, pH 7.5) and quantified using the BCA assay (23227, Thermo Fisher). Protein samples (5–10 µg for EVs; 20 µg for cell lysates) were separated by SDS-PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes, and blocked with 5% non-fat milk. Membranes were incubated overnight with primary antibodies: anti-Tubulin (ab7291, Abcam), anti-CCR5c (CCR5-501AP, Invitrogen), anti-CCL4 (PA5-114961, Invitrogen), anti-PSD95 (ab18258, Abcam), anti-Synapsin1 (6710, CST), and anti-BDNF (25699-1-AP, Proteintech). After washing, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies (Goat anti-Rabbit IgG #111035003 or Goat anti-Mouse IgG #115035003, Jackson ImmunoResearch) for 1 h. Bands were visualized using ECL (WP20005, Thermo Fisher) on a ChemiDoc XRS Plus system (Bio-Rad) and quantified with ImageJ, normalized to Tubulin [26]. All experiments were repeated in triplicate.

RT-qPCR

Total RNA was extracted with Trizol (15596026, Invitrogen) and reverse-transcribed using the PrimeScript RT Reagent Kit (RR047A, Takara). RT-qPCR was performed using the Fast SYBR Green PCR Kit (11736059, Thermo Fisher) in triplicate. GAPDH served as an internal control. Relative expression was calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method [26]. Experiments were repeated three times. Primer sequences are listed in Table S1 (synthesized by Takara).

Co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP)

Protein-protein interactions between Ccr5 and Ccl4 were examined using the Universal Magnetic Co-IP Kit (Cat# 54002, Active Motif, Shanghai, China). Briefly, equal amounts of protein (10 µg) were incubated with anti-Ccr5 (CCR5-501AP, Fabgennix, Thermo Fisher Scientific, China) or anti-Ccl4 (PA5-114961, Invitrogen, Thermo Fisher Scientific, China) antibodies at 4 °C for 3 h on a shaker. Rat IgG (sc-2025, 1:100, Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Shanghai, China) served as a negative control. Subsequently, 25 µL of Protein G magnetic beads were added, and the mixture was incubated for an additional hour at 4 °C. Beads were washed four times with Co-IP/wash buffer, and bound proteins were eluted using 2× reducing loading buffer (130 mM Tris, pH 6.8; 4% SDS; 0.02% bromophenol blue; 20% glycerol; 100 mM DTT). Interactions between Ccr5 and Ccl4 were analyzed by Western blot [26].

Immunofluorescence staining

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA) for 15 min at room temperature, blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin at 37 °C for 30 min, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies. After washing, cells were incubated with appropriate secondary antibodies and counterstained with DAPI. For tissue staining, rats (n = 6) were perfused with 4% PFA. Brains were cryoprotected in 10–30% sucrose, sectioned at 30 μm (3–5 sections per rat), and processed using the same staining protocol.

Primary antibodies included anti-anti-CCR5 (CCR5-501AP, FabGennix; Thermo Fisher Scientific (China) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), anti-CCL4 (PA5-114961, Invitrogen; Thermo Fisher Scientific (China) Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), anti-GFAP (NB300-141, Bio-Techne China Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), anti-NeuN (ab177487, Abcam), anti-IBA1 (ab178846, Abcam), anti-doublecortin (DCX; a marker of newly generated neurons; ab18723, Abcam), anti-BrdU (a marker of proliferating cells; ab6326, Abcam), anti-PSD95 (MA1-046, Invitrogen), anti-Synaptophysin (S5768, Sigma), anti-CD68 (ab283654, Abcam), and anti-TMEM119 (ab209064, Abcam). After primary antibody incubation, sections were incubated with DAPI and the appropriate secondary antibodies (all from Abcam, diluted 1:500), including goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (ab150077), goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 594 (ab150080), goat anti-rabbit Alexa Fluor 405 (ab175652), goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 488 (ab150113), goat anti-mouse Alexa Fluor 647 (ab150115), goat anti-rat Alexa Fluor 647 (ab150167), and rabbit IgG isotype control antibody (ab172730, Abcam). Confocal images were acquired using an Olympus FV3000 microscope. For quantification, 3–5 regions of interest (ROIs) were analyzed per Sect [26].

Flow cytometry

To validate the cell type specificity of Ccr5 knockdown and to distinguish resident microglia from peripherally infiltrating macrophages, additional flow cytometric analyses were performed using a CD11b+/CD45low gating strategy [34]. The experimental procedures were as follows. Hippocampal tissues were dissociated into single-cell suspensions using a GentleMACS™ Octo Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, Bergisch Gladbach, Germany, 130-096-427). Cellular debris was removed by Percoll gradient centrifugation (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, USA, P4937). Cells were resuspended in flow cytometry buffer (PBS supplemented with 2% fetal bovine serum and 1 mM EDTA) and adjusted to a final concentration of 1 × 106 cells/mL. Cells were preincubated with an Fc receptor blocking reagent (BioLegend, San Diego, CA, USA; Cat. No. 101320) for 10 min to reduce nonspecific binding, followed by incubation with a cocktail of fluorophore-conjugated surface antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C. The antibody panel included PE-conjugated anti-CD11b (Abcam, ab25533), APC-conjugated anti-CD45 (Abcam, ab10558), and FITC-conjugated anti-TMEM119 (Abcam, ab209064). Dead cells were excluded using 7-aminoactinomycin D (7-AAD; BioLegend, 420403). Data were acquired on a BD FACSCalibur flow cytometer (BD Biosciences, San Jose, CA, USA) and analyzed using FlowJo software version 10 (BD Biosciences). The gating strategy was as follows: CD45+ myeloid cells were first identified, after which CD11b⁺CD45lowTMEM119⁺ cells were defined as resident microglia, while CD11b+CD45high cells were classified as infiltrating macrophages. For each sample, at least 10,000 events were analyzed. A total of six rats per group were included (N = 6; [35]).

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 8. Data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) unless otherwise specified. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk or Kolmogorov–Smirnov test as appropriate. Comparisons between two groups were performed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test for normally distributed data or a Mann–Whitney U test for non-normally distributed data. Comparisons among multiple groups were conducted using one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) or the Kruskal–Wallis H test, followed by appropriate post hoc analyses. For experiments involving multiple factors (e.g., injury and drug treatment), two-way ANOVA was applied to assess main effects and interaction effects. Repeated-measures ANOVA was used for time-course analyses. All experiments were performed in triplicate. Statistical significance was defined as p < 0.05, and exact p-values and statistical parameters are provided in the corresponding figure legends.

Results

Single-cell transcriptomics reveals microglial activation in CKD rats

To investigate how CKD affects cell type heterogeneity across different brain regions, male rats were first subjected to 5/6 nephrectomy to mimic the pathological features of CKD. The overall experimental workflow is illustrated in Figure S1A. Body weight did not differ significantly between groups, whereas systolic blood pressure was markedly elevated in CKD rats (Figure S1B). Consistent with renal dysfunction, CKD rats exhibited significantly increased blood urea nitrogen levels and urinary albumin-to-creatinine ratios, with urinary creatinine showing an increasing trend that did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.0569) (Figure S1C). Histopathological analysis further revealed increased tubular cast formation, glomerular injury, and pronounced glomerular and tubulointerstitial fibrosis in the CKD group (Figure S1D).

Given the common clinical manifestations of cognitive impairment in CKD patients, we further assessed hippocampal-dependent learning and memory. A series of behavioral tests (including the ORT, OLT, Y-maze, Barnes maze, and fear conditioning) demonstrated significant impairments in spatial working memory, recognition memory, and learning performance in CKD rats (Fig. 1). Furthermore, hippocampal fEPSP recordings revealed decreased synaptic transmission in CKD rats (Figure S2).

To systematically characterize gene expression changes across different cell types in the hippocampus of CKD rats, we randomly selected two brain tissue samples from the CKD group and two from the control group for single-cell RNA sequencing, generating a high-quality scRNA-seq atlas (Figure S3A, B). After quality control, dimensionality reduction, and clustering, multiple cell populations were identified based on t-SNE distribution and canonical marker gene expression (Fig. 2A; Figure S3C; Table S2), including neurons, microglia, astrocytes, endothelial cells, monocytes/myeloid cells, and T cells. Comparative analysis of cell-type proportions revealed an increase in microglia from 29.6% in controls to 35.2% in CKD rats, accompanied by a reduction in neuronal representation from 11.5% to 9.9% (Fig. 2B). Because changes in cell proportion alone cannot distinguish proliferation from functional activation, subsequent analyses focused on transcriptional, molecular, and morphological indicators of microglial activation.

Differential gene expression analysis across major hippocampal cell populations revealed that microglia exhibited the most prominent transcriptional alterations in CKD. Compared with controls, CKD microglia showed marked upregulation of immune- and lysosome-associated genes, including Ctss, Cst3, Hexb, P2ry12, and Cd68, consistent with a transition from a homeostatic to an activated phenotype (Figure S4A). Functional enrichment analysis demonstrated that microglia-specific differentially expressed genes were significantly enriched in inflammatory and immune-regulatory pathways, including chemokine signaling (Figure S4B). Notably, pathways associated with systemic inflammatory diseases, such as rheumatoid arthritis, were also enriched, suggesting that microglia integrate peripheral inflammatory signals and amplify central immune responses during CKD.

Immunofluorescence analyses corroborated these transcriptomic findings. CKD rats exhibited a significant reduction in NeuN+ mature neurons in the hippocampus, alongside increased densities of DCX⁺ immature neurons and proliferating cells. In parallel, both microglia and astrocytes displayed elevated activation, as evidenced by increased Iba1 and GFAP immunoreactivity, respectively (Fig. 2C–F). Isotype control staining demonstrated the specificity of Iba1 labeling (Figure S1E). Notably, the intracellular CD68 burden in microglia was markedly increased in the CKD group, consistent with the upregulation of Cd68 observed in the scRNA-seq data, indicating enhanced lysosomal and phagocytic activity. This finding supports microglial activation rather than simple proliferation. The changes in activation markers revealed by single-cell sequencing were concordant with the morphological alterations observed by immunofluorescence, including enlarged cell bodies and shortened processes. To further evaluate microglial activation from a morphological perspective, we quantitatively analyzed microglial morphology in immunostained sections by measuring parameters such as soma area and process length. Previous studies have shown that resting microglia typically display small cell bodies with long, highly branched processes, whereas inflammatory or injury-related stimuli induce an activated morphology characterized by soma hypertrophy and shortened or reduced processes [36–38]. Consistent with these observations, microglia in the hippocampus of CKD rats exhibited a significantly enlarged soma area and markedly shortened processes compared with those in the sham group (Fig. 2G), indicative of a classical inflammatory activation phenotype. In parallel, hippocampal single-cell suspensions were analyzed using the canonical CD11b⁺/CD45low gating strategy in combination with TMEM119, a marker of resident microglia, to sort and quantify specific cell populations. The results showed that the number of CD11b⁺CD45lowTMEM119⁺ cells was significantly increased in the CKD group compared with the sham group, indicating that nephrectomy primarily induced activation and accumulation of resident microglia rather than infiltration of peripheral macrophages (Fig. 2H).

Taken together, the enrichment of inflammation-related pathways and the upregulation of activation markers such as Cd68 identified by single-cell transcriptomic analysis, together with the increased CD68 burden and the morphological shift from a resting to an activated phenotype observed by immunofluorescence, provide convergent evidence of robust microglial activation during CKD progression. The increased proportion of microglia more likely reflects their accumulation in an activated state rather than simple proliferation. Collectively, these findings identify aberrant microglial activation as a key contributor to CKD-associated brain dysfunction and suggest that targeting microglial activation may represent a promising therapeutic strategy for mitigating cognitive impairment in CKD.

CKD-induced microglial activation promotes excitatory synapse phagocytosis

To investigate the phagocytic activity of CKD microglia, we assessed the co-expression of Iba1 and CD68. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a marked increase in CD68+ lysosomal compartments within microglia in the CKD group compared with controls (Fig. 3A). We next examined overall excitatory synapse density by confocal imaging of the presynaptic marker Synaptophysin and the postsynaptic marker PSD95. Both markers, as well as their colocalized puncta, were significantly reduced in the hippocampus of CKD rats (Fig. 3B). To determine whether synaptic material was being engulfed by microglia, we quantified synaptic puncta localized within microglial lysosomes. CKD rats showed significantly increased PSD95-positive puncta within CD68-positive Iba1-positive microglia (Fig. 3C), and similarly increased Synaptophysin-positive puncta within microglial lysosomes (Fig. 3D), supporting enhanced microglia-mediated engulfment of excitatory synaptic components.

To further distinguish synapse types, we employed fluorescent phagocytosis reporters [39] and categorized excitatory presynaptic (ExPre), excitatory postsynaptic (ExPost), inhibitory presynaptic (InhiPre), and inhibitory postsynaptic (InhiPost) components (Fig. 3E). Because eGFP is acid-sensitive whereas mCherry remains stable in lysosomes, accumulation of mCherry-positive puncta within Iba1-positive microglia was used as an indicator of synaptic material undergoing lysosomal processing (Fig. 3F-G, I). In parallel, CKD microglia displayed enlarged soma area across reporter conditions, consistent with an activated morphology (Fig. 3H). Importantly, CKD significantly increased engulfment of ExPre and ExPost components, whereas no significant change was observed for InhiPre or InhiPost components (Fig. 3I). These data suggest that CKD preferentially enhances microglial-mediated turnover of excitatory synapses.

Pharmacological depletion of microglia attenuates neurological dysfunction

To determine the causal role of microglia in CKD-associated neural injury, we pharmacologically depleted microglia using the CSF1R inhibitor PLX5622. Rats were fed AIN-76 A chow containing PLX5622 (1200 ppm) starting 2 weeks prior to 5/6 nephrectomy and continuing throughout the experimental period until euthanasia at week 16. Control animals received an equivalent amount of standard AIN-76 A chow without PLX5622 (Fig. 4A).

We first examined microglial density in the hippocampus of four groups of rats (sham, PLX, CKD, and CKD + PLX) by Iba1 immunofluorescence staining and quantitative analysis (Fig. 4B, C). Compared with the sham and PLX groups, microglial density was markedly increased in the CKD group, whereas PLX5622 treatment effectively reversed this increase. Scatter plots at the individual animal level showed that all CKD rats receiving PLX exhibited substantially lower Iba1+ cell densities than their untreated CKD counterparts, with no evidence of PLX-insensitive outliers, indicating stable and robust microglial depletion throughout the 12-week treatment period.

We next examined whether microglial depletion ameliorated CKD-associated cognitive dysfunction. Consistent with earlier findings, CKD rats displayed marked impairments in recognition memory, spatial learning, working memory, and contextual fear memory. In contrast, CKD rats treated with PLX5622 exhibited significant improvements across all behavioral paradigms, including the ORT, OLT, Y-maze, Barnes maze, and contextual fear conditioning (Fig. 4D–H). Notably, behavioral performance in the CKD + PLX group approached that of sham animals.

We further analyzed the effects of PLX treatment on hippocampal synaptic plasticity. Representative LTP traces (Figure S5A) and time-course analyses of fEPSP slopes (Figure S5B, S5C) demonstrated that LTP following stimulation was markedly impaired in the CKD group. In contrast, LTP was partially restored in the CKD + PLX group, with the fEPSP slope at the final time point approaching sham levels.

Taken together, these results demonstrate that long-term PLX5622-mediated microglial depletion significantly alleviates CKD-induced cognitive deficits and hippocampal LTP impairment in this model. Based on these findings, we next employed single-cell transcriptomic analysis in untreated CKD hippocampal tissue to explore the upstream intercellular signaling pathways that drive microglial activation.

Ccl4-Ccr5 as a key ligand-receptor pair mediating neuron-microglia interaction

To avoid potential confounding effects of pharmacological or genetic interventions on the transcriptional landscape, all single-cell transcriptomic and immunofluorescence analyses in this section were performed using hippocampal tissues from sham and CKD rats only. Cell–cell communication analysis based on single-cell transcriptomic data revealed a marked enhancement of neuron–microglia signaling in the CKD group. Among the top-ranked ligand–receptor pathways, Psap–Gpr37l1, Ccl4–Ccr5, and Ptn–Ncl were prominently enriched (Fig. 5A). Given the established role of CCR5 in neuroinflammatory processes and its relevance as a therapeutic target in neurological disorders [15], we focused subsequent analyses on the Ccl4–Ccr5 signaling axis. t-SNE visualization showed that Ccr5 expression was largely restricted to microglia, whereas Ccl4 was expressed in both neurons and microglia (Fig. 5B). Violin plots indicated a mild, non-significant increase in Ccr5 in microglia and Ccl4 in neurons in CKD rats (Figure S6A-B). RT-qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed elevated Ccr5 and Ccl4 expression in the hippocampus of CKD rats (Figure S7A-B). Immunofluorescence staining further demonstrated increased Ccr5 expression in microglia and Ccl4 in neurons (Fig. 5C-D). Conclusively, upregulated Ccl4-Ccr5 signaling contributes to CKD-induced neuroinflammation and neuronal injury.

Ccr5 mediates microglial synaptic phagocytosis

To validate the role of Ccr5 in synaptic phagocytosis, we established Ccr5 knockdown (sh-Ccr5#1/2) and overexpression (oe-Ccr5) models. In vitro experiments demonstrated that sh-Ccr5#1 significantly inhibited synaptic phagocytosis, while overexpression enhanced this function (Figure S7C-F).

Based on the above in vitro findings demonstrating that Ccr5 regulates microglial synaptic phagocytosis, we next sought to determine whether Ccr5 similarly participates in the regulation of microglia-mediated synaptic clearance in vivo. To this end, adeno-associated viruses carrying shRNA targeting Ccr5 (AAV-shCcr5) or control shRNA (AAV-shNC) were stereotaxically delivered into the lateral ventricle before CKD model induction (Fig. 6A). Immunofluorescence staining with co-labeling of TMEM119, a marker of resident microglia, and CCR5 revealed that, compared with the sham + shNC group, the proportion of TMEM119⁺/CCR5⁺ cells was significantly increased in the CKD + shNC group. Notably, this increase was markedly reversed in the shCcr5-treated group (Figure S8A–B), indicating that hippocampal Ccr5 expression in resident microglia is upregulated under CKD conditions and can be effectively suppressed by AAV-mediated Ccr5 knockdown. We further examined the role of Ccr5 in regulating microglia-mediated synaptic engulfment in vivo. Following focal AAV-shCcr5 delivery to the hippocampus (Fig. 6A), RT–qPCR and Western blot analyses confirmed that Ccr5 expression was significantly reduced in the CKD + shCcr5 group compared with the CKD + shNC group (Figure S8C–D). Consistently, immunofluorescence staining revealed a marked decrease in CCR5 expression in Iba1⁺ microglia in the hippocampus of Ccr5-knockdown CKD rats (Fig. 6B). Importantly, Ccr5 knockdown significantly reduced the number of CD68⁺ lysosomes within hippocampal microglia compared with controls (Fig. 6C–D), indicating attenuated phagocytic activity. Confocal imaging of the presynaptic marker Synaptophysin and the postsynaptic marker PSD95 further demonstrated that Ccr5 knockdown led to a significant increase in excitatory synapse density in the hippocampus of CKD rats (Fig. 6E). Moreover, quantitative analysis of internalized synaptic puncta within CD68+/Iba1+ microglia showed that engulfment of both PSD95- and Synaptophysin-positive synapses was markedly reduced following Ccr5 knockdown (Fig. 6F-G).

Ccr5 knockdown restores neural function in CKD

Behavioral experiments showed that compared with the sham group, rats in the CKD + sh-NC group exhibited severe cognitive deficits, whereas behavioral performance was significantly restored in the CKD + sh-Ccr5 group (Fig. 7A-E). We then further examined the impact of Ccr5 knockdown on synaptic plasticity in CKD rats. Representative hippocampal LTP traces demonstrated that, compared with the CKD + sh-NC group, the fEPSP slope was markedly restored in the CKD + sh-Ccr5–treated group (Figure S9A). Consistently, the LTP time-course curves further supported this finding, showing that Ccr5 knockdown effectively alleviated synaptic plasticity deficits in CKD rats and restored synaptic transmission function (Figure S9B-C) in Ccr5-knockdown animals.

CKD-associated cognitive impairment enhances Ccl4-Ccr5 interaction

To clarify the role of the Ccl4–Ccr5 axis in microglia-mediated synaptic phagocytosis, STRING database analysis revealed a strong protein–protein interaction between CCR5 and CCL4, along with their predicted three-dimensional structures (Fig. 8A). Endogenous Co-IP further confirmed that CCR5 and CCL4 form a complex in CKD brain tissue, as reciprocal pulldown was observed with either antibody but not with IgG controls (Fig. 8B). Immunofluorescence analysis showed that, in the hippocampus of CKD rats, CCL4 was significantly enriched at synaptic sites. Colocalization of CCL4 with the postsynaptic marker PSD95 and the presynaptic marker Synaptophysin was markedly increased compared with sham controls (Fig. 8C-D), indicating accumulation of Ccl4 at excitatory synapses. Together with the Co-IP results, these findings suggest that synaptically localized Ccl4 provides a spatial cue for recognition by Ccr5⁺ microglia.

To directly test the functional interaction between Ccr5 and synaptic Ccl4, we performed combinatorial phagocytosis assays using pHrodo-labeled synaptosomes (Fig. 8E). Synaptosomes isolated from CKD rats with or without Ccl4 knockdown (Ccl4sh-NC vs. Ccl4sh) were co-incubated with primary microglia with or without Ccr5 knockdown (Ccr5sh-NC vs. Ccr5sh). Western blotting confirmed efficient Ccl4 silencing in synaptosomes (Fig. 8F). Quantitative analysis showed that Ccr5 knockdown significantly reduced synaptosome phagocytosis when Ccl4 was intact, whereas silencing Ccl4 abolished phagocytosis regardless of microglial Ccr5 status (Fig. 8G).

Overall, these results indicate that CKD-induced enrichment of Ccl4 at excitatory synapses provides a key recognition signal for Ccr5⁺ microglia, and that Ccl4–Ccr5 interaction is required for effective microglia-mediated synaptic engulfment.

Discussion

The association between CKD and cognitive impairment is well established [5], and growing evidence highlights a central role for microglia in mediating this process [7]. As the primary immune cells of the CNS, microglia play a critical role in maintaining neural homeostasis and actively participate in neuropathological processes [40]. Previous studies in CKD and related neurological conditions have largely focused on the neurotoxic effects of inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α and IL-1β [41]. In contrast, the contribution of microglia-mediated synaptic phagocytosis to CKD-associated neural dysfunction has remained poorly understood. Recent advances in single-cell transcriptomics and synaptic biology have substantially expanded our understanding of microglial involvement in synaptic remodeling under pathological conditions. Microglial synapse elimination mediated by TREM2 signaling and the CR3/C1q/C3 complement cascade has been shown to accelerate synaptic loss and cognitive decline in neurodegenerative diseases and traumatic brain injury [42]. CCR5, a CC chemokine receptor, has also emerged as a key regulator of microglial activation and synaptic pruning. Notably, CCR5 antagonists such as maraviroc have demonstrated the ability to suppress excessive microglial phagocytosis and restore synaptic homeostasis in models of AD and stroke [43, 44]. These advances provide important context for the present study. In this work, we demonstrate for the first time in a CKD model that the Ccl4–Ccr5 axis drives selective microglial engulfment of excitatory synapses, thereby extending the functional repertoire of this signaling axis from immune regulation to synaptic biology. Using single-cell transcriptomic profiling, our study systematically elucidates how the Ccl4-Ccr5 axis contributes to CKD-induced cognitive dysfunction by regulating microglia-mediated synaptic clearance. These findings provide a novel molecular perspective on microglial control of neural function and highlight the Ccl4-Ccr5 pathway as a potential therapeutic target for CKD-associated neurocognitive disorders.

Microglia are known to mediate synaptic loss via immune recognition pathways such as the CR3/C1q/C3 complement cascade, which targets neuronal synapses for phagocytosis. Dysregulated activation of this pathway results in excessive synapse removal and subsequent cognitive deficits [45]. Additionally, inflammatory cytokines released by activated microglia may impair synaptic function by disrupting NMDA receptor activity or downregulating BDNF, thereby inhibiting LTP and impairing memory formation [46]. Chemokines such as CCL3/4/5, secreted by microglia, can also act on neuronal CCR5 to enhance mTORC1 signaling, impair autophagic clearance, and promote neurodegenerative pathology [14]. Activated microglia can generate large amounts of reactive oxygen species (ROS), primarily through mechanisms such as NADPH oxidase activation, thereby inducing oxidative damage and promoting neuronal apoptosis [47].

Our findings demonstrate that activation of the Ccl4–Ccr5 axis is closely associated with exaggerated microglial reactivity and enhanced engulfment of excitatory synapses. While previous studies have primarily emphasized the role of the Ccl4–Ccr5 axis in immune regulation, our work extends its functional significance to the nervous system and reveals a disease-specific mechanism in CKD. Specifically, we show that increased Ccr5 expression in microglia is tightly linked to CKD-associated cognitive decline and directly mediates synaptic phagocytosis. Mechanistically, CKD induces enrichment of Ccl4 at synaptic sites, as evidenced by increased colocalization with the excitatory synaptic markers PSD95 and Synaptophysin. This synaptic Ccl4 accumulation likely serves as a recognition cue for CCR5⁺ microglia, driving preferential engulfment of excitatory (rather than inhibitory) synapses. This process leads to impaired LTP and cognitive deficits. The functional importance of this interaction is further supported by the pHrodo-based phagocytosis assays, in which combined knockdown of Ccr5 in microglia and Ccl4 in synaptosomes markedly suppressed synaptic engulfment. Compared with previous studies, our work not only establishes a strong association between elevated microglial Ccr5 expression and CKD-related cognitive impairment, but also provides direct evidence for its causal role in synaptic phagocytosis, thereby uncovering a novel molecular mechanism underlying neural injury in CKD. This pathway may be amplified by peripheral uremic toxin–induced BBB disruption, which enhances central inflammation [48]. Although reminiscent of TREM2-mediated synaptic loss in AD [49], the kidney disease–specific engagement of the CCL4–CCR5 axis suggests a distinct, kidney-derived inflammatory signaling route and offers a unique therapeutic window for intervention in CKD-associated cognitive dysfunction.

In targeting the Ccl4–Ccr5 axis, we prioritized knockdown of Ccr5 rather than direct silencing of Ccl4 for two main reasons. First, Ccr5 is expressed on microglia and serves as a key receptor that determines microglial responsiveness to upstream Ccl4 signals. Targeting Ccr5 allows relatively selective modulation of microglial downstream effects and directly addresses the mechanistic question of whether microglia serve as the central effector cells linking CKD to cognitive dysfunction [14, 50]. Second, Ccl4 is secreted by multiple peripheral and central cell types; systemic or intracerebral silencing of Ccl4 could trigger widespread immune and metabolic cascades, potentially compromising peripheral host defense and requiring more complex cell type–specific delivery strategies that exceed the technical feasibility of the current animal experiments [51, 52].

The functional outcomes support the validity of this strategy. Ccr5 downregulation markedly reduced CD68+ lysosomal burden within TMEM119+ microglia, decreased colocalization of PSD95/Synaptophysin with CD68, and was accompanied by partial restoration of hippocampal excitatory synapse density and alleviation of CKD-associated cognitive deficits. These findings support Ccr5 as a critical hub linking peripheral CKD-related inflammation to central synaptic pathology. From a translational perspective, Ccr5 represents a clinically accessible therapeutic target. CCR5 antagonists already in clinical use, such as maraviroc, have demonstrated neuroprotective effects in several neurological disease models [43]. Although the present study did not systematically evaluate the in vivo efficacy and safety of CCR5 antagonists in CKD models, future studies combining genetic knockdown with pharmacological blockade and more refined analysis of the microglia–synapse unit will be essential to validate the Ccl4–Ccr5 axis in microglia as a viable therapeutic target for CKD-associated cognitive impairment.

Microglia-mediated synaptic phagocytosis is increasingly recognized as a fundamental mechanism underlying neural circuit remodeling and cognitive regulation in the CNS [42]. In the present study, Ccr5 knockdown significantly attenuated microglial synaptic engulfment, which was accompanied by marked improvements in cognitive performance in CKD rats. This observation is consistent with findings from other neurodegenerative conditions, including AD, in which excessive microglial elimination of synapses contributes to cognitive impairment [53]. Collectively, these results further support the concept that renal dysfunction is closely linked to brain activity and cognitive regulation through microglia-dependent mechanisms.

Beyond local neuroinflammation and synaptic pathology, systemic alterations associated with CKD provide an important context for CNS injury. As CKD progresses, uremic toxins, such as indoxyl sulfate and p-cresyl sulfate, accumulate persistently in the circulation [54]. These toxins can activate inflammatory pathways, including NF-κB signaling, in cerebral microvascular endothelial cells [55], thereby facilitating the entry of peripheral proinflammatory factors and immune cells into the brain parenchyma and establishing a state of sustained neuroinflammation. In parallel, uremic toxins can directly act on glial cells and neurons to induce oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction, further exacerbating synaptic and network damage. Within this systemic context, the upregulation of the Ccl4–Ccr5 axis and the excessive activation of resident microglia observed in our study are likely to represent a key intracerebral amplification node linking peripheral CKD-associated inflammation and uremia-induced BBB disruption to central neuroinflammatory and synaptic pathology.

Clinical studies have shown that patients with CKD are at a higher risk of developing cognitive decline and AD, and that cognitive function may partially improve after kidney transplantation [56]. CKD is primarily driven by endothelial dysfunction, microvascular rarefaction (hypoxia-driven fibrosis), and metabolic disturbances such as accumulation of uremic toxins (e.g., indoxyl sulfate) and electrolyte imbalance. At the molecular level, CKD progression involves activation of the TGF-β/Smad3 signaling pathway, which promotes fibrosis, as well as oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction [57, 58]. In contrast, AD is mainly characterized by BBB disruption, cerebral amyloid angiopathy (CAA), and reduced cerebral glucose metabolism, partly due to amyloid-β (Aβ)–mediated inhibition of insulin signaling. Its molecular pathology centers on abnormal APP processing leading to Aβ aggregation and pathological tau modifications that cause neuronal microtubule destabilization [59, 60]. A common mechanistic link between CKD and AD is the renin–angiotensin system (RAS). Key RAS components, such as angiotensin II and angiotensin III, bind to AT1 receptors, inducing BBB damage and subsequent cytokine release, which in turn promotes M1 microglial polarization and Aβ accumulation [61]. Moreover, microglia, as resident macrophages in the brain, respond to RAS activation by triggering NADPH oxidase–dependent ROS production, driving a shift toward a proinflammatory M1 phenotype [62]. These activated microglia secrete inflammatory cytokines that accelerate neuronal death and exacerbate cognitive dysfunction in the cortex, hippocampus, and basal ganglia [63]. Furthermore, we demonstrate that elevated Ccr5 expression in hippocampal microglia correlates with cognitive decline, and that Ccl4, expressed in both neurons and microglia, functions as an upstream activator of this pathway. These findings reinforce the concept that peripheral inflammation can drive central immune responses and establish a mechanistic link between systemic disease and brain dysfunction [64, 65]. These data suggest a mechanistic bridge between CKD and AD and highlight Ccl4-Ccr5 as a key axis in neuroinflammation.

Despite providing important insights into CKD-associated brain dysfunction, this study has several limitations. First, our findings are based primarily on rodent models with relatively modest sample sizes. Although animal models are essential for mechanistic investigations, interspecies differences in physiology, immune regulation, and neurovascular architecture may limit direct extrapolation to human CKD. In addition, both sham and CKD groups received identical postoperative antibiotic treatment. Previous studies have shown that antibiotics can disrupt the gut microbiota and profoundly influence microglial maturation, morphology, immune gene expression, and key signaling pathways such as NF-κB, TLR, and mTOR, potentially confounding experimental outcomes [66, 67]. Second, hypertension is known to impair BBB integrity, trigger neuroinflammatory responses, and contribute to cognitive dysfunction [68, 69]. The CKD model used in this study is associated with elevated blood pressure, raising the possibility that hypertension may act as a confounding factor. Validation in additional CKD models that better dissociate renal dysfunction from hypertension will therefore be necessary. Third, this study primarily focused on a single molecular pathway—the Ccl4-Ccr5 axis—which may overlook other important signaling networks involved in the complex pathophysiology of CKD-related brain injury. Moreover, due to species and technical constraints, microglia-specific genetic ablation strategies were not feasible in the rat CKD model. Instead, we employed the CSF1R inhibitor PLX5622, which enabled robust depletion of hippocampal microglia over a 12-week treatment period and partially rescued CKD-induced cognitive deficits and LTP impairment, supporting a key pathogenic role for microglia. However, PLX5622 is administered systemically and may also affect peripheral monocyte-macrophage lineages, leading to potential systemic immunomodulatory effects. Thus, the observed neuroprotective benefits cannot entirely exclude indirect contributions from peripheral immune alterations, representing an important limitation. Future studies combining microglia-specific genetic depletion tools (e.g., Cx3cr1-Cre-driven systems) with more comprehensive peripheral immune profiling (such as flow cytometric analysis of splenic and circulating monocytes) in CKD models will be critical to distinguish central microglia-dependent effects from indirect peripheral immune influences and to more precisely define the causal role of microglia in CKD-associated brain dysfunction. In addition, the present study did not systematically assess the potential side effects and safety of CCR5 antagonists, which is essential for clinical translation.

Looking ahead, several directions warrant further investigation. Validation of the Ccl4-Ccr5 axis in human CKD samples is needed to determine its relevance across different populations and clinical contexts. Further exploration of CCR5-targeting pharmacological agents, including evaluation of their efficacy and safety, and eventual clinical trials in CKD patients will be essential. Moreover, investigating interactions between the Ccl4-Ccr5 axis and other signaling pathways may reveal additional layers of pathological complexity. In the long term, therapeutic strategies targeting CCR5 may not only benefit CKD-associated cognitive impairment but also hold promise for other neurodegenerative disorders, such as AD, thereby paving the way for more precise and personalized treatment approaches to improve patient quality of life.

Conclusion

This study provides novel mechanistic insight into the pathogenesis of CKD-associated cognitive dysfunction by identifying excitatory synapse loss driven by Ccl4–Ccr5–mediated microglial phagocytosis as a central pathological process. Genetic knockdown of Ccr5 effectively restored hippocampal synaptic plasticity and improved cognitive performance in CKD rats (Fig. 9), establishing a causal link between aberrant microglial activation and cognitive impairment. This study has significant scientific and clinical implications by identifying a novel therapeutic target for CKD-associated brain injury. The Ccl4-Ccr5 axis, as a key node of the kidney–brain axis, may represent a convergence point at which inhibition could simultaneously attenuate peripheral inflammation and central dysregulated synaptic pruning, thereby potentially reducing the risk of CKD–AD comorbidity. However, the present findings are limited to animal models, and their applicability to human disease remains to be validated. Future studies should further explore the therapeutic potential of targeting the Ccl4–Ccr5 axis in clinical settings and delineate its downstream signaling pathways, which may broaden the relevance of this axis to other neurological disorders.
