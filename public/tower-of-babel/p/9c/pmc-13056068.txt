Graph statistics theory of individualized quantitative genetics under haplotype-resolved genome assembly
Authors: Lidan Sun, Yangyang Bian, Dengcheng Yang, Runtian Miao, Yihan Meng, Jincan Che, Ziwei Li, Zimo Li, Haoning Wang, Shuang Wu, Juan Meng, Yu Wang, Christopher Griffin, Shing-Tung Yau, Rongling Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceQuantitative genetics has developed to a point at which its classical theory should be upgraded to cope with the surge of sequencing data. We propose a graph statistical theory that defines genetic parameters–dominance, epistasis, and pleiotropy as bidirectional, signed, and weighted interactions and estimate such interactions based on haplotype-resolved genome assembly data. This theory can encode alleles at all genetic loci throughout the genome of a diplotyped individual into omnigenic interactome networks, whose topological dissection can delimit the roadmap of how and how much each allele acts and interacts with other alleles. Using such individualized mapping details, our theory provides unique insight into full characterization of genetic architecture and, ultimately, polygenic editing on target individuals.

Complex traits are one of the most important objects to study in modern science, given their remarkable values in agriculture, biology, and medicine (1, 2). Yet, complex traits are also extremely difficult to study because they are highly multifactorial and interdependent (3–7). Quantitative genetic theory, pioneered by R.A. Fisher (8) in the early 20th century, can interpret and define the genetic basis of complex traits (9–11). Many approaches based on this theory, such as genome-wide association studies (GWAS), have been developed to understand trait inheritance and variation at the molecular level (12–15). Although hundreds to thousands of DNA variants have been identified for complex traits, these results are still far away from being utilized for genome editing in practice (16). Holding great promise for disease treatment, genome editing aims to alter the phenotypes of living organisms by modifying their underlying genes (17). However, the success of this procedure critically relies on a detailed understanding of how a gene functions. Some genes have been detected to be significant by traditional reductionist approaches, but this does not mean that these genes are editable because their significant effects may be due to positive regulation by other genes. On the other hand, the insignificance of genes in standard tests does not imply that they cannot be edited if their intrinsic effects are compromised by negative regulators. Such mechanistic causes of gene function cannot be revealed by traditional quantitative genetic theory.

Heritability estimated from mating designs or family-based designs is a widely used genetic parameter that quantifies the overall contribution of all loci to the total phenotypic variance without informing the genetic effects of a specific gene (18–20). Heritability estimated from GWAS can quantify the proportion of genetic variance explained by a single SNP to the phenotypic variance (13, 15). Although this single-SNP heritability provides a signal for the genetic effect of a SNP, it may be cofounded by the effects of other loci. As a population concept, the magnitude of this parameter depends on population type, sample size, and environmental condition, making it difficult to map it to specific individuals (21). More recently, GWAS analysis has developed to a point at which a groundbreaking theory is demanded to redefine the connotation of quantitative genetics, allowing genetic discoveries to be transformed into practice.

In previous studies, we have developed a generalized statistical Physics framework for charting fully informative genetic architecture carried by individual organisms from a GWAS population (21–24). This framework includes the combination of functional mapping, a dynamic model for mapping quantitative trait loci (QTL) mediating developmental traits (25–28), with evolutionary game theory (29), the allometric scaling law (30), and developmental modularity theory (31), equipped with a capacity to compute a complete set of interactions (interactome) covering all genes (32–36). Moreover, it can capture the full suite of properties a genetic interaction (epistasis) can possess, including bidirectionality (reciprocal causality), sign, and strength, and map these properties to a single individual (21). This framework is integrated with graph theory to produce FunGraph, a graph-statistical model encapsulating all interactive genes into mathematical networks (37). FunGraph overcomes the drawback of conventional GWAS analysis in the estimation of practical genetic parameters, making it possible to extract novel genetic information applicable to genomic transformation.

In this article, we reconstruct a FunGraph-based computing device to chart the comprehensive genetic architecture of a single organism using transcriptional data of its phased diploid genome. The motivation of this study arises from the advent of long-read DNA sequencing technologies for building de novo genome assemblies of reads sequenced from the two copies of chromosomes in a diploid individual (38–40). Transcriptional expression of maternally and paternally inherited alleles at each locus throughout the whole genome is profiled for different organs at different time points during ontogeny. By analyzing these expression data, the model can address the following fundamental questions: i) How do alleles interact with each other within loci (dominance) and between loci (epistasis) throughout the genome in a multilayer network? ii) How does an allele from each locus induce organ–organ crosstalk to form various ecological interactions? iii) How can gene regulatory networks interpret the causal mechanisms of gene function?iv) How can information about allelic and nonallelic interactions facilitate genome editing and genome engineering? We validate the utility of our model by analyzing transcriptomic data from an ornamental woody plant. We leverage and generalize our FunGraph approach to propose a theory of graph statistics that can analyze, display, and interpret non-Euclidean data that become increasingly available in the era of big data.

Results

Spatiotemporal Pattern of Transcriptional Profiles in Mei.

We produce a high-quality haplotype-resolved genome of Prunus mume (mei) using its cultivar Danfenghou (PD), containing the paternal haplotype (A) inherited from cold-unresistant P. mume and the maternal haplotype (B) from cold-resistant Prunus armeniaca (apricot) (40). The resistance of a plant to cold stress, defined by cold tolerance, refers to its ability to survive and function in cold temperature, and its cold hardiness, reflecting its capacity to withstand the coldest minimum temperature. We measure gene expression of alleles at 15,016 loci in the stem at a series of time points following cold treatment of 4 °C (cold tolerance) and under a range of subzero temperatures (cold hardiness), respectively. Fig. 1 illustrates the procedure of profiling the transcriptome of the stem during cold tolerance and cold hardiness. We employ FunGraph to examine how DNA variants mediate genome-wide transcriptional expression in intricate but well-orchestrated interactome networks associated with these two types of cold resistance.

We find that transcriptional expression of alleles at each locus in the stem displays diverse patterns of change with time for cold tolerance and with subzero temperature for cold hardiness (Fig. 2). For some gene loci, such as G00009–G00010 on chromosome 1, the expression of alleles derived from parents A and B differ dramatically for both cold tolerance and cold hardiness. Yet, such a difference only occurs in cold tolerance, but not in cold hardiness for some loci, e.g., G02510–G02447 on chromosome 7, or in cold hardiness, but not in cold tolerance for some loci, e.g., G02981–G03108 on chromosome 4. At some loci, alleles from haplotype A (mei) exhibit a higher level of expression than those from haplotype B (apricot), whereas the inverse pattern is true at other loci. At G02981–G03108, the expression of mei-inherited alleles increases with increasing coldness, whereas the expression of apricot-inherited alleles decreases with increasing coldness (Fig. 2).

Transcriptional expression of alleles changes with time and temperature in a cyclic way depending on growth stage, which is difficult to fit using an explicit mathematical equation (Fig. 2). We view individual stems of the PD diplotype as growing complex systems composed f m compartments (gene loci), each composed of a pair of alleles. We define the sum of the gene expression over the two alleles at a locus as the gene index at different time points or under different subzero temperatures. The expression level of individual alleles allometrically scales with gene index in a power law (Fig. 2). We find dramatic differences in the shape of the power curves between alleles at the same locus and among nonalleles inherited from the same origin of parent at different loci, with the degree of difference depending on gene loci. It appears that genes are differentially expressed in a pattern which can be better described as a function of gene index than as a function of time or temperature.

The genetic architecture of cold tolerance and cold hardiness is characterized by the allelic actions of individual loci and their (non)allelic interactions and pleiotropic effects. All these types of genetic effect can be quantified by FunGraph, whose derivation is given in SI Appendix.

Bidirectional, Signed, and Weighted Dominance.

In quantitative genetics, dominance is defined as the deviation of the genotypic value of the heterozygote from the mean of genotypic values of the two homozygotes at a locus (41). FunGraph can detect more information than simple dominance by quantifying how two alleles interact with each other in a directed way in order to mediate transcriptional expression (Fig. 1B). The new interpretation of dominance can make it more transformable in practice; for example, by altering the expression of one allele, the function of the alternative allele can be promoted or repressed. Moreover, this interpretation allows genetic imprinting, i.e., a phenomenon by which two alleles at a locus function differently, depending on the origin of their parents (42–44), to be characterized. We find that nine types of dominance are distributed throughout the whole genome for cold tolerance and cold hardiness (Fig. 3A). There is a small proportion of loci (7 to 8%) displaying mutualistic dominance (+/+), whereas no locus has antagonistic dominance (–/–) (Fig. 3 B, Upper panel). In conjunction with 11 to 14% of loci without dominance, this result suggests that the PD cultivar, as the combination of mei and apricot haplotypes, evolves a cooperative balance between two distinct genomes in some way. However, this balance may be maintained, to a much larger extent, by altruistic dominance (+/–) and predatory dominance (–/+), both of which together account for as high as 62 to 69% of all loci. We find that while one haplotype activates their alleles at many loci to exert predatory dominance, the second haplotype tends to do the same thing at an equal number of other loci. It appears that the distribution of different types of dominance follows a similar pattern for cold tolerance and cold hardiness, suggesting that this cultivar adopts some specific mechanisms to adapt to cold stress.

Among genotyped loci for the PD diplotype, 295 are homozygous, i.e., the same allele derived from mei and apricot, 239 and 207 of which display asymmetrical dominance (+/–, –/+, +/0, 0/+, –/0, 0/–) for cold tolerance and cold hardiness, respectively (Fig. 3 B, Lower panel). In theory, interactions between the same alleles should be symmetrical. Asymmetrical interaction between the same allele from different parents may be due to the existence of parent-of-origin effects, i.e., the effects of an allele are determined by whether the allele is inherited from the father or the mother (33, 34). We find that the distribution of parent-of-origin effects obeys a similar pattern for gene expression in cold tolerance and cold hardiness.

Fig. 3C illustrates the temporal patterns of different types of dominance expressed in the stem for cold tolerance and cold hardiness. At a locus on chromosome 8, both mei- (08aG02846) and apricot-inherited alleles (08bG02913) promote each other for cold tolerance, whereas at a locus on chromosome 1, two alleles (01aG03525 and 01bG03499) establish mutualistic dominance for cold hardiness. At a locus on chromosome 1, allele 01aG00117 from haplotype A promotes allele 01bG00117 from haplotype B for cold tolerance, but the latter has almost no effect on the former. Thus, to increase the expression level of 01bG00124 inherited from cold-resistant apricot, an intervention may be used to activate allele 01aG00117 from mei. At a locus on chromosome 1, allele 01bG00019 inherited from mei promotes allele 01aG00019 inherited from apricot for cold hardiness, whereas the latter inhibits the former. This suggests that by silencing the mei allele, the expression of the apricot allele can increase dramatically. Taken together, because the two species that constitute the diplotype of this cultivar differ in biological features, it seems that one species can strengthen the features (e.g., strong cold hardiness) of the other, by regulating the expression level of genes of that alternative species.

How gene expression changes in adaptation to cold stress should be attributed to different physiological processes, determined by different genetic machinery. We perform GO analysis for genes with various types of dominance in the cold-responsive stem (SI Appendix, Fig. S1). We find that genes exerting different types of dominance function differently, depending on whether the stem experiences cold tolerance or cold hardiness. It appears that alleles from different parents at genes affecting cell wall biogenesis and organization tend to cooperate with each other. For haplotype A from mei, predatory loci are generally associated with biological functions, such as response to abiotic stimuli, such as temperature, whereas its altruistic loci mostly mediate various metabolic processes. This suggests that the mei haplotype benefits from the apricot haplotype in terms of the capacity for apricot to resist cold temperature (45), whereas the apricot haplotype is promoted by mei’s metabolism.

Bidirectional, Signed, and Weighted Epistatic Networks.

Epistasis is an important phenomenon due to nonallelic interactions between different loci (46, 47). Although the definition of epistasis has evolved over time, its use lacks a manipulatable target. For example, epistasis is defined as additive × additive, additive × dominant, dominant × additive and dominant × dominant interactions, but none of these can be readily implemented into practical breeding or clinical interventions. FunGraph is powerful enough to capture all properties of epistasis by detecting and estimating its direction, sign, and weight, rather than only characterizing how the expression of any one allele at a locus depends on alleles at other loci. Also, FunGraph can discern the formation classes of two-locus nonallelic epistasis, i.e., epistatic formation within haplotype A (coupling epistasis A), epistatic formation within haplotype B (coupling epistasis B), and epistatic formation between haplotypes A and B (repulsive epistasis A × B) (Fig. 1C).

For the PD cultivar genotyped at 15,016 loci, with a total of 30,032 alleles, it is computationally challenging and, also, biologically unnecessary to reconstruct large-scale epistatic networks. To overcome this problem, we introduce two theories that form the basis of complex system robustness. Developmental modularity theory asserts that a complex system is divided into distinct modules in which agents are connected more tightly with each other than with those from other modules (31). Sparsity theory describes a limit on the number of links required to maintain the stability of a complex system (48), suggesting that an agent is linked with a small set of other agents rather than with a full set of agents (49, 50). Stem growth is a large ecosystem composed of different alleles at all loci. We calculate the sum of expression of all alleles as genome index and plot the expression of individual alleles against the genome index, fitted by the power equation (SI Appendix, Fig. S2).

We implement functional clustering to classify all alleles into 57 modules for cold tolerance and 83 modules for cold hardiness based on the similarity of genome index-dependent expression trajectories among all alleles (SI Appendix, Fig. S3). We further classify each module into distinct submodules and repeat this procedure until the number of alleles within a unit reaches Dunbar’s number (51), by which an agent can maintain stable and meaningful relationships with other agents. Even in the tractable Dunbar units, one allele is not necessarily fully linked to every other allele, making the Dunbar network a sparse one. To reconstruct sparse networks, we implement variable selection to choose a small set of the most significant alleles linked to a given allele. At the end, we reconstruct multilayer, multiplex, multiscale, and multifunctional interactome sparse networks (4MiSNetworks) that cover all possible nonalleles at all possible loci and their all possible existing epistatic interactions.

The 4MiSNetwork display a hierarchical 3-D structure at the top of which there are coarse-grained networks among modules, followed by coarse-grained networks among submodules, and at the bottom of which are fine-grained networks among nonalleles at different loci. We reconstruct 4MiSNetworks underlying the gene expression of all alleles in the stem for cold tolerance and cold hardiness (Fig. 4A). We find that promotion is more frequent than inhibition in the coarse-grained networks for the PD plant to respond to cold stress. An interaction is regarded as being outgoing for an allele if it affects another allele or being incoming for an allele if it is affected by another allele. We find that a small number of modules serve as hubs that exert outgoing interactions to many other modules, while the majority of modules (targets) receive incoming interactions from the hub (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Gene enrichment analysis identifies specific functions of genes within each hub module and each target module for cold tolerance and cold hardiness (SI Appendix, Fig. S5).

Fine-grained networks for each module or submodule allow us to chart a roadmap for how each allele affects, or is affected by, every other nonallele (Fig. 4A). Combining these fine-grained networks, we find that epistatic networks of the PD cultivar are highly sparse, with epistasis accounting for a minor percentage of all possible pair-wise links, i.e., 0.006% for cold tolerance and 0.005% for cold hardiness. Among a full set of epistasis, 22% are attributed to coupling epistasis A in the paternal haplotype inherited from P. mume, 28% attributed to coupling epistasis B in the maternal haplotype inherited from P. armeniaca, and 50% attributed to repulsive epistasis A × B between the paternal and maternal haplotypes for cold tolerance (Fig. 4B). The corresponding numbers of these three classes are 26%, 25%, and 49%, respectively, for cold hardiness.

We perform GLMY analysis (topological dissection of digraphs) (52, 53) of fine-grained epistatic networks for cold tolerance and cold hardiness (Fig. 4D). This analysis characterizes the qualitative topological structure of the network by counting components, holes, and voids (high dimensional holes) in a topological manifold corresponding to the network. In the four representative networks, we find that positive regulation forms much more complex architecture than negative regulation because the positive networks are characterized by more one-dimensional holes (rank of the first homology group) and even two-dimensional voids (rank of the second homology group), whereas the positive networks contain fewer one-dimensional holes and no two-dimensional voids. GLMY analysis of combined positive and negative regulation shows that the mixed networks have increasing numbers of one- and two-dimensional homological features, implying that the complexity of actual networks is not a simple sum of the complexity of positive and negative networks.

We decompose the gene expression trajectory of each allele into its independent expression trajectory and dependent expression trajectories affected by nonalleles (Fig. 4C). These decomposition curves provide a mechanistic understanding of how the level of expression of one allele is formed through epistasis in response to cold stress. For example, allele 01aG00945 inherited from the mei genome is observed to be highly expressed in both cold tolerance and cold hardiness, but this observed high expression is due to positive regulation by other nonalleles, rather than its intrinsic capacity because its independent expression level is quite low. Allele 01aG00945 is positively regulated by different regulators, i.e., 01bG02501 for cold tolerance and 01bG00916 for cold hardiness from the apricot genome (repulsive epistasis). In both cases, 01aG00945 also is negatively regulated by other alleles, although the extent of this negative regulation is lower than that of positive regulation. By silencing these negative regulators, the expression of 01aG00945 can be increased. Allele 01bG03897 from the apricot genome, has a high level of independent expression for cold tolerance, but its observed expression is low because it is negatively regulated by regulators 08aG00604 from the mei genome (coupling epistasis) and 08bG01941 from the apricot genome (repulsive epistasis). In practice, by knocking out these negative regulators, the intrinsic high expression of allele 01bG03897 can be demonstrated. Allele 06bG01770 from the apricot genome, has a low independent expression level for cold hardiness, but it is regulated, to an equally large extent, by a positive regulator (05aG02210) and a negative regulator (06bG01189), making its observed expression as low as its independent expression. In this case, by knocking out the negative regulator, this weakly expressed 06bG01770 allele can be activated, thus increasing its expression level. Altogether, we find that the pattern of gene expression in terms of independent vs. dependent expression components varies between cold tolerance and cold hardiness. Additionally, it is sharply different from the pattern of dominance in terms of allelic interactions within locus, which is comparatively similar between these two treatments.

By sequence analysis, we identify a pair of alleles 02aG02220 vs. 02bG02290 at PmCOR47 and 02aG05010 vs. 02bG05067 at PmCOR15A on mei chromosome 2 and 07aG01066 vs. 07bG01056 at PmCBF2 on mei chromosome 7. As shown previously, these genes are cold-resistant (54–58), although the way they function remains unknown. We reconstruct fine-grained epistatic networks involving these alleles for different processes of cold adaptation (Fig. 5A). Under cold tolerance, two alleles at the same gene tend to be located within different networks, except for two alleles at PmCOR47, whereas the inverse pattern is detected under cold hardiness. Two alleles at PmCOR47 are observed to be equally expressed, but their independent expression capacity differ, especially under cold hardiness, due to strong regulation they receive from other (non)alleles (Fig. 5B). While allele 02aG02220 derived from the mei haplotype is promoted by its apricot-derived allele 02bG02290 under cold tolerance, the former is inhibited by the latter under cold hardiness. The apricot-derived allele 02bG05067 at PmCOR15A displays a higher level of expression than its mei-derived counterpart under cold tolerance, although they receive different patterns of regulation. Both alleles 02aG05010 and 02bG05067 at PmCOR15A have a very high level of independent expression, but their observed expression levels reduce dramatically because they are inhibited, to a larger extent, through coupling and/or repulsive epistasis. Thus, by inhibiting these negative regulators, both cold-resistant alleles 02aG05010 vs. 02bG05067 can be activated, which provides useful information about genome editing for cold resistance. By a similar analysis of 07aG01066 and 07bG01056 at PmCBF2, we can design the way their expression can be activated to leverage cold coldness and hardiness by changing the expression of regulators.

The GLMY analysis of the networks suggests that the fine-grained networks containing cold-resistant alleles have two-dimensional voids, suggesting their high complexity in topological architecture (Fig. 5C). In general, measured by any homological dimension, the networks for cold tolerance are more complex than those for cold hardiness. The GLMY analysis further helps identify the pathways of resistance formation of cold-resistant alleles to cold stress (Fig. 5D). We find that simple topological architecture is formed around alleles 02aG02220 and 02bG02290. Under cold tolerance, these two alleles form a mutualistic dominance, each of which can be activated by several positive regulators, such as 01bG00703, in a subnetwork. Under cold hardiness, 02aG02220 is regulated by other alleles, but it also serves as a regulator to affect many nonalleles. Especially, it establishes amensalistic dominance to 02bG02290, with the latter regulated by other alleles through repulsive epistasis. Allele 02aG02220 forms a hole (circle/cycle) at dimension 1 under cold hardiness, suggesting that this allele, when leveraging cold resistance, forms a more complex topological structure.

Pleiotropic Control Over Organ–Organ Crosstalk.

Life can be regarded as a complex system with the capacity to organize itself, metabolize energy, grow and reproduce, as well as adapt and respond to environmental stimuli. This capacity is maintained through regulating and synchronizing multiple functions of different organs via interorgan communication (59, 60). As long-distance communication between different organs, interorgan crosstalk is mediated by specific genes and other stimuli (61). The effect of a gene on different organs can be viewed as pleiotropy, a phenomenon that has long been thought to play a central role in genetics, development, evolution, and medicine (59). Classic definition of pleiotropy lacks a capacity to integrate its directionality, sign, and strength into a unified context, thus limiting its mechanistic interpretation and application to understand organ–organ crosstalk.

FunGraph is able to identify how a gene induces organ–organ communication and crosstalk and further interpret the ecological function of how multiple organs in the organism work together as a cohesive whole. We use a second diplotype cultivar of mei, P. mume “Meiren” (PM), involving a cherry plum-derived haplotype (paternal) and mei-derived haplotype (maternal) to validate this functionality in FunGraph. By analyzing the expression data of alleles at 15,197 gene loci in leaves, fruit flesh, and fruit skin during ontogeny in the field, we apply FunGraph to characterize how these three organs crosstalk and how this crosstalk is genetically mediated.

From differentiated expression trajectories of a gene among three organs over time, we can infer organ–organ crosstalk networks induced by this gene. We find that more than one half of alleles (58 to 61%) pleiotropically govern leaf-fruit (flesh and skin) crosstalk in the PM cultivar, and this proportion seems to be similar for the mei haplotype and the cherry plum haplotype. The number of alleles inducing pairwise organ-organ cooperation (+/+, +/0, 0/+) is larger than that of alleles inducing competition (–/–, –/0, 0/–). There are more alleles by which leaves are altruistic to fruit flesh or fruit skin, while the latter is predatory on the former than alleles by which fruit flesh or skin are predatory on leaves. Also, there are more alleles by which fruit skin promotes fruit flesh while the latter inhibits the former than alleles by which fruit flesh promotes fruit skin. Discussion

Quantitative genetics has developed to an inflection point at which classic theory and methods should be leveraged and upgraded to meet the requirement of modern precision breeding and precision medicine in the era of sequencing data. While classic quantitative genetic theory is a population concept, whose strategy of utility is guided by mean-variance-based genetic parameters, modern breeding, and clinical decision-making need to be practiced on individual organisms or even individual organs, tissues, and cells (60, 61). For example, plant breeding aimed to improve the phenotype of plants is conducted directly on those individual target plants, rather than only by selecting superior elites from a population. In clinical trials, complex diseases can be treated by altering the expression pattern of specific genes in diseased persons without need to estimate the heritability of disease risks based on population variability. In this sense, the basic concepts and experimental practices of quantitative genetics are in dire need of a paradigm shift away from a population-based theory to an individualized analysis.

We propose a statistical strategy—FunGraph to characterize the genetic architecture of complex traits expressed in a single individual using its diplotype information. In recent years, improved sequencing technology and computational tools have made it possible to fully phase the sequence of a diploid genome, from which maternally and paternally inherited alleles at gene loci throughout the whole genome can be profiled (38–40). Complex traits of an individual are not only controlled by allelic expression at individual gene loci, but by allelic interactions within and between loci. Traditionally, dominance describes within-locus interactions, but this concept is defined as the masking of one allele for a different allele of the same gene, whose estimation is based on the comparison of the genotypic value of the heterozygote with the mean value of two homozygotes (41). Through the lens of evolutionary game theory, FunGraph extends dominance to a broader definition in which any two alleles of the same gene locus located on different haplotypes cooperate or compete against each other to determine the gene expression of a diplotype. This definition allows dominance to be understood as a bidirectional, signed, and weighted interaction. For a diplotyped mei cultivar grown under cold conditions to test its cold tolerance and hardiness, we use FunGraph to analyze transcriptional data of alleles from 15,016 loci, finding that altruistic and predatory relationships predominate within-locus interactions over the genome. At about one third of genetic loci, mei-inherited alleles promote apricot-inherited alleles, whereas the latter exert an inhibitory effect on the former. An additional one third of loci is found to display an inverse pattern; i.e., altruistic apricot-inherited alleles interact with predatory mei-inherited alleles. This similarity implies that two haplotypes from different species strive to seek a cooperative balance in the exclusion of different genomes.

FunGraph has unique power to characterize and estimate genome imprinting effects from a single diplotyped individual. The existence of asymmetric dominance loci, called imprinted genes, implies that parent-of-origin effects play a part in mediating gene expression and, therefore, complex traits. Identifying parent-of-origin effects is difficult in quantitative genetics, since it requires a large population within which the degrees of relatedness should be known. It is not surprising to detect a small portion of loci that are homozygous for the PD cultivar, given that it is an interspecific hybrid between two outcrossing species. Despite this, we still identify genomic imprinting effects from part of these homozygous loci, showing that parent-of-origin effects exist to determine expression profiles of genes in response to stress stimuli, such as low temperature (62).

Interactions between two nonalleles from different loci are attributed to epistasis, which is increasingly regarded as an important part of the genetic architecture for complex traits. However, the traditional interpretation of epistasis, i.e., the deviation of two-gene genotypic values from the sum of genotypic values at single loci (46, 47), is confounded by coupling and repulsive types. Coupling epistasis is defined by nonallelic interactions on the same haplotype, whereas repulsive epistasis describes nonallelic interactions between different haplotypes. FunGraph can not only distinguish between coupling and repulsive epistasis, but also conceptualize epistasis as bidirectional, signed, and weighted interactions between nonalleles. We identify a minor proportion of nonallele pairs (five or six among 100,000 pairs) that display epistasis, which suggests that although epistasis is a universal phenomenon (47), it does not occur densely throughout the genome. In a diplotyped cultivar of mei, about half of the epistatic pairs are in a coupling phase, whereas the other half are in a repulsive phase. A high proportion of repulsive epistasis suggests that two different haplotypes tend to evolve as a cohesive whole by intensive interactions.

Detailed information about dominance and epistasis detected by FunGraph can help to design experiments for transforming genetic discoveries into practical breeding. For example, mei-inherited allele (01aG00117) at a locus on chromosome 1 is detected to be highly expressed in response to cold stress, but indeed this is mainly because it receives strong positive regulation from its counterpart, apricot-inherited allele 01bG00124 (Fig. 3C). Thus, to maintain high expression of allele 01aG00117 in practice, allele 01bG00124 should be kept at a high expression level. In other cases, increasing the expression of one allele to better adapt to coldness can be strengthened by silencing negative regulators or by activating the expression of positive regulators. These regulators may be their allelic counterparts at the same locus (dominance) or nonalleles at different loci from the same haplotypes (coupling epistasis) or from different haplotypes (repulsive epistasis). It is interesting to find that some genes are not differentially expressed in response to cold stress, but they can play a role in cold tolerance or cold hardiness by either silencing those negative regulators or activating positive regulators, or both. FunGraph provides information of value, which is otherwise not easily identified by classic quantitative genetic theory, about how to best transform epistatic findings into practice.

Another significant merit of FunGraph is that it can identify the pleiotropic mechanisms underlying organ–organ interactions for an organism to function cohesively. In plants, proper fruit development requires coordinated crosstalk between fruits and leaves, because leaves contribute carbohydrates for the whole-plant via photosynthesis. In humans, crosstalk between adipose tissue, the liver, the skeletal musculoskeletal system, the pancreas, and the gut play a key role in pathogenesis of insulin resistance and β-cell dysfunction (63). All these interactive processes among organs are mediated by specific genes that are still unknown. FunGraph can characterize how different organs interact with each other in an ecological manner and the genomic machinery underlying organ–organ crosstalk within the ecosystem of the organism. In the PM cultivar, we have found a high proportion of genes mediating leaf-flesh mutualism, leaf-fruit skin altruism, and flesh-fruit skin coat altruism. In practice, by altering the expression of these genes, the dynamic relationships among the organs can be changed toward a more sustainable form of plant growth and development.

FunGraph constructs a detailed atlas of dominance, genome imprinting, epistasis, and pleiotropy that mediate complex traits through modularly delimited transcriptional interactome networks. While existing gene regulatory networks can only characterize the strength of interactions (correlation-based networks) or the direction of interactions (Bayesian networks), the gene networks by FunGraph can capture full interaction properties of bidirectionality, sign, and weight from any dimension of static expression data (32–36). FunGraph is flexible to integrate complex phenotypes of interest (such as cold stress-related physiological parameters), measured synchronically with transcriptional profiles, into gene networks from which causal relationships of phenotypes from gene expression can be inferred. Such networks offer the physiological interpretation of genetic control over cold coldness or hardiness.

FunGraph presents a thinking about quantitative genetics, whose functionality is strengthened by implementing GLMY theory (52, 53), an emerging computational tool in algebraic topology, aimed to dissect the topological architecture of digraphs (64–66). Classic concepts of quantitative genetics, such as genetic effects, genetic variance, heritability, and genetic correlation, have been useful for understanding the genetic architecture of complex traits at the population level, but they lack concrete information guiding genome transformation and genome engineering, especially for specific individuals. The integration of FunGraph and the GLMY theory enables the detailed information flow within complex systems to be charted, monitored, and traced. It can provide more practical information for CRISPR/Cas9 gene editing to enhance the expression of target traits for specific organisms or organs. The FunGraph approach can be modified and extended to analyze and interpret a full set of omics data, including epigenomics, proteomics, metabolomics, and phenomics, in order to chart the whole atlas of the genomic machinery characterizing how a specific individual functions or dysfunctions and how it can make a phase change from dysfunction to normal function. At that point, FunGraph will have become a leading-edge tool to enable precision breeding in agriculture and precision therapy in medicine. More importantly, given its generalized capacity to adapt to the properties of big non-Euclidean data that are increasingly available across a wide spectrum of scenarios, FunGraph can be leveraged to establish the theory of graph statistics, while its application to quantitative genetics leads to the creation of individualized quantitative genetics as a new subdiscipline.

Experimental Design

Plant Materials.

P. mume Sieb. et Zucc. (mei), native to South China, is a woody plant, characterized by early fluorescence, colorful corollas, various flower types, and diverse plant architecture. One important goal of mei breeding is to select cold-resistant cultivars that can grow in North China. One such cultivar is Danfenghou (PD), a hybrid between P. mume (paternal parent) and its cold-resistant relative, P. armeniaca (apricot) (maternal parent) (40). More recently, the haplotype-resolved phased genome of PD has been sequenced by DNBSEQ T1, BGISEQ-500, and PacBio Sequel II accurate circular consensus sequencing (CCS) platform. Using the diplotyped PD cultivar, we design and conduct an experiment to validate the utility and usefulness of our FunGraph model by characterizing the genetic architecture of transcriptional profiles for cold tolerance and cold hardiness.

We grafted scions of PD on wild apricot rootstocks. The grafted plants were subject to two cold-stress treatments, above-zero low-temperature treatment (cold tolerance) and subzero low-temperature (freezing) treatment (cold hardiness). In the first treatment, the plants were exposed to artificial low temperatures of 4 °C in a growth chamber (16 h light/8 h dark) for different durations: 0 h, 2 h, 4 h, 8 h, 12 h, 24 h, and 48 h. In the second treatment, the materials were first cold-acclimated for 7 d, followed by gradient freezing steps: 4 °C for 7 d, −5 °C for 12 h, −10 °C for 12 h, −15 °C for 12 h, −20 °C for 12 h, and −25 °C for 12 h. For each treatment, the cuttings from stems of three grafted plants were pooled as one biological replicate, with three such biological replicates at each time point and under each temperature. We measured transcriptional profiles of alleles on mei-inherited haplotype (A) and apricot-inherited haplotype (B) at a total of 15,016 high-quality gene loci expressed in the stems in each replicate from different time points (cold tolerance) and under different temperatures (cold hardiness), respectively.

We designed a second experiment to validate FunGraph by studying organ–organ crosstalk, we measured a mature tree of the other cultivar of mei, P. mume Meiren (PM), grown at Beijing Jiufeng National Forest Park (Beijing, China). PM is a hybrid between mei (paternal parent) and P. cerasifera (cherry plum) “Pissardii” (maternal parent), native to Southeast Europe and Western Asia (67). Leaves at different developmental stages were collected, including the apical leaf at the early developmental stage (LS1), the apical leaf (LS2), middle leaf (LS3), and basal leaf (LS4) from the same branch at the mid-developmental stage, and the apical leaf at the mature stage (LS5). Additionally, fruit samples were taken at 7, 14, 21, 28, 42, 56, 70, and 84 d after full blossom, including eight flesh stages (FS1–FS8) and three skin stages (SS2, SS5, SS7). Measurements at each time point include three biological replicates, each being three mixed samples from each organ of the PM. At each replicate, we measured transcriptional profiles of alleles on two different haplotypes at 15,197 gene loci.

RNA-Seq and Analysis.

In both experiments, total RNA was extracted from all samples using an RNA extraction kit (Tiangen, Beijing). Then RNA quality was determined using 2100 Bioanalyser (Agilent) and quantified using the ND-2000 (NanoDrop Technologies), and were sent to Shanghai BIOZERON for library construction and transcriptome sequencing.

For RNA-seq, libraries were prepared using a TruSeqTM RNA sample preparation Kit from Illumina (San Diego, CA), using 1 μg of total RNA. Messenger RNA was isolated with polyA selection by oligo (dT) beads and fragmented using a fragmentation buffer. cDNA synthesis, end repair, A-base addition, and ligation of the Illumina-indexed adaptors were performed according to Illumina’s protocol. Libraries were then size selected for cDNA target fragments of 200 to 300 bp on 2% Low Range Ultra Agarose followed by PCR amplified using Phusion DNA polymerase (NEB, Beijing) for 15 PCR cycles. Then, paired-end libraries were sequenced by Illumina NovaSeq 6000 sequencing platform (150 bp*2, Shanghai BIOZERON Co., Ltd). The raw paired-end reads were trimmed and quality controlled by Trimmomatic with parameters (SLIDINGWINDOW: 4:15, MINLEN:75). Clean RNA-seq reads were aligned to the genome assembly using Bowtie2 (68). Gene expression level was quantified as FPKM (fragments per kilobase of transcript per million mapped reads) value using Cufflinks (ver. 2.1.1) (69).
