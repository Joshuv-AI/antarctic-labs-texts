ECMO use in infants with bronchopulmonary dysplasia: a literature review
Authors: John Ibrahim, Nicholas Carr, Franscesca Miquel Verges, Natalie Rintoul, Robert DiGeronimo, John Daniel, Sarah Keene, Burhan Mahmood
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD) is a leading cause of chronic respiratory failure in infants. Severe BPD is associated with prolonged mechanical ventilation, pulmonary hypertension, and high morbidity and mortality. As disease severity increases, extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) is being reconsidered as a potential rescue therapy. Historically, ECMO has been reserved for neonates with reversible respiratory failure and those who required mechanical ventilation for less than 14 days, while infants with BPD were excluded. However, advancements in ECMO technology, anticoagulation strategies, and ventilatory management have led to a reassessment of its role in this population. Emerging reports suggest ECMO may serve as a bridge to recovery or lung transplantation for select infants with severe BPD and refractory respiratory failure. Careful patient selection remains critical, considering factors such as pulmonary hypertension severity, right ventricular dysfunction, and potential for lung recovery.Subject terms: Paediatrics, Respiration, Respiratory tract diseases

In this review, we aim to describe current medical management and outcomes for neonates with severe BPD and potential use for ECMO in this population in the setting of acute respiratory infections, review the historical literature describing the use of ECMO in patients with BPD including the published and registry outcomes for this population, and finally, identify knowledge gaps for future research in this population.

Current incidence and definitions of severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia

In the United States, bronchopulmonary dysplasia (BPD), the most common chronic lung disease in infants, impacts 10,000–15,000 infants annually, including ~50% of the infants born with birth weight <1000 g [1]. The main risk factors for BPD are prematurity and low birth weight [2, 3]. Approximately 50,000 extremely low gestational age newborns (ELGANs) are born each year in the USA, and ~35% (18,000) of these children develop BPD [4]. Almost 80% of infants who are born at 22–24 weeks of gestation are diagnosed with BPD [5], whereas only 20% of infants born at 28 weeks of gestation develop BPD. Despite improvements in neonatal care over the past 30 years, BPD rates have not declined.

About 25% of infants born <32 weeks gestation with moderate to severe BPD develop pulmonary hypertension (PH), leading to higher morbidity and mortality [6, 7]. Chronic respiratory [8–10] and cardiovascular impairments leading to eventual growth failure and neurodevelopmental delay are common among affected neonates. In fact, patients with BPD were found to more likely be hospitalized for wheezing, asthma [11] and recurrent respiratory infections [12].

The definition of BPD has undergone several evolutions due to the struggle of the scientific community to better define this multifactorial disease [13], with most definitions focusing on interventions, rather than the underlying pathophysiology and phenotype. In this article, we will use the term BPD interchangeably with chronic lung disease (CLD).

New BPD definitions have been proposed to address classification dilemmas; we summarize the evolution of BPD definitions in Table 1 [14–26]. It is worth mentioning that most research in BPD was focused on its prevention with little efforts directed towards its management.

BPD and respiratory infections

Respiratory viral infections constitute one of the main causes for respiratory morbidity and mortality in preterm infants, particularly those with BPD. The most important secondary cause of respiratory morbidity for these infants is respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), which affects ex-premature infants with increased severity of illness [27]. RSV is generally self-limited but can lead to hospitalization in infants with predisposing risk factors including prematurity and BPD. In a recent study examining RSV infection in the United States during the 2015-2016 season [28], it was found that among children 24 months old, the RSV-associated hospitalization rate for those with a reported preterm birth was higher than for term-born children. Similarly, RSV leads to 460,000 cases of bronchiolitis in France each year [29], where further analyses stratified by gestational age yielded rates of 15.4 per 1000 for those born at 29 weeks, 13.8 per 1000 for 29 to 31 weeks, 11.3 per 1000 for 32 to 34 weeks, and 7.4 per 1000 for 35 to 36 weeks. Extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) was first introduced as an alternative therapy to mechanical ventilation in RSV bronchiolitis for pediatric patients in a 1983-1988 published experience [30].

With the emergence of new viral respiratory tract infections including the COVID pandemic unleashed in December 2019, intensivists face new challenges given limited novel therapeutic options for younger pediatric patients, especially those with BPD. As Delta and other novel variants unfolded in the COVID-19 pandemic, there has been an increase in pediatric and neonatal patients with severe respiratory infections requiring significant escalation of care and in some cases, ECMO support [31–33]. The Extra-corporeal Life Support Organization (ELSO) supports the use of ECMO for COVID respiratory infections if indicated, with decision to cannulate based upon “existing indications and thresholds per currently published guidelines” [34].

Infants with severe BPD have lungs that are prone for severely decreased compliance with due to altered healing/repair process postnatally [35], uneven heterogenous compartments within the same the lung [36], hyperinflation phenotypes and their impact on difficulties with ventilation, inability to clear airway secretions/mucous trapping promoting secondary bacterial infections, increased risk for microaspiration, airway malacia further aggravated with intercurrent viral or bacterial infections [37, 38]. Those infants often experience prolonged respiratory support with high ventilator settings, that is further increased during acute decompensations with intercurrent infections, sepsis and aspiration. All this in addition to the associated vascular abnormalities or remodeling can lead to acute severe pulmonary hypertension crisis that frequently despite maximal therapy may necessitate ECMO support [39]. Ventilator challenges in infants with severe BPD

The mechanical ventilation strategies employed change over time due to alterations in the lung mechanics in patients with severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia (sBPD [18]. One of the major challenges in managing patients with BPD on ECMO is ventilator management due to the heterogeneity in the phenotypes of this multifactorial disease [40].

Adjustment of ventilator support, particularly the PEEP is particularly challenging in patients displaying the hyperinflation phenotype of severe BPD. In cases with auto-PEEP, intrinsic PEEP can increase when expiration is prolonged due to high airway resistance, which is further complicated by the following inspiratory effort. High intrinsic PEEP leads to elevated end-expiratory lung volumes and results in hyperinflation.

Another parameter is the longer iTime used in sBPD to allow for exhalation of the compartments with increased resistance, this can sometimes be up to five times the time constant needed for complete exhalation. A lower ventilator rate is required to achieve higher expiratory times to facilitate exhalation. Consequently, when lower respiratory rates are employed to maintain the same minute ventilation, those infants require higher tidal volumes [41].

Various factors can trigger acute cardiorespiratory deterioration in infants with BPD, prompting the use of ECMO support [37, 42–45]. Common indications for extracorporeal life support (ECLS) include hypoxemic respiratory failure, hypercapnia with severe respiratory acidosis, and circulatory failure that does not respond to maximal medical therapy. These can often occur in infants with severe BPD (sBPD) who are experiencing clinical exacerbations refractory to mechanical ventilation, sedation, muscle relaxation, cardiotropic support, or inhaled nitric oxide.

Infants with BPD often experience prolonged periods of high ventilation pressures and require significant levels of fractional-inspired oxygen (FiO2). Additionally, they may need pulmonary vasodilator therapies to address clinically significant moderate to severe pulmonary hypertension. These infants are at an increased risk of experiencing acute on top of chronic respiratory failure, as well as pulmonary hypertensive crises, particularly when they are affected by sepsis, viral or bacterial respiratory infections, or aspiration pneumonia. These acute decompensations can occur while still being admitted to the NICU (nosocomial) or after discharge. The paucity of studies reviewing ECMO and long-term outcomes for this neonatal population combined with the small number of infants with BPD requiring ECMO, adds to the complexity of decision-making by neonatologists and pediatric intensivists. There is also the prevailing notion that the survival rate for patients with BPD requiring ECMO is lower compared to other non-BPD neonatal respiratory ECMO patients.

The ventilator management strategies during ECMO may require utilizing higher peak inspiratory pressures (PIP), increased levels of PEEP, longer inspiratory times (i-Time), lower respiratory rates, and delivering larger tidal volumes, typically ranging from 8 to 12 mL/kg [36]. However, this approach contradicts the lung rest settings recommended by ELSO, raising questions about the best practices for ventilator management in this patient population cohort during ECMO [46, 47]. Establishing optimal lung rest settings during ECMO for patients with severe BPD can be challenging; what works for one patient may not be suitable for another with a different disease phenotype. Additionally, ventilator adjustments can be further complicated by the management requirements of underlying long-term pulmonary hypertension that may have worsened prior to ECMO initiation. The ventilator settings deemed appropriate for decannulation may also differ among patients based on their BPD disease phenotype. The limited evidence and absence of standardized recommendations for ventilator management in patients with BPD during ECMO support continues to contribute to the knowledge gap and significant variations in practice among intensivists.

ECMO use in neonatal respiratory diseases

Extra-corporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO) has traditionally been used in neonates with acute reversible pulmonary disease who have been on mechanical ventilation for less than 10-14 days before cannulation [48–51]. Historically, the most common conditions leading to ECMO use in neonates include congenital diaphragmatic hernia (CDH), meconium aspiration syndrome (MAS), and persistent pulmonary hypertension (PPHN) [46, 52]. However, the application of ECMO in infants continues to expand to a growing number of unique clinical applications. According to the ELSO Registry, there are eight classic categories within neonatal respiratory ECMO. These include CDH, MAS, PPHN/PFC, RDS, Sepsis, Pneumonia and Air Leak Syndrome (Table 2). Overall, the survival rate at hospital discharge for patients treated with ECMO for neonatal respiratory disease is 72%. The overall survival rate to decannulation from ECMO support is slightly higher at 83% [53].

The ELSO registry shows a decreasing trend in the number of neonatal respiratory ECMO cases compared to the early 90’s, likely reflecting the impact of newer therapies, effective management strategies, and overall better neonatal care [54]. In addition, the use of ECMO in neonatal respiratory disease has also seen a trend for longer runs with lower survival rates indicating its increasing use in more complicated and critically ill neonates with increased associated comorbidities [54]. This growing cohort of patients with unique clinical situations is currently grouped in the ELSO registry under the “Other” category. These patients have complex diagnoses typically involving longer and more complicated ECMO runs [55]. Patients with conditions previously deemed as contraindications to ECMO are now being considered despite no significant improvement in reported survival rates [51]. There is an increased use of ECMO in this patient cohort, constituting 42.6% of respiratory ECMO runs and 41.8% of cardiac ECMO runs for the period 2017-2021 [56, 57]. While ECMO for neonatal respiratory failure serves as a rescue therapy for reversible conditions, its application has evolved to include support for patients with end-stage pulmonary disease who are receiving maximal medical treatment and are awaiting organ transplantation [58]. The diagnosis of BPD could be considered under the “Other” indications for ECMO use in infants, although some may still view it as an irreversible lung disease.

ECMO use in neonatal respiratory diseases as a bridge to lung transplant

For infants under one year of age, the primary respiratory indications for lung transplantation include surfactant protein B (SP-B) deficiency and pulmonary artery/venous hypertension, secondary to alveolar capillary dysplasia with misalignment of the pulmonary veins (ACD-MPV) [59]. Pulmonary vein stenosis (PVS) is also a significant indication in this cohort. Severe bronchopulmonary dysplasia could also be considered a potential, albeit rare, indication that can be part of the diffuse lung disease (DLD) cohort, which also encompasses lung diseases caused by conditions such as cystic fibrosis, immunodeficiency, congenital heart disease (CHD), primary ciliary dyskinesia, and recurrent aspiration [58]. A single center study found no significant differences in survival to discharge or one-year survival between pediatric lung recipients requiring pretransplant invasive ventilatory support, ECMO, and those without support or only requiring non-invasive ventilation [60]. Thompson et al. [61] found higher rates of in-hospital mortality following transplant for those supported with invasive ventilation or ECMO but similar long-term survival when compared to those without a preoperative support requirement. They also observed a decline in mortality rates related to ECMO as a bridge to transplant, from 75% in 2010 to 33.3% in 2018, with no association between higher mortality rates and longer durations of pretransplant ECMO, contrary to previous reports. Although the average age of the patients included at transplant spanned late childhood and adolescence, the findings present essential insights for transplant teams working with neonates and infants. Furthermore, successful weaning from ECMO to pumpless, paracorporeal lung assist devices (Novalung, Maquet Quadrox-iD oxygenator) has been achieved in neonates [62].

Hibbs et al. in a study published 20 years ago, reviewed the outcomes for preterm infants (1 month to 12 months) with diagnosis of BPD, who required ECMO from 1990-1999 [63], excluding infants with CDH. Out of the eligible patients, only nine were identified; among these, seven survived, while two had ECMO support withdrawn due to massive cerebral hemorrhage and multiorgan failure. Of the seven surviving infants, 5 received venoarterial (VA) ECMO, and 2 received venovenous (VV) ECMO. Two infants initially on VV ECMO were switched to VA ECMO for cardiovascular instability. The total ECMO duration for those 7 patients ranged from 261 to 551 hours. The average intensive care stay after ECMO was 101 days, with a range of 28 to 181 days. The study reported that four patients experienced significant global developmental delays, while four others remained dependent on ventilators at 9 to 39 months corrected gestational age. Additionally, the study also queried the ELSO registry between 1987 to 1999. Seventy-six patients met the diagnosis of BPD prior to ECMO. Of the 76 infants (excluding congenital heart disease and CDH), 59 survived to discharge, yielding a survival rate of 77.6% with average ECMO duration of 318 hours. Although the results of this study showed a high survival rate among infants with BPD placed on ECMO albeit needing longer runs, it also suggests a high rate of severe morbidity among these infants.

Brown et al. reviewed the outcomes of ex-premature infants who developed acute hypoxic respiratory failure and required ECMO during the period from 1992-2001 [64]. Sixty-four premature neonates were included in the study, of which 51 infants survived to discharge (80%). The most common infective agent was respiratory syncytial virus (85% of cases). The median birth gestation at birth was 29 weeks and the median corrected age at the time of ECMO support was 42 weeks. Most cases occurred in the bronchiolitis season between October and March. Infants who had been discharged home from NICU before with no prior ECMO support were more likely to survive and those who needed ventilation at birth had a higher chance of experiencing disability during follow-up. The duration of ECMO support was relatively long, with a median of 229 hours. At follow up, 60% had long term neurodisability and 79% had chronic pulmonary problems. However, the median number of ventilation days for those patients was only 6 days precluding a diagnosis of severe BPD in this studied cohort. At the time of ECMO referral, baseline oxygen dependence was the most important predictor of death. However, no combination of the studied factors was linked to a mortality that would preclude ECMO support. Unfortunately, the follow up data were incomplete, leading to incomplete neurodevelopmental outcomes delineation. Additionally, data obtained on the number of ventilator days after ECMO support were insufficient for statistical analysis, given that many infants were transferred between hospitals after discharge from the ECMO center.

Another retrospective study spanning 8 years (1996–2003) evaluated ventilated infants less than 1 year of age admitted with RSV bronchiolitis [65]. This study included 151 infants, 14 of whom required ECMO, with a mean period of mechanical ventilation (MV) before ECMO of 3.9 days (± 4.5 days). Among the studied cohort, 58 children were born prematurely. The mean age at initiation of MV was 61 days (± 63 days) with infants ventilated for a mean of 7.8 days (± 7.5 days). The study excluded patients with tracheostomy. The mode of cannulation was VV-ECMO, and the mean duration for ECMO for survivors was 12.1 days. The overall survival rate was 71.4%. Bronchopulmonary dysplasia was the only predictive risk factor for ECMO use in those infants with bronchiolitis and refractory hypoxemia. Khan et al. [66] in an earlier study evaluated use of ECMO in 24 infants with RSV (17 of which were born premature at 23-40 weeks). Of the 13 infants mechanically ventilated, 7 had evidence of BPD. Sixteen patients received VA-ECMO, while eight received VV-ECMO support. The ECMO duration ranged from 32-647 hours with a median of 196 hours, while the survival rate 96%. Cranial ultrasound abnormalities were detected in three patients with only one of the 23 survivors showing evidence of developmental delay.

Hernandez et al. conducted a study using the ELSO registry, focusing on all pediatric cases from 60 days to 18 years of age who required ECMO from 1982 until 2017 and had a prior diagnosis of BPD and/or secondary PH [67]. A total of 281 pediatric patients were included, with 50% receiving treatment after 2015. The study excluded patients with congenital heart disease, CDH and congenital anomalies of the airway or lungs. Among the patients diagnosed with BPD, the most common primary cause of acute decompensation was viral respiratory infection (45.3%), with RSV constituting 50% of the cases. The median age of patients with BPD was 217 days (approximately 7 months), while those with BPD + PH had a median age of 240 days (around 8 months). In the BPD + PH group, only one patient required a tracheostomy, whereas none were reported in the BPD alone group. Additionally, one patient in the BPD + PH group was identified as being dependent on a ventilator before receiving ECLS. The survival rates to hospital discharge were 68.0% for patients with BPD and 86.7% for those with BPD and PH, both higher than the survival rate of 58% for other non-BPD pediatric patients receiving respiratory ECLS, as reported by ELSO. Despite including 83 infants (2 months to 1 year) in the BPD group and 12 cases in the BPD + PH group, the authors only stratified survival based on the patient age but did not do so for baseline ventilator settings, underlying diagnosis, and complications.

Several case reports have reported on the use of ECMO in infants with BPD [68]. Jeon et al. [69] in 2024 reported the use of ECMO in two patients with severe BPD and pulmonary HTN. The first patient was born at 27 weeks gestation, developed parainfluenza pneumonia and was placed on VV-ECMO at 8 months of life with a weight of 6 kg. This patient was decannulated after 43 days and the tracheostomy was performed a few days later. The patient was discharged home on DOL123 on home ventilation. At 6 months follow up, this patient was being considered for tracheal decannulation. The second patient was born at 23 weeks and developed severe BPD with refractory PH despite multiple medications. The patient eventually was placed on VV-ECMO at 10-month-old with a weight of 5 kg at the time of cannulation. The patient was decannulated after 68 days and tracheostomy was performed 1 week later. The patient was discharged home on mechanical ventilation at DOL 251. The authors proposed that early ECMO use may be advantageous for patients with BPD and PH who are vulnerable to prolonged MV dependence, mitigating risk for worsening lung damage and pulmonary hypertension.

A recent retrospective single center study spanning 12 years (from 2010-2022), reviewed premature infants born between 23 0/7-32 0/7 weeks of gestation, with a diagnosis of BPD who developed respiratory failure within the first 2 years of life and required ECMO [70]. Nineteen patients met inclusion criteria, with a median birth weight of 0.86 kg and a median gestational age of 26 weeks. Most of these patients (17 out of 19) were admitted after being discharged from home. Those admitted from home were managed in the Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU). The median age at time of cannulation was 12.1 months. Only one patient had tracheostomy prior to ECMO cannulation. Etiologies for acute on top of chronic respiratory failure included: viral infection (68.4%) with RSV being the most common, and bacterial (21.1%) pneumonia. Sixty-three percent were cannulated with double lumen venovenous cannula. The median time of ECMO support was 8 days. Echocardiograms revealed that 42% of the patients had evidence of elevated right ventricular pressures (RVP), with 23% continuing to show elevated RVP even after ECLS. Notably, 17% exhibited signs of new intracranial hemorrhage during or after ECLS. The survival rate to decannulation was 78.9% (15 out of 19), while the survival rate to hospital discharge was 63.2% (12 out of 19). This figure increased to 70% when excluding the two patients who required Extracorporeal Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation (ECPR). Among survivors discharged from the hospital, 42% (5/12) required new or increased respiratory support and 8.3% of survivors at discharge needed new treatment for pulmonary hypertension. About half (6 out of 12) had neurodevelopmental or behavioral concerns at one-year follow-up, while 25% (3 out of 12) had concerns beyond a year.

The knowledge gap for use of ECMO in infants with BPD

With lack of previous surveys exploring intensivists’ (neonatologists vs pediatric intensivists) perspective on using ECMO in the neonatal BPD population, paucity of published data on outcomes of neonates/infants with BPD diagnosed prior to requiring ECMO, evolving definition of BPD, the emergence of new respiratory viral infections with limited novel therapies posing further risk to this population, we sought to review the ELSO registry from 2000 until 2023 to determine clinical outcomes in neonates and infants with a diagnosis of BPD, who were placed on ECMO. We will further explore the potential use of the databases of the BPD collaborative and Children’s Hospital Neonatal Consortium (CHNC) as additional sources to review outcomes of such patient cohort. However, those databases often lack the data granularity needed to study those infants, particularly leading to ECMO support, including the ventilator settings, blood gas values, degree of pulmonary hypertension with Echo findings, etc. This highlights the need for a multicenter collaboration for an observational prospective trial across both NICU and PICU to capture all the granular data prior to, during and after ECMO, as well as their outcomes.

We also recently completed two surveys of neonatal and pediatric intensivists in the US and internationally to determine their perspective on criteria regarding ECMO candidacy and their approach to respiratory management during ECMO in patients with BPD. We will present those results in two subsequent manuscripts.

Conclusion

Given that the overall survival at discharge for all pediatric respiratory failure patients (including those without BPD) is 64% per ELSO and considering the higher survival to discharge in patients with BPD who required ECMO from previously published studies, intensivists should feel confident considering ECLS for infants with BPD who may have a history of requiring prolonged ventilation during the neonatal period. However, these results should still be interpreted with caution given the inherent selection bias of not offering ECMO to some patients with severe BPD, those with tracheostomy, and other associated comorbidities. In addition, subjectively based on our center experience, caution should also be exercised in infants with history of multiple central accesses and lung hyperinflation, which in some cases can yield their neck vessels challenging for ECMO cannulation as well as the use of higher ventilator settings during ECMO and its effect on ECMO hemodynamics. Central cannulation may be required in such patients with BPD, which can significantly increase ECMO-related morbidity and mortality.

Perhaps developing a risk stratification model taking respiratory and neurological pre-ECMO risk factors into account can aid in the decision making and families’ counselling. Parents of infants with BPD should also be counselled on the potential repercussions for requiring ECMO in this vulnerable population, including the need for higher respiratory support, potential development of long-term PH, as well as neurodevelopmental deficits post-ECMO. Discussions with parents about ECMO eligibility for infants with BPD should occur prior to an acute deterioration if feasible, particularly in infants with severe BPD who remain hospitalized to provide timely intervention based on parents’ wishes and maximize outcome. An urgent need exists for ELSO to develop respiratory guidelines for managing such patients on ECMO to help address the knowledge gap and ease the discomfort among neonatal and pediatric intensivists.
