In vivo imaging identified efficient antimicrobial treatment against Mycobacterium marinum infection in mouse footpads
Authors: Kentaro Yamamoto, Shota Torigoe, Yusuke Tsujimura, Masamitsu N Asaka, Kayo Okumura, Manabu Ato
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Mycobacterium marinum (M. marinum) is the most common causative bacteria of cutaneous non-tuberculous mycobacterial (NTM) infections, including fish tank granuloma. Treating M. marinum-caused infection takes longer than other NTM diseases because M. marinum is less susceptible to antimicrobial agents. A standard treatment regimen for M. marinum infection has not been established yet, and few in vivo experiments have been performed in mammals to evaluate the bactericidal effects of antimicrobials. In this study, we developed a noninvasive in vivo imaging method to assess the therapeutic efficacy of antimicrobials against M. marinum infection. The data obtained using fluorescent protein or bioluminescence from luciferase will offer valuable insights into bacteria visualization across various bacterial infections. Furthermore, through this imaging technique, we demonstrated that combining clarithromycin, rifampicin, ethambutol, and minocycline effectively cleared M. marinum from the footpad. Granulomas with necrotic abscesses formed on the footpad of M. marinum-infected mice, primarily due to neutrophils involved in the host’s cell-mediated immune response. Inflammatory cytokine and chemokine levels significantly increased 7 days post-infection, aligning with the footpad swelling and granuloma formation observed in the untreated group. Interestingly, immune mediators and cells induced by M. marinum footpad infection were crucial factors associated with hypersensitivity and granuloma formation, as seen in pulmonary tuberculosis. This novel imaging analysis using a cutaneous NTM mouse model might be a powerful tool for the comprehensive analysis of mycobacterial infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-75207-5.

Introduction

Over 200 species of non-tuberculous mycobacteria (NTM) have now been identified. Although they share many similarities with Mycobacterium tuberculosis (M. tuberculosis), NTM diseases form a group of mycobacterial infections distinct from tuberculosis. Certain NTM species were isolated from carp living in ponds contaminated with the sputum of patients with tuberculosis and suggested to be identical to or a subspecies of Mycobacterium marinum (M. marinum)1. In practice, M. marinum was first identified and named after being discovered in nodular lesions on the liver and spleen of dead saltwater fish at the Philadelphia Aquarium2. Human infection with M. marinum was reported as an outbreak very similar to tuberculosis with granuloma formation among public pool users3. Such outbreaks are rare today due to the presence of chlorine in water systems. However, the infection is now commonly referred to as “fish handler’s disease” or “fish tank granuloma” because of the high incidence of infections among individuals involved in water-related activities, such as handling aquariums (at home and in public aquariums), fishing, and swimming in natural bodies of water4–7. Interestingly, M. marinum and M. tuberculosis are genetically related, and experiments using goldfish as a model demonstrated that they closely mimic the pathogenesis of tuberculosis, including granuloma formation8,9. On the contrary, significantly fewer mammalian models are available for M. marinum infection. Additionally, therapeutic drug regimens for M. marinum-induced diseases are not standardized and rely solely on the experience of the attending physician and other case reports. Indeed, no large-scale clinical trials have been conducted, and researchers and governmental healthcare agencies have not taken the issue seriously because the disease often resolves spontaneously in healthy adults10.

Fluorescence imaging is employed to visualize biological materials, including microorganisms invisible to the naked eye. It uses fluorescent proteins, such as GFP from Aequorea victoria. Currently, as many as 1,000 fluorescent proteins are available, including commercial options. This advancement enables the easy visualization of cells, bacteria, and proteins using these fluorescent proteins as labeling markers. Fluorescent proteins transition from their ground state to an excited state when exposed to excitation light and emit energy in the form of fluorescence when returning to the ground state. In contrast, bioluminescence produces light energy through a chemical reaction in which an enzyme catalyzes a substrate’s oxidation, with ATP as the excitation source. Bioluminescence imaging (BLI) is widely used to analyze gene expression and includes reporter assays. Conversely, “in vivo imaging” is used to noninvasively visualize biological processes occurring within living animals. It is extensively employed in medical research for tracking the localized growth of tumor cells, the distribution of drugs in the bloodstream, and the progression of respiratory diseases11–13. Unlike its use in oncology and virology, its application in bacterial infectious diseases remains limited, and reports using in vivo imaging are even scarce regarding NTM disease.

For successful in vivo imaging of bacteria, the intensity of the light emitted from a single bacterial cell must be strong. Near-infrared light between 650 and 1,000 nm, the “biological optical window,” is suitable for deep tissue imaging in living mammals because of minimal optical absorption of hemoglobin, melanin, and water, resulting in low autofluorescence and light scattering14. Therefore, we focused on the following fluorescent proteins and BLI systems. We used iRFP670, which emits long-wavelength red fluorescence (excitation/emission maximum at 643/670 nm), with biliverdin IVα as a chromophore15. Biliverdin IVα is a metabolic product of heme mediated by heme oxygenase I (HoI), which is ubiquitously present in mammalian cells. The two BLI systems represent artificial modifications of the classical firefly luciferase Luc2 or the application of luciferases from different firefly species. The AkaBLI system includes a synthetic enzyme, AkaLuc, and is optimized for its specific substrate, AkaLumine, which produces exceptionally bright, long-wavelength light (emission maximum at 650 nm)16,17. Conversely, PmatLuc luciferase is derived from Pyrocoelia matsumurai, and despite using the same substrate as Luc2 (D-luciferin), its bioluminescence intensity (emission maximum at 567 nm) is four times brighter than that catalyzed by Luc218. Finally, the Lux system is derived from the products of the LuxCDABE genes in a single operon of Photorhabdus luminescens19,20. Because the Lux operon provides what is needed—the luciferase and its substrates—to produce bioluminescence (emission maximum at 490 nm), adding an external substrate is not necessary.

This study developed an in vivo imaging method suitable for mycobacterial diseases and applied it to investigate the pathogenesis of M. marinum infection in mice and evaluate the effectiveness of antimicrobial treatments. This is the first study evaluating various labeling tools to detect mycobacteria through in vivo imaging. The study’s findings can be applied to mycobacterial research and other microbiology studies. Our results revealed a correlation between the total count of bacilli and swelling footpad caused by the host immune response. Additionally, M. marinum infection formed granulomas with necrotic sites similar to those seen in human tuberculosis lesions. We also demonstrated that treating with minocycline combined with a standard antimicrobial regimen for pulmonary Mycobacterium avium complex (MAC) disease (clarithromycin, rifampicin, and ethambutol) reduced the burdens of M. marinum effectively.

Results

Evaluation of in vivo labeling tools in M. marinum-infected footpad of immunodeficient BALB/c-nu/nu mice

We constructed an integrated plasmid encoding iRFP670-HoI, iRFP670 (without HoI), AkaLuc, PmatLuc, and LuxCDABE. M. marinum strain ATCC 927 cells were transformed with these plasmids, and the transformants expressed fluorescence or bioluminescence. The light intensity in each labeled bacillus was measured in vitro using a plate reader. Regardless of the imaging tool used, a correlation was observed between cell number and intensity (Supplementary Fig. S1). The far-red fluorescence of bacilli expressing iRFP670 without HoI was barely detectable because of the inability to synthesize biliverdin. Hereafter, we refer to iRFP670 as expressed concurrently with HoI. Next, we injected a high dose of these transformants into the right hind footpad of immunodeficient BALB/c-nu/nu mice, which are immunodeficient due to a lack of T cell function, to evaluate the in vivo detection efficacy of the labeled M. marinum using each imaging tool. Fluorescent or bioluminescent images of each infected mouse were acquired at 1, 7, 14, 21, and 28 days post-infection (dpi), and the intensity values were measured (Fig. 1a and b). Subsequently, the colony-forming unit (CFU) on footpads was measured at 28 dpi (Fig. 1c). In mice infected with M. marinum expressing iRFP670, few fluorescence foci were detected at 1 dpi, but strong fluorescence signals were observed after 7 dpi, indicating the infection progression. On the contrary, the bacteria labeled with all three BLI tools exhibited very bright foci immediately after infection, which increased as the infection progressed. At 21 and 28 dpi, bioluminescent foci were occasionally observed on the tail and footpad that had not been directly injected with the bacteria. In mice infected with M. marinum expressing AkaLuc or PmatLuc, two out of three mice displayed no bioluminescence signal despite apparent signs of infection, such as a swollen footpad, during the time course of the in vivo imaging experiment (Supplementary Fig. S2). This loss of bioluminescence signal did not occur at any specific point during the infection. The bioluminescence of the Lux was approximately 10 times higher than that of the two BLI tools described above at all time points. Additionally, these bright foci did not disappear during the experiment. The Lux system allows noninvasive bacterial detection and evaluation of the pathogenesis of M. marinum infections in immunocompetent BALB/c mice

We applied our in vivo imaging tools to a mouse model of M. marinum disease by injecting moderate doses of iRFP670- or Lux-labeled M. marinum into the right hind footpad of immunocompetent mice. PBS was injected into the left hind footpad as an experimental control. The specific iRFP670 foci could not be detected at any time, whereas the Lux foci were detected up to 14 dpi (Fig. 2a). The two bacteria initially maintained almost the same CFU as the initial infection up to 7 dpi, but that number decreased dramatically at 14 and 21 dpi (Fig. 2b). Footpad swelling was maximal at 14 dpi, with the difference from the preinfection footpad thickness being 17.85 mm for iRFP670 and 18.75 mm for Lux. From 1 to 7 dpi, abscesses formed subcutaneously, leading to a rapid increase in the footpad thickness. However, after 7 dpi, the change in thickness (increase or decrease) slowed down relatively (Fig. 2a and c). The total flux of Lux on the footpad measured with the IVIS in vivo imager slightly decreased from 1 to 7 dpi. The reduction was so substantial after 14 dpi that no bioluminescence signal was detected by 21 dpi (Fig. 2d). During this period, a significant correlation between the footpad CFUs and the total bioluminescent flux was observed (Fig. S4). The number of colonies on agar medium containing kanamycin was also compared to those on agar medium without kanamycin. The results showed that almost all bacteria retained plasmid-derived kanamycin resistance by 21 dpi. Bioluminescence signal could be detected by IVIS from almost all colonies on agar medium without kanamycin (Table S1). Unfortunately, it was impossible to measure the fluorescence intensity of iRFP670 at all time points, as its signals were indistinguishable from the background noise. The same infection experiments were performed as described above using M. marinum without the Lux system, i.e., wild-type. There were no significant differences between Lux-labeled and wild-type M. marinum in changes in CFU and footpad thickness throughout the infection period (Fig. S5a–c). In vivo bioluminescence imaging of M. marinum was also performed using immunocompetent C57BL/6 mice. Moreover, there were no significant differences between the two mouse strains in changes in CFU, footpad thickness, and detected bioluminescence intensity (Fig. S6). Histopathological analysis of abscesses in footpad

We analyzed the abscesses formed in the footpad of Lux-labeled M. marinum-infected immunocompetent BALB/c mice histologically by hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining. Fite-Faraco (FF) staining, a method for detecting mycobacteria in specimens, was used to visualize M. marinum bacilli in the footpad section. (Fig. 2e and Table S2). At 7 dpi, a necrotic focus appeared extensively on the footpad, primarily composed of neutrophils and lymphocytes. The focus was partially surrounded by epithelial cells and fibroblasts, resembling a granuloma, the characteristic structure of mycobacterial infection. Bacilli were localized in the necrotic focus. At 14 dpi, necrotic foci were still present at the infection site, characterized by a wall structure composed of epithelial cells and fibroblasts. However, the total number of immune cells, including neutrophils recruited by inflammation within the focus, did not appear to decrease. Moreover, bacilli were still localized within the necrotic focus, but their distribution decreased. At 21 dpi, the size of the necrotic foci was significantly reduced, indicating the beginning of healing. The number of bacilli visible in the same field of view was also markedly low. Consistent with CFU data presented in Fig. 2b, the distribution of M. marinum in the footpad decreased as the infection progressed. The histopathological findings described above were also observed in the footpads of mice infected with wild-type M. marinum (Fig. S5d and Table S2). These results indicate that M. marinum abscesses possess a unique structure similar to mycobacterial granulomas, and the pathogenic focus persists even after bacteria removal.

Evaluation of therapeutic antimicrobial efficacy in M. marinum infection using the Lux in vivo imaging system

Currently, no defined clinical standard antimicrobial treatments for M. marinum infection are available. Therefore, we determined the minimal inhibitory concentrations (MICs) of several antimicrobials against M. marinum. Clarithromycin (CAM), rifampicin (RFP), and ethambutol (EB), which are standard drugs for treating pulmonary MAC disease, as well as minocycline (MINO), the empirical choice for M. marinum infection in clinical practice, significantly inhibited bacterial growth in this in vitro test (Table 1). We subsequently assessed the therapeutic efficacy of these antimicrobials against M. marinum infection through noninvasive in vivo imaging. The infected immunocompetent BALB/c mice were randomly separated into two groups: one received no treatment, and the other was treated with CAM, RFP, EB, and MINO, starting from 1 dpi. In the untreated group, bioluminescent foci were detected in mouse footpads up to 14 dpi, similar to the findings depicted in Fig. 2. In the treated group, faint bioluminescent foci were detected at 7 dpi and completely disappeared by 14 dpi (Fig. 3a). At 7 dpi, the calculated mean intensities [mean ± standard deviation (SD)] of foci was 1.2 × 107 ± 2.1 × 106 photons/s (p/s) in the untreated group and 2.2 × 105 ± 3.7 × 104 p/s in the treated group (Fig. 3b). We detected 9.3 × 107 ± 4.2 × 107 CFU in the untreated group and 6.5 × 104 ± 3.9 × 104 CFU in the treated group (Fig. 3c). After 14 dpi, the treated group exhibited no detectable bioluminescent signals in mouse footpads, and CFU fell below the experimental system’s detection limit of 2,000 CFU. These data demonstrate that the medication’s effects on bacterial clearance can be compared at 7 dpi using in vivo bioluminescent imaging. The differences observed in each parameter were statistically significant (P < 0.0001) for total flux and CFU count. Regarding the bacterial burden and its impact on footpad thickness, the effectiveness of the therapeutic agents was visually apparent at all observed time points (Fig. 3a, lower panel). The maximum increase in footpad thickness from preinfection levels reached 21.4 ± 0.2 mm in the untreated group and 6.2 ± 0.6 mm in the treated group (Fig. 3d). Cytokine and chemokine levels were high in mouse footpads infected with M. marinum

We analyzed inflammation profiles in M. marinum-infected immunocompetent BALB/c mouse footpads using bioplex. Following M. marinum-induced footpad swelling, we detected an increased production of various mediators at 7 and 14 dpi (Fig. 4 and Fig. S7). Consistent with the infiltration of neutrophils and lymphocytes in the tissue (Fig. 2e), related mediators, such as KC (CXCL1), IL-17A, G-CSF, RANTES (CCL5), MIP-1α (CCL3), and MIP-1β (CCL4), were upregulated (Fig. 4a and b). Moreover, levels of proinflammatory cytokines (IL-1α, IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF) and Th1 response-related cytokines (IL-2, IFNγ, IL-12p40, and IL-12p70) were also increased (Fig. 4c and d). Discussion

In vivo imaging techniques, which enable noninvasive visualization of pathogen quantity and location in animals, are very useful in oncology and research on infectious diseases. However, these techniques are less commonly used to study bacterial infection compared to viral infections and cancer research. This may be partly because bacterial cells are much smaller than mammalian cells, leading to a lower intensity of fluorescence or bioluminescence emitted from a single cell. Addressing this issue is crucial as it will reduce the experimental workload for researchers and the use of laboratory animals. M. marinum infection requires prolonged chemotherapy treatment, typically for months or years, except in cases of spontaneous healing21,22. The efficacy of antimicrobial therapy for M. marinum using laboratory mammals has not been extensively discussed; thus, truly effective antimicrobial agents have yet to be definitively established. In this study, we developed an in vivo imaging system for bacterial infection that allowed us to evaluate antimicrobial agents against M. marinum-caused symptoms.

We constructed one fluorescent protein-labeled and three bioluminescent-labeled M. marinum for in vivo imaging in immunodeficient BALB/c-nu/nu mice. The light scattering degree is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength, indicating that blue light (approximately 400 nm) scatters approximately ten times more readily than red light (approximately 700 nm). Therefore, iRFP670 was selected as the fluorescent protein. As the infection progressed, the iRFP670 signal became detectable on the immunodeficient mouse footpads, although nonspecific signals were also observed at noninfected sites. This nonspecific signal is thought to be emitted from naturally occurring elements in the skin or from the scattering of excitation light. Nevertheless, the signal intensity at the site of bacterial inoculation is significantly stronger than the nonspecific signal. Additionally, fluorescent labeling is very convenient for microscopic observation of bacteria and various in vitro applications. Therefore, we also evaluated iRFP670 as a label for in vivo imaging in the skin pathological study as shown in Fig. 2. The maximum in vivo total flux of bioluminescence was similar between AkaLuc and PmatLuc labels, which require administrating a substrate to produce a bioluminescent response. In vitro, PmatLuc exhibited a significantly greater intensity than AkaLuc. However, owing to AkaLuc’s longer bioluminescence wavelength (675 nm for AkaLuc vs. 567 nm for PmatLuc), the two may have ultimately achieved comparable brightness levels in vivo. Despite sufficient swelling of the mouse footpad indicating that the bacteria were not eliminated, a common issue with the two labeling methods was the abrupt loss of bioluminescence signal at unpredictable times. The lesion at the infected site became severe enough to likely obstruct that normal blood flow in the tissue. Therefore, the substrate administered to the mice, D-luciferin or AkaLumine, and the oxygen necessary for the chemical reaction may not have reached the bacteria in the footpad. The persistence of the bioluminescent signal from Lux-labeled M. marinum in the footpad throughout the experimental period further supports the above hypothesis. Although further research is necessary, a mechanism that enables sustained production of substrates and enzymes within bacterial cells could potentially offset the significant drawback posed by the short wavelength of Lux bioluminescence. Luciferins are rapidly metabolized in vivo, with most luciferase-generated signals reaching their peak and diminishing within a few minutes23–25. Thus, managing timing during experiments becomes exceedingly complex, often leading to significant data variability. However, the Lux system is brighter than other bioluminescent labels and does not require administrating additional substrates, making it particularly attractive for in vivo imaging studies of cutaneous or soft tissue bacterial infections. The number of studies utilizing Lux to label and visualize bacteria is steadily increasing26–28. However, the total intensity and CFU obtained from in vivo imaging in immunodeficient mice did not show a complete correlation using any of the labeling tools. This might be due to an excessive number of bacteria, which served as a light source, on the footpad. This excessive bacterial load also caused partial necrosis and rupture of the footpads, resulting in pus and bacterial leakage from the skin. After 14 dpi, signals from each label were also detected in the left hind footpads and tails where PBS was inoculated. These skin sites are likely injured as a result of excessive grooming or fighting. Therefore, M. marinum drainage from the broken abscess may have infected these wounds, resulting in detection of signals. Additionally, since we used immunodeficient mice in Fig. 1, the infection may have spread from the infected site on the right hind footpad in a disseminated manner. However, further research is needed to confirm these hypotheses. Immunodeficient mice were used to reliably detect the signal of each labeled M. marinum, which led to the experimental problems described above. Immunodeficient mice are not suitable for analyzing the pathogenesis of M. marinum infection or studying therapeutic strategies.

Studies on the pathological analysis of M. marinum-caused diseases in mammals are limited, which prompted us to investigate this issue using in vivo imaging. Unfortunately, iRFP-labeled M. marinum was barely detectable in the footpads of immunocompetent BALB/c mice. At 1 dpi, the slight signals were detected on the tip of the right hind footpad and the tail. However, since the risk of infection through a bite wound or dissemination is very low on the first day after M. marinum infection, these are considered nonspecific signals. Our data suggest that at least 1010 CFU of labeled bacilli are required to detect the fluorescent signal of iRFP670 with an in vivo imager. Thus, using iRFP for long-term bacterial detection in animal models of infectious diseases is challenging. As a result, its application might be limited to specific scenarios, such as short-term in vivo imaging with high-dose labeled bacteria or ex vivo quantification of viral counts in organs29,30. Eukaryotic cells, including tumor cells, exhibit higher fluorescence intensity because of their larger volume, making iRFP a potentially suitable tool for their in vivo imaging31,32.

The bioluminescence signal from Lux was significantly more sensitive than that of iRFP670. In a colony number comparison test using kanamycin-containing agar medium, the bacterial resistance rate to kanamycin was approximately 100%, even at 21 dpi. Bioluminescence signals were also detected from almost all colonies on kanamycin-free agar medium using the IVIS in vivo imager. There were few colonies for which no bioluminescence signal was detected. However, these were very small. Consequently, the total intensity might have been too low for the in vivo imager to capture the signal. These results suggest that there is little need to consider the possibility that a set of genes within the Lux system might be lost from M. marinum cells during this experiment. Moreover, there was no meaningful difference between the footpad lesions of BALB/c mice infected with Lux-labeled M. marinum and those infected with wild-type M. marinum. These data indicate that the expression of the Lux system does not affect the virulence or growth of M. marinum. In C57BL/6 mice, Lux-labeled M. marinum infection showed a similar pathological response to BALB/c mice. The differences in the immunological background of these two mice strains likely did not significantly affect the progression of M. marinum infection. Interestingly, the black skin of C57BL/6 mice did not cause any interference with the measurement of radiance in Lux BLI. These findings are encouraging for the future application of the Lux system in various studies on mycobacterial diseases27.

The data presented in Figs. 2 and 3 suggest that the signal detection threshold ranges between 10,000 and 100,000 bacilli. According to the CFU and flux value data, viable bacterial counts in the footpads of immunocompetent mice are mostly maintained or only slightly decreased for approximately 7 dpi. In immunodeficient BALB/c-nu/nu mice, the bacterial count in the footpad continuously increased, whereas it declined rapidly in immunocompetent BALB/c mice after 14 dpi. Collins et al. observed that the infection of T cell-depleted C57BL/6 mouse footpads with M. marinum resulted in disseminated infection spreading to the liver, spleen, lungs, the footpad opposite the infected one, and the tail. They also pointed out that M. marinum can cause systemic disseminated infections in immunocompromised patients33. These findings indicate that T cells are essential in eliminating M. marinum. On the contrary, footpad swelling in immunocompetent mice continued even after 14 dpi, when the number of bacteria decreased rapidly. These results suggest that inflammation persists after bacterial elimination, indicating that the inflammation, triggered by residual bacterial components, does not subside immediately following bacterial clearance. Complete Freund’s adjuvant is potent and comprises components like dead M. tuberculosis34. Dead bacilli of mycobacteria can also activate myeloid cells35. These reports indicate that mycobacterial components alone are sufficient to induce immune responses, supporting the notion that residual components of bacilli could induce inflammation even after M. marinum clearance.

The development of granulomatous structures, characterized by the aggregation of fibroblasts and giant cells following an initial acute inflammatory response driven by neutrophils, is typical of mycobacterial infections once cell-mediated immunity has been established36. Our histopathological analysis revealed this characteristic pathology in the footpad of M. marinum-infected immunocompetent BALB/c mice; at 7 dpi, abscesses formed with numerous neutrophils and an expanded necrotic area, followed by the development of a granuloma-like structure demarcated clearly from normal tissue. Granulomatous lesions, which occur at the infection site in both experimental animals, including fish and humans infected with M. marinum, have been observed9,37,38. Carlsson et al. reported that infecting the tails of C57BL/6 mice with a low dose of M. marinum led to the formation of granulomas characterized by central caseous necrosis with clear borders at 14 dpi. The CFU at the lesion site remained stable for a period after infection but began to decrease rapidly after 21 dpi. They also demonstrated that the visible skin pathology persisted even after a reduction in CFU39. Mouse footpads infected with Mycobacterium ulcerans, an NTM closely related to M. marinum and causing disfiguring necrotic skin lesions, displayed a predominance of mononuclear cell infiltrates with neutrophils in their footpad at 13 dpi, leading to the formation of granuloma-like structures40. These observations closely resemble the pathology and the healing course data presented in this study. These skin infections caused by M. marinum are called fish tank granuloma; therefore, granuloma formation may be considered a universal immune response to M. marinum in mammals. Granulomas in pulmonary tuberculosis are thought to be essential for physical defense by encapsulating the proliferating bacilli to prevent their dissemination and by inhibiting bacterial growth through oxygen deprivation41. In our M. marinum infection study, the number of bacteria contained within the granuloma-like structures continued to decrease after their formation, suggesting that such a host immune response is crucial in mycobacterial elimination. Although future studies are necessary to ascertain the extent to which the mouse model of M. marinum infection mimics the events of human M. marinum infection, our findings validate and expand the knowledge gained from other infection models, such as the M. marinum fish and M. tuberculosis mouse models.

The global standard of treatment for M. marinum infection has not been established. Many cases of successful therapy combining RFP and EB have been reported, and some reports indicate that their therapeutic effect is better than that of MINO alone10,22,42,43. These reports are minimal, and experiences with treating M. marinum disease vary by country and region, making the establishment of a standard of care more complicated. The MICs for MINO, CAM, and AMK fall within their respective blood concentration ranges, suggesting their potential as candidates for clinical treatment44. Based on the results from the MIC test and the findings reported in these studies, we adopted a four-drug combination therapy regimen, incorporating MINO with the three standard treatments (CAM, RFP, and EB) for NTM infection. The effectiveness of this treatment is evident in the measured data and the visual appearance of the infected site, indicating that the four-drug combination therapy is highly effective. The reduced number of bacteria resulting from treatment is also reflected in the bioluminescence intensity obtained from in vivo imaging, suggesting that this imaging system serves as a tool to monitor the pathogenesis of M. marinum disease and the effectiveness of treatments.

Bioplex analysis first indicated an inflammation profile based on cytokines and chemokines, key modulators of immune responses with diverse roles in inflammatory diseases. Here, we discuss the role of specific cytokines and chemokines in the response to M. marinum infections in the footpad of immunocompetent BALB/c mice. Immunopathological data indicated that M. marinum infection leads to the migration of various immune cells, including neutrophils, macrophages, eosinophils, and lymphocytes. Various chemokines associated with inflammations and tissue homeostasis, including KC, G-CSF, GM-CSF, RANTES, MIP-1, MIP-1, Eotaxin, and MCP-1, were all significantly elevated in the footpad at 7 dpi. Additionally, Th1/Th2/Th17-related cytokines were upregulated. The pathological condition with abscess and peripheral cytokines suggests the M. marinum infection induced a type of hypersensitivity reaction45.

In our mice model of cutaneous NTM using M. marinum, granuloma formation was observed from 7 dpi. The production of cytokines, such as IFN-γ and TNF-α, is fundamental in forming and maintaining granulomas and controling the disease. A significant increase in IFN-γ and TNF-α production was observed at 7 dpi in this immunological analysis. Additionally, a higher amount of IL-17 was detected in the footpad of untreated mice at 7 dpi compared with treated ones. IL-17A is also an essential factor inducing granuloma formation in the tuberculosis case46. These results indicate that the cytokines associated with granuloma formation were produced at the peripheral site of M. marinum infection as seen in lungs from M. tuberculosis-infected mice47. Moreover, previous reports indicated that TNF-α antibody treatment exacerbates M. marinum infections48, as manifested by M. tuberculosis reactivation triggered after such treatment49. M. tuberculosis and M. marinum possess similar virulence factors50; thus, mammal immune systems may induce similar responses regardless of the infection site. This finding is scientifically relevant, and further studies on the mediators required in M. marinum infections could help develop therapeutic options.

We established an in vivo imaging technique for mycobacterial disease in mice, using M. marinum as the model pathogenic bacteria. Our findings could be applied to in vivo imaging of mycobacterial and other bacterial infections. We have further elucidated the host immune response to M. marinum infection using in vivo imaging. This response is characterized by cellular immunity, predominantly involving neutrophils and leading to necrotic abscesses. Subsequently, bacteria are contained and eradicated through granuloma formation. Thus, the pathogenesis and host immune response in cutaneous NTM disease may not significantly differ from those observed in respiratory mycobacterial infections, such as tuberculosis and pulmonary NTM disease, suggesting a broad similarity in their mechanisms. This study could be a foundation to encourage further research into cutaneous NTM diseases. We also successfully demonstrated the efficacy of antimicrobial therapy in treating M. marinum disease. Thus, we believe our findings will contribute to establishing a standard treatment protocol for M. marinum, including identifying effective antimicrobial agent combinations and dosing frequencies.

Materials and methods

Strains and culture conditions

M. marinum strain ATCC 927 was grown at 32 °C under shaking conditions on enriched 7H9 (Middlebrook 7H9 medium supplemented with 0.2% glycerol, 0.05% Tween-80, and 10% OADC-enrichment) or enriched 7H10 agar (Middlebrook 7H10 supplemented with 0.5% glycerol and 10% OADC-enrichment). Whenever necessary, 25 µg/mL kanamycin (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals Co., Osaka, Japan) was added to the medium for clone selection.

Animals and ethics statement

Mice were housed in an animal biosafety level 2 containment facility at the Leprosy Research Center of the National Institute of Infectious Diseases at a 21 °C ± 2 °C room temperature and 40–60% humidity; subjected to a 12 h light/dark cycle (8 am–8 pm); and given food and water ad libitum, according to institutional guidelines. All animal experimental protocols were reviewed and approved by the National Institute of Infectious Diseases’ Animal Use and Care Committee (Permit Number: 122184-II, 123093-II), and all methods were conducted per the relevant guidelines and regulations. We followed the recommendations outlined in the ARRIVE guidelines for conducting research on animals.

Antimicrobials

The following reagents were used: clarithromycin (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals Co., Osaka, Japan), ethambutol (MilliporeSigma, MA., USA), rifampicin (DAIICHI SANKYO HEALTHCARE CO., LTD., Tokyo, Japan), and minocycline (Tokyo Chemical Industry Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The stock solutions were prepared by dissolving the reagents in sterilized water. Aliquots were prepared and stored at −30 °C.

Construction of plasmids for in vivo imaging

The plasmid vector used in this study is a pMV361-type E. coli-Mycobacterium shuttle vector that carries the hsp60 promoter, integrase gene, and kanamycin resistance gene. Additionally, the vector contains the attP region for integration into the attB site of mycobacterial chromosomal DNA51.

All plasmids were constructed by seamless cloning using the SLiCE method52. In brief, for iRFP670 and HoI coexpression, their coding regions were amplified by PCR using pBAD/HisB-iRFP670 15 or pSR43.6r53 as template DNAs, and the fragments were stitched together at their complementary regions. The resulting fragment was cloned between the NdeI and HpaI sites of the pKRB1 vector to yield plasmids encoding iRFP670 (pKRB156). The pKRB160 plasmid expressing iRFP670 without HoI was constructed by amplifying an iRFP670-only region and cloning it into the same site of the pKRB1 vector. For AkaLuc or PmatLuc expression, the coding regions were amplified via PCR using pcDNA3 Venus-Akaluc17 or pPmatLuc118 as template DNAs, respectively. The amplified fragments were then cloned into the NdeI and ClaI sites of the pKRB1 vector, resulting in plasmids encoding AkaLuc and PmatLuc, respectively (pKRB170, pKRB171). The plasmid encoding LuxCDABE (pKRB129) was appropriated from our previous study27. Plasmids and primers in this study are listed in Supplementary Tables 3 and 4, respectively.

pBAD/HisB-iRFP670 was a gift from Vladislav Verkhusha (Addgene plasmid # 45453; http://n2t.net/addgene:45453; RRID: Addgene_45453), and pSR43.6r was a gift from Jeffrey Tabor (Addgene plasmid # 63197; http://n2t.net/addgene:63197; RRID: Addgene_63197). The pcDNA3 Venus-Akaluc (cat. RDB15781) and the pPmatLuc1 (cat. RDB14359) vectors were provided by the RIKEN BRC through the National BioResource Project of the MEXT, Japan.

Evaluation of in vivo labeling tools in M. marinum

Specific pathogen-free BALB/cAJcl-nu/nu mice (7 weeks old, male) used in this experiment were purchased from CLEA Japan, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) and bred. For mouse infections, each bacterial transformant was grown using enriched 7H9 containing 25 µg/mL kanamycin and diluted at OD600 = 2.0 with PBS containing 0.05% Tween-80 (PBS-T). The right hind footpad was infected via subcutaneous injection with 50 µL of each adjusted bacterial solution (approximately 6 × 1010 CFU) under continuous anesthesia with 2.5% isoflurane (Viatris Inc., PA, USA) administered using an automated inhalation system (Muromachi Kikai Co., Tokyo, Japan). As an experimental control, the same amount of PBS-T was similarly injected into the left hind footpad. Following infection, in vivo images were acquired at 1 dpi and weekly after that until 28 dpi using IVIS Lumina LT (Revvity, Inc., MA, USA). CFU measurements in the mouse footpads were performed at 0 (indicating the injected bacterial CFU) and 28 dpi.

Pathological analysis and evaluation of therapeutic antimicrobial efficacy in M. marinum infection

Specific pathogen-free BALB/cAJcl mice and C57BL/6 mice (7 weeks old, male) used in this experiment were purchased from CLEA Japan, Inc. (Tokyo, Japan) and bred. Mice and bacteria were prepared as described above, and the right hind footpad was infected via subcutaneous injection with approximately 1 × 108 CFU in 50 µL PBS-T under continuous anesthesia. As an experimental control, the same amount of PBS-T was injected into the left hind footpad. In vivo imaging and CFU measurements of the mouse footpads were performed at 1 dpi and weekly after that until 21 dpi. The differences in footpad thickness at each time point were plotted, using preinfection thickness as a reference, with measurements taken using a vernier caliper. Antimicrobials were administered by oral gavage 5 days per week for 21 days. The drug doses were 10 mg/kg/day for RFP, 100 mg/kg/day for CAM and EB, and 25 mg/kg/day for minocycline. Three mice were used for each experimental group and at each time point of sacrifice. The experiments were performed in duplicate.

In vivo imaging and analysis

Anesthetized mice were laid in a closed chamber with clean air maintained through a membrane filter (0.22 μm pore size) designed by Yamazaki Seisakusho Ltd. (Tokyo, Japan). Mice were imaged via IVIS Lumina LT, and the fluorescent images were acquired using the auto exposure time setting. The acquired optical data were compensated for radiant efficiency based on the excitation: fluorescence intensity ratio, as measured by the imaging system. Bioluminescent images generated by AkaLuc or PmatLuc were acquired 30 min after intraperitoneally injecting 100 µL of 50 mM AkaLumine (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals Co., Osaka, Japan) or 50 mM D-luciferin (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals Co., Osaka, Japan). The bioluminescence images, including those from Lux, were acquired with an exposure of 300 s and filtered using the smoothing algorithm (5 × 5 pixels) to remove signal noises and set uniform color scales. We defined a contour region of interest with the threshold appropriately adjusted.

CFU measurements

Mouse legs were excised after mice were sacrificed under anesthesia (100 µL/mouse, medetomidine [NIPPON ZENYAKU KOGYO Co., Ltd., Fukushima, Japan], 20 µg/mL; midazolam [Maruishi Pharmaceutical Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan], 600 µg/mL; butorphanol [Meiji Animal Health Co., Ltd., Kumamoto, Japan], 1 mg/mL). The footpads were cut into small pieces with scissors and homogenized in 3-mL PBS using gentleMACS Octo Dissociator (Miltenyi Biotec, NRM, Germany) with gentleMACS M Tubes. Pulverized footpad samples were subjected to serial dilutions in PBS-T, plated on 7H10 agar plates, and incubated at 32 °C for 7 days.

Histological analysis and staining

All infected tissue samples were fixed with a 10% neutral buffered formalin solution (FUJIFILM Wako Chemicals Co., Osaka, Japan). Samples were paraffin-embedded, cut into 5-µm-thick sections, and stained with H&E and FF (Advantec Co., Ltd., Osaka, Japan). The stained samples were observed under a bright-field microscope (Olympus BX53, Tokyo, Japan) with 4× / 0.13 NA and 20× / 0.80 objective lenses.

MIC determination of various antibiotics

Antimicrobial susceptibility tests for M. marinum ATCC 927 were performed using the commercial kit BrothMIC SGM (Kyokuto Pharmaceutical Industrial Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The experiment was conducted per the manufacturer’s protocol after the bacteria from the glycerol stock were cultured on Ogawa’s medium. Briefly, bacterial colonies grown on Ogawa’s medium were inoculated in Mycobroth and incubated at 31 °C for 4 days until a turbidity of McFarland No. 1 was reached. The culture suspension was adjacent to a turbidity equivalent to that of McFarland No. 0.5, diluted with sterile water, and inoculated onto SGM plates. The bacteria were incubated at 31 °C for 7 days, and the MIC was determined by the naked eye according to the criteria in the kit’s attachment.

Cytokine and chemokine analysis

The homogenates from mouse footpads used for colony counts were adjusted to a final protein concentration of 1.2 mg/ml in PBS containing 0.2% TritonX-100 and 1× Protease inhibitor cocktail (Roche 11697498001). Proinflammatory cytokine and chemokine proteins were measured using a Bio-Plex Pro (Mouse Chemokine 31-plex panel, Bio-Rad) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Acquired data were analyzed using the Bio-Plex 200 system.

Statistical analysis

The resultant flux values of bioluminescent intensity were reported as the mean ± SD. The significance of each experiment was determined by analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student’s t-test using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, San Diego, CA).
