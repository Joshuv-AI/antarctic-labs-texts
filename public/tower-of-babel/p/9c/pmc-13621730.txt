Complex postpartum fever caused by ovarian vein thrombophlebitis combined with acute pyelonephritis: a rare case report
Authors: Wenzeng Chen, Qiuying Lin, Qiuhui Hong, Qingmin Wang, Li Jiang, Weiqun Ao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundOvarian vein thrombophlebitis (OVT) is a rare but serious postpartum complication. This report presents a case of OVT with acute pyelonephritis successfully managed at our hospital. By summarizing the clinical course, diagnostic process, and treatment—alongside a literature review—it aims to improve clinical recognition and management of this uncommon condition.Case presentationA 28-year-old woman in the early postpartum period was admitted to the Department of Obstetrics and Gynecology with persistent high fever and flank pain. Based on clinical symptoms, physical examination, and imaging findings, a diagnosis of ovarian vein thrombophlebitis (OVT) complicated by acute pyelonephritis was established. The patient responded well to the comprehensive treatment regimen, with significant reductions in inflammatory markers—white blood cell count (WBC), neutrophil percentage (NEUT), C-reactive protein (CRP), and procalcitonin (PCT). Coagulation function normalized, and both fever and flank pain were notably alleviated. She achieved full recovery after 15 days of hospitalization and showed no abnormalities at the 42-day postpartum follow-up.ConclusionsOVT complicated with acute pyelonephritis often manifests as persistent postpartum fever and flank pain, with poor response to first- and second-line antibiotics. Background

Ovarian vein thrombophlebitis (OVT) is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication during the puerperium. Epidemiological data indicate that the postpartum incidence of OVT ranges from approximately 0.05% to 0.18%, with an estimated rate of 1 in 9,000 following vaginal delivery and up to 1 in 800 after cesarean section [1]. Clinically, OVT often presents with persistent or intermittent high-grade fever and chills, and symptoms may persist for several weeks. Due to its non-specific signs and symptoms, timely diagnosis is often challenging, leading to delayed treatment and increased risk of complications.

Acute pyelonephritis is one of the most common and serious urinary tract infections during pregnancy. It results from an ascending infection of pathogens that involves the renal pelvis and parenchyma. Anatomical and physiological changes during pregnancy significantly increase the susceptibility to pyelonephritis, with a reported incidence of up to 8%, particularly among high-risk pregnant or postpartum women [2]. The disease typically presents with acute onset of high fever, chills, and marked flank pain. If not promptly diagnosed and treated, it may progress rapidly to life-threatening conditions such as septic shock.

The coexistence of OVT and acute pyelonephritis in the puerperium is rare. It typically presents as complicated puerperal fever, posing substantial diagnostic and therapeutic challenges. If not effectively managed, it may lead to severe complications including lower extremity deep vein thrombosis, pulmonary embolism, perirenal abscess, septic shock, and sepsis, potentially endangering maternal life.

In this study, we retrospectively analyzed a rare case of postpartum OVT complicated by acute pyelonephritis successfully managed in our hospital in October 2024. By reviewing the clinical course, diagnostic process, and therapeutic strategy, in conjunction with a literature review, we aim to enhance clinicians’ awareness, diagnostic accuracy, and management capabilities for this rare but critical postpartum complication.

Case presentation

A 28-year-old female patient presented to the emergency department at 07:45 on October 16, 2024, with complaints of lower abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding for 8 h at 37 + weeks of amenorrhea. She had no significant past medical history, denied any urinary tract, autoimmune, or thrombotic disorders, as well as any relevant family history or trauma. The pregnancy was not registered, and no prenatal examinations had been conducted.

On admission, physical examination revealed: temperature 37.6 °C, pulse 93 bpm, respiratory rate 20/min, and blood pressure 118/84 mmHg. Cardiac and pulmonary auscultation were unremarkable. The abdomen was distended, the uterus was non-tender, no costovertebral angle tenderness was noted, and there was no edema in either lower limb. Neurological examination was normal.

Obstetric examination: fundal height 34 cm, abdominal circumference 96 cm, estimated fetal weight approximately 3300 g, cephalic presentation, fetal heart rate 145 bpm, uterine contractions lasting 20 s every 3–4 min, and active fetal movements. Vaginal examination showed 60% cervical effacement, posterior position, soft consistency, closed cervical os, presenting part at station − 2, and intact membranes.

Preliminary diagnosis: Fever of unknown origin; G1P0, at 37 + weeks of gestation.

Auxiliary examinations: Cardiotocography (CST) showed category I tracing. Ultrasound indicated a live intrauterine fetus in left occiput anterior (LOA) position, biparietal diameter 9.1 cm, head circumference 32.3 cm, femur length 7.0 cm, abdominal circumference 33.7 cm, fetal heart rate 136 bpm, anterior-fundal placenta (grade I+), amniotic fluid index 8.5 cm, and umbilical artery S/D ratio of 2.0. Laboratory tests showed: WBC 11.2 × 10⁹/L, NEUT 87.0%, HGB 95 g/L, CRP 4.7 mg/L, and normal coagulation profile.

The patient experienced progressively intensified uterine contractions and delivered a live infant via spontaneous vaginal delivery at 13:40 on October 16, 2024. The neonate weighed 2900 g with an Apgar score of 10. The amniotic fluid was clear, labor was uneventful, and estimated blood loss was approximately 200 mL. A first-degree perineal laceration was repaired routinely. Postpartum, the patient had no complaints of dizziness, headache, cough, sputum production, chest tightness, palpitations, or back pain. Physical examination showed: temperature 37.8 °C, blood pressure 125/75 mmHg, normal heart and lung sounds, slight breast engorgement with minimal milk, firm uterine fundus one fingerbreadth below the umbilicus with no tenderness, scant dark-red lochia without odor, mildly swollen perineum, no swelling or tenderness in the lower limbs, normal ambulation, and unobstructed urination.

Based on clinical history and signs, the diagnosis was revised to: Fever of unknown origin—puerperal infection suspected; moderate iron-deficiency anemia; first-degree perineal laceration; G1P1, full-term vaginal delivery, singleton live birth. Oral cefuroxime 0.25 g twice daily was initiated for empirical antimicrobial therapy.

By 18:00 on the day of delivery, the patient’s temperature increased to 38.3 °C. Repeat laboratory tests showed WBC 18.1 × 10⁹/L, NEUT 89.6%, HGB 87 g/L, CRP 9.0 mg/L, and PCT 0.131 ng/mL. Intravenous cefuroxime sodium 0.75 g every 8 h was administered, along with fluid replacement and physical cooling for symptomatic support.

On postpartum day 2, the temperature rose to 38.6 °C, and the patient complained of right-sided flank pain with limited straight-leg raising on the right, but no urinary frequency, urgency, or dysuria. Physical exam revealed positive right costovertebral angle tenderness. Urinary tract ultrasound showed 1.0 cm separation of the right renal collecting system, without abnormal echoes. Urinalysis revealed ketone + 2, occult blood + 2, leukocytes + 2, 106 RBCs/µL, and 145 WBCs/µL. After infectious disease consultation, a urinary tract infection was strongly suspected, and antibiotics were escalated to intravenous ceftriaxone 2.0 g daily.

By postpartum day 4, the patient experienced persistent right flank pain, recurrent high fever with chills (up to 39.5 °C), but no respiratory or gastrointestinal symptoms. On exam, there was no breast tenderness, a soft abdomen, uterine fundus three fingerbreadths below the umbilicus (firm, non-tender), continued right costovertebral angle tenderness, scant non-odorous lochia, and mildly swollen perineum. Contrast-enhanced CT images (Fig. 1a) demonstrate multiple tubular hyperdense opacities surrounding the right kidney and ureter, contiguous with the right ovary, and accompanied by right-sided hydronephrosis (Fig. 1b). Urology and general surgery consultations initially favored urinary tract infection, and piperacillin–tazobactam 4.5 g every 8 h was initiated for upgraded anti-infective therapy.

Contrast-enhanced CT (a) reveals multiple tubular hyperdense opacities surrounding the right kidney and ureter (b, red arrow), accompanied by right-sided hydronephrosis (b, blue arrow)

On postpartum day 6, the patient’s fever peaked at 40.3 °C. Contrast-enhanced CT showed right-sided hydronephrosis with multiple tubular hyperdense structures surrounding the right kidney and ureter, extending to the right ovary, suggestive of ovarian vein thrombophlebitis. Renal ultrasound showed separation of the right renal collecting system (2.2 cm) (Fig. 2a) and a hypoechoic area (10.5 × 2.6 cm) between the lower pole of the right kidney and uterus, containing tubular structures, with adjacent soft tissue swelling and segmental dilatation (Fig. 2b). Lower limb venous ultrasound was negative.

Ultrasonography of the urinary system demonstrates a lesion at the lower pole and adjacent region of the right kidney (a, arrow). Panel (b) shows the right kidney (red arrow) and associated right-sided hydronephrosis (blue arrow)

Laboratory findings included: White Blood Cell Count (WBC) 8.6 × 10⁹/L, Neutrophil Percentage (NEUT) 81.9%, Hemoglobin (HGB) 77 g/L, Platelet Count (PLT) 311 × 10⁹/L, C-Reactive Protein (CRP) 201.2 mg/L, Procalcitonin (PCT) 0.430 ng/mL, normal coagulation function, and elevated D-dimer (3.56 mg/L). The diagnosis was revised to: puerperal fever—confirmed as ovarian vein thrombophlebitis complicated by acute pyelonephritis.

Due to persistent fever ranging from 37.6 °C to 40.3 °C over 7 days postpartum with suboptimal response to antibiotics, a multidisciplinary team (MDT) consultation was convened. Experts recommended switching to intravenous meropenem 1.0 g every 8 h, subcutaneous low-molecular-weight heparin 4000 IU every 12 h for anticoagulation, and right-sided ureteral double-J stent placement to relieve hydronephrosis. Owing to initial poor treatment compliance, the patient consented to and underwent ureteral stent placement on day 9 after repeated counseling.

After 5 days of treatment, the patient’s right flank pain was significantly relieved, and her temperature gradually normalized by postpartum day 12. Repeat labs showed: WBC 10.2 × 10⁹/L, NEUT 76.5%, HGB 80 g/L, PLT 542 × 10⁹/L, CRP 84.7 mg/L, PCT 0.055 ng/mL. Meropenem was discontinued on day 14 and replaced with oral faropenem 0.3 g three times daily. Low-molecular-weight heparin was switched to oral rivaroxaban 10 mg daily, combined with clopidogrel 75 mg daily, continued until 6 weeks postpartum.

The patient was discharged in stable condition on postpartum day 15. At 42-day follow-up, abdominal CT, complete blood count, coagulation profile, and D-dimer all returned to normal. The ureteral double-J stent was successfully removed at the urology outpatient clinic two months postpartum.

Multiple urine and blood cultures during hospitalization were negative. Cervical secretion cultures grew Staphylococcus epidermidis (+); ureaplasma culture showed Ureaplasma urealyticum > 10⁴ CFU/mL. Discussion and conclusions

Puerperal fever is one of the most common clinical presentations after childbirth, with a complex etiology. The primary consideration is puerperal infection, followed by differential diagnoses including upper respiratory tract infection, acute mastitis, urinary tract infection, and less commonly, thrombotic phlebitis. The classic triad of puerperal infection consists of fever, lower abdominal pain, and malodorous lochia [3]. In this case, the patient experienced an uncomplicated labor course, with no uterine tenderness or abnormal lochia on multiple postpartum examinations. Given the persistent postpartum high fever, right flank pain, and imaging findings, the final diagnosis was ovarian vein thrombophlebitis (OVT) complicated by acute pyelonephritis, a rare cause of complicated puerperal fever.

In recent years, with increased research into the etiology of puerperal fever, clinical recognition of OVT has improved. OVT is a form of septic pelvic thrombophlebitis that predominantly occurs during the puerperium and is often associated with pregnancy, miscarriage, cesarean section, pelvic surgery, malignancies, hereditary hypercoagulable states, and autoimmune diseases [4]. The pathogenesis is primarily based on Virchow’s triad: venous stasis, hypercoagulability, and endothelial injury. During pregnancy, hormonal changes disrupt the balance between procoagulant and anticoagulant factors, increasing fibrin deposition and reducing fibrinolytic activity, thereby predisposing to thrombosis [5]. Cesarean delivery particularly increases the risk of uterine infection, further exacerbating endothelial damage and thrombus formation [6].

Anatomically, the right ovarian vein is more commonly involved, accounting for approximately 80–90% of OVT cases [7]. This is likely due to dextrorotation of the gravid uterus compressing the right ovarian vein and inferior vena cava, along with the longer course, narrower lumen, and relatively incompetent valves of the right ovarian vein. OVT typically manifests between 2 and 15 days postpartum, with symptoms of high fever and lower abdominal or flank pain. Some patients may present with chills, nausea, vomiting, diarrhea, and tachycardia, but symptoms lack specificity, often leading to misdiagnosis as appendicitis, acute enteritis, or urinary tract infection [8]. Laboratory findings generally show elevated WBC, neutrophils, and CRP without specific markers. Imaging studies, especially contrast-enhanced CT and MRI, have high diagnostic value with sensitivity and specificity exceeding 90% [9]. CT imaging demonstrates dilated tubular structures within the retroperitoneum, originating from the ovary inferiorly and draining superiorly into the inferior vena cava or renal vein. Contrast-enhanced images reveal segmental or extensive low-attenuation filling defects within the enlarged ovarian vein, accompanied by circumferential wall enhancement. Ultrasound, though limited by bowel gas, body habitus, and operator experience, is a useful initial screening tool due to its convenience, low cost, and radiation-free nature [10].

Untreated OVT may result in severe complications such as pulmonary embolism, ureteral compression leading to hydronephrosis, ovarian infarction, septicemia, and sepsis. Literature reports a pulmonary embolism incidence of 13–25% and mortality rate around 4% in untreated OVT patients [11]. In this case, the patient developed early postpartum high fever peaking at 40.3 °C and was initially unresponsive to conventional antibiotics. Due to limited early recognition of OVT, anticoagulation and escalation of antibiotics were delayed, resulting in missed optimal treatment timing, highlighting the need for heightened clinical vigilance.

Acute pyelonephritis is a common urinary tract infection characterized by high fever, chills, flank pain, urinary frequency, urgency, and dysuria. Studies indicate that some pregnant patients exhibit atypical presentations with only mild abdominal pain, leading to missed diagnoses. Urinalysis and bacterial culture improve early detection [12]. It predominantly affects women of childbearing age, with an 8- to 10-fold higher incidence compared to men, and is especially prevalent during pregnancy. Risk factors include vesicoureteral reflux, urinary tract malformations, diabetes mellitus in pregnancy, and prolonged catheterization. Escherichia coli is the most frequent pathogen (70–80%), causing renal inflammation via adherence to urinary epithelium and toxin release, followed by Klebsiella, Enterococcus, and Pseudomonas aeruginosa [13].

Diagnosis of acute pyelonephritis relies on typical symptoms and laboratory indicators [14]. According to the American College of Radiology (ACR) Appropriateness Criteria (2022 update) [15], a urine culture demonstrating > 10,000 colony-forming units of a uropathogen per milliliter of urine constitutes the principal confirmatory diagnostic test. Positive blood cultures may further support the diagnosis. Imaging modalities like CT and ultrasonography assist in identifying renal pelvic dilatation, hydronephrosis, and parenchymal changes, providing reliable clinical evidence [16].

This patient received cefuroxime sodium, ceftriaxone sodium, and piperacillin–tazobactam sequentially, covering common pathogens; however, clinical response was suboptimal. This may have been due to ureteral obstruction limiting antibiotic delivery to the infection site or bacterial resistance to first- and second-line agents. Negative blood and urine cultures were likely influenced by prior antibiotic therapy.

Several high-risk factors were present, including lack of prenatal care, perineal laceration during delivery, moderate anemia, postpartum hypercoagulable state, and reduced mobility. The presumed pathophysiology involves increased blood volume and ovarian vein flow during pregnancy combined with hypercoagulability, resulting in venous stasis and endothelial injury that caused right ovarian vein thrombosis. Subsequent ureteral compression led to obstruction, right hydronephrosis. On this basis, pathogenic microorganisms—predominantly Escherichia coli—ascend retrogradely to invade the urinary tract and release a range of virulence factors, including P fimbriae, α-hemolysin, cytotoxic necrotizing factor 1, and lipopolysaccharide. These factors interact with Toll-like receptors (TLRs) expressed on the membranes of urothelial and renal epithelial cells, thereby activating downstream NF-κB and MAPK signaling pathways and promoting the production of proinflammatory mediators such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-6, ultimately leading to acute pyelonephritis and a systemic inflammatory response [17]. Right flank pain and limited leg elevation appeared on postpartum day 2, suggestive of possible deep vein thrombosis; however, mild right hydronephrosis on ultrasound was underestimated. Additionally, CT revealed thickening of the renal fascia and obscuration of the ureteral contour. The lesions exerted segmental, localized compression on the renal pelvis and ureter, resulting in ureteropelvic obstruction and subsequent hydronephrosis. Ultimately, following MDT discussion, a definitive diagnosis of ovarian vein thrombophlebitis complicated by acute pyelonephritis was established.

Despite antibiotic therapy, fever and inflammatory markers worsened until enhanced CT and MDT consultation confirmed the diagnosis. Treatment focused on relieving ureteral obstruction and initiating broad-spectrum antibiotics combined with anticoagulation. Low-molecular-weight heparin is the first-line anticoagulant, with well-established safety and efficacy. Antimicrobial therapy should consist of broad-spectrum, potent agents with adequate anaerobic coverage. Given that the pathogenic spectrum of ovarian vein thrombophlebitis is similar to that of other pelvic infections—primarily including streptococci, Enterobacteriaceae, and anaerobes—and that Escherichia coli is the most common causative organism in acute pyelonephritis during pregnancy, followed by Staphylococcus spp., Klebsiella pneumoniae, and Enterococcus faecalis, third-generation cephalosporins or higher are recommended as first-line agents in clinical practice.

The optimal duration of therapy remains uncertain; however, current guidelines recommend continuing antibiotics until clinical improvement is achieved, defervescence is sustained for at least 48 h, and leukocyte counts return to normal, which typically requires several days to one week. In cases complicated by septic embolism or bacteremia, prolonged therapy (usually 1–2 weeks or longer) may be necessary, with the duration tailored according to the clinical course and the specific pathogen identified on blood culture [18]. In the present case, following the addition of anticoagulation therapy, escalation of antimicrobial treatment to meropenem, and placement of a double-J ureteral stent, the patient’s body temperature gradually decreased, and no complications were observed during the course of treatment.

Current literature on OVT mainly consists of case reports, given its insidious onset and nonspecific presentation, posing diagnostic challenges. Haidar et al. [19] and Pumtako et al. [10] emphasize abdominal ultrasound, CT, and MRI as principal diagnostic tools, with contrast-enhanced CT offering the highest diagnostic yield, demonstrating ovarian vein wall thickening and intraluminal filling defects. Riva et al.’s systematic review [20] suggests that once diagnosed, immediate anticoagulation and third-line antibiotics yield fever resolution within 3–5 days, with average treatment duration of 1–3 months and overall success rates exceeding 95%.

This case of postpartum OVT complicated by acute pyelonephritis remains rare in the literature. The patient initially presented with right flank pain and limited leg elevation without other significant signs. Conventional anti-infective therapy for one week was ineffective, and diagnosis was ultimately established by imaging and MDT discussion, underscoring the importance of considering complex infections and thrombotic conditions in persistent puerperal fever.

In conclusion, ovarian vein thrombophlebitis combined with acute pyelonephritis is a rare but serious postpartum complication. Their interplay often leads to complicated puerperal fever. Due to atypical clinical manifestations and potential for misdiagnosis, early imaging and multidisciplinary collaboration are essential when persistent fever and flank pain occur postpartum with poor response to antibiotics. Early definitive diagnosis and targeted treatment improve prognosis and reduce complications. This successfully managed case provides valuable clinical insights for recognition and management of complex puerperal fever.
