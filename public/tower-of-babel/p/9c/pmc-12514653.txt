Correlation between imaging characteristics of deep venous thrombosis at computed tomography venography and acute pulmonary embolism
Authors: Yue Zhang, Zu-Bin Ouyang, Tian-You Luo, Fa-Jin Lv, Qi Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLower extremity deep venous thrombosis (LEDVT) is a common disease, which can lead to pulmonary embolism (PE), a potentially life-threatening complication. Computed tomography venography (CTV) has become an effective method to diagnose LEDVT. However, the correlation between CTV characteristics and the risk of PE remains inadequately understood. Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore the relationship between the imaging characteristics of LEDVT observed through CTV and the risk and involvement degree of PE.MethodsWe retrospectively included 130 patients, with 70 in the LEDVT without PE group and 60 in the LEDVT with PE group. We compared the clinical features of patients and CTV characteristics of thrombus proximal end between the two groups, and then developed and validated a logistic regression model for predicting PE. Furthermore, we explored the correlation between the thrombus CTV characteristics and the involvement degree of PE.ResultsLEDVT with PE was more prevalent in young and male patients (all P<0.05). Moreover, it was predominantly located in the right lower extremity, involved the femoral vein, and mainly exhibited non-pestle-shaped thrombus proximal end, filling sign, larger thrombus-to-vessel wall (T-V) gap maximum width and length, and higher density (all P<0.05). Multivariate analysis revealed that high thrombus density and a large T-V gap maximum width were independent predictors of PE. For this model, the area under the curve (AUC) of the training cohort was 0.874, whereas that of the external validation cohort was 0.858. Moreover, the involvement degree of PE was positively correlated with the T-V gap maximum width (r=0.618, P<0.001) and length (r=0.470, P<0.001).ConclusionsThe CTV features of LEDVT were closely associated with the presence and involvement degree of concomitant PE.Keywords: Lower extremity deep venous thrombosis (LEDVT), pulmonary embolism (PE), computed tomography venography (CTV), computed tomography angiography

Introduction

Lower extremity deep venous thrombosis (LEDVT) is a common disease with an incidence of approximately 10 per 100,000 person-years (1). Dislodgement of a thrombus originating from a lower extremity deep vein may cause pulmonary embolism (PE). Previous studies have reported that the incidence of PE in LEDVT patients was up to 72% (2); approximately 25% of PE patients experience immediate death, making PE a leading cause of cardiovascular-related mortality (3). Intervention should be performed as early as possible to improve outcomes among patients at high risk of PE. Several noninvasive clinical tools, such as the Wells score and the Geneva score (4,5), have been designed to diagnose patients suspected of PE, but the diagnosis can be delayed or even missed due to nonspecific signs and symptoms. Therefore, it is necessary to develop a reliable diagnostic method to evaluate the risk of PE at the time of LEDVT diagnosis.

Computed tomography venography (CTV) is an effective method with high sensitivity and specificity to diagnose LEDVT and can provide valuable information about thrombus (3). Although some researchers have indicated that the thrombus density of LEDVT is associated with the risk of acute PE (6,7), there remains limited knowledge regarding whether other imaging characteristics of LEDVT, as assessed by CTV, are related to the presence of PE. Considering that the site of thrombus detachment in LEDVT is typically at the proximal end, we hypothesized that the CTV characteristics of the thrombus proximal end may be closely correlated with both thrombus stability and the occurrence of PE.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the CTV characteristics of LEDVT in relation to the risk and involvement degree of PE, with a particular focus on the thrombus proximal end. We present this article in accordance with the STROBE reporting checklist (available at https://qims.amegroups.com/article/view/10.21037/qims-2025-836/rc).

Methods

Patients

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent amendments. The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of The First Affiliated Hospital of Chongqing Medical University (No. K2023-412) and the requirement for individual consent for this analysis was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

This retrospective study initially included 179 consecutive patients diagnosed with LEDVT who were admitted to center 1 from February 2020 to January 2023. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients with LEDVT confirmed via ultrasound or angiography; (II) those who underwent combined computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA) and CTV of the lower extremities; (III) those newly diagnosed with LEDVT; and (IV) those aged over 18 years.

The exclusion criteria were as follows: (I) patients presenting with bilateral LEDVT, upper extremity deep venous thrombosis (DVT), or visceral vein thrombosis; (II) those with a history of chronic PE; (III) those who initially present with a negative CTPA but subsequently exhibit signs or symptoms of PE during hospitalization, confirmed as PE through follow-up CTPA; (IV) those with poor image quality; and (V) those who are pregnant. Finally, this study comprised a total of 130 patients after excluding 32 with bilateral LEDVT, eight with prior chronic PE, three with initial negative CTPA but later confirmed PE through follow-up CTPA due to suspicious PE during hospitalization, and six with poor image quality. Based on the presence of PE, all patients were categorized into the LEDVT with or without PE groups.

Furthermore, an external validation cohort consisted of 64 consecutive patients who met the aforementioned inclusion and exclusion criteria and were admitted to center 2 from July 2021 to January 2024. Additionally, clinical data for all patients, including age, sex, duration of symptoms, comorbidities, risk factors, and initial laboratory tests conducted upon admission, were systematically collected.

Computed tomography (CT) protocols

For the CT protocols, those combining CTPA and CTV were conducted using the SOMATOM Definition Flash (Siemens Healthcare, Forchheim, Germany), SOMATOM Force (Siemens Healthcare, Forchheim, Germany), or Aquilion ONE (Canon Medical Systems, Otawara, Tochigi, Japan). All patients underwent CTPA using a bolus tracking protocol in the supine position. Nonionic contrast agent was injected at a rate of 4 mL/s from the median cubital vein, and scanning was performed when the detection region of interest (ROI) of the pulmonary trunk reached a threshold of 100 Hounsfield units (HU). The parameters for CTPA scanning were as follows: 80–100 kV, 100–200 mAs, and 5-mm slice thickness. Images were reconstructed with a thickness ranging from 1 to 1.25 mm. Furthermore, a CTV of the lower extremities was conducted. A total of 23 mL of an intravenous iodinated contrast agent was administered simultaneously through the bilateral dorsalis pedis veins after using a tourniquet at the ankle or knee level. Scanning was performed from the toes to the second lumbar vertebra in a caudocranial direction. The CTV scanning parameters were as follows: 70–80 kV, automated tube current modulation using a reference value of 200 mAs, and a slice thickness of 5 mm. Images were reconstructed with a thickness ranging from 1 to 1.25 mm.

CT image analysis

Based on axial and three-dimensional reconstructed CT images, the following CTV characteristics of thrombus within 10 cm of the thrombus proximal end were analyzed: (I) thrombus location: left or right lower limbs; (II) vein involvement: veins where the proximal end of thrombus is located, including inferior vena, common iliac vein (CIV), external iliac vein, femoral vein, and elsewhere; (III) morphology of thrombus proximal end: pestle-shaped (the thrombus proximal end is straight, with no gap between the thrombus and the vascular wall) (Figure 2A) or non-pestle-shaped (Figure 2B); (IV) filling sign: a gap is present between the thrombus and the vascular wall, which is filled with contrast agent after contrast-enhanced scanning (Figure 2C); (V) thrombus-to-vessel wall (T-V) gap maximum width: the maximum distance between the thrombus and vascular wall measured on axial CT (Figure 2D,2E); (VI) T-V gap length: the length of T-V gap measured based on multiplane recombination CT images; and (VII) thrombus density: for each patient, the largest thrombus slice within 1 cm of its proximal end was selected from axial CT images. For thrombi with homogeneous density, a square ROI was drawn to cover more than half of the total thrombus area. For thrombi with heterogeneous density, a square ROI of the largest possible size was carefully placed on the region with the highest density, excluding areas with contrast agent. The measurements were conducted thrice at three adjacent imaging levels, and the average values were calculated to ensure consistency (Figure 2F).

A scoring system proposed by Qanadli et al. was then used to assess the PE involvement degree based on CTPA images (8). The pulmonary arterial tree of both lungs was redivided into 20 segmental arteries (3, 2, and 5 to the upper, middle/lingual, and lower lobes, respectively). The presence of an embolus at a segmental artery level was scored as 1, whereas that at the proximal arterial level was scored as the sum of the distal segmental arteries (range, 0–20). The obstruction degree was scored as 0 (no obstruction), 1 (partial obstruction), or 2 (total obstruction). Hence, the involvement score was calculated as ∑ (n ⋅ d)/40, where n is the score of emboli in the pulmonary arteries and d is the degree of obstruction.

Two cardiothoracic radiologists, with >10 years of experience in vascular imaging who were blinded to patient data, reviewed and assessed all images. A consensus was reached in case of discrepancies.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using the software SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). For normally distributed continuous variables that were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, two independent samples Student’s t-test was used to compare parameters between the two groups; for nonnormally distributed continuous variables that were expressed as medians (25% and 75% quartiles), the Mann-Whitney U test was used; for categorical variables that were expressed as numbers and percentages, the Chi-squared test was performed. The associations between CTV parameters, including T-V gap maximum width and length, thrombus density, and PE involvement score based on CTPA images, were analyzed using Spearman’s correlation coefficient test. A P value of <0.05 was considered to indicate statistical significance.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed using clinical and CTV characteristics of thrombus that differed significantly between the two groups to determine the independent predictors of PE. The forward condition method was used to select the regression model, and the area under the curve (AUC), accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity were calculated to assess the effectiveness of the model. Additionally, the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) was used to evaluate the diagnostic consistency in CTV characteristics between two observers, and an ICC value of >0.75 indicated good agreement.

Results

Observer reproducibility

The two observers demonstrated fairly good agreement for all CTV characteristics. The ICC values for thrombus location, vein involvement, morphology of thrombus proximal end, filling sign, T-V gap maximum width, T-V gap length, and thrombus density were 1.000, 1.000, 0.984, 1.000, 0.981, 0.989, and 0.887 (all P<0.001), respectively.

Comparison of clinical characteristics between the two groups

Among the 130 patients with LEDVT, 70 were classified into the LEDVT without PE group (28 males and 42 females; mean age: 64.13±13.57 years) and 60 into the LEDVT with PE group (37 males and 23 females; mean age: 55.78±17.96 years). The LEDVT with PE group comprised a higher number of young and male patients (all P<0.05). However, no significant differences were observed in the duration of symptoms, other comorbidities (hypertension, diabetes, hyperlipidemia, coronary artery disease, and neurovascular disease), risk factors (obesity, smoking history, history of malignancy, long bone fracture within 1 month, and major surgery within 1 month), as well as results of initial laboratory tests conducted upon admission (prothrombin time, activated partial thromboplastin time, thrombin time, serum fibrinogen concentration, serum fibrinogen degradation product concentration, and D-dimer level) between the two groups (all P>0.05) (Table 1).

Comparison of CTV characteristics between the two groups

Thrombus in the LEDVT with PE group was predominantly located in the right lower extremity and involved the femoral vein, whereas thrombus in the LEDVT without PE group was primarily located in the left lower extremity and involved the CIV (all P<0.05). Moreover, compared to patients with only LEDVT, patients with LEDVT concomitant PE were more predominantly present with non-pestle-shaped thrombus proximal end and filling sign, had larger T-V gap maximum width and T-V gap length, and exhibited higher density (all P<0.001) (Table 2, Figures 3-5).

Development and validation of the multivariate logistic regression model

In the model integrating clinical and CTV characteristics of thrombus that significantly differed between the two groups, multivariable logistic regression analysis revealed that high thrombus density [odds ratio (OR): 1.090; 95% confidence interval (CI): 1.053–1.129; P<0.001] and large T-V gap maximum width (OR: 1.233; 95% CI: 1.108–1.372; P<0.001) were independent predictors of PE. The regression function of [P = 1/(1 + e5.789 − 0.086 × thrombus density − 0.209 × T-V gap maximum width)] demonstrated an AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of 0.874, 84.29%, 80.00%, and 88.57%, respectively.

The external validation cohort comprised 64 patients (26 males and 38 females; mean age: 64.30±15.11 years), including 33 patients with only LEDVT (9 males and 24 females; mean age: 66.61±66.61 years) and 31 patients with LEDVT concomitant PE (17 males and 14 females; mean age: 61.84±15.95 years). Table 3 illustrates the CTV characteristics used for externally validating the prediction model. The external validation cohort obtained an AUC, accuracy, sensitivity, and specificity of 0.858, 81.25%, 84.85%, and 77.42%, respectively.

Association of the PE involvement degree based on CTPA with CTV characteristics of thrombus in patients with LEDVT

The mean PE involvement score based on CTPA was 0.16±0.12 (range, 0.03–0.50). The mean T-V gap maximum width, T-V gap length, and thrombus density were 4.65±4.28 (range, 0.00–17.00) mm, 37.72±37.91 (range, 0.00–100.00) mm, and 65.40±14.01 (range, 31.00–89.67) HU, respectively. The PE involvement score based on CTPA was positively correlated with the T-V gap maximum width (r=0.618, P<0.001) and length (r=0.470, P<0.001). However, no correlation was noted between the PE involvement score based on CTPA and thrombus density (r=−0.043, P>0.05) (Figures 6,7).

Discussion

LEDVT is a common disease that can lead to fatal PE. Therefore, it is of great value to assess PE risk of patients with LEDVT in time for formulating individualized treatment strategies and reducing mortality rate during the disease process. This study initially explored the efficacy of a combined model that integrates the clinical and CTV characteristics of thrombi to predict the risk of PE in LEDVT patients; furthermore, it analyzed the relationship between these characteristics and the degree of PE involvement. There were several major findings from this study.

Regarding clinical features, our findings indicated that thrombus in the LEDVT with PE group was more frequently observed among younger male patients. Law et al. analyzed a cohort of 1,348,097 patients in Asia and reported that LEDVT concomitant with PE predominantly occurred in older female patients (9). Jeong et al. found no significant differences in age and sex between LEDVT patients with and without PE (6). These conflicting results may be due to variations in study design and sample size. Furthermore, this study revealed no significant differences in other comorbidities, risk factors, or initial laboratory tests upon admission between the two groups. This suggests that these clinical characteristics may be insufficient for accurately assessing the risk of PE.

In terms of CTV characteristics, we found that thrombi in the LEDVT with PE group were more frequently located in the right lower extremity and involved the femoral vein, whereas those in the LEDVT without PE group were predominantly located in the left lower extremity and involved the iliac vein, which aligns with findings reported by Shi et al. (10). These observations can largely be attributed to the anatomical features of lower extremity deep veins. The angle formed by the right CIV and the inferior vena cava is narrower than that of the left. Furthermore, compression of the left CIV by both the right common iliac artery and fifth lumbar vertebra may lead to luminal stenosis (11). These factors could contribute to blood flow stasis and thrombogenesis within the left CIV; however, it is less likely for such thrombi to enter into pulmonary circulation. Moreover, we concentrated on exploring the relationship between the morphological characteristics of thrombus proximal end within 10 cm and their association with PE risk. Our results indicated that a non-pestle-shaped thrombus proximal end along with a filling sign are strong indicators of PE secondary to LEDVT. The two patterns indicated a poor fit between the thrombus and the vascular walls; thus, a wider and longer T-V gap increased the likelihood of thrombus detachment. Conversely, a pestle-shaped thrombus proximal end and the absence of a filling sign highly suggested that the thrombus was firmly attached to the vessel wall, likely due to inflammatory processes and fibrosis, which are associated with a reduced risk of PE (12,13). Yang et al. reported that the length ratio of the floating portion to the attached portion correlated with break-off risk in cases of aortic floating thrombus (14), supporting our findings. This study demonstrated that high-density thrombi were more prone to detachment, consistent with the observations of Jeong et al. (6). Multiple studies have investigated the association between CT attenuation values of stroke thrombi and their histopathological composition, showing that erythrocyte-dominant thrombi exhibit higher CT values compared to platelet-rich or fibrin-rich thrombi (15-18). Similarly, prior studies have indicated that higher CT attenuation values in LEDVT correlate with greater red blood cell content and lower fibrin levels (6,7,19). Thus, high-density thrombi—likely composed mainly of loosely packed red blood cells—may represent an early stage of thrombus formation, typically exhibiting high susceptibility to detachment (20). As red blood cells lyse and convert to methemoglobin, fibrin forms dense interlinked networks, and collagen deposits accumulate, the thrombus becomes more stable with reduced detachment risk (21). In this advanced stage, thrombi frequently present as a lower density on CTV. Previous studies (22,23) have indicated that early ambulation promotes blood flow, reduces thrombus extension, and relieves acute symptoms such as swelling and pain in the affected limb. However, when a thrombus shows the above CTV characteristics related to PE, early ambulation should be approached with caution to prevent thrombus dislodgement.

Multivariate logistic regression analysis indicated that high thrombus density and a large T-V gap maximum width were independent predictors of PE. This model exhibited good performance, achieving an AUC of 0.874 and an accuracy of 84.29% in the training cohort, as well as an AUC of 0.858 and an accuracy of 81.25% in the external validation cohort. In the future, we propose a structured approach to translate our findings into clinical use: first, categorization of key risk factors identified through logistic regression; second, assignment of weighted scores to these factors based on the model’s β-coefficients; and third, calculation of a total PE risk score for each patient. This framework could help to develop a simplified PE risk score sheet to assist clinicians in quickly estimating PE risk in patients with LEDVT.

Furthermore, our study demonstrated that the PE involvement score derived from CTPA exhibited a positive correlation with both the maximum width and length of the T-V gap, indicating that these two CTV signs can effectively predict the degree of PE involvement. A previous study (24) analyzed the clot burden of LEDVT and PE using scoring systems proposed by Ouriel et al. (25) and Qanadli et al. (8), respectively. It indicated that LEDVT clot burden does not accurately reflect PE clot burden. In contrast, our results showed that these CTV characteristics of thrombus proximal end are more effective in predicting the involvement degree of PE compared to LEDVT clot burden.

Our study has several limitations. First, it was a retrospective study that excluded patients with bilateral LEDVT, which might have introduced selection bias. Second, we hypothesized that PE was caused by LEDVT dislodgement, but this hypothesis ignored the fact that the thrombus in pulmonary arteries might originate in situ or elsewhere. To address this concern, we made efforts to exclude patients with bilateral LEDVT, upper extremity DVT, and visceral vein thrombosis. Third, considering concerns about radiation exposure and related costs, we did not conduct follow-up CTPA for patients who were initially diagnosed as not having PE and remained asymptomatic for PE throughout their hospitalization. This approach inherently carries the risk of missing PEs that may occur after the initial CTPA. Fourth, although pregnant women have a high incidence of LEDVT, they were excluded from our study because of the potential radiation risks associated with CT examinations. Finally, the interpretation of CT signs was inherently subjective; therefore, incorporating machine learning techniques could enhance the accuracy of predicting PE risk in patients with LEDVT. Thus, further studies are warranted.

Conclusions

Taken together, our study results indicate that the CTV features of LEDVT were closely associated with the risk and involvement degree of concomitant PE. A comprehensive understanding of these characteristics may promote the development of individual treatment strategies, and ultimately improve the prognosis of patients.
