Domvanalimab plus zimberelimab in unresectable and immunotherapy refractory biliary tract cancers: a phase 2 trial
Authors: David Hsiehchen, John H Lohrey, Radhika Kainthla, Syed Mohammad Ali Kazmi, Nilesh Verma, Carrie Manwaring, Chul Ahn, Hao Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractT cell immunoglobulin and ITIM domain (TIGIT) is an inhibitory receptor that promotes immune evasion, and dual blockade of TIGIT and PD-1 may restore antitumor immunity. Here we report the primary analysis of the biliary tract cancer cohort from a phase 2 trial (ClinicalTrials.gov registration: NCT05724563) evaluating the Fc-silent anti-TIGIT antibody domvanalimab plus the anti-PD-1 antibody zimberelimab in patients with biliary tract cancers (BTCs) refractory to prior PD-1/L1 therapy. The primary endpoint was confirmed objective response rate (ORR); secondary endpoints included disease control rate (DCR), safety, 6-month progression-free survival (PFS), duration of response (DoR), and overall survival. Among 29 evaluable patients, the confirmed ORR was 10.3% (95% CI, 2.2–27.4%), with a DCR of 44.8%. The 6-month PFS rate was 17.2% (95% CI, 6.0–35.8%), and the median DoR was 13.6 months. Treatment-related adverse events occurred in 36.4% of patients and were primarily limited to low grade pruritus and rash. Emergent circulating tumor DNA alterations in RAS signaling, receptor tyrosine kinases, and DNA damage response were identified as putative mechanisms of anti-TIGIT resistance. Although the prespecified ORR threshold was not met, the durability of responses, favorable safety profile, and translational findings support further evaluation of anti-TIGIT–based combination strategies in BTCs.Subject terms: Bile duct cancer, Gall bladder cancer

Introduction

The incidence of biliary tract cancers (BTCs) continues to rise globally, which has been accompanied by an increase in disease-associated mortality1,2. The poor prognosis of BTCs is driven by their high rate of recurrence, even after curative-intent treatment, and the limited efficacy of currently available systemic therapies for advanced diseases3,4. Recent clinical trials have demonstrated the survival benefit of adding anti-PD-1/L1 antibodies with conventional cytotoxic chemotherapy in the treatment of advanced BTCs5,6. However, the improvement in survival and tumor response rates observed with anti-PD-1/L1 antibodies is modest, and most patients ultimately experience disease progression5,6.

T-cell immunoreceptor with Ig and ITIM domains (TIGIT) is expressed on activated T cells, regulatory T cells, and natural killer cells, where it competes with CD226 for binding to poliovirus receptor (PVR) ligands, thereby promoting an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment7–10. Through reinforcement of T-cell exhaustion programs, stabilization of regulatory T-cell activity, and suppression of natural killer cell cytotoxicity, TIGIT signaling is hypothesized to contribute to immune evasion7–9. In primary liver cancer models, TIGIT is frequently associated with T cell and natural killer cell dysfunction, including diminished cytokine release, proliferation, and anti-tumor cytotoxicity11–13. Although preclinical studies have demonstrated synergy between anti-TIGIT and anti–PD-1 therapies, clinical efficacy in human cancers has been inconsistent14. Early-phase trials suggested improved response rates with the addition of anti-TIGIT antibodies in immunotherapy-naïve patients with non-BTC cancers, but these findings were not confirmed in larger randomized studies14. This discrepancy highlights the need to define the independent therapeutic contribution of TIGIT blockade, as prior studies largely evaluated it within multi-agent regimens, limiting attribution.

BTCs are often enriched for TIGIT-expressing immune cells and high PVR expression, both linked to poor prognosis, suggesting TIGIT-mediated immune evasion and a potential role for its targeting to enhance PD-1 blockade efficacy15,16. To test this hypothesis, we conducted the LIVERTI phase 2 basket trial testing domvanalimab, an anti-TIGIT antibody, with zimberelimab, an anti-PD-1 antibody, in anti-PD-1/L1 therapy refractory hepatobiliary cancers. By enrolling patients with prior progression on PD-1/L1 therapy, this design enables direct assessment of whether dual TIGIT and PD-1 blockade can overcome checkpoint resistance and clarifies the contribution of TIGIT inhibition. Notably, domvanalimab is differentiated among anti-TIGIT antibodies due to an inactivated Fc domain, eliminating antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity17,18. This design may preserve TIGIT-expressing tumor-reactive lymphocytes and regulatory T cells in extratumoral tissues, with potential benefits for both efficacy and safety. The primary endpoint was investigator-assessed, confirmed objective response rate (ORR) among evaluable patients. Secondary endpoints reported included disease control rate (DCR), progression-free survival (PFS), and safety. Exploratory analyses were conducted to assess the utility of circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) as predictive and pharmacodynamic markers and to delineate genetic mechanisms that may contribute to anti-TIGIT resistance.

Herein, we report the primary analysis of the BTC cohort from the LIVERTI phase 2 trial evaluating domvanalimab plus zimberelimab in patients with advanced BTC who had previously progressed on anti-PD-1/L1 therapy. Domvanalimab plus zimberelimab demonstrated modest antitumor activity, achieving a confirmed ORR of 10.3%, a DCR of 44.8%, and a median duration of response of 13.6 months. Treatment was well tolerated, with treatment-related adverse events occurring in 36.4% of patients and primarily consisting of low-grade rash and pruritus. Exploratory ctDNA analyses suggested an association between ctDNA dynamics and clinical outcomes, while emergent alterations in RAS signaling, receptor tyrosine kinases, and DNA damage response pathways were identified as potential mechanisms of resistance to TIGIT blockade.

Results

Patient characteristics

Between 25 June 2023 and 17 March 2025, 41 patients were screened at a National Cancer Institute-designated cancer center (UT Southwestern Harold C. Simmons Comprehensive Cancer Center) and a county safety-net hospital system (Parkland Health), of which 35 met eligibility criteria and were enrolled. The main inclusion criteria for this study included disease refractory to at least one line of treatment which must have incorporated an anti-PD-1/L1 antibody, an ECOG score of 0 or 1, and adequate organ and bone marrow function. The exclusion criteria included a history of organ transplants, specified autoimmune diseases, and chronic use of systemic immunosuppressive medications (see “Methods” for inclusion and exclusion criteria). There were two patients who never started treatment and were thus not included in subsequent analyses (Fig. 1). There were 33 patients who received at least one dose of domvanalimab plus zimberelimab and were thus evaluable for safety. However, four patients only received the first cycle of treatment and did not have interval radiographic restaging due to patient withdrawal of consent (n = 1) or concomitant disease due to infectious cholangitis/cholangiopathy (n = 3) resulting in 29 evaluable patients for efficacy (Fig. 1). Patients evaluable for safety or efficacy had a median age was 67 (IQR: 62–72), were primarily male (54.5%), and had intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (78.8%) (Table 1). The number of lines of prior systemic therapy received was 1 for 18 patients, 2 for 8 patients, and 3 or greater for 7 patients. All patients progressed on prior first-line treatment with gemcitabine, cisplatin, and durvalumab or pembrolizumab. For patients who received more than one line of therapy prior to enrollment, treatment included fluorouracil plus oxaliplatin, fluorouracil plus irinotecan, nab-paclitaxel, or fibroblast growth factor receptor (FGFR) inhibitors. At the time of data cut-off, the median follow-up time was 7.1 months.

Efficacy

Of the 29 patients evaluable for the primary efficacy endpoint, the investigator determined confirmed ORR to domvanalimab plus zimberelimab was 10.3% (95% CI 0.02–27.0%), with 1 partial response (PR) seen in gall bladder cancer, and 2 PRs seen in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinoma (Table 2). The DCR including confirmed PRs and stable disease (SD) was 44.8% (Table 2). All objective responses were observed in patients with primary resistance to prior chemotherapy plus anti-PD-1/L1 therapy, and patients with disease control or durable clinical benefit (DCB), defined as a PFS of at least 6 months, were similarly enriched in patients with primary resistance (Fig. 2). Additional subgroup analyses of demographic and prognostic features did not reveal significant associations with ORR, DCR, or DCB, although the small sample size of this cohort limits the power to detect such relationships (Fig. 2). ORR, DCR, and DCB were not exclusive to any disease subgroup, with the exception of DCB only being observed in intrahepatic cholangiocarcinomas (Fig. 2). The median PFS was 2.1 months (95% CI, 2.0–4.5 months), and the 6-month PFS rate, defined as a durable clinical benefit (DCB), was 17.2% (95% CI, 0.06–35.8%) (Fig. 3a). Patients who attained objective response or disease control were associated with a significant PFS benefit (hazard ratio: 0.17; 95% CI, 0.07–0.43) and the median duration of response to domvanalimab plus zimberelimab was 13.6 months (Fig. 3b). Overall survival, a prespecified secondary endpoint, was not analyzed due to immature data at the time of the primary analysis.

Safety

Among the 33 patients evaluable for safety, treatment-emergent adverse events of any cause were observed in 29 patients (87.9%), and serious adverse events (SAEs) of any cause were observed in 14 patients (42.4%). Treatment-emergent AEs that were grade 3 or greater occurred in 18 patients (54.5%). The most common treatment-emergent adverse events included nausea (27.3%), aspartate aminotransferase increased (24.2%), fatigue (21.2%), pruritus (21.2%), alkaline phosphatase increased (18.2%), anorexia (18.2%), bilirubin increased (18.2%), and fever (18.2%) (Table 3). Treatment-related adverse events (TRAE) were defined as any adverse event that was at least possibly attributed to domvanalimab plus zimberelimab by the treating investigator and an independent safety committee, and occurred in 12 patients (36.4%). The most common TRAE of any grade was pruritus (18.2%) and rash (9.1%). All TRAEs were Grade 1 or 2, with the exception of one patient with Grade 3 pruritus. No SAEs were treatment-related. Dose delays and drug discontinuations due to treatment-emergent adverse events occurred in 6 and 2 patients, respectively.

Treatment-emergent adverse events

CtDNA as predictive and pharmacodynamic biomarkers

Prespecified exploratory analyses included molecular profiling of liquid biopsies to identify biomarkers associated with clinical outcomes and mechanisms of resistance. CtDNA was collected pre-treatment for 27 patients (two patients missed blood collections) and profiled using a tumor agnostic 83-gene panel sequencing assay (see Methods). Baseline ctDNA levels based on the maximal variant allele frequency (VAF) of detected genetic alterations for each patient specimen ranged from 0.1 to 46.0% across patients and did not significantly differ between tumor response categories (P = 0.32) (Fig. 4a). There were 2 patients that harbored FGFR2 fusions (one detected at baseline and one emerging after progression on domvanalimab plus zimberelimab), and both had PD as their best response. Among 4 patients with IDH1 mutations identified in baseline ctDNA, two achieved SD, and two had PD. A single patient with a class 3 BRAF mutation also experienced PD. No other therapeutically actionable alterations were identified. Tumor mutation burden (TMB) calculated from somatic single nucleotide variant and indels in exons of 497 genes ranged from 3.8 to 34.0 mut/Mb, and was also not significantly different between tumor response categories (P = 0.09) (Fig. 4a). However, a lower baseline TMB using a median threshold (HR 0.35, 95% CI 0.13–0.93) was associated with a statistically significant difference in PFS (Fig. 4b). In contrast, PFS was not associated with overall baseline ctDNA levels (HR 0.53, 95% CI 0.22–1.30) (Fig. 4b).

In a subset of patients (n = 19) with available data on TMB derived from tissue-based sequencing of diagnostic tissue specimens obtained prior to any systemic treatment, tissue-derived pre-treatment TMB was not correlated with ctDNA-derived TMB, and was not associated with PFS (Supplementary Fig. 1). These discordances may be attributed to the collection of tissue and blood at different timepoints, such that tumor evolution and intervening therapies, together with differences in assay platforms, genomic coverage, and analytical pipelines, may limit direct comparability in TMB estimates.

To assess whether early changes in ctDNA levels corresponded with objective responses, a second ctDNA sample was analyzed post-cycle 2 for a subset of 20 patients who had a serial blood collection. Among 3 patients with a PR, all patients demonstrated a decline in detectable ctDNA levels (5.6%, 13.2%, and 85.7% decrease) (Fig. 4c). Among the six patients with SD, four patients had increases in ctDNA levels (10.8–700%), while two patients had decreases in ctDNA levels (37.5–50%) (Fig. 4c). Notably, both patients with SD and declines in VAF were associated with DCB. Among ten patients with PD, all but two had any detectable increase in VAFs (Fig. 4c). Stratifying patients by ctDNA response as increased, stable, or decreased, using a change greater than 30% as a threshold as previously described, in a 8-week landmark analysis showed that decreases in VAF were associated with a significant improvement in PFS compared to cases with increases in VAF (HR 0.25, 95% CI 0.07–0.93) (Fig. 4d)19.

Genetic mechanisms of anti-TIGIT resistance

The genetic landscape of alterations in the BTC cohort showed a predominance of alterations in TP53 (63%), KRAS (22%), ATM (19%), CDKN2A (15%), and IDH1 (15%) (Fig. 5a). There were no altered genes that were common and exclusive to patients with a PR or DCB, suggesting substantial genomic heterogeneity among responders and the absence of a unifying driver of benefit (Fig. 5a). However, ctDNA profiling at the time of progression which was available for one patient with a PR and one patient with DCB revealed emergent alterations, including a RAF1 amplification and mutations in ATM and CHEK2 (Fig. 5b). To evaluate potential mechanisms of treatment resistance, emergent ctDNA alterations were examined in all patients with serial ctDNA profiling. Genes with emergent alterations were identified in multiple patients for several oncogenic pathways, including five patients with RAS signaling alterations, three patients with receptor tyrosine kinase alterations, and five patients with mutations in DNA damage response and cell cycle checkpoint signaling (Fig. 5c). Emergent alterations in PIK3CA, APC, SMARCA4, and MTAP were also identified in separate individual patients (Fig. 5c).

Discussion

To date, clinical evidence regarding the efficacy and safety of anti-TIGIT therapies in BTCs has remained limited. In this multicenter phase 2 trial, the safety profile of domvanalimab plus zimberelimab in patients with chemo-immunotherapy refractory BTC was aligned with the toxicities observed with anti-PD-1/L1 therapies, supporting the feasibility of combining anti-TIGIT and anti-PD-1 therapies in future treatment regimens for BTC20–23. Despite a confirmed ORR of 10.3% in a heavily pretreated population, many of whom had received more than one prior line of therapy, this outcome did not meet the prespecified efficacy threshold in the protocol. Nonetheless, the tumor response rate of current second-line chemotherapy regimens is modest, with 5% reported for standard of care FOLFOX, and is associated with a sizable number of treatment-related toxicities24. It is notable that multiple patients experienced durable disease control after treatment with domvanalimab plus zimberelimab, and the duration of responses was lengthy, suggesting that targeting TIGIT may confer sustained immune modulation capable of stabilizing disease even in the absence of overt radiographic tumor regression. In addition, because only patients who progressed after prior anti-PD-1/L1 therapy were enrolled in this trial, the clinical benefit observed with domvanalimab plus zimberelimab may be attributed to reactivation of antitumor immunity through dual blockade of TIGIT and PD-1 pathways, potentially overcoming mechanisms of adaptive resistance to prior checkpoint inhibition.

In contrast to other investigational anti-TIGIT antibodies, including tiragolumab, belrestotug, and vibostolimab, domvanalimab was engineered with a Fc-silent domain17,18. This design prevents depletion of TIGIT-expressing effector T cells and natural killer cells, thereby preserving their antitumor activity17,18. Preclinical studies have shown that Fc-silent anti-TIGIT antibodies promote sustained T-cell activation and cytokine production compared with Fc-competent counterparts, which may induce unwanted immune cell clearance14,17,18. The distinct Fc modification of domvanalimab therefore represents a key mechanistic feature that could contribute to its favorable safety profile in BTCs when combined with PD-1 blockade.

ctDNA provides a more accessible and non-invasive approach for molecular profiling and, unlike tissue-based sampling, can be readily utilized for longitudinal monitoring of tumor evolution, treatment response, and the emergence of resistance mechanisms25–27. Prior studies have demonstrated the feasibility and high concordance with tissue-based sequencing of ctDNA profiling in BTC, but prospective evaluation of ctDNA as predictive and pharmacodynamic markers in this disease is limited28,29. In this study, baseline ctDNA levels varied widely across patients but did not differ significantly between response categories. Contrastingly, ctDNA TMB was associated with improved PFS after domvanalimab plus zimberelimab. TMB has often been associated with improved immunotherapy benefit based on the premise that increased neoantigen load enhances tumor immunogenicity30. Our paradoxical results suggest that the mechanisms governing response to TIGIT blockade in BTCs may be distinct from those driving benefit with other immunotherapies and may not rely on high neoantigen load. One potential explanation is that low-TMB tumors may rely more heavily on active inhibitory signaling pathways, such as the PVR/TIGIT axis, to restrain otherwise functional immune effector populations, rendering them more susceptible to TIGIT blockade. In contrast, high-TMB tumors that were already previously treated with immunotherapy may have already derived benefit, resulting in an enrichment of intrinsically resistant disease within the remaining high-TMB population. Consistent with this, a recent study in colorectal cancer showed that tumors acquiring higher TMB were not sensitized to anti–PD-1/L1 therapy, indicating that mutational burden alone may be insufficient to overcome established resistance31. Although the association between ctDNA TMB and clinical benefit after anti-TIGIT therapy requires corroboration and further mechanistic understanding, these findings support the rationale for combinatorial targeting of TIGIT and other immune checkpoints to broaden immune engagement and potentially overcome limitations associated with low neoantigenicity. We also demonstrate that ctDNA levels exhibit early and dynamic changes that correlate with radiographic and clinical outcomes. Early molecular responses following treatment initiation were associated with objective response and durable stability of disease, as well as improved progression-free. Oppositely, rising ctDNA levels portended disease progression. These findings suggest that ctDNA may have utility as a pharmacodynamic biomarker in BTCs, but this requires further validation in larger patient samples and corroboration in other systemic treatments.

Longitudinal analysis of ctDNA uncovered tumor evolution in response to treatment with domvanalimab plus zimberelimab, highlighting molecular alterations that may drive treatment resistance. Specifically, emergent alterations in ctDNA may represent subclonal populations that were either below the limit of detection at baseline or selectively expanded under therapeutic pressure. These acquired alterations spanned several oncogenic programs, including RAS signaling, receptor tyrosine kinase activation, and DNA damage response and cell-cycle regulation, suggesting diverse evolutionary trajectories by which BTCs may escape TIGIT blockade and may be instructive of future combination strategies designed to pre-empt or overcome resistance. While further investigation in larger clinical studies are needed to validate the generalizability of RAS signaling as a resistance mechanism, RAS signaling has been previously implicated in tumor immune evasion by upregulating PD-L1 expression, suppressing antigen presentation, and fostering an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment32,33. Additional studies are warranted to test whether combining anti-TIGIT therapies with MEK or ERK inhibitors, which disrupt RAS signaling downstream of emergent RAS and RAF alterations, may enhance antitumor immunity and overcome acquired resistance to immune checkpoint blockade.

Limitations of this proof-of-concept trial include its open-label design and sample size, which is related to its exploratory scope and the relatively uncommon incidence of BTCs. Further analyses of immune correlates are necessary to identify potential biomarkers that may be used to tailor patient selection strategies to maximize clinical efficacy and inform future trial design. In addition, while the prevalence of pseudoprogression is ambiguous in BTCs, we cannot exclude atypical responses as a confounding factor in our results. In a recent study where the anti-TIGIT antibody ociperlimab was tested in combination with standard chemotherapy plus anti-PD-1 therapy in the first line setting, response rates were numerically greater in tumors with greater expression of TIGIT and PD-1 expressing immune cells, suggesting that the presence of TIGIT- and PD-1–positive immune subsets could serve as predictive biomarkers to guide patient selection but remain to be validated34. Nonetheless, the low toxicity and comparatively favorable efficacy of domvanalimab plus zimberelimab versus standard of care chemotherapy suggest that anti-TIGIT therapies may be safely combined with other therapies to broaden clinical benefit and overcome resistance mechanisms or be utilized as a chemotherapy-free treatment option for patients who are unable to tolerate more toxic regimens.

Methods

Study design and endpoints

The study was approved by the UT Southwestern institutional review board, and all patients provided written informed consent prior to enrollment. This study was an investigator-initiated phase 2, open-label basket trial conducted at 2 clinical sites (UT Southwestern Harold C. Simmons Comprehensive Cancer Center and Parkland Health). One arm of the study enrolled patients with advanced-stage BTCs, which is reported herein. The other arm of the study enrolled patients with hepatocellular carcinoma and has been previously reported35. Endpoints, main eligibility criteria, and statistical considerations overlapped across both arms in the trial35. The study design and conduct complied with all relevant regulations regarding the use of human study participants and was conducted in accordance with the criteria set by the Declaration of Helsinki. This study was registered at Clinicaltrials.gov on February 2, 2023 (https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05724563). The trial protocol is available as a Supplementary Note in the Supplementary Information file. There were no deviations that affected the trial design. Sex was self-reported by participants. Gender identity was not collected as part of the study. Sex and gender were not considered in the study design or interpretation of the results because there is currently insufficient evidence to support differential treatment effects of TIGIT and PD-1 blockade based on these variables in BTC.

The primary endpoint was the confirmed ORR per Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1 of all evaluable patients receiving at least one dose of domvanalimab and zimberelimab. ORR was defined as the number of patients with a complete response (CR) or PR that was confirmed on imaging greater than or equal to 4 weeks after initial response. Secondary endpoints included safety, 6-month PFS, duration of response, and overall survival, of which the latter will be reported in a subsequent analysis due to limited follow-up time at the time of analysis for the primary endpoint. Exploratory endpoints included correlative studies between treatment response and outcomes with clinical and laboratory studies. This study followed the Transparent Reporting of Evaluations With Nonrandomized Designs (TREND) reporting guideline and was conducted in compliance with the trial protocol.

Statistical considerations

This study is exploratory because of the absence of data on the efficacy of treatments in hepatobiliary cancers previously treated with immunotherapies and on the efficacy of anti-TIGIT therapies used in any line of treatment for hepatobiliary cancers at the time of study development. Thus, a sample size of 29 evaluable patients was determined based on statistical considerations and feasibility. This design yields a power of 85.9% if the observed response rate is 20% compared to a null benchmark of 5% with a one-sided type 1 error rate of 0.055 using an exact binomial test. Justification for the null hypothesis in bile duct cancers is based on the observed ORR of 5% with FOLFOX in the second-line setting among patients previously treated with chemotherapy in the randomized phase 3 ABC-06 trial.

Participants

Patients were enrolled between June 25, 2023 and March 17, 2025 at a National Cancer Institute-designated cancer center (UT Southwestern Harold C. Simmons Comprehensive Cancer Center) and a county safety-net hospital system (Parkland Health). Major inclusion criteria included age 18 years or older, histologically confirmed diagnosis of BTC, locally advanced or metastatic disease not amenable to surgical resection, refractory to or relapsed after prior anti-PD-1/L1 therapy as any line of therapy (patients who discontinued prior anti-PD-1/L1 therapy due to toxicities were not eligible), ECOG status of 0 or 1, adequate laboratory values (total bilirubin ≤3.0 mg/ml, international normalized ratio ≤1.7, hemoglobin ≥8.5 g/dl, aspartate transaminase and alanine transaminase ≤5 times upper limits of normal, platelet count ≥50,000/mm3, serum creatinine ≤1.5 mg/dL or creatinine clearance ≥50 mL/min, albumin ≥2.0 g/dl, absolute neutrophil ≥1000 cells/mm3), agreed to use an adequate method of contraception through the course of the study and 120 days after the last dose of study medication (male and female subjects of child bearing potential), and a negative pregnancy test if the subject was a woman of child bearing age. Major exclusion criteria included a prior liver transplant, recent history of a major surgery, underlying medical conditions that would make treatment hazardous, active autoimmune disease that requires systemic treatment within the past 2 years, active infections, medical conditions requiring systemic treatment with either corticosteroids (>10 mg/day prednisone equivalent) or other immunosuppressive medications within 14 days of study administration (inhaled or topical steroids and adrenal replacement doses >10 mg/day prednisone equivalents are permitted in the absence of autoimmune disease), symptomatic or clinically active brain metastases, and pregnancy (defined as state of a female after contraception and until the termination of gestation, confirmed by a positive hCG laboratory test) or nursing (lactating) women.

Procedures

Domvanalimab (1200 mg) and zimberelimab (360 mg) were administered as consecutive 60-min IV infusions on Day 1 of each 21-day cycle. Treatment was administered until disease progression, discontinuation due to toxicity, withdrawal of consent, or study termination. No dose reductions were allowed. Dose interruptions were permitted for immune-related toxicities, and treatment was resumed once the event resolved (grade 1 or less) and any corticosteroids used for toxicity management had been tapered. Prohibited concomitant medications or treatments during the screening and treatment phase of this trial included other antineoplastic systemic chemotherapy or biological therapy, other immunotherapy agents, other investigational agents, radiation therapy of target lesions, live vaccines, and systemic glucocorticoids for any purpose other than to modulate symptoms from an event of clinical interest of suspected immunologic etiology.

Response and progression were evaluated using RECIST 1.1 as determined by the investigator. Patients were evaluated by imaging for progression every 9 weeks following the initial study drug administration. Imaging consisted of computed tomography scans, or magnetic resonance imaging scans of the abdomen and pelvis with triple-phase acquisition. CT Chest or additional imaging was at the discretion of the investigator based on clinical suspicion of progressive disease.

All patients receiving at least one dose of study drugs were evaluated in the safety analysis regardless of their evaluability for the primary ORR endpoint. Treatment-related adverse events were defined as any adverse event at least possibly attributed to study drugs per the protocol that occurred between the first dose of study drugs through 30 days after the last dose of study drugs. Adverse events occurring beyond 30 days, at least possibly attributed to study drugs per the investigator, were also reported. Adverse events were graded according to National Cancer Institute Common Terminology Criteria for AEs, v.5.0. All serious AE occurrences, attribution, and management were reviewed by the internal gastrointestinal oncology research committee, composed of a multidisciplinary team of oncology providers, and the UTSW Data and Safety Monitoring Committee.

ctDNA analysis

Peripheral blood specimens were collected in Streck Cell-Free DNA tubes prior to starting treatment (baseline) and then approximately 6–8 weeks (post-cycle 2) after treatment initiation. Baseline specimens were obtained from 27 enrolled patients (two patients missed blood collection). Serial specimens were available for 20 patients due to patients missing blood collection, progressing prior to the planned blood collection date, or withdrawal of consent. Samples were shipped to a Clinical Laboratory Improvement Act (CLIA)-certified, College of American Pathologists-accredited laboratory (Guardant Health, Redwood City, CA). ctDNA was assessed using a targeted high-throughput hybridization-based capture technology for the detection of single-nucleotide variants, insertions, and deletions in 83 genes by paired-end synthesis-sequencing using the NextSeq 500 and/or HiSeq 2500 platforms (Illumina, Inc.)36. Putative germline mutations, including variants identified by allele fractions between 40% and 60%, prior annotation as germline mutations, and manual review, were excluded from analyses. ctDNA was detected in 28 patients based on the baseline specimen harboring any alteration with a VAF of at least 0.01%, which is the limit of detection for the assay used. TMB was calculated by counting all somatic nonsynonymous and single-nucleotide variants and delins across 1 Mb of coding regions and then algorithmically adjusted to correct for confounding biological and technical sample features as previously described36.

Statistical analysis

ORR was defined as the percentage of patients with CRs and PRs that were subsequently confirmed on follow-up restaging scan. Confidence interval for ORR was calculated using the Clopper–Pearson exact method. The DCR was defined as the percentage of patients with SD or confirmed CR/PRs as their best response per RECIST1.1. PFS was estimated using the Kaplan–Meier method. 95% confidence intervals for the median PFS and 6-month PFS rate were calculated using the Greenwood method. Descriptive analyses were used to describe treatment-related adverse events. To assess the association between ORR and clinical characteristics, confidence intervals for the odds ratio were calculated using the Baptista–Pike method. Differences in baseline VAF and TMB between response categories were assessed using the Kruskal–Wallis test. Due to the small sample size of patients within each response category, ctDNA dynamics were descriptively characterized. To categorize patients into ctDNA response groups (increased, stable, or decreased), we used a threshold of ≥30% change as previously described in a study of gastrointestinal cancers. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 28 (IBM); for biomarker analyses, P values were from two-sided tests, and results were deemed statistically significant at P < 0.05. Because the correlative studies conducted were exploratory, analyses were not corrected for multiple testing.
