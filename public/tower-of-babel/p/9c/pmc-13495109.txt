Factors influencing older adults’ intention to use social chatbots: examining technology acceptance, anthropomorphism, authenticity, and artificial intelligence social interaction
Authors: Ka Lon Sou, Wei Quin Yow, Matthew Zhi Yuan Lau, Fuxi Ouyang, Michelle Putnam
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectivesDespite emerging evidence showing that older adults have begun engaging social chatbots for companionship in recent years, little is known about the factors influencing their adoption decisions. We developed an age-friendly social chatbot and examined older adults’ intention to use it by integrating the Senior Technology Acceptance Model (STAM) with sociorelational factors relevant to human–artificial intelligence (AI) interaction.Research Design and MethodsA final sample of 140 community-dwelling older adults interacted with the chatbot for approximately 20 min and then completed questionnaires assessing their attitudes toward the chatbot, perceptions of the chatbot, and their intention to use it in daily life.ResultsHigher control belief (e.g., perceived ease of use and facilitating conditions), one of the STAM factors, predicted higher intention to use the chatbot among older adults, whereas higher self-rated health predicted lower intention. Importantly, the stronger the sociorelational factors (e.g., authenticity; AI social interaction intensity), the higher the intention to use. However, higher levels of anthropomorphism of the chatbot predicted lower intention to use. Incorporating sociorelational factors into the STAM framework accounted for an additional proportion of variance, comparable to STAM factors alone.Discussion and ImplicationsOur findings suggest that control belief, authenticity, and social interaction intensity with social chatbots are key drivers of adoption intention among older adults. Unmet needs in life may also drive chatbot engagement. However, overly human-like chatbots may deter adoption. Taken together, integrating human–AI interaction factors with the STAM framework is essential when evaluating older adults’ adoption of social chatbots.Keywords: Conversational agent, Virtual companionship, Human-like, Control belief, Age-friendly

Our findings offer insights and guidelines for designing social chatbots for older adults. Resources to use the technology, authenticity, engaging human–AI interactions, and cognitive-wellbeing factors such as the ability to address unmet needs in life are critical factors that facilitate adoption. Overly human-like features, however, may deter it. This study stressed the need to consider factors that influence human–AI interaction alongside traditional usage-based attitude models when examining older adults’ adoption of social chatbots.

Background and Objectives

Adoption of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies by older adults has accelerated markedly. In Britain, about 32% of individuals aged 70 and older reported using AI (Office for National Statistics, 2023). Across the United States, the use of AI among adults aged 50 and older doubled from 9% in 2023 to 18% in 2024 (Kakulla, 2024). This surge in adoption has coincided with rapid advancements of large language models (LLMs), which have substantially expanded the capacities of AI-powered chatbots, enabling them to understand users’ intentions and contexts, generate natural responses, and serve diverse functions such as customer service, education, and social companionship (Lin et al., 2023). Shum et al. (2018) categorize chatbots into three types: task-completion, personal-assistant, and social. Task-completion chatbots (e.g., customer service chatbots) are designed to execute specific tasks; personal-assistant chatbots (e.g., Apple’s Siri) provide reactive and proactive assistance to users; and social chatbots (e.g., Microsoft’s XiaoIce) are developed to offer companionship and sustain long-term interactions. Replika (https://replika.com/), one of the largest social chatbot platforms, had attracted more than 10 million registered users worldwide in 2022 (Ghosh, 2023).

Social chatbots have the potential to address the rising concerns of isolation and loneliness. In the United States, approximately one-third of older adults report experiencing social isolation and loneliness (Malani et al., 2025), and chronic loneliness affects about 20% of the population (Hajek et al., 2025). Similarly, in Singapore, about 17% to 47% of older adults are socially isolated, with 7.3% experiencing loneliness (Ge et al., 2022). These vulnerabilities pose a mortality risk comparable to smoking 15 cigarettes per day and are associated with increased risks for coronary disease and all-cause mortality (Holt-Lunstad et al., 2010; Naito et al., 2023; Valtorta et al., 2016). Against this backdrop, social chatbots represent a promising and scalable intervention by offering accessible, low-cost companionship through empathetic and sustained interactions. Although they do not replace human relationships, their ability to provide consistent and personalized engagement makes them a valuable complement to existing social and healthcare support systems.

Although no national statistics currently exist on older adults’ use of social chatbots, emerging products such as SeniorTalk (https://www.senior-talk.com/) are explicitly targeting this demographic. Engagement with these tools appears to be growing. For instance, a recent study found that 3 out of 19 participants (approximately 16%) who used Replika were older adults (Ma & Koike, 2025). In addition, New York State’s Office for the Aging deployed over 800 units of ElliQ, a commercial AI-powered social companion, to combat social isolation among older adults (New York State Office for the Aging, 2022).

Despite these trends, empirical research on how older adults interact with social chatbots remains sparse, and the factors influencing their adoption decisions are poorly understood. This paper examines how factors within the usage and the sociorelational dimensions shape older adults’ intentions to use social chatbots in everyday contexts.

Usage dimension in social chatbot adoption

The Technology Acceptance Model (TAM) posits that perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use shape users’ attitudes toward a technology, which in turn predict intention to use and actual use (Davis et al., 1989). The Senior Technology Acceptance Model (STAM) adapted this framework for older adults by incorporating age-related variables such as self-reported health status and gerontechnology anxiety (Chen & Chan, 2014). To reduce respondents’ burden, Chen and Lou (2020) introduced a short version of STAM that consolidates the original constructs into four: attitudinal belief (i.e., the extent to which the older adults regard the use of technology as beneficial and desirable; combining perceived usefulness and attitude toward technology), control belief (i.e., the older adults’ perception of their ability and barriers to use the technology with ease; combining perceived ease of use, self-efficacy, and facilitating conditions), gerontechnology anxiety (i.e., the older adults’ fear and self-doubt regarding their ability to learn and use technologies), and health (i.e., the contextual determinants such as physical, cognitive, and social well-being that influence older adults’ technology use; combining general health conditions, cognitive abilities, social relationship, and life satisfaction). These four variables collectively explained approximately 81.5% of the variance in older adults’ actual use of gerontechnology (Chen & Lou, 2020).

STAM has been applied across various contexts, including assistive technologies and health-related technologies (Li et al., 2019; Shin et al., 2023). For instance, Shin et al. (2023) examined how STAM factors predict older adults’ intentions to use living-assistive technologies, such as mobility aids, hearing aids, and electric beds. Among healthy participants, positive attitudes toward technology, perceived usefulness, perceived ease of use, and gerontechnology self-efficacy were all associated with higher intention to use, whereas life satisfaction showed a negative association. Likewise, several studies have reported that poorer self-reported health increases intention to use and actual use of gerontechnology (Chen & Chan, 2014; Li et al., 2019). One plausible explanation is that older adults experiencing greater health challenges are motivated to adopt technology to fulfill their unmet health needs (Shin et al., 2023).

Few studies have examined the determinants of older adults’ chatbot use, and the limited existing work has largely centered on task-completion chatbots and personal-assistant chatbots (e.g., Cao et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2023). In those studies, perceived usefulness consistently predicted intention to use nonsocial chatbots. However, they employed TAM without accounting for age-related factors, such as self-reported health. Only one study applied STAM to examine older adults’ intention to use personal-assistant chatbots, finding that both perceived usefulness and perceived ease of use were significant predictors (Song et al., 2022). However, that work did not examine the relationship between self-reported health and intention to use, a link found to be significant in other gerontechnological research (Chen & Chan, 2014; Li et al., 2019; Shin et al., 2023). To our knowledge, no study has applied STAM to evaluate older adults’ intention to adopt social chatbots.

Sociorelational dimension in social chatbots adoption

STAM captures usage factors that predict intention to use but omits factors that are specific to human–AI interactions in social technologies such as chatbots (e.g., Pentina et al., 2023; Strohmann et al., 2023). Although chatbots perform diverse utilitarian roles (Xu & Li, 2024), social chatbots are uniquely designed to facilitate social communication and foster enduring relational bonds (Shum et al., 2018; Strohmann et al., 2023). Thus, the ability of social chatbots to deliver relational value may play a critical role in their adoption.

Socioemotional selectivity theory (Carstensen et al., 1999) posits that perceived limitations in time horizons shift priorities toward close and meaningful relationships. Because older adults generally perceive their time horizons as more limited than those of younger adults (Brothers et al., 2016), they tend to value close relationships more strongly than their younger peers. Therefore, sociorelational factors shaping human–AI interactions may be particularly salient for this population. Specifically, when using chatbots, older adults may value how human-like the chatbots’ social interactions are (anthropomorphism) and how genuinely caring they appear to be (authenticity), which determines how close their interactions are with the chatbots (AI social interaction intensity [AISI]) (Pentina et al., 2023). The three-factor theory of anthropomorphism (Epley et al., 2007) predicts that socially isolated individuals have a higher tendency to anthropomorphize non-human agents. Because older adults are more likely to experience social isolation than the general population (OECD, 2025), they may be particularly receptive to anthropomorphic cues. To account for these mechanisms, we propose integrating the sociorelational factors identified by Pentina et al. (2023), i.e., anthropomorphism, authenticity, and AISI, into the STAM framework to better understand social chatbot adoption among older adults.

Anthropomorphism refers to the extent to which a technology is perceived as human-like (Li & Suh, 2022). According to the Computer Are Social Actors (CASA) framework (Nass et al., 1994), people respond to a piece of technology as a social partner when it displays social cues, such as “face, eye gaze, … [and] human-sounding voice” (Lombard & Xu, 2021, p.33). These cues shape perceptions of anthropomorphism (Lombard & Xu, 2021), which in turn, are likely to elicit more positive social judgment and stronger intention to use (Gong, 2008; Ratan et al., 2025). Empirical studies have shown that anthropomorphism predicts intention to use nonsocial chatbots (e.g., Ding & Najaf, 2024; Song et al., 2024). However, this relationship may not be strictly linear. The uncanny valley hypothesis (Mori et al., 2012) posits that affinity rises with anthropomorphism but declines sharply when a system appears almost, but not fully, human. Moreover, higher levels of anthropomorphism can heighten privacy concerns, thereby reducing acceptance intentions (Song et al., 2024). Among older adults, Enam et al. (2025) reported that while some older adults were attracted by chatbots that are human-like, others might feel uncomfortable when they try to talk like a human.

Authenticity describes the extent to which a chatbot is perceived as genuine or sincere (e.g., Lee, 2020). Existing work, primarily on nonsocial chatbots, has found positive direct and indirect associations between authenticity and intention to use (Alimamy & Kuhail, 2023; Seitz, 2024). However, interactions with social chatbots differ from those with nonsocial ones in their emphasis on long-term relational engagement (Strohmann et al., 2023). Analysis of interviews with the Replika users revealed that authenticity was characterized by the chatbot’s capacity for autonomous operation and the ability to learn, adapt, and develop through ongoing communication (Pentina et al., 2023). In addition, Al Mazroui and Alzyoudi (2024) interviewed older adults about their experience of using ChatGPT. Participants praised the model’s natural language understanding and appreciated its ability to remember preferences and past interactions, thereby personalizing subsequent dialogue. Subsequent research also found that older adults care about whether the social chatbots can remember previous conversations and follow across conversations (Enam et al., 2025; Jiao et al., 2026). These adaptive exchanges were perceived as valuable and aligned with the notion of authenticity proposed by Pentina et al. (2023). One participant described feeling like “having a companion who truly cares about [the participant] and share [his] interests,” (p. 10) indicating a close and meaningful relationship with the chatbot. Despite its theoretical relevance, the influence of authenticity on social chatbot adoption among older adults remains empirically underexplored.

Pentina et al. (2023) introduced the concept of AISI, which refers to the extent of reciprocal, real-time, mediated interaction with an AI system and reflects the degree to which such interaction resembles close human relationships (Pentina et al., 2023). In their study, AISI fully mediated the relationship between anthropomorphism, authenticity, and emotional attachment with social chatbots, suggesting that it may be a more proximal driver of intention to use than anthropomorphism or authenticity. However, no empirical work has yet examined how such sociorelational factors influence social chatbot adoption intentions among older adults, as most existing studies have focused on younger users. Although AISI-related behaviors were not observed in older adults’ use of chatbots, their relevance is evident from interactions with other social technologies such as social robots (Tan et al., 2024). In the study of Tan et al. (2024), participants described caring for the robot as they would for a child and treating it as a member of the family. Even though the robot was not designed to speak, participants frequently talked to it. Furthermore, its simple gestures, such as eye blinking, made the participants feel understood. These findings illustrate close and reciprocal interactions with social technologies and underscore the importance of sociorelational factors in shaping older adults’ intention to use social technologies, including social chatbots.

Current study

This study made two novel contributions. First, we extended the STAM beyond its use with nonsocial chatbots to examine older adults’ intention to use social chatbots. Second, we integrated sociorelational factors with STAM factors into a unified model of older adults’ adoption of social chatbots (Figure 1 presents the conceptual framework and summarizes the hypotheses). This integration is crucial for understanding older adults’ adoption of social chatbots because (1) older adults increasingly prioritize close, meaningful relationships, a shift explained by the socioemotional selectivity theory, and (2) a social chatbot’s unique purpose is to facilitate social communication and foster long-term relationships. Method

Participants

One hundred and forty-three community-dwelling older adults were recruited from active aging centers in Singapore. Older adults were eligible to participate if they met the following criteria: (1) Singaporean or Singapore permanent resident; (2) aged 60 years or older; (3) proficient in Chinese or English (self-reported); (4) normal vision and hearing (see Supplementary Methods for details on vision/hearing screening); (5) no clinical diagnosis with any physical/motor disorder related to the upper body, mental, neurological, psychiatric, sleep, or speech disorders (self-reported); and (6) not consuming any psychoactive medication. Three participants were excluded from the data analysis as data verification indicated that they did not meet the inclusion criteria. Therefore, the final sample consisted of 140 older adults (mean age = 72.66, range = 60–89; 110 women; mean Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) = 23.23, range = 8–29; for demographic details, see Table 1). Seventy-seven participants completed the study in English, and 63 in Chinese. The self-reported English proficiency of those completed in English was 8.26 out of 10 (SD = 1.80), and the self-reported Chinese proficiency of those completed in Chinese was 8.19 (SD = 1.88). See Supplementary Methods for details on assessment of language proficiency levels.

Design of the chatbot prototype

To ensure our participants could effectively use and relate to the AI technology, we developed an age-friendly LLM-powered chatbot, Ami Chat.1 Designed as a virtual companion for older adults, Ami Chat aims to reduce loneliness and promote social interaction. Because the target users were older adults, the design closely followed guidelines and recommendations from prior research on age-friendly application design (e.g., Farage et al., 2012). The age-friendly user interface incorporated several features, including speech-based rather than text-based interaction (i.e., users listened and talked to the chatbot), customizable font sizes, simple gestures such as tapping, enlarged key-control buttons, multimodal indicators (visual and textual), tutorials at first login, and a high-error-tolerant interface.

Regarding the chatbot avatar, we selected a female gender for two primary reasons. First, this design was adapted from our previous cognitive training project (Yow et al., 2024), where the female avatar was meant to resemble a typical caregiver, and it was well received by our older adult participants. Second, literature indicates that older adults generally prefer female avatars over male ones (Esposito et al., 2021; Justo et al., 2020; Ter Stal et al., 2020), potentially because female avatars are often perceived as more captivating and engaging (Esposito et al., 2021). Finally, we defined the chatbot’s personality as “warm-hearted” to align with its core purpose: serving as a caring companion that offers support when older adults are socially isolated or feel lonely.

Ami Chat’s conversation system was powered by OpenAI’s GPT-4o-mini model and was prompt-engineered to simulate a warm-hearted Singaporean woman who replies in short, colloquial responses. Scripted openers were incorporated to proactively engage users. We also incorporated screening questions related to cognitive health, mental health, and vitality, adapted from the World Health Organization ICOPE (2019) guidelines, into the bank of openers. Furthermore, we integrated OpenAI’s content moderation system to prevent users from engaging in inappropriate conversations (e.g., sexual, violence, and self-harm).

Comparing design of Ami Chat with typical chatbot design

A systematic review suggests that effective age-friendly chatbot design typically features simplicity, limited anthropomorphism, encouraging interaction, and incorporation of cognitive assessments (Du et al., 2024). Ami Chat aligns closely with these design principles. First, the chatbot prioritizes ease of use for older adults by utilizing a voice-based interface, and users only need to speak and listen, eliminating the need for typing. This design choice addresses documented difficulties older adults face with smartphone keyboards (Low et al., 2021). The chatbot’s age-friendly design is further supported by additional controls, such as a cancel button for unwanted speech inputs and simple gestures like tapping. Second, Ami Chat utilizes a cartoon female avatar to maintain a limited level of anthropomorphism. Third, the chatbot is programmed to speak in short, colloquial language to support and care for the user, with specific prompts to lift the user’s mood. Fourth, Ami Chat regularly asks cognitive screening questions adapted from the WHO ICOPE (2019) guideline, such as an inquiry about memory or orientation, to monitor the user’s cognitive wellbeing. Because Ami Chat shares core features with the typical design of age-friendly chatbots identified by Du et al. (2024), this alignment suggests that the findings derived from Ami Chat are applicable to other similar social chatbots.

Measures

Demographic and language background

We collected participants’ demographic information, including gender, age, race, marital status, years of education, and monthly household income. Participants’ language profiles were assessed using the Language Background Questionnaire adapted from previous studies (Li et al., 2024; Yow & Li, 2015).

STAM

The brief STAM (Chen & Lou, 2020) was adapted to measure the participants’ attitudes toward the social chatbot. The questionnaire consisted of 14 items measuring attitudinal belief (3 items; e.g., “You like the idea of using Ami Chat”), control belief (4 items; e.g., “You could be skilful at using Ami Chat”), gerontechnology anxiety (2 items; e.g., “You hesitate to use Ami Chat for fear of making mistakes you cannot correct”), and health (5 items; e.g., “How are your general health conditions?”). Each item was rated on a 10-point scale. The internal reliabilities (i.e., Cronbach’s alpha) of the subscales, attitudinal belief, control belief, gerontechnology anxiety, and health. were .95, .88, .83, and .84, respectively, in this study. We adopted the original Chinese translation provided by the questionnaire creators.

Anthropomorphism

The anthropomorphism questionnaire was adapted from Pentina et al. (2023). It consisted of four items (e.g., “is human-like”). Each item was rated on a 7-point Likert scale and had an internal reliability of .91 in this study. As some items were available in the Chinese version of the Godspeed Questionnaire (Bartneck, 2023), only the remaining items were translated into Chinese. A Chinese–English bilingual author translated the questionnaire items into Chinese. To verify the translation’s accuracy, a bilingual research assistant subsequently back-translated those items into English. The original and back-translated versions were compared based on conceptual equivalence. When discrepancies were identified, the Chinese translation was revised and back-translated again until there were no major discrepancies identified. The translation procedure was also applied to other questionnaires that required translation.

Authenticity

The authenticity questionnaire was also adapted from Pentina et al. (2023), and consisted of seven items (e.g., “Real,” and “Tuned to my preferences”). Each item was rated on a 7-point Likert scale, and the internal reliability of this questionnaire was .95 in this study. The items were translated into Chinese. One participant’s response contained missing data. The missing data were imputed before analyses (see Data analysis section).

AISI

The AISI questionnaire was also adapted from Pentina et al. (2023), and consisted of five items (e.g., “interactions with Ami Chat are similar to interactions with friends,” and “interactions with Ami Chat are reciprocal and mutual”). Each item was rated on a 7-point Likert scale, and the internal reliability of this questionnaire was .93 in this study. The items were translated into Chinese. One participant’s response contained missing data. The missing data were imputed before analyses.

Intention to use

We adapted the questionnaire from Venkatesh et al. (2012). This 3-item scale asked participants how much they believed they would use the chatbot in their daily life if they could bring it home (e.g., “I will always try to use Ami Chat in my daily life”). Each item was rated on a 7-point Likert scale. The internal reliability of this questionnaire was .98 in this study. The items were translated into Chinese.

Montreal cognitive assessment

We used the Singaporean version of the MoCA test (Dong et al., 2010). MoCA is a widely used cognitive assessment tool for older adults. It assesses cognitive domains including memory, attention, language, and orientation. In the memory task, the participants were asked to repeat five words immediately after listening to the list as well as at the end of the test. To ensure consistency of pronunciation across test administrators, we used prerecorded computer-generated audio clips to present the words in the memory task. Due to administrative errors (e.g., certain items were not administered), 28 participants’ responses contained missing data (see Supplementary Methods for details on missing MoCA responses). Missing items were imputed to obtain estimated MoCA scores for descriptive purposes. However, these imputed scores were excluded from the main analyses because of potential reliability issues.

Procedure

The procedure was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Singapore University of Technology and Design (IRB-24-00656). Data collection sessions were conducted individually in a room at the active aging centers. The details of the study were explained to the participants, and their informed consent was obtained prior to the data collection sessions. During the sessions, participants first completed questionnaires, including the demographic and language background questionnaire as well as the MoCA test.

Participants were then introduced to the chatbot and its purposes and briefed on guidelines for utilizing and interacting with the chatbot (see Supplementary Methods for details). They were given approximately 20 min to interact with the chatbot. Researchers remained in the room to provide technical support if necessary so they would not feel panicked when they forgot how to use the chatbot or pressed the wrong button. The researchers were also present to ensure the participants talked to the chatbot for around 20 min, so they had sufficient exposure to provide feedback. After interacting with the chatbot, participants completed a set of questionnaires assessing their attitudes toward the chatbot, anthropomorphism, authenticity, AISI, and their intention to use the chatbot in daily life. Finally, a short qualitative interview was conducted to collect participants’ general feedback and comments on the chatbot, which will be used to inform subsequent design improvements.

Using one session of use and asking participants to evaluate their intention to use a technology, such as a chatbot, is a common and accepted experimental design in the literature (e.g., Cao et al., 2024; Gnewuch et al., 2022; Seitz, 2024). It allows us to measure users’ first impression of the technology and formation of adoption intention.

Data analysis

Data analysis was performed in RStudio (Posit team, 2025). Missing values were imputed with the missForest package (Stekhoven & Buehlmann, 2012). Primary analyses used linear regression via lm() to predict intention to use the social chatbot. Continuous predictors and controls were z-transformed with scale() before modeling. A baseline model including age, gender, years of education, and monthly household income showed that only years of education significantly predicted intention to use (b = −0.46, SE = 0.18, p = .013). Consequently, all subsequent models controlled only for years of education.

For mediation, the pathways of the indirect and direct effects were established using linear regressions. The mediate() function (Tingley et al., 2014), then tested the significance of the mediation path. Confidence intervals and p-values for the mediation effect were estimated via bias-corrected accelerated (BCa) bootstrap with 10,000 resamples.

Results

We hypothesized that (1) attitudinal belief and control belief would positively predict older adults’ intention to use the social chatbot, while health would negatively predict intention to use; (2) both anthropomorphism and authenticity would positively predict intention to use; (3) AISI would positively predict intention to use; (4) AISI would mediate the relationship between anthropomorphism and intention to use; and (5) AISI would mediate the relationship between authenticity and intention to use. We also expected that including sociorelational factors in the regression model would increase the model’s explanatory power beyond that of the STAM-only model. At Step 2 (Table 2, Model 2), regression analysis showed that, as hypothesized, control belief (e.g., perceived ease of use and facilitating conditions; b = 0.64, SE = 0.20, p = .002) significantly predicted intention to use the social chatbot among older adults. In contrast, attitudinal belief (b = 0.38, SE = 0.20, p = .061), gerontechnology anxiety (b = 0.17, SE = 0.14, p = .208), and health (b = −0.01, SE = 0.15, p = .925) were not significant predictors of intention to use.

At Step 3 (Table 2, Model 3), authenticity positively predicted older adults’ intention to use the social chatbot (b = 1.02, SE = 0.19, p < .001). In contrast, anthropomorphism did not predict intention (b = −0.17, SE = 0.18, p = .341). Control belief remained a significant predictor (b = 0.52, SE = 0.18, p = .005).

At Step 4 (Table 2, Model 4), AISI was a significant predictor of older adults’ intention to use (b = 0.99, SE = 0.16, p < .001), suggesting that adoption intention was positively associated with perceived close interaction with the social chatbot, which is consistent with the proposition that sociorelational factors are important in understanding older adults’ intention to adopt social chatbot. Control belief (b = 0.54, SE = 0.16, p = .001) and authenticity (b = 0.68, SE = 0.18, p < .001) remained significant predictors, indicating that intention to use may reflect cognitive appraisals of ease of use, self-efficacy and facilitating conditions, as well as perceptions of the chatbots’ authenticity and adaptive capacities. Interestingly, anthropomorphism (b = −0.34, SE = 0.16, p = .040) negatively predicted older adults’ intention to use the social chatbot after AISI was accounted for, such that greater perceived human-likeness corresponded with lower adoption intention, diverging from prior evidence that shows anthropomorphism as a positive facilitator of intention to use the chatbot. Health (b = −0.32, SE = 0.12, p = .008) was also a negative predictor, suggesting that older adults reporting poorer health expressed stronger intention to use social chatbots, potentially due to their greater health needs.

Comparing changes in R2 across models revealed that STAM factors explained 26% of the variance in older adults’ intention to use, and importantly, adding sociorelational factors increased explanatory power by an additional 27%. Thus, sociorelational factors provide added explainability comparable to that offered by STAM factors alone, indicating their importance when evaluating older adults’ intention to use social chatbots.

These findings suggested that older adults were more likely to indicate their intention to use social chatbots when the chatbots were perceived as easy to use, when they felt confident in their ability to use them independently, and when adequate resources and support were available—reflecting higher perceived control over using the technology, authentic, and characterized by relationally close interactions. Conversely, a chatbot’s human-likeness and users’ self-reported health might hinder the intention to use. However, these negative effects should be interpreted with caution because they emerged only after adjusting for AISI. Importantly, sociorelational factors played a role in older adults’ intention to use social chatbots comparable to STAM factors, showing that both types of factors contribute similarly to older adults’ intention to use social chatbots.

AISI as mediator between anthropomorphism, authenticity, and intention to use social chatbot

We conducted two mediation analyses: (1) anthropomorphism → AISI → intention to use (accounted for years of education, authenticity, and the four STAM factors); and (2) authenticity → AISI → intention to use (accounted for years of education, anthropomorphism, and the four STAM factors). Although AISI did not mediate the relationship between anthropomorphism and older adults’ intention to use the chatbot (b = 0.17, 95% CI = [−0.03, 0.44], p = .111; see Figure 2A), it significantly mediated the relationship between authenticity and older adults’ intention to use the chatbot (b = 0.35, 95% CI = [0.14, 0.66], p = .002; see Figure 2B). These results suggested that perceived close interaction with the social chatbot explained the relationship between its authenticity and older adults’ intention to use, but not the relationship between its human-likeness and intention to use.

Discussion

This study examined factors that relate to older adults’ intention to use an AI-powered social chatbot in everyday life. We examined STAM and sociorelational factors, i.e., anthropomorphism, authenticity, and AISI, to understand how these factors shape older adults’ intention to use. The usage dimension in explaining intention to use

First, we examined the contribution of STAM factors. To our knowledge, this study is the first to apply STAM to assess how older adults’ attitudinal factors and age-related characteristics influence their intention to use social chatbots. We found that control belief positively predicted intention to use. According to STAM, there are three subcomponents under control belief: perceived ease of use (1 item), self-efficacy (1 item), and facilitating conditions (2 items). Providing the support needed to facilitate adoption, for example, could help promote actual chatbot use among Chinese older adults (Yu & Chen, 2024). Exploratory analyses suggested that the positive relationship between control belief and intention to use was indeed predominantly driven by facilitating conditions (see Table S3)—such as having the necessary resources to use the system effectively. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution as two of the subcomponents were measured with single-item indicators, which may limit their reliability and the accuracy of the estimates.

We also found that older adults who reported poorer health had a greater intention to use the social chatbot. Similar to control belief, we ran an exploratory analysis on the four subcomponents: self-reported health status (1 item), cognitive abilities (1 item), social relationships (2 items), and life satisfaction (1 item), which suggested that the negative effect of health was driven by cognitive abilities and life satisfaction (see Table S3). One possible explanation is that individuals with weaker cognitive abilities may have higher perceived stigma and feel judged when interacting with others (Johansson et al., 2015). The chatbot’s nonjudgmental responses appeared to increase their willingness to interact with it (e.g., Al Mazroui & Alzyoudi, 2024). The negative association with life satisfaction, on the other hand, may suggest that older individuals experiencing lower life satisfaction are more motivated to use technology to compensate for unmet needs in life (Shin et al., 2023). For example, older adults who feel depressed or anxious due to low life satisfaction may find that interacting with social chatbots improves their mood, which in turn motivates continued engagement (Al Mazroui & Alzyoudi, 2024; Sulandari et al., 2024). However, similar to control belief, we caution the generalizability of these findings due to the limited measurement of the subcomponents. Future research should incorporate more comprehensive and validated multiitem measures in order to better understand the effects of individual subcomponents.

Contrary to several previous studies, attitudinal belief (e.g., attitude toward technology and perceived usefulness) did not uniquely predict older adults’ intention to use social chatbots. Similar findings were reported in Chen and Chan’s (2014) original STAM proposal, and a subsequent study by the same team (Ma et al., 2016). One possible explanation is that participants feared being stereotyped as technologically dependent or impaired when using a chatbot (e.g., Kim, 2021). In an interview study, Kim (2021) found that older adults often associated personal-assistant chatbots with negative stereotypes and worried about being judged as disabled. Our findings suggest that perceived usefulness does not motivate older adults to adopt a social chatbot.

The sociorelational dimension in explaining intention to use

Among the three sociorelational factors, i.e., authenticity, anthropomorphism, and AISI, authenticity remained a significant predictor of older adults’ intention even after controlling for STAM factors. This finding extended prior evidence that authenticity influences technology adoption among younger adults and the general population (Alimamy & Kuhail, 2023; Seitz, 2024) to older users. Our findings aligned with studies that found older adults appreciated ChatGPT’s ability to remember preferences and adjust responses accordingly (Al Mazroui & Alzyoudi, 2024). Such adaptability of the social chatbot’s responses to users’ preferences enhances engagement and supports daily activities. For example, ChatGPT recommended books or recipes according to users’ tastes and dietary preferences (Al Mazroui & Alzyoudi, 2024). These results underscore the importance of authenticity and response adaptability in social chatbot design.

In our sample, anthropomorphism negatively predicted older adults’ intention to use. This finding was consistent with existing research on nonsocial chatbots, where users exhibited greater privacy concern toward highly anthropomorphic chatbots, which in turn reduced their intention to accept the service of the chatbot (Song et al., 2024). Beyond privacy considerations, the uncanny valley effect (Mori et al., 2012) could also explain this phenomenon. When a chatbot’s appearance became overly human-like, users might experience eeriness, which could reduce their willingness to interact with it (Song & Shin, 2024). Thus, although some studies have documented positive effects of anthropomorphic design features in enhancing chatbot use (e.g., Ding & Najaf, 2024), our findings indicated that there are potential downsides of excessive anthropomorphism.

To our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate a direct association between perceived closeness of AI–human interaction and older adults’ intention to use social chatbots. Al Mazroui & Alzyoudi’s (2024) reported that older adults felt as if they were talking to a close friend or companion when interacting with ChatGPT, due to its adaptive conversation style and meaningful dialogue. Participants also noted that the chatbot’s nonjudgmental nature encouraged them to share thoughts and feelings more freely (Al Mazroui & Alzyoudi, 2024), which may further deepen the sense of closeness in human–AI interaction. Similarly, a study on older adults’ interaction with LOVOT, a social robot, found that some older users perceived the device as a grandchild and cared for it like a child (Tan et al., 2024). Our findings highlighted that the more the interaction between older adults and social chatbots resembles close human relationships, the greater their intention to use such social chatbots. Pentina et al. (2023) predicted that authenticity and anthropomorphism jointly influence AISI. In our study, AISI significantly mediated the relationship between authenticity and older adults’ intention to use the social chatbot. Although AISI did not mediate the relationship between anthropomorphism and intention to use, higher levels of anthropomorphism were associated with higher AISI at a marginal level (p < .06), which in turn was related to stronger intention to use. These findings suggested that perceived closeness of interaction is a more direct predictor of adoption intention than either authenticity or anthropomorphism.

It is not surprising that authenticity enhanced the perceived closeness of AI–human interaction. As noted earlier, adaptive dialogue makes older adults feel they are conversing with a friend. Our results also indicated a more complex mechanism underlying anthropomorphism. On the one hand, a human-like chatbot encourages closer interaction, which boosts intention to use. On the other hand, being too human-like (e.g., evoking discomfort or unease in users) may reduce intention to use. This double-edged sword effect of anthropomorphism was reported by Song et al. (2024), in which they found that anthropomorphism both increased users’ intention to use a task completion chatbot via perceived competence and decreased it through privacy concerns. However, we were unable to determine which characteristics of the social chatbot evoke discomfort in our study, indicating the need for further research to elucidate how anthropomorphism affects older adults’ chatbot adoption.

The roles of the usage factors and sociorelational factors in intention to use

Lastly, incorporating sociorelational factors into the model accounted for a comparable proportion of variance in older adults’ intention to use social chatbots as compared to STAM factors alone. These results indicated that the STAM framework alone might be insufficient to fully explain social chatbot adoption, whose main function is to provide companionship rather than to complete a task (Xu & Li, 2024). Unlike other gerontechnologies, social chatbots are designed to sustain communication and build long-term relationships. Accordingly, relational dynamics appear to play a central role in their adoption. Socioemotional selective theory (Carstensen et al., 1999) also provides a useful angle for interpreting the results. The theory posits that older adults prioritize close and meaningful relationships because of their perceived limited time horizons. Older adults, therefore, may be especially sensitive to the relational quality of social chatbots, such as authenticity and reciprocity, when deciding whether to engage with them. This interpretation also raises important developmental questions for future research: first, do younger adults, whose priority tends to favor social exploration, weigh sociorelational factors less heavily when evaluating social chatbots than older adults? Second, since the theory predicts that older adults shrink their social networks to focus on closer social partners, could a greater sense of relational closeness with a chatbot displace human ties? Addressing these questions will require longitudinal and experimental designs that measure human–AI interaction, social network dynamics, and well-being over time.

Several limitations warrant discussion. First, participants interacted with the chatbot for a brief 20-min session in a controlled setting, so their experience might not have been entirely natural or representative of everyday use. However, given that intention to use typically predicts higher actual technology adoption (Heerink et al., 2008; Huang & Lee, 2022; Turner et al., 2010), intention remains a valid proxy and a strong starting point for understanding the mechanisms of older adults’ social chatbot use. Future research should investigate older adults’ long-term interactions with social chatbots in naturalistic, everyday environments to enhance ecological validity and to understand how engagement, perceptions, and intentions evolve with sustained use. Second, as noted in the Method section, Ami Chat shares core features with the typical design of age-friendly chatbots identified by Du et al. (2024). Although this alignment suggests our findings may be extendable to similar social chatbots, further research is required to confirm the extent of this generalizability. Future research could allow participants to interact with a variety of commercially available chatbots to examine whether similar perceptions and intentions emerge. Third, some participants had difficulty interacting with Ami Chat due to internet connectivity issues or speech-recognition failures.2 We did not exclude them because their experiences contributed to the perceived ease of use, a control belief factor that was a key predictor of intention to use. Fourth, our sample focused on older adults who did not report any clinical impairment or disability. Therefore, our findings may or may not be generalizable to those who are clinically impaired or disabled. We noted that older adults with cognitive impairments may prioritize factors such as perceived usefulness and rely on specific features that cognitively healthy older adults may not need, such as reminders or wayfinding to assist their daily lives (Koo & Vizer, 2019; Thordardottir et al., 2019). Future studies could consider examining the determinants of chatbot adoption in cognitively impaired populations. Fifth, it is possible that our study is susceptible to social desirability bias, as researchers were present in the same room during evaluation. One possible effect of the researchers’ presence is that participants might have been more dependent on the researchers, rather than trying to solve the problems themselves when they faced difficulty in interacting with the chatbot. They might also have refrained from sharing too much about personal thoughts (e.g., memories and plans) with the chatbot during the session because of the presence of the researcher. We acknowledge that such social desirability bias needs to be taken into account when interpreting and analyzing the conversational data between participants and the chatbot in future work. Finally, this study employed a cross-sectional design. Causal relationships between factors and intention to use could not be established meaningfully. Future research should consider employing longitudinal or experimental designs to establish causal relationships between key factors and older adults’ intention to use social chatbots.

In conclusion, this study extended the STAM framework to evaluate older adults’ intention to use social chatbots and incorporated sociorelational factors that capture human–AI interaction. Although STAM factors, such as control belief and health, remained significant predictors, sociorelational factors, such as authenticity and close resemblances of the human–AI interaction, also positively predicted older adults’ intention to use the social chatbot, even after controlling for STAM factors. Furthermore, incorporating sociorelational factors into the model increased its explanatory power beyond that of the STAM-only model. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that STAM alone is insufficient to explain older adults’ adoption of social chatbots and that human–AI social interaction should be considered a critical component in understanding social technology acceptance among this population.
