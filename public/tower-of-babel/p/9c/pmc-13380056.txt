A whole community approach with Access and Functional Needs communities: A case study of hurricane evacuation planning in Sarasota County, Florida
Authors: Tisha Joseph Holmes, Meaghan McSorley, Carol Jeffers, Chelsea Austin, Emily Powell, Chris Uejio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionDuring 2014–2020, the Florida Department of Health in Sarasota County (DOH-Sarasota) developed a program to prepare for weather-related disaster impacts on the county’s Access and Functional Needs (AFN) population, with a special focus on emergency communication and evacuation. This case report presents a retrospective evaluation of the planning process used to engage diverse stakeholders, build inter-agency collaborations, and integrate disaster and health policies to enhance community resilience.Case presentationDOH-Sarasota fashioned their multi-tiered approach on agency-facing and community-engaged activities by implementing FEMA’s Whole Community approach to better understanding AFN communities, to engage and empower both AFN residents and the organizations that serve them, and to strengthen AFN community assets in the context of hurricane preparedness.OutcomesDOH-Sarasota’s planning process aimed to build critical new partnerships, incorporate the perspectives of AFN groups in the planning process, and establish critical resource networks. Introduction

Extreme storms like hurricanes, with powerful winds and storm surges enhanced by climate change, can have enduring impacts on well-being and health in coastal communities [1,2]. Evacuation is one of the primary risk management responses to avoid injury or death in the event of a landfalling hurricane. The decision to shelter in place or evacuate is influenced by a range of interrelated factors, including individual risk tolerance and perception, presence of social networks, quality of communication, and access to shelter and/or means to evacuate [[3], [4], [5], [6]]. Additionally, various household dynamics, as well as prevailing systemic socio-economic conditions and vulnerabilities, can affect individual disaster preparedness and risk decision-making [7].

Traditional transportation models used in evacuation planning assumes reliance on private vehicles as the primary mode of transportation resulting in a focus by transportation agencies on roadway capacity, contra-flow and routing to shelters [8]. However, these assumptions do not hold for populations with access and functional needs (AFN) [9,10]. The AFN definition goes beyond disability and focuses on function – what persons need during an emergency and the programmatic, architectural and communication modifications needed to use services and facilities – as required by federal civil rights laws (Table 1) [11]. Consequently, public transit and multi-modal services are expanding the options for car-less, elderly and other populations with access and functional needs yet continue to face logistical and procedural challenges to enhance access to evacuation and shelter networks [9,10,12].

In 2010, the State of Florida developed a series of regional evacuation studies to assess the state of readiness and effectiveness of existing evacuation routes [13]. However, the studies did not address AFN evacuation considerations. In order to clarify these needs for their AFN residents, the Florida Department of Health in Sarasota County (DOH-Sarasota) procured grant funding from the Building Resilience Against Climate Effects Program in Florida (FL BRACE) mini-grant process and the National Association of County and City Health Officials Medical Reserve Corps Challenge Award to identify and prepare for critical disaster needs of the county’s AFN population.

Sarasota County is one of Florida’s fastest growing areas, with a significant portion of the 479,000 residents being retirees and over the age of 65 (37%). According to the 2020 US Census Bureau, 8.4% of residents under age 65 reported having a disability. For older adults (65–74) about 8.8% have ambulatory difficulty. Over 40% of households are categorized as asset limited, income constrained, employed (ALICE) or in poverty. More specifically, 9.7% of the population is under 100% of the federal poverty level, 11.5% being children. Approximately 15.4% of the population live with severe housing problems and 52% of households are housing cost burdened [14]. Along with socio-economic vulnerabilities, the county’s low coastal topography, high water table, and barrier islands make it particularly susceptible to coastal and rainfall induced flooding. Recent estimates indicate that approximately 40% of all assets in the County are at risk under current tidal conditions. Accounting for an intermediate increase in sea level rise by 2070, the proportion increases to 50–60%, putting significant portions of the County at risk during a worst case scenario 100 year storm surge event [15].

There is established guidance regarding the role of stakeholder participation and engagement to inform disaster risk management processes [[16], [17], [18]]. However, there are limited examples of how local government agencies work with specific at-risk groups to implement this guidance to improve public engagement around preparedness and response behaviors [19]. This retrospective case report charts Sarasota County’s efforts to redress gaps in AFN preparedness using the FEMA Whole Community Approach.

Case report

FEMA’s whole community approach

FEMA’s Whole Community approach is a federal disaster planning framework which activates a wide range of community stakeholders - individuals, community-based organizations (CBOs), faith-based organizations (FBOs), non-profits, academia, governments – establishing their roles in preparing for, responding to and recovering from disasters [20]. This inclusive planning model is guided by three core principles: 1. Community assessment, 2. Engagement and empowerment, and 3. Strengthening community assets. Examples of how these principles have been applied in local settings are described in Table 2.

Given the range of challenges AFN communities face, Sarasota employed FEMA’s approach to better understanding AFN communities, to engaging and empowering both AFN residents and the organizations that serve them, and to strengthening AFN community assets in the context of hurricane preparedness.

Understanding AFN disaster risk & preparedness

County program staff used population and climate hazard vulnerability data from federal and county databases to map areas of risk for different hurricane intensities. Spatial analysis identified concentrations of social vulnerability (Fig. 1) and medical vulnerability (Fig. 2) within storm surge areas, including locations of facility types critical to supporting AFN residents (e.g., special needs/emergency shelters). Infrastructure, such as roads, and evacuation routes were also mapped in relation to storm surge zones under different intensities (e.g. category 1–5 storm event; different sea level rise projections). Combining these data sets enabled the identification of areas of localized, compound risk zones and proximal community assets/resources (e.g., hospitals) for use in future planning.

County staff then recruited AFN-serving organizations and AFN residents located in these zones to participate in four listening session workshops in 2015 (Fig. 3).

Workshops elicited the unique challenges AFN communities face during hurricanes and strategies to address these challenges. Workshops involved a variety of discussion questions and exercises, including having participants walk through real-world scenarios AFN residents (e.g., someone who cannot see) might face in evacuating for a hurricane. Workshop feedback was collected in person through structured notetaking. Content analysis was used to determine barrier and facilitator themes across all workshops [23]. Engage and empower all AFN and those who serve them

County staff went on to collaborate with several AFN-serving organizations, sharing data and expertise, conducting joint planning, and adding infrastructure for AFN residents. The City of Sarasota requested DOH-Sarasota’s technical advice on including AFN in their city-wide, post-redevelopment, and long-term recovery plans. Additionally, DOH-Sarasota accepted the City’s invitation to sit on an advisory committee for upcoming projects where AFN needs would need to be included/considered. DOH-Sarasota also began working with County Emergency Management to register key AFN groups for the Everbridge alert system, which provides critical information to affected communities during crises such as hurricanes. Further, through a partnership with Sarasota County Area Transit, Sarasota Emergency Management developed a system to designate additional bus pickup points specifically for AFN residents.

Using findings from the community listening workshops, DOH-Sarasota also engaged in a multi-organizational planning collaborative. Emergency Management, City of Sarasota, Suncoast Disaster Healthcare Coalition, Sarasota Community Organizations Active in Disaster, and the Suncoast Center for Independent Living collectively updated and augmented climate, health and emergency response policies in county disaster plans. Relatedly, Sarasota County updated their own emergency plans with new climate vulnerability and AFN data as well.

Leverage and strengthen AFN community assets and networks

To strengthen AFN preparedness, county staff also provided preparedness resources, materials, and trainings across the county. An outreach campaign was conducted in high-risk zones with a focus on mobile home parks. Outreach materials included handouts, infographics/flyers and door hangers with messaging on water and food sanitization during power outages, shut-off practices for natural gas and water, shopping lists to feed families during and after a disaster, as well as emergency preparedness vehicle care and driving tips. Further, to help AFN families who shelter in place during a hurricane, Sarasota’s Department of Health produced a video providing basic information about shelter operations and procedures. The video was broadcasted through county media networks.

A series of disaster preparedness presentations (n = 16) were also given to AFN-serving groups, including civic organizations (e.g., Cobblestone Community Emergency Response Team, United Way Harvest House participants), faith-based institutions (e.g., Our Lady Queen of Martyrs Church), businesses, and neighborhood/living associations (e.g., the Arlington Group). Presentations covered All-Hazards Survival, first aid and CPR training, Stop the Bleed, Active Bystander, Public-Private Partnership, emergency kit preparation, household- and organization -level emergency planning consultations, and practical advice for health and medical agencies implementing the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services’ (CMS) new Emergency for Environmental Controls for Nursing Homes Rule.

Additionally, DOH-Sarasota conducted multiple rounds of All-Hazards Survival Training Workshops to AFN populations and AFN-serving organizations with direct involvement in disaster preparedness (n = 11 workshops). Workshop participants were all provided an emergency kit for attending, which included all-hazards radios, survival whistles, First Aid books, and a Sarasota County Hurricane Guide describing local resources for before, during, and after hurricanes.

The reach and short-term effectiveness of All-Hazards Survival (AHS) workshops was assessed using attendance records, pre-and post-tests, and workshop evaluation forms. Pre and post-test survey items were designed based on their fit to training content and measured preparedness knowledge using true/false statements. Evaluation forms used likert scale questions about workshop acceptability and attitudinal statements about disaster preparedness, as well as open-ended questions about workshop quality.

On average, each workshop had 24 participants (Min = 12, Max = 77). Altogether, 266 people attended the workshops, and another 226 were listed as household members of attendees (for a total reach of 492). Across all sites, 46 attendees completed a pretest (17.3% response rate) and 125 completed a posttest (47.0% response rate). Workshop facilitators noted that most workshop participants arrived shortly after the pre-test was administered, resulting in a lower response rate for the pre-test.

Content areas where participants saw the greatest improvements in knowledge according to post-test scores were around how much water on the roads is needed to create unsafe driving conditions (29% increase) and the rescue compression scenario (17% increase). Knowledge about when to remove someone from a burning car remained low, with one percent fewer correct answers at posttest (incorrect=79). This knowledge decrease could be an artifact of more posttest takers who started with low baseline knowledge, and which was not changed through workshop content.

Participant ratings of knowledge and beliefs following the workshops indicated improvements for all outcomes (Fig. 5). Across all items, 84% or more of participant answers demonstrated improvements in knowledge or beliefs, as well as high satisfaction with the workshop. Workshop characteristics that participants most reported liking were the provision of emergency kits, the inclusion of family/children in disaster planning, and first aid best practices such as correctly treating burns. Participants indicated learning the importance of having a family plan for disasters/emergencies, how to prepare for and respond to a fire (e.g., sleeping with door closed at night), and the importance of gathering supplies (e.g. extra medications, radio, etc.). Topics participants wanted more information on were evacuation routes and the locations of the closest shelters to their homes, as well as how to determine the amount of water and food to stock for emergencies.

Discussion

This case study characterizes DOH-Sarasota’s efforts to implement FEMA’s Whole Community Approach with AFN communities, adding to the limited practice-based literature [21,24,25]. Through this process, Sarasota expanded their awareness of the AFN preparedness context, revealing pockets of vulnerability throughout the county (e.g., Northern Sarasota) and specific challenges like insufficient messaging and supplies. Sarasota County engaged private and public entities to better integrate AFN needs into planning and service delivery. In response to feedback, Sarasota also used outreach, communications, and trainings in high-risk areas to get needed supplies and information directly to AFN residents. Short-term evaluations of trainings showed high acceptability among participants, while knowledge gains were less clear due to limited baseline measurement (Table 3).

County Health Departments play an integral role in facilitating collaborative interfaces given they understand institutional practices and norms and multiple levels of government (e.g., state, municipal, etc.) but also work directly within communities and have relationships with leaders and service providers already established. As McNaught et al. 2024 indicate shifting the centralized authority paradigm to a deeper co-governance approach can delegate authority of policy and decision-making [26]. DOH-Sarasota created such opportunities for AFN groups to share their experiences to inform preparedness policies. Additionally, these engagements supported the development of deeper network ties so that a variety of stakeholders including AFN-serving organizations, organizational coalitions, and a variety of municipal and County departments could be in conversation with each other. Having dedicated staff, funding and institutional infrastructure to nurture these partnerships will facilitate mainstreaming over the long term. However, in the age of constrained resources and divisive politics, barriers related to ongoing distrust and/or lack of transparency, legal constraints and power asymmetries continue to be roadblocks for deeper and sustained collaboration and co-design [26,27].

The broad definition of AFN captures a wide range of physical and socio-economic conditions and as Kailes and Enders (2006) argue, terms such as “Special Needs” can lead to ambiguous and vague planning decisions as it obscures the specific nature of needs [28]. DOH-Sarasota attempted to “meet folks where they were” rather than treating all participants as a monolith. Conducting multiple types of participatory engagements in different contexts, structures, and venues is resource- and personnel-intensive [29,30]. However, these efforts acknowledge that vulnerability is not uniform and that understanding this diversity of needs is necessary to better target resources [31]. There is still work to be done to ensure that opportunities for more representation in formal agency leadership, for example, is possible. Challenges also remain related to developing the scale and deploying a range of resources to appropriately serve a diversity of access and functional needs before, during, and after disasters.

There is need for more systemic evaluation of continued AFN engagement and outcomes, particularly in the event of extreme storms. Learning partnerships between academic, government, and community groups is one way to advance robust and systematic study of community resilience during disasters [32]. Also, co-production of evaluations with service providers to track community impact and outcomes can provide local ground truthing and evidence of change [33]. However, with reductions in funding from Federal and State sources, the ability to facilitate repeated and/or ongoing evaluation of engagement is even more constrained.

Conclusion

DOH-Sarasota’s Whole Community AFN Disaster Resilience Planning Program sought to tailor disaster preparedness and response measures to meet the localized needs of a culturally and definitionally diverse population. By finding opportunities to gather and share knowledge in sustained and reflexive ways the case study is a translatable example of how public health and emergency management agencies can expand their capacities and policies to integrate, collaborate, educate and innovate towards increased community resilience for AFN populations. Incorporating these findings to improve emergency plans would be the first step, however ensuring that strategies are implemented and maintained relationships will be necessary in an age of retracted funding from the federal and state levels.
