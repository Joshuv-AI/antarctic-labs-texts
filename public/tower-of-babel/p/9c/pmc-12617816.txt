Efficacy and Safety of Mirabegron in Males with Overactive Bladder and Lower Urinary Tract Symptoms: Analysis from an Integrated Database
Authors: Farid Abdul Hadi, Arianne Schild, Merete Kock Hansen, Swapneel Anaokar, Sender Herschorn
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessageMirabegron demonstrated efficacy among males with overactive bladder with or without benign prostate enlargement receiving medications for the management of lower urinary tract symptoms. The use of mirabegron was not associated with urinary retention in those taking alpha blockers or other medications for lower urinary tract symptoms.Keywords: β3-Adrenoceptor agonist, Benign prostate enlargement, Lower urinary tract symptoms, Overactive bladder

Introduction

Overactive bladder (OAB) is a syndrome of lower urinary tract symptoms (LUTS) defined as urinary urgency, with or without urgency urinary incontinence, usually with increased daytime frequency and nocturia, if there is no proven infection or other obvious pathology [1]. OAB is a common condition with an estimated prevalence of 7.2–31.7% [[2], [3], [4], [5]]. The prevalence of OAB is similar in men and women [6,7] and increases with advancing age [7]. OAB is associated with clinical burden, including significant impacts on health care costs and health-related quality of life [6].

Diagnosing OAB can be made more difficult by the presence of voiding symptoms in men living with benign prostate enlargement (BPE, also called benign prostate hyperplasia [BPH]) [8]. BPE is also commonly associated with LUTS [9]. Voiding and storage symptoms are the hallmark of BPE. Epidemiological studies have shown that BPE-associated LUTS are reported more frequently in older men, with the prevalence increasing from 50–75% in men aged 50 yr or older up to 80% in men aged 70 yr or older [8].

Treatment with antimuscarinics and/or β3-adrenoceptor agonists is recommended by international guidelines if conservative management strategies such as containment devices or fluid/caffeine intake reduction are ineffective [10]. Both drug classes, which inhibit detrusor muscle contraction through different mechanisms, reduce the occurrence of urinary incontinence and micturition symptoms [6]. Mirabegron is a selective β3 adrenoceptor agonist that stimulates the β3 adrenoceptors in the detrusor muscle of the bladder, causing relaxation of the bladder muscle during the storage phase of micturition. The efficacy and safety of mirabegron have been demonstrated in a comprehensive clinical trial program [[11], [12], [13]]. Mirabegron was first approved in Japan in 2011; as of June 2024, mirabegron has been approved in 67 countries, and the global cumulative patient exposure amounts to over 40 million patients. An earlier post hoc pooled analysis of mirabegron studies in adults with OAB did not specifically analyze male participants [14]. We conducted a post hoc analysis of 13 phase 2–4 studies to evaluate the efficacy and safety of mirabegron in adult male participants with OAB, with or without BPE receiving medications for LUTS management.

Patients and methods

Study design and objective

This is a post hoc analysis of 13 12-wk phase 2–4 studies with mirabegron oral controlled absorption system formulation, with similar design and exposure duration (2- or 4-wk single-blind placebo run-in followed by a 12-wk double-blind treatment period; Table 1). The main objective was to evaluate the efficacy and safety of mirabegron in adult male OAB participants with or without BPE receiving medications for LUTS management.

Analysis populations

The analysis population included participants from the OAB 12-wk phase 2–4 studies with the following four treatment arms: (1) mirabegron beginning at 25 mg then escalated to 50 mg (25/50 mg), (2) mirabegron 50 mg, (3) mirabegron 25/50 mg + tamsulosin hydrochloride (HCl) 0.4 mg, and (4) mirabegron 50 mg + tamsulosin HCl 0.2 mg.

The study population was divided into three cohorts: cohort 1, OAB males overall (including those not on any other LUTS treatment); cohort 2, OAB males with concomitant BPE taking alpha blockers; and cohort 3, OAB males with concomitant BPE taking other LUTS medications (not alpha blockers). Each cohort was stratified on the basis of age: <65 and ≥65 yr of age. BPE was identified by reporting of medical history of BPE (history defined by the following terms: benign prostatic hyperplasia, prostatic obstruction, prostatism, benign neoplasm of prostate, prostatic adenoma, transurethral prostatectomy, prostatomegaly, terminal dribbling [International Classification of Diseases tenth revision definition], urine flow decreased, urinary hesitation, and bladder outlet obstruction) within the individual studies. Patients with a neurogenic bladder were excluded from all individual studies. The cohort of OAB males with BPE taking alpha blockers was identified by all concomitant alpha blocker medications, including tamsulosin, silodosin, alfuzosin, doxazosin, and terazosin. The cohort of OAB males with BPE taking other LUTS medications was identified by concomitant medications for LUTS: antimuscarinics (solifenacin, tolterodine, fesoterodine, oxybutinin, propiverine, trospium, and darifenacin), 5-alpha reductase inhibitors (finasteride and dutasteride), and phosphodiesterase type 5 inhibitors (sildenafil, tadalafil, vardenafil, and avanafil). Full analysis sets (FASs) and safety analysis sets (SASs) were used for individual studies.

Endpoints

The primary efficacy endpoints were changes from baseline to final visit (12 wk) in the mean number of micturitions per 24 h and the mean number of incontinence episodes per 24 h. Means were calculated by the sum of all marked episodes in the micturition diary divided by the number of days where episodes were marked; the mean number of micturitions was calculated separately from the mean number of incontinence episodes. The secondary efficacy endpoints included changes from baseline to final visit (12 wk) in the mean number of urgency incontinence episodes per 24 h, mean number of urgency episodes per 24 h (Patient's Perception of Intensity of Urgency Scale [PPIUS] grade 3 or 4), mean number of nocturia episodes per 24 h, and mean volume voided per micturition. Mean daily urgency incontinence episodes, urgency episodes, and nocturia were all calculated in a manner similar to the calculation of micturitions. All recorded volumes were used for calculating the mean volume voided per micturition.

The secondary efficacy endpoints also included, as obtained from patient-reported outcomes (PROs), change from baseline to final visit (12 wk) in the total score of the Overactive Bladder Questionnaire (OAB-q), total score of the Patient Perception of Bladder Condition (PPBC) questionnaire, and the total International Prostate Symptom Score (IPSS). The 33-item self-reported OAB-q comprises an eight-item symptom bother scale (scores ranged from 0 to 100; higher scores indicated greater symptom bother) and 25 health-related quality of life items [15]. The PPBC questionnaire is a single question with scores of 1–6, with higher scores indicating worse bladder condition [16]. The seven-item IPSS questionnaire evaluates storage and voiding symptoms in participants with BPH. The total score ranges from 0 to 35 and indicates the severity of LUTS, from mild to severe [17]. IPSS data were not available for all cohorts, as most studies did not include this evaluation in the protocol. For all efficacy endpoints, the value calculated from the last diary period before the first administration of the study drug was regarded as the baseline value. The analysis variables from the individual studies were used in the summaries.

The secondary safety endpoints included frequency of treatment-emergent adverse events (TEAEs). TEAEs for all studies included in this analysis were defined as any adverse events (AEs) observed after starting the administration of the study drug. Medical Dictionary for Regulatory Activities version 20.1 was used for coding AEs. AEs of special interest were defined by the following preferred terms: urinary retention, increased postvoid residual (PVR) volume, increased blood pressure/hypertension, dry mouth, constipation, and diarrhea.

Statistical analysis

All data processing, summarization, and analyses were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS institute, Cary, NC, USA). Demographics and baseline characteristics were summarized by descriptive statistics. Efficacy endpoints were measured as changes from baseline to the end of treatment (week 12) using mixed models for repeated measures. The treatment effect of the primary endpoints was estimated, with corresponding 95% confidence intervals (CIs), using a model with treatment, visit (weeks 4, 8, and 12), age group (<65 and ≥65 yr of age), and the interaction between treatment and visit as fixed effects and the baseline value of the (corresponding) endpoint as a covariate. Participants nested within the study were included as a random effect (slope). If sample sizes were too small for the model to converge, the analysis was not reported. The results of the mixed models for repeated measures were presented with least squares (LS) means and two-sided 95% CIs for mean changes from baseline within each treatment group. Descriptive statistics were presented for safety endpoints using the SASs. No new imputation of missing data for endpoints/variables was done beyond what was done in the original studies (eg, start and stop dates of AEs and concomitant medication).

Results

In total, the post hoc analysis included 1631 participants from the 12-wk phase 2–4 studies on OAB in four treatment arms: (1) mirabegron 25/50 mg (n = 68), (2) mirabegron 50 mg (n = 944), (3) mirabegron 25/50 mg + tamsulosin HCl 0.4 mg (n = 337), and (4) mirabegron 50 mg + tamsulosin HCl 0.2 mg (n = 282). Results are presented here across both age groups, all three cohorts, and all four treatment arms. Subgroup analyses by age groups can be found in the Supplementary material (primary, Supplementary Table 1; secondary—episodes and volume voided, Supplementary Table 2; secondary—PROs, Supplementary Table 3; and safety, Supplementary Table 4).

Participant characteristics

Demographic and baseline characteristics were generally similar among the three cohorts (Table 2). Most of the participants were White (65%), followed by Asian (32%), and Black/African American (2%). The mean body mass index was similar across all cohorts, ranging from 26 to 28 kg/m2. The mean baseline PVR volume was 27.3 ml. This volume was higher (35.9 ml) in the cohort taking other LUTS medications. The mean age for the FASs was 62.4 yr. Among participants with BPE who were taking alpha blockers, the majority (61%) were aged ≥65 yr and half of them were Asian (59%). More than 65% of the participants received concomitant medications.

Improvements in LS mean change from baseline to week 12 in OAB-q total score and PPBC total score were seen in participants taking mirabegron 50 mg (all cohorts), mirabegron 25/50 mg (all cohorts), mirabegron 50 mg + tamsulosin HCl 0.2 mg (cohorts 1 and 2), and mirabegron 25/50 mg + tamsulosin HCl 0.4 mg (cohorts 1 and 2; Table 4). Secondary endpoint—safety

Details on safety results can be found in the Supplementary material, with results across all age groups summarized here. TEAEs occurred in <50% of participants across all age groups (across treatment groups); <20% of them were drug related (Table 5). Serious TEAEs were reported in <5% of participants, and drug-related serious TEAEs were reported in ≤1% of participants across all age groups (across treatment groups). No deaths were reported in any cohort. Most common TEAEs were upper respiratory tract infection, nasopharyngitis, and hypertension.

Among TEAEs of special interest, urinary retention incidence was low (0–1%) in the 50 mg treatment groups, and was comparable between participants taking only mirabegron and those with concomitant BPE taking alpha blockers. Other TEAEs of special interest in the 50 mg treatment groups included increased PVR volume (no instances) and increased blood pressure/hypertension (5–7%). Most cases of reported increase in blood pressure/hypertension were considered minor in this analysis.

No AEs of urinary retention were reported in participants with concomitant BPE taking other LUTS medications (not alpha blockers).

Discussion

As clinicians tend to put BPE treatment at the top of their list while treating OAB as the diagnosis of exclusion, it is important to understand the effects of coadministration of BPE medication with mirabegron. In routine clinical practice, male patients are frequently managed with a primary focus on prostate-related conditions instead of bladder, which highlights the need to investigate the efficacy of mirabegron in this specific patient population. The results of this post hoc analysis show that mirabegron is effective and safe in male OAB patients, with or without BPE or receiving medications for LUTS management. Additionally, patients with BPE may benefit more in the voiding symptom, reflected by higher improvement in mean volume voided per micturition. Across the three cohorts, there was an improvement from baseline to week 12 for the primary endpoints of the mean number of micturitions per 24 h and mean number of incontinence episodes per 24 h. A visual overview of this manuscript is displayed in Supplementary Fig. 1. Results from this post hoc analysis are consistent with those of a meta-analysis showing that an α1-blocker plus mirabegron and an α1-blocker plus an antimuscarinic have similar safety and efficacy profiles in male patients with LUTS secondary to BPE and OAB [18].

The secondary efficacy endpoints generally showed improvement from baseline to week 12, including the mean number of urgency incontinence episodes per 24 h, mean number of urgency episodes per 24 h (PPIUS grade 3 or 4), mean number of nocturia episodes per 24 h, total PPBC score, and total IPSS. Within each age category (<65 and ≥65 yr olds), participants taking alpha blockers had higher levels of improvement in the volume voided per micturition, as expected based on the alpha blocker mechanism of action. Safety results in the analysis were generally consistent with the known profile of mirabegron.

Urinary retention incidence was low in the 50 mg treatment groups and was comparable in participants with concomitant BPE taking alpha blockers. No AEs of urinary retention were reported in participants with concomitant BPE taking other LUTS medications (not alpha blockers). However, the number of participants taking other LUTS medications was small; therefore, these outcomes need to be interpreted with caution. The incidence of urinary retention was similar for different age groups in the 50 mg treatment group. An increase in PVR, observed previously in PLUS and MATCH [8,19], which were specifically designed for add-on mirabegron and tamsulosin administration, was observed only in the treatment groups taking tamsulosin HCl. The incidence varied between 0.6% and 1.3% across age groups and cohorts. This is a known outcome of concomitant mirabegron and tamsulosin administration [20], and the affected patient numbers were small. The use of mirabegron in males with or without BPE was not associated with an increase in PVR volume or urinary retention episodes. The incidence of hypertension was consistently low in different age groups and across different cohorts [21,22]. In total, a slightly higher incidence of gastrointestinal TEAEs occurring in ≥2% of participants in the overall cohort (dry mouth, constipation, and anal incontinence) was observed in the age group of ≥65 yr compared with the age group of <65 yr.

This analysis was designed to estimate efficacy within each cohort and subgroup, but we did not conduct statistical comparisons between groups, which may limit direct interpretability across cohorts. Additionally, the generalizability of these findings may be constrained by differences between clinical trial populations and the broader patient population seen in routine practice.

Conclusions

The results show that mirabegron improves outcomes in males with OAB, both with and without BPE, and provide evidence to support its use in clinical decision-making. Specifically, add-on mirabegron in BPE patients already receiving an alpha blocker yields meaningful symptom improvement with a favorable safety profile and minimal increases in reported urinary retention. Given these outcomes, clinicians should consider mirabegron as a viable treatment option for male patients with OAB with and without BPE.
