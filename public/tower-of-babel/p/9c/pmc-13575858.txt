Association Between Serum Uric Acid and Pulmonary Hypertension in Takayasu Arteritis With Pulmonary Artery Involvement: A Cross‐Sectional Study
Authors: Jinzhi Wang, Shuwen Zheng, Ruzetuoheti Yiminniyaze, Hanyu Zheng, Shuai Zhang, Han Tian, Yanhua Kong, Wanmu Xie, Qiang Huang, Zhenguo Zhai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTPulmonary hypertension (PH) is a severe complication of Takayasu arteritis (TAK) with pulmonary artery involvement (PAI), associated with poor prognosis. Serum uric acid (UA) has been linked to both TAK severity and PH in other settings, but its role specifically in TAK‐PAI‐associated PH remains unclear. This study aimed to investigate the relationship between serum UA and the presence and severity of PH in TAK patients with confirmed PAI. This cross‐sectional study enrolled 90 consecutive TAK patients with PAI documented by CT pulmonary angiography. All patients underwent echocardiography; those with echocardiographic signs of PH underwent confirmatory right heart catheterization (RHC). PH was defined as mean pulmonary artery pressure > 20 mmHg with pulmonary artery wedge pressure ≤ 15 mmHg (2022 ESC/ERS criteria). Serum UA was measured at baseline. Multivariable logistic regression, subgroup analyses, and restricted cubic spline (RCS) were performed. Among 90 patients, 42 (46.7%) met the PH criteria. Patients with PH had significantly higher serum UA levels than those without PH (400.5 ± 140.8 vs. 305.2 ± 91.6 μmol/L, p < 0.001). After adjusting for age, sex, eGFR, and Kerr score (disease activity), each 10 μmol/L increase in UA was associated with a 10% higher odds of PH risk (OR = 1.10, 95% CI 1.04–1.16, p = 0.001). Compared with the lowest tertile (< 284 μmol/L), the highest tertile (≥ 377 μmol/L) had an 8‐fold increased odds of PH risk (OR = 8.03, 95% CI 2.03–31.79, p = 0.003). Subgroup analyses showed consistent associations across sex, disease activity, and PAI pattern. RCS analysis revealed no significant nonlinearity (p = 0.729), indicating a predominantly linear dose–response relationship. Elevated serum uric acid is independently associated with higher odds of PH in TAK patients with PAI, exhibiting a linear dose–response pattern. A uric acid level ≥ 377 μmol/L may identify patients at substantially increased odds of PH. Serum UA could serve as a simple, noninvasive biomarker for PH risk stratification in this high‐risk population. Trial Registration: ClinicalTrials.gov: NCT07184814.

Introduction

Takayasu arteritis (TAK), a chronic, progressive, granulomatous large‐vessel vasculitis primarily affecting the aorta and its major branches, has an estimated annual incidence of 1.0–2.2 per million and a prevalence ranging from 0.9 to 40 per million [1, 2, 3]. Pulmonary artery involvement (PAI) is a frequent and severe complication of TAK, occurring in 6.9%–66.0% of patients [4, 5]. PAI often leads to pulmonary artery stenosis, occlusion, or thrombosis, ultimately resulting in pulmonary hypertension (PH) [6]. Among TAK patients with PAI, the prevalence of PH is notably high, ranging from 42.2% to 61.7% [7, 8]. The development of PH significantly worsens prognosis; previous studies indicate that within PAI patients, the mortality rate in those with PH is seven‐fold higher than in those without PH [5, 9]. Furthermore, the overall 1‐year, 3‐year, and 5‐year survival rates for TAK‐associated PH patients are reported to be only 94.0%, 83.2%, and 77.2%, respectively [6]. Therefore, the early identification of patients at high risk for developing PH among those with TAK‐PAI holds significant clinical importance [10].

Uric acid (UA), the end product of purine metabolism, has emerged in recent years as a potential biomarker in cardiovascular and pulmonary vascular diseases [11, 12]. Substantial evidence indicates a strong association between hyperuricemia and PH. Elevated UA levels are linked to endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) inhibition, increased oxidative stress, and enhanced production of inflammatory cytokines [13]. Although UA is recognized as a risk factor and prognostic marker in conditions like systemic sclerosis‐associated pulmonary arterial hypertension (PAH) and left heart disease‐related PH [14, 15], its specific role in TAK‐PH remains underexplored. Preliminary evidence suggests a potential association between hyperuricemia and vascular damage in other vasculitides [16, 17], yet comprehensive data supporting this link within the unique context of TAK‐PAI and PH are lacking.

Within the spectrum of TAK, hyperuricemia has also been associated with disease severity and vascular complications [18]. A recent study by Liao and colleagues found that a serum uric acid level ≥ 284.5 μmol/L was associated with an increased risk of PAI in TAK patients (OR = 2.108, 95% CI 1.063–4.180, p = 0.033), while a UA level ≥ 328.1 μmol/L was an independent predictor of adverse events related to PAI (HR = 2.595, 95% CI 1.198–5.622, p = 0.016) [19]. However, despite these findings, the direct relationship between serum UA and PH specifically in TAK‐PAI patients has not been investigated. Given that PH represents the most severe hemodynamic consequence of PAI and that early detection in this high‐risk population is crucial, exploring whether serum UA could serve as a simple, noninvasive biomarker for PH risk stratification in TAK‐PAI patients is both clinically relevant and timely.

Therefore, this study aims to investigate the association between serum uric acid levels and PH in patients with Takayasu arteritis involving the pulmonary arteries. We hypothesize that hyperuricemia is independently associated with an increased risk of PH within this specific population and may serve as a valuable clinical screening tool.

Methods

Study Design and Population

This was a cross‐sectional study that consecutively enrolled patients with Takayasu arteritis (TAK) hospitalized at China–Japan Friendship Hospital from January 2016 to June 2025. The study protocol complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of China–Japan Friendship Hospital (Approval No. 2024‐KY‐033‐2). This study is part of the CAPTAIN study and has been registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT07184814).

Inclusion criteria: meeting the 1990 ACR or 2022 ACR/EULAR classification criteria for Takayasu arteritis; confirmed PAI by computed tomography pulmonary angiography (CTPA), defined as stenosis, occlusion, wall thickening, or aneurysm of the pulmonary arteries; availability of serum uric acid (UA) measurement at baseline; all patients underwent echocardiography to assess pulmonary artery pressure. Exclusion criteria: use of urate‐lowering drugs (allopurinol, febuxostat, benzbromarone, etc.) within 1 month before blood sampling; estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR) < 30 mL/min/1.73 m2 (severe chronic kidney disease); PH due to other causes; acute infection or active malignancy.

For patients with clinical manifestations and imaging findings suggestive of PAI but lacking evidence of systemic large vessel involvement, making it difficult to strictly meet the classification criteria, the final diagnosis was confirmed by a multidisciplinary team consisting of experienced rheumatologists, pulmonologists, and radiologists.

Echocardiographic Screening

All patients underwent transthoracic echocardiography after admission. Echocardiographic signs suggestive of PH included tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity > 2.8 m/s or estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure > 35 mmHg. Patients with at least one of these signs were considered echocardiography‑positive and were referred for right heart catheterization (RHC). Those without the above signs did not undergo RHC.

Right Heart Catheterization and Definition of Pulmonary Hypertension

RHC was performed only in echocardiography‑positive patients. The procedure was performed via the internal jugular or femoral vein using a Swan‑Ganz catheter. The following hemodynamic parameters were recorded: right atrial pressure, pulmonary artery pressure (systolic, diastolic, mean), pulmonary artery wedge pressure (PAWP), and cardiac output (CO, thermodilution method). Pulmonary vascular resistance (PVR) was calculated as (mPAP − PAWP)/CO × 80 dyn·s·cm−5.

Definition of PH: According to the 2022 ESC/ERS hemodynamic criteria, PH was defined as mean pulmonary artery pressure (mPAP) > 20 mmHg at rest with PAWP ≤ 15 mmHg (i.e., precapillary PH).

Patients were divided into two groups: PH group: Echocardiography‑positive patients with RHC‑confirmed mPAP > 20 mmHg and PAWP ≤ 15 mmHg. Non‑PH group: (1) Echocardiography‑negative patients who did not undergo RHC; (2) Echocardiography‑positive patients whose RHC showed mPAP ≤ 20 mmHg.

Data Collection

The following demographic and clinical data were extracted from electronic medical records. Demographics: age, sex, body mass index (BMI). TAK‑related characteristics: disease duration (time from symptom onset to the current visit), Kerr score, and glucocorticoid use (yes/no). The Kerr score was used to assess disease activity, comprising four items: constitutional symptoms, signs of vascular ischemia/inflammation, imaging evidence of active disease, and elevated ESR/CRP. Each item scored 1 point, total score 0–4. In this study, a score ≥ 2 was defined as active disease, and 0–1 as inactive disease. Laboratory tests: serum uric acid (enzymatic colorimetric method), creatinine, eGFR (CKD‑EPI equation), C‑reactive protein (CRP), erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR), and N‑terminal pro‑B‑type natriuretic peptide (NT‑proBNP). CTPA: type of PAI (stenosis > 50%, occlusion, wall thickening, aneurysm) and extent (unilateral/bilateral, main/lobar/segmental pulmonary arteries). Echocardiographic parameters: estimated pulmonary artery systolic pressure, tricuspid regurgitation peak velocity, right ventricular size and function. Hemodynamic parameters (patients undergoing RHC): mPAP, PAWP, PVR, cardiac index (CI).

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (if normally distributed) or median (interquartile range) (if non‑normally distributed). Categorical variables were expressed as numbers (percentages). Comparisons between the PH and non‑PH groups were performed using independent samples t‑test or Mann–Whitney U test for continuous variables, and χ 2 test or Fisher's exact test for categorical variables. Primary analysis: Multivariable logistic regression was used to evaluate the association between serum uric acid and the risk of PH. Three models were constructed: Model 1: unadjusted. Model 2: adjusted for age and sex. Model 3: adjusted for age, sex, eGFR, and Kerr score (active/inactive).

Uric acid was analyzed both as a continuous variable (per 10 μmol/L increase) and as tertiles (T1, T2, T3). Tertile cut‑offs were determined based on the sample distribution. Nonlinear assessment: Smooth curve fitting (penalized spline method) and restricted cubic spline (RCS) were used to explore the relationship between serum uric acid and PH risk. If nonlinearity was detected (likelihood ratio test, p < 0.05), a two‑piecewise logistic regression model and threshold effect analysis were applied to identify the inflection point. All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.2.1, with packages mgcv, rms, and segmented). A two‑sided p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Regarding demographic and clinical characteristics, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in age, sex distribution, BMI, Kerr score (disease activity), eGFR, CRP, or PAI pattern (bilateral/unilateral) (all p > 0.05).

Patients in the PH group had a significantly longer disease duration at consultation than those in the non‐PH group (median 16.0 years vs. 6.1 years, p = 0.014), whereas ESR levels were lower in the PH group (median 14.5 mm/h vs. 21.5 mm/h, p = 0.020). Furthermore, NT‐proBNP levels were significantly higher in the PH group (median 296.0 pg/mL vs. 58.0 pg/mL, p < 0.001), and serum uric acid levels were also significantly elevated (400.5 ± 140.8 μmol/L vs. 305.2 ± 91.6 μmol/L, p < 0.001).

Uric acid was analyzed both as a continuous variable (per 10 μmol/L increase) and as tertiles (T1: < 284 μmol/L, T2: 284–376 μmol/L, T3: ≥ 377 μmol/L). The results are shown in Table 2.

Continuous analysis: In the unadjusted model, each 10 μmol/L increase in serum uric acid was associated with an 8% increase in PH odds (OR = 1.08, 95% CI 1.03–1.13, p = 0.001). After adjusting for age and sex, the OR was 1.10 (95% CI 1.05–1.17, p = 0.001); after further adjusting for eGFR and Kerr score, the OR remained 1.10 (95% CI 1.04–1.16, p = 0.001), all statistically significant.

Tertile analysis: In the T1 group (< 284 μmol/L, n = 30), there were 9 PH cases (30%); in the T2 group (284–376 μmol/L, n = 30), 12 PH cases (40%); and in the T3 group (≥ 377 μmol/L, n = 30), 21 PH cases (70%). Using T1 as the reference, the T2 group showed no significant association with PH (Model 3: OR = 1.61, 95% CI 0.53–4.86, p = 0.401). The T3 group (≥ 377 μmol/L) had a significantly increased odds of PH, with an OR of 8.03 in Model 3 (95% CI 2.03–31.79, p = 0.003). The trend test indicated a significant dose–response relationship between increasing uric acid tertiles and PH odds (Model 3: p for trend = 0.004).

Subgroup Analysis

To further evaluate the robustness of the association between serum uric acid and PH, subgroup analyses were performed stratified by sex, Kerr score (disease activity), and PAI pattern. Uric acid was entered as a continuous variable (per 10 μmol/L increase). In each subgroup, adjustments were made for age, sex (except for the sex subgroup), eGFR, and Kerr score (except for the Kerr score subgroup). The results are presented as a forest plot (Figure 2).

Sex subgroup: Among female patients (n = 70, PH = 35 cases), each 10 μmol/L increase in uric acid was associated with a 13% increase in PH risk (OR = 1.13, 95% CI 1.05–1.22). Among male patients (n = 20, PH = 7 cases), the OR was 1.06 (95% CI 0.98–1.15). The interaction between sex and uric acid was not statistically significant (p for interaction = 0.294).

Kerr score subgroup: Among inactive patients (n = 11, PH = 7 cases), the OR for uric acid and PH was 1.02 (95% CI 0.79–1.32). Among active patients (n = 79, PH = 35 cases), the OR was 1.08 (95% CI 1.03–1.13). The p value for interaction was 0.94, indicating that disease activity did not modify the association between uric acid and PH.

Pulmonary artery involvement pattern subgroup: Among patients with bilateral involvement (n = 55, PH = 26 cases), the OR was 1.10 (95% CI 1.02–1.18). Among patients with unilateral involvement (n = 35, PH = 16 cases), the OR was 1.06 (95% CI 1.00–1.12). The p value for interaction was 0.146, showing no significant difference.

In summary, the results of all subgroup analyses were consistent with the overall findings, and no significant effect modification was observed, indicating that the positive association between serum uric acid and PH remained stable across different sexes, disease activity states, and PAI patterns.

Curve Fitting of Uric Acid and Pulmonary Hypertension

To explore the dose–response relationship between serum uric acid and PH risk, RCS curve fitting was performed. The model was adjusted for age, sex, eGFR, and Kerr score (active/inactive). Four knots were placed at the 5th, 35th, 65th, and 95th percentiles of the uric acid distribution, corresponding to approximately 180, 260, 410, and 650 μmol/L. The results (Figure 3) showed a significant positive association between serum uric acid and PH risk (overall p = 0.02). However, the test for nonlinearity was not statistically significant (p for nonlinearity = 0.729), indicating that a linear model adequately describes the relationship, with no significant J‐shaped or U‐shaped curve identified.

Discussion

In this retrospective cross‐sectional study of 90 patients with Takayasu arteritis and pulmonary artery involvement (TAK‐PAI), we systematically investigated, for the first time, the association between serum uric acid levels and PH in this specific population. The main findings are as follows: (1) The prevalence of PH in TAK‐PAI patients was 46.7%; (2) Serum uric acid level was independently and positively associated with PH risk, with an approximate 10% increase in PH odds per 10 μmol/L increase in uric acid; (3) Compared with the lowest tertile group (< 284 μmol/L), the highest tertile group (≥ 377 μmol/L) had an approximately 8‐fold higher odds of PH (OR = 8.03); (4) Subgroup analyses showed that this association remained stable across different sexes, disease activity states, and PAI patterns; (5) RCS analysis revealed no significant nonlinear relationship, suggesting that the effect of uric acid on PH follows a predominantly linear dose–response pattern.

The association between uric acid and PH has been established in various disease settings. In idiopathic PAH and connective tissue disease‐associated PAH, hyperuricemia is widely recognized as a marker of disease severity and poor prognosis [20, 21, 22]. The present study extends this finding to Takayasu arteritis‐associated PH, a specific subtype of PH. Notably, a recent study by Zhou and colleagues found that serum uric acid ≥ 284.5 μmol/L predicted the presence of PAI in TAK patients (OR = 2.108), and uric acid ≥ 328.1 μmol/L was an independent predictor of PAI‐related adverse events [13]. Building on these findings, our study further demonstrates that, among patients with established PAI, uric acid levels are directly associated with the most severe hemodynamic consequence – PH.

The effect size observed in our study (OR = 8.03 for the highest tertile) is larger than that reported by Zhou and colleagues for PAI association (OR = 2.108) [13]. This may reflect that uric acid not only participates in the development of PAI but may also play a more important role in the progression from PAI to PH by continuously promoting pulmonary vascular remodeling. Previous mechanistic studies have confirmed that uric acid can directly promote excessive proliferation of pulmonary artery smooth muscle cells via upregulation of URATv1, contributing to pulmonary vascular remodeling [23], and also drives the development and progression of PH by inducing endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammatory responses [8]. Furthermore, continuous variable analysis showed that each 10 μmol/L increase in uric acid was associated with only an approximate 10% increase in PH odds, whereas tertile analysis revealed a sharp odds elevation in the high uric acid group, suggesting that the effect of uric acid on PH may have a clinical threshold rather than being a purely linear relationship. Although the nonlinearity test of RCS analysis did not reach statistical significance (p = 0.729), the tertile results and the visually apparent J‐shaped trend still suggest that the risk increases significantly when uric acid exceeds approximately 377 μmol/L. This threshold may serve as a reference cut‐off for clinical screening.

The mechanisms by which uric acid participates in the pathogenesis of PH may involve multiple levels. First, hyperuricemia can inhibit eNOS activity, induce oxidative stress and endothelial dysfunction, leading to an imbalance in pulmonary vasomotor tone [24]. Second, uric acid can activate the MAPK and Rho kinase pathways in pulmonary vascular smooth muscle cells, promoting their proliferation and migration and accelerating pulmonary vascular remodeling [25]. Third, as a danger‑associated molecular pattern, uric acid can activate the NLRP3 inflammasome, promoting the release of inflammatory cytokines such as IL‑1β and IL‑6. In the context of the chronic inflammation of Takayasu arteritis, this may further amplify the inflammatory response in the pulmonary vasculature [26]. Furthermore, the pulmonary artery stenosis or occlusion caused by TAK itself has already created abnormal pulmonary hemodynamics; uric acid may superimpose additional injury through the above mechanisms, accelerating the development of PH [8, 17].

Of note, the ESR level in the PH group was significantly lower than that in the non‐PH group (14.5 vs. 21.5 mm/h). This seemingly paradoxical result may reflect the unique characteristics of disease activity in TA‐PH patients: on the one hand, patients with long‐standing PH may be in a relatively low‐inflammatory state; on the other hand, some patients may have suppressed ESR due to long‐term use of glucocorticoids or immunosuppressants. Moreover, as an inflammatory marker, ESR has limitations in TAK patients, and its consistency with disease activity is not ideal.

The findings of this study have clear potential for clinical translation. First, serum uric acid is a routine, inexpensive, and reproducible laboratory marker. Incorporating it into the routine evaluation of TAK‐PAI patients may help identify individuals at high risk for PH. We recommend that TAK‐PAI patients with uric acid ≥ 377 μmol/L (the cut‐off of the highest tertile in this study) receive enhanced PH screening, including regular echocardiography and, when necessary, RHC. Second, serial uric acid monitoring may reflect disease progression or treatment response, and future studies could explore the correlation between changes in uric acid and hemodynamic parameters of PH. Third, whether urate‐lowering therapy (e.g., allopurinol, febuxostat) should be initiated in TAK‐PAI patients with hyperuricemia to delay PH progression currently lacks evidence. This study provides a theoretical basis for future prospective interventional trials.

This study has several limitations. First, the retrospective cross‐sectional design precludes establishing a causal relationship between uric acid and PH. Although we adjusted for multiple potential confounders, we cannot completely rule out reverse causality (i.e., PH leading to impaired renal function or diuretic use causing elevated uric acid) or residual confounding. The reported ORs should be interpreted as measures of association rather than causal effects. Second, the sample size was relatively limited (90 patients, 42 with PH). Although it met the basic requirement for logistic regression (EPV ≈ 10.5), more complex interaction analyses or external validation were not possible. Third, some patients (those with negative echocardiography) did not undergo RHC, so the non‐PH group may have included a small number of patients with mild PH (mPAP 20–24 mmHg not meeting current criteria); however, this bias would tend to make our results more conservative. Fourth, the single‐center design limits the generalizability of the findings. Fifth, we did not collect data on diuretic use or fractional excretion of uric acid, factors that may affect uric acid levels. Sixth, no follow‐up data were included, so we could not evaluate the predictive value of uric acid for long‐term survival or PH progression in TAK‐PAI patients.

Conclusion

In patients with Takayasu arteritis and pulmonary artery involvement, elevated serum uric acid levels are independently associated with increased odds of PH, exhibiting a dose–response relationship. Patients with uric acid ≥ 377 μmol/L had significantly higher odds of PH. This association remained stable across different sexes, disease activity states, and PAI patterns. Serum uric acid may serve as a simple, noninvasive biomarker for PH risk stratification in TAK‐PAI patients, assisting in early clinical identification of high‐risk individuals. Given the cross‐sectional design, these findings reflect an association rather than a causal relationship, and future multicenter, prospective cohort studies and interventional trials are needed to validate our findings and explore the potential value of urate‐lowering therapy in the prevention and treatment of TAK‐PH.
