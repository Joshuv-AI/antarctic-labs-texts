Multiple episodes of paediatric Clostridioides difficile infection (pCDI) in the context of autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1
Authors: Harald Michael Kirr, Anja Lütke, Nadja Klafke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryWe describe the case of a young child who experienced multiple episodes of Clostridioides difficile infection (CDI) with pronounced meteorism and watery diarrhoea. One episode progressed to severe disease requiring admission to intensive care. Initial CDI episodes were managed with metronidazole and vancomycin. For the recurrent CDI, fidaxomicin—a narrow-spectrum antibiotic with targeted activity against C. difficile—was administered, resulting in sustained clinical remission of CDI-related symptoms. During subsequent follow-up, the patient was diagnosed with autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type 1 (APS-1), a rare monogenic disorder characterised by immune dysregulation and increased susceptibility to infection. The underlying immunocompromised state likely contributed to the multiple CDI episodes. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of paediatric CDI associated with APS-1.Keywords: Gastroenterology, Pediatrics

Background

Clostridioides difficile (C. difficile) is a spore-forming, toxin-producing bacterium that causes a wide range of diseases, from mild, self-limiting diarrhoea to severe manifestations such as pseudomembranous colitis, fulminant colitis, ileus and toxic megacolon, which may lead to bowel perforation.1 Traditionally associated with older adults, C. difficile infection (CDI) is now increasingly recognised in the paediatric population, with a rising incidence in recent years.2 3 Infants and young children are often asymptomatically colonised with toxin-producing C. difficile, particularly during the first year of life.4,6 This asymptomatic carriage typically declines as the gut microbiota matures, usually by around 2 years of age.6 The transition from asymptomatic colonisation to symptomatic infection is commonly linked to alterations in the gut microbiome that promote the overgrowth of toxin-producing C. difficile, thus increasing susceptibility to CDI.6 Risk factors for paediatric CDI, similar to those in adults, include recent antibiotic exposure, significant chronic underlying diseases (such as gastrointestinal disorders), immunosuppression, malignancy, solid organ transplantation, renal insufficiency, cystic fibrosis and inflammatory bowel diseases.4 7 8 Despite the availability of effective treatments, the occurrence of multiple CDI episodes presents a substantial clinical challenge.9 Recurrent CDI affects approximately 20–30% of paediatric patients,1 with recurrence risk factors mirroring those of the initial CDI episode.9

While most paediatric cases are effectively managed with standard therapies such as metronidazole or vancomycin, narrow-spectrum antibiotics like fidaxomicin have emerged as a promising alternative in managing patients with multiple CDI episodes. Approved for paediatric use by both the European Medicines Agency (EMA) and the US Food and Drug Administration (FDA) in 2020,2 fidaxomicin has shown comparable efficacy to vancomycin in clinical trials, with a notably lower risk of recurrence, likely due to its minimal impact on the commensal gut microbiota.10,14 Despite this, clinical experience with fidaxomicin in children is still limited.

The management of recurrent paediatric CDI can also be complicated by undiagnosed or rare underlying conditions that compromise host immunity. In this context, we report the case of a young child who experienced multiple, challenging CDI episodes and was ultimately diagnosed with autoimmune polyendocrine syndrome type I (APS-1), a monogenic immunodeficiency. This case emphasises the importance of considering immune dysfunction in paediatric patients with recurrent CDI and suggests that effective management may require more than antimicrobial therapy alone.

Case presentation

Our patient is a young child in early childhood who was admitted to the hospital due to recurring episodes of watery diarrhoea, sometimes accompanied by vomiting but without fever. The diarrhoea episodes, which intermittently showed clinical improvement, had occurred for approximately 3 months, each lasting around 14 days. Additionally, the parents retrospectively reported an initial single episode of illness with self-limiting diarrhoea lasting up to 14 days about a year ago. Attempts to treat with probiotics (Saccharomyces boulardii and Escherichia coli) did not alleviate the symptoms. Further medical history revealed that the child had previously been diagnosed with bilateral bronchopneumonia. Additionally, her growth trajectory had declined, with a drop in weight percentiles, and she had developed alopecia areata. She had no history of surgical interventions.

Investigations and treatment

Clinical examination on the first admission to our hospital revealed a generally reduced overall condition. The skin appeared rosy, but skin turgor was decreased, and the mucous membranes were dry. Auscultation of the heart and lungs showed no abnormal findings. Abdominal examination showed distension with meteorism (figures14) and mild tenderness, accompanied by active bowel sounds in all quadrants. Vital signs and temperature were within normal limits. Aside from alopecia areata, the rest of the integument appeared adequate. The child’s weight was 14.6 kg, corresponding to the 27th percentile, and her length was 99.5 cm, placing her in the 75th–90th percentile.

Laboratory results revealed elevated C-reactive protein (87 mg/L), an increased erythrocyte sedimentation rate (ESR) of 24 mm/hour, and vitamin D deficiency. Abdominal ultrasound showed extensive bowel wall thickening in the lower left abdomen, a large volume of soft stool, sluggish peristalsis and significant meteorism throughout the abdomen. Stool analysis indicated an inflammatory bowel process with a calprotectin level of 712 µg/g. Pathogen testing for Salmonella/Shigella, Campylobacter, Yersinia, norovirus, rotavirus, adenovirus, protozoa and Giardia was negative. However, real-time PCR testing was positive for genes specific to toxigenic C. difficile strains. For CDI diagnosis, stool samples were routinely sent to the same laboratory, which adhered to a multi-step diagnostic protocol. This included detecting glutamate dehydrogenase (GDH) by enzyme immunoassay (EIA), followed by screening for C. difficile toxins A and B using EIA. If the toxin tests were negative, PCR was performed to directly detect the toxin gene.

Gastroscopy revealed mucosal erythema in both the gastric corpus and antrum, as well as lymphangiectasia in the duodenum, consistent with gastritis. Histopathological analysis demonstrated mild reflux-associated inflammation of the squamous epithelium in the distal oesophagus and focally enhanced gastritis (FEG) in the antral and corporal mucosa. Ileocolonoscopy showed elevated yellow-white plaques on the colonic mucosa, characteristic of pseudomembranous colitis (figures5 6). In addition, a mild increase in eosinophilic granulocytes was noted in the colonic mucosa, raising the possibility of Crohn’s disease as a differential diagnosis at that time.

A diagnosis of CDI was ultimately confirmed by the laboratory, following the established multi-step diagnostic protocol. During the hospital stay, oral antibiotic therapy with metronidazole was initiated in accordance with the then-current first-line treatment guidelines. Metronidazole was administered at 30 mg/kg body weight per day, divided into two doses over a 10-day course. Given the severity of endoscopic findings consistent with gastritis, adjunctive therapy with a proton pump inhibitor (omeprazole) was initiated. Omeprazole was administered orally at 1.5 mg/kg body weight daily for 4 weeks. To further address the colitis, oral mesalazine was prescribed. Supportive care included parenteral fluid replacement.

With initial dietary restrictions, the diarrhoea resolved. After 6 days of inpatient treatment, the patient was discharged with instructions to complete the antibiotic course at home. Vitamin D supplementation was advised due to the documented deficiency, and the family was counselled on essential hygiene measures to reduce the risk of reinfection.

Second hospital admission

Approximately 3 months after discharge, the patient was readmitted as an emergency case with an acute abdomen, clinical signs of ileus and radiological evidence of bilateral pneumonia. The family also reported new-onset constipation requiring high doses of stool softeners such as macrogol. On presentation, she was febrile (up to 40 °C), exhibited a markedly reduced general condition and had a significantly distended and firm abdomen. Chest radiography confirmed bilateral pulmonary infiltrates consistent with pneumonia (figures7 8). She was transferred to the paediatric intensive care unit and required supplemental oxygen. Stool analysis once again confirmed the presence of toxin-producing C. difficile. Further immunological work-up revealed insufficient antibody titres against tetanus and diphtheria, raising suspicion of an underlying immunodeficiency.

During this second hospitalisation, which lasted 27 days, the patient was admitted to the paediatric intensive care unit due to systemic inflammation, severe bilateral pneumonia and a pre-septic condition. Intravenous antibiotic therapy was initiated with metronidazole, vancomycin and meropenem. This escalation, including the addition of meropenem, was implemented in accordance with the hospital’s internal standard operating procedures (SOPs) for the management of critically ill paediatric patients. Intravenous pantoprazole was administered concurrently for gastric protection given the severity of the clinical presentation. An accompanying episode of pancreatitis resolved spontaneously during the intensive care stay. During endoscopic reevaluation via ileocolonoscopy, an attempt to intubate the terminal ileum was unsuccessful due to massive fecolith accumulation. Laboratory analysis at that time showed normal faecal calprotectin levels, effectively ruling out chronic inflammatory bowel disease. Following clinical stabilisation, antibiotic therapy was de-escalated to oral vancomycin monotherapy at 40 mg/kg/day, divided into four doses over 10 days. No further CDI-related symptoms were observed thereafter.

Recurrent CDI episode and follow-up

Less than 4 weeks after the second hospitalisation, the child presented to outpatient care with recurrent abdominal pain, diarrhoea and pronounced meteorism. A recurrent episode of CDI was diagnosed. Diagnostic testing was conducted at the same reference laboratory as in the previous episodes, and stool samples again tested positive for toxigenic C. difficile. A third course of antibiotic therapy was initiated, this time using the narrow-spectrum antibiotic fidaxomicin, administered orally at 200 mg twice daily for 10 days. The treatment was well tolerated, and no gastrointestinal side effects were reported. Vitamin D supplementation remained the only concurrent medication, and no additional therapies were administered. Given the patient’s immunocompromised status, follow-up stool tests were performed immediately after treatment and again 4 weeks later. Both tests were negative for C. difficile, indicating successful eradication. The family reported a marked improvement in the child’s overall health, describing it as a ‘return of vitality.’ They also noted a significant reduction in meteorism and a clear improvement in abdominal distension.

Follow-up examinations over the subsequent months revealed no recurrence of meteorism. While the previously reported constipation initially improved, it remained an intermittent issue. During episodes of gastrointestinal infection, the child consistently exhibited reduced oral intake, contributing to stagnation in weight gain. To address suspected gut dysbiosis, ongoing probiotic therapy was initiated. Additionally, daily administration of macrogol was continued to support bowel function. Over time, the child’s alopecia progressed to alopecia universalis (figures911), with near-complete loss of scalp and body hair, including eyebrows and eyelashes. Persistent nail dysplasia of the great toenail also remained evident. In light of these findings, the patient was readmitted for a 2 day inpatient evaluation approximately 8 months after the successful eradication of C. difficile.

Diagnosis of a rare genetic disorder

During the subsequent hospitalisation, comprehensive laboratory investigations revealed primary hypoparathyroidism, evidenced by a low intact parathyroid hormone (PTH) level of 13 pg/mL, accompanied by hypocalcaemia (serum calcium: 1.72 mmol/L) and hyperphosphataemia (serum phosphate: 2.28 mmol/L). In addition, the child exhibited elevated thyroid-stimulating hormone (TSH) levels (5.30 µU/mL) with normal thyroid hormone concentrations (free T3 and T4), positive anti-thyroid peroxidase antibodies (TPO-Ab), and sonographic evidence of thyroid hypoplasia.

Over time, genetic testing confirmed the diagnosis of APS-1, a rare monogenic immunodeficiency disorder, caused by a homozygous mutation in the AIRE gene (c.769C>T, p.Arg257*), a known pathogenic variant.15 The child was subsequently placed under regular endocrinological follow-up. TPO-antibody–positive hyperthyrotropinaemia was managed with oral iodine supplementation, while hypoparathyroidism was treated with vitamin D and calcium supplements. Given the presence of positive adrenal cortex antibodies and abnormal urinary steroid profiles, Addison’s disease was diagnosed. As the condition progressed, oral hydrocortisone therapy was initiated. The family was provided with an emergency medical identification card, along with detailed instructions and medications for managing a potential Addisonian crisis.

Over a follow-up period of 8.5 months, the patient’s body mass index improved, reaching the 74th percentile. However, linear growth remained slow, with an increase of 3.4 cm during this period, still 4 cm below the third percentile. At the most recent evaluation, the child’s weight was at the 10th percentile. Bone mineralisation was adequate, and bone age was in line with chronological age, with no signs of dysplasia. The patient continues to be under ongoing medical supervision and receives calcium supplementation as part of her treatment regimen.

Discussion

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first reported case of multiple CDI episodes in a child with APS-1 as an underlying condition, which was associated with a partly severe CDI course and posed a challenge for CDI management. During the second hospital admission, CDI-associated colitis induced a motility disorder and paralytic ileus, consistent with toxic megacolon, leading to the formation of faecoliths and subsequent defecation difficulties. Severe meteorism also developed as a result of intestinal dysbiosis. The occurrence of multiple CDI episodes and the more severe clinical course in this case were likely, at least in part, attributable to the underlying immunosuppressive condition.

A retrospective cohort study involving over 600 hospitalised paediatric patients with CDI confirmed that underlying comorbidities are a major risk factor for multiple CDI episodes in children. Specifically, patients with complex chronic conditions had a fourfold higher likelihood of recurrence.9 Furthermore, a 13 year retrospective study of pCDI identified over 300 affected children, with recurrent infections restricted to those with chronic conditions, affecting approximately 27% of this cohort. Among children with chronic conditions, recurrence was associated with female gender and initial treatment with metronidazole rather than vancomycin. The study also revealed a high treatment failure rate (33.3%) once a recurrence occurred.16 These findings highlight the critical impact of chronic underlying conditions in the occurrence of multiple CDI episodes in children.

APS-1 is an autosomal recessive disorder caused by mutations in the autoimmune regulator gene (AIRE), with over 60 different mutations reported to date.17 These mutations fail central immune tolerance, leading the immune system to attack the body’s tissues and causing multiple autoimmune diseases. Additionally, APS-1 patients have a compromised immune system, which impairs their ability to effectively combat certain infections, further contributing to the disease’s complexity.18 Diagnosing APS-1 can be challenging due to its rarity and diverse clinical manifestations. Besides the hallmark symptoms of chronic mucocutaneous candidiasis, hypoparathyroidism and/or Addison’s disease, other autoimmune features may also occur, such as alopecia, which can range from patchy to complete hair loss.17 18 In our case, concerns regarding an underlying immune deficiency were first raised when a notably low vaccination titre against tetanus and diphtheria was observed. However, a further diagnostic workup for an immunosuppressive disorder was not conducted at that time and was only pursued later, following the successful eradication of C. difficile after fidaxomicin therapy.

In conclusion, this case report underscores the critical importance of thoroughly evaluating immunological disorders in paediatric patients with severe or recurrent CDI. It highlights the need to consider even rare autoimmune and immunodeficient conditions, as these may contribute to more severe and frequent CDI episodes. Early identification of immune dysfunction, coupled with a personalised treatment approach, has the potential to improve clinical outcomes and reduce the incidence of recurrent infections in this vulnerable population. Ultimately, a comprehensive and individualised strategy is essential to alleviate the long-term burden on affected children.
