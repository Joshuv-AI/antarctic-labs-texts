Building envelope thermal performance testing dataset (Co-heating, QUB, ISO-9869 U-values) from a retrofitted heritage building (unoccupied dwelling)
Authors: Catalina Giraldo-Soto, Aitor Erkoreka, Amaia Uriarte, Carlos García-Gáfaro
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractA mobile Monitoring and Control System (MCS) was implemented to conduct several tests aimed at characterizing the thermal envelope of unoccupied dwellings and estimating its Heat Transfer Coefficient (HTC). These tests included U-value Tests (according to ISO 9869), QUB Tests, and Co-Heating Tests. In the Co-Heating Test, the dwelling was heated during different periods using both electric heaters and the installed aerothermal heat pump system.The two studied dwellings are living labs of a heritage building in Vitoria-Gasteiz (Spain), retrofitted in 2019 as part of the European project ENERPAT-SUDOE: Experimentation in the Eco-Renovation of Old Built Heritage.Specific ISO 9869 U-value or Thermal Transmittance Tests were conducted on the building envelope using 37 sensors strategically installed across two dwellings. In an unoccupied dwelling, the QUB Tests and Co-Heating Tests, were also performed. In total, the dataset includes 138 sensors measuring 200 physical variables to perform the test in different periods from October 26, 2019, at 15:53:00 to November 05, 2024, at 23:59:00 at 1-minute resolution.This dataset contributes to advancing the estimation of the Heat Transfer Coefficient (HTC) and the overall heat loss (UA-value) of the thermal envelope in retrofitted buildings, supporting improved energy performance evaluation.Companion dataset: multi-year in-use monitoring from the same building using a fixed MCS (Data in Brief DOI: 10.1016/j.dib.2025.112121).Keywords: Energy efficiency, Energy monitoring, Historical building, Co-heating test, QUB test, Heat transfer coefficient, U-value ISO 9869 test, Thermal envelope of building

Background

The dataset of this experimental work was compiled to test three complementary methods for studying the thermal behavior of the envelope according to ISO 9869 standard, estimating the U-value, together with the estimation of the Heat Transfer Coefficient (HTC) implementing the QUB test and the Co-Heating Test in a dwelling located in the first floor of a historic residential building, which was renovated within the framework of the European ENERPAT-SUDOE project. Although building-physics literature recognizes that short-duration experiments can yield reliable heat-loss coefficients, published datasets for occupied historic buildings (Fig. 1, Fig. 2) are limited and seldom captured with a single, time-synchronized Monitoring and Control System (MCS). To fill that gap, a doctoral thesis [6] designed an optimized a mobile MCS, which is easily installed in any dwelling and could also be coupled with the existing fixed MCS in the building. The dataset provides the experimental segments required to derive envelope transmittance and dwelling-level HTC under repeatable protocols.

Data Description

This dataset complements a separate article that publishes the multi-year in-use monitoring from the fixed MCS across the same building [7]. This manuscript reports controlled envelope tests —Co-Heating, QUB and ISO 9869 U-values— performed in an unoccupied dwelling using a mobile MCS in an unoccupied dwelling, with autonomous heater/relay control and segmented test periods. Separate publication preserves methodological clarity (protocol-driven test segments vs continuous operational streams) and supports distinct reuse. Common signal IDs and timestamps allow the two datasets to be combined when required, where these identifiers are shown in Experimental Test section with the sensor layout drawings for each dwelling and the common areas of the building. Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

Located in Vitoria-Gasteiz, the residential building featured in this study (Fig. 1) was selected as a demonstrator site for the ENERPAT-SUDOE project [[10], [11], [12]]. The monitoring was carried out in two distinct phases: one prior to and one following the eco-renovation interventions (Fig. 2) [13]. Although both phases aimed to assess similar objectives, they varied considerably in terms of scope, technical limitations, sensor configuration, and methodological design. This paper presents detailed post-retrofit monitoring, drawing on the results of the Co-Heating, QUB, and U-value tests.

The Vitoria-Gasteiz demonstrator is a multi-story building consisting of three floors and a basement (FB), which is under the dwelling of F0. The Ground Floor (F0) includes an occupied dwelling, a Facility Room (FR), and an entrance hall. The First Floor (F1) contains an unoccupied dwelling, while the Second Floor (F2) has another occupied dwelling. The heritage building had an integral rehabilitation of the exterior thermal envelope, where only the interior of the dwelling of F0 was completely retrofitted [13].

This study employed a purpose-built mobile MCS engineered for the Thermal Transmittance test (U-value), the QUB and Co-Heating tests. The unit integrates an autonomous Beckhoff control module, seven indoor-air temperature sensors, eight wattmeter, eight relays, seven electric heaters, and seven fans, surface temperature and heat flux sensors (Fig. 6). It can be rapidly installed in any dwelling and seamlessly interfaced with the building’s permanent (fixed) MCS.

Mobile Monitoring and Control System (MCS), based on [6].

This research conducted three experimental tests in two dwellings of the Vitoria-Gasteiz demonstrator (F0 and F1). Both dwellings had a fixed MCS, which was installed during the retrofitting phase, and after retrofitting the mobile MCS was integrated to its fixed MCS in F1. The fixed MCS started to collect data from October 26, 2019, at 15:53:00 to November 05,2024,4 at 23:59:00 in F0, F1, F2 and exterior to collect the weather data. In addition, in F1 the mobile MCS collected data from November 12, 2021, at 15:48:00 to November 05, 2024, at 23:59:00 in F1. The data repository (https://data.mendeley.com/datasets/n6mt4xp68j) includes physical variables collected from the fixed and mobile MCS.

In the unoccupied F1 dwelling, both the QUB and Co‑Heating tests were conducted using 92 sensors to record 154 physical variables. To quantify heat exchange between F1 and its adjoining zones (F0, F2), as well as through the party wall and exterior envelope, 38 surface‑temperature sensors and 19 heat‑flux sensors were deployed. Table 3 specify the indoor air temperature sensor installed to trigger the relay that activates the electric heating based on the setpoint for each experimental test.

The U-value Tests were conducted on the F0 and F1 thermal envelope using 46 sensors installed around the thermal envelope of both dwellings, likewise 18 sensors were installed to measure indoor air temperatures in thermal zones of the Ground Floor, F1, and F2. This floor is composed by the Facility Room (FR), a dwelling (F0) and the entrance hall, where FR is completely exposed to the exterior environment due the Aerothermal Heat Pump Systems for F1 and F0 are installed.

Additionally, the weather conditions (E) were monitored with four sensor —three on the building’s roof and one at the level of F1, where 9 physical variables were measured [6]. In total, the dataset (https://data.mendeley.com/preview/n6mt4xp68j) includes 138 sensors measuring 200 physical variables between October 26, 2019, at 15:53:00 and November 05, 2024, at 23:59:00.

Experimental tests

In the unoccupied F1 dwelling, three complementary types of in situ tests were carried out to characterise the retrofitted thermal envelope under controlled indoor conditions. Fig. 6 shows the mobile MCS installed to perform the following tests:

U-value Tests: Experimental Procedure for U-value Estimation Using Heat Flux and Surface Temperature Sensors (ISO 9869–1:2014), the objective is to estimate the thermal transmittance (U-value) of the exterior thermal envelope of building after retrofitting by measuring: Heat flux through the surface and Interior and exterior surface temperatures. These tests were conducted from October 26, 2019, at 15:53:00 to November 05, 2024, 23:59:59 with a one-minute frequency.

The Co-Heating Tests: The objective of this test is to estimate the Heat Transfer Coefficient (HTC) after the building retrofitting in the unoccupied F1 dwelling. These tests were conducted heating the dwelling with electrical heating and with heating system using aerothermal heat pump system. Likewise, for each heating case, the study has included periods with window shutters opened and closed. The test requires certain conditions to be met:

The aerothermal system could not be fully deactivated during testing to avoid pipe freeze-up. Although the thermostat setpoint was raised to block heat delivery, the system continued cycling and warming its piping circuit and radiators. To minimize unwanted heating, we closed the wall radiator valves. With the valves shut, heat circulated only through the closed-loop pipe network embedded in the walls; when reopened, both the pipe circuit and the wall-mounted radiators supplied heat to the dwelling.

The QUB Tests: The objective of this test is estimating the overall Heat Transfer Coefficient (HTC) of unoccupied dwelling F1 using a short-duration, in-situ test performed over a single night. This test is conducted after sunset to minimize solar radiation impact; the window and exterior door were closed to reduce the air infiltration (doors/windows sealed). The Indoor temperature was stabilized at 25 °C (± 0.5 °C), guarantying a minimum of 10 K between indoor and outdoor temperatures is recommended for reliable results. -Heating Phase: Heating the unoccupied F1 dwelling using electrical radiators over a fixed time (e.g., 4.5 h). Measuring the indoor air temperature rise and calculate its slope at the end of this phase.

Limitations

Electrical-load management. The contracted supply capacity of the dwelling would have been exceeded if all room heaters had been energised simultaneously. Each room was therefore fitted with one electric radiator and a mixing fan to achieve uniform air conditions. The heating sequence began with only three radiators energised, allowing the interior to reach the target set-point without breaching the power limit; once that temperature was attained, the remaining radiators were brought online to hold the uniform set-point throughout the dwelling.
