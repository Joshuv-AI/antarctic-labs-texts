Axonal protection by nicotinamide riboside with the SIRT1-AMPK pathway in TNF-induced optic nerve damage
Authors: Yasushi Kitaoka, Chihiro Tsukahara, Naoki Fujita, Ibuki Arizono, Mizuki Otsubo, Kana Sase
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundNicotinamide riboside (NR) is an NAD+ precursor and is reported to upregulate NAD+ levels in several tissues. We previously demonstrated that NR exhibited protection against axon loss induced by tumor necrosis factor (TNF) with upregulation of SIRT1 and enhancement of autophagy. We recently found that NR upregulated phosphorylated adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (p-AMPK) protein in the optic nerve. The present study aimed to examine whether a SIRT1 inhibitor prevents axonal protection by NR and alters p-AMPK expression.Methods and resultsRats were given intravitreal injection of a SIRT1 inhibitor prior to simultaneous injection of NR and TNF. The SIRT1 inhibitor alone or NR alone group was also made. SIRT1 and p-AMPK expression in optic nerve was investigated by immunoblot analysis. We examined the localization of SIRT1 and p-AMPK in the retina and optic nerve. Morphometric analysis showed axonal protection by NR in TNF-induced axon loss, and this protective effect was significantly prevented by the SIRT1 inhibitor. The SIRT1 inhibitor alone did not alter axon numbers significantly compared with the control group. The SIRT1 inhibitor significantly decreased p-AMPK levels in optic nerves in NR plus TNF-treated eyes. On the other hand, the SIRT1 inhibitor alone did not alter p-AMPK levels. Co-localization of SIRT1 and p-AMPK was observed in the retina and optic nerves in NR-treated eyes.ConclusionsSIRT1 exists upstream of AMPK, and axonal protection of NR is associated with the SIRT1-AMPK pathway.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s11033-026-11607-2.Keywords: Nicotinamide riboside・TNF・SIRT1・AMPK・Optic nerve

Introduction

Glaucoma is characterized as a pathophysiological condition in which optic nerve axonal degeneration occurs with subsequent retinal ganglion cell (RGC) death and is usually treated by reducing intraocular pressure (IOP). However, many patients may experience disease progression in spite of IOP reduction. Thus, treatments other than lowering IOP are desperately needed, and these treatments include nicotinamide intake [1].

There have been reports of the relationship between glaucoma and nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NAD) levels in the body. For instance, one report showed that nicotinamide concentration in plasma was lower in glaucoma patients compared with that in the control group [2]. A very recent report confirmed that the NAD level in peripheral blood mononuclear cells is lower in glaucoma patients compared with controls [3]. In preclinical studies, previous studies found a decline in NAD level in DBA/2J mice retinas [4] and a decline in NAD level in optic nerves in a tumor necrosis factor (TNF)-induced axon degeneration model [5]. Although systemic administration of vitamin B3/nicotinamide produced axonal protection in DBA/2J mice [4], the mechanisms of axonal protection by NAD and its precursors against optic nerve damage remain to be elucidated.

Nicotinamide riboside (NR) is known as an NAD+ precursor supplementation and has been demonstrated to upregulate NAD+ blood levels in humans [6]. A recent study using the downfield proton MRS demonstrated that a single dose (900 mg) of NR results in a measurable significant increase in brain NAD+ levels in healthy humans [7]. NR boosts NAD+ levels, augments Sir2 activities, and prolongs lifespan in yeast [8]. Sirtuin 1 (SIRT1), a homologue of the Sir2 gene, may be stimulated by restricted food intake [9], NAD+, and its precursors [10]. We previously demonstrated that NR ameliorated axonal damage induced by TNF with upregulation of SIRT1 and enhancement of autophagy [11]. We recently found that NR upregulated phosphorylated adenosine monophosphate-activated protein kinase (p-AMPK) protein levels in the optic nerve [12]. Thus, the current study aimed to explore whether a SIRT1 inhibitor prevents axonal protection by NR and alters p-AMPK expression.

Materials and methods

Animals

Eight-week-old male Wistar rats were purchased from Japan SLC (Hamamatsu, Shizuoka, Japan). The source of the rats is an albino outbred strain, which was developed at the Institute of Medical Science, University of Tokyo in 1968. Twenty-four, 52 and 3 rats were used for the axon morphometric analysis, immunoblot analysis, and immunohistochemical analysis studies, respectively. This study was managed based on the ARVO Statement for the Use of Animals in Ophthalmic and Vision Research and endorsed by the Ethics Committee of the Institute of Experimental Animals of St. Marianna University School of Medicine (approval no: 2408002, 2508009). The rats were kept in regulated rooms (23 ± 1℃; humidity at 55 ± 5%; light from 6 AM to 6 PM).

Intravitreal administrations

Intravitreal administration of TNF (Sigma-Aldrich, St. Louis, MO, cat#T5944) was achieved as reported previously with a slight modification [11]. Intravitreal injection of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) was performed as a vehicle control. Intraperitoneal injections of a mixture of medetomidine (0.15 mg/kg), midazolam (2 mg/kg), and butorphanol (2.5 mg/kg) were carried out for anesthetization 1–2 min prior to intravitreal injection. NR triflate (Toronto Research Chemicals, North York, Canada, cat#N407770) was dissolved in PBS. Simultaneous intravitreal administration of 10 ng of TNF and 200 pmol of NR or NR treatment alone was performed. For immunohistochemistry, administration of NR alone was also carried out. EX-527 (Sigma-Aldrich, cat#E7034), a SIRT1 inhibitor, was dissolved in DMSO. Intravitreal administration of 200 pmol of EX-527 or DMSO alone was performed 10 min prior to simultaneous injection of NR and TNF. Therefore, rats were received twice or once for intravitreal injections. These amounts of NR and EX-527 were based on our previous study indicating that the improvement of autophagic flux by NR was reversed by preinjection of EX-527 [11]. In addition, one group received EX-527 administration alone. One and 2 weeks after administration, the rats received intraperitoneal administration of an overdose of sodium pentobarbital (300 mg/kg) and the eyes were collected.

Immunoblotting

Optic nerve samples (4 mm in length) were collected and protein extraction was accomplished with homogenization and centrifuging 1 week after study drug administration. Two optic nerve samples were pooled into one sample. Protein concentrations were measured using the Bio-Rad Protein Assay (Bio-Rad Laboratories, cat#500-0006). The same amounts of samples (3 µg) were loaded on the mini gel. After transfer to an enhanced chemiluminescent membrane (EMD Millipore Corporation), 5% skim milk with tris-buffered saline including Tween-20 was utilized for blocking, and anti-p-AMPK antibody (1:200; Sigma-Aldrich, cat#ZRB1213), anti-AMPK antibody (1:200; Sigma-Aldrich, cat#ZRB1100), anti-SIRT1 antibody (1:200; Cell Signaling, cat#9475), or anti-β-actin antibody (1:5000; Sigma-Aldrich, cat#A2228) was used as a primary antibody. Samples were then allowed to react with anti-rabbit or anti-mouse peroxidase-labeled secondary antibody. A chemiluminescence detection system (ECL Plus Western Blotting Detection Reagents, cat#RPN2232) was utilized for visualizing immunoblotting.

Immunohistochemistry

Three eyes were collected 1 week after intravitreal administration of NR or PBS. For immersion fixation, 4% paraformaldehyde was utilized, and samples were dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Paraffinized sections were cut through the optic disc. 1% bovine serum (Roche Diagnostic GmbH, Mannheim, Germany, cat#10735078001) was utilized for blocking. p-AMPK (1:100; Sigma-Aldrich, cat#ZRB1213) or SIRT1 (1:100; Santa Cruz Biotechnology, Dallas, TX, cat#sc-74504) antibody was used as a primary antibody. Rhodamine or FITC antibody (1:100; Cappel, Aurora, OH) was used as a secondary antibody. DAPI-containing medium was used for mounting.

Quantification of optic nerve axons

Optic nerve samples (4 mm in length) were gathered and immersed in Karnovsky’s solution overnight at 4℃ 2 weeks after intravitreal administration [11, 13]. Specimens were dehydrated and embedded in acrylic resin at 70℃ for 24 h. Semi-thin sections were mounted and 1% paraphenylene-diamine (Sigma-Aldrich, cat#P6001) in methanol was utilized for myelin staining. Five images from each optic nerve were obtained at each quadrant of the peripheral area and one central area with a light microscope (Olympus, Tokyo, Japan). These images (each area was 5,850 µm2 and total area was 29,250 µm2 per optic nerve) were utilized for morphometric analysis with Aphelion image processing software (ADCIS S.A., Hérouville Saint-Clair, France).

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as mean ± SEM. Differences among groups were analyzed by one-way ANOVA with the post-hoc Tukey’s HSD test or Mann-Whitney method. If a p value was less than 0.05, the results were considered statistically significant.

Results

Effects of NR and a SIRT1 inhibitor on TNF-induced axon loss in optic nerve

Consistent with previous reports [5, 11], microscopic examination showed obvious degenerative features and marked axon losses after TNF injection (Fig. 1B) compared with that after PBS injection (Fig. 1A). Simultaneous injection with 200 pmol NR and TNF showed noticeable ameliorative effects (Fig. 1C) compared with the injection of TNF alone. To examine whether an inhibitor of SIRT1 prevents axonal protection, preadministration of EX-527 was performed before simultaneous administration with NR and TNF. Preadministration of EX-527, an inhibitor of SIRT1, apparently prevented axonal protection by NR (Fig. 1D). The effects of NR alone and EX-527 alone appeared to be similar to those in the control group (Figs. 1E, F). Morphometric analysis showed significant ameliorative effects of NR on TNF-induced axon loss, and this protective effect was totally abolished by EX-527 (Fig. 1G). The axon number in the EX-527 alone group tended to be fewer compared with that in the control group, although this difference was not statistically significant (Figs. 1F, G).

Paraphenylene-diamine staining of optic nerve axons 2 weeks after injection. (A) Control group. (B) TNF-injected group. (C) 200 pmol NR + TNF-injected group. (D) 200 pmol NR + 200 pmol EX-527 + TNF-injected group. (E) 200 pmol NR-injected group. (F) 200 pmol EX-527-injected group. Scale bar = 10 μm. (G) Morphometric analysis of axon number (CTL: n = 4, TNF: n = 4, 200 pmol NR + TNF: n = 6, 200 pmol NR + 200 pmol EX-527 + TNF: n = 6, 200 pmol NR: n = 4, 200 pmol EX-527: n = 4) (P* < 0.0005, P** < 0.0001)

Effects of a SIRT1 inhibitor on p-AMPK protein level in the optic nerve in the NR plus TNF administration group

Our recent study found that NR upregulated the p-AMPK level in the optic nerve [12]. To test whether an inhibitor of SIRT1 modifies the p-AMPK level, EX-527 was administered before simultaneous administration of 200 pmol NR and TNF. Preadministration of EX-527, an inhibitor of SIRT1, significantly decreased p-AMPK levels as compared with the preadministration of DMSO in the NR and TNF treatment group (Fig. 2).

Immunoblotting in samples from optic nerves 1 week after injection. Effects of 200 pmol EX-527 on p-AMPK protein level in the 200 pmol NR plus TNF-treated group (preinjection of DMSO and TNF + NR: n = 4, preinjection of 200 pmol EX-527 and TNF + NR: n = 4) (P* < 0.05)

Effects of TNF, NR, and a SIRT1 inhibitor on p-AMPK protein level in the optic nerve

We next examined whether TNF, NR, or EX-527 alone administration alters p-AMPK protein level. Although TNF alone and EX alone did not alter p-AMPK level, NR alone administration significantly increased p-AMPK protein level compared with the control group (Fig. 3).

SIRT1 and p-AMPK in the retina and optic nerve

We previously found that NR upregulated SIRT1 [11] and p-AMPK [12]. In the present study, we examined the localization of SIRT1 and p-AMPK in the retina and optic nerve. In control retinas, some modest colocalization of p-AMPK and SIRT1 was observed (Fig. 5). In the NR-treated retina, colocalization of p-AMPK and SIRT1 was apparent and observed in the nerve fiber layer and RGC layer (Fig. 5). In control optic nerves, modest colocalization of p-AMPK and SIRT1 was observed (Fig. 6). In the NR-treated optic nerve, colocalization of p-AMPK and SIRT1 was widely observed and resembled nerve fiber patterns (Fig. 6), consistent with our previous study results showing that p-AMPK immunoreactivity was colocalized with neurofilament-positive fibers [14].

In the NR-treated retinas, numerous colocalizations of p-AMPK and SIRT1 were observed in the retinal ganglion cell layer (arrows) and nerve fiber layer (arrowheads). Scale bar = 50 μm

In the NR-treated optic nerves, numerous colocalizations of p-AMPK and SIRT1 were observed and appeared as nerve fiber patterns (arrows). Discussion

The present study revealed that axonal protection by NR was abrogated by EX-527, a SIRT1 inhibitor, indicating that the effect of NR was mediated via SIRT1 signaling in TNF-induced optic nerve degeneration. In accordance with this finding, a previous report found that EX-527 significantly prevented NAD+-mediated amelioration in experimental autoimmune encephalomyelitis [15]. Another report also supported this finding, showing that NR prevented glutamate-induced apoptosis in the RGC damage model through the activation of the SIRT1 pathway [16]. Nicotinic acid, nicotinamide, and NR are NAD precursors, and nicotinamide is converted to nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN) by nicotinamide phosphoribosyltransferase, whereas NR is converted to NMN by nicotinamide riboside kinase 1. NMN is converted to NAD + by nicotinamide nucleotide adenylyltransferase1-3 [17]. NAD synthesis is also thought to be important in the adaptation of lipid metabolism in adipocytes. A previous report showed that NMN treatment upregulated SIRT1 expression and augmented p-AMPK protein levels in 3T3-L1 adipocytes [18]. In a neuronal system, a very recent report confirmed that NMN augmented SIRT1 and p-AMPK in the brains of aging mice and that EX-527, a SIRT1 inhibitor, prevented that upregulation [19]. Thus, it is reasonable to assume that NAD precursors may act through similar mechanisms in both nonneuronal and neuronal systems. In line with those findings, we previously showed that NR augmented SIRT1 in the optic nerve [11] and NR administration significantly upregulated the optic nerve p-AMPK protein level [12]. Our present study revealed that EX-527, a SIRT1 inhibitor, significantly decreased the p-AMPK protein level in the NR and TNF-treated optic nerve. Consistently, a previous report confirmed that inhibition of SIRT1 by siRNA effectively reduced the p-AMPK protein level in NR-treated bone marrow-derived endothelial precursor cells [20]. Although we cannot exclude the possibility of potential off-target effects of EX-527 or the possibility that EX-527 suppresses basal SIRT1 levels since the axon number in the EX-527-alone group tended to be fewer compared with that in the control group, the difference was not statistically significant and it was assumed that these were not major findings. Regarding the observation of the decrease in SIRT1 levels following EX-527 administration, one possible speculation is that pharmacological inhibition of SIRT1 by EX-527 may contribute to prolonged astrocytic and microglial activation under TNF stimulation. These glial activations may lead to RGC death [21] as well as optic nerve axonal degeneration, resulting in a reduction of SIRT1 expression. These findings are based on morphological examination and the lack of functional or behavioral assays is a limitation of our current study. Second limitation is a one-time point evaluation of protein expression. However, we previously found that axonal loss started 1 week after TNF injection [22], and the molecular events at 1 week when before axon loss becomes obvious are important for clarifying the mechanism of axonal degeneration. Third limitation is that these findings are based on only in vivo data. The quantitative experimental validation of the signaling pathway, such as the use of an in vitro model, can be useful for understanding precise molecular mechanisms. Nonetheless, taken together, these observations imply that upregulation of SIRT1 and p-AMPK by NR may play pivotal roles in axonal protection and that SIRT1 may exist upstream of AMPK signaling in certain conditions.

Although p-AMPK immunoreactivity was confirmed in optic nerves with a neurofilament pattern [14], a relationship between p-AMPK and SIRT1 has not been tested in the retina and optic nerve. Hence, the current study first demonstrated colocalization of p-AMPK and SIRT1 in the retina and optic nerve. In NR-treated retinas, p-AMPK and SIRT1-positive cells were located mainly in the RGC layer. This is supported by a previous study showing that SIRT1 immunoreactivity was mainly seen in the RGC layer [23]. In addition, colocalization of p-AMPK and SIRT1 in the nerve fiber layer of the retina is consistent with the colocalization of p-AMPK and SIRT1 in optic nerves with a nerve fiber pattern. Therefore, it is possible that p-AMPK and SIRT1 may play crucial roles in RGCs and their axons.

AMPK–autophagy interactions are other potential interesting signaling pathways. We previously demonstrated that A769662, an activator of AMPK, promoted autophagy as it increased LC3-II and decreased p62 in optic nerves [14]. Moreover, both A769662 and NR promoted autophagy and ameliorated axon loss in TNF-induced optic nerve degeneration [11, 14]. Furthermore, SRT2104, an activator of SIRT1, promoted autophagy and ameliorated axon loss induced by TNF [24]. Thus, upregulation of SIRT1 and AMPK may lead to autophagy enhancement. Another interesting aspect is mitochondrial biogenesis. A recent study demonstrated that SIRT1 overexpression alters mitochondrial biogenesis and promotes mitochondrial self-repair to preserve RGCs [25]. Although mitochondrial dysfunction may lead to the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), it was shown that the NR treatment also resulted in ROS inhibition in the RGC damage model [16]. Further study will be needed to clarify the detailed mechanism of the AMPK–mitophagy axis in RGC protection.

Caution may be needed in recommending the use of nicotinamide in glaucoma patients, since there have been reports indicating liver dysfunction with high-dose use. Therefore, the American Academy of Ophthalmology Position Statement does not recommend high-dose (3 g/day) use [26]. On the other hand, it was demonstrated that oral intake of NR at a dose of 1 g/day is safe and significantly increased blood levels of NAD+ in older adults (aged ≥ 65 years) with mild cognitive impairment [27]. A very recent study demonstrated that 12 weeks of nicotinamide intake (6 weeks of 1 g/day followed by 6 weeks of 2 g/day) significantly improved inner retinal function in normal-tension glaucoma patients [28]. Therefore, the verification of the effects of NR at appropriate doses are awaited in glaucoma.

Conclusion

NR appears to provide axonal protection as opposed to TNF-induced optic nerve damage through the SIRT1-AMPK pathway.
