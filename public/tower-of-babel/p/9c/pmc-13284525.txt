Summary of the Safety Assessment Data of the Most Common Organogermanium Food Ingredient, Asaigermanium (Poly‐trans‐[(2‐carboxyethyl)germasesquioxane])
Authors: Sota Doiyama, Takashi Nakamura
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAsaigermanium is a water‐soluble organogermanium compound created by Kazuhiko Asai and colleagues in 1967. It was first researched and developed decades ago as a pharmaceutical product, but it is now used as a raw material for food supplements and cosmetics. After more than 50 years of research and development, the physiological activities of Asaigermanium have been investigated, and the effects of Asaigermanium have been evaluated in multiple toxicological studies. However, because of the health hazards of inorganic germanium (germanium dioxide and lactic acid/citric acid–germanium, also known as the germanium–citrate–lactate complex), there have been false reports that all organogermanium compounds may be as harmful to health as inorganic germanium is. Therefore, in this review, in addition to providing an outline of the test data that have verified the safety of Asaigermanium thus far, we introduce the results from many recent toxicological studies. In addition, we describe the registered certification of Asaigermanium in the In‐House Safety Association of Health Foods of the Japan Health and Nutrition Food Association, which is a third‐party registration framework for the voluntary safety inspection of health food ingredients in Japan.

Introduction

Germanium (Ge; atomic number 32) was discovered in 1886 by the German chemist Clemens Alexander Winkler and was used as a major component in electronics during the 1950s (Winkler 1886). Inorganic germanium compounds, including GeO2 and elemental germanium, have been used mainly in industrial applications as essential materials for high‐performance optical fibers and infrared night vision scopes (Patel and Karamalidis 2021). Moreover, organogermanium compounds, in which germanium is incorporated into organic molecules, have been investigated for potential applications in synthesis, catalysis, and materials science (Haas et al. 2018). For example, Song et al. (2018) reported that structurally modified germatranes are highly reactive in palladium (Pd)‐catalyzed cross‐coupling reactions, which is of interest for cross‐coupling chemistry applications. Since then, the synthesis of germatranes and carbagermatranes, as well as their use in Pd‐catalyzed cross‐coupling reactions, has steadily progressed (Xu and Xiao 2021). More recently, organogermanium compounds have again attracted attention as useful reagents in cross‐coupling chemistry (Rogova et al. 2024), and the scope of transformations involving organogermanium (IV) compounds has expanded. In addition to these developments, their potential as synthetic tools and drug candidates in drug discovery has also been discussed (Xu et al. 2025).

In addition, the biological activity of organogermanium compounds has been studied, among which Asaigermanium is a representative compound that has been widely investigated. In this review, we summarize the available safety data on Asaigermanium and discuss these findings in the context of its historical confusion with other germanium‐containing products.

In this review, “organogermanium” is used as a general term for organogermanium compounds. “Asaigermanium” refers specifically to the material that is the subject of this review. “Ge‐132” is used only when referring to historical naming or to other reports in which that designation was used and does not necessarily indicate the specific material evaluated in the present review. “Propagermanium” is used exclusively for the distinct pharmaceutical compound reported in the cited literature.

Properties and History of Inorganic Germanium and Organogermanium

History of Organogermanium Development

Since ancient times, fungi such as Polyporaceae and plants such as Panax ginseng, which reportedly contain more than 1000‐fold greater concentrations of Ge than that in many other plants, have been considered good for health maintenance (Asai 1981). In addition, we usually ingest very small amounts of germanium in our diet (Asai 1981; Ninomiya et al. 1976). The contents of germanium in some selected plants are displayed in Table 1. Notably, Dr. Kazuhiko Asai, director of the Coal Research Institute of Japan, considers germanium to play important roles in human and animal wellness. Thus, he instructed Dr. Oikawa to synthesize water‐soluble organogermanium compounds. In addition, synthetic studies were performed in the former USSR, and many types of organogermanium compounds have been created since the 1950s. The methods for the synthesis of germanium sesquioxide compounds were suggested by Mironov et al. (1966), which led to the synthesis of one compound with the formula [(GeCH2CH2COOH)2O3]n. During the same period, Kakimoto and Oikawa (1968) synthesized a polymer composed of (HO)3GeCH2CH2COOH (3‐(trihydroxygermyl)propanoic acid [THGP]), terms Asaigermanium, the detailed characteristics of which were reported at the Japanese Chemical Society conference in 1968. Synthetic Asaigermanium is a water‐soluble organogermanium compound in which germanium binds directly to the third carbon of the organic acid, propanoic acid, the formula of which is given above. A summary of the physical and chemical properties of Asaigermanium is given in Table 2. The 3D crystal structure of Asaigermanium was solved by Tsutsui et al. (1976), and the crystal structures of the polymer and hydrolyzed THGP are shown in Figure 1.

In the early stage of its development, Asaigermanium was assigned the code name Ge‐132. Although it was originally investigated for pharmaceutical use, it was not approved as a drug. It is currently handled as food‐related material under the relevant notification of the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare. Currently, Ge‐132 is conditionally treated as a food according to the Japanese Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare (1988). As previously stated, Asaigermanium research and development has historically focused on pharmaceutical products, and its safety has been confirmed by tests conforming to pharmaceutical good laboratory practice (GLP) standards (Nakamura et al. 2023). This review summarizes the available safety data for Asaigermanium and discusses these findings in the context of historical confusion with other germanium‐containing products.

Propagermanium (development code SK‐818) is a distinct organogermanium compound that has been developed as a pharmaceutical agent for the treatment of hepatitis B patients. Although both Asaigermanium and propagermanium generate THGP upon hydrolysis, their polymeric structures differ. The former has a 12‐member ring arranged in a honeycomb structure, and the latter has an eight‐member ring arranged in a ladder formation (Mizuno et al. 2015; Yumimoto et al. 2019). These compounds with different structures are not the same; therefore, they have been named Asaigermanium (CAS Registry number (RN): 3115013‐48‐1) and propagermanium (CAS RN: 126595‐07‐1). Both compounds are considered to function upon their hydrolysis to the same monomer, namely, THGP.

Toxicity of Inorganic Germanium

Germanium dioxide affects the erythropoietic system (Hammett et al. 1922; Hammett and Nowrey 1922). However, after the continuous ingestion of inorganic germanium or its consumption in large amounts, it accumulates in the kidneys, causing severe renal failure (Tao and Bolger 1997). Rats were orally administered inorganic germanium (GeO2, 150 mg/kg/day) or organogermanium (Asaigermanium, 240 mg/kg/day) for 10 weeks, after which their kidneys were histologically examined. No tissue abnormalities were observed in the organogermanium‐treated group, but marked vacuolation and fine granules were observed in the distal convoluted duct or collecting duct epithelium in the GeO2‐treated group (Figure 2).

Furthermore, the germanium‒citrate‒lactate complex, which was sold mainly in Europe in the 1990s as an organogermanium compound that was thought to be effective against AIDS and metastatic cancer, caused many fatalities worldwide (Hess et al. 1993; Lück et al. 1999). The germanium–citrate–lactate complex has been reported to generate GeO2 upon hydrolysis in water, and the ingestion of this preparation has been associated with severe renal and systemic toxicity in humans (Sato et al. 2012). Differences in the pharmacokinetic behaviors of inorganic germanium and Asaigermanium may contribute to their different toxicological profiles.

Misconceptions About the Safety of Organogermanium

Although the potential of organogermanium has recently attracted attention, during the 1980s, foods and beverages containing inorganic germanium (germanium dioxide, GeO2), which was labeled organogermanium, became available (Schauss 1991a, 1991b; Tao and Bolger 1997). In addition, in Japan, it was reported in 1987 that four individuals who had ingested germanium compounds for 4–18 months developed renal failure (Okuda et al. 1987). However, in 1988, it was reported that this germanium compound was contaminated with GeO2 (Matsusaka et al. 1988). Furthermore, in 1990, a group including Okuda reported, on the basis of studies in humans and rats, that Asaigermanium was nontoxic (Sanai et al. 1990, 1991). These reports support the view that Asaigermanium should be distinguished from inorganic germanium compounds during toxicological evaluation, particularly because compounds containing Ge–C bonds differ substantially in chemical form from inorganic germanium species.

However, although less so than before, considerable misunderstandings remain regarding the toxicity of organogermanium compounds, including Asaigermanium. In fact, some books and websites accessible to the general public, which serve as media for scientific communication and discussion, include descriptions that conflate these compounds, thereby hindering accurate understanding. In addition, germanium compounds containing organogermanium, such as Ge‐132, are categorized in various areas worldwide as being toxic, especially by the Food and Drug Administration of the USA. Therefore, the export of organogermanium from Japan to other countries is greatly restricted. The current regulatory handling of germanium‐containing materials in several countries is summarized in Table S1 on the basis of publicly available information from the relevant national regulatory authorities.

These misconceptions about organogermanium compounds have also been a matter of concern for other groups, and many review articles have been published to promote a correct understanding of the safety of Ge‐132 (Kaplan et al. 2004a, 2004b; Menchikov and Popov 2023; Aizawa et al. 2025). For organogermanium to be appropriately accepted by consumers, it is important that scientifically accurate information be widely shared.

Physiological Effects of Asaigermanium

Asaigermanium has anti‐inflammatory (Sasaki et al. 1983, 1984; Furusawa et al. 1987), bone metabolism regulatory (Fujii et al. 1993; Matsumoto et al. 2002), analgesic (Takagi et al. 1983; Suzuki and Taguchi 1983; Nakamura et al. 2015; Sekiguchi et al. 2023), erythrocyte metabolism‐promoting (Ishida and Kimura 2001; Takeda 2020; Takeda et al. 2024), and immunostimulatory effects, which have been verified in joint research with Tohoku University (Aso et al. 1985). It has been known for a half century that Asaigermanium has various physiological activities and can modulate different biological responses. The possibility that Asaigermanium affects the cardiovascular system was also recently reported by Aizawa et al. (2025). Further clarification of the physiological effects of Asaigermanium is needed in the future.

Asaigermanium Metabolism

Previously, a carbon atom of propanoic acid of Asaigermanium was labeled with 14C for oral administration to rats, after which its absorption, distribution, and excretion were examined. The highest blood concentration of Asaigermanium was reached 3 h after administration via either single or continuous dosing, after which time the concentration gradually decreased, and almost all of the compound was excreted in the urine and feces 48 hours after the last administration (Kagoshima et al. 1986; Kagoshima and Oonishi 1986).

In a cooperative study with Tohoku University Hospital, Asaigermanium was administered in the form of THGP, which is a monomer upon dissolution in water (Figure 1), and its circulation in the body was monitored by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC–MS/MS) (Yamaguchi et al. 2015). Moreover, the maximum blood concentration of Asaigermanium was reached 3 h after its administration, after which time the concentration of Asaigermanium gradually decreased (Figure 3). These findings indicate that Asaigermanium is absorbed by the body in the form of THGP, circulates throughout the body without structural modification, and is subsequently excreted. These results are also consistent with those of previous reports from 1986 (Kagoshima et al. 1986; Kagoshima and Oonishi 1986). Taken together, these findings suggest that under the experimental conditions reported, Asaigermanium is absorbed as THGP and excreted without any indication of conversion to GeO2. This pharmacokinetic behavior may contribute to its toxicity profile; however, pharmacokinetic stability alone does not establish safety.

Therefore, safety evaluations, including studies in both animals and humans, are essential for confirming the safety of Asaigermanium. In the following section, we summarize numerous toxicity studies conducted over the past half century to evaluate the safety of Asaigermanium.

Asaigermanium Safety Assessments

Before 1986, Asaigermanium safety tests were carried out in two phases to fully confirm the safety of this compound. However, these tests did not conform to GLP standards; therefore, new standard GLP tests have been performed since 1990. With respect to antigenicity, skin sensitization tests, which were performed for cosmetic applications, are performed. An outline of the results from each test is shown in Tables 3 and 4. Overall, these results demonstrate that Asaigermanium has been systematically evaluated for safety, and subsequent sections describe each test in detail.

General Toxicity Tests (Single‐ and Repeated‐Dose Toxicity Tests)

Acute toxicity test

A single‐dose toxicity test of Asaigermanium was conducted using rats and beagle dogs under GLP‐compliant conditions. In rats (Wister SPF rats, 4 weeks old), six groups containing both males and females were established and administered Asaigermanium at doses ranging from 10,144 to 16,000 mg/kg (Asai Germanium Research Institute Co., Ltd. unpublished data). Subsequent assessments included observation of the general condition, body weight, hematological examinations, mortality, autopsy, and histopathological examinations.

Two groups of dogs (male beagle dogs, 5–6 months old) were treated with Asaigermanium at doses of 8500 mg/kg and 2125 mg/kg (Asai Germanium Research Institute Co., Ltd., unpublished data). After the dogs were fasted for approximately 16 h, a single dose of capsules containing Asaigermanium was forcibly administered orally, and various assessments were conducted in the same manner as with the rats.

The median lethal dose (LD50 value) of Asaigermanium was relatively high in rats after a single oral dose in an acute toxicity study (male: 14,500 mg/kg; female: 14,900 mg/kg), and after oral administration to dogs, even the high dose of 8500 mg/kg did not result in death (all the dogs survived throughout the test period).

According to a previously reported non‐GLP study, the administration of Asaigermanium to male and female ddY mice (6912–17,200 mg/kg) and Wistar rats (9680–14,172 mg/kg) yielded LD50 values of 12,500 mg/kg in male mice, 11,400 mg/kg in female mice, 11,700 mg/kg in male rats, and 11,000 mg/kg in female rats (Nakayama et al. 1986). These LD50 values were lower than those obtained in the GLP‐compliant study but still suggest that Asaigermanium has low acute toxicity.

Subacute/Chronic Toxicity Test

Repeated‐dose toxicity studies were conducted in rats and beagle dogs by oral administration for durations ranging from 1 month to 12 months under GLP‐compliant conditions. In rats, the dosage ranged from 100 to 4000 mg/kg/day (Asai Germanium Research Institute Co., Ltd., unpublished data), and in dogs, the dosage ranged from 50 to 2500 mg/kg/day (Asai Germanium Research Institute Co., Ltd., unpublished data). The assessments included evaluation of the general condition, body weight/feeding, urine/blood tests, ophthalmic examination, organ weights, and comprehensive pathology (including recovery observations and auditory, hepatic, and renal function assessments as necessary).

As a result, in a repeated‐dose subacute/chronic toxicity test, loose stools and diarrhea were observed after the administration of a relatively large amount of Asaigermanium to both rats and dogs, and a decrease in body mass and changes in blood and biochemistry markers (primarily electrolytes) were observed only after the administration of high dosages (>500 mg/kg/day). Therefore, only very high doses of Asaigermanium cause symptoms of toxicity.

According to a non‐GLP study, repeated administration of Asaigermanium to male and female Wistar rats at 5000 mg/kg for 2 months resulted in diarrhea and loose stools, but no mortality was observed (Sugiya et al. 1986a).

For example, as shown in Table 5, compared with other materials, ascorbic acid (vitamin C) is well known for its low toxicity, and its LD50 is very high. A similar single oral dose study reported that the LD50 of ascorbic acid in rats is 11,900 mg/kg/day (Hayashi et al. 1976), which is very similar to that of Asaigermanium (approximately 14,000 mg/kg/day), indicating the high safety of a single oral dose of Asaigermanium.

Effects on Reproduction and Developmental Toxicity

Reproductive and developmental toxicity studies of Asaigermanium were conducted during the fertility and early embryonic development phases (Seg. I; Asai Germanium Research Institute Co., Ltd., unpublished data), the embryo‐fetal development phase (Seg. II; Asai Germanium Research Institute Co., Ltd. unpublished data), and the pre‐ and postnatal development phases (Seg. III; Asai Germanium Research Institute Co., Ltd. unpublished data). The Seg. I and Seg. III tests were conducted in rats, and both rats and rabbits were used for the Seg. II tests. The dosages ranged from approximately 300–4000 mg/kg/day in rats and 350–1000 mg/kg/day in rabbits. Assessments included maternal clinical condition and body weight (with clinical pathology where appropriate), necropsy and histopathology, fetal examinations (external, visceral, and skeletal), and, for the perinatal/lactational phase, maternal behavior and offspring growth and functional endpoints.

The general toxicity test results indicated that a fairly high dose of Asaigermanium caused loosening of the stool and diarrhea, whereas an abnormally high dosage (1000 mg/kg/day or more) led to the development of more severe toxicity and death. In these studies, the fetal no‐observed‐adverse‐effect level (NOAEL) was 4000 mg/kg/day in rats in both Seg. I and Seg. II, 1000 mg/kg/day in rabbits in Seg. II, and 2000 mg/kg/day in Seg. III in rats. In these reproductive and developmental toxicity studies, no adverse developmental findings were reported within the tested NOAEL range. However, these results should be considered with the usual caution when they are applied to humans.

According to previous non‐GLP studies, Asaigermanium was administered via the tail vein to male and female Wistar rats at doses of 250–1000 mg/kg in Seg. I, II, and III reproductive and developmental toxicity studies. No adverse effects on reproductive function, teratogenicity, or fetal growth were observed even at the highest dose tested (1000 mg/kg), and the no‐observed‐adverse‐effect level was considered to be at least 1000 mg/kg (Sugiya et al. 1986b, 1986c, 1986d). Similarly, a non‐GLP study in rabbits reported no adverse effects on either dams or fetuses at 1000 mg/kg (Shimpo and Mori 1980).

Antigenicity Test

In accordance with Kagoshima and Suzuki, antigenicity was evaluated in non‐GLP tests using Hartley male guinea pigs (200–250 g) and New Zealand White male rabbits (1.8–2.0 kg) (Kagoshima and Suzuki 1986).

In the systemic anaphylaxis test, a negative control (physiological saline) and a positive control (sensitized with 3 mg/kg BSA suspended in Freund's complete adjuvant (FCA) and challenged with 10 mg/kg BSA intravenously) were used. The Asaigermanium‐sensitized group (either alone or in combination with FCA) was challenged intravenously with 10 mg/kg Asaigermanium, and the animals were observed for signs of anaphylaxis for up to 24 h after administration.

In the passive cutaneous anaphylaxis (PCA) test, antisera prepared with 100 mg/kg Asaigermanium (alone or combined with FCA) were administered intradermally (Kagoshima and Suzuki 1986). Afterward, the animals were challenged by intravenous injection of 10 mg of Asaigermanium mixed with 0.5 mL of saline and 0.5 mL of 1% Evans blue, and the reactivity was determined by the number of spots with dye leakage. In these experiments, systemic anaphylaxis was not observed, and the PCA reaction was negative; thus, Asaigermanium antigenicity was not detected.

In addition, a GLP skin sensitization test was performed in guinea pigs to investigate the possibility of a delayed allergic reaction at the maximum dose (Asai Germanium Research Institute Co., Ltd. unpublished data). Notably, no skin reactions or delayed allergic reactions were observed after the application of 0.1%, 0.6%, 3.5%, or 20% Asaigermanium. However, erythema was observed in 2 of the 10 individuals 24 h after treatment with a high concentration of Asaigermanium (20%) but not after 48 h. These results suggest that Asaigermanium application does not result in skin sensitization.

Mutagenicity Tests

The genotoxicity of Asaigermanium was evaluated by the following tests (Asai Germanium Research Institute Co., Ltd., unpublished data). The reversion mutation assay was conducted using Escherichia coli WP2 uvrA and Salmonella typhimurium TA100/TA1535/TA98/TA1537 in which doses of 312.5–5000 µg/plate were evaluated under ±S9 conditions (in accordance with the Pharmaceutical Toxicity Test Method Guidelines in 1989 and Pharmaceutical GLP Standards). Chromosomal aberration tests were carried out in mammalian (Chinese hamster) V79 cells at concentrations ranging from 0.49 to 1.95 mg/mL for the direct method and from 0.85 to 3.40 mg/mL under metabolic activation. An in vivo micronucleus test was performed by orally administering 1500–6000 mg/kg Asaigermanium to male BDF mice followed by the evaluation of their bone marrow cells.

The mutagenicity of Asaigermanium was examined by a reversion mutagenicity test using bacteria (E. coli and Salmonella), a chromosomal aberration test using cultured mammalian (Chinese hamster) cells, and a micronucleus test using rodents (mice). All the tests were negative, confirming that Asaigermanium is not mutagenic.

Carcinogenicity Tests

The carcinogenicity of Asaigermanium was investigated in two types of rodents: rasH2 mice (Doi et al. 2017) and F344 rats (Iwadate et al. 2018). These studies were conducted in accordance with GLP principles (Japanese Ordinance on Standards for Conduct of Nonclinical Studies on the Safety of Drugs, Ministerial Ordinance No. 21, March 26, 1997; partially amended by Ministerial Ordinance No. 114, June 13, 2008) and complied with the carcinogenicity testing guidelines (Pharmaceutical Affairs Bureau Notification No. 443, July 2, 1989).

In a 26‐week carcinogenicity study using rasH2 mice, CB6F1‐Tg rasH2 (rasH2) mice of both sexes received 0%–2.5% (w/w) Asaigermanium in the diet for 26 weeks (0% Asaigermanium served as the negative control). N‐Methyl‐N‐nitrosourea (75 mg/kg) was administered once intraperitoneally as a positive control. The results showed that there was no difference in the survival rate between the Asaigermanium‐treated group and the control group. In addition, the incidence of neoplastic lesions did not significantly increase after Asaigermanium administration. These findings indicate that Asaigermanium did not increase the incidence of neoplastic lesions in rasH2 mice under the conditions of this study.

On the other hand, in a 24‐month carcinogenicity study using F344 rats, F344/DuCrj rats (6 weeks old, both sexes) received 0%–2.5% (w/w) Asaigermanium in the diet for 104 weeks. Throughout the study, the clinical condition, body weight, and food consumption were assessed at regular intervals. At week 104, the animals were euthanized by exsanguination, and hematological analysis, complete gross necropsy, and full histopathological examination of all organs/tissues (via H&E staining) were performed. Overall, 1.3% of the males in the Asaigermanium group (585 mg/kg/day) and 2.5% of both the males and females in the Asaigermanium group (male: 1195 mg/kg/day, female: 1509 mg/kg) were affected by the administration of fairly high doses of Asaigermanium indicated. In this study, hypercalcemia was induced, and the incidence of pheochromocytoma in the adrenal medulla increased (Iwadate et al. 2018). As reported by Greim et al. (2009), pheochromocytomas occur relatively frequently, particularly in male rats, as a result of hypercalcemia, although there is no evidence that such tumors are induced in humans through the same mechanism. Therefore, these findings were interpreted as secondary to the disruption of calcium/phosphate homeostasis and are considered to reflect a rat‐specific mechanism. However, these findings should be interpreted with caution in terms of human risk assessment rather than being directly extrapolated to humans. Further safety studies are warranted, particularly after high‐dose administration in humans, with careful consideration of this effect. The results of other safety tests revealed that secondary toxicity, such as gastrointestinal symptoms (including diarrhea and loose stools), weight loss, and blood biochemical parameter fluctuations, occurred after the administration of extremely high doses of Asaigermanium. In humans, high doses of Asaigermanium should be approached with caution, and potential gastrointestinal symptoms should be taken into consideration.

Clinical Trials

Asaigermanium safety has also been evaluated in human studies. In 1980, Asaigermanium was orally administered to 23 healthy adult men and women aged 20–60 years at dosages of 25–75 mg/kg/day in a phase I study, and no abnormalities were observed in any of the participants (Miyao 1979). In addition, in 2025, 12 healthy adult men and women received 1000 mg/day Asaigermanium, and similarly, no abnormalities were observed in this study (Kawakami et al. 2025). These reports suggest that Asaigermanium is likely to be safe, at least in healthy individuals, provided that excessive amounts are not consumed.

Safety Tests in Other Groups

In addition to our own studies, safety studies on Ge‐132 have been conducted by various institutions worldwide. A French group performed an oral administration study in rats in 1992 (Anger et al. 1992). In that study, male rats received 1 g/kg/day orally for 6 months, and no toxicity was observed other than a slight decrease in body weight. In addition, a Hungarian group conducted safety studies in compliance with the Organisation for Economic Co‐operation and Development guidelines and GLP in 2020 (Reddeman et al. 2020). The results revealed no evidence of mutagenicity in either the bacterial reverse mutation test or the in vitro mammalian chromosomal aberration test. In the in vivo mammalian micronucleus test, no genotoxic activity was observed at dosages up to the limit of 2000 mg/kg body weight/day. In a 90‐day repeated‐dose oral toxicity study in Han:WIST rats administered 0, 500, 1000, and 2000 mg/kg body weight/day by oral gavage, there were no deaths, no treatment‐related adverse effects, and no identified target organs. The NOAEL was determined to be 2000 mg/kg body weight/day. Although the rat strains differed, these results indicate a NOAEL comparable to or higher than that observed in the chronic toxicity study described above. In addition, SK‐818 (propagermanium), a polymer of the THGP monomer that is similar to Ge‐132, has been reported to have low toxicity. In acute toxicity studies, the median lethal dose (LD50) of SK‐818 after oral administration was reported to be 7050 mg/kg in rats and 5600 mg/kg in mice (Kanda et al. 1990). Furthermore, in a phase I clinical trial in patients with breast cancer, no abnormalities were observed when 12 female patients aged 40–69 years received 30–90 mg/kg body weight/day (Masuda et al. 2020).

Health Foods Self‐Safety Inspection Certification Registration in Japan

Asaigermanium was certified and registered in the Health Foods Safety Voluntary Inspection Certification Registration System of the Japan Health and Nutrition Food Association on November 22, 2019. This registration is a third‐party certification program that acknowledges the safety of health foods and is the Japanese version of the self‐affirmed generally recognized as safe. However, this registration pertains to the ingredient‐level voluntary safety inspection framework for health food materials in Japan and does not constitute approval as a drug or authorization of a specific health claim.

Conclusion

Asaigermanium has been used for more than 55 years, and previous studies have reported a wide range of potential benefits, including immunomodulatory effects (Aso et al. 1985) and interactions with physiologically important molecules containing vicinal cis‐diols (Nakamura et al. 2015).

In this review, we described the differences between inorganic germanium and organogermanium in terms of toxicity and present the results of various toxicological studies of Asaigermanium. Asaigermanium is hydrolyzed to the simple monomer THGP, which is very metabolically stable in the body. Therefore, THGP is not broken down into different compounds; moreover, this structure is responsible for the low affinity of THGP for physiologically active vicinal cis‐diols such as ribonucleic acid and catecholamine (Nakamura et al. 2015). Furthermore, weak and reversible interactions with cis‐diol compounds may transiently modulate biological signaling pathways, as suggested by Aizawa et al. (2025), thereby providing a possible explanation for the modest but beneficial physiological effects of Asaigermanium.

This review summarizes the currently available safety‐related data for Asaigermanium from published and unpublished sources. In this review, we disclose Asaigermanium safety data for the first time. Asaigermanium is a synthetic material; however, it is used in various foods and has been shown by many toxicological tests to be safe. Toxicological tests are very expensive, but each is very important for confirming the safety of a particular synthetic artificial compound for oral intake. Typically, food ingredients are not synthetic but are derived from natural food materials; therefore, additional toxicity tests are not needed. However, organogermanium materials are synthetic and require safety information from the manufacturer.

The toxicological findings summarized in this review are specific to the material evaluated in the cited studies. The extrapolation of these findings to other organogermanium preparations should be made with caution, considering possible differences in chemical form, purity, formulation, and study design.

Finally, ensuring that consumers can use organogermanium compounds safely is important. We hope that this review will help correct the conflation of inorganic germanium and organogermanium compounds and address misconceptions organogermanium compound toxicity. In addition, researchers who study organogermanium need to evaluate its safety more carefully and make judicious assessments of its role.
