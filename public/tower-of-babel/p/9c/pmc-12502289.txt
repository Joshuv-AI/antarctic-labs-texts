The impact of early pregnancy disease control in systemic lupus erythematosus patients receiving comprehensive pharmaceutical care on pregnancy outcomes and offspring long-term health
Authors: Lian Zheng, Xixuan Yu, Shiran Li, Jinfeng Huang, Yuanyuan He, Lin Zhang, Xiaolan Xia, Jing Zhu, Jun Yue, Yan Wang, Yue Zhuang, Chao Zhou, Yue Wu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveTo evaluate the impact of early pregnancy disease activity on pregnancy outcomes and long-term offspring health in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) under a multidisciplinary model that incorporates continuous pharmaceutical care.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included pregnant patients with SLE who attended the multidisciplinary immunology- focused pregnancy clinic at our hospital and received full-course pharmaceutical care between October 2018 and October 2022. Patients were stratified into active and inactive SLE groups based on SLEDAI scores during the first 10 weeks of gestation. Baseline characteristics, obstetric complications, and fetal outcomes were compared between the groups. Offspring were followed for a minimum of 24 months to evaluate growth parameters and the incidence of autoimmune, allergic, or neurodevelopmental disorders.ResultsA total of 137 pregnancies were included (30 in the active group and 107 in the inactive group). Pharmacists were involved throughout the care process, participating in preconception risk assessment, gestational medication optimisation, and adherence management. The active group demonstrated significantly different outcomes, with higher rates of pregnancy loss (43.3% vs. 11.2%, P < 0.001), pregnancy complications (83.3% vs. 48.6%, P < 0.001), and lupus nephritis (23.3% vs. 7.5%, P < 0.05) They also required high-dose prednisone more frequently (P = 0.041). Conversely, the active group had significantly lower live birth rates (60.0% vs. 87.9%, P < 0.001), full-term delivery rates (43.3% vs. 76.6%, P < 0.001), and favourable 5-minute Apgar scores (76.5% vs. 100.0%, P < 0.001). Among the 112 children followed for 24 months, no major birth defects, autoimmune diseases or neurodevelopmental abnormalities were observed. The inactive group reported 4 cases of visual abnormalities and 2 cases of asthma, while 1 case of asthma was documented in the active group.ConclusionPharmacists, as part of the multidisciplinary team, can provide comprehensive pharmaceutical care for pregnant patients with SLE. Both active and inactive SLE groups show risks of adverse pregnancy outcomes, with maternal and fetal outcomes being better in the inactive SLE group than in the active SLE group.

Background

Systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) is a systemic autoimmune connective tissue disorder that predominantly affects women of reproductive age and is characterised by prominent immune - inflammatory manifestations. The condition involves multiple organs, exhibits recurrent flares and remissions, and associated with complex immunological abnormalities [1]. According to a study conducted between 2013 and 2017, the standardised incidence rate in urban China was 14.09 per 100,000 person-years, with a significantly higher incidence among women (26.41 per 100,000 person-years) than men (5.92 per 100,000 person-years) [2]. Although advances in the clinical management of SLE have significantly improved patient survival, pregnancy continues to pose a major challenge for affected women.

Research has established that the primary predictor of adverse pregnancy outcomes in SLE patients is disease activity during the six months preceding conception [3–5]. Although fertility in women with SLE does not differ significantly from that of age-matched non-SLE women, pregnancy-related sex hormonal fluctuations and other complex physiological interactions can trigger disease flare-ups or exacerbations, potentially compromising both maternal and fetal health. This risk is particularly elevated in women with active disease, especially those with renal or haematological involvement, within six months of conception [6–8]. However, among patients whose disease was well-controlled during this critical preconception period, pregnancy-related flares are uncommon [9]. Consequently, effective reproductive health and pregnancy management remain essential for SLE patients [10].

Continuous medication therapy is crucial during pregnancy to maintain disease control in SLE patients. Treatment discontinuation is associated with an elevated risk of disease flare-ups during pregnancy [11]. Multiple studies and clinical guidelines recommend maintaining or initiating medications such as hydroxychloroquine (HCQ), oral glucocorticoids (OGC), azathioprine (AZA), cyclosporine (CsA), and tacrolimus (TAK) throughout pregnancy [12–14]. For these patients, appropriate preconception counselling, medication adjustments, stringent disease control prior to pregnancy, and close monitoring during pregnancy are essential for optimal outcomes [15]. However, many patients lack awareness regarding importance of achieving disease remission before conception or continuing medication management during pregnancy. Some remain undiagnosed with SLE until it is incidentally detected during routine prenatal screening.

To date, no study has examined the impact of pharmaceutical care as part of comprehensive disease management on pregnancy outcomes in SLE patients. In response, we introduced pharmacists as members of a multidisciplinary team to provide holistic pharmaceutical care for these patients. We conducted a retrospective cohort study involving SLE patients who attended the multidisciplinary immunology-related pregnancy clinic (MDT) at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital between October 2018 and October 2022 and received comprehensive pharmaceutical care. The study aimed to evaluate the influence of early pregnancy disease control on pregnancy outcomes in SLE patients. Furthermore, we followed up to two years to assess growth, development, and diseases incidence, thereby investigating the long-term effects of early pregnancy disease control and pharmacological interventions on the offspring.

Materials and methods

Study design

This single-centre, retrospective study aimed to evaluate the effects of disease control during early pregnancy in patients with systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) receiving comprehensive pharmaceutical care, with a focus on pregnancy outcomes and the long-term health of offspring. We consecutively enrolled women with confirmed SLE diagnosis who were pregnant (early gestation) and attended the Immunology-related Pregnancy MDT outpatient clinic at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital between October 2018 and October 2022. All patients received comprehensive pharmaceutical care. Eligible patients were aged 18–41 years, with an SLE disease duration ranging from 0 to 20 years. Follow-up assessments were conducted throughout pregnancy and at 12 and 24 months post-delivery. Rheumatologists diagnosed SLE in all cases according to the American College of Rheumatology (ACR) criteria [16]. Data collected included obstetric history, disease activity, medication use, and pregnancy outcomes (including complication). In cases of multiple pregnancies, each gestation was recorded separately. The inclusion and exclusion criteria are detailed in Table 1. Ethical approval for this study was granted by the hospital’s ethics committee (No.: Ethical Review (Research) No. 240-1 in 2025). The collected data included basic information, pre-pregnancy and pregnancy-related symptoms and signs, laboratory test results, medications administered during pregnancy, pregnancy outcomes, and fetal details. Basic information comprised maternal age at conception, SLE duration, past pregnancy history, and previous childbirth records. Laboratory data encompassed serological and immunological test results.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Disease activity assessment

Disease activity was assessed through patient history, clinical manifestations, laboratory tests, and the Systemic Lupus Erythematosus Disease Activity Index (SLEDAI) [17, 18]. Patients with SLEDAI scores ≥ 5 were classified as having active disease, while those with scores < 5 were considered stable, leading to division into the SLE active and non-active group [19]. The grouping criteria were as follows: (1) SLE Active group: disease stability lasting less than six months; presence of pre-pregnancy clinical manifestations including SLE-related heart, brain, kidney, central nervous system involvement, skin rashes, or arthritis; abnormal laboratory findings such as anaemia, leukopenia, thrombocytopenia, decreased complement levels, progressively increasing proteinuria, and elevated dsDNA antibody titers. (2) SLE Inactive group: disease stability maintained for six months or longer; absence of pre-pregnancy manifestations of SLE-related organ involvement, skin rashes, or arthritis; stable laboratory parameters.

Responsibilities of the pharmacist

The responsibilities of clinical pharmacists included the following: (1) Collecting clinical information, conducting etiological screening, and performing medication reconciliation; (2) Assessing disease activity and pregnancy eligibility in preconception patients, followed by adjusting treatment regimens accordingly; (3) Establishing electronic health records for pregnant patients and evaluating the appropriateness and benefit–risk profile of current therapies; (4) Participating in multidisciplinary teams to develop individualised medication plans; (5) Reviewing prescriptions and assessing the safety and compatibility of concurrent medications; (6) Providing medication education and counselling to ensure proper drug use and improve adherence; (7) Developing follow-up schedules, informing patients of revisit timing and content, and using digital tools to monitor adverse drug reactions throughout treatment.

Pregnancy outcomes assessment

Pregnancy outcomes comprised pregnancy loss (including miscarriage and stillbirth) and live birth. Maternal outcomes included the following: (1) Pregnancy-related complications, such as pre-eclampsia, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes, intrahepatic cholestasis of pregnancy, thrombocytopenia, thyroid dysfunction, anaemia; (2) Delivery methods and outcomes, categorised as cesarean section or vaginal delivery [20].

Fetal outcomes include: (1) fetal loss, comprising early miscarriage (before 12 weeks of gestation), late miscarriage (between 12 and 28 weeks), and stillbirth (after 28 weeks); (2) live birth, categorised as preterm and full-term; (3) low birth weight (< 2500 g); (4) newborn health status was comprehensively assessed through Apgar scores at 1, 5, and 10 min post-delivery to determine asphyxia risk and immediate viability (based on heart rate, respiration, muscle tone, reflexes, and skin colour), combined with structured evaluation for congenital malformations. (5) Neonatal Intensive Care Unit (NICU) admission.

Follow-up on offspring growth, development, and disease incidence

Telephone follow-up were conducted to collect data on the offspring’s height and weight at ages at 1 and 2, as well as information on whether they had developed conditions such as autoimmune disorders, allergic diseases, neurodevelopmental abnormalities, or visual developmental abnormalities. The assessment was based on the Growth Standards for Children Under 7 Years Old issued by the China’s National Health Commission. For instance, the standard height range for one-year-old boys is 71.7–81.6 cm, while for girls, it is 70.4–80.1 cm. The corresponding weight ranges are 7.7–11.6 kg for boys and 8.5–10.6 kg for girls. At two years of age, the height range for boys is 82.4–94.0 cm, compared to 81.2–92.8 cm fir girls, with weight ranges of 10.4–15.4 kg and 9.8–14.8 kg, respectively.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise the data. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies (percentages), while continuous variables were presented as means (± standard deviation) or medians (interquartile range), depending on their distribution. For comparisons between two groups, continuous variables were analysed using Student’s t-test or the Wilcoxon rank-sum test, as appropriate, whereas categorical variables were assessed using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test. A two-tailed P-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS (version 27.0).

Results

Patient screening

The study included 200 SLE patients in early pregnancy who attended the immunology-related pregnancy MDT clinic at Sichuan Provincial People’s Hospital and received comprehensive pharmaceutical care between October 2018 and October 2022. Of these, 133 patients (137 pregnancies) met the inclusion criteria (Fig. 1). Sixty-seven patients were excluded for the following reasons: missing data (n = 53), uncertain diagnosis (n = 8), rheumatoid arthritis (n = 2), diabetes (n = 2), congenital heart disease (n = 1), and bipolar disorder (n = 1).

Pharmaceutical services during pregnancy for SLE patients

Clinical pharmacists, as permanent members of the MDT, delivered comprehensive pharmaceutical care to all patients in accordance with the treatment plans established by rheumatologists and obstetricians. Working collaboration with the rheumatology and obstetrics teams, they developed individualised pharmacological treatment regimens. Throughout pregnancy, pharmacists provided ongoing, tailored medication education and counselling, adapted to each patient’s specific needs. Follow-up consultations were conducted at intervals of 3 weeks to 3 months, either face-to-face or via phone, to evaluate disease control, monitor pregnancy progression, and document any adverse drug reactions. Impact of pre-pregnancy SLE disease control on pregnancy outcomes

Compared to the inactive group, the active group exhibited a significantly higher pregnancy loss rate (40.0%), with nearly half of the patients failing to carry the pregnancy to term. Among the active group, all 17 live births were delivered via cesarean section, indicating that cesarean delivery may be more prevalent in patients with active SLE.

Maternal outcomes of SLE pregnancies

Regarding complications, aside from lupus nephritis, no significant differences were observed between the two groups in the incidence of preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, gestational diabetes mellitus, intrahepatic cholestasis, thrombocytopenia, thyroid dysfunction, or anaemia. Both groups included one case of severe preeclampsia, suggesting a comparable distribution of these complications among SLE patients.

Next, we evaluated fetal and neonatal outcomes (Table 5). Of the 137 pregnancies analysed, 81.8% resulted in live births, with the inactive group demonstrating a significantly higher live birth rate (87.9%) than the active group. The active group exhibited a significantly higher rate of late miscarriage (30.0% vs. 11.2%, P = 0.02), and all cases of stillbirth (n = 2) occurred exclusively in this group. The full-term delivery rate was significantly higher in the inactive group was 76.6% compared to the active group (43.3%, P < 0.001), underscoring the importance of disease stability in early pregnancy for optimal outcomes. While the proportion of low birth weight infants was higher in the active group (29.4% vs. 15.8%), this difference did not reach statistical significance. Apgar score analysis revealed that two infants in the active group and one in the inactive group scored below 7 at 1 min. At 5 and 10 min, only the active group included infants with scores below 9. Neither group reported cases of congenital malformations or NICU admissions.

Medications use during pregnancy

Additionally, when analysing medication use during pregnancy, a significant difference was observed between the active and non-active SLE groups in the doses of corticosteroids and tacrolimus administered. However, no statistically significant differences were found in the use of other medications, including hydroxychloroquine (Table 6). The proportion of patients receiving high-dose prednisone was significantly higher in the active group than in the non-active group (P = 0.041). Data on the overall use of antirheumatic drugs and varying corticosteroid doses during pregnancy were also compiled (Fig. 3). One patient on mycophenolate mofetil was evaluated by a pharmacist at 7 + 3 weeks of gestation and opted for selective pregnancy terminate due to the high teratogenic risk associated with the medication.

Impact of early-pregnancy disease activity in systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) on long-term growth and health outcomes in offspring

Follow-up assessments of height and weight at ages 1 and 2 years evealed no significant differences in growth parameters between children born to mothers with inactive SLE and with active SLE during early pregnancy (Table 7). Regarding long-term health outcomes, in the inactive group we identified cases of astigmatism (n = 2), myopia (n = 1), persistent pupillary membrane (n = 1), and asthma (n = 2). The active group had only one case of asthma. By two years of age, no other health conditions, including autoimmune diseases, allergic disorders (such as eczema or food allergy), or neurodevelopmental abnormalities, were reported in either group.

Discussion

In recent years, as research on Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) has advanced and clinical pregnancy management has improved, an increasing number of SLE patients have successfully delivered healthy infants. This progress has consequently heightened these patients’ desire for parenthood [21]. However, compared with the general obstetric population, SLE patients remain at significantly greater risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes (APOs) [22–24]. Of particular concern is that some patients voluntarily discontinue medications, especially antimalarials, due to teratogenicity or fetal safety concerns [15, 25, 26], underscoring the critical need for proper medication education and shared decision-making. Experience from managing chronic conditions such as diabetes [27], hypertension [28], and cancer [29] has demonstrated that pharmacist-led interventions can effective improve medication adherence and clinical outcomes [30]. This study represents the first investigation into value of pharmacist involvement in a multidisciplinary team (MDT) for managing SLE-complicated pregnancies. For example, one patient with high disease activity during in early pregnancy opted for termination at 7+3 weeks after unintentional exposure to the known teratogen mycophenolate mofetil, a case that highlights the pharmacist’s critical role in preconception and early pregnancy risk assessment. Furthermore, pharmacists were tasked with the dynamic monitoring of corticosteroid dosage and disease activity throughout pregnancy, enabling individualised dose adjustments and helping to minimize steroid-related complications. It should be noted that all patients in this cohort received continuous pharmaceutical care, and no control group without pharmacist intervention was included. Consequently, the direct impact of pharmaceutical services on pregnancy outcomes could not be statistically quantified, representing one of this study’s limitations.

Our study also included a unique and clinically significant cohort: 30 pregnant women with active SLE (defined as SLEDAI ≥ 5 in early pregnancy). Such patients are generally advised to delay conception, and data on their outcomes remain limited. Compared to the inactive SLE group, women with active disease exhibited significantly higher pregnancy loss rates, with nearly half failing to carry the pregnancy to term. Rates of full-term delivery (43.3% vs. 76.6%) and live birth (60.0% vs. 87.9%) were also substantially lower in the active group. These findings consistent with the large-scale meta-analysis by Wind et al., which reported that active disease prior to conception was associated with a 66% reduction in live birth rates (OR = 0.34, 95% CI: 0.19–0.62) and served as a strong predictor of preterm birth (OR = 2.91, 95% CI: 1.96–4.33) [5]. Notably, all patients in the active SLE group delivered via cesarean section, likely reflecting obstetricians’ increased caution in ensuring maternal and fetal safety in the context active disease. Complications such as preeclampsia and thrombocytopenia are common in SLE pregnancies, as demonstrated in previous studies [21, 31, 32]. Preeclampsia, in particular, poses a significant risk to maternal health [33–35]. In the general population, preeclampsia occurs in approximately 5–8% of pregnancies, whereas the incidence in SLE pregnancies may reach 13–15% [36]. The Wind study further quantified this association, reporting a threefold increased risk (OR = 3.11, 95% CI: 2.35–4.12) [5]. In our study, the overall preeclampsia rate was 8.03%, which was lower than previously reported figures. This discrepancy may be attributable to the prophylactic use of low-dose aspirin in high-risk patients, as advised by the MDT pharmacist. Although the active SLE group exhibited a higher preeclampsia rate than the inactive group (16.7% vs. 5.6%, P = 0.063), the difference was not statistically significant. One case of severe preeclampsia was observed in each group. The incidence of other complications did not differ significantly between groups, suggesting that SLE disease activity may not be the sole determinant of obstetric complications and that individualised prevention and treatment strategies are required. Lupus nephritis (LN), one of the most common organ manifestations in SLE, poses substantial risks during pregnancy. Women with a history of LN are at significantly increased risk of flare during pregnancy (OR = 2.66, 95% CI: 1.51–4.70), which is closely associated with adverse outcomes [37–39]. In our study, LN incidence was significantly higher in the active group than in the inactive group (23.3% vs. 7.5%, P = 0.023), highlighting the importance of thorough preconception assessment and close monitoring throughout gestation, particularly in patients with renal involvement. Furthermore, existing literature demonstrates that SLE patients with antiphospholipid syndrome (APS), thrombocytopenia, proteinuria or chronic hypertension face increased risks of miscarriage, low birth weight (LBW) and preterm birth [40]. A recent meta-analysis reported higher rates of miscarriage and LBW in SLE pregnancies [41]. In our study of 137 pregnancies, the LBW incidence was 14.6%. While the LBW rate appeared higher in the active group (29.4% vs. 15.8%, P = 0.182), the difference was not statistically significant, indicating that factors beyond disease activity should be considered when predicting and managing LBW in this population. Notably, no cases of neonatal birth defects or NICU admissions were observed in either group. These findings underscore the value of comprehensive pharmaceutical care throughout pregnancy for SLE patients. The MDT-based pharmaceutical care model we developed is both feasible and replicable, potentially serve as a useful reference for pharmacists managing complex pregnancies complicated by autoimmune diseases.

Current data on the long-term safety of pharmacologic treatment during pregnancy in SLE patients remain limited and somewhat controversial [11, 42]. In this study, we performed postnatal follow-up assessments to evaluate performed height, weight, and overall health status at 12 and 24 months of age. Results revealed no significant differences in growth parameters between children born to mothers in the active versus inactive SLE groups. Importantly, all 17 children from the active group showed normal height and weight development within expected ranges at both time points. We monitored offspring for developmental and health-related conditions, including neonatal lupus, cognitive delay, visual or auditory impairments, and allergic or respiratory diseases. By age 2 years, in the inactive group, two children developed astigmatism (onset at 1 year), one child developed myopia, and one presented with persistent pupillary membrane at 1 year. Three children in this group developed asthma, including one diagnosed at 2 years. In the active SLE group, one asthma case was reported at 1 year. No other conditions, including autoimmune diseases, eczema, food allergies, or neurodevelopmental disorders, were observed during the follow-up. While this study represents one of the few reporting 2-year follow-up data in children born to reporting mothers, the small sample size and lack of a healthy control group preclude definitive conclusions regarding potential relationship between maternal disease activity/treatment exposure and pediatric outcomes. This constitutes a key study limitation. However, our findings offer reassuring preliminary evidence concerning the short-to-medium-term safety profile for offspring of SLE patients, particularly within a framework of structured perinatal management.

Several additional limitations warrant consideration. First, as a retrospective single-centre cohort study with a relatively small sample size (134 patients and 137 pregnancies), the generalizability of our findings to broader populations with varying disease severity and demographic characteristics is limited. Like other small-scale cohort studies, our work reveals trends that require validation in larger, more diverse populations. Second, all participants received continuous pharmaceutical care throughout pregnancy, leaving no comparator group without pharmacist involvement. This limitation prevented objective assessment of pharmaceutical care’s independent impact. Future studies should incorporate variables such as pharmacist intervention, drug class, dosage, and exposure timing to better evaluate their respective contributions to outcomes. Third, our retrospective dataset only included disease activity assessment at early pregnancy (within 12 gestational weeks), and did not capture longitudinal SLEDAI scores across the entire pregnancy period. Therefore, we were unable to perform a reliable analysis on the association between disease activity and glucocorticoid dosing throughout gestation. Finally, offspring follow-up faced challenges including missing data and a relatively brief observation period. While no long-term complications were detected during the 2-year follow-up, the potential of delayed-onset autoimmune or neurodevelopmental conditions cannot be ruled out. Future prospective studies with extended follow-up durations, ideally extending into school age, will be needed to assess these risks more comprehensively.

Conclusion

In summary, all pregnant patients with SLE face an increased risk for adverse pregnancy outcomes, irrespective of apparent disease control. Comprehensive, multidisciplinary monitoring and management, ideally involving obstetricians, rheumatologists, and clinical pharmacists—are essential throughout pregnancy. Key priorities include preconception counselling, timely substitution of high-risk medications, implementation of prophylactic strategies, and patient education to enhance adherence. This study reaffirms the central tenet of SLE pregnancy management: disease quiescence before conception remains one of the most critical predictors of favourable pregnancy outcomes. Future research should focus on elucidating the distinct contributions of disease activity and pharmaceutical interventions to maternal and neonatal outcomes, as well as extending offspring follow-up into later developmental stages.
