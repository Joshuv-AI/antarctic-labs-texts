ThGRF5, a 14–3-3 protein, acts as a positive regulator of salt tolerance in Tamarix hispida
Authors: Yuanyuan Wang, Ya,nan Jing, Menghua Lv, Qianqian Chen, Youzi Tian, Caiqiu Gao, Guojun Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe 14–3-3 proteins, also known as General Regulatory Factors (GRFs), are a family of highly conserved acidic proteins that mediate cellular processes and plant stress adaptation through protein–protein interactions. In this study, 9 GRF genes (ThGRF1-ThGRF9) were isolated and characterized from the halophytic species Tamarix hispida.ResultsqRT-PCR analyses showed that all nine ThGRFs responded to multiple stress response pathways. In particular, the expression levels of ThGRF4 and ThGRF5 were significantly upregulated in T. hispida roots under salt stress, suggesting their potential involvement in salt stress responses. Furthermore, an overexpression vector of ThGRF5 was constructed and transiently transformed into T. hispida. Biochemical staining and physiological parameter analyses showed that overexpression of ThGRF5 increased antioxidant enzyme activity, decreased MDA content and electrolyte leakage by decreasing the accumulation of ROS, thereby improving tolerance to salt stress.ConclusionsThese results suggested that ThGRF5 might play an important physiological role in salt tolerance in transgenic T. hispida plants. This study provides a foundation for further elucidation of salt tolerance mechanisms involving ThGRF5 in T. hispida.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12870-026-09128-y.Keywords: 14–3-3 protein, GRFs, Gene function, Salt stress, Tamarix hispida

Background

The GENERAL REGULATORY FACTOR (GRF, 14–3-3) protein is a type of acidic and soluble protein that was first discovered by Moore and Perez in 1967, primarily in the bovine brain [1]. It was not until the end of the twentieth century that the GRF protein began to be found widely in eukaryotes [2–4]. Systematic genomic analyses have revealed their extensive distribution across plant species, with 13, 6, and 8 isoforms characterized in Arabidopsis, cotton (Gossypium spp.), and rice, respectively [5–7]. In Populus trichocarpa, bioinformatic studies initially identified 12 GRF genes [8], later expanded to 14 through comprehensive genome annotation [9]. The GRF protein were classified into ε and non-ε groups based on sequence homology and electrophoretic mobility [10]. Structurally, GRF proteins feature a highly conserved core region (51% amino acid conservation in Arabidopsis) flanked by variable N- and C-terminal domains [11].

Extensive research has established that GRF proteins play multifaceted regulatory roles in plant development and environmental adaptation. Regarding flowering regulation, GRF proteins exhibited complex and diverse functional characteristics. In chrysanthemum (Chrysanthemum morifolium), the Cm14-3-3μ protein inhibits flowering through synergistic interaction with CmNRRa [12], while in semi-winter rapeseed (Brassica napus), BnGF14-2c demonstrates positive flowering promotion [13]. In bamboo (Phyllostachys violascens), PvGF14c exerted negative regulation on flowering, and the PvFT gene negatively regulates flowering through interaction with three GRF genes-PvGF14b, PvGF14c and PvGF14e [14]. Particularly noteworthy is that overexpression of tomato (Solanum lycopersicum) GRF gene can compensate for the early flowering phenotype in Self Pruning mutants [15, 16].

Beyond flowering regulation, GRF proteins are extensively involved in plant responses to abiotic stresses. For heat stress, heterologous overexpression of Haloxylon ammodendron HaFT-1 in Arabidopsis significantly enhances thermotolerance [17]. In drought and salt stress responses, overexpression of apple (Malus domestica) MdGRF11 and MdGRF13 both improves drought and salt tolerance in transgenic Arabidopsis [18, 19]. Soybean GRF proteins respond to cold and salt stress by modulating the activity of C-repeat binding factors (CBF) and SOS proteins [20]. However, some studies found that MdGRF6 overexpression increases salt sensitivity in apple calli [21], suggesting functional specificity among different GRF family members. Additionally, in mycorrhizal Populus cathayana seedlings, PcGRF10 and PcGRF11 may respond to drought stress by enhancing the antioxidant system and osmoregulation [22, 23]. These findings collectively reveal the complex regulatory networks of GRF proteins in plant stress responses.

Despite these advances, the molecular functions of GRF proteins in woody plants remain largely unexplored. In this study, nine ThGRFs were identified and characterized in T. hispida under various treatment conditions. qPCR analysis revealed that all nine ThGRFs responded to NaCl, PEG, ABA, GA3 stresses, with particularly prominent regulation in root tissues. Strikingly, ThGRF4 and ThGRF5 exhibited consistent root-specific induction across all stress treatments, showing the strongest upregulation under NaCl stress, suggesting their specialized roles in salt stress adaptation. Functional analysis of ThGRF5 demonstrated that its overexpression significantly enhanced salt tolerance in T. hispida, providing novel mechanistic insights into stress adaptation in woody halophytes. These findings establish ThGRF5 as a promising candidate for genetic engineering of stress-tolerant woody plants.

Material and methods

Plant materials and conditions

Seeds of T. hispida were cultivated in a controlled-environment greenhouse maintained at 24 °C, 75% relative humidity, with a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle and a light intensity of 250 μE m−2 s−1, using a 2:1 (v/v) autoclaved sand: turf peat mixture (pH 6.5 ± 0.2) and irrigated every 3 days with 1/2 Hoagland nutrient solution. Two-month-old seedlings (5–6 cm height) were subjected to stress treatments via irrigation every 24 h with freshly prepared solutions: (1) 200 mM NaCl, (2) 20% (w/v) PEG6000, (3) 100 μM ABA, (4) 50 μM GA₃, or (5) deionized water (control). Shoots and roots tissues from 10 seedlings were pooled at each time point (6, 12, 24, 48, and 72 h), flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at −80 °C for subsequent analysis. All treatments were performed in three independent biological triplicate. For transgenic experiments and physiological assays, sterile seedlings were grown in tissue culture bottles containing 1/2MS solid medium supplemented with 2% (w/v) sucrose, 0.7% (w/v) agar, and 0.2% (w/v) toner (pH 5.8), under a 16 h light/8 h dark cycle at 25°C.

Identification of ThGRF genes in T. hispida.

The term “GRF protein” was used as a keyword to search for GRF family genes from seven T. hispida transcriptomes [24]. Open reading frames (ORFs) were identified using NCBI ORF finder, and protein characteristics (molecular weight, isoelectric point) were analyzed using ExPASy ProtParam (https://web.expasy.org/protparam/).

Phylogenetic tree and protein structure analysis

The Phytozome database (https://phytozome-next.jgi.doe.gov/) was used to search for GRF proteins of Arabidopsis and rice, and a phylogentic tree of GRF proteins was analysed by EMBL-EBI (https://www.ebi.ac.uk/Tools/msa/) and iTOL (https://itol.embl.de/) in Arabidopsis, oryza sativa and T. hispida. The online soft tools of WoLF PSORT (https://www.genscript.com/wolf-psort.html) was used to predict the 9 ThGRFs subcellular localization. The conserved motifs of ThGRFs gene family members in T. hispida were analyzed by MEME online website (http://meme-suite.org/tools/meme). The number of parameters for finding conserved motifs was set to 10. A protein–protein interaction (PPI) network of ThGRFs proteins was generated using STRING database (https://cn.string-db.org/).

RNA extraction and qRT-PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from T. hispida tissues using the RNAprep Pure Plant Kit (BioTeKe, China). 1 µg of total RNA was transcribed into cDNA using a PrimeScript™ RT Kit (Takara, Shiga, Japan). The PCR mixture with a volume of 20 µL contained the following: 2 µL of template cDNA, 0.5 µM each of reverse and forward primer and 10 µL of TransStart Top Green qPCR SuperMix (TRANS, Beijing, China). The PCR protocol was as follows: 95 °C for 30 s, followed by 43 cycles of 98 °C for 15 s, 58 °C for 20 s, 72 °C for 45 s and 10 s at 80 °C for reading plates. All tests were triplicate. Data analysis was performed using the 2−ΔΔ(Ct) method [25]. α-tubulin and β-tubulin were selected as internal controls [26]. Primers used for qRT-PCR are listed in Table S1.

Vector Construction and Transient Overexpression of ThGRF5 in T. hispida.

The CDS of ThGRF5 was inserted into pROKII driving by 35S CaMV promoter to over-express ThGRF5 (35S::ThGRF5). All primer used were shown in Supplementary Table S1. The genetic transformation of T. hispida plants was carried out following Wang et al. [27]. Briefly, one-month-old T. hispida seedlings of uniform size were immersed in Agrobacterium suspension (OD600 = 0.6) and oscillated at 90 rpm (25 °C, 2.5 h). After washing with sterile water, seedlings were vertically cultured on MS medium containing 0.5 mg/L NAA, 2 mg/L 6-BA, 120 μM acetosyringone, and 16 mg/L DTT for 24 h. Two transformation types were generated: 35S::ThGRF5 overexpression lines (OE) and empty vector controls (CK). Two transformation types T. hispida seedlings were transferred to solid MS supplemented with 200 mM NaCl or to solid MS without NaCl for 24 h, respectively. Each experiment included three biological replicates with ≥ 60 seedlings per replicate. Each experiment included three independent biological replicates with ≥ 30 seedlings per replicate. In the legend of Supplementary Figure S2, we have clarified that the data shown are from a representative experiment, with similar results obtained from the replicate experiments.

Physiological assays and histochemical staining of transgenic plants

For physiological characterization, transiently transformed seedlings were cultured on MS medium for 48 h before being transferred to either control MS medium or MS medium containing 200 mM NaCl for stress treatment (2 h for histochemical staining; 24 h for physiological measurements using whole seedlings). Hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) accumulation was visualized by 3,3'-diaminobenzidine (DAB) staining, while cell viability was assessed using Evans blue staining [28, 29]. Quantitative physiological analyses included measurements of H₂O₂ content using a specific assay kit (Solarbio, BC3590), superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity determined with a commercial kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, A001-3) by monitoring the inhibition of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photochemical reduction at 560 nm, peroxidase (POD) activity measured with a kit (Nanjing Jiancheng, A084-3) based on the guaiacol oxidation method at 470 nm, malondialdehyde (MDA) contentquantified via the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction (absorbance read at 532 nm and 600 nm for calculation), and electrolyte leakage assessed by measuring the solution conductivity with a portable conductivity meter (model DDS-307A) before and after boiling the samples, with results expressed as a percentage of total electrolytes [30, 31]. All assays were performed with three independent biological replicates.

Statistical analysis

The Statistical Software Package for Social Science (SPSS) version 26.0 was employed to analyse the data using the t-test to identify significant differences. * indicated a significant difference from controls. Error bars represent standard deviations.

Results

Identification and characterization of ThGRFs

The nine ThGRFs (ThGRF1-ThGRF9) were identified in T. hispida, with coding sequences ranging from 732–828 bp in length. The encoded proteins consisted of 243–275 amino acids, with predicted molecular weights of 63.12–68.79 kDa. Bioinformatics analysis revealed these proteins possess acidic isoelectric points (pI 5.03–5.15) and hydrophobic characteristics (positive GRAVY values). Subcellular localization predictions indicated most ThGRFs likely reside in the chloroplast and plasma membrane (Table 1).

Protein Structure and Interaction Analysis of ThGRFs.

MEME-based motif analysis identified 10 conserved motifs across the nine ThGRF proteins, with motif lengths varying from 6 to 50 amino acids. Six common motifs (Motif5-Motif2-Motif7-Motif3-Motif1-Motif4) were shared among all ThGRFs, suggesting a conserved functional core. From Fig. 2, Motif 6 appears to be a key determinant in distinguishing the ε and non-ε subgroups (Fig. 2, Supplementary Figure S1).

The PPI network revealed 19 interactions among 9 ThGRFs, with ThGRF2 emerging as the most interconnected node (7 partners), followed by ThGRF1 and ThGRF3 (6 partners each) (Fig. 3). These findings suggest that ThGRF2, ThGRF1, and ThGRF3 may act as key regulatory hubs within the ThGRF family, potentially coordinating broader functional networks. The high connectivity of these proteins underscores their central role in ThGRF-mediated processes.

Relative expression of ThGRF in shoots and roots

qRT-PCR analysis revealed distinct tissue-specific expression profiles among ThGRFs under control conditions. ThGRF8 with the lowest expression level occurred in roots (with the highest delta Ct value), so this expression level was 1 to rectify expression of other ThGRFs (Fig. 4). Notably, ThGRF4 exhibited the highest expression level in shoots (1,820.35-fold higher than ThGRF8 in roots), while ThGRF1 displayed the strongest expression in roots (2,836.70-fold higher than ThGRF8 in roots). These results demonstrate that different ThGRF family members show preferential expression in specific tissues, suggesting they may play specialized functional roles in shoot or root development and physiology.

Expression analysis of ThGRFs under abiotic stress and hormone treatments

Response to NaCl stress

The ThGRFs exhibited distinct temporal and tissue-specific expression patterns under NaCl stress. In shoots, most genes showed initial upregulation but were significantly suppressed after 48 h, with ThGRF8 displaying the lowest expression (0.82% of control). Notably, ThGRF7 peaked at 6 h (3.26-fold increase). Root tissues displayed contrasting patterns, with eight genes showing significant upregulation except ThGRF8. Particularly, ThGRF4 and ThGRF5 demonstrated consistent upregulation in both tissues, reaching 6.77–89.40-fold increases in roots across time points (Fig. 5A, B).

Response to PEG stresses

Under PEG stress, the expression of ThGRF5 showed no significant changes in shoots (except at the early and late stages of stress). In shoots, the expression of ThGRF1, ThGRF2, ThGRF3, ThGRF4, and ThGRF8 were predominantly downregulated, while ThGRF6 and ThGRF7 were induced. In roots, the expression of ThGRF4 and ThGRF5 were significantly up-regulated at all time points, and reached the peak at 12 h, which were 18.32 and 23.25 fold higher than the control, respectively (Fig. 6A, B). ThGRF1, ThGRF2, ThGRF6 were able to produce a rapid response to PEG stress. ThGRF2, ThGRF6 reached the lowest expression levels at 6 h (26.28% and 40.77% of the control, respectively), while ThGRF1 reached its minimum at 24 h (30.47% of the control level).

Response to ABA Treatments.

Under ABA treatment, ThGRFs exhibited distinct tissue-specific expression patterns. In shoots, ThGRF3 showed consistent downregulation, while ThGRF6 and ThGRF7 were upregulated. Root tissues displayed a more pronounced response, with all genes significantly upregulated across most time points. Notably, most genes displayed a biphasic expression pattern under ABA treatment, reaching the primary peak at 6–12 h followed by a trough at 24 h and a secondary peak at 48 h. ThGRF4 and ThGRF5 demonstrated particularly strong early responses in roots, reaching 47.58- and 47.91-fold increases, respectively at 6 h (Fig. 7A, B). These results suggest that ABA plays a crucial role in modulating ThGRFs expression, with roots showing greater sensitivity than shoots.

Response to GA3 treatments

GA3 treatment induced differential regulation of ThGRFs between tissues. In shoots, ThGRF8 exhibited a fluctuating down-regulated (reaching minimum expression at 72 h), while ThGRF7 was induced and ThGRF3 remained largely unchanged. Root tissues showed robust upregulation of most ThGRFs, with ThGRF4 and ThGRF5 displaying particularly strong responses (3.20–18.20 and 6.66–21.64-fold increases, respectively). The contrasting expression patterns between shoots and roots under GA3 treatment suggest tissue-specific roles for ThGRFs in gibberellin-mediated processes (Fig. 8A, B). These findings highlight the complex regulation of ThGRFs by plant hormones, with ThGRF4 and ThGRF5 emerging as particularly responsive members to both ABA and GA3 treatments.

ThGRF5 confers salt stress tolerance via ROS scavenging and membrane Protection in transgenic plants

The transient overexpression of ThGRF5 was confirmed by qRT-PCR, with expression peaking at 48 h (Supplementary Figure S2), establishing this time point for subsequent salt stress experiments. Under normal conditions, no significant differences were observed between overexpression (OE) and control (CK) plants in biochemical staining (DAB and Evans blue) or physiological indicators (H₂O₂, MDA, SOD, POD, and electrolyte leakage). However, after NaCl stress, OE plants exhibited significantly reduced oxidative damage, as evidenced by lighter DAB (H₂O₂) and Evans blue (cell death) staining compared with CK (Fig. 9A, B), along with 39.12% lower H₂O₂ and 47.64% lower MDA levels (Fig. 9C, D).

The enhanced stress tolerance in OE plants was further supported by elevated antioxidant enzyme activity. Under salt stress, SOD and POD activities in OE plants were 11.48% and 25.32% higher, respectively, than in CK (Fig. 9E, F), indicating improved ROS scavenging capacity. Additionally, electrolyte leakage—a marker of membrane damage was significantly lower in OE plants under stress, confirming better cellular integrity (Fig. 9G).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that ThGRF5 overexpression enhances salt stress tolerance by mitigating oxidative damage, boosting antioxidant defenses, and preserving membrane stability. This protective effect is underpinned by a coordinated upregulation at both transcriptional and enzymatic levels, which synergistically enhances ROS scavenging capacity (Fig. 9G; Figure S3). The consistent reduction in stress-induced damage markers (H₂O₂, MDA, and electrolyte leakage) alongside increased SOD and POD activities underscores the protective role of ThGRF5 under NaCl stress.

Discussion

GRF proteins were key regulators in plant growth, development, stress responses, and cellular signaling [32]. The GRF gene family exhibits significant evolutionary divergence, with varying numbers across species: rice (8), Arabidopsis thaliana (13), Brachypodium (7), sorghum (6), maize (28), Populus trichocarpa (14), and cotton (6) [5–7, 9, 33]. In the present study, 9 ThGRFs containing complete ORFs were identified in T. hispida (Table 1).

In T. hispida, nine ThGRFs encoding regulatory factors with distinct structures were identified. While most GRF proteins in other species localize to the nucleus [34], prediction analysis indicates that ThGRF proteins primarily reside in chloroplasts or plasma membranes. Despite this divergence in subcellular localization, the 14–3-3 domain within these proteins remains conserved (Table 1; Supplementary Figure S1; Fig. 1). Physicochemical analysis revealed that ThGRFs are acidic and stable, resembling Arabidopsis GRFs [35]. Phylogenetically, GRFs from Arabidopsis, rice, and T. hispida cluster into ε and non-ε groups, with ThGRF5 closely related to AtGRF11 and AtGRF12 (ε-group) (Fig. 1). The ε-group is ubiquitous in eukaryotes and regulates fundamental processes like PIN protein polarity and auxin-dependent growth in Arabidopsis, though their role in abiotic stress remains unexplored.

Although the exact functions of GRFs in Arabidopsis require further investigation, accumulating evidence from various species demonstrates their crucial roles in plant development and stress responses. These proteins participate in diverse physiological processes including cell elongation, seed germination, flowering time regulation, and root development [32, 36]. Notably, gibberellic acid (GA3) is a key regulator of these developmental processes, and our finding that ThGRFs respond to GA3 treatment suggests their potential involvement in GA3-mediated developmental regulation. Consistent with this notion, studies have shown that overexpression of MiSPL3a and MiSPL3b promotes early flowering and enhances stress tolerance in Arabidopsis [37], while overexpression of OsFTL10 induces early flowering and improves drought tolerance in rice [38]. Notably, both MiSPL3a/3b and OsFTL10 have been demonstrated to interact with 14–3-3 proteins and respond to GA3 induction, further supporting the potential role of 14–3-3 proteins in plant development. Functional studies reveal that GRF overexpression enhances oxidative resistance in potato by 45% and delays leaf senescence, while GRF-deficient mutants exhibit premature leaf aging [39]. Similarly, cotton GF14 overexpression improves drought tolerance and photosynthetic efficiency [40], and Arabidopsis GRF9 regulates carbon allocation to maintain root growth under water stress [41]. Notably, the conserved functions of GRFs are not limited to developmental regulation, their roles in abiotic stress responses are increasingly documented across diverse plant species.

GRF proteins have been implicated in various abiotic stress responses across plant species. They participate in salinity, drought, cold tolerance, and ABA signaling pathways in poplar, cucumber, rice, and tomato [6, 42–47]. Our study identified distinct spatial expression patterns of ThGRFs in T. hispida, with predominant leaf expression contrasting with root-predominant BpGF14s in Brachypodium [48]. Notably, most ThGRFs responded significantly to salt, PEG, ABA, and GA3 treatments, suggesting their potential roles in stress adaptation as well as developmental processes regulated by GA3 (Fig. 8). This tissue-specific and stress-responsive expression profile implies functional specialization among ThGRF members.

To elucidate ThGRF functions, we focused on ThGRF5 and demonstrated its critical role in salt stress tolerance. Transient overexpression of ThGRF5 enhanced ROS scavenging capacity, increased antioxidant enzyme activity, and reduced MDA content (Fig. 9), mirroring the stress-responsive mechanism of MdGRF11 in apple [18]. Mechanistically, we found that ThGRF5 overexpression led to a significant transcriptional upregulation of core antioxidant genes, including SOD, POD, CAT, and GPX, under salt stress (Figure S3). This provides direct molecular evidence that ThGRF5 orchestrates the antioxidant defense system at the transcriptional level, thereby enhancing ROS scavenging and reducing oxidative damage. These findings not only confirm the conserved stress-adaptive functions of GRF proteins across species but also provide direct evidence for ThGRF5's involvement in T. hispida's salt tolerance mechanisms.

The functional diversity of GRF proteins stems from their ability to form complex interaction networks. As central hubs in signaling pathways, GRFs interact with kinases, transcription factors, and structural proteins [49]. For instance, rice GF14b/c binds Hd3a to regulate flowering [50], while apple MdGRF11 degrades MdBT2 to enhance anthocyanin biosynthesis under nitrogen deficiency [51]. Our protein interaction predictions revealed strong associations among ThGRF1/2/3, suggesting their potential to form functional homo- or heterodimers. Such interaction specificity likely underlies the functional diversification observed among GRF isoforms [49]. In subsequent studies, we will further validate these predicted interactions between ThGRFs using techniques such as co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and yeast two-hybrid assays. Determining whether these interactions form homo- or heterodimers in planta will be crucial. Furthermore, we plan to investigate the functional consequences of these specific dimerizations on key signaling pathways, particularly those related to NaCl stress response, to address their role in ThGRFs functional diversification.

Conclusion

This study identified and characterized nine GRF genes in T. hispida, revealing their distinct physicochemical properties, evolutionary relationships, and stress-responsive expression patterns, particularly highlighting ThGRF4 and ThGRF5 as key players in salt stress adaptation through enhanced antioxidant activity and reduced oxidative damage. Our findings demonstrate the potential of ThGRFs, especially ThGRF5, in improving plant stress tolerance, while future research should focus on elucidating isoform-specific functions, post-translational regulation, and practical applications in developing stress-resilient crops through genetic engineering of GRF-mediated pathways.
