Effect of ultrasound pretreatment on the drying characteristics and quality of Tremella fuciformis under heat pump drying
Authors: Yuping Cao, Li Wu, Jinxuan Liu, Qing Xia, Wei Deng, Yunzhe Guo, Yibin Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Ultrasound pretreatment, Tremella fuciformis, Heat pump drying, Moisture migration, Physicochemical properties

Introduction

Ultrasonic technology has attracted significant attention in pretreatment processes as a green, non-thermal enhancement method due to mechanisms such as cavitation, sponge effect, and microchannel formation that enhance tissue permeability, promote mass transfer, and improve microstructure [1,2]. The impact of ultrasound on microwave vacuum drying of Tremella fuciformis (T. fuciformis) was evaluated in a previous study. The results showed that ultrasound pretreatment accelerated drying, and a higher content of total sugars was observed [3]. The synergistic effects between different drying temperatures and ultrasound-assisted treatment on Camellia oleifera seeds were investigated [4]. The results suggested that under conditions of 300 W and 2 min, drying duration and energy demand were lowered by 26.7% and 16.8%, respectively. Another study employed ultrasound as a pretreatment for convective drying of Agaricus bisporus, demonstrating enhanced drying rates and improved sample color [5]. Ni et al. [6] reported that ultrasound-assisted electro-osmotic drying of Lycium barbarum significantly improved drying efficiency and rehydration performance. The effects of ultrasound contact drying (SJR + US) on sweet potato quality were evaluated in comparison with hot-air drying [7]. The results demonstrated that SJR + US significantly improved rehydration capacity, shrinkage rate, color, and texture, enhanced nutrient retention, and optimized product quality. These studies indicate that ultrasound pretreatment holds great potential for improving drying efficiency and enhancing product quality. Fig 1.

Experimental procedure diagram for ultrasonic-assisted heat pump drying of T. fuciformis.

T. fuciformis is an edible fungus with both nutritional and medicinal value, rich in polysaccharides, proteins, dietary fiber, and various bioactive compounds, exhibiting immunomodulatory, antioxidant, and antitumor activities [8]. However, fresh T. fuciformis contains over 80% moisture, making it highly susceptible to microbial contamination and enzymatic reactions, which result in poor storage stability and a short shelf life [9]. Drying can alleviate the occurrence of the aforementioned issues [10]. Among various drying technologies, the heat pump drying (HPD) has gained widespread adoption in industrial applications of many thermosensitive foods due to its advantages of low temperature, energy efficiency, and operational safety [11,12]. The high moisture content of T. fuciformis presents significant technical challenges during the HPD. On one hand, the dense tissue and low internal diffusion coefficient hinder efficient moisture migration, thereby prolonging drying time [13]; on the other hand, extended drying durations tend to induce browning reactions and nutrient degradation, including polysaccharide breakdown, protein denaturation, and color deterioration, which severely impair the sensory quality and nutritional value of the dried product [14]. Studies have shown that products obtained by solely employing the HPD exhibit significant deficiencies in quality retention and fail to meet the demands of high-quality processing [15].

Therefore, this study introduced ultrasound pretreatment as a pretreatment method for T. fuciformis before the HPD, aiming to improve drying efficiency while enhancing product quality. A systematic evaluation was conducted to investigate the effects of ultrasound pretreatment at varying acoustic energy densities on accelerating drying rate, regulating moisture migration and distribution, and improving microstructure. Concurrently, key quality parameters of dried T. fuciformis, including color, rehydration rate, soluble protein content, and crude polysaccharide content, were measured to comprehensively analyze changes in the drying characteristics. The results are expected to elucidate the underlying mechanisms by which ultrasound pretreatment optimizes the drying process of T. fuciformis, thereby providing a theoretical foundation and technical support for efficient and energy-saving drying processing.

Materials and Methods

Raw materials

Fresh T. fuciformis was purchased from Fuzhou City. The raw materials are packaged in self-sealing bags and stored at 4 °C to prevent moisture loss and preserve quality. Samples weighing 135 ± 5 g were selected for the experiments. Before ultrasound pretreatment, a cross-shaped incision was made at the bottom of each sample.

Ultrasound pretreatment

Ultrasound pretreatment of T. fuciformis was conducted using an ultrasonic cleaner (Supmile KQ-600DV, China). Fresh T. fuciformis samples were placed in a cleaning basket with a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:28 g/mL. Four ultrasound intensity levels were applied: 0.20 W/g (240 W), 0.34 W/g (360 W), 0.54 W/g (480 W), and 0.74 W/g (600 W). The ultrasound pretreatment temperature was 25 °C, with a pretreatment duration of 20 min for all groups. The control (CK) group did not receive ultrasound pretreatment, while all other conditions were kept the same. After pretreatment, all samples were subjected to the HPD experiments.

Heat pump drying

The pretreated T. fuciformis samples were placed on trays in a heat pump dryer (Zhengxu ZX-DKFXRF-6I (GWH), China) with a loading density of 2.44 kg/m2. Drying temperature was set at 60 ± 1 °C. Samples were taken and weighed every 45 min. The drying treatment was considered complete when the moisture content of T. fuciformis reached ≤ 15% (wet basis).

Moisture migration

An LF-NMR analyzer (Niumag NMI20-040H-I, China) was used to investigate the moisture migration characteristics of T. fuciformis samples [18]. A fan-shaped sample (radius: 4.5 cm; central angle: 37°; thickness: 1.4 cm) was cut from the treated T. fuciformis to include both the basal and lobe regions. The sample was placed in an NMR tube and positioned in the test zone of the instrument. The Carr–Purcell–Meiboom–Gill (CPMG) sequence was applied with the following parameters: operating frequency 20 MHz, offset frequency 625814.55 Hz, RF delay 0.02 ms, 90° pulse width 6.80 μs, 180° pulse width 12.00 μs, delay time 6000 ms, echo time (TE) 0.4 ms, and 12,000 echoes. The resulting CPMG signals were fitted using the Simultaneous Iterative Reconstruction Technique (SIRT) algorithm with 1,000,000 iterations to obtain the transverse relaxation time (T2) distribution, which reflects the migration behavior of different water states.

After LF-NMR testing, the T. fuciformis samples were scanned using a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) system to obtain moisture distribution images. The imaging was performed using a spin echo (SE) sequence. Proton density-weighted images were obtained to visually assess internal moisture distribution.

Microstructure analysis

The dried T. fuciformis was cut into approximately 2 mm × 2 mm pieces and immediately subjected to metal coating (gold sputtering) to improve conductivity before scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Zeiss Sigma 300, China) analysis [21]. The samples were then mounted on the SEM stage and scanned at a magnification of 2000 × to investigate changes in cell structure, tissue compactness, and pore distribution under different pretreatment conditions.

Data processing and analysis

All experimental results were expressed as the mean of three parallel replicates. All statistical analyses and graphical plots were generated using GraphPad Prism 10.1.2. Different lowercase letters indicate statistically significant differences among groups (p <0.05).

Results and analysis

Drying characteristics

As shown in the Fig. 2(a). The results demonstrate that ultrasound pretreatment accelerated moisture removal throughout the drying process. As the ultrasound intensity increased, the total drying time gradually decreased. When the ultrasound intensity increased to 0.34 W/g, the highest drying efficiency was achieved, with the total drying time significantly reduced from 945 min in the CK group to 855 min (p <0.05), representing a 9.52% decrease. Ultrasound pretreatment significantly promoted the drying process. Similar phenomena have been reported in the far-infrared drying of licorice [22], microwave vacuum drying of button mushrooms [23], and vacuum freeze-drying of strawberry slices [24]. This effect may be attributed to cavitation induced by ultrasound pretreatment, which enlarges intercellular spaces and loosens tissue structure, thereby enhancing moisture diffusion [25]. However, when the ultrasound intensity was further increased to 0.54 W/g or 0.74 W/g, no additional enhancement effect was observed. It was possibly due to excessive tissue damage of T. fuciformis caused by high-power ultrasound, leading to structural collapse and channel blockage, which in turn restricts effective moisture diffusion [26].

The drying curve (a) and drying rate curve (b) of T. fuciformis at various ultrasound pretreatment intensities.

The drying rate curve (the Fig. 2(b)) revealed that T. fuciformis primarily underwent a falling-rate drying period during the HPD. This phenomenon is closely related to the structure and water distribution characteristics of T. fuciformis: T. fuciformis is rich in polysaccharide gum components with strong water holding capacity, which can easily form a dense three-dimensional network structure with high initial water content (large proportion of free water and semi-bound water), severely limiting the diffusion of water in the early stage of drying [3]. During drying, continuous tissue shrinkage gradually limited the water migration pathways, particularly increasing the diffusion resistance of bound water, thereby causing a continuous decrease in the overall drying rate. However, ultrasound pretreatment broke through this limitation mainly through the cavitation effect, which destroyed the dense cell wall structure of T. fuciformis, formed microcracks and porous channels in the tissue, effectively increasing porosity and tissue permeability, thereby significantly improving the water migration rate of T. fuciformis during the drying process [27]. Relative to the CK group, the average drying rates increased by 15.42%, 17.95%, 14.92%, and 4.25% in the 0.20 W/g, 0.34 W/g, 0.54 W/g, and 0.74 W/g ultrasound-treated groups, respectively.

LF-NMR

The Fig. 3 presents the T2 relaxation distribution curves of T. fuciformis. The different peaks correspond to protons in different states, and the peak areas reflect the relative content of each proton population. The transverse relaxation time (T2) reflects the binding degree between water and tissue: shorter T2 values represent more tightly bound water, while longer values indicate weaker binding. Based on intracellular moisture mobility, water in T. fuciformis tissues can be categorized into three types: bound water (T21, < 10 ms), primarily associated with cell wall polysaccharides; semi-bound water (T22, 10 ∼ 100 ms), mainly interacting with macromolecules such as proteins in the cytoplasm; and free water (T23, > 100 ms), predominantly located in vacuoles and intercellular spaces with high mobility [28,29]. The relative contents of these water types are denoted by peak areas A21, A22, and A23, respectively.

The T2 relaxation spectra of T. fuciformis during heat pump drying under different ultrasound pretreatment intensities. T2 relaxation spectra at 0 min of drying for different pretreatment groups (a); untreated group (b); 0.20 W/g ultrasound pretreatment (c); 0.34 W/g ultrasound pretreatment (d); 0.54 W/g ultrasound pretreatment (e); 0.74 W/g ultrasound pretreatment (f).

The Fig. 3(a) presents the T2 relaxation spectra at the onset of drying (0 min) for the different pretreatment groups. As shown in the Figure, the T2 relaxation distribution curves in the ultrasonically pretreated samples shifted noticeably to the right along the x-axis, indicating an increased T2 relaxation time and weakened water-tissue binding in the T. fuciformis. In addition, the T22 showed a trend of transformation towards T23, indicating a blurred boundary between them and a significant increase in the degree of freedom of moisture. These changes may be due to microstructural changes caused by ultrasound pretreatment, such as cell wall rupture, pore formation, and expansion of intercellular channels, which weaken the binding force between high degree of freedom water (T22 and T23) and nutrients [30]. This reduction in binding strength facilitated smoother water migration during the subsequent HPD, thereby improving drying efficiency and offering greater potential for quality control.

The Fig. 3(b ∼ f) shows the changes in water states of T. fuciformis during the HPD process. As drying progressed, the T2 relaxation peaks of all groups gradually shifted toward shorter relaxation times, accompanied by a marked reduction in peak areas, indicating continuous moisture loss from the tissue of T. fuciformis and a transition of the remaining water to more tightly bound states. At 180 min of drying, the T23 (free water) and T22 (semi-bound water) peaks showed a tendency to merge, suggesting that during the drying process, the moisture in the tissue matrix will dynamically redistribute and recombine [23]. At the final drying stage (720 min), the 0.34 W/g ultrasound pretreatment group exhibited the smallest T2 peak area and weakest proton signal, indicating minimal residual moisture and optimal drying performance. This implies that moderate ultrasound pretreatment, by enhancing tissue porosity and reducing water diffusion resistance, effectively facilitates water migration and evaporation, thereby markedly improving drying efficiency. In the studies on Hami melon [31], apricot [32], and Cantharellus cibarius [18], a similar phenomenon was observed, in which ultrasound pretreatment enhanced water fluidity and improved drying efficiency. These findings are consistent with the drying behavior discussed in Section 3.1, further confirming the potential of ultrasound as an efficient pretreatment method for improving the drying quality of T. fuciformis.

MRI

MRI can visually depict the spatial distribution of moisture within the sample [33]. The proton density of H+ nuclei indicates moisture content: red regions in the image represent areas with higher moisture content and stronger signal intensity, whereas blue regions indicate lower moisture content and weaker signals [34]. The results showed that the structural changes caused by ultrasound pretreatment could not only increase the degree of freedom of water (mentioned in section 3.2), but also improve the uniformity of water spatial distribution, creating favorable conditions for improving drying efficiency [35]. The proton density-weighted images of T. fuciformis during the HPD under different ultrasound pretreatments showed that there were distinct regional differences in moisture distribution, with the T. fuciformis stem generally containing more water than its leaf (the Fig. 4). Previous studies have shown that ultrasound pretreatment can promote the redistribution of moisture within materials [31], which may influence the moisture distribution characteristics of different tissue regions. At the initial drying (0 h), the red areas in the images of ultrasound-treated samples were more evenly distributed, indicating a more uniform overall moisture distribution. As drying progresses, the colored regions gradually shrink from the outside toward the inside, indicating that moisture decreases first in the leaf tissue, followed by a reduction in stem moisture. The moisture continues to decline until the color patches completely disappear and merge with the background color. At the drying time of 720 min, the colored area of the ultrasound-treated sample was smaller, indicating that its water evaporation rate was faster during the drying process, which was consistent with the T2 relaxation curve changes during the sample drying process.

Proton density-weighted images of T. fuciformis during the heat pump drying with different ultrasound pretreatment intensities.

Microstructure

Ultrasound pretreatment exerted a significant impact on the microstructure of T. fuciformis after the HPD. As shown in the figure, the CK sample (a) exhibited a compact structure with a smooth and intact surface. In contrast, the ultrasound-treated samples exhibited varying degrees of cell rupture, tissue shrinkage, and the formation of microscopic pores. The potential mechanism of changes in the microstructure of T. fuciformis caused by ultrasound pretreatment mainly involves the “cavitation effect” and “sponge effect” generated when ultrasonic waves propagate in the medium [26]. Among them, the generation, expansion and violent rupture of tiny bubbles caused by the propagation of ultrasonic waves in liquid, accompanied by the instantaneous local cavitation effect of high temperature, high pressure and strong shear force, and the sponge effect of repeated “compression relaxation” on the alternate compression and expansion of T. fuciformis by ultrasonic waves directly promote the formation of micro-cracks, pores and structural fractures on the surface and inside of T. fuciformis. The Fig. 5 shows that the ultrasound intensities of 0.34 ∼ 0.54 W/g are beneficial to the formation of more broken and porous structural features. The improvement of fluffy and porous microstructure by ultrasound waves was also reported in the study of licorice vacuum drying [36], strawberries freeze-drying [24], and potato slices drying characteristics [37]. However, at an ultrasound intensity of 0.74 W/g, the tissue exhibited significant shrinkage. This may be attributed to local heat accumulation induced by high-intensity ultrasound, which damages the cell walls and gel-like structures, reduces structural support, and consequently accelerates moisture loss and localized tissue hardening, ultimately resulting in marked volumetric shrinkage [38,39].

The microstructure of dried T. fuciformis under different ultrasound pretreatment intensities. Untreated group (a); 0.20 W/g ultrasound pretreatment (b); 0.34 W/g ultrasound pretreatment (c); 0.54 W/g ultrasound pretreatment (d); 0.74 W/g ultrasound pretreatment (e).

Rehydration rate

The rehydration rate is an indicator used to evaluate the ability of dry products to recover to their original state after absorbing water [40], and it reflects the effects of drying processes on the structural integrity of the product [41].

The Fig. 6 illustrates the rehydration rate of dried T. fuciformis treated with ultrasound pretreatment of different intensities. Overall, ultrasound pretreatment enhanced the rehydration performance of dried T. fuciformis. Compared with the rehydration rate of the CK group, that of samples pretreated with ultrasound intensities of 0.20 ∼ 0.54 W/g increased significantly (p <0.05), whereas at an ultrasound intensity of 0.74 W/g, the rehydration rate increased slightly but showed no difference (p >0.05). This may be attributed to the fact that moderate ultrasound pretreatment is beneficial for the formation of porous structures in T. fuciformis (see Section 3.4), thereby providing a more favorable pathway for water penetration and adsorption during the rehydration process of T. fuciformis tissue, thereby further improving the rehydration rate of the dried product [[42], [43], [44]]. However, excessive ultrasound intensity may lead to partial collapse of cell walls, which hinders the rehydration of T. fuciformis dried products [45,46]. Sun et al. [18] also observed a similar phenomenon, in which excessive ultrasound pretreatment weakened the material's rehydration performance.

Chromatism

The chromatism is a key sensory parameter that shapes consumers’ purchase intention and acceptance [47]. The Fig. 7 presents the color parameters of dried T. fuciformis at various ultrasound intensities, including lightness (L*), red-green value (a*), yellow-blue value (b*), and the color difference (ΔE) derived from these parameters.

The color parameters of T. fuciformis under different ultrasound pretreatment intensities (b).

The color parameters of fresh T. fuciformis were L0 = 40.49 ± 2.15, a0 = -0.61 ± 0.20, and b0 = 1.29 ± 0.17, respectively. Compared with fresh samples, all dried T. fuciformis samples showed an increase in brightness (L*) values, indicating stronger visual brightness. This change is mainly due to the continuous loss of moisture in T. fuciformis during the drying process, which causes the cell gaps of the T. fuciformis fruiting body to shrink, the tissue structure to become denser, and the surface to become smooth and flat, thereby enhancing its ability to reflect visible light [48]. However, the difference in L* values between the ultrasound pretreatment group and the CK group was not significant (p <0.05), indicating that the effect of ultrasound pretreatment on brightness is relatively limited.

From the color difference parameters a* and b* values, compared to the fresh T. fuciformis, all dried samples showed varying degrees of increase, indicating that browning reaction inevitably occurred during the drying process, which is one of the common color deterioration phenomena under heat treatment conditions [49]. However, compared with the untreated group, ultrasound pretreatment significantly suppressed the increase in a* and b* values, indicating that it played a certain protective role in slowing down color changes. This phenomenon may be mainly because ultrasound accelerates water migration, shortens the drying time, and reduces the exposure time of the sample at high temperature. On the one hand, it inhibits the non-enzymatic Maillard reaction. On the other hand, the browning reaction catalyzed by polyphenol oxidase was inhibited. Previous studies have demonstrated that during the convective drying of Stropharia rugosoannulata slices, ultrasound pretreatment can effectively inhibit the Maillard reaction, thereby improving the color of the samples [50]. Furthermore, Barani et al. reported that ultrasound pretreatment reduced the activities of polyphenol oxidase (PPO) and peroxidase (POD) in roses, thus enhancing the color quality of dried rose flowers [51].

Although ultrasound showed certain advantages in a* and b*, no significant difference in color difference (ΔE) was found between ultrasound-treated and untreated groups (p <0.05), indicating a limited effect of ultrasound pretreatment on the overall color of T. fuciformis under the HPD.

Nutritional quality

As shown in the Fig. 8(a), Ultrasound pretreatment significantly influenced the soluble protein content in T. fuciformis (p <0.05). The results showed that ultrasound pretreatment reduced the soluble protein content in T. fuciformis, with protein loss becoming more pronounced as ultrasound intensity increased. At an ultrasound intensity of 0.74 W/g, the soluble protein content markedly decreased to 4.21 ± 0.06 mg/g (p <0.05), representing a 10.42% reduction compared to the CK group (4.70 ± 0.03 mg/g). This may be due to the fact that the soluble proteins are water-soluble compounds and are prone to migrate from T. fuciformis cells to the external aqueous phase during ultrasound pretreatment. The cavitation bubbles generated by ultrasound propagation in the liquid phase release shock waves when collapsing, accompanied by instantaneous local high temperature and high pressure, which can weaken the cell barrier function, broaden the diffusion path of intracellular substances, and accelerate the outward release of proteins [49]. In addition, ultrasound induced microjets (acoustic streaming) can continuously renew the interface layer between T. fuciformis tissue and the external medium, reduce diffusion resistance, and further promote the transfer of the soluble proteins into the ultrasound medium [52]. With the gradual increase in ultrasound intensity, physical disturbance is enhanced, the mass transfer process is further accelerated, and the degree of soluble protein loss also increases accordingly.

The soluble protein content (a) and the polysaccharide content (b) of dried T. fuciformis under different ultrasound pretreatment intensities.

As shown in the Fig. 8(b), ultrasound pretreatment significantly affected the polysaccharide content of T. fuciformis, yielding values higher than those of the CK group (21.67 ± 0.26%). With the increase in ultrasound intensity, the polysaccharide content initially increased and then decreased, reaching the highest value (24.80 ± 0.25%) at 0.54 W/g, which was 14.44% higher than the CK group. This improvement is mainly attributed to the fact that moderate ultrasonic treatment shortens the duration of high-temperature exposure during the HPD process (see section 3.1 for details), thereby inhibiting the occurrence of the Maillard reaction and reducing its thermal degradation effect on the polysaccharides [14,53,54] In addition, ultrasound pretreatment promotes the release of the polysaccharides from T. fuciformis cell tissues and enhances the detectability of the polysaccharides in dried samples [55]. However, when the ultrasound intensity increased to 0.74 W/g, the polysaccharide content decreased, possibly due to the excessive ultrasound pretreatment prolonging the drying process, thereby exacerbating the thermal degradation of T. fuciformis polysaccharides during drying, resulting in a decrease in their content [56]. Similar trends have been reported in studies on the drying process of licorice tablets [36] and Codonopsis pilosula [57], where the polysaccharide content also showed a “rise–fall” pattern with increasing ultrasound power.

Conclusion

The study systematically investigated the effects of different ultrasound pretreatment intensities on the HPD characteristics and quality of T. fuciformis. The results showed that ultrasound pretreatment significantly shortened the drying time, with the 0.34 W/g group having the shortest drying time of 855 min, a 9.52% reduction compared to the CK group (945 min). Results from LF-NMR relaxation spectra and MRI pseudo-color images indicated that ultrasound pretreatment enhanced the internal moisture mobility of T. fuciformis, improving water diffusion efficiency. SEM observations revealed that cells in untreated samples were densely arranged with intact structures, whereas ultrasound-treated samples exhibited varying degrees of cell wall rupture and collapse, resulting in the formation of additional microchannels favorable for moisture transfer. Additionally, the rehydration rate of pretreatment groups was superior to that of the untreated group, with the highest rehydration rate reaching 1220.50 ± 24.75% (0.34 W/g), a 7.25% increase over the CK group (1138.00 ± 1.41%). The polysaccharide content peaked at 0.54 W/g, increasing by 14.44% compared to the CK group, though soluble protein content decreased. Color difference changes were minimal, indicating stable product coloration. Comprehensive evaluation suggests that an ultrasound intensity of 0.34 ∼ 0.54 W/g is the optimal pretreatment range for the HPD of T. fuciformis.

In summary, moderate ultrasound pretreatment not only significantly enhances the drying efficiency of T. fuciformis but also improves its dried product quality, providing theoretical support and technical guidance for the optimization of drying processes and quality preservation of fresh T. fuciformis.
