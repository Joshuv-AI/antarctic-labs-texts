Evaluation of the Sealing Ability and Penetration Depth of the TheraCal LC as a Sealant for Pit and Fissures: An In Vitro Study
Authors: Rajendra R Eddula, Sanju Choudhary, Kiranmayi Merum, Srikanth Saraswati
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimTo compare and evaluate the sealing ability and depth of penetration for resin-modified light-cured calcium silicate, that is, TheraCal LC, used as a pit-and-fissure sealant, to that of GC Fuji VII glass ionomer-based sealant.Materials and methodsA probability sampling of 28 freshly extracted maxillary and mandibular premolar teeth was chosen and divided into two groups, each containing 14 teeth, and the study was conducted using the following materials in the respective groups: group I—TheraCal LC and group II—GC Fuji VII. Firstly, the samples were subjected to thermocycling up to 500 cycles at 5 and 55°C. Then the apices of the teeth were sealed with acrylic resin, followed by two coats of nail polish, and the samples were immersed in 5 % methylene blue dye for 24 hours. Longitudinal sections were obtained from each tooth for evaluating the sealing ability and penetration depth using a stereomicroscope at 2.5× magnification. The sectioned samples' sealing ability was evaluated based on Ovrebo and Raadal's criteria.ResultsThe procured data were analyzed using the Chi-squared test and independent t-test. The comparability between the two groups for sealing ability and penetration depth showed a statistically insignificant difference (p = 0.13, p = 0.49, respectively).ConclusionTheraCal LC as a pit-and-fissure sealant demonstrates favorable results concerning both sealing ability and penetration depth.How to cite this articleEddula RR, Choudhary S, Merum K, et al. Introduction

Oro-dental infection, that is, caries, can have detrimental effects on the physical and psychological well-being of an individual. It occurs in both primary and permanent teeth and is caused by changes in the bacterial biofilm, disrupting the harmony between tooth demineralization and remineralization. The erupting permanent molars' occlusal aspects affected with dental caries are remarkably noted in children and younger age-groups.1 The caries in pit and fissures comprise around 44% in primary dentition and around 80–90% in the permanent dentition2 due to their complex morphology, leading to increased plaque accumulation and making dental hygiene more challenging.

Caries in pit and fissures can be prevented by applying sealants. Conventionally, sealants such as glass ionomers and resin-based are used to seal these pit and fissures. Glass ionomer-based sealants offer beneficial fluoride-releasing properties and are easy to apply; however, their main disadvantage is the material's tendency to fracture, particularly due to its reduced ability to withstand occlusal forces,3 which leads to secondary caries or recurrent caries around the sealant. To counter these, many bioactive dental materials have been merged into the pit and fissure sealants to prevent recurrent caries.4–6

By using calcium silicates or calcium aluminates, predominantly known as calcium silicate-based bioactive materials, which are capable of forming apatites, thereby maintaining an alkaline pH. TheraCal LC, a new light-cured formulation used for pulp capping, was quoted in a study in 2011.7 Despite its good sealing capabilities, simple handling characteristics, and improved flowability, it was never evaluated as a sealant in pit and fissures. These properties led us to assess its potential as a sealant in pit and fissures.

Key factors to consider while applying pit and fissures are their tooth surface retention and penetration depth, as these properties directly influence the sealant's longevity and effectiveness. Hence, this present study was piloted to assess the sealing ability and depth of penetration of TheraCal LC used as a sealant in pit and fissures.

Materials and Methods

Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC number: KIDS/IEC/FACULTY/PEDO/2024/07) of a well-known recognized institution, that is, Kamineni Institute of Dental Sciences, had accepted this present in vitro intergroup comparative study. As this is a novel pilot study, all the parameters were tested using 14 samples per group.

Preparation of Samples

A sample of 28 noncarious intact premolar teeth (both maxillary and mandibular quadrants), extracted for orthodontic treatment, were used in this study. Teeth that were cracked, fractured, attrited, filled, or already sealed were excluded from the study. The selected teeth were first disinfected with hydrogen peroxide, cleaned of gross debris with pumice slurry using brushes with a slow-speed handpiece, then the teeth were rinsed with air–water spray, and a sharp explorer tip was passed through all the pits and fissures to remove the remaining pumice. The specimens were then randomly divided into two groups, each comprising 14 teeth: group I—TheraCal LC and group II—GC Fuji VII.

Group I—TheraCal LC (Bisco Inc., United States)

After removing excess moisture from each tooth, 37% phosphoric acid was used for etching the occlusal surfaces of the teeth. After 20 seconds, they were cleaned with water for about 30 seconds and air-dried. TheraCal LC was applied with a needle-tip syringe in the pits and fissures, and excess material was removed with an applicator tip. Then it was cured for about 20 seconds with a light-curing unit.

Group II—GIC (GC Fuji VII, Japan)

On the occlusal surfaces of the sample teeth, GC cavity conditioner was applied using a brush applicator for about 10 seconds and then air-dried. According to the manufacturer's instructions, glass ionomer cement was mixed and applied to the occlusal surfaces of the teeth. A disposable fine brush was used to evenly spread it over the pits and fissures. Petroleum jelly was applied to the sealed surfaces once the cement was set.

After the sealant application, the sample teeth were subjected to 500 thermal cycles of alternating temperatures between 5 and 55°C. The apex of the sample teeth was sealed with acrylic resin, and two layers of nail paint were applied, leaving 1 mm of the sealant margin. The teeth were then soaked in a 1% methylene blue dye solution at pH 7 for about 24 hours. Later, excess dye was washed under running water. From the central fossa, the teeth were sectioned longitudinally in the buccolingual direction with a saw-mounted diamond disk at low speed under water coolant.

Analysis of Data

Data was analyzed statistically using SPSS version 26.0 (Chicago, IL), and the significance level was standardized at p < 0.05. To assess the mean value and standard deviation (SD) of the respective groups, descriptive statistics were performed. Categorical variables were assessed using the Chi-squared test, and group analysis was done using the independent t-test.

Discussion

Erupting teeth that are in the maturation stage, anatomic structures that are difficult to cleanse, and further deficient maturation of the enamel were prone to high caries sensitivity.10 As a preventive strategy, over a period of time, these sealants have evolved to arrest the carious lesion in these pits and fissures.

Depending upon the oral environment, the anatomy of the fissures, the chemical and mechanical properties of the materials, and the forces generated during mastication greatly influence the integrity of the tooth and sealant interface.11 According to American Academy of Pediatric Dentistry (AAPD), the placement of sealants should usually be accomplished as soon as possible following the eruption of the tooth.12 Isolation is a key challenge when applying sealants to partially erupted teeth. To overcome this isolation problem, glass ionomer cements have been recommended as sealants.

In this study, pit and fissure sealants are GC Fuji VII and TheraCal LC. Glass ionomer pit and fissure sealants have several advantages, including antibacterial properties, high fluoride release, better handling, improved adherence to enamel, and a free-flowing consistency. Antonson et al. also stated that under wet contamination conditions, glass ionomer sealants showed better results than resin-based sealants, suggesting that the choice of material may be particularly important when treating pediatric patients.13 Fuji VII, a low-viscosity, pink-colored glass ionomer cement, has been shown to release significantly more fluoride than previous glass ionomer materials, making it an effective option for long-lasting fluoride protection.14,15

In a study conducted by Yang et al., hydrated calcium silicate incorporated into a resin-based sealant material was evaluated. It demonstrated high calcium ion release, excellent acid neutralization, and long-lasting effects. These properties help prevent secondary caries caused by microleakage, polymerization shrinkage, and gradual material loss over time.16 Interestingly, TheraCal LC is available in the market and has similar properties to those of hydrated calcium silicate. It is available in syringe form, having good sealing ability and flowability. Given these advantages, we sought to evaluate TheraCal LC as a pit and fissure sealant by comparing its performance in microleakage and penetration depth with a glass ionomer-based sealant.

TheraCal LC is a fourth-generation class II cement material in which the polymerizable component undergoes a light-activated setting reaction and is known for its high calcium release capability, according to ISO 9917-2017, part 2, clause 4.17 It is primarily used either as a protective liner or as a pulp capping agent in direct restorations. A research study conducted by Chaudhari et al. and Makkar et al. revealed that TheraCal LC is a better Ca-ion releasing material and reduces interfacial microleakage compared to light-cured calcium hydroxide,18 MTA, and Biodentine.19

The ideal requisite of a material is to obtain a good seal when used as a pit and fissure sealant. Hence, microleakage is taken into consideration when TheraCal LC is used. In our study, microleakage was assessed with methylene blue, which is not only an inexpensive, reliable method of testing microleakage,20 but also an easily accomplished test. In this study, all specimens in both the sample and control groups exhibited dye penetration to some extent. Theodoridou-Pahini et al. and do Rego and de Araujo stated that all restorative materials show microleakage, and similar results were seen in this study. The poor performance of GIC sealants could be due to the disintegration of the material in water and solubility when subjected to thermocycling.21,22

Sealants should have a good flowing ability to penetrate deep into pit and fissures, thereby forming an effective mechanical barrier.3 Penetration ability of a sealant depends on multiple factors such as properties of materials and morphology of fissures. Thus, penetration depth was taken into consideration for this study, and TheraCal LC showed a good penetration depth when compared to glass ionomer sealant; however, results were insignificant.

Within the limitations, this study accepted the set null hypothesis. This in vitro study was done to evaluate only two important properties of sealant, that is, sealing ability and depth of penetration in controlled conditions using TheraCal LC. As this was a pilot study, further research with a large sample size and long-term follow-up would provide more comprehensive data on the material's long-term performance and whether resin-modified light-cured calcium silicate can be applied as a sealant in pit and fissures.

Conclusion

In conclusion, light-cured resin-modified calcium silicate demonstrates promising results as a pit and fissure sealant in terms of both microleakage and penetration depth.

The preliminary findings of this in vitro study suggest that it could be an effective measure for prevention of pit and fissure caries. However, further investigation, including clinical trials and studies on wear resistance, is required to fully establish its long-term efficacy and clinical applicability.
