Evaluation of the attributable fraction and burden of HPV-related oropharyngeal cancers in Greece—the ORPHEAS study
Authors: A Psyrri, G Psychogios, E Kyrodimos, J Constantinidis, S Agelaki, I Boukovinas, S Lygeros, K Ploiarchopoulou, A Spathis, P Economopoulou, E Litsou, I Dimitriadis, C Athanasopoulos, S Zioga, G Trimis, L Poughias, I Panayiotides
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundHerein, we evaluated the attributable fraction (AF) of human papillomavirus (HPV)-mediated (HPV+) oropharyngeal carcinomas (OPCs) in Greece over a recent calendar period.Patients and methodsORPHEAS, a retrospective, observational, multicenter, cross-sectional study with prospective recruitment, included adult patients with OPC in 2017-2022, each of them with a high-quality, treatment-naïve tumor specimen. The primary endpoint was the HPV-AF, defined as combined positivity for p16INK4a (p16) overexpression and HPV DNA presence by central laboratory testing, among included patients. Other endpoints evaluated the HPV+/HPV− patient/disease characteristics at OPC diagnosis and the HPV subtypes for HPV+ patients.Results144/147 patients with available HPV status by central laboratory testing were analyzed [median age: 60.0 years; males: 111 (77.1%)]. The most common tumor anatomical sites were the tonsils (70/147, 48.6%) and the base of the tongue (51, 35.4%), and most patients were at the American Joint Committee on Cancer eighth edition TNM (tumor–node–metastasis) stages III (25, 22.7%) and IV (43, 39.1%). The HPV-AF was 52.1% (75/144; 95% confidence interval 43.6% to 60.5%). Most HPV+ patients were infected by an HPV type targeted by the 9-valent HPV vaccine (72/75, 96.0%), especially HPV16 (70/75, 93.3%). HPV+ compared with HPV− patients were younger (median age 58.0 versus 64.0 years; P = 0.003); more likely to have tumors in the tonsils (65.0% versus 30.4%; P < 0.001); less likely to have tumors in the base of the tongue (25.3% versus 46.4%; P = 0.008); and less frequently at TNM stage IV (20.4% versus 57.1%; overall P < 0.001).ConclusionsIn Greece, we observed a high HPV-AF (52.1%) in OPC, approximating the AFs reported for some Northern European countries. HPV+ versus HPV− patients were younger, more frequently with tonsillar tumors, and less frequently at TNM stage IV. Since most patients were infected by ≥1 HPV type targeted by the 9-valent vaccine, the HPV+ OPC burden could be mitigated through a routine HPV gender-neutral vaccination program.Key words: human papillomavirus, oropharyngeal cancer, attributable fraction, patient profile, Greece

Introduction

Oropharyngeal carcinomas (OPCs), the majority of which (90%) are oropharyngeal squamous-cell carcinomas (OPSCC),1 comprise tumors that are most commonly located in the tonsil and the base of the tongue than in other pharyngeal subsites.2,3 While environmental factors (e.g. increased tobacco use and alcohol consumption) have long been established as risk factors for OPC,4 an increasing number of OPCs is driven by human papillomavirus (HPV) infection, especially HPV type 16.5, 6, 7

HPV-mediated (HPV+) OPCs are currently considered a distinct disease component from non-HPV-mediated (HPV−) OPCs (NCCN 2022), and this is reflected in the eighth edition of the tumor–node–metastasis classification (hereafter referred to as TNM8) issued by the Union for International Cancer Control and the American Joint Committee on Cancer.8 Patients with HPV+ OPC are likely to be white men of younger age, not consuming alcohol or tobacco, with better performance status, and with fewer co-existing comorbidities than patients with HPV− OPC4,6,9; however, recent US studies report increasing incidence or prevalence in men of older age, white women, and men of other ethnicities (Rettig et al., 2018; Tota et al., 2019).10,11 Importantly, patients with HPV+ OPC have better responses to chemotherapy and radiotherapy and a clear survival benefit compared with patients with HPV− OPC, the median overall survival being 15.0 years for HPV+ OPC cases and 3.5 years for HPV− OPC cases.3,6

Thus, testing for HPV has become an essential part for the work-up in patients with OPC during the past decade. The HPV testing options include the expression of protein p16INK4a (hereafter referred to as p16) by immunohistochemistry (IHC), and HPV detection by PCR or in situ hybridization.12 Currently, the College of American Pathologists and the American Society of Clinical Oncology recommend that HPV tumor status testing in OPSCC should be carried out by p16 IHC, which is a widely available and cost-effective assay,13 with additional confirmatory testing done at the discretion of the pathologist and/or treating clinician.14,15 Available evidence suggests that p16 IHC followed by HPV DNA detection in p16-positive cases is the recommended diagnostic algorithm in OPC,16 since discordant cases (p16−/HPV+ or p16+/HPV−) had a significantly worse prognosis than patients with p16+/HPV+ OPC, and a significantly better prognosis than patients with p16−/HPV− OPC in the study by Mehanna et al.3

The frequency of HPV+ OPSCCs among all OPSCC cases [the HPV-attributable fraction (HPV-AF)] globally is currently estimated at 33%, although significant variations ranging from 0% to 85% have been reported across countries.17 In Greece, robust epidemiological data on the HPV-AF in OPC are lacking, as few studies have been conducted, which are limited by small patient samples or by restricted geographical coverage18,19; additionally, a pertinent national registry does not exist. Thus, the current study sought primarily to identify the HPV-AF in OPC in a representative sample of patients with OPC attending reference centers for their disease. Moreover, the study examined the distribution of HPV types among patients with HPV+ OPC, the patient characteristics at OPC diagnosis by HPV status, and the physician’s choice of treatment strategy. Overall, we anticipated that the study data could contribute to the estimation of potentially preventable OPC cases in Greece, thus informing the health care providers on the future steps that could be required for the management of OPC.

Patients and methods

Study design and patients

ORPHEAS was an observational, cross-sectional, single-visit study with prospective recruitment and retrospective data collection conducted in the oncology and/or ear–nose–throat (ENT) departments of eight reference hospitals in Greece. The study included patients who were diagnosed with OPC between quarter(Q)1 2017 and Q4 2022 (study period; Supplementary Figure S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103724). Patient enrollment occurred during the last 12 months of the study period (i.e. from Q4 2021 to Q4 2022). Each patient’s date of OPC diagnosis could have occurred from Q1 2017 to Q4 2022.

Data extracted from the patients’ medical records included demographic information (e.g. age, gender, weight, smoking habits, alcohol consumption) at OPC diagnosis; OPC staging at diagnosis by TNM8; OPC tumor classification as HPV+ or HPV−; HPV type for patients with HPV+ OPC; and documentation of the OPC treatment strategy, as decided by the treating physician at diagnosis. Patients were eligible for study enrollment if they were aged 18 years or older, had been diagnosed with OPC during the study period, and each had a high-quality biopsy or resection tumor sample that was obtained as part of regular clinical practice before the patient received any OPC treatment; samples with sufficient number of cancer cells for p16 IHC and HPV PCR testing were considered evaluable for HPV status assessment. Patients were excluded if they had a diagnosis of head and neck squamous-cell carcinoma in sites other than the oropharynx or had insufficient tissue for p16/HPV interpretation.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki20 and the applicable laws and regulations in Greece. Appropriate local bodies approved the study. All patients provided informed consent before their inclusion in the study; consent waivers for diseased patients were sought from appropriate local bodies.

Study outcomes and procedures

The primary endpoint was the AF of HPV+ OPC among patients with an OPC diagnosis during the study period, as assessed by biopsy or resection tumor sample analyses in a central laboratory. HPV+ patients were defined as those with a combined positivity for p16 expression and HPV DNA presence (i.e. HPV DNA+/p16+).16 HPV− patients were considered those testing negative for p16 and/or HPV DNA (i.e. HPV DNA−/p16−, HPV DNA+/p16−, or HPV DNA−/p16+). To assess the robustness of the primary finding, we conducted two sensitivity analyses whereby the AF of HPV+ OPC was re-evaluated as follows: (i) by excluding patients with discordant HPV status (HPV+/HPV−) between the medical records and the central laboratory test results and (ii) by excluding patients with discordant HPV DNA and p16 expression results by central laboratory analysis. Other endpoints assessed the distribution of HPV types among HPV+ OPC patients; described the patients’ demographic and disease characteristics at OPC diagnosis overall, by HPV status, and for newly diagnosed patients (defined as those diagnosed with OPC up to 2 months before enrollment); and described the physicians’ choice for treatment strategy, overall, by TNM staging, and by HPV status (HPV+/HPV−).

The AF of HPV+ OPCs was estimated by dividing the number of patients with HPV DNA+/p16+ OPC by the total number of patients with available HPV status. Central laboratory analyses of HPV status included p16 IHC using a monoclonal antibody (clone JC2, 1:50, Zytomed, Germany; positive samples were considered those with a strong and diffuse nuclear and cytoplasmic staining in ≥70% of tumor cells) and PCR-enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (PCR-ELISA) DEA (using SPF10 primers) for HPV DNA status. The decision to use both tests was based on the fact that their combination is more specific for the diagnosis and prognostication of the disease, despite the fact that HPV DNA PCR testing is highly sensitive.7,16,21,22 Line probe assay strips were used for HPV genotyping. OPC staging at diagnosis was based on TNM8 classification. Pack-years were defined as the equivalent of smoking one pack of 20 cigarettes per day per year, and one unit of alcohol was defined as 10 ml or 8 g of pure alcohol, which approximates the amount of alcohol the average adult can process in an hour consumed per week.

Sample size and statistical measures

An overall sample size of 150 patients was considered sufficient to provide an estimate of the HPV-related OPC proportion with an adequate margin of error of ∼8.0% (assuming the worst-case scenario in terms of statistical accuracy of a hypothetical proportion of 50.0%; a = 5.0%). All patients who fulfilled the inclusion criteria were included in the analyses. Tumor data were analyzed using descriptive statistics [continuous variables—median values with quartiles and 95% confidence intervals (CIs); categorical variables—absolute numbers and relative frequencies].

The measure of agreement of HPV status (HPV+/HPV−) between the patients’ medical records and central laboratory testing, as well as between DNA and p16 expression results, was assessed using Cohen’s weighted kappa, whereby values ≤0.20 indicate none to slight agreement, 0.21-0.40 fair agreement, 0.41-0.60 moderate agreement, 0.61-0.80 substantial agreement, and 0.81-1.00 almost perfect agreement. For the comparison of demographic and disease characteristics at OPC diagnosis between patients with HPV+ and HPV− OPC, the chi-square and the Mann–Whitney U tests were used for categorical and continuous variables, respectively. All statistical tests were two-sided and were carried out at a 0.05 significance level. All statistical analyses were carried out with the SAS® version 9.4.

Results

The attributable fraction of HPV-associated OPC

In total, 147 patients diagnosed with OPC between 10 February 2017 and 26 October 2022 were included in the study (Table 1). For the primary endpoint analysis, 144 (98.0%) of all patients with available HPV status (i.e. HPV+ or HPV− by central laboratory testing) were included; three (2.0%) patients tested positive for HPV DNA but had unknown p16 expression results and were therefore excluded from the analysis. The HPV-AF, taking into account only p16+/HPV+ cases, was 52.1% (75/144 patients; 95% CI 43.6% to 60.5%; Figure 1). Of the remaining patients, 34.0% (49/144) were HPV DNA−/p16−, 2.8% (4/144) were HPV DNA−/p16+, 10.5% (15/144) were HPV DNA+/p16−, and 0.7% (1/144) were HPV DNA− and unknown p16 status.

One hundred and twenty-three (85.4%) patients had available HPV status in their medical records. The first sensitivity analysis included 96 of the 123 (78.0%; weighted kappa = 0.559) patients who had concordant HPV status (+ or −) between medical records and central laboratory testing; based on these patients, the estimated HPV-AF was 55.2% (53/96 patients; 95% CI 44.7%to 65.4%; Figure 1). Of the 27 (of 123, 22.0%) patients with discordant HPV status between medical records and central laboratory testing, (i) 11 were HPV+ by central testing and HPV− in medical records, of whom 10 had HPV-16 and one HPV-6, and (ii) 16 patients were HPV+ in medical records and HPV− by central testing, of whom three had HPV-16 and 13 had unknown HPV genotype as they tested negative for HPV DNA.

Of all 144 patients, one (0.7%) had unknown p16 status. The second sensitivity analysis included 124 of 143 (86.7%; weighted kappa = 0.727) patients who had concordant HPV DNA and p16 expression results (i.e. 13.3% had discordant results); based on these patients, the estimated HPV-AF was 60.5% (75/124 patients; 95% CI 51.3% to 69.1%; Figure 1).

Patient and disease characteristics, overall and by HPV status, at OPC diagnosis

The demographic and disease characteristics at OPC diagnosis for patients with available HPV status by central laboratory analysis are summarized in Table 1. Overall, patients were middle-aged [median (Q1-Q3) age: 60.0 years (53.5-68.4 years)] and predominantly male (111/144, 77.1%; male/female ratio: 3.4/1). Of patients with available data (n = 136), similar numbers were either current (52/136, 38.2%) or former (51/136, 37.5%) smokers (cigarette smokers only). The most frequently affected oropharyngeal sites were the tonsils (70/144 patients, 48.6%) and the base of the tongue (51/144 patients, 35.4%). Of patients with available tumor staging data (n = 110), most (43/110, 39.1%) were diagnosed with stage IV disease, the remainder being almost equally distributed across stages I, II, and III.

By HPV status, significant between-group differences were observed in patient age, smoking habits, alcohol consumption, location of the tumor in the tonsils and the base of the tongue, and tumor stage (Table 1). Briefly, HPV+ patients were younger compared with the HPV− patients [median (Q1-Q3) age: 58.0 years (50.5-65.3 years) and 64.0 years (58.3-70.4 years), respectively; P = 0.003]. Regarding smoking (overall P value 0.022), a lower proportion of HPV+ than HPV− patients were current smokers (29.0% and 47.8%, respectively); conversely, a numerically higher proportion of HPV+ than HPV− patients were never smokers (33.3% and 14.9%, respectively). Thus, the proportion of patients with HPV+ OPC who were ever smokers was 66.7%. HPV+ patients consumed a lower median (Q1-Q3) number of cigarettes per day [20 (20.0-30.0) and 48 (20.0-60.0), respectively; P = 0.001], and had a lower median (Q1-Q3) number of pack-years [34 (20.0-45.0) and 77 (30.0-100.0), respectively; P = 0.016] than HPV− patients. A lower proportion of HPV+ than HPV− patients were consuming alcohol (24.6% and 50.0%, respectively; P = 0.005), with the median (Q1-Q3) number of alcohol units being 14 (4.5-25.0) and 22 (14.0-30.0), respectively (P = 0.041). A higher proportion of HPV+ than HPV− patients had tonsil tumors (65.3% and 30.4%, respectively; P < 0.001); conversely, a lower proportion of HPV+ than HPV− patients had tumors in the base of the tongue (25.3% and 46.4%, respectively; P = 0.008). Regarding OPC staging, 110 had available data [HPV+: 54/110 (49.1%) and HPV−: 56/110 (50.9%)]; a higher proportion of HPV+ than HPV− patients were in stages I (33.3% and 3.6%, respectively) and II (24.1% and 16.1%, respectively), while the opposite was observed for patients in stage IV (20.4% and 57.1%, respectively). Metastases (Μ1) were observed in 5.6% (3/54) and 7.1% (4/56) of HPV+ and HPV− patients, respectively.

Thirty-four (23.1%) patients with OPC were newly diagnosed (Supplementary Table S1, available at https://doi.org/10.1016/j.esmoop.2024.103724). The median (Q1-Q3) patient age was 64.2 years (53.9-70.7 years), and most patients were male (27, 79.4%). Regarding smoking, 15/34 (44.1%), 11/34 (32.4%), and 5/34 (14.7%) patients were former, current, and never smokers. The most frequently affected oropharyngeal sites were the base of the tongue (17/34 patients, 35.4%) and the tonsils (12/34 patients, 35.3%). Of patients with available tumor staging data (n = 28), most (14/28, 50.0%) were diagnosed with stage IV disease. Metastases (Μ1) were observed in 2/28 (7.1%) patients who were incidentally HPV+.

HPV genotyping

Of the 75 patients with HPV+ OPC by central laboratory analysis, most (70 of 75, 93.3%) were infected by HPV16, either alone or in combination with other HPV types (Table 2). Notably, 72 (of 75, 96.0%) patients with HPV+ OPC were infected with the high-risk HPV types 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58, which are targeted by the HPV 9-valent vaccine (Table 2).

Treatment patterns

Of all patients (n = 147), data on the chosen treatment strategy at OPC diagnosis were available for 125 (85.0%) patients (Figure 2A). Most of these patients received radiotherapy (113, 90.4%), followed by chemotherapy (102, 81.6%) and surgery (22, 17.6%), either alone or in combination. The most frequent combination treatment was chemotherapy plus radiotherapy (81 patients, 64.8%), followed by surgery and adjuvant radiotherapy (11 patients, 8.8%). Regarding the individual agents prescribed, most patients treated with chemotherapy (96 of 102, 94.1%) received platinum-based therapy; all patients treated with targeted therapy (4, 100%) received cetuximab; and of patients treated with immunotherapy, two (50.0%) received nivolumab, one (25.0%) patient received atezolizumab, and one (25.0%) patient received pembrolizumab. Twenty-two patients were planned to undergo surgery. No numerical differences were observed in the treatment choice by HPV status (Figure 2B), and no statistically significant differences were observed in the treatment choice by TNM stage (Figure 2C).

Discussion

ORPHEAS was a multicenter, observational, cross-sectional study with prospective recruitment and retrospective data collection that assessed the AF of HPV+ OPCs in Greece. According to current knowledge, this is a unique study within the Greek OPC setting as it was conducted in eight reference centers covering a substantial geographical area, included patients diagnosed over a recent period (2017-2022), and used both p16 IHC and HPV DNA PCR testing by a central laboratory to assess HPV status.

We showed that the HPV-AF in OPC was 52.1%. This rate was supported by the outcomes of two sensitivity analyses showing HPV-AFs of 55.2% when patients with discordant HPV status between medical records and central laboratory results were excluded and 60.5% when patients with discordant HPV DNA and p16 IHC test results by central laboratory analysis were excluded.

Although not directly comparable, the HPV-AF found in this study is substantially higher than the rates of 22.0% and 18.2% reported previously by Romanitan et al.18 and Tsimplaki et al., respectively.19 The first of these studies was conducted in a single oncology hospital in Piraeus, Greece, and included 59 samples of patients with tonsillar and base-of-the-tongue OPC who were diagnosed between 1986 and 2007, and the second was conducted in the two centers in Athens, Greece, and included 22 patients with OPC who were treated between 2013 and 2015; both studies assessed HPV status with HPV DNA PCR only in pretreated tumor samples. A possible reason for the between-study difference in HPV-AFs is the variation in study designs and diagnostic algorithms used for HPV status determination. The difference in time periods could also have contributed to the variation, as the incidence of HPV-associated OPC has increased in recent years. It seems that the HPV-AF in Greece has increased over the years, currently surpassing those reported for Italy (46.1% and 48.3%)23,24 and approximating rates of several Northern European countries (range: 51.8%-60.0%).25, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30 Notably, even higher rates (∼70%) have been reported for the United States and Sweden,31, 32, 33 while recent reports for Spain consistently report rates ≤10%.3,34 Our study was not designed to explore the reasons driving the HPV-AF in OPC, especially since we did not assess the patients’ sexual behavior, which is an established risk factor for the disease.35

With regard to the patients’ demographic and disease characteristics at OPC diagnosis, the findings of this study are broadly similar to those reported in the literature.3,17 We observed statistically significant between-group differences in patients’ age, smoking status, alcohol consumption, tumor location, and disease staging. In particular, HPV+ OPC patients were younger than HPV (58.0 and 64.0 years, respectively); of note, the median age of the Greek patients with HPV− OPC was slightly higher than that previously reported (61 years).3 Compared with all patients, the newly diagnosed patients were older, and higher proportions of patients had a base-of-the-tongue than tonsil tumors and stage IV disease. Regarding the treatment modalities, our study showed no significant differences between HPV+ and HPV− patients with OPC, with the most frequent treatment combination being chemotherapy plus radiotherapy, given to almost two-thirds of patients. In an analysis of >30 000 patients included in the United States National Cancer Database who had been diagnosed with OPC between 2010 and 2015, most (53.6%) received radiotherapy with or without chemotherapy, and a substantial proportion of patients received surgery with or without chemotherapy and/or radiation.31

In addition to strengthening the HPV-AF shown in this study, our sensitivity analyses showed good (78.0%) agreement in HPV status between the local and central laboratory tests and high (86.7%) concordance between p16 and HPV DNA testing by the central laboratory. Intersample concordance or discordance has been reported in several studies.21,34,36 A recent large analysis of individual patient data (n = 7654) reported that ∼11.0% of p16+ patients had no evidence of HPV presence and that ∼8.0% of p16− patients showed HPV presence.3 These rates are similar to the intersample discordance of 13.3% reported in our study.

The increased HPV-AF reported in this study emphasizes the need for public health interventions to mitigate the HPV+ OPC burden. Regarding primary prevention, in June 2020, the US Food and Drug Administration expanded the prophylactic use of the Human Papillomavirus 9-valent Vaccine, Recombinant (6, 11, 16, 18, 31, 33, 45, 52, and 58) to include the indication of oropharyngeal and other head and neck cancers caused by HPV16/18/31/33/45/52/58 under accelerated approval.37 Another intervention is the effective implementation of HPV vaccination on a gender-neutral basis. Indeed in 2022, the Greek National Immunization Committee recommended the expansion of HPV vaccination to the male population;38,39 thus, currently, the vaccine is officially recommended and reimbursed for children and adolescents 9-18 years of age for the prevention of cervical, penile, and oropharyngeal cancer. Regarding treatment strategies for HPV+ and HPV− patients, which are currently overall similar,12 those may be diversified in the future as patients with HPV+ OPC have a clear survival advantage compared with HPV− OPC patients and de-intensification studies in HPV-associated OPC are ongoing40; also, quality of life is becoming a key parameter of treatment for patients with HPV+ OPC since these patients are younger and it is expected that they will live longer. At present, adequate de-escalation strategies aimed at decreasing long-term treatment toxicities while maintaining the survival benefit with standard treatment have not been identified; however, substantial research is ongoing in this area.4

Concerning genotype distribution, this study reported that HPV16 was identified in almost all patients (single infection: 88.0%; co-infection with other HPV types: 93.3%), in agreement with the literature.41 Interestingly, the overwhelming majority (96.0%) of HPV+ patients were infected with one or more of the high-risk HPV types targeted by the 9-valent vaccine. Currently, a controlled clinical trial assessing the efficacy of vaccination in the prevention of oral HPV infections is ongoing (NCT04199689)42; however, already available real-world data showed that vaccination with the 4-valent HPV (16, 18, 6, and 11) vaccine significantly reduced the prevalence of oral HPV vaccine-type-related infections in vaccinated versus unvaccinated individuals, regardless of gender and, notably, for men.43 These findings, combined with the high prevalence of HPV types targeted by the 9-valent vaccine in the population of ORPHEAS patients with HPV+ OPC, can inform decision makers and vaccinators on the benefits of gender-neutral vaccination against HPV+ OPC in our country. Of note, one patient had a single infection with HPV6; this patient was not considered high-risk HPV type due to the lower oncogenic potential of HPV6. Considering that HPV6/11 have been isolated in variable proportions of penile and laryngeal cancers and that genomic similarities between these two genotypes and HPV16 mainly involved the E7 gene (rather than the E6 and E5 genes—indicating a limited ability to block cell differentiation),44 we took the most conservative approach.

We acknowledge certain limitations of our study. Patient selection bias cannot be excluded as the participating study sites did not cover all geographical areas of Greece, possibly restricting the generalizability of the results to the entire Greek population. Moreover, as the study retrospectively collected data from the patients’ records, the rate of missing data could also be a source of bias concerning the final study results. Every effort was made to ensure that all available data were recorded in the report forms, with the proportion of patients with missing data being <20% in any of the key recorded variables.

In conclusion, ORPHEAS showed that the HPV-AF in OPC was 52.1% over a recent calendar period (2017-2022) in Greece, with HPV16 being the most commonly isolated HPV type. Compared to patients with HPV− OPC, patients with HPV+ OPC were younger, less frequently ever smokers or alcohol consumers, who had more frequently tonsillar tumor, less frequently base of the tongue tumor, and less frequently disease stage IV. Based on the physician’s treatment choice upon OPC diagnosis, most patients received radiotherapy and chemotherapy; however, no statistically significant associations were observed between treatment choice and HPV status or TNM8 stage. Since the majority of patients were infected by one or more HPV types targeted by the 9-valent prophylactic vaccine, the burden of HPV+ OPC should be mitigated through the routine HPV vaccination program in Greece, and consideration could be given to extending a gender-neutral HPV vaccination campaign to older age groups.
