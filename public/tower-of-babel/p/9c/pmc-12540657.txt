A multivariate analysis of the influencing factors for job satisfaction and well-being of academics
Authors: Silvia Puiu, Mihaela Tinca Udriștioiu, Iulian Petrișor
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research investigates the factors that impact job satisfaction, job performance, and well-being among academics, aiming to better understand their contribution. The methodology consists of using partial least-squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) on the data gathered from applying a survey on the academics in the main universities in Romania. The proposed model includes five variables: job stressors, the manager’s support, job performance, job satisfaction, and well-being. The findings show a strong negative relationship between job stressors and academics’ satisfaction and a positive one between satisfaction and both job performance and well-being. Another relevant result refers to the important impact of the manager on the academics’ well-being and job satisfaction. The findings are helpful for managers in developing adequate educational strategies in universities to raise the well-being and satisfaction of academics who teach generations of students.Keywords: Job satisfaction, Well-being, Academics, Job stressors, Job performance, Educational managers, Educational strategies

Introduction

The present paper represents a continuation of the research on students1 because both perspectives (students and academics) should be analysed for quality education. This research is focused on similar variables, such as stressors, well-being, received support (for students, the support comes from academics, and for academics, the support comes from the managers), satisfaction, and efficiency in their activities (academic engagement for students and job performance for academics).

The motivation to continue the research derives from the results of the previous study and the literature that shows the role of well-being in the performance of employees and the results of the organisation in the long run2–5. Organisations in general and universities for our study can shape better strategies meant to increase the results of both their professors and students, ultimately leading to higher visibility of the higher education organisation in the community.

The novelty of this research consists in the multifactorial perspective on the well-being of academics and their performance, which incorporates five variables in the research model we propose. Many studies focus on one or two variables, but we also wanted to see the impact of the management on the well-being and job satisfaction of the academics. The study is focused on Romania, a former communist country in which most universities are public institutions. The country has its cultural characteristics, which influence the individuals’ perceptions of aspects related to psychology, such as their well-being, satisfaction, and relationship with their managers. The former study1 showed significant difficulties in comparing countries for variables that apply to people living in a specific social, cultural, and economic background. The value of the paper consists in other researchers’ interest in replicating the study for universities in other countries. Other authors demonstrated that well-being is subjective, and the culture of a country can determine significant differences in the perception of the individuals in the study6–8. From the perspective of this study, this increases the value of studies focused on individual countries, enriching the literature on this topic. Grossi et al.8 (p. 387) emphasise that “culture has a relevant role as a determinant of individual psychological well-being”.

Literature review

Job stressors

Work stress impacts many aspects of an individual’s life and profession, and academics are not excluded. Thus, many studies focus on the influence of working in stressful conditions on academics’ satisfaction, performance, and well-being to make the needed changes9–13. Leun et al.9 analysed the impact of the stress encountered by academics in Hong Kong universities on their satisfaction, their results showing a negative correlation.

Xu and Wang10 identified three types of stress for academics (related to their research, teaching, and administrative activities) and found that all of them negatively affect their level of satisfaction. Brewer and McMaha-Landers11 noticed that the frequency of stressors is more important than their intensity, and among the stressors, those related to the lack of organisational support have a higher relevance for academics.

Besides the negative impact on satisfaction, job stressors were found to negatively influence academics’ performance14–18. Khan et al.14 noticed that stress affects professors’ productivity, thus reducing their performance. Asaloei et al.15 showed that stress affects both “teachers’ performance and school effectiveness” (p. 352). Sarabia and Collantes18 noticed the usefulness of attending “seminars related to stress management” (p. 6), which decreases stress and improves performance.

The following hypotheses were formulated after reviewing the literature on the topic of job stressors and their impact:

Hypothesis 1 (H1)

Job stressors directly and negatively influence job performance in academics.

Hypothesis 2 (H2)

Job stressors directly and negatively influence academics’ job satisfaction.

Hypothesis 3 (H3)

Job stressors directly and negatively influence academics’ well-being.

Job performance

Many studies point to the correlation between job satisfaction and job performance in education, with the former directly influencing the latter19–24. Baluyos et al.19 studied teachers in the Philippines and found a positive correlation between the two variables while also noting the high level of satisfaction among respondents. Kadtong et al.20 highlight that the correlation is bilateral, with satisfaction leading to higher performance, while the opposite is also true, with high performance contributing to increased teachers’ satisfaction. The authors mention the importance of raising salaries, which is an important component that ensures job satisfaction. Amin22 states the numerous factors that can influence performance, which can be intrinsic, such as motivation and satisfaction, or extrinsic, such as the conditions in the workplace. Koedel et al.23 found a positive correlation between the ratings received by professors for their performance and their satisfaction. Wartenberg et al.24 found the same positive relationship both for the performance rated by someone else (external) and for the professors’ perception of their performance.

Malkoc and Dal25 found that the support received by sports professors from their supervisors leads to a high level of engagement, but they also noticed that the support decreases when the competency level of the professors is higher. The authors observed differences between men and women, with women receiving more support. Similar results were noticed by Elif et al.26, who stated the positive impact of the support received by teachers from their managers on their work commitment. Ghavifekr and Sani27 emphasise the important role of the manager in ensuring the professors’ high performance.

Job satisfaction

Dreer28 studied the impact of well-being on the satisfaction of professors in Germany and noticed that the highest predictor was represented mainly by the positive emotions they felt, which is an important part of well-being. Assaf and Antoun29 showed that the “well-being of teachers and their job satisfaction are interconnected” (p. 1). Song et al.30 distinguish between satisfaction felt by professors towards their work and satisfaction with the salaries they receive. The authors emphasise that income can negatively affect the professors’ well-being.

Hussain et al.31 found a significant correlation between teachers’ job satisfaction and well-being, but with important differences between public and private schools, with those in public schools being more satisfied than the others. Wang et al.32 studied Chinese teachers and noticed the positive impact of the satisfaction related to teaching on their well-being, highlighting the role played by resilience in managing the stressors in their activities.

Stockard and Lehma33 state “that the most important influences on satisfaction involve variables related to social support and school management” (p. 742). Bogler and Nir34 argue that there is a difference between intrinsic and extrinsic satisfaction, and the support received from the academics’ organisation significantly impacts their empowerment in the workplace and, in the end, their satisfaction levels.

The following hypotheses were formulated about job satisfaction, job performance, and well-being:

Hypothesis 4 (H4)

Job satisfaction directly and positively influences academics’ job performance.

Hypothesis 5 (H5)

Job satisfaction directly and positively influences academics’ well-being.

The manager’s support

Studies correlating the relationship with the manager or the organisational support received by professors refer implicitly to their subjective perceptions. Wang35 noticed a significant impact from the organisational support received by the teachers, according to their subjective perceptions, on their well-being as well as performance. Sadia and Saima36 found that stressors affect the professors’ well-being, but the relationship is mediated by the support they receive. Wang et al.37 highlight the “prominent and positive effects” of organisational support on the teachers’ well-being. The study conducted by Yu et al.38 revealed that professors’ engagement impacts their well-being and that organisational support mediates this relationship. In their study on UK teachers, Skinner et al.39 emphasise that teachers’ motivation is not the only factor determining their well-being and dedication, with the management dimension becoming more and more important in this regard.

The impact of the manager’s support on job performance, satisfaction, and well-being is reflected in the following hypotheses we formulated:

Hypothesis 6 (H6)

The manager’s support directly influences the academics’ job performance.

Hypothesis 7 (H7)

The manager’s support directly influences academics’ job satisfaction.

Hypothesis 8 (H8)

The manager’s support directly influences the academics’ well-being.

Well-being

Shen and Slater40 stated that stressors lead to a lower level of well-being for academics, negatively affecting their mental health. Elliott and Blithe41 identified differences between men and women, with women in a US university being more affected. They had a poor work-life balance and less managerial support, which increased their stress and reduced their well-being. Roos and Borkoski42 appreciate that academics’ well-being impacts not only the academics but also their students’ results. Turner and Garvis43 (p. 1) highlight that “academic staff are reporting increased levels of burnout and stress and decreasing well-being”.

Well-being is closely linked to academic performance44–48. Silveira-Martins et al.44 use the term “organisational flourishing” to describe the professors’ positive emotions at their workplace, which was proven to lead to higher performance. James et al.45 emphasise that academics’ well-being contributes to that of their students. The results in two countries (the United Kingdom and Australia) showed that the pressures (factor of stress) negatively impact the professors’ well-being and performance. Hammoudi Halat et al.46 show that the numerous challenges faced by academics (teaching, research, and administrative duties) lead to an increased level of stress, affecting their mental health and well-being. Another study47 conducted in private institutions in Delhi revealed that the lack of freedom and flexibility in doing their activities negatively impacts their well-being and, in the end, their performance. As the authors47 (p. 416) mention, “workplace happiness is a means to achieve sustainable academic performance”. Another study on Italian academics48 showed a positive correlation between their well-being and productivity at the university.

The following hypothesis was formulated about the impact of well-being on the job performance of academics:

Hypothesis 9 (H9). Academics’ well-being directly and positively influences their job performance.

Research methodology

Partial least-squares structural equation modelling (PLS-SEM) was used as a research methodology, and SmartPLS software49 was used to process data. The data were gathered by applying an online survey built in Google Forms that was sent to the public institutional e-mails of academics from the main universities in Romania (the public universities in the large cities), covering various domains from science, social science, art, and others. The survey was sent to 900 academics from the civil public universities in Romania, according to the official list published by the Ministry of Education and Research. We excluded military universities and private ones.

The data was collected between October 2023 and February 2024. There were significant challenges in gathering the minimum number of respondents required by the 10-time rule50 due to the reluctance of respondents to express their perceptions about their managers, several questions being sensitive (related to satisfaction with promotion, salary, and working conditions). In this context, the response rate is 19.88%. The survey was addressed to academics, meaning employees who teach and conduct research in public universities in Romania. We want to highlight that academics in this country perform both the activities—teaching and research—and they cannot choose only one activity.

A total of 179 answers were received, among which 67% were not in a managerial position in the past, 64.8% were women, 49.2% had more than 20 years in their universities, and 96.6% declared that the teaching position was their main job. Each respondent agreed to the informed consent they were presented with before submitting their responses. The anonymity was guaranteed by not collecting their email addresses and not asking questions that might identify the respondent (exact age and position in the university). In this way, we wanted to reduce their reluctance in offering honest replies to sensitive questions regarding their job satisfaction and the support they receive from their managers.

In Fig. 1, we propose a research model with five variables: job stressors, the relationship with the direct manager, job satisfaction, job performance, and academics’ well-being. For each variable, several items were included. The survey presented them as statements on a Likert scale from 1 (total disagreement) to 5 (total agreement).

Discussion

Among the nine hypotheses, only five were validated. This study shows that the stressors faced by academics negatively affect their satisfaction (H2), which follows other studies in the literature9–13. The means in Table 2 show that the stressors have lower values (from 2.039 for harassment and discrimination, meaning disagreement, to 3.827 for their demanding work, corresponding to a slight agreement). Considering the academics’ perception regarding their stressors as being rather low, this might be explained by the difficulties of Romanian academics in showing that they are not strong enough to counterattack the challenges at work, and their opinions might be falsely lower. Manescu et al.56 mention the stigma associated with mental health in Romania, which might explain the low scores for stress. The satisfaction felt has higher means (the highest registered for satisfaction regarding the activity with students and the flexibility they have at work). The lowest scores for satisfaction were registered for promotion opportunities, professional development provided by the university, and low salaries.

The first hypothesis could not be validated—professors’ perception of stress not affecting their performance. Considering the results of Manescu et al.56, it would be expected not to recognize that stress and pressures affect your activity. The highest scores for performance were for the activity with their students, according to the feedback received from them and their manager. This research did not support the third hypothesis linking stress and well-being. We notice from Table 2 that the highest scores for well-being were registered for the quality of their work to be interesting and motivating (4.039—agreement), and the lowest scores were for the academics’ capacity to detach from work (1.955) and not feel depleted of energy (2.503). Other studies found a negative correlation between these two variables40–43. Both hypotheses H4 and H5 were validated, showing a strong correlation between satisfaction and performance19–24 and between satisfaction and well-being28–32. Differences between countries can occur when there are cultural differences that explain the various perceptions on issues like mental health, burnout, and well-being1,8,56.

The correlation between management support and performance was not validated—H6, which in other studies was noticed as significant25–27,35. Still, our findings show a strong correlation between management support and satisfaction, validating H7, which was noticed in other papers33,34. In Table 2, we notice that the highest scores regarding management support were for items related to the availability of their manager when they have a problem (3.888) and the capacity of the manager to communicate clearly and regularly with them (3.631). We also notice from Fig. 2 that the impact of management support on well-being (0.358) is higher than for job satisfaction (0.297). The correlation between management support and well-being (H8) follows other studies35–39.

The ninth hypothesis was not supported, as this research was not able to validate the positive correlation between well-being and academic performance, as was the case in other studies44–48. Hypotheses regarding the negative correlation between stress and job performance and the positive correlation between management support and performance (H1 and H6) or between performance and well-being (H9) were not supported for our sample consisting of Romanian academics in higher education institutions. This result could be explained by several factors, such as those related to specifics of the Romanian culture56 and the difficulty of admitting that they are stressed and accepting help from a supervisor who might interpret this as weakness or incompetence57,58.

Conclusions

The main findings in our study show that academics in the higher education system in Romania report lower stress levels, with the highest scores for the demanding character of their work, followed by the pressure to publish research papers and books, and get funds for publishing. Being reported as rather low, the perceived overall stress does not affect their performance and well-being. Only the negative correlation between stress and satisfaction was validated. Another finding is related to the positive impact of satisfaction on their performance and well-being. Academics reported higher levels of satisfaction in teaching and lower levels regarding the opportunity to be promoted, their salary, and the professional development offered by the university.

Academics’ satisfaction and well-being were directly and positively influenced by having their manager’s support. Academics trust their managers to consult with them whenever a problem occurs, and they perceive the manager as someone who clearly communicates what must be done. This study did not support the hypothesis that the manager’s support impacts their performance. Only job satisfaction was shown to impact their academic performance.

Theoretical implications refer to the usefulness of our study for other researchers who might want to replicate the study for other countries and professors in the primary and secondary education systems. Practical implications derive from the fact that managers in the higher education system can use the findings to implement newer strategies that focus more on the well-being and satisfaction of the academics in public civil universities. Satisfaction is influenced positively by the manager’s support and negatively by the stressors at work, so managers can implement human resources strategies that consider reducing the stressors and offering more support for academics. Considering that satisfaction positively influences well-being, it is important for decision makers in universities to pay close attention to the working conditions and improve them, while making sure that the academics are paid in accordance with the high-quality services they provide. Communication is essential in order to know what the needs of academics are and how these might change in time to influence their performance, satisfaction, and well-being.

Other studies have shown that academics’ well-being influences students’ well-being, so this issue has significant value for academic management, which wants to increase academic performance for both students and professors. In the end, this translates into a better image of the university, great visibility at local, national, and international levels, improved results in learning and teaching, research, and partnerships with other educational institutions worldwide.

The limits of this research are related to the fact that we addressed only one country, the survey was online, and it reflects only the perceptions of those academics willing to reply. Also, it is a study of perceptions regarding management support, performance, satisfaction, and well-being, and perceptions are inherently subjective. Still, valuable conclusions can be extracted from this research. Case studies like this have value because each country has psychological and cultural differences that can influence perceptions. The replication potential ensures that the research can be applied to other countries.

Future research can consider analyzing the impact of academics’ well-being on students’ well-being. Another research direction consists of studying managers’ perceptions of these issues because they are the decision factors in universities. In Romania, most educational institutions are public, and many decisions are centralized. Still, universities have increased autonomy. Considering the different problems faced by professors in primary and secondary schools, this is another valuable topic that can be studied in the future.
