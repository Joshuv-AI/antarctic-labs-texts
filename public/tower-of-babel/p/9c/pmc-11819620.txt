Liquid-based cytology in the diagnosis of oral pre-cancer, cancer, and other oral lesions – A narrative review
Authors: Usha Hegde, Sreeshyla H. Sheshanna, HP Jaishankar, R Shashidara
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Though cytology is one of the most simple and economical diagnostic techniques, several disadvantages set it back as a good diagnostic tool. Liquid-based cytology (LBC) is an advanced diagnostic cytological technique that is being technically modified from conventional cytology to produce better sample preparation to aid in improved diagnosis. It produces a more representative sample of the specimen, with reduced obscuring background material which allows for faster and more reliable screening. Instead of being spread onto a glass slide and fixed, the samples are suspended in a vial of liquid preservative, processed to break the clot and remove the debris. The cell pellet is then placed onto the glass slide to obtain a thin monolayer of cells with a clean background. LBC is found to have several advantages against the conventional techniques. LBC has been researched in cervical cytology and has shown good sensitivity, specificity, improved sampling, and quality of smears. It has been adopted to study several oral lesions and has been found to be a reliable substitute for conventional smear. This article reviews LBC technique and its applications to oral diseases, in specific to oral cancer and pre-cancer.

INTRODUCTION

Oral squamous cell carcinoma (OSCC, oral cancer) is one of the most common malignancy, with high morbidity and mortality.[123] There are numerous advances in the management of the cancer, yet the 5-year survival has remained approximately 50% for the past 50 years.[12] Oral cancer occurs as a multi-step neoplastic process, progressing from a pre-cancerous stage. This offers the advantage for early diagnosis in its pre-cancerous stage. The prognosis for the patients with OSCC treated early is much better, with 5-year survival rates as high as 80% and additionally improving the quality of life after early treatment.[123] Thus, early detection is very crucial. There are numerous diagnostic adjuncts available for the screening and diagnosis of oral pre-cancer and cancer, with none established as ideal.

Cytology is one of the simple and economical techniques that can be used for the detection and screening of cancers.[34] Though this technique is highly successful in screening cervical cancers, its use in oral cancers is limited due to the sampling technique that provides less sample or superficial sample without inclusion of the deeper cell layers or because the sample is bloody in nature or may contain necrotic cells, thus resulting in high false positive rates.[345] These drawbacks associated with exfoliative cytology set it back as an ideal diagnostic tool. The result of search for the better improved cytological technique is LBC.[46]

LBC has been used extensively in cervical cytology and has shown good sensitivity, specificity, improved sampling, and quality of smears. Numerous comparative studies have found several advantages of LBC against the conventional techniques [Table 1]. Cellularity and specimen adequacy are improved, cellular features are well preserved, a good smear background is obtained due to removal of mucus, blood, and inflammatory cells, and, hence, there is overall improved interpretation of the data.[45678] However, a few studies in contrast have found LBC to be the same as conventional cytology.[910] Microbiopsies can be prepared from the tissue fragments in LBC which can be used for the diagnosis of cancers, and they have shown good preservation of the basement membrane. The residual material can also be used for further investigations.[111213]

Advantages of liquid-based cytology

LIQUID-BASED CYTOLOGY – TECHNIQUE

LBC technique is in use since 1990s.[1415] Technically, the samples are collected using a brush-like device, and instead of making smears on slides, it is immersed into a vial of preservative fluid, usually with an alcoholic content, so that most of the sampled cells are retained. Samples are transported to the laboratory and are processed for removing obscuring material, mucus, and blood with a clearing solution and centrifuged by density gradient centrifugation for collecting cells. The centrifugation separates the cells according to their specific weight. Granulocytes, erythrocytes, and debris that have a lower weight than the epithelial cells accumulate above the density gradient and are then discarded. Upon discarding the supernatant, the pellicle so formed is admixed with a cellular base solution which is finally transferred to a clean slide to produce a thin monolayer of cells with a clean background [Table 2].[6891016]

Steps in the liquid-based cytology technique

The collected sample can be processed by two methods. In the direct to vial method, the sample collected by the spatula is directly mixed into the liquid fixative provided for use in LBC. In the split sample technique, the sample collected is initially smeared onto the glass slide for use in conventional cytology and then placed in a preservative fluid for use in LBC.[6] Instruments that are used for the collection of the sample are the plastic Ayre’s spatula, cytobrush, cotton swab, and cytoprep instruments.[6] The currently available products that use liquid-based methodology are ThinPrep, SurePath, Cytoscreen, and Labonard Easy Prep.[1016]

THINPREP

Developed in 1996, ThinPrep was the first LBC technique to be approved by Food and Drug Administration (FDA). It is available in semi-automated (T2000) or fully automated (T3000) forms. While T2000 machine processes slides individually, T3000 machine process up to 80 specimens per cycle.

The transport fluid is PreservCyt. Once fixed, each vial is placed individually in the ThinPrep Processor [Figure 1]. There are three key phases to the process.[69]

A reprocessing step for unsatisfactory specimens sampled with the ThinPrep Pap test has been approved by FDA, and it includes a wash step with 10% glacial acetic acid in CytoLyt. This test is approved by FDA for conducting molecular tests.[9]

SUREPATH (FORMERLY AUTOCYTEPREP OR CYTORICH LBC)

This technique was developed in 1999 and is the second LBC technique to be approved by FDA. The sample is collected using a plastic collection device, the head of which is then detached into the transport fluid (CytoRich) in the collection vial [Figure 1]. Vials are vortexed, and the cell suspension is treated through a density gradient centrifugation process. The centrifuge tubes are loaded onto an AutoCytePrep ‘robot’ which handles 48 samples at a time. The cell pellet is re-suspended, and an aliquot is transferred to a settling chamber mounted on a microscope slide. The cells are allowed to sediment under gravity to form a thin layer on the slide. Excess fluid and cells are removed, and the slide is then stained automatically as part of the process. If the preparation is considered inadequate or unsatisfactory, it is possible to revert to the original cell pellet and prepare another slide using a larger aliquot of suspension. This technique is not approved by FDA for HPV testing.[91016]

CYTOSCREEN

It is a manual method of sample preparation. The sample collecting device is known as Cytoprep, and the transport fluid is CYTeasy. Samples are vortexed, and a photometric reading is taken to estimate the cellularity of the sample. An aliquot of the sample is centrifuged onto a glass slide that is then stained using normal laboratory procedures.[1016]

LABONARD EASY PREP

It is a manual method of sample preparation that uses Cytoprep brushes for sample collection and Cytoscreen for fixation. An aliquot of sample fluid is placed in a separation chamber attached to a glass slide containing absorbent paper punched to produce a 250 mm hole. Eight chambers are placed together in a clamping unit. The plastic chamber retains the cell suspension in place during sedimentation, whilst the absorbent paper gently removes the fluid resulting in a dry, thin layer of cells. The cells sediment onto the slide in a thin layer, and slides are stained using normal laboratory procedures.[9]

IMAGING SYSTEMS

Computer-guided imaging systems have been introduced for the interpretation of slides. These systems combine imaging technology with human interpretive expertise to improve cancer screening efficiency and performance. ThinPrep Imaging System (TIS) imager identifies 22 fields of interest containing abnormal cells, which are then examined by a cytopathologist.[910] The other two imaging systems developed by SurePath Pap test are the BD FocalPoint Slide Profiler (formerly AutoPap Primary Screening System) and the BD FocalPoint GS Imaging System.[9] Clinical trial data for the ThinPrep Imaging System and the BD FocalPoint GS Imaging System showed increased disease detection when compared with manual screening methods.[9] All these commercially available LBC techniques are quite resource-intensive.

DISCUSSION

Though exfoliative cytology is used widely in the non-oral sites, such as the uterine cervix, urinary tract, and lungs, its use in oral cavity is at sparse. 4 Presently, LBC is widely used in the United States, many European countries, and elsewhere, and it accounts for more than 90% of PAP test performed in the United States.[17]

Since its introduction, extensive studies have been carried out on LBC in cervical cancer screening and detection, in detection of other cervical pathologies, and in other non-gynaecological pathologies. Varying results have been reported with the studies.[6917] Though numerous studies have been done in cervical cytology, only a few studies have been carried out in the oral cavity.[14] Methodologies for studying LBC are varied, such as the pathologies studied, technique employed, inclusion–exclusion criterias, sampling device used, adequacy of samples, and comparison with histopathology. While a few studies have compared LBC against conventional techniques using the split sample technique, others have compared LBC with scalpel biopsy. While most of the studies are on pre-malignant and malignant lesions, a few are done on healthy volunteers and others on different oral lesions.[37811121418192021222324252627282930313233343536373839404142434445]

SAMPLING DEVICES

The device used for sample collection influences the technique and reporting. In most of the studies, the samples were collected with cytology brushes used in cervical pathologies, dermatological tools, oral cytobrushes such as oral CDx, plastic spatula, and wooden spatula.[3814182027282930] Cervical cytology brushes may produce inadequate samples due to their non-rigid nature, resulting in false negative results.[14] Delavarian et al.[14] have used oral CDx brush. With this brush, the sample could be obtained from deep epithelial layers, and hence, such sampling may aid in decreasing false negatives and inadequate results and improving the sensitivity.

In a study, cytobrushes, oral CDx, and dermatological curettes were compared in 104 cytology samples obtained from healthy volunteers. Cellularity, quality of the preparation, and cell types were analysed, and no difference was found in the sampling from these instruments.[20] The three collecting instruments, the endocervical brush, plastic spatula, and LBC kit brush, were compared using both conventional and LBC techniques, and the endocervical brush was found to be better and most appropriate.[18] In a study by Kujan et al.,[8] a cervix brush and cytobrush plus GT, specially for the lateral border of the tongue, were used. The problem with the usage of these cervical brushes is their size and shape, which samples only superficial and very few basal and parabasal cells.[8] However, in a study by Afrogheh et al.,[3] a cervical cytobrush was used to obtain a transepithelial cytology specimen and the author is of the opinion that these brushes are ideal instruments for oral cytology as their long and flexible plastic handles allow for the easy collection of samples even from less accessible areas. Similar good results have been recorded with cervical brushes in other studies as well.[3]

In cases of hyperkeratotic lesions, it may be difficult to obtain a transepithelial sample, and hence, use of sharp instruments such as a metal spatula, sharp spoon, or amalgam spatula has been suggested, but they may be associated with a greater degree of discomfort.[3] Newly designed Orcellex brushes by Kujan et al.[30] for specific use in the oral cavity yielded a voluminous sample of cells from all the epithelial cell layers, ranging from 35,000 to 90,000 cells with a mean of 55,500 cells. Studies have shown that only 20% of cells collected using various collecting devices can be mechanically transferred onto the glass slide, which is one of the major reasons for the high false negative rates associated with oral cytology.[38] Conversely, mixing the sampling device into the LBC sampling fluid maximises the cell collection sample such that an adequate cellularity is obtained.[8]

TECHNICAL MODIFICATIONS

The techniques employed for the study are varied. While a few studies are done using the commercially available techniques such as Thinprep and Surepath, the other authors have modified the regular LBC to make it either more economical or technically better.[78121415192728293031323334] Delavarian et al.[14] have used a modified LBC technique wherein the glass tube with formalin preservative along with the brush was centrifuged so as to take off the maximum possible cells from the brush and sediment them, which was further centrifuged and smeared. Using this modified technique, 25 patients with 26 lesions with clinically diagnosed cases of leukoplakia, oral lichen planus, squamous cell carcinoma, and verrucous carcinoma were studied. Good sampling including all the cell layers was obtained. The study recorded a sensitivity and specificity of 88.8% and 100%, respectively. The positive predictive value and negative predictive value were 100% and 80%, respectively. Clinical diagnosis was in agreement in 88.4% with histopathological findings. Histopathological agreement was achieved in 92.3% of cases.[14]

Centrifuged LBC (CLBC) method was adopted by Ahmed et al.[15] to overcome the expensive automated devices available commercially. The scrapped sample was placed in a suspended solution consisting of ethanol, glacial acetic acid, and normal saline and then spun in cytospin for 10 min at 3000 rpm. The supernatant was discarded and replaced by acid alcohol. A coating medium with either glycerin or albumin was added prior to smearing. This modified technique, apart from being inexpensive, also reduced background staining and inadequate smears. On comparison, 92% of CLBC smears revealed clear, well distributed smears in comparison to 70% in the direct. CLBC smears showed uniform distribution of cells, a clear background, well preserved morphology, reduced artifacts, and superior quality staining.[15]

In another CLBC technique, the brush with the sample was dipped and shook in a suspending solution composed of 20 ml of 95% ethanol + 6 ml acetic acid + 74 ml normal saline for 10 minutes and centrifuged. The cell pellet obtained was re-suspended in 95% alcohol, and the suspension was poured over a horizontally placed glass slide and left for 2 hours to allow sedimentation of cells. Using this technique, there was a statistically significant difference only in clear background, and in all other parameters, the difference was not significant. A clear background was noted in 68% of smears for CLBC and 26% in conventional with minimal microbial colonies present in CLBC. The use of acetic acid in the suspending solution and cytocentrifugation removes the mucin, blood, debris, microbial colonies, and other artifacts present in the background.[19] The simple cytocentrifugation technique in oral pre-malignant and malignant lesions also showed a statistically significant results over the conventional except for mitosis. Cytocentrifugation enabled good interpretation by preserving the cellular details and reducing the overlapping of cells.[21]

A cost-effective alternative, based on cytocentrifugation, the Shandon Papspin LBC (SPLBC), has been studied. The cytobrush was immersed and gently rinsed in a vial containing 3 ml Shandon Cytorich Red Collection Fluid preservative, which was then centrifuged. The supernatant was discarded, the sediment was agitated by a vortex mixed, 3 ml of a preservative was added and homogenized, 4–5 drops of cellular suspension was pipetted into the EZ Cytofunnel, which was then loaded into a Shandon Cytospin centrifuge. It was then centrifuged to get a 5 mm circle of cellular deposit on the screening area of the glass slide. With this technique, an excellent specimen quality was obtained with well-maintained cytomorphology, a clear background, and even distribution of epithelial cells, recording a sensitivity of 96%, a specificity of 100%, and a low inadequacy rate of 1.4%. This procedure can be a cost-effective alternative to the currently available automated LBC techniques, particularly for use in resource-limited countries.[3]

Staining techniques for LBC have been evaluated by a few authors. On comparison of Pap (Papanicolaou) and MGG (May-Grünwald-Giemsa), Pap showed better staining qualities. Excellent staining was seen in 69% of Pap samples as against 22% by MGG.[15] Conversely, similar staining qualities have been recorded in other studies.[15] Other authors too have studied similar or slightly modified CLBC and MLBC, modified LBC techniques performing better than the conventional techniques.[2728293441]

Comparative analysis of the technique

Most of the studies have compared conventional cytology with LBC, with a few studies also comparing with the standard histopathology. LBC technique was better in analysing various parameters in comparison to the conventional technique. Thin uniform distribution of cells, optimized sample fixation, clear background, preservation of cellular morphology, enhanced nuclear details, superior staining qualities, reduced artifacts, and diminished false negatives were recorded with LBC [Table 3]. Conventional techniques showed blood contamination and cell overlapping and diagnosis was time-consuming. A clear background in LBC is obtained due to the lysis of mucus and blood elements, reduction in cellular clumping, overlapping, and preservation of cellular morphology due to sufficient fixation and release of artifacts by washing [Figure 2].[81518272829323334]

Cytological smears obtained with LBC with good appreciation of cells, microbes, and a clear background

A comparative analysis of LBC versus conventional cytology in 44 patients by Hayama et al.[7] showed an overall improvement in sample preservation, specimen adequacy, visualisation of cell morphology, and reproducibility. On specimen analysis, a statistically significant 41% overall improvement in smear thickness, 66% in cell distribution, a reduction in cell overlapping, and presence of blood and inadequacy rate of 6.8% were recorded. The cytopathic effects produced by infections and acantholytic and other features produced in autoimmune diseases were better observed in LBC samples.[7] In a study by Kujan O et al.,[8] the liquid-based preparation slides showed good sample preservation, specimen adequacy, and better visualisation of cell morphology. Only 1.3% of the smears were found to be inadequate. On an average, 25,500 cells could be observed in each slide, thus recording a good cellularity.

A total of 473 patients diagnosed with PML were subjected to conventional cytology (89) using cytobrushes and LBC (384) using dermatological curettes, and results were compared with histology. A sensitivity and specificity of 85.7% and 95.9% were recorded for the conventional technique, and 95.1% and 99% were recorded for LBC. The smears were inadequate in 12.4% of the conventional technique and 8.8% for LBC.[11] Microbiopsies were prepared from tissue fragments collected with LBC. LBC gave better results than the conventional technique.[11] 89 conventional smears using cytobrushes and 411 LBC samples using dermatological curettes were compared to histological diagnosis. The sensitivity was 86.5% in the conventional technique and 94.7% in liquid-based cytology, the specificity was 94.3% and 98.9%, respectively, and inadequate samples were present in 12.4% and 8.8% of cases, respectively. In most of these studies, LBC gave better results, with both enhanced sensitivity and specificity.[12]

The sensitivity, specificity, and concordance between conventional cytology and LBC were evaluated among 182 patients with primary OSCC and 179 individuals with normal buccal mucosa. Among the cases, the cytologic methods demonstrated 96.9% sensitivity, 75.0% specificity, and 96.3% accuracy; among the controls, the methods showed 91.0% sensitivity, 57.5% specificity, and 82.7% accuracy. Conventional cytology and LBC demonstrated a diagnostic concordance with histopathology of more than 90%. When compared to each other, conventional cytology and LBC showed a high sensitivity and reasonable specificity, with conventional cytology having higher specificity.[22] LBC is compared with scalpel biopsy in some studies, which determines the efficiency of LBC better. High diagnostic agreement of 92.3% was noted between LBC and histopathology. Positive results were obtained with all OSCCs, four of six dysplastic lesions. Two clinically diagnosed OSSCs proved to be granular cell tumours, and they had negative cytology results.[14]

While a reliable agreement has been recorded between LBC and histological diagnosis by Gürhan et al.,[33] negative cytological diagnosis showing a positive histological diagnosis of SCC has been recorded in 8.3% of patients by Kokubun et al.[4344]

LBC to histology for diagnosis of OSCC in 48 patients demonstrated a sensitivity of 69%, a specificity of 100%, a positive predictive value of 100%, and a negative predictive value of 71%. Eight false negatives and no false positives were observed.[45]

A new oral cytologic grading and scoring system was developed and used for scoring PSLBC slides, which was compared with histopathology. A cytologic score of <3 indicated a reactive/low-grade lesion, and >3 indicated a high-grade lesion or invasive SCC with a good sensitivity of 95% and a specificity of 96%. Correct cytologic diagnosis was made in 49 of 51 histologically dysplastic/malignant lesions. This scoring system is simple, reliable, and reproducible but requires further evaluation with future large-scale studies.[3] The cost analysis of an LBC against single biopsy has shown at least 26% less and more than 42% less in cases of multiple biopsied lesions. LBC preparation has been found to be very effective in evaluating the frequency of micronuclei.[39]

LBC IN ORAL LESIONS

LBC has been used to evaluate infectious conditions as well, and it allowed better visualisation of hyphaes and/or pseudohyphaes.[2425] Though LBC was technically superior than CEC, it did not show satisfactory results as a safe method to the diagnosis of oral candidasis.[25]

Studies of salivary gland tissues have shown comparable results for LBC and CS smears. With LBC preparations, there was a decrease in extracellular and stromal elements and the quality of background matrix material was low. Chondromyxoid matrix was better appreciated on CS.36 The sensitivity, specificity, positive predictive value, and negative predictive value were found to be 100% in diagnosing salivary gland lesions when compared to CS. LBC was also very useful in preoperative salivary gland tumour diagnosis based on immunocytochemical staining.[37]

The study of traumatic, inflammatory, or benign hyperplastic oral lesions along with OPMDs and malignant lesions with LBC has shown a good sensitivity and specificity of 95.6% and 84.9%, respectively.[31]

The study of oral pemphigus vulgaris (PV) using LBC has demonstrated cytological mimicry of oral PV to malignancy, cautioning the appropriate interpretation of such lesions in an absence of proper clinical information.[40]

Applications of residual LBC

The residual material after obtaining cytosmear can be used for the preparation of microbiopsies.[1112] Microbiopsies were prepared from the LBC samples obtained from 164 patients with PML using dermatological curettes, and their histological findings were correlated with that of scalpel biopsy. The diagnosis was in agreement in 91.14% of cases. The sensitivity was better for microbiopsies, 97.65%, than for scalpel biopsy, 85.88%. The basement membrane zone was observed in 69.62% of microbiopsies. Due to its improved efficiency and reduced false negatives, the microbiopsy can be used as an effective first level diagnostic procedure, especially in follow-up cases and multiple lesions. The remaining material can be stored for a longer time, cell block can be prepared from it and used for further investigations such as immunocytochemistry and other molecular techniques [Figure 3].[413]

LBC and molecular studies

MCM, a marker for early detection of SCC, was immunohistochemically analysed in 101 oral smears prepared by conventional and LBC. MCMs were detected better in LBC smears. LBC showed strong clinical performance by substantially reducing the frequency of inadequate samples, greater consistency in staining, and ease of interpretation.[5] The immunocytochemical assay with cytokeratin pool AE1/AE3 reactions was positive in all tested malignant cases, and the visualisation of immunostained cells was very clear in all the cases. This assay also proved to be advantageous as the amount of antibody required was reduced and hence the reduced cost.[7] Immunocytochemistry for FHIT and PCR for HPV detection were carried out on healthy volunteers. FHIT staining was consistent in all the cases, and a good immunoreactivity was recorded.[8] LBC is also found to be reliable for the detection of HPV DNA in OSCC.[35] HPV detection in oral samples was found to be reliable. Overall, the molecular analysis of LBC samples was feasible and reliable. Hence, this method could be used for assessing the prognostic and predictive markers in oral pre-cancer–cancer.[8]

Smears from 11 tongue squamous cell carcinoma patients were processed by LBC and evaluated for morphometric indices and CK13 and CK17 immunohistochemically. Most orange G-stained superficial cells were negative for CK13 (99.4%) and CK17 (98.6%). Expression of CK17 was related to higher-grade cellular atypia in the same parameters as well as the nuclear circular rate. Immunoexpression of CK13 and CK17 in light-green-stained superficial cells was associated with more severe morphological atypia. Combined morphometry and immunoexpression of CK13 and CK17 was suggested to be useful for cytological diagnosis of the squamous cell population.[23] Cell block reared from LBC samples can be a reliable approach to investigate oral epithelial lesions using immunocytochemistry.[42]

LBC and artifacts

The literature suggests that there is reduction in the number of artifacts in LBC. On the contrary, there may be the absence of some cytomorphological alterations that are necessary to avoid erroneous diagnoses. In salivary gland lesions such as pleomorphic adenoma, the alterations in the quantity and quality of the background matrix may misguide the diagnosis. Similarly, there are some artifacts peculiarly seen in LBC, and hence, it is necessary to be familiar with them to avoid misdiagnosis. The author emphasises the necessity of familiarity with such artifacts to avoid misdiagnoses.[2636] Though the reduction in inflammatory cells, erythrocytes, and mucus is considered advantageous in cervical pathology, in oral smears, these components may be very significant in the diagnosis of some oral lesions.[18]

FINAL REMARKS

LBC is a rapid, non-invasive, relatively painless procedure, and only a low level of discomfort has been recorded with this technique.[38] However, it requires more sophisticated laboratory equipment and well-trained staff, and it is reported that the performance of the test will improve after training.[79] On the contrary, the technique may provide an overall cost benefit, especially in developing countries where repeat investigations due to inadequate sampling are avoided.[531] LBC is used in the evaluation of pre-malignant and malignant lesions, in suspected cases of infections such as herpes, candidiasis, diseases caused by different strains of human papillomavirus (HPV), Chlamydia trachomatis, and Neisseria gonorrhea, in detection of glandular lesions, and in molecular-based diagnosis and studies. Presently, most of laboratories in the west have shifted to liquid-based cytology. This has reduced the rate of inadequate samples and has increased the detection rate, and moreover, at least some of the additional cost of liquid-based cytology is compensated by fewer repeat tests.[17] Though LBC is proved to be advantageous in many ways, it cannot be a substitute for biopsy.[1915]
