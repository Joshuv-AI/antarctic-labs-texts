Isotopic H2 and NH4+ supplementation enhances volatile fatty acid and microbial protein synthesis during in vitro rumen fermentation
Authors: Yurong Cao, Xiumin Zhang, Siyu Yi, Zhiwei Kong, Zhen He, Hongzhao Yuan, Rong Wang, Qiushuang Li, Zhiyuan Ma, Zhiliang Tan, Min Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe volatile fatty acid (VFA) and microbial protein production are tightly linked to hydrogen (H2) metabolism in rumen. The H2 can be split by hydrogenases into protons and electrons, and is regarded as the most primordial source of reducing equivalents within the rumen microecosystem. However, the quantitative role of H2 in VFA and microbial protein formation has not been thoroughly studied. Additionally, while rumen fermentation is influenced by ammonia-N availability, the combined effects of H2 and NH4+ supplementation on VFA and microbial protein synthesis have not been fully explored. The objective of this study was to explore the role of proton and electron transfer from H2 in VFA and microbial protein synthesis based on the synergistic effect of H2 and NH4+ supplementation. In vitro ruminal batch culture was employed for the complete block design with factorial arrangement of four treatments including H2 (0 or 40 mL 2H2) and NH4+ (0 or 8 mmol/L 15NH4+) supplementations.Results2H2 supplementation increased dry matter (DM) degradation (P = 0.005), methane (CH4) production (P < 0.001), 2H abundance (P < 0.001) in microbial protein and individual VFAs. However, 2H abundance in microbial protein, acetate, propionate and butyrate are low with the value less than 2.0‰. The 15NH4+ supplementation increased microbial protein production (P = 0.001), microbial 15N abundance (P < 0.001), and microbial 15N incorporation (mg/g degraded DM, P < 0.001), and concurrently reduced CH4 production (P < 0.001). Moreover, 2H2 supplementation further enhanced the stimulatory effect of 15NH4+ on microbial protein production.ConclusionThese results indicate limited incorporation of protons for the synthesis of VFA and microbial protein, and the enhancement of VFA and microbial protein production may be associated with increased electron availability caused by the 2H2 supplementation. The isotopic dilution and hydrogen exchange with other pools cannot be ruled out as contributing factors, which should be explored in the future studies.Keywords: H2, NH4+, Microbial protein, Volatile fatty acids

Introduction

Ruminants harbor a unique microbial ecosystem capable of converting plant material and non-protein nitrogen (N) into high-value food products such as milk and meat. These microorganisms can break down complex cellulose into volatile fatty acids (VFA), and incorporate ammonia-N into carbon chains for microbial animo acids synthesis. Volatile fatty acids and microbial protein serve as a crucial energy and amino acids source for the host animals and act as precursors for some key metabolic end products of fat and protein [1, 2]. However, the fermentation process in the rumen also generates methane (CH4), a potent greenhouse gas that not only results in a loss of energy from ingested feed but also contributes to global warming and climate change [3].

The fermentative carbohydrate degradation to acetate generates reducing equivalents (e.g., NADH, FADH), part of which is used for the biosynthesis of VFA, microbial protein and other metabolites. The remaining reductant is oxidized to produce molecular hydrogen (H2) via hydrogenases, which is primarily utilized by methanogenic archaea to maintain a low H2 partial pressure for the continuous fermentation in rumen [4]. In addition, a variety of hydrogenotrophic bacteria, including acetogens and fumarate reducers, are prevalent in the rumen microbiome and can utilize H2 to produce acetate and propionate, respectively [5, 6]. Microbial protein synthesis involves the assimilation of ammonia-N into amino acids, a process that requires reducing equivalents, and could be closely associated with H2 metabolism in the rumen [7]. The NH4+ supplementation could increase microbial protein synthesis, which is beneficial for microbial growth and may promote fermentative VFA production. Thus, there may be a synergistic effect of H2 and NH4+ supplementation on ruminal fermentation and microbial protein synthesis, which remains to be investigated.

Oxidation of H2 catalyzed by hydrogenases links to cellular redox reactions through electron carriers such as NAD⁺ and ferredoxin [8, 9]. These redox processes are associated with pathways involved in VFA formation and microbial growth [6, 9]. An increase in the supply of H2 may subsequently affect the synthesis of VFA and microbial protein. However, the extent to which H2-derived proton is directly incorporated into fermentation products, versus indirectly influencing metabolism through changes in electron availability and redox balance, remains unclear. In particular, the relative contribution of hydrogen proton from H2 to VFA and microbial protein synthesis has not been well quantified.

To address thus knowledge gap, this study was designed to investigate the effects of H2 and NH4+ supplementation on rumen fermentation and microbial protein synthesis using in vitro ruminal batch cultures with labeled 2H2 and 15NH4+.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All animal procedures used in this study were approved by the Animal Care Committee, Institute of Subtropical Agriculture, Chinese Academy of Sciences, Changsha, China (approval number ISA-2024-0109). Three healthy adult male Xiangdong black goats (25.0 ± 2.00 kg average body weight) with ruminal cannula were used to collect rumen content. All animals are owned by the institute.

Experimental design of batch culture

A complete block design with a factorial arrangement of four treatments was employed by supplementation of headspace H2 (0 or 40 mL 2H2) and NH4+ (0 or 8 mmol/L 15NH4+). The 2H2 was purchased from Wuhan Isotope Inc. (Catalog ITC80132, Wuhan, China, ≥ 99.5 atom % 2H), whereas 15NH4+ was obtained from 15NH4Cl (Catalog 299251, Sigma-Aldrich Shanghai Trading Co Ltd., Shanghai, China, ≥ 98 atom % 15N). Three independent batches (conducted on different days) served as the experimental units of block. Within each batch, each treatment had 8 bottles for technical replicates. Four of these bottles were used for measuring dissolved gases at 3 and 6 h (two bottles per time point) of incubation, whereas the remaining four bottles were used for cumulative gas production measurement. After 48 h of incubation, two of these four bottles were used for analysis of fermentation end products (VFA, ammonia‑N) and microbial protein, and the other two bottles were used for measurement of substrate degradation. Eight additional bottles per batch served as blanks (inoculum without substrate) to correct for background production.

Procedures of in vitro ruminal batch culture

The rumen inoculum was collected from the goats fed with a total mixed ration containing 500 g/kg rice straw and 500 g/kg concentrate (554 g corn grain, 198 g wheat bran, 185 g soybean meal, 30 g soybean oil, 12 g calcium carbonate, 11 g sodium chloride and 10 g premix with vitamins and microelements per kg DM), with 910 g/kg of OM, 105 g/kg of CP, 245 g/kg of starch, 450 g/kg of neutral detergent fiber (NDF) and 16.1 MJ/kg of gross energy. Total mixed ration was offered twice daily in equal amounts (2 × 300 g; total of 600 g per animal daily) at 08:00 and 18:00, and animals had free access to water.

In vitro ruminal batch culture was conducted as described by Wang et al. (2016). Briefly, each in vitro batch was conducted with mixed rumen inoculum from three donor goats before morning feeding, with the three batches conducted on different days. The rumen inoculum was prepared by filtering the rumen contents through four layers of sterile cheesecloth into a pre-warmed insulated bottle. Artificial saliva was prepared according to McDougall’s method [10], but the NaCl was replaced with an equimolar amount of NaHCO3 to maintain the chloride concentration unchanged after the 15NH4Cl supplementation, thereby avoiding an increase in Cl− levels that could affect microbial activity. The final pH values after fermentation (ranging from 6.34 to 6.56) were within the range observed in our previous in vitro studies using standard McDougall’s buffer [11, 12], suggesting that the modification did not compromise buffering capacity under the present experimental conditions. Buffered rumen inoculum was subsequently prepared by mixing rumen inoculum with artificial saliva at a ratio of 1 to 4. For bottles with NH4+ supplementation, the buffered rumen inoculum contained 8 mmol/L NH4+ enriched to 5 atom % 15N, prepared by diluting 15NH4Cl (≥ 98 atom % 15N) with unlabeled NH4Cl. Substrate (0.6 g; 0.3 g corn straw + 0.3 g corn meal) was weighed into a 140-mL serum bottle, and 60 mL of buffered rumen inoculum was added under a stream of CO2. For bottles with H2 supplementation, 40 mL of H2 was injected into the corresponding incubated bottles using a gas-tight syringe, and the same amount of headspace gas was removed from the bottles prior to H2 injection to maintain the ambient pressure.

The bottles were immediately sealed with butyl rubber stoppers, randomly assigned to treatments and incubated at 39.5℃ using an in vitro batch incubator. Headspace gas production and corresponding gases compositions were measured at 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, 36, and 48 h of incubation. Headspace gas production was measured based on the headspace pressure transducer (CYG130-12, Kunshan Shuangqiao Sensor Measurement Controlling Co., Ltd., Kunshan, Jiangsu, China), and headspace pressure was released through the utilization of a 50-mL graduated syringe until the reading in the manometer visual display equaled zero. A 6 mL of gas samples was collected from 50-mL graduated syringe, and injected into 3.5-mL evacuated glass tube for subsequent measurement. Tubes were stored at room temperature (approximately 22 °C) and analyzed for CH4 concentration within 6 h of collection to minimize diffusive loss.

For total gas production, CH4 production, total and individual VFA concentrations, and substrate degradation, the values from substrate‑containing bottles were corrected by subtracting the corresponding values from blank bottles. Other parameters (pH, ammonia‑N, isotopic abundances, and microbial protein production) were not blank‑corrected, as they are either unaffected by blank subtraction or are expressed relative to degraded substrate.

Sampling process

Fermentation fluid (35 mL of the 60 mL) from the four corresponding bottles (3 and 6 h, 2 bottles each timepoint) was used to extract the dissolved gases from liquid to gas phase following previously described procedures [13]. Briefly, A 35-mL subsample of fermentation fluid was expeditiously transferred into a 50-mL plastic syringe that was fitted with a three-way stopcock. The valve was then closed and the syringe was cooled to room temperature. The apparatus comprised a 20-mL syringe that had been filled with 10 mL of N2 gas and connected to the 50-mL plastic syringe via the three-way stopcock. Subsequently, the N2 gas was injected into the 50-mL syringe via the three-way stopcock. The syringe was shaken at a frequency of 200 r/min for a period of 5 min to extract the dissolved gases in the fermentation fluid into the N2 gas. The gas volume was recorded using the scales in the 20-mL syringe. The temperature of the room was also measured. Then two 6 mL gas samples from each 20-mL syringe were collected into two 3.5-mL evacuated tubes for the determination of gas composition.

The remaining four bottles were sampled at 48 h of incubation. The pH was measured from the four bottles immediately using a portable pH meter (Starter 300; Ohaus Instruments Co. Ltd, Shanghai, China). Two bottles were used for the samples collection of following parameters. Approximately 2 mL of liquid without particles was collected and centrifuged at 15,000 🞨 g for 10 min at 4 °C, the supernatant (1.5 mL) was acidified using 0.15 mL of 25% (w/v) metaphosphoric acid and stored at − 20 °C for measuring VFA and ammonia-N. Two 8-mL samples were collected after intense shaking for homogenizing the liquid and particle fractions, then isolating the rumen microorganisms by the methods modified from Czerkawski [14]. Briefly, the fermentation contents samples were mixed with an equal volume of saline, incubated in a water bath at 39℃, and shaken at 200 r/min for 1 h. The mixture was then strained through two layers of gauze, and the filtrate was centrifuged at 150🞨g for 10 min to sediment protozoa. The supernatant was then centrifuged at 22,000🞨g for 15 min to sediment bacteria. Both pellets were examined under a light microscope (SZ6100, Jiangnan Novel Optics Co., Ltd., Nanjing, China.) to confirm the absence of visible feed particles. The protozoal and bacterial pellets were then combined and thoroughly mixed to obtain a representative microbial sample for subsequent analysis of microbial N content, microbial 15N abundance, and 2H abundance. The combined microbial sample was dried in an oven at 60 °C for at least 24 h before analysis.

The incubation contents from the remaining corresponding two bottles were filtered into pre-weighed Gooch filter crucibles and dried at 105 °C until constant weight; further, they were weighed to calculate substrate degradation.

Analysis of fermentation end products

Methane concentration was measured using gas chromatograph (GC, Agilent 7890 A, Agilent Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA) with a flame ionization detector (FID). The high‑purity N2 was used as carrier gas at a flow rate of 2.0 mL/min. The inlet temperature was set at 200 °C. The H2 and air flows for the FID were set to 40 and 400 mL/min, respectively, and the detector temperature was maintained at 300 °C. The CH4 was separated by a HP-PLOT Q capillary column (30 m × 0.32 mm × 10 μm) maintained at 50 °C. The CH4 production at each time point was calculated according to the equation (VCH4 = Total gas volume × CH4 concentration), the total CH4 production was calculated by the sum of CH4 production at different time points. The H2 concentration were analyzed using GC with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). High‑purity N2 was used as both the carrier and reference gas. The carrier flow was 2.0 mL/min, and the reference flow was 20 mL/min. The detector temperature was set to 200 °C with the filament turned on. The H2 was separated by a Molsieve 5 A molecular sieve column (25 m × 0.53 mm × 30 μm) held at 50 °C. Dissolved hydrogen (dH2), and dissolved methane (dCH4) were then calculated according to the Herry’s Law using the equation proposed by Wang et al. [13].

Frozen acidified liquid samples were thawed and centrifuged at 15,000🞨g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the individual VFA concentration in the supernatants were measured using a gas chromatograph (Agilent 7890 A, Agilent Inc.) according to previously described methodology of Wang et al. [13]. The ammonia-N concentration in the liquid samples was measured according to methods described by Chaney and Marbach [15].

Analysis of isotopic 2H and 15N in microbial biomass

The abundances of 2H and 15N in the microbial biomass were measured using an elemental analyzer (Flash 2000; Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) according to the methods modified from Lehn et al. [16] and Hyodo et al. [17], respectively. For the details, solid samples were freeze-dried, ground to a fine powder, and weighed (1.0–2.0 mg) into tin capsules for 15N analysis or silver capsules for 2H analysis. All capsules were sealed without protruding spikes. Isotope ratios were measured using a Thermo Scientific Flash 2000 HT elemental analyzer coupled to a MAT253 isotope ratio mass spectrometer (IRMS) via a ConFlo IV interface (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). For 15N, samples were combusted at 1020 °C in an oxygen-rich atmosphere, the resulting N2 was separated on a GC column (50 °C) and introduced into the IRMS. For 2H, samples were pyrolyzed at 1400 °C in a glassy carbon reactor, and H2 was analyzed under continuous helium flow (100 mL·min⁻¹). The MAT253 was operated at 10 kV with Faraday cups for masses 28/29 (N2) and 2/3 (H2). The H3+ factor was maintained below 10 ppm·nA⁻¹. Raw data were corrected for drift and linearity using ISODAT software. δ15N values are reported relative to AIR-N2, and δ2H relative to VSMOW, with analytical precisions of ≤ 0.3‰ and ≤ 3.0‰, respectively.

Analysis of isotopic 2H in VFA

Frozen acidified liquid samples were thawed and centrifuged at 15,000🞨g for 10 min at 4 °C, and the supernatants (200 µL) were added 24 µL HCl (2 mol/L), then vortexed for 5s, followed by the addition of 360 µL of cold Methyl tert-butyl ether (MTBE). The mixture was vigorously vortexed for 15 s and centrifuged at 3,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. Approximately, 60 µL of the upper MTBE layer was recovered and stored at -20 °C until analysis. After sample preparation the 2H analysis in VFA using gas chromatography mass spectrometry with a high-temperature conversion interface and isotope ratio mass spectrometry (GC-MS-HTC-IRMS; DeltaV advantage, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) with procedures adapted from those reported by Strojnik et al. [18] and Li et al. [6]. An autosampler was used to inject 1µL of sample into the Trace GC, which was equipped with a splitless injector and a capillary column (DB-FFAP, Agilent, 30 m*0.25 mm*0.25 μm). The inlet temperature was set at 250 °C, and helium was used as the carrier gas with a flow rate of 1.2 mL min− 1. The GC oven temperature was initially held at 60 °C for 2 min, followed by an increase to 220 °C at a rate of 20 °C min− 1, where it was held isothermally for 0.5 min.

For 2H analysis, each compound was passed through a high temperature reactor and subjected to high-temperature pyrolysis to develop H2 gas. The isotopic composition of H in the working reference materials was then measured using HTC-IRMS. The values are denoted in delta in relation to the international Vienna-Standard Mean Ocean Water (V-SMOW) for δ2H. Data were collected and calculated using the Isodat 3.0 software (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The H3+ factor was measured before and after a series of sample analyses and ranged over the course of all analyses less than 10 ppm/nA in a linear range of 1 to 10 V.

Within each batch, the values of the technical replicate bottles per treatment were averaged to obtain a single value per treatment per batch. The three independent batches served as the experimental units. Results

Gases production and rumen fermentation

The 2H2 supplementation increased dry matter (DM) degradation (P < 0.01) and CH4 production (P < 0.001), but decreased total gas production (P < 0.01). The 15NH4+ supplementation increased ammonia-N concentration (P < 0.05) and decreased CH4 production (P < 0.001, Table 1). The 2H2 supplementation increased pH from approximately 6.34 to 6.56. The 2H2 supplementation increased total VFA and acetate productions (P < 0.05) with a decreased butyrate production (P < 0.01), whereas 15NH4+ supplementation did not alter the VFA profile. 2H2 supplementation decreased dissolved hydrogen (dH2) concentration at 3 h (P = 0.071) and 6 h (P < 0.01) of incubation (Fig.1), whereas 15NH4+ supplementation increased dH2 concentration at 3 h of incubation. Meanwhile, the 2H2 plus 15NH4+ supplementation increased dissolved CH4 (dCH4) concentration at 6 h of incubation.

Discussion

Effects on dissolved gases at early stage of fermentation

Rumen gases exist in two forms: dissolved gases in liquids and gases in headspace, the two forms of gases can be converted into each other according to equilibrium solubility of Henry’s law [13]. The dH2 in liquid phase can be available for ruminal microorganisms, and typically present in a supersaturated state [19]. This indicates that an increase in headspace gH2 concentration does not necessarily correspond to a proportional rise in the concentration of dH2. Additionally, the low solubility of H2 in liquid phase limits the conversion of gH2 to dH2 [13, 20]. In our study, despite supplementation of significant amount of gH2 (40 mL) was infused into the headspace of bottle, the gH2 appeared to be poorly retained in rumen liquid phase, leading to an inconsistent result in dH2 concentration. Interestingly, 2H2 supplementation showed a lower dH2 concentration, which may indicate enhanced H2 consumption under elevated H2 partial pressures, such as hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis and acetogenesis [5, 6, 21]. Under the specific conditions of present study, the elevated headspace H2 pressures appeared to stimulate H2 consumption for VFA and CH4 production, thereby enhancing rumen fermentation and reducing dH2 concentration. Additionally, the reduced and inconsistent dH2 levels following 2H2 supplementation may be influenced by the distinct physicochemical properties of hydrogen isotopes. Gas solubility can be influenced by multiple factors, such as molecular size, polarizability, and solvent interactions. Experimental data showed that 2H2 displays lower solubility in water than H2 (approximately 1.3 mg/L compared to 1.6 mg/L under standard conditions) [20, 22]. This difference is consistent with known isotope effects on molecular interactions, such as changes in zero‑point energy and polarizability, which can affect solvation. Therefore, the lower solubility of 2H2 may partially account for the observed reduction in dH2 concentration. Other factors, including potential isotope effects on enzymatic reactions, differences in sampling timing, and analytical variability, can also contribute to the observed reduction in dH2 concentration.

Effects on total gas and methane production

The decrease in total gas production after 2H2 supplementation may be partly explained by the utilization of H2 and CO2 for hydrogenotrophic methanogenesis (CO2 + 4H2 → CH4 + 2H2O) and acetogenesis (2CO2 + 4H2 → CH3COOH + 2H2O), as revealed by the observed increase in CH4 and acetate production. The enhanced H2 availability has the potential to stimulate these H2-consuming pathways, thereby increasing the overall molar consumption of gaseous substrates (H2 and CO2), and likely contributing to a net decrease in total gas volume. However, in consideration of the complexity of gas-liquid equilibrium in the closed in vitro system, other factors such as differences in gas solubility and isotope effects associated with 2H2 may also influence the total gas volume.

Methanogenesis serves as the primary pathway for H2 disposal in the rumen [21], and is stimulated by increased H2 partial pressures [23]. As anticipated, 2H2 supplementation led to an increase in dCH4 concentration at 6 h of incubation with greater CH4 production after 48 h of incubation. This finding indicates that additional H2 supplementation promoted methanogenesis, which aligns with previous in vitro [24] and in vivo [25] studies. Additionally, the higher dry matter degradation and VFA production could also help increase the CH4 production, because the process of carbohydrate degradation to acetate production produces hydrogen, which facilitates methanogenesis [21].

In contrast, 15NH4+ supplementation decreased CH4 production. During anaerobic processes, the methanogens have low tolerance and likely to stop growth due to NH₄⁺ inhibition [26]. Our previous study found that the concentrations of NH4+ above 15 mM could have an inhibitory effect on methanogens [12]. However, such CH4 reduction at present study was unlikely to be attributed to the inhibitory effects of NH₄⁺, as the concentration of ammonia-N measured in this study was maintained below 15 mM (approximately 12 mM in bottles supplemented with NH4⁺). In addition, the incorporation of ammonia-N into carbon chains in microbial protein synthesis associates with reductant disposal, and enhancing this process can decrease the electron availability for H2 production and inhibit the methanogenesis subsequently [7].

Effects on fermentative degradation and VFA production

The DM degradation was promoted by the 2H2 supplementation, indicating that enhancement of H2 supply capacity could improve the microbial activity for carbohydrate degradation, which consequently increase the total VFA production. The 2H2 supplementation also increased final pH from approximately 6.34 to 6.56. This pH range falls within the typical range for in vitro rumen fermentation systems. When the pH value changes from 6 to 7, a higher pH value may promote rumen fermentation [27], which is consistent with the greater VFA production after H2 supplementation. Therefore, the higher pH attributed to H2 supplementation could facilitate the substrate degradation and VFA production.

Many hydrogenotrophic microbes can use H2 for VFA production and contribute significantly to energy harvesting pathways in ruminants [5, 6, 8]. The 2H2 supplementation markedly increased the acetate, butyrate and propionate productions after 48 h of in vitro ruminal incubation. Hydrogenotrophic acetogens, having the function of converting H2 and CO2 into acetate via reductive Wood-Ljungdahl pathway, is widespread and active within rumen microbiome [8, 28]. It was not surprising that 2H2 supplementation markedly increased acetate concentration after 48 h of in vitro ruminal incubation. The elevated acetate concentration can in turn stimulate butyrate production, as many butyrate-producing bacteria are capable of converting acetate into butyrate [29].

Another competitive pathway for H2 utilization is to reduce dicarboxylic acids, such as fumarate, for propionate production, which is carried out by hydrogenotrophic fumarate-reducers [30]. In line with this, our study found that 2H2 supplementation increased propionate concentration after 48 h of in vitro ruminal incubation. These findings further support the widespread and active hydrogenotrophic bacteria within rumen microecosystem [6]. Additionally, the increased incorporation of inorganic H2 into VFA production underscores the potential of 2H2 supplementation to enhance VFA production, although the specific contribution of hydrogenotrophic products to total fermentative VFA production warrants further investigation.

Effects on fermentative microbial protein synthesis

The process of amination during microbial protein synthesis is primarily facilitated by the glutamate dehydrogenase pathway and the glutamine synthetase-glutamate synthase pathway [31, 32], both of which are closely associated with electron incorporation [33]. However, the absence effect of 2H2 supplementation alone on microbial N production might due to the undersupply of NH4+, which limit the amination process. When combined with 15NH4⁺ supplementation, the 2H2 increased microbial protein production, indicates stimulated hydrogenotrophic process that contribute to amination.

Previous studies indicate that rumen microbial protein production can increase by up to 16% when ruminal ammonia-N concentration rises (from 7.83 mmol/L to 10.89 mmol/L) through the feeding of urea-treated rice straw to dairy steers [34], which is consistent with the increased microbial protein production by NH4⁺ supplementation in present study. Additionally, we observed a significant interaction effect between H2 and NH4+ supplementation during microbial protein synthesis, with the highest microbial N production occurring under combined H2 and NH4+ supplementation. The interaction indicates a synergistic effect, the increase in microbial protein production with combined supplementation was greater than the sum of the individual main effects. This synergy suggests that the availability of both reducing equivalents (from H2) and ammonia‑N promotes more efficient nitrogen incorporation into microbial protein.

Effects on the proton transfer in VFA and microbial protein synthesis

Although 2H2 supplementation enhanced hydrogenotrophic pathways involved in VFA production, the absolute abundance of 2H recovered in individual VFA remained relatively low (averaging less than 1.5‰). This low level of isotopic enrichment may reflect several concurrent phenomena. Firstly, the large pool of unlabeled hydrogen atoms already presents in water and organic substrates within the fermentation medium likely exerts a strong dilution effect on newly incorporated protons derived from 2H2. Moreover, extensive hydrogen exchange between water and metabolic intermediates during VFA biosynthesis can obscure the direct incorporation of protons from gaseous H2.

Nevertheless, the observed promotion of VFA production with 2H2 supplementation clearly indicates a stimulation of fermentation. The low 2H enrichment does not preclude the involvement of protons in VFA synthesis; rather, it suggests that the bulk of the stimulatory effect on VFA production may be primarily mediated by enhanced electron flow. Electrons derived from H2 serve as the electron donors within the microbial ecosystem, and the electron-bifurcating hydrogenase can reversibly bifurcate electrons from H2 to NAD+ and ferredoxin [35]. Homoacetogenic acetate production, for example, involves the incorporation of electron from H2 via reductive Wood-Ljungdahl pathway [36]. Propionate and butyrate represent largest electron sinks within the rumen ecosystem, accounting for about one-third of the electrons generated during rumen microbial metabolism [37]. Therefore, the additional 2H2 supplementation provides electrons for redox cofactors and could promote the rumen fermentation with greater VFA production.

Similarly, the low 2H enrichment in microbial protein (< 0.3‰) suggests limited direct proton incorporation, and the observed synergy likely operates through electron transfer rather than direct proton transfer. The increase in microbial protein production is consistent with the interpretation that enhanced electron supply, rather than direct proton incorporation, may support reductive amination pathways during amino acid synthesis [33]. However, the low 2H enrichment in microbial protein does not conclusively rule out proton involvement, as isotopic dilution and hydrogen exchange with metabolic intermediates could mask direct label incorporation. Further studies employing flux analysis or position-specific labeling are needed to disentangle the relative contributions of electrons and protons derived from H2.

Conclusion

The H2 supplementation facilitates the VFA production, likely through the activity of hydrogenotrophic microorganisms. Although direct incorporation of hydrogen protons into VFA and microbial protein appears limited under the present experimental conditions, the stimulatory effects of H2 supplementation on these products may be primarily mediated by increased electron availability. However, given the potential for isotopic dilution and hydrogen exchange in the closed system, the precise contributions of electrons versus protons derived from H2 warrant further investigation. NH4+ supplementation facilitated microbial protein synthesis with an enhancement of electron incorporation, which could help reduce CH4 production. The combined supplementation of H2 and NH4+ showed a synergistic effect on promoting microbial protein synthesis. This study highlighted the value of the dual-isotope (2H and 15N) tracer design in linking hydrogen and nitrogen metabolism in the rumen. As the batch culture system used here may lead to metabolite accumulation that could mask or inhibit certain microbial activities, future in vivo studies are needed to validate these findings under physiological conditions.
