Levels of evidence supporting American, European and international guidelines in psychiatry, 2014–2024: a systematic review with quantitative synthesis
Authors: Troels Boldt Rømer, Signe N. Andersson, Michael Eriksen Benros
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractQuestionTo what extent are psychiatry guidelines supported by high-level evidence?Study selection and analysisGuidelines from the American Psychiatric Association, European Psychiatric Association, WHO and World Federation of Societies for Biological Psychiatry (2014–2024) were selected. Recommendations were graded by guideline authors’ levels of evidence (LOE) appraisal (standardised to the Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development and Evaluations framework (high, moderate, low, very low)) and by the highest-level study referenced (meta-analysis, Randomisedrandomised Controlled Tri controlled trial (RCT), observational study, expert opinion, etc.).Findings24 guidelines, containing 545 recommendations, were included. Of 82 guidelines screened, 29 (35%) had not been updated in a decade. 63 (11.6%) recommendations were rated by guideline authors as based on high LOE. The proportion was the highest for pharmacotherapies (41/281 (14.6%)) and the lowest for somatic assessment (0/13 (0%)). The proportion of high LOE recommendations varied between publishers (European Psychiatric Association: 20 %, WHO: 1.6 %). For high LOE recommendations, only those concerning pharmacotherapies cited meta-analyses based on double-blind studies using adequate controls. A large proportion (n=241 (44.2%)) of recommendations cited either a meta-analysis of RCTs (n=155 (28.4%)) or ≥two RCTs (n=86 (15.8 %)). There were few recommendations primarily addressing self-harm (n=2), autism (n=3), attention-deficit/hyperactivity disorder (n=3), prevention (n=3), patient involvement (n=3) or discontinuation (n=6).ConclusionsClinical guidelines in psychiatry frequently cite RCTs, but the evidence is often downgraded by guideline authors, highlighting the need for better quality trials. LOE varies across areas, with pharmacotherapies supported by the highest quality evidence. Organisations should commit to a timely update of guidelines covering all areas of psychiatry.

Background

Evidence-based medicine integrates high-level evidence with clinical judgement and patient preferences as the foundation for clinical decision-making.1 High-level evidence is often obtained from randomised controlled trials (RCTs), in which randomisation is used to distribute confounders equally among treatment and control groups, thereby reducing the risk of bias.2 RCTs can provide estimates of causal effects (both beneficial and harmful) related to clinical decisions such as initiation of treatment, screening and discontinuation of care—particularly if trials are well blinded and use adequate controls. Clinical guideline recommendations are frequently graded according to the level of evidence (LOE) supporting each recommendation, ranging from high-level evidence (evidence from RCTs, meta-analyses of RCTs or—to a lesser extent—consistent conclusions from high-quality observational studies) to low-level evidence (recommendations based on case studies or expert opinion only). Ideally, guidelines should be based primarily on high-level evidence from RCTs to enable physicians and patients to evaluate expected benefits and harms of care. However, clinical practice and trial conduct are often not well integrated.3 As a result, only 5%–20% of guideline recommendations are supported by high-level evidence within fields such as cardiology,4 intensive care5 and infectious disease medicine.6

Objective

Currently, studies on the LOE across major international guidelines within psychiatry are lacking, and only one smaller, prior study has evaluated the LOE supporting recommendations within national psychiatry guidelines in Germany and Scotland.7 Furthermore, no studies have provided data on the LOE supporting recommendations within different diagnostic groups or across types of recommendations (pharmacological treatments, non-pharmacological treatments, organisation of treatment, etc.) in psychiatry. Our study is the first to systematically quantify the LOEs in psychiatry guidelines from highly influential societies and organisation across America, Europe and internationally using comparable evidence evaluation methods.

Study selection and analysis

A study protocol was pre-registered prior to data extraction (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/M7YSF).

Data sources

Guidelines were identified on the websites of the American Psychiatric Association, European Psychiatric Association (EPA), World Federation of Societies for Biological Psychiatry (WFSBP) and WHO through the links listed in the protocol on 15 July 2024, with an updated search on 22 June 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Guidelines were included if they:

Were published in 2014–2024 (both years included). Older guidelines were considered legacy guidelines, which do not represent current treatment recommendations.

Were published in 2014–2024 (both years included). Older guidelines were considered legacy guidelines, which do not represent current treatment recommendations.

Addressed mental disorders and clinical management thereof.

Addressed mental disorders and clinical management thereof.

Listed recommendations graded by LOE and clearly cited evidence for individual recommendation.

Listed recommendations graded by LOE and clearly cited evidence for individual recommendation.

Were in English.

Were in English.

Non-guideline documents, such as policy papers, were not included. If two or more guidelines on the same topic were published by the same publisher, only the most recent guideline was included.

Recommendations regarding child and adolescent mental health were included if they were specified in the guidelines from the pre-selected publishers.

Data extraction

For each recommendation, we recorded the type of recommendation (pharmacological treatment, organisation of treatment, psychiatric assessment, etc.) and the primary diagnostic category (schizophrenia/psychosis, bipolar disorder, general psychiatry, etc.). We recorded the strength (strong/weak) and the authors’ grading of evidence for each recommendation. However, different tools were used across publishers, and we therefore standardised these to The Grading of Recommendations, Assessment, Development, and Evaluations (GRADE) framework, with each recommendation graded as based on high, moderate, low or very low-level evidence (online supplemental tables S1 and S2).8 Furthermore, we recorded the study at the highest LOE cited in the evidence appraisal for each recommendation (meta-analysis of RCTs, multiple RCTs, single RCT, etc.). Based on this, we computed a score derived from the widely used American College of Cardiology/American Heart Association (ACC/AHA) classification system, where class A indicates evidence from meta-analyses of RCTs or multiple RCTs, class B indicates data from a single randomised controlled study or observational studies and class C indicates data from cases or expert opinion only.9 For RCTs, we recorded the comparator condition (active control and placebo, active control, placebo, treatment as usual or waitlist/no treatment) and blinding (double, single or unblind). Data were extracted by one reviewer (TBR). A second reviewer (SNA) validated all extracted data (including evidence appraisal by guideline authors, the highest-level study cited and RCT characteristics) and re-appraised classifications of recommendations into diagnostic categories and recommendation type. A third author was asked in case of disagreements (MEB). Guideline quality was assessed using the Appraisal of Guidelines for Research and Evaluation (AGREE) II tool, domain 3 (‘Rigour of Development’) by one reviewer (TBR).

Evaluation of high-quality recommendations

In a post-hoc analysis, we evaluated the meta-analyses supporting the recommendations which appeared to be based on the best-quality evidence (ie, were based on high LOE according to authors (GRADE) and cited a meta-analysis of RCTs). We evaluated the cited meta-analyses by examining if they provided effect estimates supporting the recommendation based exclusively on studies that (a) were double-blind (participants and outcome assessors) and (b) used placebo, sham or active control interventions with comparable dose, length and intensity to that of the intervention group.

Statistics and data presentation

We report the number and proportion of recommendations by GRADE appraisal, ACC/AHA classification and study type overall and by disorder, publisher, year, type and class of recommendations. Logistic regression was used to test for associations between publication year and proportion of moderate/high-level versus low/very low-level recommendations. We report the percentage of recommendations with high LOE as compared with findings from studies conducted in other medical specialties, as prespecified in the protocol.4610 All analyses were conducted in R (V.4.3.2).13

Patient and public involvement

There was no direct involvement of patients or the public in design or conduct of the study.

Findings

82 guidelines were screened for eligibility and 24 were included. The primary reason for exclusion was publication prior to 2014 and no subsequent update (n=29). In total, 545 recommendations were extracted.14 For the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses diagram and checklist, see online supplemental figure S1 and table S3. For a list of all included and excluded guidelines, see online supplemental tables S4 and S5.

Guideline authors’ evidence appraisal (standardised to GRADE)

Overall, 63/545 (11.6 %) recommendations were classified by guideline authors as being based on high LOE, 165/545 (30.3%) as moderate LOE, 128/545 (23.5%) as low LOE and 189/545 (34.7%) as very low LOE (table 1, figure 1a). In general, strong recommendations were more often based on high (22.9%) or moderate (45.0%) LOE than weak recommendations (0.4% and 15.5%, respectively) (table 1). In general, high LOE recommendations cited ACC/AHA level A evidence (77.8%) more frequently than moderate (50.3%), low (57.0%) or very low (19.0%) LOE recommendations (online supplemental table S6). The proportion of recommendations based on high or moderate level evidence did not show a significant trend during the study period (p=0.07) (online supplemental figure S2). The proportion of recommendations rated as based on high LOE varied by guideline publisher, with 20.0% of recommendations rated as high LOE for EPA and 1.6% for WHO (figure 1c, table 1).

Studies cited in clinical practice guidelines

Overall, a large proportion of recommendations cited either a meta-analysis of RCTs (155/545 (28.4%)), multiple RCTs (86/545 (15.8%)) or a single RCT (81/545 (14.9%)) (online supplemental table S7); however, a considerable number of recommendations only cited case series (47/545 (8.6%)) or expert opinion (85/545 (15.6%)).

Converting these findings to the ACC/AHA classification, 241/545 (44.2%) recommendations cited class A evidence (multiple RCTs), 167/545 (30.6%) cited class B evidence (single RCT or observational studies) and 137/545 (25.1%) cited class C evidence (table 1, figure 1b). A noteworthy minority of strong recommendations only cited class C evidence (44/249 (17.7%)) (table 1). The types of studies cited by guideline publishers varied substantially, as the WHO primarily cited class A evidence (57/61 (93.4%)), while numbers were lower for other publishers, for example, WFSBP (88/245 (35.9%)) (table 1).

LOE by types of recommendations

The number of recommendations varied by type, with only three recommendations mainly concerning patient involvement and 281 concerning pharmacotherapy (table 1, figure 2a). For some recommendation types, ≤10 recommendations were identified (patient information and involvement, prevention, discontinuation of treatment and specification of treatment goals) (table 1). For the remaining types, the number of recommendations based on high LOE (as appraised by authors) varied substantially, with the highest proportion for pharmacological treatment (41/281 (14.6%)) and the lowest for organisation of treatment (1/46 (2.2%)) and somatic assessment of patient with severe mental disorders (0/13 (0%)) (table 1, figure 2a).

The types of studies cited likewise varied between types of recommendations (figure 2c, online supplemental table S7). For non-pharmacological treatments, 80/100 (80.0%) of recommendations cited class A evidence (meta-analysis of RCTs or multiple RCTs) while the same was the case for 131/281 (46.6%) of recommendations on pharmacotherapies and 21/46 (45.7%) of recommendations on organisation of treatment (table 1). LOE by diagnostic groups

The number of recommendations recorded for each diagnostic group varied from 152 recommendations primarily concerning schizophrenia/psychosis to two recommendations primarily concerning suicide/self-harm (table 1, figure 2b). For some diagnostic groups (autism, anxiety disorders, ADHD and suicide/self-harm), specific recommendations were only given in the WHO guidelines and counts were therefore small (<10). For the remaining diagnostic groups, the proportion of recommendations based on high LOE (as rated by authors) was the highest for unipolar depression (15/34 (44.1%)) and schizophrenia/psychosis (18/152 (11.8%)) and the lowest for organic disorders (1/21 (4.8%)) (table 1). For all diagnostic groups, a substantial number of recommendations were not based on high or moderate LOE (figure 2b, table 1).

The types of studies cited varied between diagnostic groups (figure 2d, table 1 and online supplemental table S7), ranging from 21/34 (61.8%) of recommendations citing class A evidence (meta-analysis of RCTs, multiple RCTs) in unipolar depression to 28/118 (23.7%) for eating disorders (among diagnostic groups with >10 recommendations).

Characteristics of cited RCTs

In total, 322 (59.1%) recommendations cited at least one RCT (online supplemental table S7). Of these, 212 (65.8%) cited at least one double-blind RCT, 83 (25.8%) cited a single-blind RCT and 27 (8.4%) only cited open-label RCTs (online supplemental table S8). Blinding varied across types of recommendations, with the majority (57/84 (68%)) of recommendation citing single-blind RCTs for non-pharmacological treatments and double-blind RCTs (189/204 (93%)) for pharmacological treatments (figure 3a, online supplemental table S8).

Recommendations frequently cited RCTs using placebo (107/322 (33.2%)), an active control (89/322 (27.6%)) or both (98/322 (30.4%)) as control conditions (online supplemental table S9). However, some recommendations only cited RCTs using treatment as usual (24/322 (7.5%)) or no treatment (4/322 (1.2%)) as control condition. RCTs using placebo control were often cited by recommendations on pharmacological treatments, either alone (102/204 (51%)) or in combination with active controls (80/204 (39%)), whereas recommendations on non-pharmacological treatments frequently cited RCTs using active controls (56/84 (67%)) (figure 3b, online supplemental table S9).

Evaluation of high-quality recommendation

25 recommendations were rated as high LOE and cited at least one meta-analysis (figure 3c). This represents a subset of the 63 high LOE recommendations, as the majority cited study designs other than meta-analyses. Of the cited meta-analyses, 8/25 provided effect estimates based only on double-blind studies using placebo, sham or an adequate active control (equivalent to 1.5% of all recommendations (n=545)). All of these were on pharmacotherapies. 11/25 only provided estimates based on RCTs that were not double-blind and did not use comparable control conditions. All of these were on non-pharmacological treatments, treatment organisation or psychiatric assessment.

Comparison against other medical specialties

In a pre-specified analysis, we compared our results to a priori identified studies from other medical specialties. When comparing the proportion of recommendation based on high LOE, results varied between 5%–23% from specialties including infectious disease medicine,6 intensive care,5 nephrology,10 cardiology,4 anaesthesiology12 and rheumatology (figure 1d).11

Sensitivity analysis

Using AGREE II Domain 3 (Rigour of Development) scores, we classified guidelines as being high quality if scores were ≥70% (6 of 24 guidelines; online supplemental table S4). Limiting the primary analysis to high-quality guidelines resulted in a lower proportion of high LOE recommendations (3/155 (1.3%)), but a higher proportion of recommendations citing Class A evidence (93/155 (60.0%)) (online supplemental table S10). In a separate sensitivity analysis, limited to guidelines published in the last 5 years of the study period (2019–2024), resulted in a similar proportion of high LOE recommendations as in the primary analysis (30/311 (9.6%)) and a slightly larger proportion citing Class A evidence (154/311 (49.5%)) (online supplemental table S11).

Discussion

In this first systematic review on the evidence level supporting clinical practice guidelines in psychiatry from major American, European and international bodies, we found that guideline authors rated just 11.6% of recommendations as supported by high-level evidence. However, more than 40% of recommendations cited at least two RCTs in their evidence appraisal. The proportion of recommendations based on high-level evidence varied between diagnostic categories (from 44.1% for unipolar depression to 4.8% for organic disorders) and types of recommendations (from 14.6% for pharmacological treatments to 0% for somatic assessment of patients with mental disorders) and guideline publishers (20.0% for EPA and 1.6% for WHO). In the cited meta-analyses supporting recommendations rated as high LOE, only recommendations regarding pharmacotherapies provided effect estimates based solely on double-blind studies using placebo, sham or an adequate active control. Recommendations from guidelines showing a stronger rigour of development (AGREE II Domain 3 scores ≥70 %) tended to cite more Class A evidence (60.0 %), but contained fewer recommendations rated as based on high LOE (1.3%).

The proportion of recommendations evaluated as high LOE in psychiatric guidelines is comparable to estimates from other medical specialties, where studies find between 5% and 23% of recommendations to be based on high LOE.4610 Of note, studies in different specialties use different methodologies to assess the strength of evidence. While our comparison was limited to studies using either the GRADE approach or the ACC/AHA classification, estimates are not directly comparable, and cross-speciality findings should not be over-interpreted. However, these estimates point to the common challenge of limited high-quality data from randomised controlled trials in many areas of medicine.

We identify a gap between the proportion of recommendations considered high-level by guideline authors (11.6%) and the number of recommendations citing at least two RCTs (44.2%). This indicates that studies which are, by design, considered high-level evidence are ‘downgraded’ by guideline authors. While the reason for this is not directly addressed in the study, our findings suggest that available RCTs provide sub-optimal evidence for guideline formulation. Reasons could include that RCTs only provide indirect evidence, do not address clinically relevant questions, show conflicting results, do not include clinically relevant populations or that the quality of the RCTs may be hampered by low power, unblinding, missing data on outcomes and side effects and use irrelevant endpoints and publication bias—all of which are issues previously reported for clinical trials in general15 16 or trials in psychiatry specifically.1723 Nonetheless, most recommendations referencing RCTs do cite double-blind, placebo-controlled or active-controlled studies; however, proportions vary between types of recommendations. Recommendations not addressing pharmacological treatments were almost exclusively based on open-label or single-blind studies. While blinding is inherently difficult for, for example, psychological interventions, our findings align with previous studies, which call for stronger attempts to control for expectation bias and, to the extent possible, blind assessors and patients in trials of non-pharmacological interventions.18

The choice of recording the study at the highest LOE cited by a recommendation (such as an RCT) ignores that the largest body of evidence supporting a recommendation might come from other types of studies (such as observational studies). To provide a more complete picture of the evidence base, we selected recommendations which appeared to be based on the best quality evidence (ie, citing meta-analyses of RCTs while being graded as based on high LOE by authors). Of these meta-analyses, only 32% (all on pharmacological treatments) provided effect estimates solely based on double-blind RCTs with relevant comparator conditions. This illustrates how results from studies with high and low risk of bias are often combined in meta-analyses, potentially biasing overall effect estimates.

Evidence supporting guidelines is, at best, a surrogate marker for the evidence supporting clinical practice in psychiatry. First, guidelines might not be adhered to in clinical practice. Second, not all recommendations have equal impact. For example, a recommendation to initiate an anti-depressant for a moderate, unipolar depressive episode will affect many patients, whereas a recommendation to initiate clozapine for treatment-resistant schizophrenia will affect a smaller number. Therefore, to conclude that 11.6% of recommendations in psychiatry are based on high-level evidence does not mean that only 11.6% of clinical decisions are based on high-level evidence.

Even though evidence from RCTs is considered the reference standard, one may argue that RCTs might not be needed when sufficiently strong observational data supports an intervention24; when there is no question of equipoise; when randomisation is unethical or infeasible; or when the investigated intervention represents a basic human right (such as housing or access to healthcare as such).25 In addition, certain types of recommendation (eg, regarding patient involvement or experience of care) are not adequately studied in RCTs and other approaches, such as qualitative research or mixed methods, may be more relevant. However, as interventions (both pharmacological and non-pharmacological) have the potential to both be beneficial and harmful, high-level evidence from RCTs is needed to assess risk-benefit ratios.2629 Increasing the number of interventional recommendations backed by well-conducted RCTs in psychiatry is therefore of utmost importance. Furthermore, the discrepancy between LOE between different diagnostic groups and different types of interventions may, in part, be explained by unequal allocation of resources and the high costs of conducting RCTs. More equitable funding structures and use of innovations in trial methodology, such as platform trials, decentralised trials or pragmatic trials with automated data collection through electronic health records, could help close this gap.

Many guidelines screened (29 out of 82) were excluded as they had not been updated within the last decade. Research within psychiatry is evolving continuously, and guidance on the treatment of important conditions, such as unipolar depression, should have been updated within the last decade. Using novel approaches, such as the living systematic review methodology, could ensure a timelier update of the current evidence base. Development and update of guidelines are no trivial undertaking, but often have large impact on clinical practice. Institutional commitment to updates within shorter timeframes (years rather than decades) and allocation of resources to guideline development from relevant funding bodies should be prioritised as a part of research dissemination and quality improvement.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This pre-registered study represents the first comprehensive investigation of the evidence-level supporting clinical practice guidelines addressing mental disorders across the largest international psychiatric societies and the WHO. Furthermore, we provide data by types of recommendations and diagnostic groups. By combining authors' appraisals with an examination of the types of studies cited, we provide a comprehensive appraisal of the evidence base compared with only using authors’ evidence appraisal (which can be biased by pre-existing beliefs and conflicts of interest) or only evaluating study characteristics (which discards context-specific knowledge possessed by authors).

The study has several limitations. When accessing the studies cited by recommendations, we did not evaluate the extent to which the cited studies agreed with the recommendation nor did we evaluate the quality of the individual, cited studies. The study did not appraise the extent to which guidelines cited the same studies, evaluated the evidence similarly or recommended similar actions. We did not access whether recommendations pertaining to specific patient demographics, such as female, elderly or minority patients, differed in their evidence base. Other guidelines could have been included in the present study, such as those published by the National Institute of Clinical Excellence (NICE).30 However, NICE does not consistently grade the LOE supporting individual recommendation and does not list evidence supporting individual recommendations, but rather evidence supporting thematically grouped recommendations. An appraisal of NICE guidelines is therefore outside the methodological scope of this study. While we sought to pre-select guideline publishers representing agenda-setting institutions within psychiatry, the results of our findings may not generalise to guidelines from other geographical regions or subfields. This, for example, pertains to child and adolescent mental health, which none of the publishers have as their primary area of focus. In addition, while international guidelines from WHO and WFSBP were included, guidelines from middle- and low-income countries were not accessed (primarily due to language barriers) and potential inequities could not be investigated.

Conclusions and clinical implications

A total of 11.6% of clinical practice recommendations in psychiatry are backed by high-level evidence, which is comparable to findings in other medical specialties. However, more than 40% of recommendations cite at least two RCTs in their evidence appraisal, suggesting that authors downgrade evidence from RCTs and, therefore, future trials in psychiatry should provide better quality data to inform clinical decision-making. This could be done through the design of larger trials with adequate blinding, sufficient control conditions and use of relevant outcomes. Evidence levels vary substantially between types of recommendations and diagnostic categories. Funding bodies and researchers should prioritise the design and execution of high-quality, pragmatic trials in under-evidenced areas such as the organisation of care and management of somatic comorbidities. Furthermore, a shared approach to evidence appraisal across psychiatry guidelines should ideally be implemented to facilitate transparency and societies should commit to continuous updates of published guidelines.
