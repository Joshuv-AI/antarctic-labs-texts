Striatal Tau Pathology Underlies Monoaminergic Disruption in Progressive Supranuclear Palsy
Authors: Chong Dong, Jing-Hong Ma, Hong-Wen Qiao, Wen-Qi Xu, Zhu-Qin Gu, Olivier Barret, Gilles D. Tamagnan, Wei Mao, Er-He Xu, Chun Zhang, Jie Lu, Piu Chan, Shu-Ying Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Purpose:Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) is a tauopathy marked by widespread tau aggregation and prominent monoaminergic dysfunction, yet the relationship between regional tau burden and monoaminergic impairment remains unclear. In this study, we determine whether regional tau burden is associated with nigrostriatal monoaminergic dysfunction in PSP using PET imaging.Patients and Methods:A subset of 20 patients with PSP and monoaminergic impairment, identified by 18F-FP-DTBZ PET, was recruited and subsequently underwent 18F-APN-1607 PET to assess tau deposition. All images were spatially normalized and analyzed based on predefined volumes of interest. Erosion-based cerebral white matter was used as the reference region to calculate standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) for 18F-APN-1607 PET.Results:The SUVRs of 18F-APN-1607 PET differed significantly between patients with PSP and healthy controls in the globus pallidus, substantia nigra, subthalamus nucleus, and red nucleus, while a significant difference in 18F-FP-DTBZ SUVRs was observed in all the striatal regions and substantia nigra. Regression analysis discovered negative associations between tau load and monoaminergic disruption in the putamen and caudate, whereas tau accumulation in the substantia nigra was not significantly associated with nigrostriatal impairment.Conclusions:The pallido-nigro-luysian axis and nigrostriatal system serve as sensitive targets for detecting tau deposition and monoaminergic disruption in PSP. Local striatal tau pathology and tau burden were associated with monoaminergic disruption in PSP.

Progressive supranuclear palsy (PSP) is a progressive neurodegenerative disorder belonging to the category of atypical parkinsonism, characterized by supranuclear ophthalmoplegia and postural instability, along with parkinsonism and cognitive impairment.1,2 It is pathologically associated with 4-repeat tau aggregation in neurons and glial cells,3 which further induces neuronal damage in the frontal lobes, basal ganglia, cerebellum, and brainstem.4,5 In contrast, the clinical manifestations of PSP, such as rigidity, bradykinesia, and posture instability, were largely contributed to by prevalent monoaminergic deficits in the nigrostriatal system, yet the direct evidence linking tau burden to monoaminergic dysfunction in PSP remains sparse.

Second-generation tau tracers such as 18F-PI-2620 and 18F-APN-1607 have provided intuitive tools to detect tau burdens in the brains of patients with PSP.6–8 Prior studies have demonstrated characteristic uptake patterns of 18F-APN-1607 in PSP and revealed its correlation with clinical severity.7 Nigrostriatal presynaptic imaging 18F-FP-DTBZ PET targeting on VMAT2 enables quantification of presynaptic dysfunction,9,10 with minimal confounding from aging, disease duration, or dopaminergic medications.11 Previous studies have demonstrated its sensitivity in detecting nigrostriatal deficits.12–14 Our prior research further applied 18F-FP-DTBZ in PSP, demonstrating more profound and widespread nigrostriatal impairment in PSP compared with PD.15

Building on the premise that tau deposition may contribute to monoaminergic neuronal damage, this study combined tau PET imaging using 18F-APN-1607 and presynaptic monoaminergic PET imaging using 18F-FP-DTBZ in patients with PSP, targeting on whether the underlying associations are driven by the tau pathology localized to the cell bodies versus the terminals of nigrostriatal projections within the nigrostriatal system, thereby elucidating potential links between tau pathology and monoaminergic dysfunction in PSP.

METHODS

Enrollment and Exclusion Criteria

As part of the China Initiative on Neurodegeneration in Aging (CHINA) cohort of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University, we enrolled patients with PSP and HC from March 2022 to July 2024. Informed consent was obtained from all participants. Patients with PSP were diagnosed as probable or possible PSP according to the 2017 Movement Disorder Society clinical diagnostic criteria for progressive supranuclear palsy. The HC group was required to be aged 40–80 years, with informed consent signed by themselves or their legal representatives/caregivers, and be able to provide accurate cognitive and demographic information of the subjects. Exclusion criteria included imaging-confounding findings of significant cerebrovascular disease, space-occupying lesions, or hydrocephalus; as well as recent use (<5 half-lives) of psychoactive drugs (antidepressants, antipsychotics, hypnotics, stimulants, anticholinergics, or antihistamines).

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Xuanwu Hospital, Capital Medical University [No. (2019)032] and complied with the Declaration of Helsinki and its amendments.

Clinical Evaluation

Assessments of clinical scales were performed after >12 hours of withdrawal from antiparkinsonian medications. Motor symptoms were evaluated using the MDS Unified Parkinson Disease Rating Scale (MDS-UPDRS-III), Hoehn-Yahr (H-Y) Scale, and PSP Rating Scale (PSPRS). Global cognitive evaluation included the Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA) and Mini-Mental State Examination (MMSE). In addition, domain-specific cognitive assessments were completed, including the Frontal Assessment Battery, Digit Span Forward and Backward Test, Verbal Fluency Test, and Number Sequencing Test.

Image Acquisition and Analysis

PET imaging was performed using a uMI 510 PET/CT scanner (United Imaging Healthcare, Shanghai, China), with the scanning time exceeding 12 hours after the last antiparkinsonian medication administration. The synthesis of 18F-FP-DTBZ or 18F-APN-1607 followed previously established standard methods.16–18 Participants were intravenously injected with a dose of ~222 MBq (6 mCi) of 18F-FP-DTBZ, then rested in a quiet environment for 90 minutes and scanned for 15 minutes in the PET scanner. An expert committee visually reviewed the 18F-FP-DTBZ PET scans to confirm nigrostriatal monoaminergic impairment before performing the 18F-APN-1607 PET. For tau imaging, participants were intravenously injected with ~370 MBq (10 mCi) of 18F-APN-1607 and rested comfortably for 60 minutes. 18F-APN-1607 PET imaging was performed over a 30-minute acquisition period (60–90 min postinjection).

PET images were reconstructed using an ordered-subsets expectation maximization (OSEM) algorithm with time-of-flight (TOF) information (2 iterations, 24 subsets), followed by a 3.0-mm full-width-at-half-maximum (FWHM) Gaussian filter. The final reconstructed voxel size was 1.82×1.82×2.78 mm for both the 18F-DTBZ PET and 18F-APN1607 PET. CT scanning parameters were 120 kV, 180 mA, and a pitch of 0.9375.

Image processing and analysis were performed using PMOD software (version 4.4). PET images were spatially normalized to tracer-specific templates for 18F-APN-1607 and 18F-FP-DTBZ. The 18F-APN-1607 template was constructed in Montreal Neurological Institute (MNI) space using data from 16 subjects who underwent both 18F-APN-1607 PET/CT and 3D T1-weighted MRI, following procedures implemented in YiXiLab (https://www.yixilab.com) and PMOD, while the 18F-FP-DTBZ template was previously established.12

Volumes of interest (VOIs) included the cortex (frontal, parietal, temporal, occipital lobes); limbic system (entorhinal cortex, hippocampus, amygdala); subcortical regions (the caudate, putamen, globus pallidus, thalamus, subthalamic nucleus, nucleus accumbens); dentate nucleus; and brainstem regions (substantia nigra, red nucleus, dorsal raphe). The delineation of the VOIs was derived from the Hybro atlas,19 the AAL3 atlas,20 and atlases in MNI space according to our previous study.12 To identify evidence of Alzheimer disease (AD)-related early-stage tau deposition, the entorhinal cortex was defined as a VOI based on the Braak staging scheme using the IIT Human Brain Atlas.21,22

For 18F-APN-1607, the reference region selected a 3-pixel eroded cerebral white matter to eliminate partial volume effects with the erosion procedure in PMOD,23,24 whereas the bilateral occipital cortex was used as the reference region for 18F-FP-DTBZ.12 Standardized uptake value ratios (SUVRs) for each VOI of 18F-APN-1607 and 18F-FP-DTBZ were calculated with the corresponding reference region.

Criteria for Positive 18F-APN-1607 Tau PET Imaging

In this study, a “2-region positive approach” was applied to determine the individual tau positivity of APN-1607 PET, to ensure the specificity of recognizing PSP.6 The defined regional SUVR Z-score was defined as: (individual subject’s SUVR—mean SUVR in HC)/SD of SUVR in HC. A regional Z-score ≥2 defined a positive finding for semiquantitative interpretation of the region. Tau PET positivity was defined at the individual level as the presence of positive findings in 2 or more of the aforementioned brain regions.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous data were expressed as mean±SD, while categorical data were presented as median (interquartile range). For between-group comparisons of normally distributed data with homogeneous variance, the Student t test was used; the Welch t test was applied for normally distributed data with heterogeneous variance. Skewed data were analyzed using the Mann-Whitney U rank-sum test. The Fisher exact probability test was used for categorical data comparisons. For the comparison of multiple regional SUVRs, FDR correction of P-values was performed using the Benjamini-Hochberg (BH) procedure. For analyzing the correlations between regional tau load, Pearson correlation was used for normally distributed data, and Spearman correlation analysis was used for non-normally distributed data. The simple linear regression was used to elucidate the relationship between SUVRs of the 2 tracers, with regional tau deposition as the independent variable (x) and regional monoaminergic impairment as the dependent variable (y). For nonlinear regression, a logarithmic transformation was applied to the independent variable (x). Multiple linear regression was conducted in the analysis of the 2 tracers’ relationship in the striatum to eliminate the intervention of age, gender, and duration. Statistical significance was defined as P<0.05.

Increased Tau Uptakes and Decreased Monoaminergic Bindings in PSP Participants

A significant increase in the SUVRs of 18F-APN-1607 PET was observed in the globus pallidus, subthalamic nucleus, substantia nigra, and red nucleus of patients with PSP. Also, a trend of higher tau deposition in the putamen (P=0.064), but not in the caudate (P=0.808), was noted in the striatal regions without false discovery rate (FDR) correction (Figs. 1, 2). No significant differences in SUVRs of 18F-APN-1607 were found in the cortex, hippocampus, and amygdala between PSP and HC with FDR-corrected results (Supplemental Table 2, Supplemental Digital Content 1, http://links.lww.com/CNM/A609).

No significant association was observed between tau deposition in the substantia nigra and monoaminergic integrity in the nigrostriatal system (Fig. 4A). DISCUSSION

This study explored the associations of tau aggregates and monoaminergic disruption using 18F-APN-1607 and 18F-FP-DTBZ PET in patients with PSP. Our results suggested that monoaminergic impairment in the putamen and caudate was negatively associated with local tau deposition in the striatum, other than tau burden in the substantia nigra. In the putamen, VMAT2 uptake showed a clear, approximately linear decline with increasing tau deposition, while in the caudate, this association was significant but less evident.

Our research findings indicated that the core tau deposition lies in the pallidus-nigra-luysian axis, which is consistent with previous postmortem evidence in patients with PSP.6,25 Increased tau uptake was also observed in the red nucleus, consistent with previous reports.26 Along with subcortical regions, frontotemporal involvement was frequently observed, reflecting the clinical heterogeneity of PSP on cognition.1 We evaluated tau deposition in regions corresponding to the early Braak stages of AD and observed positivity rates of 25% in both the entorhinal cortex and hippocampus. This pattern may suggest the presence of concomitant AD-type pathology. Notably, Kovacs et al25 reported that up to 40.2% of pathologic confirmed PSP cases present with concomitant AD or primary age-related tauopathy, particularly involving the hippocampus and amygdala.

Significant monoaminergic impairment was noted in the nigrostriatal system (substantia nigra, caudate, and putamen) of patients with PSP in the study, which is in line with our previous report and other evidence on dopaminergic disruptions of PSP.15,27

According to our predefined criteria for tau positivity, the overall tau PET-positive rate in the PSP cohort was 80.0%. This is broadly in agreement with previous reports demonstrating an approximate 85% sensitivity of 18F-PI-2620 and 18F-APN-1607 for distinguishing PSP from other groups.7,14 This level of sensitivity may be interpreted in the context of limitations of tau PET imaging in 4-repeat tauopathies, where tau tracers’ binding characteristics are not optimized as those in Alzheimer disease.8,28 Notably, in our cohort, the tau PET-positive rate in patients with PSP-P was only 33.3%. This observation aligns with prior studies showing that non-Richardson syndrome PSP exhibits a lower tau PET positivity rate compared with PSP-RS.14 Furthermore, recent pathologically verified studies have demonstrated that older age and male sex correlate with lower tau PET positivity rates.29

Regarding the associations of tau-PET signals and VMAT2 binding, negative associations were observed between striatal tau burden and monoaminergic disruption. The effect is more pronounced in the putamen than in the caudate. Multiple linear regression analyses adjusting for age, gender, and disease duration further confirmed these associations.

In contrast, no significant association was observed between nigral tau burden and monoaminergic disruption in the nigrostriatal projection, either within the substantia nigra or in the striatum. The finding differs from that of Ferschmann et al,30 who reported a moderate correlation between unilateral nigral tau deposition and putaminal dopaminergic impairment, possibly reflecting differences in disease spectrum and imaging modalities.

However, the lack of observed association in the substantia nigra should be interpreted with caution. Firstly, the biological substrates captured by tau PET and VMAT2 PET may differ. Pathologic tau accumulation can occur across multiple cell types, including neurons and glial cells, and within distinct subcellular compartments such as axons, dendrites, and synaptic terminals, whereas VMAT2 is predominantly localized in the presynaptic terminals of monoaminergic neurons.31 Consequently, the absence of a significant correlation in the substantia nigra may reflect differences in tracer binding across cell types or compartment-specific pathology. In addition, previous studies of tau tracers have demonstrated limited sensitivity for detecting 4-repeat tauopathies, and potential off-target binding to neuromelanin may confound signal quantification in relevant regions.8

Taken together, our results suggest that the severity of terminal dysfunction may be related to local striatal tau burden, rather than to soma dysfunction. These findings are consistent with those of Liang et al,32 who reported a moderate negative association between DAT loss and tau accumulation in the left putamen; however, the association in the substantia nigra was not explored in their study. Postmortem evidence also indicates that synaptic tau accumulation in PSP correlates with synapse loss.33 Overall, these findings point to a region-specific vulnerability of striatal monoaminergic terminals in the presence of focal tau pathology, and may help explain the mechanisms underlying tau-related monoaminergic disruption in PSP.

Some limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, the measurements of SUVR in brainstem regions, especially the substantia nigra, may be influenced by partial volume effects and regional atrophy in PSP. Therefore, the lack of observed association in the substantia nigra may reflect methodological and technical limitations in signal quantification. Second, the sample size was relatively modest, and the study design was cross-sectional. Thus, observed associations between tau burden and regional monoaminergic impairment do not permit temporal or causal relationships and warrant validation in larger longitudinal cohorts. Third, the significant associations were mainly established in tau PET-positive patients, which might introduce a risk of overfitting. Finally, separate HC groups were used for the 2 PET tracers. Participants in the HCAPN-1607 group had relatively lower years of education (8.43±7.48 y) and MMSE and MoCA scores, although these remained within the education-adjusted normal range for Chinese elderly populations.34,35

CONCLUSIONS

Our study suggests that the pallido-nigro-luysian axis may be relevant to the assessment of tau deposition in PSP. Striatal tau deposition showed a stronger association with local monoaminergic disruption in PSP than with nigral tau deposition.
