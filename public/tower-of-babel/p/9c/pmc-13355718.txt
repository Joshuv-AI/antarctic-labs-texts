Structural evolution and prebiotic-like potential of insoluble dietary fiber-polyphenol complexes from Rosa roxburghii Tratt during in vitro digestion and colonic fermentation
Authors: Xiaoli Zhou, Yan Chen, Shengyan Zhang, ShuangQin Zhang, Tailan Liu, Nian Liu, Yanxin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractRosa roxburghi Tratt. (RRT) pomace is rich in insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) and polyphenols, but free polyphenols are susceptible to premature loss during digestion. This study constructed an IDF–polyphenol complex (IDF-PP) from RRT pomace via non-covalent adsorption and evaluated its gastrointestinal fate and in vitro colonic fermentation behavior. IDF-PP reduced premature phenolic loss during simulated upper gastrointestinal digestion and enabled a more gradual release during fermentation. Structural analyses showed fermentation-induced remodeling, generating a porous residual matrix while retaining part of the polysaccharide framework. Compared with free polyphenols and IDF, IDF-PP increased short-chain fatty acid production, especially butyrate, and was associated with higher relative abundance of Prevotella-centered Bacteroidetes taxa and Faecalibacterium, together with lower relative abundance of Proteobacteria. Fermentation products also showed enhanced antioxidant and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities. These findings suggest that RRT pomace-derived IDF-PP is a promising fermentation-responsive functional ingredient with potential to modulate gut microbial fermentation.Keywords: Rosa roxburghii Tratt, Insoluble dietary fiber, Polyphenol–fiber complex, Phenolic release, In vitro colonic fermentation

Introduction

Gut health is closely associated with the homeostasis of the intestinal microbiota, which plays important roles in nutrient metabolism, immune regulation, and maintenance of the intestinal barrier. Increasing evidence suggests that gut dysbiosis, characterized by alterations in microbial composition and metabolic activity, is associated with various metabolic and inflammatory disorders, including inflammatory bowel disease and colorectal cancer (Afzaal et al., 2022; Ahmadi et al., 2025). Among the modifiable factors shaping gut microbial ecology, diet is considered one of the most direct and influential determinants. Dietary bioactive compounds, especially dietary fibers (DFs) and polyphenols, can serve as substrates or modulators for intestinal microbes, thereby affecting microbial composition and metabolite production (Fernandes et al., 2023; Koch, 2019). For example, specific polysaccharides and DFs have been reported to support the growth of beneficial bacterial taxa and promote the production of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which are closely related to intestinal fermentation and barrier function (Huang et al., 2025; Sun et al., 2023). Therefore, the development of dietary ingredients capable of modulating gut microbial fermentation has attracted increasing interest in functional food research.

Polyphenols and DFs are both recognized as important dietary components with microbiota-modulating potential. In recent years, increasing attention has been paid to DF–polyphenol complexes because of their potential synergistic effects during gastrointestinal digestion and colonic fermentation. Free polyphenols (FPs) are often susceptible to degradation, transformation, or loss during upper gastrointestinal digestion, which may reduce their bioaccessibility and limit the amount reaching the colon (Xie et al., 2022). In contrast, naturally occurring dietary fiber-bound polyphenols (BPs), such as those found in rye bran and carrot, may be better retained during upper gastrointestinal digestion and subsequently released during colonic fermentation, where the DF matrix can be degraded by intestinal microbiota (Cheng et al., 2024; Dong et al., 2020; Hou et al., 2024). However, the amount and composition of naturally bound phenolics are highly dependent on the raw material and may be insufficient for achieving sustained functional effects. To improve the delivery and utilization of phenolics, constructed polysaccharide/DF–polyphenol complexes have been developed in recent studies (Liu, Jia, et al., 2025; Liu, Meng, et al., 2025). In these systems, DFs can interact with polyphenols through non-covalent interactions, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and physical entrapment within porous fiber networks. Such interactions may enhance the stability of polyphenols during gastrointestinal digestion and facilitate their gradual release during microbial fermentation in the colon (Mei et al., 2021; Tang et al., 2021). Therefore, constructing DF–polyphenol complexes represents a promising strategy for improving phenolic delivery and modulating colonic fermentation.

Rosa roxburghii Tratt (RRT), a medicinal and edible fruit mainly distributed in the Yunnan–Guizhou Plateau of China, is rich in bioactive compounds, including polyphenols, vitamin C, flavonoids, and polysaccharides, and has been reported to possess antioxidant, hypoglycemic, and anti-inflammatory activities (Wang et al., 2020; Zhou et al., 2024). During industrial juice and beverage processing, a large proportion of the fresh RRT mass is generated as pomace, leading to environmental pressure and loss of valuable components, particularly insoluble dietary fiber (IDF) and phenolic compounds (Huang et al., 2022). Our previous study showed that native BPs in RRT pomace-derived IDF could be released during colonic fermentation and exhibited prebiotic-like potential (Zhou, Qin, Chen, Wang, Lin, & Meng, 2025). Nevertheless, the utilization of RRT-derived FPs remains limited by their instability during gastrointestinal digestion. Constructing an IDF–polyphenol complex (IDF-PP) from RRT pomace may provide a feasible strategy to improve the stability and colonic delivery of FPs while contributing to the value-added utilization of RRT processing by-products.

Based on this background, the present study aimed to elucidate the gastrointestinal fate and colonic fermentation behavior of an IDF–polyphenol complex IDF-PP prepared from RRT pomace. IDF extracted from discarded RRT pomace was used as a natural carrier to adsorb RRT polyphenols through non-covalent interactions. Using simulated gastrointestinal digestion and in vitro fecal fermentation, this study focused on three main aspects: phenolic protection and release along the gastrointestinal–colonic continuum; fermentation-induced structural remodeling of the IDF matrix and its association with microbial fermentation, SCFA production, and microbiota shifts; and changes in the antioxidant and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities of fermentation products. This work provides mechanistic insight into the “carrier protection–gradual release–microbiota-associated fermentation” behavior of RRT pomace-derived IDF-PP and supports its potential development as a fermentation-responsive dietary fiber–polyphenol ingredient with prebiotic-like properties.

Materials and methods

Materials

Fresh RRT was harvested from the RRT cultivation base located in Gujiao Town, Longli County,Guizhou Province, China. The pomace was immediately freeze-dried, pulverized, and passed through a 60-mesh sieve. The resulting powder was stored at −20 °C until further use.

Preparation of IDF-PP from RRT pomace

Extraction of polyphenol from RRT

FPs were extracted from RRT pomace powder according to the method of Qin et al. (2024), with slight modifications. Briefly, RRT pomace powder was mixed with 60% aqueous ethanol at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:20 (w/v). The mixture was extracted under ultrasound treatment at 480 W and 50 °C for 1 h, followed by centrifugation at 1806 ×g for 10 min. The extraction was repeated twice, and the supernatants were combined. The combined extract was concentrated under reduced pressure at 45 °C and then lyophilized to obtain the free polyphenol fraction FPs, which was stored at −20 °C until further use.

Extraction of IDF from RRT pomace

IDF was extracted by enzymatic hydrolysis according to the methods of Cheng et al. (2025) and Kaur et al. (2024), with slight modifications. RRT pomace powder was dispersed in distilled water at a solid-to-liquid ratio of 1:20 (w/v), and the pH was adjusted to 4.5. Cellulase (100 mg, 50 U/mg; Shanghai Yuye Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) was added, and the mixture was hydrolyzed at 50 °C for 1 h. The pH was then adjusted to 6.0, followed by the addition of heat-stable α-amylase (50 mg, 50 U/mg; Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). The mixture was incubated at 95 °C for 40 min. After centrifugation (1806 ×g, 10 min), the precipitate was collected and thoroughly washed with distilled water to remove residual enzymes, soluble sugars, and other soluble components. The residue was freeze-dried and used as RRT pomace-derived IDF. The final IDF was stored at −20 °C until further use.

The purity of the prepared IDF was determined according to GB 5009.88–2023 using an enzymatic-gravimetric method. Briefly, a known amount of freeze-dried IDF sample was enzymatically treated to remove digestible starch and protein. The remaining insoluble residue was collected by filtration, washed, dried, and weighed. The residue weight was corrected by subtracting protein, ash, and blank values. IDF purity was calculated as follows:

IDF purity (%) = (R − P − A − B)/m × 100.

Where R is the dry weight of the insoluble residue, P is the protein content in the residue, A is the ash content in the residue,B is the blank correction, and m is the dry weight of the IDF sample used for analysis.

Preparation of IDF-PP

The IDF-polyphenol complex (IDF-PP) was prepared according to Phan et al. (2017), with minor modifications. Briefly, 200 mg of IDF was mixed with 10 mL of FP solution at a concentration of 9 mg/mL. The mixture was incubated in a shaking incubator at 180 rpm and 28 °C for 1 h to allow adsorption. A control group containing IDF without added FPs was prepared under the same conditions. After incubation, the mixture was centrifuged at 11,290 ×g for 10 min. The precipitate was collected and washed twice with 20 mL distilled water until there was no residual polyphenols in the washing solution to remove loosely adsorbed or unbound phenols, and freeze-dried to obtain IDF-PP.

In vitro simulated gastrointestinal digestion

Static in vitro gastrointestinal digestion was performed according to the international INFOGEST 2.0 protocol, with slight modifications (Li, Wang, et al., 2025; Luz et al., 2024). The static INFOGEST model was selected because of its standardization, reproducibility, and suitability for comparing matrix-dependent release behavior among different samples.

Simulated salivary fluid (SSF), simulated gastric fluid (SGF), and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) were prepared according to the INFOGEST guidelines. For the IDF-PP group, 0.29 g of IDF-PP was used. The comparison among IDF-PP, IDF, and FPs was conducted on a phenolic-content-equivalent basis. 0.29 g of IDF-PP provided approximately 11.32 mg GAE of phenolics. The total phenolic contents of IDF and FPs were determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method, and the sample amounts were calculated using the equation msample = CIDF-PP × mIDF-PP/Csample. Accordingly, 0.4019 g of IDF and 0.01 g of FPs were used to provide the same initial phenolic content as 0.29 g of IDF-PP.

The digestion procedure was conducted as follows:

Oral phase: Samples were mixed with SSF containing human salivary amylase at a final activity of 75 U/mL and CaCl₂ at a final concentration of 0.75 mM. The pH was adjusted to 7.0, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C and 100 rpm for 2 min.

Gastric phase: The oral bolus was mixed with SGF containing porcine pepsin at a final activity of 2000 U/mL and CaCl₂ at a final concentration of 0.15 mM. The pH was adjusted to 3.0 using 1 M HCl, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h under continuous agitation.

Intestinal phase: The gastric chyme was mixed with SIF containing pancreatin at a final trypsin activity of 100 U/mL, fresh porcine bile extract at a final concentration of 10 mM, and CaCl₂ at a final concentration of 0.6 mM. The pH was adjusted to 7.0 using 1 M NaOH, and the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 2 h.

After each digestion phase, the enzymatic reaction was stopped immediately by cooling the digesta on ice. For the gastric phase, the pH was adjusted to 7.0 before further processing. The digesta was centrifuged at 11,290 ×g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatants were filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane and used to determine the released unbound phenolics. The remaining solid residues were thoroughly washed three times with 20 mL of distilled water to remove residual enzymes, bile salts, and soluble digestion products, lyophilized, and used as fermentation substrates in the subsequent in vitro colonic fermentation experiment.

In vitro fecal fermentation

Fermentation medium preparation

The fermentation medium was prepared following Zhou, Qin, Chen, Wang, Lin, and Meng (2025) by dissolving 5.0 g peptone, 4.5 g yeast extract, 0.5 g bile salts, 0.1 g NaCl, 2.0 g NaHCO3, 0.5 g L-cysteine hydrochloride, 0.04 g K2HPO4, 0.01 g MgSO4·7H2O, 0.01 g CaCl2·6H2O, 0.02 g hemin, 2.0 mL Tween 80, 10 μL vitamin K1, and 1.0 mL resazurin (1.0%, w/v) in 1.0 L of distilled water. The pH was adjusted to 7.0 (0.1 M HCl) before autoclaving at 121 °C for 15 min.

Preparation of fecal inoculum

Fresh fecal samples were collected from three healthy adult donors one male and two females, aged 22–28 years, with normal body mass index BMI and no known gastrointestinal, metabolic, or other chronic diseases. None of the donors had taken antibiotics, probiotics, or prebiotics for at least three months prior to sample collection. All donors provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Guiyang University Approval Number: GYU-FPE-2025-005.

Equal amounts of fecal samples from the three donors were pooled and homogenized with sterile physiological saline to obtain a 15% (w/w) fecal slurry. The slurry was filtered through four layers of sterile gauze to remove large particles and immediately used as the fecal inoculum. All procedures were performed under anaerobic conditions as far as possible.

In vitro colonic fermentation

The lyophilized digested residues obtained from Section 2.3 were used as fermentation substrates. Each fermentation vessel contained the digested residue, 18 mL of pre-reduced sterilized fermentation medium, and 2 mL of fecal inoculum. A blank control CK, containing fermentation medium and fecal inoculum without added substrate, was included. Anaerobic conditions were established using an N₂-flushing and sealed-vessel system. Briefly, the fermentation vessels were continuously flushed with sterile N₂ to displace residual oxygen, immediately sealed, and incubated at 37 °C for fermentation.

Independent fermentation tubes were prepared for each sampling time point 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h. At each time point, one tube was removed and centrifuged at 11,290 ×g for 10 min. The supernatants were collected for phenolic content, antioxidant activity, α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, and SCFA analyses. The bacterial pellets were collected and stored at −80 °C for 16S rRNA gene sequencing. As each tube was sampled only once, no volume replacement was required during fermentation.

Determination of phenolic content

The total phenolic content was determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method according to Zhou et al. (2024), with gallic acid as the standard. Briefly, 200 μL of sample solution was mixed with 1 mL of Folin–Ciocalteu reagent and allowed to react for 6 min in the dark. Then, 2 mL of 7% Na₂CO₃ solution and 2 mL of distilled water were added. The mixture was incubated for 40 min, and the absorbance was measured at 760 nm using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer (Agilent Cary 60, USA). Results were expressed as mg gallic acid equivalents per gram of dry weight (mg GAE/g DW).

Determination of pH and apparent microbial growth

Fermentation activity was temporarily terminated by placing the samples in an ice bath for 20 min. The pH of the fermentation broth was measured using a digital pH meter. The optical density at 600 nm (OD₆₀₀) was recorded using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer according to Zhang et al. (2024). Because OD₆₀₀ measurements in complex fecal fermentation systems may be affected by matrix colour, suspended particles, and insoluble residues, OD₆₀₀ was interpreted as an apparent indicator of microbial biomass accumulation rather than an absolute measure of bacterial growth. Blank subtraction was performed at each time point.

Antioxidant activity assays

The ABTS and DPPH radical scavenging activities of the digestates and fermentation supernatants were measured according to Zhang et al. (2024) with slight modifications.

For the DPPH assay, 0.005 g of DPPH was dissolved in anhydrous ethanol and diluted to 100 mL to prepare the DPPH working solution. Then, 25 μL of sample was mixed with 200 μL of DPPH working solution in a 96-well microplate and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. The absorbance was measured at 517 nm using a Multiskan SkyHigh microplate reader (Thermo Fisher Scientific, China).

For the ABTS assay, the ABTS stock solution was prepared by dissolving 200 mg of ABTS and 34.4 mg of potassium persulfate in water and allowing the mixture to react in the dark at room temperature for 12–16 h. Before use, the stock solution was diluted with distilled water or ethanol to an absorbance of 0.75–0.80 at 734 nm to obtain the ABTS working solution. Then, 25 μL of sample was mixed with 200 μL of ABTS working solution in a 96-well microplate and incubated in the dark at room temperature for 5 min. The absorbance was measured at 734 nm using the same microplate reader.

α-glucosidase inhibitory activity

The α-glucosidase inhibitory activity was determined using p-nitrophenyl-α-D-glucopyranoside (pNPG) as the substrate. The reaction system contained 10 mmol/L pNPG and 2 U/mL α-glucosidase in 0.1 M phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, pH 6.8). Briefly, 100 μL of sample solution was mixed with 200 μL of α-glucosidase solution and incubated at 37 °C for 10 min. Then, 100 μL of pNPG solution was added to initiate the reaction. Determination of SCFAs

SCFAs in the fermentation supernatants were quantified by gas chromatography with flame ionization detection (GC-FID). Briefly, 2 mL of fermentation supernatant was mixed with 0.5 mL of 25% phosphoric acid, vortexed, and centrifuged at 2286 ×g for 5 min. The supernatant was filtered through a 0.22 μm membrane before analysis. SCFAs were analyzed using an Agilent 6890 GC-FID system equipped with an SP-1200/H₃PO₄-coated Chromosorb W-AW column (1.82 m × 0.2 mm, 80/100 mesh, HP Inc., USA). The injector temperature was set at 200 °C and the detector temperature at 250 °C. Nitrogen was used as the carrier gas at a flow rate of 17.6 mL/min, with a split ratio of 8:1. The air and hydrogen flow rates were 400 and 40 mL/min, respectively. The oven temperature program was as follows: initial temperature 90 °C, held for 1 min; increased to 170 °C at 60 °C/min; then to 200 °C at 15 °C/min; and finally to 220 °C at 20 °C/min. Individual SCFAs were identified and quantified by comparison with external standards.

Gut microbiota analysis

Total genomic DNA was extracted from bacterial pellets using the MagBeads FastDNA Kit (MP Biomedicals, CA, USA) according to the manufacturer's instructions. The V3–V4 region of the bacterial 16S rRNA gene was amplified using universal primers 338F and 806R. Amplicon sequencing was performed on an Illumina NovaSeq platform by Shanghai Baipu Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Raw reads were demultiplexed, quality-filtered, denoised, and processed using QIIME 2. Representative sequences were taxonomically annotated using the Greengenes2 database (gg2_2022_10). Relative abundance profiles at different taxonomic levels were generated for microbial community composition analysis. Alpha diversity was evaluated using the Chao1, Shannon, and Simpson indices. Differentially abundant taxa among groups were identified using linear discriminant analysis effect size (LEfSe; LDA score > 2.0, P < 0.05). Hierarchical clustering for the heatmap was performed using Euclidean distance and Ward's linkage method.

Structural characterization

FTIR analysis

Lyophilized samples were analyzed using a Fourier-transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) spectrometer (Nicolet iS20, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA). Spectra were recorded over the range of 4000–400 cm−1 with 64 scans at a resolution of 4 cm−1. Data were processed using OMNIC software.

SEM analysis

The lyophilized samples were fixed on conductive adhesive tape, sputter-coated with gold, and observed using a scanning electron microscope (GeminiSEM 300, ZEISS, Germany) at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

XRD analysis

X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were recorded using an X-ray diffractometer (SmartLab SE, Rigaku, Japan) operated at 40 kV and 200 mA with Cu Kα radiation. Data were collected over a 2θ range of 5°–50° at a scanning rate of 1.2°/min. After baseline correction of the diffraction profiles, the relative crystallinity was estimated from the integrated areas of the crystalline and amorphous regions. The crystallinity index (CrI) was calculated as CrI (%) = Ac / (Ac + Aa) × 100, where Ac and Aa represent the crystalline and amorphous areas, respectively.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD) of three biological replicates (n = 3). Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics 26.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Differences among groups were analyzed by one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA), followed by Duncan's multiple range test for multiple comparisons. A value of P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant, and P < 0.01 was considered highly significant. In the bar graphs, different uppercase letters indicate significant differences among samples under the same treatment, whereas different lowercase letters indicate significant differences for the same sample under different treatments (P < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Before evaluating the gastrointestinal digestion and colonic fermentation behavior of the samples, the basic characteristics of the prepared fractions were summarized. The extracted IDF showed a purity of 83%. Based on our previous quantitative HPLC-MS/MS analysis of the RRT polyphenol extract (Zhou et al., 2024), the major phenolic compounds included salicylic acid, caffeic acid, isoferulic acid, gallic acid, sinapic acid, rosmarinic acid, catechin, and quercetin.

1 dynamic changes in polyphenol content during in vitro digestion and fermentation

As shown in Fig. 1, the total phenolic content exhibited distinct dynamic patterns among the CK, FPs, IDF, and IDF-PP groups during simulated gastrointestinal digestion and colonic fermentation. The CK group showed only a weak and slowly increasing background signal throughout fermentation, which remained much lower than those of the IDF and IDF-PP groups. This suggests that the fermentation medium itself contributed only a limited reducing background in the Folin–Ciocalteu assay, thereby allowing better discrimination of substrate-associated phenolic release.

Dynamic changes in total phenolic content and phenolic degradation/release rates of CK, FPs, IDF, and IDF-PP during simulated gastrointestinal digestion and in vitro colonic fermentation. CK represents the blank fermentation control without substrate addition. Values are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3). Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences among digestion or fermentation times within the same group (P < 0.05), and different uppercase letters indicate significant differences among different groups at the same time point (P < 0.05).

During the upper gastrointestinal digestion phase, the FPs group showed a significant decrease in phenolic content (P < 0.05), with a degradation rate of 47.77% after small intestinal digestion. This loss may be related to the instability of RRT polyphenols under changing pH conditions and enzymatic exposure, which can promote oxidation, degradation, or structural transformation of phenolic compounds (Hou et al., 2024; Monroy-García et al., 2025). In contrast, the IDF and IDF-PP groups exhibited limited phenolic release during oral, gastric, and small intestinal digestion, suggesting that incorporation into the IDF matrix improved phenolic retention during the upper gastrointestinal phase. Such retention was likely associated with interactions between polyphenols and the IDF matrix, including hydrogen bonding, hydrophobic interactions, and physical entrapment, as inferred from the release behavior and supported by the FTIR and XRD results. This observation is consistent with previous reports showing that polyphenols associated with dietary fiber matrices can be effectively protected during gastrointestinal digestion (Hou et al., 2024).

During colonic fermentation, the FPs group remained at a relatively low phenolic level, indicating that most free phenolics had been depleted or transformed before the fermentation stage. In contrast, the phenolic content of the IDF-PP group increased markedly during fermentation. Relative to its initial phenolic loading (16.15 ± 0.11 mg GAE/g DW before digestion/fermentation), the release rate reached 62.59% at 24 h. This release pattern indicates that the IDF matrix delayed phenolic liberation during upper gastrointestinal digestion but promoted their release under fermentative conditions. The fermentation-associated release may be related to microbial degradation and structural loosening of the IDF matrix, which facilitated the liberation of phenolics retained within or bound to the fiber network (Su et al., 2022). However, because total phenolics were determined using the Folin–Ciocalteu method, the contribution of microbial metabolites and other reducing compounds cannot be completely excluded, although the CK control indicated that the background signal of the fermentation matrix was substantially lower than that of the substrate-containing groups.

The IDF group also showed an increase in apparent phenolic content during fermentation, reflecting the release of native bound phenolics from RRT pomace-derived IDF. Nevertheless, the IDF-PP group consistently exhibited significantly higher phenolic content than the IDF group, indicating that exogenous polyphenol loading increased the phenolic reservoir available for colonic fermentation. Overall, these results demonstrate that IDF-PP exhibited a favorable “upper gastrointestinal retention–colonic fermentation release” behavior. This release profile may contribute to the higher antioxidant capacity of the fermentation broth during fermentation and may also be associated with the modulation of gut microbiota composition and short-chain fatty acid production observed in this study.

Evolution of pH and OD600 values during in vitro fermentation

Changes in pH and OD₆₀₀ during in vitro colonic fermentation are presented in Fig. 2. The pH value reflects acidification of the fermentation system, which is generally associated with the production of acidic metabolites such as SCFAs, whereas OD₆₀₀ provides an indirect indication of apparent microbial growth. Considering the complexity of fecal fermentation broths, OD₆₀₀ was interpreted as an apparent proxy for microbial biomass accumulation rather than an absolute measure of bacterial growth.

Different uppercase letters indicate significant differences among groups at the same fermentation time (P < 0.05). Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences among fermentation times within the same group (P < 0.05). CK, blank control; FPs, free polyphenols; IDF, insoluble dietary fiber; IDF-PP, insoluble dietary fiber–polyphenol complex.

As shown in Fig. 2A, all groups exhibited comparable initial pH values at 0 h. From 6 to 48 h, the pH values of all substrate-supplemented groups (FP, IDF, and IDF-PP) were significantly lower than that of the CK group at each time point (P < 0.05), indicating that the observed acidification exceeded the baseline acidification caused by the fecal inoculum alone. During fermentation, pH values decreased significantly in all substrate-supplemented groups (P < 0.05), suggesting active microbial fermentation and accumulation of acidic metabolites. Among the treatments, the IDF-PP group showed the strongest acidification and maintained the lowest pH from 12 to 48 h. This result suggests that IDF-PP was more effectively utilized during fermentation than FP or IDF alone. The enhanced pH decrease may be associated with the fermentability of the IDF matrix and the fermentation-associated release of RRT polyphenols, both of which may have contributed to microbial metabolic activity and acidic metabolite accumulation. This interpretation is further supported by the subsequent SCFA profiles and gut microbiota composition data. At 24–48 h, the pH values of the IDF and IDF-PP groups tended to stabilize, possibly due to depletion of readily fermentable substrates (Ji et al., 2024), the buffering capacity of the medium, and partial neutralization by alkaline metabolites such as ammonia.

The OD₆₀₀ values increased progressively during fermentation, particularly in the IDF and IDF-PP groups (Fig. 2B). The IDF-PP group showed significantly higher OD₆₀₀ values than the CK and IDF groups during 6–48 h (P < 0.05), reaching a maximum at 24 h. This suggests that IDF-PP supported greater apparent microbial biomass accumulation during fermentation. The higher OD₆₀₀ may be attributed to the combined effects of the fermentable dietary fiber matrix and associated polyphenols, which together provided substrates for microbial metabolism.

Taken together, IDF-PP induced stronger acidification and greater apparent microbial biomass accumulation during fecal fermentation than FP or IDF alone. These findings suggest that the IDF-PP complex enhanced fermentation activity, as reflected by lower pH and higher OD₆₀₀ values, providing a basis for the subsequent changes in SCFA production and gut microbiota composition.

Dynamic changes in antioxidant capacity during in vitro digestion and fermentation

Phenolic compounds are natural antioxidants with free radical-scavenging and metal-chelating capacities, which may contribute to the regulation of intestinal oxidative stress (Zheng et al., 2021). Their functional performance is closely related to their stability during upper gastrointestinal digestion and their availability during colonic fermentation (Gómez-Maqueo et al., 2020). Therefore, DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging activities were monitored to evaluate the dynamic antioxidant profiles of FPs, IDF, and IDF-PP during simulated digestion and fermentation.

As shown in Fig. 3, the FPs group showed high antioxidant activity during oral digestion, followed by a significant decline during gastric and small intestinal digestion (P < 0.05). During colonic fermentation, the antioxidant activity of FPs remained low and decreased further, indicating that only limited antioxidant capacity remained upon entering the fermentation stage. By contrast, IDF and IDF-PP maintained relatively stable antioxidant activities during upper gastrointestinal digestion, suggesting that the IDF matrix helped delay the loss or transformation of antioxidant-active components during digestion. This protective effect may be associated with inferred non-covalent interactions and physical entrapment between phenolics and the IDF network (Peng et al., 2023).

During colonic fermentation, the DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging activities of IDF and IDF-PP increased progressively and reached maximum values at 24 h (P < 0.05). This trend was consistent with the increase in phenolic release during fermentation, suggesting that released phenolic substances may contribute to the enhancement of antioxidant capacity. The fermentation-associated improvement may also be related to microbial degradation or structural loosening of the IDF matrix, which could facilitate the release of native BPs and adsorbed polyphenols. In addition, microbial conversion of complex phenolics into smaller metabolites, as well as the formation of other fermentation-derived reducing substances, such as peptides, amino acids, and other reductive microbial products, may also contribute to the measured antioxidant activity. Because DPPH and ABTS assays respond to a broad range of reducing and radical-scavenging compounds, the observed increases cannot be attributed exclusively to phenolic release. This interpretation should therefore be made with caution, in line with the potential non-specific interference previously noted for the Folin–Ciocalteu assay.

After 48 h of fermentation, the antioxidant activities of the IDF-containing groups decreased slightly but remained higher than those of FPs. This reduction may be related to further microbial transformation or consumption of released phenolics, resulting in metabolites with altered radical-scavenging capacities (Vitaglione et al., 2008; Zhang et al., 2019). Importantly, IDF-PP consistently exhibited significantly higher DPPH and ABTS scavenging activities than IDF during fermentation (P < 0.05), suggesting that polyphenol loading contributed to a greater pool of antioxidant-associated components available under colonic fermentation conditions. Taken together, IDF-PP showed better retention of antioxidant activity during upper gastrointestinal digestion and stronger antioxidant enhancement during colonic fermentation than FPs or IDF alone. These results suggest that the IDF-PP complex may serve as a potential carrier system for improving the colonic retention and gradual release of antioxidant-associated components, including phenolic compounds.

Dynamic evolution of α-glucosidase inhibitory activity during in vitro digestion and fermentation

α-Glucosidase is a key intestinal enzyme involved in carbohydrate hydrolysis and represents an important target for controlling postprandial glucose release. Phenolic compounds are known to inhibit α-glucosidase, but their inhibitory potential depends largely on their stability during gastrointestinal digestion and their availability after microbial fermentation. Therefore, α-glucosidase inhibitory activity was monitored to evaluate the functional changes of FPs, IDF, and IDF-PP during simulated digestion and fermentation.

As shown in Fig. 4, FPs exhibited relatively high inhibitory activity during oral digestion, followed by a significant decline during gastric and small intestinal digestion (P < 0.05). During colonic fermentation, the inhibitory activity of FPs remained low and decreased to 7.87 ± 1.17% at 48 h. This pattern was consistent with the decrease in phenolic content and antioxidant activity, suggesting that FPs were susceptible to degradation, transformation, or interactions with digestive and fermentation components, leading to a marked loss of α-glucosidase inhibitory capacity. By contrast, IDF and IDF-PP maintained relatively stable inhibitory activities during upper gastrointestinal digestion and exhibited progressive increases during colonic fermentation. At 48 h, the inhibitory activities of IDF and IDF-PP increased by 16.95 and 24.32%, respectively, compared with those after small intestinal digestion. The increase in the IDF group may be related to the gradual release of native BPs from the fiber matrix. Importantly, IDF-PP showed significantly higher α-glucosidase inhibitory activity than IDF throughout fermentation (P < 0.05), indicating that polyphenol loading enhanced the pool of bioactive compounds available under simulated colonic conditions.

The enhanced inhibitory activity of IDF-PP may result from the protective and release-regulating effects of the IDF matrix. Non-covalent interactions and physical entrapment between IDF and polyphenols could reduce the premature loss of active compounds during upper gastrointestinal digestion. During fermentation, microbial degradation or loosening of the IDF structure may promote the release of native BPs and adsorbed polyphenols. Meanwhile, microbial biotransformation may generate phenolic metabolites with altered enzyme-inhibitory activities. These processes may collectively contribute to the increased α-glucosidase inhibition observed in the IDF-PP group.

Taken together, IDF-PP effectively preserved α-glucosidase inhibitory activity during simulated digestion and enhanced inhibitory activity during colonic fermentation. These results suggest that IDF-PP has potential as a dietary fiber–polyphenol carrier for improving the gastrointestinal stability and fermentation-associated availability of α-glucosidase inhibitory compounds, although in vivo validation is still required.

Production of SCFAs during in vitro fermentation

SCFAs, mainly acetate, propionate, and butyrate, are major microbial metabolites generated during colonic fermentation and are closely associated with intestinal homeostasis, luminal pH regulation, epithelial energy supply, and immune modulation (Wang et al., 2020; Zhou, Qin, Chen, Wang, Du, et al., 2025). To assess the effect of IDF-PP on microbial fermentation, the concentrations of major SCFAs and isovaleric acid were monitored over 48 h of in vitro colonic fermentation.

As shown in Fig. 5A, acetate was the dominant SCFA in all groups. CK maintained low acetate levels throughout fermentation, reflecting limited substrate availability. In contrast, IDF-PP markedly promoted acetate production, reaching a maximum of 1.762 ± 0.005 mg/mL at 24 h and remaining at a high level at 48 h. The acetate concentration in the IDF-PP group was significantly higher than those in the FPs and IDF groups at 24 and 48 h (P < 0.05). This suggests that IDF-PP provided a favorable substrate system for acetate production, likely through the fermentable IDF matrix and the potential microbial modulatory effects of released polyphenols on microbial metabolism (Hou et al., 2024).

Changes in the concentrations of SCFAs and isovaleric acid during in vitro colonic fermentation. (A) Acetic acid. (B) Propionic acid. (C) Butyric acid. (D) Isovaleric acid. Values are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3). Different uppercase letters indicate significant differences among groups at the same fermentation time point (P < 0.05). Different lowercase letters indicate significant differences among fermentation time points within the same group (P < 0.05). CK, blank control; FPs, free polyphenols; IDF, insoluble dietary fiber; IDF-PP, insoluble dietary fiber–polyphenol complex.

Propionate production followed a distinct pattern (Fig. 5B). All substrate-containing groups produced more propionate than CK during fermentation. FPs induced relatively high propionate levels during the early and middle fermentation stages, whereas IDF reached its highest level at 48 h, possibly due to the delayed fermentation of resistant fiber fractions. In contrast, IDF-PP did not consistently show the highest propionate production, indicating that its fermentation-promoting effect was SCFA-specific rather than universal.

For butyrate, IDF-PP showed a clear advantage (Fig. 5C). From 12 to 48 h, the IDF-PP group exhibited significantly higher butyrate concentrations than the other groups (P < 0.05), with the maximum level observed at 24 h. This indicates that the IDF-PP complex may favor butyrate production during fermentation. The enhanced butyrate generation may be associated with the sustained fermentation of the IDF matrix and the possible regulatory effects of released polyphenols on microbial metabolism. Further microbiota analysis is required to confirm whether specific butyrate-producing populations were enriched.

Isovaleric acid, a branched-chain fatty acid mainly derived from amino acid fermentation, was predominantly detected in the IDF-PP group (Fig. 5D). From 6 to 48 h, its concentration in the IDF-PP group was significantly higher than that in the other groups (P < 0.05), suggesting that IDF-PP may also influence nitrogen-related microbial metabolism. However, because branched-chain fatty acids are associated with proteolytic fermentation, the increase in isovaleric acid should be interpreted with caution, as it does not necessarily indicate a beneficial metabolic shift.

Taken together, IDF-PP promoted acetate and butyrate production more effectively than FPs or IDF alone, whereas its effect on propionate was less pronounced. These findings suggest that the IDF-PP complex altered microbial fermentation patterns and may contribute to intestinal benefits through modulation of the microbiota–SCFA axis. Further studies combining microbiota profiling and metabolomic analysis are needed to clarify the specific microbial and metabolic mechanisms involved.

Microbial community composition during in vitro fermentation

Modulation of overall microbial community structure during in vitro fermentation

Gut microbiota composition is closely related to host metabolic homeostasis, and dietary substrates are important drivers of microbial community structure and function. To assess the modulatory effect of IDF-PP on gut microbiota, 16S rRNA gene sequencing was performed on samples collected after 24 h of in vitro colonic fermentation.

As shown in Fig. 6A, the rank-abundance curves displayed smooth tails, suggesting relatively diverse and even microbial communities across groups. The species accumulation curve increased with sample number and gradually approached a plateau (Fig. 6B), indicating that the sequencing effort captured a substantial proportion of microbial diversity. Because the same mixed fecal inoculum was aliquoted into all fermentation systems, the initial microbial input was standardized across groups. The Chao1 index showed no significant differences among the CK, IDF, and IDF-PP groups (Fig. 6C; P > 0.05), indicating that microbial richness was not markedly affected by substrate treatment after 24 h of fermentation. In contrast, both the Shannon and Simpson indices differed significantly among groups (P < 0.05), with the IDF-PP group showing the highest values (Fig. 6D,E). Because Chao1 remained unchanged while Shannon and Simpson increased, the observed diversity shift was likely mainly driven by improved community evenness rather than changes in richness (Li, Li et al., 2025; Wang et al., 2022). This suggests that IDF-PP contributed to a more balanced microbial community structure during fermentation.

Microbial community diversity and structure after 24 h of in vitro colonic fermentation.

(A) Rank-abundance curves. (B) Species accumulation curve. (C) Chao1 index. (D) Shannon index. (E) Simpson index. (F) PCA score plot. (G) Venn diagram. Values are expressed as mean ± SD (n = 3). Significant differences among groups were determined at P < 0.05. CK24, blank control fermented for 24 h; IDF24, insoluble dietary fiber fermented for 24 h; IDF-PP24, insoluble dietary fiber–polyphenol complex fermented for 24 h.

PCA revealed separation in community structure among the CK, IDF, and IDF-PP groups (Fig. 6F). The first two principal components explained 97.5% of the total variance. PERMANOVA further indicated that the between-group differences in community structure were statistically significant (P < 0.05), supporting the separation observed in the PCA plot. In particular, the distinction between IDF and IDF-PP suggests that polyphenol incorporation was associated with a microbial community profile different from that of IDF alone. The Venn diagram showed that CK, IDF, and IDF-PP contained 765, 538, and 566 unique ASVs, respectively, with 172 ASVs shared among all groups (Fig. 6G). These results further indicate substrate-dependent differences in microbial composition. The unique ASVs in each group may reflect differences in the detectability and relative abundance of low-abundance taxa under different fermentation conditions. Further taxonomic annotation is required to clarify which microbial groups contributed to these differences.

Taken together, IDF-PP altered the overall microbial community structure during in vitro fermentation, mainly by increasing community evenness and generating a clustering pattern distinct from those of CK and IDF. These findings suggest that the IDF-PP complex may exert a substrate-specific modulatory effect on gut microbiota, which may be related to the enhanced fermentation metabolite production observed in Section 3.5.

Taxonomic profiling and specific enrichment of gut microbiota

Taxonomic profiling was performed at the phylum, family, and genus levels after 24 h of in vitro colonic fermentation to further clarify the microbial shifts induced by IDF-PP. At the phylum level, Firmicutes, Bacteroidetes, and Proteobacteria dominated the microbial communities (Fig. 7A). Compared with CK, both IDF and IDF-PP reduced the relative abundance of Proteobacteria, whereas IDF-PP markedly increased the relative abundance of Bacteroidetes. Since many members of Bacteroidetes possess extensive carbohydrate-active enzyme systems and are efficient degraders of complex polysaccharides (Ma et al., 2021), their higher relative abundance may reflect enhanced microbial utilization of the IDF-based matrix. The decreased Firmicutes/Bacteroidetes ratio in the IDF-PP group should therefore be interpreted as a substrate-dependent microbial response rather than a direct indicator of host health status (Dong et al., 2020).

In the heatmap, red and blue indicate higher and lower standardized relative abundance, respectively. At the family level, IDF-PP altered the distribution of dominant bacterial families (Fig. 7B). Compared with CK, the IDF-PP group showed higher relative abundances of Bacteroidaceae, Prevotellaceae, and Lachnospiraceae, suggesting increased representation of taxa associated with glycan degradation and SCFA production. In contrast, Enterobacteriaceae and Enterococcaceae showed lower relative abundances in the IDF-PP group. Because these families include facultative anaerobic and opportunistic members, this pattern may reflect a shift in community composition under IDF-PP fermentation. However, direct inhibition cannot be inferred from relative abundance data alone.

The genus-level heatmap showed distinct clustering patterns among CK, IDF, and IDF-PP samples (Fig. 7C), suggesting substrate-dependent variation in microbial abundance profiles. Genera such as Escherichia, Citrobacter, Serratia, and Fusobacterium showed relatively higher abundance in the CK group and lower abundance after IDF or IDF-PP fermentation. In contrast, different genera with potential roles in complex carbohydrate degradation and SCFA production appeared more abundant in the IDF and IDF-PP groups. Specifically, Prevotella, Faecalibacterium, Butyricicoccus, Oscillospira, Dialister, and Megamonas showed higher relative abundance patterns in the IDF-PP group, whereas Bacteroides, Parabacteroides, Roseburia, Anaerostipes, and Coprococcus were more abundant in the IDF group. The higher relative abundance of Prevotella in the IDF-PP group may be related to enhanced plant polysaccharide degradation and the generation of fermentation intermediates for microbial cross-feeding. Meanwhile, the higher relative abundance patterns of Faecalibacterium and Butyricicoccus may be associated with the enhanced butyrate production observed in Section 3.5. Overall, these heatmap patterns suggest that IDF and IDF-PP were associated with different carbohydrate-utilization and SCFA-related bacterial profiles. Statistical identification of discriminative taxa was further assessed by LEfSe analysis in the following section.

Collectively, these results indicate that IDF-PP was associated with a distinct microbial profile, featuring higher relative abundance of taxa related to carbohydrate fermentation and SCFA production, together with lower relative abundance of several potentially opportunistic taxa. In particular, the subsequent LEfSe analysis further supported the differential association of Prevotella- and Faecalibacterium-related taxa with the IDF-PP group. These microbial shifts were consistent with the enhanced acetate and butyrate production in the IDF-PP group, although further metagenomic, metabolomic, and absolute quantification analyses are needed to verify the functional contribution of these taxa.

LEfSe-based identification of discriminative bacterial taxa

To statistically identify bacterial taxa associated with the group differences suggested by the genus-level heatmap, LEfSe analysis was performed after 24 h of in vitro colonic fermentation (Fig. 8). Taxa with a significance threshold of P < 0.05 and an LDA score > 2.0 were considered discriminative features. The cladogram and LDA score plot revealed distinct microbial biomarkers across the three groups, indicating substrate-dependent taxonomic shifts. LEfSe identified the IDF group associated with Bacteroidaceae, Bacteroides, Roseburia, Parabacteroides, Blautia, and Actinobacteria-related taxa. Bacteroides and Parabacteroides are well known for their capacity to degrade complex glycans, while some Actinobacteria-related taxa, while some Actinobacteria-related taxa, particularly bifidobacterial lineages when present, have been associated with carbohydrate fermentation and acetate production (El Kaoutari et al., 2013; Fukuda et al., 2011; Rivière et al., 2016). Roseburia and Blautia include members associated with SCFA metabolism, with Roseburia commonly regarded as a butyrate-associated genus (Louis & Flint, 2017). These taxa may partly explain the acetate and butyrate production observed during IDF fermentation.

The IDF-PP group was mainly distinguished by Prevotella-centered Bacteroidetes taxa, including Bacteroidetes, Bacteroidia, Bacteroidales, Prevotellaceae, and Prevotella, together with the SCFA-associated genus Faecalibacterium. Prevotella members are commonly associated with the degradation of plant-derived polysaccharides through diverse carbohydrate utilization systems, whereas Faecalibacterium is widely recognized as a butyrate-associated genus (Hou et al., 2024; Louis & Flint, 2017). Their enrichment suggests that IDF-PP was associated with a microbial consortium with potential for plant polysaccharide utilization and SCFA-associated fermentation. This interpretation is also consistent with the structural remodeling of the IDF-PP matrix observed during fermentation, which may have increased microbial access to fermentable substrates and fiber-associated polyphenols.

In contrast, CK was characterized by enrichment of several Proteobacteria-related and facultative anaerobic taxa, including Proteobacteria, Gammaproteobacteria, Enterobacteriales, Enterobacteriaceae, Escherichia, Citrobacter, Morganella, and related taxa. Since these groups include opportunistic members, their enrichment may reflect the microbial profile favored under the control fermentation condition with limited exogenous fermentable substrates. However, because LEfSe analysis is based on relative abundance, these taxa should be interpreted as group-associated biomarkers rather than direct evidence of bacterial growth promotion or inhibition.

Collectively, LEfSe analysis showed that IDF and IDF-PP were associated with different microbial biomarker profiles. IDF was associated with Bacteroides-, Parabacteroides-, Roseburia-, Blautia-related taxa, whereas IDF-PP was primarily associated with Prevotella-centered Bacteroidetes taxa and Faecalibacterium. These findings suggest that polyphenol complexation was associated with a shift in the microbial utilization pattern of IDF, resulting in a distinct fermentation-associated taxonomic profile consistent with the enhanced SCFA production observed during IDF-PP fermentation.

Correlation between bacterial genera and fermentation-related functional parameters

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to explore the associations between representative bacterial genera and fermentation-related parameters (Fig. 9). Prevotella, Faecalibacterium, and Butyricicoccus were positively correlated with total phenolic release rate, DPPH and ABTS radical-scavenging activities, α-glucosidase inhibitory activity, and SCFA production, while showing negative correlations with pH. These associations were consistent with the higher relative abundance of plant-polysaccharide-utilizing and SCFA-associated taxa observed in the IDF-PP group. Bacteroides and Parabacteroides also showed positive correlations with several SCFA-related and functional indicators, which is consistent with their potential role in fiber fermentation. In contrast, Escherichia and Morganella were positively correlated with pH but negatively correlated with SCFA production and several functional parameters. These results further supported an association between microbial community shifts and fermentation-derived functional changes. Nevertheless, these correlations should be interpreted as associations rather than direct evidence of causality.

Structural transformation of IDF-PP during in vitro fermentation

To explore the structural changes associated with fermentation-induced degradation and polyphenol release, IDF and IDF-PP were characterized by FTIR, SEM, and XRD before and after 24 h of in vitro colonic fermentation.

FTIR spectral analysis

FTIR spectroscopy was used to assess the changes in characteristic functional groups of IDF and IDF-PP before and after fermentation (Fig. 10). The unfermented IDF sample exhibited typical polysaccharide-related bands, including a broad O—H stretching band at 3408 cm−1, a C—H stretching band at 2926 cm−1, and a strong carbohydrate fingerprint band at 1040 cm−1, which is commonly assigned to C–O–C/C–O–H vibrations of pyranose rings and glycosidic linkages (Fig. 10A). The band at 1731 cm−1 was tentatively assigned to ester carbonyl-related vibrations that may arise from residual esterified polysaccharide components or other carbonyl-containing constituents in the fiber matrix, whereas the signal around 1637 cm−1 may be associated with bound water, carboxyl groups, or aromatic-related vibrations (Hou et al., 2024).

FTIR spectra of IDF and IDF-PP before and after 24 h of in vitro colonic fermentation.

(A) FTIR spectra of IDF before fermentation and after 24 h fermentation. (B) FTIR spectra of IDF-PP before fermentation and after 24 h fermentation. Blue lines indicate unfermented samples, and red lines indicate residues after fermentation. (For interpretation of the references to colour in this figure legend, the reader is referred to the web version of this article.)

After 24 h of fermentation, the main absorption bands of IDF decreased markedly, particularly those at 3408, 2926, 1731, and 1040 cm−1. Such band attenuation only suggests a relative reduction in characteristic carbohydrate-related functional groups within the fermented residue. Therefore, these FTIR changes should be regarded as indirect evidence of structural alteration rather than direct proof of specific bond cleavage, microbial utilization, or enzyme activity (Wu et al., 2025).

As shown in Fig. 10B, the unfermented IDF-PP complex exhibited a slight shift in the O—H stretching band (3413 cm−1) and altered absorption features in the 1500–1700 cm−1 region, which may suggest possible interactions between polyphenols and the IDF matrix (Li et al., 2024). However, the specific interaction modes could not be confirmed by FTIR alone. After fermentation, the overall spectral intensity of IDF-PP also decreased, indicating structural alteration and a relative reduction in characteristic functional groups within the fermented residue. Compared with fermented IDF, however, the fermented IDF-PP residue retained clearer characteristic bands, especially the O—H stretching band and the carbohydrate fingerprint peak around 1054 cm−1.

These results suggest that IDF-PP may have undergone less extensive structural alteration than IDF during fermentation, This pattern could be related to the presence of polyphenol-associated interactions within the fiber matrix, which may have influenced the accessibility of some carbohydrate domains. Such structural characteristics may be relevant to the distinct fermentation behavior of IDF-PP, although no direct causal relationship with SCFA production can be established from the FTIR data alone.

SEM analysis

SEM was used to visualize the morphological changes of IDF and IDF-PP before and after 24 h of in vitro colonic fermentation, including particle morphology, surface roughness, and pore formation (Fig. 11). The unfermented IDF showed large and irregular fragments with relatively compact surfaces and obvious particle aggregation (Fig. 11A1). Although some surface cracks were observed, no pronounced surface cavities or channel-like structures were evident at the observed scale. After 24 h of fermentation, the morphology of IDF changed markedly. The original compact particles became more fragmented and loose, with irregular cavities and flocculent fragments appearing on the surface (Fig. 11A2). These observations suggest substantial surface disruption of the IDF matrix during fermentation and are consistent with the attenuation of polysaccharide-related FTIR bands (Su et al., 2022).

SEM images of IDF and IDF-PP before and after 24 h of in vitro colonic fermentation.

(A1) Unfermented IDF; (A2) IDF residue after 24 h fermentation; (B1) unfermented IDF-PP; (B2) IDF-PP residue after 24 h fermentation.

Compared with IDF, the unfermented IDF-PP complex exhibited a relatively looser morphology, with more surface-visible cavities and irregular channels-like features (Fig. 11B1). This difference may be associated with the presence of polyphenols in the IDF matrix, which could influence particle aggregation and surface organization. After fermentation, the IDF-PP residue displayed increased surface roughness and more apparent pore−/cavity-like structures, with locally honeycomb-like features observed in some regions (Fig. 11B2). These observations suggest surface erosion and structural alteration of the IDF-PP matrix during fermentation, while a discernible residual scaffold was still retained (Wu et al., 2025).

The pore−/cavity-like features observed after fermentation reflect visible morphological alteration of the sample surface, but SEM alone cannot determine internal accessibility, porosity, or diffusion behavior. Compared with the more fragmented morphology of fermented IDF, the residual framework observed in fermented IDF-PP may indicate less extensive structural disruption. Overall, these SEM observations are consistent with the FTIR results and suggest that polyphenol association may influence the fermentation-related structural changes of IDF.

XRD analysis

XRD analysis was conducted to evaluate changes in the ordered and disordered structural domains of IDF and IDF-PP before and after 24 h of in vitro colonic fermentation (Fig. 12). Both unfermented IDF and IDF-PP exhibited cellulose I-like diffraction patterns, with a broad diffraction signal around 2θ = 14.9° and a main peak near 2θ = 21.5°, indicating the coexistence of relatively ordered crystalline domains and less ordered or amorphous regions in the dietary fiber matrix. To further compare structural differences among samples, the CrI was calculated.

XRD patterns of IDF and IDF-PP before and after 24 h of in vitro colonic fermentation.

(A) IDF before and after fermentation. (B) IDF-PP before and after fermentation. The characteristic diffraction peaks near 2θ = 14.9° and 21.5° were used for qualitative structural comparison, and crystallinity index values were calculated as described in Section 2.11.3.

Compared with IDF, the diffraction profile of the unfermented IDF-PP complex showed some variation, suggesting that polyphenol incorporation may have altered the relative organization of the fiber matrix. The calculated CrI values showed that unfermented IDF and IDF-PP had CrI of 15.81% and 16.19%, respectively. This slight difference suggests that the association of polyphenols with the fiber matrix may have influenced the relative contribution of ordered and disordered domains; however, the underlying structural basis cannot be determined by XRD alone.

After 24 h of fermentation, the the CrI increased to 16.24% for IDF and 17.51% for IDF-PP. This trend may be consistent with the preferential removal of less ordered components during fermentation, resulting in a relative enrichment of more ordered domains in the residual material. In addition, the characteristic diffraction peak of fermented IDF-PP remained relatively distinct, suggesting that part of the ordered fiber framework was retained after fermentation.

This observation is consistent with the SEM results showing that fermented IDF-PP retained a more discernible residual framework than fermented IDF. Overall, the XRD data suggest that IDF-PP underwent structural alteration during fermentation while maintaining a relatively more ordered residual matrix. This structural persistence may be related to the distinct fermentation behavior of IDF-PP observed in this study, although direct causal links to polyphenol release or SCFA production cannot be established from XRD data alone.

Integrating the FTIR, SEM, and XRD results provides a tentative structural explanation for the different fermentation behaviors of IDF and the IDF-PP complex. FTIR showed that both IDF and IDF-PP underwent microbial degradation, while the fermented IDF-PP residue retained clearer polysaccharide-related absorption bands than fermented IDF. SEM further revealed that IDF-PP developed a rougher and more cavity-rich surface after fermentation while still maintaining a recognizable residual framework, in contrast to the more fragmented morphology observed for IDF. XRD analysis also indicated that more ordered domains were relatively enriched in the residual material after fermentation.

Taken together, these observations suggest that polyphenol complexation may have influenced the structural disintegration pattern of IDF during fermentation. The retained residual framework in IDF-PP may be associated with less extensive structural disruption than that observed for IDF alone, which might partly explain its distinct fermentation behavior. In addition, the observed morphological changes may be relevant to the release behavior of fiber-associated polyphenols during fermentation (Li et al., 2024; Peng et al., 2023), although this could not be directly verified in the present study. Therefore, the proposed structure–function relationship should be regarded as inferential. Further quantitative analyses, such as residue mass loss, pore-size distribution, release kinetics, and time-resolved structural characterization, are needed to validate this interpretation.

Conclusions

This study demonstrated the feasibility of preparing an IDF-polyphenol complex from RRT processing by-products, which exhibited distinct behavior during simulated gastrointestinal digestion and in vitro colonic fermentation. The IDF matrix was associated with improved retention of polyphenols during simulated digestion, possibly due to non-covalent interactions and physical entrapment. During in vitro fermentation, IDF-PP exhibited structural changes while retaining part of its original framework, suggesting that polyphenol complexation may influence the fermentation-related disintegration behavior of IDF. Compared with FPs or IDF alone, IDF-PP was associated with differences in microbial composition, including relatively higher abundances of Prevotella-related Bacteroidetes taxa and Faecalibacterium, together with lower abundances of Proteobacteria and Enterobacteriaceae-related genera. These changes were observed alongside higher SCFA levels, especially acetate and butyrate, and stronger antioxidant and α-glucosidase inhibitory activities during fermentation. Collectively, these findings suggest that IDF-PP has potential as a fiber-based carrier of phenolic compounds with promising stability during simulated digestion and favorable characteristics during in vitro fermentation.

However, the conclusions of this study should be interpreted within the limitations of the experimental design. The fermentation experiments were conducted in vitro, and therefore may not fully reflect the complexity of the human colonic environment. In addition, the structural analyses were mainly based on qualitative or semi-quantitative characterization, and the proposed relationships among structural changes, polyphenol release, and fermentation behavior were not directly validated by mechanistic or kinetic experiments. Further studies combining quantitative structural analysis, release kinetics, multi-omics approaches, and in vivo validation are needed to confirm the physiological relevance and underlying mechanisms of the observed effects.
