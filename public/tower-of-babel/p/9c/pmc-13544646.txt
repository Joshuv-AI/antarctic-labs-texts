Prospective cohort study to estimate the safety of direct discharge to home from the intensive care unit
Authors: Shikha Yadav, Ravi Prakash, Zia Arshad, Ram Gopal Maurya
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Context:Traditionally, intensive care unit (ICU) discharge has followed a tiered approach of managing cases, i.e., patients are transferred to a general or parent surgical ward or high-dependency unit before being discharged to home. This practice assumes that some additional time is mandatory to observe patients who are recovering from the illness. With latest improvisations in postdischarge services for direct discharge to home (DDH), the balance is shifting in favor of DDH.Aims:To compare the clinical outcomes and safety of ICU patients discharged directly to home with those transferred to hospital wards before being discharged.Settings and Design:Prospective cohort study done at a tertiary care hospital.Methods:According to patient’s discharge or transfer to ward (TTW), they were grouped in either of the two: Group D: Direct discharge to home (DDH), Group W: TTW (minimum stay in ward 24 h). All patients were followed up to 3 months of discharge/transfer.Statistical Analysis Used:Continuous data of two independent groups were compared by independent Student’s t-test, whereas categorical data by Chi-square (χ2) test were used. A two-tailed (α =2) P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.Results:The results showed that ICU patient discharged directly to home had better post-ICU recovery for 90 days’ follow-up.Conclusions:ICU patients discharged directly to home have significantly better clinical outcomes and safety, when applied to carefully selected ICU patients compared to those transferred to hospital wards before being discharged.

INTRODUCTION

The intensive care unit (ICU) plays a vital part in healthcare, delivering advanced life-support measures and continuous monitoring to patients with critical conditions.[1] With advancements in critical care and supportive management, survival rates among ICU cases have optimized significantly. However, a considerable proportion of critically ill patients need prolonged ICU stay due to their complexity of disease which affects their recovery.[2] The vacuity of ICU beds when coupled with rising demand has led to ICU overcrowding, a growing concern in both developed and resource-constrained settings. In few cases, home surroundings may also lack the equipment, expertise, and caregiver readiness necessary to support complex post-ICU care.[3] For DDH to be implemented safely, it is essential to set up clear patient selection criteria, ensure robust discharge planning, and implement reliable systems for postdischarge monitoring. Despite potential benefits, DDH raises the important safety concerns. Patients recently discharged from ICU may still be vulnerable to clinical deterioration, including infections, respiratory compromise, or relapses of the underlying illness. To enhance the safety and effectiveness of DDH, healthcare systems must invest in structured postdischarge support frameworks, including tele-health services, home nursing care, and scheduled follow-up appointments. Patient and caregiver education should be emphasized as the part of discharge planning to ensure early recognition and response to potential complications.[4] ICU care is not without challenges. It is associated with significant healthcare expenditure and ICU beds are often limited, particularly in low- and middle-income countries.[5] In addition, survivors of prolonged ICU stay frequently experience postintensive care syndrome, a condition characterized by cognitive, physical, and psychological impairments that may persist long after hospital discharge.[6] These issues highlight the importance of not only delivering life-saving interventions but also focusing on long-term recovery and rehabilitation. The coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic underscored the global dependence on ICU services and exposed systemic vulnerabilities, including workforce shortages, equipment constraints, and ICU triage limitations.[7] In response, many healthcare systems are now revisiting ICU care models to ensure better preparedness, flexibility, and continuity of care. As the demand for intensive care services continues to rise, it becomes imperative to explore strategies that refine ICU utilization without compromising patient outcomes. Innovations in these areas hold promise not only in improving patient experiences but also in addressing the growing burden on critical care systems worldwide. While initially considered controversial, emerging studies support its safety and potential benefits in carefully selected patient populations. Moreover, predictive screening tools and risk stratification models are increasingly being used to identify patients best suited for DDH, based on the factors such as clinical stability, social support, and homecare readiness.[8] This prospective cohort study was done to evaluate the effectiveness of direct discharge to home from ICU as compared to conventional transfer to ward (TTW).

METHODS

This prospective, cohort, clinical study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital. Patients were enrolled and followed for 3 months. Informed written consent was obtained from all participants/attendants after a thorough explanation of the study. The trial included patients >18 years, of either gender admitted to the ICU for more than 48 h. According to patient’s discharge or TTW, they were divided in either of the two groups: Group D: direct discharge to home (DDH) or Group W: TTW (minimum stay in ward 24 h).

All the enrolled patients were followed up till 3 months of discharge/transfer, and the following data was recorded: Personal Details, Disease severity (APACHE II), Comorbidities, Mechanical ventilation, Muscle Relaxant use, requirement of renal replacement therapy (RRT), Vasopressor Support, Length of ICU stay, Diagnosis, and Outcome (Discharge/Transfer/Revisit/Readmit/Mortality).

Patient selection criteria for direct discharge to home

Successful execution of DDH depends on thorough patient selection. Identifying candidates who are clinically stable, functionally independent, and low risk for deterioration is key. Discharge criteria included clinical improvement of primary illness, hemodynamic stability without vasopressors, no ventilatory or oxygen support, stable neurological status, functional readiness, caregiver support and professional home healthcare services. The decision to transfer the patient to ward or discharge to home was taken by ICU team independently. Patient TTW were further treated by their parent team and discharged once the condition specific discharge criteria were met. The decision to discharge is sole discretion of parent team.

Discharge criteria for DDH were determined by the ICU team based on clinical stability rather than one single physiological parameter. Patients were considered eligible for DDH if there was improvement of the primary reason for ICU admission hemodynamically stability was there without vasopressor and ventilatory support, stable neurological status varying from patient to patient. DDH was based on our team management in regard of patient clinical status, anticipated postdischarge follow ups, social support, and discharge (home or ward). As a result, DDH was considered in clinically stable patients requiring extended postcare discharge. The patient discharged was followed up in critical care follow-up clinic and their problems were addressed through this clinic.

The statistical analysis of the above data was prepared on SPSS software (version 22.0, IBM India Private Limited is headquartered in Bengaluru, Karnataka, India). The continuous data were summarized in mean ± standard deviation, whereas discrete (categorical) in number (n) and percentage (%). Continuous two independent groups were compared by independent Student’s t-test, whereas categorical by Chi-square (χ2) test. A two-tailed (α =2) P < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

DISCUSSION

This prospective cohort study evaluated and compared the safety of direct discharge to home (DDH) from the ICU with the traditional pathway of discharging patients through TTW before final discharge. A total of 601 critically adult patients who admitted > 72 h in ICU were evaluated. Of total, 219 were directly discharged to home (DDH) and 382 transferred to ward and then discharged (TTW). The outcomes were assessed at three time points – 30-, 60- and 90-days post-discharge – focusing on readmission rates, unplanned revisits, and mortality.

Patients transferred from the ICU to the ward were typically under the care of their primary surgical team. These patients no longer require major organ support or intensive monitoring and were therefore considered suitable for ward-level care. Following transfer, they underwent a period of continued observation and management and were subsequently discharged home. Patients in the DDH group were clinically stable, with no requirement for ward-level escalation, and were considered suitable for direct discharge home, with DDH ensuring continuity of care at the point of discharge.

Patients in the TTW group were significantly older (years) compared to those in the DDH group (years). There was a statistically significant difference in sex distribution between the groups, with a higher proportion of males in the TTW group compared to the DDH group. The APACHE II score, which indicates disease severity, was comparable between both groups. The overall distribution for comorbidities was statistically significant contradicting traditional expectations that comorbid patients are more likely to require ward-based monitoring post-ICU. The findings suggest that direct discharge from ICU is not limited to young or low-risk patients. With appropriate selection and support, even older patients with stable comorbidities can be safely discharged home. This aligns with evolving ICU discharge practices and reinforces the importance of individualized, criteria-driven discharge planning.

A significantly greater proportion of patients in the TTW group required mechanical ventilation (87.2%) and muscle relaxants (86.6%) compared to the DDH group (66.2% each). The need for RRT was also lower in the DDH (26.0%) compared to the TTW (34.0%). Vasopressor requirement was more frequent in the TTW (62.3%) than in the DDH (47.9%). Interestingly, although not statistically significant (P = 0.181), the mean ICU stay was slightly longer in the DDH (13.32 ± 6.97 days) compared to the TTW (12.53 ± 6.47 day). The slightly longer ICU stay in the DDH group in this study may reflect prolonged monitoring to ensure discharge readiness directly from ICU, bypassing the ward step-down period. These findings highlight that patients discharged directly home tend to have lower requirements for critical interventions (e.g., ventilation, vasopressors, and RRT), supporting their stability and eligibility for DDH. Despite a nonsignificant difference in ICU length of stay, the data support the growing evidence base that DDH can be safely implemented in selected patients, reducing hospital burden without compromising safety.

The analysis revealed that in 30 days, most of the Direct Discharge to home patients had normal recovery, while TTW patients showed a higher frequency of revisits and early readmissions. By 60 and 90 days, the disparity widened: TTW patients experienced slightly higher cumulative mortality and increased readmission rates, particularly in older age groups. Importantly, the readmission and revisit rates in the DDH group remained low, reinforcing the safety of direct discharge in stable patients. OF was again substantially lower in the DDH group. Our findings support that direct discharge home from ICU is clinically safe in appropriately selected patients. Across all three postdischarge intervals (30, 60, and 90 days), patients in the DDH group exhibited comparable, and often better, outcomes than those in the TTW group. These results align with global studies that suggest ICU patients who meet physiological discharge criteria may not require an intermediate step-downward stay.

Given the burden on ICU and ward beds in most Indian hospitals, safe implementation of DDH protocols could significantly improve resource utilization without compromising patient outcomes. Loss to follow-up poses a potential limitation in data accuracy and completeness, especially if those lost to follow-up represent a subgroup at higher risk of adverse outcomes. Logically, DDH patients may be selected based on safety, so it is natural that they may be less sick, more stable, so they may have better organ functions.

Limitations

Due to ethical and safety issues, patients who are more stable and have lesser number of comorbidities were considered for DDH. Hence, there may be a more favorable patient profile in DDH. As this was an observational study, residual confounding cannot be completely excluded. With accumulation of more evidence and improvement in community-based health services, patients with complex conditions can be considered for DDH.

CONCLUSIONS

The patients who discharged directly to home show cost-effective management during ICU stay as compared to those who transferred to hospital wards before being discharged. There are several potential explanations for why DDH patients fared equally well or better by faster reintegration into the home environment, which is often psychologically beneficial and supports early mobilization and avoiding wards may reduce the risk of nosocomial infections and patient deconditioning.

This study provides important insights into how ICU discharge planning can be optimized: DDH can safely reduce ICU congestion and improve resource utilization, especially where ward beds are scarce. A standardized discharge checklist or readiness protocol could further enhance safety. Postdischarge monitoring, including telephonic follow-up, and early outpatient reviews should be an essential part of DDH programs. The model’s success hinges on effective triaging and follow-up systems, which may not be uniformly available in all healthcare settings.
