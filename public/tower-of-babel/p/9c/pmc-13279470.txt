Hepatitis B virus RNA levels and clinical characteristics of persistent viremia in HBsAg-positive patients undergoing nucleos(t)ide analog☆
Authors: Yuzhu Shi, Yuchen Xie, Ying Chen, Xinru Guo, Li Yang, Xuedong Song, Fumin Feng, Jianhua Lu, Erhei Dai
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•HBV RNA is an independent risk factor for persistent viremia (PV) during NAs therapy.•PV patients with positive HBV RNA exhibit more severe liver injury and cirrhosis.•Serum HBV RNA strongly correlates with HBeAg, reflecting cccDNA transcriptional activity.•Monitoring HBV RNA aids in risk stratification and guides optimized antiviral management.Keywords: Hepatitis B virus RNA, Nucleos(t)ide analogs, Persistent viremia, Liver injury

Introduction

Hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection is a major global public health problem and may present as either acute or chronic infection. Chronic hepatitis B (CHB), which develops in a proportion of infected individuals, primarily affects the liver and may lead to persistent hepatic inflammation and progressive liver damage. Over time, CHB can progress to liver cirrhosis (LC) and, in some cases, hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC).(Tao et al., 2022) According to a report by the World Health Organization (WHO), approximately 254 million people worldwide were living with chronic hepatitis B(CHB) as of 2022, with an estimated 1.2 million new infections annually. In the same year, hepatitis B was responsible for approximately 1.1 million deaths, primarily due to LC and HCC. (World Health, 2024) The current standard treatment for chronic hepatitis B involves long-term antiviral therapy, particularly first-line nucleos(t)ide analogs (NAs) such as entecavir (ETV), tenofovir disoproxil fumarate (TDF), tenofovir alafenamide fumarate (TAF), and tenofovir amibufenamide (TMF). However, some patients treated with NAs fail to achieve a virological response and exhibit persistent viremia (PV), thereby increasing the risk of HCC and liver fibrosis progression.(Kim et al., 2017; Fan et al., 2020)

While antiviral therapy plays a significant role in reducing the risk of HCC, persistent HBV DNA positivity after treatment may increase the risk of liver disease progression. Monitoring serum HBV DNA levels during NAs therapy is essential to assess the virological response. However, NAs do not directly target covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA), which remains an active reservoir for viral replication. Consequently, detecting serum HBV DNA alone does not fully reflect cccDNA activity in NA-treated patients. Liver biopsy, the gold standard for detecting cccDNA, is an invasive procedure and is unsuitable for routine clinical practice. In this context, serum HBV RNA levels have emerged as a more accurate, non-invasive marker of cccDNA activity, as they more precisely reflect the transcriptional activity of the virus compared to serum HBV DNA.(Lu et al., 2017; Tsuge et al., 2013; Giersch et al., 2017; Wang, Du, et al., 2017) Studies suggest that serum HBV RNA can serve as a sensitive marker of viral replication, providing a clearer picture of the activity of the virus in NA-treated patients. The accumulation of HBV RNA and low viral replication can increase liver inflammation and damage. Virus-like particles generated by HBV RNA in liver cells are released into the bloodstream when cells are damaged, creating a cycle that further exacerbates liver dysfunction.(Wang, Yu, et al., 2017)

This study aimed to analyze the distribution of HBV RNA levels in patients with PV and to investigate the clinical characteristics associated with HBV RNA positivity in this population. Given the limited understanding of clinical differences among HBV RNA-positive PV patients in existing research, this study seeks to fill this gap. By examining the relationship between HBV RNA positivity, liver function, and virological features, this study aims to provide critical insights to facilitate risk stratification and the development of personalized management strategies for patients with PV.

Materials and methods

Patients and subjects

This cross-sectional study enrolled 1254 patients who had received NAs therapy for over 48 weeks at six hospitals in Hebei province between June 2023 and March 2024, namely the Fifth Hospital of Shijiazhuang, the People's Hospital of Baoding, the Seventh Hospital of Tangshan, the Infectious Disease Hospital of Handan, the Third Hospital of Cangzhou, and the Infectious Disease Hospital of Zhangjiakou. The inclusion criteria were defined as follows: 1) HBsAg-positive inpatients, 2) Patients who had been continuously treated with nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) for ≥48 weeks and remained on regular medication at enrollment. The exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) individuals younger than 18 years of age, 2) pregnant or lactating women. Demographic and laboratory data were collected, including 974 patients with HBV DNA levels below the lower limit of detection (20 copies/mL) and 280 patients with PV. In this study, CVR was defined as serum HBV DNA below the lower limit of detection at the study time point after at least 48 weeks of NAs therapy. All clinical data, including clinical diagnoses and time since onset, were assessed at the same cross-sectional time point (i.e., after at least 48 weeks of NAs therapy). To minimize confounding variables and ensure comparability, 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) was applied, resulting in a final cohort of 554 patients evenly distributed between the complete virological response (CVR) group (n = 277) and the PV group (n = 277). The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Fifth Hospital of Shijiazhuang(2,022013).

Sample collection and HBV RNA detection

After standard clinical blood tests, serum is collected according to a standardised protocol (2 ml per subject) and cryopreserved in an ultra-low temperature freezer at −80 °C. HBV RNA was extracted using the HBV-RNA Viral Nucleic Acid Quantification Kit (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China) and the Natch 32A Nucleic Acid Extractor (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China). Serum HBV RNA levels were quantified using the Hepatitis B Virus RNA Detection Kit (PCR-fluorescent probe method) (Sansure Biotech, Changsha, China) and the SLAN-96P Fully Automated Medical PCR System (Hongshitech, Shanghai, China). The assay is a reverse transcription real-time PCR assay targeting HBV pregenomic RNA (pgRNA) with a specific set of primers and a fluorescent probe. An internal control is included to monitor nucleic acid extraction quality and PCR inhibition. Quantification was performed using four kit-provided calibrators (HBV RNA quantitative reference materials A–D), each containing pseudovirus with the target sequence. The assay has a limit of detection of 50 copies/mL, a lower limit of quantification of 100 copies/mL, and a linear range from 1.0 × 10² to 1.0 × 10⁹ copies/mL. Results were expressed as log copies/mL.

Data collection

Patient demographic information, clinical data, and imaging results were retrieved from the hospital's electronic medical record system. Demographic information included gender, age, clinical diagnosis (CHB, LC, HCC), and medication types (ETV, TDF, TAF, and combination therapy). Laboratory indicators included alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alkaline phosphatase (ALP), gamma-glutamyl transferase (GGT), albumin (ALB), globulin (GLB), total bilirubin (TBIL), direct bilirubin (DBIL), cholinesterase (CHE), hepatitis B surface antigen (HBsAg), hepatitis B e antigen (HBeAg), hepatitis B e antibody (HBeAb), and HBV DNA. Imaging data included abdominal ultrasound, gastroscopy, and abdominal computed tomography (CT). HBsAg, anti-HBs, HBeAg, HBeAb, anti-HBc, and HBV DNA results were retrieved from the hospital electronic medical record system and had been generated as part of routine clinical laboratory testing. HBV serological markers were measured using commercial assays from Autobio Diagnostics Co., Ltd. (Zhengzhou, China). For the quantitative serological assays, the reporting units and cutoff values were as follows: HBsAg, IU/mL, cutoff <0.05; anti-HBs, mIU/mL, cutoff <10; HBeAg, IU/mL, cutoff <0.1; HBeAb, PEIU/mL, cutoff <0.15; and anti-HBc, PEIU/mL, cutoff <0.7. Serum HBV DNA was quantified using the Hepatitis B Virus Nucleic Acid Assay Kit from DaAn Gene Co., Ltd. (Guangzhou, China), and results were reported in IU/mL. The lower limit of detection in routine clinical testing was 20 IU/mL.

Statistical methods

To reduce potential confounding factors and ensure balance between the groups, propensity score matching (PSM) was applied. A 1:1 nearest-neighbor matching approach was applied with a caliper tolerance of 0.05 to ensure optimal matching of complete virological response (CVR) patients and persistently virus-positive (PV) patients based on propensity scores. Propensity scores were estimated using logistic regression modeling, incorporating variables that significantly influenced group assignment. After matching, the balance between the groups was assessed by comparing standardized mean differences (SMDs) for each covariate. Statistical significance was determined by a P-value < 0.05.

Continuous variables that were not normally distributed were expressed as median (interquartile range [IQR]) and compared between groups using the Mann-Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, and group comparisons were made using the chi-square test or Fisher's exact test, depending on the data distribution. Statistically significant variables were analyzed using multivariate logistic regression, and correlations between variables were assessed using nonparametric Spearman's rank correlation analysis. All statistical tests were two-sided, and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 27.0 software.

Serum HBV RNA was detected in 306 out of 554 patients, with 194 (70.04%) testing positive in the PV group and 112(40.43%) in the CVR group (P < 0.001) (Table 2).

Factors associated with HBV DNA persistent positivity after antiviral therapy

Based on the results of the univariate analyses, indicators with p < 0.05 were included in the logistic regression analysis to identify independent factors influencing the occurrence of PV in patients. Variables such as ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, ALB, PA, CHE, HBsAg, HBeAg level, HBeAb status, and HBV RNA status were included as covariates in the logistic regression model. The effect of HBV RNA on PV incidence was assessed while adjusting for multiple confounders. The results indicated that HBV RNA remained statistically significant, suggesting that it was an independent factor influencing the occurrence of PV in patients, as shown in Table 3.

Comparison of clinical characteristics in PV patients between HBV RNA-Positive and HBV RNA-Negative groups

Based on the HBV RNA copy number threshold (100 copies/mL), the 277 patients in the PV group were categorized into two groups: the HBV RNA-negative group (<100 copies/mL) and the HBV RNA-positive group (≥100 copies/mL). Liver function indices and virological characteristics were then compared between the two groups.

ALT and ALB levels were significantly higher in HBV RNA-positive patients (41.20 U/L and 37.70 g/L, respectively) than in HBV RNA-negative patients (32.80 U/L and 33.95 g/L, p < 0.05). CHE levels were also significantly elevated in the HBV RNA-positive group (5277.00 IU/L) compared to the HBV RNA-negative group (3873.00 IU/L, p < 0.001).

HBsAg levels were significantly higher in HBV RNA-positive patients (2.70 log10 IU/mL) than in HBV RNA-negative patients (2.37 log10 IU/mL), with a significant difference (P < 0.001). The proportion of HBeAg-positive patients was 63.92% in the HBV RNA-positive group, compared to 6.02% in the HBV RNA-negative group (P < 0.001). Conversely, 26.29% of HBV RNA-positive patients were HBeAb-positive, while 60.24% of HBV RNA-negative patients were HBeAb-positive (p < 0.05). HBV DNA levels were 2.67 log10 IU/mL in HBV RNA-positive patients and 2.17 log10 copies/mL in HBV RNA-negative patients, with a significant difference (p < 0.001), as shown in Table 4.

Correlation analysis between HBV RNA and HBV DNA, HBsAg, and HBeAg

Correlation analysis was performed between HBV RNA, HBV DNA, HBsAg and HBeAg in the 277 HBV DNA-positive patients. Serum HBV RNA levels were positively correlated with HBV DNA (Spearman r = 0.561, p < 0.001), HBsAg (Spearman r = 0.451, p < 0.001), and HBeAg (Spearman r = 0.722, p < 0.001), as shown in Fig. 4a, b, c.

Correlation analysis between serum HBV RNA and HBV DNA, HBsAg, and HBeAg in 277 PV patients. (a) Serum HBV RNA levels were positively correlated with HBV DNA (Spearman r = 0.561, p < 0.001). (b) Serum HBV RNA levels were positively correlated with HBsAg (Spearman r = 0.451, p < 0.001). (c) Serum HBV RNA levels were positively correlated with HBeAg (Spearman r = 0.722, p < 0.001).

Subsequent subgroup analysis stratified by HBeAg status revealed divergent correlation patterns (Table 5). In HBeAg-positive individuals, serum HBV RNA exhibited significant positive correlations with HBV DNA (r = 0.582, P < 0.001$), HBsAg (r = 0.356, P < 0.001), ALT (r = 0.375, P < 0.001), and AST (r = 0.173, P = 0.05). Notably, in HBeAg-negative individuals, although the correlations of HBV RNA with HBV DNA (r = 0.251, P = 0.002) and HBsAg (r = 0.199, P = 0.024) remained statistically significant, the strength of these associations was markedly attenuated compared to those in the HBeAg-positive phase. Furthermore, the significant associations with liver injury markers were entirely lost in HBeAg-negative patients, with no correlation observed for ALT (r = 0.145, P = 0.080) or AST (r = 0.113, P = 0.114).

Correlation between serum HBV RNA and HBsAg in CVR patients

While HBV DNA serves as a reliable marker during active viremia, it becomes undetectable in patients with complete virological response (CVR) under antiviral therapy. To further evaluate whether serum HBV RNA can still reflect the residual transcriptional activity of cccDNA when HBV DNA is suppressed, we analyzed the correlation between HBV RNA and HBsAg levels in the cohort of CVR patients. Spearman correlation analysis revealed a statistically significant positive correlation between serum HBV RNA and HBsAg levels in this specific population (r = 0.395, p < 0.001), highlighting its sustained value as a viral monitoring marker, as shown in Fig. 5.

Discussion

Previous studies have shown that some patients fail to achieve CVR despite receiving antiviral therapy, largely because of the persistence of covalently closed circular DNA (cccDNA) in hepatocytes. Current antiviral therapies cannot completely eliminate cccDNA, which remains stable within liver cells. The persistence of cccDNA has been widely regarded as a major obstacle to complete viral clearance in patients with chronic hepatitis B and as an important factor contributing to the progression to liver cirrhosis and HCC (Dusheiko et al., 2023; Martinez et al., 2021; Tsukuda and Watashi, 2020). Direct measurement of cccDNA and its transcriptional activity typically requires a liver biopsy, an invasive procedure that poses risks and limitations. In recent years, circulating HBV RNA has emerged as a promising non-invasive biomarker that can effectively reflect cccDNA activity.(Deng et al., 2022)

In our study of 1254 HBV‑infected patients treated with nucleos(t)ide analogues (NAs) for >48 weeks, the incidence of persistent viremia (PV) was 22.3%. Similarly, an 8 year follow up study by Li et al. (Li et al., 2023) reported that among CHB patients treated with entecavir for 78 weeks, only 77.2% achieved complete virologic response (CVR), corresponding to a treatment failure rate of 22.8%. Taken together, both studies demonstrate that although NAs therapy effectively suppresses the virus in most patients, a considerable proportion still exhibit a poor treatment response. Another study established a significant association between changes in HBV DNA levels during follow-up and the risk of HCC in patients with similar baseline HBV DNA levels. This suggests that persistently elevated HBV viral loads increase the risk of HCC and underscores the importance of reducing viral loads during treatment.(Chen et al., 2011) Previous studies had also demonstrated that patients who continue to exhibit positive HBV DNA levels after treatment were closely associated with the development of HCC and the progression of liver fibrosis.(Kim et al., 2017; Fan et al., 2020)

Our study found that patients in the PV group had significantly higher levels of ALT, AST, ALP, GGT, ALB, TBIL, DBIL, and CHE than those in the CVR group, indicating more severe liver injury in patients with PV. PV may reflect persistent intrahepatic viral activity, particularly ongoing transcriptional activity from intrahepatic cccDNA, which could contribute to continued liver injury despite antiviral therapy. In addition, compared with the CVR group, patients in the PV group had higher HBsAg levels and a higher rate of HBeAg positivity, suggesting more active viral replication. This finding is consistent with previous studies showing that HBsAg and HBeAg are important indicators of viral replication and liver injury and are commonly used to assess viral activity(Li et al., 2023; Zhang et al., 2021; Pan et al., 2012; Marcellin et al., 2013). However, the higher HBeAb positivity rate in the PV group suggests some patients may be in the immune clearance phase, indicating that the degree of viral replication could vary depending on immune status and disease stage. In this study, the HBV RNA positivity rate in the PV group was 70.04%, significantly higher than in the CVR group, and the HBV RNA expression level was also markedly elevated. This suggested that serum HBV RNA levels were highly sensitive and reflect the virus's transcriptional activity in the liver. A prospective multicenter study examined the causes of persistent HBV DNA positivity and found that elevated HBV RNA levels were a critical marker for assessing viral replication and liver damage (Li et al., 2023). Previous studies have shown that serum HBV RNA levels were significantly higher in patients with HCC than in those with LC. This suggested that persistently elevated HBV RNA levels were linked to more severe symptoms and faster disease progression (Wang et al., 2024). Logistic regression analysis in this study indicated that HBV RNA levels and status were independent risk factors for persistent HBV DNA positivity. Additionally, ALT, AST, and HBeAb status influenced persistent HBV DNA positivity. Collectively, these results suggested that persistent viral positivity in PV patients might drive disease progression and significantly increased the risk of complications, including LC and HCC.

Based on these results, we further analyzed the characteristics of HBV RNA-positive and HBV RNA-negative patients in the PV group. ALT levels were significantly higher in the HBV RNA-positive group compared to the HBV RNA-negative group. ALT was a widely used serum marker of HBV-associated liver injury, and its elevation typically correlates with an active immune response and exacerbated hepatocellular damage, consistent with previous studies.(Liaw and Chu, 2009) Studies have shown that elevated HBV RNA levels correlate positively with ALT levels, reflecting viral replication and host immune activity. Furthermore, persistent HBV RNA expression could worsen liver disease.(Wang, Yu, et al., 2017) The mechanisms might involve an inflammatory response mediated by its 5′-ε region, promoting necroinflammation and fibrosis due to viral replication.(Guidotti et al., 2015; Ferrari et al., 2017) A study found a positive correlation between HBV RNA and ALT levels (r = 0.263,= 0.001). This further supported HBV RNA as a sensitive marker for viral replication and liver damage.(Xiang et al., 2021) In this study, the incidence of combined LC and SP was significantly higher in the HBV RNA-positive group, confirming that sustained viral replication accelerates liver injury and leads to more severe clinical manifestations. This finding aligns with previous studies suggesting that persistent viral replication was a key factor in liver fibrosis progression, with exacerbated fibrosis potentially leading to portal hypertension and complications like splenomegaly.(Li et al., 2017) Comparing viral markers, HBeAg positivity was significantly higher in the HBV RNA-positive group, while HBeAb positivity was more common in the negative group. Viral replication was more active in the HBV RNA-positive patients, whereas stronger immune responses were observed in the HBV RNA-negative group. Further analysis revealed a positive correlation between HBV RNA, HBV DNA, HBsAg, and HBeAg. Previous studies have shown that HBV RNA was closely correlated with HBV DNA, reflecting viral replication.(Wang et al., 2018; Roncarati et al., 2022; van Campenhout et al., 2018; Wang et al., 2016) This study found the strongest correlation between HBeAg levels and serum HBV RNA, suggesting that HBeAg-positive patients exhibit higher cccDNA transcription activity, directly influencing HBV RNA levels (van Campenhout et al., 2018). A moderate correlation was also found between HBV RNA and HBsAg, with elevated HBV RNA levels likely accompanied by increased viral transcriptional activity, leading to enhanced HBsAg synthesis (Jansen et al., 2016).

In the stratified analysis, although the correlations remained statistically significant, the correlation coefficients between serum HBV RNA and HBV DNA/HBsAg were markedly lower in HBeAg-negative patients than in HBeAg-positive patients, which is consistent with previous findings (Zhang et al., 2023). This attenuation may have important biological implications. During the HBeAg-positive phase, active cccDNA transcription simultaneously drives viral replication, reflected by HBV DNA, and viral protein expression, reflected by HBsAg, thereby resulting in strong correlations among these virological markers. In contrast, during the HBeAg-negative phase, this synchronized relationship appears to become partially dissociated. Previous studies have suggested that HBsAg in HBeAg-positive patients is mainly derived from cccDNA transcription, whereas in HBeAg-negative patients, a substantial proportion of HBsAg may originate from integrated HBV DNA(Wooddell et al., 2017; Grudda et al., 2022).. Since serum HBV RNA serves as a direct surrogate for cccDNA transcriptional activity, its correlation with HBsAg naturally weakens when HBsAg production shifts to an integrated DNA source. Furthermore, the weaker correlation between HBV RNA and HBV DNA in this phase may reflect a broader decoupling of viral transcription and replication during the immune-control or late phases of chronic infection(Gu et al., 2024). These findings highlight the nuanced dynamics of viral markers across different disease phases.

Furthermore, our findings support the continued utility of serum HBV RNA in patients who have achieved complete virological response under NA therapy. Although NAs potently suppress HBV reverse transcription and lead to undetectable serum HBV DNA, they do not directly eliminate cccDNA or completely abolish its transcriptional activity. In this context, the significant correlation between serum HBV RNA and HBsAg observed in our CVR cohort may indicate persistent residual viral transcription (Lu et al., 2017). However, the correlation was only moderate, which may be explained by the complex origin of HBsAg. HBsAg can be transcribed not only from residual cccDNA but also from HBV DNA integrated into the host genome, especially during long-term nucleos(t)ide analogue therapy (Grudda et al., 2022). Therefore, serum HBV RNA may provide complementary information to HBsAg by more closely reflecting residual cccDNA transcriptional activity in CVR patients.

The results of this study suggested that HBV RNA is an independent risk factor for the development of PV and could serve as an important marker for viral replication activity and disease severity in HBV patients. Elevated HBV RNA levels in PV patients are indicative of more severe liver damage. HBV RNA levels are strongly correlated with viral replication markers such as HBV DNA, HBsAg, and HBeAg, particularly with HBeAg, further supporting HBV RNA as a sensitive marker of viral replication and liver injury. These findings suggest that HBV RNA may serve as an important marker of viral replication activity and disease severity in patients with HBV infection. PV patients with elevated HBV RNA may represent a subgroup with more active viral replication and more severe liver injury, and therefore may benefit from closer monitoring and more intensive individualized management. In contrast, PV patients who are HBV RNA-negative and CVR patients who remain HBV RNA-positive may represent distinct clinical states, and their management should be guided by a comprehensive assessment of virologic, biochemical, and clinical parameters. Further studies are needed to clarify the prognostic significance and optimal management strategies for these discordant patient groups.

Although this study demonstrated the potential value of HBV RNA levels in reflecting viral replication activity and liver injury, several limitations should be acknowledged. First, the causal relationship between HBV RNA levels and disease progression requires further investigation, as the cross-sectional design of this study did not allow dynamic monitoring of HBV RNA fluctuations or assessment of their predictive value for disease progression. Second, the detection and quantification of HBV RNA have not yet been globally standardized, and methodological variations may affect the comparability of results across studies and limit the generalizability of our findings. Third, CVR was defined based on a single HBV DNA measurement at the study time point, and serial measurements were not consistently available to confirm sustained virological suppression. As a result, some patients with intermittent viremia may have been misclassified as having CVR, which would likely bias the comparisons toward the null and potentially underestimate the true differences between groups. Furthermore, because the routine HBV DNA assay had an LOD of 20 IU/mL, ultrasensitive methods might reclassify some CVR patients (especially RNA-positive ones) as having intermittent low-level viremia. Future prospective, multicenter studies with long-term follow-up are warranted to further clarify the value of HBV RNA in predicting disease progression in patients with HBV infection.

Conclusion

This study demonstrates that HBV RNA is a crucial biomarker of assessing viral replication activity and liver damage. In chronic HBV patients undergoing treatment with NAs, HBV RNA positivity is indicative of increased viral replication activity and more pronounced liver damage. HBV RNA levels are strongly correlated with viral replication and liver damage. HBV RNA testing serves as a tool for evaluating active viral status, particularly in patients with elevated ALT levels and HBeAg positivity, thereby offering a scientific foundation for the development of personalized treatment strategies for HBV patients.
