Correlation between corneal concentration and minimum inhibitory concentration of antifungals in patients undergoing therapeutic keratoplasty for fungal keratitis
Authors: Vinay Patil, Aafreen Bari, Prafulla Kumar Maharana, Nishat Ahmed, Ritu Nagpal, Rajesh Sinha, Tushar Agarwal, Namrata Sharma, T Velpandian, Jeewan Singh Titiyal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Objectives:To correlate corneal concentration and minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) of antifungal drugs in patients undergoing therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty (TPK) for fungal keratitis.Methods:The study included 43 cases of culture- and smear-proven fungal keratitis that underwent TPK due to inadequate responses to topical natamycin (NTM) 5% and/or topical voriconazole (VCZ) 1%. Liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LCMS) was used to analyze the host corneal tissue of the patients to measure the intracorneal concentrations of NTM and VCZ. Antifungal susceptibility testing was performed using the E-test method with standard-dose antifungal E-strips on the same corneal tissue.Results:The mean age of study participants was 48.11 ± 15.22 years. Aspergillus sp. was the most commonly identified fungus. The study found that the mean intracorneal concentration of NTM was 80.76 ± 107.59 µg/gm (n = 30/43), while that of VCZ was 0.0962 ± 0.25 µg/gm (n = 32/43). Twenty-one cases had MIC-90 values for both antifungal drugs. Over 85% of cases had an intracorneal concentration lower than VCZ MIC-90, and 61.9% of cases showed NTM intracorneal concentration higher than its MIC-90.Conclusion:Despite achieving corneal concentration higher than the MIC-90, cases with recalcitrant fungal keratitis had a poor clinical response to NTM, suggesting other possible reasons, such as poor drug penetration and drug resistance.

Fungal keratitis is an important cause of infectious keratitis and is associated with a poor prognosis and corneal blindness.[12] Lack of effective antifungal agents with good ocular penetration has been identified as a major cause for the same.[3] Recently, resistance to antifungal agents has also been proposed as a determinant of treatment outcomes.[45] Ever since the approval of natamycin (NTM) by the US-FDA approved for the management of fungal keratitis, there have not been many new approvals in this field.[3] Various new antifungal agents like Caspofungin, Posaconazole, and Itraconazole have been developed in the past and put to clinical use.[3] However, topical NTM, voriconazole (VCZ), and amphotericin B (AMB) remain the most commonly used antifungal drugs in clinical practice.[367]

Numerous studies have been done in the past to study their efficacy and safety; however, research pertaining to their pharmacokinetics is mostly limited to animal models. Zhao et al.,[8] in an animal study, reported better ocular penetration of topical VCZ 1% when compared to topical NTM 5% in New Zealand white rabbits by assessing the corneal and aqueous concentration of these drugs. However, an extrapolation of such results in human eyes, especially under circumstances of ocular inflammation, which per se can affect the ocular bioavailability of any drug, is irrational. Minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) is another important aspect that is crucial for effective control of ocular infections. The reports of the MIC levels of commonly used topical antifungal drugs are limited. This may be due to the lack of available ocular standards, well-defined MIC breakpoints to determine susceptibility and resistance of fungal isolates. However, it is one of the best available pointers toward the susceptibility of a fungal pathogen in ocular infections.

There is limited knowledge on the corneal penetration of NTM and VCZ in human eyes with fungal keratitis and their ability to achieve therapeutic concentrations in these settings. This study was conducted to analyze the same. A direct correlation of the corneal concentration of NTM and VCZ achieved, in cases of fungal keratitis by its topical application and its MIC level for the isolated fungi, which yet remains unexplored, will also be highlighted.

Methods

In this prospective observational study, all cases of recalcitrant fungal keratitis that presented to the cornea clinic of a tertiary eye care center in India from October 2019 and March 2021 and were planned for therapeutic penetrating keratoplasty (TPK) were recruited. A written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and institutional ethics committee approval was obtained (IECPG-532/29.08.2019). The study was conducted adhering to the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study protocol

All potassium hydroxide wet-mount (KOH) smear and/or culture positive cases of recalcitrant fungal keratitis that were on topical NTM or topical VCZ or both and planned for TPK with age >18 years were included in the study, whereas cases of perforated corneal ulcer or with impending perforation, mixed microbial keratitis (on smear or culture analysis), and those on systemic or targeted (intracameral/intrastromal) VCZ therapy were excluded. The patient’s particulars that included history, age, sex, occupation, systemic illness, duration of symptoms, medical treatment, and drug history (topical and systemic) were noted. The baseline parameters, such as corrected distance visual acuity (CDVA), location, size, and depth of the ulcer, were recorded. The size of the epithelial defect and stromal infiltrate was measured using a slit lamp in the greatest dimension and along an axis perpendicular to it, while the depth of the infiltrate was assessed by comparing it with the adjacent healthy corneal thickness. Ultrasonography B scan was done to rule out coexistent endophthalmitis in cases where the posterior segment could not be visualized. CDVA was recorded using the Snellen and logarithm of minimum angle of resolution (log MAR) scales. The microscopy (KOH wet-mount) and Sabouraud dextrose agar (SDA) culture reports of the corneal scraping specimens obtained from the cases were noted.

After explaining the procedure and the indication, patients signed a written informed consent before undergoing TPK. The host corneal tissue obtained during TPK was divided soon after trephination into two equal halves under sterile conditions. One-half of the tissue sample was placed in a microcentrifugation tube and stored at −80°C. This sample was later subjected to liquid chromatography mass spectrometry (LCMS) to measure the intracorneal concentration of NTM and VCZ. The other half was sent to the microbiological assessment, where the obtained fungal isolate was tested for antifungal susceptibility testing using the E-test method with standard-dose antifungal E-strips (Himedia Laboratories Pvt Ltd., Mumbai) of NTM and VCZ.

LC-ESI-MS/MS analysis of NTM and VCZ

All the chemicals and solvents used were of LCMS/MS analytical grade. HPLC-MS experiments were performed using a triple quadrupole tandem mass spectrometer (4000 Q-Trap, AB Sciex, Foster City, CA, USA) coupled with a high-performance liquid chromatography system (HPLC, Agilent Technologies, 1260 Infinity, Santa Clara, CA, USA) that comprised of a quaternary pump (G1311C), multi sampler (G7167A), thermostatic column compartment (G1316A) with a variable wavelength UV detector (G1314F) and online degasser. All the parameters of the tandem mass spectrometer and HPLC were controlled by Analyst software, version 1.7.1 (AB Sciex, Foster City, CA, USA) and OpenLAB control panel software (Agilent Technologies, 1260 Infinity, Santa Clara, CA, USA), respectively.

An analytical separation of NTM, VCZ, standard deviation of measurement (SDM), and internal standard (IS) was developed using a Purospher Star C18 analytical column (50 × 4.6 mm, 3.5 µm, Merck, Darmstadt, Germany). All analytes were eluted using an isocratic mobile phase combination consisting of solvent A (18.2 MΩ milliQ water with 0.1% FA), solvent B (methanol with 0.3% FA), and solvent C (acetonitrile with 0.1% FA), pumped at a flow rate of 0.5 mLmin-1 at a ratio of 40:40:20. The total run time of the method was 5 min. The post-run equilibration time was kept for 1 min between each sample. The temperature of the autosampler tray and the column oven were maintained at 10 ± 1°C and 30 ± 1°C, respectively, and samples were injected at a volume of 10 µL for analysis.

The mass spectrometer was operated with an electrospray ionization (ESI) source in positive ion mode using a Turbo Ion Spray source (AB Sciex, Foster City, CA, USA). The fragment ion spectrum of all analytes was recorded to acquire the product ions of both analytes. Multiple reaction monitoring (MRM) mode was used for the absolute quantification of NTM and VCZ in human cornea tissue samples using SDM as an IS. Specific precursor-to-product mass ion transitions of m/z 666.2/503.2, m/z 350.1/281.1, and m/z 311.0/156.0 were used for NTM, VCZ, and SDM, respectively.

Source-dependent parameters optimized and maintained in MRM mode were curtain gas (30 psi), collision gas (10 psi), ion spray voltage (5500 eV), turbo heater temperature (500°C), gas 1 (45 psi), and gas 2 (60 psi). Dwell time for each mass ion transition of NTM and IS was kept at 100 msec, and 50 msec for VCZ. The details have been in the supplementary document.

Corneal tissue extraction

All tissue samples were allowed to thaw at room temperature and chopped into smaller pieces using sterile McPherson scissors. A volume of 500 µL ice-cold extraction solvent was added to all samples and homogenized using a Polytron Homogenizer (USA) under an ice bath. The samples were then sonicated for 5 min and cold centrifuged at 15,000 g for 5 min at 4°C. From these samples, an aliquot of 10 µL was mixed with 90 µL of dilution solvent, vortexed for 10 sec, and cold centrifuged at 15,000 g for 2 min. Supernatant amounting to the volume of 25 µL was placed in a 384-well plate, and 10 µL was subjected to LCMS/MS analysis. Samples that were found outside the calibration range were appropriately diluted, and concentration was calculated by multiplying suitable factors.

Fungal culture and drug sensitivity testing

The part of the corneal tissue was cultured onto an SDA media plate. Cases in which there was growth of fungal colonies in SDA media, after 48 hours of inoculation, were considered positive on culture. The fungal growth was then inoculated to measure the MIC by the Epsilometric test. The E-strips containing a standard-dose of drug [NTM (0.016–256 µg/mL) and VCZ (0.002–32 µg/mL)] were placed in the culture plate and were incubated at 30°C for 48 hours. The obtained zone of inhibition around the E-strip was noted, and the point of intersection of the zone of inhibition with the E-strip was the value of MIC in µg/mL. The MIC values obtained for each isolate were noted for NTM and VCZ.

Statistical analysis

All the obtained data were entered into a Microsoft Excel sheet for analysis. The statistical analysis was done using STATA 12.1 software. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), median, frequency, and range as applicable. Non-parametricc data were analyzed with Mann–Whitney U test. A Spearman correlation test was done to establish a correlation between two variables, represented by Spearman rho. The value of the correlation coefficient +1 suggested a positive correlation and −1 suggested a negative correlation. A coefficient value nearing 0 suggests a weak relationship between the two variables. P values <0.05 weres considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 43 cases with a mean age of 48.11 ± 15.22 years (range: 18–78 years, were recruited in the study. Males constituted 84% (36/43) of the cases, while the rest were females. Majority (23/43) had history of trauma with vegetative matter as risk factor for keratitis, and only two cases had diabetes mellitus. The demographic details and clinical characteristics have been enlisted in Table 1. In 35 of 43 cases (81.4%), the fungus could be isolated on culture, while the remaining eight cases were positive on KOH smear.

Baseline characteristics

The ulcer was central in location in 74.4% cases (n = 32/43), paracentral in 20.1% cases (n = 9/43), and peripheral in 4.6% cases (n = 2/43). The mean ulcer and infiltrate area were 34.33 ± 12.88 mm2 and 35.28 ± 16.29 mm2, respectively. Based on severity, 67.44% (n = 29/43) cases had moderate corneal ulcer, while 32.56% (n = 14/43) had severe corneal ulcer. Of the 43 cases, fungal keratitis was diagnosed in eight cases based on smear positivity, seven cases based on culture positivity, and 28 cases based on both [Table 2]. Aspergillus sp. (51.4%, n = 18/35) was the most frequently identified fungus on culture, with A. flavus present in 12 cases, A. fumigatus in five cases and A. niger in one case. Other fungi included Fusarium (n = 5/35), Cladosporium (n = 5/35), Alternaria (n = 3/35), Mucor (n = 2/35), Rhizopus (n = 1/35), and Penicillium (n = 1/35). Pre-operatively, three-fourth cases (74.4%, n = 32/43) were on combination antifungal therapy (topical NTM and VCZ), while only 25.6% cases (n = 11/43) were on topical NTM therapy alone. The mean duration from initiation of antifungal drug to TPK for NTM (n = 43/43) was 17.53 ± 10.37 days, and for VCZ (n = 32/43), it was 14.03 ± 7.68 days. The mean time gap from the last dose of antifungal drop to TPK for NTM was 3.29 ± 0.73 hours, and VCZ was 3.39 ± 0.94 hours.

Intracorneal antifungal drug concentration

The observed mean intracorneal concentration for NTM (n = 30/43) was 51.83 ± 46.49 µg/gm (median value 58.9 µg/gm; range: 0.0113 to 480.556 µg/gm). The observed mean intracorneal concentration for VCZ (n = 32/43) was 0.0015 ± 0.001 µg/gm (median value 0.003 µg/gm; range: 0.0005 to 1.0827 µg/gm).

Spearmen correlation analysis showed poor correlation between intracorneal concentration of VCZ with duration of treatment (rho = −0.11, P = 0.56), frequency of treatment (rho = −0.57, P = 0.001), and time gap from last dose of VCZ (rho = −0.23, P = 0.20). A similar analysis for NTM showed poor correlation between intracorneal concentration of NTM with duration of treatment (rho = 0.067, P = 0.71), frequency of treatment (rho = −0.41, P = 0.02), and time gap from last dose of NTM (rho = 0.05, P = 0.78). Also, no correlation was noted between the mean area of corneal ulcer and intracorneal concentration of NTM (spearman rho = −0.0049) and VCZ (spearman rho = 0.2965); grade of ulcer and corneal concentration of VCZ (P = 0.0584) and NTM (P = 0.5117); depth of the ulcer and corneal concentration of NTM (P = 0.0612) and VCZ (P = 0.431); location of the ulcer and the intracorneal concentration of NTM (P = 0.534) and VCZ (P = 0.343); mean area of infiltrate and intracorneal concentration of NTM (spearman rho = −0.019) and VCZ (spearman rho = 0.2437).

Minimum inhibitory concentration of antifungal drugs

The MIC 90 value was obtained for NTM and VCZ from the host tissue sample for 21 cases. A value of >256 µg/mL was obtained in over half (n = 11/21) of the received samples for NTM and >32 µg/mL in nearly one-fourth (n = 5/21) of the received samples for VCZ. Among the cases for which MIC 90 values were available, two cases had recurrence of infection post-TPK. Both these cases had MIC 90 for NTM >256 µg/mL. The MIC 90 for VCZ in these cases was 0.25 µg/mL and 4 µg/mL, respectively. Table 3 enlists the MIC 90 of fungi isolated in the study for VCZ and NTM.

Correlation of antifungal drug intracorneal concentration and MIC-90

A total of 21 cases had data available for both antifungal drug MIC-90 and intracorneal concentration [Table 4]. Discussion

Management of fungal keratitis is challenging in the majority of cases. Poor bioavailability of the available antifungal drugs, rising cases of drug resistance, and lack of testing facilities for drug sensitivity are a few of the factors responsible for poor response to therapy. In previous animal studies by O’Day et al.,[9] the efficacy of various concentrations of amphotericin B in fungal keratitis was tested. They concluded that strains that were resistant to the drug were unaffected at even the highest concentrations. In another study, amphotericin B was compared to NTM in rabbit models of fungal keratitis. The in vivo therapeutic concentrations were compared to in vitro MIC values. Amphotericin B demonstrated a clear dose-response effect while NTM failed to do so.[10] A deficit of knowledge about antifungal drug penetration and sensitivity in human eyes in the background of fungal keratitis has led to rampant use of cocktail therapy worldwide. Hence, it is important to explore the achieved intracorneal concentration and MIC-90 levels of the commonly used antifungal agents to understand their dynamics in human eyes with fungal keratitis. In the current study, we analyzed the intracorneal concentration and MIC-90 of NTM and VCZ in the host corneal tissue of fungal keratitis planned for TPK. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study in the literature that evaluated these findings in human eyes with fungal keratitis.

The poor penetration of drugs has been proposed as a major factor in treatment failure in microbial keratitis. In our series, we included cases which were showing poor response to antifungal therapy. More than half of the cases for NTM and almost one-fourth of the cases had MIC values much higher than the intracorneal concentration of these drugs. This supports the age-old concept of poor penetration of antifungal drugs in the cornea.

The development of resistance to triazole antifungal medications has been well-documented, particularly in patients with long-standing fungal infections. Previous studies have demonstrated that assessing antifungal susceptibility in the laboratory can effectively predict the clinical response and outcomes in cases of fungal keratitis.[11] In this study, some of the cases had MIC values beyond the maximum value on the E-strip. It could be due to several factors, but the most concerning explanation could be due to poor sensitivity of the organism to the antibiotics.

The obtained intracorneal concentration of any drug suggests its ability to penetrate the cornea; hence, a higher value suggests good penetration and ocular bioavailability. On the other hand, the MIC value serves as the basis for assessing the category of susceptibility or resistance of the pathogen to a given antibiotic. Hence, clinical resolution in cases wherein the intracorneal concentration is higher than the MIC-90 value was expected. However, in the current study, a lack of clinical response was seen in over half of the cases, wherein the intracorneal concentration of NTM was more than its MIC-90. This suggests that there may be other factors responsible for poor response to antifungal therapy. Poor host immune response, poor bioavailability, drug resistance in microorganisms, and environmental factors may play a significant role in poor healing of keratitis, along with drug resistance. Also, it may be possible that the in vitro MIC-90 values may not correlate with in vivo MIC-90 values, and hence, the same microorganisms that are inhibited in vitro environment did not show clinical response in the patient. The presence of various inflammatory mediators in the cornea in the background of fungal keratitis could also be responsible for the same.

Fungal keratitis involves robust innate immune activation via fungal-specific PAMPs, with elevated IL-6 and IL-8 levels linked to sustained neutrophil infiltration. There is also a prominent Th2 and mixed adaptive response and prolonged inflammation. The corneal surface also harbors several antimicrobial and anti-inflammatory agents, including secretory IgA, mucin glycoproteins, and antimicrobial peptides such as human β-defensin-2 (HBD-2) and LL-37, which are upregulated in filamentous fungal keratitis. The host immune response to fungal pathogens involves recognition of pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs)—including chitin, β-glucan, and mannan—by pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) such as toll-like receptors (TLRs), C-type lectin receptors (CLRs), nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-like receptors (NLRs), and scavenger receptors (SRs). This recognition initiates a cascade of cytokine and chemokine release (e.g. ILs, TNFs), promoting recruitment and activation of neutrophils and macrophages at the infection site.

Additionally, fungal hyphae can stimulate corneal epithelial cells to activate CD4+ T cells, CD8+ T cells, and B cells, leading to secretion of Th2-type cytokines (IL-4, IL-13) and IgG production. While immune cell infiltration aids in pathogen clearance, persistent inflammation may contribute to tissue damage and ulceration, potentially mediated by enzymes such as matrix metalloproteinases. Elevated tear levels of IL-6 and IL-8 in fungal keratitis suggest their roles in polymorphonuclear leukocyte (PMN) recruitment and activation. However, the mechanisms underlying selective PMN infiltration into the tear film remain to be fully elucidated.[1213]

The study correlated the corneal concentrations of NTM and VCZ with various ulcer characteristics like ulcer area, depth, infiltrate area, location, and grade of ulcer. We did not find any correlation between corneal concentration and the ulcer characteristics. Thus, it appears that ulcer characteristics have a little or no role in penetration of NTM and VCZ in fungal keratitis. Besides, there was no correlation between the duration of treatment and frequency of instillation with corneal concentration of NTM and VCZ. Thus, it appears that the corneal concentration of NTM and VCZ follows the general principles of pharmacokinetics and increasing the frequency beyond a particular point does not impact the corneal concentration of these drugs. Our findings are contradictory to the age-old concept of administering topical drops in an increasing frequency to achieve a higher corneal concentration.

In this study, only 21 samples were selected for the antifungal assay. These were selected by a technical expert from the Department of Ocular Pharmacology at Rajendra Prasad Centre for Ophthalmic Sciences, AIIMS, Delhi. The other samples were excluded due to excessively high values of drugs in the analysis, as it could be due to contamination of the samples with particulate matter of NTM.

One interesting observation in our study was that over 85% of the cases (n = 18/21) had MIC-90 for VCZ more than its intracorneal concentration, while only 61.9% cases (n = 13/21) had MIC-90 for NTM more than its intracorneal concentration. Some samples exhibited an MIC-90 for both NTM and VCZ. It could be due to better penetration of NTM compared to VCZ. Several studies have suggested that NTM is more effective in fungal keratitis compared to VCZ.[7] The findings of our study support this observation.

Our study has several noteworthy limitations that need to be acknowledged. Apart from the limited sample size, it is essential to highlight that in 13 of our samples, and the analysis could not be conducted due to sample degradation, as concluded by the technical experts. Consequently, all findings and conclusions derived from this study are considered exploratory and require further investigation. Another limitation of the study is that the intracorneal concentration was not directly correlated with the overall response to therapy would have been better. It is crucial to conduct additional research to validate and substantiate these initial hypotheses. The in vitro and in vivo concentration of antifungals depends on tissue permeability and drug susceptibility. The fungus isolated from a specific geographic zone may possess unique characteristics in terms of resistance and sensitivity to a particular drug.

Therefore, it is plausible that microorganisms in this region might possess distinct characteristics compared to those found in other parts of the world.[14] Thus, the generalizability of our findings to different geographical regions should be approached with caution. To address these limitations adequately, future studies should be designed to overcome these challenges and incorporate a more diverse and representative sample population from various regions. By doing so, we can enhance the robustness and applicability of the findings and gain a comprehensive understanding of the topic under investigation.

Conclusion

To summarize, the findings of this study indicate that the poor penetration of antifungal drugs is an important factor in cases of recalcitrant keratitis. Although several studies have emphasized this fact, the current study provides direct evidence by measuring the intracorneal concentration of the antifungal drugs in keratitis patients. Patient factors and resistance against antifungal drugs could have implications for the treatment of fungal keratitis. However, it is crucial to conduct additional research to validate these results and ascertain their applicability to other geographical areas. Further studies are necessary to advance the management of fungal keratitis and enhance treatment strategies.
