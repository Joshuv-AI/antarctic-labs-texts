Nonsymmorphic symmetry enforced Weyl nodal metal in chalcopyrite MnGeAs2
Authors: Surasree Sadhukhan, Sudipta Kanungo
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Band degeneracy-driven topological phases in condensed matter physics have paved the way for a distinct type of Weyl systems. Ongoing research delves into quasi-particles beyond Dirac and Weyl semimetals, investigating their interactions and necessitating the discovery of new quantum materials. Furthermore, the coexistence of Type-I and II Weyl crossings in magnetic Weyl systems, protected by nonsymmorphic crystalline symmetry, represents a rare and intriguing combination. Our study focuses on the experimentally synthesized non-centrosymmetric chalcopyrite MnGeAs2, characterized by broken time-reversal symmetry. Notably, the Type-I and -II gapless Weyl nodal line co-occur in this material, and they exhibit resilience against the strong spin-orbit coupling (SOC) protected by the nonsymmorphic d-glide mirror symmetry. Moreover, the calculated Berry curvature-driven anomalous Hall conductivity (AHC) attains a substantial value of approximately 300 S/cm near the Type-I Weyl nodal line at 26 meV below the E\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_f$$\end{document}. Further, the AHC gets enhanced to 350 S/cm for the mimicked thin film variant of the MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}. This study provides insights into the relatively less-explored Weyl nodal line semimetals robust against the strong SOC.

Introduction

Extensive investigation of the exotic band degeneracy in the energy-momentum spectrum, the so-called band structure, opens up the possibilities to realize various novel quasi-particle behaviors in the symmetry-protected topological quantum materials, where even special non-symmorphic symmetry plays a vital role in stabilizing exotic features in the topological semi-metals. According to the dimensionality of the degeneracy of the band dispersion, semi-metallic phase can be classified as a Nodal point (NP), a Nodal line (NL), or a Nodal surface. The zero dimensional degenerate NP topological semi-metals are usually formed due to the accidental band crossing and are further classified as Dirac semi-metal1–6, Weyl semi-metal7–12, triple point semimetal13–16, and many more17–22. Further, if two bands touch each other through a line due to special symmetry protection and can form a one-dimensional line in the momentum space, they are called Nodal line semi-metals (NLSM). The low-energy excitation of NLSM is massive along the tangent to the nodal line and massless along the two transverse directions in momentum space. The recent discoveries of the nodal line NLSM23–33 have broadened the concept of degeneracy-driven topological semi-metal altogether34,35.

Depending on the degree and symmetries involved in the one-dimensional band degeneracy, NLSM can be categorized into Dirac and Weyl-type NLSM. The degeneracy of Dirac NLSM is protected by the mirror-\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}$$\end{document} (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {P}$$\end{document}: Parity or Inversion; \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T}$$\end{document}: Time reversal) symmetry, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}$$\end{document} asymmetric cases26,36–40, can lead to the Weyl NLSM. In most of the reported cases41–47, the NLSM is vulnerable against the strong spin-orbit coupling (SOC). Recently, some non-symmorphic symmetry, which involves translation with fractional lattice constants in the form of glide or screw symmetry, enforced essential band crossing which is robust against the SOC, leads to exotic Dirac NLSM48–51 in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}$$\end{document} symmetric cases and Weyl NLSM in \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}$$\end{document} asymmetric cases52. In addition, the emergence of magnetic order is typically associated with the breaking of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T}$$\end{document} symmetry and occasionally results in a reduction of crystalline symmetry. Consequently, identifying closed nodal loops in the FM materials poses a significant challenge in the presence of SOC53–56.

Understanding the mechanism of the emergent essential band degeneracy in Weyl NLSM in the absence of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal {T}$$\end{document} symmetry and protected against SOC by non-symmorphic symmetry has the potential to bring new comprehension to overall topological phases. Moreover, it is much desired to search for good candidate materials showing such exotic topological phases26,51,57,58, which are lacking in the literature. Interesting point that, the most of the reported Weyl NLSM in the presence of strong SOC forms a degenerate nodal line is of Type-I class60,61, where valence and conduction bands have slopes of equal but opposite signs. On the contrary, the Type-II Weyl NLSM, where valence and conduction bands have slopes of the same sign59, is comparatively very rare60,61. Both Type-I and II share the same topological characteristics; however, due to the violation of Lorentz invariance62 in Type-II, the Fermi surface (FS) is drastically different and gives rise to distinctive properties like anisotropic magnetoresistance, tilted surface bands, etc, compared to Type-I. As a more recent advancement in the NLSM, topological phases have emerged as a hybrid NLSM with coexistence of both Type-I and II nodal line63–67.

In this present context, we investigated a WNL metal with coexisting Type-I and II closed loop nodal lines, protected by diamond-glide symmetry in the ferromagnetically (FM) ordered phase of non-centrosymmetric (i.e., \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}$$\end{document} asymmetric) chalcopyrite MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}. MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} is well studied in the context of the dilute magnetic semiconductor (DMS)68,69, but topological aspects are completely unexplored. Experimentally, MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} was synthesized both in the bulk form70,71 as well as MBE-grown thin film form71,72. The electronic structure calculations68,69 of bulk MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} show antiferromagnetic semiconductor ground state, while the experimental magnetization measurements of the bulk as well as thin film of MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} clearly show the paramagnetic (PM) to FM transition at around 340 K70–72 and the hysteresis below the transition temperature. Since the experimental magnetization measurements are not available for the bulk system, theoretically, it has been shown that with the specific structural modifications70, the FM state can be energetically lower than the AFM state even in bulk MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}. The topological nodal line semimetal or metal should have the following three unique electronic structure features75, viz. i) closed loop-like Fermi surface, ii) non-trivial band crossing which carries \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\pi$$\end{document} Berry phase in a closed loop encircling the loop, and iii) drumhead-like surface states. In the present case of MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}, we observed all these three features in the calculations. In MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} we observed Weyl nodal lines (WNL) in the FM-metallic phase, that are robust against the strong SOC, due to the non-symmorphic diamond-glide symmetry perpendicular to [1,±1,0] protection, which is a coupled symmetry of reflection and fractional translation along three crystallographic directions. The Fermi energy contributed from both majority and minority spin channels, followed by a band inversion between the As-s and p orbitals at the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Gamma$$\end{document} point. We also found coexistence of two distinct Type-I and Type-II gapless Weyl nodal lines protected by the d-glide plane perpendicular to the [1,±1,0] with fractional glide component, in the presence of strong SOC. We also investigated the nature of the Fermi arcs and the Wannier charge centers (WCC) using the Wilson loop method. The prominent and well-isolated drumhead-shaped surface spectral function confirms the Type-I closed nodal line. The calculated non-zero Berry curvature driven AHC at Fermi energy around 150 S/cm and reached a maximum up to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document}300 S/cm at the Weyl nodal point, which is just 26 meV below the Fermi energy. To extend our result within the experimental reach, we mimicked the thin film attributes of MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} and showed that the WNL features remain unchanged with significant enhancement of AHC in the case of the thin film compared to the bulk. To the best of our knowledge, such systems are comparatively less known to the community, which are experimentally synthesized and are very important for symmetry-protected topological metallic phases in general.

Calculation methodology

Density functional theory (DFT) based electronic structure calculations were performed using the plane-wave basis set based on a pseudo-potential framework as incorporated in the Vienna \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\textit{ab-initio}$$\end{document} simulation package (VASP)76,77. The generalized gradient exchange-correlation approximated (GGA) functional was employed following the Perdew–Burke–Ernzerhof (PBE) prescription78. For the plane-wave basis, a 600 eV cut-off was applied. The structural optimization was performed by relaxing the internal atomic positions toward the equilibrium until the Hellman–Feynman force becomes less than 0.001 eV/\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{\AA }$$\end{document}, keeping the lattice parameters fixed at the experimentally determined values70. A k-point mesh of 8 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 8 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\times$$\end{document} 4 in the Brillouin zone, and the electronic convergence criteria were set to be at 10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$^{-7}$$\end{document} eV for self-consistent calculations. The tight-binding model is constructed with the Wannier function basis set to investigate its topological properties. It is a selective energy method that produces a low-energy, few-orbital model Hamiltonian defined in the effective Wannier function basis by integrating out the degrees of freedom that are not of interest through the renormalization method. For our down-folding calculations, we have kept only Mn-d, Ge-p, and As-p orbitals as active degrees of freedom in the projection and downfolded all other degrees of freedom. The above-said orbitals significantly contribute near the Fermi energy and play a crucial role in driving the topological properties. The surface spectral function, distribution of Berry curvature in 2D Brillouin zone, AHC (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\sigma$$\end{document}), and the Fermi arcs were calculated using Wannier9079–81 and WannierTool82 starting from full DFT calculations. We have calculated the AHC by integrating the Berry curvature over the full Brillouin zone.

Structurally, it is a zinc-blende-derived superlattice structure, where each anion As has two Mn and two Ge cations as nearest neighbors, as shown in Fig. 1a. It is worth noting that two different kinds of anion-cation distances result in anion As occupying the Wyckoff position of (0.744, 0.25, 0.125). Since we have done the detailed calculations with the FM states, the dynamical stability of the FM state has been crosschecked using the phonon modes calculations and the missing phonon density of states in the negative frequency region confirms the dynamical stability of the crystal structure in the FM configuration, as shown in Fig. 1e. Moreover, the thermodynamic stability has been found by obtaining Helmholtz negative free energy and positive entropy as a function of temperature, as shown in the inset of Fig. 1e.

Topological signature of nodal line

To understand the band crossing found in the band structure of the Mn-3d manifold, we have plotted Fig. 3a total band structure with SOC of MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} in the FM phase, where the conduction and valence bands cross each other multiple times in different high-symmetry paths. The above analysis confirms the coexistence of both Type-I and Type-II symmetry- protected WNLs as marked in Fig. 3, in the presence of SOC. Point to be noted is that the existence of the WNL metallic phase in the presence of SOC, so- called in spin-orbit metal, is very rare and requires special symmetry protection to get that. The symmetry analysis26 pointed out that having the non-symmorphic symmetry is mandatory to get WNL-metal in the presence of SOC. However, in the present case of MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}, we found that the d-glide mirror plane perpendicular to [1,±1,0] is very crucial in protecting the degeneracy to form nodal lines. In the presence of the fractional translational symmetry (Glide or screw), the emergence of the additional band degeneracy at the BZ boundary can be understood from the perspective of the band folding in the folded BZ. Since this additional band degeneracy solely originates from the non-symmorphic symmetries associated with fractional translation, that symmetry can not be broken with the application of the SOC via unique anti-unitary protection, combined with time reversal and the glide translation90–92.

To confirm this point, we have calculated the band structure in the presence of SOC by breaking the corresponding mirror symmetry by a tiny shift of one Ge atom (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document} 0.1 \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\text{\AA }$$\end{document}) in the primitive unit cell within the ab plane as shown in Fig. 3d. As a result, the symmetry of the Ge-displaced MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} reduces from the tetragonal \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$I\bar{4}2d$$\end{document} to the monoclinic Sp. Gr. C2 (Sp. Gr. No. 5). Interesting point to be noted that the C2 space group is symmorphic. We found that due to the breaking of the glide mirror non-symmorphic symmetry perpendicular to [1,±1,0], the band degeneracy corresponding to the Type-I Weyl crossing has now broken, and a small SOC-driven gap opens up near the Fermi energy as shown in Fig. 3d. The point to be mentioned here is that the Weyl crossing, which forms the Type-I WNL, originates from the Mn-3d and As-sp bands without any contribution from the Ge atoms. In that case, technically, a tiny displacement of the Ge atom should not affect the bands very close to the Fermi energy. However, the tiny displacement of the apparently non-contribution Ge atom is enough to break the required glide symmetry to break the essential band degeneracy that originated from Mn and As bands. This exercise proves the importance of the glide mirror symmetry in protecting the band degeneracy at the WNL in the present case.

The surface spectral function corresponding to the Type-I WNL, as shown in Fig. 6a, clearly demonstrates the expected drumhead-like features and is not hybridized with the bulk bands; however, it decays into the bulk band exponentially. The drumhead-shaped isolated surface states in the Type-I case indicate the closed loop of nodal line present in the momentum space. On the contrary, for the Type-II case, the surface spectral function corresponding to the Fermi arc dispersion forms a tilted one-dimensional degenerate complex line, which does not look like a drumhead nature for obvious reasons. Moreover, the spectral line does not terminate at the Weyl points marked by the red and blue cones in Fig. 6b, rather it decays into the bulk bands. Comparing the Type-I and II surface spectral functions, it’s very evident that in Type-I, very clear isolated drumhead surface states exist, whereas in Type-II, the surface states are not so much isolated from the bulk bands; however, the coexistence of Type-I and II nodal lines in MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} in the FM phase is confirmed.

To understand the effect of the thin film geometrical changes in the AHC, we have recalculated it and shown it in Fig. 7c. The main features of the AHC as a function of energy remain almost unchanged; however, the peak value of the AHC has now increased to 380 S/cm and coincides with the Type-I WNL energy window as marked in the left panel of Fig. 7c. It is well established that the AHC is usually enhanced in the case of thin film compared to the bulk99–101. Also, the application of pressure or strain can also increase the AHC102,103. In our specific study, we have observed a subtle yet noticeable increase in intrinsic AHC with a minor application of strain. This result aligns with the broader understanding that applying strain contributes to the enhancement of Berry curvature, resulting in an elevated AHC consistent with the literature.

To understand the intriguing mechanism of the AHC enhancement, we have calculated and compared the Berry curvatures (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document}) of the Bulk and the thin film variant (strained structure). Figure 7d shows the Berry curvature plot as a function of the K-path in the BZ. Two important features we observed from these plots. Firstly, the Berry curvature in momentum space exists only in the vicinity of the Fermi arc and is shaped equivalent to the shape of the Fermi arc, which also confirms the nodal line feature of the bulk band crossing. Secondly, the plot indeed clearly indicates that the Berry curvature contribution for the thin film is higher than the bulk. As the integration of the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\Omega$$\end{document} over the momentum space gives the AHC, therefore AHC is proportional to the area under the curve of Fig. 7d. The comparative plot clearly indicates that the Berry curvature contribution is higher in the case of the thin film-like strained structure than in the bulk. We have also calculated Berry curvature in the \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$K_y-K_z$$\end{document} plane as shown in the inset of Fig. 7e for both bulk and thin film-like strained structures, which clearly shows that the net finite Berry curvature exists, which integrates over the entire BZ giving the intrinsic AHC in the present cases. Moreover, in the case of thin film, the Berry curvature has been enhanced compared to the bulk, which predominantly hinges on the detailing of the band structure. We observed that the AHC increases in proximity to the Weyl crossing, but conversely, it experiences a decrease in the vicinity of the Fermi energy in the case of thin films. Consequently, we can conclude that the increase in the anomalous Hall conductivity in the thin film variant can be attributed to the nature of the dispersion of the electronic bands.

An interesting observation we found is that, in the case of the thin film, the Type-I and Type-II WNL are much closer (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document}107 meV)in the energy scale, whereas the difference between the Type-I and II in the bulk case is very large (\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document}197 meV). Since, evolution of the AHC in the cases of the coexisting Type-I and II Weyl nodal topological phases has not been investigated in detail, it is not very well established what are influence of the simultaneous presence of both types of WNL on the AHC. However, from our analysis, we can infer that, as the energy difference between Type-I and II WNL decreases, the AHC value increases slightly. However, detailed investigations are required in this direction.

The analysis of the thin film modified MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} clearly established that the topological characters in the FM phase are robust with the simultaneous existence of Type-I and II WNL and are expected to observe large AHC very close to E\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_f$$\end{document}. Moreover, if by a small external perturbation, the FM phase can be stabilized in the bulk MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document}, a similar WNL can be observed. Most importantly, the FM ordering occurs at high temperature; therefore, the obtained large AHC can also be found in that high-temperature range, which can also be measured easily in the experiments.

Conclusion

We have investigated \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\mathcal{P}\mathcal{T}$$\end{document} asymmetric MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} in the presence of strong SOC in the context of the emerging topological nodal line. An interesting fact of this investigation is that, in most of the cases, the nodal line have been obtained in the absence of the SOC; however, in the present case, we observed the coexistence of Type-I and II one-dimensional degenerate nodal line in the presence of the SOC in the FM ordered phase due to the specific d-glide mirror type non-symmorphic symmetry protection. The robustness of the topological characters of the Weyl-type band crossing, which form the nodal lines, has been established via the investigation of the band inversion, normalized Berry curvature, and the WCC. The nature of the WNL has been identified via a closed loop of Fermi arcs and the manifestation of the Fermi arc dispersion in the surface spectral functions. The uniqueness of the WNL has been translated to the electronic transport of AHC, achieving the high value of \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\approx$$\end{document}300 S/cm exactly at the same energy as the Type-I WNL. To place our results in the experimental context, we have mimicked the thin film of the MnGeAs\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_2$$\end{document} by straining the structure equivalent to the experiments and re-investigated the topological characters and found that they retained the feature of the coexistence of Type-I and II WNL with an enhanced peak value of the AHC just below the E\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$_f$$\end{document}. We concluded that the enhancement of the AHC is entirely driven by the enhancement of the Berry curvature in the mimicked thin film systems compared to that of the bulk. These first-hand results from \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$ab-initio$$\end{document} studies inspire the experimental community to explore more of the time-reversal symmetry broken WNL topological phases in the strong SOC regime.
