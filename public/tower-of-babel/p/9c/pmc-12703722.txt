Perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness prospectively influence real-world social support-seeking behavior among emerging adults with first-episode psychosis
Authors: Aubrey M. Moe, Xiaoxuan Cai, Chenze Li, Heather M. Wastler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Introduction:Social dysfunction is a core feature of first-episode psychosis (FEP) that broadly impacts effective interpersonal interaction. FEP often begins during emerging adulthood, which is a critical period for developing and maintaining social relationships. Social support positively influences the early course of FEP, yet little is known about factors that influence social support-seeking behavior. Individuals with psychosis have elevated levels of negative interpersonal beliefs related to feeling like a burden to others (i.e., perceived burdensomeness; PB) and disconnection from others (i.e., thwarted belongingness; TB). However, how these beliefs impact perceptions of social support or support-seeking behavior is unknown.Methods:42 FEP participants completed laboratory-based assessments of social relationships and negative interpersonal beliefs, followed by 4 weeks of ecological momentary assessment (EMA) to assess real-world social support-seeking behavior. Correlational analyses and generalized linear mixed-effect models examined the contribution of PB and TB to both laboratory-based and real-world social perception and behavior.Results:Perceived burdensomeness (PB) and thwarted belongingness (TB) were negatively correlated with perceived social support. EMA revealed a within-person effect whereby individuals were most likely to seek social support when experiencing more negative affect than was typical of them. Higher levels of baseline PB and TB were predictive of less social support-seeking behavior in everyday life.Conclusion:Negative interpersonal beliefs are related to both perceptions of social support and real-world social support-seeking behavior in FEP. Beliefs about burdening others and individual-level variation in negative affect may be particularly impactful on social behavior. Collectively, interpersonal beliefs influence real-world social behavior in FEP.

Introduction

Social dysfunction is a core feature of psychotic illnesses (Handest et al., 2023) that contributes to broad impairments in an individual’s ability to appropriately and effectively navigate various activities of daily living (e.g., interpersonal relationships, independent living, occupational functioning). Social difficulties are present by the initial onset of the disorder (Horan et al., 2006) (i.e., first-episode psychosis; FEP) and tend to persist throughout the course of the psychotic illness (Velthorst et al., 2017). Social dysfunction in FEP is associated with greater social isolation, loneliness, and fewer interpersonal relationships (Horan et al., 2006; Eglit et al., 2018; Degnan et al., 2018; Erickson et al., 1989) – all of which contribute to lower levels of social support among individuals with FEP. Critically, social support is known to positively influence the early course of psychosis (Norman et al., 2005) (i.e., lower rates of re-hospitalization and reduced psychotic symptoms) and lower levels of social support in FEP are further associated with poorer functional outcome (Erickson et al., 1989).

In recent years, an increasing number of researchers have noted the utility of developmental lens in understanding social dysfunction in FEP (Chan, 2017; McGorry, 2011; Moe and Breitborde, 2019; Breitborde and Moe, 2019). Psychotic disorders tend to emerge in years of life spanning the late teens and into the 20s (Häfner et al., 2003) (i.e., emerging adulthood (Arnett, 2000)) – a period of life that also overlaps with a distinct developmental phase characterized by marked exploration of self-identity, relatedness to others, and navigation of uncertainty. The onset of psychosis during a period of life characterized by normative instability and change may have particularly relevant and reverberating impact on critical developmental milestones (Moe and Breitborde, 2019) – including difficulties in developing and maintaining a strong and accessible social network. Critically, a lack of social support may increase the vulnerability of individuals with FEP to interpersonal stressors that may worsen symptoms or distress (Trotta et al., 2013; Campbell and Morrison, 2007). The frequency of social interaction with peers in FEP is predictive of clinical recovery over a two year period (Bjornestad et al., 2017) - perhaps due to simultaneously increasing one’s ability to access social support that facilitates coping (Thoits, 2011; MacDonald et al., 1998). Perceptions of social support may be particularly important, with recent data from individuals with psychosis suggesting that perceived social support is more impactful on experiences of illness-related stigma than the actual size of one’s social network (Świtaj et al., 2021; Mueller et al., 2006). Collectively, social support is a critical and potentially modifiable factor in FEP. At the same time, little is known about the factors that influence perceptions of social support or social support-seeking behavior among young people with FEP.

Psychotic disorders are also characterized by broad cognitive alterations, including increased defeatist performance beliefs (DPB) and negative expectancy appraisals (NEA) (Beck and Rector, 2005; Couture et al., 2011). Broadly, DPB include overgeneralized, negative self-attributions of personal inadequacy or dysphoria secondary to perceived or expected failure that may interfere with goal-directed behavior (Beck and Rector, 2005; Campellone et al., 2016). NEA similarly include broad low expectations for successful outcomes – including low expectations for acceptance by other people (Rector et al., 2005). Research has demonstrated that FEP individuals have heightened DBP and NEA that are associated with motivation (Couture et al., 2011) and functional outcomes (Filip et al., 2024), thus suggesting that these beliefs may be an important target for psychosocial intervention. Notably, the measures of DBP/NEA often utilized in the psychosis literature are broad and intended to assess the impact of dysfunctional attitudes across a variety of contexts (Campellone et al., 2016). This approach permits a comprehensive understanding of DBP/NEA yet may also hinder a more precise understanding of how specific themes or types of dysfunctional attitudes may impact distinct aspects of behavioral outcomes.

Notably, individuals with FEP demonstrate alterations in social and interpersonal beliefs. More specifically, data suggest individuals with psychosis are likely to endorse interpersonal beliefs of feeling like a burden to others (i.e., perceived burdensomeness; PB) and disconnection from others (i.e., thwarted belongingness; TB) (Wastler et al., 2021; Heelis et al., 2016; Silva et al., 2015). Critically, the few studies that have investigated PB and TB in FEP have focused on suicide risk and the interpersonal psychological theory of suicidal behavior. While these studies have reported important findings on how PB and TB are associated with suicide risk in FEP (Wastler et al., 2021; Heelis et al., 2016), to our knowledge there have not yet been studies which focus instead on how these negative interpersonal beliefs may impact social behavior. Importantly, existing studies outside of the psychosis literature have demonstrated the feasibility of assessing the influence of PB and TB on real-world outcomes (Glenn et al., 2022; Pérez et al., 2024). Given existing evidence that broad defeatist beliefs and negative expectancy appraisals in psychosis influence functional outcomes (Couture et al., 2011; Filip et al., 2024; Ventura et al., 2014), investigating the influence of the specific interpersonal beliefs of PB and TB is an important next step. Further, studies capable of linking these negative interpersonal beliefs to real-world social support-seeking behavior may provide critical insights on how interpersonal processes unfold in FEP and guide effective intervention for social dysfunction.

Thus, our primary objectives in the present study were to: (i) assess cross-sectional associations between PB, TB, and perceived social support, (ii) examine whether individuals engage in real-world social support-seeking behavior when experiencing negative emotion, and (iii) to assess the prospective influence of PB and TB on longitudinal patterns of real-world social support-seeking behavior among emerging adults with first-episode psychosis (FEP). Use of a combined data-set comprised of baseline clinical assessments followed by four weeks of ecological momentary assessment (EMA) surveys permits a comprehensive examination of the factors that influence social relationships and social support-seeking behavior in real-time. Importantly, this approach allows for a more precise understanding of social processes in FEP that may guide alignment of interventions with targets that are most impactful on social behavior as it emerges in the real-world context. We expected that PB and TB would be negatively associated with social support. Next, considering evidence that emerging adults in non-clinical populations report seeking social support during stressful times (Lee and Goldstein, 2016) we expected that emerging adults with FEP would seek social support in response to negative affect. Given evidence that other types of broad cognitive alterations (e.g., defeatist beliefs, negative expectancy beliefs) impact social behavior among individuals with psychosis (Campellone et al., 2016; Filip et al., 2024), we further predicted that baseline PB and TB would prospectively influence social support-seeking behavior as assessed in the EMA data.

Methods

Participants and procedures

The current study is a secondary analysis of a project designed to examine the real-time relationship between emotion regulation and suicidal ideation among individuals with FEP (Wastler et al., 2025a, 2025b). 47 individuals with FEP were recruited from The Ohio State University Early Psychosis Intervention Center (EPICENTER) (Breitborde et al., 2015). Participants were eligible if they 1) met diagnostic criteria for a schizophrenia spectrum disorder or affective disorder with psychotic features per the Structured Clinical Interview for DSM-V (First et al., 2015), 2) were within 5 years of psychosis symptom onset, 3) were between the ages of 18 and 35, and 4) had no evidence of an intellectual disorder as determined by the Wide Range Achievement Test Reading subtest (Wilkinson and Robertson, 2006). Of the 47 participants, 3 dropped out before completing the baseline visit and 1 additional participant dropped out before completing ecological momentary assessment (EMA). One additional participant was excluded from analyses because it was later determined that they were outside the 5-year window for their psychosis onset. Another additional participant was excluded due to missing data for perceived burdensomeness. Thus, 42 participants were included in analyses for aim one (baseline measure correlations) and 41 participants were included in analyses for aims two and three (EMA analyses). Participants completed baseline clinical interviews and self-report measures followed by 28 days of EMA (4 surveys per day delivered at pseudorandom times based on each participant’s personal sleep schedule). Each participant received a maximum number of 112 EMA survey prompts during the study period. All EMA items assessed experiences “since the last assessment”. This study was reviewed and approved by The Ohio State University Institutional Review Board.

Baseline measures

Perceived burdensomeness (PB) and thwarted belongingness (TB) were assessed using the Interpersonal Needs Questionnaire (Van Orden et al., 2012) (INQ). The INQ is a 15-item self-report measure that assesses beliefs that one is a burden and that others would be better off without them (i.e., PB) and feelings of loneliness and disconnectedness from others (i.e., TB). Items were rated on a 7-point Likert scale (1-not at all true for me to 7-very true for me), with higher scores indicating greater PB or TB. The 8-item Emotional Support subscale of the NIH Toolbox Adult Social Relationship Scales (Cyranowski et al., 2013) was used to assess baseline social support. Items were rated on a 5-point Likert scale (1-Never to 5-Always) with higher scores indicating greater perceived social support.

Ecological momentary assessment

Negative affect was assessed by asking participants, “Since the last assessment, how much have you felt [emotion]”. The following negative emotions were assessed: sad, anxious, irritable, hopeless, and guilty (Armey et al., 2020; Forkmann et al., 2018; Kleiman et al., 2017; Ludwig et al., 2020; Strauss et al., 2019). Items were rated on a 1–5 Likert scale, with higher scores indicating greater intensity of each emotion (1-Very slightly or Not at all to 5-Extremely). An overall negative affect score was calculated by summing over the five items. Social support seeking behavior was part of a broader battery that assessed emotion regulation strategy use (Medland et al., 2020). Participants were asked, “Since the last assessment, how much have you responded to your unpleasant emotions by…” followed by each emotion regulation strategy presented on a separate page (Wastler et al., 2025a). Items were assessed on a 0–4 Likert Scale (Not at all to Always) with higher scores indicating greater use of each specific strategy. Similar to previous studies (Strauss et al., 2019; Visser et al., 2018), social support seeking behavior was assessed using the regulation item, “talking about your feelings to others.”

Statistical analyses

First, bivariate correlations (Pearson’s r) were utilized to examine baseline associations between perceived burdensomeness (PB), thwarted belongingness (TB), and social support.

Next, using EMA data, we employed generalized linear mixed-effect models (GLMM) to examine: i) the concurrent relationship between negative affect and social support seeking behavior within the same time-point in the longitudinal EMA data, and ii) the longitudinal association between the primary outcome, social support-seeking behavior, and baseline PB and TB. A Poisson distribution was utilized to account for the count-based nature of social support-seeking behavior. A step-wise modeling approach was applied to systematically evaluate random intercepts and random slopes provided at each EMA prompt. Interactions between key variables were also explored. All analyses were performed in R (Bates et al., 2015).

Specifically, we first examined the concurrent associations between negative affect and social support-seeking behavior. Fixed effects included time, within-person negative affect, and between-person negative affect (see Supplementary materials for definitions). Random intercepts were included to account for individual differences in negative affect, while random slopes for within-person negative affect captured within-person variability in the strength of the association across individuals. Random slopes for between-person negative affect were initially examined but excluded based on likelihood ratio tests (see Supplementary materials).

Finally, similar Poisson GLMM models were used to evaluate the prospective influence of baseline PB and TB on EMA reports of social support-seeking behavior. Given our observation that negative affect was associated with social support-seeking behavior, we included it as a covariate in this model. Fixed effects included time, within- and between-person negative affect, PB, and TB. Random intercepts and random slopes for within-person negative affect were retained. Random slopes for PB and TB were examined, but excluded due to convergence issues, reflecting the complexity of the models relative to the available sample size.

Results

Demographic characteristics are presented in Table 1. The sample was predominantly Caucasian (55.8 %), non-Hispanic (93 %), and Male (57.1 %). Of the 4592 possible EMA prompts (112 per participant across; 41 participants), 3824 responses were recorded (83.3 % overall compliance; median = 86.6 %) with an average of 3.33 responses per day (SD = 0.75). This response rate approximates or exceeds those reported in comparable EMA studies (Jones et al., 2021; Parrish et al., 2021; Granholm et al., 2020). The average duration between surveys completed was 3.22 h (Median = 3.03 h). Overall, participants reported engaging in support seeking behavior in 1756 (45.9 %) of recorded surveys.

We first examined baseline correlations between perceived burdensomeness, thwarted belongingness, and social support. Consistent with our hypotheses, social support was significantly correlated with perceived burdensomeness (r = −0.586, p < 0.001) and thwarted belongingness (r = −0.706, p < 0.001) in the expected direction (i.e., higher scores of perceived burdensomeness or thwarted belongingness were associated with lower levels of social support).

Next, we examined the real-time concurrent relationship between negative affect and social support seeking behavior using EMA data (Table 2). Results indicated that greater within-subject negative affect (B = 0.129, SE = 0.033, p < 0.001) was associated with greater support seeking behavior; in other words, when a participant experienced more negative affect than they typically do, they were more likely to seek social support. Between-subject negative affect was not associated with support seeking behavior (B = −0.006, SE = 0.060, p = 0.925).

Finally, we examined whether baseline perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness were associated with real-time support seeking behavior assessed using EMA (Table 3). Results indicate that lower levels of baseline thwarted belongingness were associated with greater support seeking behavior during the EMA period (B = −0.029, SE = 0.014, p = 0.044), even with negative affect included in the model. Similarly, lower levels of baseline perceived burdensomeness were associated with greater support seeking behavior during the EMA period (B = −0.106, SE = 0.031, p < 0.001), even with negative affect included in the model. When both perceived burdensomeness and thwarted belongingness were included in the same model, effects remained significant for perceived burdensomeness (B = −0.104, SE = 0.037, p = 0.005), but not thwarted belongingness (B = −0.002, SE = 0.017, p = 0.917).

Discussion

The findings of the present study suggest that beliefs of perceived burdensomeness (PB) and thwarted belongingness (TB) are negatively associated with perceived social support in FEP. Furthermore, we observed a within-person effect of negative affect on social support-seeking behavior during EMA, whereby individuals with FEP were most likely to seek social support when they experienced more negative affect than was typical of them. Finally, we found that PB and TB at baseline prospectively influence real-world social support-seeking behavior. More specifically, the EMA data revealed that higher levels of baseline PB and TB were predictive of less social support-seeking behavior in everyday life. Notably, when the effects of baseline PB and TB were considered within a single model, PB remained predictive of social support-seeking behavior during EMA while TB was no longer a significant predictor. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first study to demonstrate the prospective influence of negative interpersonal beliefs (i.e., PB and TB) on real-world social support-seeking behavior among emerging adults with FEP.

Our observations related to the within-person effects of negative affect on social support-seeking behavior may have important implications. Specifically, we found that when an individual with FEP reports more negative affect (i.e., feeling worse) than they usually do (i.e., based on their own average level of negative affect across the study period), they were more likely to seek social support. Notably, when examining between-participant effects we did not observe any differences in social support-seeking behavior among individuals with FEP who have higher dispositional levels of affect compared to those with lower dispositional levels of negative affect. These findings, which suggest that the influence of negative affect on social behavior among FEP individuals may not be apparent with a single or across-persons measurement yet may be more accurately discerned with individualized repeated assessment, provide a more precise understanding of how negative affect impacts social support-seeking behavior in FEP.

Critically, our findings suggest that negative interpersonal beliefs influence how social support-seeking behavior manifests among young people with psychosis within their respective social environments. Though previous research has demonstrated that defeatist performance beliefs and negative expectancy appraisals influence functional outcomes in FEP, the specific impact of PB and TB has not previously been explored. While higher levels of both PB and TB were independently predictive of less social support-seeking behavior, PB continued to predict unique variance in social behavior even when TB was also considered within the model. A possible interpretation of this finding is that, among emerging adults with FEP, beliefs about burdening others (i. e., PB) may be more impactful on social support-seeking than whether one believes that they have been or will be excluded by others in social situations (i.e., TB). With replication and further study, these results suggest that negative interpersonal beliefs – particularly those associated with PB – may be important targets for psychosocial intervention. Addressing maladaptive thinking is a core tenant of cognitive-behavioral therapy (CBT) (Barlow, 2014), which is also an empirically-support treatment for FEP (Müller et al., 2014). Individuals with FEP who experience social difficulties may thus benefit from therapeutic strategies focused on understanding and addressing negative interpersonal beliefs. Given the developmental tasks associated with emerging adulthood (e.g., forming and maintaining social relationships), this approach may also address a unique need for FEP individuals during this phase of life.

Though not assessed in the current report, our results also raise important considerations for understanding suicidality in early psychosis. Previous studies of suicide risk in severe mental illness have noted social support is a protective factor which mitigates suicide behavior and risk (Montross et al., 2005; Hu et al., 2023). Notably, recent studies from the suicide literature suggest PB ant TB both influence and are influenced by social processes in clinical and non-clinical samples of adults (Parrish et al., 2021; Chu et al., 2018). As we have now demonstrated the influence of TB and PB on social support-seeking behavior in FEP, our findings bring about important questions for how this association may converge with the suicide literature. It is possible that diminished social support-seeking is a measurable behavioral manifestation of negative interpersonal beliefs which may have explanatory value in clarifying the relationship between PB, TB, and suicide risk. Notably, the current study only assessed the prospective influence of PB and TB on social support-seeking behavior. Future studies which more comprehensively measure the dynamic interactions of these factors may provide additional insight into the nature and directionality of these associations in FEP.

In addition to the longitudinal influence of PB and TB on social support-seeking behavior, we also observed a concurrent correlation whereby greater levels of negative interpersonal beliefs was associated with less perceived social support among FEP individuals. As previous studies have noted that perceived social support is associated with lower levels of psychiatric symptoms (Fan et al., 2021) and better quality of life (Prabhakaran et al., 2021) among people with psychosis, understanding factors which may influence these perceptions may guide identification of potential treatment targets. Though our results suggest that negative interpersonal beliefs (i.e., PB and TB) are related to both: i) perceptions of social support, and ii) the use of social support-seeking behavior in FEP, questions remain with regard to how these factors may interact and/or influence on another. More specifically, future FEP studies may test more directed models of the temporal relationships between negative interpersonal beliefs, more broad perceptions of social support, and behavioral choices related to whether one does or does not seek social support when experiencing emotional distress.

This study has several notable strengths. First, utilization of ecological momentary assessment (EMA) to understand social-support seeking behavior permitted a more precise understanding of how negative interpersonal beliefs influence social behavior as it emerges in the real-world setting and further mitigated potential limitations of measurement approaches that require retrospective reporting on behavior. Additionally, the EMA approach allowed us to examine both the within- and between-subject effects of emotion on social behavior and further clarify how the influence of negative affect emerges at the individual level. Next, our use of a sample of emerging adults with FEP provides greater insight on the relevance of negative interpersonal beliefs during a specific developmental phase of life. Further, our investigation of how specific types of negative beliefs and expectations impact specific types of social outcomes (i.e., engaging in social support-seeking behavior) guides a more precise understanding of previously observed associations between broad defeatist performance beliefs (DBP) and negative expectancy appraisals (NEA) and outcomes in psychotic disorders (Filip et al., 2024). Collectively, our results highlight negative interpersonal beliefs as potential targets for augmenting social support-seeking behavior in FEP.

There are several limitations of the current study which are important to consider. Notably, negative interpersonal beliefs were only assessed at baseline. How PB and TB may vary over time or in tandem with social support-seeking behavior among FEP individuals is currently unknown. Next, though social support-seeking behavior was assessed comprehensively as part of EMA, individual’s evaluations of the outcome of those support-seeking behaviors (e.g., if the other person was available/responded, if the support-seeking was helpful) were not assessed in the present study and remain and important area for future investigation. Though our EMA approach to assessing social support-seeking behavior is consistent with previous studies (Strauss et al., 2019; Visser et al., 2018), seeking social support is not limited to discussing negative emotions with others and thus future studies should consider a broader assessment strategy. Relatedly, the type (i.e., from friends/peers, family, partners, or other sources) and format (i.e., face-to-face, via texting, on social media) of social support sought by FEP individuals was not assessed. Given existing evidence that friendship/peer relationships may be particularly important among emerging adults with psychosis (Bjornestad et al., 2017; Moe et al., 2021) and that social media use should also be considered as a form of social interaction in FEP (Tran et al., 2025), future studies which probe specific aspects of social support-seeking are needed. Next, existing studies noting the relevance of DBP/NEA in psychosis provided a conceptual framework that guided our investigation of negative interpersonal beliefs in the present study. At the same time, we did not directly assess DBP/NEA and thus are unable to investigate potential associations between those variables and PB and TB. Finally, we utilized a relatively small sample of individuals with FEP which may have limited power for detecting between-person differences. At the same time, the use of EMA did allow a rich data on social support-seeking within each study participant which permitted differentiation of within-individual effects of PB and TB on social behavior. Finally, this was an uncontrolled study of FEP individuals and thus how our pattern of results converges with or differs from individuals without FEP is not known. Collectively, future studies in larger cohorts of individuals with FEP would be helpful in extending and clarifying our current pattern of results.

In summary, our findings suggest that negative interpersonal beliefs (i.e., PB and TB) are related to both perceptions of social support and real-world social support-seeking behavior among FEP individuals. Importantly, this work also highlights that young people with FEP do seek social support when dealing with negative emotional experiences – with our findings providing preliminary information on factors which may drive this type of social behavior. The use of EMA to assess social support-seeking behavior in FEP appears feasible and further yields rich data which can provide clarification on the interplay of negative beliefs and behavior in the real-world environment. Given our lack of a control group in this study, future research with comparison groups will be critical to delineating the specificity of our observations to FEP. Finally, our findings provide supportive evidence for future studies which may assess the extent to which social support-seeking behavior may contribute to other important clinical outcomes in FEP (e.g., suicide ideation and behavior).
