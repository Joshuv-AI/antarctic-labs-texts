ADHD diagnostics and severity assessment using topological manifold learning of resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI)
Authors: Yan Xue, Yuxiang Zhou, Xiaoxu Na, Xiawei Ou, Yongming Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractNon-intrusive neuroimaging technology offers fast and robust diagnostic tools for neuro-disorder disease diagnosis, such as Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD). Resting-state functional magnetic imaging (rs-fMRI) has been demonstrated to have great potential for such applications due to its unique capability and convenience in providing spatial-temporal brain imaging. One critical challenge of using rs-fMRI data is the high dimensionality for both spatial and temporal domains. Thus, direct use of rs-fMRI data for the diagnosis will usually perform poorly due to the “curse of dimensionality.” This paper proposes a novel nonlinear dimension reduction technique for rs-fMRI data for easy downstream analysis, such as diagnostics, regression, and visualization. The proposed method integrates the Curvature Augmented Manifold Embedding and Learning (CAMEL) algorithm with key rs-fMRI features, such as Amplitude of Low-Frequency Fluctuations (ALFF), Regional Homogeneity (ReHo), and Functional Connectivity (FC). The ADHD diagnosis problem is formulated as a classification problem in the reduced latent space and is validated with 551 data points from an open fMRI database. Compared to available literature models and results, 13 %–26 % improvement in diagnostic accuracy is observed. Additionally, the proposed methodology also supports individualized ADHA severity assessment by regression analysis in the latent space and provides a potential tool for personalized treatment. Finally, an ADHD sensitivity map is developed, highlighting brain regions associated with ADHD scores and providing interpretable insights into ADHD's neural underpinnings.Keywords: ADHD, rs-fMRI, CAMEL, Dimension reduction, Diagnosis

Introduction

Attention-Deficit/Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD) is a common neurodevelopmental disorder affecting both children and adults. It is characterized by symptoms such as inattention, hyperactivity, and impulsivity, which can significantly impact academic performance, social interactions, and personal relationships (Canario et al., 2021). ADHD is estimated to affect approximately 5–7 % of children worldwide, with many cases persisting into adulthood, resulting in a prevalence of 2–5 % among adults (Faraone et al., 2021; Polanczyk et al., 2015). Although the exact etiology of ADHD remains uncertain, research suggests it arises from a complex interplay of genetic predispositions, environmental influences, and neurobiological factors.

Despite significant advances in understanding ADHD, current diagnostic methods still rely heavily on clinical assessments and subjective symptom evaluations, which can be prone to error and limit the objectivity of diagnoses (Loh et al., 2022). The need for precise, objective, and scalable diagnostic tools is critical, particularly as ADHD continues to affect individuals across all age groups and remains associated with considerable social and occupational challenges. Neuroimaging, especially rs-fMRI, has emerged as a promising avenue to address these limitations by providing insights into the neural mechanisms underpinning ADHD through non-invasive brain activity mapping.

Neurobiological studies have highlighted structural and functional abnormalities in brain regions associated with attention, impulsivity, and executive functions in individuals with ADHD (Shaw et al., 2018). These findings suggest that ADHD involves disruptions in the neural networks responsible for cognitive and behavioral regulation. Resting-state functional magnetic resonance imaging (rs-fMRI) has become a valuable tool for investigating these underlying mechanisms, as it measures spontaneous blood-oxygen-level-dependent (BOLD) signal fluctuations in the brain during rest, providing insights into the brain's intrinsic functional organization and connectivity without requiring task performance. Compared to task-based fMRI, rs-fMRI is particularly advantageous in the context of neurodevelopmental disorders, especially among children, due to its reduced demands on subject compliance and cognitive effort. Recent studies have leveraged topological dimensionality reduction and machine learning to extract meaningful patterns from rs-fMRI data, showing promise in improving diagnostic accuracy and identifying neurobiological markers of ADHD and related conditions (Xu et al., 2023; Yu-Feng et al., 2007; Zhang et al., 2019).

Among the many rs-fMRI-derived features explored in ADHD research, Amplitude of Low-Frequency Fluctuations (ALFF) (Zou et al., 2008), Regional Homogeneity (ReHo) (Zang et al., 2004), and Functional Connectivity (FC) (Honey et al., 2009) are among the most commonly used. Each captures distinct neurophysiological dimensions, such as regional activity intensity, local synchronization, and inter-regional coordination, respectively. However, studies employing these features often report divergent neuroanatomical findings, reflecting the heterogeneity of ADHD and the feature-specific nature of signal representation (Cao et al., 2006; McBurnett and Pfiffner, 2007; Yang et al., 2011).

These inconsistencies highlight the need for an integrative approach that combines multiple rs-fMRI features to yield a more comprehensive and robust characterization of ADHD-related brain alterations. At the same time, the high dimensionality and complex nonlinear structure of rs-fMRI data present significant challenges for traditional machine learning models, often leading to overfitting and limited interpretability.

This study aims to address these challenges by proposing a unified framework that integrates voxel-level (ALFF, ReHo) and region-level (FC) features using a curvature-augmented manifold learning method (CAMEL) for nonlinear dimensionality reduction. The proposed model performs both classification and regression tasks to support categorical ADHD diagnosis and individualized symptom severity estimation. Furthermore, the model generates interpretable ADHD sensitivity maps to visualize the contribution of brain regions to diagnostic outcomes, enhancing clinical relevance and transparency.

Through this integrated strategy, we seek to bridge the gap between data-driven modeling and neurobiological interpretability in ADHD research. Our work contributes to the growing body of literature aimed at developing objective, reproducible, and clinically applicable biomarkers for ADHD diagnosis and personalized assessment.

Data acquisition and preprocessing

The rs - fMRI data used in this study was obtained from the ADHD200 dataset (“The ADHD-200 Sample,” 2011), an initiative aimed at accelerating the understanding of the neural basis of ADHD through open data-sharing and discovery-based science. The original dataset comprised 776 resting-state fMRI and anatomical datasets aggregated across 8 independent imaging sites, including 491 typically developing individuals and 285 children and adolescents with ADHD, aged 7–21 years. The dataset also included phenotypic information such as diagnostic status, ADHD symptom measures, age, sex, IQ, and medication status. Preliminary quality control assessments were performed for all resting-state fMRI scans, ensuring that data quality was maintained.

For the purposes of this study, we selected a subset of the ADHD-200 dataset consisting of participants from three imaging sites: Peking University, Kennedy Krieger Institute, and New York University Child Study Center. This selection was driven by data completeness. These sites provided both imaging data and full diagnostic information, including ADHD Index scores. In contrast, many other sites in the dataset lacked either ADHD severity measures or complete diagnostic labels, which prevents their inclusion in the classification and regression tasks. The final dataset included 551 participants: 174 diagnosed with ADHD and 377 healthy controls.

Data preprocessing was conducted using the DPABI Matlab toolbox (Yan et al., 2016), a widely used software designed for resting-state fMRI data analysis. The preprocessing steps included slice timing correction, realignment to correct for head motion, coregistration to the participant's anatomical T1-weighted image, normalization using DARTEL to map data to a standard brain template, and spatial smoothing with a Gaussian kernel (FWHM of 6 mm) to enhance the signal-to-noise ratio.

Feature extraction

Following preprocessing, three key rs-fMRI features were extracted: Amplitude of Low-Frequency Fluctuations (ALFF), Regional Homogeneity (ReHo), and Functional Connectivity (FC). These features capture complementary aspects of brain function across different spatial scales.

ALFF was computed by transforming voxel-level BOLD time series into the frequency domain and averaging the power spectrum within the 0.01–0.08 Hz band. This feature reflects the intensity of spontaneous neural activity in a given region.

ReHo was calculated using Kendall's coefficient of concordance (KCC), which quantifies the synchronization of time series among a voxel and its nearest neighbors, thus capturing local functional coordination.

FC was derived using a seed-based correlation method applied to the time series of 90 brain regions defined by the Automated Anatomical Labeling (AAL) atlas, producing a 90 × 90 inter-regional connectivity matrix. FC was computed using Pearson correlation between region-level time series.

To reduce dimensionality and remove irrelevant features prior to manifold learning, we applied a filter-based feature selection strategy within each training fold of the cross-validation. Specifically, for ALFF, ReHo, and FC features, we computed the Pearson correlation coefficient between each feature and the ADHD Index. Features with an absolute correlation below 0.2 were excluded. This threshold was chosen to retain features with at least moderate linear association with the target variable while removing noisy or uninformative signals. For example, for ALFF and ReHo, computed at the voxel level, the original feature dimensionality was 271,633, while FC initially included 4005 unique region-pair connections. After filtering, the retained feature counts varied slightly across folds, with a representative example yielding 2633 ALFF features, 606 ReHo features, and 894 FC features. This selection process was performed independently within each training fold to prevent data leakage (i.e., no data from the validation split is used for this thresholding).

The retained ALFF and ReHo voxel features were then concatenated with FC region-level features to form an integrated multi-scale feature vector, supporting subsequent dimensionality reduction and learning tasks, as shown below.

Dimensionality reduction and downstream learning

Resting-state fMRI data inherently involves high-dimensional features, as each brain region or voxel may contribute to complex patterns of connectivity and neuro activity. This dimensionality poses a challenge for traditional machine learning models, often resulting in the “curse of dimensionality,” where the data's sparsity impairs the ability to generalize effectively. Dimensionality reduction helps overcome these challenges by projecting the data into a lower-dimensional space, facilitating more efficient downstream learning and reducing the risk of overfitting. Linear techniques, such as Principal Component Analysis (PCA), have demonstrated some effectiveness (Sidhu et al., 2012), but their assumptions of linearity and stationarity limit their applicability to rs-fMRI data, which is inherently nonlinear and temporally dynamic. To address these limitations, nonlinear manifold learning algorithms—also known as nonlinear embedding techniques—have been explored. Algorithms such as Locally Linear Embedding (LLE) and Isomap (Haak et al., 2018; Liu et al., 2013) aim to uncover a low-dimensional manifold that retains the intrinsic geometry of the original high-dimensional space. While these methods have shown promise in neuroimaging contexts, they often face scalability issues and sensitivity to noise, making them less robust in real-world ADHD datasets.

In this study, we adopt the Curvature Augmented Manifold Embedding and Learning (CAMEL) algorithm (Liu, 2024), a scalable nonlinear dimensionality reduction method that incorporates both local neighborhood structure and global curvature information. CAMEL is an advanced method inspired by concepts from physics and topology, specifically the attractive-repulsive force dynamics used in graph-based dimensionality reduction models. Unlike traditional manifold learning techniques relying only on distance metrics, CAMEL introduces a novel “curvature-augmented force” that allows it to capture curvature information in high-dimensional data, which is shown to preserve both distance and curvature information in the low-dimensional representation of the original data (Liu, 2024). This curvature-induced force enables CAMEL to preserve not only local geometric relationships but also topological continuity in the latent space. These preserved structures are especially important in rs-fMRI data, where subtle and nonlinear variations in brain connectivity may correspond to underlying neurodevelopmental patterns associated with ADHD. By retaining curvature and neighborhood continuity, CAMEL facilitates the discovery of diagnostically meaningful low-dimensional manifolds that reflect clinical severity (as seen in Fig. 6) and class separability (Fig. 4), thus providing both analytical utility and interpretability. In our implementation, the CAMEL embedding with 2–3 dimensions was directly used as input to the downstream classifiers, without any additional feature selection. This choice was based on cross-validation experiments that showed comparable or superior performance with low-dimensional embeddings compared to higher-dimensional ones (up to 10). The rationale and robustness of this decision are further illustrated in the sensitivity analysis (see Section 3.7 and Fig. 2, Fig. 3). A detailed algorithm derivation and comparison with different dimensionality reduction methods is beyond the scope of this paper, and interested readers are referred to (Liu, 2024).

In this study, CAMEL was applied to the extracted ALFF, ReHo, and FC feature vector to reduce their dimensionality before feeding them into machine learning models for ADHD classification and severity assessment. The embedding produced by CAMEL was then used for subsequent machine learning tasks, including classification to diagnose ADHD and regression to assess the severity index. This approach allowed for an efficient representation of the data, reducing computational complexity while preserving crucial diagnostic features. In addition, we chose CAMEL for its theoretical ability to preserve both local and global structure in high-dimensional data. These advantages, supported by prior benchmarking results (Liu, 2024) make it well suited for rs-fMRI–based prediction tasks.

Like many dimensionality reduction methods, CAMEL has some parameters and hyperparameters that need to be determined or calibrated. The first parameter is the input feature dimension and output feature dimension. In the proposed ADHD study, the input feature dimension is 4133 (2633 ALFF + 606 Reho + 894 FC). The output feature dimension can vary depending on the specific goals. For example, the output dimension is 2 or 3 for visualization purposes, for which each patient feature vector is mapped to a 2D or 3D point and can be visualized in a 2D/3D plot. For machine learning tasks, a higher output dimension can be used to enhance the prediction accuracy. In the proposed study, we have tried the output dimension 2–10 and used 2–3 for the classification/regression tasks shown later. CAMEL relies on the nearest neighbor graph for its force computing, and the number of neighbors is a parameter tuned for each application (McInnes et al., 2018). In the current study, 30 nearest neighbors are used. CAMEL has both distance-based and curvature-based force computing and the weight factor for curvature force needs to be adjusted for different applications. We use 0.0001 in the proposed study. Finally, CAMEL can be used for both unsupervised and supervised learning and there is a label weight factor (0 for unsupervised and 1 for fully supervised). We use a factor of 0.3 to limit the label information on the low-dimension structure, which aims to reduce overfitting in classification and regression. Once the dimension reduction is done, the downstream learning can be used for classification or regression tasks for different applications. The proposed learning task in this study aimed to classify ADHD diagnosis and predict ADHD severity, in reduced latent space obtained by CAMEL derived from resting-state functional MRI (rs-fMRI) data. Neural network (Hoekzema et al., 2014) is selected as the primary downstream learning algorithm due to its ability to handle complex, non-linear relationships and its robustness in dealing with high-dimensional data. A 5-fold cross-validation was used to evaluate model performance, ensuring consistent representation across ADHD and control groups. Metrics such as accuracy, sensitivity, and F1-score were used to assess model effectiveness (Sokolova and Lapalme, 2009). Hyperparameter tuning was performed using a grid search approach (Bergstra and Bengio, 2012) to optimize parameter settings, selecting the best configuration based on cross-validation results. This process ensured that the model achieved optimal predictive accuracy and generalizability.

ADHD-sensitivity map

One unique contribution of the proposed study is to develop a model-based imaging modality to enhance the interpretability of ADHD prediction. An ADHD-sensitivity map is proposed as an outcome of the regression model, which aims to identify the relative contributions of different brain voxels to ADHD severity prediction. This process involved hierarchical multiscale model analysis (e.g., both voxel-based and region-based analyses). First, brain region sensitivity analysis is conducted by systematically masking the FC features corresponding to individual brain regions and evaluating the resulting impact on model performance using a feature ablation approach (Zeiler and Fergus, 2014). The importance of each brain region was quantified based on changes in R2 performance after masking. For example, a large increase of R2 after adding the specific region indicates the strong contribution of the model's predictive power for ADHD and is considered highly relevant to ADHD severity. Next, several high-contributing brain regions are selected for voxel-level sensitivity map construction. Voxel-level features (e.g., the amplitude of low-frequency fluctuations (ALFF) and regional homogeneity (ReHo)) from these high contributing regions are extracted, which provides fine-grained information about local brain activity and connectivity patterns. The importance of each voxel region was quantified based on changes in R2 performance after masking. These importance scores were normalized and plotted on top of the brain structural image to show the sensitivity of each voxel to the ADHD severity, which is called the ADHD sensitivity map. The sensitivity in the current study is normalized to be in the range of [0, 1]. 0 indicates no apparent contribution to ADHD severity evaluation, and 1 indicates the voxel has the largest contribution to ADHD severity among all voxels checked.

Literature baseline models

To contextualize the proposed model's performance and ensure a meaningful benchmark, we reviewed a wide range of machine learning approaches previously applied to ADHD diagnosis using resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) data. Although only a subset of these methods is included in our quantitative evaluation due to dataset compatibility and availability of results, this comparative study underlines the methodological gaps our approach aims to address.

Classical machine learning methods such as Support Vector Machines (SVM) and Random Forests have been extensively explored for ADHD classification. SVMs are particularly valued for their ability to model complex decision boundaries and perform well in high-dimensional spaces. For example, a machine learning framework was developed using SVM to integrate both imaging and non-imaging features in order to identify functional connectivity alterations associated with ADHD (Riaz et al., 2017). Despite their effectiveness, SVMs require careful kernel selection and parameter tuning, and their performance may degrade when feature dimensionality is high and not appropriately managed.

Random Forests have also demonstrated strong performance due to their robustness to overfitting and ability to handle nonlinear relationships. A representative study proposed a discriminative subnetwork selection approach to extract meaningful connectivity patterns between ADHD and control subjects, followed by classification using Random Forests (Du et al., 2016). In addition to providing competitive classification accuracy, Random Forests yield feature importance metrics, which can be valuable for model interpretation. However, they may be computationally inefficient on large-scale datasets, and the ensemble structure can limit transparency in decision-making.

Deep learning techniques have gained increasing attention for ADHD diagnosis using resting-state fMRI (rs-fMRI) due to their capacity to model complex spatial and temporal dependencies. Among them, convolutional neural networks (CNNs) are widely adopted for their ability to automatically extract hierarchical features from neuroimaging data. For instance, 3D CNN models have been developed to capture local spatial patterns, and multi-modal CNNs have been proposed to integrate functional and structural MRI data for enhanced classification performance (Zou et al., 2017; De Silva et al., 2021). Other CNN-based models, such as SCCNN-Attention, incorporate attention mechanisms to focus on diagnostically relevant regions (Zhang et al., 2020), while spatio-temporal CNNs have been designed to jointly learn spatial and temporal features (Mao et al., 2019). Variants employing brain-region-focused learning or time-distributed frameworks have further improved performance, with enhancements from data augmentation and multimodal integration (Kim et al., 2023; Sims, 2022). Despite their strengths, CNNs typically require large training datasets to avoid overfitting and offer limited interpretability, which may hinder clinical deployment.

Beyond CNNs, recurrent neural networks (RNNs) have been used to exploit the temporal nature of rs-fMRI data. Bidirectional Long Short-Term Memory (Bi-LSTM) models and multi-layer LSTMs have demonstrated the ability to model time-series data from both forward and backward directions, leading to improved classification accuracy (Liu et al., 2020; Saurabh and Gupta, 2024). In addition, adaptive spatial-temporal models have been proposed to capture global temporal dependencies from spatially structured fMRI sequences (Qiu et al., 2024).

Graph-based deep learning approaches, particularly Graph Neural Networks (GNNs), have emerged as powerful tools for analyzing brain connectomes. These models effectively represent the brain as a graph, capturing topological and relational patterns in functional connectivity. Dynamic graph convolutional networks have been applied to model temporal changes in connectivity (Zhao et al., 2022), while multi-task GNNs have been developed to diagnose multiple neurodevelopmental disorders, including ADHD and ASD, using shared representations (Huang et al., 2020). However, GNNs are often computationally demanding and sensitive to the quality of graph construction, which can be affected by noise in rs-fMRI data.

Ensemble-based models have been explored as effective alternatives to deep neural networks in rs-fMRI-based ADHD classification. For instance, gcForest, a multi-layer cascade forest model, was extended with a multi-grained scanning strategy to integrate multi-resolution features for improved diagnostic performance in small and imbalanced neuroimaging datasets (Shao et al., 2019). Similarly, XGBoost, a gradient boosting ensemble method, has shown competitive accuracy and efficiency in ADHD studies (Shao et al., 2019), although its reliance on handcrafted features and extensive preprocessing may limit its generalizability.

Beyond ensemble strategies, hybrid learning frameworks have gained increasing attention for combining the advantages of deep learning and traditional machine learning. For example, A recent study introduced a hybrid model that integrates convolutional neural networks (CNNs) with feature selection techniques to improve classification accuracy and interpretability (Dey et al., 2024). Transfer learning has also emerged as a practical solution to the challenge of limited training data. As demonstrated by the Connectomics in NeuroImaging Transfer Learning Challenge, leveraging pre-trained models has proven effective in enhancing generalizability for ADHD and related neurodevelopmental conditions (Schirmer et al., 2020). To further mitigate data scarcity and domain heterogeneity, data augmentation techniques have been employed to enhance cross-site generalizability. A systematic evaluation of these strategies on multi-site rs-fMRI datasets demonstrated substantial improvements in model performance for ADHD classification (Çiçek et al., 2019).

Among the reviewed methods, we selected those trained and evaluated on the ADHD-200 dataset for direct performance comparison (see Section 3.1, Table 4). These methods serve as empirical baselines against which our proposed CAMEL-based integrated model is benchmarked.

Model training and hyperparameter selection

The proposed modeling pipeline was evaluated using five-fold cross-validation, repeated ten times with different random seeds. Within each fold, the model was re-trained from scratch using only the training data, with the test fold strictly held out to avoid any leakage during model selection or evaluation.

Hyperparameter tuning was performed within the training folds using a grid search strategy. For each training set, a subset was used as an internal validation set to select optimal parameters for both CAMEL (e.g., number of neighbors, curvature weights) and the MLP regressor (e.g., hidden layer size, learning rate, regularization). The final selected configuration, which yielded consistently high performance across different folds, was fixed and used for all experiments. In addition to standard hyperparameter selection, we conducted a sensitivity analysis on the dimensionality of the CAMEL embedding. We varied the number of latent components from 2 to 10 and evaluated performance across both classification and regression tasks. As shown in Fig. 2, Fig. 3, classification accuracy peaked at 2 dimensions and remained stable afterward, while regression R2 scores were highest at 3 dimensions, with marginal gains observed beyond that. Given the computational cost of learning higher-dimensional embeddings and the diminishing performance improvements, we selected 2 dimensions for classification and 3 dimensions for regression in our final pipeline configuration. This choice balances predictive accuracy with model efficiency and interpretability. It is also noted that the proposed methodology has very stable performance, which shows a consistent and small standard deviation from all checked dimensions.

Discussion and conclusions

This study presents a unified framework for ADHD diagnosis and severity assessment that integrates voxel-level (ALFF and ReHo) and region-level (FC) rs-fMRI features within a nonlinear manifold embedding (CAMEL). Across classification and regression tasks, the proposed model outperformed baseline methods trained on the ADHD-200 dataset. These results demonstrate the advantage of combining multi-scale neuroimaging features and topological data representations for capturing the complex brain activities associated with ADHD. Several discussions are provided below to highlight the important findings from the proposed study.

Comparison with baseline methods

Compared to traditional machine learning models (e.g., SVM, Random Forests) and recent deep learning architectures (e.g., SCCNN-Attention, ASSRNN), our model achieved superior performance in both classification accuracy and severity prediction. Existing models often relied on single-modality or unimodal feature representations, which may limit their ability to capture the full spectrum of ADHD-related brain dynamics. Our CAMEL-based approach, by contrast, leverages complementary features across spatial scales and embeds them into a low-dimensional space that preserves intrinsic topological structure. This enhances class separability and enables better generalization to unseen data.

Feature complementarity and integration benefits

The use of rs-fMRI-derived features (i.e., ALFF, ReHo, and FC) has become a central focus in recent ADHD neuroimaging research, as each characterizes distinct yet complementary aspects of brain function. However, prior studies often examined these features independently, leading to fragmented and sometimes inconsistent findings across populations. Integrating these features enables a more comprehensive understanding of ADHD-related neural alterations and improves model generalizability.

Amplitude of Low-Frequency Fluctuations (ALFF) measures the intensity of spontaneous neural activity in the low-frequency range. Several studies have reported ALFF abnormalities in individuals with ADHD. For example, decreased ALFF in the frontal cortex has been associated with impaired cognitive control mechanisms (Yang et al., 2011), while other findings showed ALFF alterations in regions such as the right inferior frontal gyrus, left sensorimotor cortex, and bilateral cerebellum (Zang et al., 2004). These studies suggest that regional ALFF changes may reflect functional disruptions in ADHD, although ALFF lacks the ability to characterize broader connectivity patterns.

Regional Homogeneity (ReHo) quantifies the local synchrony of neural activity across neighboring voxels. Altered ReHo patterns have been observed in frontal-striatal-cerebellar circuits and the occipital cortex in boys with ADHD (Cao et al., 2006), suggesting dysregulation in localized brain dynamics. While ReHo effectively captures fine-grained local connectivity, it provides limited information on long-range interactions between brain regions. Moreover, variability in ReHo findings across studies indicates the importance of integrating it with other feature types for improved robustness.

Functional Connectivity (FC) characterizes interactions between distributed brain regions and has revealed ADHD-related disruptions in large-scale networks, particularly those involving the anterior cingulate cortex and the posterior cingulate cortex/precuneus (McBurnett and Pfiffner, 2007). FC is particularly effective for capturing system-level abnormalities but lacks the spatial granularity of voxel-wise features such as ALFF and ReHo.

Given these complementary perspectives, combining ALFF, ReHo, and FC can help reconcile inconsistencies in prior findings and provide a more holistic representation of brain dysfunction in ADHD. This study demonstrates that integrating these features significantly improves both classification and regression performance, outperforming models based on single-feature inputs. The classification accuracy of our integrated model (86.7 %) exceeded previously reported results using individual feature modalities, and regression performance (R2 = 0.85) further supports its ability to model continuous symptom severity.

This integrative framework not only enhances the reproducibility and diagnostic utility of rs-fMRI-based models but also offers a plausible explanation for previously inconsistent neuroanatomical findings - namely, that different features emphasize different spatial and functional properties of ADHD. A multi-feature strategy may thus be essential for capturing the full complexity of ADHD's neural characteristics.

Neurobiological interpretation and clinical implications

The proposed ADHD sensitivity map offers an advanced imaging modality for results interpretation. The results revealed that the posterior cingulate cortex, parahippocampal gyrus, and precuneus were among the most predictive regions for ADHD. These areas are core components of the default mode network (DMN), which has been consistently linked to attention regulation, introspective processes, and cognitive control. Disruptions in DMN connectivity have been widely reported in ADHD and are associated with inattention and hyperactivity symptoms. Our voxel-level sensitivity map highlights the spatial heterogeneity of ADHD-related alterations. The ability to localize important features at both region and voxel scales enhances the interpretability of model predictions and provides potential targets for neuromodulation or clinical intervention.

The proposed model not only improves classification performance but also supports continuous ADHD severity assessment, which is an important step toward personalized diagnostics. Most existing approaches focus on binary diagnosis, which overlooks symptom heterogeneity. By framing the problem as both classification and regression, our model enables fine-grained assessment, potentially informing individualized treatment planning. The visualization capability embedded in our CAMEL framework provides interpretable maps of patient-level and region-level importance. This aligns with clinical needs for transparent AI tools that can support practitioner decisions.

Limitations and future work

While the above findings suggest potential for clinical translation, the model has not yet been validated on external datasets, and generalization across imaging sites, protocols, and age groups remains to be established in future work.

Although the proposed study has demonstrated excellent performance, it has its own limitations in the model settings. To mitigate overfitting, we employed dropout and early stopping during training, and applied repeated cross-validation with fold-internal parameter selection to ensure generalization. The current framework did not explicitly consider the uncertainties, especially the separation of epistemic uncertainties (model uncertainty) and aleatoric uncertainties (intrinsic uncertainties). This is critical for out-of-distribution (OOD) classification and regression tasks, such as different age groups and sites between training and testing. There are some existing studies in the machine learning community to address this issue, and they need further model development and model validation.

The current model structure is for the “static” application of ADHD diagnostics and prediction. ADHD can evolve with age (e.g., development in children). Revision and development of a new modeling structure to handle time (age)-dependent neuroimaging data will provide more insights into ADHD's neural development characteristics. One apparent difficulty in this direction is the acquisition of continuous and consistent neural imaging data for the same patient, which has not been largely explored in the open literature.

Finally, the proposed study only relied on the rs-fMRI data and did not use other neuroimaging data or clinical data. In future work, we also plan to investigate the correlation between the CAMEL-derived embedding dimensions and phenotypic measures (e.g., symptom severity or behavioral profiles) to further enhance interpretability. Future extensions may also explore feature-specific embeddings or graph-theoretical measures derived from functional connectivity networks to enhance the structural interpretability of representations. The inclusion of additional features in the proposed framework is straightforward, but the impact on the performance is unclear. Significant additional studies are needed to verify the effectiveness of multimodality data for ADHD diagnosis and prediction. Additionally, although CAMEL was selected for its theoretical advantages and prior benchmarking results, future work could explicitly compare its performance to traditional methods such as PCA or ISOMAP on ADHD-related rs-fMRI datasets to further validate this choice.
