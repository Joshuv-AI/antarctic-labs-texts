Impact of a Short-Term High-Fat Diet on Lifespan and Tumorigenesis After Early-Life Exposure to Radiation
Authors: Yi Shang, Takamitsu Morioka, Yoshiko Amasaki, Chizuru Tsuruoka, Masaaki Sunaoshi, Hiromi Yanagihara, Keiji Suzuki, Shizuko Kakinuma
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionChildren are highly susceptible to radiation’s carcinogenic effects. Lifestyle, in particular, diet-induced obesity (DIO) is closely linked to cancer risk factors. Most prior studies, however, have concentrated on the impact of long-term lifestyle factors.ObjectiveWe assessed the impact of a short-term, high-fat diet (HFD) during young or adult on lifespan shortening and tumor development after infant radiation exposure.MethodologyB6C3F1 male mice were irradiated at 1 week of age with 4 Gy of gamma rays or did not undergo irradiation (0 Gy). After weaning, they were assigned to HFD or normal-fat diet (NFD) for four weeks during young (4–8 weeks old) or adult (20–24 weeks old) stages. Body weight measurement, plasma biochemical analysis, lifespan analysis, and histopathological analysis for tumor diagnosis were carried out.ResultsA temporary gain of body weight and hyperlipidemia were observed in mice fed the HFD. Although short-term HFD intake had limited impact on lifespan shortening and tumor development, a short-term HFD at a young age significantly increased spontaneous hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) risk.ConclusionThe influence of a short-term HFD on DIO-associated cancer, such as HCC, should be specifically noted. Lifestyle normalization has potential for reducing cancer risk.Keywords: high-fat diet, diet-induced obesity, radiation, tumorigenesis, hepatocellular carcinoma

1. Introduction

Epidemiological studies of atomic-bomb survivors, patients undergoing radiotherapy, and nuclear power plant accident survivors show that exposure to ionizing radiation increases the risk of cancer development as well as the risk of lifespan shortening. Children are considered to be at a higher risk of developing cancer than adults because of the greater radiosensitivity of multiple organs at that age and longer lifespan after exposure. 1 In recent years, with the increasing use of radiation in the pediatric field, there has been great concern about second cancers after diagnostic or therapeutic radiation exposure.2,3 Among individuals with early radiation exposure, their post-irradiation lifestyle may further affect any subsequent cancer development. Given the long process of tumor development after radiation exposure, the modification of lifestyle factors that affect the radio-tumorigenesis process should be considered. Epidemiological studies indicate that long-term lifestyle factors such as smoking, alcohol consumption, and dietary habits can modify cancer risk. 4 Diet-induced obesity (DIO), in particular, is closely linked to higher cancer risk. A high-fat diet (HFD) is a well-known risk factor for several types of cancers, including breast cancer, colon cancer, prostate cancer, and hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). 5 Accurate risk assessment of the combined effects of radiation exposure and a HFD is essential for providing evidence-based health guidelines to prevent second cancers.

Epidemiological studies suggest that the insufficient information on the combined effects of radiation exposure and the type, extent, and duration of lifestyle factors may confound the estimation of cancer risk.6-8 Animal models have proven useful for better understanding the effects of radiation exposure and/or lifestyle factors.9-11 Our earlier reports showed that post-irradiation calorie restriction has a preventive effect on radiation-induced lifespan shortening and cancer (such as HCC) development in B6C3F1 mice.12,13 We hypothesized that a short-term HFD, particularly during a developmentally sensitive window, would synergize with radiation to promote certain DIO-associated tumors like HCC. In this study, we chose to use B6C3F1 mice for lifespan and tumorigenesis assessments, as both hematopoietic tumors and solid tumors can be effectively induced in this strain.12,14,15 Using this mouse model, we examined the combined effects of gamma-ray exposure and a short-term HFD on lifespan shortening and tumor development. We also measured the tissue-type–specific responses to radiation and a short-term HFD, as well as the effect of HFD timing on these responses. Understanding the effect of a short-term HFD is essential for predicting the risk of radiation-induced cancer and for proposing cancer prevention strategies after childhood radiation exposure.

2. Materials and Methods

2.1. Mice and Irradiation

Male offspring of a cross between C57BL/6NCrl females and C3H/HeNCrl males (B6C3F1) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory Japan, Inc. (Kanagawa, Japan) and were maintained under specific-pathogen free conditions on a 12-h light/dark cycle at 23 ± 2°C with 50 ± 10% humidity. Mice were housed 5/cage, received a standard laboratory diet (MBR-1; Funabashi Farm Co., Ltd., Chiba, Japan), except for HFD and normal-fat diet (NFD) administration (see below), with water ad libitum. Mice were either non-irradiated or irradiated at 8 days of age with 4 Gy gamma rays from a 137Cs source (0.48 Gy/min). The mice were weaned at 4 weeks of age. The body weight of each mouse was monitored weekly during the HFD period, every 2 weeks during the first 14 weeks, every 4 weeks for up to 1 year, and then every 8 weeks throughout its remaining lifespan. Euthanasia was performed using terminal isoflurane anesthesia. This study was conducted between October 2015 and February 2022. All experiments were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of the National Institutes for Quantum Science and Technology (Approval No. 15-1016, and 18-1003), and all efforts were taken to minimize suffering. The welfare of animals at the institution was reviewed by an external program of the Japanese Association of Laboratory Animal Facilities of National University Corporations and the Japanese Association of Laboratory Animal Facilities of Public and Private Universities.

2.2. Diet

Groupings and treatments of mice are shown in Supplementary Figure 1. Briefly, all mice were randomly divided into either an HFD or an NFD group at weaning (4 weeks of age). For the HFD groups, mice were fed the D12492 rodent diet (Research Diets, Inc. New Brunswick, NJ, USA), which contains 60% fat by energy, from 4 weeks of age (4W) or 20 weeks of age (20W) for 4 weeks. For the NFD groups, mice were fed the D12450J rodent diet (Research Diets), which contains 10% fat by energy over the same time periods (Supplementary Table 1). All mice were fed standard lab diet MBR-1 (Funabashi Farm, Japan) before and after the HFD or NFD periods.

2.3. Pathology

Mice were inspected daily by experienced researchers. When they were judged to reach one of the pre-determined humane endpoints (e.g., abnormal posture, abnormal respiratory movement, anemia, or a large tumor burden) they were euthanized by exsanguination under isoflurane anesthesia (∼4% in air) within 2 hours and necropsied. A small number of mice that died unexpectedly were autopsied immediately. For lifespan and tumorigenesis analysis, 60–64 mice in each group were used. Sample size was initially determined based on our previous study. 12 An a priori power calculation was performed for both survival analysis and tumor incidence. Although the initial sample size was estimated to be 20–30 animals per group based on survival and incidence endpoints, it was increased to 60–64 animals per group to account for variability, censoring, and analyses of multiple tumor types, thereby ensuring robust and reproducible results. For liver histological and plasma biochemical analyses, 3 mice from each experimental group were necropsied at the time shown in Supplementary Figure 1. All organs were removed, rinsed with ice-cold phosphate-buffered saline, and rapidly fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin overnight. They were then embedded in paraffin and sectioned transversely (3–4 µm thick). Paraffin sections underwent routine hematoxylin and eosin staining. Pathological diagnoses were performed by two pathologists (YS and TM) who were blinded to the mouse treatment, and samples were managed using randomly assigned identification numbers. In cases of discordant diagnoses, consensus re-evaluation meetings were conducted among pathologists until agreement was reached.

2.4. Biochemical Analysis of Plasma

Heparinized whole blood collected at various timepoints (Supplementary Figure 1) from euthanized mice at necropsy was centrifuged at 1000 × g for 10 min at room temperature. Plasma samples were stored at –80°C until analysis. Mice were not fasted before necropsy to accurately reflect their metabolic condition under the respective dietary interventions. Aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine transaminase (ALT), glucose (Glu), total cholesterol (T-CHO) (L-type Wako CHO-M kit; Wako Pure Chemical Industries, Ltd., Osaka, Japan), and triglyceride (TG) (L-type Wako Triglyceride M kit; Wako) levels were measured by Nagahama Life Science Laboratory (Shiga, Japan). All specimens were stored as a part of the Japan-Storehouse of Animal Radiobiology Experiments (J-SHARE) archive. 16

2.5. Statistical Analysis

Weight measurements were analyzed with the Student’s t-test. Tumor incidence in the HFD and NFD groups was analyzed by Fisher’s exact test. Overall survival and survival without specific tumor types were analyzed by Kaplan–Meier survival curves, and the statistical significance of differences in survival among groups and the hazard ratios (HRs) were evaluated with the log-rank test. Because no cases of early-occurring lymphoma were observed in the 0 Gy mice from any of the diet groups, the calculation of HR for irradiation was not applicable for this tumor type. Biochemical analysis data, relative liver weight, and relative epididymal fat weight were analyzed with two-way analysis of variance (ANOVA). P < 0.05 was considered significant. Prism (Version 10.4.2, GraphPad Software Inc. San Diego, CA, USA) was used to perform statistical calculations and to draw graphs.

3. Results

3.1. Gamma-Ray Exposure Induces Irreversible Body Weight Loss, Whereas a Short-Term HFD Induces Temporary Body Weight Gain

The mean body weight for each experimental group is shown in Figure 1. The suppressive effect of early-life gamma-ray exposure on body weight gain was significant. Mice in the 4-Gy groups had lower body weight at weaning, and this difference persisted throughout their lifespan (P < 0.0001). In contrast, the differences in body weight between the HFD and NFD groups were only significant during the HFD feeding period. The body weight gain caused by the HFD recovered to its associated NFD level at 21 days (0 Gy_4W_HFD vs 0 Gy_4W_NFD), 14 days (4 Gy_4W_HFD vs 4 Gy_4W_NFD), 28 days (0 Gy_20W_HFD vs 0 Gy_20W_NFD), and 140 days (4 Gy_20W_HFD vs 4 Gy_20W_NFD) after the end of the HFD period. A difference in body weight between the HFD and NFD mice from each radiation treatment group was not observed during the rest of the lifespan.

3.2. Gamma-Ray Exposure Shortens Lifespan, Whereas the Impact of a Short-Term HFD on Lifespan Shortening is Not Observed

Changes in overall survival rates are shown in Figure 2A. Mean survival time for each group and the percentage of lifespan shortening by radiation or HFD are given in Table 1. The mean lifespan of 0 Gy_4W_NFD and 0 Gy_4W_HFD was 886.5 ± 24.4 days and 861.4 ± 21.7 days. Early-life gamma-ray exposure resulted in statistically significant lifespan shortening, reducing mean overall lifespan by 362.4 days (40.9%) in the comparison of 4 Gy_4W_NFD vs 0 Gy_4W_NFD and by 371.2 days (43.1%) in the comparison of 4 Gy_4W_HFD vs 0 Gy_4W_HFD. In contrast, no significant difference was found in the comparison of 0 Gy_4W_HFD vs 0 Gy_4W_NFD nor of 4 Gy_4W_HFD vs 4 Gy_4W_NFD, suggesting a weak impact of a short-term HFD on lifespan shortening. Similar results were also observed in groups given a NFD or HFD from 20W of age (Table 1). Changes in tumor-free survival rates are shown in Figure 2B. Mean tumor-free lifespan, including the days and percentage of lifespan shortening, is shown in Table 1. As mentioned below, most of the mice died as a result of tumor load in this model; the strong effect of gamma-ray exposure and the weak impact of a short-term HFD on tumor-free lifespan shortening showed the same trend as overall lifespan shortening.

3.3. Gamma-Ray Exposure Promotes Tumor Development, Whereas the Effect of a Short-Term HFD on Cancer Prevalence is Tissue Dependent

In this study, lymphomas, liver tumors, lung tumors, and hemangiosarcoma were frequently detected. Early-occurring lymphomas, which were the main cause of early death within 1 year of age, were observed only in mice from the irradiated groups. (Table 2). The incidences were 16.7–26.7%, significantly higher than mice in the 0-Gy groups with the same diet, none of which developed early-occurring lymphoma. Table 3 and Figure 2C show the early-occurring lymphoma–free lifespan and the associated HRs. As there were no cases of early-occurring lymphoma in the 0-Gy groups, HR could not be calculated. There is no doubt, however, that gamma-ray exposure significantly increased the risk of early-occurring lymphomas in these mice. In the comparisons between the NFD and HFD groups, there were no significant differences (HR = 1.42 in 4 Gy_4W_HFD vs 4 Gy_4W_NFD, P = 0.86; HR = 0.80 in 4 Gy_20W_HFD vs 4 Gy_20W_NFD, P = 0.61). After 1 year of age, all groups developed late-occurring lymphomas (Figure 2D). The incidence of total lymphomas (both early and late occurring) ranged from 17.2% to 33.3%, irrespective of fat intake or irradiation (Table 2). However, the risk assessment showed that the HRs for gamma-ray exposure of 4 Gy were significantly higher irrespective of diet or the age at HFD intake compared with the 0-Gy groups (Table 3 and Figure 2E; HRs = 2.43–4.77, P ≤ 0.0058 in 4-Gy groups vs 0-Gy groups). In contrast, a short-term HFD had no obvious impact on lymphoma risks in either the 0-Gy or 4-Gy groups.

In this mouse model, liver tumors were the most frequently detected tumor type. Hepatocellular adenoma (HCA) and a few hepatoblastomas were identified, with hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC) being the predominant subtype. Despite the diet and age at intake, the incidence of HCA was lower in irradiated groups relative to their corresponding 0-Gy groups, albeit at a level that did not reach statistical significance (Table 2), whereas the lifespan shortening caused by gamma-ray exposure was associated with an increased HCA risk (Table 3 and Figure 2F; HRs = 2.53, 3.11, and 2.81 in the 4W_NFD, 4W_HFD, and 20W_NFD groups, respectively; all P ≤ 0.0018), except for the 20W_HFD groups (HR = 2.06, P = 0.12). Exposure to 4 Gy of gamma-rays increased the incidence of HCC from 32.3% to 58.3% (P = 0.0061) in the 4W_NFD groups and from 30.0% to 48.3% (P = 0.061) in the 20W_NFD groups (Table 2). Lifespan analyses showed significant HCC-related lifespan shortening in gamma-ray–irradiated NFD groups (Table 3 and Figure 2G; HR = 8.51 in 4 Gy_4W_NFD vs 0 Gy_4W_NFD, P < 0.0001; HR = 6.94 in 4 Gy_20W_NFD vs 0 Gy_20W_NFD, P < 0.0001). In the groups fed HFD at 4W, although the incidence of HCC was similar between irradiated and non-irradiated mice (50.0% and 48.4%, respectively, without statistical significancy), lifespan was significantly shortened (Table 3 and Figure 2G; HR = 4.80, P < 0.0001). Both the increase in HCC incidence (Table 2, 38.3% to 63.3%, P = 0.01) and the increase in lifespan shortening were significant in the 4 Gy_20W_HFD vs 0 Gy_20W_HFD group (Table 3 and Figure 2G; HR = 6.47, P < 0.0001). These data indicate that early-life gamma-ray exposure is a certain risk factor for HCC development, consistent with an epidemiological study and our previous report.12,17

We then focused on the effect of a short-term HFD intake on these liver tumors. Unlike all diet-related comparisons of HCA or HCC occurrence in 4-Gy groups and 0 Gy_20W groups, short-term HFD intake exerted a clear and statistically significant effect on HCC development in the 0 Gy_4W groups. Mice from the 0 Gy_4W_HFD group showed a significantly increased risk of HCC compared with the 0 Gy_4W_NFD group (Table 3 and Figure 2G; HR = 1.79, P = 0.037). This result indicates that in the absence of radiation exposure, short-term HFD intake during early life can substantially elevate HCC risk, emphasizing the critical role of early-life dietary habits in liver carcinogenesis. The HCC pathological grade was similar across all groups (Supplementary Figure 2A).

Lung tumors were also frequently detected in this mouse model. Both AD and AC were observed in all groups (Table 2). Gamma-ray exposure of 4 Gy significantly increased the risk of lifespan shortening due to AD and AC and of all lung tumors among all groups except for the risk of AD in the comparison of the 4 Gy_4W_HFD vs 0 Gy_4W_HFD group (Table 3 and Figure 2I–K). As for lymphomas, a short-term HFD did not substantially affect lung tumor development (either AD or AC). HRs for the HFD groups vs NFD groups were without statistical significance (Table 3).

We also observed hemangiosarcomas in all groups (Table 2). A significant shortening effect of radiation on hemangiosarcoma-free lifespan was observed in the 4W groups (Table 3 and Figure 2L; HR = 4.57 for the NFD groups and HR = 2.61 for the HFD groups). In the 20W groups, however, the effect of radiation on lifespan shortening relative to this tumor type was not significant (Table 3). There was no significant difference between NFD and HFD intake, irrespective of gamma-ray exposure or the age at HFD intake.

In this mouse model, other tumors developed, such as Harderian adenocarcinoma, pituitary adenocarcinoma, histiocytic sarcoma, rhabdomyosarcoma, small intestine adenocarcinoma, pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma, thyroid adenocarcinoma, cholangiocarcinoma, renal cell carcinoma, and stomach signet-ring cell carcinoma, with an individual incidence of <5%. Although it was difficult to compare the incidence of each tumor type across groups, the onset of these other tumors when viewed collectively was clearly earlier in the 4-Gy groups (Table 2). Exposure to 4 Gy gamma-rays significantly increased the risk of lifespan shortening with respect to these tumors, with HRs ranging from 2.96 to 7.69 (Table 3 and Figure 2M). In contrast, short-term HFD intake starting from 4 weeks or 20 weeks of age did not significantly affect the development of these tumors.

Finally, we analyzed the contributions of gamma-ray exposure and a short-term HFD to tumor type multiplicity. The distribution of the number of tumor types in mice associated with each group did not change substantially (Supplementary Figure 2B), but the lifespan of mice bearing multiple primary tumors (i.e., more than two primary tumors) was significantly shortened by gamma-ray exposure (Table 3 and Figure 2N), with HRs ranging from 4.10 to 5.52 (P < 0.0001) but not by a short-term HFD administered at either 4 weeks or 20 weeks of age.

Together, these results indicate that early-life gamma-ray exposure significantly increased the risk of almost all tumor types in this mouse model. Although the impact of a short-term HFD on most tumors was weak, the effect of a short-term HFD at a young age on HCC risk cannot be ignored.

3.4. Gamma-Ray Exposure Promotes Lipid Accumulation in Hepatocytes, Although the Effect of a Short-Term HFD is Reversible

To elucidate whether a short-term HFD induces fat accumulation, metabolic disorders, liver function abnormalities, and/or pathological changes, we measured the weight of liver and epididymal fat (Supplementary Figure 3), carried out a histological analysis of liver tissue (Figure 3), and quantified plasma ALT, AST, Glu, TG, and T-CHO (Supplementary Figure 4) from the time point at which the HFD or NFD was first administered to 500 days of age.

Histological analysis of liver tissue from the eight experimental groups of mice. (A, B) Representative histological images of liver sections as a function of time before, during, and after HFD and NFD administration for the (A) 4W and (B) 20W mice. Staining was carried out with hematoxylin and eosin. Images in the lower group for each panel show a larger region relative to the corresponding image (black square) indicated by the same lowercase letter in the upper group. Arrows denote fat droplets in hepatocytes. Notable lipid accumulation is indicated by red rectangles

The liver weight in the 4W_HFD groups was lower than that in the NFD groups, whereas the weight of epididymal fat of mice in the 4W_HFD groups was higher than that in the 4W_NFD groups, indicating that a short-term HFD intake from a young age affects total body fat accumulation rather than lipid accumulation in hepatocytes (Supplementary Figure 3A to 3D, left panel). Figure 3A shows histological changes in liver tissues over time before and after HFD intake, starting from 4 weeks of age. In the 0 Gy_4W_NFD and 0 Gy_4W_HFD groups, small lipid droplets were present in the tissue sections starting at 400 days of age. No notable lipid droplets were observed during or immediately after 4 weeks of HFD treatment (Figure 3A). In contrast, obvious lipid accumulation began to accelerate from 200 days of age in the 4 Gy_4W_NFD and 4 Gy_4W_HFD groups, although notable lipid droplets were also observed during 4 weeks of HFD treatment in the 4 Gy_4W_HFD group (Figure 3B) but disappeared immediately by 63 days of age (Figure 3C). These results were consistent with our previous report. 13

Plasma levels of ALT, AST, Glu, and TG did not change significantly for all but 0 Gy_4W_NFD vs 4 Gy_4W_NFD at 77 days of age in AST (Supplementary Figure 4A to 4D, left panel). T-CHO levels in the 0 Gy_4W_HFD mice at 56 days, the last time point of the HFD treatment, were higher than those of the 0 Gy_4W_NFD group, but this difference was immediately reversed at 63 days and showed no subsequent difference (Supplementary Figure 4E, left panel). Collectively, gamma-ray irradiation promoted lipid accumulation in hepatocytes, whereas a short-term HFD in young mice promoted lipid accumulation in fat tissue rather than in the liver.

Among the 20W groups, there were no significant differences in liver weight or epididymal fat weight between the corresponding NFD and HFD groups (Supplementary Figure 3A to 3D, right panel), indicating that the impact of a short-term HFD starting at 20 weeks of age on fat distribution was limited. Figure 3B shows histological images of liver tissues before, during, and after HFD intake starting at 20 weeks of age. In the 0 Gy_20W_NFD group, a small amount of lipid droplets was observed from 400 days of age. A short-term HFD starting from 20 weeks of age (140–168 days) caused temporary fat accumulation in liver tissue in the 0-Gy group, which had returned to pre-HFD levels from 200 days of age (Figure 3D). In this group of mice, slight fat accumulation was present again from 400 days of age. In the irradiated groups, even among mice fed the NFD, fat accumulation was observed, starting by 200 days of age in the 4 Gy_20W_NFD group (Figure 3E) and by 168 days of age in the 4 Gy_20W_HFD group (Figure 3F). In the latter group, this obvious fat accumulation continued until 500 days of age, the end of the follow-up period.

Plasma levels of ALT and AST increased during HFD intake without statistical significance but returned to typical levels by 200 days of age and were not significantly different thereafter with exception between 0 Gy_20W_NFD vs 0 Gy_20W_HFD at 300 days of age in ALT (Supplementary Figures 4A and 4B, right panel). Plasma levels of Glu, and TG did not change significantly for all but two comparisons among the 20W groups temporarily. Plasma T-CHO levels of the 4W_HFD groups increased significantly at the end of HFD intake (56 days of age) but returned to pre-HFD levels at 77 days of age (Supplementary Figure 4E, left panel). Plasma T-CHO levels of the 0 Gy_20W_HFD group were significantly higher at 168 days than those in the 0 Gy_20W_NFD group, and this difference lasted until 200 days of age (Supplementary Figure 4E, right panel). Taken together, these results indicate that gamma-ray exposure promoted lipid accumulation in hepatocytes, regardless of a short-term HFD treatment from 20W. Any effects of a short-term HFD at 20 weeks of age on liver histology and function were not obvious.

4. Discussion

Children are highly susceptible to radiation exposure, which can cause tumor development and lifespan shortening. Using B6C3F1 male mice, a wild-type strain that is frequently used for genotoxic assessment, including of chemical carcinogens and radiation,14,18 we examined the long-term impact of early-life gamma-ray exposure and a short-term HFD on lifespan and tumorigenesis. We showed that neonatal irradiation markedly shortened lifespan and increased the risk of lymphoma, HCC, lung tumors, hemangiosarcoma, and other tumor types in these mice. The pronounced reduction in survival and elevated cancer risk after neonatal irradiation are consistent with previous reports.12-15 Compared with the impact of 4 Gy gamma-ray irradiation, a 4-week HFD starting at either 4 or 20 weeks of age had limited effect on these outcomes. Lifespan did not differ between the HFD and NFD groups, regardless of irradiation or the age at which the HFD was started. Similarly, the risks of, lymphoma, lung tumor, hemangiosarcoma, and other tumor types remained unchanged, suggesting that short-term metabolic stress during early or young adulthood may not be sufficient to modify the long-term progression of these tumor types. We also confirmed the interactions between radiation and HFD by COX proportional hazards model for overall lifespan and major tumor types (data not shown). The lack of a significant interaction suggests that HFD does not modify the effect of radiation on these outcomes.

B-cell and T-cell lymphomas are frequently observed in this strain.19,20 Mouse lymphoma serves as a model of human B-cell lymphoma or T-cell acute lymphoblastic leukemia. Genetic mutations, immune dysregulation, infectious pathogens such as Epstein-Barr virus, and exposure to environmental factors (e.g., benzene, radiation) all contribute to the etiology of lymphoma. 21 Our findings also indicated that 4 Gy of gamma-ray radiation significantly increased the risk of all lymphomas. Moreover, early-occurring lymphomas developed only in the irradiated groups. To date, no definitive causal link has been found between HFD intake and lymphomagenesis. Our findings are consistent with this, as we observed no significant effect of a short-term HFD on lymphoma induction.

Lung tumors are another frequently detected tumor type in this model. In humans, whereas tobacco smoking remains the predominant risk factor, other environmental exposures—including ionizing radiation—also contribute to lung carcinogenesis. Epidemiological studies have provided evidence that atomic-bomb survivors exhibit a significantly elevated risk of lung cancer, especially for individuals exposed to higher doses of ionizing radiation.22,23 Again a link between HFDs and lung cancer has not been well established. Some studies suggest that diets high in saturated fats may be associated with increased risk, particularly among non-smokers, but findings have been inconsistent.24,25 In this study, a short-term HFD did not show any significant contribution to lung tumorigenesis. Further investigation is warranted, however, to determine whether a definitive link exists.

In this mouse model, HCC is the only tumor type that has been associated with both DIO and radiation exposure. In humans, excessive calorie intake causes obesity and the manifestation of a systemic inflammatory state, which presents in the liver as metabolic dysfunction associated steatohepatitis (MASH). Some patients exhibit progression from MASH through cirrhosis to HCC. 26 Ionizing radiation is also a known risk factor for HCC. The Life Span Study of Japanese atomic-bomb survivors has consistently reported a significantly increased risk of liver cancer associated with radiation exposure, especially in cases where the age at exposure was <10 years. 17 Earlier, we demonstrated that early-life exposure to radiation upregulates fatty-acid uptake and lipid accumulation in the liver, leading to MASH and increasing the risk for HCC. 13 Future studies should investigate how a HFD modifies radiation-induced liver carcinogenesis and should take into effect the timing, duration, and extent of the HFD, especially with respect to MASH-derived HCC development. Our present study demonstrated that the carcinogenic effect of early-life irradiation (HR ∼8.51) was substantially greater than that of short-term HFD intake (HR = 1.79). This marked difference in effect size suggests that the strong HCC-promoting pressure induced by radiation may mask or override the more subtle effects of dietary factors. This may explain the lack of a detectable HFD effect in irradiated groups. The interaction between radiation and HFD calculated by Cox proportional hazards model showed a trend toward significance but did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.07). Interestingly, the only statistically significant diet-related increase in HCC risk was observed in non-irradiated mice, in which those fed a HFD immediately after weaning (0 Gy_4W_HFD) exhibited higher risk of HCC than did those fed a NFD (0 Gy_4W_NFD). Although our pathological and biochemical follow-up of liver tissue response after irradiation and HFD feeding did not reveal persistent metabolic abnormalities in these mice, transient increases in plasma T-CHO levels and epididymal fat accumulation were observed during the HFD period. These findings in conjunction with an increase in HCC risk suggest that in the absence of strong tumor-promoting stress from radiation, this early-life temporary metabolic disturbance may trigger long-lasting alterations in hepatic microenvironment, such as altered lipid signaling, low-grade inflammatory response, metabolic reprogramming and gut microbiota composition, which may predispose hepatocytes to spontaneous carcinogenesis later in life. 27 This age-dependent effect implies that the developmental window immediately after weaning is particularly vulnerable to dietary perturbations. The mechanism that consistent with the increased HCC risk observed despite the apparent reversibility of metabolic changes represents a testable hypothesis for future studies.

We note that the statistical difference in HCC risk between 0 Gy_4W_HFD and 0 Gy_4W_NFD mice was significant, but HFD exposure during adulthood or post-radiation did not produce similar effects. Although many previous studies have reported that chronic or prolonged HFD intake promotes inflammation, oxidative stress, and tumor development,26,28 the absence of significant impacts under these conditions indicates that as well as physiological context that mentioned above, timing and duration are also critical determinants of dietary impact on tumorigenesis. It is also noteworthy that subsequent dietary normalization successfully counteracted early metabolic alterations induced by short-term HFD feeding. This finding highlights the potential importance of lifestyle intervention in mitigating long-term cancer risk, even after early-life metabolic stress.

5. Conclusion

Exposure to gamma-ray radiation in early life is an evident risk factor for lifespan shortening and tumor development. Although a short-term (4-week) HFD had only a modest impact on overall lifespan and tumorigenesis, its effects on DIO–associated cancers, such as HCC, should not be overlooked. These findings underscore the importance of lifestyle normalization as a key intervention strategy. This approach may be relevant not only for the general public but also for at-risk populations, e.g. childhood cancer survivors who received radiotherapy. In such populations, modification of post-exposure lifestyle factors may offer a practical means to reduce long-term cancer risk following radiation exposure. Further studies based on this risk assessment investigation will provide potential intervention avenues to mitigate cancer risk.
