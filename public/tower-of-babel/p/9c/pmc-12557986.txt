C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio as a predictor of 28-day mortality in critically ill pediatric patients: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Jie Chang, Xiaojuan Zhang, Wei Wang, Liping Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectiveThis large-scale real-world study aimed to evaluate whether the C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR), an indicator of systemic inflammation and nutritional status, predicts 28-day mortality in critically ill pediatric patients.DesignRetrospective cohort study.MethodsWe analyzed data from 8,000 children admitted to a tertiary pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) in China between 2010 and 2019. CAR was calculated using initial CRP and albumin levels upon admission. Patients were stratified into quartiles based on CAR values. The primary outcome was 28-day all-cause mortality; in-hospital mortality was a secondary outcome. Multivariable Cox regression and Kaplan–Meier survival analyses were performed, along with subgroup analyses to validate robustness.ResultsHigher CAR levels were significantly associated with increased 28-day mortality. Patients in the highest CAR quartile had a hazard ratio of 1.51 (95% CI: 1.07–2.13; p = 0.018) compared with the lowest quartile. The fully adjusted model including CAR achieved an AUC of 0.820 (95% CI: 0.798–0.843), indicating good discriminatory ability. Kaplan-Meier curves confirmed reduced survival in the high CAR group (p = 0.0042). The association remained consistent across all subgroups.ConclusionCAR is significantly associated with short-term mortality in critically ill children. As a low-cost, easily accessible biomarker, CAR may serve as a valuable early risk stratification tool in real-world pediatric intensive care settings.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12887-025-06261-9.

Introduction

The pediatric intensive care unit (PICU) plays a vital role in the treatment of children facing life-threatening illnesses. However, in settings with limited medical resources, mortality among these patients remains concerningly high despite progress in critical care techniques and technologies [1]. Early identification of children at higher risk of death is crucial for guiding timely interventions and optimizing healthcare resource allocation [2]. Several prognostic tools—such as the Pediatric Risk of Mortality (PRISM) and the Pediatric Index of Mortality (PIM)—have been commonly applied to estimate outcomes. Nevertheless, these models may not fully capture ongoing physiological dynamics or incorporate essential biological markers that reflect inflammatory activity and nutritional status.

C-reactive protein (CRP) is a classic acute-phase reactant that increases rapidly in response to cytokine-driven inflammation due to infection, trauma, ischemia, or other systemic insults [3, 4]. Albumin (ALB), on the other hand, is widely recognized as a marker of nutritional status and hepatic protein synthesis capacity [5, 6]. The C-reactive protein-to-albumin ratio (CAR) has recently emerged as a promising composite biomarker, integrating the body’s inflammatory burden with nutritional reserve. Elevated CAR values have shown strong correlations with worse outcomes in various diseases, including sepsis, acute kidney injury, malignancy, and severe infections such as acute pancreatitis [6–9]. Among critically ill populations, a higher CAR has been linked to increased mortality, suggesting its potential role in early risk stratification [8].

Although many studies have evaluated CAR in adult or mixed cohorts, large-scale research focusing on its predictive value in critically ill pediatric patients—particularly regarding 28-day mortality—remains scarce. For example, Chaurasiya et al. identified fluid overload as a significant predictor of poor outcomes in critically ill children aged 1 month to 18 years, while the CAR did not show a statistically significant association with mortality [10]. In contrast, Ghauri et al. found that dynamic changes in the CAR during the first three days of illness were strong predictors of surgical intervention and mortality in neonates with necrotizing enterocolitis [11]. However, these studies either focused on specific disease entities or involved relatively small sample sizes. Our study, based on a large real-world cohort of critically ill neonates and children in China, aims to further evaluate the predictive value of CAR for 28-day mortality and explore its potential as a clinically feasible and cost-effective early warning marker.

Materials and methods

Data source

This study utilized clinical data from the PICU database, which includes anonymized medical records of critically ill children admitted to the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University School of Medicine, between 2010 and 2019 [12]. The database contains information on 13,944 ICU stays involving 12,881 unique pediatric patients, providing a comprehensive range of variables such as demographic characteristics, vital signs, laboratory findings, and clinical outcomes. The use of this database was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the Children’s Hospital, Zhejiang University (Approval No. 2019_IRB_052). All data were de-identified in accordance with HIPAA regulations, and the requirement for individual informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of the study.

Study design and participants

This study followed a retrospective cohort design, including all critically ill pediatric patients admitted to the PICU, defined as all PICU admissions regardless of specific diagnosis. Individuals aged ≤ 28 days or > 18 years, as well as those with missing data for CRP or ALB, were excluded. For individuals admitted more than once, only the initial PICU admission was analyzed. Data extraction and outcome definition

Survival data were retrieved from the “Patients” table in the PIC database, while information on hospital stays and death times was sourced from the “Admissions” table. Laboratory results were obtained from the “Lab Events” table, focusing on the initial measurements recorded upon ICU admission. CRP, albumin, and all other covariates were extracted from laboratory tests performed within the first 24 h after PICU admission. For patients with multiple CRP or albumin results during this period, the first measurement obtained after PICU admission was used for analysis. If CRP and albumin were not measured at exactly the same time, we paired the values obtained closest in time within the 24-hour window. The CAR was calculated using the formula: CAR = (CRP/ALB) × 10.

The main outcome was 28-day mortality, which referred to any death occurring within 28 days after admission to the PICU, regardless of discharge status. Secondary outcomes included in-hospital mortality, defined as death at any time throughout the hospital stay.

Statistical analysis

Baseline characteristics were compared across CAR quartiles (< 0.29, 0.29–1.38, 1.38–8.83, ≥ 8.83). Categorical variables were shown as proportions and analyzed through the chi-square test. For continuous variables with normal distribution, results were presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD), and group comparisons were carried out using independent samples t-tests. For non-normally distributed data, results were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR), and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test was applied.

Univariate and multivariate Cox proportional hazards regression models were used to investigate the relationship between CAR and 28-day mortality. Logistic regression was applied to explore its association with in-hospital mortality. Variable selection was based on univariate significance (p < 0.05), clinical relevance, or influence on the model outcome greater than 10%. Multivariable models were constructed as follows: Model I was unadjusted; Model II included adjustments for age and sex; Model III additionally accounted for temperature, respiratory rate, heart rate, and bacteremia;

Model IV incorporated further laboratory variables such as white blood cell count (WBC), platelet count (PLT), potassium, phosphate, magnesium, pH, partial pressure of oxygen (PO₂), lactate, creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB), glucose, prothrombin time (PT), and thromboplastin time (PTT). To assess trends, CAR quartiles were treated as continuous variables by using the median value for each quartile. Subgroup and interaction analyses were stratified by age, sex, and presence of bacteremia. Kaplan–Meier survival curves were generated, and group differences were analyzed using the log-rank test.

Missing values for all variables were less than 20%. To handle missing data, a multivariate imputation strategy was performed. This method involved an iterative imputation approach using the Bayesian Ridge estimator at each round of the imputation cycle [13].

To assess the robustness of our findings, we performed sensitivity analyses. First, we additionally adjusted for liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, and malnutrition in the multivariable Cox regression models, as these conditions may affect baseline albumin levels and confound the association between CAR and mortality. Second, we repeated the analyses after excluding patients with these conditions. Third, we conducted a complete-case analysis by repeating the fully adjusted models after excluding all patients with missing covariate data to confirm the consistency of the results and to evaluate the potential impact of missingness, despite the use of multiple imputation when missing data were < 20%. Additionally, we conducted a subgroup analysis stratified by albumin infusion status to assess potential confounding from fluid resuscitation on baseline albumin levels. Finally, to account for potential heterogeneity across patient populations, we performed additional multivariable Cox regression analyses with further adjustment for ICU categories (general ICU, SICU, CICU) and CK-MB levels, which reflect case-mix differences and cardiac injury. In addition, to evaluate the discriminatory ability of the model, we calculated Harrell’s C-index for the fully adjusted Cox regression model including CAR.

Statistical analyses were conducted using R software (version 4.4.1; The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria, http://www.R-project.org) and Free Statistics software version 2.0 (Beijing FreeClinical Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). A two-sided p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

The selection of participants

Figure 1 shows the screening process for 13,941 cases retrieved from the PIC database. Among them, 1,060 entries were excluded due to repeated admissions. After this step, 12,881 unique individuals remained eligible. Of these, 1,019 lacked CRP data or had a value of zero, and 1,293 were excluded due to missing ALB records. Additionally, 2,569 children aged 28 days or younger were also excluded, resulting a final study cohort of 8,000 cases.

Study participants and baseline characteristics

A total of 8,000 critically ill children were included and stratified into four groups based on CAR quartiles: Q1 (CAR < 0.29), Q2 (0.29 ≤ CAR < 1.38), Q3 (1.38 ≤ CAR < 8.83), and Q4 (CAR ≥ 8.83). Significant differences among groups were observed in age, ICU and hospital stay duration, as well as in-hospital and 28-day mortality (P < 0.001). The median age was 18.0 months, with males accounting for 55.7% of the population. With increasing CAR levels, ICU and hospital stays lengthened, with Q4 patients experiencing the longest durations (P < 0.001). In-hospital and 28-day mortality also showed upward trends, reaching 6.7% and 5.8% respectively in Q4. Bacteremia incidence rose in parallel, peaking at 16.2% in the highest CAR group. In laboratory findings, Q4 participants had significantly lower PLT and albumin levels (P < 0.001), while CRP, CK-MB, and glucose were markedly higher (P < 0.001). Association between CAR and mortality in various models

Both univariable and multivariable Cox regression analyses (see Supplementary Table S1 and Table 2) demonstrated a significant correlation between CAR and 28-day mortality. As shown in Table 2, univariable analysis indicated that elevated CAR levels were significantly linked to increased risk of 28-day mortality (HR = 1.006, 95% CI: 1.002–1.01, P = 0.003). After adjusting for all covariates, this association remained significant in the multivariable model (HR = 1.01, 95% CI: 1.002–1.01, P = 0.007). Mortality risk showed an upward trend with increasing CAR values compared with the reference group (Q1 < 0.29). In Model IV, the hazard ratios for Q2, Q3, and Q4 groups were 1.17 (95% CI: 0.84–1.64, P = 0.351), 1.23 (95% CI: 0.88–1.71, P = 0.221), and 1.51 (95% CI: 1.07–2.13, P = 0.018), respectively. Sensitivity analysis

The results remained consistent after additional adjustment for liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, and malnutrition, conditions that may influence albumin levels and confound the association between CAR and mortality (Supplemental Table S3). Similarly, excluding patients with these conditions did not materially change the associations between CAR quartiles and 28-day mortality (Supplemental Table S4). To evaluate the potential impact of missing covariate data, we conducted a complete-case analysis by repeating the fully adjusted models after excluding all patients with missing covariate data, which yielded results consistent with the primary analysis (Supplemental Table S5). The C-index for the fully adjusted Cox regression model including CAR was 0.816 (Supplementary Table S6), indicating good model discrimination. We further performed a subgroup analysis stratified by albumin infusion status (Supplementary Table S7). The results were consistent across subgroups, and no significant interaction between CAR and albumin infusion status was observed (P for interaction = 0.28). Finally, to address heterogeneity across patient populations, we performed additional multivariable Cox regression analyses further adjusting for ICU categories (general ICU, SICU, CICU) and CK-MB levels, which account for case-mix differences and cardiac injury, respectively. The associations remained robust, with children in the highest CAR quartile (Q4) consistently exhibiting a significantly elevated risk of 28-day mortality compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (HRs 1.51–2.04, all P < 0.05; Supplementary Table S8).

Discussion

Our findings demonstrated a significant positive association between CAR and 28-day mortality in pediatric intensive care patients. When analyzing CAR as a continuous variable, the unadjusted model showed that higher CAR levels were linked to an elevated mortality risk (HR: 1.006, 95% CI: 1.002–1.01, P < 0.05). After adjusting for all potential confounders, this association remained consistent, with an adjusted HR of 1.01 (95% CI: 1.002–1.01, P = 0.007). In categorical analysis, the crude model also revealed a notable association between CAR and 28-day mortality, with the Q4 group having a 1.59-fold greater risk compared to the Q1 group. After multivariable adjustment, the Q4 group still had a 1.51-fold higher all-cause mortality risk compared with the Q1 group, underscoring the prognostic value of elevated CAR levels.

CRP is a hepatic acute-phase protein that rises in response to cytokine stimulation following trauma, ischemia, and inflammation [14]. It has been widely recognized as a prognostic biomarker in various diseases, including sepsis, pneumonia, cancer, heart failure, and postoperative complications [15–19]. However, its predictive ability for ICU readmission and mortality remains controversial [20]. Additionally, its prognostic value in sepsis is debated, as some studies suggest that CRP alone may not be a sufficiently precise predictor of survival outcomes in septic patients [21, 22].

ALB is a liver-synthesized protein widely used as an indicator of nutritional status. Recent studies have also highlighted its emerging role in innate immunity, where it functions as a host defense agent during infections [23, 24]. In critically ill patients, ALB is recognized as an important prognostic biomarker, with lower levels closely associated with increased 30-day all-cause mortality in acutely hospitalized patients [25]. Notably, a decline in ALB has been identified as an independent predictor of mortality in septic patients [26–28]. However, ALB as a standalone prognostic marker has limitations, as its levels can be influenced by various pre-existing conditions or systemic diseases, such as liver disease, kidney dysfunction, and malnutrition [23, 29]. Thus, relying solely on CRP or albumin as a biomarker remains challenging [30, 31].

CAR is an emerging biomarker that integrates inflammation and nutritional status, providing a more comprehensive assessment of disease severity. From a biological perspective, elevated CRP levels in the CAR reflect systemic inflammation and cytokine release triggered by infection, tissue injury, or other stressors, which can lead to endothelial dysfunction, capillary leak, and multi-organ damage. Conversely, reduced albumin levels often indicate poor nutritional status, increased vascular permeability, and hepatic synthetic dysfunction during acute illness. The combination of these two parameters in CAR therefore captures both the intensity of the inflammatory response and the degree of nutritional and metabolic compromise, which together contribute to worse clinical outcomes. It offers greater predictive value than CRP or albumin alone [32, 33]. Previous studies have confirmed the prognostic value of CAR in neonatal sepsis [34], Kawasaki disease [35], COVID-19 [36], and complications in the ICU [37]. Notably, CAR has shown superior accuracy in predicting mortality compared with CRP or albumin alone. For example, studies have shown that CAR at ICU admission is a significant independent predictor of mortality in patients with severe sepsis, septic shock, and other life-threatening conditions [38, 39].

Ranzani and colleagues demonstrated that the CAR was a robust indicator of 90-day mortality in ICU patients suffering from severe sepsis or septic shock [39]. In pediatric populations, however, evidence has been mixed: Chaurasiya et al. found that CAR was not significantly associated with mortality in critically ill children aged 1 month to 18 years [10], whereas Ghauri et al. reported that dynamic changes in CAR during the first three days of illness strongly predicted surgical intervention and mortality in neonates with necrotizing enterocolitis [11]. Similarly, in pediatric ICU patients, the modified nutritional index, which includes CRP and more sensitive biomarkers like transthyretin, was elevated in non-survivors [40].

Our study further supports a strong association between CAR and 28-day mortality in critically ill pediatric patients admitted to the PICU. Compared to CRP or albumin alone, CAR demonstrated superior accuracy in predicting mortality risk, highlighting its potential role in risk stratification and clinical decision-making. Importantly, this study included a diverse cohort of critically ill patients, enhancing the generalizability of the findings. The inclusion of individuals from diverse clinical settings further validates the importance of CAR as a broad prognostic marker in critically ill populations.

To strengthen the credibility of our findings, we conducted multiple sensitivity analyses. These included (1) additional adjustment for liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, and malnutrition, (2) repeating the models after excluding these conditions, (3) excluding patients with missing covariate data to minimize bias from imputation, (4) a subgroup analysis stratified by albumin infusion status to assess potential confounding from fluid resuscitation, and (5) further adjusting for PICU subtypes (SICU and CICU) and CK-MB levels to address potential heterogeneity across patient populations. Across all analyses, the association between higher CAR levels and increased 28-day mortality remained robust, supporting the stability and reliability of our results (Supplementary Tables S3–S8).

Our study has several limitations. First, the retrospective nature of the design may introduce inherent selection and confounding biases, despite efforts to account for potential confounders. Additionally, we were unable to determine whether albumin infusion occurred before or after PICU admission, which might have influenced baseline albumin levels through dilution effects during fluid resuscitation. Although a subgroup analysis stratified by albumin infusion (Supplementary Table S7) yielded consistent results without significant interaction, potential confounding from fluid management practices cannot be fully excluded. Notably, patients with liver disease, nephrotic syndrome, or malnutrition, which could potentially influence albumin levels, were not excluded at baseline. However, additional sensitivity analyses that adjusted for these conditions, as well as analyses excluding such patients, yielded consistent results, supporting the robustness of our findings. Nevertheless, the possibility of residual confounding from unmeasured variables cannot be completely ruled out. Second, being conducted at a single center might limit generalizability and may affect result accuracy, highlighting the need for multicenter studies to verify our conclusions. Furthermore, the lack of external validation limits the applicability of our findings to broader clinical settings. Third, only baseline CAR values upon ICU admission were analyzed, without tracking longitudinal changes. Lastly, although our study offers descriptive insights, it does not establish a causal link between CAR and clinical outcomes. Future well-designed multicenter prospective studies are essential to validate these findings. Despite the availability of various prognostic scores for critically ill pediatric patients, CAR remains a promising and easily accessible biomarker. To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale study to evaluate the association between CAR and 28-day mortality in critically ill children admitted to the PICU, providing novel evidence supporting its potential clinical relevance.

Conclusion

The CAR is significantly associated with short-term mortality in critically ill pediatric patients admitted to the PICU. Higher CAR levels on admission were linked to increased 28-day and in-hospital mortality, as well as prolonged ICU and hospital stays. Our large real-world cohort provides robust data supporting CAR’s potential as a low-cost and easily accessible tool for early risk stratification in pediatric intensive care settings.
