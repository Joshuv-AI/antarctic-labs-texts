Rare CNS manifestation of high-risk CLL with excellent response to triplet targeted therapy
Authors: Anusha Swaminathan, Shrinidhi Nathany, Surbhi Singh, Aastha Gupta, Ishita B Sen, Paritosh Garg, Aakriti kothari, Kanika Verma, Vikas Dua, Rahul Bhargava
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Clinical practice points•Central nervous system (CNS) involvement in chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) is rare, occurring in fewer than 1% of patients in contemporary clinical cohorts.•CNS disease may occur even in patients with early-stage or treatment-naïve disease, suggesting dissemination independent of systemic tumour burden.•No consistent biological predictors of CNS involvement have been identified, although adverse genomic features such as TP53 disruption are frequently present in reported cohorts.•Targeted therapies including Bruton tyrosine kinase inhibitors and venetoclax demonstrate measurable CNS penetration and clinical activity in CNS-involved B-cell malignancies.•This case illustrates that triplet targeted therapy combined with intrathecal chemotherapy can achieve rapid neurologic recovery, cerebrospinal-fluid clearance, and durable remission in biopsy-proven CNS involvement by CLL.Keywords: Central nervous system involvement in CLL, Rare presentation in CLL, Novel targeted therapies

Introduction

Chronic lymphocytic leukemia (CLL) and its tissue-equivalent, small lymphocytic leukemia (SLL), are hematologic malignancies defined by the clonal expansion and progressive accumulation of mature, neoplastic B-lymphocytes within the bone marrow, peripheral blood, and various lymphoid tissues. While the systemic manifestation of this disease—typically presenting as painless lymphadenopathy, splenomegaly, or lymphocytosis—is its recognized clinical hallmark, the symptomatic infiltration of the central nervous system (CNS) remains an exceptional rarity [1]. Such cases present formidable diagnostic and therapeutic challenges, as standard treatment guidelines are primarily designed for systemic disease and often lack specific protocols for extranodal involvement of this nature [2]. Research into these rare presentations suggests that CNS involvement occurs in fewer than 1% of patients in contemporary clinical cohorts [3].

We report the case of an Indian gentleman in his late 40s who was initially diagnosed with SLL in 2019. At the time of his initial diagnosis, his disease was asymptomatic, and he did not meet the International Workshop on CLL (iwCLL) criteria for the initiation of systemic treatment.

[4] Consequently, for the four years following his diagnosis, he was managed under a "watch and wait" strategy, remaining clinically stable with no evidence of progressive marrow failure or symptomatic lymphadenopathy.

The patient’s clinical course changed abruptly when he presented with a three-week history of progressive neurological deficits. His primary complaints were left-sided facial numbness and a noticeable deviation of the left eye, which resulted in persistent double vision (diplopia). Significantly, the patient reported no constitutional "B symptoms" such as fever, drenching night sweats, or unintentional weight loss. Furthermore, there were no associated symptoms of increased intracranial pressure, such as headaches, vomiting, or seizures, and no history of limb weakness. His medical history was otherwise unremarkable, with no prior history of head trauma, CNS infections, or known autoimmune disorders.

Upon physical examination, the patient was found to be hemodynamically stable. He exhibited generalized lymphadenopathy involving the cervical, axillary, and inguinal regions. A detailed neurological evaluation confirmed a left oculomotor nerve palsy characterized by mild exotropia and limited adduction. Fundus examination showed no evidence of papilledema, which supported the clinical impression that intracranial pressure was not significantly elevated at the time of presentation.

Initial laboratory investigations at the time of his original SLL diagnosis had already revealed high-risk genomic features. Fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) and molecular testing had identified a TP53 mutation and an unmutated IGHV status, both of which are recognized markers of aggressive disease and are known to complicate the clinical management of CLL.

[5] These genomic abnormalities are inherently linked to more aggressive disease biology and suboptimal responses to traditional therapies, as the complexity of TP53 disruptions significantly impacts the prognosis of untreated CLL [6].

At the time of his current neurological presentation, basic blood work showed a modest lymphocytosis, although the absolute lymphocyte count remained relatively low at less than 10,000/µL. Given the rapid onset of focal neurological deficits in the setting of known SLL, the primary diagnostic concern was Richter’s transformation with secondary CNS involvement, although direct CNS infiltration by CLL without high-grade transformation was also considered a strong possibility.

To achieve a definitive diagnosis, the patient underwent a cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) study along with biopsies of the lymph nodes and nerve roots. Baseline CSF analysis was highly suggestive of leptomeningeal disease, showing markedly elevated protein levels and the presence of 34% abnormal B-lymphoid cells with a CLL phenotype. Histopathological examination of the tissue fragments revealed diffuse infiltration by small lymphoid cells admixed with intermediate-sized cells and macrophages. These cells were seen directly infiltrating nerve fibers and bundles (Fig. 4). These findings established a definitive diagnosis of direct CNS and nerve-root involvement by CLL, rather than a high-grade transformation.

Discussion

Epidemiology and Prevalence of CNS-CLL The management of CNS involvement in CLL is hindered by its extreme rarity, which precludes the conduct of large-scale, prospective clinical trials. Retrospective cohorts have consistently shown that symptomatic CNS involvement is an unusual complication of the disease [7]. While historical autopsy studies have suggested that occult leukemic infiltration of the meninges may occur in up to 20% of cases, modern clinical data confirms that symptomatic disease is reported in fewer than 1% of patients. A major international retrospective study conducted by the European Research Initiative on CLL (ERIC), which analyzed 48 cases across 15 countries, utilized a population-based registry in Denmark to estimate a true clinical prevalence of only 0.14%.

Neurotropism and Genomic Risk Factors defining characteristic of CNS-CLL, as seen in this patient, is its ability to manifest independently of the systemic tumor burden. Data from the ERIC study and cohorts from the Mayo Clinic indicate that a significant proportion of patients—between 48% and 63%—are treatment-naïve at the time of CNS presentation. Many of these patients have early-stage disease (Binet A) according to standard staging systems. This clinical pattern supports the concept of CLL neurotropism, where specific malignant clones possess a unique predilection for the CNS regardless of the systemic disease stage.

Efforts to identify biological predictors for this neurotropism have yielded various findings. The ERIC study observed an enrichment of unmutated IGHV (75.9%) and trisomy 12 (39.4%) in their CNS cohort. While TP53 aberrations—which were present in our patient—were identified in approximately 24% of CNS cases, it remains unclear if this mutation directly drives CNS dissemination. However, the differential prognosis of single versus multiple TP53 abnormalities continues to be a subject of intense investigation, as these mutations are inherently linked to aggressive disease biology and suboptimal responses to traditional therapies.

Evolution of Treatment Paradigms Historically, the treatment of CNS-CLL relied on fludarabine-based chemo-immunotherapy (CIT), which offered a 5-year progression-free survival (PFS) of approximately 43%. However, CIT is notoriously ineffective in patients with TP53 disruptions, where it is associated with significantly poorer outcomes and rapid relapse. The advent of targeted therapies has revolutionized the landscape of CLL management. Modern strategies emphasize the use of agents with high CNS bioavailability and the ability to bypass the blood-brain barrier. The decision to utilize a triplet targeted regimen (BTK inhibitor, BCL-2 inhibitor, and anti-CD20 antibody) is informed by emerging data suggesting that this combination provides the deepest and most durable remissions in high-risk, TP53-aberrant CLL. Phase II data from trials like CLL2-GIVe have shown high rates of undetectable minimal residual disease and landmark 3-year PFS rates in the 70–80% range for high-risk patients [8].

The integration of acalabrutinib, venetoclax, and obinutuzumab in treatment-naïve patients with high-risk features has also shown impressive results in recent studies, facilitating deeper responses in patients who traditionally have poor outcomes [9]. Furthermore, long-term results of randomized trials have confirmed the efficacy of venetoclax-based combinations in achieving sustained remissions in previously untreated CLL [10].

Bruton Tyrosine Kinase (BTK) Inhibitors and CNS Penetration -BTK inhibitors have emerged as a cornerstone of the management of high-risk CLL. Long-term efficacy data for agents such as ibrutinib have demonstrated significant benefits in patients with TP53 aberrations [11]. In addition to systemic efficacy, second-generation agents like acalabrutinib have provided real-world evidence of safety and efficacy in various patient populations [12]. A critical advantage of BTK inhibitors in the context of CNS disease is their ability to cross the blood-brain barrier and achieve therapeutic concentrations in the CSF. Both ibrutinib and acalabrutinib have been successfully used to treat multifocal CNS involvement in CLL [13]. Acalabrutinib, in particular, has demonstrated a new therapeutic approach for consolidation and maintenance in CNS-involved B-cell malignancies, offering a favourable safety profile alongside effective signaling inhibition [14]. In our case, this systemic approach was supplemented by periodic intrathecal chemotherapy to prevent sanctuary-site relapse and ensure rapid local control. The patient’s clinical response was swift, with neurological symptoms beginning to resolve by the third week of therapy.

Venetoclax in CNS Disease The BCL-2 inhibitor venetoclax has also demonstrated clinical utility in this setting. Although it achieves lower relative concentrations in the CSF compared to BTK inhibitors, it has been confirmed to penetrate the cerebrospinal fluid and may be effective in chronic lymphocytic leukemia with CNS involvement [15]. Clinical reports have highlighted venetoclax as a novel therapeutic agent capable of inducing remission in leptomeningeal disease [16]. Venetoclax was introduced to our patient's regimen two months after the initiation of BTK inhibition to provide synergistic targeting of the residual leukemic clones and to solidify the remission.

The patient's response was monitored through serial CSF flow cytometry and advanced neuroimaging. At one month, the percentage of abnormal B-cells in the CSF had decreased from 34% to 23%. By month three, flow cytometry confirmed the complete clearance of abnormal B-cells. Parallel neuroimaging at month four showed near-complete resolution of oculomotor nerve enhancement, and by month seven, MRI confirmed maintained remission. Conclusion

This case highlights the clinical utility and remarkable efficacy of a triplet targeted regimen in managing rare, high-risk CNS manifestations of CLL. By integrating acalabrutinib, venetoclax, and obinutuzumab with intrathecal chemotherapy, a rapid and durable cytological and radiological remission was achieved in a patient with a TP53-disrupted genotype. Given the current absence of validated stopping rules for therapy in the context of CNS disease, the patient will remain on continuous BTK inhibitor therapy with scheduled quarterly CSF monitoring to ensure sustained remission. This case aligns with the growing body of literature demonstrating that BTK-inhibitor-based combinations significantly outperform traditional CIT in high-risk cohorts. Achieving early CNS clearance—as seen in our patient—is a critical prognostic marker, associated with improved overall survival in responders.

This report contributes to the standard literature supporting triplet targeted therapy as a compelling frontline option for biopsy-proven CNS involvement in high-risk CLL populations.
