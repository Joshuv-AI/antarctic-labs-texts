Systematic Review of Exercise Training and Musculoskeletal Health in Patients Receiving Dialysis
Authors: Abdel Rahman Salameh, Wannasit Wathanavasin, Solos Jaturapisanukul, Benjamin Bizer, Charat Thongprayoon, Wisit Cheungpasitporn
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundExercise is often recommended for patients receiving dialysis, but uncertainty remains regarding its benefit on musculoskeletal and nutritional outcomes.MethodsWe conducted a systematic review and random-effects meta-analysis of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing structured exercise (aerobic, resistance, or combined) with usual care, sham, or placebo in patients receiving dialysis. Searches of PubMed, Scopus, and Embase were performed in May 2025 without language or date limits. Primary outcomes included muscle mass, muscle strength (handgrip strength [HGS]), and physical performance (6-minute walk test [6MWT], sit-to-stand test [STS], short physical performance battery [SPPB], and timed up and go test [TUG]). Secondary outcomes were nutritional indicators (serum albumin, body mass index [BMI], and cholesterol), and health-related quality of life (36-item short form health survey [SF-36] score).ResultsThirty-one RCTs including 2535 patients were analyzed. Exercise significantly improved muscle strength and physical performance (HGS + 2.21 kg; 6MWT + 48.2 m; 60sSTS + 4.96 repetitions), exceeding minimal clinically important differences. Gains were consistent across SPPB, TUG, STS-5, and STS-10. However, no significant effects were observed on muscle mass or nutritional markers (serum albumin, BMI, and cholesterol). Quality of life improved in physical domains but not consistently in the SF-36 mental component score. No publication bias was detected except for the SF-36 domain.ConclusionExercise training in patients receiving dialysis significantly improves strength, functional capacity, and physical quality of life but does not increase muscle mass or alter nutritional markers. These findings reinforce structured exercise as an evidence-based adjunct to dialysis care and highlight the need for complementary nutritional and anti-inflammatory strategies to more effectively target sarcopenia.

Declining musculoskeletal health is a major complication in patients with chronic kidney disease, particularly those on long-term maintenance dialysis. It limits physical function and independence, increases cardiovascular risk, and worsens survival.1 Patients receiving dialysis are highly susceptible to sarcopenia because of metabolic acidosis, accumulation of uremic toxins, inadequate nutritional intake, and the hypercatabolic stress of dialysis therapy.2 These factors lead to loss of muscle mass, reduced strength, and impaired physical performance. Together with physical inactivity, they contribute to a high prevalence of frailty, reported in up to 80% of patients receiving dialysis.3

Exercise interventions, including aerobic and resistance training, have increasingly been studied as nonpharmacologic strategies to address these complications. Aerobic exercise (e.g., walking or cycling) improves cardiorespiratory fitness, whereas resistance training improves muscle mass, strength, and functional independence via anabolic and anti-inflammatory effects.4, 5, 6 Regarding timing, intradialytic exercise appears feasible and may improve adherence, though safety concerns remain, particularly hemodynamic instability and arrhythmias.7 Interdialytic programs, in contrast, face practical barriers such as staffing limitations, cost, and reduced patient motivation.8

Recent guidelines from Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative, the UK Kidney Research Consortium, and the Japanese Society of Renal Rehabilitation recommend moderate-intensity physical activity for patients receiving dialysis, when tolerated.9, 10, 11 Despite these recommendations, important uncertainties remain. The optimal type, dose, setting, and duration of exercise in this population are unclear.12 It is also uncertain whether aerobic, resistance, or combined programs are most effective for improving long-term outcomes, including physical function, nutritional status, and mortality. Heterogeneous protocols and outcome measures further limit the development of standardized, broadly applicable exercise recommendations.9, 10, 11, 12

To address these gaps, we conducted a systematic review and meta-analysis evaluating the effects of exercise interventions on musculoskeletal outcomes—muscle mass, strength, and physical performance—as well as nutritional status and health-related quality of life in patients receiving dialysis. By synthesizing evidence from all available RCTs, we aim to provide updated clinical guidance on integrating structured exercise into routine dialysis care and to examine the influence of dialysis modality, exercise type, timing, intensity, and duration on relevant outcomes.

Methods

This systematic review was conducted in compliance with the 2020 Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses guidelines for reporting interventions. The review protocol was registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews, with the identification CRD420251026217.

Search Strategy

A comprehensive search was conducted in multiple electronic databases, including PubMed, Scopus, EMBASE. The database search was conducted on 3 May 2025 by 2 independent reviewers (AS and WW). The search strategy incorporated medical subject headings or MeSH terms and free-text keywords related to the Population-Intervention-Control-Outcome criteria including exercise training, dialysis, muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance (full search terms listed in Supplementary Table S1). No language or date restrictions were used.

Eligibility Criteria

The eligibility criteria for this review were defined using the Population-Intervention-Control-Outcome framework. Studies included had to meet the following criteria: (i) adults (≥ 18 years) with end-stage kidney disease undergoing hemodialysis (HD) or peritoneal dialysis (PD); (ii) the interventions had to consist of exercise training programs, including resistance, aerobic, or combined modalities; (iii) comparators were described as usual care, no exercise, sham exercise, or placebo. Eligible studies needed to report at least 1 primary outcome related to musculoskeletal health including, muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance or reported as sarcopenia outcome. Secondary outcomes included nutritional indicators (i.e., serum albumin, BMI, total cholesterol) and health-related quality of life. Only RCTs were included to ensure robust evaluation of the effects of exercise interventions. The exclusion criteria comprised the following 3 points: (i) reviews, conference abstracts, meta-analyses, and responses to letters; (ii) studies involving nonhuman species; and (iii) studies in which the intervention was only a recommendation of exercise.

Data Extraction

Two reviewers (AS, WW) independently screened titles, abstracts, and full-text articles for eligibility. Any disagreement was resolved by a third reviewer (SJ) or through a consensus. The following data were extracted from each report: the first author, year of publication, research country, participant numbers, dialysis modality and vintage, sex and age of participants, presence of diabetes, details of intervention (type, intensity, frequency, and duration), musculoskeletal health outcomes (muscle mass, muscle strength, and physical performance), nutritional indicators (body weight, BMI, waist circumference, fat mass, and serum albumin), quality of life (SF-36 score), and duration of follow-up.

Risk of Bias Assessment

The risk of bias in each included study was also independently assessed by 2 reviewers (AS and WW) using the revised Cochrane risk-of bias tool for randomized trials (RoB2) as recommended by the Cochrane, 2022. Any discrepancies were resolved by the third author (SJ).

Statistical Analysis

The meta-analysis was conducted using random-effects model to calculate pooled effect estimates with their 95% confidence intervals (CI).13 Results of both primary and secondary outcomes, measured on a continuous scale, were reported as weighted mean differences (WMD) with 95% CI. If a study did not provide change values for outcomes of interest, the mean ± SD of their levels at baseline (preintervention) and endpoint (postintervention) were collected. We then calculated the pre and post changes in mean and SD for each group in the study using the formula: mean change = mean postintervention - mean baseline. If the SD of change was not provided, it was computed using the following formula: SD change = √[(SD baseline2 + SD final2) - (2 × R × SD baseline × SD final)].14 All the statistical analyses were performed using the Stata statistical package (version 18, StataCorp LLC, Texas). Statistical significance was determined with a 2-tailed P-value < 0.05.

Heterogeneity was evaluated using the I2 statistics and the P-value from the Dixon’s Q- test. The I2 index ranges were between 0% and 100%. I2 values of 25%, 50%, and 75% have been suggested to indicate low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively. To explore sources of heterogeneity, we performed subgroup meta-analyses according to continents, dialysis modality, type, timing, intensity and duration of exercise and risk of bias of the study. Meta-regression analyses were conducted to determine whether study characteristics accounted for the observed heterogeneity, using continuous variables, including mean age, dialysis vintage, study sample size, year of publication, exercise duration and exercise intensity. A forest plot was used to visually illustrate the heterogeneity among the included studies. Publication bias was formally evaluated using funnel plots and the Egger’s test.

Results

Search Results

A total of 2969 studies, identified through the database search, were potentially relevant. After removing 850 studies as duplicates, 2119 study abstracts were screened based on inclusion and exclusion criteria. Sensitivity Analysis

To assess the robustness of the pooled WMD estimates, a sensitivity analysis was conducted by systematically removing 1 study at a time (leave-1-out method). The findings demonstrated that heterogeneity among the studies did not change significantly with respect to both primary and secondary outcome.

Meta-Regression

Meta-regression analyses of outcomes involving more than 10 studies (i.e., HGS and 6MWT) showed that the effects of exercise training on improvement of muscle strength (HGS) and physical performance (6MWT) in patients receiving dialysis were not associated with age of participants, dialysis vintage, study sample size, year of publication, intensity of exercise, or exercise duration (Supplementary Table S5).

Assessment of Publication Bias

Publication bias was assessed using visual inspection of funnel plots and Egger’s regression model. The funnel plots for the 6MWT (Supplementary Figure S5), HGS (Supplementary Figure S6), serum albumin (Supplementary Figure S7), and 60sSTS (Supplementary Figure S8) outcomes appeared symmetrical. Correspondingly, Egger’s test did not indicate significant publication bias for these outcomes (P = 0.20 for 6MWT, P = 0.50 for HGS, P = 0.50 for serum albumin, and P = 0.47 for 60sSTS). However, the funnel plot for the SF-36 outcome was asymmetrical (Supplementary Figure S9), and Egger’s test indicated significant publication bias (P = 0.044).

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis of 31 RCTs including 2535 patients receiving dialysis demonstrated that exercise significantly improved muscle strength, physical performance, and health-related quality of life, particularly physical function and the SF-36 physical component score. These benefits were consistent across race, dialysis modality, exercise timing, type, and duration. A trend toward improvement in the SF-36 mental component score was observed but did not reach statistical significance. No significant effects were found for muscle mass or nutritional markers.

Several recent systematic reviews and network meta-analyses have evaluated exercise interventions in dialysis populations, including publications from 2023 to 2025.46, 47, 48 However, most focused on specific exercise modalities or isolated physiological endpoints rather than providing an integrated synthesis of musculoskeletal, nutritional, and quality-of-life outcomes. To address the knowledge gap, our study incorporated prespecified subgroup analyses and meta-regression models examining key exercise prescription variables, including modality, timing, intensity, and duration. This approach enables a more nuanced understanding of the exercise characteristics, most notably associated with improvements in musculoskeletal health and patient-reported outcomes. Accordingly, this study provides an updated and integrative evaluation of the multidimensional impact of exercise training in dialysis populations.

Sarcopenia and malnutrition are common in end-stage kidney disease and strongly associated with cardiovascular events and mortality.49,50 Although exercise is proposed to preserve muscle mass and function,51, 52, 53 muscle mass, assessed by SMMI and ALMI, did not significantly improve in our analysis. Two RCTs27,28 evaluating SMMI showed no significant benefit of exercise, and 2 studies32,37 assessing ALMI similarly demonstrated no effect. These findings align with previous reports that exercise alone rarely produces measurable hypertrophy in patients receiving dialysis. The lack of effect likely reflects multifactorial drivers of muscle wasting in end-stage kidney disease, including inflammation, amino acid losses during dialysis, anabolic resistance, and impaired insulin-like growth factor 1/insulin signaling, which blunt anabolic responses despite regular training.54, 55, 56, 57 The relatively long dialysis vintage of included participants (mean 58.1 months, approximately 4.8 years) indicates that most patients were prevalent patients of end-stage kidney disease with prolonged exposure to dialysis therapy, which may further attenuate hypertrophic responsiveness and limit gains in muscle mass. The prolonged dialysis exposure may also reflect a population less likely to be transplant candidates, potentially indicating greater comorbidity burden. Whether exercise responsiveness differs in patients listed for transplantation warrants further investigation, as transplant eligibility was not consistently reported across included trials.

These findings suggest that combining individualized nutritional optimization with exercise may be necessary to enhance muscle mass. In contrast, muscle strength improved significantly with exercise. Across 13 RCTs (1229 patients), handgrip strength increased by 2.21 kg, with consistent effects across subgroups. These improvements likely result from enhanced muscle quality, neuromuscular adaptations,58 and recruitment of additional motor units rather than increase muscle hypertrophy alone.59,60 Early-phase neural adaptations appear to predominate, as resistance and combined training can enhance motor unit recruitment, firing frequency, and intermuscular coordination, thereby increasing force production without measurable changes in muscle cross-sectional area.55, 56, 57 In patients receiving dialysis, the chronic uremic milieu—characterized by systemic inflammation, anabolic resistance, impaired insulin/ insulin-like growth factor 1 signaling, and amino acid losses during dialysis—may blunt muscle protein synthesis and limit hypertrophic responses despite adequate training stimuli.52, 53, 54 Consequently, observed functional improvements are more likely driven by gains in neuromuscular efficiency and muscle quality rather than increases in lean mass.51,55 This dissociation between strength improvement and unchanged muscle mass underscores the importance of evaluating functional outcomes beyond body composition metrics in dialysis populations.

Physical performance improved across standardized measures. Exercise reduced sit-to-stand times (STS-5: -1.46 s; STS-10: -4.42 s) and increased 6-minute walk distance by 48.21 m. Although no chronic kidney disease-specific minimal clinically important difference is established, thresholds of 20 to 40 m higher from other chronic disease populations suggest clinical relevance.61 These improvements likely reflect enhanced endurance and cardiorespiratory fitness,62,63 consistent with evidence that exercise augments oxidative capacity and capillary density in patients receiving dialysis.58,64 Subgroup analyses showed consistent effects across most strata, with the only significant modifier being exercise type, where aerobic or combined programs produced greater 6MWT gains than resistance training alone (P for interaction = 0.01).

Secondary outcomes showed mixed effects. Exercise did not significantly improve nutritional indicators, including serum albumin, BMI, or total cholesterol. These findings likely reflect the proinflammatory and catabolic environment of dialysis, suggesting that exercise alone is insufficient to improve nutritional status and that multimodal strategies, such as combining exercise with dietary interventions, may be required.47,65 The relatively short duration of most included trials (typically 3–6 months) may have limited the ability to detect changes in serum albumin, which reflects longer-term nutritional and inflammatory status rather than acute anabolic responses. Importantly, serum albumin and BMI, commonly used nutritional indicators, are substantially affected by several dialysis-related factors such as systemic inflammation, fluid shifts, and dialysis adequacy in patients receiving dialysis. These parameters may therefore reflect downstream consequences of the chronic uremic and inflammatory milieu rather than serving as direct indicators of muscle adaptation or optimal nutritional nutrition. The dissociation between functional improvement and unchanged ‘classic’ nutritional markers underscores the limitation of relying solely on these laboratory indices to assess musculoskeletal health in patients receiving dialysis.

Exercise consistently improved health-related quality of life, particularly in physical domains.53 Our analysis demonstrated significant gains in the SF-36 physical function domain (WMD: 10.34), with subgroup analyses showing greater benefits with resistance training, in Asian cohorts, and in shorter interventions (≤ 12 weeks). Additionally, a significant improvement was observed in the physical component score (WMD: 6.18), confirming that exercise enhances perceived functional capacity and physical well-being. The effects on the SF-36 mental component score were less consistent, with only a nonsignificant trend towards improvement observed in the mental component score. Although exercise is biologically plausible to confer psychological benefits, potentially through modulation of monoamine neurotransmitter, including dopamine, noradrenaline, and serotonin,66,67 our analysis did not observe a consistent improvement in SF-36 mental component scores. This lack of effect may be attributable to short intervention periods and the complex and multifaceted determinants of mental health in dialysis populations.

Our systematic review and meta-analysis support integrating exercise into dialysis care as a standard supportive therapy, given its beneficial effects on muscle strength, physical performance, and quality of life—particularly the physical component. However, the absence of muscle mass gains suggests that exercise alone is insufficient to counteract sarcopenia, emphasizing the need for multimodal approaches that include nutritional supplementation and strategies targeting inflammation and the catabolic dialysis environment. Importantly, consistent improvements in quality-of-life highlight exercise as a holistic, patient-centered intervention.53 Implementing supervised intradialytic or accessible home-based programs may enhance sustainability, reduce healthcare costs, and improve both clinical outcomes and patient experience.68 From an implementation standpoint, these findings align with frameworks such as Reach, Effectiveness, Adoption, Implementation, Maintenance,69 which emphasize not only effectiveness but also reach, adoption within dialysis units, fidelity of delivery, and long-term sustainability. Given that intradialytic exercise can be delivered within existing treatment sessions, integration into routine dialysis workflows may be achievable with structured protocols and staff engagement.

This study has several strengths. By synthesizing 31 RCTs with over 2500 participants, it represents 1 of the most comprehensive evaluations of exercise in dialysis. Inclusion of diverse geographic regions enhances generalizability, and assessment of aerobic, resistance, and combined modalities allows broad comparison. Adherence to Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses and use of the Cochrane RoB2tool strengthen methodological rigor. Nonetheless, considerable heterogeneity in exercise protocols, small sample sizes, and short follow-up durations limit the ability to define optimal prescriptions or assess the durability of benefits. Although subgroup analyses were conducted, it is important to note that several critical factors that might affect the outcomes of interest, such as baseline nutritional status and concurrent nutritional interventions, could not be analyzed because of insufficient data reported in the included trials. Outcomes related to muscle mass and nutritional indicators were evaluated in only a few studies, reducing certainty in these domains. Moreover, the evidence base was heavily weighted toward HD, with sparse data for PD, and few trials examined long-term outcomes such as hospitalization, survival, or adherence in real-world practice. Nutritional supplementation practices were not controlled and may have attenuated detectable differences in serum albumin between exercise and control groups. Furthermore, the majority of participants received conventional HD, with only 1 study by Assawasaksakul et al.32 employing hemodiafiltration. Although emerging evidence suggests that dialysis modality, including hemodiafiltration or medium cut-off membranes, may influence physical performance and inflammatory milieu, our study primarily evaluate structured exercise interventions rather than dialysis techniques. Future research should examine potential interactions between dialysis modality and exercise responsiveness.

Future research should enroll larger and more diverse cohorts, particularly patients on PD, and use longer, standardized interventions. Multimodal strategies combining exercise with nutritional and anti-inflammatory approaches may be required to overcome anabolic resistance. Trials should incorporate patient-reported outcomes, including fatigue and frailty, alongside functional and clinical endpoints. Comparative effectiveness and economic analyses of intradialytic versus home-based delivery models are also needed to inform sustainable integration into routine care.

In conclusion, exercise significantly improves muscle strength, physical performance, and physical quality of life in patients receiving dialysis but has limited effects on muscle mass and conventional nutritional markers. Integrating structured exercise, ideally within multimodal care strategies, offers a practical and patient-centered approach to improving outcomes in this high-risk population.
