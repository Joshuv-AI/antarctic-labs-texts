HSP90 promotes tumor associated macrophage differentiation during triple-negative breast cancer progression
Authors: Lingjia Hong, Manami Tanaka, Masato Yasui, Mariko Hara-Chikuma
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) originating from monocytes are crucial for cancer progression; however, the mechanism of TAM differentiation is unclear. We investigated factors involved in the differentiation of monocytes into TAMs within the tumor microenvironment of triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC). We screened 172 compounds and found that a heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) inhibitor blocked TNBC-induced monocyte-to-TAM differentiation in human monocytes THP-1. TNBC-derived conditional medium (CM) activated cell signaling pathways, including MAP kinase, AKT and STAT3, and increased the expression of TAM-related genes and proteins. These inductions were suppressed by HSP90 inhibition or by knockdown of HSP90 in TNBC. Additionally, we confirmed that TNBC secreted HSP90 extracellularly and that HSP90 itself promoted TAM differentiation. In a mouse tumor model, treatment with an HSP90 inhibitor suppressed tumor growth and reduced TAMs in the tumor microenvironment. Our findings demonstrate the role of HSP90 in TAM differentiation, suggesting HSP90 as a potential target for TNBC immunotherapy due to its regulatory role in monocyte-to-TAM differentiation.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-024-73394-9.

Introduction

Macrophages play a pivotal role in immune maintenance, contributing to both pathogen defense and homeostasis. Depending on the stimuli, macrophages can polarize into two broad categories: antitumoral M1 macrophages, which promote proinflammatory responses, and protumoral M2 macrophages, which promote anti-inflammatory responses1,2. In the tumor microenvironment (TME), tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) have M2-like phenotype, contributing to immune evasion, tumor growth, angiogenesis, and metastasis2–4. Therefore, TAM-targeted therapeutic strategies for cancer progression, including blocking macrophage recruitment, reprogramming of TAM, depletion of TAMs, and inhibition of TAM activation, have been developed1,5,6. For example, inhibition of colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) or CCL2/CCR2 can deplete TAMs or prevent TAM survival7–9. Studies have suggested that in TME, monocytes infiltrate in response to cytokines and chemokines secreted by cancer cells, where they differentiate into TAMs10. Cancer cell-derived lactic acid or retinoic acid enhanced TAM polarization in TME11,12. However, because the factors and mechanisms involved in TAM differentiation remain largely unknown13, therapeutic agents that control TAM differentiation are limited in cancer, including breast cancer.

Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women in the 21st century. Human breast cancer is classified into 3 main subtypes based on the expression of estrogen receptor (ER), progesterone receptor (PR), or human epithelial receptor 2 (HER2). These subtypes include single-, double-, and triple-negative breast cancer (TNBC; ER- PR- HER2-)14. TNBC, which accounts for > 10% of all breast cancers, has high malignant potential and poor prognosis, and develops resistance to anticancer drugs during treatment15,16. Because TNBC is characterized by a lack of established molecular targets for therapy, it is less effective than other breast cancers for which targeted therapies exist. TNBC is treated with several therapeutic drugs, including chemotherapy with EGFR or poly ADP-ribose polymerase1 (PARP1) pathway inhibitors17,18. In the breast cancer microenvironment, TAMs were involved in malignant transformation19–21. Therefore, there is a growing demand for the development of new treatment strategies that do not focus on targeting molecules but rather on alternative approaches, such as targeting the TME22,23.

In this study, we aimed to identify the factors and mechanisms involved in TAM differentiation within the TNBC microenvironment. We utilized human-derived monocytes (THP-1), TNBC cells, and compounds library, including kinase inhibitors and cancer-targeted drugs, to identify agents involved in monocyte-to-TAM differentiation. We report that inhibition of heat shock protein 90 (HSP90) blocks TAM differentiation, and HSP90 secreted by TNBC cells promotes monocyte-to-TAM differentiation.

Results

TNBC induced polarization of monocyte into TAM

We first established an experimental system to confirm that monocytes differentiate into TAM-like cells under the influence of breast cancer cells. Human-derived THP-1 cells, widely used in monocyte model studies2, were employed for this purpose. THP-1 cells treated with phorbol 12-myristate 13-acetate (PMA) differentiate into M0-type macrophages and can further develop into M1 or M2 macrophages with lipopolysaccharide (LPS) and interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) or interleukin-4 (IL-4) and interleukin-13 (IL-13), respectively24,25. Coculturing THP-1 monocytes with cancer cells, including colon cancer and breast cancer cells, polarized them into TAM-like cells26.

Therefore, we cultured THP-1 monocytes with conditioned medium (CM) derived from three types of characterized breast cancer cells to observe their differentiation. Incubation with CM from MDA-MB-231, a TNBC cell line, induced a morphological transformation of THP-1 cells, characterized by the development of numerous projections and pseudopodia, features typically associated with macrophages, whereas the single-negative breast cancer cell line MCF7 and the Her-2 positive breast cancer cell line MDA-MB-453 did not affect the morphology of THP-115 (Fig. 1a). Consistently, RT-PCR analysis showed that CM derived from TNBC MDA-MB-231 significantly increased the expression of TAM-associated genes, such as CD163 and CD206 (Fig. 1b). Fluorescence-activated cell sorting (FACS) analysis confirmed the increase in TAM-associated membrane proteins including CD163 and CD206, in THP-1 cells after incubation with CM of MDA-MB-231 (Fig. 1c). Coculture of THP-1 with MDA-MB-231 increased the expression of TAM-associated genes, including MMP9, CCL2, CD163, and CSF-1 (Fig. 1d). These results demonstrate that an extracellular molecule secreted by TNBC MDA-MB-231 is involved in monocyte-to-TAM differentiation.

HSP90 inhibitor suppressed MDA-MB-231-induced polarization of THP-1 monocytes

To identify the key molecule involved in monocyte differentiation, we used SCADS inhibitor kits, which contain small molecule compounds of 95 kinase inhibitors and 77 molecularly targeted cancer inhibitors (Supplementary Tables 1, 2). THP-1 monocytes were incubated with each inhibitor and MDA-MB-231 CM for 3 d, and CD163 mRNA expression was determined as a marker of differentiation into TAM-like cells. The library of molecularly targeted cancer inhibitors included mTOR inhibitors, which has been reported to suppress differentiation into TAMs27. Consistently, our results revealed that mTOR inhibitors suppressed the increase of CD163 mRNA expression. In addition, retinoids, which was shown to promote TAM differentiation12, enhanced increased CD163 expression in our assay (Supplementary Fig. 1). These results indicated that our assay system can assess differentiation into TAMs. Figure 2a summarizes the effect of each kinase inhibitor on the CM-induced increase in CD163 expression. Among the 95 compounds, the HSP90 inhibitor radicicol showed one of the highest inhibitory effects (Supplementary Table 1).

We investigated whether HSP90 inhibition affected TNBC CM-induced THP-1 differentiation using HSP90 inhibitors radicicol and ganetespib. We first tested the cytotoxicity of the two inhibitors on THP-1 monocytes. When THP-1 cells were cultured with different doses of HSP90 inhibitors, > 1 µM ganetespib and > 5 µM radicicol were significantly cytotoxic to THP-1 cells (Fig. 2b). RT-PCR analysis of CM-incubated THP-1 cells treated with ganetespib (1 µM) or radicicol (5 µM) revealed that both inhibitors suppressed MDA-MB-231-induced gene expression related to TAM transcription (Fig. 2c). We found that even at lower concentrations, HSP90 inhibitors suppressed CM-induced differentiation (Supplementary Fig. 2). Additionally, FACS analysis showed that the ganetespib significantly suppressed the increase in expression of TAM-related proteins, including CD68 and CD163 (Fig. 2d). These findings suggest that HSP90 inhibition has an inhibitory effect on monocyte-to-TAM differentiation.

HSP90 inhibitor suppressed MDA-MB-231-induced cell signaling activation during monocyte differentiation

The mechanism of monocyte-to-TAM differentiation, including the cellular signals involved, is unclear. On the other hand, the HSP90 inhibitor has been reported to downregulate EGFR or AKT protein and phosphorylation in breast cancer cells, including MDA-MB-23128,29. Studies showed that the HSP90 inhibitor 17-demethoxygeldanamycin hydrochloride inhibited the MAPK, Akt, or nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) pathway in various cell types30–32. Therefore, we investigated whether stimulation of MDA-MB-231 activates cellular signals, including MAPK, AKT, and NF-κB pathways, and whether inhibition of HSP90 suppresses these signals. THP-1 monocytes were incubated with CM in the presence or absence of HSP90 inhibitors for 24 h. CM stimulation activated MAPK/Erk1/2, AKT, STAT3, and NF-κB p65 pathways (Fig. 3a). The addition of HSP90 inhibitors effectively suppressed CM-induced phosphorylation, suggesting a pivotal role of HSP90 in the activation of these signaling pathways. We demonstrated that even very low concentration (0.05 µM) of ganetespib markedly inhibited CM-induced activation of these signaling pathways (Fig. 3b). We evaluated the time course of the inhibitory effect of ganetespib on CM-induced activation of cell signaling. CM activated HSP90 client proteins within 3 h, which was sustained for > 24 h (Fig. 3c). These findings indicate that MDA-MB-231 derived CM activates signaling pathways, including MAPK, AKT, STAT3, and NF-κB, during the differentiation of monocytes to TAM-like cells, which were suppressed by HSP90 inhibition.

HSP90 directly promoted monocyte-to-TAM differentiation

A recent study showed that HSP90 and TβRII are abundantly present in the exosomes of MDA-MB-231 breast cancer cells33. To determine whether these molecules are involved in monocyte-to-TAM differentiation, THP-1 monocytes were incubated with recombinant HSP90 or TβRII protein for 5 d. Microscopy images suggested that HSP90 stimulated THP-1 into a TAM-like form, but TβRII had no effect (Fig. 4a). FACS analysis showed that HSP90 significantly increased CD11b and CD206, as an index of macrophage and TAM polarization, respectively (Fig. 4b). PCR analysis showed that HSP90 upregulated TAM-related genes dose dependently (Fig. 4c). We verified that the addition of HSP90 to THP-1 monocytes induced the phosphorylation of Erk1/2 and AKT, consistent with the CM stimulation results (Fig. 4d). The HSP90 inhibitor ganetespib suppressed the HSP90-induced increase in TAM-related gene expression (Fig. 4e). Our results provide evidence that among the factors released by TNBC cells, HSP90 is important for monocyte-to-TAM differentiation.

Deficiency of HSP90 in MDA-MB-231 reduced TAM differentiation and cell signaling activation

As shown in Fig. 1b, TNBC MDA-MB-231 induced the most monocyte-to-TAM differentiation among the three subtypes of human breast cancer cells. To verify the involvement of cell-derived HSP90 in this differentiation process, HSP90 levels were examined in the CM from the three breast cancer cell lines by enzyme-linked immunoassay (ELISA). Figure 5a demonstrated that MDA-MB-231 secreted the highest level of extracellular HSP90 compared to the other subtypes, supporting the role of HSP90 in monocyte-to-TAM differentiation.

To further investigate the involvement of HSP90, HSP90 expression was knocked down in MDA-MB-231 cells using HSP90-siRNA. The introduction of HSP90-RNAi successfully reduced both HSP90 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 5b, c). ELISA showed that the concentration of extracellular HSP90 in the CM from MDA-MB-231 was also decreased by HSP90 knockdown (Fig. 5d). THP-1 cells were incubated with CM from HSP90-knockdown cancer cells, following the same procedure as in Figs. 1 and 2, and differentiation into TAMs was assessed by RT-PCR analysis. Figure 5e shows that the CM-induced expression of TAM-associated genes, including CCL2 and MMP9, were significantly suppressed by HSP90 knockdown compared to control cells. Additionally, HSP90 knockdown in MDA-MB-231 cells suppressed the CM-induced activation of signaling pathways in THP-1 monocytes, including MAPK/Erk1/2, AKT, and STAT3 (Fig. 5f). These results provide strong evidence that HSP90 derived from MDA-MB-231 cells is involved in the differentiation of monocytes into TAMs.

HSP90 inhibitor suppressed tumor growth in an in vivo syngeneic cancer model

We examined whether ganetespib could inhibit the differentiation of mouse-derived monocytes into TAMs. The cell survival assay showed that > 0.5 µM ganetespib was toxic to mouse derived bone marrow (BM) cells and the mouse TNBC cell line 4T1 (Supplementary Fig. 3). To more closely simulate in vivo conditions, we co-cultured mouse BM cells with 4T1 cells using transwell in the presence of ganetespib at lower concentrations (0.01 and 0.1 µM) compare with human cells. RT-PCR analysis showed that ganetespib suppressed cancer cell-induced TAM-related gene expression in mouse cells, suggesting the involvement of HSP90 in TAM differentiation in mice (Fig. 6a).

Finally, we determined whether the HSP90 inhibitor ganetespib could inhibit tumor growth in a syngeneic mouse model using 4T1 breast cancer cells. BALB/c mice bearing cancer cells were treated with ganetespib or vehicle twice a week (Fig. 6b). Administration of ganetespib significantly suppressed tumor growth compared with vehicle treatment (Fig. 6c, d). Immunoblotting confirmed the high expression of HSP90 in 4T1 breast cancer cells (Fig. 6e). FACS analysis of tumor infiltrating lymphocytes showed that ganetespib treatment increased the number of CD4+ T cells and decreased the protein expression of PD-1 on CD4+ T cells, possibly due to different degrees of cancer progression (Fig. 6f). At 28 days, the tumor sizes were too different to accurately compare the number of TAMs on FACS.

Next, to verify our findings of in vitro experiments in a mouse model, we transferred naïve BM cells from BALB/c mice into 4T1 tumor tissues 10 d after transplantation; ganetespib was administered 1 d before and 2 d after BM cell transfer (Fig. 6g). Three days after BM cell transfer, tumor infiltrating lymphocytes were examined by FACS analysis. Ganetespib decreased the number of macrophages (CD45+ CD11b+ F4/80+ Ly6C−) compared with vehicle treatment (Fig. 6h). In addition, FACS analysis showed that ganetespib treatment decreased the number of CD206+ TAM-like macrophages (CD45+ CD11b+ F4/80+ CD206+) characteristic of TAM. These results showed that the HSP90 inhibitor suppressed tumor growth with attenuated TAM differentiation.

Discussion

The findings of this study revealed that HSP90 plays a pivotal role in monocyte-to-TAM differentiation within the TNBC microenvironment. HSP90, an evolutionary conserved molecular chaperone, plays a critical role in the stability and activation of > 200 proteins and has been particularly implicated in the support of various oncoproteins34,35. The use of HSP90 inhibitors radicicol and ganetespib effectively suppressed this process by downregulating TAM gene and protein expression and blocking key signaling pathways, including MAPK, AKT, and STAT3. Our in vivo experiments using a xenograft mouse tumor model demonstrated that HSP90 inhibition not only impedes tumor growth, but also attenuates the polarization of monocytes into immunosuppressive TAMs. Additionally, we discovered that HSP90 released from TNBCs promoted differentiation of monocytes into TAM-like macrophages. These findings underscore the crucial role of HSP90 in TAM differentiation in the TME during TNBC progression and can lead to the development of an HSP90 inhibitor that targets TNBC.

Studies have shown that HSP90 is closely associated with HER2, playing a role in fostering its mutations and aiding in the development of TNBC36,37. HSP90 inhibitors are enrolled in clinical trials for cancer therapy, targeting acute myelogenous leukemia, non-small-cell lung cancer, and breast cancer38–40. The HSP90 inhibitor 17-N-allylamino-17-demethoxygeldanamycin is under phase III evaluation for castration-resistant prostate cancer, whereas STA-9090, a synthetic HSP90 inhibitor, is being assessed in a phase I/II clinical trial for leukemia36. However, the mechanisms by which HSP90 inhibitors suppress TAM differentiation are unclear. This gap in knowledge indicates the need to elucidate these mechanisms and optimize the therapeutic potential of HSP90 inhibitors in cancer treatment.

Several factors have been reported to be involved in regulating TAM differentiation13. Studies have reported that lung cancer cell-derived lactic acid has an important signaling role in the induction of several key features of TAM polarization and the subsequent promotion of tumor growth11. Reduced retinoic acid expression in sarcoma TME decreased TAM generation and increased dendritic cell proliferation12. In addition, depletion of leukemia inhibitory factor, IL-6, and M-CSF in ovarian cancer ascites suppressed TAM-like cell induction41. Macrophages showed lower expression of protumorigenic genes and upregulation of genes associated with antigen presentation when CSF1R was blocked in lung tumor cells42. The study showed that HSP90α, secreted during the endothelial-to-mesenchymal transition of endothelial cells, contributed to M2-polarization in macrophages. This polarization is significant in promoting the growth of pancreatic ductal adenocarcinoma tumors43. A recent study showed notable presence of HSP90 in the extracellular vesicles of MDA-MB-231 cells, identified through ultra-performance liquid chromatography–mass spectrometry analysis33. Based on other studies and our findings, we suggest that HSP90 is a remarkable molecule involved in TAM differentiation and tumor progression in TNBC. Further research is needed on the detailed mechanisms of how HSP90 is involved in breast cancer progression.

Our findings demonstrate that HSP90 is crucial for monocyte-to-TAM differentiation in the TNBC microenvironment. These results not only enhance our understanding of the role of HSP90 in cancer progression, but also highlight its value as a therapeutic target in the treatment of various malignancies, including TNBC.

Limitations of the study

Our study has certain limitations. Specific interaction mechanisms between HSP90 inhibitors and other cellular components in the TME are not fully elucidated. Furthermore, the complexity of TME interactions in human cancers may not be entirely replicable in mouse models, and thus, our findings need to be validated in more diverse and clinically relevant settings.

Methods

Cell culture

4T1 (JCRB) and THP-1 (American Type Culture Collection, ATCC) were cultured in RPMI 1640 medium (ATCC) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and penicillin–streptomycin (100 u/mL and 100 µg/mL). MDA-MB-231 (ATCC), MCF7 (Riken BRC), and MDA-MB-453 (Riken BRC) cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM) (Fujifilm) containing 10% FBS and penicillin–streptomycin.

Cell growth and viability assay

To assess cell growth, the number of THP-1 or BM cells was quantified using the CellTiter-Glo Luminescent Cell Viability Assay (Promega, Madison, WI, USA) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Cell viability was analyzed with the 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5 diphenyltetrazolium bromide (MTT) assay (Nacalai) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

CM preparation and stimulation of THP-1 monocytes

In total, 105 MDA-MB-231 (ATCC), MCF7 (Riken BRC), or MDA-MB-453 (Riken BRC) cells were cultured in DMEM containing 10% FBS and penicillin–streptomycin on a 100-mm culture dish. At 90% cell confluence, culture medium was changed to starved DMEM, and cells were cultured for 48 h. CM were collected, centrifuged, and filtered (0.45-µm pore, Sartorius).

THP-1 cells (ATCC) were incubated for 5 d with CM in RPMI 1640 containing HSP90 inhibitor, 10% FBS, and penicillin–streptomycin.

THP-1 cells were co-cultured for 5 d with recombinant human HSP90α protein (AP-160-050, R&D Systems Inc., Minneapolis, USA.) or TGFBR2 protein (09-142, Carna biosciences, Japan) in RPMI 1640 containing 10% FBS, and penicillin–streptomycin with or without HSP90 inhibitor.

Coculture of THP-1 and breast cancer cells

THP-1 and MDA-MB-231 were cocultured in 24-well Transwell plates (0.4-µm pore size, 662641, GIBCO) with DMEM containing 10% FBS for 5 d.

Inhibitor screening in THP-1 cells

Drug screening was performed using SCAD inhibitor kits (kit III, ver. 1.7 for kinase inhibitors, kit IV for cancer molecular targeted inhibitor) obtained from the Molecular Profiling Committee, Grant-in-Aid for Scientific Research on Innovative Areas “Platform of Advanced Animal Model Support” from the Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology, Japan (KAKENHI 16H06276). THP-1 monocytes were plated in a 96-well plate (106 cells) and incubated with the inhibitor (final 10 µM) for 1 h. Then CM derived from MDA-MB-231 was added to the cells. After 3 d incubation, RNA was extracted with CellAmp Direct RNA Prep kit (Takara, #3732) and mRNA expression was analyzed by One Step SYBR PrimeScript RT-PCR Kit II (Takara, RR086A).

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative RT-PCR

Total RNA was extracted using TRIZOL (Invitrogen) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. cDNA was reverse transcribed from total RNA using Prime Script RT Reagent Kit (Takara Bio, Otsu, Japan). cDNA concentration was measured using a NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer. Quantitative RT-PCR was performed using SYBR Green I (Takara Bio) and StepOne Plus Real-Time PCR System (Thermo Fisher). The results were analyzed with the comparative Ct method to establish relative expression curves, and 18 S RNA was used as internal control.

Immunoblotting

Cells were lysed with RIPA buffer and protease inhibitor cocktail tablets (Roche Diagnostics, Indianapolis, IN, USA). The amount of protein in each lysate was measured by the BCA assay. Protein was resolved in 10% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred to PVDF membranes. Membranes were blocked with 2% BSA solution in TBS-T containing 0.1% Tween 20 (FUJIFILM Wako Pure Chemical Corporation), incubated overnight at 4 °C in primary antibodies, followed by conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Signals were detected with Immobilon Western Chemiluminescent HRP Substrate (EMD Millipore Corporation, Burlington, MA, USA). The antibodies used were as follows: anti-AKT (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p-AKT (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-ERK1/2 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p-ERK1/2 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-STAT3 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p-STAT3 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p65 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-p-p65 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), anti-GAPDH (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology), and anti-HSP90 (1:1000; Cell Signaling Technology).

Flow cytometry analysis

Tumor tissues were minced and digested with a mixture of liberase (25 µg/mL; Roche) and DNase I (10 µg/mL, Sigma) and pipetted every 20 min. After incubation, the suspensions were filtered using a 70-μm strainer and centrifuged for 5 min at 1000 rpm to collect cell pellets. Single cell suspensions were stained in FACS buffer (PBS containing 1% BSA and 2 mM EDTA) with fluorochrome-conjugated antibodies. All the antibodies used in this study were obtained from Thermo Fisher Scientific (Tokyo, Japan) or BioLegend (Tokyo, Japan). The stained cells were analyzed using CytoFLEX (Beckman Coulter Life sciences, Brea, CA). Data were analyzed using the FlowJo software.

Enzyme-linked immunoassay

The concentrations of HSP90 in CM were detected using HSP90 ELISA kit (KE00054, PGI Proteintech Group Inc., Manchester, UK) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

RNA interference (RNAi)

MDA-MB-231 cells were grown in 60 mm tissue culture dishes in antibiotic-free DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS for 24 h to reach 30–50% confluence. ON-TARGET plus SMART pool human HSP90 small interfering RNA (siRNA) (Dharmacon, L-005186) was transfected into the cells according to the manufacturer’s protocol, with non-targeting siRNA (Dharmacon, D-001810) was used as a control. Briefly, MDA-MB-231 cells were incubated with 10 or 20 nM siRNA-HSP90 mixed with Lipofectamine™ 2000 siRNA Transfection Reagent (11668019, Invitrogen) in reduced serum medium Opti-MEM (31985062, GIBCO). The transfected cells were incubated for 6 h and then refreshed with DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS for an additional 48–72 h.

Coculture of BM monocytes and breast cancer cell

BM cells were collected from the femur and tibia. BM cells were cultured in 24-well transwell (0.4-µm pore size, 662641, GIBCO) with 4T1 cells with RPMI 1640 containing ganetespib (1 µM, Funakoshi CS-0697), 10% FBS, and penicillin–streptomycin (100 u/mL and 100 µg/mL) for 5 d.

Syngeneic tumor model

BALB/c mice were purchased from Japan SLC, Inc. Female mice (7–10-week-old) were injected with 4T1 breast carcinoma cells (4 × 106 cells in 100 µL PBS) intradermally into the right flank. Ganetespib treatment was started when tumor size reached 50–60 mm23 (around day 7). The mice were randomized into two groups. Ganetespib (100 mg/kg weight, formulated in 10/18 DRD; 10% DMSO, 18% Cremophor RH 40, 3.6% dextrose, and 68.4% water) or 10/18 DRD (vehicle), was administered twice a week by tail-vein injection. Tumor size was measured every 3 d with a digital caliper and calculated using the following formula: volume = (width2 × length)/2. At the end of observation, tumor tissues were removed and weighed.

For in vivo monocyte transfer, naïve BM cell suspension (100 µL of 106 cells/mL in PBS) were intratumorally injected into 4T1 tumor tissues.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using the two-tailed Student’s t-test or one- or two-way ANOVA by GraphPad Prism9.
