Comparison of the permeability of DPP-4 inhibitors—sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin—in placental barrier cell models and exploration of their transport mechanisms by LC-MS/MS
Authors: Mengru Bai, Qian Shen, Yong Wu, Mingyang Chen, Dan Liu, Nengming Lin, Wei Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUncontrolled hyperglycemia in pregnancy poses risks to both mother and fetus, but treatment options are limited. Sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin are dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors effective in type 2 diabetes. However, data on their placental transfer and safety in pregnant women is lacking. Therefore, this study compared the permeability of these drugs in multiple placental cell models, including BeWo, HTR-8/SVneo, and primary human trophoblast cells, and attempted to explore their transport mechanisms.MethodsA cost-effective liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry method was developed and validated to quantify the four DPP-4 inhibitors. The accumulation of these drugs in placental cell models was compared, and the BeWo Transwell transport assay was conducted to assess transport rates. Transporter inhibitors, siRNA knockdown, and transporter overexpression cell models were used to explore the transmembrane transport mechanism of these drugs.ResultsLinagliptin had the highest accumulation in placental cells, followed by alogliptin, sitagliptin, and vildagliptin. The apparent permeability coefficient of linagliptin, alogliptin and sitagliptin was similar in BeWo cells, while vildagliptin was the lowest. The accumulation of linagliptin, alogliptin, sitagliptin and vildagliptin in BeWo cells at 37 °C were significantly higher than those at 4 °C, indicating the presence of carrier-mediated uptake. However, studies using transporter inhibitors, siRNA knockdown, and transporter overexpression cell models showed that equilibrative nucleoside transporters (ENTs), concentrative nucleoside transporters (CNTs), and organic anion transporter (OAT) 4 do not facilitate the placental transfer of linagliptin, alogliptin, sitagliptin, and vildagliptin.ConclusionsVildagliptin had the lowest accumulation and permeability in placental cells among the four DPP-4 inhibitors. There might be carrier-mediated transmembrane transport of linagliptin, alogliptin, sitagliptin and vildagliptin, but ENTs, CNTs and OAT4 were not involved in. Background

Hyperglycemia during pregnancy includes diabetes mellitus in pregnancy, prediabetes, and gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM). Among these conditions, GDM is the most prevalent, characterized by varying degrees of abnormal glucose tolerance first identified during pregnancy. The current prevalence of GDM is 14% globally and ranges between 7.1% in North America and the Caribbean and 27.6% in the Middle East and north Africa region, and the incidence increases significantly in many countries [1–3]. Hyperglycemia during pregnancy poses numerous short- and long-term health risks for both mother and infant, including gestational hypertension, reproductive tract infections, and an elevated risk of postpartum type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease [4, 5]. For the fetus, it can result in preterm birth, dystocia, macrosomia, neonatal hypoglycemia, and an increased risk of metabolic diseases in adulthood [6].

The primary treatment for hyperglycemia in pregnancy involves dietary management and exercise; however, pharmacological intervention becomes necessary for those who do not achieve adequate glycemic control. Insulin is the preferred hypoglycemic agent during pregnancy, as it is a biological macromolecule that does not readily cross the placental barrier. Despite their efficacy, certain treatments are associated with limitations such as painful injections, frequent administration, complex procedures, high costs, and inconvenient storage requirements [7, 8]. In contrast, oral hypoglycemic agents provide advantages in terms of ease of use and convenience. Currently, only metformin and glibenclamide are recommended for managing gestational hyperglycemia [8, 9]. However, there are multiple contraindications to these two drugs. It is acknowledged that metformin frequently induces gastrointestinal discomfort and vitamin B12 deficiency, whereas glibenclamide is associated with hypoglycemia and other adverse effects. This underscores the urgent need to investigate novel pharmacological options that offer safer and more effective treatments for pregnant female with hyperglycemia.

Dipeptidyl peptidase-4 (DPP-4) inhibitors represent an emerging class of oral hypoglycemic agents that function by inhibiting the DPP-4 enzyme, thereby preventing the degradation of endogenous glucagon-like peptide-1 and glucose-dependent insulinotropic polypeptide, which contributes to their hypoglycemic effect. Although DPP-4 inhibitors such as sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin, have demonstrated safety, efficacy, and tolerability in the clinical management of type 2 diabetes in adults, they are not currently recommended for gestational hyperglycemia due to insufficient data on their clinical safety during pregnancy. Animal studies found no fetal toxicity with linagliptin and alogliptin [10, 11]. Sitagliptin at 100 times the human dose caused slight rib malformations in rat offspring with maternal toxicity [12]. Vildagliptin at 10-fold human dose led to rib deformities and weight loss in fetal and maternal rats, and at 9 times the dose, it caused fetal weight loss and skeletal issues in rabbits with maternal toxicity [13]. Other oral antidiabetic drugs, like sodium-glucose cotransporter 2 inhibitors and glucokinase activators, showed significant developmental toxicity in animal studies. Most DPP-4 inhibitors, however, showed no embryo toxicity, indicating safer potential use during pregnancy.

In addition to animal reproductive toxicity, drug safety in pregnancy also depends on fetal exposure, linked to placental drug transfer. Studies show sitagliptin and linagliptin cross rat and rabbit placentas [10, 12], and alogliptin crosses rat placentas [11], but the placental transport of vildagliptin is not well understood. To date, data regarding the human placental transport of the four DPP-4 inhibitors have not been documented. The syncytiotrophoblasts constitute the primary component of the placental barrier, serving to protect the fetus from numerous exogenous substances. Typically, only a limited number of lipophilic small molecules can permeate the placenta via passive diffusion. Nevertheless, certain hydrophilic compounds, such as metformin [14, 15], sulpiride [16], entecavir [17], and emtricitabine [18] can cross the placenta through transporter-mediated mechanisms due to the presence of various solute carrier (SLC) and ATP-binding cassette (ABC) transporters, located on either the maternal or fetal side of the syncytiotrophoblasts. BeWo (a human choriocarcinoma cell line) is a classic model for studying drug placental transport. HTR-8/SVneo (human extravillous trophoblasts from early pregnancy) and primary human trophoblast cells express multiple drug transporters, which can also be used to study the mechanism of drug uptake and transport [19].

Therefore, we initially developed a straightforward yet sensitive liquid chromatography-tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) method for the quantification of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin and alogliptin concentrations. Subsequently, we assessed the uptake and transport of four DPP-4 inhibitors using several placental cells, including BeWo, HTR-8/SVneo, and primary human trophoblast cells, and then investigated the underlying mechanisms of transmembrane transport. This study offers significant insights into the safety evaluation of DPP-4 inhibitors during pregnancy.

Materials and methods

Chemicals and reagents

Sitagliptin (purity=97%), vildagliptin (purity ≥ 99%), linagliptin (purity ≥ 99%), alogliptin (purity ≥ 99%), adenosine, phlorizin, dipyridamole, were provided by Aladdin Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Vildagliptin–d7 (internal standard, IS) was purchased from ChemStrong Scientific Co., Ltd (Shenzhen, China). Guanosine, cytidine, and uridine were from Meilun Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Dalian, China). 6-carboxylfluorescein (6-CF), probenecid, S-(4-nitrobenzyl)-6-thioinosine (NBTI), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), and ammonium acetate were provided by Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). HPLC-grade methanol and acetonitrile were provided by Merck&Co., Inc. (Darmstadt, Germany). Distilled water was from Watson Group Ltd. (Hong Kong, China). Hank’s balanced salt solution (HBSS) was from Biosharp Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Hefei, China).

Cell culture

BeWo, HTR-8/SVneo cells and corresponding culture medium were provided by Procell Life Science&Technology Co., Ltd. (Wuhan, China). BeWo cells were cultured in F12K supplemented with 15% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. HTR-8/SVneo cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin, respectively.

CHO cells, CHO cells stably transfected with full-length human organic anion transporter (OAT) 4 cDNA (CHO-hOAT4) and blank vector (CHO-mock) were donated by Professor Huidi Jiang (Zhejiang University) [20], and cultured in DMEM/F12 with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin. The cells were cultured at 37 °C in a humidified 5% (v/v) CO2 incubator.

Primary human trophoblast cells

Primary human trophoblast cells were isolated from human placenta uncomplicated and without viral infection delivered at full term (38 to 40 weeks) using a method we reported previously [16, 18, 20]. All procedures were performed in compliance with relevant laws and institutional guidelines and have been approved by the Ethics Committee of Affiliated Hangzhou First People’s Hospital (number: 2025ZN102-1, date: 20250408), School of Medicine, Westlake University. The privacy rights of human subjects have been observed, and all pregnant women signed their informed consent prior to the experiment. Primary human trophoblast cells were cultured in DMEM high glucose containing 15% FBS and 1% penicillin-streptomycin, and the cellular accumulation assays were carried out after 24 h of incubation. The purity of primary trophoblast cells was determined by CK7 immunohistochemical staining, and cells with CK7 positive staining rate greater than 90% were used for subsequent experiments.

Cellular accumulation assays

The accumulation of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin in BeWo, HTR-8/SVneo, and primary human trophoblast cells were conducted as the method described previously [16, 21]. After being washed and pre-incubated with HBSS (NaCl, 137.93 mM; KCl, 5.33 mM; KH2PO4, 0.44 mM; NaHCO3, 4.17 mM; Na2HPO4, 0.34 mM; CaCl2, 1.26 mM; MgSO4, 0.81 mM; D-glucose, 5.56 mM; HEPES, 25mM; pH 7.4) at 4–37 °C, the cells were exposed to HBSS containing sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin at designated concentrations to start the uptake process. At the designated time, the buffer was removed, and the cells were subsequently washed with ice-cold HBSS three times and 100 µL of 0.1% SDS was added into each well. The cells were fully lysed by pipetting up and down forcibly at least 20 times, and then liquid and foam were transferred to centrifuge tubes. After centrifugation at 12,000 g for 10 min, the cell lysate was obtained. The concentrations of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin in the cell lysates were analyzed by LC-MS/MS, the total protein content was detected using NanoDrop One (Thermo, USA), and drug accumulation was normalized to protein content to control cells number variation.

For uptake inhibition, BeWo cells were exposed to sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin with or without nucleoside transporter inhibitors (NBTI, phlorizin, dipyridamole, adenosine, guanosine, cytidine, and uridine) for 5 min.

CHO-hOAT4 and mock cells were seeded in 24-well plates at 2 × 105/well. Accumulation studies were carried out according to the method above. The activity of the transgenic cells was tested by using classical substrate 6-CF, and inhibitor probenecid.

Transwell experiments

BeWo cells formed a compact monolayer as previously described [22]. Briefly, they were seeded at 2 × 105 cells/mL in a 12-well Transwell with a 0.4 μm pore membrane (Corning Costar, New York, USA). The apical (AP) chamber received 0.5 mL of culture medium, while the basolateral (BL) chamber received 1.5 mL. The medium was changed every other day. Monolayer formation and integrity were assessed using transepithelial electrical resistance (TEER) and sodium-fluorescein leakage assays [22]. After 7 days, TEER exceeded 60 Ω⋅cm2, and the sodium-fluorescein permeability coefficient was below 1.0 × 10− 6 cm/s, confirming suitable monolayer permeability for transport experiments.

BeWo cells were treated with sitagliptin (1 µM) [23], vildagliptin (1 µM) [24], linagliptin (10 nM) [25], or alogliptin (0.3 µM) [26] at their respective maximum plasma concentrations (Cmax) under standard adult dosage. These compounds were introduced to either the AP or BL chamber to assess transfer in the AP→BL or BL→AP direction over various durations (15 to 180 min) at 37 °C. Blank HBSS solution was added to the opposite chamber. Samples of 60 µL were collected at each time point, with an equal volume of blank solvent replenished. Drug concentrations in the AP side, BL side, samples, and cells were measured using LC-MS/MS.

Knockdown of equilibrative nucleoside transporter (ENT)1 and ENT2 in BeWo cells

The knockdown method for ENT1 and ENT2 in BeWo cells and siRNA sequences were previously described in our study [21], then the mRNA and protein levels of ENT1/2 were confirmed, with ribavirin used to verify functional knockdown. Alogliptin accumulation studies were performed.

Real-time quantitative PCR (qRT-PCR) analysis

qRT-PCR was used to measure mRNA levels of ENT1/2 in BeWo cells, the detail methods and primer sequences were previously described [21].

Western blot

Cells were washed with ice-cold PBS, lysed in RIPA buffer (Beyotime, P0013B) with PMSF (Beyotime, ST506), mixed with loading buffer (Sangon, C508320) and then boiled. Protein samples were separated via electrophoresis (Mini-PROTEAN Tetra System, Bio-Rad), transferred to 0.45 μm PVDF membranes (Millipore, IPVH00010), blocked with 5% BSA in TBST, and incubated overnight with ENT1 (Proteintech, 11337-1-AP) or ENT2 antibodies (Abcam, EPR11674). Membranes were washed and treated with HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies.

Concentrative nucleoside transporter (CNT)1/2/3 and ENT1/2 overexpression

CHO cells were transfected with human ENT1/2 and CNT1/2/3 using the method previously reported [21], then alogliptin accumulation studies were performed.

LC-MS/MS analysis and method validation of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin

Sample preparation

To determine the concentrations of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin, 60 µL of cell lysate was mixed with 120 µL acetonitrile (containing 20 nM vildagliptin-d7), vortexed for 5 min, and centrifuged at 13,000 g for 15 min. A 2 µL supernatant sample was injected into LC–MS/MS.

Chromatography

A LC-30AD system (Shimadzu, Kyoto, Japan) along with an ACQUITY UPLC BEH C18 column (2.1 × 50 mm, 1.7 μm, Waters, USA) was used in our study. The gradient elution used 10 mM ammonium acetate-water (A) and acetonitrile (B) as mobile phases (0–1.5 min, 10%-90% of B; 1.5–2.4 min, 90% of B; 2.4–2.5 min, 90%-10% of B; 2.5–4.0 min, 10% of B; 0.3 mL/min). Column and autosampler temperatures were 35 °C and 15 °C, respectively.

Mass spectrometric conditions

Analysis was performed using a 4500 triple quadrupole mass spectrometer (AB Sciex, Redwood City, CA, USA) in positive mode, with specific m/z transitions and optimized parameters listed in Table 1. The entrance potential was 12 V, cell exit potential was 10 V, spray voltage was 5500 V, and source temperature was 550 °C for all compounds.

Preparation of calibration standards and quality control (QC) samples

Stock solutions of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, alogliptin, and vildagliptin-d7 were prepared in DMSO (10 mM) and stored at -20℃. Working solutions were made by diluting stocks with 50% methanol to concentrations of 0.03, 0.06, 0.15, 0.6, 1.5, 3, 6 and 15 µM. Calibration standards were created by mixing 2 µL of working solution with 58 µL of blank cell matrix, resulting in final concentrations of 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200 nM for sitagliptin and vildagliptin, and 1, 2, 5, 10, 20, 50, 100, 200, 500 nM for linagliptin and alogliptin. QC samples for method validation were prepared at low (3 nM), medium (40 nM for sitagliptin and vildagliptin; 120 nM for linagliptin and alogliptin), and high (160 nM for sitagliptin and vildagliptin; 400 nM for linagliptin and alogliptin) concentrations.

Method validation

The validation of the method was performed in accordance with the guidelines set forth by the United States Food and Drug Administration [31]. Selectivity was assessed by analyzing both a blank cell matrix and a spiked blank cell matrix at the lower limit of quantification (LLOQ). Calibration curves were constructed using a linear weighted (1/x) least squares regression model, which plotted the peak area ratios of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin relative to the internal standard (IS) against their nominal concentrations. The accuracy and precision, both intra-batch and inter-batch, were evaluated at the LLOQ, as well as at low, middle, and high QC levels, with calculations of relative standard deviation (RSD). The matrix effect and extraction recovery for the compounds were determined according to previously described methods [32].

Stability tests were conducted at low and high QC levels, encompassing freeze-thaw cycles (from − 20 °C to room temperature, repeated three times), long-term storage (at -20 °C for 30 days), and autosampler stability (at 15 °C for 24 h).

Statistical analysis

Data was presented as mean ± standard deviation (SD). At least n = 3 in this study, which represents three independent experiments, each with 3–5 technical replicates. Statistical analyses were performed using Graph Pad Prism 9.0 (Graph Pad Software Inc., San Diego, USA). Student’s t-test was used to compare differences between two groups. ANOVA with Dunnett’s multiple comparison test was used to evaluate the statistical differences among multiple groups. Values of P < 0.05 were considered statistically significant.

Sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin transport in BeWo transwell model

To comprehensively assess the transplacental passage of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin, the in vitro BeWo Transwell model was employed. As depicted in Fig. 5A-D, the transmembrane transport volumes of the four DPP-4 inhibitors in both the maternal-fetal (AP→BL) and fetal-maternal (BL→AP) directions exhibited a linear increase over a 180-min period. The Papp values (Fig. 5E) were comparable in the AP→BL and BL→AP directions for linagliptin (ER = 1.0), alogliptin (ER = 1.17), sitagliptin (ER = 1.19), and vildagliptin (ER = 0.99). Furthermore, the Papp values for all four DPP-4 inhibitors exceeded 2 × 10− 6 cm/s, suggesting moderate permeability. The Papp were observed as follows: linagliptin ≈ alogliptin ≈ sitagliptin > vildagliptin, which suggested that vildagliptin might exhibit the lowest rate of transplacental transport among the four DPP-4 inhibitors.

ENTs and CNTs did not participate in the transmembrane transport of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin and alogliptin

To further elucidate the transmembrane transport mechanisms of these DPP-4 inhibitors, we analyzed their physicochemical properties (Table 5). Due to the low logD values of linagliptin and alogliptin, these compounds are suggested to be hydrophilic. However, their cellular accumulation and transmembrane transport rate are relatively high, and their purine-and pyrimidine-like structures lead us to hypothesize that nucleoside transporters may be involved in their transmembrane transport. Our results showed that the accumulation of linagliptin, sitagliptin, and vildagliptin were not affected by NBTI (an ENT-specific inhibitor) and phlorizin (a CNT inhibitor) (Fig. 6A-C). In contrast, the accumulation of alogliptin was reduced by nucleoside transporter inhibitors NBTI, dipyridamole (an ENT-specific inhibitor), and phlorizin in BeWo cells (Fig. 6D). To further explore the potential involvement of nucleoside transporters in alogliptin uptake in BeWo cells, we utilized siRNAs to knock down ENT1 and ENT2. The mRNA and protein levels of ENT1/2 were confirmed (Fig. 6E, F), with ribavirin used to verify functional knockdown (Fig. 6G). However, alogliptin accumulation remained unchanged (Fig. 6H). Additionally, we employed CHO cells to overexpress multiple nucleoside transporters. Interestingly, the accumulation of alogliptin in CHO-hENT1/2 and CHO-hCNT1/2/3 cells was lower than in CHO-mock cells, and the nucleoside transporter inhibitor adenosine exhibited no inhibitory effect (Fig. 6I). These results suggest that ENTs and CNTs may not be involved in the cellular uptake of linagliptin, alogliptin, sitagliptin, and vildagliptin.

Sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin and alogliptin were not substrates for OAT4

The mRNA expression of drug transporters in BeWo, HTR-8/SVneo, and primary human trophoblast cells was shown in Fig. 6J. OAT4 has been identified as the sole OAT transporter significantly expressed in the human placenta [33], yet it exhibits minimal expression in BeWo and HTR-8/SVneo cells. Consequently, we utilized CHO-hOAT4 cells to investigate the potential transport of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin and alogliptin by OAT4. Initially, we validated the functionality of CHO-hOAT4 cells using 6-CF, a known substrate of OATs (Fig. 7A). The accumulation of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin and alogliptin in CHO-hOAT4 cells did not differ from that observed in mock cells, and the inhibitor probenecid exhibited no inhibitory effect (Fig. 7B-E). These findings indicate that sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin are not substrates for OAT4, suggesting that OAT4 is unlikely to facilitate the transport of these compounds in the placenta.

Discussion

In this study, we evaluated the uptake and transport of four DPP-4 inhibitors, sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin, using multiple placental barrier cell models. Accurate detection of these drugs in cells is crucial, but existing methods focus on plasma, not cellular matrices. Due to low drug concentrations, small sample volumes, and interference from endogenous substances, a highly sensitive method is needed. We developed and validated a novel LC-MS/MS method for simultaneously quantifying these inhibitors in cell matrices, meeting the needs of future research.

In standard adult doses, the maximum plasma concentrations of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin are approximately 1, 1, 0.01, and 0.3 µM, respectively [23–26]. Therefore, we performed transport assays using the above concentrations in BeWo Transwell model, and the results showed that all four DPP-4 inhibitors could easily cross the placental barrier within 180 min with Papp > 2 × 10− 6 cm/s, and similar transport rates in both the maternal-fetal and fetal-maternal directions, with efflux ratios below 1.5 (Fig. 5E).

Linagliptin and alogliptin exhibited higher cellular accumulation and Papp in BeWo cells rates than vildagliptin (Figs. 3, 4 and 5), which doesn’t align with their logD and pKa values (Table 5). And the concentration-dependent uptake assays at 37 ℃ and 4 ℃ suggested that transporters may mediate their transmembrane transport (Fig. 4). Human placenta and BeWo cells express multiple nucleoside transporters, including equilibrative and concentrative types, which transport natural purine and pyrimidine nucleosides and structurally similar drugs [17]. Given that linagliptin and alogliptin contain purine and pyrimidine structures, respectively, we hypothesized that nucleoside transporters might facilitate their transport based on structural similarity. Although the nucleoside transporter inhibitor NBTI, dipyridamole and phlorizin blocked alogliptin uptake in BeWo cells, adenosine, cytidine, guanosine, and uridine had no inhibitory effect (Fig. 6D). The accumulation of linagliptin in BeWo cells was unaffected by nucleoside transporter inhibitors (Fig. 6A). In addition, siRNA knockdown experiments and transporter overexpression cell models showed that alogliptin was not a substrate for ENT1/2 or CNT1/2/3 (Fig. 6E-I). The above results indicate that ENTs and CNTs might not transport linagliptin and alogliptin in the placenta cells. The molecular recognition of substrates by ENTs and CNTs depends not only on the purine/pyrimidine base, but critically on the integrity of the ribose moiety [34]. Although linagliptin and alogliptin possess heterocyclic structures, their benzene rings, piperidine rings, or fatty side chains may introduce steric hindrance, impeding the docking of heterocyclic rings into the transporter binding pocket and consequently preventing recognition as substrates.

Overexpression of the nucleoside transporter unexpectedly decreased alogliptin uptake (Fig. 6I). A paper reports alogliptin ‘s pKa as 8.5 and logP as -1.4 [35], differing from our article, but both agree it’s hydrophilic. At pH 7.4, alogliptin mainly exists in a cation form with limited passive diffusion, suggesting carrier-mediated transport might aid its absorption, distribution, and excretion. Researchers found a fruit-juice-sensitive transporter involved in the intestinal absorption of alogliptin, but not organic anion transporting polypeptide (OATP) 2B1 [35]. Another study using cell models and knockout mice showed alogliptin was a substrate for mouse multidrug and toxin extrusion protein (MATE) 1, not human MATE1 [36]. We speculate that nucleoside transporter overexpression might impact the fruit-juice-sensitive transporter, increase efflux transporters, or change cell membrane potential or ionic gradients, thereby reducing the uptake of alogliptin, but the specific reasons need to be further explored.

Research on DPP-4 inhibitors and transporters is limited. Previous study showed that linagliptin is a substrate of organic cation transporter (OCT) 2 and P-glycoprotein (P-gp), but not OAT1/3/4 [37]. Sitagliptin is transported by human OAT3, OATP4C1, P-gp [38], and MATE1 [36], while vildagliptin interacts with P-gp [39] but not MATE1 [36]. Despite high OAT4 expression in the placenta, none of the four DPP-4 inhibitors are OAT4 substrates (Fig. 7), indicating OAT4 might not transport sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin in the placenta. In addition to ENTs, CNTs and OAT4, drug transporters that are abundantly expressed in the placenta include organic cation transporter (OCT) 3, carnitine/organic cation transporter (OCTN) 1/2, organic anion-transporting polypeptides (OATPs), and ABC efflux transporters [40]. Consequently, further investigation is necessary to explore the role of these transporters in the placental transport of DPP-4 inhibitors, with the aim of elucidating the mechanisms underlying their placental transfer.

Research indicates that the serum level or activity of DPP-4 are significantly elevated in GDM patients, implying a potential association between DPP-4 and the pathogenesis of GDM [41–43]. DPP-4 inhibitors are known for their low risk of inducing hypoglycemia and gastrointestinal side effects, positioning them as potential therapeutic agents for managing hyperglycemia during pregnancy. Presently, clinical studies regarding the use of DPP-4 inhibitors in GDM are concentrated on the postpartum period [44–46]. Notably, a small-scale (206 participants) double-blind randomized controlled trial has demonstrated that administering sitagliptin during the second trimester can enhance fasting blood glucose levels, serum insulin levels, β-cell function, and insulin resistance in GDM patients, without adverse effects in either mothers or newborns [47]. Furthermore, a recent extensive multicenter observational cohort study found no increase in major congenital malformations in newborns exposed to DPP-4 inhibitors during early pregnancy compared to those exposed to insulin [48]. Nevertheless, there remains a paucity of research on the short- and long-term safety of DPP-4 inhibitor exposure in offspring.

If DPP-4 inhibitors become beneficial for managing hyperglycemia during pregnancy, it is advisable to select the drug with the minimal placental transport rate, because the lower fetal exposure, the higher pregnancy safety. Our study indicates that vildagliptin exhibits the least accumulation in three placental barrier cell models and the smallest transport rate in BeWo cells, suggesting minimal fetal exposure. However, experiments in rats and rabbits have shown that vildagliptin can cause skeletal malformations in fetuses. On the contrary, alogliptin and linagliptin, which have higher transport rates, do not show reproductive toxicity in animals. We speculate that it may be due to the species difference between humans and animals, or the inconsistency between in vitro and in vivo experiments.

Limitations and recommendations for future research

Forskolin can induce BeWo cells to differentiate into syncytiotrophoblasts, resembling the placental barrier. However, our previous research showed that forskolin-treated BeWo cells have significantly lower expression of multiple drug transporters [17]. Thus, like previous ones [16, 18], this study used untreated BeWo cells. Primary human cytotrophoblasts cells were isolated, and syncytiotrophoblasts formed spontaneously after 72 h. However, drug transporter levels were lower at 72 h compared to 24 h [16], so experiments were conducted at 24 h in our study. In future studies, the use of syncytiotrophoblasts could be considered to better mimic the physiological placental barrier.

The human placenta is more complex than cell models, so caution is needed when applying in vitro results to in vivo situations. Although BeWo cells are commonly used to study placental transport, their transporter expression is different from that of human placentas. HTR-8/SVneo cells also lack multiple transporters, and primary human trophoblast cells are most similar to placenta in transporter expression. Human and animal placentas also show inconsistent transporter patterns. Ethical concerns limit drug transport studies in pregnant women, so researchers use multiple in vitro models, including placental cell lines, primary trophoblast cells, transporter overexpression models, placental villi explants, and human placenta perfusion, to study drug transplacental transport and its mechanisms.

Our study provides a reference for future research on placental transport mechanisms but does not offer direct medication recommendations for GDM patients. Further studies are needed to assess the safety and efficacy of DPP-4 inhibitors for hyperglycemia in pregnant women.

Conclusions

A cost-effective LC-MS/MS method was developed and validated for the simultaneous quantification of sitagliptin, vildagliptin, linagliptin, and alogliptin in cell matrices, and applied to investigate their uptake and transport in placental cell models. Vildagliptin showed the lowest accumulation and permeability. There might be carrier-mediated transport of these drugs, but ENTs, CNTs and OAT4 were not involved in. To our knowledge, this is the first study comparing the uptake and transport of these four DPP-4 inhibitors in placental cell models, offering a reference for future research on their placental transport and pregnancy safety.
