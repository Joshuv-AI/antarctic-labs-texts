3D reconstruction of a model phytophagous mite at nano-resolution reveals a peculiar ingestion and digestion system and structural adaptation for a tiny animal
Authors: Jian-Sheng Guo, Dan-Ting Li, Guan Wang, Xin-Qiu Wang, Bernard Moussian, Chuan-Xi Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe fine structure of the globally distributed phytophagous mite, Tetranychus urticae, has been elucidated using serial block-face scanning electron microscopy (SBF-SEM) combined with 3D reconstruction. The reconstructed models reveal previously unknown internal adaptations tailored to its unique lifestyle. These include a specialized food ingestion structure to facilitate effective cell-sucking, a semi-closed-loop tracheal system optimized for efficient oxygen diffusion in a minute organism, and a pair of well-developed silk glands utilized for water repellency, defense and dispersion. Notably, the midgut occupies nearly the entire body cavity, housing 189 (male) and 636 (female) suspended digestive cells, except for the reproductive system. No observable connections or channels were found linking two midgut regions (ventriculus and posterior midgut), nor were valve structures or controlling muscles detected in this region in most cases, suggesting the presence of an intermittent, dynamically regulated midgut. This adaptation may address the functional demands of unique digestive cells suspended within the ventriculus. However, a funnel-shaped midgut capable of forming temporary channels for the disabled transport of digestive cells from the ventriculus to the posterior midgut was identified. These findings profoundly advance our understanding of how minute animals adapt their internal systems to specialized lifestyles.Keywords: Tetranychus urticae, 3D reconstruction, Internal structure, Serial block-face scanning electron microscopy, Digestive tract

Introduction

Structure profoundly governs function in biology, and the transition from 2D to 3D imaging has revolutionized our understanding of the intricate mechanisms underlying physiological processes. Recent advancements in volume electron microscopy, such as those discussed by Peddie et al. [1], are poised to reshape the scientific landscape, as highlighted by Nature as “seven technologies to watch in 2023” [2].

Mites, as members of the chelicerates, rank second only to insects in terms of terrestrial animal diversity. Thriving in diverse environments and distributed globally, they play vital ecological roles. Among them, the two-spotted spider mite, Tetranychus urticae Koch (Acari: Tetranychidae), is a highly polyphagous pest that poses significant threats to both global food security and ecological equilibrium [3,4]. With a wealth of genetic tools, a high-quality genome sequence, and ease of maintenance in laboratory settings, T. urticae has emerged as a valuable model species for the study of phytophagous mites. Phytophagous mites exhibit extraordinary biological adaptations rarely seen in arthropods. These minute organisms utilize specialized stylets to ingest cellular debris and cell sap, while their enormous digestive cells remain suspended in the ventriculus lumen rather than being sequestered into epithelial cells-a digestive strategy distinct from typical arthropod models. Their sub-millimeter body size (male T. urticae 325 µm length × 249 µm width) necessitates hyper-efficient respiratory systems to support high metabolic rates during constant movement. Remarkably, these mites combine silk production for offspring protection and aerodynamic dispersal with sophisticated internal systems that remain poorly characterized despite decades of light/electron microscopy studies [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11], [12]]. There are a lot of unresolved anatomical mysteries, for example, what structural adaptations enable their delicate stylets to generate sufficient suction force through micro-scale food canals to process viscous cell contents? How do enormous digestive cells remain suspended in the ventriculus while senescent cells are selectively expelled into the hindgut? What respiratory innovations support their oxygen demands during constant movement? What cellular mechanisms drive the development of highly efficient silk glands and silk-extrusion apparatuses?

In this study, using SBF-SEM and 3D reconstruction techniques, we aim to reveal a novel 3D internal structure that demonstrates adaptive co-evolution with the unique lifestyles of micro-arthropods, particularly their ingestion and digestive systems. We hypothesize that these findings will not only elucidate behavioral strategies in minute arthropods but also provide critical insights for optimizing mite pest management strategies through enhanced biological understanding.

Materials and methods

Animals

The T. urticae used in this research were sourced from the Institute of Vegetable and Flowers, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences. These mites were cultured on Vigna unguiculata (Linn.) Walp., commonly known as beans, under controlled environmental conditions. The rearing temperature was maintained at 26 ± 0.5 °C, with a relative humidity of 60% ± 5%, and a photoperiod of 16:8 h (light:dark). In this study, 5 male mites and 2 female mites were analyzed by 3D reconstruction. And 5 male mites and 5 female mites were analyzed by conventional 2D SEM to observe the mouthparts, spinneret and body.

Observation of the stylet movement

To observe the movement of the stylets, a group of mites were carefully placed in a Petri dish, and the dish was securely sealed using parafilm. To facilitate the observation, a few drops of sugar solution were applied onto the surface of the parafilm. Subsequently, an additional layer of parafilm was placed on top to enclose the nutrient solution within. The movement of the stylets was then closely monitored and recorded using a stereomicroscope, capturing a video for further analysis and examination.

Samples and preparation

For this study, we meticulously prepared T. urticae samples and captured digital images of the entire bodies of male, female and larva, as well as specific body parts, ensuring a high-resolution capture that would allow for the reconstruction of muscles, nerves, glands, and tracheae (Supplementary file 1, Table S1). The sample preparation was conducted following the procedure described in the study of Wang et al. [13].

Data collection

To prepare the resin blocks for imaging, a Leica EM trimmer was used to carefully trim the blocks until the surface of the black tissue within the block became visible. These trimmed resin blocks were then securely attached to a stub using electrically conductive colloidal silver (TED PELLA, Lot No.: 169773610). A coating of approximately 30 nm platinum was applied to the resin blocks using a sputter coater (Leica, ACE200).

The 3D data acquisition process was carried out using a SEM (Thermo Fisher, Teneo VS) equipped with an ultramicrotome within the specimen chamber. This unique setup allowed for simultaneous sectioning of the resin blocks and imaging of the sample surface. The section thickness and pixel size for each sample are provided in Table S1. During imaging, an acceleration voltage of 2.5 kV and a current of 0.2 nA were applied in backscatter mode using a VS DBS detector. The image store resolution was set at either 6144 × 6144 pixels with a dwell time of 2 µs per pixel or 8192 × 8192 pixels with a dwell time of 1 µs per pixel.

For the acquisition of 3D data of sperm, the resin blocks were mounted on a 90° stub and coated with platinum using an ion sputtering apparatus (Leica, EM ACE200) for a duration of 300 s. The SEM (Thermo Fisher, Teneo VS) with an ultramicrotome in its specimen chamber was again utilized to trim the resin blocks and capture EM images of the target area. After locating the target area, the resin blocks were transferred to a 45° pre-tilted SEM stub and coated with platinum for 300 s using an ion sputtering apparatus (Leica, EM ACE200).

Imaging of the target area was performed using a dual-beam SEM (Thermo Fisher, FIB Helios G3 UC) in the serial surface view mode. The slice thickness was set at 10 nm, and imaging was conducted at 30 keV and 0.79 nA. Each serial surface was imaged at an acceleration voltage of 2 keV and a current of 0.2 nA in backscatter mode (BSE) using an in-column backscatter electron detector (ICD). The sample stage was tilted at an angle of 7° during the milling process and 45° when capturing electron beam images. The image frame size was set to 4096 × 3536 pixels, with a dwell time of 3 µs per pixel.

Image processing and segmentation

The images were aligned, filtered, manually segmented, and used to generate surfaces using the Amira 2021.1 (Thermo Fisher Scientific) software.

Conventional SEM

For conventional scanning electron microscopy (CSEM) analysis, the T. urticae that were feeding on bean leaves were first subjected to a rapid freezing process using liquid nitrogen. After freezing, the samples were prepared following a procedure described in the previous study [14]. To ensure the preservation of the samples’ structure, the dehydrated samples were subjected to critical point drying using an automated critical point dryer (Leica, CPD300). This process involved replacing the ethanol with a solvent such as liquid carbon dioxide under controlled conditions to avoid the collapse of delicate structures.

Next, the dried samples were mounted on stubs using double-sided carbon tape, and a thin layer of conductive material, specifically 10 nm of gold, was applied to the sample surfaces using a Leica ACE200 sputter coater. This conductive coating helps to minimize charging effects and improve the image quality during SEM. Finally, the prepared samples were examined using a field emission scanning electron microscope (Thermo Fisher, Helios UC G3) to obtain high-resolution images of the T. urticae’s external morphology and surface features.

Terminology

To maintain consistency and facilitate comparison with previous studies, the authors of this research chose to adopt established terminology for structures that have been previously described and identified in other arthropods, particularly in the Tetranychidae or other mites [12,[15], [16], [17], [18], [19]]. Rather than introducing new terms, the authors utilized existing abbreviations for these structures, which are provided in Table S2 (Supplementary file 1) for reference. By using established terminology, the findings of this study can be easily related to the existing body of knowledge in the field.

Results

Structures of male and female T. urticae bodies

T. urticae are diminutive arachnids, with males measuring approximately 325 µm in length and females approximately 598 µm in length. The males have a maximum width of 172 µm, while females measure 249 µm in width. Due to the notable size disparity between male and female mites, our study predominantly centers on the high-resolution 3D reconstruction of the male T. urticae based on SBF-SEM and 3D reconstruction. We have conducted comprehensive 3D reconstructions encompassing the entire T. urticae body and the underlying organ systems responsible for functions like food ingestion, digestion, excretion, glands, locomotion, gas exchange, perception, and reproduction beneath the integument (as illustrated in Fig. 1, and supplementary results 1.1). To display what all these segmentations are based on, movies (Video 1–3 and 6) were created to show the 2D images and 3D reconstruction results of male T. urticae. The abbreviations for different structures used in the text are provided in Table S2 (Supplementary file 1).

Notably, our investigation of T. urticae revealed numerous previously undocumented internal structures. Key discoveries include: a interconnected, semi-closed tracheal network though constituting only ∼0.3% of the male T. urticae’s body volume (total volume: 9110 µm³, see detail information in the supplementary results 1.2), this respiratory system likely enables efficient oxygen diffusion within the mite’s compact body; a pharyngeal plunger, operated by collagenous fibers and muscles, represents a novel ultra-efficient suction apparatus for cellular debris; our observations further characterize an incomplete alimentary canal with distinct discontinuities (see below); a sophisticated silk-spinning system comprises paired silk glands containing large secretory cells producing ultra-fine silk for water repellency, defense mechanisms, dispersal and drifting, and inter-individual communication (see detail information in the supplementary results 1.3); other auxiliary physiological systems, include paired salivary glands and prosomal glands for digestive secretion (see detail information in the supplementary results 1.4); complex muscular system enabling motility (see detail information in the supplementary results 1.5); highly condensed central nervous system regulating physiological/behavioral functions (see detail information in the supplementary results 1.6), peculiar reproductive systems for offspring production (see detail information in the supplementary results 1.7), wide spread endosymbiont distribution in the body (see detail information in the supplementary results 1.8) are also reconstructed and documented. In the following sections, we mainly focus on the most interesting discoveries: ultra-efficient suction apparatus-structural adaptations in the stylet-pharynx complex enabling viscous cell sap aspiration and the discontinuous midgut-compartmentalized organization of food processing and waste elimination.

Characteristic food ingestion structure

The mouthpart of T. urticae is mainly composed of chelicera, rostrum and palpi, which are adapted to plant feeding. From the perspective of feeding behavior, mites ingesting cellular debris and cytoplasmic sap are likely to face greater challenges in obtaining nutrients compared to other insects that feed on phloem sap. Given the minuscule size of mites, they may require more robust sucking structures to adapt to their feeding pattern and to meet the demands of their lifestyle. The pharynx plunger is located above the foregut immediately after the pharynx (Fig. 2f). The pharynx dilator projects posteriorly and ventrally and is equipped with a robust overlying plunger. This plunger is actuated by two sets of collagenous fibers and 14 pairs of pharyngeal dilators. Additionally, there are six pairs of pharyngeal depressors situated between the sets of dilator muscles (Fig. 2i,j). It is hypothesized that the pharyngeal depressors may work antagonistically to the pharyngeal dilators. An interesting observation was made by comparing the 3D models of the heads with protruded and withdrawn stylets; when the stylets were protruded, the distal pharyngeal dilators at the end of the plunger expanded outwardly. In contrast, the pharyngeal dilators in the head with withdrawn stylets were closely apposed. This observation suggests that when the stylets are protruded, the mite is piercing plant cells, while with withdrawn stylets, the mite is ingesting plant fluids (Figs. 2c,f,i,j, 6).

Intermittent dynamically regulated digestive system

The alimentary canal of T. urticae comprises several distinct regions, including the foregut, midgut, hindgut, and anal sac (Fig. 3a,b). The midgut of T. urticae, comprising the ventriculus, caeca, and posterior midgut, dominates the opisthosoma, nearly filling its entirety. The midgut of the male T. urticae accounts for approximately 43.8% of its body volume, with a total volume of 1,241,700 µm³ (as detailed in Table 1). It is characterized by a single-layered epithelium enveloping the midgut lumen. The lumen is divided into three cranial caeca (c1) and two caudal caeca (c2), interconnected through the ventriculus. Each of the c2 possesses three lobes (i, ii, iii) positioned laterally (Fig. 3a3). The caeca contain three distinct epithelial cell types with varying shapes. The ventriculus and the caeca harbor large, free-floating digestive cells at different developmental stages that contribute to digestion and resorption. Large cells housing short microvilli projecting into the ventriculus are also absorptive in function. Additionally, the large cells with small vesicles form the inner wall of the c2, extending laterally along the anterior-posterior axis of the T. urticae body, adjacent to the posterior midgut. However, no clear opening or channel connecting the ventriculus to the posterior midgut was observed in either males or females in a state without digestive cells in the posterior midgut of T. urticae. To further confirm the peculiar, dynamically regulated alimentary canal, we generated 3D models of newly hatched T. urticae larvae (Fig. S1) and three additional adult mites. The alimentary canal in T. urticae larvae resembles that of adults, showing an incompletely continuous structure. However, the midgut lumen of larvae does not yet contain digestive cells (Fig. S1e,f). As feeding progresses, digestive cells develop from digestive precursor cells near the posterior midgut.

The posterior midgut, a significant tube running along the dorsal midline, originates above the posterior portion of the ventriculus, enlarges and continues posteriorly above c2, and connects to the hindgut rectum towards its end. The dorsal and lateral walls of the posterior midgut are composed of microvillous cells with long microvilli projecting into the lumen, resulting in an enlarged cell surface (Fig. 3a4,a5). Vacuoles, similar to those present in the large cells, are also evident in the long microvilli near the membrane. It can be hypothesized that substances may be transported between the large cells and the long microvilli (i.e., between the ventriculus and the posterior midgut) via processes like pinocytosis and/or phagocytosis (Figs. 3c and S2). The ventral wall of the posterior midgut consists of another type of microvilli with short microvilli projecting into the lumen. The short microvilli, along with the ventricular epithelial cells, contain numerous electron-lucent vacuoles, likely lipid droplets, suggesting that these cells may obtain nutrients from the lumen (Fig. 3b1). Following the posterior midgut is the hindgut, comprising the narrow, cuticle-lined rectum and the anal slit (Fig. 3b2,b3). The posterior midgut of female mites displays similar 3D and 2D structures at high resolution (Fig. S3, Video 4). The rectum forms a meandering and narrow slit leading to the anus, serving as an organ for both excretion and defecation.

The ventriculus and the caeca house numerous digestive cells (189 in the male model and 636 in the female model) at various developmental stages, as evidenced by their distinct ultrastructures (Fig. 4a). Newly formed digestive cells with classic nuclei and abundant endosymbionts are identifiable (Fig. 4b), while mature digestive cells exhibit extensive vesicles and a nucleus that is challenging to discern (Fig. 4c,d). Endosymbiont signals are found both inside and outside the digestive cells, whereas some empty cells with clear membranes and vesicles lack them (Fig. 4b). Strikingly, we observed a direct connection between the end of the ES and the digestive cells, suggesting that food initially contacts these cells (Fig. 4g). Prior studies have suggested that digestive cells can migrate from the midgut to the posterior midgut, given that a similar cellular structure has been observed in the posterior midgut using light microscopy. Our high-resolution electron microscopy images reveal no discernible opening or channel connecting the ventriculus to the posterior midgut in most cases. Furthermore, no valve structures or controlling muscles were observed at the junction of the ventriculus joining the posterior midgut.

Additionally, the posterior midgut contains excretion particles without a membrane (Fig. 4h), which differs from the empty digestive cells, maybe guanine crystals (Fig. 4e) [20,21]. We have tracked several mites, recorded the movement of digestive cells and the excretion process of the mites (Video 7 & 8). Therefore, in most cases, the digestive system of mites is in a discontinuous state.

A funnel-shaped midgut mediates the transport of aging digestive cells from the posterior midgut to the posterior midgut

Contrary to canonical digestive tract architectures described in lectures, our 3D reconstruction revealed no anatomical conduits or valve-like structures connecting the ventriculus to the posterior midgut and the absence of specialized musculature or sphincter-like systems in this region, collectively suggesting the existence of an intermittent, non-continuous digestive pathway. However, correlative evidence from light microscopy (Fig. 1b) and 3D reconstruction analyses further corroborated that aging intact digestive cells were retained within the posterior midgut (Fig. 5), implying active translocation mechanisms for old digestive cells. To elucidate this transport mechanism, we performed targeted 3D reconstructions of mites exhibiting digestive cell migration. This approach identified a structurally distinct funnel-shaped midgut forming direct epithelial connections between the ventriculus and posterior midgut, containing two digestive cells in the posterior midgut (Fig. 5). Notably, the funnel-shaped midgut exhibited contractile morphology during cellular translocation, demonstrating its capacity to form transient conduits for senescent digestive cells. This anatomical feature not only resolves the paradox of intact digestive cells’ retention in the posterior midgut but also notably facilitates differential processing of viable and senescent cells through compartmentalized midgut regions.

Discussion

In this study, we employed SBF-SEM to conduct integrated 3D reconstructions of male and female T. urticae at the nano-scale, allowing us to visualize internal structures in greater detail. While the previous study [14] focused on a whole 1st instar nymph of N. lugens with a length of 600 µm, we were able to capture more intricate internal structures, including the reproductive systems, of the smaller T. urticae adults. However, sample preparation for mites presented a challenge in SBF-SEM due to their waterproof cuticle, which hindered normal fixation and staining processes. To overcome this challenge, we induced wounds by cutting the legs, which facilitated more effective fixation and staining while minimizing sample damage. Consequently, the 3D models of T. urticae did not include the legs, and the total volume calculations excluded the legs. Nonetheless, we were able to identify the position of the legs in newly hatched larvae, which possessed only three pairs of legs. The position of the fourth pair of legs could be determined by specific differentiated cells, and we also reconstructed the differentiated cells of the fourth pair of legs that form during the larvae’s development into protonymphs (Fig. S1).

SBF-SEM has proven to be a versatile technique capable of handling various sample sizes, from tiny insects to collagen fibrils [22]. Leveraging this technology, we reconstructed detailed integrated systems of T. urticae involved in food ingestion, digestion, excretion, gland function, locomotion (musculature), gas exchange, perception, and reproduction. We have not only identified the locations and a number of different kinds of muscles, and the composition and function of the complete glandular system, but also described the nervous system that is highly condensed in the central nervous mass and from which the radiating nerves extend to various regions of the body. Furthermore, the models provided new evidence supporting previous studies and revealed novel findings regarding the internal structures, including the presence of a connected semi-closed-loop tracheal system in the hysterosoma, the involvement of collagenous fibers and muscles in powering the PP, the well-developed protective silk glands, and the potential interruption of continuity in the alimentary canal.

The process of food ingestion, digestion, and excretion in T. urticae is a fascinating and intricate phenomenon. These mites are highly adaptable herbivores, possessing specialized mouthparts akin to phloem-feeding insects. Unlike other herbivores that subsist on plant phloem sap, T. urticae directly targets individual cells within the leaf mesophyll [23]. Research on T. urticae feeding behavior has elucidated their use of specialized structures called stylets to penetrate the leaf mesophyll and ingest the contents of individual mesophyll cells one at a time [24]. Nevertheless, the exact mechanism of how T. urticae feeds on plant mesophyll remains a topic of debate due to the challenges in observing spider mite feeding and misconceptions about their morphology. One proposed mechanism suggests that T. urticae ingest plant cell contents through their stylets [17,24], while another theory posits that T. urticae “laps” the cell contents, utilizing capillary forces to bring them to the plant's surface and then into their buccal cavity [25,26]. Our results showed that stylet protrusion pierces plant tissues while secreting saliva, followed by partial withdrawal and interlocking with the propharynx to form a food channel as pharynx lumen expansion generates active fluid uptake through powerful pumping action, distinguishing this mechanism from passive lapping behavior (Fig. 6). During feeding, the mites manipulate their stylets, leading to the insertion of a single stylet into the first layer of the parafilm membrane (Video 5). The path of food within the gnathosoma involves the sucked-up plant cell contents traversing the stylet channel to reach the prelabial cavity, where the two stylets are no longer interlocked [17,24]. This path aligns with the observation that there are no connections between the stylet tubes and the mouth. When the labrum is pressed against the dorsal wall of the supralabral cavity and the concave side of the stylets, cell contents enter the pharynx through the pharyngeal opening. The labrum can be lowered to obstruct the pharyngeal rod, but allows the discharge of secretory material from the salivary gland into the prelabial cavity. The labrum’s up-and-down movement potentially aids in mixing secretory materials and cell contents before they are drawn into the pharynx [24,27]. The process of food ingestion can be interpreted as the food entering the pharynx through the pharyngeal rod is regulated by the movement of the pump, driven by circular muscles and controlled by the opposing functions of protractor dilator muscles and protractor elevator muscles. This study reports, for the first time, that the pharyngeal plunger is located in a semi-open cuticular cavity on the outer wall of the esophagus. Its movement is not solely driven by interconnected dilator muscles but also influenced by depressor muscles. After traversing the pharynx, the food is discharged from the esophageal valve into the ventriculus. Here, the digestive cells absorb the contents through pinocytosis or phagocytosis and proceed with their digestion. Though concrete evidence of active digestion by the digestive cells is still lacking, remnants of plant cellular contents have been observed within these cells [12,28,29]. Interestingly, the orientation of the ESV differs between feeding and non-feeding positions, suggesting the esophageal valve's rotation when food is discharged from the esophageal valve into the ventriculus (Fig. 3d).

Bensoussan et al. [12] employed a range of histological and microscopy techniques, along with various tracer dyes, to describe the organization and properties of the T. urticae alimentary system. They identified the cellular nature of floating vesicles in the midgut lumen and demonstrated the presence of a distinct filtering function between the ventriculus and the posterior midgut, capable of separating molecules based on size. The work also presented contrasting views on the transport of intact vesicles from the ventriculus through the posterior midgut for excretion in feces [12]. This controversy has left questions unanswered regarding the retention of free-floating cells in the caeca, the transport mechanism, and the potential discontinuity of the mites' digestive tract. Unlike insects, which rely on extracellular digestion, T. urticae carries out intracellular digestion within the free-floating cells of the caeca and ventriculus [12]. Our comprehensive T. urticae model reconstruction has unveiled valuable insights into the organization of the alimentary canal. It has come to our attention that the ventriculus is in close proximity to the PM, yet no direct connection between them is evident in most cases of T. urticae. Despite examining multiple T. urticae samples, including different sexes and larvae, we were unable to identify the sphincter muscle responsible for regulating the passage from the ventriculus to the posterior midgut. Furthermore, previous electron microscopy and confocal microscopy analyses did not reveal a clear opening or channel linking the ventriculus to the posterior midgut, a finding consistent with the concept of a distinct filtering function that separates molecules by size between the ventriculus and the posterior midgut [12]. However, in a T. urticae exhibiting the posterior midgut abundant with digestive cells, we observed a funnel-shaped midgut serving as an anatomical junction between the midgut and the posterior midgut. The funnel-shaped midgut may form a temporary channel by an unknown mechanism for senescent digestive cells to pass through. This configuration suggests a specialized structural adaptation for the transition of digestive processes from the midgut to the posterior midgut. We tracked several mites and have recorded the movement of digestive cells and the excretion process of the T. urticae. We found that most of the time, in both adult mites and nymph mites, the posterior midgut is in a state without digestive cells (Fig. S4). However, at a few specific times, certain digestive cells enter the posterior midgut, where they may undergo associated digestion and reabsorption before exiting the body. And then the digestive cells are excreted intact through the rectum, which may explain the process of digestive cells being excreted completely after entering the posterior midgut from the ventriculus [12]. Consequently, our findings suggest that the alimentary canal is “discontinuous” in most cases, thereby separating the digestive cells floating in the ventriculus from entry into the posterior midgut, which represents a structural adaptation to accommodate specialized digestive cells suspended within the ventriculus. Although the integrity of the digestive cells, including the nucleus, has been demonstrated in previous studies, we have not identified any specific spatial structure for the excretion of intact digestive cells in the posterior midgut. Moreover, we propose that the large midgut cells, which contain numerous mitochondria, play a role in nutrient reabsorption and contribute to the formation of fecal pellets comprising undigested and unabsorbed material. In addition, the digestive cells that enter directly into the posterior midgut may undergo re-digestion and absorption here. Accordingly, we provide the following summary of the characteristics of the digestive system in the T. urticae: (1) The midgut is remarkably voluminous, occupying nearly the entire abdominal cavity except for the space allocated to the reproductive system. (2) Digestive cells within the midgut are spheroidal in shape, suspended in the luminal fluid, and present in high abundance, forming a unique cellular consortium, the site of nutrient digestion, absorption and energy storage. (3) The midgut is anatomically divided into two segments: the ventriculus and the posterior midgut. Instead, a specialized functional structure controls the transient opening of the junction between these two regions. (4) The posterior midgut is positioned dorsally to the ventriculus. Most parts of the ventriculus and the posterior midgut are close together, suggesting that certain substances may be transferred between them through mechanisms such as pinocytosis or other endocytic processes. These structural specializations collectively suggest an evolutionary compromise between maximizing digestive capacity and maintaining physiological flexibility in this microarthropod. The suspended digestive cells coupled with dynamic gut compartmentalization may represent a novel adaptation for rapid nutrient assimilation in phytophagous species with high metabolic demands. Beyond the posterior midgut, we have identified a complex cuticle-lined rectal epithelium that connects to the anus, serving as the pathway for the excretion of end products through the anal slit. These findings have led to the development of a comprehensive model illustrating the process of food ingestion, digestion, and excretion in T. urticae, as depicted in Fig. 6.

Conclusion

Overall, this research provides a comprehensive understanding of the precise positions and spatial relationships among various structures and organ systems in the minute spider mite. The elucidation of the overall structures provides evidence for T. urticae’s adaptation to its unique lifestyle. This knowledge lays a solid foundation for future studies investigating the physicochemical and biochemical mechanisms underlying food ingestion, digestion, respiration, and perception in T. urticae.
