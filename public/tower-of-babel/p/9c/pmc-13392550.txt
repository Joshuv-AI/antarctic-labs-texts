Strengthening Ebola virus disease preparedness in Somalia: Lessons from the 2026 Eastern DRC outbreak for early warning and rapid response
Authors: Shukri Hassan Mohamed, Hibo Abdullahi Mohamed, Liban Abdullahi Sudi, Yakub Burhan Abdullahi
Published: 2026

Dear Editor

The 2026 outbreak of Ebola disease caused by Bundibugyo virus in the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Uganda underscores the importance of rapid detection, isolation, and coordinated response in preventing regional spread.1 CDC scenario modelling projected that, assuming 50 cumulative deaths by May 24, 2026, successful isolation of only 20% of infected persons, and no additional interventions, 65% of simulations would exceed 20,000 cumulative cases within three months.2 For Somalia, this warning is particularly urgent because persistent gaps in surveillance, health workforce capacity, laboratory referral, and infection prevention and control could delay the detection and containment of an imported case.3,4

Bundibugyo virus disease is not only a severe infection; it tests whether a health system can detect suspected cases early, confirm diagnoses rapidly, isolate and care for patients safely, protect frontline workers, and maintain community trust during an outbreak4, 5, 6. The 2026 outbreak illustrates these challenges: epidemiological investigations indicated that transmission had probably begun several weeks before the outbreak was officially declared, while shortages of diagnostic reagents contributed to delays and backlogs in laboratory confirmation.6 Evidence from Uganda reinforces this concern: a rapid systematic review and meta-analysis found that each additional day between the first case report and specimen collection was associated with an estimated 3.8-percentage-point increase in the outbreak-level case fatality ratio at a baseline risk of 45%.5

Somalia has strengthened its outbreak surveillance architecture through the implementation of IDSR and EWARN; however, persistent gaps in reporting completeness, system integration, and response capacity could still delay the detection and containment of an imported Bundibugyo virus case.6,7 Following its reactivation in 2017, the EWARN expanded to all six federal member states and the Banadir Administrative Region by December 2020, while the proportion of reports submitted on time increased from 24.6% in 2017 to 96.8% in 2020.7 However, reporting completeness remained below 60% throughout the evaluation period, only 14.1% of the investigated alerts met the relevant suspected case definition, and limited integration with other surveillance systems continued to constrain EWARN's effectiveness.7 These surveillance limitations are compounded by Somalia's reported International Health Regulations (IHR) implementation capacity of 33%, surveillance capacity of 30%, and health workforce capacity of 25%, which constrain rapid detection, case investigation, and coordinated outbreak response.3

Somalia's vulnerability is driven largely by structural conditions, including displacement, insecurity, weak health-system capacity, and climate-related disruption, which increase exposure and hinder timely outbreak detection and response.8 Somalia's decentralized outbreak-response architecture distributes responsibilities across federal and state authorities and numerous humanitarian partners; when coordination mechanisms are weak, parallel operations can produce duplicated assessments, overlapping activities, uneven service coverage, and delays in releasing essential commodities, thereby slowing the unified response required for a suspected Ebola event.8 Climate shocks may not directly drive Bundibugyo virus transmission, but in Somalia, they can indirectly weaken Ebola preparedness by displacing communities, disrupting access to healthcare, damaging water and sanitation infrastructure, and diverting limited resources from surveillance, laboratory referral, and outbreak response.8 These pressures disproportionately affect Somalia's approximately 3.3 million internally displaced people, whose frequent movement, overcrowded living conditions, limited access to formal healthcare, and unstable residence can complicate early case detection, contact identification, safe isolation, and sustained follow-up during Ebola response.8,9

Facility-level infection prevention and control readiness is particularly important because delayed recognition, unsafe triage, inadequate isolation, and weak standard precautions can permit healthcare-associated transmission and expose frontline workers during an imported Bundibugyo virus event.4,9 A 2024 nationwide assessment of 1,219 health facilities found widespread IPC deficiencies: only 24% had rules for standard precautions, 18% had healthcare waste management rules, and 39% had basic hand hygiene supplies.4 These deficiencies are operationally significant because inadequate standard precautions, waste management, and hand hygiene capacity could facilitate healthcare-associated transmission, delay safe patient isolation, and expose health workers and other patients during an imported Bundibugyo virus event.4,9 However, a practical community-level model already exists in Somaliland: in 2021, volunteers using the SMS-based Nyss platform generated 138 signals in Awdal, of which 83% met the community case definitions and were escalated to health authorities, while 85% were verified within 24 hours.10

These lessons indicate that Somalia should not wait until a suspected imported Bundibugyo virus case is detected before testing its surveillance, laboratory referral, isolation, infection prevention and control, risk communication, and emergency coordination arrangements; these capacities should be assessed, exercised, and strengthened before an alert occurs.1,9

The Federal Government of Somalia, federal member states, donors, and technical partners should therefore prioritize an interoperable EWARN–IDSR system; a continuously functional public health emergency operations mechanism with clear incident-management authority; pre-agreed specimen transport and regional reference-laboratory pathways; trained rapid-response teams; community alert, contact-tracing, and risk-communication networks; and facility-level triage, isolation, personal protective equipment, and IPC capacity3,4,6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11.

Therefore, Somalia's preparedness should be measured not by whether an imported Bundibugyo virus case occurs, but by whether the first alert triggers immediate investigation, safe specimen referral, rapid isolation, protected frontline care, effective contact tracing, and trusted community engagement1,4, 5, 6, 7,9,11.
