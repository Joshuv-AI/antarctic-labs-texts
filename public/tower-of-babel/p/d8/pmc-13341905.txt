Trajectory of urinary incontinence symptoms following total hip arthroplasty for hip osteoarthritis
Authors: Masaru Kadowaki, Sayaka Fukutani, Kiminori Ushio, Yuji Uchio
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionUrinary incontinence (UI), a prevalent quality-of-life–limiting condition, frequently coexists with hip osteoarthritis, potentially through pelvic floor dysfunction related to obturator internus muscle involvement. Several reports suggest improvement after total hip arthroplasty (THA), although postoperative time-course changes remain unclear. This study aimed to clarify the temporal trends in UI after THA and examine association of postoperative UI trajectories with functional recovery.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study included 118 female patients who underwent THA for hip osteoarthritis between October 2021 and March 2024. UI, assessed using the International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire-Short Form (ICIQ-SF), was defined as ICIQ-SF ≥ 1 preoperatively and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months postoperatively, and classified as stress (SUI), urge (UUI), or mixed (MUI). Hip-related patient-reported outcomes were evaluated using the Japanese Orthopaedic Association Hip Disease Evaluation Questionnaire (JHEQ). Associations with BMI, age, surgical approach (direct lateral/Bauer vs. modified Watson-Jones/OCM), and UI type were tested using Mann–Whitney U, chi-squared, and repeated-measures ANOVA tests.ResultsOf 118 patients (mean age at surgery, 67 years; mean BMI, 24.3 kg/m2) preoperatively, 73 (61.9%) reported UI (mean ICIQ-SF 7.88), including SUI (n = 20), UUI (n = 26), and MUI (n = 27), with a higher baseline severity in MUI than in UUI. The mean ICIQ-SF improved markedly by 3 months (3.88) with a significant time effect (p < 0.001, η2 = 0.39), but then plateaued through 12 months. Although most patients improved, 45/73 (61.6%) had persistent UI at 12 months. These patients had higher preoperative ICIQ-SF and significantly worse postoperative JHEQ (notably pain) across multiple time points, while BMI, approach, and UI type showed no significant group differences.ConclusionUI symptoms improved significantly within the first 3 months following THA; however, subsequent recovery was limited. Persistent UI was associated with poorer functional recovery and higher pain levels.

Introduction

Urinary incontinence (UI), a distressing condition, is prevalent worldwide [1]. According to the International Consultation on Incontinence, UI is characterized by involuntary leakage of urine [2]. The most common UI types are stress UI (SUI), urge UI (UUI), and mixed UI (MUI), with their prevalence reported as 50%, 14%, and 32%, respectively [3]. In SUI, urine leakage results from increased pressure within the abdomen due to actions such as coughing, sneezing, or laughing, as well as during physical activities including running, jumping, and lifting objects [2]. Regarding UUI, individuals experience a sudden and intense need to urinate. On the other hand, MUI is a combination of SUI and UUI. Various factors, including genetics, aging, female sex, marital status, obesity, alcohol consumption, smoking, certain mental, cognitive, and functional disorders, and surgeries, contribute to the risk of developing UI [4, 5]. While the symptoms do not affect mortality, they can significantly alter various aspects of everyday life, substantially diminishing the quality of life (QOL) [6]. With the increase in global life expectancy, UI is emerging as a more critical health issue requiring medical intervention.

Osteoarthritis, the most prevalent condition affecting the musculoskeletal system, results from a range of physiological, biomechanical, and biochemical alterations in the joint cartilage [7]. Hip osteoarthritis is a significant source of pain and physical impairment in patients. This condition results in discomfort and limited movement, contributing to physical disability. In addition, patients with hip osteoarthritis have a high frequency of UI symptoms, as high as 29–67% [8–11]. As suggested, atrophy of the obturator internus muscle is a factor in the coexistence of osteoarthritis of the hip and UI [8, 9]. The obturator internus is one of the short external rotator muscles of the hip and is responsible for external rotation and abduction of the hip joint [12]. Anatomical studies have revealed that the muscle forms part of the pelvic wall, is the largest muscle among the pelvic floor muscles, and forms an extensive contact with the levator ani muscle, which plays an important role in urinary control [13]. Therefore, in hip osteoarthritis, atrophy of the obturator internus muscle may be contributing to decreased function of the pelvic floor muscles, causing UI.

Of the several available treatments for hip osteoarthritis, the most effective for relieving symptoms, such as pain and limited range of motion, is total hip arthroplasty (THA) [14]. Furthermore, it has also been reported that UI symptoms in patients with osteoarthritis of the hip improve after THA surgery [8–11, 15].

However, these reports are limited to evaluations at specific points in time after surgery, and it is not fully understood when and how postoperative UI symptoms change over time. Therefore, this study aimed to clarify the temporal trends in UI symptoms after THA in patients with hip osteoarthritis.

Patients and methods

This retrospective observation study included 118 female patients who underwent THA for hip osteoarthritis between October 2021 and March 2024. For the surgical approach, the direct lateral approach (Bauer technique [16]) was used in 75 joints, while the modified Watson-Jones approach (Orthopaedische Chirurgie München: OCM technique [17]) was used in 43 joints.

The International Consultation on Incontinence Questionnaire-Short Form (ICIQ-SF), endorsed by the International Continence Society, provides a subjective assessment of UI symptoms by scoring the frequency and volume of UI and its impact on daily activities [18, 19]. The maximum score is 21 points, with increasing severity as the score increases. This questionnaire includes items asking under what circumstances urine leakage occurs, and UI was classified into three types based on the responses. Specifically, those who responded with “leaks when you cough or sneeze” or “leaks when you are physically active/exercising,” or both, were defined as having SUI. Those who answered “leaks before you can get to the toilet” were defined as having a UUI. Patients who met the criteria for both SUI and UUI were diagnosed with MUI. The ICIQ-SF was administered before THA and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months postoperatively to investigate changes over time. We defined urinary incontinence as ICIQ-SF score of ≥ 1.

The Japanese Orthopaedic Association Hip-Disease Evaluation Questionnaire (JHEQ) was administered to assess hip function before THA and at 3, 6, 9, and 12 months postoperatively. JHEQ is a patient-reported outcome measure (PROMs) consisting of three subscales: pain, function, and mental, and is considered to accurately reflect patients’ QOL [20]. It has also been reported to correlate well with clinician-reported outcomes [21].

We examined the trend in ICIQ-SF scores over time from preoperatively to 12 months post-THA across all cases. Next, we compared the differences in the changes in the ICIQ-SF scores over time according to the type of UI. Then, to investigate the factors associated with UI symptoms after THA, patients were divided into two groups: those who still had UI symptoms 12 months after surgery (ICIQ-SF > 1) and those whose symptoms had resolved (ICIQ-SF = 0). Patient factors (age, BMI), surgical approach, ICIQ-SF score before THA, and UI type were compared between the two groups. The trend in ICIQ-SF scores over time, from preoperatively to 12 months postoperatively, was also compared between the two groups.

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 27.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). The Mann–Whitney U test was conducted for age and BMI comparisons between the two groups, while the chi-squared test was used to assess the association between surgical approach and UI type. Repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA) was used for intergroup comparisons of trends in ICIQ-SF scores over time, from pre-THA surgery to 12 months post-surgery. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

This study was conducted following the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and approved by the Research Ethics Committee of [blinded] (Approval No. 9240). No funding was received for this study.

Results

The 118 included patients had a mean age at surgery, height, body weight, and body mass index (BMI) of 67.3 ± 10.4 years, 152.5 ± 6.1 cm, 56.7 ± 10.1 kg, and 24.3 ± 4.1 kg/m2, respectively. Before THA surgery, 73 of 118 patients (61.9%) had UI symptoms, and had mean age, height, weight, and BMI of 69.5 ± 8.8 years, 152.2 ± 6.2 cm, 57.8 ± 10.4 kg, and 24.9 ± 4.3 kg/m2, respectively. Of these 73 patients, 36, 15, and 22 had unilateral osteoarthritis of the hip, bilateral involvement with the contralateral side untreated, and bilateral involvement with the contralateral side post-THA, respectively. The mean preoperative ICIQ-SF score was 7.88 ± 4.3. By UI type, SUI, UUI, and MUI developed in 20 (27.4%), 26 (35.6%), and 27 (37%) patients, respectively (Table 1). The UI types showed no significant differences in age, height, weight, or BMI; however, the ICIQ-SF score was significantly higher in MUI than in UUI. At 3 months postoperatively, 28 (38.4%) patients reported symptom resolution, and the average ICIQ-SF score decreased from 7.88 ± 4.3 to 3.88 ± 3.6. However, the scores remained relatively stable thereafter (3.95 ± 4.1, 4.10 ± 4.3, and 4.00 ± 4.3 at 6, 9, and 12 months, respectively). Of the 73 patients, 28 experienced resolution of symptoms, while 38 showed improvement; however, urinary incontinence persisted in 45 (61.6%) 12 months after THA. Discussion

This study showed that UI associated with hip osteoarthritis rapidly improved within 3 months after THA, although the improvement plateaued subsequently. Previous reports have only shown an improvement rate at a single time point; however, this study was able to demonstrate changes over time after surgery. Additionally, the progression varied depending on the UI type, and among the group in which UI persisted 12 months postoperatively, preoperative ICIQ-SF scores were higher, and postoperative PROMs were poorer. According to Tamaki et al., 43% of patients had UI symptoms before THA, while 64% showed improvement 3 months after [9]. While Okumura et al. reported that 74% of patients showed improvement 3 months after THA [11], Abed et al. reported improvement at the time of discharge in 62% [15]. The improvement rates reported by these previous studies were all at a certain point in time; no previous studies have investigated when UI symptoms begin to improve after THA and how they progress. This study results are consistent with previous reports indicating improvements in UI symptoms approximately 3 months after THA and further present new findings that this improvement does not persist beyond 3 months. Several authors have suggested that UI symptoms associated with hip osteoarthritis are caused by decreased pelvic floor muscle function following atrophy of the obturator internus muscle, and that these symptoms may improve after THA as the function of the obturator internus muscle is restored [8, 9, 11]. Okumura et al. found that UI symptoms improved as early as 3 days after THA surgery, and that at three months postoperatively, the group with improved UI showed superior improvements in hip joint range of motion [11]. Since at three days after surgery walking ability is still insufficient, the improvement in UI symptoms cannot be explained by the walking function. This effect is considered immediate, brought about by the release of joint contracture and restored movement of the obturator internus muscle following THA. Baba et al. also suggested that correction of leg length shortening by THA restores the tension in the obturator internus muscle, and that increased tension in the pelvic floor muscles may contribute to improvement in UI [8]. The observed improvement may reflect such immediate effects of THA, which could explain why UI symptoms improved soon after surgery but showed no further change after 3 months. Therefore, factors related to UI other than internal obturator muscle atrophy not improved by THA probably remain, which may explain the lack of further improvement after 3 months.

There have also been reports of differences in UI symptoms associated with hip osteoarthritis depending on the type. According to Okumura et al., the percentages of patients whose UI symptoms improved 3 months after THA was 73%, 40%, and 94% for SUI, UUI, and MUI, respectively [11]. Abed et al. reported no difference in preoperative ICIQ-SF scores between SUI and UUI groups, and the reduction rate at discharge after THA was 44.7% for SUI and 28.5% for UUI [15]. In this study, there was no difference in the preoperative ICIQ-SF scores between the SUI and UUI groups. After THA, ICIQ-SF scores decreased in both groups, and repeated-measures ANOVA showed no significant differences in changes over time between the groups. At a point in time, SUI appears to show better postoperative recovery than UUI. However, as observed over the 12-month postoperative period in this study, there was no difference, and the relationship between UI type and recovery remains unclear. Because MUI comprised both SUI and UUI components, it is considered to involve more severe symptoms [22, 23], and in this study, the preoperative ICIIQ-SF scores were higher. Similar to the scores for SUI and UUI, the ICIQ-SF score for MUI decreased over the 3-month period following THA, indicating a reduction in UI symptoms. However, because the severity was high prior to the surgery, the score remained relatively high, and even 12 months after surgery, it retained similar level as the preoperative ICIQ-SF scores for SUI and UUI. Thus, postoperative symptoms improved after THA regardless of the UI type. However, because the pathologies of each type differ, the mechanism by which THA alleviates symptoms remains unclear. This improvement cannot be solely explained by the recovery of the obturator internus muscle function, and further research is needed to clarify this.

In this study, UI symptoms were alleviated in 28 (38.4%) patients 12 months after THA, and their PROMs at 3 months postoperatively were better than those of the group in which UI symptoms persisted. Pelvic floor muscle dysfunction is widely considered a cause of UI. In hip osteoarthritis, UI is suspected to be due to reduced pelvic floor muscle function following obturator internus atrophy. Additionally, it has been reported that movements of the gluteal and trunk muscles are also related to the function of the pelvic floor muscles [24]. As previously mentioned, an immediate effect of THA is its impact on the internal obturator muscle; however, the functional recovery of the muscles around the hip joint over the course of several months may also contribute to the improvement of UI symptoms. In the group in which UI was cured 12 months after surgery, the ICIQ-SF score dropped significantly at 3 months postoperatively and the symptoms remained unchanged up to 9 months but disappeared by 12 months postoperatively, in contrast to the overall trend and the trend in the group in which UI persisted until its disappearance after 12 months. In the group in which UI disappeared, PROMs at 3 months postoperatively improved significantly, and although not statistically significant thereafter, they remained favorable. This suggests that improvement in hip joint function and pain may have had some positive effect on UI symptoms, possibly leading to improved pelvic floor muscle function through the recovery of muscles around the hip joint. In the group in which UI was resolved, the pain domain of the PROMs remained consistently favorable postoperatively. Although there is no direct evidence that pain affects UI symptoms, pain-related dysfunction of the pelvis or spine may interfere with pelvic floor muscle function. Therefore, restoring hip function and alleviating pain may be important for improving UI symptoms.

This study had several limitations. First, the small sample size may have introduced variabilities in factors related to UI, such as age and body habitus, and limited statistical power. Future studies with larger sample sizes and careful control of confounding factors related to UI but unrelated to hip osteoarthritis are needed to achieve more accurate analysis. Furthermore, we based our premise on previous reports suggesting that UI associated with hip osteoarthritis may be related to atrophy of the obturator internus muscle. However, this study did not include direct measurement to determine whether the obturator internus muscle was atrophied in the target population, nor did it demonstrated improved rotational mobility of the hip following THA. Consequently, recovery of obturator internus muscle function was not established, and this proposed mechanism remains purely speculative. Future studies should quantify the degree of preoperative atrophy of the obturator internus muscle and examine whether postoperative recovery of hip muscle strength and joint range of motion after THA is associated with the alleviation of UI. Furthermore, this study did not conduct a stratified analysis of the prevalence of hip osteoarthritis regarding its laterality (unilateral vs bilateral), or postoperative status following THA. Although previous reports on UI associated with hip osteoarthritis have not addressed laterality, it is plausible that the affected side may influence outcomes if the atrophy of the obturator internus muscle is contributory. Moreover, given that the pathophysiological mechanisms differ by UI type, stratified analyses by UI type is may further clarify the mechanism underlying UI associated with hip osteoarthritis.

In summary, UI is a common comorbidity in patients with hip osteoarthritis undergoing THA, with over 60% experiencing symptoms preoperatively. This study demonstrated that a significant improvement in UI occurs primarily within the first 3 months after surgery, with limited further recovery thereafter. Although stress and UUI showed similar postoperative trajectories, MUI remained more severe and persistent. Patients whose UI resolved by 12 months postoperatively exhibited better early postoperative hip function and pain outcomes, suggesting a link between functional recovery and improvement in urinary symptoms. These findings highlight the importance of early postoperative rehabilitation focused on hip function to potentially enhance urinary continence outcomes. Furthermore, since improvement in UI symptoms plateaued after 3 months following THA, factors other than hip joint dysfunction that may be causing osteoarthritis—which cannot be improved by THA alone—are also related to UI symptoms. Therefore, adding rehabilitation such as pelvic floor muscle training, which is generally recommended for UI and targeting pelvic floor muscle dysfunction, should also be considered. Further research is warranted to elucidate the underlying mechanisms and develop targeted interventions for persistent UI following THA.

In conclusion, of patients with hip osteoarthritis who underwent THA, 61.9% had UI symptoms before surgery. At 3 months postoperatively, symptoms resolved in 38.4% of patients, but recovery stagnated, and at 12 months, symptoms persisted in 61.6% of patients. SUI and UUI showed similar trends preoperatively to 12 months after, but MUI was severe even before surgery, and high ICIQ-SF scores persisted. In the group in which UI was cured at 12 months postoperatively, the ICIQ-SF dropped significantly by 3 months after surgery and continued to decline gradually until 12 months, whereas the PROMs at 3 months postoperatively were significantly better.
