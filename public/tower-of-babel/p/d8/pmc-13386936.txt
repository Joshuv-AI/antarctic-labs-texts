Structural tensions between health policy and medical ethics: a qualitative study of physicians’ experiences under Türkiye’s health transformation programme
Authors: Dilek Bulut
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTürkiye’s Health Transformation Programme (HTP), launched in 2003, reorganised the country’s healthcare system through interlocking mechanisms: a Performance-Based Revolving Fund (PBRF) — a pay-for-performance scheme — linking physician income to procedure volume; a Central Appointment System (CAPS) imposing daily patient quotas; and a General Health Insurance (GHI) scheme conditioning coverage on premium payment. No integrated qualitative analysis has examined the cumulative impact of multiple HTP mechanisms across all four principles of the Beauchamp and Childress framework using practice-level physician accounts.MethodsAn interpretive, constructivist qualitative design employing reflexive thematic analysis was used. Twenty-two specialist physicians participated across two phases: seven in a pilot phase for scenario development (not in the analytical dataset) and fifteen in the main interview phase, of whom twelve were retained for analysis after consensus review (n = 12 analytical sample; mean seniority approximately 25 years; criterion-based purposive sampling across five districts of Muğla, Türkiye). Data were collected via semi-structured interviews structured around four researcher-developed hypothetical scenarios. Primary coding was undertaken by the researcher and progressively validated through consensus discussion with the academic supervisor and a professor of medical ethics on the doctoral thesis monitoring committee. Transcripts were returned to participants for review (COREQ item 23).ResultsFour primary themes and one cross-cutting pattern emerged. Participants described the PBRF as eroding beneficence and non-maleficence through patient-poaching, defensive clinical decision-making, and progressive normalisation of ethical violations. The hospital enterprise model was described as subordinating patient welfare to revenue priorities. Participants reported that CAPS quotas render genuine informed consent structurally difficult to achieve, while complaint mechanisms deepen perceived autonomy deficits. The GHI’s premium-debt exclusion was described as transferring institutional injustice onto individual physicians. A cross-cutting theme captured participants’ accounts of the transformation of professional identity from patient stewardship to a shift-completion mentality — consistent with the trajectory from moral distress toward moral injury described in the international literature.ConclusionsPhysicians in this study perceive HTP policies as creating cumulative tensions with the four-principle framework. These perceptions mirror patterns documented in neoliberal reform contexts across Australia, France, Iran, Botswana, and Canada. They constitute interpretive accounts drawn from a small purposive sample of senior practitioners in a single province, rather than structural diagnoses of the system. Findings argue for integrating practice-level physician accounts into health-policy ethical evaluation alongside efficiency metrics; the scenario methodology offers a replicable approach for practice-informed policy-ethics research rather than a direct structural diagnosis of the system.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-026-01486-4.Keywords: Health policy, Medical ethics, Neoliberal health reform, Pay-for-performance, Physician autonomy, Moral distress, Qualitative research, Türkiye

Background

Neoliberal health reform and ethical erosion: global evidence

The neoliberal paradigm that has dominated global health governance since the 1980s reframes healthcare as a component of competitive markets rather than a public obligation. Structural adjustment programmes promoted by the World Bank and the International Monetary Fund have systematically introduced privatisation, corporatisation, and performance-based payment mechanisms across low- and middle-income countries [1–3]. Stubbs and colleagues’ analysis [2] documents that the resurgent austerity agenda — driven through international financial institutions — places renewed pressure on health systems and threatens core values including universality, equity, and professional autonomy. Gilmore and colleagues [1] argue that the commercial determinants of health are intrinsically in tension with both Beauchamp and Childress’s four-principle framework [4] and international human rights norms.

Healthcare services exhibit structural features — uncertainty of need, profound information asymmetry between clinicians and patients, supplier-induced demand, and significant externalities — that distinguish them from conventional markets and produce what health economists describe as market failure [5]. Within this asymmetric-information setting, the physician occupies a dual role as both supplier and, via the principal–agent relationship with the patient, shaper of demand; this makes physician behaviour an especially sensitive interface between policy design and ethical practice. Empirical analyses of structural adjustment programmes [3] have documented their adverse population-health consequences, illustrating how macro-policy reforms imposed through international financial institutions can produce direct ethical and health-outcome harms even where short-term efficiency indicators improve. Taken together, these features suggest that neoliberal health policy represents not merely a governance challenge but a structural ethical design problem whose effects are most visible at the point of clinical practice.

The four-principle framework under systemic pressure

Beauchamp and Childress’s four principles — beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, and justice [4] — are widely employed as an organising framework in health-policy ethics. This study adopts a constructivist epistemological stance consistent with the reflexive thematic analysis methodology employed [6]; the four-principle framework was selected for its analytical breadth and widespread international currency, while acknowledging that care ethics and structural-justice frameworks might illuminate complementary dimensions of the same phenomena. Their application in reform contexts reveals recurrent patterns of tension between principle-based ethics and policy design.

Regarding beneficence and non-maleficence

Marketisation fosters defensive medicine — encompassing both positive forms (clinically unwarranted investigations) and negative forms (avoidance of high-risk cases) [7, 8]. Ries and colleagues’ qualitative study [9] found that the primary motivational driver was not fear of litigation but rather fear of appearing incompetent to peers. Within Türkiye, Solaroglu and colleagues [10] documented a marked increase in defensive practice following HTP implementation, corroborated by Türkiye’s continued top OECD ranking for MRI utilisation across 2017–2023 [36, 39].

Regarding autonomy and informed consent

Patient autonomy requires adequate time, information, and freedom from coercion to make genuine healthcare decisions, operationalised through informed consent [11]. Oueslati and colleagues’ qualitative synthesis [12] established that shared decision-making is contingent on a relational, trust-based interaction between clinician and patient, and Caldwell and Hibbs [13] argue that the time and informational pressures of contemporary clinical practice render genuinely informed consent practically very difficult to achieve. Bokek-Cohen and Gabay [14] further demonstrate that autonomy can be systematically undermined by institutional practices that lead patients to question their own perceptions — a phenomenon they term medical gaslighting. Liang and colleagues’ qualitative study of Chinese hospitals [15] documents how ‘patient-centred care’ rhetoric can entail tensions with patient autonomy in practice — physicians’ accounts revealing the discrepancy between official patient-centredness rhetoric and the conditions in which clinical encounters actually unfold.

Regarding justice

Pratt and colleagues [16] argue that justice must be positioned at the analytical centre of health-policy research, extending beyond individual protections to structural distribution mechanisms. The justice principle can enter into genuine tension with autonomy in reform contexts — for example, a patient-quota mechanism that constrains the quality of individual consultation may simultaneously serve justice by expanding population access. This internal tension within the four-principle framework is taken up in the Discussion. Baskin and colleagues [17] place justice at the core of healthcare de-implementation decisions, insisting these cannot be delegated to individual clinicians.

Moral distress and moral injury as interpretive frames

The most extensively documented psychological sequela of neoliberal health reform among clinicians is moral distress — knowing the ethically correct course of action but being institutionally constrained from pursuing it [18]. Moral injury, as conceptualised by Dean and colleagues [19], refers to a lasting psychological wound arising from failure to honour deeply held moral commitments, extending beyond distress to a rupture of one’s moral self-understanding. Moral distress and moral injury are distinct but interrelated constructs: distress describes the recurring experience of constraint, whereas injury describes the cumulative damage that results when such distress remains sustained and unresolved. The present study uses these concepts as interpretive frames, not as measured outcomes; it documents patterns in physicians’ accounts that are consistent with the trajectory from moral distress toward moral injury, rather than constituting a clinical measurement of the latter. Fisher and colleagues [20] establish the chronicity of moral distress even prior to COVID-19, and Usset and colleagues [21] document an inverse association between high moral-injury scores and intention to remain in the profession — a link directly relevant to the workforce-sustainability implications considered later.

Türkiye’s HTP and its ethical consequences

Türkiye’s Health Transformation Programme, launched in 2003, unified health financing under the General Health Insurance (GHI), established Family Medicine at the primary-care level, and introduced the Performance-Based Revolving Fund (PBRF) system in public hospitals. Its short- and medium-term effects — expanded access, improved maternal and child health indicators, reduced regional disparities — have been independently verified [22]. The programme’s longer-term ethical consequences, however, have attracted growing systematic scrutiny.

Aksoy and Ilkilic’s qualitative study [23] found that system-imposed constraints significantly shaped Turkish physicians’ ethical reasoning in end-of-life decisions. Kavas and colleagues’ mixed-methods study [24] documented physician alienation from their patients, their profession, and themselves; a decline in occupational motivation; and a progressive market-alignment of professional identity. Aktas and colleagues [25] showed that physicians navigating P4P as “street-level bureaucrats” developed varied compliance strategies, with individual resistance capacity declining under structural pressure. The most visible system-level outcome has been the acceleration of physician emigration: Genç’s analysis [26] documents that rising violence and inadequate pay are driving particularly younger physicians abroad, and Önal and colleagues [27] frame this migration as an ethical exit — the preservation of beneficence, non-maleficence, and professional autonomy by leaving the system. Balekang and colleagues’ Botswana-based qualitative study [28] further documents healthcare workers rationalising policy-oath conflicts as “beyond my control” — a rationalisation mechanism that appears as a structural response pattern whenever health-system pressure intensifies.

Literature gaps and study contribution

Three gaps characterise the existing literature at both the global and Türkiye-specific levels. First, most studies focus on a single policy component (P4P or GHI), and no integrated analysis has simultaneously examined all four Beauchamp–Childress principles against multiple HTP components. Second, available assessments rely predominantly on administrative data or survey instruments; first-hand accounts from healthcare providers in real-time practice settings remain underrepresented. Third, qualitative work on the rationalisation of ethical violations and on the interpretive trajectory from moral distress toward moral injury is extremely limited in the Türkiye context, and Turkish findings have not been systematically contextualised within the global comparative literature.

Study aim and objectives

Aim

The overall aim of this study is to explore how senior specialist physicians practising in Muğla, Türkiye perceive and describe the ethical conflicts that emerge from their encounters with the Health Transformation Programme (HTP) after approximately two decades of its implementation. The study focuses on physicians’ experiential accounts and the interpretive patterns they construct, rather than on adjudicating structural properties of the health system itself.

Specific objectives

Three objectives structure the inquiry:

(i)to elicit, through scenario-based in-depth interviews, physicians’ accounts of how four HTP mechanisms — the Performance-Based Revolving Fund (PBRF), the Public Hospital Unions (PHU) enterprise model, the Central Appointment System (CAPS), and the General Health Insurance (GHI) premium-conditionality mechanism — interact with the four principles of Beauchamp and Childress’s framework in their lived clinical practice;

to elicit, through scenario-based in-depth interviews, physicians’ accounts of how four HTP mechanisms — the Performance-Based Revolving Fund (PBRF), the Public Hospital Unions (PHU) enterprise model, the Central Appointment System (CAPS), and the General Health Insurance (GHI) premium-conditionality mechanism — interact with the four principles of Beauchamp and Childress’s framework in their lived clinical practice;

(ii)to examine whether recurring cross-scenario patterns emerge inductively in physicians’ narratives beyond the principle–mechanism pairings built into the scenario design, and to characterise any such patterns interpretively; and.

to examine whether recurring cross-scenario patterns emerge inductively in physicians’ narratives beyond the principle–mechanism pairings built into the scenario design, and to characterise any such patterns interpretively; and.

(iii)to situate the physicians’ perceptions within the comparative international literature on neoliberal health reform, professional identity, and the moral distress–moral injury trajectory.

The guiding research question follows from these objectives: How do senior Turkish specialist physicians, across more than two decades of practice spanning the pre- and post-2003 reform threshold, describe the conflicts they experience between HTP policy mechanisms and the four core principles of medical ethics, and what interpretive responses do they develop to those conflicts?

Methods

Study design and setting

This study adopts an interpretive, constructivist paradigm consistent with reflexive thematic analysis [6, 29]. The focus is on how physicians construct and narrate meaning from their experience of HTP policy exposure. The field site is Muğla province, in western Anatolia. Site selection criteria were: (i) coverage of multiple districts representing secondary and tertiary care; (ii) access to physicians practising in both public and private sectors; and (iii) availability of physicians who had practised both before and after the 2003 HTP. Fieldwork was conducted in five districts: Menteşe, Fethiye, Bodrum, Milas, and Marmaris.

Participants

Twenty-two specialist physicians participated in the study across two methodologically distinct phases. A pilot phase involved seven physicians recruited to inform scenario development; their accounts were used to identify practice-level ethical problem clusters but were not included in the analytical dataset. The main analytical phase comprised fifteen physicians, recruited through criterion-based eligibility filtering followed by stratified purposive selection across five districts.

The main-phase participant pool comprised specialist physicians who had practised for at least ten years before 2003 and at least ten years thereafter (total seniority not exceeding 30 years), held no administrative or managerial role, and worked in secondary or tertiary care facilities. This criterion-based purposive sampling design was adopted as a methodological necessity: only physicians who had directly practised on both sides of the policy threshold could provide the historical comparative perspective required by the research question [30].

Specialist physician lists obtained from the provincial health directorate were screened against the inclusion criteria. Among those eligible, three physicians per district — two from public and one from private hospitals — were selected through stratified purposive sampling. Of the nineteen specialist physicians invited to the main study (contacted by telephone via the provincial health directorate’s published rosters, with one in-person follow-up visit where required), four declined citing workload or lack of interest. Fifteen physicians thus completed semi-structured interviews. Following interview transcription and member-checking, three of these accounts were subsequently excluded from the analytical dataset during the consensus analytic process: two on grounds of insufficient narrative depth in scenario engagement (interviews substantively shorter than the 50-minute floor and dominated by general commentary rather than scenario-anchored reflection), and one because the participant’s specialist practice trajectory did not in fact straddle the 2003 reform threshold as required by the inclusion criterion. The final analytical sample therefore comprised twelve specialist physicians (n = 12). No participant withdrew during interviewing or requested withdrawal of their transcript at member-check. Participation was voluntary.

Sample adequacy was assessed against Malterud and colleagues’ information power framework [31]: the study’s narrow and specific aim, its strong theoretical foundation in the Beauchamp–Childress framework and the moral distress–moral injury literature, the density of high-quality dialogue enabled by the scenario method, the narrow inclusion criterion (mean seniority approximately 25 years; direct pre- and post-reform practice), and the case-based analytical strategy collectively support the adequacy of the n = 12 analytical sample for the study’s purposes. All twelve analysed participants contributed to the thematic structure through the consensus analytic process described below (Table 1).

Data collection: the hypothetical scenario method

Rationale for scenario-based inquiry

The hypothetical scenario (vignette) method — in which researcher-constructed case descriptions elicit participants’ responses to realistic ethical dilemmas — was selected because it integrates real-world situations with theoretical frameworks and enables participants to express their ethical reasoning through familiar practice contexts [32, 33]. Compared with closed survey instruments and brief interviews, this approach facilitates deeper exploration of lived ethical experience while providing an indirect means of accessing topics that participants may find difficult to address through direct questioning [6, 29].

Scenario development

The scenarios were developed through a rigorous multi-stage process, each step systematically documented (Table 2). Note that pilot group participants (Stage 3) were recruited via purposive selection of the first physician, followed by network/referral sampling for subsequent participants; this differs from snowball sampling, which is designed for hard-to-reach populations. Twelve physicians meeting eligibility criteria were invited to the pilot phase; seven participated. Case examples shared by the pilot group were coded thematically by the researcher, yielding eight recurring problem clusters; the independent medical ethicist reviewed this preliminary coding at Stage 4 to guard against confirmatory framing before scenarios were drafted. Six draft scenarios were constructed (Stage 4) by pairing each of the eight pilot-derived themes with the most analytically proximate Beauchamp and Childress principle [4] and HTP policy component. Two drafts were subsequently consolidated into adjacent scenarios at Stage 5 following expert panel review, on the grounds that their ethical principle and policy component overlapped substantially with two other draft scenarios; the criterion for retention was analytical distinctiveness across both the ethical principle and the HTP mechanism dimensions. Four final scenarios were thus retained, each foregrounding a unique principle–component pairing.

Data collection procedure

The interview guide used in this study was developed specifically for this study following the multi-stage process described in Table 2; it has not been previously published elsewhere. An English-language version is provided as Supplementary File 1. Interviews were conducted face-to-face in a semi-structured format at participants’ workplaces. Mean interview duration was 68 min (range: 50–90 min). Each interview was audio-recorded following written and verbal informed consent. The interview structure comprised three phases: (i) opening questions on the HTP context and general practice experience; (ii) systematic presentation of and in-depth probing on each hypothetical scenario; (iii) invitation to raise ethical patterns beyond scenario scope. Fieldwork commenced in June 2018 and concluded in August 2019, conducted intermittently across this period in five districts to accommodate participants’ clinical schedules. All participants were anonymised using alphabetical codes (Dr.A–Dr.O); recordings were encrypted and shared with no third party.

Data analysis

Audio recordings were fully transcribed by the researcher. Following transcription, transcripts were returned to each participant for review and correction (COREQ item 23); all fifteen interviewed participants reviewed their transcripts and confirmed their accuracy, with minor wording clarifications incorporated where indicated. This member-check procedure provided an additional safeguard against transcription error and against researcher-imposed interpretive distortion at the initial data-generation stage, thereby strengthening dependability and confirmability of the subsequent analysis.

Analysis followed reflexive thematic analysis [6, 29] conducted in three phases: (i) open coding — identification of meaning units and recurring patterns in raw text; (ii) categorisation — grouping codes into thematic clusters; (iii) theme generation — definition of overarching themes providing conceptual explanatory power [34]. Primary coding of all twelve analytical transcripts was undertaken by the researcher (DB). Analytical decisions — including the coding frame, candidate theme structure, and final thematic architecture — were progressively validated through consensus discussion with the academic supervisor (ÖŞG; Prof. Dr. Özlem Şahin Güngör) and a professor of medical ethics on the doctoral thesis monitoring committee (Prof. Dr. Müesser Özcan; the same individual who served on the four-member scenario validation panel — see Table 2 footnote). Where discussion surfaced alternative codings or framings, these were resolved through deliberative consensus rather than by separate rater agreement; this procedure is appropriate to the interpretivist orientation of reflexive thematic analysis, which treats analytical judgement as situated and reasoned rather than calculable [6, 29].

Researcher reflexivity

The researcher (DB) is an academic in health management with sustained scholarly engagement with HTP policy. DB is a non-physician, affording analytic distance from the physician community but raising the reciprocal risk of over-identifying with physician narratives while under-weighting patient, nurse, and administrative standpoints. Several strategies managed this: the consensus analytic procedure described above; a four-member expert panel that challenged scenario framing; and a reflexivity journal documenting analytic decisions. The cross-cutting theme of professional identity transformation was not anticipated in the scenario design and emerged inductively during the categorisation phase.

Results

The findings reported in this section represent patterns in physicians’ accounts of their lived experience under the Health Transformation Programme (HTP), rather than demonstrable structural properties of the health system. Themes emerged through the consensus analytic process described in the Methods, in which the researcher’s primary coding of all twelve analytical transcripts was progressively validated through discussion with the academic supervisor and a professor of medical ethics on the doctoral thesis monitoring committee. All twelve analysed participants contributed to the thematic structure through this process; in the sections that follow, we present direct quotations from participants whose accounts most clearly exemplified each thematic pattern, and we use summary attribution to indicate which physicians raised particular concerns when their accounts converged. Each direct quotation is attributed by anonymised code, gender, and years of seniority. Where a frequency expression appears (e.g., “virtually all”, “a majority”), an approximate count is reported parenthetically as n/N (denominator N = 12, the analytical sample); these counts include both explicit attestations and clearly implied references in participants’ accounts and may vary by ± 1 with finer-grained coding. Four primary themes are organised around the four scenarios, followed by one cross-cutting pattern that emerged inductively from the analysis.

Note on theme scope: while each scenario was designed to foreground one ethical principle, analysis revealed that participants consistently extended their responses across multiple principles (see Table 5). For instance, S2 (designed around beneficence/non-maleficence) consistently elicited justice and autonomy concerns as participants described how the hospital enterprise model positions physicians as agents of both clinical and financial authority. This expansion reflects the overlapping character of ethical principles in practice rather than analytical overreach.

Theme 1: erosion of deontological relations (scenario 1)

In response to the first scenario, virtually all participants identified the patient-poaching pattern from personal experience and described it as emblematic of “classic performance anxiety”. A distinction was drawn between pre-PBRF informal corruption (popularly termed bıçak parası, unofficial surgical fees) and the post-PBRF form: performance incentives had rendered a similar ethical violation more visible, more pervasive, and subject to progressive rationalisation. (n = 11/12)

“Classic performance anxiety with ‘patient-poaching’. Physicians earn money proportionate to how many operations they perform and how much performance they produce. That is what the incentive system means. The patient becomes an object.” (Dr.B, Male, 22 years’ seniority).

“There used to be patients who paid under the table; now there are ‘performance-point patients’. At least before it was not this explicit, not this raw.” (Dr.P, Male, 25 years’ seniority).

“The physician is no longer a colleague and friend — they are now a rival. The deontological and collegial relationship between physicians has been replaced by competition driven by performance points.” (Dr.B, Male, 22 years’ seniority).

Defensive medicine: the ethical cost of systemic risk management

Participants described a pronounced increase in defensive practice following HTP implementation [7, 10]. This manifested in two forms: positive defensive medicine (clinically unwarranted investigations) and negative defensive medicine (avoidance of high-risk, low-point cases). The asymmetry between performance points assigned to a procedure and its risk level was identified as a direct determinant of clinical decision-making.

“If a patient required a high-risk surgical intervention, after 2003 no physician would touch that patient. The system had set the price; the risk–reward balance was impossible to achieve.” (Dr.K, Male, 27 years’ seniority).

“We are afraid and we stop taking risks. We refer patients to large hospitals. A patient comes and in 2–3 minutes we have written the referral — we send them to a place where we do not know what will happen to them.” (Dr.F, Female, 21 years’ seniority).

The intersection of malpractice anxiety and time-compressed polyclinic conditions described by participants is consistent with population-level imaging-utilisation patterns. According to Sağlık İstatistikleri Yıllığı, hospital MRI scanners rose from 58 in 2002 to 1,001 by 2023, while annual MRI examinations grew from 11.3 million in 2015 to 18.9 million in 2023 [36, 37]. By 2021 the exam-to-scanner ratio reached 17,955 in Türkiye, against an OECD average of 4,771 [38], with Türkiye continuing to rank highest among OECD countries for MRI utilisation [39].

Rationalisation of ethical violations

While all participants acknowledged detecting ethically incongruent conduct, a substantial proportion reported these violations becoming normalised over time. This rationalisation corresponds to the moral distress literature’s account of progressive accommodation of ethically compromising conditions under sustained institutional pressure [18, 28, 40]. (n = 8/12)

“Ethical erosion occurs. These things happen but you cannot change them alone. You resist and resist, but only up to a point. Whatever the system requires, you end up doing that too. You find yourself in an inescapable bind.” (Dr.D, Male, 28 years’ seniority).

“I can say the incentive system has corrupted even the most honest physicians. Before 2003 I never performed unnecessary interventions. After 2003, surgical procedures began on patients who did not need them. We were all implicated — some more, some less.” (Dr.D, Male, 28 years’ seniority).

Theme 2: ethics sacrificed to hospital revenue (scenario 2)

In relation to the second scenario, a majority of participants had personally attended hospital management meetings in which clinical goals were displaced by revenue priorities. (n = 8/12) A recurrent pattern was the use of indirect pressure — phrases such as “of course the decision is yours; so is the responsibility” — functioning as what several participants termed “the stick beneath the robe”. (n = 3/12)

“From reminders about not ordering a pre-operative test because it comes out of the package, to how admitting a patient under a different physician will increase revenue — after all that is laid out, it ends with: ‘Of course you are the physicians; it is your decision what is appropriate for your patients.’ Stick beneath the robe.” (Dr.T, Female, 24 years’ seniority).

“Physicians do not think about cost — they think about their patients. Revenue generation should not be the primary goal of physicians, nurses, or healthcare personnel.” (Dr.N, Female, 25 years’ seniority).

“What kind of art can you produce if you give the artist only black and white? Healthcare has become exactly this: restricted palette, restricted time, restricted material.” (Dr.N, Female, 25 years’ seniority).

Transfer of demand-side agency to the system

Participants described patterns corresponding to supplier-induced demand in health economics [5]: under performance pressure, the physician’s informational advantage over patients is deployed against the patient’s interest. This transfer of demand-side agency from individual physician to institutional incentive structure threatens both beneficence and non-maleficence.

“A five-year-old had a ruptured spleen: operating would yield 600–900 points; watchful waiting, at most 30. The spleen was saved; the immune system preserved. But I became the ‘bad surgeon’ who does few operations. Who measures the benefit you provide to the patient?” (Dr.R, Female, 27 years’ seniority).

“A private hospital expected 50 angiographies per week; that physician did 20. After three months, the contract was terminated. This is the warning shot for other physicians: driven to stand outside the norms of medical ethics by the logic ‘better the patient is humiliated than me’.” (Dr.B, Male, 22 years’ seniority).

“How can a physician direct the patient? I explain it to them in a way that they understand what I am proposing and they follow. The real question is: who ultimately determines what the patient ‘needs’?” (Dr.M, Female, 24 years’ seniority).

Effect of the PHU model on material quality

Participants in both public and private sectors described cost pressure adversely affecting selection of medical materials — directly threatening the beneficence–non-maleficence balance.

“Chinese suture material costs one unit; European material costs five. You use ten of the cheaper thread in one case because it keeps breaking, and at every knot you worry whether it will hold. Quality work requires quality material. But hospital management wants reduced expenditure.” (Dr.K, Male, 27 years’ seniority).

“How can I remain focused on the patient’s benefit when the system is continuously pressing me to think about expenditure, about revenue targets? The patient’s best interest becomes a secondary consideration.” (Dr.M, Female, 24 years’ seniority).

Theme 3: the CAPS autonomy paradox (scenario 3)

The third scenario generated the strongest convergence among participants. The consensus was that a CAPS quota requiring 46–52 patients per day structurally precludes realisation of the autonomy principle. Within a consultation window of 7–10 min — well below the WHO and Turkish Thoracic Society standard of ≥ 20 min [11] — the informational and relational preconditions for genuine informed consent cannot be met. (n = 12/12)

“The patient cannot articulate their real complaint; I am making diagnoses based on complaints that were never actually expressed. I leave everything to the laboratory and radiology. This is progressively distancing us from proper medical practice.” (Dr.P, Male, 25 years’ seniority).

“In 7–10 minutes I need to: have the patient on the examination table, shoes off, check the pedal pulses, the carotid arteries, take blood pressure, auscultate the heart. As I describe it, it sounds absurd — but it is not.” (Dr.R, Female, 27 years’ seniority).

“Patient–physician trust begins with eye contact, with information exchange, with time. The system has eliminated all three.” (Dr.F, Female, 21 years’ seniority).

The complaint mechanism as perverse incentive

Almost all participants described the volume of formal complaint responses required (via SABİM, CİMER, BİMER channels) as a significant drain on clinical time. A paradox emerged: the physician who attempts higher-quality engagement faces greater complaint exposure. (n = 11/12)

“Waiting patients are ready to start an argument because their queue position has passed. The physician must submit a defence — justified or not. Not submitting renders you culpable. Nothing can be done without entering the system.” (Dr.G, Male, 27 years’ seniority).

“We submit defences to SABİM, CİMER, BİMER all at the same time on the same complaint. While doing this we are still expected to see 50 patients.” (Dr.F, Female, 21 years’ seniority).

The polyclinic paradox

Many participants attributed the abolition of the referral gatekeeping system with secondary and tertiary care overload. (n = 7/12) The per-capita physician consultation rate rose from 3.1 in 2002 to 9.5 in 2018 and further to 11.4 by 2023 [36], placing Türkiye fourth-highest among OECD countries [39], not because the population became sicker, but because consultations could not address the underlying problem within the time available.

“The reason a patient visits four different outpatient clinics is that they could not resolve their problem in seven minutes at the first. Those with genuine serious problems are the most harmed by this. The number of physicians who engage with risk and take clinical responsibility has declined.” (Dr.M, Female, 24 years’ seniority).

Theme 4: GHI and the justice impasse (scenario 4)

The fourth scenario elicited participant accounts characterised by a consistent moral framing: that the GHI’s premium-conditionality mechanism transfers institutional injustice onto the physician–patient encounter. Participants consistently identified GHI’s premium debt–conditioned exclusion as creating a “financial wall” that violates the justice principle systematically. (n = 12/12).

“The Ministry of Health puts the physician in front of the patient; it does not put itself there. It does not want to appear as the bad actor.” (Dr.B, Male, 22 years’ seniority).

“They cannot do anything without a system registration. The person without GHI coverage is almost certainly also a person with low income — precisely the person who should not be left outside the system.” (Dr.M, Female, 24 years’ seniority).

“Why do we not provide this service free to those without GHI coverage? The physician is not responsible for this. The system is responsible. But the system is invisible, so I am the one who faces the patient.” (Dr.E, Female, 19 years’ seniority).

Medical impoverishment and physicians’ boundary-testing responses

Several participants described developing individual workaround strategies — minimum investigation with maximum service delivery, routing patients through emergency care, minimising procedure coding. (n = 5/12). In their accounts, these adaptations were experienced as an extension of professional identity rather than subversion of it. We use the analytic term conscientious circumvention to describe these responses: individual ethical agency deployed in opposition to perceived structural injustice, motivated by patient advocacy rather than self-interest.

“What do I do? I tell them: get your debt settled before the disease progresses; if you have a car, sell it. The real problem is: the patient says they can pay for medication but has premium debt. I try to offer maximum service with minimum investigations — it is exhausting work.” (Dr.K, Male, 27 years’ seniority).

“A patient with a serious condition requiring treatment must have a solution found for them — by the institution, at the very least. They must not be left in a void. This situation is deeply distressing.” (Dr.D, Male, 28 years’ seniority).

“No physician wants to send their patient away in that situation. But the system leaves us no choice. We work around it as best we can.” (Dr.G, Male, 27 years’ seniority).

Moral distress accumulation

The fourth scenario analysis revealed that chronic ethical powerlessness was eroding the moral capacity of individual physicians. This process — the gradual retreat from moral engagement under sustained institutional pressure — is consistent with the trajectory through which moral distress accumulates and progresses towards moral injury [18, 21].

“This patient’s tragedy no longer truly concerns me. It is like coming out as Don Quixote and fighting for people who then laugh at you. This is not how it should have been. This was not my dream at university. I do not know how we got here.” (Dr.B, Male, 22 years’ seniority).

“After a while, through the frequency of these cases, you become desensitised. ‘Moral corruption is occurring; I do not want to carry this or know about it’ — eventually.” (Dr.F, Female, 21 years’ seniority).

Cross-cutting theme: professional identity transformation

Across all four scenarios, a consistent pattern was observed in virtually all participants (n = 11/12): the reshaping of professional identity under structural pressure. Long-serving physicians (mean seniority 26 years) drew explicit historical comparisons between their pre-HTP and post-HTP sense of self. This theme was not anticipated in the scenario design; it emerged inductively during the categorisation phase (Table 6).

Discussion

This study elicited four themes through which physicians describe their HTP-era practice as producing conflicts with core principles of medical ethics — conflicts that, as the physicians’ accounts suggest, can accumulate into patterns consistent with moral distress and the progressive transformation of professional identity. Each theme is discussed below in comparative dialogue with the international literature. We emphasise throughout that the themes summarise physicians’ interpretive accounts of their lived experience; comparative dialogue with international findings is pursued to situate these accounts rather than to establish structural-causal claims.

Rationalisation of ethical violations: moral distress trajectory

The most analytically significant pattern in participants’ accounts — the progressive normalisation and rationalisation of ethically incongruent conduct — aligns with patterns documented in the growing international literature on moral distress among clinicians working in neoliberal health reform contexts. An important conceptual clarification is warranted. Dean and colleagues’ framework [19] defines moral injury as a lasting psychological wound arising from the failure to honour deeply held moral commitments — including betrayal by trusted institutions. Moral distress, as conceptualised by Buchbinder and colleagues [18], describes the recurring experience of knowing the ethically correct course while being structurally prevented from pursuing it. In the present study, the rationalisation pattern — “whatever the system requires, you end up doing that too” — is best read as a marker of accumulated moral distress. We document patterns consistent with the trajectory from moral distress toward moral injury; we do not claim to have measured moral injury as a clinical outcome. The identity transformation captured in the cross-cutting theme reflects that trajectory rather than confirming an endpoint, and clinical confirmation of moral injury among this population would require specifically designed prospective or scaled assessment studies.

Grumbach and Willard-Grace [41] situate moral injury as arising from institutional betrayal and the subordination of patient welfare to commercial interests — conditions described throughout the present findings. Within the Türkiye context, Kavas and colleagues’ mixed-methods study [24] provides the closest independent parallel: P4P conditions generated physician alienation from patients, profession, and self. The convergence between two methodologically independent Turkish studies on the same identity-transformation pattern strengthens confidence in the Türkiye-specific findings as documented physician perceptions, while leaving open the question of how far similar patterns exist among physicians who did not participate in either study.

Bondjers and colleagues [40] show that protective work environments can buffer distress while systemic determinants operate beyond individual resilience. Fisher and colleagues [20] establish the chronicity of moral distress prior to COVID-19, confirming its systemic character. Mathews and colleagues [42] examined a Canadian family-physician cohort and support the argument that individually-targeted interventions are insufficient responses to systemic moral distress; system-level structural change is needed.

Defensive medicine: a cross-national structural pattern

The defensive medicine pattern described by participants — positive forms (unwarranted investigations) and negative forms (avoidance of high-risk cases) — aligns with a widening international evidence base on clinician responses to reformed health system contexts. Ries and colleagues’ Australian qualitative study [9] identified fear of peer judgement rather than legal liability as the dominant motivation among the physicians they interviewed. Eftekhari and colleagues’ Iranian qualitative study [43] describes how malpractice pressure in the absence of adequate legal infrastructure can generate systematic positive-defensive tendencies, and Radwan and colleagues’ mixed-methods study in Palestine [44] documents parallel findings in a further national context.

Distinctive within the Türkiye configuration, as participants described it, is the asymmetry between performance-point rewards and procedural risk: high-risk cases tend to be precisely those for which the point allocation is lowest relative to complication probability and medico-legal exposure. As Dr.K articulated, for a complex case such as a patient with co-morbid coronary artery disease and diabetes, the performance points awarded for post-surgical management were described as disproportionately low relative to the clinical effort and legal exposure — producing, in the participant’s framing, a systematic incentive structure that disadvantages patients requiring the most intensive specialist care. The international literature on structural health-system reforms [3] supports an interpretation of this tension as the normative core of the problem: financial incentives can improve some efficiency indicators while simultaneously impairing beneficence, non-maleficence, and justice.

These population-level findings from Türkiye’s Ministry of Health statistics are consistent with the structural-incentive interpretation participants articulated. In the post-data-collection period (2019–2023), MRI examinations grew at a substantially faster rate than equipment installation, producing an exam-to-scanner ratio approximately 3.7 times above the OECD average for MRI utilisation intensity [36–39]. While neither aggregate data nor qualitative accounts can independently establish causation, the convergence of three independent evidence sources — physicians’ practice-level accounts (this study), Turkish Radiology Society institutional analysis [38], and Ministry of Health statistical trends [36, 37] — strengthens the interpretive case that imaging utilisation in Türkiye reflects a combination of clinical need, defensive-practice anxiety, performance-incentive structure, and procurement arrangements that contractually guarantee minimum scan volumes. The patterns documented here therefore appear to be intensifying rather than resolving in the present-day post-reform context.

The CAPS autonomy paradox: rights discourse and structural impossibility

Participants described a striking paradox in the CAPS mechanism: policies rhetorically framed around patient rights compress the time required for genuine informed consent. Liang and colleagues [15] document the same paradox in China. Oueslati and colleagues’ synthesis [12] establishes relational trust, adequate time, and genuine openness as preconditions for shared decision-making — conditions participants described as structurally unavailable within the CAPS quota.

It should be acknowledged, however, that the CAPS quota that constrains individual consultation quality may simultaneously serve the justice principle by enabling a larger population to access specialist services within publicly funded settings. This internal tension within the four-principle framework is genuinely difficult: more equitable access (justice) achieved at the cost of adequate consultation depth (autonomy/beneficence) represents a trade-off that cannot be resolved by reference to any single principle alone. Participants in this study consistently prioritised consultation depth over throughput volume — a value weighting that reflects their experiential position as individual clinicians rather than as population health planners. A public-health planner, a patient-advocacy organisation, or a lower-income service user facing a waiting list for specialist access might weight this trade-off differently. Policy design must navigate this tension rather than dissolving it.

Within 7–10 min, the full cycle of information provision, question answering, preference elicitation, and consensus-reaching that constitutes genuine informed consent is difficult to sustain. Participants described complaint mechanisms as further compounding the paradox: formal complaint channels designed to empower patients function, in clinicians’ perceptions, as de facto deterrents to extended clinical engagement. Bokek-Cohen and Gabay’s concept of medical gaslighting [14] offers an analytic frame for reading some of these experiences as structural products of time and burden, rather than individual-practitioner disposition — though the application of this concept to the Türkiye context is exploratory rather than demonstrated.

GHI and the justice principle: premium conditionality as structural exclusion

Participants described the GHI premium debt–conditioned exclusion mechanism as a structural ethical failure against the justice principle. Pratt and colleagues [16] argue that justice in health policy must extend beyond individual rights to the structural mechanisms of distribution. Berkowitz [45] documents that insurance-based coverage gaps disproportionately affect low-income groups, informal-sector workers, and those with least premium payment capacity — precisely the population Dr.M identified as those “who should never be left outside the system”.

The physicians’ responses to GHI exclusion — workarounds, routing through emergency, coding minimisation — were described by participants as a form of individual ethical agency deployed in opposition to what they perceived as structural injustice. We propose the analytic term conscientious circumvention to capture this response pattern: ethically motivated individual departures from administrative rules, undertaken in the name of patient advocacy rather than self-interest, and experienced by participants as an extension rather than violation of professional identity. Usset and colleagues’ research [21] finds that high moral injury scores are inversely associated with intention to remain in the profession; the demoralisation described here should therefore be read not only as individual psychological outcome but, at a systemic scale, as a potential workforce-sustainability risk.

Professional identity transformation

The cross-cutting transformation participants described — from patient stewardship to shift-completion mentality — parallels documented trajectories of professional enclosure across reform contexts internationally. Kavas and colleagues [24] document in the Turkish context the same attenuation of collegial solidarity and team ethos under P4P — again offering an independent methodological reinforcement of the pattern documented here. Bagenal’s global analysis of health students [46] supports the argument that creativity, self-efficacy, and patient orientation can erode during medical education — plausibly contributing to the structural production of the “shift mentality” participants describe in this study. The “children who do not know their native language” metaphor captures the perceived intergenerational rupture between medicine-as-vocation and medicine-as-credentialled-occupation.

Kavas and colleagues’ research on Turkish medical students’ professional identity [47] documents erosion of moral repertoire and professional identity across six years of medical education — consistent with the interpretation that the transformation described by this study’s senior participants begins earlier in professional formation than HTP exposure. Önal and colleagues’ qualitative framing of physician emigration as ethical exit [27] links the identity-transformation trajectory to a broader systemic outcome — workforce attrition — that has accelerated in Türkiye since the original data collection.

Türkiye’s specificity and international comparisons

Türkiye’s structural configuration combines features that intensify the normative pressures reported across comparable reform contexts: physician income structures tightly dependent on P4P; cost pressures sustained against frozen supplementary payment rates; outpatient overload arising from the abolition of gatekeeping; and simultaneous malpractice and performance pressures. Aktas and colleagues’ street-level bureaucracy framing [25] offers one analytically useful account of how physicians navigate these pressures — through varied compliance strategies whose individual-resilience component diminishes as structural pressure accumulates.

Balekang and colleagues’ Botswana study [28] documents the rationalisation pattern “it is beyond my control” in a geographically distant and culturally distinct setting — offering comparative evidence that structural conditions may shape individual ethical responses through similar mechanisms across reform contexts. The patterns identified in this study thus carry potential relevance across comparable reform contexts, warranting verification through future multi-site studies Stubbs and colleagues’ analysis [2] argues that market mechanisms can improve some responsiveness indicators while systematically undermining professional autonomy and the universality of health rights. This study’s thematic findings are consistent with this analytic claim; they do not empirically establish it beyond the particular interpretive accounts documented here.

Interpretive constraints: retrospective accounts, positionality, and scenario design

Three interpretive constraints warrant particular analytical attention rather than placement as peripheral limitations. They shape how the findings of this study should be read and what weight they can bear.

Retrospective accounts and nostalgic idealisation

Reliance on accounts spanning more than two decades of practice introduces substantive risks of recall bias, retrospective rationalisation, and potential nostalgic idealisation of pre-reform conditions. These are not peripheral methodological concerns but central interpretive constraints. The data themselves contain signals that partially — without eliminating — offset this risk: participants repeatedly acknowledged pre-2003 ethical problems (notably the informal-payment practice colloquially termed bıçak parası) and their own post-reform complicity (“we were all implicated” as one participant put it), indicating that recollections were not uniformly favourable toward the pre-reform period. Nevertheless, the contrasts drawn between the two periods must be read as perceived contrasts reconstructed from the present, not as historically verified structural differences.

Physician-participant positionality and standpoint-dependency

Physicians are embedded actors in the system they describe, not disinterested observers. They hold professional, economic, and organisational interests — legitimately, including interests in income, working conditions, and clinical discretion — that are not always separable from, and are sometimes difficult to disentangle from, patient-welfare concerns. The critiques articulated in this study — particularly those concerning throughput quotas, performance-based income, and managerial pressure — structurally resemble arguments that organised medicine has historically mobilised against systemic reform in other national contexts. This standpoint-dependency does not invalidate the physicians’ accounts — physician voice is a vital and under-represented epistemic resource in health-policy ethics — but it requires careful framing: this study documents how senior specialist physicians construct the ethical landscape of HTP from their position within the system, a perspective that public health planners, patient-advocacy organisations, or lower-income service users might weight differently.

Scenario-instrument architecture and confirmatory risk

The scenario-based design, while methodologically productive and theoretically transparent, carries an inherent confirmatory risk: because each scenario was constructed to pair one ethical principle with one HTP component, the data generated are partly shaped by the analytical architecture of the instrument itself. The inductive emergence of the cross-cutting identity-transformation theme — which was not pre-specified in the scenario design — partially mitigates but does not eliminate this risk. Participants generated findings beyond the scenario structure, but the scenario scaffolding clearly shaped the analytical terrain within which those findings were generated. Future research using open narrative interviewing or grounded theory approaches would provide a methodological counterpoint and a test of the extent to which the patterns documented here survive outside a scenario-scaffolded elicitation context.

Limitations

Alongside the interpretive constraints set out above, this study is subject to four further limitations. First, the single-province design centred on Muğla may not capture the dynamics of large metropolitan centres or eastern provinces. Second, the inclusion criterion of long pre- and post-reform seniority produced a sample of senior physicians (mean approximately 25 years); the perspectives of younger, system-socialised physicians are absent. Third, the study draws solely on physician accounts. The voices of patients, nurses and other health professionals, hospital managers, and GHI administrators are not represented. Nurses, in particular, occupy a distinctive epistemic position on ethical tensions in Turkish health services. Fourth, the interview data were collected between June 2018 and August 2019. The intervening period has seen material changes — including COVID-19, incremental PBRF revisions, and accelerated physician emigration — that may have altered some patterns documented here.

On retrospective recall and the idealisation of the pre-2003 period

All twelve analysed participants were senior specialists asked to reflect on practice conditions extending up to twenty-five years before interview. This design — methodologically necessary for the comparative pre/post-2003 question — introduces well-documented risks of recall bias, retrospective rationalisation, and nostalgic idealisation of pre-reform conditions. With one exception (Dr.P’s acknowledgement of pre-2003 bıçak parası), participants’ accounts of the pre-reform period are uniformly favourable. It is unlikely that the pre-2003 system was ethically uncomplicated across all four Beauchamp–Childress dimensions; the present study cannot adjudicate this and the contrast between pre- and post-2003 in participants’ accounts should therefore be read as a perceived contrast rather than as a documented historical change in actual practice conditions.

On physician positionality

Specialist physicians are not disinterested observers of health-system reform. They are embedded actors with professional, economic, and organisational stakes in the design of any payment, scheduling, or coverage architecture; concerns articulated under the language of “autonomy” or “ethical erosion” may at times overlap with — or be inseparable from — interests in working conditions, remuneration, and discretion. This positionality does not invalidate the accounts collected (which document a perceived ethical-policy tension that is itself the object of inquiry), but it does require that the findings not be read as a neutral structural audit. Future work should triangulate physician accounts with patient, nurse, hospital-management, and policymaker perspectives, particularly given that the international historical record contains episodes in which organised medicine has invoked autonomy-language to resist universalist reforms whose justice implications were favourable to the population at large.

Policy implications

These findings argue that ethical evaluation of health policy cannot be adequately conducted solely through efficiency metrics. Based on physicians’ perceptions documented in this single qualitative study, the following directions warrant further investigation; they are presented as hypothesis-generating implications requiring validation through larger mixed-methods and multi-stakeholder research:

PBRF redesign — performance-based payment designs warrant examination through multi-dimensional evaluation encompassing case complexity, patient outcomes, and ethical compliance, rather than procedure volume alone.

CAPS consultation minimum — differentiated consultation time allocations by specialty and case complexity warrant examination. International benchmarks suggest minimums of approximately 20 min; how such minimums can be achieved without reducing population access requires careful modelling.

GHI premium debt — the practice of premium-debt-conditioned denial of non-elective care raises serious concerns warranting empirical and normative investigation [45].

Clinical ethics infrastructure — strengthened hospital-level clinical ethics capacity, including periodic ethics audit of P4P design choices, points to a potentially significant policy direction.

Educational dimension — educational implications warrant empirical investigation; this study does not support specific curricular prescriptions.

Conclusions

This study presents physicians’ accounts of four patterns of ethical tension between Türkiye’s Health Transformation Programme and the Beauchamp and Childress four-principle bioethics framework, derived from practice-level experiences of specialist physicians with direct practice across the pre- and post-reform periods.

First, the Performance-Based Revolving Fund was described by participants as structuring physician behaviour through market incentives that, in their accounts, generate recurring conflicts with beneficence and non-maleficence: patient poaching, unwarranted interventions, and risk-averse clinical decision-making. Participants reported that ethical violations are progressively rationalised and normalised.

Second, participants described the conversion of public hospitals into autonomous enterprises as creating conditions in which institutional financial priorities displace patient welfare: management pressure, supplier-induced demand, and material quality decline collectively constitute, in physicians’ accounts, a structural strain on beneficence and justice.

Third, participants reported that the Central Appointment System’s patient-volume quotas render genuine informed consent and shared decision-making structurally difficult to sustain. This pattern also reveals a genuine internal tension within the four-principle framework: a quota that limits the depth of individual consultation may simultaneously serve justice by enabling broader population access. Policy design must explicitly navigate this trade-off rather than treating either principle as lexically prior.

Fourth, participants described the General Health Insurance’s premium debt–conditioned exclusion mechanism as transferring institutional injustice onto individual physicians while compressing meaningful patient advocacy.

These four patterns are not isolated in participants’ accounts; they are experienced cumulatively, converging in a cross-cutting pattern of professional identity transformation. Physicians’ cumulative accounts suggest that exposure to HTP mechanisms is experienced not as passive erosion of ethical values but as active reshaping of professional self-understanding — captured here through participant accounts spanning over two decades of practice. These patterns are consistent with the trajectory from moral distress toward moral injury described in the international literature, though clinical confirmation of moral injury as an outcome in this population would require specifically designed prospective studies.

These patterns may be relevant across comparable reform contexts, as similar dynamics have been documented in Australia, Iran, France, Canada, and Botswana. They warrant verification through future multi-site, multi-stakeholder studies. The analytical contribution of the present study is to surface that all four Beauchamp–Childress principles are implicated in physicians’ accounts simultaneously and cumulatively — an integrated pattern unavailable from single-component studies of the HTP.

The most fundamental implication is epistemological: ethical evaluation of health policy cannot be adequately performed from a distance. It requires systematic integration of practice-level clinician experience alongside, not instead of, efficiency-based evaluation. The methodology deployed here — researcher-developed hypothetical scenarios developed through multi-stage expert-panel validation, pilot testing, and reflexive analysis — offers a replicable methodological approach for practice-informed health-policy ethics research, rather than a direct structural diagnosis of the system under study.
