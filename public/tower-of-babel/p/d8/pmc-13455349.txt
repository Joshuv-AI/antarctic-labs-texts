Heterodimerization with signaling-inert TLR8b converts TLR8a from proviral to antiviral sensor in Ctenopharyngodon idella
Authors: Jingjing Zhang, Maolin Lv, Shijie Wang, Yuezong Xu, Pengxu Wang, Bo Tang, Zhiwei Liao, Rui Jiang, Guanyu Chen, Ningxia Xiong, Chunrong Yang, Jingxia Liu, Jianguo Su
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractToll-like receptor 8 (TLR8) is crucial for detecting viral and bacterial ssRNA in mammals. However, its functional characteristics in early vertebrates remain largely unclear. Here, we employed the economically important fish Ctenopharyngodon idella, which harbors two TLR8 homologs (CiTLR8a/b), as a representative model. CiTLR8a/b are trafficked to early endosomes and lysosomes by UNC93B1, where they dynamically form homodimers and heterodimer, and bind ssRNA and dsRNA. Upon ligand binding, they first recruit MyD88, then TIRAP as adaptors. Unexpectedly, CiTLR8a enhances viral replication by suppressing downstream IFN, NF-κB, and AP-1 pathways in vitro and in vivo. In contrast, CiTLR8b is signaling-defective despite ligand binding and adaptor recruitment. Most strikingly, heterodimerization with signaling-inert CiTLR8b switches CiTLR8a into an antiviral sensor by remodeling intracellular TIR-domain conformation through extracellular LRR-ligand interaction. Further, structural and mutagenesis analyses identify critical functional determinants: specific residues (CiTLR8a-V116/S164; CiTLR8b-S43/N790) required for dsRNA binding, and key N-glycosylation sites (N138/N189) and cysteines (C3/C4/C6/C7) in CiTLR8a responsible for its proviral functions, with Cys6/Cys7 being critical for CiTLR8a/b proper localization. TLR8 evolution follows a stepwise pattern: absent in cyclostomes, it emerges in jawed vertebrates and expands via lineage-specific duplications (whole-genome in teleosts; tandem in amphibians/reptiles), is lost in avians, and persists as a single copy in mammals. Notably, Cyprinid TLR8 can bind dsRNA besides ssRNA. Collectively, our findings elucidate the functions, adaptive mechanisms, and evolutionary trajectory of TLR8 in lower vertebrates, uncovering a unique regulatory system involving its two homologs. Introduction

Toll-like receptors (TLRs) are a key class of pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) that play a crucial role in immune defense by recognizing microbe-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs) and damage/danger-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs). TLRs are classified into six subfamilies: TLR1, TLR3, TLR4, TLR5, TLR7, and TLR11 [1]. TLR8 belongs to the TLR7 subfamily and specializes in recognizing intracellular nucleic acids [2]. Notably, TLR7 and TLR8 can be activated by ssRNA from viruses and bacteria, with a preference for sequences rich in uridine and guanosine, respectively [3, 4]. They can also be activated by synthetic short ssRNA molecules with nucleic acid-like structures, such as imiquimod (TLR7) [5], Resiquimod (R848; both TLR7 and TLR8) [6], and certain synthetic single-stranded oligonucleotides [7]. Additional compounds, including CL097, CL075, and VTX-2337, have also been identified as direct activators of TLR7 or TLR8, revealing their potential as pharmacological targets [8]. Furthermore, TLR7 is mainly expressed in B cells and plasmacytoid dendritic cells, while TLR8 is predominantly expressed in monocytes, myeloid dendritic cells, and neutrophils [9]. These distinct expression profiles indicate non-redundant roles for TLR7 and TLR8 in orchestrating immune responses. Consequently, TLR8 has emerged as a particularly attractive target for therapeutic modulation. For instance, TLR8-specific agonists, such as Motolimod, have entered clinical trials for cancer immunotherapy, enhancing anti-tumor immunity by activating tumor-associated macrophages [10]. TLR8 antagonists, such as CU-CPT9a, have demonstrated therapeutic potential in inhibiting the production of autoantibodies in lupus mouse models [11]. These distinct functional and regulatory properties establish TLR8 as a pivotal hub connecting pathogen recognition, immune activation, and the maintenance of self-tolerance.

The functional specificity of TLRs is strongly connected to their subcellular localization. TLR1/2/4/5/6 are predominantly located on the cytomembrane and primarily recognize microbial surface components such as lipopolysaccharide (LPS), peptidoglycan (PGN), and bacterial lipoproteins [12, 13]. In contrast, TLR3, TLR7, TLR8, and TLR9 are distributed in endosomes or lysosomes and specialize in recognizing viral or bacterial nucleic acids [14]. This compartmentalized distribution not only underlies functional differentiation but also enables intracellular proteases to cleave TLRs, thereby limiting their overactivation and establishing a critical barrier against aberrant recognition of self-nucleic acids [14]. Notably, TLR8 undergoes conformational changes and exposes ligand-binding sites exclusively in acidic environments (pH = 5.5-6.0), a feature that prevents nonspecific activation by cytoplasmic self-RNA [15]. The chaperone proteins precisely regulate TLR localization: the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) protein UNC93B1 selectively binds TLR3/7/8/9 and directs their transport to lysosomes [16, 17], while gp96 and PRAT4A (the protein associated with TLR4) participate in the maturation process of cell surface TLRs (TLR1, TLR2, TLR4) [18, 19]. Studies demonstrate that UNC93B1 deficiency leads to complete retention of nucleic acid-sensitive TLRs in the ER [20], confirming the decisive role of transport mechanisms in TLR functionality.

In the evolution of the vertebrate immune system, teleosts exhibit remarkable expansion and diversification of PRRs. Mammals possess 13 TLRs (human TLR1-10, mouse TLR1-9, and TLR11-13); in contrast, teleosts have 20 functional TLRs through whole-genome duplication (WGD) and gene structure remodeling [21]. Additionally, there are significant multi-copy phenomena involving TLR4, TLR5, TLR8, TLR20, TLR22, etc. in teleosts [21]. This genomic evolution reflects not only an increase in the number of TLRs but also the unique functional evolution of immune genes in teleosts. The WGD event in teleosts has facilitated subfunctionalization and neofunctionalization within the TLR family, driving lineage-specific adaptations in pathogen recognition [22, 23]. The sex chromosome system of teleosts retains relatively primitive features, still keeping active X/Y chromosome recombination and exchange, which forms a sharp contrast with the highly differentiated sex chromosomes of mammals [24–26]. Mammalian TLR7 and TLR8 are located on the sex chromosome and are associated with sexually-differential immune regulation. However, most teleosts lack distinct sex-determining chromosomes. Sex determination is influenced by multiple genetic factors and can also be affected by environmental cues [23, 24]. The expansion and evolution of PRRs in the immune system of bony fish exhibit many interesting features, which are likely due to their diverse evolutionary history and the selection pressures to adapt to various aquatic environments. These immunological differences between teleosts and mammals offer valuable perspectives for understanding the evolutionary mechanisms of vertebrate immunity.

Teleosts account for more than half of all extant vertebrates, and grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) is the top-yield farmed fish worldwide [22, 27]. Using grass carp as a model, we investigated the functional evolution of TLR8 gene in early vertebrates. Grass carp possesses two TLR8 homologs: CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b (CiTLR8a/b), originating from whole-genome duplication [21]. Both of them are associated with resistance/susceptibility to grass carp reovirus (GCRV) [28, 29]. In the present study, we found that CiTLR8a/b are transported to early endosomes and lysosomes by CiUNC93B1, where they dynamically form homodimers and heterodimer, bind both ssRNA and dsRNA, and recruit myeloid differentiation primary response protein 88 (MyD88) and TIR domain-containing adaptor protein (TIRAP) to initiate immune responses. CiTLR8a promotes viral replication, whereas CiTLR8b does not directly activate immune signals. CiTLR8b functions as a natural regulatory switch by forming a heterodimer with CiTLR8a, thereby reversing CiTLR8a’s activity for an antiviral effect. Specific dsRNA-binding sites, N-glycosylation sites, and cysteine sites are critical for CiTLR8a/b ligand recognition, signal transduction, and protein stability respectively. Notably, Cyprinid TLR8 possesses the ability to bind dsRNA besides ssRNA. These findings reveal adaptive functional changes of early vertebrate TLR8 gene and provide crucial insights for the adaptive evolution of immune system, aiding in the development of immunotherapy strategies.

All the experiments included in this article were performed with the guidance of the Laboratory Animal Center of Huazhong Agricultural University. Cell lines and GCRV

C. idella kidney (CIK) cell line (CCTCC, RRID: CVCL_CV32) and fathead minnow muscle (FHM) cell line (ATCC, RRID: CVCL_3820) were provided by the China Center for Type Culture Collection and the American Type Culture Collection. The grass carp ovary (GCO) cell line (CCTCC, Standard number: SC/T 7016.3–2012) was generously provided by Professor Fan (Yangtze River Fisheries Research Institute, Chinese Academy of Fishery Sciences). They were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, USA) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (Gibco, USA), and 100 U/mL penicillin and 100 U/mL streptomycin, in a humidified atmosphere of 5% CO2 incubator (Thermo Scientific, USA) at 28 ℃. GCRV-II-097 strain (1.16 × 108 TCID50/mL) was isolated from diseased grass carp and stored at -80 ℃ [30].

Experimental animal, bacterial lysate, and reagent

Healthy grass carp (3–5 g for RNA interference, 15 g ± 5 g for other experiments in vivo) were purchased from Huanggang Farm and temporarily raised in a 300 L glass tank at 28 ℃ with continuous aeration and daily feeding. The animals were adapted for two weeks before the experiments. GCRV was detected in random five individuals by RT-PCR to rule out the possibility that these fish were virus carriers (related primers were listed in Table S2). The cDNA templates of zebrafish (Danio rerio), common carp (Cyprinus carpio), channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus), rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), African clawed frog (Xenopus laevis), Chinese softshell turtle (Pelodiscus sinensis), and human (Homo sapiens) were provided by our laboratory, stored at -80 °C [31].

Preparation process of histopathological sections: after 7 days of immersion infection with GCRV-II, spleen and head kidney were collected from grass carp (weight 15 g ± 5 g) and immediately fixed in 10% neutral buffered formalin for 24 h, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned. Fix the 4 μm sample on a slide coated with aminopropyl triethoxysilane. After dewaxing in xylene, the sections were rehydrated and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Finally, it was mounted with neutral gum, and images were taken using the SQS-40P system (Shenzhen, China).

Aeromonas hydrophila (ATCC 7966) and Staphylococcus aureus (ATCC 12600) were grown to log-phase and measured at OD600 (1.0 = 5 × 108 CFU). The dsDNA and ssRNA were prepared and purified from A. hydrophila according to our previous method [32]. Meanwhile, bacterial lysates of A. hydrophila and S. aureus were prepared. The flagellin C (FlaC) was prepared by the previous method in the laboratory [31].

Grass carp (15 g ± 5 g) were intraperitoneally injected with S. aureus (4 µL/g), A. hydrophila (4 µL/g), and GCRV-II (4 µL/g). Collected spleen and head kidney after 0, 6, 12, 24, and 48 h (n = 6). CIK cells were stimulated with PBS, LPS (1 µg/mL), PGN (1 µg/mL), FlaC (1 µg/mL), S. aureus lysate (1 µg/mL), A. hydrophila lysate (1 µg/mL), dsDNA (from A. hydrophila, 200 µg/mL), ssRNA (from A. hydrophila, 200 µg/mL), polyinosinic: polycytidylic acid (poly(I: C) (1 µg/mL), or GCRV-II (MOI = 0.1), and then mRNA expressions of CiTLR8a/b were measured by quantitative real-time RT-PCR (qRT-PCR) (n = 3). Grass carp (15 g ± 5 g) were intraperitoneally injected with PBS, LPS (4 µg/g), PGN (4 µg/g), FlaC (4 µg/g), dsDNA (4 µg/g), ssRNA (4 µg/g), S. aureus (4 µL/g), A. hydrophila (4 µL/g), poly(I: C) (4 µg/g), and GCRV-II (4 µL/g). Collect head kidney macrophages after 3, 6, 12, and 24 h (n = 6). Total RNA was isolated and reverse transcribed for qRT-PCR.

Plasmid construction, transfection, and RNA interference in vitro

The cDNA sequences of CiTLR8a/b were obtained from the transcriptome database (GenBank numbers were shown in Table S2). The open reading frame encoding CiTLR8a/b was amplified by PCR. Expression vectors were constructed using pCMV-N1-eGFP, pDsRed-N1, and pcDNA4-myc-HisA vector plasmids. The coding region sequences of CiTLR8a/b were amplified using the corresponding primers of homologous recombination (Table S2). Subsequently, the corresponding plasmids were digested with a restriction endonuclease, and the products were ligated to the relevant expression vectors with homologous recombinases (Vazyme, China) to construct the recombinant plasmids. DrTLR8a/b, CcTLR8-1/2/3, IpTLR8-1/2, OmTLR8a1/a2/b1/b2, XlTLR8-1/2, PsTLR8-1/2/3, and HsTLR8 were constructed onto the pCMV-N1-eGFP vector by the same method. All relevant primers are listed in Table S2.

The localization or adaptor vectors (LAMP2-RFP, RAB5-RFP, RAB7-RFP, CiUNC93B1-RFP, MyD88-RFP, TIRAP-RFP, and TRIF-RFP used in the text and luciferase reporter plasmids (pIRF3pro-Luc, pIRF5pro-Luc, pIRF7pro-Luc, pIFN1pro-Luc, pIFN3pro-Luc, pAP-1/JunDpro-Luc, pNF-κB1pro-Luc, and pNF-κB2pro-Luc) were all constructed in our previous studies [32–34]. Plasmid was transfected into cells using the FuGENE®6 transfection reagent according to the instruction.

To interfere with mRNA expression of CiTLR8a, CiTLR8b, CiUNC93B1, CiMyD88, and CiTIRAP in vitro, 18 siRNA sequences (Table S3) were designed and synthesized by GenePharma (China). After transfection for 24 h, the interference efficiency of the 18 candidate siRNAs was evaluated using qRT-PCR. The negative control siRNA was provided by GenePharma (China).

Experimental design of RNA knockdown in vivo

Interference experiments were conducted using siRNA technology based on DNA carrier plasmids [35]. Use of the siRNA online website (https://www.genscript.com/design_center.htmL), specific interference sequences of CiTLR8a/b were designed (Table S2). After synthesizing the above sequences and their reverse complementary sequences, the two sequences were analyzed and combined to obtain a dsDNA fragment containing the sticky end of the corresponding endonuclease, which was connected to the siRNA expression plasmid pRNAT-CMV3.1/Neo (Genscript, China). Negative control sequence, using online tools (https://www.genscript.com/ssl-bin/app/scramble), design and use the same method above, will interfere with the sequence to connect to pRNAT-CMV3.1/Neo (Genscript, China) construct siControl plasmid. A large number of endotoxin-free plasmids were extracted from the constructed plasmids, and plasmid DNA was injected into the grass carp muscle according to the above method. The spleen tissues were collected on third and fifth days post injection to detect the expression changes of CiTLR8a/b genes.

qRT-PCR assay and dual-luciferase reporter assays (DLRs)

Total RNA was extracted using RNAiso Plus (TaKaRa, Japan) and reverse transcribed using a reverse transcription kit (ABclonal, China). qRT-PCR was performed on a LightCycler 480 II system (Roche, Switzerland) using SYBR Premix Ex Taq II reagents (TaKaRa, Japan) with specific primers (Table S2). Gene expression was normalized to EF1α (for cell samples) or 18 S rRNA (for tissue samples) [36]. The data were analyzed using the 2−ΔΔCT method. All the experiments were repeated three times.

FHM or GCO cells were seeded in 24-well plates at a density of 5 × 105 cells/mL for 12 h. Then, cotransfection was performed with 380 ng of expression plasmids, 380 ng of target promoter-luciferase plasmids, and 38 ng of pRL-TK (internal control reporter vector) at a ratio of 10:10:1 for 24 h. Next, cells were stimulated with MAMP (1 µg/mL), GCRV-II (MOI = 0.1), bacterial lysate (100 ng/mL), and PBS was used as a negative control. After 24 h incubation, cells were washed with PBS, lysed with passive lysis buffer (Promega, USA), and assayed for luciferase activities in a luminometer by the DLRs System (Promega, USA). The luciferase reading of each sample was first normalized against that of the pRL-TK levels, and the relative light unit intensity was presented as the ratio of firefly luciferase to renilla luciferase. All the experiments were performed in triplicate and repeated at least three times.

Recombinant protein expression, purification, and MAMP-binding activity

Fragments encoding the extracellular region of CiTLR8a/b were obtained by PCR amplification with primers listed in Table S2. Subsequently, the purified PCR products were ligated to the pGEX-4T1 expression vector after digestion with the appropriate restriction enzymes (EcoR I and Not I). The recombinant proteins were expressed in Escherichia coli BL21 cells, denatured in 8 M urea, and purified by affinity chromatography using glutathione S-transferase-nitrilotriacetic acid (GST-NTA) beads (GenScript, China), according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

The activity of CiTLR8a/b-LRR (leucine-rich repeat ) binding MAMPs was measured by ELISA. The operation was as follows: 96-well microtiter plates were coated with MAMPs (10 µg/well), washed with TBS containing Tween 20, and blocked with 1% BSA. Next, 100 µL of CiTLR8a/b-LRR protein at different concentrations was added to the corresponding wells, using the empty vector-expressed protein as a negative control. After incubation at 18 ℃ for 3 h, the plates were washed three times with TBST for 3 min each time, and 100 µL of anti-GST (monoclonal, 1:1000) was added as the primary Ab. After incubation at 37°C for 1 h, the plates were washed three times with TBST, and 100 µL of goat-anti-mouse IgHRP conjugate (1:3000) was added as a secondary Ab, together with 1 h incubation. The plates were washed four times with TBST for 5 min, and 100 µL of 3,3’,5,5’-tetramethylbenzidine solution was added to each well and then incubated at room temperature in the dark for 15 min. The reaction was stopped by adding 50 µL of 2 M H2SO4. The absorbance was measured on Synergy™ 4 Hybrid Microplate Reader (BioTek, USA) at 450 nm. The wells filled with 100 µL TBS were used as a negative control. The examination was performed by three independent experiments.

Confocal fluorescence microscopy (CFM)

To investigate the subcellular localization of CiTLR8a/b, CIK cells (2 × 105 cells/mL) were cultured in a confocal dish (Biosharp, China) for 12 h until the cell monolayer reached approximately 70–90% confluence. Subsequently, the corresponding plasmids were co-transfected into the cells using FuGENE®6, following the manufacturer’s instruction. Then, the cells were cleaned with PBS, and all cells on the microscope cover slide were fixed with 4% formaldehyde at 37 ℃ for 8 min, and then incubated in 1 µg/mL Hoechst 33,342 in the dark for 10 min to stain the nuclei. After washing the stained cells with PBS, confocal microscopy imaging was performed. Similarly, in the cytomembrane localization study, after fixing the cells with 4% formaldehyde at 37 ℃ for 8 min, the DID red fluorescent probe (Beyotime, China) was used to incubate the cells in the dark at room temperature for 20 min, and the cytomembrane was stained. Finally, the nucleus was stained with Hoechst 33,342. The stained cells were visualized at ×100 magnification under a FluoView (Olympus, Japan).

Pull-down, co-immunoprecipitation (Co-IP), and immunoblotting (IB)

In pull-down experiments, FHM cells or GCO cells were cultured in 10 cm2 dishes for 12 h. When the cell monolayer fusion reached about 70%-90%, the relevant plasmids were transfected, and the relevant experiments were performed. Protease inhibitors were added to the immunoprecipitation lysis buffer, and the cells were lysed in an ice bath for 30 min, followed by centrifugation at 12,000 g for 5 min at 4 ℃ to remove cell debris. The supernatant was transferred to a new tube, incubated with biotin-ssRNA or biotin-poly(I: C) for 2 h at 4 ℃, and then mixed with streptavidin agarose beads for 2 h in a 3D shaker at 4 ℃. After centrifugation at 3,000 g for 5 min, the beads were collected and washed four times with lysis buffer. Subsequently, the beads were suspended in 20 µL of 2 × SDS Sampling Buffer and denatured at 95 ℃ for 10 min, followed by IB detection.

In Co-IP experiments, FHM cells or GCO cells were cultured in 10 cm2 dishes for 12 h. When the cell monolayer fusion reached about 70%-90%, the associated plasmids were co-expressed for the experiments. Cells were lysed in immunoprecipitation lysis buffer (containing protease inhibitors) in an ice bath for 30 min, and then centrifuged at 12,000 g for 30 min at 4 ℃ to remove cell debris. The supernatant is transferred to a new tube and incubated with 1 µg of Ab overnight at 4 ℃ with gentle shaking. Protein A + G Sepharose beads (30 µL) (Biotek, USA) were added to the mixture and incubated at 4 ℃ for 2 h. After centrifugation at 3000 g for 5 min, the beads were collected and washed four times with lysis buffer. Subsequently, the beads were suspended in 20 µL of 2 × SDS loading buffer, denatured at 95 ℃ for 10 min, and then analyzed by IB.

For IB analysis, protein extracts were separated by 6–12% SDS-PAGE gels and transferred onto nitrocellulose membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were blocked in fresh 3% nonfat dry milk dissolved in TBST buffer at 4 ℃ overnight, then incubated with appropriate primary Abs for 2 h at room temperature: anti-GST (monoclonal, 1:1000), anti-GFP (monoclonal, 1:1000), anti-β-tubulin (polyclonal, 1:5000), anti-myc (monoclonal, 1:1000), anti-RFP (monoclonal, 1:1000), anti-MyD88 (polyclonal, 1:1000), and anti-TIRAP (polyclonal, 1:1000), respectively. Then wash three times with TBST buffer and incubate with the secondary Ab at room temperature for 1 h. After washing three times with TBST buffer, the nitrocellulose membranes were scanned and imaged by an Odyssey CLx Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, USA). The results were obtained from three independent experiments.

Native-PAGE

GCO cells were cultured in 10 cm2 dishes for 12 h. Subsequently, pCMV-CiTLR8a-GFP, pCMV-CiTLR8b-GFP, and empty vector pCMV-N1-eGFP were transfected for 24 h, respectively. Then, cell samples were collected for lysis. 5 × native loading buffer (Beyotime, China) was added proportionally to the protein lysis buffer to prepare a native polyacrylamide electrophoresis gel. In the non-denaturing protein electrophoresis buffer, 10 µL of the sample was added to the native polyacrylamide electrophoresis gel. The protein marker was used with 5 µL of native marker (Meilunbio, China). Since the native protein electrophoresis marker maintains the natural structure of the protein and lacks any dye, no bands are visible during electrophoresis. Therefore, after gel transfer, the membrane can be stained with ponceau S. The membrane was washed with PBS three times, and native protein marker bands can be seen on the membrane. Considering that the subsequent blocking and Ab incubation would wash away the ponceau S staining solution, the corresponding marker bands were cut out, and the membrane was preserved. The blocking and Ab incubation process was the same as IB. Finally, the nitrocellulose membranes were scanned and imaged by an Odyssey CLx Imaging System (LI-COR Biosciences, USA). The final luminescence results and marker ponceau S staining results were merged to obtain the IB results containing the native protein marker.

Use the default Settings of NetNGlyc-1.0 Server (http://www.cbs.dtu.dk/services/NetNGlyc/) and NetOGlyc-4.0 Server (https://services.healthtech.dtu.dk/services/NetOGlyc-4.0/) separately to predict N-glycosylation sites and O-glycosylation modification sites, and import the results into PyMOL to locate CiTLR8a/b ectodomain. Both graphical abstract and pathway maps were plotted on the BioGDP website (https://biogdp.com/) [41].

Statistical analysis

SPASS 16.0 and GraphPad Prism 6.0 were used for statistical analysis and presentation graphics. Subcellular localization statistics were performed using the ImageJ software (Pearson’s correlation coefficient) [42]. All data were in mean ± standard deviation format with at least three independent experiments. Error bars indicate the mean ± SD (n = 3). The two-tailed Student test was used to compare the means between the two groups. p < 0.05 value is considered to be a statistically significant difference, and p < 0.01 value is an extreme difference. For the multiple comparisons among groups, different lowercase letters within each group indicate significant difference calculated through the Kruskal- Wallis test followed by Dunn’s multiple comparison test (p < 0.05).

Results

CiTLR8a/b assemble into dimers in the resting state and recognize not only ssRNA but also dsRNA in an acidic environment

The dimerization of type I transmembrane receptors is essential for signal transduction [43]. Before ligand recognition, both HsTLR8 and HsTLR9 exist as pre-formed inactive dimers, maintaining a delicate balance between immune quiescence and rapid activation [38, 44]. CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b are co-localized to a certain extent (Fig. 1A). Then, CiTLR8 was detected in two bands by native PAGE, one at ~ 180 kDa and another at ~ 360 kDa, corresponding to the monomer and dimer forms of CiTLR8, respectively (Fig. 1B). Under the condition of β-mercaptoethanol (β-ME) reduction, all the CiTLR8a/b dimers disappeared and were converted to monomers (Fig. 1C-E). These results indicate that CiTLR8 exists mainly in a dimer form, with a weak monomer present in the resting state. Further, CiTLR8a/b can interact to form homodimers and heterodimer (Fig. 1C-E). However, they cannot interact with CiTLR7 (Fig. 1F and G). Typically, CiTLR8a/b consists of three domains: a LRR domain responsible for ligand binding, a TM domain, and a cytoplasmic TIR homologous domain that recruits downstream adaptors (Figure S1A). We modeled and visualized the possible binding forms using AlphaFold3, SWISS-MODEL, and PyMOL software. The predictive template modeling (pTM) score > 0.5 and interface prediction template modeling (ipTM) score > 0.6 imply that the overall predicted folding of the CiTLR8a/b dimer may be similar to the real structure, suggesting that the prediction results are credible (Figure S1B). To sum up, CiTLR8a/b mainly exists in dimerization and partially in monomers in the resting state to activate rapidly after ligand stimulation.

In mammals, TLR8 senses ssRNA from viruses and bacteria and activates immune responses [3, 45]. However, the exact ligand of TLR8 remains unclear in teleosts. To investigate the ligands of CiTLR8a/b, we first examined the tissue distribution. CiTLR8a/b widely express in 14 tissues of healthy grass carp and highly express in gill, head kidney, brain, spleen, and trunk kidney tissues (Figure S2A). Then, mRNA expressions of CiTLR8a/b in head kidney (central immune organ) and spleen (key peripheral immune organ) are significantly up-regulated at the indicated time points after GCRV-II (dsRNA virus) or A. hydrophila challenge (Fig. 1H). Subsequently, we employed six MAMPs (LPS, PGN, poly(I: C), FlaC, ssRNA, and dsDNA) and 3 microbes (A. hydrophila, S. aureus, and GCRV-II) to examine mRNA expressions in CIK cells post-stimulation. The results show that CiTLR8a/b are significantly upregulated after ssRNA, poly (I: C), and GCRV-II stimulation (Fig. 1H), implying that CiTLR8a/b respond to dsRNA (poly (I: C) and GCRV-II) in addition to ssRNA. The findings are consistent with our previous report [46]. Further, consistent results were obtained in grass carp head kidney macrophages (Fig. 1I). Furthermore, the promoter activities of IFN1, a representative antiviral member of type I IFN, and NF-κB1, which is responsible for pro-inflammatory cytokines, were used to detect whether CiTLR8a/b responded to poly (I: C) and ssRNA (Fig. 1J and K). The results show that ssRNA, poly (I: C), and GCRV-II activate the promoter activities of IFN1 and NF-κB1, while LPS, PGN, dsDNA, and flagellin do not.

To provide direct evidence for CiTLR8a/b binding ssRNA and dsRNA, the recombinant proteins of CiTLR8a/b LRR domain were expressed and purified in vitro (Fig. 1L). Then, ELISA results showed that CiTLR8a/b-LRR proteins have strong binding ability to ssRNA and poly(I: C), and did not bind LPS, PGN, dsDNA, and FlaC (Fig. 1M-O). Meanwhile, CiTLR7-LRR and CiTLR19-LRR were used as positive controls for binding ssRNA and dsRNA, respectively (Figure S2B and C), which were consistent with previous reports [32, 47]. Further experiments revealed that in a neutral environment (pH = 7.4), TLR8a/b-LRR could interact with biotin-ssRNA, but not with biotin-poly(I: C) (Fig. 1P). Considering that the structural changes in the extracellular domain of CiTLR8a/b during prokaryotic expression and purification may affect the protein function, we verified this by eukaryotic expression system. Surprisingly, CiTLR8a/b binds neither ssRNA nor dsRNA at pH = 7.4 (Fig. 1Q). Taking into account that the binding of CiTLR8a/b to ligands may be pH-dependent. Furthermore, the pull-down results confirmed that at an acidic pH = 5.5, CiTLR8a/b-LRR proteins can interact with biotin-ssRNA and biotin-poly (I: C) (Fig. 1R and S). The consistent results were obtained at the eukaryotic level in GCO cells (Fig. 1T and U). Finally, we demonstrated that CiTLR8a/b bind poly(I: C) in a dose-dependent manner (Fig. 1V and W). In summary, an acidic environment is necessary for CiTLR8a/b binding ligands. CiTLR8a/b can bind not only ssRNA but also dsRNA through homodimer/heterodimer.

CiTLR8a/b localize in early endosomes and lysosomes, transported by CiUNC93B1

The subcellular localization of TLRs plays a crucial role in recognizing ligands. Observation using CFM revealed that CiTLR8a/b is not distributed on the cell membrane (Fig. 2A and B). Further, CiTLR8a/b co-localize with the early endosome marker RAB5 and the lysosome marker LAMP2, but not with the late endosome marker RAB7 (Fig. 2C and D). The quantitative co-localization analysis of multiple cells supports these results (Figure S3A). CiTLR8a/b have a high correlation with RAB5 and LAMP2, while the correlation with RAB7 is relatively low. This suggests that they mainly locate in early endosomes and lysosomes, rather than late endosomes. The ER resident protein UNC93B1 is essential for the intracellular transport and lysosome targeting of TLR7/8/9 [17, 20]. The preliminary analysis indicates that both CiTLR8a/b co-localize with CiUNC93B1 (Fig. 2E and F). The quantitative analysis results support this trend (Figure S3A). The acidic amino acid residues between the ectodomain with LRRs and the TM domains in TLR3/7/9 play a crucial role in interacting with the receptor UNC93B1 [17]. Here, we observed that the CiTLR8a/b extracellular domains show similar acidic residues (Fig. 2G). Furthermore, the Co-IP assay indicates that CiTLR8a/b can interact with CiUNC93B1 (Fig. 2H and I), rather than with CiPRAT4A and CiPRAT4B (Fig. 2J). The results indicate that CiUNC93B1 plays a key role in the correct transport and localization of CiTLR8a/b.

CiTLR8a/b recruit not only CiMyD88 (first) but also CiTIRAP (subsequently) as adaptors upon ligand binding

TLR adaptors are proteins containing TIR domains that interact with TLRs, such as MyD88, TIRAP, and TIR domain-containing adaptor inducing IFN-β (TRIF) [13]. When stimulated by MAMPs or DAMPs, TLR recruits specific combinations of adaptors. Here, we observed that CiTLR8a/b can co-localize with CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP (Fig. 3A and B). The quantitative co-localization analysis of multiple cells supports this result (Figure S3B). Then, CiTLR8a/b can interact with CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP post dsRNA stimulation (Fig. 3C, D, F, G). Interestingly, although CiTLR8b can co-localize with CiTRIF (Fig. 3B), and the quantitative analysis indicates a relatively high correlation between them (Figure S3B), there is no interaction between them (Fig. 3E and H). These results suggest that although CiTLR8b and CiTRIF have overlapping or adjacent distributions in space, they cannot form stable or direct interaction complex. These findings suggest that CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP serve as adaptors for CiTLR8a/b. Subsequently, we found that in the absence of dsRNA ligand stimulation, CiTLR8a/b cannot interact with CiMyD88 or CiTIRAP (Figure S5A-D), indicating that the recruitment of downstream adaptors by CiTLR8a/b is strictly ligand-dependent. Next, after CiTLR8a overexpression, the promoter activities of CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP are significantly lower than those of the control group, except for CiTRIF. After CiTLR8a + CiTLR8b overexpression, the promoter activities of CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP are significantly upregulated. However, after overexpression of CiTLR8b, it does not affect the promoter activities of the three different adaptors (Fig. 3I-K). Then, qRT-PCR assays shows that these results are consistent at the mRNA level (Figure S5E and F). Overexpression of CiTLR8a significantly downregulates the expression of CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP; co-expression of CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b significantly upregulates their expression, whereas overexpression of CiTLR8b alone has no significant effect. MyD88 and TIRAP are key adapters that link receptors with downstream signaling molecules in the TLR pathway [13]. When overexpression of CiTLR8a downregulates their expression, their binding to CiTLR8a is reduced, thereby inhibiting the activation of downstream NF-κB1 and IFN1 signaling. When CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b are co-expressed and upregulate their expression, their binding to the receptor is increased, thereby enhancing the activation of downstream NF-κB1 and IFN1 signaling. Overexpression of CiTLR8b alone does not alter their expression and therefore has no significant effect on downstream signaling.

To determine how CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP are recruited, the overexpression of CiTIRAP almost does not affect the promoter activity of CiMyD88 (Fig. 3L), while the overexpression of CiMyD88 significantly affects the promoter activity of CiTIRAP (Fig. 3M). Further, we designed siRNA of CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP (Figure S4B and C). The results show that knockdown of CiTIRAP has no significant effect on the CiMyD88 promoter activity (Fig. 3N). However, the knockdown of CiMyD88 significantly downregulates the CiTIRAP promoter activity (Fig. 3O). Previous reports indicated that there is no interaction between CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP [47]. To determine the recruitment sequence of CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP, Co-IP is used after interfering with the endogenous CiMyD88 or CiTIRAP in GCO cells post ligand stimulation (Fig. 3P and Q). The detection results show that the protein expression levels of CiMyD88-RFP and CiTIRAP-RFP decrease significantly at 24 h post interference. Interfering with endogenous CiMyD88 significantly affects the interactions between CiTLR8a/b and CiTIRAP. In contrast, interfering with the endogenous CiTIRAP has no significant effect on the interactions between CiTLR8a/b and CiMyD88. To sum up, CiTLR8a/b recruits CiMyD88 and CiTIRAP under the stimulation of ligands, while the recruitment of CiTIRAP depends on CiMyD88.

In human TLRs, there is a conserved proline residue in the BB-loop of the intracellular domain except for TLR3, related to the binding of MyD88 [48]. Therefore, the partial amino acid sequences and structures in the TIR domain of HsTLR8, CiTLR8a/b, DrTLR8a/b, and CiTLR19 were compared (Fig. 3R). The results show that both CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b possess conserved proline in the BB-loop. Previous studies showed that the CiTLR19 BB-loop lacks the conserved proline, recruiting TRIF instead of MyD88 as adaptor [32]. To further explore the interaction patterns between CiTLR8a/b and CiMyD88/CiTIRAP, the prediction models of CiTLR8a/b and adaptors were presented using AlphaFold3 and PyMOL (Figure S5G-I). Meanwhile, the precise TIR-TIR binding region prediction model was also constructed and shown (Fig. 3S-U). The results demonstrate that CiTLR8a/b and CiMyD88 interact through the BB-loop, whereas no direct interaction was observed between CiTLR8a/b and CiTIRAP. However, when CiTLR8a/b recruits CiMyD88, an association between CiTLR8a/b and CiTIRAP becomes possible. To sum up, CiTLR8a/b first recruits CiMyD88 through the BB-loop, which in turn facilitates the recruitment of CiTIRAP, leading to the formation of a tetrameric complex that participates in the transduction of CiTLR8a/b signaling.

CiTLR8a facilitates viral replication, CiTLR8b alone fails to regulate the immune response, while CiTLR8a/b suppresses viral propagation in vitro and in vivo.

As mentioned above, CiTLR8a/b can sense dsRNA. We further investigated the antiviral role of CiTLR8a/b against dsRNA virus (GCRV-II) infection. The results show that overexpression of CiTLR8a promotes viral gene (VP4 and VP56) transcription, while co-overexpression of CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b inhibits viral gene transcription (Fig. 4A and B). Meanwhile, overexpression of CiTLR8a can significantly inhibit the transcription of key immune molecules IFN1, IFN3, IRF5, IRF7, NF-κB1, NF-κB2, and AP-1/JunD (Fig. 4C-J). However, the co-overexpression of CiTLR8a + CiTLR8b is contrary to this trend, significantly promoting the expression of the above genes (Fig. 4C-J). Then, CiTLR8a/b knockdown experiments were performed in CIK cells. Among the nine candidate siRNAs, CiTLR8a-s6 and CiTLR8b-s9 are the most effective in interfering with CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b, respectively (Figure S4D and E). The results of qRT-PCR indicate that the knockdown of CiTLR8a inhibits the expression of viral genes VP4 and VP56, while increasing the expression levels of IFN1, NF-κB1, and AP-1/JunD genes (Fig. 4K). Correspondingly, knocking down CiTLR8a + CiTLR8b results in the opposite trend (Fig. 4K). Further, the results of DLRs show that promoter activities of IFN1, IFN3, IRF5, IRF7, NF-κB1, NF-κB2, and AP-1/JunD in CiTLR8a overexpressed cells are significantly reduced after poly (I: C) stimulation (Fig. 4L-S). Correspondingly, the opposite trend occurs in the cells co-expressed with CiTLR8a + CiTLR8b (Fig. 4L-S). There is no significant change in mRNA expression and promoter activity of IRF3 (Fig. 4E and N). Solute carrier family 15 member 4 (SLC15A4) is an endolysosome-resident amino acid transporter that is crucial for trafficking and colocalization of nucleic acid-sensing TLRs and their ligands to endolysosomes [49]. TLR adaptor interacting with SLC15A4 on the lysosome (TASL) is an essential protein in lysosomal TLR pathway, which interacts with SLC15A4 [50]. Our previous research indicated that grass carp has two TASL subtypes, namely CiTASLa and CiTASLb [47], which play a crucial role in recruiting CiIRF5 and CiIRF7 via pLxIS motif and activating downstream pathways (Fig. 4T). Furthermore, overexpression of CiTLR8a significantly reduces cell viability, while knockdown of CiTLR8a significantly increases cell viability. Conversely, overexpression of CiTLR8a+CiTLR8b promotes cell viability, while down-regulation of CiTLR8a+CiTLR8b has the opposite effect. Meanwhile, neither overexpression nor knockdown of CiTLR8b has a significant effect on cell viability (Fig. 4U and V). Direct observations of cellular lesions reflect the consistent results (Figure S6A). To our surprise, after CiTLR8a/b form a heterodimer, they can exert powerful antiviral functions and antagonize the proviral response of CiTLR8a homodimer. CiTLR8b may play a switch role in regulating the immune responses of CiTLR8a. This is of great significance for maintaining the balance of the interaction between virus and host. These results indicate that CiTLR8a plays a proviral role in the immune response, and CiTLR8b reverses the function of CiTLR8a through heterodimerization, which balances the virus-host battle.

Most importantly, we confirmed the functions of CiTLR8a/b in vivo. Using the siRNA technology based on DNA vector plasmids, the interference efficiencies of CiTLR8a-psia1 and CiTLR8b-psib2 are the highest on the fifth day post muscle injection of siRNA and are used for subsequent experiments (Figure S4F and G). Firstly, the survival rate of grass carp was checked post immersion infection with GCRV-II for 21 days. A large number of deaths occur in all groups on the 5th to 7th day, reaching a stable state from the 9th to the 21st day. The survival rate of the CiTLR8a knockdown group is the highest (52%), while the CiTLR8a+CiTLR8b knockdown group exhibits the lowest survival rate (20%). There is no significant difference in the survival rate between the CiTLR8b knockdown (40%) and control (38%) groups (Fig. 4W). The results indicate that CiTLR8a can promote viral replication and infection, while CiTLR8a+CiTLR8b has significant inhibitory effects on GCRV-II infection. TLRs are heavily glycosylated type I transmembrane proteins [22]. To explore the effect of N-glycosylation modification of CiTLR8a/b, we first predicted the N-glycosylation sites. The results show that there are 19 N-glycosylation sites (Asn-X-Ser/Thr) in CiTLR8a/b, respectively, and all the sites were visualized using PyMOL (Fig. 6A) and listed in Fig. 6B. Mutate all the predicted sites mentioned above to alanine for subsequent studies on the effects of the mutations on signals. Therefore, we performed site-directed mutations on 19 N-glycosylation sites of CiTLR8a/b, respectively. Cysteine sites (Cys3/Cys4/Cys6/Cys7) forming disulfide bonds maintain proviral function of CiTLR8a and cysteine sites (Cys6/Cys7) influence CiTLR8a/b subcellular localization

Disulfide bonds, as the most stable covalent connection in proteins, form through the oxidation of cysteine, participate in the folding of the peptide chain, maintain the spatial conformation, and play a decisive role in receptor function. Mutations in cysteines that form disulfide bonds can affect the expression, stability, and intracellular localization of TLR3 [53]. The TLR7-Cys98 and TLR7-Cys475 regulate its proteolytic activation and ligand recognition specificity through intramolecular disulfide bonds [54]. Here, taking the crystal model of HsTLR8 as a reference, the protein models of CiTLR8a/b were constructed through Alphafold3 and SWISS-MODEL. Meanwhile, the ESPript sequence-structure analysis show that there are 6 pairs of potential disulfide bond formation sites in the extracellular domain of CiTLR8a/b (Figure S9). The corresponding cysteine sites were visualized using PyMOL in the CiTLR8a/b homodimer models, respectively (Fig. 7A). It is worth noting that since C2 site can only form disulfide bonds in HsTLR8. The potential 6 pairs of cysteine sites of CiTLR8a/b were denoted as C1, C3, C4, C5, C6, and C7 respectively (Fig. 7B). To evaluate the functional correlation of disulfide bonds, we mutated the cysteine of each participating in disulfide bond to alanine. Results show that under poly(I: C) stimulation, the C3-234 A, C4-237 A, C6-748 A, and C7-750 A mutants of CiTLR8a completely lost the ability to inhibit the activities of IFN1 and NF-κB1 promoters. While the mutations of C1-22 A and C5-452 A have no significant effect (Fig. 7C and D). In contrast, mutating any CiTLR8b cysteine residue has no effect on signal transduction (Fig. 7E and F). Next, pull-down results indicate that single-site mutations have no significant effect on the ligand binding of CiTLR8a/b (Fig. 7G and H). These results indicate that disulfide bonds mainly act on receptor activation rather than the ligand binding stage.

Further, we investigated the influence of disulfide bonds on the subcellular localization of CiTLR8a/b. CFM imaging show that the C3-234 A and C4-237 A mutants of CiTLR8a/b completely co-localize with the WT receptor, while C6/C7 mutants result in obvious positioning anomalies (Fig. 7I and J). We noted that C6 and C7 mutations locate at LRR adjacent to TM regions, but are different from the acidic amino acid residues (Fig. 2G). These results indicate that the disulfide bonds formed at the C6/C7 sites play a crucial role in regulating the proper localization of TLR8 in the endosomal cavity.

To investigate why the C3-234 A and C4-237 A mutations abolish the signaling function of CiTLR8a without affecting ligand binding and subcellular localization, we examined the impact of these mutations on dimer formation. Given that TLRs need to form dimers to initiate signal transduction, we performed Co-IP assays to assess the effects of the C3-234 A and C4-237 A mutations on CiTLR8a homodimerization and heterodimer formation with CiTLR8b. The results show that the mutations don’t affect neither CiTLR8a homodimerization nor heterodimerization with CiTLR8b (Fig. 7K-N). This finding is consistent with previous studies on bovine TLR8, in which systematic mutagenesis of all the cysteine residues revealed that, although most cysteines are indispensable for receptor signaling, neither single nor combined mutations impair TLR8 dimerization or self-association [43]. Based on these findings, we speculated that the C3-234 A and C4-237 A mutations may disrupt key disulfide bonds essential for maintaining the correct conformation of the CiTLR8a ectodomain, thereby impairing ligand-induced receptor activation, and consequently affecting the effective signal transduction upon ligand stimulation.

CiTLR8b switches CiTLR8a function via intracellular TIR-domain conformation regulated by the extracellular LRR-ligand interaction

Given that both CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b bind ssRNA and dsRNA and form homodimers and heterodimer, we generated different chimeric receptors to elucidate the functional roles of their extracellular and intracellular domains in ligand binding and signal transduction. TLRs typically recognize ligands via extracellular LRR domains, which trigger conformational changes of the intracellular TIR domain to initiate downstream signaling [55]. Accordingly, we constructed two chimeras: CiTLR8a extracellular domain fused to CiTLR8b intracellular domain (A-B), and CiTLR8b extracellular domain fused to CiTLR8a intracellular domain (B-A), and further designed ten combinations (Fig. 8A). Co-IP results show that all chimeras dimerize normally (Fig. 8B-H), indicating that domain swapping doesn’t disrupt receptor dimerization. Subsequently, ligand binding assays showed that under acidic condition, both A-B and B-A chimeras efficiently bind ssRNA and dsRNA (Fig. 8I and J), indicating that swapping the extracellular domain doesn’t affect ligand binding. Further, DLR assays reveal that upon R848 or poly(I: C) stimulation, combinations containing only CiTLR8a intracellular domain (A-A, B-A, and A-A + B-A) significantly suppress IFN1 and NF-κB1 promoter activities, whereas those containing only CiTLR8b intracellular domain (B-B, A-B, and B-B + A-B) show no significant effect (Fig. 8K and L). This suggests that CiTLR8a TIR domain is capable of inhibiting signal transduction, whereas CiTLR8b TIR domain, despite its ability to recruit adaptors, cannot independently drive downstream signaling. Notably, all the combinations containing both CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b intracellular domains (A-A + B-B, A-B + A-A, A-B + B-A, and B-A + B-B) induce significant signaling activation, indicating that although CiTLR8b-TIR lacks intrinsic signaling capacity, it can modulate signal output through conformational regulation within heterodimer (Fig. 8K and L). B-A + A-A exhibits the strongest signal inhibition, while A-A + B-B and A-B + B-A show the highest activation, suggesting that signal output is determined by TIR domain composition, and modulated by the extracellular domain (Fig. 8K and L). This phenomenon resembles the mechanism in TLR2/1 and TLR2/6 heterodimers, where ligand-induced conformational changes dictate signaling strength [56, 57]. We speculated that although CiTLR8b can bind to the ligand and recruit MyD88/TIRAP, its TIR domain is unable to effectively convert the recruited adaptors into the functional assembly of the downstream Myddosome complex, remaining in an inactive conformation. In CiTLR8a/b heterodimer, CiTLR8b alters the overall conformation of the receptors through the interaction between CiTLR8a/b and ligands, thereby regulating the spatial arrangement of CiTLR8a/b TIR domains and converting CiTLR8a from an inhibitory state to an active state, thereby achieving the functional transformation of signal output.

To further validate the mechanism, we performed co-transfection assays with varying expression plasmid ratios (Fig. 8M-O). The results show that signal output from the CiTLR8a/b system depends on the relative proportions of distinct dimeric configurations. As the ratio of CiTLR8b-TIR increased, signal output undergoes dynamic changes, indicating that CiTLR8b does not passively participate but rather modulates CiTLR8a signaling in a dose-dependent manner within heterodimer (Fig. 8M-O). In A-A + A-B combination, although A-B itself lacks signaling activity (Fig. 8K), its presence significantly enhances signal activation, further supporting that CiTLR8b-TIR alters the functional state of CiTLR8a-TIR through conformational regulation within the complex (Fig. 8O). To distinguish the roles of ligand binding and conformational regulation in the functional switch of CiTLR8b, we employed ligand-binding-deficient mutants A-mut (CiTLR8a-V116A+S164A) (Fig. 5G) and B-mut (CiTLR8b-S43A+N790A) (Fig. 5E) in co-transfection assays with varying expression ratios. In the A-A + B-mut combination, B-mut reduces the effective proportion of A-A homodimers, leading to a gradual decrease in signaling strength with increasing B-mut ratio (Fig. 8P). In contrast, A-mut + B-B and A-mut + B-mut combinations show no significant change in IFN1 signaling, as they lack functional sites capable of ligand binding (Fig. 8Q and R). Based on these results, we elucidated the mode of ligand binding and signal regulation in CiTLR8a/b heterodimer. On one hand, CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b exhibit a synergistic effect in ligand binding, where both simultaneously participate in ligand binding to stabilize the dimer conformation. On the other hand, the contributions of the two members in signal regulation show significant functional asymmetry. Specifically, CiTLR8a is mainly responsible for signal transduction, while CiTLR8b affects the overall receptor conformation, thereby regulating the signal output of CiTLR8a. Different extracellular domain sources and the proportion of CiTLR8b can significantly affect the signal intensity (Fig. 8K-R), indicating that CiTLR8b finely controls the signal output by regulating the conformational state. Collectively, CiTLR8b primarily reshapes CiTLR8a function through conformational regulation within heterodimer. Heterodimer formation is critical for functional switching and signal enhancement. CiTLR8b acts as a conformational regulator in heterodimers, converting CiTLR8a from an inhibitory to an activating state by altering the spatial configuration of TIR domain. This mechanism not only explains why CiTLR8b is nonfunctional alone but functional in cooperation, but also provides new molecular insights into conformational regulation of TLR heterodimer, laying a theoretical foundation for future structural studies.

Cyprinid TLR8 binds dsRNA besides ssRNA in acidic environment

CiTLR8a/b can recognize ssRNA and dsRNA in an acidic environment. We wondered whether the function is conserved in evolution. Firstly, we collected 35 TLR8 homologous sequences (Table S1) from 20 representative vertebrates to establish a phylogenetic tree (Fig. 9A). TLR8 clustering is consistent with taxonomic evolution. Meanwhile, TLR8 loses in aves, suggesting that this gene may be under strong selection pressure, and its functional requirement may have been replaced by other gene during the evolution. Then, we compared the copy number and chromosomal positioning of TLR8 gene across 11 representative vertebrates (Fig. 9B and C). TLR8 exhibits a dynamic evolutionary history across vertebrates. It is absent in cyclostomes, emerges as a single copy in Chondrichthyes, and is expanded in some teleosts via WGD, giving rise to multiple variants. In contrast, some amphibians possess two TLR8 members through tandem duplication (TD). A notable secondary expansion occurs in certain reptiles, generating 2–3 TLR8 variants through TD [58]. TLR8 is completely absent across the avian lineage [59]. In mammals, TLR8 is a single-copy gene. These results indicate that the gene copy number of TLR8 exhibit diversity during the evolution of vertebrates. From its initial absence, through a single-copy state, to expansion via WGD and TD, it undergoes loss in the avian lineage before eventually stabilizing as a single-copy gene in mammals. This dynamic evolution reflects the transformation of immune strategies in species living in different environments, revealing the diverse adaptive strategies adopted by innate immune genes in response to different pathogen pressures and physiological environments.

However, IpTLR8-1 (Fig. 9I), IpTLR8-2 (Fig. 9J), OmTLR8a1 (Fig. 9K), OmTLR8a2 (Fig. 9L), OmTLR8b1 (Fig. 9M), OmTLR8b2 (Fig. 9N), XlTLR8-1 (Fig. 9O), XlTLR8-2 (Fig. 9P), PsTLR8-1 (Fig. 9Q), PsTLR8-2 (Fig. 9R), PsTLR8-3 (Fig. 9S), and HsTLR8 (Fig. 9T) only interact with ssRNA in acidic environment and do not interact with dsRNA in either neutral or acidic environments. Further, after stimulation with R848 or poly(I: C), DrTLR8a significantly downregulates the promoter activities of IFN1 and NF-κB1, while DrTLR8b significantly upregulates the promoter activities of IFN1 and NF-κB1. The combination of DrTLR8a and DrTLR8b shows a stronger synergistic upregulation of the promoter activities of IFN1 and NF-κB1 (Fig. 9U and V). Correspondingly, after stimulation with R848 or poly(I: C), overexpression of HsTLR8, PsTLR8-1, and XlTLR8-1 significantly increases the promoter activities of IFN1 and NF-κB1 in the R848 group, not poly(I: C) group (Fig. 9W and X). Furthermore, taking the mammalian TLR8 as an example, we conducted a molecular docking analysis on the interaction between HsTLR8 and poly(I: C). The results show that the potential binding regions of HsTLR8 to poly(I: C) mainly locate at the N-terminal and C-terminal (Figure S7B). The predicted strongest binding energy is only − 0.79 kcal/mol, indicating that its binding ability is extremely weak. Theoretically, it does not have binding ability and might belong to a non-specific interaction [39]. The four key dsRNA binding sites (CiTLR8a-V116/S164 and CiTLR8b-S43/N790) identified in CiTLR8a/b are not conserved in HsTLR8 sequence (Figure S9). In order to detect whether the key binding sites for poly(I: C) between grass carp and zebrafish are conserved, we constructed the corresponding DrTLR8a-I126A, DrTLR8a-S174A, DrTLR8a-I126a+S174A, DrTLR8b-Y43A, DrTLR8b-S791A, and DrTLR8b-Y43A+S791A mutants. DrTLR8a/b mutants at the corresponding sites does not reduce the binding ability for poly (I: C) (Fig. 9Y and Z). These results suggested that the key binding sites of TLR8a/b for poly(I: C) in zebrafish and grass carp are different. Notably, TLR8 emerges in Chondrichthyes. Due to restriction of the relevant protection regulations on Chondrichthyan, we didn’t explore the ligand binding and immune response of TLR8 in Chondrichthyans. The results indicate that Cyprinid TLR8 can effectively bind dsRNA besides ssRNA.

Based on our research results, we proposed the schematic model to illustrate how TLR8 regulates the immune signaling pathways (Fig. 10). In C. idella, TLR8a and TLR8b homodimer/heterodimer not only bind ssRNA but also dsRNA. Both are located in the early endosomes and lysosomes through UNC93B1 transport. Recruit MyD88 first, and then recruit TIRAP as adaptors. TLR8a promotes viral replication, CiTLR8b binds ligands and recruits adaptors, but does not regulate signal transduction. Interestingly, TLR8b promotes the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines by forming TLR8a/b heterodimer, activating the NF-κB1 and AP-1/JunD pathways. At the same time, the TLR8a/b-MyD88/TIRAP-SLC15A4-TASLa/b-IRF5/7-IFN1/3 axis triggers antiviral immunity. By contrast, mammalian TLR8 in the endosome just binds ssRNA, not dsRNA, and only recruits MyD88 as an adaptor. Through the TLR8-MyD88-SLC15A4-TASL-IRF5-IFNα/β pathway, it induces antiviral immunity and simultaneously activates pro-inflammatory cytokines through the NF-κB and AP-1/JunD pathways.

Schematic diagram of TLR8 signaling pathways in C. idella and mammals. In C. idella, TLR8, as a dual-function receptor located in the lysosome, is capable of recognizing not only ssRNA but also dsRNA. It initiates downstream signaling by recruiting MyD88, followed by TIRAP as adaptors, which subsequently activate the AP-1, NF-κB, and type I IFNs signaling pathways to ultimately induce an antiviral immune response. In contrast, mammalian TLR8, although also located in the lysosome, can only recognize ssRNA and cannot bind dsRNA. Its signal transduction depends on MyD88 and activates the AP-1, NF-κB, and type I IFNs signal cascades to exert antiviral immune effects. Discussion

In humans, several key innate immune genes cluster on the X chromosome, such as TLR7 and TLR8, and their dysregulation closely associates with autoimmune diseases like systemic lupus erythematosus [60]. Although grass carp has differentiated X/Y chromosomes, its sex determination mechanism is not dominated by a complete sex chromosome but is coordinately regulated by multiple sex-linked genes on chromosome 10 [25]. Here, we noticed that CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b locate on chromosome 9 and 10 (sex-linked region), respectively. After experiencing the WGD event, teleosts acquire greater evolutionary flexibility in gene copy number. The multiple copy coexisting and multiple dimerization combinations contrast sharply with the strict single-copy model of TLR8 in mammals. Both CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b can form homodimers and heterodimer, and recruit adaptors. This difference highlights distinct evolutionary strategies in different vertebrate lineages in response to pathogen pressure and underscores important evolutionary implication.

TLRs are type I transmembrane receptors, and dimerization is crucial for signal transduction. We found that in the resting state without ligand stimulation, CiTLR8a/b mainly exists in the form of dimers and a few monomers. Further studies show that CiTLR8a homodimer promotes viral replication, which is consistent with CiTLR8a knockout result in vivo [61]. Although CiTLR8b can bind to ligands and recruit adaptors, it cannot independently drive downstream signal transduction. After forming a heterodimer with CiTLR8a, CiTLR8b reverses the pro-viral effect of CiTLR8a. We further clarified the molecular basis of this functional transformation through the construction of chimeras. Although both CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b can recognize ssRNA and dsRNA, their signal output is mainly determined by the intracellular TIR domain conformation, while the extracellular domain is responsible for ligand binding and conformational regulation. Although the TIR domain of CiTLR8b can recruit adaptors (MyD88/TIRAP), it may remain in an inactive conformation and cannot effectively promote the assembly of the downstream Myddosome complex [48]. It is noteworthy that in the heterodimer, CiTLR8b plays a key role as a conformational regulator. It mediates conformational changes through the extracellular domain, reshaping the spatial arrangement of the CiTLR8a TIR domain, transforming it from an inhibitory state to an active state, thereby achieving the functional transformation of signal output. Different chimeric combinations and ratio experiments further demonstrate that the signal strength not only depends on the composition of the TIR domain but also is regulated by the source of the extracellular domain and the proportion of the dimer conformation, exhibiting a clear dose-dependent relationship. This suggests that CiTLR8b not only participates in ligand binding but also achieves dynamic regulation of signal intensity by modulating the conformational state of CiTLR8a. Moreover, the analyses of ligand-binding-deficient mutants indicate that the formation of functional heterodimer depends on the bilateral ligand binding ability, suggesting that dual ligand binding may stabilize the dimer conformation to promote signal transduction. We revealed that CiTLR8b acts as a “conformational regulator” in the heterodimer, regulating the spatial configuration of the TIR domain of CiTLR8a, reshaping its functional state, and achieving the functional transformation from pro-viral to anti-viral. CiTLR8a/b form a mutually balancing bidirectional regulatory network in the downstream signaling pathways. This conformational-dependent signal balance mechanism provides a theoretical framework for understanding the regulation of innate immunity and the maintenance of immune homeostasis.

The localization of TLRs is mainly controlled by proteins such as UNC93B1 (TLR5/7/8/9) [16], MD2 (TLR4) [62], gp96 [19], and PRAT4A (TLR1/2/4/7/9) [18]. We found that CiTLR8a/b are transported through CiUNC93B1 rather than CiPRAT4A or CiPRAT4B to early endosomes and lysosomes. After TLRs bind to ligands, different TLRs recruit specific adaptors. Previous studies showed that all the human TLRs (except TLR3) have a proline residue in the BB-loop, which can bind to MyD88 [55]. We found that both CiTLR8a/b and DrTLR8a/b have the conserved proline in the BB-loop, which implies that MyD88 may be a theoretical adaptor for TLR8a/b in grass carp and zebrafish. Interestingly, we found that CiTLR8a/b recruit not only CiMyD88 but also CiTIRAP as adaptors. Notably, this recruitment occurs only upon ligand stimulation. This is consistent with the classical activation mechanism of the TLR family, in which ligand-induced conformational rearrangement of the receptor is a prerequisite for recruiting downstream adaptors and initiating signal transduction [44, 63]. Moreover, CiTLR8a/b cannot directly recruit CiTIRAP as an adapter. They firstly recruit CiMyD88 through the BB-loop, and then CiMyD88 acts as a medium, forming a tetramer structure together with CiTIRAP. This finding is different from the recruitment sequence of mammalian TLR2 and TLR4 [64]. We not only addressed the transportation proteins of TLR8 but also clarified the recruitment mechanisms of adaptors in grass carp.

To determine the key binding sites of CiTLR8a/b to dsRNA, we screened the predicted potential binding sites. The results indicate that under acidic conditions, the four sites CiTLR8a-V116A/S164A and CiTLR8b-S43A/N790A reduce the binding activity to dsRNA respectively. Further, simultaneous mutations at the two sites CiTLR8a-V116A+S164A or CiTLR8b-S43A+N790A completely eliminate the binding ability of CiTLR8a/b to dsRNA. Notably, after poly(I: C) stimulation, only CiTLR8a-V116A/S164A increase the activity of the IFN1 promoter, while all the mutants of CiTLR8b have no significant effect on the IFN1 promoter. Mutations at the corresponding key dsRNA binding sites of CiTLR8a/b in zebrafish do not affect its binding to dsRNA. In the previous study, the key poly(I: C) binding sites of CiTLR5a/b were also not conserved in DrTLR5a/b [31]. Here we compared the sequences among CiTLR8a/b and HsTLR8 and found that only few ssRNA binding sites are conserved, while ssRNA binding sites between CiTLR7 and HsTLR7 are highly conserved [47], indicating that TLRs have sequence and species specificity in ligand binding.

The precise functional regulation of TLRs closely relates to glycosylation modification [65, 66]. The two N-glycosylation site mutants (N138A and N189A) enhance the IFN1 signal transduction function mediated by CiTLR8a, but do not affect ligand binding, which might be due to a decrease in protein dynamics that reduces the stability of the ligand binding region [67]. N-glycosylation modification of TLR8 has a significant impact on the maintenance of protein function, while there is no significant effect in all the examined aspects after inhibiting O-glycosylation.

As cysteine-rich transmembrane receptors, the extracellular LRR domain of the TLR family usually contains multiple conserved disulfide bonds, which are crucial for maintaining the stability and function of proteins [53, 54]. By comparing with the crystal structure of HsTLR8, we discovered six pairs of completely conserved cysteines involved in the formation of disulfide bonds in TLR8a/b of grass carp and zebrafish. Among them, any one of the four loci of C3/C4/C6/C7 of the mutant CiTLR8a can completely lose the ability to suppress the promoters of IFN1 and NF-κB1. However, mutating any CiTLR8b cysteine residue has no significant effect on signal transduction. Meanwhile, the mutation of a single cysteine site that forms disulfide bonds has no significant effect on the binding of CiTLR8a/b to ligands. Previous study indicated that cysteine, involved in the formation of disulfide bonds, can affect the expression, stability, and intracellular localization of TLR3 [53]. C6 and C7 mutations in CiTLR8a/b lead to the subcellular localization abnormality. It is worth noting that although C3 and C4 mutations in CiTLR8a completely lose the ability to inhibit downstream signals, its ligand binding ability, subcellular localization, and dimer formation ability are not significantly affected. This speculation is highly consistent with the previous research conclusion in bovine TLR8: Cysteine mutations do not affect the dimerization and ligand binding of the receptor, but can completely block signal transduction [43]. The results indicate that the CiTLR8a/b disulfide bonds mainly function in the conformational transition during receptor activation rather than directly mediating ligand binding, and may affect intracellular localization.

In teleosts, amphibians, and reptiles, which are exposed to high pathogen pressure and variable environments, TLR8 tends to increase its copy number to enhance immune defense capacity through quantity and diversity [58, 59]. TLR8 gene is lost in aves, whose function may have been compensated by other TLR members, maintaining defense efficacy through the overall reconstruction of the immune system [58]. In mammals, TLR8 tends to stabilize as a single-copy gene and is integrated into a complex regulatory network to balance antiviral defense and autoimmune risk [37]. These findings suggest that the functions of orthologous genes are not fixed but adaptively differentiate according to the ecological niche and physiological needs of species. This difference reflects the adaptive evolution of pathogen recognition strategies among different vertebrate lineages. Considering the enormous diversity of fish species and the evolutionary pressures derived from their adaptation to distinct ecological niches, the functional divergence of TLR8 across different fish lineages remains to be further elucidated in future studies.

Conclusions

In conclusion, we found neofunctionalization of two TLR8 homologous genes, TLR8a and TLR8b, in C. idella. CiTLR8a/b binds not only ssRNA but also dsRNA ligands in an acidic environment. Both CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b localize to early endosomes and lysosomes via CiUNC93B1 transportation, recruit CiMyD88 first, followed by CiTIRAP through the BB-loop to form stable tetramer structures. CiTLR8a promotes viral replication and is highly expressed in the susceptible group, CiTLR8b is signaling-inert, while CiTLR8a/b heterodimer suppresses viral propagation. CiTLR8b switches CiTLR8a function via TIR-domain conformational regulation. Thereby, CiTLR8a/b play a dynamic regulatory role in receptor dimerization. CiTLR8a/b heterodimer enhances IFN1 and IFN3 expression via the TLR8a/b-MyD88/TIRAP-SLC15A4-TASLa/b-IRF5/7-IFN1/3 signaling cascades, and strengthens proinflammatory responses via NF-κB1 and AP-1 pathways. Both CiTLR8a and CiTLR8b possess two crucial dsRNA binding sites, located in different regions of CiTLR8a/b, and both exhibit sequence specificity and species specificity, jointly triggering dynamic immunity. Furthermore, N-glycosylation sites (N138A/N189A) and the cysteine sites (C3/C4/C6/C7) play key roles in the pro-viral function of CiTLR8a. This study elucidates the evolutionary divergence of TLR8 and its dynamic dimer-mediated immune functions. These findings advance our understanding of TLR activation mechanisms, gene neofunctionalization, and evolutionary adaptability.
