Clinical Profiles of Biobank-Linked and Non-Biobank Patients with Liver Cancer: An Exploratory Analysis Using Nationwide Cancer Registry and Claims Data
Authors: Chun-Ying Chen, Ru-Yu Huang, Yi-Hsin Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposePatients represented in biobank cohorts may differ from the broader registry-defined cancer population. This study aimed to describe demographic, clinical, and survival differences between biobank-linked and non–biobank-linked patients with liver cancer in a nationwide cancer registry.Patients and MethodsThis retrospective, registry-based exploratory study linked patients from the Taiwan Liver Cancer Network (TLCN), a multicenter liver cancer biobank, to the Taiwan Cancer Registry (TCR) database and identified patients diagnosed with liver cancer (ICD-O-3: C22) from 2007 to 2020. Comorbidities and survival follow-up were obtained from the National Health Insurance Research Database and the Death Registry. Patients were classified as TLCN-linked or non–TLCN-linked according to linkage status with the TLCN. Group differences were described using absolute standardized mean differences (ASMDs), and overall survival was summarized using Kaplan–Meier estimates and compared using Log rank tests.ResultsWe compared 4790 TLCN-linked patients with 126547 non-TLCN-linked patients identified in the cancer registry. Using an ASMD threshold of 0.20, several meaningful between-group differences were observed. Compared with non-TLCN-linked patients, TLCN-linked patients were younger and had higher proportions of men (79.0% vs 69.9%), early stage (stages I–II: 78.6% vs 57.2%), and surgery as first-course treatment (94.8% vs 50.75%), as well as a lower proportion of recorded metastasis (1.5% vs 12.6%). Differences in most comorbidities were small by ASMD. Among patients who received surgery, median overall survival was longer in TLCN-linked patients than in non–TLCN-linked patients across stages; for example, among stage I patients, median survival was 10.89 versus 7.27 years, respectively.ConclusionAlthough TLCN biospecimens and associated data provide an important resource for translational research, TLCN-linked patients differed from non–TLCN-linked patients in the nationwide registry. These findings highlight the importance of considering the composition of the TLCN cohort when interpreting the applicability of future studies using TLCN biospecimens and associated data.Keywords: liver cancer, biobank, standardized mean difference, survival analysis

Introduction

Modern precision medicine and drug development research require an understanding of a wide range of factors, including genetic profiles, as essential information. To support these investigations, many biobank-based cohorts have been established to provide the necessary collection of genetic data.1,2

Biobanks differ in how they recruit participants. Some large population biobanks, such as UK Biobank and the All of Us Research Program, rely on voluntary participation,3,4 whereas others are assembled through healthcare systems, hospitals, clinical encounters, or disease-specific care pathways.5,6 In both settings, the composition of a biobank cohort may be shaped by how participants enter the resource rather than by probability sampling of the broader disease-defined population. Volunteer-based biobanks may be influenced by sociodemographic factors, health behaviors, healthcare access, and willingness to participate, while hospital-based biobanks may be shaped by institutional participation, referral patterns, clinical eligibility, biospecimen availability, and consent procedures.3–7 As a result, patients linked to a biobank may differ from the broader population defined by disease registries.

These issues are particularly relevant for cancer biobanks. Cancer patients may be included in a biobank during diagnosis, treatment, surgery, tissue procurement, or follow-up care at selected institutions. As a result, biobank-linked cancer patients may include disproportionate numbers of individuals treated at participating hospitals, those with available biospecimens, and those clinically able and willing to provide consent.6–8 However, empirical descriptions of how cancer patients linked to biobanks differ from broader registry-defined cancer populations remain limited.9,10 Clarifying these differences may provide useful clinical context for future translational and outcome-oriented studies using biobank data.11 Using the nationwide cancer registry as the primary common data source for both groups, this exploratory study aimed to describe the demographic and clinical profiles, and survival patterns of patients with liver cancer linked to a biobank and to compare these features with those of the remaining patients with liver cancer identified in the registry.

Materials and Methods

Study Design and Data Sources

We conducted a retrospective, registry-based exploratory cohort study by linking records from the Taiwan Liver Cancer Network (TLCN) with population-based registries and administrative databases from the Health and Welfare Data Science Centre (HWDC), Ministry of Health and Welfare (MOHW), Taiwan.12,13 These databases included the Taiwan Cancer Registry (TCR), National Health Insurance Research Database (NHIRD), and Taiwan Death Registry (TDR).

The TLCN was established in 2005 as a liver cancer research network with a centralized tissue bank and database.14 The TLCN recruited patients with liver cancers from five major medical centers located in northern, central, and southern Taiwan. Participating centers followed a standardized protocol to obtain informed consent and collect biospecimens, questionnaire data, medical record abstracts, and clinical, pathological, and epidemiological information. Liver cancer diagnosis in the TLCN could be based on histology, cytology, radiology, or other predefined clinical diagnostic criteria. Tumor tissue, adjacent non-tumor tissue, and blood samples were initially processed at the participating hospitals and transferred to the central tissue bank at the National Health Research Institutes (NHRI). The TLCN biospecimens and database are managed by the NHRI Biobank.15

The TCR databases consist of population-based cancer registries: the annual report (AR), long-form (LF), and short-form (SF) data files. The AR data file includes all cancer patients reported by hospitals and is used to generate annual cancer incidence for the Taiwan Cancer Registry Annual Report.16 The TCR-Long Form (TCR-LF) was established to include details on cancer staging, first primary treatment, and follow-up information of selected cancers since 2002. In 2007, a more extensive list of cancer data elements was implemented to provide sufficient information for cancer control monitoring.17

The NHIRD has detailed information on medical services for patients, including registry for beneficiaries, ambulatory care claims, inpatient claims, and prescriptions dispensed at pharmacies from the National Health Insurance program, which is a compulsory single-payer health care system which has been implemented since 1995 in Taiwan. At the end of 2009, approximately 23,000,000 people were enrolled in the NHI program, representing more than 99% of the Taiwan population.

The study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. It was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the National Health Research Institutes, Taiwan (EC1110108-E), and the Ethics and Governance Committee of the NHRI Biobank (TLCN 220178).

Study Cohorts

Since detailed diagnosis, staging, and treatment information for liver cancer were available only from 2007 in the TCR-LF data, we extracted patients diagnosed with liver cancer (ICD-O-3 topography code: C22.0, C22.1) from 2007 through 2020. Study groups were defined according to the linkage status between the TLCN and TCR-LF. Patients with liver cancer identified in the TCR-LF and successfully linked to the TLCN were classified as the TLCN-linked group. Patients with liver cancer identified in the TCR-LF but not linked to the TLCN were classified as the non–TLCN-linked group. This cohort sample was constructed by linking the encrypted personal identification (ID) numbers of TLCN patients to the TCR, TDR, and NHIRD. These linked datasets provided information at cancer diagnosis, including diagnosis dates, diagnostic codes, baseline comorbidities and liver-related diseases, first-course cancer treatment information, and follow-up information on death status and date of death from 2007 through 2020. Data management and analysis were conducted in the Health and Welfare Data Science Centre (HWDC). The HWDC implements a privacy protection strategy that permits only pre-approved researchers to access these patient-level databases, and data management and analyses are performed within a designated area. To prevent the possibility of recognizing personal identity, HWDC reviews all analysis results and allows only statistics computed from cells of size three or more to be distributed.

Outcome Measurements and Covariates

The outcome of interest was overall survival. Overall survival was defined as the time from the date of liver cancer diagnosis recorded in the TCR-LF to death or the end of database follow-up, December 31, 2020.

Most data elements were extracted from TCR-LF, including age, sex, year of diagnosis, metastasis status, cancer stage, and first-course cancer treatments. Cancer stage was recorded according to the seventh edition of the American Joint Committee on Cancer (AJCC) staging system. Pathologic stage was preferred; when unavailable, the clinical stage was used instead. Recorded distant metastasis was defined as a clinical M or pathologic M entry indicating distant metastasis in the TCR-LF. Entries that did not indicate distant metastasis, including missing, unknown, not applicable, or no pathologic confirmation of distant metastasis, were grouped as no recorded distant metastasis for the descriptive analysis. Cancer treatments included surgery, radiation therapy, and systemic anticancer therapy, which were defined using TCR-LF. Comorbidities and liver-related diseases were identified from NHIRD claims data during the year before liver cancer diagnosis and treated as dichotomous variables. We selected comorbidities from the disease categories included in the Charlson’s Comorbidity Index (CCI), but did not calculate a weighted CCI score. Comorbidities were defined by relevant diagnosis codes (International Classification of Diseases, 9th Revision, Clinical Modification, ICD-9-CM or ICD-10) provided by Quan et al,18 with recorded in at least two outpatient claims or one inpatient claim. The CCI categories for any malignancy and metastatic solid tumor were not included because all participants had liver cancer, and metastasis was assessed separately; AIDS/HIV was not included in the present descriptive analysis.

Statistical Analysis

The baseline characteristics were presented as frequency distributions for categorical variables, and comparisons were conducted using Chi-square tests. Missing or unknown values were not imputed. For registry-derived variables, missing, unknown, or not applicable codes were handled according to the predefined categories for each variable. For claims-based comorbidities and liver-related diseases, the absence of qualifying diagnosis codes during the baseline assessment period was classified as no recorded disease. Absolute standardized mean differences (ASMDs) were calculated to describe the magnitude of the between-group differences in demographic and clinical characteristics. An ASMD <0.20 was interpreted as indicating a small difference. The Kaplan-Meier method and Log rank test were used to estimate and compare overall survival (OS). In addition, we conducted a subgroup analysis focusing on liver cancer patients who had undergone surgery, for whom a tissue sample would likely be available for biobank enrollment.

All tests were two-sided, and p-values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. Data management and statistical analysis were performed using SAS version 9.4 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Results

We obtained clinical and pathological data from TLCN for 6000 patients. Among them, six participants had duplicate records. For the TCR-LF data, there were 131548 liver cancer patients (ICD-O-3: C22.0, C22.1), of whom 131337 were diagnosed during 2007–2020. Among them, 4790 patients were linked to the TLCN. We further compared the characteristics between the TLCN-linked patients (n=4790) with the rest of the liver cancer patients in the cancer registry (n=126547) (Supplementary Figure 1). Using an ASMD threshold of 0.20, TLCN-linked patients differed meaningfully from non-TLCN-linked patients in age, sex, AJCC stage, recorded distant metastasis, and first-course treatment patterns (Table 1). TLCN-linked patients had a more favorable tumor profile, with a higher proportion of AJCC stage I–II (78.6% vs 57.2%) and a lower proportion of metastasis (1.5% vs 12.6%). They were also more likely to have surgery (94.8% vs 50.7%) and less likely to receive radiotherapy, chemotherapy, or targeted therapy. Differences in selected comorbidities and liver-related diseases were generally smaller by ASMD, although cirrhosis and moderate or severe liver disease were less frequently recorded among TLCN-linked patients. P values are shown in Table 1 for descriptive reference.

Comparison of Clinical Characteristics and Treatments Between TLCN-Linked Group and Non-TLCN-Linked Group

The median survival times for TLCN-linked patients, compared with non-TLCN-linked patients, are 7.15 years and 1.92 years. When stratified by stage, it was 10.72 vs 5.72 years for stage I patients and 10.89 vs 7.27 years for patients who underwent surgery (p-values of Log rank test <0.0001) (Table 2). Similar patterns were also observed for patients in stages II, III, and IV. As shown in Figure 1, at different stages, the survival of TLCN-linked patients is better than non-TLCN-linked patients (p-value for the Log rank test <0.0001). The 1-, 3-, and 5-year survival rates in TLCN-linked group were 87.8%, 71.0%, and 58.9%, respectively, whereas non-TLCN-linked group were 61.7%, 49.2%, and 41.2%, respectively. The differences in 5-year survival rates were 19.5% (73.4% vs 53.9%) in stage I, 19.8% (59.0% vs 39.2%) in stage II, 18.6% (28.6% vs 9.8%) in stage III, and 8.8% (11.2% vs 2.4%) in stage IV. In addition, the survival curves for patients who underwent surgery showed the same trend (Supplementary Figure 2).

Discussion

In this exploratory study, patients with liver cancer linked to the TLCN differed from non-TLCN-linked patients identified in the nationwide registry-defined population. TLCN-linked patients tended to have a more favorable clinical profile, characterized by an earlier stage, fewer recorded metastases, and more frequent receipt of first-course surgery. Differences in selected comorbidities, liver-related diseases, and systemic anticancer treatment categories further indicated that TLCN-linked patients were not simply a random subset of the registry-defined liver cancer population. The unadjusted survival patterns should be interpreted descriptively, as they may reflect baseline clinical differences between groups. These findings provide empirical context for interpreting future studies using TLCN data, particularly when outcomes or treatment patterns are compared with the broader registry-defined liver cancer population.

These differences should be interpreted in relation to the way the TLCN cohort was defined. In general, biobank enrollment requires patient-informed consent and the availability of tissue samples, which may affect the clinical composition of the cohort and make its generalizability to the broader population defined by the registry uncertain. In the TLCN, participants were recruited through selected medical centers with biospecimen collection and informed consent procedures. Therefore, the TLCN-linked group may have preferentially included patients who received care at participating hospitals, had available tumor or blood specimens, were clinically suitable for tissue procurement, or were willing to participate. A strength of the TLCN as a liver cancer biobank is its linkage of biospecimens with structured clinical, pathological, and epidemiological information, which supports translational research. However, its recruitment approach may limit the applicability of findings from TLCN-linked patients to the broader registry-defined liver cancer population.

This interpretation is consistent with previous disease-specific biobank studies. The BioBank Japan liver cancer cohort described the clinical, histologic, and survival profiles of patients represented in a liver cancer biobank and compared selected characteristics with external national or published reference data.19 Notably, that cohort included a high proportion of men and appeared slightly younger than patients in a nationwide survey of primary liver cancer in Japan. A recent evaluation of the Swedish U-CAN lymphoma biobank linked biobank participants to nationwide lymphoma and population-based registers to assess coverage, generalizability, and validity.9 That study reported incomplete coverage of the source lymphoma population and found that biobank participants were younger, had better performance status, had fewer comorbidities, and showed better overall survival than the broader lymphoma population, although cause-specific survival and stage distribution were similar. Our study adds a complementary perspective by using the nationwide cancer registry as the primary common data source to describe how TLCN-linked patients differ from non–TLCN-linked patients with liver cancer in the same registry-defined population, rather than evaluating agreement in variable definitions across biobank and registry data sources.

To our knowledge, few studies have used nationwide cancer registry data to describe how patients represented in a liver cancer biobank differ from other registry-defined patients with liver cancer. A strength of this study is the use of registry-derived information as a common basis for comparing TLCN-linked and non–TLCN-linked patients within the same registry-defined population. Several limitations should be considered. First, this was an exploratory descriptive study. The analyses were intended to characterize differences between TLCN-linked patients and non–TLCN-linked patients identified in the registry, rather than to estimate causal effects of biobank participation or to identify independent prognostic factors. Second, the biobank-linked group was derived from selected participating hospitals, and observed differences may reflect the TLCN recruitment pathway, including institutional participation, biospecimen availability, and consent procedures. Accordingly, the TLCN-linked group should not be interpreted as representing all registry-defined patients with liver cancer. Third, although additional liver cancer–specific prognostic variables were available, this exploratory study did not perform adjusted prognostic modeling. Survival was summarized using unadjusted Kaplan–Meier methods. Therefore, unmeasured or unadjusted confounding may have contributed to the observed survival differences, which should be interpreted as descriptive differences rather than adjusted associations with TLCN linkage status.

Conclusion

In conclusion, linking the TLCN with nationwide registry data provided an empirical description of how patients represented in the TLCN differed from non–TLCN-linked patients with liver cancer. The observed differences in demographic and clinical profiles, treatment patterns, and unadjusted survival support careful interpretation of future studies using TLCN biospecimens and associated data.
