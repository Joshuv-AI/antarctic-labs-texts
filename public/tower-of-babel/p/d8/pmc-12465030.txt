Beyond sequence: A physics-informed machine learning framework for predicting DNA mutations
Authors: M Suárez-Villagrán, N Mitsakos, JH Miller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis paper investigates how incorporating information from a quantum tight-binding model can enhance the predictive capability of machine learning models for identifying mutation-prone sites in mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA). We employ quantum Hamiltonian techniques and machine learning to explore mutations in mitochondrial DNA's hypervariable segment 1 (HVR1). This region is recognized for its high variability and is frequently used in genealogical DNA testing and research. Our approach considers the local energy associated with each base pair, as well as the interactions among electrons within the DNA chain. For this study, we analyze data from the Mitomap database. Our findings suggest that both the local ionization energies and the context-dependent nature of the base pairs significantly influence the locations of mutations within DNA. Specifically, our machine learning model can extract valuable insights when examining homopolymeric runs—regions where a single base pair repeats multiple times within a sequence.Keywords: Machine learning, Mutations, Quantum biology, Homopolymeric runs, Hypervariable region 1, DNA, Mitochondrial DNA

Introduction

Our DNA is composed of four base pairs: guanine (G), adenine (A), thymine (T), and cytosine (C) [1]. These base pairs are arranged to form the entire genetic code of an individual. Each person has a unique genetic code that serves as a biological fingerprint [2], [3]. This genetic code evolves over time due to the evolutionary process and is passed from parents to their children during development in the mother's womb. The combination of parental DNA creates a new genetic code sequence. However, mutations can occur during the copying and mixing of genetic material. Those germline mutations are integrated into the DNA of each cell in the offspring's organism.

There is another type of mutation that occurs after conception: somatic mutations. These mutations occur in any cell of the body but not in germ cells. They are not hereditary and are not passed on to offspring. Somatic mutations can affect a few or many cells in an organism. Examples of disorders caused by somatic mutations include cancer [4], some neurological disorders [5], [6] like epilepsy [7], skin damage [8], etc. The causes of this type of mutation are environmental factors, radiation exposure, viruses, lifestyle, and errors during DNA replication and repair [9]. Exposure to high magnetic field gradients can cause changes in cell shape at the cellular level, which subsequently affects cell function and behavior [10], [11]. These gradients can disrupt or alter biological pathways [12], resulting in cell elongation and the redistribution of organelles [13].

When DNA is exposed to oxidizing agents, such as reactive oxygen species (ROS) or ionizing radiation, an electron can be removed from any of the nucleotides within the DNA [14]. The energy required to remove an electron is known as ionization energy, and it varies between different base pairs [15]. Guanine has the lowest ionization energy among nucleotides (EG<EA<EC<ET) [16], [17], making it more likely that an electron will be removed. Once the electron is removed from the guanine, it leaves behind a site of positive charge, or hole, which can move along the DNA strand until stabilized and localized [18], [19], [20], [21]. This positively charged site is highly reactive, leading to the formation of various oxidative lesions [20], [22], [23]. These lesions can promote tautomerization, oxidative base deamination [24], and the formation of wobble pairs [25], with a key example being 8-oxoguanine (8-oxoG) [26], [27], [28], [29], [14]. The crucial step relating 8-oxoG to mutations is its ability to mispair during DNA replication. Typically, guanine (G) pairs with cytosine (C) in a Watson-Crick base pairing manner (as illustrated in Fig. 1a). However, the oxygen present at the 8-position in 8-oxoG (see Fig. 1b) allows it to adopt a different conformation, known as the syn-isomer, which can form a stable Hoogsteen base [30] pair with adenine. This occurs because 8-oxoG, being an oxidized form of guanine, can take on a syn conformation (where the base is rotated around the glycosidic bond) and form two hydrogen bonds with adenine (see Fig. 1c). In the subsequent round of replication, this newly incorporated adenine will pair with thymine (see Fig. 1d). As a result, an initial G:C base pair, following oxidative damage to G and subsequent misincorporation, will mutate from a G:C pair to a T:A pair.

Studying and understanding the underlying physical mechanisms and patterns of mutations can help predict where they are more likely to occur within the genome [31], [32], [33]. Such knowledge is crucial for developing effective prevention and intervention strategies, and significant research efforts have been directed toward this critical issue [32], [34], [35]. For instance, mutations in specific genes, such as BRCA, have been identified as predisposing individuals to a higher risk of developing conditions like breast and ovarian cancer [36]. Given the vast and complex nature of the genome, traditional methods are often insufficient to uncover the subtle, non-linear patterns that govern mutational events. This challenge has driven a rapid development in computational and machine learning techniques to enhance our understanding of DNA. However, there remains a need for models that can integrate the fundamental physical properties of DNA into their predictive frameworks. In this paper, we address this gap by introducing a physics-informed machine learning approach that leverages a quantum tight-binding model to enhance the prediction of mutation-prone sites, thereby contributing a novel methodology to this critical area of research.

Quantum Hamiltonian for DNA

We describe DNA as a two-legged ladder [37] with elements likely to move from a specific location in the DNA to an adjacent location, either to the left or right along the same chain or to the nearest neighbors in the perpendicular direction [16]. The elements moving on the DNA are “electron-hole” [38], [22]. Electrons have a negative charge; once an electron is removed from its location in a base pair of the DNA, through ionization, radiation, redox-active chemical agents, etc. [39], [38], it leaves behind a site of positive charge, or hole [40], [41] This newly created hole can migrate until it stabilizes [18] and may potentially trigger a base-pair mismatch during replication [19], with a certain probability known as the “hole probability” [42], [41] To represent the DNA chain, we use a quantum Hamiltonian written as [40], [42], [41], [16]:

The movement of the hole-electron within the DNA is defined by the parameter (t∥=1.0 eV) for neighbors connected along the same chain and (t⊥=0.5 eV) for components along the perpendicular chain [43]. This follows the tight-binding model. Each base in DNA is characterized by its ionization energy ϵlm: ϵA=8.24eV, ϵC=8.87eV, ϵT=9.14eV, and ϵG=7.75eV [40], [41], [42], [16]. These local energies contribute to the diagonal components of a square matrix, while the components t∥ and t⊥ appear off-diagonal in the matrix. We impose periodic boundary conditions such that parallel hopping connects the last site in each chain to the first element. We get a square matrix 2NX2N. We obtained the eigenenergies Ei and probability amplitudes Ψi(m,l). We used the Boltzmann distribution to get a pseudo-thermal distribution of all the states P(m,l)=∑i=02N−1|Ψi(m,l)|2exp⁡{−(Ei−E0)/kBT}. Here, E0 is the lowest eigenenergy, kB is the Boltzmann's constant and T is the temperature. We selected kBT=0.047 eV [41], [42].

Lower ionization energy of a DNA base is directly linked to an increased mutation probability, primarily through its heightened susceptibility to oxidative damage. When DNA is exposed to agents that cause ionization, such as reactive oxygen species or ionizing radiation, electrons can be abstracted from the DNA bases, forming reactive radical cations or “electron holes”. Among the canonical bases, guanine (G) possesses the lowest ionization potential, making it the most prone to this initial oxidation event. These electron holes are not static; they can migrate along the DNA double helix. Bases with lower ionization potentials act as preferential “traps” for these migrating holes, leading to a localized positive charge. This localization renders the base highly reactive and susceptible to further chemical modifications, such as the formation of 8-oxoguanine from guanine. These modified bases can then mispair during DNA replication or interfere with accurate repair mechanisms, ultimately leading to point mutations. Furthermore, the local sequence context significantly influences a base's effective ionization potential and its propensity for charge localization, thereby impacting its mutability.

The following section will demonstrate the important contribution of the quantum tight-binding model's derivative in a predictive context. The physical information, including the ionization energies and quantum probabilities derived from this model, will be leveraged as features within a machine learning framework. A predictive model is then trained to identify mutation-prone sites within the mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), allowing us to quantitatively assess the contribution of the quantum-informed features.

Machine learning

In recent years, machine learning has aided in advancing various fields [44], [45], [46], [47], [48], [49], [50], including genomics [51], [52], thereby advancing our understanding of vast and complex biological data [53]. These methods, ranging from traditional statistical models to advanced deep learning architectures, are widely used to discover patterns within DNA sequences [52]. For example, Support Vector Machines (SVMs) [44] are powerful discriminative classifiers known for their ability to handle high-dimensional data and identify optimal hyperplanes for separating distinct classes. In genomics, SVMs are used for tasks such as classifying disease states based on gene expression profiles [54], predicting protein functions from sequence data [55], and identifying regulatory elements in DNA [56], among others. Their effectiveness in learning complex, nonlinear relationships through various kernel functions makes them particularly well-suited for the nuanced patterns of genetic variation.

Ensemble methods, such as Random Forest, are widely used because they can effectively handle large sets of features and identify complex relationships among genetic variants [57]. Random Forests work by building numerous decision trees during the training process and then providing the most common class (for classification tasks) or the average prediction (for regression tasks) from these individual trees [44]. This ensemble approach mitigates overfitting and improves generalization [44]. In the genomic landscape, Random Forests have been effectively used to predict disease susceptibility [58], identify key genetic markers associated with traits, and even forecast gene expression levels [59]. The inherent feature importance measures offer valuable insights into which genomic features are most influential in a given prediction, enhancing biological interpretation. More recently, Transformer deep learning architectures, originally designed for natural language processing, have emerged as highly effective tools for sequence analysis in genomics [60]. Their success is due to the self-attention mechanism, famously introduced in the paper “Attention is All You Need” [61], which enables the model to assess the significance of various regions within an input sequence when making predictions, thereby effectively capturing long-range dependencies. This capability is particularly advantageous for DNA studies, as it facilitates the identification of regulatory elements and mutational hotspots that may be located at significant distances from the genomic regions they affect. Transformers have been applied to predicting enhancer regions, identifying promoter sequences, and generating context-specific representations of nucleotide sequences [62], and have shown strong accuracy in genomic prediction tasks [63], [62]. The integration of these diverse machine learning methodologies provides a comprehensive framework for tackling the complexities of DNA mutation prediction. Typically, a DNA sequence segment can serve as a direct descriptor for a machine learning model aiming to predict genomic events. However, it is often beneficial to augment these raw descriptors with insights from physical models, a “physics-informed” approach. This provides the machine learning model with valuable information that would otherwise be challenging for it to discover on its own. In the following section, we introduce the quantum tight-binding model as a powerful tool for deriving such features.

Methodology

We focus on HVR1, which is a region of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) that exhibits significant variation in mutations between individuals and spans base pairs 16024-16569. The ground truth for our machine learning analysis came from the nucleotide sequence for HVR1 within Mitomap [64], which includes information on its location within the control region, mutations, and types of cell and tissue. As illustrated in Fig. 2b, mutation locations are labeled one, while mutation-free locations are labeled zero. The most common nucleotide change occurs when the base pair transitions from T to C, accounting for 24.6% of the cases. It is closely followed by the change G to A observed in 24.3% of the cases, C to T in 17.9%, and A to G in 16.4%. Other point mutations have an observed frequency below 5% each, as shown in the pie chart in Fig. 2c.

To establish our baseline predictive capability, we used observation windows from the DNA sequence as feature descriptors for our machine learning models. When an electron is removed from a specific base pair in DNA, it creates a hole or positive charge that can move within the DNA structure. Research has shown that the distance this hole can travel varies, ranging from a few base pairs to several hundred base pairs, depending on the specific characteristics of the DNA [15], [20], [42], [21]. However, the likelihood of the hole moving decreases with distance from the initial base pair where ionization occurs, influenced by contextual dependencies and chemical reactions taking place within the DNA. Through an empirical analysis of different window sizes, we determined that an observation window of nine DNA positions, four positions to the left and four to the right of each target location, was optimal.

A sequential split was performed to prevent data leakage, allocating the first 70% of the HVR1 sequence as the training region and the remaining as the test set. The final portion of the HVR1 sequence chosen for the test region is particularly challenging for prediction, as it is closer to and sometimes overlaps with the regulatory elements1 that are critical for mitochondrial function. We further divided our training region into 5 stratified cross-validation folds, ensuring that each fold maintained a representative distribution of the genomic context. To guarantee the independence of our data splits and prevent any feature overlap, samples close to the border were excluded from every training set. We then evaluated a range of models, including Support Vector Machines (with linear and RBF kernels), Logistic Regression, K-Nearest Neighbors, Naive Bayes, Decision Trees, Random Forest, and a Transformer deep learning architecture [44], [61].

Specifically for the deep learning model, we used a straightforward Transformer architecture for a binary classification task. The input, representing a DNA sequence segment, is first passed through a custom Positional Embedding layer. This layer projects the input into a higher-dimensional space and augments it with positional encoding, enabling the model to understand the order of the features within the sequence. The augmented sequences are then processed by two consecutive Transformer Block layers. Each block is a standard Transformer encoder, featuring a multi-head self-attention mechanism that allows the model to weigh the importance of all features in the sequence relative to one another, effectively capturing long-range dependencies and complex interactions. Following this, the processed sequence is flattened through global average pooling and fed into a classification head composed of several dense layers with ReLU activation, culminating in a single sigmoid neuron for binary classification. The model was compiled with the Adam optimizer and a binary cross-entropy loss function. Its training was guided by two key callbacks on a small, sequentially held-out validation set: a ReduceLROnPlateau callback, which automatically decreases the learning rate when the validation Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUC-PR) stops improving, and an EarlyStopping callback to prevent overfitting.

By comparing the mean Average Precision (AP) of each model across all cross-validation folds, we established the Transformer architecture as the highest performing, demonstrating consistent predictive capabilities. Subsequently, we augmented the sequence-based features with physical information derived from the quantum tight-binding model. The three values incorporated into the feature set were the ionization energy, the hopping parameter, and the quantum probability for each location along the DNA sequence, as detailed in Section 2. This augmentation of the DNA sequence window with quantum features resulted in an increase in mean AP across all cross-validation splits, a performance improvement that was also observed consistently on the independent test set. In a final analysis, we trained the Transformer model using only the three-dimensional quantum features, and this configuration yielded a further increase in mean AP. This improvement was consistently observed across all cross-validation splits and was particularly notable on the challenging test set, demonstrating the significant information content and predictive power of the quantum-derived features.

Results

To evaluate the predictive power of our physics-informed feature augmentation, we used the Transformer model. We first established a baseline using a 5-fold cross-validation scheme, as described in the previous Section. Fig. 3 presents the precision-recall curves from the model's prediction on the validation set of each fold, comparing the model trained on raw DNA sequence features (blue) against the model trained on physics-informed quantum features (orange). The results demonstrate a consistent and significant performance advantage for the physics-informed model across all folds. This is further substantiated by the quantitative metrics in Table 1, where the mean Average Precision (mAP) of the quantum-feature model consistently surpasses that of the baseline model, highlighting the robustness of the augmented feature set. We performed a final, unbiased evaluation on our single, held-out test set, which as highlighted in the previous Section, is identified as particularly challenging for prediction due to its proximity to the regulatory elements. Fig. 4 shows the precision-recall curves for this test set. Panel (a) reveals that the model using only raw DNA features exhibited poor generalization capacity, with its curve approximating the no-skill classifier. In stark contrast, Panel (b) shows that the model augmented with physics-informed features achieved a robust and well-defined curve, indicating a strong ability to generalize. This significant performance gain is quantified in Table 2, where the mAP for the raw DNA model was 0.544, while the mAP for the quantum-feature model was 0.829. These results provide compelling evidence that incorporating physics-informed features is not only beneficial but essential for achieving high predictive performance on complex and unseen genomic data.

Conclusion & discussion

In the field of genetic mutation prediction, no single feature is sufficient to capture the full biological impact of a variant. The most successful models integrate a diverse array of data, encompassing multiple categories to achieve high accuracy. These features typically include: Evolutionary data, which reflects millions of years of natural selection through measures like sequence conservation; Functional and Contextual information, which details the mutation's effect on regulatory elements and biological pathways; Structural features, describing the impact on protein stability and function; and Clinical and Population data, which provides direct annotations and allele frequencies. It is important to note that our study, given its focus on fundamental physicochemical properties, aims to predict the location of mutational events rather than their functional consequence or pathogenicity. Therefore, our analysis includes all observed mutations in the HVR1 region, irrespective of their known clinical significance or harmfulness, as our feature set is not designed to discriminate between these categories.

Our study introduces a novel and complementary category to this framework: physics-informed features. We demonstrate the potential of this approach to enhance a machine learning model's understanding of the context-dependent nature of mutations within the Hypervariable Segment 1 (HVR1) of mitochondrial DNA. By utilizing a quantum mechanical model based on the tight-binding Hamiltonian, we effectively captured the intricate physicochemical dynamics of mutation events in this highly variable region. Our findings provide compelling evidence that incorporating these quantum-derived features significantly improves predictive capability, particularly for mutations within homopolymeric runs. This suggests a strong, quantifiable relationship between a base's local physicochemical context and its mutational potential. Furthermore, our results underscore the biological significance of HVR1 in genetic research, given its known correlation with mutations across various cell and tissue types. In conclusion, our work highlights the value of integrating fundamental physical principles into machine learning models for genomics. While our study focused on a specific DNA segment, it opens the door for further exploration into additional mutation contexts and other genomic regions. Future research can build upon this foundation by expanding the scope of such physics-informed architectures and refining model parameters, ultimately leading to a more comprehensive understanding of genetic variability and its implications for health and disease.
