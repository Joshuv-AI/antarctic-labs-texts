Metal–organic macrocycles as molecular modulators of amyloid-β aggregation and cytotoxicity
Authors: Chanju Na, Gajendra Gupta, Mingeun Kim, Yena Choe, Junseong Lee, Chang Yeon Lee, Mi Hee Lim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Metal–organic macrocycles provide a versatile platform for integrating multiple chemical functions within a single, well-defined architecture, yet their potential for regulating pathological protein aggregation remains largely unexplored. Here we report rationally designed metal–organic macrocycles that combine piano-stool ruthenium or iridium complexes with a photoactivatable bis(difluoroboron)-1,2-bis((1H-pyrrol-2-yl)methylene)hydrazine (BOPHY)-based ligand to enable dual-mode modulation of amyloid-β (Aβ) aggregation associated with Alzheimer's disease. These macrocycles directly engage Aβ species through surface interactions and, upon light activation, induce oxidative modifications via singlet oxygen, collectively altering aggregation behavior and aggregate morphologies. As a result, they suppress the formation of toxic Aβ assemblies and attenuate Aβ-induced cytotoxicity. Overall, this work establishes metal–organic macrocycles as effective modulators of amyloidogenesis and provides a potential strategy for controlling complex protein aggregation processes in neurodegenerative diseases.

Introduction

Coordination-driven self-assembly offers a powerful strategy for constructing well-defined chemical platforms, in which structural and functional properties can be precisely tuned through the choice of metal ions and organic linkers.1–3 Such systems have been widely applied in catalysis, sensing, and gas storage, and are increasingly being extended to biological contexts.4–7 Among these, metal–organic macrocycles represent architecturally well-defined, discrete assemblies with controllable sizes, shapes, and coordination environments, enabling predictable and tunable interactions with biomolecular targets.8–10 Incorporation of functional ligands further endows these systems with additional reactivities, including redox activity and photodynamic behavior, providing opportunities to engineer multifunctional chemical platforms.11–13 Despite these advantages, the potential of metal–organic macrocycles as molecular modulators of pathological biomolecular processes remains largely underexplored.

Neurodegenerative diseases are driven by the aberrant aggregation of peptides and proteins into structurally heterogeneous and dynamically evolving assemblies.14,15 These processes arise from complex networks of noncovalent interactions, including hydrophobic, electrostatic, and hydrogen-bonding interactions, leading to the formation of oligomers, protofibrils, and amyloid fibrils that are closely linked to cellular toxicity.16–22 Alzheimer's disease (AD), the most common form of dementia, is characterized by the accumulation of aggregated amyloid-β (Aβ) peptides as a central pathological hallmark.23,24 Accordingly, considerable efforts have been devoted to developing chemical reagents capable of modulating Aβ aggregation and toxicity. Among them, metal complexes have attracted significant attention because of their ability to coordinate with Aβ species, together with their structural tunability and diversity.25–27 Despite these efforts, achieving effective control remains a significant challenge due to the intrinsic complexity, heterogeneity, and adaptability of its assembly pathways.

These challenges highlight a key limitation of conventional approaches: single-mode interventions are often insufficient to control multifactorial aggregation processes. Instead, effective strategies must simultaneously engage Aβ through molecular interactions while inducing chemical transformations to redirect aggregation pathways.16,28 In this context, light-triggered oxidative modifications have emerged as a promising approach, enabling site-specific chemical changes that alter aggregation behavior and reduce the formation of toxic species.29–32 Integrating such photochemical reactivity with controlled molecular recognition within a single, well-defined system remains an outstanding challenge, however.

In this study, to address this challenge, we developed metal [ruthenium (Ru) or iridium (Ir)]–organic macrocycles as dual-functional modulators that integrate direct Aβ interactions with photoinduced oxidative reactivity, as illustrated in Fig. 1. These photosensitizer macrocycles were rationally designed by combining piano-stool Ru(ii) or Ir(iii) complexes33,34 with a photoactivatable BOPHY-based ligand [BOPHY = bis(difluoroboron)-1,2-bis((1H-pyrrol-2-yl)methylene)hydrazine35], enabling synergistic surface binding and singlet oxygen (1O2)-mediated oxidation. This integrated architecture modulates Aβ aggregation pathways and alters aggregate morphologies, ultimately suppressing the formation of toxic species and attenuating Aβ-induced cytotoxicity. Importantly, the well-defined macrocyclic architectures organize multiple functionalities within a confined molecular framework, enabling their cooperative interplay and synergistic action. Overall, this work demonstrates that metal–organic macrocycles can serve as versatile chemical platforms for regulating amyloid formation and offers a broadly applicable approach for controlling complex biomolecular assembly processes associated with neurodegenerative disorders.

Results and discussion

Rational design and preparation of metal–organic macrocycles

Efficient modulation of Aβ aggregation requires strategies that proceed through multiple pathways, as single-mode approaches are often insufficient to control its complex and heterogeneous assembly behavior. To address this challenge within a single molecular system, we designed metal [Ru(ii) or Ir(iii)]–organic macrocycles (RuL and IrL) through coordination-driven self-assembly, as presented in Fig. 1a. To enable direct interactions with Aβ, piano-stool Ru(ii) or Ir(iii) complexes were selected as building units due to their ability to engage peptide species via coordination, electrostatic, and π interactions.29,36 These complexes feature the low-spin d6 configuration that confers kinetic inertness, minimizing metal dissociation under biologically relevant conditions.37,38 To introduce oxidative reactivity, a pyrrole–BF2-based photosensitizer (BOPHY)35 was incorporated into the ligand framework (Fig. 1a). Compared to conventional boron dipyrromethene (BODIPY) scaffolds, BOPHY possesses an extended π-conjugated framework that imparts distinct photophysical properties. For example, a representative BOPHY derivative displayed a larger Stokes shift (ca. 16–45 nm) and a higher fluorescence quantum yield (Φ = ca. 0.81–0.89) than its corresponding BODIPY analogue (Stokes shift = ca. 6–14 nm; Φ = ca. 0.61–0.79).39 In addition, the BF2 unit enhances structural rigidity and photostability, making BOPHY well-suited for biological applications.35,40 Coordination of Ru(ii) or Ir(iii) to the BOPHY-based ligand is further expected to enhance photodynamic activity through the heavy-atom effect, promoting intersystem crossing (ISC) and enabling efficient 1O2 generation for peptide oxidation.29,41,42 Collectively, this design integrates molecular recognition and photoinduced oxidative reactivity within a single macrocyclic framework.

The dipyridyl BOPHY ligand (BOPHYPy2; L) was synthesized as outlined in Scheme 1. The precursor BOPHY-I2 was prepared via Schiff base formation followed by BF2 chelation to construct the BOPHY core, and subsequent electrophilic iodination. The ligand L was then obtained in moderate yield through a multistep Suzuki–Miyaura coupling reaction. Full characterization was achieved using 1H, 13C, and 19F nuclear magnetic resonance (NMR) spectroscopy, 1H–1H correlation spectroscopy (COSY), and high-resolution electrospray ionization mass spectrometry (HR-ESI-MS) (Fig. S1–S3). In the 1H NMR spectra, the signals at 7.20 and 8.71 ppm were assigned to the pyridyl protons, while the 19F NMR spectrum showed a quartet at −141.72 ppm corresponding to FB–F resonances. The mass value of L was determined to be 493.2106 m/z.

Synthetic routes to (a) L, (b) RuL, and IrL. Reagents and conditions: (i) RuCl, AgCF3SO3; (ii) IrCl, AgCF3SO3; (iii) CH3OH, N2 (g), 24 h, room temperature.

Subsequently, the macrocycles (RuL and IrL) were obtained via coordination-driven self-assembly of the ligand (L) with the corresponding metal precursors (RuCl and IrCl) in the presence of silver triflate (AgCF3SO3) in methanol (CH3OH) at room temperature (Fig. 1 and Scheme 1). Chloride abstraction by AgCF3SO3 facilitates coordination of the pyridyl donors to the metal centers, leading to the formation of discrete macrocyclic assemblies in high yield. The formation of RuL and IrL was confirmed by a combination of spectroscopic and mass analyses (Fig. S4–S9). In the 1H NMR spectra, RuL exhibited characteristic signals at 5.70 and 5.93 ppm corresponding to the aromatic protons of the p-cymene ligand, along with peaks at 7.40 and 8.50 ppm assigned to the pyridyl protons of L. Similarly, IrL displayed a singlet at 1.60 ppm and doublets at 7.51 and 8.49 ppm, corresponding to the C5(CH3)5 moiety and pyridyl groups, respectively.

Consistent with these observations, the 19F NMR spectra of RuL and IrL showed quartets at −141.67 and −141.93 ppm for the FB–F resonances, along with signals for triflate counterions at −79.94 and −79.92 ppm, respectively. Mass spectrometric analysis further supported macrocycle formation, revealing dicationic and tetracationic species arising from the loss of two or four triflate counteranions. For RuL, the peaks at 1301.2059 m/z and 576.6309 m/z were assigned to [M–2CF3SO3]2+ and [M–4CF3SO3]4+, respectively, while IrL showed a peak at 1485.3357 m/z corresponding to [M–2CF3SO3]2+. These results confirm the successful construction of well-defined metal–organic macrocycles that integrate metal-based interaction sites with a photoactive ligand framework.

Structural characterization and photophysical properties

To elucidate the molecular structures of the ligand L and the resulting metal–organic macrocycles (RuL and IrL), single-crystal X-ray diffraction (SCXRD) analyses were carried out. As shown in Fig. 2a, Tables S1 and S2, single crystals suitable for X-ray analysis were obtained for L and IrL. The structure of L confirms the incorporation of two pyridyl groups into the BOPHY chromophore, replacing the iodine(i) atoms in the precursor BOPHY-I2. The pyridyl nitrogen (N) atoms are separated by 17.786 Å, with the pyridine rings oriented out of the BOPHY plane, likely due to steric effects. A dichloromethane (CH2Cl2) molecule was also observed within the crystal lattice as a solvent of crystallization. The crystal structure of IrL reveals a well-defined rectangular architecture, in which two dipyridyl BOPHY ligands bridge two [Ir2(C5(CH3)5)2(dhnq)] units through pyridyl coordination, forming a discrete (2 + 2) metalla-rectangle. It should be noted that the anisotropic displacement parameters for IrL could not be reliably refined due to limited crystal quality.

We next investigated the photophysical properties of L, RuL, and IrL in both chloroform (CHCl3) and buffered aqueous solution at room temperature (Fig. 2 and S10–S12). In CHCl3, L exhibits intense Abs bands in the visible region, with two prominent maxima at 456 nm and 480 nm (Fig. 2b). These bands originate from strongly allowed π–π* electronic transitions of the rigid and highly conjugated BOPHY chromophore and are assigned to the lowest singlet electronic transition (S0 → S1).35 Upon coordination, RuL and IrL display slightly blue-shifted Abs maxima at 452 nm and 478 nm. This hypsochromic shift likely results from the perturbation of the electronic distribution of the BOPHY unit upon metal coordination. Despite this shift, the spectral features remain largely conserved, indicating that the primary electronic transition is localized on the BOPHY core within the macrocyclic framework.

Fluorescence measurements further support this assignment. Upon excitation at 440 nm, L shows a sharp emission maximum at 496 nm with a shoulder around 530 nm, indicative of vibronic structure in the excited singlet state. RuL and IrL display similar emission profiles, confirming that the emissive excited state remains largely localized on the photoactivatable ligand.43 In contrast, their emission intensities are significantly reduced (by approximately four to five-fold) relative to L (Fig. 2b), accompanied by a marked decrease in Φ from 82.0 (±0.3)% (for L) to 1.0 (±0.1)% and 4.4 (±0.1)% (for RuL and IrL), respectively (Fig. 2c). This pronounced quenching upon metal coordination is attributed to enhanced nonradiative decay pathways, including heavy-atom-induced spin–orbit coupling (SOC) that promotes ISC to the triplet state, as well as possible photoinduced electron or energy transfer from the excited BOPHY unit to the metal centers.44 These processes suppress fluorescence, while favoring population of the triplet excited state, consistent with enhanced photodynamic activity in the metal–organic macrocycles.

Under buffered aqueous conditions, the photophysical properties remain dominated by the BOPHY chromophore, consistent with those observed in organic solvents. L, RuL, and IrL exhibit electronic Abs maxima at 391 nm (for L), 443 nm (for RuL), and 446 nm (for IrL), respectively, while the corresponding metal precursors (RuCl and IrCl), which lack the BOPHY unit, show no discernible Abs features (Fig. 2c and S10). Compared with those in organic media, the Abs bands are slightly blue-shifted, likely reflecting solvent-dependent electronic effects, whereas metal coordination induces a modest bathochromic shift in aqueous solution, presumably through stabilization of charge-transfer excited states.45 Furthermore, both macrocycles display only modest spectral changes during the early stages of incubation in buffered aqueous solution at 37 °C. Although their characteristic Abs bands gradually diminish upon prolonged incubation, suggesting partial dissociation or decomposition, RuL and IrL still retain ca. 20% and 26% of their initial Abs, respectively, after 48 h (Fig. S11). In the emission spectra, L presents a maximum emission at 555 nm, while RuL and IrL exhibit blue-shifted emission maxima at 491 nm and 492 nm, respectively (Fig. S12), indicating the modulation of the S1–S0 transition upon metal coordination. Overall, these results demonstrate that the BOPHY unit governs the photophysical properties of the macrocycles across different environments, while metal coordination subtly tunes the electronic structure and excited-state behavior.

Building on these findings, we evaluated the 1O2 generation abilities of L, RuL, and IrL in CHCl3 using 1,3-diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) as a chemical probe to quantify the amount of 1O2 in solution.46 Upon reaction with 1O2, DPBF is converted to a non-fluorescent endoperoxide, resulting in a decrease in its characteristic Abs at 410 nm. As shown in Fig. 2d and S13, light irradiation in the presence of L, RuL, and IrL led to a rapid decay of the DPBF Abs within 5 min, indicating efficient 1O2 production by all three compounds.

Quantitative analysis revealed high 1O2 formation efficiencies for all compounds, following the trend IrL [91.1 (±1.6)%] > L [89.4 (±1.1)%] > RuL [83.1 (±1.7)%]. These results indicate that the photodynamic activity is largely governed by the intrinsic photophysical properties of the BOPHY chromophore, which enable efficient population of the triplet excited state via ISC.47 Importantly, incorporation of L into the metalla-rectangular frameworks preserves this photoactivity, while metal coordination further modulates excited-state dynamics. The relatively lower 1O2 generation efficiency of RuL may arise from additional nonradiative decay pathways, including metal-centered (MC) states and metal-to-ligand charge-transfer (MLCT) processes that compete with ISC.48 In contrast, IrL exhibits enhanced 1O2 production, likely due to the stronger heavy-atom effect of Ir, which promotes SOC and facilitates ISC, thereby compensating for coordination-induced quenching.49

Taken together, these results demonstrate that the BOPHY-based ligand retains its photodynamic capability upon incorporation into metal–organic macrocycles. At the same time, the identity of the metal center fine-tunes 1O2 generation through modulation of excited-state dynamics. This combination of preserved photoactivity and tunable reactivity highlights the potential of these systems as effective photoresponsive modulators for the oxidative control of biomolecular processes.

Impact on the formation of Aβ aggregates under aerobic conditions (inhibition studies)

We investigated the effects of metal–organic macrocycles on the aggregation of Aβ42, the predominant component of senile plaques,50 and compared their reactivity with that of the ligand L and the corresponding metal complexes (RuCl and IrCl). The resultant Aβ42 species were analyzed by gel electrophoresis with western blotting (gel/western blot) using two anti-Aβ antibodies (6E10 and anti-Aβ22–35) as well as by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Gel/western blot analysis provides molecular weight (MW) distributions of soluble and gel-penetrating insoluble Aβ aggregates (≤245 kDa). The use of two antibodies recognizing distinct epitopes (Phe4–Tyr10 for 6E10 and Glu22–Met35 for anti-Aβ22–35)29,51 enables complementary detection of Aβ species and minimizes bias arising from epitope accessibility.52,53 TEM further complements these analyses by visualizing larger Aβ aggregates excluded from the gel matrix, offering morphological information.

In the inhibition experiments, freshly prepared Aβ42 was incubated with the compounds in the absence or presence of light to probe their effects on the formation of Aβ42 aggregates. As shown in Fig. 3, S14 and S15, after 24 h of incubation, under both dark and photoactivated conditions, RuL and IrL modulated Aβ42 aggregation with distinct effects. Under aerobic conditions without irradiation, both macrocycles altered the MW distribution of Aβ42 species, as evidenced by increased smearing in 17–245 kDa (for RuL) and 35–245 kDa (for IrL) ranges, respectively, along with a reduction in monomeric bands near 5 kDa in the 6E10-stained gel (Fig. 3b). RuCl and IrCl, in line with their anticipated influence on Aβ aggregation, produced similar redistribution patterns characterized by broad smearing above ca. 17 kDa and diminished low-MW species. In contrast, the ligand L induced comparatively modest changes, primarily increasing band intensity in the 75–245 kDa region. Similar trends were observed with the anti-Aβ22–35 antibody, confirming that the metal–organic macrocycles effectively redistributed Aβ42 species under dark conditions.

Upon photoactivation, RuL and IrL induced more pronounced changes in the MW distributions, showing clearer redistribution of Aβ42 aggregates for both antibodies. Notably, the anti-Aβ22–35-stained gels displayed more amplified variations compared to those detected under dark conditions, indicating that light-driven processes further enhance aggregation modulation. RuCl and IrCl also altered MW distributions upon irradiation; however, their effects were comparable under both dark and light conditions. In contrast, the ligand L exhibited noticeable reactivity only in the presence of light, where more evident changes were monitored using the anti-Aβ22–35 antibody.

TEM studies further corroborated the aggregation-modulating effects of the macrocycles, their ligand, and the corresponding metal complexes (Fig. 3c, d and S15). In the absence of light irradiation, RuL and IrL induced pronounced morphological changes, yielding predominantly short fibrillar assemblies with average lengths of 162 (±20) and 186 (±22) nm, respectively, compared with the dense fibrillar networks formed by compound-untreated Aβ42 [578 (±40) nm]. RuCl and IrCl likewise generated shorter, partially stacked fibrillar assemblies [196 (±19) and 236 (±23) nm, respectively], whereas L predominantly formed dense fibrillar bundles with an average fibril length of 617 (±32) nm similar to that of Aβ42 control.

Upon light irradiation, RuL and IrL produced more dispersed and sparsely distributed fibrils with average lengths of 174 (±21) and 152 (±19) nm, respectively, corresponding to reductions of ca. 70% and 74% relative to untreated Aβ42 [575 (±29) nm]. In the case of IrL, less dense and more loosely organized fibrillar networks were notably observed. RuCl and IrCl exhibited morphologies comparable to those observed under dark conditions, characterized by short fibrils [201 (±15) and 210 (±22) nm, respectively] accompanied by minor amorphous aggregates. Photoactivated L, however, induced distinct morphological changes, producing less dense networks of short fibrils [254 (±21) nm], corresponding to an approximately 59% reduction in average fibril length relative to the dark condition.

To further elucidate the effects of the macrocycles on Aβ42 aggregation kinetics, turbidity assays were performed under both dark and photoactivated conditions (Fig. S16). Under dark conditions, Aβ42 exhibited a sigmoidal turbidity profile with a lag time (tlag) of ca. 0.21 h and a half-time (t1/2) of ca. 0.89 h. Treatment with RuL and IrL delayed both the nucleation and elongation phases, increasing the tlag and t1/2 values (for RuL, tlag = ca. 0.42 h and t1/2 = ca. 1.3 h; for IrL, tlag = ca. 1.3 h and t1/2 = ca. 1.8 h), while also producing higher initial turbidity than the Aβ42 control. These effects were further enhanced under light irradiation, where photoactivated RuL and IrL produced more pronounced delays in aggregation kinetics and lower final turbidity than the corresponding dark samples, suggesting that photoactivation further redirects Aβ42 aggregation pathways.

Moreover, we monitored the time-dependent secondary structural changes of Aβ42 with and without the macrocycles by circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy under both dark and photoactivated conditions (Fig. S17). Under both conditions, RuL- and IrL-treated Aβ42 rapidly developed a characteristic negative band at ca. 218 nm within 3 h, indicative of β-sheet formation, with the effects being more pronounced for IrL. The β-sheet signal continued to increase during incubation and reached an intensity comparable to that of compound-free Aβ42 after 6 h. These findings indicate that RuL and IrL promote the early formation of β-sheet-containing Aβ42 species while suppressing their subsequent growth and maturation into fibrillar assemblies, ultimately yielding short fibrillar structures. Collectively, these results demonstrate that RuL and IrL modulate Aβ42 aggregation under aerobic conditions in both the absence and presence of light, with enhanced effects upon irradiation. The distinct aggregate behaviors observed reflect differences in the metal center identity, ligand environment, and photodynamic activity. In comparison, RuCl and IrCl exhibit comparable, light-independent impact on Aβ42 aggregation, consistent with a mechanism dominated by direct interactions, whereas L modulates aggregation only under photoactivated conditions.

Influence on Aβ assembly formation under anaerobic conditions and in the presence of reactive oxygen species (ROS) scavengers

To further elucidate the role of O2, gel/western blot analyses were performed under anaerobic conditions (Fig. 3e). In the absence of O2, RuL, IrL, RuCl, and IrCl continued to alter the MW distributions of Aβ42 assemblies, exhibiting broad and intensified smearing above 17 kDa in both 6E10- and anti-Aβ22–35-stained gels regardless of light exposure. In contrast, L produced minimal effects under these conditions, even upon irradiation. These results indicate that RuL, IrL, and the corresponding metal complexes modulate Aβ42 aggregation through mechanisms that are not strictly dependent on light or O2, whereas L requires both light and O2 to exert its effects. This distinction suggests that the metal-containing species predominantly modulate Aβ42 aggregation through direct interactions with the peptide, likely involving coordination and hydrophobic contacts.

To further investigate whether ROS-mediated processes contribute to the photoinduced reactivity of the macrocycles, we examined the effects of RuL, selected as a representative macrocycle, on Aβ42 aggregation in the presence of sodium azide (NaN3) and dimethyl sulfoxide (DMSO), well-established scavengers of 1O2 and hydroxyl radicals (˙OH), respectively54,55 (Fig. S18). Even in the presence of either NaN3 or DMSO, photoactivated RuL produced MW distributions distinct from those of the corresponding dark samples, accompanied by intensified smearing above ca. 35 kDa. Quantitative analyses of the gel lanes over ca. 5–245 kDa further revealed more than two-fold increases in the signal intensity relative to the corresponding dark samples under both scavenger-treated conditions. These findings indicate that the light-induced reactivity of RuL was not abolished by either ROS scavenger under our experimental conditions. Because NaN3 and DMSO may also influence the photophysical and photochemical properties of RuL, however, these results should not be interpreted as excluding the involvement of ROS-mediated processes.

Together, RuL and IrL effectively modulate Aβ42 aggregation under both dark and photoactivated conditions, exhibiting dual-mode reactivity involving direct interactions with Aβ42 and light- and O2-dependent oxidative modifications. Specifically, their effects arise from a combination of direct surface association with Aβ42 and photoinduced oxidative modifications mediated by 1O2. This light-induced reactivity was not abolished by ROS scavengers under our experimental conditions. In comparison, RuCl and IrCl primarily influence Aβ42 aggregation through light-independent interactions, while L acts exclusively through light- and O2-dependent oxidative pathways.

Effects on the disassembly of preformed Aβ aggregates (disaggregation studies)

We next evaluated the ability of the metal–organic macrocycles to disassemble preformed Aβ42 aggregates or change their subsequent aggregation pathways. Preformed Aβ42 aggregates were generated by incubating Aβ42 for 24 h, followed by treatment with L, RuL, IrL, RuCl, and IrCl, light irradiation for 5 min, and further incubation for an additional 24 h. As depicted in Fig. 4, S14 and S19, RuL and IrL effectively altered the MW distribution of preformed Aβ42 aggregates under both dark and photoactivated conditions. In the absence of light, both macrocycles reduced the band intensity of low-MW species while inducing broad smearing in the 35–75 kDa range in the 6E10-stained gel (Fig. 4b). Similar trends were observed with the anti-Aβ22–35 antibody, showing decreased monomeric bands together with increased high-MW smearing. RuCl and IrCl exhibited comparable effects, whereas the ligand L showed minimal influence under these conditions.

Upon photoactivation, RuL and IrL induced more substantial redistribution of Aβ42 species. In the 6E10-stained gel, a substantial reduction in signal intensity across the full MW range (5–245 kDa) was observed, while anti-Aβ22–35 analysis revealed enhanced smearing relative to the control, indicating extensive remodeling of the aggregate population. RuCl and IrCl also reduced monomeric band intensity, but their redistribution patterns differed from those of the macrocycles, consistent with their distinct modes of action. In contrast, L displayed MW distributions similar to the compound-free control even under light and aerobic conditions, suggesting limited disaggregation capability.

As illustrated in Fig. 4c, d and S19, TEM analyses further supported the disaggregation behavior of the compounds. In the absence of light activation, RuL and IrL triggered pronounced morphological changes, yielding sparsely distributed short fibrillar assemblies with average lengths of 172 (±15) and 225 (±37) nm, respectively, compared with the large, dense fibrillar aggregates observed in untreated Aβ42 [563 (±39) nm]. Notably, IrL also produced a minor population of amorphous aggregates (Fig. S19). RuCl and IrCl exhibited similar effects, generating short fibrils [194 (±19) and 204 (±25) nm, respectively] along with mixed amorphous or filamentous assemblies. In contrast, L largely preserved dense fibrillar bundles with an average fibril length of 506 (±69) nm, comparable to that of compound-free Aβ42.

Upon light irradiation, RuL and IrL further remodeled the aggregate morphology, producing stacked short fibrils accompanied by a minor population of elongated fibrils. Consistent with these observations, the average fibril lengths of RuL- and IrL-treated Aβ42 fibrils were 187 (±29) and 254 (±22) nm, respectively. In contrast, RuCl and IrCl displayed morphologies comparable to those observed under dark conditions, with average fibril lengths of 169 (±18) and 185 (±28) nm, respectively, indicating limited light-dependent effects. Photoactivated L, however, induced noticeable morphological changes, forming less dense fibrillar networks with a reduced average fibril length of 388 (±37) nm. Nevertheless, the fibril population remained heterogeneous, consisting of both short and elongated fibrillar structures.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that RuL and IrL effectively affect the disassembly of preformed Aβ42 aggregates under both dark and photoactivated conditions, with more pronounced effects upon irradiation. The distinct size distributions and morphologies observed under light exposure suggest that photoinduced oxidative processes contribute to aggregate disassembly and reorganization. In contrast, RuCl and IrCl primarily act through direct interactions with Aβ42 species, while L alters aggregate morphology mainly under photoactivated conditions without substantially affecting size distribution.

Impact on Aβ-induced cytotoxicity

Given that the metal–organic macrocycles modulate Aβ42 aggregation under both dark and photoactivated conditions, we next evaluated their effects on Aβ42-induced toxicity in human neuroblastoma SH-SY5Y (5Y) cells using the MTT assay [MTT = 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)-2,5-diphenyltetrazolium bromide] (Fig. 5). Prior to these studies, the intrinsic cytotoxicity of L, RuL, IrL, RuCl, and IrCl was assessed. Due to limited solubility, concentrations up to 2.5 µM were examined. As shown in Fig. S20, under dark conditions, RuL and IrL exhibited moderate cytotoxicity, with cell viabilities of ca. 86% and ca. 74% at 0.5 µM, respectively, and a concentration-dependent decrease at higher concentrations. In contrast, L, RuCl, and IrCl showed relatively low cytotoxicity, with cell viabilities above 80% up to 1 µM and ca. 79%, ca. 71%, and ca. 74% at 2.5 µM, respectively.

Upon light irradiation, all compounds except for IrCl displayed significantly increased cytotoxicity. Notably, RuL and IrL reduced cell viability to below ca. 30% even at 0.5 µM, consistent with their efficient generation of ROS. Photoactivated L also exhibited enhanced cytotoxicity, while RuCl showed increased toxicity, potentially due to photoinduced ligand dissociation that enhances its interactions with cellular components.56 In contrast, IrCl displayed only minor changes, likely reflecting its lower ligand lability associated with the strong ligand field of the 5d Ir center and the σ-donating C5(CH3)5 ligand.

Following these studies, Aβ42 species preincubated with the compounds in the absence and presence of light were applied to cells to verify their cytotoxicity effects (Fig. 5). Under dark conditions, RuL and IrL mitigated Aβ42-induced cytotoxicity, increasing cell viability by ca. 26% and ca. 11%, respectively. RuCl and IrCl also exhibited protective effects (ca. 10% for RuCl and ca. 16% for IrCl). In contrast, L reduced cell viability by ca. 10%, possibly resulting from its intrinsic cytotoxicity.

Upon light exposure, RuL retained a moderate protective effect, improving cell viability by ca. 14%, whereas IrL showed no measurable cytoprotective effect. The increased turbidity of photoactivated IrL under the cell culture conditions (Fig. S21) suggests that its limited solubility may contribute to the diminished cellular activity. RuCl and IrCl produced cytoprotective effects comparable to those observed under dark conditions. In contrast, L altered Aβ42 aggregation under irradiation, but the resulting species did not mitigate Aβ42-induced cytotoxicity. Taken together, these results reveal that metal–organic macrocycles can alleviate cytotoxicity associated with Aβ42 under both dark and photoactivated conditions, with RuL showing the most pronounced protective effect. Notably, photoinduced reactivity alone was insufficient to attenuate Aβ42-triggered toxicity, suggesting that effective interactions with Aβ species are essential for modulating cytotoxicity outcomes.

Molecular interactions with Aβ

To gain mechanistic insight into the interactions between the metal–organic macrocycles and Aβ42, we first examined changes in hydrodynamic radii using dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 6a). Treatment with RuL and IrL resulted in significantly larger Aβ42 assemblies compared to the compound-free sample, consistent with direct association between the macrocycles and Aβ42 species. Specifically, RuL produced size populations centered at ca. 164 and ca. 531 nm, while IrL generated even larger assemblies (ca. 164 and ca. 1110 nm), distinct from the corresponding complexes alone (ca. 164 nm for RuL and ca. 142 nm for IrL). In contrast, the metal complexes RuCl and IrCl yielded smaller species, with size distributions centered at ca. 142 nm with a minor population around 21 nm (for RuCl) and ca. 38 and ca. 164 nm (for IrCl), suggesting distinct modes of interaction with Aβ42. Together with the fluorescence binding studies (vide infra; Fig. 6b–d), these findings support interactions between the metal–organic macrocycles and Aβ42. Because DLS measures the hydrodynamic sizes of particles in solution, however, these size changes cannot distinguish direct macrocycle–Aβ42 association from macrocycle-induced remodeling of Aβ42 assemblies. Thus, the observed increases in particle size likely reflect changes in the aggregation state of Aβ42 associated with macrocycle treatment. Of note, DLS analysis of L was not feasible due to optical interference arising from its strong luminescence.

To further characterize the interactions between the macrocycles and Aβ42, fluorescence quenching experiments were performed using HiLyte™ Fluor 647-labeled Aβ42 (HF647Aβ42), which enables the fluorescence-based analysis of compound–peptide interactions (Fig. 6b).57 Upon titration of L, RuL, IrL, RuCl, and IrCl into HF647Aβ42, RuL, IrL, and RuCl induced a pronounced decrease in the intrinsic fluorescence of HF647Aβ42, whereas L and IrCl produced limited fluorescence quenching, indicating relatively weak interactions with Aβ42 under our experimental conditions. Fluorescence quenching reached saturation only for RuL, IrL, and RuCl, allowing the estimation of their apparent dissociation constants (Kd). As depicted in Fig. 6c, the apparent Kd values were determined to be 0.17 (±0.01), 0.57 (±0.08), and 0.26 (±0.02) µM for RuL, IrL, and RuCl, respectively, demonstrating that the metal–organic macrocycles bind Aβ42 more strongly than their corresponding ligand (L). Furthermore, Job plot analyses revealed that RuL, IrL, and RuCl display multivalent binding with binding stoichiometries of n = 4.0 for RuL and n = 2.3 for IrL and RuCl, suggesting that multiple Aβ42 peptides associate with a single complex (Fig. 6d). Overall, these findings indicate that both RuL and IrL bind Aβ42 with high nanomolar affinities through a multivalent binding mode. Such binding characteristics likely contribute to the effective modulation of Aβ42 aggregation by the metal–organic macrocycles.

We next investigated chemical modifications of Aβ42 triggered by the compounds using ESI-MS (Fig. 7). In the absence of light, no oxidative modifications were detected. Instead, IrL and IrCl showed a peak at 1613 m/z, assigned to adduct formation between Aβ42 and the IrC5(CH3)5 moiety, indicative of direct binding to the peptide (Fig. 7b). Upon light exposure (5 min), RuL and IrL generated oxidized Aβ42 species, as evidenced by a peak at 1510 m/z corresponding to a +14 or +16 Da mass shift of the 3+-charged Aβ42 monomer, consistent with single oxidation. Collision-induced dissociation (CID) analysis (ESI-MS2) revealed oxidized b fragments beginning at b6, suggesting His6, His13, His14, or Met35 as potential oxidation sites (Fig. 7c).

With extended irradiation (10 min), L also induced detectable oxidation, albeit to a lesser extent than RuL and IrL. In addition, to assess whether the macrocyclic architecture enhances peptide oxidation beyond the additive effects of its individual components, we examined Aβ42 oxidation induced by physical mixtures of L and the corresponding metal complexes (RuCl and IrCl). Specifically, L (2 equiv.) and RuCl or IrCl (2 equiv.) were individually incubated with Aβ42 under conditions identical to those used for the corresponding macrocycles. As shown in Fig. S22, the physical mixtures induced peptide oxidation to an extent comparable to that of the corresponding macrocycles, indicating that macrocyclization itself does not significantly improve oxidative efficiency.

These results show that L, RuL, and IrL can generate comparable levels of 1O2 and trigger the peptide oxidation. In contrast, RuCl and IrCl, which lack a photosensitizing moiety, do not trigger detectable oxidation under irradiation. Therefore, RuL and IrL modulate Aβ42 through dual mechanisms: direct binding via surface association and photoinduced oxidative modification upon light exposure. This integrated reactivity distinguishes them from the ligand L, which primarily mediates oxidation, and from RuCl and IrCl, which predominantly act through direct interactions.

Conclusions

Metal–organic macrocycles that integrate and tailor multiple functionalities provide a promising platform for modulating complex peptide aggregation processes. In this work, we designed the metal [Ru(ii) or Ir(iii)]–organic macrocycles (RuL and IrL) as dual-mode modulators that combine direct Aβ interactions with photoinduced oxidative reactivity within a single, well-defined architecture. These systems form discrete metalla-rectangle frameworks and retain the photophysical properties of the BOPHY chromophore, enabling tunable 1O2 generation upon coordination.

Our results demonstrate that the macrocycles (RuL and IrL) effectively suppress the elongation and maturation of Aβ42 fibrils under both dark and photoactivated conditions, with enhanced reactivity observed upon light irradiation. In contrast, the individual components exhibit only partial functionality: the corresponding metal precursors (RuCl and IrCl) primarily interact with Aβ42, whereas the ligand (L) operates through light- and O2-dependent oxidative mechanisms. This dual-mode reactivity enables effective and controllable modulation of Aβ42 aggregation pathways and attenuates Aβ42-induced cytotoxicity, with RuL showing the strongest protective effect. Mechanistic investigations further reveal that the macrocycles directly associate with Aβ42via a multivalent binding mode with nanomolar apparent binding affinities, promote the formation of larger assemblies, and trigger site-specific photooxidation.

Overall, this study establishes metal–organic macrocycles as effective chemical modulators of Aβ aggregation and toxicity. By integrating multiple chemical functionalities within a single molecular framework via coordination-driven self-assembly, these systems enable notable control over peptide aggregation pathways. More broadly, this work provides a potential strategy for reprogramming complex protein aggregation pathways and controlling biomolecular processes.
