Fortification of fermented soy milk using whey protein isolate/gelatin microhydrogels-loaded with sesame bioactive peptides
Authors: Zahra Norouzi, Leila Nateghi, Ladan Rashidi, Sara Movahhed
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Sesame seed protein isolate (SSPI) and hydrolysate (SSPH) were encapsulated in microhydrogels made of gelatin (GL) and whey protein isolate (WPI) at varying concentrations. The study assessed encapsulation efficiency (EE), swelling degree (SW), and antioxidant activity. The best microhydrogel, with the highest EE (84.20 % ± 0.24 %, significantly higher than other treatments, P < 0.05), SW (31.67 % ± 0.16 %, P < 0.05), and radical scavenging activities (DPPH: 83.28 %, ABTS: 85.73 %), was formed with 3 % GL, 5 % WPI, and SSPH (100 μg/mL), designated as T8. This formulation showed superior antioxidant properties compared to SSPI. T8 had a particle size of 1094 nm, a surface charge of −40.5 mV, and a denaturation temperature of 91.52 °C. Its controlled release of SSPH in stomach and intestine conditions (35.34 % and 59.19 %, respectively) suggested protective effects. T8 was incorporated into soy milk with probiotic L. rhamnosus, improving acidity, viscosity, and sensory properties, while reducing syneresis and pathogen bacteria counts.

Introduction

Functional foods, which offer health benefits beyond basic nutrition, are extensively produced due to their ability to improve digestion, support the immune system, and reduce the risk of chronic diseases (Pourghorban et al., 2022; Yousefi et al., 2024). Fermented soy milk is one such functional drink, made by fermenting soy milk with beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus or Bifidobacterium species (Tan et al., 2023). Fermentation enhances nutritional and health-promoting properties by increasing mineral bioavailability (calcium, iron, zinc), reducing anti-nutrients like phytic acid, and improving digestibility of proteins and complex sugars through partial breakdown, thus making nutrients more bioavailable (Liu et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2023). Soy milk also contains isoflavones (phytoestrogens) which, along with probiotics, may reduce low-density lipoprotein (LDL) cholesterol levels and contribute to hormone balance, particularly in women (Liu et al., 2025; Tan et al., 2023). Bioactive peptides obtained from plant seeds show diverse health-promoting effects (Fathi et al., 2022). These peptides, short chains of amino acids derived from enzymatic hydrolysis of seed proteins using proteolytic enzymes like pepsin, trypsin, or alkaline protease, offer various functional and nutraceutical benefits (Du et al., 2023; Fathi et al., 2022). Specifically, sesame seed peptides exhibit antioxidant, antihypertensive, anti-inflammatory, antimicrobial, cholesterol-reducing, and immunomodulatory activities (Aondona et al., 2021). However, incorporating sesame seed peptides into food systems presents challenges related to their stability and bioavailability during processing and digestion. Peptides can degrade under harsh gastrointestinal conditions, lose bioactivity due to interactions with other food components, or negatively affect sensory attributes (Chen et al., 2024; Gómez-Mascaraque et al., 2016). These limitations reduce their functional efficacy and highlight the need for protective delivery systems. To maximize the bioactive potential of peptides, they must be protected from harsh acidic and basic conditions (Gómez-Mascaraque et al., 2016). Hence, targeted delivery and controlled release of bioactive components can be designed based on natural biopolymers such as various proteins and polysaccharides (Gohari et al., 2024; Bahrami et al., 2025; Bahrami Freadooni et al., 2025). Microhydrogels are small, water-swollen biopolymer networks that serve as effective carriers for bioactive compounds (Lopes et al., 2022). These delivery systems embed sensitive molecules such as vitamins, peptides, or plant extracts, protecting them from degradation and enabling controlled release in the gastrointestinal tract, food, pharmaceutical, and cosmetic applications (Liu et al., 2020; Naderi et al., 2024; Bahrami Freadooni, Nateghi, & Rashidi, 2025, Bahrami, Nateghi, Rashidi, Nobandegani and Ghorbanpour, 2025). Gelatin, a protein derived from collagen, is widely used to create microhydrogels due to its excellent gelling properties, biocompatibility, and biodegradability. Gelatin forms physical gels upon cooling through hydrogen bonding and helix formation, producing stable three-dimensional networks capable of entrapping bioactive compounds for controlled release and protection (Gómez-Mascaraque et al., 2016). Whey protein isolate (WPI), another biopolymer, forms microhydrogels through heat- or pH-induced gelation (Liu et al., 2020). Additional fabrication techniques like ultrasonic and high-pressure treatments enhance protein hydrogel formation by increasing disulfide bonding and hydrophobic interactions, improving the compactness and ordering of hydrogel structures and enhancing bioactive compound delivery (Bu et al., 2024; Tomczyńska-Mleko et al., 2023). The choice of gelatin (GL) and whey protein isolate (WPI) for microhydrogel fabrication is justified by their synergistic gelation properties. Composite hydrogel systems combining WPI and gelatin have been shown to form denser and more uniform three-dimensional networks through synergistic interactions during gelation, which significantly enhance encapsulation efficiency and structural stability (Lin et al., 2023). Interactions between WPI and gelatin during gelation form denser and more uniform three-dimensional networks, improving peptide entrapment and protection against environmental degradation (Yan et al., 2023). By optimizing WPI and gelatin ratios, the release kinetics of encapsulated peptides can be finely tuned.

Previous studies on protein hydrolysate encapsulation have largely focused on single-protein systems such as whey or casein hydrogels, or on animal-derived hydrolysates such as fish and dairy peptides (Bu et al., 2024; Gómez-Mascaraque et al., 2016; Lin et al., 2023). In contrast, plant-derived hydrolysates, particularly those from sesame seed, have received little attention despite their promising bioactivities. Moreover, the synergistic use of whey protein isolate (WPI) and gelatin to create composite microhydrogels with improved encapsulation efficiency and release control has not yet been explored for sesame seed protein hydrolysate (SSPH). Finally, while hydrogel encapsulation is often studied in vitro, its integration into a functional fermented beverage such as soy milk—where both peptide stability and probiotic viability must be preserved—remains underexplored. Sesame seed proteins are susceptible to degradation and loss of bioactivity in complex food matrices, which necessitates the development of protective encapsulation systems. Therefore, this study aims to develop glycerol/whey protein isolate (GL/WPI) composite microhydrogels encapsulating sesame seed protein isolate (SSPI) and sesame seed protein hydrolysate (SSPH). The microhydrogels will be evaluated for encapsulation efficiency, swelling degree, antioxidant activity, particle characteristics, thermal behavior, morphology, and chemical properties. The best-performing microhydrogel, based on encapsulation efficiency, will be incorporated into fermented soy milk along with Lactobacillus rhamnosus. The impact of microhydrogel incorporation on the physicochemical (acidity, pH, protein content, peroxide value, syneresis, viscosity), microbiological (total microbial count, pathogenic bacteria, L. rhamnosus viability), and functional properties of the fermented soy milk will be assessed. This approach aims to enhance the nutritional and functional qualities of fermented soy milk through microencapsulation and probiotic supplementation.

Materials and methods

Materials

Whey protein isolate (WPI) was purchased from Hardline Nutrition (Kavi Food Ltd. Co., Istanbul, Turkey) and its protein content was measured by Kjeldahl method as 88.5 % (w/w). Gelatin from porcine skin with a gel strength of ∼175 g Bloom and type A was provided Sigma-Aldrich Co. (Germany). Glycerol, methanol, ABTS (2,2′-azino-bis(3-ethylbenzothiazoline-6-sulfonic acid)), DPPH (2,2-diphenyl-1-picrylhydrazyl), and Ferric reducing antioxidant power (FRAP) assay kit (colorimetric) were purchased from Merck Co. (Germany).

Encapsulation of sesame seed protein isolate and its hydrolysate

GL and WPI solutions were mixed for 2 h under 500 rpm at ambient temperature to reach the ratios mentioned in Table 1a (Lopes et al., 2022). The obtained mixtures were stored over night at 4 °C. Then, the mixtures were sonicated at 100 W for 1 min and then, sesame seed protein isolate (SSPI, 88.90 % w/w protein content) and sesame seed protein hydrolysate (SSPH) with degree of hydrolysis of 16.37 % (produced in our previous study using Alcalase at 5 % w/w; hydrolysis for 4 h under temperature of 50 °C) were added to the prepared hydrogels. The concentration of SSPI and SSPH reached 100 μg/mL in final microhydrogel formulations. The obtained microhydrogels were agitated under 800 rpm for 10 min. The microhydrogels had pH of 6.5. After then, the hydrogels-loaded with SSPI and SSPH were centrifuged at 9000 rpm for 30 min. The residual sediment was collected and mixed with 5 mL of double distilled water and the obtained mixture was freeze-dried.

Characterization of microhydrogels-loaded with SSPI or SSPH

Encapsulation efficiency

Encapsulation efficiency (EE%) of developed microhydrogels was defined as the ratio of encapsulated SSPI or SSPH to total SSPI or SSPH × 100 (Liu et al., 2023). Accordingly, EE (%) was measured using the bicinchoninic acid (BCA) Protein Assay Kit (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA) to assess the amount of SSPI or SSPH remaining as free and unencapsulated by the hydrogel system. The encapsulated (bound) materials were determined by difference as total SSPI or SSPH – free SSPI or SSPH. One mL of freshly prepared hydrogels was transferred into microcentrifuge tubes and centrifuged at 8000×g for 10 min at room temperature to separate the unencapsulated protein from the hydrogel matrix. The supernatant was carefully collected. Then, 0.5 mL of supernatant was filtered using a 3 kDa ultrafiltration centrifugal filter (EMD Millipore, Temecula, CA, USA) at 10,000×g for 15 min to remove any residual hydrogel particles and ensure only free SSPI or SSPH passed through. After then, 0.1 mL of filtrate was mixed with the prepared BCA reagent at 20:1 (v/v). The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 2 h to allow complete color development. Prepared samples were kept at room temperature for 2 h and then absorbances were read at 562 nm using a UV–vis spectrophotometer. Moreover, hydrogels without SSPI or SSPH were centrifuged, filtered, and used as blanks for the spectrophotometer. A standard calibration curve was prepared using known concentrations of SSPI or SSPH in the same buffer as the hydrogel supernatant, and free protein concentrations were determined from this curve. Finally, EE (%) was calculated using the formula:(1)EE%=Total protein−Free protein/Total protein×100

Swelling degree

Swelling degree (%) of produced microhydrogels was measured by immersing the hydrogels in distilled water and their weights were determined at time intervals, until reaching a constant weight (Kaur et al., 2020). The dried samples (about 0.2 g) were introduced in premoistened tea bags, sealed, and placed in a water media to be swollen. After initial 2 h, samples were discharged from water media at 5 min intervals. The adhered surface water was removed by swabbing with a filter paper, and then samples were weighed. Then, samples at 15 min intervals for 5 h and subsequently once a day for 8 days were weighed until equilibrium. The swelling degree (SW%) was computed by considering the weight of tea bag (W1), dried hydrogel (W2), and also weight of swollen hydrogel (W3) using the relation ((Eq. (2)) below:(2)SW%=W3−W2−W1W2×100

Antioxidant activity

DPPH radical scavenging activity

The DPPH radical scavenging activity of developed hydrogels was assessed according to Zhang et al. (2022). Based on the suggested method, microhydrogels were warmed at 40 °C. For measurement, 1 mL of hydrogel was mixed with 1 mL of fresh 100 μM DPPH ethanolic solution, and the obtained mixture was incubated in darkness for 30 min at ambient temperature. The absorbance of mixture was read at 517 nm using an UV–vis spectrophotometer. Also, 1 mL of deionized water was mixed with 1 mL of DPPH solution, the absorbance was read, and considered as control. Finally, DPPH RSA (%) was determined using the relation below:(3)DPPHRSA%=AC−ASAC×100

ABTS radical scavenging activity

ABTS RSA (%) of hydrogels was measured according to the method of Lin et al. (2024). Briefly, 3.5 mM ABTS was mixed with 1.2 mM potassium persulfate solution and kept for 16 h to provide ABTS radical cation and protected from light. The absorbance of the prepared solution was set to 0.7 ± 0.02 by diluting. For measurement of ABTS RSA (%), 1 mL of hydrogel was mixed with 1 mL of ABTS·+ solution using experimental centrifuge tubes, and kept for 10 min at room temperature. The absorbance was recorded at 734 nm using an UV–vis spectrophotometer. The ABTS RSA was computed using the relation below:(4)ABTSRSA%=AC−ASAC×100

Ferric reducing antioxidant power

For measurement of Fe (III) reducing activity or FRAP, 1 mL of hydrogel was mixed with 2.5 mL of phosphate buffer 0.2 M (pH 6.6) and 2.5 mL of potassium ferricyanide 1 %, and the obtained mixture was incubated at 50 °C for 30 min. After then, 2.5 mL of trichloroacetic acid 10 % was added to the reacted mixture, and centrifuged at 1650×g at room temperature for 10 min. The supernatant was collected (2.5 mL) and mixed with 2.5 mL of deionized water and 0.5 mL of FeCl3 and kept for 10 min. The absorbance was recorded at 700 nm while deionized water was considered as control. It should be note that a higher absorbance denoted higher reducing activity (Zhang et al., 2022).

Particle features

Size distribution

The droplet size distribution of selected hydrogel (T8) was measured using Mastersizer 3000 (Malvern Instruments Ltd., Worcestershire, UK) (Lin et al., 2023; Sheybani et al., 2023). The hydrogel was diluted 100 times using double distilled water and NaOH was used to set the pH at 7.0. Droplet size was reported as mean diameter.

Zeta potential

The zeta-potential of selected hydrogel (T8) was measured using a Zetasizer (Nano-ZS, Malvern Instruments, Worcestershire, UK) (Lin et al., 2023; Sheybani et al., 2023). The hydrogel was diluted 100 times using double distilled water and NaOH was used to set the pH at 7.0. The zeta potential was reported as mean values.

Thermal properties

Thermal properties of freeze-dried selected microhydrogel (T8) were assessed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC, Mettler Toledo DSC instruments, USA) (Liu et al., 2020). For measurement, 15 mg of sample were weighed and placed in an aluminum pan. Then, the pan was hermetically sealed and heated from 0 to 200 °C at a rate of 10 °C/min. Also, a sealed empty pan was heated at same conditions and considered as a reference. The constituents of microhydrogel which were GL, WPI, and SSPH were also assessed by DSC and compared to selected microhydrogel.

Morphological properties

The selected freeze-dried microhydrogel was assessed for morphological features using scanning electron microscope (SEM, FEI Nova NanoSEM 430, Oregon, USA) (Ozel et al., 2017). The microhydrogel was gold sputtered at a thickness of 5 nm and then analyzed using SEM under an accelerating voltage of 26 kV. The picturing was conducted at magnification of 500×.

Chemical properties

Chemical properties of freeze-dried selected microhydrogel and its constituents were assessed by Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR, Thermo Fisher Scientific Co., Ltd., Waltham, MA, USA). For measurement, the microhydrogel sample and its constituents were pressed in KBr pellets before FTIR analysis. Then, spectrum acquisition was performed at resolution of 4 cm−1 (Liu et al., 2020).

Gastrointestinal fate

The in vitro release of encapsulated SSPH in selected microhydrogel (T8) was studied based on the method of Mohammadi et al. (2023). The simulated gastrointestinal tract (GIT) conditions including stomach (gastric) and small intestine phases were prepared and then digestion was performed at 37 °C using a shaker incubator.

Gastric phase

Simulated gastric fluid (SGF) was prepared by dissolving 2 g of NaCl, 7 mL of HCl 6 M, and 3.2 g of pepsin into 1 L of distilled water. Then, the microhydrogel was mixed with the SGF at a volume ratio of 1:1 while the pH was adjusted to 1.5 and sample incubated for 2 h (Rashidi et al., 2022).

Small intestine phase

The simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) was prepared by dissolving 6.8 g of K2HPO4, 8.775 g of NaCl, 5 g of bile salts, and 3.2 g of pancreatin into 1 L of distilled water. First, the pH of gastric SGF was set at 6.8 to provide an optimum condition for pancreatin activity. Then, microhydrogel sample collected from the end of the SGF phase was mixed with SIF at a volume ratio of 1:1 and incubated for 2 h. To terminate the enzyme activity, the mixture was heated at 85 °C for 15 min (Rashidi et al., 2022).

Release profile

The release profile of encapsulated SSPH from selected microhydrogel was investigated through monitoring its release rate at the predetermined time intervals during the simulated digestion system (Mohammadi et al., 2023). For determination of release rate, 1000 μL of digested sample was removed from each digestion phase and cooled down using an ice bath. The hydrolysate release was quantitated by transferring the digested sample to upper section of ultra-centrifugal Amicon filter, and subsequently centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 15 min. The filtrate was collected to determine the released content of SSPH using o-phthaldialdehyde (OPA) assay. Also, a standard curve was plotted using L-leucine. Fermented soy milk preparation

Soy milk preparation

The soy milk was produced using the method of Kumari et al. (2022). For this, soy beans were submerged overnight in distilled water containing 0.5 % NaHCO3. Then, the rinsed soy beans were warmed, dehulled, and blended 4 × with water to extract the filtered soy milk, subsequently heated at 90 °C for 10 min.

Lactobacillus rhamnosus inoculum preparation

Lactobacillus rhamnosus (L. rhamnosus) inoculum was developed using De Man–Rogosa–Sharpe agar (MRS agar) by incubation at 37 °C for 24 h.

Development of fermented soy milk

L. rhamnosus was inoculated into soy milk at count of 109 CFU/mL. Also, the selected microhydrogel (T8) was added to soy milk. Then, the soy milk was incubated at 43 °C to reach pH of 4.7. After, the fermented soy milk was agitated to have a homogenous texture. The developed fermented soy milk was poured in 100 g containers packaging and stored at 4 °C and analyzed during 14 days of storage. The developed samples were provided in Table 2a.

Characterization of fermented soy milk

pH and acidity

pH of developed soy milk samples was measured using a digital calibrated pH-meter (Thermo scientific pH meter) at 25 °C. The measurement was performed from the separated whey section (Kumari et al., 2022). Also, the titratable acidity of samples was assessed by titrating with NaOH (Kumari et al., 2022). For this, 10 g of samples were poured in funnels and then 10 mL of distilled water were added to dilute the samples. Approximately, 0.5 mL of phenolphthalein as an indicator were added and then titrated with NaOH 0.1 N. Syneresis of prepared soy milk samples was measured using a method described by Wu et al. (2023). Soy milk samples were centrifuged at 1000 rpm for 10 min and the supernatant was weighed. The syneresis was computed using the relation below:(7)Syneresis%=weight of the generated supernatantgtotal weight ofsoymilk sampleg×100

Viscosity

The viscosity of soy milk samples was determined using an Anton Paar viscometer (Anton Paar GmbH, Headquarters. Anton-Paar-Straße 208,054 GRAZ AUSTRIA). An aliquot of 100 mL soy milk samples was taken and processed with a parallel plate geometry (i.e., PP50 probe having 50- mm diameter, 1.0-mm measurement gap) at 20 °C under a constant shear rate. The collected data were analyzed using Rheoplus software (Anton Paar). The viscosity was expressed based on centipoise (cP).

Microbial properties

Pathogen bacteria count

Coliform, Escherichia coli (E. coli), and Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus) counts were determined using violet red bile dextrose agar (VRBD) and Brain heart infusion broth (BHI) (Kumari et al., 2022). The cultures were prepared based on the suggested method on their label and samples were cultured on media and prepared plates were incubated at 37 °C for 16 h.

Probiotic bacteria count

The survivability of L. rhamnosus in fermented soy milk samples was studied during 14 days of storage (Kumari et al., 2022). Samples were subjected to serial tenfold dilutions in sterile Ringer solution to provide five dilution factors. Then, 0.01 mL aliquots of samples were culture on MRS agar and plates were incubated at 37 °C for 24–48 h under anaerobic jar. The enumeration was conducted to determine the colony-forming units (CFU/mL).

Sensory analysis

Sensory analysis of the developed soy milk samples was conducted following the methodology described by Kumari et al. (2022). Fermented and non-fermented soy milk samples were served chilled from the refrigerator and evaluated for sensory attributes including flavor, color, odor, texture, and overall acceptability. The sensory evaluation was conducted using a panel of twelve semi-trained judges, selected based on their prior experience with dairy and plant-based product assessments to ensure reliability and consistency in scoring. The panelists evaluated the samples for flavor, odor, color, texture, and overall acceptability using a standardized 5-point Hedonic scale (1 = dislike extremely, 2 = dislike very much, 3 = like moderately, 4 = like very much, and 5 = like extremely), which is a widely accepted method for sensory testing in food science. All evaluations were carried out under controlled conditions to reduce environmental influences on sensory perception.

According to institutional guidelines and local regulations, ethical approval was not required for this sensory evaluation as it involved non-invasive procedures with voluntary participation. All participants were fully informed about the study's purpose and provided their voluntary consent to participate. The study was conducted in accordance with established protocols to protect the rights, privacy, and confidentiality of all participants, and no personal identifying information was collected or disclosed.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were carried out in three replications and data were reported as means and standard deviations. Data were analyzed by One-way ANOVA and mean difference analysis was conducted by Duncan test using SPSS statistical analysis version 22 at a confidence level of 95 % (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

Microhydrogel analysis

Encapsulation efficiency

Encapsulation Efficiency (EE%) of developed microhydrogels structured by GL (1, 3, and 5 % w/w) and WPI (5 and 10 % w/w) containing SSPH and SSPI was assessed, and results are provided in Table 1a. The T8 microhydrogel, formulated with 3 % GL, 5 % WPI, and SSPH as the core agent, exhibited the highest EE value of 84.20 % (p < 0.05). When SSPH was encapsulated using a lower GL concentration (3 % w/w) combined with higher WPI (10 % w/w), the EE decreased significantly to 74.73 % for T11 (p < 0.05). Similar trends were observed for SSPI encapsulation, where the highest EE (72.68 %) was also achieved at 3 % GL and 5 % WPI. The lowest EE values for both SSPI and SSPH were obtained when GL and WPI concentrations were 5 % and 10 % w/w, respectively. These results indicate that controlled concentrations of GL and WPI are crucial for optimal encapsulation.

Moreover, the hydrolyzed soy protein (SSPH) demonstrated significantly higher EE compared to intact SSPI (p < 0.05). It is hypothesized that hydrolysis unfolds the protein structure, exposing surface-active segments, particularly hydrophobic regions, which enhances interaction with the hydrophobic parts of GL and WPI. SSPH also contains peptides with a broad range of molecular weights, altering the hydrophobic-hydrophilic balance and thereby improving encapsulation properties (Rezvankhah et al., 2024).

Similar findings have been reported in the literature. For instance, Lv et al. (2023) demonstrated that controlled hydrolysis of soy protein isolate (SPI) by Flavourzyme influenced the encapsulation efficiency of SPI hydrolysates nanoparticles. On this basis, controlled hydrolysis of 7S globulin exposed hydrophobic amino acids, enhancing surface hydrophobicity of SPI hydrolysates nanoparticles, therefore improving its surface-active properties (emulsifying features) (Lv et al., 2023). It has been reported that excessive hydrolysis of the 7S portion leads to weak hydrophobic interactions between the hydrophobic amino acids, thereby restricting the binding site for β-carotene (Lv et al., 2023). While Lv et al. primarily emphasized the relationship between hydrolysis degree, hydrophobic amino acid exposure, and emulsifying properties of SPI hydrolysate nanoparticles, our results extend this understanding by showing that hydrolysis of SSPH not only improves surface activity but also enhances encapsulation efficiency within GL/WPI hydrogels. Importantly, we demonstrate that optimal GL/WPI ratios further maximize EE, whereas excessive polymer concentrations reduce EE—highlighting a balance between protein–matrix and matrix–matrix interactions. Thus, our findings both align with Lv et al. in confirming the importance of controlled hydrolysis and differ by illustrating the synergistic role of hydrogel composition in improving peptide entrapment.

The GL/WPI composite hydrogel matrix offers several advantages over other encapsulation matrices. Xu et al. (2024) utilized a GL/WPI hydrogel to encapsulate N-acetylneuraminic acid (NeuAc), observing a flexible and smooth surface with a continuous three-dimensional network and high porosity (86.92 %), ideal for bioactive entrapment. This structural integrity facilitates improved retention and bioactivity of encapsulated compounds compared to single-component hydrogels or beads. Comparatively, in sodium alginate gel beads encapsulating curcumin/gum Arabic/gelatin microcapsules, Lin et al. (2024) reported that increasing microhydrogel component concentration from 0.8 % to 1.6 % raised EE from 34.07 % to 50.59 %, but further increase to 2.4 % reduced EE to 47.41 %, attributed to oversaturation and aggregation within the matrix. Similarly, in our study, excessive GL and WPI likely intensified interactions between these polymers, reducing their availability to interact with SSPH or SSPI and consequently lowering EE. Thus, the GL/WPI system improves encapsulation efficiency by providing a balanced hydrophilic-hydrophobic environment that accommodates hydrolyzed peptides more effectively than other matrices. The synergy between moderate concentrations of GL and WPI ensures sufficient network formation without oversaturation, enhancing peptide entrapment and potentially improving bioactivity and stability of the encapsulated compounds.

Swelling degree

SW (%) of developed microhydrogels was assessed and results were presented in Table 1a. The highest SW was obtained for microhydrogel T8 (31.67 %) which contained SSPH while SW was obtained 26.61 % for microhydrogel T2 which had SSPI in similar concentration of microhydrogel constituents. Based on results in Table 1a, at lower or higher concentration of GL (1 or 5 %) compared to moderate concentration (3 %), significantly lower SW values were achieved for microhydrogels whether encapsulated SSPH or SSPI. Moreover, a concentration of 10 % WPI led to lower SW (%) compared to a concentration of 5 % (w/w). Thus, using SSPH as a core for encapsulation by moderate concentration of hydrogel components rendered higher SW (%) compared to SSPI used as a core for encapsulation by lower or higher concentration of GL and higher concentration of WPI. In similar conditions, it was hypothesized that SSPH due to unfolded structure efficiently interacted with water molecules to be swollen compared to SSPI. Based on reports, degree of protein-protein, protein-water or protein-ion interactions are crucial for swelling response of each biopolymer (Ozel et al., 2017). Modification of protein structures by hydrolysis can unravel the protein structure, expose the surface-active segments (hydrophilic and hydrophobic), thus improving water absorption and SW (Lv et al., 2023). Hydrolysis of SSPI likely caused higher water absorption by produced hydrolysates. On another side, pH is a key factor in determination of swelling. In heat and cross-linked induced WPI-GL composite hydrogels, it was reported that swelling ratio was increased with increasing pH for both kinds of hydrogels (heat and enzyme cross-linked) (Yan et al., 2023). Indeed, at higher pH values, stronger electrostatic repulsions are formed between the protein and peptide molecules, which may inhibit the formation of dense protein aggregates (Yan et al., 2023). This is consistent with the easy swelling of protein network. Othe reports also have demonstrated that in WPI-based hydrogels, at more alkaline pH values, significantly swelling can occur due to strong charge repulsion among the polypeptide chains (Ozel et al., 2020).

Antioxidant activity

The antioxidant activity of microhydrogels was assessed based on three methods including DPPH and ABTS RSA (%) and FRAP assays (Fig. 1). According to DPPH RSA (%) (Fig. 1a), the highest antioxidant activity or electron-donating capacity was dedicated to microhydrogel T8 which rendered the highest EE value. Moreover, T11 and T7 exhibited significantly higher DPPH RSA compared to microhydrogel-loaded SSPI (p < 0.05). This trend suggests that higher encapsulation efficiency enhances the availability of bioactive peptides, enhancing electron-donating capacity and overall antioxidant activity. The superior antioxidant activity of SSPH-loaded microhydrogels compared to SSPI-loaded ones can be attributed to the presence of highly bioactive peptides generated during hydrolysis by Alcalase (Aondona et al., 2021). Hydrolysis exposes hydrophobic segments and generates short- and medium-chain peptides, which are highly active in donating electrons to DPPH radicals (Aondona et al., 2021). In contrast, SSPI contains hydrophobic amino acids in buried positions, limiting their interaction with radicals until hydrolysis occurs (Aondona et al., 2021; Du et al., 2024; Ghorbani et al., 2025). SSPH contains approximately 23.2 % hydrophobic amino acids (w/w) compared to 22.1 % in SSPI, enhancing its potential for interactions with hydrophobic DPPH radicals.Fig. 1DPPH (a) and ABTS (b) radical scavenging activities and Ferric reducing antioxidant power (c) of microhydrogels containing sesame seed protein isolate (SSPI) and sesame seed protein hydrolysate (SSPH).Fig. 1

DPPH (a) and ABTS (b) radical scavenging activities and Ferric reducing antioxidant power (c) of microhydrogels containing sesame seed protein isolate (SSPI) and sesame seed protein hydrolysate (SSPH).

Regarding the ABTS RSA (Fig. 1b), the highest ABTS was obtained for T8, and then T11 and T7 exhibited significantly higher antioxidant activity compared to those obtained for microhydrogels-loaded SSPI (p < 0.05). Hydrolysis exposes hydrophilic amino acid sequences, facilitating interactions with hydrophilic ABTS radicals (Jafarirad et al., 2023). Additionally, GL and WPI protein fractions contribute to antioxidant activity, with positively and negatively charged amino acids such as arginine, histidine, lysine, aspartic acid, and glutamic acid enhancing radical scavenging (Sinthusamran & Benjakul, 2018).

The FRAP assay evaluates the reducing power of antioxidants by measuring the conversion of Fe3+ to Fe2+ (Aondona et al., 2021). Whatever the antioxidant power is higher, higher absorbance value is obtained at a wavelength of 700 nm, indicating high reduction power of antioxidant compound (Aondona et al., 2021). Based on Fig. 1c, microhydrogel T8 and subsequently microhydrogels T11 and T7 exhibited significantly higher reducing effects (p < 0.05). Results showed that microhydrogels containing SSPH had remarkably higher reducing effects compared to microhydrogels-loaded SSPI. SSPH had exposed antioxidant peptides and amino acids which potentially interacted with Fe3+ and converted them into Fe2+. Regarding the amino acid composition of SSPH, leucine, isoleucine, histidine, methionine, tyrosine, lysine, and tryptophan may also contribute to the reducing power of peptides (Aondona et al., 2021). Overall, microhydrogel T8 was selected as the optimal formulation due to its highest EE and superior antioxidant activity.

Particle analysis

Particle size and particle charge of selected microhydrogel (T8) were assessed and results were represented in Fig. 1Sab. Accordingly, the particle size of generated microhydrogel was 1094 nm and the respective particle charge was −40.5 mV. Microhydrogel was synthesized at pH of 6–6.5 and proteins and peptides have negative charges in this extent. Indeed, proteins and peptides have negative charges above the isoelectric point while they have positive charges below the isoelectric point (Liu et al., 2020). Moreover, amino acid composition of SSPH can also influence the surface charge of microhydrogel. Based on data, SSPH indicated 15.5 % (w/w) negatively charged amino acids while 10 % (w/w) exhibited positively charged amino acids (Aondona et al., 2021). Chitosan and gelatin based hydrogels that were used for encapsulation of vitamin B1 formed particle with a size of 959.5 nm (Kaur et al., 2020).

DSC analysis

Thermal properties of selected microhydrogel (T8) were evaluated and results were presented in Fig. 2. Regarding the constituents of microhydrogel, GL indicated an endothermic peak at 79.23 °C with a normalized enthalpy value of 19.83 mJ/mg. This could be related to the conformational changes in the second structure of GL, implying melting or denaturation point. WPI indicated an endothermic peak at 83.59 °C with a normalized enthalpy value of 29.66 mJ/mg. It could be attributed to denaturation point of WPI based on changes in conformational structure. In terms of SSPH, an endothermic peak was detected at 82.89 °C with an enthalpy value of 23.01 mJ/mg. According to Fig. 2, the selected microhydrogel T8 indicated an endothermic peak at 91.52 °C with a normalized enthalpy value of 56.88 mJ/mg. Fabrication of microhydrogel significantly increased the melting or denaturation point. Moreover, the energy needed to change the microhydrogel structure was remarkably increased. Indeed, formation of stable three-dimensional network can confer higher thermal stability. The endothermic peaks at 75–95 °C for WPI-based gels can be attributed to the thermal denaturation of protein (Liu et al., 2020). In a study, WPI showed an endothermic peak at about 80.3 °C which could be attributed to the denaturation of β-lactoglobulin (Liu et al., 2020). Based on literature, WPI-chitosan complex hydrogel which was tailored for the controlled release of curcumin exhibited higher thermal stability (endothermic peak at 93.7 °C) than that of WPI (endothermic peak at 80.3 °C) (Liu et al., 2020). It was reported that as much as chitosan concentration was increased, the denaturation temperature was increased, leading to greater thermal stability. The reacted O-carboxymethyl chitosan and WPI greatly increased thermal stability than un-cross-linked (Liu et al., 2020). In vitro release

In vitro release of SSPH from microhydrogel T8 was studied through cumulative release in simulated gastric fluid (SGF) and simulated intestinal fluid (SIF) (Fig. 3c). The microhydrogel was tailored using a GL-WPI based system while SSPH was encapsulated within the hydrogel matrix. The release of SSPH occurred in two stages, corresponding to the SGF and SIF phases. According to simulated conditions, releasing of SSPH was initiated in SGF and ranged between 0 and 35.34 %, indicating a burst release characterized by a steep slope on the release curve (Fig. 3c). Indeed, around 50 % of the total SSPH release occurred in gastric conditions, which could be related to the highly acidic environment changing the surface charge of the microhydrogel from negative to positive. The intense protonation of GL and WPI under acidic conditions led to an increase in charge load and density on the biopolymer matrix. Moreover, the presence of pepsin in SGF influenced the structure of GL and WPI, causing the hydrogel to swell more. These changes, due to electrostatic interactions and pepsin activity, likely increased the swelling of the biopolymers, which influenced the release behavior. In the second stage, SSPH release reached 59.19 % in SIF after an additional 4 h (Fig. 3c). This sustained release suggests the microhydrogel system effectively controlled SSPH diffusion, protecting it from rapid release and enzymatic degradation by pancreatin in the intestinal environment.

Similar results have been reported for the release of N-Acetylneuraminic acid from hydrogels stabilized by gelatin/whey protein isolate (Xu et al., 2024). Encapsulation of peptides has demonstrated protective effects against proteases in the stomach and intestine (Xu et al., 2024).

Syneresis

The soy milk samples were tested for syneresis values during the storage time and results were presented in Fig. 4a. For all samples, syneresis of soy milk samples was significantly increased due to whey separation. The highest syneresis values were obtained in samples which did not have L. rhamnosus (S1-S4). In contrast, samples inoculated with L. rhamnosus (S5–S8) exhibited lower syneresis. The probiotic bacteria fermented the soy milk, producing lactic acid and reducing the pH. The resulting increase in acidity decreased the electrostatic repulsion among soy protein molecules, allowing them to aggregate and form a more cohesive gel network capable of entrapping water, thus reducing syneresis. Among the inoculated samples, S8, which contained both L. rhamnosus and free microhydrogel, showed the lowest syneresis. This suggests that microhydrogel particles further stabilized the protein network, possibly by acting as fillers that physically hindered whey separation and reinforced the gel structure. These findings align with previous reports where fermentation by L. rhamnosus reduced pH and promoted protein coagulation, enhancing gel strength and water-holding capacity (Okur et al., 2025). Viscosity

The viscosity value of soy milk samples was measured during the storage time and results were represented in Fig. 4b. The viscosity values were significantly increased during the storage. Soy milk samples including S1-S4 indicated significantly lower viscosity values compared to samples including S5-S8. On day 14, samples S2 and S3 had lower viscosity values than S1 and S4. The highest viscosity value was obtained for S8 on day 14. Results showed that inoculation of L. rhamnosus (S5) remarkably increased the viscosity which might be related to aggregation of proteins in soy milk by reduction of pH. Addition of free or encapsulated SSPH along with L. rhamnosus reduced the viscosity value. Peptides are amines which can influence pH value. Thus, increase in pH value by addition of SSPH can increase the protein electrostatic repulsion which can stabilize the proteins in the soy milk, reducing the viscosity. Probiotic count

The impact of incorporating free SSPH and encapsulated SSPH (within the T8 microhydrogel) into soy milk on L. rhamnosus viability was evaluated over a 14-day storage period (Table 2b). A steady decline in probiotic counts was observed, with sample S7 showing the lowest viable population at day 14, while S5 and S8 maintained relatively higher counts. The addition of both free and encapsulated SSPH led to reduced L. rhamnosus viability, likely reflecting antimicrobial activity derived from low-molecular-weight peptides capable of destabilizing bacterial membranes, chelating essential metal ions, or promoting oxidative stress—mechanisms commonly implicated in hydrolysate bioactivity (Manzoor et al., 2022). Intriguingly, sample S8 (encapsulated SSPH) retained higher viability than free SSPH treatments. This suggests that encapsulation may attenuate the immediate antimicrobial exposure, enabling a more controlled release of SSPH. This concept parallels findings from other encapsulation systems: Zhou et al. (2023) demonstrated that SPI-based microcapsules enhanced probiotic survival by delaying release during spray-drying and acid or thermal exposure. Afzaal et al. (2022) also confirmed enhanced B. bifidum viability when probiotics were embedded in WPI–alginate hydrogels during storage and simulated digestion. Meanwhile, Mahmud et al. (2022) showed that WP–GA encapsulation achieved high efficiency and preserved probiotic viability even under harsh gastrointestinal simulation conditions. Collectively, these findings underscore the dual role of SSPH: while exerting antimicrobial effects, encapsulation within microhydrogel matrices can strategically modulate these effects, protecting probiotic viability by controlling peptide exposure. This balance between functional bioactivity and survivability is critical, especially in food matrices, and underscores the urgency of optimizing encapsulation strategies to preserve probiotic populations while retaining peptide functionality.

Sensory evaluation

Sensory properties including flavor, odor, color, texture, and total acceptability of developed soy milks samples were determined and results were represented in Table 2c. Regarding the sensory parameters, the highest scores were obtained for S7 which was incorporated with L. rhamnosus and also encapsulated SSPH within the microhydrogel T8. Indeed, addition of probiotic bacteria promoted well-balanced sensory properties which could be due to fermentation effects. Fermentation can promote acid production, and also some flavor compounds related to probiotic metabolism. Conclusions

SSPI and SSPH were successfully encapsulated within GL–WPI-based microhydrogels, with SSPH microhydrogels exhibiting higher encapsulation efficiency (EE) and swelling capacity (SW%) compared to SSPI. Microhydrogels containing SSPH also demonstrated superior antioxidant activity and enhanced thermal stability, with T8 showing a dense, integrated structure and enabling controlled, sustained release of bioactive peptides. Incorporation of L. rhamnosus into soy milk increased acidity and viscosity, while addition of microhydrogel T8 reduced pathogenic bacterial counts and syneresis, demonstrating protective effects on probiotic viability. Furthermore, sensory evaluation indicated that soy milk containing L. rhamnosus and microhydrogel T8 received the highest overall scores, confirming that microhydrogel incorporation improves consumer-relevant properties. Collectively, these results show that GL–WPI microhydrogel encapsulation of SSPH effectively enhances both the functional and sensory properties of soy milk, fulfilling the main objective of improving bioactivity, stability, and overall product quality.
