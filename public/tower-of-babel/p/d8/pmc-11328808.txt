How do young men narrate the redemption story of a sexual assault perpetrator?
Authors: Brianna C Delker, Paige Michel, Camille A Fogel, Aubrie L Patterson, Greyson Mize, Thea Huber, Kate C McLean
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground: Little is known about how young men who have committed sexual assault might acknowledge wrongdoing and eventually change and make amends. There are practical barriers to seeking the real redemption stories of perpetrators.Objective: To explore hypothetical pathways to young men’s accountability-taking and amends (i.e. redemption) after perpetration of sexual assault.Method: In a pre-registered, qualitative story completion study, we presented heterosexual, cisgender college men (N = 54) with a date-based sexual assault story written by a fictional male perpetrator. Participants were prompted to complete the story so that the protagonist, who initially denies wrongdoing, eventually changes and becomes a violence prevention advocate.Results: A thematic analysis of the redemption stories revealed that this study’s speculative task was a challenging one. Half of the stories did not provide an explanation for how the perpetrator was able to acknowledge wrongdoing. Overall, individualistic themes (e.g. he introspected) were more common than relational, community, or societal facilitators of redemption.Conclusions: Without infrastructure for accountability-taking and repair, or narrative exemplars to draw from in public life, it is difficult to envision redemption from violence. Rare gender-based, structurally attuned analyses of sexual violence in the stories point the way towards a more transformative vision of redemption.KEYWORDS: Sexual violence, rape, violence against women, college men, redemption, story completion

Most perpetrators of sexual violence do not receive public attention, and are not held accountable within or outside the criminal justice system (Lonsway & Archambault, 2012). On college campuses, for instance, fewer than 1 in 10 sexual assaults are reported to formal authorities (Stoner & Cramer, 2019). When student survivors do report to Title IX administrators, the most common outcome is access to victim services, not for offenders to be found responsible (Richards, 2019). Many who commit sexual violence may be unrecognized even to themselves: while 1 in 3 college men (29.3%) report engaging in behaviours defined as sexual perpetration, only about 1% acknowledge perpetration (Anderson et al., 2021). As such, there are countless young men whose trajectories after committing sexual violence are unknown. We know little about how (if ever) they arrive at acknowledgement of wrongdoing. Even less is known about how they might eventually change and make amends after harming another person. This knowledge gap leaves us without tools to develop effective prevention and intervention strategies. In an article on justice and the #MeToo movement, scholar Salamishah Tillet poses the core question, ‘how does one actually atone for violence they inflict on others?’ (2021, p. 1). Given practical and legal constraints to asking perpetrators to tell such stories, we address Tillet’s question by asking young men to write the redemption story of a fictional sexual assault perpetrator, using a qualitative story completion method (Clarke et al., 2017).

The narrative psychology of redemption and atonement for wrongdoing

Narrative psychology is the study of how people make meaning out of their experiences by constructing and sharing their stories. A prominent theme in stories constructed to make meaning out of difficult life experiences is redemption (McAdams & McLean, 2013). Redemptive stories begin with a bad or emotionally negative life event, which is narrated to convey positive outcomes despite adversity (McAdams & McLean, 2013). For Western emerging adults, redemptive storytelling may help accomplish salient developmental tasks such as self-exploration, identity development, and personal growth (Perlin & Fivush, 2021). In the United States, redemption has been termed a cultural ‘master narrative’ (McLean et al., 2020). These are scripts that guide personal story construction, and that reflect dominant cultural values about what makes a good story and a good person (Syed & McLean, 2023).

When redemptive stories begin with an act of transgression, the narrator has done something bad, rather than something bad happening to them. Transgression typically prompts moral emotions such as guilt and shame, along with challenges to one’s sense of self (Mansfield et al., 2010; Maruna & Ramsden, 2004; Pasupathi et al., 2015). Transgressions can be narrated in ways that ruminate ashamedly on the ‘bad self’ (Lilgendahl et al., 2013), or in a redemptive format that conveys positive personal transformation: a story of atonement. Reflecting the origins of redemption in many religions, atonement evokes a sequence in which someone has committed wrongdoing (sinned) and must seek to atone (repent), i.e. admit wrongdoing, feel remorse, apologize, and commit to amends that repair harm done and/or show that one has changed for the good. Justice and human rights processes such as rehabilitation, reconciliation, and restitution show the wide reach of atonement beyond its faith-based origins (Herman, 2023; McAdams, 2013).

Narrative research on redemptive storytelling about violence perpetration

There is little empirical research on redemptive storytelling about the specific transgression of sexual assault. The sparse narrative research on this topic tends to focus on criminally charged, convicted, and/or incarcerated offenders, a highly unrepresentative sample of the overall population of individuals who commit sexual assault. Bletzer and Koss (2013) analysed 10 apology-to-victim letters written by adult men after they completed 12 months of therapy and a restorative justice programme for individuals charged with misdemeanour (e.g. indecent exposure) and felony sex offenses. The apology letters were rated for the degree of demonstrated ‘responsible personal growth’ (p. 8). In general, apology letters received high scores for acknowledgement of harm to victim, expression of gratitude to the programme, willingness to learn/change, and ‘ego neutralization’ (refraining from self-congratulatory statements). Despite this, variability was high in the category of responsibility-acceptance. The authors concluded that the programme had helped to rehabilitate these men in the sense of improving their understanding of the sexual offense and enhancing their empathy for victims.

The apology letter study was not designed with the aim of tapping into the full arc of narrative atonement following perpetration. A rare exemplar of this is a study of life story interviews with 75 incarcerated men about their conversion to Christianity, which revealed a range of redemptive themes (Maruna et al., 2006). For instance, conversion helped men to manage the shame of their transgressions and to cope with the powerlessness and dehumanization of imprisonment. Some also engaged in a process of making meaning of their incarceration as a ‘gift or opportunity’ (p. 175) from God. Converts shifted the focus from their own psychological crises to a future-oriented, generative focus on serving others and promoting the next generation. Such redemptive themes also appear in the addiction recovery narratives of individuals engaged in mutual aid-based programmes such as Alcoholics Anonymous (AA; Maruna & Ramsden, 2004). The process of narrative reconstruction in AA is a social one: storytellers are embedded within a recovery community, one with a professed mutual obligation, and they share a common language, that of the 12 steps. Both recovery programmes and religious conversion provide a well-worn path or script for atonement from transgression, but little is known about such paths outside of these structured settings.

The present study

We situated our study outside of the carceral system, which serves a narrower slice of the population, to ask how a hypothetical perpetrator of sexual assault arrives at acknowledgement of wrongdoing and makes amends. Using the qualitative story completion method, we presented college men with a date-based sexual assault story stem written by a fictional male perpetrator. We then asked our participants to imagine that the protagonist had changed and became a violence prevention advocate. Participants were asked to tell the story of how this happened. We anticipated that these young men’s stories would not necessarily reflect their own lived experiences per se, but rather their psychological perspectives on the topic of sexual violence perpetration/redemption. Given masculine privileges and gender socialization, we assumed that men’s stories would be shaped by dominant social/cultural understandings of violence against women and accountability-taking for harm done. With the story completion task being a speculative one, we also wanted to get a sense of how realistic participants perceived their hypothetical redemption stories to be. Our over-arching research questions were:

Method

We pre-registered the study aims, methods, and materials on the Open Science Framework (OSF) prior to data collection, https://osf.io/6p75f.

Participants

Participants were 54 self-identified heterosexual, cisgender male undergraduate students at a public university in the Pacific Northwest who were recruited in 2022 via the Department of Psychology Participant Pool (n = 39), or via campus flyers and list-serve announcements (n = 15). Participants were, on average, 21 years old (M = 21.51, SD = 4.69). The majority self-identified as white, non-Hispanic/Latinx (n = 34; 72.2%); 4 participants identified as white and Hispanic/Latinx; and 16 identified as mixed-race, Black or African-American, American Indian or Alaska Native, or Asian. (Exact cases per group are not provided as an extra anonymity precaution for participants at this predominantly white university.)

Additional participant information relevant to the study topic follows. On a scale from Extremely Liberal (1) to Extremely Conservative (9), participants self-identified as, on average, between Somewhat Liberal (3) and Neither Liberal nor Conservative (5) (M = 3.80, SD = 1.83, range = 1–9). On a scale from Very Poor (1) to Excellent (5), participants rated the quality of sex education that they received in middle and high school as between Poor (2) and Average (3) (M = 2.57, SD = 1.06, range = 1–5). Less than half (n = 24; 44.4%) reported having taken any courses, workshops, or trainings around sexual assault prevention since high school. Most participants reported that they have used online dating sites such as Tinder to meet romantic partners (n = 32; 59.3%).

Procedures

Our university’s Institutional Review Board approved the study protocol. Eligibility criteria were to be a self-identified heterosexual cisgender male undergraduate student, age 18 or older. Prospective participants completed standard prescreening questions (as part of the Participant Pool) or a three-item screening survey on Qualtrics.com (accessed via campus recruitment flyers or list-serv announcements). The study incentive was an e-gift-card or, for psychology students, the choice of gift card or research credits towards their course (alternative credit options were available).

When students signed up for this approximately 45-minute study, they received a private Zoom link. One of three research assistants, the majority of whom were female-presenting, hosted the private Zoom call. The RA provided the participant with the Qualtrics.com study link and facilitated the informed consent process. RAs turned off their Zoom camera during the study, but let each participant know that they were available to answer questions. Participants could choose to keep their own cameras on or off; most kept them off. After a participant completed the study, the RA performed the debriefing process. In recognition that some participants would possibly be survivors of sexual violence themselves, debriefing materials included a list of survivor resources, including the organization 1in6 Men.

Materials

Qualitative story completion materials

Methodological guidelines for qualitative story completion research (Clarke et al., 2017; Moller et al., 2021) informed our design of study materials. To read the story stem and story completion instructions in full, please refer to Supplemental Materials.

Story Stem. In the first page of the study, participants were instructed to read a passage by a 21-year-old male college student, Cody. The name Cody was selected for its connotation of neutral socioeconomic status and young white male identity (Johfre, 2020). ‘Cody’s Story’ is a 370-word first-person story, written at the 5th grade level, about his experiences with ‘this girl, Laura’ who he met on the dating website Tinder. On their second date, he sexually assaults her, an event that he does not perceive as such. Laura resists his advances and he continues to coerce her verbally and physically, until she stops moving and he ‘had sex with her’ (sexually assaults her). When she tentatively tells him, several weeks later, that he sexually assaulted her, he accuses her of lying, and denies that he did anything wrong.

Members of this research team had prior research experience drafting and piloting perpetrator vignettes. Our team drafted the story over an extensive period of co-conceptualization, discussion, and literature review regarding common components of sexual assault perpetration. Components that appear in the story stem include misperception of cues and degree of sexual interest; sexual scripts (e.g. denial of resistance – that women mean ‘yes’ when they say ‘no’); hostility; an impersonal perspective on sex; biological essentialism/male urges; and post-assault excuses, denial, and victim blame (Abbey & Jacques-Tiura, 2011; Harsey & Freyd, 2020; Hipp et al., 2017; Wegner et al., 2015). Despite alcohol’s association with assault on college campuses, we intentionally did not mention alcohol in the story stem. We wanted to ensure that participants’ redemptive stories were conceptually distinct from stories about redemptive recovery from addiction. Providing evidence in support of the story stem’s realism and ecological validity, all but one participant agreed that ‘The events in this story are likely to happen in real life.’

Story Completion Instructions. After the Story Stem, participants were instructed to ‘imagine yourself being in Cody’s shoes – even if you would not think, say, or do these things yourself’ and to imagine that, a number of years after the events described in the passage, Cody now

volunteers every weekend at an advocacy organization, working with other men to stop sexual violence. Please take as much room as you need to imagine that you are Cody telling the story of how he changed, grew, and became an advocate.

Participants were asked to write about half a page. After much consideration about the prompt instructions, we chose violence prevention advocacy as a face-valid operationalization of Cody’s atonement. Advocacy was a concrete narrative endpoint for participant-storytellers to write towards, one which signified the protagonist’s personal growth and restorative commitment to making amends.

Follow-up survey questions about the story stem and story completions

Survey questions assessed participant perceptions of the perceived plausibility and familiarity (e.g. ‘This is a common story’) of the story stem (nitems = 4) and their story completions (nitems = 3), on a 5-point-Likert response scale ranging from Strongly Disagree to Strongly Agree. This scale was used in prior vignette research on trauma storytelling (McLean et al., 2020). Additionally, participants responded to questions about their own experiences with writing the stories (this data will be presented in a separate report).

Researcher description

Our majority female-identified research team is of mixed professional status (psychology faculty, graduate, and undergraduate students) and includes individuals who are survivors of gender-based violence; who are the loved ones of survivors and perpetrators; and/or who have clinical or advocacy experiences in violence prevention (e.g. domestic violence survivor advocacy, support groups for transgender and nonbinary survivors of interpersonal violence, and clinical intervention with adolescent male sex offenders). As part of our commitment to methodological integrity, we engaged in an ongoing process of reflexivity as a team. For instance, we discussed how our values, personal backgrounds with the phenomena being studied, scholarly and clinical knowledge, and team dynamics informed our reactions to and observations of the data (Levitt et al., 2018).

Regarding theoretical assumptions that guide this work, we have conceptualized sexual violence as a gendered form of harm, one rooted in patriarchal values, gender norms in relationships, and masculine privilege and entitlement within societies structured by gender inequality (United Nations, 2018). Of course, gender-based oppression intersects with additional dimensions of oppression, power and privilege on the basis of race, ethnicity, class, socioeconomic status, and so on. A feminist epistemology (Bennett, 2021) acknowledges that the naming of what is abusive is itself a value-laden process, one that can invalidate and delegitimize the relational experiences of women and gender-diverse people (Delker, 2021).

Qualitative data analysis approach

Thematic analysis is a systematic approach to qualitative data coding and theme development, one that acknowledges the active role of researchers in constructing meaning (themes) across data items. Our collaborative process followed six phases (Clarke et al., 2015): familiarization, coding, ‘searching’ for themes, reviewing themes, defining and naming themes, and writing the report. In the data familiarization process, we met regularly to discuss our impressions, along with areas of convergence and divergence. Our observations of participant narrative choices could have led us down numerous avenues of inquiry. For parsimony’s sake, we developed two focal research questions of the story completion data which we present here (a third will be addressed in a separate report):

Q1: How do stories represent Cody’s journey to acknowledge wrongdoing?

Q2: How do stories represent Cody’s journey to advocacy as a form of atonement?

Using ATLAS.ti 23.3.0 for Mac (2023), we each coded the data, meeting regularly to discuss what we had coded and why. After we had each coded the full dataset twice, we proceeded with the ‘“searching” for themes’ phase. We each generated candidate themes, or central organizing concepts to explain our codes. By searching for candidate themes separately but reviewing them collaboratively, we were able to manage our perspectives, reconcile minor discrepancies, and develop a thorough understanding of the data that was richer for containing multiple views. An example of a minor discrepancy was that all team members independently coded ‘time passing’ where this concept appeared in the stories, but we needed to discuss as a team whether the passage of time was a sufficient explanation for how Cody realized wrongdoing, or if it should be considered a lack of explanation. After discussion, areas of minor discrepancy were resolved by the first author, and all coders arrived at consensus on the final set of themes and definitions.

Question 1: How do stories represent Cody’s journey to acknowledge wrongdoing?

Theme 1: He Engages in a Process of Introspection. In about a quarter of stories (n = 14; 25.9%), Cody engages in a process of introspection. Sometimes his introspection is a solitary activity, facilitated by a long weekend alone to himself (S36), ‘spontaneously writing’ (S29), or most often, reflection as time passes: ‘As the months passed I thought about the night I spent with Laura, thinking back on why so many people sided with her […]’ (S45). In one case, Cody ‘isolated myself for years, to better understand myself and my actions. Meditating and finding a new inner peace’ (S24). In a few cases, he engages in deliberate perspective-taking to try to empathically ‘step into someone else’s shoes’ (S28), to imagine Laura’s perspective and feelings: ‘I tried to understand and imagine how she would feel and hurt in that situation’ (S32).

Some storytellers imagined that shame would be attached to this introspective reckoning with one’s wrongdoing. Grappling in private with how wrong what he did to Laura was, one Cody writes that ‘It was just like one of those embarrassing moments from a while ago that just keeps coming back to you, but this was a lot worse’ (S46). Nearly half of the stories include words like shame, ashamed, or embarrassed. One Cody, ‘who couldn’t bear to look at myself in the mirror’, writes (of himself): ‘I was disgusted, truly disgusted’ (S7). Another Cody: ‘I feel there’s a stain on who I am’ (S35). These stories suggest that not only did Cody do harm, but he himself may be irrevocably changed by what he did. Overall, about one-fifth of stories contain some degree of reflection on such identity-related challenges to fully reckoning with and accepting his wrongdoing. As one narrator puts it, ‘I was too wrapped up in my own self-image of being a good person […]’ (S23).

Therapy is a catalyst for Cody’s realization of wrongdoing in only four stories. These stories present therapy as an opportunity for learning, reflection, and self-understanding. The Cody who isolated himself for years first attended therapy ‘for several years to better understand why I thought her fighting back was a turn on’ (S24). In therapy, Cody learns to ‘break down my actions and thought processes behind them’ (S25), finding that he ‘ignored the signs that she was uncomfortable and scared’ (S12). Because of therapy, he can now ‘read and know when a girl wants to have sex’ (S20).

Theme 2: Others Explain His Wrongdoing. In this theme, others in Cody’s life explain his wrongdoing to him (n = 9; 16.7%). These stories imply that it took repeated or cumulative efforts by friends to impress upon Cody his wrongdoing. ‘[… F]amily members and friends […] continuously told me what I did was wrong’ (S30) and ‘[M]y friends had brought it up to me many times saying she’s saying these things about me and that she seemed very honest and traumatized’ (S50).

Several participants specified that it was a female friend, or even the victim herself, who prompted Cody’s acknowledgement of wrongdoing. ‘I needed to learn from my mistake so I would ask her [the victim] to show me my wrongdoings as well as asking other female friends what the right thing to do [sic]’ (S28). Another Cody spoke with a close female friend from childhood about his experience. This female friend told him that what he did ‘would absolutely be classified as sexual assault’, prompting Cody to realize he had committed assault because he has ‘huge respect for her [his childhood friend] and value[s] her opinion’ (S2).

In another story, set several years after the sexual assault, Cody has graduated from college and is married to another woman. Laura unexpectedly contacts him and requests that they meet. Upon meeting at a cafe, she tells him that she became pregnant ‘after our encounter’, and ‘had to abort the baby’ due to pregnancy complications (S49). Cody continues:

This hit me hard […] I didn’t know what to say. She told me that I should say nothing, but she thought it was important for me to know and that I should do better then she left. Ever since that point I have thought about those words and vowed to do better not for me but for other people in simular [sic] situations. (S49)

In this version of Cody’s redemption, the emotional impact of his actions is not connected to his acknowledgement of committing assault. Instead, he is emotionally impacted due to the loss, through abortion, of the baby he had conceived with Laura. Although he commits at the end to ‘do better’, Cody never names what wrong he committed, explains why he did it, or specifies who are the other people in similar situations for whom he will do better.

Theme 3: Social Consequences Prompt His Realization of Wrongdoing. In these stories, Cody persists in his denial of wrongdoing until the damage to his social life forces a reckoning (n = 9; 16.7%). Sometimes this damage is vague or implied (‘After the rumors had spread that I had sexually assaulted someone, my coaches, parents and teachers all took notice’, S19). But more often, the damage is explicit. His ‘relationships become strained, and I even lost contact with people I considered unique in my life’ (S30); his friends ‘left’ him (S4) or were ‘cold’ (S45) and sided with Laura; or rumours prompted him to transfer colleges (S11) or lose his job (S50).

In two of these stories, a new female love interest rejects Cody, serving as a catalyst for his realization of wrongdoing with Laura. In one case, he interprets a female classmate’s invitation to hang out as a sign that ‘she was making a move on me’, but instead he gets ‘friend-zoned hard’ (S11). Several years pass, over which time this woman becomes Cody’s ‘best friend’ and makes him realize ‘how fucked up my way of thinking was in regards to women’ (S11). In the other case of rejection, Cody graduates, moves to another state, and gets a job, where he meets a coworker ‘Roxanne1,’ with whom ‘immediately I was smitten’ (S29). He ‘persist[s]’ in asking her out over the course of a month, despite her refusals. Eventually, he asks her why she won’t go out with him, and Roxanne tells him that he

acted like a creep, and she felt I only liked her for her body. When I asked what I could do to change her view of me, I was tasked with writing a poem about all her redeeming features […] One night while spontaneously writing, I began to cry. Writing about someone I loved and why I loved them made me reflect upon myself. I began to think of who I was in the past and what I had done. I started to imagine Roxanne going through the same thing as Laura. (S29)

The assigned task to write a poem about his love for Roxanne thrusts Cody into a state of intense emotion and self-reflection. These, in turn, enable him to experience an indirect sort of empathy for Laura, and to reckon with his wrongdoing.

Theme 4: Sexual Assault Survivor Stories or Education Prompt Awareness of Wrongdoing. These stories (n = 9; 16.7%) imply that Cody recognizes himself or what he did by hearing about the experiences of sexual assault survivors (some strangers, some loved ones, typically female) or by learning about sexual assault (in an educational context). These two experiences are mutually exclusive, in that no narrative presents Cody both bearing witness to survivor stories, and engaging in formal education. But what unites these experiences under a single theme is Cody perceiving his own experiences as connected to something larger. In some cases, Cody recognizes himself as having committed harm by a process of identification with another harm-doer. One Cody was tricked by a former friend, in the style of an addiction intervention, into attending a survivor support group. At first angry at his friend for sending him to the group, this Cody ‘began to listen’ (S50) to the survivors and found that

their stories sounded familiar. a handful of them were nearly identical to the things i did. When i saw how distraught they were, how scared and sad about the things different people had done to them, my heart dropped, and i understood what i did. (S50)

Another Cody

wanted to change so bad in therapy but I still couldn’t understand until one day my sister was sexually assaulted and in the same situation as what happened to me and I was so angry with the man who did it. Years had gone by but I never understood the pain until I saw it with my own eyes hearing my sister cry in the middle of the night and seeing her scream from having a bad dream about getting assaulted again. At that moment I finally understood what I did. (S12)

A few months after the assault, another Cody

still felt that I hadn’t done anything wrong. But then I saw this video on YouTube, it was about a college girl [who] explained how she was sexually assaulted. I don’t remember why I watched it, but I remember not being able to click away. The details of her story were eerily similar to my night with Laura those many months ago. I remember crying, when I realized the full extent of what I actually did, the impact it would have on Laura. […] I started looking online for more victims sharing their stories, and realized how many their [sic] were, and how so many of them bared similarities to that night months ago. I think that was when I came to except [sic] what I’d done. (S7)

In these stories, Cody expresses feeling emotionally impacted by, along with empathy for, survivors. In doing so, he recognizes himself as having caused such harm to Laura.

Other stories present similar narrative turning points for Cody, but in a less emotive and more perfunctory manner. One Cody ‘was forced to take a course on the topic of sexual violence, where I had to learn about its impact on a person over a lifetime’ (S19). Another Cody ‘attended a consent seminar and realized how wrong my view on the situation was’ (S45). In yet another story, Cody ruminates for a year about what he did to Laura, then contacts her and continues to pressure her, despite her ‘reluctance,’ to meet with him in person, so he can apologize. ‘After our meeting I still felt bad and wanted to do something more. I began to research sexual assault and gained insight about the subject’ (S27). In this story, apologizing to Laura does not absolve Cody of his guilt. But research helps him to gain ‘insight’ into the academic subject of sexual assault; this Cody later alludes to getting ‘a degree or certified or whatever’ on the subject (S27).

Another Cody presents an academic setting as the catalyst for his awakening, but with a narrative depth that evokes the emergence, over time, of his critical consciousness surrounding white male privilege. This Cody takes a college course on Gender and Society:

It was an experience that changed my worldview. At first it was really hard to listen to, the stuff that the teacher was putting out seemed like such bull. There were only like six white guys in a class of 80, and everything that the teacher said seemed like it was blaming us for all of the world’s problems. Then she had this lecture in class about how we aren’t responsible for the things that happened with other people in the past, but we are responsible for making a change for the future, and for the things that we do in our lives. Little by little, the lessons started to get through to me […] I was here to learn […] everything that I could about how to be a better person. I realized that I was wrong about so many things […]. (S6)

This story seems to endorse the transformative potential of education. Cody learns a new way of looking at the world. He questions his assumptions and engages in personal growth, recognizing his responsibility to help transform unjust social conditions.

Theme 5: No Explanation for Realization of Wrongdoing. In half of the stories, Cody provides no explanation for how he realized that what he did was sexual assault (n = 27; 50%). Although not a direct explanation, some participants (n = 7; 13%) stated that it took time for Cody to realize that what he did was wrong (‘It was really hard to deal with the shame. I couldn’t forgive myself for a long time,’ S1). Included in this theme are two stories where he denies wrongdoing altogether. In the story where he is ‘forced’ to take a course on sexual violence, he ‘came to a realization that even if I didn’t commit sexual assault, I can still become a part of the solution for it’ (S19). In the other story characterized by denial, the participant wrote that if Laura ‘felt uncomfortable she should have left in the first place [and] shouldn’t have been giggling […] Where I’m from women stand up for themselves and know how to say no but it is different out here in these liberal city’s [sic …]’ (S15). This is one of three participants who could be considered to have not responded to the story completion prompt; the other two stories consisted only of ‘messed up’ (S33) and ‘Ashamed. I can’t think of how his rape story would lead him to advocacy work’ (S5).

Question 2: How do stories represent Cody’s journey to advocacy as a form of atonement?

Theme 1: Advocacy with Potential Benefits to Survivors and Others. In half of the stories (n = 27; 50%), Cody becomes an advocate to prevent future sexual violence from happening, ‘so no girl has to experience that fear and violation’ (S24). While this desire is, of course, a core motivation to become an advocate, it is also how the story completion instructions introduced Cody’s advocacy. In a few cases, Cody’s stated desire to prevent future harm may have been borrowed, in a perfunctory way, from the study instructions. For example, in one brief story, Cody states that he has changed, but provides no explanation for how he changed, nor why he became an advocate. He concludes: ‘Although I can never take back what I’ve done I can at least try to help others in the future by advocating for others to put an end to sexual assault’ (S10).

Other stories add more explanatory substance to the violence prevention role. Several stories note that the admission of one’s wrongs is a way to affirm the experiences of survivors like Laura. More commonly, stories frame sexual violence prevention as accomplished by educating other men about sex and consent: ‘I want to teach other boys and men how it’s not okay to make assumptions of what a girl wants’ (S39), and

It needs to [be] known and sometimes taught what is good and what is bad during intercourse. There are plenty of signs that a woman does not want to proceed with intercourse, and it doesn’t only have to be the word ‘no.’ (S28)

The implication of these stories seems to be that if men (sometimes the gender-neutral ‘people’) just knew differently, they would do differently. In half of the sample’s stories, Cody does demonstrate knowledge of consent and/or the nature and prevalence of sexual assault: ‘Consent must be clearly given, not assumed’ (S37); ‘No matter how it is phrased, or how serious or non-serious that it sounds, no always means no’ (S14); ‘It is extremely sad how many counts of sexual assault occurs on campuses’ (S34); and ‘Every day, people like Laura and I experience the same sort of scenario that we were in’ […] (S51).

Sometimes the future harm that Cody aims to prevent is not exclusively harm to victims, but the potential harm to men’s reputations and lives. I ‘have to stop other people from […] ruining their own lives’ (S34); ‘I also don’t want another person to have to realize and destroy their life to learn that the only way to have consensual [sex] is through a firm yes’ (S30); and ‘I can just about guarantee you [young men] will find yourself in a situation where you need to slow down, back off and listen or you will risk inflicting violence on someone. That’s not a burden you want to live with’ (S44).

In five stories, Cody conveys a desire to spread awareness of structural roots of sexual violence, such as gender inequality and masculine entitlement. In these stories, Cody-as-advocate frames sexual violence as a phenomenon with roots deeper than his own individual folly: it is ‘part of our society and that needs to change’ (S41); an ‘ongoing issue in our society’ (S26); ‘a kind of mindset that many men have’ (S25); and a ‘plague of ignorance and selfish inconsideration’ (S42). One narrator elaborates:

As men in society we have the privilege of security and safety knowing we don’t have to fear potential threats in our day to day lives. […] Unfortunately that isn’t a privilege that women see today. And it is our job as men to have a thorough and complete understanding of what male privilege looks like and we can be aware of ourselves and do our best to always keep a comforting and safe environment whenever we are with a woman. This starts with knowing what consent is. (S48)

This Cody then paraphrases, in detail, the definition of consent from the Tea and Consent video (Blue Seat Studios, 2015). On the one hand, this narrator richly evokes male privilege for his audience. On the other hand, statements such as that men must ‘always keep a comforting and safe environment whenever we are with a woman’ (S48) belie the narrative challenge of how to reckon with male privilege without tipping into paternalism. In this vein, another Cody-as-advocate names men’s obligation to be ‘protectors and guardians of society’ (S17), and still another urges the men in his audience ‘to protect women’ (S12).

Theme 2: Personal Benefits of Advocacy Role. In contrast to – but sometimes co-occurring with – the other-focused reasons for advocacy in the previous theme, these stories imply that advocacy benefits Cody personally (n = 11; 20.4%). He feels positive reinforcement from his role, ‘seeing that I was genuinely doing something good, that’s what makes me show up every weekend’ (S7). More commonly, he feels relief from the personal distress (guilt, shame, regret) that motivated his advocacy: ‘I instead distracted myself from the pain I had caused by making it my mission to help people heal from this type of thing’ (S18) and, ruminatively, ‘Its one of my biggest mistakes in my life, and why I’m all speaking to you today. I carry the guilt with me everywhere. When I seem to escape it that quiet voice reminds me of it all over again’ (S47).

Some stories explicitly frame advocacy as a means for Cody to become a better person. For instance, advocacy helps him to become a role model for his future children. ‘One day I want to have children and I don’t want to look them in the eye and say what I did was okay’ […] (S12). In another story, the female love interest who ‘friend-zoned’ Cody inspires him to become an advocate, helping him to demonstrate that he is no longer ‘that person anymore’ (S11). Laura herself urges Cody to become an advocate in two stories – the story where she disclosed that she was pregnant and got an abortion, and the story where Cody was transformed by his Gender and Society course. ‘[Laura and I] still talk sometimes, particularly about advocacy for trauma victims and things that I can still do to improve myself and make myself better’ (S6).

Lastly, in two cases, advocacy serves a frankly instrumental purpose for Cody – a means to a particular end. In one case, he starts volunteering as an advocate to fulfil an academic requirement (S6). In another case – the story of Roxanne quoted in a prior section – Cody becomes an advocate as a precondition to having a sexual relationship with Roxanne. After he gives her his poem about her ‘redeeming’ features, he asks her out and ‘confesses’ his story

about Laura while crying my eyes out. [Roxanne] just looked at me without emotion. After the story was over, she told me that if I volunteered at an advocacy organization for sexual assault, she would give me a chance. You know the rest of the story. I’ve been here for ten years and my lovely wife Roxanne has been with me every step of the way. Thank you, Roxanne. For all those in attendance, I hope my story can inspire some change in your lives and bring about more good in the world. (S29)

Although this Cody’s instrumental approach to advocacy (to have sex with other women) raises ethical eyebrows, there is a certain truth to the notion that words are not enough, when judging whether someone has changed for the better. The words need to be accompanied by some kind of action – in Roxanne’s stipulation, Cody’s advocacy.

Theme 3: Advocacy as Amends for Harm Done. In these stories, Cody frames his advocacy as a form of reparation for the harm he did to Laura (n = 12; 22.2%). In some stories, Cody cites Biblical concepts such as amends, atonement, and repentance: ‘But I have to atone for my actions, even if there’s no means for forgiveness’ (S32); ‘I can make amends by helping promote sexual assault awareness and preventing people from making the same mistake’ (S35); and ‘This is my way of repenting for my wrongs’ (S52). In other stories, Cody speaks of a desire to balance out wrongdoing in a cosmic sense, to restore a moral order to the universe: […] ‘the only way I could see to try and put more good back into the world than bad’ (S18) and ‘If you imagine the good and bad things we do in life as big weights on a big scale, I added a huge weight to the negative side, so it is my day to do what I can to tip that scale back in the other direction to be a good person’ (S48).

In about half of the stories with the theme of amends, Cody conveys that his work as an advocate cannot truly make up for what he did to Laura. ‘Even though I know my volunteer work can’t make up for what I did I still would like to think my actions now show what kind of man I truly am’ (S13); ‘I can work to become a better person. But there’s nothing I can do to take away the fear and mistrust that I inflicted on her’ (S44); ‘I can’t fix that mistake, and the pain I’ve caused’ (S35); and ‘There were [sic] no making things right though. Not fully, the best I could do was acknowledge my own fault and take the time to change myself and my worldview for the better’ (S23). These types of statements convey that advocacy work reflects personal growth and positive identity change for Cody, even if it is not possible to repair harm done to the victim in the sense of righting past wrongs.

Theme 4: No Explanation for Becoming an Advocate. In about a quarter of the stories (n = 13; 24.1%), narrators do not provide an explanation for why or how Cody became an advocate.

Responses to follow-up survey questions about the story stem and story completions

While all but one participant in the sample agreed that the events in the story stem were likely to happen in real life, their responses were more mixed about whether Cody would be likely to share his story (stem) with others: 42.6% disagreed that Cody would be likely to share the story stem with others, and 37% agreed (the rest neither agreed nor disagreed; see Figure 2). Most participants agreed that the story stem is a common story (68.5%) and like stories that they have heard before (72.2%). In apparent contradiction of these responses, only 7 participants (13%) reported that a male peer has confided to them that they had sexual contact with someone without that person’s consent.

Questions about the sexual assault story stem and the redemptive story completions written by participants.

Compared to their perceptions of the story stem’s familiarity, participants tended to report more disagreement that the redemptive stories that they wrote were common (46.3%) or stories that they had heard before (35.2%; see Figure 2). Despite this, half of the sample agreed that they had heard stories before like the redemptive story that they wrote (50%) and that the redemptive story was likely to happen in real life (51.9%). This seems discrepant with the fact that so few participants report that a male peer has confided to them about having committed sexual assault, which would presumably be part of their peer’s redemption story. One explanation for this discrepancy could be that, for the questions about the commonness of redemption stories, participants were thinking of having heard public (not peer) examples of redemption following perpetration. But when asked directly if they were aware of any examples of a male public figure who has acknowledged and grown after committing sexual assault, only five participants (9.3%) could provide a name. The names provided were ‘Al Franken maybe?’; ‘Asis [sic] Ansari, maybe Al Franken, Neil DeGrasse Tyson (a little)’; ‘Ezra Miller’; ‘Kobe Bryant’; and ‘Tiger Woods.’

Discussion

In this study, college men were asked to write the redemption story of a hypothetical sexual assault perpetrator, ‘Cody,’ who had denied wrongdoing when confronted by the female victim. This task was a challenging one for participants. In their stories, half of the sample conveyed that Cody realized his wrongdoing, but not how he was able to arrive at this insight after his initial denial. Additionally, about a quarter of the sample did not explain how or why Cody became a prevention advocate, which was the redemptive narrative endpoint towards which participants were instructed to write their stories. Although some stories provided minimal narrative detail about Cody’s redemptive transformation, others represented his journey in ways that were attuned to structural contexts of gender-based violence. These suggest promising possibilities for sexual assault perpetrator redemption if such discourse and awareness are systemically fostered.

How findings fit within the narrative psychology of perpetration and redemption

Many participant stories included at least some of the characteristic narrative elements of redemption, and specifically, atonement. These narrative elements include moral emotions such as guilt and shame (Pasupathi et al., 2015) and the perpetrator’s struggle to reckon with identity-related challenges (Maruna et al., 2006). Some storytellers evoked the ways in which shame and the desire to hold onto a positive sense of self can collude with denial, delaying the admission of wrongdoing. Others traced Cody’s redemptive arc of positive personal transformation by stating that he has become a ‘better person,’ as evidenced by his role as an advocate. Perlin and Fivush (2021) argue that emerging adulthood is a period of developmental transition when redemptive stories fruitfully serve as a vehicle for identity exploration, a claim supported by our findings. Overall, only 3 out of 54 participants were unable or unwilling to write stories in response to the prompt. This provides indirect support for the theory that, in the U.S., redemption is a recognizable, dominant cultural narrative for storying negative life events (McLean et al., 2020). It also supports the rich potential of story completion as a method to research stigmatizing, ethically sensitive topics (Moller et al., 2021).

Difficulty storying the perpetrator’s acknowledgement of wrongdoing

As mentioned previously, half of the sample did not explain how the protagonist was able to acknowledge that he committed sexual assault, after his initial denial. For a number of reasons, this finding is not unexpected. Without infrastructure for accountability-taking such as campus sexual misconduct processes (which no participant mentioned in his story), it is indeed challenging to imagine how he would acknowledge wrongdoing. These young men’s struggle to psychologically contend with and narrativize the perpetrator’s acknowledgement could reflect their embeddedness within a mainstream, dominant cultural context that minimizes interpersonal violence generally (Delker, 2021) and sexual violence specifically (Hipp et al., 2017; Wegner et al., 2015).

In more practical terms, our participants likely were not able to model their stories after any real-life public examples. Patterns of minimization, denial, and attacking the victim’s credibility appear to be more common, or at least more visible, reactions from those publicly accused of sexual violence (Harsey & Freyd, 2020; Huber & Mize, 2023). Asked to name any male public figures who are exemplars of personal growth following perpetration, only five participants provided a name. None of the named figures could be considered unambiguous exemplars. Puzzlingly, though, half of the sample reported in survey questions that they have heard stories like the fictional redemptive story that they wrote. This raises perplexing questions about who or what stories they are referring to, questions worth exploring in future research.

Positioning stories within the social ecology of sexual violence

Positioned within the social ecology of sexual violence (Dworkin & Weaver, 2021; SAHMSA, 2014), stories tended to centre on an individual level of analysis, rather than relational or contextual explanations for Cody’s redemption. For instance, in a quarter of stories, Cody engaged in a process of personal introspection to acknowledge wrongdoing. In about one-fifth of the stories, he highlighted the advocate role’s personal benefits to himself (e.g. to fulfil an academic requirement, or to cope with his guilt). In many ways, it is not surprising that stories present individualistic explanations for his experiences. Practically, introspection makes sense as a private activity that would not require Cody to disclose his transgression to others, risking reputational and legal consequences. Developmentally, it is a tall order to expect emerging adults to inhabit the generative, community-minded role of an advocate (Maruna & Ramsden, 2004). And methodologically, this study prompted participants to write about a specific individual’s redemptive arc. In terms of how people narrate their own life experiences, this prompt would pull for individual psychological explanations.

Less frequently, participant stories depicted the relational, community, institutional, or societal dimensions of Cody’s redemptive arc (see Figure 3). For instance, in some stories, Cody realized his wrongdoing when he participated in therapy; heeded efforts of loved ones to hold him accountable; bore witness to survivor stories among his friends and family, which increased his empathy; or engaged in an educational experience that raised his critical consciousness of gender- and race-based oppression (and of his own white male privilege). Among storytellers who depicted advocacy as having potential benefits to survivors and others (half of the sample), it was common to frame advocacy as the prevention of future sexual violence, with educating others about sex/consent being a major means to accomplish this. But only five storytellers implied that, as advocates, they wanted to spread awareness of societal roots of sexual violence. In doing so, no participant used language as politically inflected as ‘rape culture’ or ‘patriarchy’ in his story, nor referred directly to asymmetries of power and privilege in relationships that are rooted in structural gender inequality. This suggests that there are missed opportunities for colleges to educate students in modes of critical inquiry that enable them to make structural analyses of problems that can otherwise be dismissed as an issue of individual ‘bad apples.’

Limitations

There is no singular story of sexual assault. We designed the story stem for this study based on research and careful conceptualization, and in support of its ecological validity, all but one participant found it to be plausible and realistic. But the scenario intentionally omitted several possible situational and developmental aspects of campus sexual assault that may have inspired our participants to complete the story in different ways. The first is alcohol use, an increasingly salient feature in campus assault and misperception of sexual cues (Orchowski & Berkowitz, 2022). Another factor that we wish to highlight is the reality that some young men who commit sexual assault may have been exposed to interpersonal violence themselves. For instance, they may have experienced childhood sexual or physical abuse, or parental coercive control (Callaghan et al., 2018), or experienced or witnessed homophobic or transphobic teasing by male peers in adolescence, all of which increase the risk for committing violence against girls and women (Brush & Miller, 2019). By omitting the protagonist’s developmental history in the story stem, we foreclosed the narrative possibility that he would transform an intergenerational cycle of violence. And no storyteller spontaneously wrote such a narrative arc. What this arc could look like is an open question. Additionally, the young men in this sample were neutral-to-somewhat-liberal in their average self-reported political orientation. Our findings cannot speak to redemptive storytelling on more socially/politically conservative campuses, or across a variety of campus subcultures. These questions can be explored in future research designed to uncover differences in avenues to atonement within or across campuses and their subcultures.

Sexual violence prevention implications and future research directions

This study’s findings underscore the call-to-action in a recent scholarly review, that much work is needed to implement and rigorously evaluate sexual assault prevention initiatives on college campuses (Orchowski et al., 2020). Stories in this study reflected both the absence of explanation for perpetrator acknowledgement of wrongdoing and the presence of problematic themes surrounding accountability-taking and atonement. For instance, in some stories, Cody embraces an instrumental, self-serving rationale for advocacy (e.g. to have sex with an attractive woman); pressures the victim to accept his apology; or implies that the work of ‘redeeming’ male perpetrators is incumbent upon women, even the victim herself.

Less common was Cody being held accountable by other men in his life. For the men in his life to take on this role, they ideally would have built up a pre-existing, collective capacity to do so. Gender-based collectives, specifically anti-sexist men’s groups, are one possible (and relatively untapped) context for long-term sexual violence prevention and intervention work (CrimethInc, 2020). Rooted in transformative justice principles, men’s groups could provide not only an ‘infrastructure for accountability responses’ to sexual violence, but also proactive and collective ‘self-education and action against patriarchy’ (p. 53). As ongoing groups, they would offer opportunities for reflection, emotional intimacy, mutual accountability, and positive personal transformation that are not ‘contingent on a ‘perpetrator’ label’ (p. 53). Such efforts to imagine what accountability looks like outside the carceral system owe much to international abolitionist feminist scholars and organizers (see Davis et al., 2022).

In keeping with this project’s focus on men, future research can also consider how men’s experiences of marginalization and vulnerability based on other aspects of their identities (e.g. race, immigration) might serve as pathways to anti-violence work (Casey et al., 2018). Moreover, no stories in this study referred directly to broader sociopolitical movements to end gender-based violence such as #MeToo, though one narrator depicted Cody bearing witness to survivor stories online as a catalyst for his empathy. Updated research is needed to understand whether and in what ways young men have engaged with #MeToo stories, and if this engagement can influence distorted attitudes about sex, or help ‘call in’ men to anti-violence work.

Conclusions

By asking young men to tell redemption stories, this study explored the outer limits of the dominant cultural imagination about accountability for sexual violence. Narrative psychology provides a rich conceptual framework to explore possibilities for accountability-taking and repair outside the fraught arena of the criminal justice system. But a truly transformative story about violence requires not only a perpetrator’s rehabilitation. It requires the efforts of all genders and communities to repair harm, and to address the multilayered social conditions that enable such harm. What a good story that would be.
