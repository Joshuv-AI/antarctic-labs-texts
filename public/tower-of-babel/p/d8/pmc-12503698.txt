Trust in radiation treatment for cancer challenged by childhood trauma
Authors: Christine Fader
Published: 2025

“No way am I doing that treatment.”

My husband, Michael, uttered those words through gritted teeth as he stumbled to our car after being fitted for his radiation mask. In over 20 years of marriage, I had never seen him in such a terrifying and terrified state.

We had been told repeatedly that his early-stage oropharyngeal cancer was “very curable, but you probably won’t like how we get there.” This was because treatment’s side effects could be severe and persistent, affecting his quality of life. The 35 radiation treatments would likely impact his beard, taste, swallowing, and skin, as well as his speech and breathing.

However, none of that scared him.

Michael had initially opted unequivocally for surgery, but the operating room time required to remove the tumour and complete a neck dissection wasn’t available for a couple of months. Because of the risk for tumour growth while waiting, the surgeon referred Michael to a radiation oncologist who recommended curative-intent radiation every weekday for 7 weeks. They set up an appointment to make a mask, which was required to ensure accurate delivery of radiotherapy to the cancer, while minimizing damage to surrounding tissue.

When the mask was being made, Michael told the staff that he was claustrophobic, so they suggested antianxiety meds, which he refused. As they moulded the pliable material over his face and neck, he couldn’t speak, but the staff chatted continuously, presumably in a well-intended attempt to calm him. It had exactly the opposite effect.

Patients experiencing claustrophobia when wearing a radiation face mask is not uncommon. However, Michael’s problem with wearing a mask ran deeper than claustrophobia.

At home, 4 hours after the appointment, my daredevil, fearless husband was still ashen and shaking uncontrollably. Apart from repeating that he was going to refuse treatment, Michael had not said a word. I knew he was serious, because he was far and away the most stubborn person I had ever met — a personality trait that had, until that point, saved his life many times.

A cancer diagnosis is often thought of as a worst-case scenario, but that is not true for everyone. “I’ve survived much worse than cancer,” Michael had reminded me soon after his diagnosis. Why, then, was a radiation mask such a deal breaker?

Born into poverty in a small village in northern Ontario, Michael had survived the untimely death of his father, then severe abuse from his stepfather, and still more abuse in foster care. His repeated attempts to run away from foster care led to truancy charges, landing him in the now-notorious St. Joseph’s Training School. He retained only shards of painful memories from these periods, as his brain protected him somewhat by locking away the bulk of the trauma. After his release, he lived under a railway trestle bridge, by himself, through a frigid northern winter with only a discarded sleeping bag for protection. He had survived all of this and more before reaching age 13, underweight and harbouring compounded, untreated injuries from physical and sexual abuse.

Watching Michael go through cancer was my “worst thing,” but it was definitely not his. Our foray into the cancer world had exposed us to sensitive and kind staff, but there were no indications in our bustling cancer centre that it was safe to talk about how Michael’s personal history might interact with his treatment.

The mask fitting had been unbearable for Michael because his childhood experiences of smothering, choking, and restraint were intensely reactivated. He told me that taking the suggested antianxiety medication would only serve to further exacerbate the horror, as he strongly needed to feel and stay fully in control.

This was different from fear. In addition to his incredible childhood survival story, Michael had been a military skydiver, been shot at, worked hundreds of metres underground in mines, and dangled off cliff edges and roofs. He did not look injured or timid to outsiders, yet he lived in a constant state of hypervigilance, guarding against possible dangers at every turn. He wore only v-neck shirts to ensure nothing touched his neck. Within seconds of being in a new situation or location, he determined exits and escape routes. He had managed his entire adult life this way, but the mask — even in the name of life-saving treatment — was not going to get his consent.

“We have to tell them you were abused,” I said to Michael, the day after the mask fitting, “Not the whole story, just that you’re a survivor.”

“How much do I have to reveal?” he asked, his eyes filling with shame, fear, and anger.

“We’ll close the door of the appointment room and say that you’re a childhood abuse survivor — no details required.”

He nodded, once. “I’ll think about it. I want to stay alive for you. Otherwise, I definitely would not be doing this.”

We requested a short discussion before his first treatment, but Michael couldn’t speak at the start. So I told them, “He is a child abuse survivor.” The respectful acceptance from the oncologist helped Michael ask that all staff refrain from speaking to him in the treatment room and after it was over. Michael didn’t disclose that, as a child, he used to “fly away” in his mind to escape whatever horrific situation he was enduring at the time. To endure the mask, he needed to get into that zone. He also needed to be left alone to wash his face and neck immediately after each treatment session, to regain composure before meeting me in the waiting room.

I don’t know the details of how the message was communicated within the team. Perhaps it was added to Michael’s chart, but the different radiation therapists who treated him every day all seemed to know the drill. Each person was a lifesaver.

The mask was still awful for Michael for each of the 35 days, and I wasn’t sure my heart would survive. However, his treatments were successful, and he had no evidence of disease afterwards.

Unfortunately, Michael was diagnosed with a second, unrelated cancer about a year after his first. He spent the final 4 months of his life in hospital with lower-limb paralysis, unable to go home because of shortages of community palliative and nursing care.

It’s hard to convey the extreme vulnerability Michael endured during his last months, paralyzed and on a fentanyl pump — as well as methadone and other medications — for intractable, excruciating cord compression pain. We had learned through both cancers to tell staff he was an abuse survivor, and how to care for him without triggering his trauma. When I raged at the tragic bookends of his life, a friend told me, “As a child, he had no one. Now, he has you.”

But he was very ill, and I was exhausted. His clinical binder was huge and constantly being thinned, losing a great deal of his story. Michael was seen by many clinicians, often briefly and during the night. We lost the bandwidth to keep reminding them — as they dealt with Michael’s vitals and meds, provided intimate care, and conducted painful examinations — to approach gently and with warning, introduce themselves, and obtain verbal consent for touch. These may seem like minor interactions, but they occurred frequently. The lack of trauma-informed care during many of these interactions compounded an already overwhelming and devastating time.

Never one to feel sorry for himself, Michael reminded me, “There’s always hope,” as we held hands across his hospital bed one Tuesday. It wasn’t the first time he had said it, but it was the last. He died the next morning.

Reflections on Michael’s experience

While researching strategies to help Michael, it surprised me to learn that he was far from alone.

In 2022, the prevalence of any child abuse in Canada was 34%; the prevalence of child sex abuse was 11%.1 Studies also show that patients with cancer and previous trauma have worse treatment compliance, are more likely to refuse treatments, and die sooner and more frequently than those without previous trauma.2,3 It is important to not overlook this large patient population.

Many cancers disproportionately affect people from lower socioeconomic, marginalized, racialized, Indigenous, immigrant, and military populations. These populations may also have higher rates of pre-cancer trauma. Michael met several predisposing categories.

Trauma-informed health care involves understanding the prevalence of trauma and its effects, and integrating this knowledge into all aspects of health care practice to promote healing and minimize the risk of re-traumatization.

Michael’s experience made me think about others in a similar situation. Would asking, “Is there anything we should know about you before we do ‘X’ or touch ‘Y’?” make patients feel safer disclosing a history of trauma? It would have helped us feel less uncertain and alone about opening the conversation.

Medical radiation technology students to whom I delivered a session about trauma-informed care were shocked to learn that they might be inadvertently re-traumatizing their patients, not only those with head and neck cancers, but patients likely to be exposed to procedures with intimate contact, like those with breast or prostate cancer. They brainstormed strategies to maintain and increase trust within their scope of practice, and realized they could ask about readiness before each touch, explain each step of the procedure, and re-confirm consent more frequently.

My hope, in Michael’s honour, is that trauma-informed health care will become part of a universal precautions toolkit. People working in health care hope that they contribute to cures, greater longevity, and reduced suffering. Without trauma-informed processes, care partnerships for patients with a personal trauma history won’t fully realize those goals.
