Discovery and characterization of novel hematopoietic prostaglandin D synthase inhibitors from traditional Chinese medicine: the bioactive potential of dihydroberberine in treatments of Duchenne muscular dystrophy and inflammation-related diseases
Authors: Cheng-Han Li, Min-Che Tung, Cheng-Kuan Tsai, Tz-Chuen Ju, Tien-Sheng Tseng
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractInflammation plays a central role in various diseases, necessitating effective anti-inflammatory agents. Prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), a key mediator synthesised by haematopoietic prostaglandin D synthase (H-PGDS), is linked to allergic and inflammatory conditions. This study employed pharmacophore-based screening to identify inhibitors targeting H-PGDS. The model Phar-A2HR2 identified EMy (IC50 = 88.9 ± 1.1 µM) as a potential inhibitor. Further analysis revealed that its analog EMy-5 (dihydroberberine) demonstrated the most potent inhibitory activity (IC50 = 3.7 ± 1.1 µM) and binding affinity (KD = 3.2 ± 0.74 µM). Molecular dynamics simulations revealed stabilising interactions, including π-π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic contacts, in the H-PGDS-EMy-5 complex. Functional assays confirmed that EMy-5 significantly reduced PGD2 production in KU812 cells. These findings highlight EMy-5 as a promising candidate for the treatment of inflammatory and allergic diseases, including Duchenne muscular dystrophy, demonstrating both potent inhibitory activity and strong binding characteristics.Keywords: Prostaglandin D2, haematopoietic prostaglandin D synthase, pharmacophore-based inhibitor screening, traditional Chinese medicine, Duchenne muscular dystrophy

Introduction

Inflammation is a complex physiological response triggered by harmful stimuli, infections, or tissue injuries, serving as an essential component of the defense mechanism of immune system.1,2 This multifaceted process aims to eliminate harmful agents, prevent further damage, and initiate tissue repair and healing.3 While inflammation is critical for maintaining homeostasis, dysregulated or chronic inflammation underlies the pathogenesis of numerous diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis, atherosclerosis, and asthma, which collectively affect millions worldwide.4–7 Consequently, targeting inflammatory pathways has emerged as a cornerstone in therapeutic strategies aimed at mitigating disease progression and alleviating symptoms. Arachidonic acid-derived prostaglandins are key lipid mediators that play pivotal roles in modulating the inflammatory response.8,9 Among these, prostaglandin D2 (PGD2) is a biologically active acidic lipid mediator that exerts diverse physiological and pathological effects.10–13 PGD2 is implicated in the regulation of body temperature, hormone secretion, olfactory functions, sleep cycles, and pain perception, in addition to its anti-thrombotic activity through the inhibition of platelet aggregation.10–13 The actions of PGD2 are mediated by two distinct G protein-coupled receptors (GPCRs): the DP1 and DP2 receptors. The DP2 receptor, also known as CRTH2 (chemoattractant receptor-homologous molecule expressed on Th2 cells), plays a central role in Th2-associated immune responses.14,15 The DP1 receptor is predominantly expressed on dendritic cells, whereas the DP2 receptor is found on Th2 cells, eosinophils, and basophils. Activation of DP2 receptors contributes to eosinophil chemotaxis and serves as a critical driver of Th2-mediated inflammatory processes, including those associated with allergic and inflammatory diseases.14,16,17

PGD2 is synthesised through the catalytic activity of two distinct isoforms of prostaglandin D2 synthase: the haematopoietic prostaglandin D2 synthase (H-PGDS) and the lipocalin prostaglandin D2 synthase (L-PGDS).18,19 These enzymes differ in their tissue distribution and functional roles. L-PGDS is predominantly expressed in cardiac tissue, testis, and the brain, where it contributes to various physiological processes.20–22 In contrast, H-PGDS, a glutathione-dependent enzyme, primarily catalyses PGD2 synthesis in inflammatory contexts and is highly expressed in mast cells, antigen-presenting cells, megakaryocytes, and T helper 2 (Th2) lymphocytes.20–24 H-PGDS belongs to the sigma (σ) class of glutathione S-transferases (GST) and catalyses the conversion of cyclooxygenase-derived PGH2 to PGD2 with remarkable specificity.25,26 This specificity is largely attributed to the unique structural characteristics of its catalytic domain, which is broader and deeper than the active sites typically observed in other GST family members.27,28 These structural features not only enable precise catalysis but also distinguish H-PGDS as a critical mediator in inflammatory signalling pathways. Given its central role in PGD2 production and the subsequent promotion of Th2-driven inflammatory responses, H-PGDS has emerged as a promising therapeutic target for managing allergic and inflammatory diseases. Inhibition of H-PGDS has demonstrated significant protective effects in preclinical models, particularly in mouse studies of allergic airway inflammation.29,30 These findings underscore the therapeutic potential of H-PGDS inhibitors, which provide effective strategies for treating conditions such as asthma, atopic dermatitis, and other inflammation-related diseases.

Recent advancements in determining the crystal structure of H-PGDS have provided critical insights into enzyme-substrate interactions, paving the way for the rational design of H-PGDS inhibitors. Substantial research and numerous patents have focused on the potential of these inhibitors to treat a variety of inflammatory disorders, including allergic reactions, asthma, and other inflammation-associated diseases.24,30–32 Several inhibitors have been explored, such as TAS-204,33 TAS-205[34], HQL-79,34,35 KMN-698,36 HPGDS inhibitor I,37 and TFC-007.38 Among these, TAS-205 advanced to a Phase I clinical trial in 2018, where it was tested in 23 boys with Duchenne muscular dystrophy.39 Despite these developments, the clinical translation of H-PGDS inhibitors has faced significant challenges. A primary obstacle is the discrepancy in pharmacokinetics and pharmacodynamics observed between preclinical animal models and human trials. This underscores the necessity for innovative therapeutic strategies that extend beyond traditional H-PGDS inhibition for more robust clinical evaluation and application. Natural products have gained increasing attention as pivotal resources in modern drug discovery. These natural sources offer a plethora of bioactive compounds with distinctive molecular structures and pharmacological properties that often confer enhanced safety profiles and reduced side effects compared to synthetic drugs.40 Their utility spans a wide array of therapeutic areas, addressing conditions such as chronic inflammation, cancer, and neurological disorders.41 As integrative medicine continues to gain traction, Natural products is recognised not only for their roles in complementary therapies but also as reservoirs of novel compounds with the potential to enhance the efficacy and safety of conventional pharmacological treatments. Given these advantages, natural products hold significant promise for the discovery and development of novel H-PGDS inhibitors.

In this study, we sought to identify, characterise, and develop novel inhibitors of human H-PGDS from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) database. To achieve this, we conducted an integrative approach combining, pharmacophore-based inhibitor screening, biochemical and biophysical analyses, cell-based assays as well as molecular simulations. We discovered and characterised dihydroberberine (EMy-5), a berberine derivative, as a highly effective inhibitor of H-PGDS, a pivotal enzyme in the biosynthesis of the pro-inflammatory mediator PGD2. Through comprehensive screening of bioactive compounds from a TCM database, EMy-5 exhibited superior inhibitory activity, with an IC50 value of 3.7 µM, surpassing the performance of the known H-PGDS inhibitor HQL-79. Additionally, label-free Localised Surface Plasmon Resonance (LSPR) analysis confirmed strong binding affinity of (EMy-5 to H-PGDS (KD = 3.2 µM). Furthermore, molecular docking and dynamic simulation studies revealed that EMy-5 establishes stable interactions with key residues in the H-PGDS active site via hydrogen bonding, π-π stacking, and hydrophobic forces, forming a structurally stable and energetically favourable protein-ligand complex. Moreover, EMy-5 significantly reduced PGD2 synthesis in KU812 cells also demonstrating an absence of cytotoxic effects on mammalian cells, emphasising both its pharmacological efficacy and safety profile. The findings underscore the potential of EMy-5, derived from berberine of foods, plants, and herbs, could serve as promising sources for the development of innovative anti-inflammatory and anti-allergic therapies targeting the PGD2 pathway, meriting further preclinical and clinical investigation.

Materials and methods

Preparations of the recombinant H-PGDS protein

The construction of the human H-PGDS expression plasmid involved the seamless integration of a synthetic H-PGDS gene into the pET22(b)+ vector using Clone EZ seamless cloning technology. Gene synthesis and subsequent cloning into the designated expression vector were performed by Yao-Hong Biotechnology Inc. The resulting H-PGDS plasmid was transformed into E. coli Shuffle T7 (DE3) (New England Biolabs) cells and cultivated in LB medium (Sigma–Aldrich) containing 50 mg/L ampicillin (Sigma-Aldrich) at 37 °C. Protein expression was induced by adding 1.0 mM IPTG (Sigma–Aldrich) once the optical density at 600 nm (OD600) reached 0.6. Induction was carried out at 24 °C for 18 h to optimise protein folding. The cells were then harvested by centrifugation at 6,000 rpm for 10 min and resuspended in lysis buffer containing 20 mM Tris-HCl (Sigma–Aldrich) and 150 mM NaCl (Sigma–Aldrich) (pH 7.4). Cell lysis was achieved using a high-pressure homogeniser (Constant Systems Cell Disruptor CF1). The lysate was centrifuged to collect the supernatant, which was subjected to a two-step purification process. Initially, nickel-nitrilotriacetic acid (Ni-NTA) affinity chromatography (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) was employed to capture the His-tagged H-PGDS protein. This was followed by further purification using a Hitrap Capto Q column (Cytiva Life Sciences, MA, USA) through fast protein liquid chromatography (FPLC), ensuring high protein purity. The purity of the final H-PGDS preparation was assessed via SDS-PAGE with Coomassie blue staining, confirming the production of pure protein.

Receptor–ligand pharmacophore generation and pharmacophore-based inhibitor screening (ligand pharmacophore mapping)

To elucidate the critical features required for ligand interactions with target proteins, receptor-ligand pharmacophore modelling was performed. The pharmacophore model was developed using the crystal structure of the H-PGDS-quinoline-3-carboxamide complex (PDB ID: 6N4E). The modelling process was conducted with the receptor-ligand pharmacophore generation module in Discovery Studio 2021 (Accelrys Software, Inc., San Diego, CA, USA). In constructing the pharmacophore model, the H-PGDS structure served as the “Input Receptor’, while the quinoline-3-carboxamide molecule was designated as the “Input Ligand.” Parameters were configured with “Minimum Features” and “Maximum Features” set to 10 and 30, respectively, and the maximum number of pharmacophores limited to 10. For conformation generation, the “fast method” was utilised in conjunction with the “rigid fitting method’. Default settings were applied to all other parameters. The generated pharmacophore model was subsequently employed for ligand-pharmacophore mapping to identify potential inhibitors based on the defined pharmacophore features. A total of 1,500 compounds from traditional Chinese medicine (TCM) database (https://qatcm.nricm.edu.tw/key-components/) were screened by fitting them to the pharmacophore model. This screening utilised the “flexible” fitting method, while all other parameters were maintained at their default values.

H-PGDS inhibition assay

The FP-Based Inhibitor Screening Assay Kit-Green (Cayman Chemical, 600007) was employed to quantify the H-PGDS inhibitory ability of the screened inhibitors. All assays were conducted in black, nonbinding 384-well plates, with recombinant human H-PGDS protein, glutathione, and a fluorescent probe in a defined assay buffer to achieve a final volume of 47.5 µl per well. Compounds were prepared in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich) stock solutions, and 2.5 µl of each test compound was added to respective wells to generate a range of compound concentrations. Plates were then incubated at room temperature for 1 h to allow for adequate binding interactions. Measurements were recorded on a Synergy H1MF plate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc.), with the excitation and emission wavelengths set to 470 nm and 530 nm, respectively, in fluorescence polarisation mode. The inhibitory effect of each compound on H-PGDS was calculated based on the change in mP values, using the following equation: inhibition % = (mP100% − mPsample)/mP100% ×100, in which mPsample denotes the fluorescence polarisation value for wells with test inhibitors, and mP100% indicates the value for wells without inhibitor addition. Each experimental condition was assessed in triplicate for robust statistical analysis. The IC50 for each compound, defined as the concentration required to reduce the mP by 50%, was estimated by plotting mP values against the log-transformed compound concentrations, enabling determination of binding affinity and efficacy.

Localised Surface Plasmon Resonance (LSPR)

Localised Surface Plasmon Resonance (LSPR) measurements were performed using an OpenSPR instrument (Nicoya Lifesciences Inc.) to evaluate the binding interactions between human H-PGDS and inhibitors. Human H-PGDS was covalently immobilised on an amine sensor chip through crosslinking. The crosslinking reaction utilised 1-ethyl-3–(3-dimethylaminopropyl) carbodiimide (EDC) (Sigma–Aldrich) and N-hydroxysuccinimide (NHS) (Sigma–Aldrich) in a molar ratio of 1.5:1.5:1, followed by purification of the H-PGDS-EDC/NHS complex using a PD SpinTrap G-25 column (GE Healthcare Life Sciences). The PD SpinTrap G-25 column was equilibrated with 400 µl of 1× PBS (Sigma–Aldrich) by centrifugation at 800 × g for 1 min, and the flowthrough was discarded. Subsequently, the column was transferred to a clean collection tube, and 150 µl reaction mixture of H-PGDS-EDC/NHS was carefully applied to the centre of the packed resin bed. The separation of unreacted reagents and the H-PGDS-EDC/NHS complex was achieved by centrifugation at 800 × g for 2 min, allowing for the collection of the eluate containing the purified protein solution. The resulting sample was used for downstream applications. The purified H-PGDS sample was diluted to a concentration of 50 µg/ml in 1× PBS and immobilised onto the sensor chip surface. Blocking was achieved using a 1× PBS solution containing 1% BSA (Sigma–Aldrich) at a flow rate of 20 µl/min to minimise non-specific interactions. For assay measurements, the running buffer was switched to PBST (Sigma–Aldrich) supplemented with 2 mM MgCl2 (Sigma–Aldrich) and 2 mM GSH (Sigma–Aldrich). The inhibitors, initially prepared as 10 mM DMSO stocks, were diluted into the same PBST buffer to various concentrations and injected sequentially over the immobilised H-PGDS surface. Binding interactions were monitored in real time to generate sensorgrams, which were analysed using TraceDrawer software. The kinetic parameters, including the dissociation rate constant (Koff) and the association rate constant (Kon), were calculated and used to derive the equilibrium dissociation constant (KD). This KD value provided a quantitative assessment of the binding affinity between H-PGDS and the inhibitors.

Cell culture

Human embryonic kidney 293 T (HEK293T) cell line was kindly provided by Dr. Ming-Chuan Hsu.42

The KU812 cell line was obtained from Bioresource Collection and Research Centre, Taiwan. HEK293T cells were maintained in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma–Aldrich), 100 U/ml penicillin (Sigma–Aldrich), and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin (Sigma–Aldrich) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 environment. Upon reaching 80% confluence, the culture medium was removed, and the cells were washed with 1 × PBS. The cells were then treated with 2 ml of 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Sigma–Aldrich) for 4 min at 37 °C to detach them from the culture surface. Following detachment, the reaction was terminated by adding 2 ml of DMEM (Sigma–Aldrich) with 10% FBS, and the cells were centrifuged at 800 rpm for 3 min to pellet. The cell pellet was resuspended in 4 ml of DMEM with 10% FBS, and the cell density was adjusted to 5,000 cells per 100 µl. The cell suspension was subsequently seeded into a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h to allow adhesion prior to cytotoxicity testing. KU812 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma–Aldrich), supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 0.1 mg/mL streptomycin. The cells were maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For experimental assays, KU812 cells (5 × 105 cells) were transferred to RPMI-1640 medium containing 0.1% FBS and incubated in glass tubes. Cells were treated with a range of inhibitor concentrations for 30 min, then stimulated with 1 µM A23187 (Sigma–Aldrich) for an additional 30 min. Following the incubation period, cell suspensions were centrifuged at 350 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Supernatants were carefully collected from the centrifuged tubes for the quantitative determination of PGD2 concentration.

Cytotoxicity

HEK293T cells were cultured in DMEM (Sigma–Aldrich) supplemented with 10% FBS (Sigma–Aldrich), 100 U/ml penicillin (Sigma–Aldrich), and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin (Sigma–Aldrich) at 37 °C in a 5% CO2 atmosphere. Cells were plated at a density of 5,000 cells per well in a 96-well plate and incubated for 24 h to ensure attachment. Following this, the medium was removed, and cells were treated with varying concentrations of test compounds diluted in fresh DMEM (Sigma-Aldrich) with 10% FBS for an additional 24 and 48 h, respectively. KU812 cells were cultured in RPMI-1640 medium (Sigma-Aldrich), supplemented with 10% FBS, 100 U/ml penicillin, and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin, and maintained at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere containing 5% CO2. For cell viability assays, KU812 cells (5 × 105 cells) were transferred to RPMI-1640 medium containing 0.1% FBS and treated with the test compounds in glass tubes for 24 and 48 h, individually under the same culture conditions. After treatment, both HEK293T and KU812 cells were processed for crystal violet staining. The medium was discarded, and cells were washed once with 1× PBS. Crystal violet staining was performed by adding 100 µl of a 0.1% crystal violet (Sigma–Aldrich) solution to each well or tube, followed by a 15-min incubation at room temperature. Excess dye was removed, and the samples were washed with distilled water. To solubilise the bound crystal violet, 100 µl of 33% acetic acid (Sigma-Aldrich) was added, and absorbance was measured at 570 nm using a microplate reader (Synergy H1MF plate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc.)). Cell viability was calculated as a percentage relative to control wells with no treatment, using the formula: Cell viability (%) = (OD570 of treated wells/OD570 of control wells) × 100.

Measurement of PGD2 concentrations in samples

The levels of PGD2 in the cell culture supernatants were quantified using a PGD2-MOX Enzyme Immunoassay (EIA) kit (Cayman Chemical) in accordance with the manufacturer’s guidelines. The experimental procedure was as follows: KU812 cells were seeded and treated with EMy-5 at final concentrations of 3.7, 37, and 100 µM. EMy-5 was reconstituted in DMSO (Sigma-Aldrich), and the DMSO concentration in all treatments, including controls, was maintained at 0.5%. Both control and non-treatment groups received 0.5% DMSO without the drug. The cells were incubated under standard culture conditions (37 °C, 5% CO2) for 24 h. Afterward, the cells were stimulated with 1 µM A23187 (Sigma-Aldrich) for 30 min. Post-stimulation, the cell culture supernatants were collected by centrifugation at 800 rpm for 3 min and stored at −80 °C until further analysis. The concentration of PGD2 in the supernatants was measured using the PGD2-MOX EIA Kit (Cayman Chemical). Briefly, 200 µl of the sample or 900 µl of PGD2 standard was mixed with an equal volume of methyloximating (MOX) reagent (1:1, v/v) and heated at 60 °C for 30 min to stabilise the PGD2-MOX derivatives. The prepared samples, along with the tracer and antiserum, were added to IgG-coated microplates and incubated overnight at 4 °C. The following day, the wells were emptied, washed five times with wash buffer, and incubated with 200 µl of Ellman’s reagent (Sigma-Aldrich) at room temperature for 2 h in the dark. The optical density at 414 nm (OD414) was measured using an ELISA reader (Synergy H1MF plate reader (BioTek Instruments, Inc.)) to quantify the PGD2 concentration.

Molecular dynamics simulations

The structural conformations of the screened inhibitors (EMw, EMx, EMy-1, and EMy-5) from ligand-pharmacophore (Phar-A2HR2) mapping were investigated using MD simulations. The construction of the protein–ligand complex proceeded as follows. Initially, the conformation of the ligand was acquired from the outcome of ligand-pharmacophore mapping. Subsequently, the ligand fitted to the pharmacophore is relocated within the protein structure, which serves as the basis for generating a pharmacophore model. Before an MD simulation, the complex structures of the protein-ligand were subjected to the “Prepare proteins” method in Discovery Studio 2021 to remove any errors in these structures. Preparations of the H-PGDS–inhibitor complexes involved using the “Prepare Proteins” protocol in Discovery Studio 2021 to address structural inconsistencies. Each complex was solvated in an orthorhombic simulation box under the CHARMm force field, with a physiological ionic strength of 0.15 M NaCl to ensure system neutrality. Solvation details included the addition of water molecules, sodium ions, and chloride ions: 5,952 water molecules, 20 sodium ions, and 16 chloride ions for the H-PGDS control; similar configurations were applied for the inhibitor-bound complexes, with minor variations based on system size. Energy minimisation was conducted in two phases: the first comprising 5,000 steps using the steepest descent method and the second with 5,000 steps employing the conjugate gradient method, ensuring optimised initial geometries. This was followed by a heating phase over 20 ps to gradually increase the temperature to 300 K, and an equilibration phase of 500 ps using the Standard Dynamic Cascade protocol in Discovery Studio 2021. Subsequent production MD simulations spanned 100 ns under the NVT ensemble at 300 K, with trajectory snapshots recorded at 500 ps intervals. Electrostatic interactions were calculated using the Particle Mesh Ewald (PME) method, while the SHAKE algorithm was employed to constrain hydrogen-containing bonds, allowing a time step of 2 fs. The Generalised Born implicit solvent model further enhanced computational efficiency while maintaining accuracy. Post-simulation analyses focused on key parameters such as root mean square deviation (RMSD), root mean square fluctuation (RMSF), and radius of gyration (Rg), utilising the “Analyze Trajectory” tool in Discovery Studio 2021. These metrics provided a comprehensive understanding of the dynamic behaviour and structural integrity of the H-PGDS-inhibitor complexes compared to the initial structures. RMSD analysis quantified the overall conformational stability of the protein-ligand systems, while RMSF provided residue-specific flexibility insights. Rg values were used to evaluate structural compactness, reflecting the inhibitors’ impact on the protein’s stability and dynamic behaviour during the simulation trajectory.

Binding free energy calculation

Binding free energy (ΔG) serves as a fundamental thermodynamic indicator of the interaction strength between a ligand and its target protein .43 In this study, ΔG values were estimated using the molecular mechanics Poisson–Boltzmann surface area (MM/PBSA) approach, a computationally efficient and widely validated method for quantifying the absolute binding affinity of ligands at the active site of proteins.44 This method enables the assessment of ligand-induced stabilisation or destabilisation of protein–ligand complexes, providing insights into their potential pharmacological efficacy. All binding free energy calculations were performed using the “Binding Free Energy – Single Trajectory” protocol implemented in Discovery Studio 2021. For each protein–ligand complex, ΔG values were computed across all conformations generated during the simulation trajectory, and the average binding free energy was subsequently determined to represent the thermodynamic profile of each complex.

Results

Receptor–ligand pharmacophore generation

To efficiently identify potent inhibitors of H-PGDS, it is crucial to analyse the functional features that facilitate interactions between H-PGDS and its known inhibitors. Pharmacophore modelling has proven to be a vital tool for identifying and characterising essential ligand elements, enabling precise and efficient receptor binding.45,46 This approach translates protein properties into corresponding ligand features, providing a robust framework for understanding the critical attributes that govern inhibitor-target protein interactions.47 The HPGDS–quinoline-3-carboxamide complex (PDB ID: 6N4E) was selected as the template for pharmacophore modelling due to its high-resolution (1.65 Å) crystal structure and well-defined binding interactions. The co-crystallized ligand interacts with key active site residues (e.g., Trp104, Tyr152, Ser64), allowing us to extract critical pharmacophoric features such as hydrogen bond donor/acceptor sites and aromatic moieties. These features served as the foundation for virtual screening of novel inhibitors. The crystal structure of the H-PGDS-quinoline-3-carboxamide complex (PDB ID: 6N4E) represents a valuable resource for pharmacophore modelling. Utilising this structure, a pharmacophore model was developed through receptor-ligand pharmacophore generation (Figure 1(A and B)). In this process, H-PGDS was defined as the receptor, while the quinoline-3-carboxamide molecule served as the ligand. This process culminated in the generation of a pharmacophore model designated as Phar-A2HR2 (Figure 1(C and D)). Phar-A2HR2 comprises two hydrogen-bond acceptors (depicted as green spheres), one hydrophobic feature (illustrated as a cyan sphere), and two ring aromatic features (represented as orange spheres) (Figure 1(D)). These pharmacophore models offer a comprehensive representation of the essential features necessary for quinoline-3-carboxamide binding and interacting with H-PGDS.

Pharmacophore‐based inhibitor screening

High-throughput screening of inhibitors through pharmacophore modelling necessitates the careful selection of a robust pharmacophore scaffold to facilitate effective ligand-pharmacophore mapping. In this study, we utilised the pharmacophore model Phar-A2HR2 to systematically screen a compound library consisting of 1,500 molecules sourced from TCM database. The ligand-pharmacophore mapping process involved aligning the three-dimensional coordinates of each ligand to the distinct pharmacophore features of Phar-A2HR2, including two hydrogen-bond acceptors, one hydrophobic feature, and two aromatic ring features (Figure 1(D)). This alignment allowed for the precise evaluation of how well each compound matched the pharmacophore. Fit values were calculated to quantify the quality of the match, with higher scores indicating stronger and more favourable interactions between the ligand and the pharmacophore. These values provided a basis for ranking the compounds, enabling the identification of the most promising candidates. Following this rigorous analysis, the ten top-ranked compounds were selected as potential lead inhibitors for further experimental validation (Figure 2). These candidates exhibited superior fit scores, suggesting their high potential for effective interaction with H-PGDS. The chemical structures of these top-ranked compounds are detailed in Figure S1.

H-PGDS inhibitory activity

A fluorescence polarisation-based inhibition assay was utilised to investigate the inhibitory abilities of top 10 candidates (from ligand-pharmacophore mapping) against H-PGDS. Initially, the H-PGDS inhibitory effects of compounds were assessed at 100 µM. The results showed that the compounds and Top3 (EMw), Top6 (EMy), and Top7 (EMx), were able to inhibit the activity of H-PGDS greater than 40% (Figure 3(A) and Figure S1), while the others were less potent. Subsequently, EMw, EMx, and EMy were subjected to further inhibition assessments at various concentrations to determine their IC50 values. The results demonstrated that EMw, EMx, and EMy exhibited a dose-dependent inhibition against H-PGDS (Figure 3(B)). The IC50 values for EMw, EMx, and EMy were determined to be 132.5 ± 1.3, 269.9 ± 1.1, and 88.9 ± 1.1 µM, respectively. Notably, EMy demonstrated the stronger inhibitory potency against H-PGDS, leading to subsequent experimental assessments of inhibitory efficacy in its structural analogs and derivatives. About 6 analogs of EMy were subsequently evaluated in inhibitory assays to assess their effects on the activity of H-PGDS (Figure 4). The results showed that, at a concentration of 100 µM, the inhibitions of compounds EMy-1 and EMy-5 against H-PGDS were 42.8 and 100%, respectively (Figure 5(A)). Besides, these compounds showed the dose-dependent inhibition effect against H-PGDS (Figure 5(B)). The IC50 values for EMy-5 and EMy-1 were determined to be 3.7 ± 1.1 and 47.8 ± 1.1 µM, individually, indicating enhanced inhibitory potency against H-PGDS, compared to that of EMy.

The binding affinity of EMy-5 towards H-PGDS

EMy-5 was identified as the most potent inhibitor against H-PGDS. To further confirm the binding and interaction of EMy-5 to H-PGDS, the LSPR experiments were performed. During the experiments, EMy-5 was tested at concentrations of 3.125, 12.5, 25, and 50 µM against H-PGDS. The resultant sensorgrams revealed a fast association and slow dissociation binding pattern of EMy-5 targeting H-PGDS, resulting in a KD value of 3.2 ± 0.74 µM (Figure 6).

Cytotoxicity

A cytotoxicity assessment of EMy-5 was performed using HEK293T cells to determine its effects on cell viability and potential cytotoxicity. Cells were treated with EMy-5 at concentrations of 0.37, 3.7, and 37 µM and analysed at both 24 and 48 h post-treatment. Crystal violet staining was used to quantify viable cells at each time point. As shown in Figure 7(A), at both 24 and 48 h, cell viability remained comparable across all EMy-5 concentrations, indicating no significant cytotoxic impact in HEK293T cells. To further evaluate the safety profile of EMy-5 in a haematopoietic context, KU812 basophilic cells were treated with the same concentration range and assessed for viability at 24 and 48 h using the cell viability assay. As shown in Figure 7(B), no significant cytotoxicity was observed at any time point or concentration, including at 10× IC50. These results suggest that EMy-5 does not induce acute cytotoxic effects in either HEK293T or KU812 cells under the tested conditions.

EMy-5 suppressed PGD2 production in KU812 cells

Next, we evaluated the potency of EMy-5 against PGD2 production. To investigate the anti-inflammatory potential of EMy-5, its inhibitory effect on PGD2 production was assessed using human basophilic KU812 cells. KU812 cells were stimulated with the calcium ionophore A23187 for 30 min, resulting in a significant increase in PGD2 levels in the culture supernatant compared to non-treated controls. Following stimulation, EMy-5 was administered at concentrations of 3.7, 37 and 100 µM. The results demonstrated that the average amounts of PGD2-MOX of KU812 cells without EMy-5 treatments was 502 (pg/ml). Interestingly, with the treatments of 37 and 100 µM of EMy-5, average amounts of PGD2-MOX significantly reduced to 240 and 20 (pg/ml), respectively (Figure 7(C)). These results revealed that EMy-5 can effectively inhibit PGD2 synthesis in cells.

Molecular modelling and simulations reveal the mode of actions of inhibitors

MD simulations were performed to determine binding behaviours of the inhibitor to H-PGDS. The average RMSD, RMSF, and Rg of H-PGDS-EMw, H-PGDS-EMx, H-PGDS-EMy-1, and H-PGDS-EMy-5 for all conformations were calculated through simulations with a duration of 100 ns and compared with those of the control (H-PGDS alone) to determine the stability of the complexes. The total energies of H-PGDS alone and in complex with inhibitors as a function of simulation times were shown in Figure 8(A). The average RMSD for H-PGDS alone was 0.98 Å, smaller than that of the H-PGDS-EMy-5 complex (1.27 Å; Figure 8(B)) and H-PGDS-EMy-1 (1.56 Å). However, the RMSD was higher for H-PGDS-EMw and H-PGDS-EMx, equal to 1.81 and 1.95 Å, respectively. Fluctuations in individual residues within the simulation period are plotted in Figure 8(C) (RMSF). The residues for H-PGDS, H-PGDS-EMy-1, and H-PGDS-EMy-5 had similar RMSF patterns, but those of the H-PGDS-EMw and H-PGDS-EMx complexes differed greatly, suggesting that these ligands affected the stability of H-PGDS and altered its dynamic behaviour. The Rg for H-PGDS was more stable, and fluctuated slightly between 16.87–17.03 Å (Figure 8(D)). Similarly, for H-PGDS-EMy-5, Rg fluctuated slightly between 16.88–17.12 Å; for H-PGDS-EMy-1, Rg fluctuated between 16.91–17.11 Å. By contrast, Rg values of H-PGDS-EMw and H-PGDS-EMx complexes apparently fluctuated between 16.91–17.24 and 16.81–17.24 Å, respectively. These results implied that EMy-5 and EMy-1 formed a thermodynamically stable complex with H-PGDS, whereas complexes with EMx and EMw were less stable. Furthermore, the average binding free energies of the H-PGDS–ligand complexes, calculated using the MM-PBSA method, were −17.1 kcal/mol for EMy-5, −14.2 kcal/mol for EMy-1, and 0.3 and 0.9 kcal/mol for EMw and EMx, respectively (Figure 8(E)). These results indicate that EMy-5 and EMy-1 form thermodynamically stable complexes with H-PGDS, while EMw and EMx exhibit significantly weaker and less stable interactions.

Analyses of molecular interactions of inhibitors with H-PGDS

To better understand the molecular interactions between the identified inhibitors – EMx, EMw, EMy-1, and EMy-5 – and H-PGDS, structural analyses were performed using the non-bond interaction analysis module in Discovery Studio 2021. The conformations of EMx, EMw, EMy-1, and EMy-5 in complex with H-PGDS were obtained at MD simulation time point of 100 ns. The results showed that EMx, EMw, EMy-1, and EMy-5 bound into the active site of H-PGDS with distinct positions and orientations by interacting with functionally essential residues (Figure 9 and Figure S2). EMx, EMw, EMy-1, and EMy-5 interacted with active site residues of H-PGDS through various charge–charge interactions, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic contacts (Figure 9 and Figure S2). At a simulation time of 100 ns, EMx interacted with H-PGDS through 1 hydrogen bond, two and 13 hydrophobic interactions (Figure S2). At the same time point, EMw was bound to H-PGDS with 1 hydrogen bonds, 1 sulfur–x interaction, and 14 hydrophobic contact (Figure S1). In contrast, EMy-1 interacted with 12 hydrophobic contacts (Figure 9). EMy-5 interacted with H-PGDS through 1 hydrogen bond and 14 hydrophobic contacts (Figure 9).

Discussion

Inflammation poses a substantial challenge to global health, affecting populations across both industrialised and developing nations.48–51 Chronic inflammation is a contributing factor to a variety of severe conditions, such as cancer,52 asthma,53 rheumatoid arthritis,54 atherosclerosis,55 and periodontitis.56 These challenges have driven the ongoing search for anti-inflammatory agents that are not only highly effective but also associated with minimal side effects. Despite the effectiveness of conventional therapies, their significant adverse effects highlight the urgent need for safer and more precise treatment strategies. Among these, nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs) are extensively utilised for managing inflammatory diseases; however, their clinical utility is often constrained by the high incidence of associated side effects.57–66 A key mediator of inflammation, particularly in allergic responses, is PGD2. PGD2 is involved in several inflammatory processes, including vasodilation, immune cell recruitment, platelet aggregation, smooth muscle relaxation, and sleep induction.12,67–72 Elevated levels of PGD2 have been shown to exacerbate allergic and asthmatic symptoms in animal models, suggesting its pivotal role in the progression and severity of these conditions.73–75 Consequently, PGD2 has emerged as an important target for the development of novel therapeutic interventions aimed at managing allergic inflammation. The enzyme H-PGDS, which is crucial in the biosynthesis of PGD2, has been implicated in conditions like allergic rhinitis, further highlighting its potential as a therapeutic target for anti-inflammatory and anti-allergic drugs. By targeting HPGDS to regulate PGD2 production or inhibiting PGD2 receptor activation, it may be possible to mitigate the bronchoconstrictor effects commonly seen in allergic diseases. Clinical support for these strategies is evident from the use of H-PGDS inhibitors, such as tranilast, and PGD2 receptor antagonists like ramatroban, which have proven effective in reducing allergic symptoms.76,77 A recent, extensive review has cataloged a variety of H-PGDS inhibitors, evaluating their therapeutic potential and providing a comprehensive overview of advancements in this area.78 This growing body of research underscores the importance of further investigation into H-PGDS inhibitors and PGD2 receptor antagonists as promising avenues for developing safer and more targeted anti-inflammatory and anti-allergic therapies. Recently, natural product has attracted substantial research attention due to its generally lower toxicity profiles and fewer adverse effects.79–81 The contributions of natural product to public health are significant, positioning it as a promising reservoir of natural therapeutics.

CADD, encompassing molecular docking and pharmacophore modelling, has emerged as a powerful and cost-efficient strategy for the identification of bioactive compounds with defined therapeutic potential.82,83 Pharmacophore modelling, particularly receptor-ligand pharmacophore generation (structure-based pharmacophores; SBPs), translates the structural features of a target protein into ligand-specific attributes, enabling the design of inhibitors with tailored binding properties.45,46 Here, we utilised the crystal structure of the H-PGDS-quinoline-3-carboxamide complex (PDB ID: 6N4E) to construct a pharmacophore model for the rational screening of potential inhibitors. Using H-PGDS as the receptor and quinoline-3-carboxamide as the ligand, we developed the Phar-A2HR2 pharmacophore model, characterised by two hydrogen-bond acceptors, one hydrophobic feature, and two aromatic ring features (Figure 1(D)). This model encapsulates the key molecular interactions required for ligand binding and provides a robust framework for identifying novel inhibitors targeting the H-PGDS binding pocket. Application of the Phar-A2HR2 model in virtual screening yielded ten top-ranked candidate compounds (Figure 2), each of which was subsequently evaluated for inhibitory activity. About 3 molecules (EMw, EMx, and EMy) showed apparent inhibitions against H-PGDS (Figure 3(A)) with dose-dependent manner (Figure 3(B)). In particular, EMy (IC50 = 88.9 ± 1.1 µM) was observed to exert obviously inhibitory ability against H-PGDS. We subsequently evaluated the inhibitory effects of EMy’s analogs on H-PGDS. Preliminary evaluations of the inhibitory effects of structural analogs on H-PGDS demonstrated that EMy-1 and EMy-5 exerted apparent inhibition at 100 µM (Figure 5(A)). In contrast, EMy-2, EMy-3, EMy-4, and EMy-6 exhibited little or no inhibitory effects to H-PGDS. Subsequent inhibition assays demonstrated that EMy-1 (IC50 = 47.8 ± 1.06 µM) and EMy-5 (IC50 = 3.7 ± 1.1 µM) exhibited dose-dependent inhibitory effects on H-PGDS (Figure 5(B)). Notably, EMy-5 displayed a significantly lower IC50 value (3.7 µM; Figure 5(B)) in comparison to the reference inhibitor HQL-79 (IC50 = 53.2 µM), underscoring its superior inhibitory efficacy. Further affinity analysis via LSPR demonstrated that EMy-5 binds to H-PGDS with a dissociation constant (KD) of 3.2 ± 0.74 µM (Figure 6).

To gain insight into the binding dynamics of H-PGDS with the inhibitors EMx, EMw, EMy-1 and EMy-5, we performed 100-ns MD simulations to assess the stability of the complexes in terms of the key metrics RMSD, RMSF, and Rg (Figure 8), using H-PGDS alone as a control. The average RMSD for H-PGDS alone was 0.98 Å, reflecting its inherent structural stability. In comparison, the complexes H-PGDS-EMy-5 (1.27 Å) and H-PGDS-EMy-1 (1.55 Å) exhibited slightly higher RMSD values (1.27 Å) (Figure 8(B)), suggesting that these inhibitors induce mild destabilisation without significantly disrupting the overall protein structure. Conversely, the H-PGDS-EMx and H-PGDS-EMw complexes demonstrated markedly higher RMSD values (1.95 Å and 1.81 Å, respectively), indicating more pronounced conformational deviations and potential destabilisation effects. Additionally, the RMSF analysis provided further insight into residue-level flexibility. The RMSF profiles for H-PGDS-EMy-1 and H-PGDS-EMy-5 (Figure 8(C)) were similar to that of the unbound H-PGDS, indicating minimal impact on local dynamics. However, the RMSF patterns for H-PGDS-EMx and H-PGDS-EMw exhibited significant deviations (Figure 8(C)). Rg analysis, a measure of structural compactness, revealed that H-PGDS alone maintained stable Rg values ranging from 16.87 to 17.03 Å (Figure 8(D)), confirming its compact structure during the simulation. Likewise, the H-PGDS-EMy-1 and H-PGDS-EMy-5 complexes showed minor Rg fluctuations (16.88–17.12 Å and 16.91–17.11 Å, respectively), suggesting that these inhibitors preserved the compactness of H-PGDS. In contrast, H-PGDS-EMx and H-PGDS-EMw exhibited broader Rg ranges (up to 17.24 Å) (Figure 8(D)), consistent with their destabilising effects and increased structural fluctuations. Collectively, these findings highlight the potential of EMy-5 as a potent inhibitor that maintain the structural stability and dynamics of H-PGDS, making it strong candidate for further therapeutic development.

Further non-bond interaction analysis revealed the detailed atomic interactions of inhibitors to H-PGDS. At a simulation time point of 100 ns, the molecular interaction profiles of the complexes formed by EMw, EMx, EMy-1, and EMy-5 demonstrate a coordinated mechanism of stabilisation through π-π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions (Figure 9 and Figure S2). Across all four complexes, the inhibitors engage in π-π stacking interactions with the aromatic residue W104, which plays a pivotal role in aligning the aromatic rings of inhibitors and stabilising its position within the binding pocket of H-PGDS. This interaction not only facilitates proper orientation but also strengthens the overall H-PGDS-inhibitor interaction. Hydrogen bonding significantly contributes to the affinity of inhibitor for the binding site. In EMw, a hydrogen bond with residue R14 was observed, reinforcing the binding stability (Figure S2). In EMx, the same hydrogen bond was formed with R14 may further enhance the affinity of inhibitor (Figure S2). Importantly, EMy-5 formed a hydrogen bond with Y152, suggesting an enhanced specificity and a more refined binding mode (Figure 9). This interaction may contribute to greater stability and selectivity within the active site of H-PGDS, leading to the most potent inhibition of its activity. Hydrophobic interactions, consistently observed across all four complexes, also play a crucial role in stabilising the ligands. Residues such as M11, M99, I155, C156, and L199 form hydrophobic contacts with the inhibitors, minimising solvent exposure and enhancing the overall stability of the complex. These interactions contribute to the exclusion of water molecules from the binding pocket, fostering the formation of a more rigid and stable H-PGDS-inhibitor complex. In addition, the detailed molecular interactions of EMw, EMx, EMy-1, and EMy-5 highlight the importance of π-π stacking, hydrogen bonding, and hydrophobic interactions in stabilising the ligands within the binding pocket of H-PGDS. Notably, EMx, EMw, EMy-1, and EMy-5 all interact with key amino acid residues R14, M99, and W104, consistent with findings from previous studies on known inhibitors, such as HQL-79.29 The hydrogen bond observed in EMy-5 indicated potential improvements in binding specificity and affinity, offering insights into their therapeutic potential as small-molecule inhibitors or modulators of H-PGDS activity. These findings suggest that EMy-5, with its enhanced specificity and refined binding mode, could represent the most effective inhibitor among these identified inhibitors.

DMD is a progressive genetic disorder marked by chronic muscle degeneration and inflammation, where PGD2 plays a pivotal role in exacerbating disease pathology. In DMD patients, elevated PGD2 levels have been detected in skeletal muscles and inflammatory cells during the early stages of the disease, a period characterised by extensive muscle necrosis and inflammation.84,85 The pathological effects of PGD2 include the amplification of inflammatory responses, impairment of muscle regeneration, and promotion of fibrosis, all of which accelerate disease progression.85,86 The detrimental role of PGD2 has been corroborated in mdx mice, an established preclinical model for DMD, which closely mimics the acute pathological phase observed in humans. Elevated PGD2 levels in mdx mice have been linked to significant muscle damage, necrosis, and chronic inflammation, particularly in hindlimb muscles such as the gastrocnemius and tibialis anterior during the early phases of muscular dystrophy.85–87 Studies have shown that selective pharmacological inhibition of H-PGDS effectively reduces PGD2 production, leading to marked decreases in muscle necrosis and inflammation. For instance, treatment with HQL-79, a known H-PGDS inhibitor, has demonstrated significant efficacy in reducing PGD2 levels, alleviating inflammation, and mitigating muscle damage in mdx mice.84–86 While HQL-79 has shown promise in preclinical studies, its limited potency restricts its utility in addressing the severe inflammation and muscle damage characteristic of DMD. In our study, we demonstrated that EMy-5 significantly inhibited the activity of H-PGDS (IC50 = 3.7 ± 1.1 µM) outperforming than that of HQL-79 (IC50 = 53.2 µM) (Figure 5(B)). EMy-5 significantly reduced PGD2 levels in KU812 cells in a dose-dependent manner (Figure 7(C)), highlighting its ability to mitigate inflammation. The observed dose-dependent reduction in PGD2-MOX levels upon EMy-5 treatment supports the hypothesis that EMy-5 directly inhibits H-PGDS. The near-complete suppression at 100 μM indicates potent enzymatic inhibition, consistent with its potential as a therapeutic candidate targeting PGD2-related inflammatory pathways. Moreover, the cytotoxicity experiments showed that EMy-5 has no apparent cytotoxicity towards HEK293T cells (Figure 7(A)), consistent with the reports that EMy-5 exhibited no cytotoxicity in the L5178Y TK mouse lymphoma cell line, lacked mutagenic activity, and showed an oral LD50 greater than 2000 mg/kg in rats.88,89 While KU812 basophilic cells are not directly involved in the pathogenesis of Duchenne muscular dystrophy (DMD), they were selected in this study due to their high endogenous expression of H-PGDS, enabling sensitive and reliable quantification of PGD2 production in response to pharmacological inhibition. This cell line has been widely used in prior studies validating H-PGDS inhibitors. Although KU812 cells are classically associated with allergic inflammation rather than muscular dystrophy, their utility as a functional assay system for PGD2 biosynthesis remains well established. Notably, our cytotoxicity assays revealed no significant toxic effects in KU812 cells at the concentrations tested, further supporting the compound’s preliminary safety profile in haematopoietic cells (Figure 7(B)). HEK293T cells were used in parallel cytotoxicity assessments (Figure 7(A)) due to their human origin, high transfectability, and widespread use in early-stage drug screening for general toxicity evaluation. However, we acknowledge that these cell lines do not recapitulate the pathological environment of DMD. To strengthen the translational relevance of our findings, future studies will incorporate more disease-relevant models, such as C2C12 myoblasts or RAW264.7 macrophages, to evaluate both efficacy and toxicity in contexts that better reflect the inflammatory and degenerative features of DMD. Together, these results emphasise the potent inhibitory capacity EMy-5, promising pharmacokinetic characteristics, and robust binding affinity to H-PGDS, establishing it as a highly viable candidate for further optimisation and development.

Of note, humans are exposed to EMy-5 following the oral administration of berberine, which is metabolised by gut microbiota-derived nitroreductases into its more absorbable form, dihydroberberine.90,91 Berberine, a naturally occurring benzylisoquinoline alkaloid, has been a cornerstone of traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) for centuries.92 Berberine is primarily sourced from the roots, rhizomes, and bark of plants in the Ranunculaceae,93 Rutaceae,94 and Berberidaceae95 families, including medicinal plants such as barberry, goldthread, goldenseal, and tree turmeric.96 Widely recognised for its therapeutic potential, berberine has been extensively used in herbal medicine, particularly for gastrointestinal infections,97 as well as for its hypoglycaemic and lipid-lowering effects, which have shown promise in managing metabolic disorders like diabetes and obesity.98–101 Additionally, berberine has demonstrated broad therapeutic properties, including antimicrobial, neuroprotective, anticancer, and anti-inflammatory effects.96,102–104 Despite its diverse applications, the clinical use of berberine is significantly limited by its low bioavailability due to extensive first-pass metabolism in the gut.100,101 To address this limitation, EMy-5 has emerged as a promising alternative with an enhanced pharmacokinetic profile. Studies indicate that EMy-5 exhibits superior oral absorption efficiency and improved bioavailability compared to berberine.105

EMy-5 was identified through a structure-based pharmacophore screening strategy that specifically targets the active site of H-PGDS, highlighting its role as a selective H-PGDS inhibitor. In our study, EMy-5 demonstrated significantly greater inhibitory efficacy against H-PGDS than the reference inhibitor HQL-79 (IC50 = 53.2 µM). EMy-5 also exhibits superior bioavailability compared to its precursor, berberine, due to enhanced oral absorption. Derived from natural sources such as foods, plants, and herbs, dihydroberberine’s favourable safety profile further supports its therapeutic potential. Notably, the catalytic site of H-PGDS is structurally distinct from those of mPGES-1 (involved in PGE2 biosynthesis) and 5-LOX/LTA4H (key enzymes in the LTB4 pathway), suggesting a low likelihood of off-target effects. Although our current study did not directly evaluate the impact of EMy-5 on other eicosanoids like PGE2 or LTB4, literature on structurally related H-PGDS inhibitors supports their selectivity. For example, HQL-79 and TAS-204 potently suppress PGD2 synthesis with minimal inhibition of COX-1/2, mPGES-1, and 5-LOX, indicating negligible interference with alternative prostanoid or leukotriene pathways.29,106 Furthermore, prior studies on berberine and its analogs suggest modulation of inflammatory signalling without direct enzymatic inhibition of COX or LOX isoforms.93,107,108 Given the structural similarity of EMy-5, a comparable selectivity profile is anticipated; however, direct experimental validation is necessary to confirm this prediction. Collectively, these findings support the hypothesis that EMy-5 exerts its anti-inflammatory activity primarily through selective inhibition of the PGD2–CRTH2 signalling axis, with minimal off-target effects, underscoring its promise as a candidate for further pharmacological investigation and clinical development.

In addition to our primary focus on H-PGDS inhibition by EMy-5, it is important to consider the broader anti-inflammatory potential of this compound within interconnected signalling networks. H-PGDS catalyses the production of prostaglandin D2 (PGD2), a lipid mediator known to promote allergic and inflammatory responses.109,110 PGD2 signals through DP1 and DP2 (CRTH2) receptors, which can activate downstream pathways including NF-κB, a central transcription factor regulating inflammatory gene expression.111 Thus, inhibition of H-PGDS by EMy-5 may indirectly suppress NF-κB activation and subsequent pro-inflammatory cytokine production. Although this study did not experimentally assess direct effects of EMy-5 on key components of the NF-κB pathway such as IKKβ phosphorylation or p65 nuclear translocation, previous literature has reported that berberine and related derivatives can modulate NF-κB signalling by interfering with upstream kinases and receptor-mediated pathways.112–114 These findings suggest that EMy-5 might exert multifaceted anti-inflammatory actions beyond H-PGDS inhibition. In addition to H-PGDS and NF-κB-related kinases such as IKKβ, enzymes like MAPKs (e.g. p38, ERK1/2), COX-2, and upstream mediators such as TLR4-associated kinases may also serve as relevant inflammatory targets influenced by berberine derivatives, and deserve future systematic evaluation.115–118 The integration of these pathways highlights a complex inflammatory network wherein targeting H-PGDS may have amplified therapeutic benefits by modulating not only PGD2 production but also related signalling axes such as NF-κB and MAPK pathways. Future investigations are warranted to elucidate whether EMy-5 directly affects NF-κB signalling components, including IKKβ activity, p65 nuclear translocation, and downstream cytokine expression in relevant immune or inflammatory cells. Such studies will further clarify the comprehensive anti-inflammatory mechanism of EMy-5 and support its development as a promising therapeutic agent targeting multiple facets of inflammation.

Berberine derivatives have been extensively investigated for their anti-inflammatory properties in a variety of diseases, including rheumatoid arthritis (RA), inflammatory bowel disease, and metabolic syndrome. Previous studies have demonstrated that berberine exerts its anti-inflammatory effects primarily through the modulation of key signalling pathways such as NF-κB, MAPK, and JAK/STAT, leading to reduced expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines like TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β.118 For instance, berberine treatment significantly ameliorated symptoms in animal models of RA by suppressing synovial inflammation, cartilage degradation, and joint destruction.119,120 However, despite promising preclinical data, the clinical translation of berberine has been hampered by its poor oral bioavailability, rapid metabolism, and low plasma concentrations.121,122 These pharmacokinetic challenges have driven research into more bioavailable derivatives and metabolites, including dihydroberberine, which demonstrate enhanced absorption and stability.90,123,124 Our findings on EMy-5 expand this body of work by demonstrating potent and selective inhibition of H-PGDS, a novel enzymatic target implicated in prostaglandin D2-mediated inflammatory pathways. This distinct mechanism complements the broader anti-inflammatory signalling modulation previously attributed to berberine and its analogs, suggesting a multifaceted therapeutic potential. Compared with other known H-PGDS inhibitors developed from synthetic small-molecule scaffolds – such as TFC-007, HQL-79, and F092 – EMy-5 demonstrates a more favourable overall pharmacological profile. TFC-007, a benzoxazolone derivative, exhibits potent inhibition of H-PGDS, but its clinical application is limited by insufficient data on oral bioavailability and in vivo metabolic stability.125 HQL-79, one of the earliest orally available H-PGDS inhibitors, shows relatively weak inhibitory activity and suboptimal selectivity, with known off-target effects on related prostaglandin synthases such as L-PGDS.29 F092, a more recent compound with excellent binding affinity, offers improved selectivity but suffers from poor aqueous solubility and pharmacokinetic limitations in vivo.126 In contrast, EMy-5 offers a balanced pharmacological profile: moderate H-PGDS inhibition (IC50 = 3.7 μM), enhanced oral bioavailability, metabolic stability, and low systemic toxicity. These benefits are likely attributed to its EMy-5 scaffold, which leverages gut microbiota-mediated conversion mechanisms to improve absorption and systemic exposure. Such characteristics distinguish EMy-5 from both first-generation H-PGDS inhibitors and conventional anti-inflammatory agents. Further comparisons with existing synthetic inhibitors highlight the translational potential of EMy-5. While many H-PGDS inhibitors display excellent enzymatic inhibition in vitro, their therapeutic advancement is often impeded by poor pharmacokinetic properties or safety concerns. EMy-5, however, overcomes these barriers through its natural product-derived origin, structural simplicity, and favourable ADMET (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, and toxicity) profile. Functionally, EMy-5 significantly reduces PGD2 production in KU812 cells in a dose-dependent manner, affirming its biological relevance. Unlike previous studies on berberine derivatives that focused on general anti-inflammatory activity via canonical pathways (e.g., NF-κB, MAPKs), this study emphasises the importance of targeting upstream enzymatic mediators such as H-PGDS to modulate disease-specific inflammatory processes more precisely. Collectively, these findings underscore the potential of EMy-5 as a next-generation anti-inflammatory agent. Its integration of enzymatic specificity, natural product pharmacology, and drug-like properties addresses critical limitations of current therapies for inflammatory conditions such as rheumatoid arthritis, asthma, atopic dermatitis, and other PGD2-associated diseases. Further in vivo investigations and translational studies are warranted to validate its efficacy and therapeutic applicability.

To strengthen the interpretive framework of our findings, it is essential to clarify the aetiology and pathogenesis of the inflammatory diseases under investigation, particularly rheumatoid arthritis (RA) and related autoimmune conditions. RA is a systemic autoimmune disorder characterised by chronic synovial inflammation and progressive joint destruction, triggered by complex interactions among genetic predisposition, environmental factors, and dysregulated immune responses.127–129 Central to its pathogenesis is the aberrant activation of immune cells, leading to the sustained production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-6, and IL-1β, which perpetuate synovitis and tissue damage. At the molecular level, several key signalling cascades have been implicated in the development and maintenance of inflammation. The NF-κB, MAPK, and JAK/STAT pathways are prominently involved in transducing extracellular inflammatory cues into transcriptional responses that drive cytokine production, immune cell recruitment, and tissue remodeling.130–132 The dysregulation of these pathways not only amplifies the inflammatory milieu but also sustains chronic disease progression. Berberine and its derivatives have demonstrated broad-spectrum anti-inflammatory effects by targeting these same signalling pathways, suppressing NF-κB activation, reducing phosphorylation of MAPKs, and inhibiting JAK/STAT signalling, thereby decreasing the expression of downstream inflammatory mediators.93,118,133 These mechanistic insights provide a solid foundation for interpreting the anti-inflammatory effects observed in our study. In summary, our findings support EMy-5 as a potent, selective, and bioavailable H-PGDS inhibitor with significant translational potential. Its integration of rational drug design, validated biological efficacy, and favourable pharmacological properties positions it as a strong candidate for further preclinical development targeting PGD2-associated inflammatory disorders.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our investigation utilised a pharmacophore-based methodology, complemented by biochemical and biophysical analyses, as well as cell-based assay to identify natural product inhibitors targeting H-PGDS. The developed pharmacophore model, Phar-A2HR2, effectively identified EMy as a potential inhibitor. Importantly, EMy-5, the structural analog of EMy, demonstrated superior inhibitory potency (IC50 = 3.7 µM) compared to the known inhibitor HQL-79, along with strong binding affinity (KD = 3.2 µM) confirmed via LSPR analysis. Molecular docking and dynamic simulations revealed that EMy-5 interacted with critical residues within the H-PGDS active site through hydrogen bonding, π–π stacking, and hydrophobic interactions, forming a stable and thermodynamically favourable protein–ligand complex. Furthermore, EMy-5 effectively suppressed PGD2 production in mammalian cells without exhibiting cytotoxicity, highlighting its therapeutic potential and safety profile. These findings underscore EMy-5 as a promising candidate for the development of novel anti-inflammatory and anti-allergic agents targeting the PGD2 pathway, warranting further preclinical and clinical evaluations.
