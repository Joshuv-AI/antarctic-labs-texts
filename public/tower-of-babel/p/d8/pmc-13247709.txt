Data of short-term thermal energy storage strategy with medium-deep borehole heat exchanger
Authors: Jun Liu, Ruifeng Wang, Sheng Zhang, Zhaosong Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis dataset presents the heating performance of an innovative short-term thermal energy storage strategy with the Medium-Deep Borehole Heat Exchanger (MDBHE). The existing thermal energy storage strategy is long-term, which charges the thermal energy into the underground during the non-heating season and discharges the thermal energy during the heating season via the MDBHE. The long-term thermal energy storage suffers from a low energy storage efficiency due to the cross-season heat dissipation from the energy-storage zone to the surrounding soil. The proposed short-term thermal energy storage strategy extends the thermal energy storage resource of the MDBHE from the non-heating season to the heating season, which alternatively switches the energy-charging and energy-discharging modes during the heating season. Since the thermal interaction time between the energy-storage zone and the surrounding soil is limited to the switching period (i.e., one day in the demonstration scenarios with different charging temperatures and charging-discharging ratios), the proposed strategy achieves high charging and discharging efficiencies, leading to high overall energy-storage efficiency and large heating capacity. This study presents data on the heating performance of the proposed strategy, including the soil temperature, inlet and outlet temperatures, heating capacity, and energy storage efficiency under different scenarios, as well as the comparative results with the traditional intermittent operation of the MDBHE without thermal energy storage and the existing long-term thermal energy storage of the MDBHE.Keywords: Geothermal energy, Medium-deep borehole heat exchanger, Short-term thermal energy storage, Charging efficiency, Discharging efficiency

Background

Geothermal energy utilization using Medium-Deep Borehole Heat Exchanger (MDBHE) represents a highly promising low-carbon solution for building heating. However, the continuous discharge of thermal energy leads to progressive thermal attenuation, thereby diminishing the heating performance of MDBHE [1,2]. Although intermittent operation [3] and long-term thermal energy storage [4] can partially mitigate this thermal attenuation, both approaches have certain limitations. In intermittent operation, the MDBHE stops operating during the non-heating season, and the surrounding soil temperature recovers through terrestrial heat flux. During the heating season, the MDBHE alternates between running and stopping modes (i.e., 12 h of running followed by 12 h of stopping per day in this study, according to Huan et al. [5]). During the running mode, the MDBHE discharges thermal energy, causing the surrounding soil temperature to decrease. During the stopping mode, the surrounding soil temperature recovers solely through terrestrial heat flux. Since the terrestrial heat flux is small, it requires a sufficiently long time for the terrestrial heat flux to replenish the geothermal energy discharged by the MDBHE [5]. However, because the duration of the stopping mode (e.g., 12 h per day) is relatively short, the soil temperature can only partially recover, resulting in limited mitigation of the thermal attenuation problem of the MDBHE [3]. Under the long-term thermal energy storage, the MDBHE discharges thermal energy during the heating season and continuously charges thermal energy into the underground during the non-heating season. In this way, the geothermal energy supplied by terrestrial heat flux, combined with the additional thermal energy stored during the non-heating season, can balance the energy discharged during the heating season. Therefore, long-term thermal energy storage can mitigate the thermal attenuation problem of the MDBHE. However, most of the long-term stored thermal energy dissipates significantly (e.g., into underground non-storage zones and the ground surface environment), resulting in low overall energy-storage efficiency [4].

This paper proposes a novel short-term thermal energy storage strategy based on the alternate switching of energy-charging and energy-discharging modes. Under this strategy, the MDBHE ceases operation during the non-heating season, and the surrounding soil temperature recovers through terrestrial heat flux. During the heating season, the MDBHE operates with short-time-interval switching between energy-charging and energy-discharging modes to reduce the heat exchange area between the energy-storage region and the surrounding soil, thereby reducing thermal energy dissipation. This strategy can overcome the drawbacks of intermittent operation and long-term thermal energy storage, and can effectively mitigate the thermal attenuation problem of the MDBHE, improving its heating capacity and overall energy-storage efficiency. Compared with intermittent operation, the proposed short-term thermal energy storage strategy actively stores thermal energy into the underground during the energy-charging mode, thereby accelerating soil temperature recovery. Compared with long-term thermal energy storage, the proposed short-term thermal energy storage strategy has a shorter charging duration, resulting in a smaller heat exchange area between the energy-storage zone and the surrounding soil, thereby reducing thermal energy dissipation and achieving higher overall energy-storage efficiency. Accordingly, we systematically compare the MDBHE heating performance under the proposed strategy with that under intermittent operation and long-term thermal energy storage. The resulting dataset covers multiple operating conditions (e.g., different charging temperatures and charging–discharging duration ratios) and provides a reproducible benchmark for evaluating MDBHE performance under alternative operating strategies.

Data Description

The dataset is organized into six main files:

1. Boundary conditions.xlsx

This file contains the fundamental parameters of the MDBHE model, including (i) structural parameters (e.g., pipe diameter and wall thickness), (ii) thermal properties (e.g., thermal conductivity and specific heat capacity), and (iii) depth-dependent thermo-physical properties of the surrounding formation. It also provides the detailed configurations of all operating conditions and simulation settings. It provides a comprehensive overview of the model's specifications and the different scenarios analyzed throughout the study. The short-term thermal energy storage strategy configuration includes two charging temperatures (40 °C and 70 °C) and seven varying charging-discharging duration ratios (6:18, 8:16, 10:14, 12:12, 14:10, 16:8, and 18:6). Specifically, the charging-discharging duration ratio refers to the proportion of time spent in the energy-charging mode relative to the energy-discharging mode within each charge–discharge cycle under the short-term thermal energy storage strategy. For example, a ratio of 6:18 indicates that the MDBHE operates in the energy-charging mode for 6 h, followed by the energy-discharging mode for 18 h within a 24-hour cycle. These ratios are critical because they determine the thermal recovery and heat discharge dynamics within the near-borehole energy-storage zone during alternating operation. For comparison, intermittent operation scenarios are set with corresponding stop-run ratios. Additionally, a long-term thermal energy storage scenario is established with a charging temperature of 70 °C.

2. Circulation fluid inlet and out temperature data.xlsx

This file contains two worksheets, providing a comprehensive overview of the inlet and out temperatures of the MDBHE under various operational conditions in both energy-discharging and energy-charging modes. The ``Energy-discharging mode'' worksheet presents variations in inlet and outlet temperatures for eight short-term thermal energy storage scenarios, seven intermittent operation scenarios, and one long-term thermal energy storage scenario in the energy-discharging mode. The ``Energy-charging mode'' worksheet offers similar data, including variations in inlet and outlet temperatures for the same eight short-term thermal energy storage scenarios and one long-term thermal energy storage scenario in the energy-charging mode.

3. Soil and circulation fluid temperature distribution data.xlsx

This file contains five worksheets. In these worksheets, the X, Y, and Z coordinates define a three-dimensional system, with the MDBHE positioned at the origin and depth along the Z-axis.

The ``Soil temperature distribution-1” worksheet presents variations in the soil temperature distribution around the MDBHE during the energy-charging modes under the short-term thermal energy storage strategy. This worksheet includes eight distinct scenarios, each corresponding to different charging temperatures and charging-discharging ratios.

The ``Soil temperature distribution-2” worksheet presents changes in the soil temperature surrounding the MDBHE during an 8-month thermal energy storage phase under the long-term thermal energy storage.

The ``Water temperature-1” worksheet presents variations in the internal circulating fluid temperature distribution within the MDBHE during the energy-charging mode under the short-term thermal energy storage strategy. This worksheet includes the same scenarios as the ``Soil temperature distribution-1'' worksheet.

The ``Water temperature-2” worksheet presents variations in the internal circulating fluid temperature distribution for the long-term thermal energy storage strategy, illustrating the changes in circulating fluid temperature within the MDBHE during the 8-month energy storage phase.

Finally, the ``Thermophysical parameters'' worksheet lists the thermophysical properties of both the soil and circulating fluid, providing essential data for calculating thermal energy changes within the soil and circulating fluid.

4. Heat extraction and storage capacity.xlsx

This file contains two worksheets, offering a comprehensive overview of the heat extraction and heat storage performance of the MDBHE under various operational conditions in both energy-discharging and energy-charging modes. The ``Energy-discharging mode'' worksheet presents variations in heat extraction capacity for the same scenarios as those in the ``Circulation fluid inlet and outlet temperature data.xlsx''. The ``Energy-charging mode'' worksheet provides similar data, including variations in heat storage capacity, for the same scenarios as those in the ``Circulation fluid inlet and outlet temperature data.xlsx''.

5. Efficiency.xlsx

This file contains two worksheets, each detailing the variations in thermal energy storage efficiency of the MDBHE under short-term and long-term thermal energy storage conditions.

The ``Short-term thermal storage'' worksheet presents the changes in energy storage efficiency across eight distinct short-term thermal energy storage scenarios. These scenarios, based on different charging temperatures and charging-discharging ratios, provide a comprehensive overview of short-term thermal energy storage efficiency under varying operational conditions.

The ``Long-term thermal storage'' worksheet provides an analysis of thermal energy storage efficiency under long-term thermal energy storage, offering a detailed evaluation of the MDBHE's performance over extended periods.

6. Case folder

The Case folder contains the complete model files and input configurations for each simulation scenario. For convenience, each subfolder corresponds to one case, including the proposed short-term thermal energy storage strategy, intermittent operation, and long-term thermal energy storage operation. Readers can directly run the provided case files in OpenGeoSys to reproduce the simulation results, and can readily modify the input parameters to conduct additional parametric studies.

Experimental Design, Materials and Methods

A three-dimensional numerical model was developed using the open-source simulation software OpenGeoSys (version 6.3.2). The governing equations in the model are based on the Navier-Stokes framework, incorporating the principles of mass, momentum, and energy conservation. The simulation domain was defined with the following boundary conditions: (i) a Dirichlet condition was applied at the top boundary; (ii) the lateral boundary was positioned sufficiently far from the MDBHE to prevent thermal interference and was treated as adiabatic, using a Neumann (no-flux) condition [6]; (iii) a Neumann boundary condition representing terrestrial heat flux was applied to the bottom boundary. The detailed parameters of the established MDBHE model can be found in the file “Boundary conditions.xlsx”. This file includes the basic geometric parameters of the MDBHE (such as borehole depth and pipe diameter), as well as the thermophysical properties of the subsurface media and their variation with depth.

The short-term thermal energy storage strategy for the MDBHE operates in alternating cycles of energy-charging and energy-discharging modes. During the energy-charging mode, which typically occurs under relatively low heating load conditions (e.g., daytime in residential buildings [7]), low-grade thermal energy is stored underground through the circulating fluid (Fig. 1a), which increases heating capacity during the subsequent energy-discharging mode. In the energy-discharging mode, which generally corresponds to higher heating load conditions (e.g., nighttime in residential buildings [7]), geothermal energy is discharged from the surrounding soil to the building via the circulating fluid (Fig. 1b). As thermal energy is discharged, the surrounding soil temperature decreases, thereby increasing the temperature difference between the circulating fluid and the surrounding soil in the next energy-charging mode, further improving the charging efficiency during the subsequent energy-charging mode. The relatively short duration of the energy-charging mode minimizes thermal interaction between the energy-storage zone and the outer soil layers, allowing the stored thermal energy to be effectively utilized during the energy-discharging mode. This process contributes to high overall energy-storage efficiency (Eq. (3)) and ensures a stable heating performance of the MDBHE.

In this study, a series of simulation cases was designed to systematically evaluate the thermal performance of the short-term thermal energy storage strategy. In this strategy, the MDBHE alternates between energy-charging and energy-discharging modes throughout the heating season. The building geothermal system comprises an aboveground subsystem (e.g., heat pump and water storage tank [8]) to meet heating load demands and an underground subsystem (i.e., MDBHE) supplying geothermal energy. In previous studies focusing on the underground subsystem, the MDBHE's inlet temperature is typically fixed (e.g., 15 °C [9]) to examine the effects of boundary conditions on performance. Accordingly, in the energy-discharging mode, the MDBHE's inlet temperature is fixed at 15 °C. In the energy-charging mode, two charging temperatures (40 °C and 70 °C [3]) were adopted to investigate their influence on heating performance, and seven charging–discharging duration ratios (6:18, 8:16, 10:14, 12:12, 14:10, 16:8, and 18:6 [10]) were considered to examine the effects of charge–discharge timing. For comparison, corresponding intermittent operation cases were simulated using the same run–stop duration ratios. Finally, the thermal performance of the short-term strategy was compared with that of long-term thermal energy storage, in which thermal energy is continuously charged into the ground at 70 °C for eight months during the non-heating season, followed by alternating 12-hour energy-discharging and 12-hour idle cycles during the heating season.

The data presented demonstrate the heating performance of the MDBHE under varying charging temperatures and charging-discharging ratios within the short-term thermal energy storage strategy, alongside a comparison with its heating performance under intermittent operation and long-term thermal energy storage conditions.

The MDBHE model developed using OpenGeoSys has been previously validated through analytical solutions, numerical benchmarks, and field experiments [5,6,11]. In this study, the numerical model validation was performed using experimental data published by Cai et al. [12], as shown in Fig. 2. The comparison indicates a maximum relative deviation of 3.2% between the simulated and measured outlet temperatures, confirming the high accuracy of the MDBHE model.
