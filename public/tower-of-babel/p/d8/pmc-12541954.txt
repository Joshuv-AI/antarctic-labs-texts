Development and validation of a predictive model for postoperative pulmonary complications after colorectal cancer surgery: a retrospective study
Authors: Yaqi Huang, Yuling Zhong, Wenlin Wei, Ning Zheng, Nan Lin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPostoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) significantly impact patient outcomes after colorectal cancer (CRC) surgery. Studies on PPCs associated with CRC remain limited. This study was designed to create and verify a risk prediction model through the identification of factors associated with a higher risk of PPCs after CRC surgery.MethodsPatients who underwent CRC surgery at Fujian Medical University Union Hospital from September 2019 through September 2021 were included in this study. They were divided into training and validation groups and categorized into the PPC group and non-PPC group. We used multivariable logistic regression analysis to determine independent predictors of PPCs. A nomogram was constructed using the identified risk factors and later validated via receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves, calibration curves, and decision curve analysis to assess its predictive performance.ResultsA total of 1861 patients were enrolled and allocated to training and testing sets at an 80:20 ratio. The analyzed results revealed that surgical site, surgical approach, duration of surgery, age, perioperative blood transfusion, asthma, and fasting plasma glucose were risk factors for PPCs in patients with CRC. The receiver operating characteristic curve areas under the curve (AUCs) for the training and validation sets were 0.734 and 0.732, respectively. We found that the model calibration curve showed favorable consistency, whereas decision curve analysis (DCA) revealed a substantial expected net benefit.ConclusionsThe model demonstrates high predictive accuracy and efficiency for PPCs in patients undergoing CRC surgery.Trial registrationThis study was registered at the Chinese Clinical Trials Registry (http://www.chictr.org.cn) on November 28, 2024, with registration number ChiCTR2400093107.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12957-025-04045-0.

Introduction

Postoperative pulmonary complications (PPCs) represent significant adverse events in patients after colorectal cancer (CRC) surgery [1–3]. These complications—including pneumonia, respiratory failure, and atelectasis—are linked to prolonged inpatient stays and an elevated risk of postoperative death [4, 5]. PPCs that manifest within 30 days postoperatively are not influenced by the preoperative risk statuses of patients and are associated with decreased long-term survival rates [6]. Therefore, addressing the challenges posed by PPCs is critical for improving postoperative recovery and long-term survival among patients with CRC [7].

Previous studies have reported multiple preoperative risk factors for the development of PPCs, including a smoking history, pre-existing lung diseases, advanced age, and prolonged anesthesia duration [8–10]. Various intraoperative and postoperative factors—such as anesthesia techniques, perioperative blood transfusions, surgical procedures, ventilation strategies, respiratory and activity interventions conducted both preoperatively and postoperatively, and use of vasoactive drugs—can further increase the risk of developing PPCs [11–13].

Studies on PPCs following CRC surgery are relatively limited. Most studies have focused on identifying risk factors or investigating specific contributors to PPCs. However, a predictive model for PPCs is needed. Most reports on predictive models have focused on minimally invasive colorectal surgeries [14]. To our knowledge, no high-quality studies have investigated PPCs across various types of CRC surgeries or developed nomograms to predict PPCs in these populations. Analyzing the factors connected with PPCs in different types of patients with CRC and establishing predictive models would provide substantial clinical value and assist in the development of effective clinical interventions targeted at reducing their incidence in this patient population. The objective of this study was to identify the risk factors for PPCs in patients with CRC by analyzing data from a three-year clinical database and to design a nomogram for the prediction of these complications.

Materials and methods

Patients

This research enrolled 1861 patients who underwent elective curative surgery for colorectal cancer at the Colorectal Surgery Department of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital between September 2019 and September 2021. The criteria for inclusion were as follows: (a) patients who underwent elective curative resection for colorectal cancer with general anesthesia between September 1, 2019, and September 30, 2021; and (b) complete clinical pathological data. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (a) infectious diseases (systemic inflammatory response, sepsis, septic shock, etc.); (b) patients who were preoperatively admitted to the intensive care unit; (c) benign tumors, non-curative surgeries, or presence of distant metastases; and (d) insufficient medical records to determine whether the patient experienced PPCs.

Data collection

Clinical data were collected using the electronic medical record system of Fujian Medical University Union Hospital. The demographic characteristics included age, gender, height, weight, and body mass index (BMI). Medical history included disorders of the digestive system (gastrointestinal ulcers, diabetes), cardiovascular system (NYHA classification, hypertension, ischemic heart disease, cardiomyopathy, coronary heart disease, atrial fibrillation, heart failure, defibrillator implantation, previous valve repair, pacemaker implantation, PCI, CABG, history of cerebrovascular disease, and angiography within 1 week prior to surgery), respiratory system (asthma, COPD, bronchiectasis, emphysema, and chronic pneumonia), others (preoperative blood transfusion, history of malignancy, previous abdominal surgery, recent smoking within 1 year, and alcohol abuse), and preoperative medication use (diuretics, cardiac glycosides, ACE inhibitors/ARBs, CCBs, and beta-blockers).

The preoperative routine laboratory blood test data included biochemical indicators (globulin, albumin, hyperlipidemia) and complete blood count indicators (hemoglobin, red blood cell count, platelet count, platelet volume, white blood cells, absolute neutrophil count, absolute lymphocyte count, absolute monocyte count, and lymphocyte-to-monocyte ratio). The reference values for all serological indicators in this study are based on the standards used by the laboratory of the hospital.

The intraoperative variables included the American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification, surgical approach (open surgery, laparoscopic, or conversion from laparoscopic to open), surgical site (rectum or colon), duration of surgery, estimated blood loss, urine output, fluid management (crystalloid and colloid volumes), intraoperative medications (diuretics, dexmedetomidine, beta-blockers, anticholinergics, neuromuscular blockers, vasopressors, antihypertensives, hormones), and volume of red blood cells transfused during surgery.

Outcome and definition of PPCs

The primary outcome was the incidence of PPCs during hospitalization after radical surgery for colorectal cancer. PPCs refer to the composite incidence of postoperative lung complications. A positive definition is applied when any postoperative complication occurs. The complications include pneumonia, bronchospasm, atelectasis, pulmonary congestion, respiratory failure, pleural effusion, pneumothorax, and pulmonary embolism. The diagnoses for atelectasis, pleural effusion, and pneumothorax were established using chest computed tomography (CT) or X-rays and evaluated by reviewers designated by a study team operating under blinded conditions. Supplementary Table S1 presents the definitions of various PPCs.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R version 3.6.3. Continuous variables underwent normality testing via the Kolmogorov-Smirnov test. Variables with a normal distribution are presented as mean ± standard deviation, and the independent samples t-test was applied to compare groups. The Mann-Whitney U test was utilized for variables showing non-normal distribution. These data are expressed as median with interquartile range: M (P25, P75). Categorical variables are presented as count and percentage, and they were compared using the χ² test.

The original dataset was randomly divided into training and testing sets with an 80:20 ratio. The training set was utilized for model development, while the validation set was reserved for model evaluation. LASSO regression was first conducted on the training dataset to identify relevant variables. These selected variables were employed as independent variables in a multivariable logistic regression analysis to identify independent risk factors linked to PPCs. The identified risk factors were incorporated into a risk model developed via the “rms” package in R to predict PPC probabilities and calculate scores for the relevant variables within the model. The discriminative ability of the nomogram was evaluated using the concordance index and the area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve (AUC). The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test was conducted to assess the overall fit of the model. Decision curve analysis (DCA) was performed to determine the clinical benefits of the risk model by quantifying the net benefit across a range of reasonable risk thresholds. Given that pneumonia is the most clinically significant and prevalent component among PPCs, a sensitivity analysis specifically focused on pneumonia was conducted, including its incidence rate, AUC value, and Hosmer–Lemeshow curve. P-values below 0.05 (two-tailed) denoted statistical significance.

Results

Incidence of PPCs

A total of 2000 patients initially underwent CRC surgery. Based on the exclusion criteria, 139 cases were excluded, including 70 benign tumors, 3 non-radical surgeries, 1 case that had already been admitted to the intensive care unit preoperatively, and 65 cases with missing clinical data. Ultimately, 1861 patients were enrolled in the study. 229 of the 1861 patients experienced PPCs, resulting in an incidence rate of 12.31%. The number of patients included in both the training and test sets fulfilled the study’s criteria [15]. Among the patients with data enrolled in the training set, 180 developed PPCs, yielding an incidence rate of 12.10%. In the validation set, 49 patients (13.14%) developed PPCs.

All clinical data were meticulously collected and organized (Table 1). No significant baseline characteristic differences were observed between the two groups other than fasting plasma glucose, confirming the rationality of the training and testing set division.

Selection of the risk predictors

The original dataset was randomly split into training and testing sets at an 80:20 ratio. LASSO regression was employed to screen predictive variables based on the training data because several variables were included in this study, and correlations between variables were present. Ten-fold cross-validation was applied to determine the penalty term, lambda (λ). The optimal log(λ), estimated at −4.662 (λ = 0.009447197), minimized model error, and 22 variables were chosen for subsequent logistic regression analysis. They included “surgical site,” “surgical approach,” “resection of adjacent organs,” “duration of surgery,” “weight,” “BMI,” “age,” “current smoker,” “preoperative anemia,” “hypoproteinemia,” “perioperative transfusion,” “asthma,” “bronchiectasis,” “chronic pneumonia,” “ASA,” “bleeding volume,” “intraoperative diuretics,” “intraoperative anticholinergic drugs,” “intraoperative glucocorticoid,” “preoperative ACEI,” “preoperative CCB,” and “fasting plasma glucose” (Figs. 1A-B).

Construction of the PPCs prediction nomogram

The 14 independent risk factors derived from multivariate logistic regression analysis were integrated into a predictive model to develop a nomogram, which visualizes the regression analysis results (Fig. 2) and assigns corresponding weights to each factor. The weighted scores of the 14 risk factors were totaled to compute the risk prediction for PPCs after radical CRC surgery. The nomogram applied in this research indicated that the predicted PPCs risk spanned from 1% to 100%.

Internal validation of the nomogram prediction model

The predictive model was designed to evaluate the risk of PPCs in patients undergoing CRC surgery. The areas under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve for the training and testing sets were 0.727 and 0.730, respectively, suggesting strong discriminative ability (Figs. 3A-B). The calibration chart illustrates the correlation between the actual and predicted values, with the blue line representing the actual values. The dashed diagonal line signifies an ideal prediction, while the cross marker denotes the deviation between predicted and observed values. The Hosmer-Lemeshow goodness-of-fit test demonstrated χ² values of 7.357 (p = 0.4987) and 8.7211 (p = 0.3663) for the training and testing sets, respectively. The p-values > 0.05 indicated good fit.

The calibration plots (Figs. 3C-D) demonstrate the agreement between calibration curves of the training and test set models and the corresponding predicted versus observed values. Sensitivity analysis

Among the composite outcomes of PPCs following colorectal surgery, newly-onset pneumonia exhibited the highest incidence and greatest clinical significance. In the entire cohort, the incidence of pneumonia was 10.586% (197/1861 cases), and the AUC value was 0.724. The model showed good calibration, with a non-significant Hosmer-Lemeshow test result (p = 0.331) and a mean absolute error of 0.023. Discussion

CRC is the fourth most commonly diagnosed malignancy worldwide, with a steadily increasing incidence [16, 17]. Surgical resection remains the primary curative treatment [18]. However, PPCs following CRC surgery prolong hospital stays and increase related costs. They also increase patient mortality rates [4, 7]. Early studies show that the incidence of PPCs after CRC surgery varies between 5% and 20%, with most indicating an approximate rate of 10% [19–21]. The data of the 1861 patients involved in this single-center retrospective case-control study revealed a PPC incidence of 12.3%, which is consistent with the rates reported in previous literature.

Previous studies on PPCs following CRC surgery have primarily focused on identifying risk factors using univariate and multivariate analyses. In contrast, our research identified PPC risk factors and developed a predictive model. This model may assist clinicians in formulating personalized treatment plans and intervention strategies to improve patient prognosis. First, we selected the probable risk factors related to PPCs based on existing literature and our rich clinical experience. LASSO regression was employed for the initial variable screening. Following this, we conducted a multivariable stepwise logistic regression analysis, which allowed us to identify 14 independent risk factors: surgical site, surgical approach, resection of adjacent organs, duration of surgery, BMI, age, current smoking status, preoperative anemia, perioperative transfusion, asthma, intraoperative diuretics, intraoperative anticholinergic drugs, intraoperative glucocorticoids, and fasting plasma glucose. Eventually, a nomogram predictive model was constructed using these 14 risk factors.

The model demonstrated good discriminative ability, with AUC values of 0.727 and 0.730 in the training and validation sets, respectively. The calibration curves of both training and validation models showed close agreement with the standard curve, as evidenced by their calibration plots. The decision curve analysis revealed a high expected net benefit of the model, illustrating its predictive accuracy and clinical utility. Internal validation showed that the nomogram has significant clinical utility in predicting the risk of PPCs following CRC surgery and is suitable for individualized patient risk analyses.

This study identified open surgery as a risk factor for PPCs. This finding is not unexpected, as prior research has documented a comparable association [1, 22–24]. Open surgery usually requires larger incisions. This increases damage to abdominal muscles and tissues, leading to greater postoperative pain and prolonged recovery. Consequently, the early postoperative mobility and respiratory function of patients are adversely affected. This study also demonstrated that prolonged surgery significantly increased the risk of PPCs (adjusted OR 1.422 per hour, 95% CI 1.200–1.657.200.657; p < 0.001) after multivariable adjustment. This may be attributed to lung injury caused by prolonged mechanical ventilation during surgery. Additionally, respiratory suppression due to extended anesthesia may also contribute [25]. Prolonged surgeries are also associated with greater surgical trauma and more significant postoperative pain, which can hinder deep breathing and effective coughing postoperatively and increase the incidence of PPCs [26].

Previous studies have reported that malnutrition, such as preoperative anemia and low BMI, is a significant risk factor for postoperative complications because it weakens the immune system and negatively impacts wound healing and recovery [2, 20, 27]. In our study, preoperative anemia and BMI were significant risk factors for postoperative complications in the univariate analysis, but they did not show significant effects in multivariate analysis. This may be due to accounting for multiple risk factors or having too few cases of postoperative complications. Previous studies have reported conflicting findings regarding age as a risk factor for postoperative complications. Most have reported that aging is associated with decreased physiological function, a weakened immune system, and increased comorbidities, all of which elevate the incidence of PPCs [19, 21, 28]. In contrast, some research argues that age is not independently related to perioperative complications [29]. This study revealed a 2.5% incremental risk of PPCs with each additional year of age.

Our findings highlight perioperative blood transfusion as a significant independent risk factor for PPCs, and the following mechanisms have been attributed to it. First, stored blood products release inflammatory factors that may cause immunosuppression, characterized by suppressed natural killer cell activity and reduced Th1/Th2 ratio. This increases the risk of pulmonary infections after transfusion. Second, transfusion-induced systemic inflammatory response, including the release of cytokines and other inflammatory mediators, may aggravate pulmonary inflammation. Third, impaired postoperative recovery capacity of patients who have undergone transfusion contributes to ventilation–perfusion mismatch and atelectasis progression [30–34].

This predictive model provides clinicians with evidence-based decision support to improve the accuracy of their clinical judgment and optimize medical decision-making. It can assist physicians in identifying patients with high risks of PPCs after CRC surgery through precise risk assessment and facilitate personalized treatment and optimization of therapeutic strategies. This approach is aimed at reducing the incidence of PPCs, improving treatment outcomes for patients, and enhancing overall healthcare efficiency.

Notwithstanding the study’s strengths—such as a large sample size and internal validation—several limitations exist. First, the study design may introduce selection and information bias. Second, the selected variables were mainly based on clinical observations, which might have resulted in the exclusion of some important factors. Third, this study was performed at a single institution and lacks multi-institution validation, which may limit the generalizability of the results. Fourth, the inability to conduct long-term follow-ups with patients prevented us from evaluating how PPCs affect long-term survival rates. Future research should consider prospective cohort studies that incorporate multi-center data to further validate our findings and enhance the accuracy of the predictive model. Finally, the relatively large number of variables used in this study should be reduced.

Conclusions

This research established an effective predictive model that identified several independent risk factors for PPCs in colorectal cancer patients. The model demonstrated excellent differentiation and calibration in predicting pulmonary complications following CRC surgery. Clinicians can use it to make scientific and personalized treatment decisions by examining these factors.
