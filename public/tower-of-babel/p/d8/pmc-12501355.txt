Outcomes of Cystic Versus Solid Renal Masses After Surgery: A Prospective Single-centre Comparative Cohort Study
Authors: Salvatore Granata, David Ka-wai Leung, Alessio Pecoraro, Antonio Crescente, Chi-fai Ng, Jeremy Y.C. Teoh, Sergio Serni, Lorenzo Masieri, Riccardo Campi, Collaborators
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Take Home MessageA non-negligible proportion of patients with localised renal masses, especially if solid or Bosniak III cystic, harboured a benign tumour, warranting better preoperative tumour characterisation. Midterm oncological outcomes were similar between malignant solid and cystic renal masses, confirming the need for risk-based postoperative follow-up regardless of tumour nature.

Introduction

Cystic renal masses, defined by solely or predominantly cystic lesions of the kidney with ≤25% solid components [[1], [2], [3], [4]], pose unique diagnostic and management challenges.

While up to 6% of all asymptomatic renal masses represent cystic renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [5], contemporary diagnosis of cystic RCC is still complex and nuanced, due to the caveats of renal tumour biopsy [6] and the lack of definitive diagnostic characteristics at conventional imaging. In this regard, the Bosniak classification, initially introduced in 2005 [5] and updated in 2019 [6], is used routinely for risk stratification of cystic renal masses aiming to guide decision-making and therapeutic management [7]. Of note, cystic renal tumours include a wide spectrum of histopathological entities [8], further complicating diagnostic and therapeutic algorithms.

From a clinical standpoint, despite previous studies reporting a relatively low metastatic potential and favourable prognosis of cystic RCC [9], contemporary guidelines do not provide specific recommendations for the management of cystic renal masses. Moreover, the latest version of the European Association of Urology (EAU) guidelines on RCC stressed the importance of shared decision-making to balance the risk of overtreatment with the potential risk of progression during surveillance [10]. While ablative therapies or stereotactic body radiation therapy (SBRT) is a less attractive option for cystic renal masses, the specific indications for active surveillance (AS) versus surgery vary across the EAU, American Urological Association, and Canadian Urological Association guidelines [[11], [12]].

Owing to the current uncertainties regarding diagnosis, treatment, and outcomes of patients with cystic renal masses, in this study, we sought to compare the clinical characteristics, histopathological features, and perioperative and oncological outcomes between patients with resected cystic and solid renal masses at a referral academic centre.

Patients and methods

Study design, patient population, and dataset

After ethical committee approval, data were collected prospectively from patients with cT1-T2 solid or cystic renal masses suspected to be RCC who underwent surgery with curative intent from January 2017 to May 2024 at our institution. Anonymised patient characteristics, tumour features, surgical outcomes, histological findings, and follow-up data were recorded systematically in a dedicated electronic database in REDCap (https://project-redcap.org). Patients who underwent AS only, or those with incomplete clinical or follow-up data, lymph node involvement (cN1), or metastatic disease (cM1) were excluded from the study. Tumour complexity was reported according to the Preoperative Aspects and Dimensions Used for an Anatomical (PADUA) score and classified as low (PADUA score 6–7) versus intermediate (PADUA score 8–9) and high (PADUA score ≥10) complexity.

The estimated glomerular filtration rate was calculated using the Chronic Kidney Disease Epidemiology Collaboration formula [13]. Tumour stage was classified according to the 2017 TNM classification system [14]. Postoperative complications are reported according to the Clavien-Dindo grading system [15].

Renal mass characterisation

Cystic renal masses were classified using the Bosniak 2019 classification to assess malignancy risk [6] by two experienced genitourinary radiologists (>10 yr of experience), based primarily on the preoperative contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CECT) results. The Bosniak grades were all agreed on and endorsed by the two radiologists, without formal assessment of inter-reader variability due to operative reasons. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and contrast-enhanced ultrasound (CEUS) were employed on a case-by-case basis, particularly in very small (<2 cm) renal masses and/or in patients with contraindications to iodinated contrast agents. None of the patients underwent a preoperative biopsy. Staging was performed using CECT of the abdomen (±chest), according to the EAU guidelines [10].

Decision-making process and surgical intervention

All patients were treated according to international guidelines [10], and the management was driven mainly by a shared decision-making process led by the treating physician. Multidisciplinary discussion was employed on a case-by-case scenario, considering the individual patient-, tumour-, and kidney-related factors [16]. In patients with Bosniak IV lesions, surgical treatment was prioritised in all patients. As for Bosniak IIF and III lesions, the options of AS and surgery [17] were offered to all patients, with initial surveillance prioritised for elderly/comorbid/frail patients, as well as patients with small or very small renal masses [17]. AS patients whose Bosniak IIF/III lesions showed interval growth or new radiological suspicion, and those who indicated preference towards surgery would be counselled properly and subjected to surgery. Surgery was performed by experienced teams using partial/radical nephrectomy and with a robotic or open approach, according to the individual patient- and tumour-related characteristics. Robotic partial/radical nephrectomy was performed using the da Vinci Si or X robotic platform, in a three- or four-arm configuration, according to the specific anatomical and nephrometric characteristics of the tumour. A detailed overview of our surgical technique for robotic partial nephrectomy is available in previous publications [[18], [19], [20]].

Histopathological analysis

A histopathological evaluation of renal masses was conducted by expert genitourinary pathologists (n = 3), each with at least 10 yr of experience, according to established guidelines [10] and based on the most recent updates from the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of kidney tumours [21]. For this study, we focused on the following histopathological features: tumour maximum diameter, histotype, pT stage, and Leibovich 2003 score (for clear-cell RCC [ccRCC]).

Follow-up

According to the established guidelines [10], our follow-up protocol was tailored to the patient’s risk of recurrence. Namely, patients with ccRCC were stratified into low-, intermediate-, and high-risk categories according to the 2003 Leibovich score [22]; patients with non-ccRCC were risk stratified based on pathological staging and histological grading.

Objectives of the study

The main objectives of the study were the following:1.To assess the proportion of patients with cystic renal masses who underwent surgery with curative intent.2.To compare the clinical characteristics, and intra- and perioperative outcomes in patients with cystic versus solid renal masses, focusing on the potential predictors of malignant histology.3.To compare the oncological outcomes (disease-free survival [DFS], defined as the time from randomisation to the first documented local or distant recurrence of RCC or death due to any cause, whichever occurred first [23] in patients with malignant cystic vs solid renal masses). DFS was calculated from the date of surgery to the date of recurrence, death, or last follow-up, whichever occurred first. Patients without an event were right censored at the last known follow-up date (data cut-off May 13, 2024).4.To compare the clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with Bosniak IIF-III versus IV cystic renal masses.

Statistical analysis

The statistical analysis plan for this study followed the established reporting guidelines [24]. Descriptive statistics were used to report patient and tumour characteristics, perioperative complications, and histological features among patients with cystic versus solid renal masses. Categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages, while continuous variables were reported as medians with interquartile ranges (IQRs), where appropriate.

To compare clinical characteristics, perioperative outcomes, and histopathological features in patients with cystic versus solid renal masses, the chi-square test was used for categorical variables and the Mann-Whitney U test was applied for continuous variables. A multivariable logistics regression analysis was employed to identify the predictors of malignancy among routinely collected patient- and tumour-related factors. Kaplan-Meier survival analyses were used to evaluate DFS; Kaplan-Meier curves were generated for both solid and cystic tumours, and also stratified by pathological T staging. Any between-group differences were assessed using the log-rank test. A sensitivity analysis was performed to compare the clinical characteristics and outcomes of patients with Bosniak IIF-III versus IV cystic renal masses.

All statistical analyses were performed using SPSS version 26.0 (IBM Corp, Armonk, NY, USA) and R version 4.2 (R Foundation for Statistical Analysis, Vienna, Austria). A p value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

A histological analysis revealed a lower prevalence of benign lesions in the cystic group than in the solid group (14% vs 23%, p < 0.001). Among the 745 malignant tumours, the distribution of RCC histotypes differed significantly between the two groups, with a lower prevalence of ccRCC and a higher prevalence of papillary RCC (pRCC) among cystic malignant masses (Table 2). The proportion of ccRCC patients (n = 466) with low and intermediate risks according to the Leibovich 2003 score was higher among those with cystic renal masses. Not a single patient with cystic ccRCC was deemed to be at a high risk according to the Leibovich 2003 score.

At a multivariable analysis, increasing tumour diameter (odds ratio [OR] for each 1 cm increase: 1.14; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.04–1.24, p = 0.004), smoking status (OR for current/former vs no smoker: 1.46; 95% CI: 1.05–2.03, p = 0.019), and tumour nature (OR for Bosniak IV vs solid mass: 6.03; 95% CI: 1.87–19.48, p = 0.003) were significant predictors of malignant histology (Supplementary Table 1).

Oncological outcomes in patients with malignant renal masses

Overall, 86% of cystic masses and 74% of solid masses (p < 0.001) harboured a malignant renal mass and were included in the DFS analysis (Fig. 2, Fig. 3, and Supplementary Fig. 1). The median follow-up for patients who were disease free was 27 mo (IQR 12–48). In the overall cohort, the estimated DFS rates in patients with malignant tumours were 97%, 95%, 92%, 90%, and 89% at 12, 24, 36, 48, and 60 mo, respectively (Fig. 2), with no differences between patients with solid and cystic masses (p = 0.7; Fig. 3). Discussion

Cystic renal masses are relatively common in routine urological practice [5] and pose distinct diagnostic and therapeutic challenges to clinicians. While most cystic lesions are benign and do not require any follow-up, a subgroup (ie, Bosniak III and IV cystic renal masses [10]) can harbour RCC and might pose oncological concerns. In this scenario, clinical decision-making in patients with cystic renal masses remains nuanced [10]: on the one hand, preoperative conventional imaging is unable to provide a definitive diagnosis, and renal tumour biopsy is contraindicated [10]; on the other hand, cystic renal tumours include a heterogeneous spectrum of histopathological entities with different aggressiveness and metastatic potential [10]. Lastly, nonsurgical options such as ablative therapies or SBRT are either not technically feasible or not indicated, leaving AS as the only alternative option to upfront surgery [25].

Taken together, the caveats of contemporary diagnostic and therapeutic practices evidently explain the overtreatment of patients with cystic renal masses [25], despite a relatively large body of evidence confirming the generally more indolent nature of cystic RCC [3].

Aiming to provide real-world data to support current guideline recommendations, our study comprehensively characterised the clinical, pathological, and midterm oncological outcomes of resected cystic versus solid renal masses at a referral academic centre over an 8-yr period.

Our study provides key insights to contextualise the on-going controversies and uncertainty about the management of cystic renal masses.

A key finding of the study is that cystic renal masses represent a non-negligible proportion (13%) of resected localised renal tumours in contemporary surgical practice at a referral institution. While in our study, almost all measured preoperative patient- and tumour-related factors were similar between patients with solid and cystic renal masses, patients with cystic renal tumours could have potentially undergone a period of AS before surgery, with a consequent “selection” towards surgical candidates with larger and/or growing cystic renal masses. Unfortunately, we could not formally evaluate this concept due to a lack of data on presurgery surveillance in our registry. However, the finding that the proportion of malignant pathology after nephrectomy was significantly higher among cystic renal masses (86% vs 74%), especially Bosniak IV cysts (96%), corroborates the hypothesis that patients with cystic renal masses included in our cohort were “selected” for nephrectomy due to unfavourable characteristics (eg, large tumour diameter, adverse radiological features, fast growing rates on surveillance, etc.) and do not represent all-comers with an indeterminate Bosniak III-IV renal mass. Of note, Bosniak IV tumour nature was an independent predictor of malignancy in our study cohort, while Bosniak IIF-III was not (Supplementary Table 1). This concept might not be reproducible in other cohorts of resected renal masses, as it is inherently related to patient selection for nephrectomy in both the solid and the cystic renal mass cohort. According to a meta-analysis based on 471 patients, the risk of malignancy of Bosniak IIF and III can go up to 46% (95% CI 28–66%) and 79% (95% CI 68–88%), respectively [7]. The fact that 4% of the resected Bosniak IV cysts turned out to be benign highlighted that no diagnostic system is perfect and that continuous refinements of diagnostic tools (eg MRI and CEUS) [6] and scoring systems are necessary. Yet, regardless of the potential impact of cystic nature on the risk of malignancy, our analysis identified tumour diameter and smoking status as independent predictors of malignant histology after nephrectomy, confirming previous data [[10], [26], [27], [28]] and providing an opportunity to refine current clinical criteria to select patients for novel molecular imaging and/or percutaneous renal biopsy before shared decision-making [[29], [30], [31]].

Notably, the histological spectrum of cystic renal masses (including ccRCC, pRCC, and a variety of “other” histotypes, with just one chromophobe RCC [chRCC]) was remarkably heterogeneous and significantly different from that of solid renal masses (which predominantly included the established pattern of ccRCC, pRCC, and chRCC; Table 2). These observations reinforce the concept that cystic RCC does not represent a single disease but rather a spectrum of histopathological entities with different biology and malignant potential [[9], [32], [33]]. In fact, resected cystic RCC in our cohort had more favourable pathological features (ie, pT stage, International Society of Urological Pathology grade, and Leibovich score in ccRCC) and thus theoretically lower metastatic potential than their solid counterparts, despite larger median tumour diameters. Similarly, a propensity-score–matched retrospective cohort study reported that unilocular cystic RCC tends to have better nuclear grade, pathological stage, and prognosis, as compared with solid RCC [34].

Another key finding of the study is that, while midterm oncological outcomes in our cohort were favourable regardless of the tumour’s nature (with estimated 3- and 5-yr DFS rates of 92% and 89%, respectively; Fig. 2), cystic and solid malignant tumours showed similar outcomes (Fig. 3). Unlike previous studies showing better oncological outcomes in patients with cystic renal tumours, our cystic mass cohort might have included patients who were “selected” for adverse/high-risk features (eg, size, growing pattern, etc.), making the observed DFS outcomes in patients with malignant renal masses likely related to tumour biology rather than radiological nature (solid vs cystic at preoperative imaging). These findings warrant further investigation in prospective studies. Among patients with malignant cystic renal masses, DFS was not significantly different between those with Bosniak IV and Bosniak IIF-III RCCs (Fig. 4). Yet, larger studies with longer follow-up might allow the detection of a prognostic impact of the 2019 Bosniak classification in patients with malignant cysts [10].

Our findings should be interpreted with caution considering the study limitations. First, despite prospective data collection, the design of the study is retrospective, with an inherent risk of selection bias, detection bias, attrition, and residual confounding. Our database also lacked granular data regarding the decision-making process in not only individual patients with cystic or solid renal masses, but also renal mass patients who opted for AS. Moreover, the classification criteria of RCC at the histopathological analysis evolved over time, increasing the risk of potential misclassification of tumour histotypes. Second, our follow-up data are not yet mature, making it difficult to interpret our oncological outcomes beyond a midterm follow-up. For the same reason, we could not analyse the overall survival in patients with solid versus cystic renal masses due to a low number of events. Lastly, the study time frame was relatively long (8 yr), making it challenging to disentangle the effect of changes in clinical practice (for both diagnosis and management of patients with solid vs cystic renal masses) on the study outcomes. Nonetheless, guideline recommendations on the management of patients with localised renal masses suspected of RCC did not change significantly during the past decade.

Despite these limitations, our comparative cohort study reports one of the largest series in the literature on the clinical, surgical, pathological, and oncological outcomes of resected cystic renal masses, providing a robust foundation for further research in this field.

Our real-world data from a referral academic centre confirm that localised indeterminate renal masses are still substantially overtreated in contemporary surgical practice, with 25% of patients harbouring benign tumour entities at final pathology. Our findings call for future individualised preoperative assessment of tumour characteristics (by conventional and/or novel imaging modalities) to reduce the burden of overtreatment and provide value-based care.

Moreover, our study reinforces the need for risk-based postoperative follow-up regardless of tumour nature, given the similar risk of disease recurrence during follow-up.

Future research should explore novel advanced imaging [35], molecular markers [36], and genetic profiling [37], to improve prediction of tumour biology and identification of patients who are more likely to experience disease recurrence. Future research should be grounded into multidisciplinary prospective observational registries capturing granular data on the following: imaging features to risk stratify solid and cystic lesions, natural history of solid/cystic renal masses undergoing surveillance or nonsurgical options, histopathological outcomes after surgery in patients with cystic renal masses according to the latest WHO classification of renal tumours, and finally oncological and patient-reported outcomes after active treatment (with or without previous expectant management).

Conclusions

A non-negligible proportion of patients with surgically treated localised renal masses, especially if solid or Bosniak III cystic, harboured a benign tumour, warranting better preoperative tumour characterisation to provide value-based care. In our cohort including patients who were selected for surgery, malignant cystic masses showed more favourable pathological features than but similar midterm DFS to solid masses, reinforcing the contemporary recommendation for risk-based postoperative follow-up, regardless of tumour nature.
