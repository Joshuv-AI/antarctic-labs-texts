Changes in Permanent Contraception Rates in Pennsylvania Associated With the Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization Decision
Authors: Alice M. Abernathy, Courtney A. Schreiber, Seiyoun Kim, Paula Chatterjee
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
We observed an increase in permanent contraception procedures in Pennsylvania post-Dobbs despite ongoing legal protections for abortion.

The June 2022 U.S. Supreme Court decision Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization removed constitutional protections to the right to abortion, permitting states to regulate abortion within their jurisdictions.1 Currently, abortion is banned in 12 states and permitted with restrictive gestational duration limits in an additional 28 states.2 These bans and restrictions, along with the threat of future legal uncertainty, have reshaped abortion and reproductive health care. Since the Dobbs decision, the number of reproductive-aged females who live more than 60 minutes from an abortion-providing facility has doubled due to closures.3 These legal and spatial inequities affect nearly one-third of all reproductive-aged females in the United States. Changes in geographic access to abortion facilities also have been associated with increased requests for4 and the provision of5 self-managed medication abortion and with growing demands for permanent contraception among both females and males.6–10

Changes in reproductive health behaviors have been detected in states that limited legal abortion access in the post-Dobbs era6,9–12; however, little is known about states in which abortion remained legal. People in states where the legal right to abortion was preserved may still have perceived threats to their abortion care given the national salience of the Dobbs decision. Perceptions of the ramifications of the Dobbs decision may have been particularly consequential for structurally marginalized populations who differentially receive permanent contraception. Before Dobbs, Black and Hispanic females,13 as well as females with lower incomes,14 lower levels of educational attainment,15 and higher parity13,16 more commonly received permanent contraception. Despite increased use of permanent contraception, these populations face barriers to accessing it when desired.14,17–19 The perceived threat of the Dobbs decision changing the legal status of abortion in Pennsylvania, combined with geographic differences to abortion care in the state, may have had substantial effects on reproductive health behaviors: Even modest increases in the distance to abortion care have been shown to reduce accessibility of abortion, and may have influenced both abortion and birth rates.20–22

In this study, we examined changes in permanent contraception procedures before and after the Dobbs decision in Pennsylvania. In Pennsylvania, abortion remained legal in the post-Dobbs era, though there were perceived threats to its legal status immediately post-Dobbs23–27 and abortion care remains subject to intense scrutiny and debate.28–31 We hypothesized that permanent contraception rates increased in Pennsylvania post-Dobbs, and that these effects would vary by measures of structural marginalization.13,15,32 Furthermore, we hypothesized that these effects would be more pronounced among individuals who lived further from the nearest abortion-providing facility, because geographic distance is a barrier to abortion care.20–22,33,34

METHODS

We used an all-payer claims database from the PHC4 (Pennsylvania Health Care Cost Containment Council) for reproductive-aged males and females (15–44 years) from January 1, 2019, to December 31, 2023. The PHC4 data set includes more than 3 million ambulatory and outpatient all-payer claims (including Medicaid and self-pay) per year from all Pennsylvania ambulatory surgery centers and hospitals,35 where most tubal permanent contraception procedures take place36 and nearly 20% of vasectomies occur.37 The PHC4 conducts their own validity assessments38 to ensure reliability of reporting from all surgery centers and hospitals in the state. Data are released quarterly. The PHC4 data include patient-level information on race, ethnicity, and insurance status, and has geographic identifiers for patient residence ZIP codes.

From the American Community Survey (ACS), we obtained ZIP code–level estimates of the reproductive-aged population (aged 15–44) in Pennsylvania for each year of the study period along with 5-year estimates (terminal year 2022) of the percentage of the population living in poverty and the percentage of the population older than age 25 years who received at least some college education. Finally, we used the Myers Abortion Facility Database39 to identify clinics that provided abortions in Pennsylvania.

The primary exposure was the official public release of the Dobbs decision (June 24, 2022). The preexposure period (pre-Dobbs) was January 1, 2019, to June 31, 2022 (study quarters 1–13). The postexposure period (post-Dobbs) was July 1, 2022, to March 31, 2023 (study quarters 14–17).

The primary outcome, receipt of a permanent contraception procedure, was identified using International Classification of Diseases Tenth Revision codes, Current Procedural Terminology codes, and Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System codes (Appendices 1 and 2, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E330). We totaled permanent contraception procedures across the state per quarter of the study period. We calculated procedure counts per quarter divided by the reproductive-age population in Pennsylvania, as reported in the ACS. Permanent contraception procedures were included for both males and females. We did not include codes related to the Essure device (a permanent implantable device for contraception available from 2002 to 2018), which was not accessible during the time frame of interest.

From the PHC4, we obtained patient-level measures of age, race, ethnicity, and insurance status to evaluate a priori hypotheses that younger patients,6 patients of racial or ethnic minority status,13 and patients who were insured by Medicaid13 may differentially receive permanent contraception procedures more often relative to other populations. Also, from the PHC4, we extracted a set of sex-specific outcomes (procedures for unilateral or bilateral mastectomy) to conduct a falsification test. Falsification tests are a statistical approach used to disprove or “falsify” a hypothesis. We would not expect mastectomy rates to be affected by the Dobbs decision. If we did observe a trend break or significant change in mastectomy rates coincident with the Dobbs decision, this would serve to “falsify” our hypothesis that factors associated with the Dobbs decision affected reproductive health behaviors. However, the absence of a trend break or significant change in mastectomy rates coincident with the Dobbs decision would support our hypothesis that the changes in permanent contraception rates are likely due to factors associated with the Dobbs decision, rather than unmeasured confounders affecting broader surgical procedure trends in females.

We matched patient ZIP codes to ZIP Code Tabulation Area (ZCTA)–level measures of poverty status and educational attainment to evaluate whether there was variation in receipt of permanent contraception procedures among patients living in poverty or with lower levels of educational attainment.15 We cross-walked patient level ZIP code from the PHC4 to ZCTAs from the U.S. Department of Housing and Urban Development.40 After each ZIP code was matched to a ZCTA, we used ZCTA-level data from the ACS to determine the proportion of the population that completed some college or equivalent, as well as the proportion of the population that reported living in poverty in the previous year.

We dichotomized variables for educational attainment and poverty status according to the mean of the distribution. For example, 70% of patients in the sample resided in ZCTAs where the mean educational attainment was completing some college or more; thus, the variable for educational attainment was dichotomized at the mean, such that lower educational attainment reflected an individual living in a ZCTA where less than 70% of the population completed some college and higher educational attainment reflected an individual living in a ZCTA where 70% or more of the population completed some college. Higher poverty status was categorized using ZCTAs in which more than 15% of the population was living in poverty; lower poverty status was categorized using ZCTAs in which less than 15% of the population reported living in poverty.

Finally, using the Myers Abortion Facility Database,39 we calculated the distance from a patient's population-weighted ZIP code centroid40 to the nearest abortion facility in 2022. We categorized this into 30-minute, 30 to 60 minute, and more than 60-minute driving times from each abortion facility using ArcGIS Pro 10.8.2. Tertiles were calculated consistent with prior studies that demonstrated variation in likelihood of abortion receipt by distance3 and standards for access to specialty care.41 There were no clinic closures that changed the drivetime tertiles during our study period.

We calculated descriptive statistics of patients seeking permanent contraception procedures, including sex, age, race, ethnicity, and insurance type, pre- and post-Dobbs. We additionally calculated ZCTA-level characteristics of educational attainment, poverty and driving distance to abortion facility among those who received a permanent contraception procedure pre- and post-Dobbs.

We then plotted unadjusted rates of permanent contraception procedures (tubal and vasectomy) per 100,000 reproductive-aged population (15–44 years) per year, pre- and post-Dobbs. To evaluate for trend breaks in permanent contraception trends over time, we used interrupted time series analysis with significance set to P<.05. Interrupted time series analysis is a robust quasi-experimental design specifically well suited to evaluate health effects of policy changes.42,43 Interrupted time series analysis permitted evaluation of the pre-Dobbs trends in permanent contraception procedure rates in Pennsylvania, which was “interrupted” by the Dobbs decision. We measured the immediate level change at the time of Dobbs and subsequent post-Dobbs perturbations contrasted to a modeled counterfactual, which projected expected trends in permanent contraception procedure rates in the absence of the Dobbs decision. Changes in the level and slope represented estimates of the effect of the Dobbs decision on permanent contraception rates. The outcome variable was permanent contraception procedure the explanatory variable was quarter.

To evaluate effect heterogeneity, we conducted unadjusted interrupted time series analyses for permanent contraception rates in females, disaggregated by ZIP-code level measures of structural marginalization including poverty, educational attainment, and drive time to abortion care. We conducted an additional interrupted time series analysis of permanent contraception procedures among females, adjusting for age and quarter. Finally, we repeated this analysis using a sex-specific falsification test of unilateral or bilateral mastectomy as the outcome. We hypothesized that the Dobbs decision should not be associated with significant trend breaks in the number of unilateral or bilateral mastectomy procedures per total population; therefore, lack of association between the Dobbs decision and mastectomy procedures would increase confidence in the associations between rate changes in permanent contraception procedures and the Dobbs decision.

Finally, we used the Quandt likelihood ratio test to determine the most likely breakpoint in the permanent contraception trend across the study period. The Quandt likelihood ratio calculates an F-test statistic for all potential breakpoints in a time series; if the F-test statistic is large, there is a high likelihood that a trend break occurred at the time point. The Quandt likelihood ratio tests all potential breakpoints in time series data; therefore, if the largest F-statistic were to coincide with the public release of the Dobbs decision, the Quandt likelihood ratio would support our hypothesis that the trend break occurred in association with the Dobbs decision and validate our use of an interrupted time series analysis model with the Dobbs decision as the prespecified breakpoint. The study was deemed exempt by the University of Pennsylvania IRB.

RESULTS

There were 453,244 permanent contraception procedures among females and males aged 15–44 years in Pennsylvania from 2019 through the first quarter of 2023 (Table 1), with 83.2% occurring among females and 16.8% among males. Patients undergoing these procedures were predominately aged 30–34 years (28.1%), reported their race as White (73.9%) and ethnicity as non-Hispanic (89.7%), and had private insurance (59.4%) (Table 1).

There also were differences in permanent contraception procedure rates among females in the post-Dobbs period according to the distance to abortion-providing clinics (Fig. 4 and Table 2). There was a statistically significant trend break and level increase in permanent contraception procedure rates among females at all driving distances: those living less than 30 minutes, 30–60 minutes, and more than 60 minutes driving distance to abortion care, with the greatest level increases for individuals residing 30–60 minutes (2,196/100,000 people 15–44, P=.004) and more than 60 minutes from abortion care (1,754, P=.002).

In a falsification test in which we evaluated trends in a procedure that we would not expect to change before and after the Dobbs decision (unilateral or bilateral mastectomy), we did not find temporal trend breaks or significant changes in utilization rates over the study period (Appendices 5 and 6, available online at http://links.lww.com/AOG/E330). The absence of trend breaks at the time of Dobbs and the stability of mastectomy rates over the study period increase confidence in the associations observed between the Dobbs decision and changes in permanent contraception rates.

DISCUSSION

In this study of the Pennsylvania reproductive-aged population, for whom the legal right to abortion was preserved after the Dobbs decision, we found a significant, though short-lived, increase in permanent contraception rates. The immediate jump in permanent contraception procedures, driven almost entirely by females, was greatest among individuals residing in areas with higher levels of educational attainment and those living in higher income areas. We also found increases in permanent contraception procedure rates among individuals living further from abortion care. These changes likely reflect a rapid, immediate response to the national salience of Dobbs, at which time there was much uncertainty surrounding how the decision would change policy in Pennsylvania. As the legal landscape in Pennsylvania remained stable (ie, abortion remained legal and this messaging was communicated to the populace), there was a decreased, though still elevated, post-Dobbs trend relative to the pre-Dobbs baseline.

Prior work has shown increases in permanent contraception procedures in the post-Dobbs era,6,7,10 and our study builds on this by demonstrating that even in states where the legal status of abortion did not change, the threat may have been sufficient to drive changes in reproductive health behaviors. These changes can be conceptualized as a “chilling effect” wherein threats associated with messaging around a health policy change can affect the behavior of patients, even when the actual nature of care delivery may not change. In this case though Pennsylvanians ostensibly did not experience actual reductions in abortion access, their reproductive health behaviors may have shifted due to the perceived threat of limited abortion access associated with Dobbs.

There have been several examples of the chilling effects of health policies over the past decades: Self-reported health improved among populations that benefited from the Deferred Action for Childhood Arrivals program from 2012 to 2015 but then deteriorated after political uncertainty about the continuation of the program emerged in 201544; the public charge rule, where the threat of health care organizations serving a policing role for undocumented populations was sufficient to dissuade such populations from seeking care.45,46 In the case of the Dobbs decision, a chilling effect could operate through multiple pathways. For practitioners, abortion may be perceived as more difficult to provide due to public and political threats; for patients, abortion may be perceived as less accessible, which, in turn, may induce behavioral changes related to contraception choices.47,48

We observed higher rates of permanent tubal contraception post-Dobbs among White individuals, individuals with private insurance, individuals residing in more affluent areas and areas with higher levels of educational attainment. These findings may be consistent with literature demonstrating increased fulfilment of permanent contraception postpartum among socioeconomically advantaged groups.17,18 There are well-documented barriers to permanent tubal contraception by insurance status: Many requests for permanent contraception immediately postpartum go unfulfilled49 and unlike private insurance, Medicaid mandates a 30-day waiting period between consent and receipt of the procedure.50 These issues disproportionately affect marginalized populations, who are more likely to be insured by Medicaid,51 as are birthing people51 who reside in states that enacted abortion bans post-Dobbs.52,53 Taken together, these findings suggest that although disadvantaged groups13 more commonly received permanent contraception before Dobbs, they may experience greater barriers to acting on an interest in obtaining permanent tubal contraception post-Dobbs. Barriers to receiving permanent tubal contraception, when desired, may hinder reproductive autonomy. This differential effect may become more pronounced if both contraceptive accessibility and geographic abortion access are limited.

We also observed differences in permanent contraception rates, pre- and post-Dobbs, by distance to abortion care. The magnitude of the rise in permanent tubal contraception rates was proportional to distance from abortion care. Abortion is less accessible3 for individuals who must travel farther for abortion care and is associated with higher rates of self-managed medication abortion54 and delays in receiving abortion care.34 This is consistent with prior literature showing nonlinear effects of distance to abortion clinic on abortion and birth rates.20–22

There are limitations to this analysis. First, the coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19)-19 pandemic introduced procedural trend volatility in the pre-Dobbs era; for this reason, we included 2019 data to improve trend projection. The fall in procedures related to the pandemic was brief and unlikely change our trend projections pre-Dobbs (Fig. 1). Relatedly, receipt of permanent contraception may have been contingent on access to insurance, which fluctuated during the early stages of the pandemic due to broad economic decline.55,56 Second, most vasectomies are performed in ambulatory clinics, so we may have underestimated vasectomy rates in our sample. Third, we were unable to assess the effect of parity, because these data were not available in the PHC4 data. Finally, use of International Classification of Diseases and Current Procedural Terminology codes relies on accurate and complete coding practices, potentially leading to misclassification or underestimation. Claims do not capture nuances of patient intent motivating reasons to seek a permanent contraception procedure was performed.

In summary, there were significant changes in permanent contraception rates among females in Pennsylvania after the Dobbs decision, which varied across some measures of structural marginalization. These findings add to the current understanding of reproductive health behavior shifts in the post-Dobbs era by demonstrating effect heterogeneity by sex, educational attainment, income and distance to abortion-providing clinics. Our observations suggest that, even where abortion remains legal, perceived threats to abortion access may be associated with changes in permanent contraceptive-seeking behavior. Federal and state policymakers should consider the broad ramifications of national political conversations on reproductive health behaviors and publicize preserved access to abortion care where it remains intact.48
