Meta-learned dynamic hierarchical fusion for robust multi-scale object classification
Authors: Prashanta Kumar Patra, Amitav Mahapatra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
A key limitation of existing feature fusion methods is their static nature: they learn dataset-specific integration policies during training that remain fixed at inference. This restricts adaptability to new domains and input characteristics, often causing brittle generalization, hierarchical information decay, and inefficient relational modeling. We propose Meta-Learned Dynamic Hierarchical Fusion (MDHF), which reframes feature fusion as context-conditioned policy generation. MDHF uses a bi-level meta-learning objective to learn a transferable prior over fusion strategies, enabling sample-specific dynamic integration via a single gradient-free forward pass. This is realized through three co-designed modules: (1) Meta-Learned Dynamic Attention for input-adaptive feature weighting, (2) Bidirectional Hierarchical Integration to prevent information decay, and (3) Adaptively Sparse Graph Fusion for efficient O(n log n) relational reasoning. Extensive evaluation across five benchmarks—including standard (CIFAR-10, ImageNet-200), fine-grained (Stanford Cars, Oxford-IIIT Pets), and robustness (Caltech-101) tasks—shows that MDHF consistently outperforms 15 baselines. It achieves fine-grained gains (e.g., + 1.3% on Stanford Cars, + 1.1% on Oxford Pets over DGFT), uses 60–68% fewer parameters than comparable transformer-based models, and delivers 36% faster inference than FC-Former. Under strong adversarial attack (AutoAttack, ε = 8/255), it retains 85% of clean accuracy. Boundary condition analysis further quantifies its data efficiency, cross-domain generalization, and predictable limits under extreme domain shifts and texture-only tasks. These results establish MDHF as a principled, efficient paradigm for context-aware visual recognition, balancing adaptive generalization with inference-time efficiency for dynamic, multi-domain applications.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47008-5.

Introduction

Feature fusion represents an important capability in modern computer vision, allowing models to combine information from multiple spatial scales and representational levels in order to improve object classification performance1,2. Early fusion strategies relied on simple operations such as concatenation or linear weighting1, which offered limited capacity to capture complex relationships among features. More recent approaches have introduced attention mechanisms3,4, hierarchical architectures2,5, and graph-based models6 to better exploit contextual and relational information. While these developments have led to notable performance gains, most existing fusion methods share a common design assumption: the fusion strategy is learned during training and remains fixed at inference time5,7.

This assumption introduces practical limitations. Channel attention mechanisms typically learn global importance patterns that are applied uniformly to all inputs once training is complete, even when input semantics vary significantly3,4. Graph-based fusion approaches often rely on predefined or statistically derived connectivity patterns, resulting in relational structures that do not adapt to new feature configurations and that scale poorly with feature resolution6. Hierarchical fusion frameworks, such as feature pyramid architectures, commonly employ fixed aggregation rules with predominantly unidirectional information flow, which can lead to gradual loss of fine-grained details as features propagate across levels. Together, these characteristics suggest that enabling context-dynamic fusion policies—where the integration strategy adjusts to the input—could improve robustness and efficiency. However, generating such policies in a generalizable, inference-efficient manner remains an area requiring further exploration7,8.

To investigate this, we propose Meta-Learned Dynamic Hierarchical Fusion (MDHF), a framework for context-dynamic fusion. Rather than committing to a single, static fusion strategy, MDHF leverages meta-learning to generate context-dynamic fusion parameters based on the observed feature distributions of each sample. The framework integrates three complementary mechanisms: meta-learned attention for input-specific channel modulation, bidirectional hierarchical interaction to balance semantic abstraction with spatial detail, and dynamically sparsified graph fusion to model relational dependencies efficiently. This design allows MDHF to adjust how features are combined without requiring retraining or task-specific fine-tuning. MDHF is designed to support robust generalization across diverse visual domains, as empirically evaluated in Sect. 5.

Although meta-learning has been explored for objectives such as loss reweighting, optimization control, and domain adaptation7,9 its application to feature fusion remains limited. In particular, simpler conditional inference mechanisms typically rely on fixed parametric mappings, which restrict their ability to adapt fusion strategies across diverse and unseen feature configurations at inference time. In contrast to prior approaches that apply meta-learning at the training or optimization level, MDHF operates directly on feature interactions to generate input-conditioned fusion policies through conditional forward computation. This distinction shifts the focus from learning a single static policy to learning a policy generator, allowing the model to respond more flexibly to unseen inputs while maintaining the efficiency of a single forward pass.

The main contributions of this work are threefold. First, we formulate the problem of context-dynamic feature fusion as a meta-learning task over a distribution of domain transformations, enabling context-conditional policy generation at inference (Sect. 3.2). Second, we present a unified hierarchical architecture that combines meta-attention, bidirectional feature exchange, and sparse graph reasoning to improve both representational efficiency and scalability. Third, we provide theoretical and empirical analysis demonstrating that MDHF achieves favourable computational complexity while consistently improving classification performance across multiple benchmark datasets. Experimental results show that these gains are achieved alongside reductions in parameter count and inference cost. The remainder of the paper is organized as follows. Section 2 reviews related work in feature fusion and meta-learning. Section 3 details the proposed MDHF framework and its theoretical properties. Section 4 describes the experimental setup, with results and analysis presented in Sect. 5. Section 6 discusses limitations and broader implications, and Sect. 7 concludes the paper.

Related work

Feature fusion has evolved through a series of design choices that balance adaptability and computational efficiency. Early approaches relied on fixed operations such as concatenation, summation, or linear weighting. Although computationally efficient, these static strategies apply identical fusion rules to all inputs, limiting their ability to accommodate variations in semantic content or domain shift10,11. Extensions based on statistical alignment12 or kernelized combinations13 introduced greater flexibility but often faced scalability challenges or limited capacity to capture nonlinear feature interactions.

Attention mechanisms marked a notable step toward input-adaptive processing. Channel and spatial attention modules14,15 enable networks to emphasize informative features, while transformer-based architectures16,17 extend this principle through self-attention to model long-range dependencies. Despite their representational power, these approaches often incur quadratic complexity with respect to spatial resolution and rely on highly parameterized models, which can limit scalability in data-constrained settings. Graph-based fusion methods18,19 further introduce structured relational reasoning by modeling features as nodes and edges. While effective for capturing complex interactions, dense graph constructions face O(n²) memory and computation costs and are sensitive to graph design choices.

A complementary research direction addresses generalization through static model adaptation rather than per-input reconfiguration. Meta-learning methods have been applied to learn transferable parameter initializations20, optimize training trajectories21, or adapt sample weighting strategies22 to improve performance across tasks. In parallel, domain adaptation and generalization approaches—widely explored in applications such as mechanical fault diagnosis23 and industrial process monitoring24—aim to learn invariant representations or fixed cross-domain adjustments. Although effective for improving average cross-domain performance, these methods retain a fixed inference-time fusion strategy and thus may struggle to accommodate fine-grained, sample-level heterogeneity encountered at deployment.

Recent high-performance fusion architectures have achieved robust multiscale feature integration through attention‑based knowledge distillation25, multistage hierarchical fusion26, convolutional MLP-based feature aggregation27, and modality transfer with refinement frameworks28 and transformer-based multi-scale feature fusion29. Despite these advances, such architectures generally preserve static fusion policies once trained and often inherit quadratic computational complexity, limiting their adaptability at inference.

Consequently, an opportunity remains for methods that enable efficient, context-dynamic feature fusion. The proposed MDHF framework addresses this gap by formulating feature fusion as a meta-learning problem over a distribution of domain transformations. MDHF generates context-specific fusion policies within a single forward pass while mitigating scalability limitations through sparse relational modeling. This perspective distinguishes MDHF from prior attention, graph, and meta-learning approaches that adapt representations or training dynamics, but not the fusion policy itself at inference.

Architectural overview and design philosophy

The Meta-Learned Dynamic Hierarchical Fusion (MDHF) framework is designed to address the limitations of static fusion approaches by generating input-specific fusion policies. As illustrated in Fig. 1, the architecture integrates three co-designed components that collectively enable context-dynamic fusion—where integration strategies adjust to input characteristics while maintaining computational efficiency. This differs from prior work that either applies fixed policies or incurs quadratic complexity for adaptation. The design follows a hierarchical principle: a core meta-learned attention mechanism enables dynamic policy generation, while auxiliary enhancement modules (bidirectional integration, sparse graphs, meta-gating) provide complementary stability, efficiency, and adaptability.

Terminology clarification

In MDHF, adaptation refers specifically to the meta-learning process during training that learns to generate effective fusion policies. During inference, the model performs conditional forward computation—using the meta-learned prior \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M$$\end{document} to produce input-specific attention weights without any parameter updates. This differs fundamentally from “online adaptation” or “test-time adaptation,” which involve gradient-based parameter optimization during deployment.

This core mechanism enables input-conditioned fusion policy generation.

Design Justification

This meta-learning formulation provides an inductive bias for generating generalizable fusion policies, addressing the limitation of conditional networks that often overfit to training-domain correlations (see ablation study in Sect. 5.5).

The dominant computational cost in MDHF arises from the sparse graph fusion module, which achieves O(n log n) complexity through differentiable top-k sparsification, compared to O(n²) for dense graph approaches. For typical feature maps with n = 196 spatial positions, this corresponds to 96% edge sparsity and proportional memory savings. Critically, this efficiency is realized through gradient-free conditional inference during deployment, unlike test-time adaptation methods that require backpropagation and parameter updates at inference time.

Methodological justification for meta-learning

The selection of a bi-level meta-learning framework is motivated by a fundamental trade-off between generalization capability and inference efficiency that simpler adaptive mechanisms cannot adequately address. While conditional inference methods (e.g., hypernetworks, FiLM) learn direct input-to-parameter mappings that operate efficiently during inference, they lack a strong inductive bias for out-of-distribution generalization, often overfitting to correlations specific to the training distribution. Conversely, test-time adaptation strategies introduce dynamic behavior through gradient-based updates at inference, but at the cost of breaking the standard single-forward-pass deployment paradigm, incurring significant latency, and introducing instability risks.

The proposed MDHF framework navigates this trade-off by employing meta-learning to develop a generalizable policy-generation function, rather than learning a static adaptive mapping or performing online optimization. The bi-level optimization structure—comprising inner-loop task-specific adaptation and outer-loop meta-optimization—provides a crucial inductive bias: it learns initialization parameters and a meta-attention prior \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M$$\end{document} that together enable the generation of robust, context-specific fusion policies through a single, gradient-free forward pass. This design explicitly separates the learning of adaptation (during meta-training) from the execution of adaptation (during inference), ensuring both strong cross-domain generalization and practical deployment efficiency.

The conceptual advantage of this approach is validated empirically in Sect. 5.5 and 5.6, where MDHF demonstrates superior generalization compared to its statically-trained counterpart and achieves more sample-efficient adaptation than gradient-based meta-learning baselines, while maintaining inference latency comparable to standard conditional networks.

Implementation and training details

Shared configuration

For all methods, training was conducted under identical experimental conditions to ensure fair comparison. Models were optimized using AdamW with an initial learning rate of 0.001 and weight decay of 0.01, together with a cosine annealing learning-rate schedule and a batch size of 64. Standard data augmentations were applied during training, and early stopping was employed when validation accuracy failed to improve for 15 consecutive epochs.

MDHF-specific configuration

The proposed MDHF framework was trained using the meta-learning procedure described in Algorithm 1. Meta-training employed 5-way classification tasks with 15 support and 10 query samples per class. Inner-loop adaptation was performed for five gradient steps with learning rate α = 0.01, while outer-loop meta-optimization used a meta-batch size of eight tasks and learning rate β = 0.001. In the sparse graph fusion module, relational connectivity was restricted to \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:k=\lceil\:{\mathrm{l}\mathrm{o}\mathrm{g}}_{2}n\rceil\:$$\end{document}edges per node, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:n$$\end{document}denotes the number of spatial feature locations.

Computational environment and cost-benefit analysis

Experiments were conducted on NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs. The complete meta-training procedure for MDHF on ImageNet-200 required approximately 192 GPU-hours, representing a 71% overhead compared to training a statically-weighted variant (112 GPU-hours). This cost is attributable to the bi-level optimization framework, which processes multiple tasks per meta-batch. To quantify the training-inference trade-off, we compare: MDHF achieves 45 ± 2ms inference time versus 62 ± 3ms for FC-Former (36% faster), while maintaining robust cross-domain accuracy (68.30 ± 0.8% on Sketch benchmark versus 59.10 ± 1.2% for the static variant). Given that fine-tuning static models typically requires 40–60% of original training time per domain, MDHF’s upfront investment becomes favorable for deployments across three or more target domains where repeated adaptation would otherwise be necessary.

Results and analysis

This section presents a comprehensive evaluation of the proposed MDHF framework, emphasizing mechanistic interpretation, practical relevance, and generalization boundaries. Quantitative results are complemented with qualitative analyses and targeted ablations to clarify why and when context-dynamic fusion provides advantages over static alternatives.

Overall performance across benchmarks

We first assess whether meta-learned fusion policy generation yields practical improvements over static fusion strategies. Table 3 summarizes MDHF’s performance against representative baselines from three methodological categories: established fusion architectures, contemporary transformer- and graph-based methods, and meta-learning approaches. Across all five benchmarks, MDHF achieves the highest average accuracy (97.7%) while using the fewest parameters (0.91 M). The magnitude of improvement correlates with dataset characteristics. On fine-grained tasks with high inter-class similarity and substantial intra-class variation—Stanford Cars and Oxford-IIIT Pets—MDHF outperforms the best-performing baseline (DGFT) by + 1.3% and + 1.1%, respectively. These settings appear to benefit most from input-conditioned fusion, as the discriminative relevance of features (e.g., texture, part geometry) can vary across samples. Conversely, on coarse-grained datasets with more globally consistent class semantics (CIFAR-10), the advantage is more moderate (+ 0.7% over FC-Former), suggesting that static fusion can suffice when discriminative structure does not change significantly across inputs.

Qualitative analysis supports this quantitative trend. Figures 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8 and 9 presents comprehensive attention and feature visualization analyses, including Grad-CAM++ attention maps, comparing MDHF with contemporary baselines across three datasets. MDHF exhibits input-adaptive focus—for example, attending to distinct vehicle parts in Stanford Cars or breed-specific facial features in Oxford Pets—whereas baseline methods show more diffuse, input-invariant attention patterns. This difference aligns with MDHF’s design objective: generating sample-specific fusion policies rather than applying fixed weighting schemes.

All reported differences are statistically significant (paired \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:t$$\end{document}-tests with Bonferroni correction, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:p<0.001$$\end{document}). Effect sizes and confidence intervals are detailed in Supplementary section S2. Notably, MDHF achieves these gains alongside parameter efficiency, using 59–67% fewer parameters than contemporary transformer-based models indicating that dynamic fusion does not necessitate architectural expansion. These results delineate an operational regime in which dynamic fusion offers clearest benefits: tasks requiring discrimination of subtle, sample-varying cues rather than globally consistent categorical distinctions. For simpler classification problems, the additional complexity of policy generation may provide diminishing returns relative to well-tuned static fusion.

Scalability and inference efficiency

We next examine whether MDHF’s adaptive fusion incurs prohibitive computational costs. Results indicate that its co-designed components translate dynamic fusion capacity into measurable efficiency gains without compromising performance. When trained on only 20% of CIFAR-10, MDHF retains 98.2% of full-data accuracy, outperforming DGFT (94.7%) and FC-Former (95.1%) under identical conditions (Fig. 2; Tables 4 and 5). This demonstrates that the meta-learned fusion controller encodes a transferable prior over feature interactions, enabling effective strategy reuse in data-limited scenarios, whereas fixed-weight baselines degrade substantially. This suggests that learning to generate fusion policies provides better sample efficiency than learning fixed policies, supporting dynamic fusion’s value in data-constrained scenarios. At deployment, MDHF reduces latency by 36% (45 ± 2 ms vs. 62 ± 3 ms for FC-Former) and memory by 22%, with negligible accuracy loss (≤ 0.3%). These gains derive from two mechanisms: (1) sparse graph fusion (Sect. 3.2.5), which constrains relational reasoning to O(n log n) by preserving only the most informative interactions, and (2) bidirectional hierarchical exchange (Sect. 3.2.3), promoting feature reuse and avoiding redundant pairwise computations of dense attention.

The observed efficiency accompanies a 71% meta-training overhead relative to static variants (Sect. 4.3). This upfront cost is offset in multi-domain deployments, as it eliminates per-domain fine-tuning typically requiring 40–60% of the original training time. Inference scales near-linearly from 128² to 512² pixels, confirming that sparsified modelling mitigates resolution-dependent complexity. On GPU hardware (RTX A6000) and embedded platforms with moderate parallelism (Jetson Nano, FP16), MDHF sustains real-time inference at 720p (> 38 FPS). Performance remains constrained on ultra-low-power devices lacking parallel compute, defining a practical deployment boundary and highlighting optimization opportunities for resource-constrained settings.

Robustness to perturbations

We evaluate MDHF’s behavior under distribution shift to assess whether dynamic fusion confers inherent robustness to both adversarial and natural corruptions. Robustness was assessed under a white-box threat model (full knowledge of architecture and parameters) using the Stanford Cars dataset for its fine-grained discrimination challenges. Attacks included FGSM (ε = 4/255, 8/255, 16/255), PGD-10 (ε = 8/255, 10 iterations, step size 2/255, random restart), and AutoAttack (ε = 8/255). Perturbations were normalized to the [0,1] pixel range following standard ImageNet preprocessing. All attacks were constrained to 2 min per 1000 examples on an NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU. Baselines were evaluated without adversarial training to ensure assessment of inherent model robustness. Under these attacks (Table 6), MDHF maintains significantly higher accuracy than contemporary baselines. At ε = 8/255, MDHF achieves 83.4% accuracy under AutoAttack versus 68.2% for FC-Former (+ 15.2%, p < 0.001). The advantage scales with perturbation strength, from + 6.8% under mild FGSM (ε = 4/255) to + 13.5% under stronger FGSM (ε = 16/255). Feature-space analysis (Fig. 3) shows MDHF preserves 87% of clean feature discriminability under attack, with 75% lower representational distortion than transformer baselines, suggesting robustness stems from architectural characteristics rather than gradient obfuscation. Under systematic corruptions (Table 7), MDHF exhibits slower degradation than static fusion and attention-based approaches. With Gaussian noise (σ = 0.25), MDHF maintains 93.1% accuracy versus 78.9% for FC-Former (+ 14.2%, p < 0.001). Under 25% random label noise, performance declines only 4.9% versus 8.3% for baselines. Combined visual transformations (rotation, contrast changes, cropping) yield consistent retention above 95% accuracy (Fig. 4). This resilience appears linked to dynamic feature reweighting enabled by the meta-attention prior \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\:M$$\end{document}, which down-weights corrupted channels while preserving discriminative signals through bidirectional hierarchical information flow.

Feature representation and interpretability

To move beyond aggregate accuracy, we examine the structure and interpretability of MDHF’s learned representations. The meta-learned fusion policy produces more disentangled and semantically organized embeddings than static baselines, as evidenced by higher mutual information, normalized mutual information, and cluster purity (Table 8). t-SNE visualizations (Fig. 6) reveal tighter intra-class clustering and clearer inter-class separation, particularly for fine-grained categories such as visually similar breeds in Oxford-IIIT Pets. These patterns indicate that input-conditioned fusion selectively amplifies discriminative cues while suppressing redundant features, yielding compact and well-separated class manifolds. Interpretability is further assessed via SHAP and Grad-CAM + + analyses. SHAP beeswarm plots (Fig. 7) show that MDHF distributes feature importance evenly across low-, mid-, and high-level cues, producing tightly clustered, stable attributions. Transformer-based baselines overemphasize high-level semantics, while graph-based methods focus on mid-level structure. Class-specific SHAP analyses (Fig. 8) reveal adaptive feature weighting: wheel and body geometry for SUVs, roof angle and silhouette for coupes, and balanced cues for sedans, confirming context-aware prioritization. Grad-CAM + + visualizations across CIFAR-10, Oxford-IIIT Pets, and Caltech-101 (Fig. 9) corroborate these findings spatially. Across all examples, MDHF produces compact, semantically aligned activation maps concentrated on discriminative object structures (e.g., cat face, elephant head and trunk, frog head/body) while suppressing background responses. In contrast, FC-Former and MoNetViT exhibit more diffuse attention extending into background regions, DVHGNN generates fragmented activations, and MSFANet shows comparatively weaker localization. These observations indicate that MDHF achieves more precise and semantically meaningful localization across diverse object categories.

Dynamic attention under severe occlusion

Under severe occlusion (60% right-side), MDHF dynamically reallocates attention to visible discriminative regions (grille, headlights, wheel area), whereas FC-Former exhibits diffuse attention extending into occluded areas (Fig. 10). The normalized attention difference map highlights the contrast between input-conditioned and fixed fusion strategies, providing mechanistic evidence that MDHF performs input-conditioned feature integration rather than applying a static attention template.

Dynamic attention policy adaptation

To further evaluate context-dependent fusion, we visualize MDHF and the static AMSF baseline under five domain transformations (Fig. 11). For the controlled transformation analysis in Fig. 11, we selected a canonical image by gomagoti (2013) from Wikimedia Commons (CC BY-SA 2.0) rather than a sample from existing pet datasets. This choice provides a clean, unoccluded baseline with minimal background distraction, enabling systematic isolation of transformation effects on attention mechanisms without confounding variables such as occlusion or pose variation present in standard datasets. MDHF exhibits pronounced redistribution of attention mass across conditions (mean JSD = 0.41 ± 0.07), whereas AMSF remains largely invariant (0.08 ± 0.02). Transformation-consistent localization is evident: contour-aligned responses under sketch abstraction, broader contextual activation under fog corruption, and directional stabilization under motion blur. Quantitatively, MDHF demonstrates a 5.1× larger divergence in attribution distributions than AMSF (p < 0.001), providing mechanistic evidence of input-conditioned feature integration and explaining its robustness and fine-grained performance gains.

Together, SHAP and Grad-CAM + + analyses demonstrate that MDHF achieves stable, hierarchy-aware, and dynamically adaptive feature fusion, translating its meta-learned design into consistent, interpretable, and context-sensitive model behaviour.

Component ablation and functional prioritization

Given the architectural complexity of MDHF, we conducted prioritized ablation studies to examine the functional contribution of each component and to distinguish essential mechanisms from auxiliary refinements. The results indicate that performance improvements arise from a structured and synergistic design rather than from indiscriminate architectural expansion.

Across all datasets, removal of the meta-learned attention mechanism results in the largest performance degradation (Supplementary Table S13), identifying it as the primary contributor to MDHF’s adaptive behaviour. This finding supports its role in enabling input-dependent fusion policies, which cannot be replicated by static attention alone. Bidirectional hierarchical integration provides the next largest contribution, improving stability across scales and mitigating information attenuation commonly observed in sequential fusion pipelines. In contrast, sparse graph fusion and meta-gated aggregation yield smaller but consistent gains, contributing efficient relational modelling and adaptive output weighting, respectively. Importantly, the ablation results reveal limited redundancy between components. The full model consistently outperforms configurations in which individual modules are removed or combined additively, indicating positive interaction effects rather than simple accumulation of independent gains. To isolate the impact of the learning objective from architectural structure, we further compare MDHF with an architecturally identical variant trained using standard supervised learning (MDHF-Static). On the cross-domain Sketch benchmark, MDHF achieves a + 9.2% accuracy improvement over this static counterpart (Supplementary Table S14). This result indicates that the observed robustness under distribution shift is primarily attributable to the meta-learning objective, rather than to auxiliary architectural components alone. Together, these ablations clarify the functional hierarchy within MDHF: meta-learned attention provides the principal mechanism for adaptive generalization, while supporting components offer complementary benefits that enhance stability and efficiency without dominating performance. This decomposition addresses concerns regarding architectural over-engineering by demonstrating that gains are concentrated in a small number of well-defined mechanisms.

Meta-learning adaptation vs. test-time optimization

This section directly addresses the fundamental design choice raised in Sect. 3.5: why use meta-learning instead of simpler online adaptation? The results quantify the trade-off. MDHF enables gradient-free, input-conditioned behaviour at inference, fundamentally distinguishing it from test-time adaptation (TTA) and gradient-based meta-learning methods like MAML. While MAML can adapt, it does so by performing backpropagation during inference—incurring 47–57% more gradient steps for few-shot convergence (Table 9) and introducing unacceptable latency and complexity for real-time deployment. In contrast, MDHF generates input-specific fusion policies through a single forward pass, without any parameter updates. This efficiency stems from the meta-learned attention prior (M) that encodes cross-task knowledge, enabling effective one-step policy generation. The data in Table 9 shows that MDHF achieves comparable or superior adaptation quality with substantially lower computational overhead than gradient-based alternatives. This validates our methodological argument: MDHF occupies a favourable middle ground between the inflexibility of static conditional networks and the prohibitive cost of online optimization. The associated trade-off is the one-time cost of meta-training (approximately 71% longer than static training, Sect. 4.3). This upfront investment buys persistent operational advantages: 36% faster inference and the elimination of per-domain fine-tuning. Therefore, MDHF is optimally suited for dynamic, multi-domain deployments where long-term efficiency and adaptability are critical, while simpler models remain adequate for stable, single-domain settings.

Practical considerations and limitations

MDHF’s performance exhibits predictable operational boundaries under extreme, ultra-fine-grained, and resource-constrained scenarios. While it maintains a 4–11% accuracy advantage over baselines under large domain shifts (e.g., synthetic → real, VisDA-C; Supplementary Table S15), a 35.8% absolute drop indicates that meta-learned priors cannot fully compensate for fundamentally divergent low-level statistics. On ultra-fine-grained tasks (FGVC-Aircraft; Supplementary Table S16), gains are modest (+ 1.8% versus FC-Former), reflecting a spatial granularity threshold where discriminative features occupy fewer than 10 × 10 pixels at 14 × 14 feature maps. Insufficient meta-training diversity ( < ~ 5 domain styles) erodes adaptation advantages (Supplementary Table S17), highlighting the importance of exposure to sufficient variation for generalizable policy learning. MDHF’s inductive bias toward structural consistency imposes trade-offs on texture-focused tasks (DTD; Supplementary Table S18), where specialized baselines outperform by 5.3%. Deployment profiling further delineates practical limits: while GPU and TPU platforms achieve high efficiency (Supplementary Table S19), ultra-low-resource devices, such as microcontrollers with < 1 MB RAM, experience substantial overhead.

Mechanistic evaluation under rare, out-of-distribution scenarios further clarifies MDHF’s boundaries. Under extreme bird’s-eye viewpoints outside the meta-training distribution p(τ), MDHF exhibits diffuse, low-confidence attention, whereas FC-Former occasionally succeeds due to coincidental alignment with residual structural cues (Fig. 12). The attention difference map and accuracy versus viewpoint deviation reveal sharp degradation beyond ~ 60°, representing a hard generalization boundary of meta-learned fusion policies. Collectively, these results define a predictable operational envelope: MDHF is most effective in heterogeneous, fine-grained, and moderately shifted domains; however, its optimal performance depends on sufficient meta-training diversity, resolution-aware feature representations, and the availability of parallel computational resources. These limitations provide actionable guidance for future adaptation strategies in extreme, texture-centric, or resource-constrained scenarios.

Synthesis: principles of meta-learned policy generation

Across accuracy, efficiency, and robustness evaluations, MDHF’s advantages arise from learning how to generate fusion policies rather than fixing them a priori. Performance gains are largest under high visual ambiguity (e.g., fine-grained recognition), where static fusion is suboptimal and input-conditioned policy generation dynamically emphasizes discriminative cues. Cross-domain robustness is shown to stem from the meta-learning objective itself—not architectural complexity—as evidenced by the substantial degradation of an architecturally identical, statically trained variant. Efficiency is achieved through co-design: sparsified relational modeling and bidirectional hierarchies embed computational savings directly into the architecture, enabling dynamic behaviour without inference-time optimization. Robustness, however, is bounded by the meta-task distribution, with performance degrading under extreme, out-of-distribution shifts. Collectively, these results define MDHF’s operational regime: it is most effective for fine-grained, multi-domain settings where inference efficiency and adaptive generalization are critical, while simpler static fusion may suffice for stable, single-domain scenarios.

Discussion

Discussion: implications of meta-learned fusion policies

The results in Sect. 5 demonstrate that feature fusion is most effective when formulated as a policy generation problem rather than a fixed architectural choice. MDHF realizes this shift by learning a meta-level prior over fusion strategies, enabling input-conditioned feature integration while maintaining the efficiency of a single forward pass at inference. This perspective explains why MDHF consistently improves accuracy, robustness, and efficiency across heterogeneous domains while remaining parameter-efficient. Crucially, ablation results show that an architecturally identical model trained without meta-learning fails under domain shift, confirming that MDHF’s gains stem from the learned policy prior rather than from attention or graph structure alone. Interpretability analyses further support this view: unlike static fusion, MDHF dynamically reallocates attention under ambiguity, occlusion, and domain change, producing more structured and semantically aligned representations in a single forward pass. At the same time, observed degradations under extreme distribution shifts indicate that policy-based adaptation is inherently bounded by the support of the meta-training distribution, highlighting a principled trade-off between adaptability and extrapolation.

Conclusion and future work

We introduce Meta-Learned Dynamic Hierarchical Fusion (MDHF), reframing feature fusion as input-conditioned policy generation. Through bi-level meta-learning, MDHF acquires a transferable prior over fusion strategies, enabling dynamic, sample-specific integration in a single forward pass. This approach aims to balance the rigidity of static fusion with the latency of test-time adaptation, demonstrating context-aware adaptability while maintaining inference efficiency. Extensive evaluation across standard, fine-grained, and robustness benchmarks shows that MDHF consistently outperforms static fusion and contemporary transformer- or graph-based methods, particularly when discriminative cues vary across samples. Mechanistic analyses highlight gains in data efficiency, inference speed, and robustness, while delineating predictable limits under extreme domain shifts and ultra-fine-grained tasks.

Future directions include expanding the meta-task distribution, multi- and cross-modal fusion, uncertainty-aware policy generation, and hardware-efficient deployment, including atop pre-trained foundational models. MDHF establishes a principled, interpretable, and efficient paradigm for context-aware, multi-domain visual recognition.
