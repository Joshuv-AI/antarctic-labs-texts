Lipoprotein(a) and Cardiovascular Risk: Emerging Therapeutic Perspectives with Implications for Chronic Kidney Disease
Authors: Luca Di Lullo, Paola Peverini, Antonio Bellasi, Andrea Dello Strologo, Claudio Ronco, Pasquale Saporito, Aldo Franculli
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLipoprotein(a) (Lp(a)) is an low-density lipoprotein (LDL)-like particle containing apolipoprotein B100 covalently bound to apolipoprotein(a). Elevated Lp(a) is now recognized as a genetically determined, independent, and likely causal risk factor for atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease, calcific aortic valve stenosis, and residual cardiovascular risk despite optimal LDL cholesterol control. Current European and North American recommendations support measuring Lp(a) at least once in adulthood, particularly for cardiovascular risk refinement. Lp(a) levels are predominantly inherited and are only modestly influenced by lifestyle or conventional lipid-lowering therapies.SummaryChronic kidney disease (CKD) represents a clinically relevant setting in which Lp(a) levels may rise as renal function declines, contributing to the excess cardiovascular burden and residual risk (including severe vascular calcification) left unaddressed by historical statin trials in the end-stage renal disease population. However, the causal role of Lp(a) in CKD-related cardiovascular disease remains incompletely defined.Key MessagesNovel RNA-based and small molecule therapies, including pelacarsen, olpasiran, lepodisiran, zerlasiran, and muvalaplin, have shown marked Lp(a)-lowering effects, but definitive cardiovascular outcome data are still missing.

Introduction

For several decades, lipoprotein(a) [Lp(a)] remained an underrecognized component of cardiovascular risk assessment despite accumulating evidence, suggesting its role in vascular disease. Initially described by Berg in 1963 as a distinct lipoprotein antigen, Lp(a) was considered a biochemical curiosity because its physiological function remained uncertain and available laboratory methods lacked standardization [1]. However, advances in genetics, molecular biology, and large-scale epidemiological studies have progressively transformed understanding of Lp(a), positioning it as one of the most important inherited cardiovascular risk factors.

Current estimates suggest that approximately 20–25% of the global population exhibits elevated Lp(a) concentrations, although substantial interindividual and ethnic variability exists [2]. Plasma concentrations are predominantly determined by inheritance through the LPA gene locus and become relatively stable after childhood. Unlike LDL cholesterol, Lp(a) concentrations are minimally influenced by diet, physical activity, or environmental factors [3].

The burden of cardiovascular disease in the chronic kidney disease (CKD) population – a condition affecting approximately 10%–15% of the global population – is exceptionally high. Notably, patients with CKD are up to 10–20 times more likely to die from cardiovascular events than to progress to end-stage renal disease (ESRD). Consequently, cardiovascular mortality accounts for the majority of deaths in advanced stages, being responsible for 40%–50% of all fatalities among patients undergoing dialysis [4]. Historical trials investigating statin therapy in ESRD – such as the 4D (Die Deutsche Diabetes Dialyse Studie) and AURORA (A Study to Evaluate the Use of Rosuvastatin in Subjects on Regular Hemodialysis: An Assessment of Survival and Cardiovascular Events) trials – failed to demonstrate a significant reduction in cardiovascular mortality [5, 6]. These failures highlighted the complex and distinct pathophysiology of uremic atherosclerosis, which is not solely driven by LDL cholesterol. In this context, elevated Lp(a) has emerged as a plausible explanation for a substantial portion of this residual risk burden, contributing not only to atherothrombotic events but also to the highly prevalent calcific aortic valve stenosis seen in CKD patients. The identification of Lp(a) as an independent risk factor sets the stage for exploring novel, targeted therapies in a population where traditional lipid-lowering approaches fall short.

Large observational cohorts, Mendelian randomization analyses, and genome-wide association studies have demonstrated strong associations between elevated Lp(a) and a broad spectrum of cardiovascular diseases, including coronary artery disease, myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, peripheral arterial disease, and calcific aortic valve stenosis [7, 8]. Importantly, genetic studies strongly support a causal role rather than a simple epidemiological association [8].

The growing interest in Lp(a) also derives from its contribution to residual cardiovascular risk. Despite widespread use of statins and intensive LDL-C reduction strategies, many patients continue to experience cardiovascular events. Elevated Lp(a) has emerged as a plausible explanation for part of this residual risk burden [9].

Consequently, recent guideline recommendations increasingly advocate Lp(a) measurement at least once during adulthood, especially in individuals with premature atherosclerotic cardiovascular disease (ASCVD), familial hypercholesterolemia, recurrent events despite optimal therapy, or a family history of early cardiovascular disease [2, 10].

The emergence of highly effective Lp(a)-targeted therapies has further stimulated interest in this lipoprotein. For the first time, profound reductions in circulating concentrations exceeding 80–90% appear achievable, raising the possibility that selective Lp(a) lowering may become a new paradigm in preventive cardiology [11].

Structure and Pathophysiology

Lp(a) is a complex lipoprotein particle structurally resembling low-density lipoprotein (LDL). It consists of an LDL-like core containing apolipoprotein B100 covalently linked via a disulfide bond to apolipoprotein(a), or apo(a), a distinctive glycoprotein synthesized primarily by hepatocytes [12].

The unique biological properties of Lp(a) largely derive from apo(a). Structurally, apo(a) exhibits significant homology with plasminogen because it contains multiple kringle domains, particularly kringle IV type repeats. Among these, kringle IV type 2 displays marked variability in copy number, producing substantial heterogeneity in apo(a) isoform size [13] (Fig. 1).

This variability is clinically important because smaller apo(a) isoforms generally correspond to higher circulating Lp(a) concentrations and increased cardiovascular risk [14]. The inverse relationship between apo(a) size and plasma concentration reflects differences in hepatic production efficiency and particle secretion rates.

Although the physiological role of Lp(a) remains incompletely understood, several hypotheses have been proposed. Suggested functions include wound healing, tissue repair, host defense mechanisms, and transport of oxidized phospholipids; however, no definitive biological role has been established [15].

The pathogenic effects of Lp(a) appear multifactorial and involve proatherogenic, proinflammatory, and potentially prothrombotic pathways (Fig. 1). First, Lp(a) contributes directly to atherogenesis through cholesterol deposition within the arterial wall, similar to LDL particles. Once retained in the subendothelial space, Lp(a) promotes lipid accumulation and foam cell formation [16].

Second, Lp(a) acts as a major carrier of oxidized phospholipids, molecules increasingly recognized as potent mediators of vascular inflammation. Oxidized phospholipids stimulate endothelial activation, increase expression of adhesion molecules, recruit inflammatory cells, and induce smooth muscle cell proliferation [17].

These mechanisms accelerate plaque formation and contribute to instability of established lesions. A third proposed mechanism concerns thrombosis. Because apo(a) structurally resembles plasminogen, competition for binding sites may impair fibrinolytic activity and promote thrombus persistence [18]. Apo(a) may interfere with plasmin generation and reduce clot dissolution, although the clinical relevance of this pathway remains debated.

Additionally, Lp(a) appears strongly implicated in calcific aortic valve disease. Experimental studies suggest that oxidized phospholipid cargo transported by Lp(a) stimulates osteogenic signaling pathways and promotes differentiation of valvular interstitial cells into calcifying phenotypes [19]. These diverse biological properties likely explain why elevated Lp(a) is associated not only with coronary disease but also with valvular pathology and systemic vascular disease.

Lp(a) Amplification of Injury in the Uremic Milieu

The pathophysiological impact of Lp(a) is profoundly amplified in the context of renal impairment [2]. The uremic milieu is characterized by chronic inflammation, increased oxidative stress, and endothelial dysfunction, which synergize with the proatherogenic properties of Lp(a) [8]. Furthermore, Lp(a) is intimately linked to CKD-mineral and bone disorder. The oxidized phospholipids transported by the apo(a) moiety are potent inducers of osteogenic transformation in vascular smooth muscle cells. In the presence of uremia and dysregulated calcium-phosphate metabolism, this pathway drastically accelerates vascular and valvular calcification, representing a CKD-specific mechanism of injury that standard lipid-lowering therapies cannot halt [20].

Lp(a) and Cardiovascular Risk

Lp(a) is now considered one of the strongest inherited determinants of ASCVD. Unlike conventional lipid fractions, circulating Lp(a) concentrations are largely genetically determined by the LPA gene locus and remain relatively stable throughout life after early childhood. Approximately 70–90% of interindividual variability in Lp(a) concentrations is genetically driven, with apo(a) isoform size being a major determinant of plasma levels. Smaller apo(a) isoforms are generally associated with higher Lp(a) concentrations and increased cardiovascular risk [15, 21, 22]. This genetic basis explains why lifestyle interventions, including diet and exercise, have minimal influence on circulating levels (Fig. 1).

Substantial epidemiological, genetic, and mechanistic evidence supports a causal relationship between elevated Lp(a) and cardiovascular disease. Mendelian randomization studies have played a central role in establishing causality by demonstrating that genetically elevated Lp(a) concentrations are associated with a higher lifetime risk of myocardial infarction, ischemic stroke, peripheral arterial disease, heart failure, and calcific aortic valve stenosis [23]. Unlike observational associations alone, these studies strengthen the concept that Lp(a) is not merely a biomarker but an active contributor to disease pathogenesis.

The cardiovascular effects of Lp(a) are thought to result from a combination of proatherogenic, proinflammatory, and potentially prothrombotic mechanisms. Structurally, Lp(a) resembles LDL but carries an additional apolipoprotein(a) moiety, which transports oxidized phospholipids. These oxidized phospholipids contribute to endothelial dysfunction, vascular inflammation, monocyte recruitment, smooth muscle proliferation, and plaque instability [24]. Furthermore, because apo(a) shares structural homology with plasminogen, interference with fibrinolytic pathways has been proposed as an additional mechanism contributing to thrombosis, although this remains incompletely understood [25].

Importantly, cardiovascular risk associated with Lp(a) appears to be continuous rather than threshold-based. Although concentrations ≥50 mg/dL (approximately ≥125 nmol/L) are commonly used as clinically relevant cut-offs, evidence suggests that risk progressively increases across a broad range of concentrations [15, 26]. Data from large population studies have shown that individuals with Lp(a) levels above the 90th–95th percentile exhibit substantially greater rates of cardiovascular events compared with individuals with lower concentrations [27].

Recent analyses have also highlighted the role of Lp(a) as an important determinant of residual cardiovascular risk despite optimal LDL-C management. Even among patients achieving intensive LDL-C reduction through statins and other lipid-lowering therapies, elevated Lp(a) remains associated with recurrent cardiovascular events [28]. This observation has become particularly relevant in secondary prevention settings and may explain why some individuals continue to experience events despite guideline-directed treatment.

Several patient populations may particularly benefit from Lp(a) assessment, including individuals with premature ASCVD, recurrent cardiovascular events despite apparently adequate lipid control, familial hypercholesterolemia, or a strong family history of early cardiovascular disease. Consequently, contemporary guidelines increasingly support measurement of Lp(a) at least once during adult life for cardiovascular risk refinement [21, 29].

An additional clinically important aspect of Lp(a)-associated risk concerns calcific aortic valve stenosis. Genetic and observational studies consistently demonstrate associations between elevated Lp(a) and accelerated valvular calcification [19, 30]. Oxidized phospholipids transported by Lp(a) may promote osteogenic differentiation of valvular interstitial cells, leading to progressive calcium deposition and valve degeneration [31, 32]. Notably, no currently available therapy has convincingly slowed progression of calcific aortic stenosis, raising interest in Lp(a)-targeted interventions as a potential therapeutic strategy (Fig. 1). Collectively, these findings have shifted the clinical perception of Lp(a) from a secondary lipid parameter to a major inherited cardiovascular risk factor and an increasingly attractive therapeutic target.

Lp(a) in CKD

CKD is associated with profound lipid and lipoprotein abnormalities, systemic inflammation, oxidative stress, vascular calcification, and markedly increased cardiovascular risk [33, 34]. Several studies suggest that Lp(a) levels tend to increase as kidney function declines, and patients with CKD often show higher Lp(a) concentrations than individuals with preserved renal function [35–37]. A recent review reported that CKD patients exhibit both increased cardiovascular risk and elevated Lp(a) concentrations, although only a limited number of studies have specifically addressed the independent contribution of Lp(a) in this population [35].

The relationship between Lp(a) and kidney disease may be bidirectional. Reduced renal catabolism, proteinuria-associated changes in hepatic lipoprotein synthesis, inflammation, and altered apo(a) isoform distribution may contribute to higher Lp(a) levels in CKD [36, 38, 39]. Conversely, genetic and observational studies suggest that elevated Lp(a) concentrations may be associated with incident CKD and progressive renal dysfunction, although causality remains incompletely established and results remain somewhat heterogeneous [40, 41].

This heterogeneity is largely attributable to the confounding effects of the malnutrition-inflammation complex syndrome prevalent in advanced CKD and ESRD, which can paradoxically lower cholesterol fractions, while overall cardiovascular risk escalates, obscuring the predictive value of Lp(a) in observational cohorts. To clarify this relationship, longitudinal observations from the Chronic Renal Insufficiency Cohort (CRIC) study have been instrumental. Data from CRIC indicate that higher Lp(a) levels are independently associated with an increased risk for future kidney failure, although its ability to predict recurrent ASCVD events in CKD appears more prominent in patients without concurrent diabetes [42]. Additionally, findings from the SHARP (Study of Heart and Renal Protection) trial clearly demonstrated that while lowering LDL-C via a statin/ezetimibe combination reduces atherosclerotic events in CKD, a severe residual risk remains. This remaining burden is heavily influenced by unmodified, genetically determined lipid fractions such as Lp(a), identifying a high-risk population that requires targeted interventions beyond standard LDL-C control [43].

In dialysis patients, Lp(a) may contribute to accelerated atherosclerosis, vascular calcification, endothelial dysfunction, and thrombotic risk, but currently available evidence remains heterogeneous [35, 44]. In the setting of kidney transplantation, successful engraftment is generally associated with a reduction in Lp(a) concentrations, reflecting improved renal function and restoration of metabolic catabolic pathways. However, this physiological benefit is often heavily confounded by the choice of immunosuppressive regimens. For instance, mammalian target of rapamycin inhibitors are known to potentially exacerbate systemic dyslipidemia to a greater extent than calcineurin inhibitors, further complicating the post-transplant lipid profile [38, 45].

At present, Lp(a) measurement in CKD may provide useful information for cardiovascular risk stratification, particularly in patients with high residual risk despite standard management; however, specific CKD-tailored treatment thresholds and intervention strategies have not yet been established [38, 39].

Current and Emerging Lp(a)-Lowering Strategies

Lifestyle and Conventional Lipid-Lowering Therapy

Lifestyle changes have major cardiovascular benefits, but generally do not meaningfully reduce Lp(a) because Lp(a) is largely genetically determined. Statins may slightly increase or have neutral effects on Lp(a), but they remain essential for LDL-C lowering and overall cardiovascular risk reduction. Ezetimibe has little effect on Lp(a). PCSK9-targeted therapies – which include both monoclonal antibodies and small interfering RNA (siRNA) – demonstrate excellent efficacy in profoundly reducing LDL-C [46]. While they reduce Lp(a) only modestly, usually by about 20–30%, they are currently useful mainly for this dramatic LDL-C reduction and residual risk management. Lipoprotein apheresis can reduce Lp(a), but it is invasive, resource-intensive, and generally reserved for selected patients with progressive cardiovascular disease and very high Lp(a) [47] (Table 1).

Pelacarsen

Pelacarsen (formerly TQJ230 or AKCEA-APO(a)-LRx) is an antisense oligonucleotide specifically designed to inhibit hepatic production of apolipoprotein(a). Similar to other GalNAc-conjugated therapies, pelacarsen uses receptor-mediated uptake into hepatocytes, allowing selective inhibition of apo(a) synthesis and reduction of circulating Lp(a) concentrations [48].

Pelacarsen represented one of the earliest targeted pharmacological approaches developed specifically against Lp(a). Initial phase 1 and phase 2 investigations demonstrated substantial dose-dependent reductions in Lp(a), with maximal reductions approaching or exceeding 80% [43]. Importantly, the degree of Lp(a) lowering observed with pelacarsen far exceeded reductions achievable with conventional therapies such as PCSK9 inhibitors.

The phase 2 randomized study by Tsimikas and colleagues demonstrated reductions up to approximately 80–90% among patients with established cardiovascular disease and elevated baseline Lp(a). Furthermore, therapy significantly reduced oxidized phospholipid burden, supporting a potential effect on pathways implicated in plaque progression and vascular inflammation [49].

Safety analyses showed generally acceptable tolerability. The most commonly reported adverse events included injection-site reactions and mild flu-like symptoms. Significant hepatic toxicity, thrombocytopenia, or severe systemic adverse effects were not observed in early studies, although larger populations and longer follow-up remain necessary for comprehensive safety assessment [49].

Pelacarsen currently occupies a central position in the field because of the ongoing phase 3 Lp(a)HORIZON trial. This study enrolled more than 8,000 patients with established cardiovascular disease and elevated Lp(a) levels and is designed to determine whether substantial Lp(a) reduction translates into reductions in major adverse cardiovascular events [50]. Unlike earlier lipid studies relying primarily on surrogate biomarker endpoints, Lp(a)HORIZON represents the first large-scale outcomes trial specifically designed to establish the clinical benefit of targeted Lp(a) reduction [50].

The results of this trial may have broad implications for preventive cardiology and potentially redefine cardiovascular risk management in patients with elevated inherited Lp(a). If successful, pelacarsen could become the first approved therapy specifically targeting this important cardiovascular risk factor (Table 1).

However, it is crucial to note that the Lp(a) HORIZON trial primarily targets patients with preserved or moderately reduced renal function, structurally excluding those with advanced CKD (eGFR < 30 mL/min/1.73m2) or patients on dialysis. This exclusion highlights a critical clinical gap, as the ESRD population stands to benefit significantly from targeted therapy given their unaddressed calcific and atherothrombotic burden.

Olpasiran

Olpasiran is a hepatocyte-directed siRNA designed to reduce hepatic synthesis of apolipoprotein(a) through selective degradation of LPA messenger RNA. The molecule is conjugated with N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc), facilitating targeted delivery to hepatocytes via the asialoglycoprotein receptor and allowing infrequent administration schedules. This strategy exploits the prolonged intracellular persistence of siRNA-mediated gene silencing and may provide durable suppression of Lp(a) production [51].

Interest in olpasiran increased substantially following results from the phase 2 OCEAN(a)-DOSE trial, a randomized study involving patients with established ASCVD and elevated Lp(a) concentrations above 150 nmol/L. Multiple dosing regimens were investigated, including administration every 12 or 24 weeks. Treatment produced profound and sustained reductions in circulating Lp(a), with reductions exceeding 90% at higher dose regimens [52]. Importantly, suppression persisted long after administration, suggesting prolonged pharmacodynamic activity [53].

Beyond lowering total Lp(a), olpasiran also reduced oxidized phospholipid content associated with apoB and apo(a), raising the possibility that therapy may modify inflammatory and proatherogenic pathways beyond simple particle reduction [2, 7]. This observation is biologically important because oxidized phospholipids are believed to mediate much of the vascular toxicity attributed to Lp(a) [54].

Safety findings from phase 2 studies have been reassuring. Adverse events were generally mild and consisted mainly of injection-site reactions, with no major hepatotoxicity or clinically relevant laboratory abnormalities reported [51–54]. However, available studies involved relatively limited patient populations and short follow-up periods, making long-term safety assessment essential.

The key unanswered question remains whether marked biochemical reductions in Lp(a) translate into improved cardiovascular outcomes. To address this issue, the ongoing phase 3 OCEAN(a)-Outcomes trial is evaluating whether olpasiran reduces major adverse cardiovascular events among patients with established ASCVD and elevated Lp(a). Results from this study are expected to represent a critical step toward defining the clinical utility of Lp(a)-targeted therapy [55] (Table 1).

Similar to the pelacarsen trials, the OCEAN(a)-Outcomes study excludes patients with severe renal impairment. Consequently, the efficacy and safety profile of olpasiran in the advanced CKD and transplant populations will require dedicated future nephrological registries.

Lepodisiran, Zerlasiran, and Muvalaplin

Lepodisiran is a long-duration siRNA targeting hepatic apo(a) production. In the ALPACA trial, lepodisiran reduced mean Lp(a) concentrations substantially from 60 to 180 days after administration, with reported reductions approaching 94% at high doses and no serious drug-related adverse events in the mid-stage trial [56] (Table 1).

Zerlasiran is another siRNA-based approach under clinical investigation. Recent reports suggest durable Lp(a) reductions in the range of approximately 80–85% over extended follow-up, although outcome data are not yet available [57] (Table 1).

Muvalaplin is an oral small molecule that inhibits Lp(a) particle assembly. In early-phase and phase 2 studies, muvalaplin reduced Lp(a) by up to approximately 65–86%, depending on dose and assay method. Its oral route makes it particularly attractive, but cardiovascular outcome evidence is still pending [58] (Table 1). Across all these novel agents, pharmacokinetic safety in advanced uremia and interactions with dialytic modalities remain critical areas for future investigation.

Clinical Implications

The increasing recognition of Lp(a) as an independent and genetically determined cardiovascular risk factor has important implications for contemporary cardiovascular prevention and risk stratification [59]. Historically, Lp(a) measurement was reserved for selected clinical situations, largely because of uncertainty regarding assay standardization, limited therapeutic options, and incomplete understanding of its causal role in disease development. However, the convergence of epidemiological, genetic, and mechanistic evidence has substantially altered this perspective [60].

Current evidence supports the concept that elevated Lp(a) contributes significantly to residual cardiovascular risk despite achievement of recommended LDL-C targets [61]. Indeed, numerous patients continue to experience cardiovascular events even under intensive statin treatment and optimal lipid management. Elevated Lp(a) may partially explain this residual burden and identify individuals whose cardiovascular risk is underestimated by conventional algorithms [62].

Current international guidelines increasingly support at least one lifetime measurement of Lp(a), particularly among individuals at increased cardiovascular risk. The European Atherosclerosis Society consensus statement and recent updates from the National Lipid Association recommend broader use of Lp(a) testing as part of cardiovascular risk refinement. However, implementation remains heterogeneous, and Lp(a) testing is still underutilized in routine clinical practice [28, 60].

Interpretation of Lp(a) measurements presents several practical challenges. Considerable heterogeneity exists among available assays, particularly because apo(a) isoform size variability may influence measurements. Furthermore, values may be reported in either mg/dL or nmol/L, and conversion between these units is not straightforward because particle mass and particle number are not linearly interchangeable. These methodological considerations complicate comparisons among studies and may influence clinical decision-making [9].

An additional clinically relevant consideration concerns the continuous nature of Lp(a)-associated risk. Although concentrations ≥50 mg/dL or approximately ≥125 nmol/L are commonly considered clinically significant, cardiovascular risk appears to increase progressively across increasing concentrations without a clear biological threshold. Consequently, risk interpretation should ideally consider the broader clinical context rather than relying solely on predefined cutoffs [9].

Importantly, elevated Lp(a) currently influences management strategies even in the absence of approved Lp(a)-specific therapies. In patients with markedly elevated concentrations, clinicians frequently adopt more aggressive reduction of modifiable cardiovascular risk factors, including intensified LDL-C lowering, blood pressure optimization, smoking cessation, lifestyle modification, and tighter metabolic control [63].

PCSK9 inhibitors may be particularly relevant in selected high-risk populations because they reduce both LDL-C and Lp(a), although the magnitude of Lp(a) reduction remains modest compared with newer RNA-based therapies [64]. Lipoprotein apheresis remains a therapeutic option in selected individuals with progressive cardiovascular disease and extremely elevated Lp(a), although availability and cost substantially limit widespread use [65].

Clinical implications may be particularly important among patients with CKD, a population characterized by markedly elevated cardiovascular risk and complex metabolic disturbances. Emerging evidence suggests that Lp(a) may help identify CKD patients at particularly high residual cardiovascular risk despite conventional treatment [35]. However, specific thresholds and management recommendations for CKD populations have not yet been established.

The emergence of highly effective targeted therapies including pelacarsen, olpasiran, lepodisiran, and other RNA-based agents may substantially reshape clinical management over the coming years. Outcome trials currently underway will determine whether profound reductions in Lp(a) concentrations translate into meaningful reductions in cardiovascular events. Positive results could fundamentally alter cardiovascular prevention paradigms and support broader implementation of routine Lp(a) screening [48–58, 66]. Overall, incorporation of Lp(a) into cardiovascular risk assessment increasingly represents an important step toward precision medicine and individualized prevention strategies [9].

Discussion

Over the past decade, Lp(a) has evolved from a relatively neglected lipoprotein particle into one of the most important inherited determinants of cardiovascular risk. Accumulating evidence from epidemiological studies, Mendelian randomization analyses, and mechanistic investigations strongly supports a causal role for elevated Lp(a) in ASCVD, residual cardiovascular risk, and calcific aortic valve disease. These findings have substantially reshaped current concepts of cardiovascular risk assessment and increasingly support the incorporation of Lp(a) into routine preventive strategies.

Beyond its role in the general population, Lp(a) appears particularly relevant in selected high-risk clinical settings, including familial hypercholesterolemia, premature cardiovascular disease, and CKD. In CKD populations, where vascular calcification, inflammation, oxidative stress, and accelerated atherosclerosis coexist, elevated Lp(a) may contribute to the excessive cardiovascular burden observed across all stages of renal dysfunction. Nevertheless, important uncertainties remain regarding its precise pathophysiological contribution and its potential role as a therapeutic target in kidney disease.

A major paradigm shift has emerged with the development of highly effective Lp(a)-lowering strategies. Novel RNA-based therapies, including pelacarsen, olpasiran, lepodisiran, and additional agents currently under investigation, can achieve reductions in circulating Lp(a) concentrations exceeding 80–90%, far beyond those obtainable with currently available lipid-lowering therapies. For the first time, selective targeting of inherited Lp(a)-mediated cardiovascular risk appears biologically and clinically feasible.

Despite this progress, several important questions remain unresolved. The optimal thresholds for intervention, timing of screening, cost-effectiveness of widespread implementation, and long-term safety of novel therapies require further clarification. Most importantly, definitive evidence is still needed to establish whether profound biochemical reductions in Lp(a) translate into meaningful reductions in cardiovascular morbidity and mortality.

The results of ongoing outcome studies, including Lp(a)HORIZON and OCEAN(a)-Outcomes, may therefore represent a pivotal moment in preventive cardiology. Positive findings could fundamentally redefine cardiovascular prevention strategies and establish Lp(a) as a central therapeutic target within precision medicine approaches. Future investigations should also address underrepresented populations, particularly patients with advanced CKD, dialysis dependence, and kidney transplantation, in whom residual cardiovascular risk remains exceptionally high.

Overall, the field of Lp(a) research is entering a transformative phase. What was once considered a relatively obscure biomarker may soon become a routine component of individualized cardiovascular risk assessment and a target for precision therapeutic intervention.
