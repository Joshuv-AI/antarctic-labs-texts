Vesico-cutaneous fistula presenting as medial thigh sepsis following salvage prostate cancer radiotherapy
Authors: Rachel Cockburn, Anthony Kiosoglous
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Vesico-cutaneous fistula is a rare complication following prostate cancer treatment, often involving a complex interplay between prior radiation and subsequent instrumentation. We present a case of a 71-year-old male presenting with acute medial thigh sepsis. Imaging revealed a vesico-cutaneous fistula tracking from a posterior-inferior bladder defect, through the pubic symphysis (causing osteomyelitis), and into the adductor compartment. Though a salvage cystectomy was not feasible, urinary diversion alone was successful in ameliorating the patient's symptoms and achieving clinical control. This case emphasizes maintaining a high index of clinical suspicion for urinary fistulation in irradiated patients with atypical soft tissue presentations.

Introduction

The evolution of prostate cancer management has seen a rise in the use of multimodal therapies, including radical prostatectomy followed by salvage radiotherapy for biochemical recurrence. While effective, these treatments carry a risk of late-onset urological complications.1 Among the most challenging is the development of a vesico-cutaneous fistula, a rare pathological process often delayed by years or even decades following the initial insult.

Fistula formation in the pelvic region is typically associated with penetrating trauma or immediate iatrogenic injury. However, in the context of prostate cancer survivors, the pathophysiology is frequently established in chronic radiation-induced injury.2 Such fistulae often involve the pubic symphysis, creating a “uro-symphyseal" tract that can propagate into adjacent anatomical compartments. Because these presentations can mimic primary musculoskeletal or soft tissue infections, they often present a diagnostic dilemma for clinicians. This report details a complex case of thigh sepsis resulting from a vesico-cutaneous fistula and discusses the diagnostic and management strategies for this rare entity.

Case presentation

A 71-year-old male presented to the emergency department with acute, painful swelling and erythema of the right medial thigh. The patient appeared systemically unwell, with localized signs suggestive of deep-seated sepsis. His medical history was significant for localized prostate cancer treated ten years prior with a radical prostatectomy, followed by salvage radiotherapy due to rising PSA levels.

In the decade following his oncological treatment, the patient suffered from recurrent refractory bladder neck contractures. These were complicated by the development of dystrophic calcification at the bladder neck, a known consequence of chronic radiation injury and subsequent tissue ischemia. To manage these complications, the patient underwent multiple endoscopic interventions, including repeated dilatations, bladder neck incisions, and cystolithopaxy to remove calcified debris.

Upon presentation with thigh symptoms, initial clinical suspicion favoured a primary soft tissue infection or abscess. However, given his extensive urological history, advanced cross-sectional imaging was performed. Magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) and computer tomography (CT) demonstrated a significant defect in the posterior-inferior bladder wall. The patient was initially stabilized with broad-spectrum intravenous antibiotics. Given the complexity of the fistula, a salvage cystectomy with ileal conduit formation was planned. However, intraoperatively, the procedure had to be abandoned. The surgeon encountered “frozen" pelvic anatomy with inoperable pelvic adhesions and extremely poor tissue quality, likely exacerbated by the previous radiotherapy and chronic inflammation.

A shift to conservative management was necessitated. Long-term urinary diversion was achieved via a suprapubic catheter to keep the bladder defunctionalized. This was coupled with a prolonged course of targeted antibiotic therapy and the application of compression to the medial thigh compartment to encourage the closure of the fascial planes. Over several weeks, the patient showed significant clinical improvement, the thigh sepsis resolved, and he was successfully managed without further major surgical intervention.

Discussion

Vesico-cutaneous fistulae are uncommon and usually follow trauma or surgery. However, in urological oncology, they represent a late-stage manifestation of radiation-induced tissue damage.3,4 The pathophysiology of radiation injury involves progressive microvascular compromise, endarteritis obliterans, and subsequent fibrosis.5 This biologically compromised environment impairs normal wound healing and predisposes patients to refractory bladder neck contractures.6

A critical feature in this case was the presence of dystrophic calcification. This calcification likely reflected the severity of the radiation-induced tissue necrosis and acted as a chronic nidus for inflammation. Repeated endoscopic manipulation of this fragile, calcified tissue can precipitate mucosal breakdown, leading to urinary extravasation into the pre-vesical space and eventual fistulation.

The presentation of this fistula as medial thigh sepsis is particularly rare. While uro-symphyseal fistulae are known to cause pubic osteomyelitis,4 the extension into the adductor compartment occurs in a minority of cases.7 One multicentre study by the Trauma and Urologic Reconstruction Network of Surgeons (TURNS) reported that infection propagates along these fascial planes in approximately 38% of radiation-associated urinary fistulation cases.2 Because the clinical signs are distal to the bladder, there is a significant risk of misdiagnosing these patients with primary musculoskeletal pathology.8

Early and precise imaging is the cornerstone of diagnosis. As demonstrated in this case, while fluoroscopy and CT are useful for identifying bladder defects, MRI is superior for defining the full extent of the fistulous tract and its involvement with pelvic musculature. A multidisciplinary approach involving urologists, radiologists, and infectious disease specialists is often required to navigate these complexities.

Management remains highly challenging. While surgical reconstruction or urinary diversion is often the preferred definitive treatment, it is frequently fraught with high complication rates. The “frozen pelvis" encountered in this patient highlights the technical difficulties of salvage surgery in an irradiated field where tissue planes are obliterated by fibrosis. Retention of the defunctionalised bladder following urinary diversion carries a recognised risk of complications, including pyocystitis, particularly in the absence of a patent urethra to allow drainage. In selected patients, a supratrigonal cystectomy may be considered to mitigate this risk while avoiding the technical challenges associated with complete cystectomy in a hostile, irradiated pelvis.2 In such scenarios, conservative strategies, including long-term catheterization and prolonged antimicrobial therapy, can provide an effective alternative for clinical control and symptom relief.

Conclusion

Vesico-cutaneous fistula is a rare but devastating delayed complication of prostate cancer treatment, particularly following salvage radiotherapy. This case illustrates that urinary fistulae can present as atypical thigh sepsis, requiring a high degree of clinical suspicion. Early cross-sectional imaging (CT/MRI) is vital for accurate diagnosis. While surgical intervention is often limited by poor tissue quality, conservative management with diversion and antibiotics remains a viable pathway for complex case.
