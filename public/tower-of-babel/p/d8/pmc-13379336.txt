Introduction

The management of patients with cardiac implantable electronic devices (CIEDs) undergoing radiation therapy (RT) requires balancing oncologic necessity with device safety. RT poses a recognized but incompletely characterized risk to CIEDs. For transvenous CIEDs, several mechanisms of malfunction in the setting of radiation have been described, such as semiconductor damage, electromagnetic interference, and neutron scatter, yet there is a paucity of data for subcutaneous implantable cardioverter-defibrillators (S-ICD).1,2 The EMBLEM S-ICD system (Boston Scientific, Marlborough, MA), with its lateral thoracic generator and subcutaneous lead, is typically considered less vulnerable to RT, given the absence of intracardiac components. Here, we report a rare case of incessant S-ICD reset during pelvic external beam RT (EBRT) despite appropriate shielding per standard protocol and being outside the direct treatment field. This S-ICD malfunction required device extraction and new transvenous system implantation. To our knowledge, this represents the first reported case of its kind.

Case report

A 53-year-old man with Brugada syndrome (type 1 electrocardiographic pattern), prior syncope, ventricular tachycardia, and a family history of sudden cardiac death who underwent S-ICD implantation in 2010 (Boston Scientific EMBLEM A219 generator, 3400 electrode) and then generator replacement in 2016 and October 2023 because of battery depletion (Figure 1A–1C). The patient had several documented ventricular arrhythmias and appropriate therapies from his ICD during the 14 years of follow-up before current presentation (Figure 1B). The S-ICD system functioned appropriately with stable parameters and effective sensing. In January 2024, he was diagnosed with prostate adenocarcinoma with no significant metastatic lesion (T1cN0M0, stage IIIA, Gleason score 7, and prostate-specific antigen 25 ng/mL). Therefore, the patient began receiving hormonal therapy (enzalutamide/apalutamide/abiraterone), EBRT, and brachytherapy in March 2024. EBRT was delivered using intensity-modulated radiation therapy technique and bladder neck sparing brachytherapy. Intensity-modulated radiation therapy has been selected to achieve greater sparing of normal critical structures including bladder and rectum. The treatment dose was 180 cGy × 28 = 5050 cGy, with 5 fractions per week. Additional internal radiation treatment with low dose rate brachytherapy dose was 110 Gy.

Despite the S-ICD generator, positioned in the typically left lateral thoracic region, located outside the direct treatment field, and shielded per standard protocol, the patient noticed repeated audible alarms from the CIED after receiving a total of 60 Gy in 20 fractions over 4 weeks (3 Gy per fraction) through his RT course. This alarm prompted immediate medical attention. Device interrogation revealed a full reset of the system, with restoration to factory-programmed settings, complete memory loss with programming changes, and a power-on reset. The lead and generator parameters were unchanged from the previous evaluation except a small increase in the impedance (100–104 Ω) and lower sensing of R-wave amplitude. There were no abnormalities regarding charge time, battery voltage, or lead integrity. With the help from the technical service of the device manufacturer, the S-ICD was extensively tested and reprogrammed in various configurations. However, constant S-ICD resets occurred without provocation. The manufacturer was unable to identify a specific etiology for this repetitive resetting. Because of this, the clinical team determined that the S-ICD was not safe to remain active because of the risk of inappropriate therapies in addition to potential ineffective therapy for the true ventricular arrhythmias. Given the urgency of completing the RT protocol and the time-sensitive nature of the nonmetastatic prostate cancer treatment, the decision was made to deactivate the S-ICD. The patient was bridged with a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator (LifeVest, Zoll Medical, Chelmsford, MA) during the remainder of RT. He completed RT with no arrhythmic episodes or shocks during the bridging period. We retested the device repeatedly after RT completion and found that the recurrent device resetting was persistent. There was no improvement of device functionality. 6 weeks after RT completion, the patient underwent successful S-ICD extraction followed by implantation of a new transvenous single-chamber ICD system (Boston Scientific D232 with a 0672 right ventricular lead) with a left pectoral generator location. Transvenous ICD system was preferred over S-ICD system at this time, given the patient’s ventricular tachycardia (Figure 1B) could have been terminated with antitachycardia pacing. Furthermore, the patient has been taking β-blocker and quinidine, which required rare pacing. Postprocedure recovery was uncomplicated, and the new system demonstrated stable function for 20 months of follow-up.

Bench analysis of the extracted S-ICD system by the manufacturer’s engineers revealed no specific mechanical defect. The detailed laboratory testing confirmed a device memory error. It was reported as the error resulted in software resets performed in an attempt to correct an identified memory inconsistency. The cause of the memory inconsistency was most likely the high energy particles the device was subjected to during the patient’s RT. The device performed as expected in this situation.

Discussion

Management of patients with CIEDs undergoing RT is critical to balance the risks to the device and the benefits for cancer treatment. Here, we present a unique case that highlights a rare malfunction of an S-ICD temporally associated with pelvic RT. Unlike transvenous systems, S-ICDs lack intracardiac components, reducing susceptibility to direct current induction. However, the generator’s solid-state circuitry remains vulnerable to ionizing radiation and secondary scatter, particularly from high-energy photon beams (>10 MV).3

Previous studies have primarily reported transient oversensing or telemetry disruption during RT; persistent resets or irreversible malfunction are exceedingly rare. The manufacturer’s analysis did not confirm radiation causality, yet the temporal relationship and absence of hardware failure suggest radiation-related damage to the memory components or the microprocessor unit. Although RT effects on transvenous systems have been described, data regarding S-ICDs remain limited. Here, we describe a rare case of radiation-associated malfunction of an S-ICD requiring extraction and highlight management strategies.

There are several clinical implications of our case: (1) Even when the device is shielded and distant from the target field, scatter radiation and neutron exposure can affect sensitive electronics. (2) Device resets may present solely as audible alarms or loss of stored data; prompt interrogation is essential. (3) For patients requiring time-sensitive RT, temporary deactivation with a wearable defibrillator support offers a safe alternative to halting therapy. (4) Post-RT evaluation should include interrogation, impedance review, and, when feasible, bench testing of explanted hardware to improve understanding of such events. (5) Further collaboration between radiation oncologists, medical physicists, and electrophysiologists is critical to define dose thresholds for modern S-ICD systems and guide shielding protocols.

Indeed, radiotherapy may induce CIED dysfunction by damaging the memory of the generator. It can occur transiently during RT (“soft error”) or induce reversion of the device software into “reset” or “backup” modes (“hard error”) as in our case. An electrical reset reverts the software to factory settings with rudimentary CIED programming and thus suppresses high voltage therapies. Generator replacement is necessary in cases of permanent hardware damage. The mechanisms of CIED damage by radiation are complex and dependent on many factors ranging from the type of RT energy to cumulative absorbed dose.4,5 Neutron contamination because of photon beam energy >10 MV, electron energy >20 MeV, or proton therapy are considered high risk for CIED malfunction. A cumulative dose >5 Gy also imparts high risk of CIED damage. Ionizing radiation can result in excess positive charge within device circuitry, potentially resulting in malfunction, which may explain the repeated device resets seen in our case. The impact of shielding on pulse generators to inhibit scatter radiation and potential CIED malfunction is minimal.

Conclusion

S-ICD malfunction during RT is rare but clinically significant. This case underscores the need for vigilance, multidisciplinary coordination, and consideration of bridging strategies when managing patients with S-ICDs undergoing pelvic radiation. As device complexity increases, systematic reporting of similar events will be essential to refine safety guidelines. RT can cause S-ICD malfunction through mechanisms that remain incompletely understood but are likely related to direct exposure to scatter radiation. Clinicians should maintain attentiveness for device resets during RT, especially for pelvic fields where scatter may reach subcutaneous pulse generators. Coordination between electrophysiology and radiation teams is essential to optimize patient safety.
