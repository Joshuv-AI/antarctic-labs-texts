Cognitive Stimulation Therapy in menopause: feasibility randomised controlled trial
Authors: Aimee Spector, Alexandra Faulkner, Helena Gilchrist, Sunaina Sethi, Lexi He, Caroline Gurvich, Rachel Evans
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAimThe aim of this study was to evaluate the feasibility, acceptability, and potential effects of a novel, group-based online intervention for cognition in menopause transition, ‘CST-Meno’, through a feasibility randomised controlled trial.MethodForty-one women were eligible to participate, with 33 randomised to CST-Meno (n = 17) or treatment as usual (n = 16). CST-Meno was delivered over six weeks within four, two-hour sessions. Feasibility and acceptability outcomes included recruitment, retention, attendance, and measure completion. Exploratory analysis examined changes in subjective and objective cognition, quality of life, vasomotor symptoms, anxiety, and depression.ResultsCST-Meno demonstrated acceptability as an intervention, with strong retention, good attendance, and no adverse effects related to content or delivery. With recruitment and retention targets exceeded, willingness to be randomised high, and outcome measures well tolerated, the feasibility and acceptability of the trial design were demonstrated. Exploratory analysis showed small changes in all outcomes, some favouring CST-Meno and others favouring treatment as usual. However, the trial was not powered to detect changes, so these findings were interpreted with extreme caution.ConclusionCST-Meno is feasible to conduct both as a randomised controlled trial and as an acceptable intervention to participants. This warrants further research with a larger, diverse sample, exploring both effectiveness and cost-effectiveness.Lay summaryCognitive changes (‘brain fog’) are highly prevalent during menopause transition. However, there are no treatments shown to help. We developed an online, group intervention called ‘CST-Meno’ (four, two-hour sessions), which provides information and management strategies. Thirty-three women were randomly assigned to receive either CST-Meno or usual care. Everyone was given the same tests (including cognition, mood, and quality of life) at the beginning and end of the study. CST-Meno appeared acceptable to participants, with easy recruitment, good attendance, no adverse effects, few dropouts, and most completing outcome measures. There were no notable changes in outcomes between those attending CST-Meno and usual care; however, our sample was too small to detect clinical changes. We concluded that a larger trial with a more diverse sample is needed. This would help us understand whether CST-Meno improves cognition and other outcomes and if it leads to any economic benefits, such as reduced time off work.

Introduction

Menopause transition (MT) is a bio-psycho-socio-cultural process (Hunter & Edozien 2017) affecting all people with ovaries (hereon referred to as ‘women’, recognising that this includes some non-binary or transgender people). Each woman’s menopause journey differs and can involve a variety of symptoms, including hot flushes/night sweats (vasomotor symptoms), joint discomfort, mood changes, and urogenital complaints. Cognitive complaints in MT, often referred to as ‘brain fog’, commonly include difficulties recalling words and numbers, misplacing items, trouble concentrating, and forgetfulness (Maki & Jaff 2022). These cognitive symptoms are mostly subjective, meaning that individuals notice changes. However, objective cognitive performance, as measured by neuropsychological tests, generally remains within normal ranges (Grummisch et al. 2023).

Despite significant limitations of the longitudinal data, including inadequate measurement of cognition, such studies highlight the presence of clinically significant cognitive changes for around 10% of women (Maki & Jaff 2022). Biologically, both oestrogen and progesterone receptors in the brain play an essential role, particularly in areas associated with memory, such as the hippocampus. Rapid hormonal fluctuations during perimenopause and their associated effect on sleep and vasomotor symptoms, coupled with psychosocial stressors such as anxiety, depression, and workplace pressures, are all proposed to play a part (Gurvich et al. 2026). Subjective cognitive complaints are increasingly recognised as one of the most troubling symptoms in MT, with a cross-sectional survey of nearly 15,000 people (Spector et al. 2026) finding brain fog to be the highest reported symptom (at 86%), in comparison with only 54% experiencing hot flushes. Another recent UK survey (Proctor et al. 2026) indicated that primary care practitioners are frequently mismanaging women in MT, struggling to cope cognitively, for example, by prescribing antidepressants or providing no (or inaccurate) information. This can result in both significant financial costs to health systems and the individual and heightened anxiety and excess disability for prolonged periods, likely to subsequently impact the ability to work and daily functioning (Bazeley et al. 2022).

In many countries, first-line treatment for menopause symptoms is menopause hormone therapy (MHT) (National Institute for Health and Care Excellence 2023). Whilst MHT has an established evidence base for vasomotor symptoms, there is currently no evidence that it supports cognitive changes. Furthermore, many women cannot or choose not to take MHT or cannot access it (Hillman et al. 2020). In addition to the biopsychosocial causes of brain fog, the impact on women’s lives is likely to be exacerbated by anxiety and negative appraisal of symptoms. This provides a rationale for psychosocial treatment options, particularly those that help to reframe negative thoughts using CBT and maintain skills through providing compensatory strategies.

A recent systematic review and meta-analysis (Spector et al. 2024) found benefits to depression, anxiety, and quality of life following cognitive behavioural therapy (CBT) and mindfulness-based interventions in MT. Only three CBT studies examined the impact on memory and concentration, with the small effect size suggesting some promise for a more holistic approach to cognition. Our recent search for psychological interventions specifically targeting cognition in MT identified only two small feasibility studies. One tested psychoeducation about cognition and memory-specific strategies in peri- and postmenopausal women, finding some improvement in memory contentment, frequency of forgetting, and use of specific memory strategies (Unkenstein et al. 2016). The second study evaluated ‘cognitive remediation’ (psychoeducation, compensatory strategies, and lifestyle modification), finding no change in objective cognitive performance or mood but improvements in subjective cognition (Ballantyne et al. 2021). Both studies had limitations, including being uncontrolled, lacking statistical power, and having poorly targeted samples (e.g. inclusion criteria applied regardless of memory status). Nonetheless, there were promising trends in subjective memory, positive feedback regarding the normalisation of memory problems, and suggestions for methods of implementation.

To address this clinical and research gap, this study describes the development and evaluation of a brief online group intervention (cognitive stimulation therapy in menopause: CST-Meno), designed to target objective and subjective cognition in MT. Method

Overview

This research involved two stages: i) intervention development and ii) a single-blind feasibility RCT. The trial was registered on Clinicaltrials.gov (reference: NCT06940466) and reported in accordance with the CONSORT guidelines for pilot and feasibility studies (Eldridge et al. 2016). Ethical approval was obtained from the UCL Research Ethics Committee (reference: 0765).

Intervention development

CST-Meno was iteratively developed following the MRC framework for the development of complex interventions (Shahsavari et al. 2020). This involved i) our systematic review of existing psychosocial interventions to support cognition in menopause (D Proctor, L He & A Spector, under review), ii) a survey (n = 216) and focus group (n = 16) study exploring preferences of women in MT for a new cognitive intervention (D Proctor, M Hunter, L He & A Spector, in press), and iii) current research on cognition in menopause from the collaborating team in Australia, led by co-author Gurvich (Zhu et al. 2022, 2024). A consensus meeting was then held with eleven psychologists and researchers from England, Australia, and China, all with a special interest in menopause. During this meeting, key themes from the background research were discussed, and ideas from previous patient public involvement (PPI) were incorporated. Suitable outcome measurement was also discussed. A draft CST-Meno intervention was then created and shared with another set of clinicians (n = 7) who had expertise in one or more of the following areas: menopause, neuropsychology, and research. Adaptations were made based on their feedback, and the CST-Meno intervention was finalised for use in the study.

Feasibility trial

Participants

As a feasibility trial, formal power calculations were not undertaken. The sample size target was based on the precision needed for estimates to inform a definitive RCT. Recommended sample sizes range from 24 to 50 (Julious 2005, Sim & Lewis 2012); thus, we aimed to randomise 40 participants (20 per arm). Following NIHR (2019) guidance, a sample of 40 provided adequate precision (95% CI: 67–93%) for an expected 75% retention rate to determine trial progression. 34 participants were recruited between June and July 2025. To be eligible, participants were required to i) be biologically female; ii) be aged between 40 and 60; iii) fall in the late-reproductive, early–late perimenopause, or early post-menopause stages (including individuals with surgically induced menopause) according to the STRAW + 10 standard criteria (Harlow et al. 2012); iv) be on a stable dose of hormonal or antidepressant/antipsychotic medication for at least six months; v) have self-reported cognitive difficulties impacting quality of life (assessed by the menopause rating scale question 7, Heinemann et al. 2004); vi) have sufficient English skills to enable participation; vii) have access to and ability to use Microsoft Teams; and viii) be available to attend either a morning or evening group on pre-specified dates.

Participants were excluded if they i) had any new medication regimen within the past six months, ii) were pregnant, or iii) had a diagnosis of dementia or head injury (e.g. traumatic brain injury or stroke).

Procedure

Participants were recruited using voluntary sampling methods, with advertisements shared on social media pages, menopause-specific forums and newsletters, in-person menopause-related events in London, and in libraries, cafes, and establishments across London and Hertfordshire. Participants accessed the study via a provided URL or by scanning a QR code. After reading the study information sheet, participants were asked to complete a pre-consent form and screening measures online, hosted on Qualtrics. Those deemed eligible were contacted to provide informed consent to participate and then sent online baseline questionnaires to answer (within two weeks prior to the intervention starting) and a link to book a virtual neurocognitive assessment. All post-intervention outcomes (for both groups) were to be completed within two weeks after the intervention ended (weeks 6–8). Participants were instructed not to disclose their allocation arm to the assessor to maintain blinding. Following baseline assessment, randomisation was performed by an independent researcher using a computer-generated randomisation programme without any blocking, stratification, or other restrictions. Another researcher informed participants of their allocation, ensuring that outcome assessors remained blind to group assignment.

CST-Meno intervention and TAU

CST-Meno consisted of four, two-hour online sessions, delivered over six weeks on Microsoft Teams. Theoretically, it was informed by cognitive behavioural therapy, cognitive training, and broader ‘cognitive stimulation’, defined as group activities aiming to promote cognitive and emotional enhancement (Clare & Woods 2004). Given the multi-modal nature of the programme, ‘cognitive stimulation therapy’ was agreed upon as the most fitting description amongst the research team. The structured format included i) psychoeducation about menopause and brain health; ii) teaching of specific cognitive skills, such as using mental imagery to support consolidating information and task-switching skills; iii) cognitive behavioural therapy, considering the link between thoughts, feelings, and behaviours in both negative coping cycles and alternative healthy coping cycles; and iv) peer support and reflection. Mindfulness exercises were also involved (Table 1). Groups were facilitated by two psychologists: one group by two trainee clinical psychologists and the other by a senior clinical psychologist and a trainee clinical psychologist. All had previous experience and training in CBT and delivery of therapy groups. Participants received weekly handouts summarising key concepts and suggested home practice. Facilitators offered technical support before and during the sessions if needed. Two scheduled breaks between sessions were included, allowing participants time to reflect and consolidate their skills. Two CST-Meno groups were delivered, a morning and an evening group, with participants indicating availability and preference. Participants allocated to CST-Meno continued their own TAU, enabling the study to evaluate the additional impact of CST-Meno.

Endings

Endings

TAU was defined as continuation with usual care, which was not altered during trial participation. Once the study had concluded, all participants in the TAU group received a PDF summary document of the content from the CST-Meno intervention, providing them with the opportunity to engage with the material if they chose to do so.

Quantitative data

Demographic information was collected at baseline and included age, menopause stage, ethnicity, gender identity, geographical location, education level, and medication use (specific details of medications were not collected). The following measures were used:

Cognition

Multifactorial memory questionnaire (MMQ, Troyer & Rich 2002)

A 57-item, self-report questionnaire with three scales measuring different aspects of metamemory: satisfaction, ability, and strategy. The MMQ has demonstrated reliability and validity across diverse populations.

Menopause cognition questionnaire (MENO-COG) (in development; L He, N Jaff, C Stoner, C EI Bauo, A Spector, personal communication)

A 28-item self-report questionnaire that measures self-reported cognitive issues during MT over the past 2 weeks, ranging from 0 (never) to 4 (all the time). Initial psychometric properties show good internal consistency and construct validity. The Cronbach’s alpha in this sample was 0.9.

Neurocognitive assessments

Rey auditory verbal learning test (Schmidt 1996)

An objective measure of verbal learning and memory where participants repeat back the same list of fifteen words across five trials immediately. Delayed recall is assessed after 20–30 min. It has adequate divergent and convergent validity and good test-retest reliability and has been used in multiple studies exploring cognition in menopause (Hogervorst et al. 2022).

Trail making test (Marvin 2012)

An objective measure of visual processing speed and executive function. It has good content and construct validity and adequate to excellent internal consistency and test–retest reliability.

Digit span subtest, Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale IV (Wechsler 2008)

An objective measure of auditory attention and working memory, shown to have good to excellent validity and reliability. It has been widely used in assessing cognition in menopause.

Controlled word association task (Ruff et al. 1996)

A brief measure of verbal fluency, displaying adequate to good validity and reliability and limited practice effects. It has been used to assess verbal fluency during menopause (Berent-Spillson et al. 2012).

Other domains

Menopause-specific quality of life (MENQOL, Hilditch et al. 1996)

A 29-item questionnaire assessing quality of life over four domains: vasomotor, psychosocial, physical, and sexual. The MENQOL is reported to be reliable and valid for women aged 47–62.

Hot flush rating scale (HFRS, Hunter & Liao 1995)

A self-report measure of the frequency and problem rating of hot flushes and night sweats (‘vasomotor symptoms’) over the past week. It has reasonable test–retest reliability (r = 0.8), good internal consistency (α > 0.87), and good concurrent validity.

Patient health questionnaire 9 (PHQ-9, Kroenke et al. 2001)

A self-report measure of depression over the past 2 weeks, with symptoms rated 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). The PHQ-9 is reported to have a high reliability, α = 0.89, and is considered valid.

Generalised anxiety disorder 7 test (GAD-7, Spitzer et al. 2006)

A self-report measure of anxiety over the past 2 weeks, with symptoms ranging from 0 (not at all) to 3 (nearly every day). It has excellent internal consistency within a clinical population (Löwe et al. 2008).

Statistical analysis

Primary analysis focused on feasibility outcomes, summarising rates of recruitment and retention. No baseline statistical testing was conducted on demographics. Exploratory statistical analysis on the outcome measures was conducted using SPSS 31 for Mac, summarising the data descriptively and using ANCOVA models to assess group differences at follow-up. Models were adjusted for baseline scores, and adjusted mean differences are presented with 95% confidence intervals. Cohen’s d effect size is reported and interpreted as (d = 0.2) a small effect, (d = 0.5) a medium effect, and (d = 0.8) a large effect (Cohen 1988). As this is a feasibility study, a complete case analysis was run.

Results

Forty-one participants were recruited, of whom thirty-three completed pre-intervention online questionnaires and virtual neurocognitive assessments. They were then randomised to either CST-Meno (n = 17) or TAU (n = 16). Figure 1 illustrates participant flow, and Table 2 provides full demographic details. Ages ranged from 40 to 58 (mean: 51 years), with the majority in early–late perimenopause or early post-menopause based on the STRAW + 10 criteria. The majority (n = 27) identified as ‘white’ or ‘other white background’. Two participants had been through surgical menopause, and almost two-thirds (n = 20) of participants were currently taking MHT.

Feasibility of recruitment and retention

Of the 254 individuals who opened the screening questionnaire, 41 (go: 35–40) completed it fully, met the inclusion criteria, and consented to participate. Thirty-three (review: 28–34) were subsequently randomised. Between consenting to participate and randomisation, eight participants withdrew from the study for various reasons, including family stressors and incomplete neurocognitive assessments (see Fig. 1). The eligibility rate, therefore, was 16%, and the recruitment rate was 80%. Recruitment to the upper limit of the target sample size (go: 35–40) took 35 days. Overall, the study had a high retention (91%), as measured by completing at least some outcomes at follow-up. The main reason for attrition was being unable to contact participants.

CONSORT diagram of participant flow through the study.

Post-intervention outcome measure acceptability was high. Full or partial completion was achieved in 91% (n = 30) of participants, meeting the go indicator (≥80%). Completion of post-intervention questionnaires (88%) was better than post-intervention neurocognitive assessments (82%). Participants who did not complete these assessments were uncontactable (n = 3), were no longer able to participate (n = 3), or stated that they were unhappy with their TAU allocation (n = 1).

Acceptability of randomisation

Thirty-three participants were randomised. There were no immediate post-randomisation withdrawals, and nobody stated that they were withdrawing due to group allocation. This suggests that randomisation was acceptable. Reasons for dropout included mental health decline, work commitments, or being uncontactable after randomisation.

Acceptability of the intervention

Overall, participants attended a mean of 2.7 of four sessions (68%, review), and 65% attended at least half of the sessions (Table 3). Mean attendance rates differed between the morning group (79%) and the evening group (57%), indicating the need to explore optimal timing of sessions in future research. There were no reported adverse events.

The differences between intervention and control arms were generally modest (Table 5). There were seven cognitive measures showing a trend towards favouring CST-Meno (RAVLT delay, digit span forwards, digit span backwards, CWAT categories, MENO-COG total, MMQ ability, and MMQ strategy) and five showing a trend towards TAU (RAVLT total learning, TMT trial A, TMT trial B, CWAT letters, and MMQ satisfaction). For the other measures, there was a mix between trends towards favouring CST-Meno (MENQOL vasomotor, MENQOL physical, and MENQOL sexual) and TAU (MENQOL psychosocial, PHQ-9, GAD-7, and HFRS). These results should be interpreted with caution due to the small sample size, which is not powered to interpret group differences.

Discussion

Summary of findings

This study examined the feasibility, acceptability, and preliminary trends in CST-Meno as a treatment for cognition in MT. The intervention demonstrated high acceptability, with strong retention, good attendance, and no adverse effects related to content or delivery. Combined, these indicate the acceptability of CST-Meno as an intervention within this population. Recruitment and retention targets were exceeded, willingness to be randomised was high, and outcome measures were well tolerated. Overall attrition was low, demonstrating the acceptability of the trial. Exploratory analyses showed some non-significant improvements in some domains and declines in others, slightly favouring CST-Meno in terms of cognitive outcomes. However, the trial was not powered to detect changes in outcomes; hence, these results should be interpreted with extreme caution. Overall, the results suggest that a fully powered RCT of CST-Meno is feasible.

The rapid recruitment rate indicated that subjective cognitive complaints are highly prevalent, similar to previous research (Zhu et al. 2025) and that women are keen to explore intervention options. Experiencing cognitive changes during menopause is associated with low mood, anxiety, and vasomotor symptoms, as observed in this study, and continues to suggest the complex interplay between symptoms (Richard-Davis et al. 2022). Our results align with those of two previous studies on cognitive interventions in MT (Unkenstein et al. 2017, Ballantyne et al. 2021), which similarly did not report improvements in mood measures following their interventions, although they were also underpowered.

Strengths and limitations

The strengths of this study include the stepwise, evidence-based development of CST-Meno, the methodology used to evaluate feasibility, and the broad inclusion criteria, increasing generalisability. The latter included women experiencing surgical menopause, frequently excluded from intervention studies, and those both taking and not taking MHT. Another strength was the broad range of subjective and objective cognitive measures used. In a future fully powered trial, this would enable deeper exploration into the association between both factors and any functional impact reported.

A key limitation was the limited diversity of the sample, with 81.9% identifying as ‘white’ or ‘other white’. Furthermore, this sample was highly educated, likely linked to the doctoral students reaching out to their professional networks for recruitment. Another limitation is that the study did not test for fidelity, implying that the extent to which CST-Meno groups were delivered as intended was not assessed. Whilst being comprehensive, our neuropsychological assessments, conducted via video call, had some limitations. Participants completed assessments from home, in an uncontrolled environment. Although largely consistent between time points, we could not control for external distractions. Clear interruptions that may have invalidated testing were recorded, and corrupted data (for example, one individual’s RAVLT delayed recall score) were removed. Furthermore, the TMT requires assessors to follow the test live and correct participant errors. This could sometimes be challenging to administer via video call and resulted in three trials being excluded from the analysis. Assignment of participants to the same assessor for pre- and post-study assessments aimed to minimise testing bias.

Implications for future research

The findings support the progression to a definitive trial assessing both effectiveness and cost-effectiveness of CST-Meno, with considerable efforts made to increase the diversity of the sample. Whilst steps were taken to improve diversity in this feasibility trial, such as directly approaching centres and social media accounts catering to women from minoritised backgrounds, future work should continue to improve engagement, including increasing the physical presence of researchers. It would be important to collect information on the working status of participants and explore the most suitable times of day to run CST-Meno. Whilst work commitments and policies are just one barrier to group attendance, more needs to be done at a higher government level to support and protect women. It may be that developing CST-Meno as a self-directed intervention, for example, via an app, could reduce inequalities in access and potentially be a preferable and more flexible option for some people. Measuring fidelity and adding an additional follow-up assessment, for example, at six months, would be helpful in exploring any longer-term benefits of the intervention. In a future fully powered trial, we would explore trends in outcomes across groups, including those of different ages and ethnicities and those using or not using HRT. Finally, a cost-effectiveness evaluation would be essential to inform the understanding of the health economic implications of CST-Meno. These might include changes in health and social care use, lost working days, and workforce productivity.

Conclusion

Menopause interventions remain limited and often directed towards MHT, which is not fully accessible or acceptable to all individuals and has not demonstrated any clear benefits for cognition. More holistic, evidence-based psychosocial interventions are urgently needed, and a fully powered RCT of CST-Meno is warranted. Future implementation options are broad and include the workplace, primary care, and community settings. The latter aligns with the NHS’s current plan to shift towards a neighbourhood health model.
