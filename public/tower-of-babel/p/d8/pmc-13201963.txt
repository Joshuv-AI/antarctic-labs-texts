Spinal dural arteriovenous fistula (SDAVF) is the most common type of spinal arteriovenous shunt, characterized by an abnormal connection between a radiculomeningeal artery and a medullary vein. This shunting leads to venous hypertension, causing spinal cord congestion and progressive myelopathy.1 Although SDAVF is a relatively common spinal vascular disorder, its varied initial presentation often results in diagnostic difficulties and delayed intervention. Initial misdiagnoses commonly include degenerative spinal conditions, peripheral neuropathies, and demyelinating disorders.2 Recent studies report that up to 78% of SDAVF cases are initially misdiagnosed, and such delays are associated with poorer neurological outcomes.3,4

Although radicular pain is a common initial manifestation, it most often coexists with myelopathy.4–6 Cases presenting with isolated radiculopathy are rare, and there are no previous reports of symptoms occurring contralateral to the fistula. Here, we report a case of thoracic SDAVF with a shunt near the left intervertebral foramen, presenting with isolated right-sided radicular pain. Its atypical presentation resulted in a delayed diagnosis. We also review the relevant literature.

Illustrative Case

A 63-year-old man presented with a 1.5-year history of refractory right scapular pain. He was initially diagnosed with cervicobrachial syndrome and managed conservatively at an orthopedic department for 6 months. However, the pain gradually extended to the right anterior chest and was exacerbated by physical activity, prompting evaluation with MRI. While MRI showed perimedullary flow voids, no intramedullary T2 hyperintensity was observed (Fig. 1A and B). At this point, a spinal vascular shunt was suspected, and the patient was referred to our department. A physical examination revealed no obvious neurological deficits, aside from the patient’s chronic right-sided scapular and anterior chest pain. Contrast-enhanced CT suggested a shunt at the thoracic level, and subsequent spinal angiography confirmed an SDAVF at the left T5–6 level. The shunt was supplied by the radiculomeningeal artery originating from the left T6 segmental artery, with cranial drainage into the perimedullary venous plexus (Fig. 1C and D). Preoperative axial imaging did not clearly show direct compression of the contralateral nerve root (Fig. 1E). Surgery was indicated to prevent myelopathy and to explore the cause of the refractory radicular pain. A T4–6 laminectomy was performed to provide bilateral exposure for investigation of the contralateral symptoms. Intraoperatively, an arteriovenous shunt was identified where a vessel penetrated the dura at the left T5–6 level (Fig. 2A). Indocyanine green (ICG) videoangiography confirmed high shunt flow into the perimedullary venous plexus (Fig. 2B). A temporary clip was applied to the shunt point. While the shunt was occluded, the right T5 nerve root was explored and found to be compressed by an adherent, parallel-running arterialized draining vein (Fig. 3A). Direct decompression was performed in addition to shunt disconnection to ensure definitive neurological improvement, as significant mechanical compression of the nerve root was observed intraoperatively. The adhesions were carefully dissected to fully decompress the nerve root (Fig. 3B–D). This temporary clip was maintained for a total of 20 minutes, during which time the stability of motor evoked potentials (MEPs) and somatosensory evoked potentials (SEPs) was confirmed at 5-minute intervals. After confirming that there were no decreases in either MEPs or SEPs, the shunt was coagulated and divided. Discussion

Observations

This case represents an atypical presentation of SDAVF, manifesting solely as radicular pain contralateral to the shunt and without any signs of myelopathy. SDAVF typically presents with progressive myelopathic symptoms such as paraparesis and sensory disturbances, caused by spinal cord edema secondary to venous hypertension and congestion.1 Characteristic MRI findings include longitudinal T2-weighted hyperintensity within the spinal cord and serpentine dorsal flow voids reflecting engorged perimedullary veins.7

However, a previous report suggested that lesions at the cervical level or those with predominantly cranial venous drainage may not necessarily result in myelopathy, likely due to anatomical and hemodynamic factors that mitigate venous congestion.8 In our patient, MRI showed no intramedullary T2 hyperintensity, likely owing to the predominantly cranial venous drainage of the fistula toward the cervical region.

Several mechanisms have been proposed to explain radiculopathy in SDAVF. One theory suggests that perimedullary venous hypertension impairs venous drainage, causing engorgement and edema. This potentially extends to the nerve root entry zone, affecting the dorsal root ganglia.9 Another proposed mechanism is direct mechanical compression of the nerve root by an arterialized draining vein. A previous report identified such physical compression as a distinct cause of radiculopathy in SDAVF.10 This mechanism is analogous to trigeminal neuralgia, in which a vein coursing along the nerve root has been shown to cause chronic contact or adhesion, contributing to neuralgic pain.11 In the present case, intraoperatively, an adherent, arterialized draining vein was found compressing the right T5 nerve root. Shunt disconnection combined with nerve root decompression resulted in complete symptomatic relief. The present case suggests that, in addition to venous congestion, mechanical irritation from a contralateral arterialized vein represents a major factor inducing the symptoms.

Despite the mechanisms, SDAVF manifesting primarily as isolated truncal radicular pain without myelopathy is exceedingly rare. To our knowledge, only 2 similar cases have been reported. In all 3 cases, including ours, unilateral chest pain was the initial and predominant symptom, persisting for an extended period before diagnosis. For instance, previously reported patients experienced symptoms for 6 months to 4 years before a correct diagnosis was established.10,12 Those cases involved symptoms ipsilateral to the fistula; this is the first report of an SDAVF presenting with symptoms solely contralateral to the shunt origin. However, this phenomenon is pathophysiologically plausible, as SDAVF can induce venous hypertension and congestion throughout the entire spinal venous system. Therefore, it should be recognized that symptoms may manifest not only contralaterally but also at spinal levels distant from the fistula site.

In the current case, the patient’s right scapular and anterior chest pain persisted for approximately 1.5 years. In all 3 cases, radicular pain persisted without typical SDAVF signs, such as myelopathy or spinal cord edema on MRI. Because truncal pain is commonly attributed to musculoskeletal or cardiopulmonary conditions, patients with this atypical presentation often face significant diagnostic delays. Our patient was managed for cervicobrachial syndrome, while another reported case required extensive cardiac and pulmonary investigations before the SDAVF was identified.12 Such delays are associated with poorer neurological outcomes, highlighting the importance of early recognition even in atypical presentations.

An additional noteworthy feature shared across the 3 cases was symptom exacerbation during respiration or physical exertion. In our patient, exercise tended to aggravate the chest pain. This phenomenon is likely attributable to an increase in shunt flow during exertion, which leads to transient engorgement of the arterialized draining veins and subsequent worsening of venous congestion.13 Furthermore, this venous engorgement may lead to direct mechanical compression of the nerve root, potentially exacerbating radicular symptoms. Previous literature has identified various factors that elevate intraspinal venous pressure, including prolonged standing, singing, pregnancy, and prone positioning, as triggers for the acute deterioration of neurological symptoms in SDAVF.7,14,15 Therefore, when neurological symptoms are exacerbated by maneuvers or conditions that increase venous pressure, spinal shunt diseases, including SDAVF, should be strongly considered in the differential diagnosis.

Lessons

SDAVF should be considered in the differential diagnosis of refractory truncal radiculopathy, even in the absence of classic radiological findings such as spinal cord edema. As this case demonstrated, isolated radicular pain potentially serves as the sole clinical manifestation, and the symptomatic nerve root may manifest contralateral to the shunt origin. Clinicians should recognize that an arterialized draining vein is capable of causing direct mechanical compression of the nerve root, mimicking common musculoskeletal or cardiopulmonary conditions. Early diagnosis and prompt intervention facilitate complete symptomatic resolution and prevent progression to irreversible myelopathy.
