A model of academic‐practice collaboration for facilitating informatics capacity and building a learning health system framework in public health
Authors: Sripriya Rajamani, Sarah Solarz, Miriam Halstead Muscoplat, Aasa Dahlberg Schmit, Ann Gonderinger, Chris Brueske, Jennifer Fritz, Emily Emerson, Genevieve B Melton
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and ObjectiveThe data modernization initiative (DMI) is a multi‐year, multi‐billion‐dollar endeavor toward a robust public health information infrastructure. The various DMI projects (interoperability, analytics, workforce, governance) present an opportunity for a learning health system (LHS) framework in public health. The objective is to share an academic‐practice partnership model between the University of Minnesota (UMN) and the Minnesota Department of Health (MDH) in advancing public health informatics (PHI) and its relationship to an LHS model.MethodsThe UMN‐MDH partnership was conceptualized in 2018 as a 1‐year pilot with annual renewals through a time/cost‐sharing faculty position with PHI expertise. The partnership focus was decided based on MDH's needs and mutual interests, with the core collaborating faculty (SR) being an embedded researcher at MDH. Responsibilities included supporting electronic case reporting (eCR), interoperability projects, and assisting MDH staff with PHI presentations/publications. The partnership has expanded to PHI workforce development through a national grant and now includes an interest in applying the LHS framework to MDH‐DMI work.ResultsThe MDH‐DMI team has embarked upon 13 projects for assessment through an LHS approach: systems interoperability projects between MDH and healthcare/local public health (n = 6); systems modernization for MDH programs (n = 5); informatics workforce development (n = 1); and program governance (n = 1). Each project has been evaluated and/or has current/future assessment plans to synthesize learnings and create a feedback loop for iterative improvement. The partnership has been mutually beneficial as it met agreed upon metrics across both institutions. The program's productivity is showcased with shared authorship in 10 peer‐reviewed proceedings/publications, 22 presentations and 16 posters across local/national conferences.ConclusionThe current case report of the UMN‐MDH partnership is a relatively recent exemplar to support tangible LHS demonstration in public health. Building LHS momentum at MDH and other public health entities will require LHS champion(s) and continued academic collaboration.Keywords: academic‐practice collaboration, data modernization initiative (DMI), health information interoperability, learning health systems, public health, public health informatics

BACKGROUND AND SIGNIFICANCE

Public health as an information‐intense enterprise and role of informatics

Data is the cornerstone of public health and a key component in achieving the mission of protecting and promoting the health of all people in communities. The delivery of numerous services in public health depends on data from a multitude of stakeholders. Transforming these data to information, and consecutive translation to knowledge is essential for an effective public health enterprise and underscores the importance of informatics. The Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists advocated for a “public health data superhighway” 1 to transform the nation's approach to detecting and responding to global health challenges. A modern data infrastructure can provide timely, complete, and granular data leading to actionable information 2 , 3 when addressing health disparities and inequities. 4 , 5 This need for robust data and surveillance systems was brought to limelight during the COVID‐19 pandemic, subsequent Mpox outbreaks and the current surge in Measles cases. The growing importance of informatics for public health enablement is recognized with a Healthy People 2030 objective under consideration for enhancing the use and capabilities of informatics in public health (PHI‐R06). 6

Data modernization initiative as a mechanism to apply a learning health system framework to public health

The data modernization initiative (DMI) has invested billions of dollars to further this vision to get faster and better actionable data for decision‐making across various levels in public health ranging from federal, state, tribal, local, and territorial agencies. 7 , 8 The DMI is a multi‐year national effort led by the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC). Its goal is to develop an integrated, real‐time public health data and surveillance architecture. The DMI advances five priorities, and numerous projects ranging from interoperability, analytics, systems upgrades, new technology implementations, workforce development, and governance are organized under this DMI framework.

These DMI projects present a perfect opportunity for utilizing a learning health system (LHS) 9 , 10 framework in the area of public health. The LHS model emphasizes continuous cycles across research and practice wherein findings from the evaluation are used to improve practice iteratively, generating new evidence and knowledge. Importantly, an LHS model aligns well with an academic‐practice partnership. An LHS framework also facilitates to systematically synthesize the learnings and emerging best practices to be organized, shared, and scaled across the multitude of stakeholders which include policymakers.

Academic‐practice partnership to facilitate LHS framework

The National Academy of Medicine emphasizes informatics as a key component of LHS where data‐information‐knowledge drive improvements and innovation. 10 There is paucity of informatics talent in public health with currently less than 1% of public health staff in informatics roles nationally. 11 Building informatics capacity is a challenge in public health agencies due to multitude of factors ranging from a scarcity of trained personnel, limited public health informatics (PHI) training opportunities and lack of robust connections with academia for training and recruitment.

Academic collaboration is an important option to fill this need by tapping into both the informatics training and the research/evaluation capabilities. As depicted in Table 1, there is alignment and synergistic relationship across these entities on informatics training/workforce development and evaluation capabilities/assessment needs. The objective of this case report is to elaborate on an academic‐practice partnership model between the University of Minnesota (UMN) and the Minnesota Department of Health (MDH) which began with a PHI focus and is being expanded to facilitate a LHS model. By sharing the current approach, this case study provides an opportunity to share lessons learned and to generate best practices for an LHS in public health in partnership with other institutions.

METHODS

There have been various formal and informal partnerships between UMN and MDH for many years which have laid the groundwork. Informatics‐focused UMN‐MDH partnerships were initiated in 2007 by Dr. Martin LaVenture, a pioneer in PHI, but have been informal. In his role as the Director of the Minnesota e‐Health Initiative, Dr. LaVenture advocated for integrating the public health perspective in an LHS model and highlighted the value of services provided by governmental public health organizations at the local, state, and federal levels. 10 This led to MDH in collaboration with the Minnesota eHealth Initiative being an early endorser of the core values of an LHS. 12 In addition, MDH has been a leader in advocating for informatics‐savvy public health agencies and sharing guidance on vision, strategy, and operations. 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 Adding PHI expertise to support DMI projects and retaining the momentum of the above were the drivers for an informatics‐focused academic‐practice collaboration.

The need for an informatics‐focused partnership which is more formal with contractual agreements between MDH and UMN was recognized, and a 1‐year pilot was initiated in 2018 with annual renewals. This was conceptualized initially as a time and cost‐sharing proposition for a faculty with operational public health experience and informatics expertise. The core bridging faculty (SR) currently has an appointment with the informatics program at the UMN School of Nursing, is a member of the UMN Center for LHS Sciences, 17 and directly collaborates with the Director of the UMN Center for LHS Sciences (GBM). Together with other UMN or MDH learners and staff and collaborating UMN faculty, the projects for partnerships have ranged from electronic case reporting (eCR) implementation, 18 supporting MDH programs with informatics expertise, student recruitment for projects, and other associated collaboration. For the bridging faculty member, due to the concurrent academic appointment and expertise in writing publications and proposals, the responsibilities included assisting MDH staff with conference abstracts and presentations, peer‐reviewed manuscripts for publications, and joint funding opportunities. The partnership has expanded over the years with additional grant funding focused on systems evaluation and PHI workforce development.

This collaboration has also provided an opportunity to look at the numerous DMI projects through an LHS lens. The projects for implementations and assessments were determined by MDH priorities, CDC‐driven grant metrics and mutual interests. These are discussed regularly during the weekly DMI meetings attended by core bridging faculty (SR) along with the MDH team. The five components for DMI at MDH aligned with the national model and comprised of: planning and coordination; data and information system modernization; advanced data analysis, visualization and reporting; enhanced data interoperability and data exchanges; and enhanced workforce capacity and competencies (Figure 1). The six phases of a LHS approach (internal and external scan, design, implement, evaluate, adjust, disseminate) from Greene et al., 19 were adapted to fit the public health context. These six phases (identify public health needs, design informatics solutions, implement technology tools, evaluate the fit and impact, adjust and recalibrate, disseminate results and lessons) were applied to DMI at MDH as shown in Figure 1. Certain projects under the DMI umbrella were identified to be explored in greater detail for a LHS approach and noted to include an evaluation component to synthesize learnings. These projects had specific teams with relevant subject matter experts for planning and implementation, and learnings were synthesized in collaboration with end‐users and relevant stakeholders. For example, the eCR team at MDH had weekly meetings since April 2020 to discuss various aspects which include issues identification, system enhancements and user feedback. These learnings are brought back to the core DMI team during weekly discussions (n = 64 as of May 2024). The overall approach comprised of the core collaborating faculty (SR) being an embedded researcher and an integral member of some of the electronic data exchange project implementations and the core MDH‐DMI team.

RESULTS

The core DMI team at MDH has currently embarked upon 13 projects for an LHS approach which are presented in Figure 2. These DMI projects have been evaluated and/or have current/future assessment plans to gather lessons learned from stakeholders (including users and management) and create a feedback loop for iterative improvement. These are comprised of a total of 13 projects: systems interoperability projects between the public health agency (MDH) and healthcare/local public health (n = 6), systems modernization projects in programs within MDH (n = 5), informatics workforce development (n = 1) and overarching governance (n = 1) (Figure 2).

Select data and systems modernization projects at Minnesota Department of Health (MDH) for applying the learning health system framework. eSHARE, Electronic System for Health Assessment of Refugees; MEDSS, Minnesota Electronic Disease Surveillance System; MIIC, Minnesota Immunization Information Connection.

Table 2 summarizes each of the projects of this collaboration and provides information about each project's associated DMI priority(ies), funding source(s), key take‐aways, evaluation and synthesis of learnings and dissemination. The productivity is showcased with shared authorship across 10 peer‐reviewed proceedings/publications, 22 presentations and 16 posters across local/national conferences from 2018 to 2024. As a testament to the mutually beneficial nature of the partnership, public health experts from MDH provide guest lectures and research seminars to the University (UMN) audience, and MDH serves as an informatics practicum site for hosting and mentoring students and creating additional career paths.

One highly successful project has been MDH's implementation of eCR, a DMI priority project. Automated eCR reporting for COVID‐19 was implemented during the pandemic with support from the national eCR team. The academic partnership worked using an LHS framework, which in turn has led to sharing the learnings, implementation approach, and publication of findings in a premier informatics journal 20 (Table 2). The evaluation and synthesis of learnings has also been shared at numerous national meetings and conferences (also summarized in Table 2). Presently, MDH has advanced its eCR capabilities for reporting of all infectious diseases and is looking to expand eCR further to non‐communicable disease reporting. 25

Another successful project completed through this UMN‐MDH collaboration was the interoperability between the state disease surveillance system (Minnesota Electronic Disease Surveillance System) and the state immunization information system (MIIC: Minnesota Immunization Information Connection). The evaluation for this electronic exchange of immunization data from MIIC to Minnesota Electronic Disease Surveillance System was extramurally supported by an R03 grant from the Agency for Healthcare Research and Quality 26 (Table 2). Results were published in a reputed informatics journal, 21 and the interoperability tool development was shared with the immunization community through the American Immunization Registry Association annual meeting and with epidemiologists through the Council of State and Territorial Epidemiologists annual conference. Immunization reporting to public health and access of clinical decision support offered by MIIC were evaluated and findings shared at MEDINFO 2023, a global informatics conference and subsequently published in the proceedings of the meeting. 22

Finally, the UMN‐MDH collaboration was critical to the successful competitive funding of a federal PHI workforce development grant through the Office of the National Coordinator for health informatics and technology 27 , 28 (refer Table 2 for details). This grant has supported free informatics training to MDH staff and other learners offered by the Public Health Informatics Institute. The course titled, “Designing and Managing Public Health Information Systems: eight Steps to Success course” offered by Public Health Informatics Institute has had three cohorts completed (overall with 147 finished training to date). In addition, three more cohort sessions are scheduled, with the final one focused on tribal public health.

DISCUSSION

While the application of an LHS model to demonstrate learning, evaluation and iterative/continuous improvement is a robust and promising approach with demonstrations across healthcare delivery settings, few public health agencies today provide tangible demonstrations and academic‐public health examples are even fewer. The current case report of the UMN‐MDH partnership is a relatively recent exemplar of this model and provides a tangible example of the benefits of this approach. Becoming a LHS is a journey and MDH needs to address many factors (leadership, coordination, education, partnership, dissemination, sustainability) identified by the core DMI team. In addition, there is a need to identify a LHS champion(s) 29 and a determination to address these factors to continue on this path. Adopting a LHS approach provides MDH an opportunity to increase its impact on the population in profound ways. The LHS model holds promise in creating a continuous improvement culture towards effective use of data for decision‐making. This approach supported by strong leadership and informatics‐savvy workforce ensures the organization is constantly learning through every project.

Building LHS momentum at MDH will require an ongoing academic‐practice partnership in PHI, with sound governance and sustainability. Currently, partnership governance is guided by a consensus‐driven approach and UMN‐MDH shared decision‐making team model. This collaboration based on mutual respect extends to all facets of research ranging from resource allocation, dyad‐model of co‐presentations for various outreach efforts and co‐authorship of publications. Future plans could include exploring a formal relationship such as the academic health department model. 30 In addition to adoption of a LHS framework of thinking, academic‐practice linkages such as the UMN‐MDH cooperation are critical to building informatics workforce capacity in public health, for research collaborations for faculty and to provide training opportunities for students.

There are limitations to this case study, one of which being this is a single example, and so the approach and results may not be generalizable. There have been various formal and informal partnerships between UMN and MDH for many years, and this collaboration is specifically focused on PHI expertise. This model based on one/few motivated faculty may not be viable in the long run and incentives need to be created to scale this up for sustainability. Options such as formal joint appointments will help to bolster current informatics capacity faster and strategically position public health agencies to adopt a LHS model. Additional structures which facilitate this academic‐practice informatics alliance needs to be created, scaled up and institutionalized, along with faculty incentives for conducting practice‐oriented research and performance metrics which reward the impact of the work.

The National Commission to Transform Public Health Data Systems 5 advocates for a robust public health data system to identify problems, allocate resources, and promote health equity. A 2023 national symposium 2 , 3 attended by biomedical informatics experts and public health leaders discussed policy, education, research, and development strategies around PHI. There is increasing attention to the role of DMI in rebuilding the public health data and information infrastructure. A systematic approach guided by LHS framework, a multi‐stakeholder learning community and a supportive socio‐technical infrastructure 31 are foundational and will ensure lessons learned are incorporated into a continuous feedback cycle. Using an academic‐practice partnership model to bolster PHI capacity, generate knowledge and institutionalize a LHS model at MDH is a step towards the eventual goal of an equity‐focused and enhanced public health system.
