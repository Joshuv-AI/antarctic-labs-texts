Two-dimensional SnSe2/Bi2Se3 vertically stacked heterostructures for enhanced photoresponse through tuning layer coverage
Authors: Liying Wen, Dongsheng Hu, Ruihong Zhang, Xiaoxiang Hu, Yan Luo, Hui Chen, Yifang Liang, Yong Li, Shifeng Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryTwo-dimensional (2D) materials have attracted much attention due to their unique structures and excellent optoelectronic properties. In this work, we fabricated c-axis-aligned SnSe2/Bi2Se3 2D heterostructures via a hydrothermal technique, where the epitaxial growth of Bi2Se3 nanoplates is achieved on SnSe2 microplate substrates. The electronic states at the interfaces can be regulated facilely via tuning the Bi2Se3 nanoplate coverage. The heterostructure not only increases the wavelength range over which light is absorbed, but also promotes the separation and migration of photo-excited charge carriers. Therefore, the heterostructure-based photodetector attains a photoresponse that outperforms those devices composed of pure SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 by a factor of 3.0 and 4.9, respectively. Furthermore, the optimal heterostructure generates a photocurrent of 289 μA cm−2 under ultraviolet illumination, which surpasses those of recently reported state-of-the-art photodetectors. Accordingly, this work establishes an effective approach for developing low-cost, high-performance optoelectronic devices based on 2D chalcogenides.Subject areas: Applied sciences, Materials science, Nanomaterials

Introduction

Photodetectors have attracted significant interest for use as pivotal photoelectric signal-conversion devices.1 This interest has been driven particularly by growing cross-sector demands, such as defense, consumer electronics, environmental monitoring, healthcare, and optical communications.2,3,4,5,6,7,8,9,10 However, conventional semiconductors exhibit limited performance in photodetectors due to low specific surface area, weak photoresponse, and inefficient charge carrier transport. Accordingly, the development of next-generation photonic applications depends upon the discovery of new materials and structures.

A promising class of candidate materials for this purpose includes transition metal dichalcogenides (TMDCs), which are two-dimensional (2D) semiconductors formed of select transition metals, such as Sn, W, and Bi, and a chalcogen element (S, Se, or Te) that exhibit distinct electronic and optoelectronic properties.11,12 In particular, their narrow bandgaps enable broad-spectrum light absorption, and TMDCs like tin diselenide (SnSe2) are composed of naturally abundant constituents, which offers good potential for cost-effective photodetector applications. Furthermore, a number of TMDC structures like Bi2Te3, WTe2, and Bi2Se3 serve as topological insulators that demonstrate exceptional charge carrier mobility and a very narrow bandgap.13,14,15 Moreover, semiconductor TMDCs have been combined with TMDC topological insulators via weak van der Waals forces to form 2D heterostructures with engineered energy band structures offering unique optoelectronic functionalities that cannot be obtained via a single material alone.16 Similarly, Luo et al.17 recently reported the development of 2D heterostructures based on SnS2/Bi2Se3 linked via weak van der Waals forces with enhanced photoresponse, and Yi et al.18 developed 2D Bi2Se3/SnSe2 heterostructures for rapid NO2 detection. Furthermore, phase-controlled SnSe/SnSe2 film junctions with tunable photoresponse have been prepared, while 2D SnSSe porous nanoplates with atom-level heterojunctions were fabricated for use in photoelectrochemical detectors.19 However, the photoresponse performance of existing heterojunctions is strongly dependent on the high quality crystallinity of the 2D TMDC materials. Accordingly, the available means for facilitating the large-scale production of 2D TMDC/TMDC heterostructures are not cost effective. Additionally, the means of improving the photoresponse and stability of 2D TMDC/TMDC heterostructures by effectively regulating the electronic band structure and electron transfer pathways between the two TMDCs remain poorly developed.

The present work addresses these issues by developing 2D c-axis-aligned SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructures through a simple hydrothermal method, where the epitaxial growth of Bi2Se3 nanoplates with lengths of ∼500 nm is achieved on SnSe2 microplate substrates with lengths of ∼2 μm. The electronic structure at the interface is effectively regulated by controlling the electron transfer pathways between the Bi2Se3 nanoplates and underlying SnSe2 microplates facilely via adjustments in Bi2Se3 nanoplate coverage, because as the coverage increases, a stronger built-in electric field can be constructed between them, which is more conducive to electron migration, thereby enhancing the performance of its photoelectric current response. The obtained heterostructure not only captures more optical energy by broadening the wavelength range of adsorbed light, but also promotes the photon-electron conversion efficiency by accelerating the separation and migration of photoexcited charge carriers. Photodetector applications of the proposed 2D SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure under ultraviolet (UV) illumination conditions yield a photoresponse that outperforms those of pristine SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 photodetectors by factors of 3.0 and 4.9, respectively. In addition, the proposed heterostructure generates a much greater photocurrent of 289 μA cm−2 than that of recently reported photodetectors. Accordingly, this work provides guidance for designing 2D heterostructures with engineered energy band structures appropriate for high-performance photoelectronic applications.

Both SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 are 2D materials. Therefore, Bi2Se3 nanoplates can be grown layer-on-layer epitaxially with high crystallinity and limited interface defects, regardless of a large lattice mismatch between the two materials. Indeed, conditions of lattice mismatch can induce a preferential growth orientation by directing a preferred layer-by-layer stacking if the lattice mismatch is smaller compared to that of basal planes, such as SnSe2/SnS2 heterostructures. Hence, the much smaller mismatch associated with the vertical growth of Bi2Se3 along its (001) basal plane onto SnSe2 (001) surface than that of other growth orientations directs a preferred layer-by-layer stacking. This is made evident by the regular progression in the relative heights of the SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 peaks in the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure materials synthesized with varying SnSe2 powder concentrations, where the intensity of the Bi2Se3 peak decreases and the intensity of the SnSe2 peak increases with increasing SnSe2 content. Accordingly, these are composition-tunable composites, where both phases maintain excellent crystallinity, and demonstrate the successful integration of SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 components without phase degradation.

Atomic states and energy band alignments

The Sn, Bi, and Se chemical states of Bi2Se3 nanoplate, SnSe2 microplate, and SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composites synthesized with different SnSe2 contents can be evaluated based on the XPS Sn 3d, Bi 4f, and Se 3d spectra presented in Figures 6A–6C, respectively. As shown in Figure 6A, the Sn 3d spectrum of pristine SnSe2 exhibits two peaks at binding energies 495.09 eV and 486.56 eV corresponding to Sn 3d3/2 and Sn 3d5/2 spin doublet states, respectively. These peak binding energies confirm that the Sn atoms near the sample surface existed primarily in the Sn4+ oxidation state. Deconvolution of the largest Sn 3d5/2 spin doublet peak observed for the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composite (0.5 g) using two Gaussian curves in Advantage yields a relatively small peak with a binding energy of ∼487 eV, which corresponds to the Sn4+ oxidation state. Meanwhile, the majority of Sn atoms reside within a common state corresponding to the much lower binding energy of ∼486 eV. However, practically all Sn atoms in the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composite (5.0 g) can be observed to exist in this lower binding energy state, and the binding energy of this state (486.1 eV) is slightly less than that observed for the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composite (0.5 g). In contrast, the Bi 4f spectra observed in Figure 6B for pristine Bi2Se3 and the two SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composites exhibit negligible peak shifts between the samples. The trend observed in the Sn 3d spectra is further reflected by the Se 3d spectra presented in Figure 6C, where the Se 3d3/2 and Se 3d5/2 spin doublet peaks obtained for the heterostructure composite also shift to lower binding energies compared to those of pure SnSe2. The collective negative shift observed for both the Sn and Se core-level peaks in the heterostructure composite suggests an increase in electron density around the SnSe2 microplates, implying that these components gain electrons within the heterojunctions. Conversely, analysis of the Se 3d spectrum observed for pure Bi2Se3 reveals an opposite effect, where the Se 3d peaks of the heterostructure composite have shifted to higher binding energies relative to those of pure Bi2Se3. Accordingly, these consistent shifts in the core-level spectra demonstrate an internal electron transfer from Bi2Se3 nanoplate to SnSe2 microplate components when in physical contact. Therefore, these findings represent strong evidence for the occurrence of electronic interactions between the SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 components in the heterostructures. Furthermore, the slightly lower Sn 3d5/2 binding energy observed for the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composite (5.0 g) suggests that the lower Bi2Se3 coverage of this composite induces stronger electronic interactions and charge transfer effect at the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterojunction interface. In Figure 4, it can be observed that as the amount of SnSe2 added increases, the coverage of Bi2Se3 gradually decreases, thereby mutually verifying this phenomenon.

Schematics illustrating potential electron transfer mechanisms between the SnSe2 microplate and Bi2Se3 nanoplate components in the 2D SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructures synthesized with different SnSe2 contents are presented in Figure 7, where the depiction of Bi2Se3 nanoplate coverage on the SnSe2 microplate is based on the STEM images presented in Figure 6. The lower Sn 3d5/2 binding energy observed for the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composite (5.0 g) with low nanoplate coverage than that of the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composite (0.5 g) with high nanoplate coverage suggests that the electrons gained by the SnSe2 microplate from the Bi2Se3 nanoplates is inversely proportional to nanoplate coverage. Accordingly, increasing nanoplate coverage does not actually result in the transfer of more electrons from Bi2Se3 to SnSe2, but rather less. This can most likely be attributed to the fact that multiple contacts exist between the Bi2Se3 nanoplates on the SnSe2 (001) surface under heavy nanoplate coverage, which enables easier transfer of electrons between the Bi2Se3 nanoplates bonded by covalent bonds than between Bi2Se3 nanoplates and the SnSe2 microplate linked by weak van der Waals forces. In contrast, the low number of contacts formed between Bi2Se3 nanoplates under low coverage ensures that electrons transfer predominantly between contacting SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 components in the heterostructure composites. Furthermore, the extent of electron transfer between the SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 components in the heterostructures directly correlates with their functional performance, where a greater degree of electron transfer would lead to superior optoelectronic properties.

Schematic illustrations of potential electron transfer mechanisms between an SnSe2 microplate and Bi2Se3 nanoplate components in the 2D SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure composites

The energy band alignment of the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure composite (5.0 g) can be extrapolated from the UV-Vis-NIR diffuse reflectance spectra and corresponding Tauc plots respectively presented in Figures 8A and 8B for the SnSe2, Bi2Se3, and SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composite materials, in conjunction with the UPS spectra and XPS valence band spectrum respectively presented in Figures 8C–8F for the pure SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 materials. As shown in Figure 8A, the optical absorption edge of the pure SnSe2 microplate powder is at a wavelength of around 1,000 nm, while both the pure Bi2Se3 nanoplate powder and the heterostructure composite powder maintain relatively high optical absorbance over the entire wavelength range measured up to 2,500 nm. The bandgap energies of the materials can be deduced from the Tauc plots in Figure 8B, which yield standard bandgap energies of 1.22 eV for pure SnSe2 and 0.42 eV for pure Bi2Se320 and a bandgap energy of 1.11 eV was obtained for the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure composite developed in this work. Accordingly, we can conclude that the incorporation of Bi2Se3 nanoplates reduced the equivalent bandgap energy of the heterostructure, which would facilitate the capture of a greater proportion of the solar spectrum compared to that of the pure SnSe2. The results of UPS measurements presented in Figures 8C and 8D respectively yield work functions of 3.82 eV for SnSe2 and 3.77 eV for Bi2Se3. Meanwhile, the XPS valence band spectra presented in Figures 8E and 8F respectively yield valence band maxima (VBM) of 0.97 eV below the Fermi level for SnSe2 and 0.19 eV below the Fermi level for Bi2Se3. These results were applied to extrapolate the band energy diagram presented in Figure 8G, which shows Bi2Se3 possessing a higher Fermi level than that of SnSe2 Through this, we can also observe that a Z-type heterojunction is formed. The Fermi level of Bi2Se3 is higher than that of SnSe2. The band offset (ΔE1) between the CBM of SnSe2 and VBM of Bi2Se3 is calculated to be 0.39 eV.21 The transfer of electrons occurs from the CBM of Bi2Se3 to the CBM of SnSe2, and then from the VBM of SnSe2 to the CBM of Bi2Se3, thereby facilitating carrier migration.22 Therefore, electrons would transfer from Bi2Se3 to SnSe2 upon contact to maintain the same Fermi energy, as was indicated by the XPS analysis associated with Figure 6 above. Accordingly, contact would establish a built-in electric field directed from the Bi2Se3 side to the SnSe2 side at the heterojunction interface, which would enhance the photoresponse of the heterostructure composite by promoting effective electron-hole separation.

Photoelectronic responses

The current-time characteristics obtained over 10, 20-s photocurrent response cycles comprising 10 s of monochromatic illumination at a 30 W/cm2 intensity followed by 10 s of darkness are presented in Figures 9A–9E for photodetector devices composed of SnSe2 layers, Bi2Se3 layers, and SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure composites with different SnSe2 contents by weight. As can be seen, all photodetector devices demonstrated excellent stability over ten consecutive cycles with negligible photocurrent degradation. However, the devices yielded different maximum photocurrents under different monochromatic wavelengths. These trends can be more fully appreciated by the plot of the maximum photocurrents of the devices in Figure 10F as a function of the wavelength. As can be seen, the maximum photocurrent obtained by the photoconductor device utilizing the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure composite (5.0 g) synthesized with the smallest Bi2Se3 nanoplate coverage was uniformly greatest under all monochromatic wavelengths considered. Comparing these maximum photocurrent values with those obtained by photoconductor devices utilizing the pure SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 powders under each monochromatic wavelength considered yields the following analysis. Under UV illumination (365 nm), the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 (5.0 g) heterostructure achieved a maximum photocurrent of 289.07 μA, and outperformed the devices composed of pure SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 by factors of 2.5 and 3.3, respectively. Under blue illumination (470 nm), the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 (5.0 g) heterostructure generates a maximum photocurrent of 218.94 μA, which is greater than the devices composed of pure SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 by factors of 2.0 and 4.4, respectively. Under green illumination (530 nm), the maximum photocurrent of 181.52 μA obtained by the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 (5.0 g) heterostructure is greater than those of the devices composed of pure SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 by factors of 3.0 and 4.9, respectively. Under red illumination (625 nm), the optimal heterostructure yielded a maximum photocurrent of 120.41 μA, which exceeded those of exceeding those of the devices composed of pure SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 by factors of 2.2 and 4.4, respectively. Finally, the maximum photocurrent of 62.18 μA obtained by the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 (5.0 g) heterostructure in the NIR region (850 nm) outperformed those of the devices composed of pure SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 by factors of 1.3 and 4.3, respectively. These results can be directly related to the schemata presented in Figure 7 and the band energy diagram presented in Figure 8G, where optoelectronic properties were associated with the extent to which electron transfer occurred between the SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 components in the heterostructures, and where contact between the Bi2Se3 nanoplates on the SnSe2 microplates establish a built-in electric field promoting effective electron-hole separation. Furthermore, as the coverage of Bi2Se3 decreases, the photoelectric current response between them gradually increases, which can also be observed in Figure 4; this also effectively demonstrates that a lower coverage is more conducive to improving its electron migration rate and path. Furthermore, through IPCE tests conducted at 0.6 V and 1.2 V, we can observe that the IPCE of SnSe2-Bi2Se3 (5.0 g) is improved compared to both Bi2Se3 and SnSe2. This indicates that the composite material enhances carrier migration, thereby improving the performance of the composite (see supporting information, Figure S1).

The PL spectroscopy results obtained for photodetector devices composed of SnSe2 layers, Bi2Se3 layers, and the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 (5.0 g) heterostructure composite are presented in Figure 12. These results can be analyzed according to a previously reported methodology.23 As indicated by the steady-state PL spectra shown in Figure 12A, all photodetector devices exhibited a strong PL emission peak at a wavelength of 435 nm, which is attributable to the recombination of excitons. However, the pure SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 devices exhibited particularly intense PL emission peaks, whereas the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure composite presented a greatly reduced PL intensity. This significant quenching of the PL signal provides strong evidence indicating that the 2D heterostructure composite offers enhanced charge separation efficiency.24 The time-resolved PL decay curves obtained for the three photodetector devices are presented in Figure 12B. This experimental data were fitted with bi-exponential decay functions to determine the fluorescence lifetimes of the three photodetector devices quantitatively according to a previously reported method.25 The obtained fluorescence lifetimes are compared as a bar chart in Figure 12C. As can be seen, the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composite exhibits a markedly enhanced fluorescence lifetime, which is longer than those of pristine Bi2Se3 and SnSe2 by factors of approximately four and two, respectively. This prolonged lifetime characterizes the enhanced charge separation efficiency of the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 composite by representing a substantial reduction in available non-radiative recombination pathways, resulting in more stable and persistent charge carriers. This property is highly advantageous for high-performance photodetector applications requiring prolonged detection stability.

Discussion

The present work addressed the lack of available means for producing high-performance TMDC heterostructures in a cost effective manner at large scale, while also addressing the poorly developed means of effectively regulating the electronic band structure and electron transfer pathways between the two materials by developing 2D c-axis-aligned, vertically stacked SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructures via a simple two-step hydrothermal technique. Specifically, the process achieved the epitaxial growth of Bi2Se3 nanoplates with thicknesses of ∼500 nm on SnSe2 microplate substrates with thicknesses of ∼2 μm. The electronic band structure and electron transfer pathways between the two materials were adjusted facilely by controlling the average coverage of Bi2Se3 nanoplates on the individual microplates during synthesis via adjustments in the concentration of SnSe2 microplates with a fixed Bi2Se3 content, where the surface coverage of Bi2Se3 decreases with increasing SnSe2, a decreasing SnSe2 content results in greater Bi2Se3 coverage on the surface. The 2D Bi2Se3 nanoplates are prone to grow on the SnSe2 microplates layer-by-layer along their [001] directions, which form 2D heterostructures with high crystallinity. In addition, the extremely small bandgap energy of Bi2Se3 substantially reduces the equivalent bandgap energy of the heterostructure composite, and thereby increases the wavelength range over which light is absorbed. Furthermore, a built-in electric field at the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterojunction interface promotes the separation and migration of photo-excited charge carriers. Comprehensive evaluation of SnSe2/Bi2Se3 photodetector devices with the optimal SnSe2 content (5.0 g) confirms that the SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure composite achieves (1) enhanced photoresponsivity, (2) exceptional operational stability, where the initial photoresponse is maintained over prolonged operation, (3) consistent photoresponse across varying input irradiance values, and (4) accelerated photoresponse dynamics. As a result, the photoresponse of photodetectors composed of the optimal 2D SnSe2/Bi2Se3 (5.0 g) heterostructure composite outperforms photodetector devices based on the pristine SnSe2 and Bi2Se3 materials by factors of 3.0 and 4.9, respectively, under UV illumination at a wavelength of 365 nm and irradiance of 30 mW/cm2. Finally, the simple hydrothermal processing applied herein enables the cost-effective fabrication of high-performance SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure composites with photocurrent responses in the UV range that are 70% greater than that of the next best recently reported photodetector device. Accordingly, the current work provides an effective strategy for synthesizing 2D TMDC heterostructures with regulated electronic band structures providing enhanced photoresponse and high operational stability suitable for next-generation photodetectors.

Limitations of the study

Although this work enriches the theory of stacking growth for 2D materials, and reveals the mechanism of modulating electronic structures and electron transfer pathways through variations in layer coverage, it also acknowledges several limitations. The visualized evidence of electron migration between the heterostructures and the practical applications for the optoelectronic devices still need to be further verified and explored.

Method details

Materials

All chemicals used herein were purchased from Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd., and include tin (II) chloride dihydrate (SnCl2·2H2O), selenium dioxide (SeO2), hydrazine hydrate (N2H4·H2O), ethylene glycol (C2H6O2), sodium hydroxide (NaOH), ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA; C10H16N2O8), polyvinylpyrrolidone (PVP; (C6H9NO)n), ascorbic acid (C6H8O6), selenium (Se), and bismuth oxide (Bi2O3). In addition, a Nafion polymer dispersion, representing a dispersion of a perfluorosulfonic acid polymer in a mixture of ethanol and water, was purchased in 3A Materials. The deionized ultrapure water (UPW) used in the experiments was obtained using a commercial UPW system manufactured by the Sichuan ULUPURE Company. All chemical reagents were used without further purification.

Synthesis of pure SnSe2, Bi2Se3, and SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructures

The processes applied for fabricating 2D SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructures via simple hydrothermal methodology is illustrated in Figure 13. As can be seen, the process consists of preparing SnSe2 microplates, which serve as substrates for the epitaxial growth of Bi2Se3 nanoplates.

The SnSe2 microplate components were prepared by the following hydrothermal method. Firstly, 2 mmol of SnCl2·2H2O and 4 mmol of SeO2 were dissolved in 40 mL of deionized water, and the mixture was stirred in a magnetic stirrer operating at 400 rpm for 30 min. Afterward, 2 mL of N2H4·H2O was added, and the solution was stirred further at 400 rpm for 5 min. The resulting solution was transferred into a hydrothermal autoclave and heated at a temperature of 120°C, 150°C, 180°C, 210°C, or 240°C for 24 h. After being allowed to cool naturally to room temperature, the samples were washed three times with deionized water, followed by drying in an oven at a temperature of 60°C in air for 12 h. The resulting product was obtained as a black-colored powder.

We prepared SnSe2/Bi2Se3 powders by the following hydrothermal method. First, 20 mL of ethylene glycol was measured and transferred into a beaker. Then, 160 mg of solid NaOH pellets were added to the glycol, and the mixture was stirred in a magnetic stirrer operating at 600 rpm for 2 h to ensure complete dissolution of NaOH. A varying amount of SnSe2 powder (0.5 g, 1.0 g, 2.0 g, or 5.0 g) was added to the solution, and then subjected to thorough mixing 2 h. Here, varying the concentration of SnSe2 microplates with a fixed Bi2Se3 content enables the average coverage of Bi2Se3 nanoplates on the individual microplates to be adjusted facilely, where the surface coverage of Bi2Se3 decreases with increasing SnSe2, and a decreasing SnSe2 content results in greater Bi2Se3 coverage on the surface. Afterward, 400 mg of PVP and 438.4 mg of EDTA were combined in a beaker, and stirred in a magnetic stirrer operating at 800 rpm for 1.5 h to achieve complete dissolution. Next, reactants Bi2O3 (0.93192 g) and Se powder (0.4738 g) were added to the reaction mixture in conjunction with ascorbic acid (1.0567 g) as a reducing agent, and stirring continued at 800 rpm for an additional 2 h. The mixture was then transferred from the beaker to a high-pressure reactor and heated in an oven at a temperature of 210°C in air for 24 h. After allowing to cool naturally to room temperature, the product was washed three times using deionized water and absolute ethanol via high-speed centrifugation conducted at 10,000 rpm for 10 min per cycle. The washed product was then dried in an oven at a temperature of 60°C in air for 6 h, yielding a black powder. Pure Bi2Se3 was synthesized following the identical procedure without adding the SnSe2 powder.

Characterizations

The phase composition and crystal structure of pure SnSe2, pure Bi2Se3, and SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterojunction composite materials were characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD) using a Bruker D8 ADVANCE diffractometer (Billerica, MA, USA) operating with CuKα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å) at a voltage of 40 kV and current of 40 mA. The morphology, lattice fringes, and elemental distributions of the materials were analyzed by scanning transmission electron microscopy (STEM) using a Thermo Fisher Scientific Talos F200X microscope (Waltham, MA, USA) in conjunction with an integrated X-ray energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS) unit. Elemental surface composition and atomic states were determined by X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) using a Thermo Scientific Escalab 250XI spectrometer (Waltham, MA, USA), where binding energies were calibrated to the standard binding energy of the C1s peak at 284.8 eV. The generation, separation, and migration of optically excited charge carriers within the photodetector devices were evaluated by UV-visible-near-infrared (UV-vis-NIR) spectrophotometry and UV photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS) conducted using ultraviolet photoelectron spectroscopy (UPS, Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). The transition and separation efficiency of electron-hole pairs in the photodetector devices were evaluated via photoluminescence (PL) spectroscopy using the FLS1000 fluorescence spec-trometer, Edinburgh Instruments Ltd. Electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were captured for the photodetector devices using a CH Instruments 700E electrochemical workstation (Shanghai, China) with a saturated calomel reference electrode, platinum (Pt) counter electrode, and an indium tin oxide (ITO) film deposited on glass serving as the working electrode.

Photodetector performance evaluation

Photodetector devices were fabricated using pure SnSe2 microplate, pure Bi2Se3 nanoplate, and SnSe2/Bi2Se3 heterostructure powders with different Bi2Se3 nanoplate coverage values. The following fabrication process was applied for all devices uniformly. First, 10 mg of the respective powder was dispersed in 1 mL of an aqueous ethanol solution composed of 750 μL deionized water and 250 μL ethanol via ultrasonication conducted for 10 min. After adding 50 μL of the Nafion dispersion, the mixture was subjected to further ultrasonication for 30 min to form a homogeneous suspension. A 150 μL aliquot of the suspension was drop-cast onto an ITO-coated glass substrate and air-dried at room temperature. The area of the ITO glass required for the experiment is 1 × 1 cm2.

For transient photocurrent testing, the photodetector was connected to a digital source meter integrated with the 700E electrochemical workstation. The electrochemical setup employed during testing included the ITO film deposited on glass serving as the working electrode, an Ag/AgCl reference electrode, a Pt wire counter electrode, and a 0.2 mol L−1 Na2SO3 electrolyte solution. The electrochemical assembly was secured in a light-tight enclosure to eliminate ambient light interference. All devices were tested under monochromatic light with a wavelength of 365, 470, 530, 625, or 850 nm and a uniform irradiance of 30 mW/cm2. Hence, the tests were conducted under UV, blue, green, red, and NIR wavelengths, respectively. Tests were composed of 10, 20-s cycles comprising 10 s of illumination followed by 10 s of darkness. All photoresponse currents were recorded at a bias voltage of −0.1 V.

Quantification and statistical analysis

The sample phase data was characterized by X-ray diffraction (XRD). The sample morphology data was characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and high-resolution transmission electron microscopy (HRTEM). The ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy data was characterized by ultraviolet-visible spectrophotometer. Electrochemical impedance and photoelectric current response were analyzed for performance by an electrochemical workstation. Original data generated corresponding charts using Origin software.
