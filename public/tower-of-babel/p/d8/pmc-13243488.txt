Serpina1e mediates the exercise-induced enhancement of hippocampal memory in male mice
Authors: Hyunyoung Kim, Sanghee Shin, Jeongho Han, Kangseok Yun, Jong-Seo Kim, Hyungju Park
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Exercise enhances learning and memory, not only through improved cardiometabolic but also through body-brain interactions mediated by secreted factors. Given the prominent role of skeletal muscle during exercise, muscle-derived factors, myokines, are believed to mediate the exercise-induced cognitive enhancements. Here, we demonstrate that intramuscular Serpina1e is upregulated following exercise in male mice. Systemic delivery of recombinant Serpina1e or intramuscular overexpression of Serpina1e reproduces exercise-induced memory enhancements in sedentary male mice. Conversely, muscle-specific depletion of Serpina1e abolishes hippocampal memory enhancement, indicating a requirement of muscle-derived Serpina1e for these cognitive benefits. Mechanistically, elevated plasma Serpina1e stimulates neurogenesis, brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression, and neurite growth in the hippocampus by crossing the blood–cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) and blood–brain barrier. Our findings identify Serpina1e as a key mediator of skeletal muscle-brain interaction that enables the beneficial effects of exercise on cognitive function.

Introduction

Exercise is considered a noninvasive intervention for cognitive enhancement in healthy young and aged individuals1–3. In addition to exercise capacity showing a strong positive correlation with academic ability in healthy humans4, the restorative effect of exercise on cognitive function in normal aging and in patients with neurological diseases such as Alzheimer’s disease, Parkinson’s disease, depression, epilepsy, and stroke has been suggested5. A part of these positive effects of exercise on cognitive functions is thought to be mediated by interactions between peripheral organs and the brain through the exercise-induced secretory factors called exerkines6 including blood plasma proteins7,8, especially via the actions of secretory proteins9–11.

Skeletal muscle plays a pivotal role during exercise and is the primary organ that responds to physical activity through alterations in gene expression and protein content12. There are several pathways that mediate muscle-brain communication13, including myokines, metabolites, and exosomes. Given that skeletal muscle is an active endocrine organ that releases cytokines called myokines14, exercise-induced myokines could be potential intermediaries for improved learning and memory through skeletal muscle–brain communication15. Indeed, several in vitro or ex vivo studies identified myokines such as Cathepsin B (Ctsn)9 and Irisin16 as being involved in exercise-induced cognitive enhancements. Despite these findings, the use of exercise interventions for cognitive and mental health issues has been limited17, as the detailed mechanisms of myokines involved in exercise-induced muscle-brain communication and their influence on cognitive functions are not yet fully understood.

Here, we identify exercise-induced myokines using an organ-specific secretome-labeling tool and their functions on cognitive functions.

Results

Construction of a mouse model for in vivo labeling of myokines

To construct an in vivo myokine labeling tool, we first produced a knock-in mouse model using an expression cassette for TurboID18, an efficient biotin ligase, which contains the KDEL sequences to restrict TurboID localization into the endoplasmic reticulum lumen (ER). V5-tagged TurboID-KDEL (V5-TurboID-KDEL; TurboID-ER) expression was designed to be induced by Cre-dependent removal of the stop codon by placing a LoxP-Stop-LoxP (LSL) component between the CAG promoter and the TurboID-ER coding sequence (LSL-TurboID-ER; Supplementary Fig. 1). We validated the Cre-dependent expression of TurboID-ER by injecting Cre-expressing AAV into the LSL-TurboID-ER knock-in mice (Supplementary Fig. 1). This result indicates that TurboID-ER can be selectively expressed in Cre-expressing cells, resulting in the biotinylation of secretory proteins synthesized and secreted from Cre-expressing source cells.

To label myokines released from skeletal muscles with biotin, LSL-TurboID-ER mice were crossed with Acta1-Cre (a skeletal muscle-specific Cre driver), resulting in ACTuR (Acta1-Cre;LSL-TurboID-ER) mice (Supplementary Fig. 2a). We observed that ACTuR mice selectively expressed TurboID-ER in skeletal muscles, which enabled the selective biotinylation of myokines (Supplementary Fig. 2b–d). To detect biotinylated myokines in ACTuR mice, we intraperitoneally injected biotin for 6 days and harvested several organs, such as the brain (cortex and hippocampus), liver, lung and heart, and the hindlimb muscles. TurboID-ER-induced biotinylated proteins were significantly distinct from endogenous biotinylated proteins (Supplementary Fig. 2c, d). Given the enrichment of TurboID-ER expression in the ER of skeletal muscles (Supplementary Fig. 3) but no secreted TurboID-ER protein in plasma (Supplementary Fig. 2e), we concluded that TurboID-induced biotinylated proteins are selectively derived from skeletal muscles.

No alterations in anxiety or depression level, or adverse effects on motor function or learning and memory were detected in ACTuR mice (Supplementary Fig. 4). Collectively, we concluded that the physiological and cognitive functions of ACTuR mice are comparable to those of wild-type mice and that ACTuR mice were suitable for labeling and detecting exercise-induced myokines in vivo.

Detecting aerobic exercise-induced myokines from the blood plasma of ACTuR mice

We next explored exercise-induced myokines using ACTuR mice. First, wild-type (WT) mice subjected to four weeks of voluntary aerobic exercise using a running wheel exhibited enhanced memory performance in the contextual fear conditioning (CFC) test (Fig. 1a–c). The Morris water maze test with five daily trials (short-learning version), which is less sensitive to subtle cognitive differences but enough for detecting spatial memory performance, showed the exercise-induced increase in the retrieval of spatial memory with no significant changes in the learning rate (Supplementary Fig. 5). These enhanced memory performances were positively correlated with exercise intensity (Fig. 1d; Supplementary Fig. 5) and lasted for three weeks (Supplementary Fig. 6). Using the same exercise protocol, we harvested blood plasma of biotin-administered ACTuR mice following four weeks of exercise to detect exercise-induced biotinylated proteins (putative myokines in ACTuR; Fig. 1f) in plasma. As a negative control, the same volume of phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), instead of biotin, was injected into ACTuR mice. Western blot analysis with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated streptavidin revealed that the amount of plasma biotinylated proteins was increased in exercised mice compared to sedentary biotin-injected ACTuR mice (Fig. 1g, h). This exercise-induced increase in plasma myokine levels was also correlated with exercise intensity (Fig. 1i). These results demonstrate that the ACTuR mouse model is a useful tool for detecting exercise-induced plasma myokines. The beneficial effect of exercise on cognitive functions may be mediated by these exercise-induced myokines.

Identification and molecular characterization of exercise-induced myokines

To identify molecular identities of exercise-induced plasma myokines, we employed a super-resolution proximity labeling (SR-PL)19,20 method using liquid chromatography‒tandem mass spectrometry (LC‒MS/MS; Fig. 2a). Our MS/MS analysis revealed that a total of 131 proteins were biotinylated in the plasma samples, suggesting that these proteins derived from skeletal muscles. These biotinylated proteins were quantified based on label-free quantification (LFQ) approach with good reproducibility among biological replicates (Supplementary Fig. 7a). Of the 131 proteins, we selected 78 proteins that were found in at least 3 replicates for further analysis. These 78 proteins overlapped well with the mouse plasma proteome21(86%; Fig. 2b) and skeletal muscle proteome reference datasets22(88%; Fig. 2c). Principal component analysis (PCA) showed groups of clusters among replicates in each experimental group (Supplementary Fig. 7b). As demonstrated in further analysis, the levels of most of the identified myokines (60 of 78 myokines; ~77%) significantly increased after exercise (p-value < 0.1, -log2(fold change)> 0.6; Supplementary Data 1; Fig. 2d, e). Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis of upregulated exercise-induced putative myokines categorized them as cellular components of extracellular region, which coincides with the feature of secreted proteins (Fig. 2f). Furthermore, biological processing terms such as homeostasis, immune response and response to cytokine were enriched from GO term analysis (Supplementary Fig 7e), all of which are known to be related to exercise23,24. Additional STRING network analysis of exercise-enriched putative myokines demonstrated highly interactive networks among these proteins, further suggesting that these proteins are likely to be involved in similar functional categories (Supplementary Fig 7d).

Our proteomics analysis identified previously reported exercise-induced factors such as Clusterin10 and Bche7, supporting the previous findings that both Clusterin and Bche are exercise-induced peripheral factors and suggesting their partial derivation from skeletal muscles (Supplementary Data 1).

Serpina1 family proteins are novel exercise-induced myokines

In this study, we focused on Serpina1 proteins, whose role in an exercise-induced improvement of cognitive function is completely unknown. The Serpin family of proteins is abundant in blood and known to be involved in serine protease inactivation25, blood coagulation, and inflammation26, as well as being associated with normal liver and lung function27,28. In line with our proteomics results, plasma concentrations of the Serpina1 family were reported to be elevated after six weeks of wheel running7. While liver-derived Serpina1 can trigger multiple signaling pathways elevating cellular energy and glucose metabolism29, how muscle-derived Serpina1 contributes to exercise-associated cognitive changes is still unclear. Our GO analysis revealed that the term negative regulation of peptidase activity was enriched in these genes (Supplementary Fig. 7), but the functions of Serpina1 proteins have yet to be reported in relation to exercise-induced modulations of cognitive functions. Considering its anti-inflammatory functions26, we hypothesized a possible association of Serpina1 with cognitive enhancement.

We found that skeletal muscle-derived Serpina1 family proteins, such as Serpina1a and Serpina1e, were significantly increased in the plasma of exercised ACTuR mice (Fig. 2g). Because our exercise protocol elevated expression levels of Serpina1 transcripts in skeletal muscle (Supplementary Fig. 8), exercise-induced Serpina1 proteins from skeletal muscles may contribute to the pool of circulating Serpina1 proteins. To validate an increase in muscle-derived Serpina1 in blood plasma following exercise, we estimated the relative amounts of plasma Serpina1 proteins derived from skeletal muscles or non-skeletal muscle tissues (Fig. 3a). Myokines could be isolated by enriching the biotinylated proteins in the plasma fraction of ACTuR mice using streptavidin-conjugated beads (Fig. 3a). After enrichment for biotinylation, the plasma proteins would be separated into two pools: those derived from skeletal muscle (representing myokines) and the leftover non-skeletal muscle-derived proteins (representing proteins derived from other organs). We found a significant increase in skeletal muscle-derived Serpina1 in exercised animals compared to sedentary animals (Fig. 3b, e; Supplementary Fig. 9). In contrast, the level of non-biotinylated Serpina1 family proteins remained unchanged (Fig. 3d, Supplementary Fig. 10), although the plasma level of Bche (Butyrylcholinesterase), which is known to be mainly derived from the liver, was significantly elevated by exercise (Supplementary Fig. 10). These data suggest that the contribution of Serpina1 proteins from non-skeletal muscle organs to the exercise-induced elevation in systemic Serpina1 is negligible. Collectively, these results validate our proteomic analysis, indicating that chronic exercise upregulates skeletal muscle-derived Serpina1 in the circulation.

Both systemic and muscle-specific elevation of Serpin1e level enhances hippocampal memory

Because the level of skeletal muscle-derived Serpina1 was elevated in the plasma of exercised mice, we next tested whether Serpina1 itself can mimic the beneficial effects of exercise on learning and memory. To artificially elevate the circulating plasma levels of Serpina1, we administered V5-tagged recombinant Serpina1 proteins into the bloodstream. Among the five paralogs of Serpina1 in C57BL/6 mice28,30, Serpina1a-d proteins are reported to be highly conserved and expressed at similar levels, with functions of inhibiting the activity of proteases such as elastases30. In contrast, Serpina1e is expressed at a lower level than the other Serpina1 paralog proteins and does not have protease inhibitor activity30. Accordingly, we selected Serpina1a and Serpina1e for further studies to test their ability to modulate cognition.

V5-tagged recombinant Serpina1a and Serpina1e (Serpina1a-V5 and Serpina1e-V5, respectively; 150 µg) were intravenously (i.v.) injected through the retro-orbital vein of WT sedentary mice every three days for 4 weeks, while the same volume of PBS was injected as a control (Fig. 4a). These systemic administrations of Serpina1a or Serpina1e resulted in no alterations in basal fear behavior (Fig. 4b; Supplementary Figs. 11, 13), anxiety levels or general behaviors (Supplementary Fig. 15). Suppression of body weight gain was observed only in the mice injected with Serpina1a (Supplementary Fig. 13), consistent with the exercised mice (Supplementary Fig. 6d). Our results showed that sedentary mice administered recombinant Serpina1e exhibited improved performance in the CFC test (Fig. 4c; Supplementary Fig. 11), while Serpina1a had no such effect (Supplementary Fig. 13). Our preliminary results from the MWM test (Supplementary Fig. 12) also demonstrated a consistent improvement in spatial memory performance following the administration of recombinant Serpina1e. Hippocampal memory was still improved after systemic co-injection of Serpina1a and Serpina1e (Supplementary Fig. 14), again supporting the functional role of Serpina1e in memory enhancement.

Next, we validated the beneficial effects of Serpina1e on cognitive functions by testing the memory performance of WT mice with skeletal muscle-specific Serpina1e overexpression (Fig. 4d). Serpina1e was overexpressed in the skeletal muscles of the four limbs via intramuscular (i.m.) delivery of adeno-associated virus (AAV9) containing CMV promoter-driven Serpina1e (Fig. 4d). Our data showed that sedentary mice with muscular Serpina1e overexpression also exhibited improved performance in the CFC test and novel object recognition (NOR) test (Fig. 4e-g), with no significant alterations in locomotion or anxiety levels (Supplementary Fig. 16b, c). Because overexpression of Serpina1e in skeletal muscle significantly elevated the plasma Serpina1 level (Fig. 4h, i; Supplementary Fig. 16d, e), an increase in systemic Serpina1e by muscle-derived Serpina1e attributes to enhancements in hippocampus-dependent cognitive functions.

Reduction of Serpina1e in skeletal muscle prevents exercise-induced cognitive enhancement

Given that muscle-derived Serpina1e promotes hippocampal memory, we next investigated whether muscle Serpina1e is necessary for exercise-induced hippocampal memory enhancement. First, we generated a lentivirus containing hCas9 and two guide RNAs targeting Serpina1e (LV-hCas9:T2A:Puro-U6>Serpina1egRNA1-U6>Serpina1egRNA2; S1eKO) to reduce the Serpina1e transcript level. A lentivirus expressing EGFP and mCherry (LV-EGFP:T2A:Puro-EF1a>mCherry) was used as a control. The selective reduction of Serpina1e by the S1eKO virus was validated in AML12 cells treated with either control or S1eKO lentivirus (Supplementary Fig. 17a–d). A muscle-specific reduction in Serpina1e could be achieved by intramuscular injection of the S1eKO lentivirus (Supplementary Fig. 17e, f). To examine the requirement of Serpina1e for exercise-induced memory enhancement, we intramuscularly injected control or S1eKO lentivirus into the four limb muscles (Fig. 4j). Two weeks after viral injection, mice were randomly assigned to either the sedentary groups (CTL and S1eKO) or exercised groups (CTL/EX and S1eKO/EX). The mice subsequently underwent NOR and CFC tests following four weeks of wheel-running exercise.

Our results showed that S1eKO mice without exercise exhibited normal basal activity and hippocampal memory, suggesting that a reduction in muscle Serpina1e in non-exercised animals does not affect overall behavioral or cognitive functions (Fig. 4k–m; Supplementary Fig. 17j, k). However, S1eKO/EX mice, despite similar amounts of exercise (Supplementary Fig. 17h), failed to show post-exercise enhancement in memory retrieval in the NOR and CFC tests (Fig. 4k, m). Furthermore, no positive correlation between running distance and memory performance was found (Supplementary Fig. 17i). Collectively, these results indicate that exercise-induced cognitive enhancement is dependent on muscle-derived Serpina1e.

After all behavioral tests, hindlimb tissues were collected to evaluate Serpina1e mRNA expression levels in the skeletal muscles of control and S1eKO lentivirus-injected mice. We found that S1eKO lentivirus sufficiently reduced Serpina1e mRNA expression in skeletal muscle and abolished the exercise-induced upregulation of Serpina1e expression (Supplementary Fig. 17f).

Taken together, our results indicate that muscle-derived Serpina1e is both necessary and sufficient for exercise-induced cognitive enhancement.

Plasma Serpina1e can directly migrate to the brain

To dissect the detailed mechanisms by which plasma Serpina1e enhances hippocampal memory, we investigated whether plasma Serpina1e could cross the blood–cerebral spinal fluid (CSF) and brain–blood barriers to reach the brain. We first tested whether recombinant Serpina1e administered in plasma could be found in CSF. Pure CSF samples with negligible blood contamination (Supplementary Fig. 18a–c) were collected one hour after intravenous injection of recombinant Serpina1e-V5 (Fig. 5a). We could detect recombinant Serpina1e-V5 in not only plasma but also CSF (Fig. 5b; Supplementary Fig. 18d–f), indicating that peripheral Serpina1e can cross the blood–CSF barrier. Moreover, recombinant Serpina1e-V5 was also detected in hippocampal tissues an hour or three hours after intravenous injection (Fig. 5c, d; Supplementary Fig. 18g–i). Given that plasma recombinant Serpina1e can be directly transported to the brain, hippocampal learning and memory may be directly affected by Serpina1e. To test this possibility, Serpina1e (100 ng per hemisphere) was intracerebrally (i.c.) administered into the bilateral dorsal hippocampus of WT sedentary mice every three days for 4 weeks (Fig. 5e; Supplementary Fig. 19). No alterations in body weight gain or general behavior (Supplementary Fig. 19b–d) were observed from these mice, similar to the findings in mice intravenously administered Serpina1e (Supplementary Fig. 11c; Supplementary Fig. 15). Twenty-four hours after the last administration, the NOR and CFC test was performed to examine the effects of the hippocampal delivery of Serpina1e on hippocampal cognitive functions (Fig. 5e). Both NOR and CFC test results showed that hippocampal memory was significantly enhanced by the hippocampal delivery of recombinant Serpina1e (Fig. 5f, h), consistent with the effects of the plasma or skeletal muscle-specific overexpression of Serpina1e on hippocampal memory (Fig. 4). Together, these results suggest that the positive impact of exercise on hippocampal function is at least in part mediated by plasma Serpina1e that has directly migrated to the hippocampus.

Promotion of hippocampal neural growth signaling by Serpina1e

Next, we explored the mechanisms underlying the Serpina1e-induced enhancement of hippocampal learning and memory. Aerobic exercise facilitates synaptic plasticity, which is instrumental in learning and memory1, and promotes growth signaling-related molecular and cellular events such as facilitated neurogenesis and brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) expression in the brain32.

We thus investigated whether Serpina1e can directly promote BDNF expression and hippocampal neurogenesis (Fig. 6a). Our results showed that the administration of recombinant Serpina1e to sedentary mice significantly increased BDNF protein expression (Fig. 6b). In addition, the number of 5-bromo-2-deoxyuridine (BrdU) and NeuN double-positive cells in the dentate gyrus (DG) of the hippocampus was also upregulated, while the number of BrdU-positive but NeuN-negative cells remained unchanged (Fig. 6c–f), indicating increased neurogenesis. Conversely, mice injected with the S1eKO lentivirus into skeletal muscles failed to exhibit exercise-induced upregulation of BrdU/NeuN double-positive cells, whereas the basal neurogenesis rate remained unaffected by muscle-specific reduction of Serpina1e (Fig. 6g, h; Supplementary Fig. 17l, m). Neurotrophic factors, such as BDNF, promote neurite growth and increase the morphological complexity of neurons33. To test whether Serpina1e can directly induce similar structural changes, we treated primary cultured neurons with recombinant Serpina1e (Supplementary Fig. 20a). We observed that Serpina1e treatment increases total neurite length and branch number (Supplementary Fig. 20b−e), and morphological complexity (Supplementary Fig. 20f). These effects appear to be dose-dependent, as structural changes in cultured neurons increased in proportion to the concentration of recombinant Serpina1e. Furthermore, treatment with human SERPINA1 produced similar positive effects on neurite outgrowth (Supplementary Fig. 20g−l), suggesting that the neuronal growth function of SERPINA1 is conserved in Serpina1e.

To validate this function in vivo, we investigated whether muscle-derived Serpina1e promotes hippocampal neuronal growth. We utilized Thy1-EGFP mice34, which allows us to visualize single-neuron morphology, with intramuscular injection of AAV9-CMV-mSerpina1e:T2A:EGFP. Our results showed that intramuscular overexpression of Serpina1e increases dendritic length, branch number, and neurite complexity in hippocampal neurons (Fig. 6i−n), consistent with our in vitro results (Supplementary Fig. 20). Additionally, we evaluated general dendritic arborization in the hippocampus by quantifying the level of Microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2), a marker highly enriched in the dendritic compartment and strongly associated with branched neurites35,36. In line with in vivo single neuronal morphological analysis, the average MAP2 intensity in the hippocampal CA1 and DG area of muscle Serpina1e-overexpressed mice was significantly higher than that of control mice (Supplementary Fig. 21). These results indicate that muscle-derived Serpina1e promotes structural complexity in hippocampal neurons.

Discussion

Our study identifies Serpina1e as a novel myokine that mediates exercise-induced cognitive improvement. Although previous reports have shown increases in plasma Serpina1 (mouse) and SERPINA1 (human) following exercise7,10, our study is the first to reveal that Serpina1e is secreted from skeletal muscles. Using ACTuR mice, which enable the selective biotin-labeling of skeletal muscle-originated proteins, we detected exercise-induced myokines in plasma through direct LC-MS/MS detection of biotinylated lysine residues in enriched biotinylated proteins (Fig. 2)20.

Our LC-MS/MS analysis did not detect the reported myokines such as Irisin16 and Ctsb9, likely due to their low plasma concentration or inherent limitations of TurboID-based labeling. In fact, TurboID-mediated biotinylation targets only surface-exposed lysine residues within ~10 nm of the enzyme, and post-translational modifications (e.g., acetylation) or structural constraints may further restrict labeling20. Moreover, the in vivo labeling efficiency of TurboID is relatively low37 and is inherently influenced by the subcellular localization of both the enzyme and target substrates18. Consequently, cytosolic or non-classically secreted myokines, such as Irisin and Cathepsin B, which may bypass the conventional ER-Golgi secretion pathway, are unlikely to be biotinylated and thus undetected in our system. These technical limitations, inherent to proximity labeling approaches, underscore the future need for complementary strategies to capture the full spectrum of the exercise-responsive secretome.

Despite these limitations, we have identified exercise-induced plasma proteins such as Clusterin10 and Bche7, and the Serpina1 family. These findings demonstrate that plasma proteins previously considered as liver-derived can also be secreted from skeletal muscle. Although the liver has been suggested as the main source29,38, we found that Serpina1e is upregulated in skeletal muscle at both mRNA and protein levels (Figs. 2 and 3; Supplementary Figs. 8 and 9), whereas liver expression and non-muscle-derived Serpina1 remained unchanged (Fig. 3; Supplementary Fig. 10). Similarly, an organism-wide secretome atlas covering 21 cell types across 10 tissues showed that Serpina1e was reported as secreted from both liver and skeletal muscle, with comparable contributions under sedentary conditions39. Collectively, these data indicate that skeletal muscle, rather than the liver, is a physiologically relevant and exercise-responsive source of circulating Serpina1e, and that the ACTuR system is a powerful tool for discovering novel myokines.

Functionally, we demonstrate that an increase in systemic or hippocampal Serpina1e levels is sufficient to enhance BDNF expression, neurogenesis, neurite growth, and hippocampal memory (Figs. 4–6). On the other hand, exercise-induced memory enhancement depends on Serpina1e expression in skeletal muscles (Fig. 4). Together, these results underscore the essential role of Serpina1e in exercise-induced cognitive enhancement. A key remaining question is the exact molecular mechanisms implicated in Serpina1e-mediated cognitive benefits of exercise. Further investigations into both the molecular differences between muscle- and non-muscle-derived Serpina1e and their downstream signaling pathways are warranted.

Our findings suggest that Serpina1e contributes to cognitive enhancement as part of a cooperative myokine or exerkine network. This potential cooperation is exemplified by the functional overlap between Serpina1e and Fndc5/Irisin, both of which promote hippocampal neurogenesis and BDNF expression. We further found that manipulation of Serpina1e expression positively correlated with Fndc5 expression in skeletal muscle, while Ctsb remained unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 22). These observations support the idea that exercise-induced cognitive enhancement depends on a coordinated functional network of multiple myokines. In the brain, downstream signaling pathways of Serpina1e and Fndc5/Irisin may converge on overlapping mechanisms, including uncoupling protein 1 (UCP1)-linked signaling29,40. Additionally, the anti-inflammatory properties of Serpina1e36,41,42 may correlate with those of Irisin43,44. Similar lactate-FNDC5 crosstalk was previously demonstrated to promote BDNF expression during intense exercise45. Therefore, our data, together with results showing the loss of exercise-induced hippocampal enhancement by muscle-specific Serpina1e knockdown (Fig. 4k, m; Supplementary Fig. 17i) or genetic deletion of Fndc543, highlight essential and potentially cooperative roles for Serpina1e and FNDC5/Irisin in the cognitive benefits of exercise.

Nevertheless, the differential actions of exercise-induced factors on cognitive functions cannot be excluded. Irisin and Ctsb are linked to the distinct downstream pathways, including cAMP/PKA signaling16 and the Wnt and Notch receptor pathways46, respectively, suggesting multiple mechanistic routes for neurotrophic events in the hippocampus. The anti-inflammatory effects of exercise on the brain may also be achieved differentially by other exerkines, such as Clusterin (Fig. 2d; Supplementary Data 1)10. Therefore, future studies should address whether these myokines act synergistically or independently to improve cognitive functions.

By restoring both neuronal loss and decreased BDNF levels in the hippocampus, which are prominent in the aged brain47,48, exercise has been suggested to ameliorate cognitive decline in middle-aged or old mice2,49. Our study using young adult mice revealed that exercise-induced Serpina1e is effective in elevating both hippocampal neurogenesis and BDNF, hallmarks of exercise-induced effects. It is thus possible that Serpina1e-dependent mechanisms, together with other exercise-induced myokines, play significant roles in the exercise-induced restoration of age-associated cognitive decline. Further studies are required to test this possibility.

Several limitations remain to be resolved before translating our findings to human. First, given the structural and functional divergence between human (SERPINA1) and murine Serpina1a-e genes, whether our findings extend to human muscle and brain should be extensively investigated. Second, the human SERPINA1 gene is a direct target gene of the estrogen receptor and a predictor of survival rate in patients with breast cancer50. Serpina1e-induced effects may thus be influenced by sex-specific mechanisms. Although the current study focused on male animals to avoid any potential effects of sex-dependent physiological and behavioral variabilities, future studies are warranted to consider comparative investigations of Serpina1e-mediated cognitive modulation in both sexes.

Overall, this study presents a novel myokine, Serpina1e, which we propose is a key mediator of exercise-induced cognitive enhancements. Our findings identify the Serpina1e pathway as a promising therapeutic target for ameliorating cognitive dysfunction in normal aging or neurodegenerative diseases.

Methods

All mouse experiments were performed in accordance with all ethical regulations and institutional guidelines approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) of the KBRI (IACUC-24-00020-M1).

Animals

The following mouse lines were used: C57BL/6 mice (The Jackson Laboratory), ACTA1-Cre mice (The Jackson Laboratory), Thy1-EGFP (The Jackson Laboratory) mice, LSL-TurboID-ER (loxP-stop-loxP-TurboID-V5-KDEL generated from Cyagen) mice, and ACTuR (ACTA1-Cre:LSL-TurboID-ER) mice,. ROSA26 was targeted by a dual-guide RNA (GGCAGGCTTAAAGGCTAACCTGG, CTCCAGTCTTTCTAGAAGATGGG) for the insertion of the TurboID-ER coding sequence via CRISPR/Cas9-mediated editing to generate LSL-TurboID-ER mice. To label myokines and identify exercise-induced myokines, the Acta1-Cre and LSL-TurboID-ER lines were crossed to produce ACTuR (Acta1-Cre:LSL-TurboID-ER) mice. All the mouse lines were maintained on a C57BL/6 genetic background. The mice were housed in a specific pathogen-free room at the Laboratory Animal Center in the Korea Brain Research Institute (KBRI) that was kept at 20–24 °C and 40–60% humidity under a 12–12 h light–dark cycle with dark hours between 20:00 and 08:00. The feed (ENVIGO) and water are supplied for 24 h. Young male adult (8–11-week-old) mice were randomly assigned to different groups for the experiments. For western blot analysis and LC‒MS/MS with myokines in plasma, ACTuR mice were used. For behavioral analysis after protein injections, protein infusions or viral infections, C57BL/6 wild type mice were used. For morphological analysis of single hippocampal pyramidal neurons, Thy1-EGFP mice were used.

Behavioral experiments

Behavioral testing of sedentary or exercised mice began the day after the last day of exercise. Behavioral testing of mice intravenously injected with Serpina1a or Serpina1e for 4 weeks began the day after the final injection. Also, behavioral testing of mice that received multiple infusions of Serpina1e into the hippocampus began the day after the final infusion. Additionally, behavioral testing of mice intramuscularly injected AAV into all four limbs began after 4 weeks from the injection day. The experimenters were blinded to the treatment during testing and analysis.

Voluntary exercise

Male mice (aged 8 to 11 weeks) were housed in pairs either in activity cages with access to an activity wheel or in standard housing cages (controls) for 4 weeks. All exercise experiments were performed in activity cages with a running wheel installed. The total running distance was calculated based on the number of wheel revolutions recorded by a revolution counter. Activity cages with a running wheel and revolution counter were purchased from Ugo Basile. Exercised mice were housed in the activity cages in pairs with free access to unlocked wheels at night (20:00-08:00) by following a 5-day-per-week regimen for four weeks. The control mice were housed in pairs in standard animal facility cages.

Open field test

The open field test was used to determine general activity levels and gross locomotor activity. The assessment took place in a square arena within a specially designed sound attenuating chamber. The arena was 30 cm (L) × 30 cm (W) × 25 cm (H). The mouse was placed in the corner of the testing arena and allowed to explore the arena for 5 min while being tracked by an automated tracking system of SMART3.0 (Panlab) software. Parameters, including distance moved, velocity, and time spent in predefined zones of the arena, were recorded. The arena was cleaned with distilled water and 70% ethanol at the end of the trial.

Elevated plus maze

The customized EPM apparatuses had two open arms measuring 30 cm in height and 6 cm in width and two closed arms with a maze wall height of 20 cm and 45 cm in height from the floor. The mice started the EPM test at the center of the maze and freely explored for 5 min while the video was recorded. Parameters, including distance moved and time spent in predefined zones of the arena, were recorded and analyzed with SMART 3.0 (Panlab) software. The arena was cleaned with distilled water and 70% ethanol at the end of the trial.

Forced swimming test

The forced swimming test was performed in a plastic cylinder (26 cm height, 11 cm diameter) filled to a depth of 18.5 cm with water (24–25 °C). The mice were exposed to the plastic cylinder for 15 min on the first day. After 24 h, the mice were placed into the same plastic cylinder for 5 min, and the data were recorded by a video camera. The immobility time and latency to the first bout of immobility were manually evaluated.

Rotarod test

The accelerated rotarod test was performed on a rotarod (Ugo Basile) with two sessions at 1 h intervals. Each session was subjected to three trials for 5 min with an acceleration of 4–40 rpm, and the results were recorded. For the analysis, the trial showing the longest latency to fall from the second session was selected as the indicator of motor coordination.

Morris water maze test

The Morris water maze was conducted in a circular pool (120 cm diameter). Tempera paint was added to the water until it became opaque, and a hidden platform (17 cm diameter) was placed 1 cm below the water surface. The water temperature was maintained at ± 1 °C from 21 °C. Black curtains surrounded the water tank, and distinct visual cues were hung from the curtains. The mice were monitored via the EthoVision XT (Noldus Information Technology) video tracking system directly above the water tank, and the parameters were measured using EthoVision software. Pseudorandomized platform locations and drop locations were recorded for each mouse, with one trial lasting 60 s. The trial ended either when the subject rested on the hidden platform for 5 s or when the duration of the trial expired. The trial was repeated four times for each mouse during each day of training. Five days of training were performed, and two days of probe trials were conducted following training. The time to reach the platform was recorded to obtain a learning curve graph of latency to escape. The time spent in the target quadrant, swimming speed, total distance moved, and number of platform crossings were analyzed.

CFC tests

CFC tests were performed in sound-attenuating chambers equipped with steel shocking floors (Panlab). Freezing behavior in the cages was recorded by a low-light camera, and the shock and freezing behavior analyses were automatically conducted by Packwin software (Panlab). During fear-conditioning training, the mice were habituated to the shock cage for 3 min and then subjected to pairing of a context (box) with a single foot shock for 2 sec at 0.5 mA. The mice were left in the shock cage for an additional 1 min after the shock and then returned to their home cage. The next day, the mice underwent contextual recall testing; contextual testing was performed in the same cages that were used during the training trial. Mouse freezing behavior was tracked over a period of 3 min. To examine long-term memory, the mice were subjected to contextual recall testing in the same context three weeks after the training day.

NOR test

NOR tests were conducted in the same arena where the open field tests were previously carried out. On the first day, the mice were habituated to the empty arena for 5 min. After habituation, a pair of identical objects were positioned at distinct corners of the arena, and subsequently, the mice were given a 5-min exploration period within the arena. This procedure facilitated the establishment of baseline familiarity with the objects. After 24 hours, the mice were introduced to a familiar object or a novel object within the same area to assess their discriminatory ability. The mice with total exploration time under three seconds were excluded because they do not exhibit enough time for exploration to recognize familiar object and novel object. The discrimination index (DI) was calculated using the time spent exploring a novel object and the time spent exploring a familiar object. Preparation of biotinylated myokines in plasma and tissues

To induce the biotinylation of myokines in ACTuR mice, we intraperitoneally injected biotin (24 mg/kg) dissolved in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS, Gibco). ACTuR mice were euthanized between 08:30 and 10:00. The mice were anesthetized with 1 ml of isoflurane (Piramal) in a sealed plastic box in preparation for perfusion or plasma collection. Blood was collected from the right heart ventricle with 5 µl of 0.5 M EDTA (Sigma‒Aldrich) and centrifuged at 4 °C for 20 min at 15,000 x g to collect the plasma. A protease inhibitor cocktail (P.I.C., Quartett) was added to the plasma. Tissues (brain, skeletal muscle, liver, lung, and heart) were harvested and homogenized with a bead homogenizer (Taco) and RIPA lysis buffer (Rockland). The tissue lysates were harvested from the supernatants of the tissue samples following centrifugation at 4 °C for 20 min at 15,000 x g.

Western blot analysis

The protein concentration was determined using a BCA assay kit (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The samples were boiled for 3 min at 95 °C in Laemmli buffer (Bio-Rad) and separated on precast 4‒20% polyacrylamide gels (Bio-Rad). Afterward, the proteins were transferred onto 0.2 µm PVDF membranes via a Turbo transfer system (Bio-Rad). The membranes were blocked for 1 h in 3% skim milk (Sigma‒Aldrich) in 1x TBS (Bio-Rad) with 0.1% Tween 20 (Sigma‒Aldrich), incubated overnight at 4 °C with the primary antibody, incubated for 2 h at room temperature with the HRP-conjugated secondary antibody and developed using an ECL prime solution (Thermo Scientific). The antibodies used were rabbit anti-V5 (Cell Signaling Technology), rabbit anti-Serpina1 (Invitrogen), HRP-conjugated anti-rabbit IgG (Bio-Rad), streptavidin-HRP (Thermo Scientific) and rabbit anti-β-actin-HRP antibodies (Cell Signaling Technology) and Ponceau S (Rockland).

Mass spectrometry

Sample preparation

Harvested plasma samples were lysed with 2% SDS in 1× TBS (25 mM Tris, 0.15 M NaCl, pH 7.2; Thermo Scientific) supplemented with a 1 × protease inhibitor cocktail. The lysates were clarified via ultrasonication (Bioruptor, diagenode) for 15 min in a cold-water bath. To remove free probes, 6 times the sample volume of cold acetone (−20 °C, Sigma‒Aldrich) was added to each lysate and kept at −20 °C. After at least two hours, the samples were centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 15 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was gently removed, and acetone precipitation was repeated with 5 mL of cold acetone containing a ⅙ volume of 1× TBS. After the supernatant was removed, the pellet was solubilized with 8 M urea (Sigma‒Aldrich) in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC, Sigma‒Aldrich). The protein concentration was measured, and the samples were denatured at 650 rpm for 1 h at 37 °C. After denaturation, the samples were reduced with a final concentration of 10 mM dithiothreitol (Sigma‒Aldrich) and incubated at 650 rpm for 1 h at 37 °C. The samples were alkylated by adding 40 mM iodoacetamide (Sigma‒Aldrich) to the final concentration and mixing at 650 rpm for 1 h at 37 °C. The samples were diluted eight times with 50 mM ABC, and CaCl2 (Alfa Aesar) was added to achieve a final concentration of 1 mM. Approximately 2 mg of protein sample was digested by adding trypsin (Thermo Scientific, 50:1 w/w) and incubated at 650 rpm for 6–18 h at 37 °C. After trypsinization, the digested peptide samples were incubated with 150 µL of streptavidin beads (Pierce, 88817), which were prewashed with 2 M urea in 1× TBS four times prior to sample enrichment. The samples were incubated for 1 h at room temperature with end-over-end rotation. To remove nonspecifically bound peptides, the beads were washed twice with 2 M urea in 50 mM ABC and then finally washed with distilled water. To elute the biotinylated peptides, elution buffer [80% acetonitrile (Sigma‒Aldrich), 0.2% TFA (Sigma‒Aldrich), and 0.1% formic acid (Thermo Scientific)] was added, and the mixture was incubated at 60 °C for 5 min. Each supernatant was transferred to new tubes, and the elution step was repeated 2–3 times. The combined elution fractions were dried using a SpeedVac (Eppendorf). The samples were stored at −20 °C or injected for mass spectrometry directly. To ensure consistency in sample preparation, all samples were processed in parallel to minimize variability and maintain experimental rigor. The dried samples were reconstituted in 50 mM ABC and 50% of each sample was injected for LC-MS/MS analysis.

LC‒MS/MS analysis of enriched peptide samples

Analytical capillary columns (100 cm × 75 µm i.d.) and trap columns (2 cm × 150 µm i.d.) were packed in-house with 3 µm Jupiter C18 particles (Phenomenex, Torrance). The long analytical column was placed in a column heater (Analytical Sales and Services) regulated to a temperature of 45 °C. The NanoAcquity UPLC system (Waters, Milford) was operated at a flow rate of 300 nL/min over 2 h with a linear gradient ranging from 95% solvent A (H2O with 0.1% formic acid) to 40% solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The enriched samples were analyzed on an Orbitrap Fusion Lumos mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) equipped with an in-house customized nanoelectrospray ion source. Precursor ions were acquired (m/z 300-1500) at 120 K resolving power, and the precursor for MS/MS analysis was isolated at 1.4 Th. Higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) with 30% collision energy was used for sequencing with an auto gain control (AGC) target of 1e5. The resolving power for the acquired MS2 spectra was set to 30k with a maximum injection time of 200 ms.

Sample preparation of non-biotinylated plasma proteins

Plasma samples from ACRuR mice were incubated with magnetic streptavidin beads (Dynabead M280, Invitrogen) for 30 min at room temperature. Sample tubes were placed on a magnetic stand, and supernatant (non-biotinylated protein fraction) were collected after magnetic separation. Plasma samples (2 ul) were then subjected to urea digestion in 100 uL of 8 M Urea prepared in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC). The samples were sonicated and centrifuged at 13,000 x g. Protein concentration was determined by BCA assay to calculate the appropriate amount of trypsin. Reduction and alkylation were performed with 10 mM DTT at 37 °C for 1 h and 40 mM IAA at 37 °C for 1 h, respectively. The samples were then diluted with 50 mM ABC to adjust the urea concentration below 1 M. trypsin digestion was carried out overnight at 37 °C in the presence of 1 mM CaCl2 at a 100:1 (protein:typsin) ratio. The reaction was quenched with TFA to a final concentration of 0.4%. Peptide were cleaned up using solid-phase extraction (SPE). The elutes were dried using a SpeedVac and reconstituted in 0.1% formic acid in H2O.

LC‒MS/MS analysis of non-biotinylated plasma proteome analysis

In-house packed analytical column was placed in a column heater (PRSO-V2 column Oven, Thermo) regulated to a temperature of 45 °C. The Vanquish Neo, UHPLC system, (Thermo scientific) was operated at a flow rate of 300 nL/min over 45 min with a linear gradient ranging from 99% solvent A (H2O with 0.1% formic acid) to 40% solvent B (acetonitrile with 0.1% formic acid). The enriched samples were analyzed on an Orbitrap Astral mass spectrometer (Thermo Scientific) equipped with an a Nanospray Flex ion source, Thermo Scientific ES072. MS spectra were collected in the Orbitrap every 1.0 s at a resolving power of 240,000 at m/z 200 with a normalized AGC target of 500% with maximum injection time of 5.0 ms. MS/MS scans were acquired in the Astral analyzer over a 380-980 m/z range with a normalized AGC target of 500% and a maximum injection time of 2.5 ms and an HCD collision energy setting of 27 %. Isolation widths of 2 Th were used.

Proteomic data analysis

MS data processing and protein identification for enriched peptide samples

All MS/MS data were searched by MaxQuant (version 1.6.2.3) with the Andromeda search engine at 10 ppm precursor ion mass tolerance against the Mus musculus proteome database (UP000000589, 55,367 entries, UniProt (www.uniprot.org), 2021-06-22). Label-free quantification (LFQ) and match-between runs were used with the following search parameters for the TurboID experiment: partial trypsin digestion, fixed carbaminomethylation on cysteine, dynamic oxidation of methionine, protein N-terminal acetylation with biotin (+ 226.0775) labels of lysine residues. A false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 1% was obtained for unique labeled peptides and unique labeled proteins. LFQ intensity values were log-transformed for further analysis, and missing values were filled by imputed values representing a normal distribution around the detection limit. To impute the missing values, first, the intensity distributions of the mean and standard deviation were determined; then, for the imputation values, a new distribution based on a Gaussian distribution with a downshift of 1.8 and width of 0.3 standard deviations was created for the total matrix.

MS data processing and protein identification for non-biotinylated plasma proteins

Acquired raw data was processed in DIA-NN version 2.1.0. Searched against mouse proteome database (UP000000589, 17,349 entries, UniProt (www.uniprot.org), 2025-05-29) as library free search. Label-free quantification (LFQ) and match-between runs were used with the following search parameters. Fixed carbamidomethylation on cysteine, dynamic oxidation of methionine, protein N-terminal acetylation. A false discovery rate (FDR) of less than 1% was obtained and LFQ intensity values were log-transformed for further analysis.

Principal component analysis (PCA) and GO enrichment analysis

Perseus (ver. 1.6.2.3) raw values were used to perform the PCA, and each set of experiments was normalized to the interval (0-2) independently and subjected to hierarchical clustering analysis with the following options: Euclidean distance with the option of preprocessing with k-means clustering, 10 maximal number of iterations and preserving order of column constraints. Defined genes within each cluster were subjected to GO enrichment analysis via the DAVID online tool (https://david.ncifcrf.gov/). The background used for GO analysis for each cluster was set as the entire proteome of Homo sapiens.

Semi-quantitative RT-PCR

To measure the mRNA expression of exercise-induced genes (Serpina1a, Serpina1e, Fndc5, Ctsb) in tissues (hindlimbs, liver, lung, and heart), the mRNA was manually extracted after exercise using Trizol (Thermo Scientific) and Chloroform (Sigma‒Aldrich). For Semi-quantitative reverse transcription and polymerase chain reaction (semi-RT-PCR), 4 μg of total RNA was used for complementary DNA (cDNA) synthesis using the SuperScript Ⅲ First-Strand Synthesis system (Thermo Scientific) according to the manufacturer’s protocols. Semi-quantitative expression analysis of target genes was performed on ProFlex PCR Systems (Thermo Scientific). Rplp0 was used as an endogenous reference gene for data normalization. Semi-RT–PCR was performed in 20 μl reaction volumes and primer sequences are described in Supplementary Table 1.

PCR amplifications for Serpina1a and Serpina1e genes were performed for 38 cycles, consisting of denaturation and at 95 °C for 15 s, annealing at 56.5 °C for 15 s and extension at 72 °C for 30 s, followed by an initial denaturation step at 95 °C for 1 min. For the Rplp0, Fndc5, and Ctsb genes, 28 cycles were performed using the same PCR protocol.

Immunohistochemistry

The mice were anesthetized as previously described and transcranially perfused with 4 % paraformaldehyde (PFA, Biosesang) following perfusion with 1x PBS. The muscle tissue was stored in 4% PFA for post-fixation overnight and then switched to 30% sucrose solution for an additional 48 h in preparation for sectioning.

TurboID-ER expression in the skeletal muscle

To validate whether TurboID-ER is expressed in ER in skeletal muscle of ACTuR mice, limb muscle of ACTuR mice was collected for immunostaining. Serial horizontal sections of muscle tissues (thickness, 20 µm) were collected using a cryostat (Leica, CM3050S) and immediately mounted on the slide glass (Marienfeld). Attached muscle sections were washed with pre-warmed PBS and blocked with blocking solution (10% normal goat serum, 1% mouse on mouse blocking reagent, 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated in primary antibody solution. The primary antibody used for immunostaining was a mouse anti-Calregulin antibody (1:20, Santa-Cruz) and a rabbit anti-V5 (1:400, Cell signaling), and the fluorescent secondary antibodies were diluted with blocking solution at a concentration of 1:250 and incubated with the sections for 2 h at room temperature. The fluorescent secondary antibody used was a goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated antibody (Thermo Scientific) and a goat anti-mouse IgG Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated antibody (Thermo Scientific). Nuclei were fluorescently labeled with DAPI containing mounting solution (Vectashield).

BrdU labeling

To examine the effects of Serpina1e on hippocampal neurogenesis, 5-bromo-2′-deoxyuridine (BrdU, Sigma‒Aldrich) was injected intraperitoneally at 50 mg/kg into sedentary mice. The BrdU stock was resuspended in PBS at 10 mg/ml and stored at −20 °C. The control mice were injected with an equivalent volume of PBS.

Serial coronal sections of the hippocampus (thickness, 40 µm) were collected using a cryostat (Leica, CM3050S) and washed with PBS. All brain sections were blocked with blocking solution (10% normal goat serum, 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated in primary antibody solution. All brain sections stained for BrdU were pretreated with 2 M HCl for 30 min at 37 °C before blocking. The primary antibody used for immunostaining was a rat anti-BrdU antibody (1:500, Abcam), and the fluorescent secondary antibodies were diluted with blocking solution at a concentration of 1:200 and incubated with the sections for 2 h at room temperature. For double staining with NeuN, rabbit anti-NeuN antibody (1:1000, Abcam) was treated with a rat anti-BrdU antibody. The fluorescent secondary antibody used was a goat anti-rat IgG Alexa Fluor 568-conjugated antibody (Thermo Scientific). For double staining with NeuN, a goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated antibody (Thermo Scientific) was used. Nuclei were fluorescently labeled with DAPI containing mounting solution (Vectashield). To count the number of BrdU-positive cells, six series of DG slices (at intervals of 120 µm) from the dorsal hippocampus of each hemisphere (12 sections per mouse) were selected. The BrdU(+)/NeuN(+) and BrdU(+)/NeuN(−) cell numbers from the six DG slices in each hemisphere were averaged to produce the mean cell numbers, which are shown as individual dots in the bar graph.

In vivo neurite arborization analysis

To test whether Serpina1e can induce neuronal growth in vivo as shown in cultured cells (Fig. 6), neurite marker Microtubule-associated protein 2 (MAP2)-positive dendrites were measured. Serial coronal sections of the hippocampus (thickness, 20 µm) were collected using a cryostat (Leica, CM3050S) and washed with PBS. All brain sections were blocked with blocking solution (10% normal goat serum, 0.2% Triton X-100 in PBS) for 1 h at room temperature and then incubated in primary antibody solution. The primary antibody used for immunostaining was a rabbit anti-MAP2 antibody (1:1000, Sigma-Aldrich), and the fluorescent secondary antibodies were diluted with blocking solution at a concentration of 1:200 and incubated with the sections for 2 h at room temperature. The fluorescent secondary antibody used was a goat anti-rabbit IgG Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated antibody (Thermo Scientific) was used. Nuclei were fluorescently labeled with DAPI-containing mounting solution (Vectashield).

Intravenous administration of Serpina1a and Serpina1e

Recombinant Serpina1a and Serpina1e with V5 and His tags were synthesized (GenScript). To conduct behavioral tests, neurogenesis analysis and BDNF analysis, we retro-orbitally injected Serpina1a or Serpina1e into sedentary C57BL/6 mice at 150 µg/100 µl for 4 weeks every three days, for a total of 9 times. For the Serpina1 transmission experiments, sedentary mice were injected retro-orbitally with Serpina1e at 300 µg/100 µl and anesthetized to collect CSF and hippocampal tissues.

Intramuscular injection of AAV9 and lentiviruses

To investigate whether muscular Serpina1e elicits cognitive enhancement, we delivered a single dose of AAV9-CMV-Serpina1e-T2A-EGFP (VectorBuilder; 1.06 × 10e11 GCs/mouse) into each of the four limbs of C57BL/6 mice. For the control mice, the same dose of AAV9-CMV-EGFP (VectorBuilder; 1.07 × 10e11 GC/mouse) was used instead. In addition, to evaluate whether muscular Serpina1e promotes neuronal outgrowth, the same strategies using AAVs were used to Thy1-EGFP mice. Behavioral assessments and morphological were performed four weeks after viral infection.

To examine whether the role of muscle-derived Serpina1e on exercise-induced beneficial effects, we intramuscularly single injected LV-hCas9:T2A:Puro-U6>Serpina1egRNA1-U6>Serpina1egRNA2 (VectorBuiler; 5 x 10e6 TU/mouse) to reduce the Serpina1e expression in skeletal muscle into four limbs. For the control mice, the same dose of LV-EGFP:T2A:Puro-EF1a>mCherry (VectorBuiler; 5 x 10e6 TU/mouse) was used. Behavioral assessments started two weeks after viral infection.

Infusion of Serpina1e into the hippocampus

To address the direct effects of Serpina1e treatment on the hippocampus, we infused Serpina1e into the molecular layer of the hippocampus (AP −1.95, ML ± 1.20, and DV −1.70; Supplementary Fig. 19e) with the same timeline as systemic injections. A custom dual-guide cannula was designed with a 26-gauge, 7.8 mm pedestal height, 2.4 mm distance and 2 mm distance below the pedestal (RWD). The dual-guide cannula was implanted and covered with a dummy cannula with a 1 mm projection to fit the dual-guide cannula. A total of 100 ng/250 nl of Serpina1e per hemisphere was infused through the implanted cannula for 4 weeks every 3 days at 100 nl/min. The actual infused site in the hippocampus was determined by injecting DiI dye through the implanted cannula when all behavioral tests had concluded.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays of BDNF

To measure BDNF levels in the hippocampus, C57BL/6 mice were intravenously injected with Serpina1e for 4 weeks every 3 days and then sacrificed 1 hour after the last injection. Hippocampal tissues were harvested following perfusion with PBS and lysed with lysis buffer (1% Triton X-100, 0.1% Tween 20, 100x P.I.C., and 1x PBS). The BDNF concentration was determined via enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays (Novusbio) according to the manufacturer’s instructions.

Cell culture

Cell line culture

AML12 cell (ATCC) which is a hepatocyte cell line derived from a liver of mouse was cultured in normal medium (10% FBS (Thermo Scientific), 5000 U/ml penicillin and 5000 µg/ml streptomycin in DMEM (Gibco)) for Serpina1e knockdown efficiency tests in vitro. The cell line was authenticated by its supplier (ATCC) and not tested for mycoplasma contamination. Primary cortical neuron culture Cortical neurons were prepared from the brains of C57BL/6 embryos at embryonic days 16–17. Cortices were dissected in dissection medium (10 mM HEPES in HBSS, Gibco), incubated in 0.25% trypsin-EDTA (Gibco) in a 37 °C water bath for 20 min, and then triturated with 2 ml of plating medium (2 mM L-glutamine (Gibco), 0.45% glucose (Sigma‒Aldrich), 10% FBS (Thermo Scientific), 5000 U/ml penicillin and 5000 µg/ml streptomycin in MEM (Gibco)). Dissociated cells were filtered through a cell strainer and plated on poly-L-lysine-coated coverslips in 12-well plates (3 × 105 cells/well) in plating media. After one hour, the plates were replaced with maintenance medium (2% B27 supplement, Thermo Scientific), 2 mM L-glutamine, 5000 U/ml penicillin and 5000 µg/ml streptomycin in neurobasal medium (Gibco).

Acquisition and analysis of fluorescence images

Immunofluorescence images were obtained, quantified, and analyzed by an observer who was blinded to the treatment. To validate TurboID-ER expression in ER in skeletal muscle, fluorescence images were acquired by using a STELLARIS 8 microscope (Leica, 100x/1.40 NA) with the pinhole set to 1 Airy unit. For neurogenesis analysis via BrdU staining, fluorescence images were acquired by using a STELLARIS 8 microscope (Leica, 40x/1.10 NA) with the pinhole set to 1 Airy unit. Images were taken at the suggested optimal value interval based on magnification and analyzed using ImageJ (National Institutes of Health). Six series of DG slices (290.77 µm x 290.77 µm at intervals of 120 µm) from the dorsal hippocampus of each hemisphere (12 sections per mouse) were selected and the number of BrdU-labeled cells was manually counted. For neurite analysis using MAP2 staining, Z-stack images of MAP2-positive dendrites (775.19 µm x 775.19 µm x 7 µm) were acquired by using a STELLARIS 8 microscope (Leica, 20x/0.75 NA) with the pinhole set to 1 Airy unit and zoom factor 0.75x. 2D-projected with maximum intensity was measured to compare the complexity of dendrites, represented by MAP2 intensity, between control and muscle Serpina1e-overexpressed mice. For the neurite morphological assessment of individual neurons, sparsely labeled GFP-positive neurons in the hippocampus were imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (20x/0.75 NA). Z-stacks were acquired with a 2 um z-step for a total of 40 steps, covering a total thickness of 80 um. The acquired z-stacks were processed into maximum intensity projections (MIP). Individual neurons were manually isolated from the MIP images via Region of Interest (ROI) extraction to ensure singular cell analysis. The isolated images were further processed for skeletonization using ImageJ/FIJI. Morphological parameters, including total neurite length and dendritic complexity via Sholl analysis (calculating the number of neurite crossings at intervals of 10 um from the soma), were quantified to evaluate the structural properties of the neurons. To analyze neurite morphology of primary neurons, cortical neurons at DIV7 were used. The neurons were imaged using a Leica SP8 confocal microscope (20x/0.75 NA). The images were processed for skeletonization via ImageJ/FIJI, and the total neurite length, branch number, and branch length were analyzed. The skeletonized images were further analyzed via the Sholl analysis plugin in ImageJ/FIJI, which calculates the number of neurite crossings at intervals of 10 µm from the cell body.

Statistics and Reproducibility

All the statistical analyses were performed via GraphPad Prism 10 with 95% confidence, and all the values are expressed as the mean ± SEM. One-way ANOVA with Dunnett’s post hoc tests was used to compare three or four groups, and unpaired two-tailed Student’s t tests were used to compare two groups. Because the data in Fig. 4k did not pass the normality test, we used a non-parametric Kruskal-Wallis test. The data in Fig. 6n and Supplementary Fig. 20f were analyzed via two-way ANOVA with Sidac’s and Dunnett’s post hoc test, respectively. The statistical tests for each experiment are provided in the legends of the respective figures. P values less than 0.05 were considered statistically significant. All experiments were repeated at least 3 times independently.
