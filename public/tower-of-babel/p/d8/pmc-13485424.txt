Clinical Characteristics, Management, and Visual Outcomes of Spontaneous Corneal Perforation: A Single-Center Retrospective Study in South Korea
Authors: Hwi Yeong Yoon, Joon Young Hyon, Hyun Sun Jeon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo describe the clinical characteristics, etiologies, management patterns, and visual outcomes of spontaneous corneal perforation.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed 18 eyes of 17 patients with spontaneous corneal perforation treated at a tertiary center in South Korea between 2014 and 2022 with follow-up through December 2024. Cases were classified as noninfectious, infectious, or mixed. Clinical features, interventions, and visual outcomes were analyzed. Univariate analyses were performed to examine associations between clinical and demographic variables and final visual acuity.ResultsThe mean patient age was 58.0 ± 25.1 years. Noninfectious etiology accounted for 50.0% of cases; patients in this group were significantly younger than those with infectious or mixed etiologies (41.0 ± 26.8 years vs. 73.1 ± 9.4 years, p = 0.036). Blepharokeratoconjunctivitis (BKC) was the most common cause in younger patients, while neurotrophic keratopathy with superimposed infection predominated in older patients. Primary surgical treatments included amniotic membrane transplantation (50.0%), corneal patch graft (38.9%), and penetrating keratoplasty (16.7%), with 38.9% requiring more than one procedure. Final best-corrected visual acuity was significantly better in the noninfectious group than in the combined infectious/mixed group (0.86 ± 1.14 logMAR vs. 2.19 ± 1.02 logMAR, p = 0.038). Final visual acuity was significantly correlated with age (Spearman rho = 0.738, p = 0.003); patients with systemic comorbidities also showed significantly worse outcomes (p = 0.008), a finding that may partly reflect confounding by age.ConclusionsSpontaneous corneal perforation shows distinct clinical patterns by etiology and age. Younger patients with noninfectious disease achieved more favorable visual outcomes, while older patients with infectious or mixed etiologies more often required repeated interventions focused on globe preservation. Early recognition and sustained management of BKC in younger patients and close monitoring of corneal integrity in older patients with neurotrophic keratopathy may reduce the risk of progression to perforation.Keywords: Amniotic membrane transplantation, Blepharokeratoconjunctivitis, Infectious keratitis, Neurotrophic keratopathy, Spontaneous corneal perforation

Corneal perforation is a vision-threatening condition that demands prompt surgical intervention to maintain globe integrity [1,2]. Spontaneous corneal perforation, defined as full-thickness corneal breakdown in the absence of direct mechanical trauma, typically represents the terminal stage of an underlying ocular disease rather than an isolated event [1–3].

The range of responsible conditions is broad, spanning microbial keratitis, peripheral ulcerative keratitis (PUK), neurotrophic keratopathy (NK), and chronic inflammatory surface diseases such as blepharokeratoconjunctivitis (BKC), among others [4,5]. Each condition follows a distinct natural history and treatment response. Surgical options for tectonic closure—including tissue adhesives, amniotic membrane transplantation (AMT), and keratoplasty—have been described in varying clinical contexts [1,6,7], but the choice of intervention is rarely straightforward and typically depends on the specific etiology, the extent of tissue loss, and the condition of the remaining ocular surface [2,3].

Previous reports on nontraumatic corneal perforation have been largely limited to single-center case series that addressed specific etiologies or treatment techniques without systematic comparison across etiological groups [3,8,9]. A recent population-based study using US Medicare claims data provided the first large-scale epidemiological overview but was constrained by the absence of clinical detail, with etiology unknown in 74% of cases and no data on visual outcomes or surgical decision-making [10]. Data from East Asian populations remain particularly scarce. In this study, we describe the clinical characteristics, etiologies, management strategies, and visual outcomes of spontaneous corneal perforation observed at a single tertiary referral center in South Korea and examine clinical factors associated with visual outcomes.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (No. B-2606-1052-102) and was performed in accordance with the tenets of the Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments. The requirement for informed consent was exempted for the current study with the approval of the Institutional Review Board because of the investigation’s minimal risk to participants.

We reviewed the electronic medical records of patients treated for nontraumatic spontaneous corneal perforation at our institution between January 2014 and December 2022. Follow-up records were reviewed through December 2024. Spontaneous corneal perforation was defined as a full-thickness corneal defect occurring in the absence of mechanical trauma, chemical or thermal injury, or ocular surgery within 3 months before perforation. Patients were included if they had a clinically confirmed diagnosis of spontaneous corneal perforation and underwent definitive surgical management at our center, with at least one postoperative outpatient visit to confirm anatomical closure, defined as absence of full-thickness defect on slit-lamp examination. Definitive surgical procedures included tectonic or reconstructive procedures performed in an operating room under sterile conditions, encompassing penetrating keratoplasty (PKP), lamellar corneal patch graft, conjunctival procedures, and sutured AMT. Cases managed exclusively with nonsurgical means—such as therapeutic contact lenses, sutureless amniotic membrane lenses, or outpatient glue application—were excluded, as were cases with incomplete medical records.

Cases were categorized into three etiological groups: noninfectious (sterile corneal melting due to autoimmune or chronic inflammatory surface disease), infectious (definitive clinical or microbiological evidence of active microbial keratitis at the time of perforation), and mixed (preexisting noninfectious conditions complicated by superimposed microbial infection). The classification was based on the combination of clinical presentation, slit-lamp examination findings, and available microbiological data (corneal culture or smear results) at the time of perforation. Cases in which slit-lamp examination findings were consistent with sterile melting without microbiological evidence of infection were classified as noninfectious; cases with positive cultures or unequivocal clinical features of microbial keratitis (e.g., satellite lesions, feathery margins) were classified as infectious; and cases with documented preexisting noninfectious surface disease (e.g., NK, herpetic keratitis, autoimmune keratitis) in which secondary microbial infection was confirmed or strongly suspected were classified as mixed.

Collected data included patient demographics, underlying systemic and ocular diseases, clinical course, preoperative and postoperative medical treatments (including topical and systemic antibiotics, antifungals, anti-inflammatory agents, immunosuppressants, and adjunctive treatments such as therapeutic contact lenses and autologous serum), surgical procedures performed, and best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA) at the final follow-up visit.

Snellen BCVA was converted to logMAR for analysis. Non-Snellen visual acuity measures, including counting fingers (CF), hand motion (HM), light perception (LP), and no light perception (NLP), were converted to logMAR values using commonly adopted approximations in the literature (CF = 2.0, HM = 2.3, LP = 2.7, NLP = 3.0) [11]. Eyes for which BCVA was not recorded at the final visit were excluded from the visual outcome analysis. One patient contributed both eyes to this cohort (cases 5a and 5b); only the first eye to present with perforation (case 5b) was included in statistical analyses to maintain the independence of observations. Continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard deviation with range. Between-group differences in continuous variables among three etiological groups were assessed using the Kruskal-Wallis test; pairwise comparisons between the noninfectious group and the combined infectious/mixed group were performed using the Mann-Whitney U-test. Associations between final BCVA (logMAR) and age, sex, etiology, systemic comorbidities, prior ocular history, and subsequent surgery were examined in univariate analyses, using Spearman rank correlation for age and the Mann-Whitney U-test for the remaining categorical variables. An exploratory multiple linear regression incorporating all six variables simultaneously was also performed as a supplementary analysis, with results presented in Supplementary Table 1. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS ver. 29.0 (IBM Corp.); p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 18 eyes from 17 patients were included. Among the 17 patients, the mean age was 58.0 ± 25.1 years (range, 13–84 years), with a female patient predominance (10 patients, 58.8%). The mean follow-up duration after the primary surgical procedure was 24.2 ± 14.0 months (range, 0.7–54.4 months). The age distribution was not uniform across the cohort; younger patients ranged in age from 13 to 45 years, while older patients ranged from 56 to 84 years, with no patients falling in the intervening decade (46–55 years). Systemic comorbidities were present in 13 patients (76.5%), most commonly hypertension (41.2%), followed by asthma, diabetes mellitus, and coronary artery disease (17.6% each).

By etiological group, noninfectious causes accounted for nine eyes (50.0%), mixed for six eyes (33.3%), and infectious for three eyes (16.7%). Representative clinical photographs illustrating the spectrum of spontaneous corneal perforation across noninfectious, infectious, and mixed etiologies are shown in Fig. 1A–1R. The mean age was 41.0 ± 26.8 years in the noninfectious group, 70.7 ± 10.8 years in the mixed group, and 78.0 ± 2.6 years in the infectious group. Although the difference among the three etiological groups did not reach statistical significance (Kruskal-Wallis test, p = 0.111), the noninfectious group was significantly younger than the combined infectious/mixed group (41.0 ± 26.8 years vs. 73.1 ± 9.4 years; Mann-Whitney U-test, p = 0.036). Prior ocular disease unrelated to the primary perforating condition was documented in 12 eyes (66.7%). Detailed clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1.

In the noninfectious group, perforation most commonly resulted from progressive chronic inflammatory disorders—BKC in five eyes and PUK in two eyes (cases 5a and 5b, a bilateral case in one patient). Five of these eyes (cases 1, 2, 4, 5a, and 5b; representing four patients) had no prior ophthalmic history before the index inflammatory disease, and perforation occurred as a late complication of uncontrolled surface inflammation. The remaining two noninfectious cases (cases 14 and 16) were related to surgery-associated corneal thinning, including one case following pterygium removal and one associated with corneal decompensation secondary to intraocular lens dislocation. In the infectious and mixed groups, patients were predominantly older and had more complex ocular histories, including prior cataract surgery, ocular trauma, band keratopathy, or prior herpetic disease. Mixed-etiology cases typically involved NK with superimposed bacterial or fungal infection, reflecting the susceptibility of anesthetic corneas to secondary colonization. Notably, NK was the underlying condition in five of the six mixed-etiology cases, making it the single most common predisposing factor for perforation in older patients. Fungal keratitis was diagnosed in five eyes, the three infectious cases (cases 11–13) and two mixed-etiology cases with superimposed fungal infection (cases 8 and 9), consistent with the deep stromal penetration and destructive behavior characteristic of filamentous fungi; the causative organism was confirmed on culture in two of these eyes (Alternaria species in case 9, an unidentified mold in case 11).

Preoperative management focused on infection control and ocular surface stabilization before surgical repair. Broad-spectrum topical antibiotics were used perioperatively in all cases. Topical antifungal agents were used in all three infectious cases and in two mixed-etiology cases with superimposed fungal infection. Oral antifungal therapy was additionally administered in one infectious case and one mixed-etiology case. In the noninfectious group, anti-inflammatory treatment was the mainstay of the preoperative approach. Topical steroids, cyclosporine (0.05%–0.1%) and tacrolimus (0.1%) were used to control corneal melting; oral doxycycline was added to target lid margin inflammation in some BKC-related cases; and systemic steroids and methotrexate were administered in autoimmune-mediated cases, including PUK and rheumatoid arthritis (RA)-associated keratouveitis cases. Therapeutic contact lenses were used in a wide range of cases across all etiological groups, including BKC, PUK, mixed, and iatrogenic cases, serving primarily as epithelial protection rather than definitive treatment. Autologous serum was used in the keratouveitis case (case 7). Two eyes (cases 12 and 17) with suspected secondary endophthalmitis received intraocular antibiotic injections at the time of surgery (intravitreal in case 12 and intracameral in case 17). Postoperatively, topical steroids were used in 17 of 18 eyes (94.4%), with the exception of case with uncontrolled active infection.

AMT was the most frequently performed primary surgical procedure (9 eyes, 50.0%), followed by corneal patch graft (7 eyes, 38.9%) and PKP (3 eyes, 16.7%). When a combined procedure was performed in a single surgery session, each component was counted separately; therefore, the frequencies are not mutually exclusive. In the noninfectious group, corneal patch grafts and AMT were the main modalities; the two PUK cases required corneoscleral patch graft given the extent of peripheral tissue loss. Cyanoacrylate glue was used adjunctively in three cases: as a temporary tectonic measure prior to definitive repair in two mixed-etiology cases (cases 10 and 15), and alongside a lamellar corneal patch graft in one noninfectious case (case 6). Conjunctival flap surgery was performed in three cases (cases 9, 13, and 17) as a salvage or adjunctive procedure. In cases 13 and 17, it was performed as a primary salvage option when tissue destruction precluded tectonic keratoplasty, while in case 9, it was employed as a late adjunctive measure following repeated tectonic failures.

Subsequent surgery was required in 7 of 18 eyes (38.9%) due to recurrent melting, primary graft failure, or inadequate tectonic stability. The proportion was higher in the infectious and mixed groups (44.4%, 4 of 9 eyes) than in the noninfectious group (33.3%, 3 of 9 eyes). Two eyes required particularly extensive surgical courses, all of which belonged to the mixed-etiology group. Case 7, with RA-associated keratouveitis and superimposed bacterial infection, underwent four surgical sessions including conjunctival resection, AMT, lamellar corneal patch graft, and PKP. Case 9, with NK and superimposed fungal infection, underwent five surgical sessions comprising corneal debridement, corneal patch graft, AMT, PKP, and conjunctival flap surgery.

Final BCVA (logMAR) was recorded at the last visit in 15 of 18 eyes (case 13 was lost to follow-up before formal visual acuity assessment; cases 3 and 11 had documented visits but no BCVA recorded). For statistical comparisons, case 5a was additionally excluded to preserve independence of observations (case 5b, the first-presenting eye, was retained), yielding 14 eyes. The mean final BCVA was 1.52 ± 1.24 logMAR overall and was 0.86 ± 1.14 in the noninfectious group (n = 7), 2.05 ± 1.04 in the mixed group (n = 6), and 3.00 in the infectious group (n = 1). Among younger patients, four eyes (cases 1, 2, 4, and 6) achieved a final logMAR BCVA of 0.3 or better, whereas both eyes of the bilateral PUK patient (cases 5a and 5b; descriptive, not included in statistical comparisons) remained at HM due to the severity of peripheral tissue destruction. In patients aged 60 years or older, outcomes were poor in all but one case (case 14), with 7 of 8 eyes reaching a logMAR BCVA of 1.0 or worse.

On univariate analysis, final logMAR BCVA was significantly correlated with age (Spearman rho = 0.738, p = 0.003) and was significantly worse among patients with systemic comorbidities (Mann-Whitney U-test, p = 0.008). Final BCVA also differed significantly between the noninfectious and combined infectious/mixed groups (0.86 ± 1.14 logMAR vs. 2.19 ± 1.02 logMAR; Mann-Whitney U-test, p = 0.038), although the three-group comparison did not reach significance (Kruskal-Wallis test, p = 0.066). Sex (p > 0.999), prior ocular history (p = 0.112), and subsequent surgery (p = 0.282) were not significantly associated with final BCVA. However, none of the variables reached statistical significance in the exploratory multivariable model, likely reflecting overfitting and multicollinearity rather than a true absence of association (Supplementary Table 1).

Discussion

This retrospective study describes the clinical characteristics, management, and visual outcomes of spontaneous corneal perforation across a range of etiologies at a single tertiary referral center in South Korea over a 9-year period. Unlike previous claims-based analyses that lacked clinical detail [10] or single-center series that focused on individual etiologies or surgical techniques [3,8], the present study examined the full clinical spectrum of spontaneous corneal perforation with systematic comparison across etiological subgroups, capturing detailed information on underlying disease, surgical decision-making, and visual outcomes.

The age distribution of our cohort showed two distinct age clusters—younger patients presenting with noninfectious inflammatory disease and elderly patients with infectious or mixed etiologies—rather than a continuous distribution across age groups. In younger patients, chronic ocular surface inflammation, particularly BKC, can lead to progressive stromal thinning and eventual perforation if not adequately controlled. In older patients, age-related changes in corneal structure and tear film instability, often compounded by prior ocular disease or surgery, create conditions that favor both neurotrophic breakdown and secondary infection [12,13]. However, given the small sample size, this observation should be interpreted descriptively and cannot be taken as evidence of a true bimodal distribution in the broader population.

Age was significantly correlated with final visual acuity on univariate analysis (Spearman rho = 0.738, p = 0.003), consistent with prior reports [9]. This finding is further supported by a recent population-based analysis of the US Medicare population, in which older patients with nontraumatic corneal perforation were significantly less likely to undergo keratoplasty and more likely to undergo enucleation or evisceration [10], suggesting that advancing age shifts the clinical goal from visual rehabilitation toward globe preservation across different health-care settings. Older age is associated with reduced corneal healing capacity, including epithelial regeneration and tear film stability, which likely limits visual recovery regardless of the specific cause of perforation [12,13]. The association between systemic comorbidities and worse visual outcomes likely reflects, at least in part, confounding by age given the strong collinearity between the two variables in this cohort; it is nonetheless reported here because comorbidity burden is readily assessed in clinical practice and may be useful when counseling patients about expected outcomes. Etiology, meanwhile, distinguished visual outcome only when compared as two groups rather than three, suggesting that the mixed and infectious groups behave similarly with respect to visual prognosis.

The noninfectious group consisted predominantly of younger patients with chronic inflammatory ocular surface disease, most commonly BKC or PUK. In BKC, chronic lid margin inflammation leads to persistent epithelial breakdown and stromal thinning, which can progress insidiously to full-thickness breakdown [14,15]. In our cohort, several noninfectious cases had no prior ocular history before developing their inflammatory condition, underscoring that perforation can occur as a primary complication of under-recognized surface disease. This observation highlights the importance of early recognition and sustained treatment of BKC, particularly in pediatric and young adult patients, in whom chronic lid margin disease may be overlooked as a cause of progressive corneal thinning that may ultimately lead to perforation. Visual outcomes in the noninfectious group were significantly better than in the infectious/mixed group (Mann-Whitney U-test, p = 0.038), likely because corneal melting in BKC tends to be more localized and because younger patients have greater tissue recovery potential. However, the two PUK cases (cases 5a and 5b) were notable exceptions, as bilateral severe peripheral stromal melting resulted in persistently poor visual outcomes despite intensive systemic immunosuppression, illustrating that noninfectious perforation can also follow a severe clinical course.

Infectious and mixed-etiology cases presented in older patients with a markedly different clinical course. The predominance of fungal keratitis in the infectious group likely reflects its aggressive clinical course, with deep stromal invasion and persistent tissue destruction often leading to treatment failure [16]. The mixed-etiology cases predominantly involved NK with superimposed bacterial or fungal infection. In NK, loss of corneal sensation not only eliminates the patient’s early warning of deterioration but also impairs the epithelial healing response, leaving the corneal surface susceptible to secondary microbial invasion even without overt breakdown [17–19]. As this combination of impaired innervation and defective healing creates a hostile environment for surgical repair, tectonic stabilization rather than visual rehabilitation was the realistic endpoint for most of these patients. Though the visual prognosis is limited, prompt recognition and aggressive treatment of secondary infection in these patients nonetheless remain critical. Recombinant human nerve growth factor has shown efficacy in promoting corneal epithelial healing in NK [20], but was not available at our institution during the study period. Whether early neurotrophic intervention could reduce the risk of secondary infection and subsequent perforation in this population is an important question for future research.

BKC in younger patients and NK in older patients appear to represent the two principal pathways to spontaneous corneal perforation in this cohort. Both conditions are characterized by chronic, insidious progression that may be underappreciated until advanced tissue loss has occurred. This underscores the need for heightened clinical vigilance in patients with these diagnoses, including regular assessment of corneal integrity and timely escalation of treatment before perforation becomes imminent.

Surgical strategy was guided by the extent of tissue loss and the primary underlying condition rather than by a fixed protocol. AMT served as the primary procedure in the majority of cases due to its anti-inflammatory properties and the ease with which it can be applied to irregular perforations [21,22]. Corneal patch grafts were preferred for larger or structurally significant defects, and PKP was reserved for cases requiring optical rehabilitation or when earlier repairs had failed [2,23]. Cyanoacrylate glue was used as a temporary measure to achieve short-term tectonic stability before more definitive repair and conjunctival flap surgery was performed as a salvage option when the degree of tissue loss precluded keratoplasty [24–26]. The rate of subsequent surgery (38.9%) in our series is consistent with figures reported in other retrospective series of nontraumatic corneal perforation [3,8], reflecting the clinical reality that a single intervention is often insufficient in severe or recurrent cases. The distinction between management goals, visual recovery in younger patients with noninfectious disease versus globe preservation in older patients with infectious or mixed causes, was a recurring theme in our series. This pattern is consistent with population-level data showing that older patients with corneal perforation are more likely to undergo enucleation or evisceration rather than keratoplasty [10] and may be relevant when counseling patients and their families regarding expected outcomes after tectonic repair.

This study has several limitations. The retrospective design, small sample size, and single-center setting restrict the generalizability of the findings, and the clinical heterogeneity of the cohort limits direct comparisons between groups. Our inclusion criteria were also restricted to cases that underwent definitive surgical management. This allowed detailed characterization of a more severe subset of spontaneous corneal perforation, but excluded cases that resolved with conservative treatment alone, which likely biased our cohort toward more severe presentations and limits generalizability to the full clinical spectrum of the disease. Perforation size was not recorded in a standardized format and could not be retrospectively estimated; the primary surgical procedure may serve as an indirect surrogate for the extent of tissue loss. Three eyes were excluded from visual outcome analysis due to the absence of recorded BCVA, which may introduce selection bias. The conversion of non-Snellen acuity measures (HM, LP, and NLP) to logMAR values, while following published conventions, introduces imprecision that should be considered when interpreting the visual outcome data. Age, etiology, and systemic comorbidities were closely interrelated in this cohort, and univariate analysis alone cannot fully separate their individual contributions to visual outcome.

In conclusion, spontaneous corneal perforation encompasses a clinically heterogeneous group of conditions that differ substantially by patient age and underlying etiology. In our series, younger patients with noninfectious inflammatory disease, primarily BKC, achieved more favorable visual outcomes following tectonic repair, while older patients with infectious or mixed causes, particularly fungal keratitis and NK with superimposed infection, more often required repeated interventions and showed limited visual recovery. Age was the most consistent factor associated with worse final visual acuity, likely reflecting reduced corneal healing capacity rather than any specific comorbidity. In practice, management goals differed substantially by etiology—visual recovery was often achievable in younger noninfectious cases, while globe preservation was the primary concern in older patients with infectious or mixed disease. In particular, sustained management of BKC in younger patients and close monitoring of corneal integrity in older patients with NK may reduce risk of progression to perforation. Further study with larger cohorts is needed to confirm these observations and to establish whether early intervention in these at-risk groups can reduce the incidence of this sight-threatening complication.
