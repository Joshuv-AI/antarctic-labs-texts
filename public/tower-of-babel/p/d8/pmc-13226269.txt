Causal direct drivers of greenhouse gas emissions in 40,722 wastewater treatment plants in China
Authors: Boxuan Wang, Aliya Abulimiti, Jiale Liang, Jinhao Kang, Xiuheng Wang, Nanqi Ren
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractWastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) are essential for public health but are highly energy-intensive, accounting for approximately 1.6% of global greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions. Developing cost-effective carbon mitigation strategies requires identifying the dominant drivers of these emissions. However, existing analyses often treat operational variables as independent predictors or lack the causal validity required to disentangle complex, interacting emission sources across diverse regions. Consequently, the fundamental mechanisms causing distinct GHG emissions in the resource-constrained wastewater sector remain poorly understood. Here we use an integrated machine learning and causal inference framework to quantify the drivers of GHG emissions across 40,722 Chinese WWTPs over a decade. We reveal that aggregate emission intensities have rebounded recently, driven primarily by increasingly stringent water discharge standards that necessitate chemical- and energy-intensive nitrogen removal. Although ammonia nitrogen removal exerts the strongest individual causal effect on emissions—where a 10% increase drives a 5.65% surge in total intensity—decarbonizing the regional power grid offers a highly potent and practical mitigation lever. Specifically, a 10% reduction in the regional grid electricity emission factor accounts for a 4.45% equivalent reduction in total emissions, demonstrating a profound cross-sectoral coupling. Our findings highlight a critical tension between local water quality improvements and global climate goals, suggesting that future environmental regulations must harmonize ecological benefits with their intrinsic carbon costs.Keywords: Wastewater treatment plants, Greenhouse gas emissions, Causal inference, Direct driver, Interpretable machine learning

Introduction

Wastewater treatment plants (WWTPs) are part of the critical infrastructure needed for public health, but are energy-intensive, accounting for approximately 3% of global electricity consumption and approximately 1.6% of greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions [1]. Given limited resources for the construction and operation of wastewater infrastructure [2], there is a critical need to identify the key factors driving GHG emissions in these systems. This information is essential for developing cost-effective carbon mitigation strategies.

China is a compelling location to study this challenge. With the world's largest wastewater treatment capacity, estimated at approximately 57.1 billion m3 in 2020, China's GHG emissions have more than doubled from 13.34 Mt CO2 equivalent (CO2-eq.) in 2005 to 30.95 Mt CO2-eq. in 2020 [3]. Additionally, the GHG emission intensity (GEI) from Chinese WWTPs exhibits spatial heterogeneity due to varying regional climates and economic development [4]. This has resulted in a large dataset from Chinese WWTPs that provides robust information for building models capable of identifying causes of GHG emissions in complex environmental systems.

Identifying dominant emission sources and their causes is necessary for effective policies to reduce GHG emissions. Studies have used various methodologies, including historical data comparison [5], logarithmic mean divisia index [6], and operational data integrated methods [7]; whereas, more recently interpretable machine learning (IML) use has surged for its ability to quantify the contribution of different input data to predict outcomes, demonstrating its advantages in handling the high-dimensional, non-linear, and interactive-nature of wastewater data [8,9].

Despite the growing use of IML to interpret GEI drivers, current studies provide limited causal insight. First, their empirical coverage is often restricted to single plants [10] or specific regions [11], and therefore fails to capture structural differences in emission sources due to regional socio-economic factors or residents' habits [6]. As a result, the mechanisms causing distinct GHG emission sources in the resource-constrained wastewater sector remain poorly understood. Second, many studies have demonstrated that basic pollutant removal indicators (e.g., COD and NH4+-N removal) and water quality parameters serve as robust predictors of energy consumption and sludge yield, underscoring a strong intrinsic coupling between variables [11]. Yet many studies treat these variables as independent predictors. Others include a large number of potential mediator variables to improve predictive accuracy [[11], [12], [13], [14], [15]]. This practice obscures the true causes of GHG emissions by confounding the causal chain and preventing the elucidation of intrinsic mechanisms. Third, most studies use a single model, thereby overlooking possible interactions or distinct algorithmic advantages [16,17]. Further, standard IML outputs show correlation but lack the causal validity required for effective policymaking [18,19]. Structural causal models provide a promising framework for estimating causal effects [9,20] and present a significant opportunity to enhance IML interpretation.

To address these gaps, this study uses an integrated analytical model that fuses mediator exclusion, multi-source feature selection, and causal inference to achieve an optimal prediction and mechanistic quantification of GEI sources. Leveraging a decade of operational data from 40,722 Chinese WWTPs, this model realizes its four core objectives. (1) Using structural equation modeling (SEM) to identify and exclude mediator variables, thereby isolating candidate direct drivers. (2) Combining four feature selection methods to filter noise while utilizing ten distinct machine learning algorithms to construct optimized models for total GEI and four emission sources, which significantly enhances generalization. (3) Applying IML techniques to deconstruct the traditional machine learning "black box" and accurately identify the main causes of GHG emissions. (4) Integrating causal inference with IML to embed causality into interpretable results, thereby quantifying causal dependencies and deepening our theoretical understanding of the complex mechanisms causing GEI.

Materials and methods

The study design comprised six steps: data acquisition and preprocessing, GHG-emission accounting, SEM analysis, feature selection, model evaluation and comparison, and model interpretation (Fig. 1).

Data sources and preprocessing

The dataset for the WWTPs was obtained from the National Urban Sewage Treatment Management Information System and validated by cross-referencing with previously published datasets [4,21]. This dataset covers annual operational records from 2009 to 2019 for 40,722 municipal WWTPs across 31 provinces and municipalities in the Chinese mainland. Key information includes geographical location, discharge standards, electricity consumption, treatment process technologies, and sludge yield. The dataset includes inflow and effluent concentrations for six critical water quality parameters: chemical oxygen demand (COD), biochemical oxygen demand (BOD), suspended solids (SS), ammoniacal nitrogen (NH3-N), total nitrogen (TN), and total phosphorus (TP). We used outflow-quality data to infer missing discharge-standard classifications for specific plants. While Chinese regulations stipulate additional parameters, these six parameters are widely recognized as the most representative indicators of wastewater characteristics [22]. We rigorously cleaned the data by excluding entries with missing water quality values and anomalous records in which effluent concentrations exceeded influent concentrations. Descriptive statistics after preprocessing detail the average influent and effluent concentrations and operational parameters at both the national and regional levels (Supplementary Table S1–S16).

GHG emissions accounting approach

System boundaries

This study used a bottom–up accounting approach to estimate GHG emissions from the WWTP and included the quantification and subsequent aggregation of emissions from each plant. A systems boundary is systematically demarcated into three distinct phases: wastewater treatment (Phase I), sludge treatment and disposal (Phase II), and effluent discharge (Phase III). This approach (Fig. 1) identified and quantified four GHG emission sources, including:

Phase I-1 (Direct GEI): On-site direct emissions originating from biological treatment processes, encompassing fugitive N2O and CH4 generation, as well as fossil-derived CO2 resulting from the mineralization of external carbon sources.

Phase I-2 (Indirect GEI): Off-site indirect emissions resulting from the use of electricity and the upstream production of chemicals (e.g., for phosphorus removal and external carbon sources).

Phase II (STD GEI): Sludge treatment and disposal emissions, encompassing both on-site from thickening/dewatering and off-site from final disposal (e.g., landfills, incineration, or land application).

Phase III (DCT wastewater GEI): Off-site downstream emissions arising from residual pollutants in the discharged effluent (receiving water body emissions).

We omitted GHG emissions from the construction, demolition, and disposal phases of WWTPs [23,24]. Furthermore, we excluded non-electric energy consumption and potential carbon offsets from reclaimed water reuse. This exclusion is justified by the predominant role of electricity in WWTP energy profiles and the relatively low rate of wastewater reuse [6].

Structural equation modeling analysis

SEM is widely used to quantify direct relationships among variables and to examine the effects of mediating factors within indirect pathways [27,28]. With the objective of pollutant removal in WWTPs [29], we designated the following variables as candidate direct drivers: the influent concentrations and concentration reductions for six water quality indices (exemplified by the notation CODinf and COD removal), nutrient ratios (COD/TN, COD/TP, BOD/COD), and wastewater flow.

Before modeling, multicollinearity was addressed through iterative variance inflation factor screening (threshold <10) and data standardization. The SEM framework established direct paths from these candidate direct drivers to five hypothesized mediators using maximum likelihood estimation. These hypothesized mediators comprised key operational parameters: electricity consumption, sludge production, sodium acetate consumption, polyaluminum chloride (PAC) consumption, and the electricity source EF (regional grid cleanliness).

The identification criterion was rigorous: if the structural model demonstrated acceptable goodness-of-fit and significant direct paths existed between the direct drivers and a hypothesized mediator, that variable was confirmed as a mediator. In this study, mediator exclusion focused on direct drivers of the hypothesized mediators’ paths, as the hypothesized mediator-to-outcome relationships have been well established in previous research [30,31]. Some evidence indicates that electricity consumption, chemical consumption, and sludge production significantly influence GHG emissions from WWTPs [30,31]. Therefore, with the hypothesized mediator-to-outcome path already confirmed in the literature [30,31], our analysis prioritized testing which candidate drivers significantly affect these known mediators within our dataset. Consequently, confirmed mediators were excluded from the subsequent machine feature set to prevent them from masking the root causes of emissions. Model fit was evaluated using the comparative fit index (CFI), tucker-lewis index (TLI), and the root mean square error of approximation (RMSEA). We considered model fit acceptable when CFI exceeded 0.90, and RMSEA fell below 0.08 [32]. Analyses were conducted in Python 3.9 using semopy 2.3.1.

Before developing the predictive model, we used four distinct feature selection algorithms to mitigate multicollinearity and capture complex nonlinear relationships between the predictors and the outcome variable. These methods were selected for their proven efficacy in handling high-dimensional environmental data:

Following the exclusion of mediator variables via SEM (Section 2.3), we established an initial feature pool comprising 21 candidate direct drivers. These included treatment processes, wastewater flow, discharge standards, influent and removal concentrations of the six water quality indices, and temporal (year) and spatial (province) factors. We then derived the final variable set through an iterative, performance-driven integration of feature selection algorithms. For LASSO, we retained features with non-zero coefficients after cross-validated regularization. For the methods (RF-MDA, RF-MDG, and SVM-RFE), we used an elbow criterion on the ranked importance bar plots to select initial variables, then expanded the feature set as needed to achieve optimal predictive performance while minimizing noise variables. Detailed criteria for feature selection in each model are provided in Supplementary Text S1. To ensure robustness, we adopted a consensus-based strategy in which only factors identified as significant by at least two of the four independent methods were retained for final predictive models. For models exhibiting superior performance, we evaluated a stricter consensus threshold, retaining only variables identified by at least three methods, to assess the potential for additional feature parsimony. All feature selection analyses were implemented in Python 3.9 with the scikit-learn package.

Model evaluation and comparison

The dataset was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and testing (30%) sets using stratified sampling to ensure consistent distribution across distinct categories. We used a comprehensive suite of ten machine learning algorithms for model development, including logistic regression, ridge regression, elastic net regression, k-nearest neighbors, decision tree (DT), RF, extreme gradient boosting, artificial neural network (ANN), light gradient boosting machine, and categorical boosting (CTB). Hyperparameters for each model were fine-tuned via Bayesian optimization coupled with five-fold cross-validation (optimized parameters are detailed in Supplementary Table S31).

We established distinct optimized predictive models for total GEI and for four sources: direct GEI, indirect GEI (further subdivided into electricity-use GEI and chemical-production GEI), STD GEI, and DCT wastewater GEI.

We then evaluated model performance based on goodness-of-fit and generalization capability and performed detailed residual analyses to ensure the interpretability and stability of the optimal models. A radar chart visualizes the comparative performance across three key metrics: coefficient of determination (R2), root mean square error (RMSE), and mean absolute error (MAE). We further assessed the predictive accuracy, residual characteristics, and error distribution of the optimal models through comprehensive graphical diagnostics: (1) predictive accuracy, observed-versus-predicted plots with a 1:1 reference line; (2) residual characteristics, analyzed via probability density plots, box plots of residual distribution, and residuals-versus-predicted plots (with a zero-reference line) to detect heteroscedasticity; (3) normality, standardized residual normal Q–Q plots; and (4) error magnitude, cumulative distribution function plots of absolute errors, marked with the 90th percentile, to quantify error magnitude and dispersion. All analyses were performed in Python 3.9 using the catboost, lightgbm, sklearn, xgboost, matplotlib, skopt, seaborn, and scipy packages.

Model presentation and explanation

Interpretable machine learning methods

We used Shapley additive explanations (SHAP), an interpretation method grounded in cooperative game theory, to quantify the contribution of explanatory variables in GEI predictions. For each GEI source, SHAP was applied to the optimized predictive models to calculate Shapley values for each feature across all samples, thereby deriving global feature importance. A larger absolute Shapley value indicates a higher contribution of the variable to the model output [33]. All SHAP analyses were implemented in Python 3.9 with the shap package. Causal inference

We used causal inference to elucidate the causal relationships between key direct drivers and the GEI of WWTPs. Causal effects were defined within a structural causal model framework using directed acyclic graphs (DAGs) [20,34]. To ensure the validity of the causal structure, a permutation-based causal graph falsification test [35] was used to assess graph robustness. Only causal graphs that included the top two or three most critical direct drivers based on SHAP importance rankings and passed this validation were used for subsequent inference.

For each analysis, one variable was designated as the treatment, GEI as the outcome, and other variables as potential confounders. We also explored the causal effects of key direct drivers on specific GHG emission sources. In this linear regression framework, a negative causal estimate implies that an increase in the treatment variable is associated with a quantitative reduction in GEI. Specifically, the estimated causal effect value represents the magnitude of change in GEI resulting from a one-unit change in the treatment factor.

We estimated the average treatment effect using linear regression, and then assessed the correctness and robustness of these estimates through three refutation tests: (1) placebo treatment, in which the treatment variable was replaced with a random variable and the estimated effect was expected to approach zero; (2) random common cause, in which a random confounder was added and the estimate was expected to remain stable; and (3) data subsample, in which the effect was re-estimated using a subset of data and expected to remain similar to the original estimate.

Causal inference was implemented in Python 3.9 using the dowhy package.

Results and discussion

Spatiotemporal variations in GHG emission intensity of WWTPs across China

To elucidate the key drivers affecting GEI of WWTPs, it is essential to first characterize their spatiotemporal distribution. The evolution of GEI across five major emission sources and the variations in electricity EFs for WWTPs in China and its seven geographical regions from 2009 to 2019 are illustrated (Fig. 2). By comparing GEI across distinct regions, temporal periods, and emission sources, our findings were mixed on the dominant sources and underlying drivers of emissions in the Chinese wastewater sector.

National-scale analysis (Fig. 2a) indicated that from 2009 to 2019, the aggregate GEI of WWTPs exhibited a decline-then-rebound trajectory, failing to decrease synchronously with the decarbonization of the national energy structure. This phenomenon stems primarily from increasingly stringent discharge standards necessitating higher effluent quality. Specifically, the average TN removal concentration fluctuated around 21.65 mg L−1 between 2009 and 2013, before progressively rising to 24.20 mg L−1 by 2019. However, concurrent reductions in influent COD, attributable to in-sewer microbial degradation of organics or leakage in the collection network (Supplementary Table S1–S8), resulted in low influent C/N ratios. The combination of carbon-deficient influent and strict nitrogen removal requirements necessitated a 61% increase in external carbon source use (to 0.0375 kg sodium acetate m−3 in 2019) and an 18% rise in electricity consumption (to 0.4221 kWh m−3 in 2019) over the decade (Supplementary Table S9). Consequently, the proportional contributions of direct and indirect GEI sources increased. The composition of the average national GEI in 2019 identified electricity use (32.21%) and STD (18.36%) as the dominant emission sources.

Regional-scale analysis (Fig. 2b–h) revealed that between 2009 and 2019, most regions mirrored the national decline-then-rebound trend (e.g., East, Central, and Northwest China), while Northeast China exhibited continuous growth in GHG emissions. This underscores that GHG emissions from Chinese WWTPs remain a critical concern requiring targeted intervention. The 2019 GEI profiles indicate that electricity use and STD were the primary drivers across all regions, though significant inter-regional heterogeneity exists. In Northwest China, over 55% of GEI originated from electricity use (32.94%) and STD (25%) (Fig. 2c). Conversely, GEI in Southwest China showed a balanced contribution profile from direct emissions, electricity use, chemical production, and STD, which accounted for 23.43%, 24.86%, 23.61%, and 24.72%, respectively (Fig. 2g). As evidenced by the electricity EF curves (Fig. 2), the Southwest's grid emission factor plummeted from 0.61 in 2009 to 0.32 in 2019, that was significantly lower than other regions (0.44–0.89) demonstrating the substantial impact of energy decarbonization on sector-specific carbon emissions. Furthermore, the Northwest (Fig. 2c) and North China (Fig. 2d) regions, located in the Yellow River Basin, were characterized by high population density, elevated pollutant concentrations, and a coal-dominated energy mix, resulting in significantly higher GEI than in other regions. Given the disparate regional endowments of hydropower and photovoltaic resources, the divergence in dominant GEI sources between regions is projected to widen as the energy transition progresses.

Total GEI optimal model development and key direct drivers

Mediator exclusion via SEM and ensemble variable selection

To optimize the feature set for machine learning, SEM was first used to delineate the structural relationships between potential mediators and core direct drivers (Table 1). The analysis revealed no statistically significant pathways (P > 0.1) between the electricity EF and four fundamental operational indicators (COD/TN, TP removal, NH3-N removal, and wastewater flow). This lack of significance indicated that variations in electricity EF were independent of wastewater characteristics and could not be explained by these parameters. Consequently, electricity EF was classified as a direct driver rather than a mediator. Conversely, the other four parameters, sodium acetate consumption, PAC consumption, electricity consumption, and sludge yield, exhibited significant dependencies on water quality drivers. They were confirmed as mediators and, consistent with our methodological framework (Section 2.3), were excluded from the subsequent feature set to prevent masking effects of the direct drivers. The SEM demonstrated excellent goodness-of-fit, with an RMSEA of 0.029 (well below the 0.08 threshold) and CFI/TLI values of 1.00 (exceeding 0.90).

Model development and comparison

Following data preprocessing and feature selection, we developed ten distinct machine learning models using a stratified 7:3 training-testing split. We then visualized the R2, RMSE, and MAE values for both training and testing sets to evaluate and compare predictive performance (Fig. 4).

The DT model exhibited the most significant disparity between training and testing performance, with R2 plummeting from 0.6628 during training to a mere 0.0359 in testing, the highest error rate among all models (Supplementary Fig. S5). Despite rigorous hyperparameter tuning, the DT model's predictive accuracy did not improve, indicating that the algorithm was ill-suited to the dataset's characteristics. In contrast, linear models (e.g., elastic net regression and ridge regression) significantly outperformed DT on the test set (R2 of 0.6761 and 0.6764, respectively). However, their inferior performance on the training set compared to non-linear models underscores their inherent inability to capture the complex non-linear dynamics of GEI drivers.

Ultimately, the random forest model demonstrated superior comprehensive performance, achieving a training R2 of 0.9123, a testing R2 of 0.7307, and an RMSE of 0.19 (Fig. 4). Notably, unlike previous studies that bolstered model metrics by incorporating mediator variables (e.g., electricity consumption, sludge yield, chemical consumption), this study achieved high predictive accuracy relying solely on direct drivers. This validates the robustness of our feature selection strategy (Section 3.2.1).

Beyond identifying key drivers using the optimal RF model, this study prioritized exploring the causal mechanisms that affect total GEI. We conducted a comprehensive performance evaluation to assess the practical utility of the RF model (Fig. 5). Error analysis (Fig. 5a) revealed minimal discrepancies between predicted and observed values for most samples; significant deviations occurred only when observed GEI exceeded 2 kg CO2-eq. m−3. Given that only 223 WWTPs in the entire dataset exceeded this threshold, the model's overall reliability was corroborated.

Further residual analysis (Fig. 5b–e) indicated that residuals approximate a normal distribution (median near zero). The Q–Q plot shows points aligning closely with the reference line, with only slight deviations at the tails, likely attributable to minor heteroscedasticity when predicting extreme values. The empirical cumulative distribution of absolute errors (Fig. 5f) shows a 90th percentile of 0.21 kg CO2-eq. m−3, indicating that most prediction errors fall within an acceptable range. This precision surpasses the uncertainty levels associated with standard Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) inventory methods. Recent evidence suggests IPCC methods may overlook approximately 22.8% of fossil-derived CO2 emissions [36]. In summary, despite minor deviations in extreme-value prediction and slight tail non-normality, the RF model exhibits a rational residual distribution and robust predictive capability.

Optimal model interpretability

To deconstruct the RF model and elucidate the key direct drivers of Total GEI, we used SHAP values to quantify each predictor's contribution. Based on the global feature importance and SHAP summary analyses, the top five influential variables were identified as NH3-N removal, electricity EF, discharge standard (Class 1A), TNinf, and year (Fig. 6a and b).

NH3-N removal was the paramount predictor (SHAP = 0.080), exceeding the other variables (SHAP <0.050). This predominance probably reflects two mechanisms. First, biological nitrogen removal can generate N2O, which has a global warming potential approximately 265 times that of CO2 (significantly higher than CH4) and therefore contributes strongly to direct emissions. Second, under typical conditions of low influent C/N ratios in China, rigorous nitrogen removal necessitates substantial external carbon addition. The production and consumption of these carbon sources significantly amplify both indirect and direct GEI. TNinf also exhibited high importance (SHAP = 0.040; ranked fourth), whereas organic and phosphorus loads, represented by CODinf and TPinf, showed moderate influence, ranking sixth and ninth, respectively.

Additionally, the categorical variables discharge standard Class 1A and Class 1B ranked third and eleventh, respectively. This corroborates the trend identified in Section 3.1: increasingly stringent discharge standards are a primary driver of GEI escalation. Among treatment processes, the anaerobic-anoxic-oxic (AAO) process exerted the strongest influence (SHAP = 0.023), surpassing conventional activated sludge (CAS), sequencing batch reactor (SBR), and anoxic–oxic (AO). Although the AAO process typically has lower theoretical emission factors for specific gases than AO, its overall impact on total GEI in the model is greater. This highlights that assessing the low-carbon potential of treatment technologies requires holistic machine-learning modeling rather than relying on single-factor emission coefficients.

Delving into the detailed dependencies (Fig. 6b), most key numerical variables showed positive correlations with total GEI, with notable exceptions: wastewater flow and COD/TP. The negative correlation of COD/TP is attributed to its role in biological phosphorus removal: a higher ratio indicates sufficient internal carbon for polyphosphate-accumulating organisms, reducing the need for chemical precipitants and thus lowering GEI.

Moving beyond correlation, this study integrated domain knowledge with causal algorithms to construct and validate causal effect graphs (Fig. 6c). Consistent with the SHAP results, NH3-N removal exhibited the most significant causal impact on GEI, with an estimated causal effect coefficient of 12.09. In this linear regression model, the coefficient indicates that a 1-unit increase in NH3-N removal is associated with a substantial increase in total GEI. TNinf demonstrated a moderate causal impact, with an estimated effect of 5.21. In comparison, electricity EF exhibited a positive but comparatively smaller causal effect coefficient of 0.5049. While this coefficient is significantly lower than that of NH3-N Removal (0.50 vs. 12.09), simply comparing coefficients ignores the differing scales and physical variability of the predictors.

To accurately compare their effect on emissions, we conducted a sensitivity analysis using the 2019 national averages as baselines: NH3-N removal, 23.62 mg L−1; TNinf, 33.54 mg L−1; electricity EF, 0.5368. The results revealed that a 10% increase in these variables relative to their baselines led to increases in total GEI of 5.65%, 1.71%, and 4.45%, respectively.

This comparative analysis yields a critical insight: although NH3-N removal had the highest sensitivity coefficient (5.65%), the sensitivity of electricity EF (4.45%) is nearly equivalent. Crucially, historical trends (2009–2019) show that while NH3-N removal increased by 11.83% (driving emissions up), the electricity EF decreased by 16.58% nationally, with a dramatic 47.48% reduction in the Southwest region. Consequently, despite its smaller raw coefficient, the electricity EF serves as a more potent practical lever for GHG mitigation through grid decarbonization than process adjustments alone. The robustness of these causal estimates was verified using placebo treatment, random common cause, and data subsample refutation tests.

Key direct drivers and causal effects across distinct GEI sources

This study extended the unified analytical framework encompassing feature selection, model optimization, and interpretability analysis to five distinct emission sources: Direct GEI, Indirect GEI (comprising electricity-use GEI and chemical-production GEI), STD GEI, and DCT wastewater GEI. The objective here was twofold: one, identify key direct drivers for each emission source and, two, pinpoint the primary sources of prediction error within the aggregate Total GEI model.

Integrated model development and performance comparison

We used a consistent methodology to develop predictive models for the five GHG sources. The consensus of four feature selection algorithms determined the final predictor set for each source (Fig. 7). The analysis revealed a significant overlap between these source-specific predictors and the fundamental water quality variables identified in the causal analysis of total GEI. NH3-N removal was selected in four source models, while CODinf and TPinf were selected in three. This recurrence validates the rationality and accuracy of our proposed model. Detailed selection processes are documented in Supplementary Figs. S6–S34 and Supplementary Tables S32–S36.

We screened the inputs and limited them to nine parameters to investigate the mechanism by which direct drivers influence GHG emission sources. Incorporating these three variables (along with categorical features), all testing R2 values exceeded 0.88, with 50% of the models surpassing 0.97 (Fig. 8). DCT wastewater GEI. Although accounting for only approximately 10% of emissions and exhibiting a declining trend, this source warranted attention given the risk of rebound emissions if strict standards are relaxed (Section 3.2.3). Consistent with the total GEI model, the optimal model for DCT wastewater GEI was identified as RF, with a testing R2 of 0.8423 (Supplementary Figs. S51–S52). Interpretability and causal mechanisms of the four GEI source models

Based on SHAP and causal analyses of the optimal models, this section elucidates the heterogeneity of key direct drivers across emission sources. Discharge standards (Class 1A or Class 1B) emerged as ubiquitous dominant drivers for direct GEI (SHAP = 0.065), indirect GEI (SHAP = 0.042), and DCT GEI (SHAP = 0.0058). This underscores that stringent standards exacerbate emissions across multiple sources, providing a mechanistic explanation for the critical impact of standards on total GEI identified in Section 3.2.3. However, specific drivers and causal magnitudes vary significantly.

Direct GEI. Beyond standards, treatment processes play a more pronounced role here than in the total GEI model. We identified six distinct processes as key predictors, compared with three for total GEI (Supplementary Fig. S53a). Water quality parameters TN Removal (SHAP = 0.037) and NH3-N Removal (SHAP = 0.018) were equally critical. A generally positive correlation was observed for both, though negative effects occurred at lower concentrations (Supplementary Fig. S53b). This was primarily because high TN/NH3-N removal rates simultaneously increased N2O generation and lowered the C/N ratio, necessitating the addition of external carbon that mineralized to CO2.

To quantify causal relationships, we estimated the causal effects of these drivers (Fig. 9; DAGs in Supplementary Fig. S54). The causal hierarchy differs from the SHAP ranking. This discrepancy does not imply that either analytical approach is unreliable. SHAP importance reflects a feature's contribution to predictive accuracy within the machine learning model, whereas causal effect estimates quantify the magnitude of change in GEI resulting from an intervention on that feature. These two metrics capture different dimensions of variable influence and are therefore complementary rather than contradictory. In this case, although TN removal showed a lower SHAP importance than NH3-N removal in the predictive model, its causal intervention effect on direct GEI was substantially larger (4.9168 versus 2.5474). This contrast underscores the value of integrating both predictive and causal perspectives to obtain a more comprehensive understanding of how direct drivers influence WWTP emissions. Sensitivity analysis revealed that, relative to the 2019 national baseline, a 10% increase in TN removal led to a 4.49% increase in direct GEI, whereas a similar increase in NH3-N removal led to a 12.02% increase in direct GEI. Consequently, NH3-N Removal was identified as the most influential causal driver for direct GEI. These estimates were validated via placebo treatment, random common cause, and data subsample tests.

Indirect GEI. SHAP analysis of the indirect GEI optimal model and its sub-models (Supplementary Figs. S55–S57) revealed that chemical-production GEI was affected by NH3-N removal (SHAP = 0.044) and COD/TP (0.028), while electricity use GEI was driven by electricity EF (0.044) and Wastewater Flow (0.039). Collectively, four factors dominated indirect GEI: NH3-N removal (0.059), electricity EF (0.047), and wastewater flow (0.044). Additionally, discharge standards Class 1A or Class 1B exerted significant influence (SHAP = 0.042 and 0.026, respectively). Combined with the direct GEI analysis, this confirms that strict standards amplify both direct emissions and indirect emissions from energy and chemical consumption. Therefore, a scientific trade-off between ecological benefits (effluent quality) and carbon increments is likely imperative.

Causal analysis based on fundamental key direct drivers (Fig. 9) revealed source-specific disparities. Although NH3-N, TP, COD, and electricity EF were relevant to all, the magnitude of causal effects varied across emission sources. Consistent with the distinction drawn in the direct GEI model, SHAP rankings indicate predictive importance, whereas causal effect estimates reflect the outcomes of targeted interventions. Here, NH3-N removal exhibited a high causal effect on chemical production GEI (4.3721), but a lower effect on electricity use GEI (1.6607). This underscores the necessity of source-specific causal analysis. Overall, consistent with the total GEI model, NH3-N removal remained the most influential causal variable for indirect GEI (Fig. 9; DAGs in Supplementary Fig. S58), with a 10% increase in concentration leading to a 6.15% relative increase in emission intensity (based on the 2019 baseline).

DCT wastewater GEI. SHAP analysis (Supplementary Fig. S59) indicates that discharge standards remained the primary driver but showed a negative correlation with emission intensity; stricter standards reduced the concentrations of COD and TN entering receiving waters, thereby lowering downstream GEI. Secondary drivers TNinf (SHAP = 0.028) and CODinf (SHAP = 0.025) showed divergent causal effects (Fig. 9). Applying the same conceptual framework as above, the SHAP values reflect each variable's contribution to model predictions, whereas the causal effect estimates quantify the impact of interventions. Here, TNinf has a markedly higher causal effect value (0.0884) than CODinf (0.0466), suggesting that TN contributed more significantly to GHG generation in receiving water bodies than COD, despite their comparable SHAP importance. Notably, electricity EF displayed a counterintuitive positive correlation with DCT GEI. This is likely a temporal confounding effect: high electricity EFs corresponded to earlier years (e.g., 2009–2012) when national discharge standards were looser, leading to higher pollutant discharge and consequently higher DCT GEI (DAGs in Supplementary Fig. S60).

Comparison of key drivers and causal effects across distinct GEI sources

The key direct drivers and their estimated causal effects vary significantly across emission sources (Fig. 9). A striking disparity was observed in the magnitude of effects. In the aggregate total GEI model, the estimated causal effect of NH3-N removal reached 12.09. However, source-specific modeling decomposed this impact: the effect value is only 4.92 for direct GEI and markedly lower at 1.66 for electricity-use GEI. This decomposition underscores that source-specific modeling provides higher-resolution delineation of the pathways through which key factors influence emissions.

Further, the electricity EF exhibits a distinct non-monotonic causal behavior across hierarchical levels. Specifically, its baseline causal effect on electricity use GEI is 0.4061. At the indirect GEI level, which aggregates electricity and chemical consumption, this effect was diluted to 0.3700, likely due to the inclusion of non-electric factors, such as chemical use. However, at the total GEI level, the effect exhibited an anomalous rebound, amplifying to 0.5409. This significant superposition effect suggests that the electricity EF functions not merely as a calculation parameter for indirect emissions but as a comprehensive proxy indicator. It likely reflects the complex coupling between regional energy structures and the operational state of WWTPs. This finding highlights the necessity of granular, source-apportioned modeling. Only by distinguishing the heterogeneous driving mechanisms across sources can we effectively decouple these complex interactions and isolate the core variables that affect each subsystem's carbon footprint.

The SHAP interpretability results from the optimal source-specific models (Table 3) corroborate these findings. Although NH3-N removal has the highest SHAP value for total GEI, it is not the dominant factor for electricity-use GEI. Therefore, when employing IML methods to elucidate GHG driving mechanisms, it is recommended to conduct modeling that integrates both aggregate and source-specific perspectives to achieve a comprehensive and reliable understanding.

Research limitations and future directions

While this study provides a comprehensive, data-driven framework for analyzing GHG emissions in WWTPs, several limitations warrant attention in future research.

First, due to the unavailability of plant-specific data regarding chemical dosages and sludge disposal pathways, this study relied on empirical equations and average emission factors for calculations. This reliance may have introduced uncertainties into the quantitative estimation of GEI.

Second, the scope of this study was primarily focused on elucidating the relationships and causal effects between direct drivers and GHG emission sources. Although effective for macro-scale analysis, this methodological framework may inherently contribute to residual errors in source-specific model development (particularly for STD GEI), as internal process state variables (e.g., SRT, MLSS) were not explicitly included.

Third, the construction of causal graphs faces the universal challenge of the exclusion restriction assumption. This assumption requires that no unobserved direct causal pathways exist between the drivers of the treatment variable and the outcome variable [38]. Although three distinct refutation tests were employed to bolster the robustness of the causal graphs, their absolute validity cannot be unequivocally verified in an observational study.

Future research should extend these data-driven analytical models to specific wastewater treatment technologies, constructing process-differentiated models. This approach would enable a deeper dissection of the driving processes and mechanisms behind GHG emissions in municipal WWTPs, yielding greater insights. Concurrently, incorporating a broader array of operational variables that directly characterize process states (e.g., dissolved oxygen, MLSS, and SRT) would enhance model accuracy while effectively mitigating the effects of noise. Such advancements will facilitate the development of more reliable methods for causal structure verification and precise techniques to control for unobserved confounders. Ultimately, this will significantly enhance the capability to identify robust causal relationships from observational datasets within the context of data-driven environmental research.

Conclusion

Leveraging a massive dataset of 40,722 Chinese WWTPs, this study established an integrated analytical framework fusing structural equation modeling for mediator exclusion, causal inference, and interpretable machine learning. This framework provides a novel perspective for identifying the direct drivers and deep-seated causal mechanisms of GHG emissions, offering critical insights for precise carbon mitigation management. By focusing on key factors such as NH3-N removal, electricity EF, TPinf, treatment processes, and discharge standards, the study draws the following core conclusions:

(1).The primary GHG emission sources of WWTPs across China exhibit significant spatiotemporal heterogeneity. In the context of the ongoing national energy transition, this regional divergence is projected to amplify, necessitating region-specific mitigation strategies.
