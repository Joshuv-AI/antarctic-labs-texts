Building better antibody responses: The interplay of infection, vaccination, and immune imprinting
Authors: L Carissa Aurelia, Kevin J Selva, Amy W Chung
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAntibodies are critical for protection against a range of viral respiratory diseases, including SARS-CoV-2, but the induction of durable and potent antibody responses continues to be a challenge. Beyond neutralization, there is growing appreciation for the potential protective role of Fc-mediated functions, especially against immune-evasive variants. Induction of polyfunctional antibody responses is a complex multifactorial process that is shaped by interactions between various antibody features, viral properties, and host factors. Here, we review lessons learned from the COVID-19 pandemic regarding how prior infection and different vaccination strategies can modulate plasma and mucosal antibody profiles, including isotypes, immunoglobulin G subclasses, Fc glycosylation, and consequently, antibody functions. Additionally, we discuss the challenges of immune imprinting and its effects upon antibody responses to viral variants. This review provides insights into optimizing antibody-based strategies for COVID-19 prevention and treatment, emphasizing the need for further research to understand the mechanisms behind effective antibody responses and their impact upon clinical outcomes.

Introduction

Antibodies provide immune protection against respiratory viruses via an array of antiviral functions, mediated by their two functional regions. The antigen-binding (Fab) region determines antigen specificity and mediates neutralization (Fig. 1). Neutralization prevents viral attachment and entry into host cells, thereby preventing infection, and has been identified as the main correlate of protection against a range of viral infectious diseases, including SARS-CoV-2 (1, 2). As such, induction of durable and broadly neutralizing antibodies is the principal goal of most antiviral vaccination strategies. This, however, remains a challenge given that potent neutralizing antibodies are rare, limited to specific epitopes, highly susceptible to viral mutations, and wane over time (3–5).

Overview of antibody-mediated activities against SARS-CoV-2. Binding of antibodies to the target antigen can result in neutralization and induction of Fc-mediated responses, including phagocytosis, cellular cytotoxicity, antibody-mediated cytokine release, antigen presentation, and antibody-mediated complement activation.

Beyond neutralization, antigen-bound antibodies can engage with the innate immune system or complement proteins via their fragment crystallizable (Fc) region to initiate effector functions. This includes, but is not limited to, antibody-dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC), phagocytosis (ADCP), complement deposition (ADCD), and cytokine release (Fig. 1). Unlike neutralization, induction of Fc effector functions is not limited to binding of antibodies to specific neutralizing epitopes; instead, they can be triggered by binding to any conformationally accessible epitopes and are therefore less susceptible to viral mutations. Thus, there has been growing interest in understanding ways to induce polyfunctional antibody responses that are capable of mediating neutralization and Fc effector functions to maximize protection against viral pathogens (6). Here, we discuss lessons learned, as well as highlight knowledge gaps about functional antibodies in the context of SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination.

SARS-CoV-2 antibody targets

SARS-CoV-2 consists of four major structural proteins: the spike glycoprotein, the nucleocapsid (N) protein, the membrane (M) protein, and the small membrane (E) protein. The spike protein facilitates viral entry and is further cleaved into the spike 1 (S1) and spike 2 (S2) subunits, which are responsible for host cell attachment and host cell fusion, respectively. The majority of neutralizing antibodies target the receptor-binding domain (RBD) of the S1 subunit, which mediates binding to the human angiotensin-converting enzyme 2 (ACE2) receptor on host cells (5, 7–10). The N-terminal domain (NTD) of S1 is another target of neutralizing antibodies, with antibodies targeting the antigenic supersite of the NTD generally exhibiting high neutralization activity (11, 12). Despite the high neutralization potency of anti-RBD and anti-NTD antibodies, they display limited neutralization breadth, as both the RBD, especially the receptor-binding motif, and NTD are highly mutable (11). Consequently, acquisition of extensive RBD and NTD mutations, especially among the Omicron subvariants, has allowed the virus to escape neutralizing antibodies (12, 13). In contrast to neutralizing antibodies, Fc-mediated functions against variants are better maintained (13–15), likely reflecting the ability of antibodies to target conserved non-neutralizing epitopes and trigger Fc effector functions.

The S2 is another major target of antibodies. Although most antibodies targeting S2 lack or have limited antineutralizing activity, they have been demonstrated to mediate Fc effector functions such as ADCC (16). Importantly, anti-S2 antibodies, however, display greater cross-reactivity to SARS-CoV-2 variants and with other betacoronaviruses compared with antibodies against the RBD and NTD due to the higher degree of conservation of S2 (17, 18). Hence, their activity against variants is typically better preserved compared with antibodies to RBD or NTD (16, 18).

Outside of the spike protein, antibodies against N and M, as well as accessory proteins such as ORF3, have been observed following infection and following vaccination with inactivated vaccines in the case of anti-N antibodies (19–21), although such antibodies are typically non-neutralizing. However, these viral proteins are present on virus-infected cells, and as such, antibodies to these proteins have been demonstrated to induce Fc effector functions such as ADCC and ADCD (21–23).

Fc-mediated responses against SARS-CoV-2

Fc-mediated responses are triggered following the formation of immune complexes consisting of antigen-bound antibodies and Fc receptors or complement protein C1q. Both SARS-CoV-2 infection and vaccination elicit antibodies that are capable of mediating Fc effector functions (24–28) and are suggested to contribute, at least in part, to protection. Several nonhuman primate vaccine studies have found that Fc effector functions, including ADCP, ADCD, and antibody-dependent natural killer (NK) cell activation (ADNKA), along with neutralizing antibody titers, correlated with protective efficacy (29–31). In mice, FcγR-mediated functions were shown to contribute to protection against Omicron variants (32). Enhancing the Fc effector functions of non-neutralizing mAbs delays viral replication and protects mice from lethal challenge when combined with a neutralizing mAb (33). In humans, whether Fc effector functions alone are sufficient for protection is still unclear, partly due to the interconnected nature of Fc effector functions with neutralizing activity, which makes dissecting their sole contribution challenging to investigate.

Fc effector functions following infection, however, appear to play both protective and pathogenic roles. Several studies have observed reduced spike-specific ADCC and/or ADCP activity in severe COVID-19 patients (25, 34, 35), suggesting Fc effector functions may contribute to disease resolution (28–30). Early in the pandemic, concerns of antibody-dependent enhancement (ADE) of infection were raised, although there is still very limited preclinical and clinical evidence of ADE of infection following vaccination (36). However, several studies have now demonstrated antibody-mediated inflammation following infection that may contribute to more severe disease. In particular, elevated inflammatory cytokine release linked to enhanced FcγRIIIa binding as a result of increased titers of immunoglobulin G (IgG) lacking core fucose (afucosylated IgG) has been observed in severe COVID-19 patients and is postulated to contribute to pathology (37, 38). Additionally, studies in mice and in vitro have demonstrated FcγR-mediated infection of monocytes and macrophages can result in pyroptosis and inflammasome activation, resulting in inflammation and lung pathology (39–41). Elevated antibody-dependent neutrophil phagocytosis (ADNP), ADCD, FcγRIIIa binding, and activation have also been observed in hospitalized and deceased COVID-19 patients (26, 34). Similarly, elevated IgG capable of activating monocytes and afucosylated IgG have been identified as antibody signatures associated with adults and multisystem inflammatory syndrome in children, respectively (42, 43). However, reduced SARS-CoV-2-specific IgG3, a potent mediator of Fc effector functions, was observed in postacute COVID-19 syndrome patients, suggesting a role of Fc receptors in viral control (44). Collectively, these studies suggest a context-dependent role of Fc effector functions in infection. This variability likely stems from the multiple different antibody features and effector cells that can influence Fc effector functions, which will be discussed below.

Factors influencing Fc-mediated responses to SARS-CoV-2

Antibody isotypes induced by SARS-CoV-2

Of the five human Ig isotypes (IgG, IgM, IgA, IgE, and IgD), SARS-CoV-2 vaccination and infection predominantly induce IgG, IgA, and IgM. Seroconversion to IgM typically occurs first, before class switching to IgG and IgA. All three isotypes are all capable of neutralization (45, 46); however, most studies thus far have predominantly described IgG-mediated Fc effector functions.

IgG subclasses in sera

IgG can be further divided into four subclasses: IgG1, IgG2, IgG3, and IgG4, named in order of abundance in healthy human sera. Despite the similar basic structure and high homology in the amino acid sequences of human IgG constant regions, IgG subclasses display structural variation that results in differences in FcγR affinities, antigen binding, mucosal and placental transport, and half-life (47). Of the four subclasses, IgG1 and IgG3 display the highest affinity for FcγRs and C1q and are potent mediators of Fc effector functions, while IgG2 and IgG4 have reduced affinity for, or limited capacity to bind, FcγRs and are consequently poor mediators of certain effector functions (47, 48). Polymorphisms of IgG subclasses, known as allotypes, may also potentially contribute to different SARS-CoV-2 antigen-specific vaccine responses, with a recent study observing enhanced SARS-CoV-2 antigen-specific IgG and FcγR binding following vaccination of individuals with certain IgG1 allotypes (49), although these differences need to be confirmed in larger, more diverse cohorts.

Intriguingly, the nature of SARS-CoV-2 antigen exposure can impact IgG subclass distribution. Table 1 summarizes the changes in IgG subclasses following infection, vaccination, number of doses, and/or vaccine platform. Like most viruses, IgG1 is the main subclass induced by SARS-CoV-2 infection and by all vaccination regimens (51, 52, 56, 60, 66). High IgG3 titers are also induced following infection (45, 50, 69) and in response to protein-based vaccines (56, 66) but are reduced with repeated mRNA vaccination (52, 62). A growing number of studies have described an increase in IgG4, and to a lesser extent IgG2, following repeated mRNA vaccination (4, 52–54, 56–58, 63, 64). This rise in IgG4, however, was not observed to the same extent when at least one protein-based or adenoviral-vectored vaccine was administered for the priming doses (56, 60, 66, 68). Induction of IgG4 by inactivated vaccines is unclear, with conflicting reports on IgG4 titers observed following homologous vaccination with inactivated vaccines or in combination with other vaccine platforms (55, 61, 65).

Collectively, these studies indicate that vaccine platforms and doses can shape IgG subclass distribution (Table 1). Interpreting subclass levels reported by different studies, however, can be challenging. Although general trends, such as the absence or presence of a given subclass, are fairly consistent, the levels or fold changes reported can be highly variable due to the use of different assays. Even in situations where the same assay is used, the levels reported are highly variable between different studies due to different detection reagents, assay protocols, and/or target antigens used and a lack of universal subclass controls. This highlights the need for standardized and validated SARS-CoV-2 antibody subclass controls and/or detection reagents, although this is an ongoing challenge given how rapidly SARS-CoV-2 mutates.

As IgG subclasses exhibit different abilities to induce Fc effector functions, there is growing interest in understanding the Fc-mediated activities associated with the changes in the subclass profiles. Several studies have reported negative correlations between IgG4 titers and Fc-induced functions (52, 54, 57, 62, 66). Moreover, IgG4 depletion studies and studies using IgG4 monoclonal antibodies (mAbs) have demonstrated that IgG4 can directly compete with more Fc-functional IgG1 or IgG3, resulting in reduced ADCC and ADCD (57, 62). Reduction in Fc effector functions accompanying elevated IgG4, however, does not appear to be universal. IgG4 was able to mediate and thus improve ADCP activity following vaccine boosting (62). Furthermore, the level of inhibition of ADCC and ADCD by IgG4 was also influenced by several factors, including subclass titers, the target epitope, FcγR polymorphism, and viral variant (57, 62), highlighting that Fc-mediated responses are shaped by additional factors beyond changes in subclass titers alone. In addition to dampened Fc functions, higher proportions of IgG4 relative to other IgG subclasses have also recently been associated with reduced neutralization (67, 68, 70). It should also be noted that the link between subclass composition and clinical outcomes is relatively unclear. One study observed that increased IgG4/IgG1 ratios were linked with increased risk of breakthrough infections, albeit in a limited cohort (70). This was hypothesized to be driven by reduced neutralization and Fc-mediated functions, although this was not demonstrated mechanistically (70). This highlights the need for more studies to investigate the mechanisms driving antibody-mediated responses to SARS-CoV-2 and their linkage to clinical outcomes.

IgG subclasses in mucosa

Unlike IgG subclass responses in plasma, which have been studied extensively, studies investigating mucosal IgG subclass responses following infection and vaccination are limited (Table 2). IgG is predominantly produced systemically and transported to mucosal surfaces via the neonatal Fc receptor (80). However, local IgG may also be produced within mucosal-associated lymphoid tissues that may differ in clonal repertoire from circulating IgG, thus warranting further investigation. Following vaccination and infection, IgG1 has been reported to be the dominant subclass in mucosal secretions, including saliva, breast milk, tears, and endotracheal aspirates (44, 54, 60, 72, 73, 75–77, 79). As with plasma, repeated mRNA vaccination results in increased mucosal IgG4, which was negatively correlated with FcγR engagement (54). In contrast, priming with an adenoviral-vectored vaccine followed by a single mRNA vaccine boost did not induce mucosal IgG4 (60). While four additional mRNA boosts following an adenoviral vector prime did result in modest levels of IgG4, they were significantly lower than homologous mRNA vaccination (67). Studies examining IgG subclass distribution in the mucosa by other vaccine platforms and their influence on antibody function are lacking, thus highlighting the need for more in-depth mucosal studies examining antibody profiles, as well as their influence on clinical outcomes.

Immunoglobulin A

IgA, the second most abundant antibody type in blood, exists as two subclasses, IgA1 and IgA2. While IgA is mostly present as a monomer in blood, it is also expressed as dimeric IgA which gets rapidly secreted into mucosal fluids, such as saliva, nasal fluid, tears, and breast milk, forming the dominant antibody isotype there (81). Whether monomeric and dimeric IgA share clonal repertoires is unclear, although it has been suggested that their repertoires are distinct (82). Table 2 summarizes the distribution of IgA subclasses in mucosal samples following SARS-CoV-2 exposures.

Systemic and mucosal IgA antibodies are readily expressed following the onset of COVID-19, contributing to viral neutralization (46, 83, 84). Importantly, rapid surges in mucosal IgA were associated with quicker viral clearance following infection (54, 85, 86). Early mucosal IgA responses were also similarly linked to viral control in the human SARS-CoV-2 infection challenge study (87). These aspects highlight how IgA antibodies respond quickly to limit the initial infection.

Although IgA responses decay rapidly in blood, mucosal IgA remains elevated for up to 7 months following infection (83, 88). Spike-specific secretory IgA also responded quicker and was further boosted following SARS-CoV-2 reinfection (85, 89). Elevated IgA responses, especially at the mucosa, have also been associated with a decreased risk of subsequent breakthrough infections (86, 90), and linked to milder or asymptomatic COVID-19 infections (91, 92). Furthermore, secretory IgA has also been associated with the prevention of infectious virus shedding (85). Taken together, rising levels of mucosal IgA may contribute to population immunity amid declining COVID-19 vaccination/booster rates.

Although vaccination and boosters are effective at inducing systemic IgA responses (54, 93), the initial intramuscular COVID-19 vaccination induced only a small rise in mucosal IgA responses, and subsequent COVID-19 vaccines and boosters failed to significantly improve mucosal IgA responses (90, 93, 94). This has sparked the development of COVID-19 vaccines, aimed at targeting the mucosa to promote localized secretory IgA responses (95). However, at present, challenges with vaccine reformulation, route of delivery, and preexisting immunity have resulted in limited success in eliciting robust mucosal IgA in humans (95).

FcγR classes and polymorphisms

FcγRs are a family of cell surface receptors that are part of the immunoglobulin superfamily that vary in size, structure, distribution on effector cells, and affinity for IgG subclasses (Fig. 2). In humans, these include the high-affinity FcγRI, which can bind to monomeric IgG, and the low-affinity FcγRII and FcγRIII subfamilies, which are activated upon cross-linking by antigen-bound IgG (98, 99). They can also be classified as activating (FcγRI, FcγRIIa, and FcγRIII) or inhibitory (FcγRIIb) receptors. Genetic polymorphisms of FcγRs have also been identified. For example, there are two main FcγRIIa polymorphisms: FcγRIIa-H131 and FcγRIIa-R131. Similarly, two main FcγRIIIa polymorphisms exist: FcγRIIIa-V158 and FcγRIIIa-F158. FcγRIIa-H131 and FcγRIIIa-V158 display higher affinity for IgG1 and IgG3 (96). Moreover, IgG4 can weakly bind FcγRIIIa-V158 but not FcγRIIIa-F158 and has higher affinity for FcγRIIa-R131 compared with FcγRIIa-H131. Thus, both the subclass composition and FcγR class and polymorphisms influence the overall strength of IgG–FcγR interactions and have been shown to mechanistically impact anti-SARS-CoV-2 Fc-mediated activities (62). Clinically, FcγRIIa-R131 has been associated with increased disease severity and mortality in both SARS-CoV-2 and SARS-CoV (100, 101). There are conflicting reports, however, on the role of FcγRIIIa variants, with one study suggesting an association between FcγRIIIa-V158 and reduced risk of severe COVID-19 (102), while another describes a higher incidence of FcγRIIIa-V158 in severe and deceased patients (103), albeit both studies occur in small cohorts. The role of FcγR polymorphisms in vaccine efficacy and hybrid immunity, however, is underexplored. Moreover, studies of FcγR polymorphisms have mainly focused on clinical outcomes, but the mechanisms by which they drive such outcomes are unclear, and better understanding their functional contributions may provide better insight into the protective and pathogenic roles of FcγR-mediated functions.

The class and extent to which FcγRs are expressed can vary by effector cells. For example, monocytes, macrophages, and neutrophils can express a wide range of FcγRs, including FcγRI, FcγRIIa, FcγRIIIa, and FcγRIIIb depending on the subset, while NK cells express mainly FcγRIIIa (104). As such, different effector cells have been demonstrated to play different protective or pathogenic roles in SARS-CoV-2. Monocytes and alveolar macrophages, in particular, have been shown to contribute to maximal in vivo therapeutic efficacy of mAbs and vaccine-induced protection in mice, indicating a role for ADCP in protection (105, 106). It should also be noted that the population of FcγR-effector cells, and consequently expression levels of FcγRs, can vary between compartments. Indeed, a study in macaques has highlighted that antibody-mediated NK cell functions were a stronger correlate of protection within serum and not the mucosal surfaces, where phagocytic activities were the main drivers of protection (107). Thus, effector cell location and their FcγR expression should also be taken into consideration when determining the protective or pathogenic role of Fc-mediated responses.

IgG Fc glycosylation

Beyond IgG subclasses, posttranslational glycosylation of IgG at asparagine 297 (N297) in the Fc has been described to modulate IgG Fc effector functions. IgG glycans consist of a core N-acetylglucosamine (GlcNAc) and mannose residue, which can be differentially modified by the addition of galactose, sialic acid, fucose, and bisecting GlcNAc residues. IgG lacking core fucose (afucosylated IgG) displays enhanced FcγRIIIa affinity and, consequently, ADCC activity (108), while increased galactosylation is associated with enhanced C1q binding and ADCD (109). Importantly, different glycosylation profiles are induced by SARS-CoV-2 vaccination compared with infection, and this also can vary by disease severity. Elevated afucosylated antispike IgG1 has been described in severe COVID-19 patients and was associated with increased FcγRIIIa binding and production of proinflammatory cytokines, which is postulated to contribute to pathology (37, 38, 110). In contrast, increased antispike IgG galactosylation and sialylation were observed following vaccination, although transient reductions in bisecting and fucosylated antispike IgG have also been reported (56, 111). However, vaccine-induced glycosylation patterns can vary depending on vaccine platform and prior infection history (111) and are influenced by host factors, including age, sex, and comorbidities (37, 112). It should also be noted that glycosylation studies have mainly focused on IgG1 or IgG as a whole, with limited studies examining glycosylation of other isotypes or subclasses. Hence, further investigation on the functional roles of glycans to other isotypes and subclasses and in relation to other factors that can mediate antibody functions is warranted.

Factors influencing neutralization against SARS-CoV-2

Role of antibody isotypes and subclasses in neutralization

Although the activities of the Fab and Fc regions are traditionally considered to be independent of each other, there is a growing appreciation of the role of the Fc region upon antibody binding and thus neutralization. Studies have shown that the neutralizing activity of mucosal IgA against emerging Omicron variants is the result of antibody valency (113). Dimeric secretory IgA displayed stronger cross-reactivity across COVID-19 variants than monomeric IgA or IgG from blood (54). Furthermore, converting recombinant IgG mAbs to dimeric or secretory IgA rescued their ability to bind and neutralize Omicron variants, though this improvement was epitope dependent (114, 115). Similarly, IgG3 has been thought to mediate enhanced neutralization due to its long and flexible hinge. Indeed, studies have demonstrated that expressing SARS-CoV-2 mAbs as IgG3 resulted in both enhanced neutralization potency and cross-reactivity compared with expressing the same antibody as other IgG subclasses (116, 117). Furthermore, one study has suggested that IgG3 contributed to the majority of neutralizing antibodies in pooled convalescent plasma (45), further highlighting the potential for IgG3 to mediate improved neutralization. It should also be noted that different IgG3 polymorphisms exist that result in varying hinge lengths (48). One study has reported increased frequency of the shorter hinge length polymorphism in critical COVID-19 patients, albeit in a limited cohort (118), highlighting that further studies investigating the role of IgG3 hinge length in mediating antibody responses and their association with protection are warranted.

Unlike IgA and IgG3, the influence of IgG4 on neutralization is unclear, and often conflicting. IgG4, typically the last IgG subclass to be induced, acquires the most somatic hypermutations among all subclasses (119). Thus, in vivo IgG4 is hypothesized to possess the highest antigen affinity, although this has yet to be demonstrated. Increased antibody avidity has been demonstrated to accompany the rise in IgG4 (52). However, the study did not demonstrate that IgG4 and not a general increase in avidity across all subclasses contributed to this increase, although IgG4 has been shown to reach similar avidity as IgG1 in another study (57). Conversely, IgG4 in vivo possesses the unique ability to form half molecules and undergo Fab arm exchange with other IgG4 half molecules, a random process that results in bispecific antibodies (120). Previous studies of IgG4 against allergens have suggested that the majority of IgG4 in vivo is bispecific and acts as if functionally monovalent with limited ability to cross-link antigen (121), although this has not been shown in the context of SARS-CoV-2 vaccination. Some studies have suggested that bivalency is required for neutralization, especially against Omicron variants (122, 123). This loss in bivalency may result in reduced neutralization, which some studies have found to be negatively correlated with IgG4 titers or IgG4/IgG1 ratio (50, 66, 70). However, other studies have demonstrated that combining two different SARS-CoV-2 antibodies into a bispecific format can enhance the neutralization breadth and potency of the parent antibodies (123, 124) due to increased binding stoichiometries and potential interspike cross-linking. Overall, the consequence of bispecificity on the neutralization potency of IgG4 may be epitope dependent and further studies investigating the proportion of bispecific IgG4 in vivo and the epitopes targeted are warranted, while also taking into consideration potential changes in antigen affinity of IgG4 compared with other subclasses and isotypes.

Immune imprinting

To combat the continuous emergence of SARS-CoV-2 variants that evade preexisting humoral immunity, vaccine compositions have shifted to include the spike sequences of more recently circulating strain. Boosting with variant-containing vaccines enhances the overall breadth of neutralization and improves neutralizing titers against both the matched Omicron variant and downstream Omicron variants, although neutralizing activity against Omicron subvariants was still lower compared with the ancestral strain and is largely mediated by cross-reactive ancestral strain antibodies (125–128). This is in part due to immune imprinting, a phenomenon in which revaccination results in high titers of antibodies that cross-reacts to the original strain encountered, while induction of strain-specific antibodies is limited (129, 130). Hybrid immunity has been suggested to partially alleviate immune imprinting, although this can be variable between individuals, depending on the initial degree of imprinting, which in turn is influenced by various factors, including the initial strain encountered, vaccine platform, and number of exposures (131–134). It should be noted that immune imprinting studies have predominantly focused on neutralization, and the influence on Fc-mediated responses is comparatively underexplored (Fig. 3). However, studies have demonstrated that the factors influencing imprinting, including vaccine platform, hybrid immunity, and sequence of exposure, can also influence the degree of Fc-mediated responses (16, 135, 136). Imprinted immune responses largely target conserved, but poorly neutralizing epitopes (132), which limits Omicron-specific neutralization but would still allow for induction of Fc effector functions and may contribute to the maintenance of Fc-mediated responses (15, 128). As mentioned above, however, induction of Fc effector functions is not solely dependent on binding antibody titers and is highly linked to IgG subclass composition. Studies have suggested boosting with both bivalent mRNA and XBB.1.5 spike protein results in a greater boost in Omicron-specific IgG4 than IgG1 (4, 66), although IgG4 titers remained lower. Nonetheless, further studies investigating the extent to which imprinting influences each subclass, how this changes with different vaccine regimens and/or with hybrid immunity, and their specific impact on various Fc-mediated functions are needed.

Immune imprinting is influenced by an individual's SARS-CoV-2 exposure history (top panel). This in turn influences the features of the antibody response against SARS-CoV-2, some of which have been well described, including neutralization breadth and potency, total binding antibody titers and epitopes targeted. Further work is required to dissect the impact of immune imprinting upon antibody subclass and isotype distribution and Fc effector functions.

Conclusions

Induction of durable protective antibody responses is critical for maintaining protection against respiratory viruses. Further improvements in our current vaccination strategies are needed to elicit both neutralizing and Fc-mediated responses against emerging viral variants. This, in turn, requires vaccination to induce the right balance of antibody isotypes and subclasses targeting both potently neutralizing as well as conserved epitopes to maximize protective antibody responses in both plasma and mucosal surfaces.

Induction of such responses, however, is an ongoing challenge, especially with the continuous emergence of immune-evasive variants. Fc effector functions, in addition to neutralizing antibodies, may enhance antibody-mediated protection but understanding their relative contribution to protection and the specific effector functions that best correlate with protection remains challenging due to the interconnected nature of antibody functions. Further work is needed to understand how different vaccine platform combinations (including those currently in preclinical development and clinical trials), hybrid immunity, immune imprinting, and host genetics influence antibody composition, quality, and functions. This, in turn, should be combined with clinical data to better determine which mechanisms correlate best with clinical outcomes. Altogether, improved understanding of the mechanisms behind effective antibody responses and how they relate to clinical outcomes may guide future strategies to improve the effectiveness of vaccination and mAb therapeutics against SARS-CoV-2 and other infectious diseases.
