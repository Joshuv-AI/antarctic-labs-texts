X-ray and γ-ray beam interstellar communication and implications for SETI
Authors: Lucas Beveridge, Gerrit Bruhaug
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe possibility of detecting artificial signals transmitted by alien civilizations via collimated X-ray or gamma-ray beams is investigated. The prospect of using such beams for human communication within the solar system and beyond is also discussed. Detector responses were simulated for input signals and analyzed using relative entropy. For simplicity, all signals were assumed to use on-off keying (OOK) modulation. “Real” signals were generated by taking digital files and sequentially feeding their raw binary data to the detector simulator, the resulting normalized information content of the detector signals was plotted and compared to random noise signals. Since jpeg files contain compressed information, these served as a proxy for artificial alien signals. This showed that there is a clear difference in measured information content between natural and artificial signals, even with relatively poor time resolution in the detector causing the signals to be smeared (dead-time/rise-time intervals many times longer than the duration between signal pulses). It was found that so long as the signal lasts for at least several rise-time/dead-time intervals, the distinction between random and artificial signals is obvious. A space-telescope with high time resolution for searching for such signals is briefly described and its basic requirements are outlined.Keywords: SETI, Interstellar communication, X-ray communication, Gamma-ray communicationSubject terms: Astronomy and planetary science, Physics

Introduction

The typical focus of SETI and other searches for artificial, interstellar signals has been in the radio/microwave regime1. Historically this has made excellent sense given the rise in radio astronomy and the creation of extremely sensitive detectors as well as powerful emitters in that wavelength band. More recently, the focus has begun to shift to infrared/optical searches2, inspired the rise of laser technology and the inherent advantages provided by optical sources over radio/microwave sources for long distance communications. Optical (or infrared) sources have much smaller wavelengths, which in turn translates to lower power and/or smaller focusing optics for the same distance as compared to radio/microwave sources. The higher bandwidths of optical sources also translate to higher data transfer rates, and the nature of the source radiation itself results in fewer natural sources of interference. The search for optical sources of artificial signals continues today3 in conjunction with the already ongoing radio/microwave searches4. These same arguments can in turn be extended to even smaller wavelengths, namely X-rays and gamma-rays. This paper will explore the potential benefits of X-ray or gamma-ray communications over interstellar distances and outline the data analysis needed to look for artificial signals in data sets from current or near future orbital X-ray and gamma-ray detectors. In recent decades various X-ray and gamma ray beam sources have been created, ranging from plasma-based X-ray lasers5 to inverse Compton based gamma ray beams6. Although these are primarily used for basic science, they raise the possibility that such a device could be used for communication through interplanetary or interstellar space. Plasma based X-ray lasers were first demonstrated in 19805 and have been shown to lase in wavelengths as short as 0.15 nm (8.27 keV photon energy)7, while being developed for applications as wide ranging as materials science and national defense6. Free electron X-ray lasers are now operating up to 24 keV of X-ray photon energy (~0.05 nm wavelength) and with many watts of X-ray power available in a widely tunable (250 eV – 24 keV) and high pulse rate (120 Hz – 929 kHz) source8. Future designs seek to combine these two X-ray laser technologies and achieve even higher X-ray laser powers9. The use of X-ray optics already allows for keV class X-ray to be focused to near diffraction limited spots8 and may allow for X-ray recycling cavities in the near future to further increase total laser quality and efficiency9. Reflective X-ray optics now reach up to 100 keV in photon energy ( ~0.012 nm wavelength)10, allowing for the potential of diffraction limited beams at even <0.01 nm wavelengths.

Gamma ray sources are more difficult to develop, but many decades of work have left firm theoretical ground for potential gamma ray lasers using either antimatter13 or excited nuclear states as the source material14,15. More near term work has shown the ability to generate bright gamma ray, although not coherent, beams using inverse Compton scattering of optical lasers by a highly relativistic electron beam6 and even 100’s of MeV gamma ray sources using particle channeling in crystal lattices16. Using these techniques, 10’s of mW class MeV gamma-ray beams are regularly generated today6. Future plans to utilize ultrahigh intensity lasers can potentially reach far higher gamma ray energies17,18, as well as generate better beams for communication use. Gamma ray optics also remain challenging19, but are an area of active research and there is hope for improved options in the future with Laue lenses20. In addition, even without the high-quality optics needed to reach diffraction limited spots, the extremely small wavelengths of gamma rays may still result in superior communication beams over other choices.

Although at first glance, one would assume that the generation of X-ray or gamma ray beams is too inefficient for use for communications, but this is not true even with today’s technologies. The greater than eight orders of magnitude difference in diffraction limited spot size over more conventional radio/microwave options can provide communication benefits even at extremely low photon generation efficiencies. Figure 2 shows that even a paltry 0.01% wall plug efficiency, the X-ray source still provides a superior power vs optic size as compared to the radio or optical sources. Considering that current XFEL’s have an electron beam to X-ray beam efficiency of ~0.1%23 and can achieve wall-plug to electron beam efficiencies of >80%24 with superconducting technology, efficiency of X-ray generation efficiency does not appear to be a concern. This provides a strong argument for using even current generation X-ray sources for communication applications. To further boost the efficiency, the electron beam can also be recycled at extremely high efficiency for both XFEL and ICS based sources. This is a technique has already been used for light sources with energy recovery exceeding 99% in practice25,26 and would presumably be used by any advanced civilization performing interstellar communication.

It should also be noted that X-ray and gamma-ray detectors are extremely sensitive, so there will not be large losses in total input power to detected signal. Single photon counting X-ray detectors are already in use at X-ray light sources today27. Detectors of similar capability are being actively developed for gamma-rays for nuclear imaging applications28. Even if the detectors induce an order of magnitude, or more, of signal loss though, the four orders of magnitude advantage over the 1 m laser, and many times larger advantage over 21 cm radio waves, will not be greatly eroded.

Although the km-scale size of current day XFELs would seem to be a constraint, one must consider that current day XFELs are not the limit of the technology. Advanced accelerator and undulator techniques are showing promise for XFELs on the <100 m scale29. Plasma based accelerators30 and/or laser-undulators31 may further shrink these systems to single digit meters. All these techniques and more could be assumed to be used by a civilization seeking to communicate at long distances in space. Inverse Compton scattering also opens the possibility for even more compact32,although less efficient, X-ray beam or gamma-ray generation options. At the single digit meter scales, one could even imagine X-ray or gamma-ray communications being integral to individual spacecraft. These results then prompt the question, what would an extraterrestrial X-ray or gamma-ray signal look like and could our current detectors discriminate it from the natural background?

Analysis

For simplicity we assume the modulation scheme of on-off keying (OOK) operating at a single photon energy. This means we are focusing on a single wavelength, and the transmission power is high enough that any signal is composed of numerous photons every second. Yet, the analysis we present can be adapted to various other modulation techniques. If each photon were capable of representing a single bit, the theoretical data rate would be extremely high. Over interstellar distances, however, reliable communication requires many photons per bit to compensate for propagation losses and ensure detection at the receiver.

Using on–off keying (OOK) at 414 keV, which corresponds to a prominent gamma-ray emission line of 239Pu and is rarely produced by natural sources, a transmitter could, in principle, achieve a maximum data rate of 3.467 × 1014 bit s⁻1 with a radiated power of 23 W at the source (equivalent to the Voyager probe high-gain antenna)33, assuming each photon (or its absence) encodes one bit (414keV = 6.633x10- 14 J). In practice, multiple photons must be used to represent each bit to maintain signal integrity over long distances.

It’s essential to note that these figures don’t account for error correction in the signal but give a basic idea of the minimal data rates. More efficient modulation techniques include Amplitude Modulation (AM), Frequency Modulation (FM), and Phase Modulation (PM), although an OOK signal can encompass all these methods34 for the sake of the analysis done in this paper. These techniques can be applied to the previously mentioned X-ray and gamma-ray sources, but with varying challenges. Signal modulation is easiest with free electron lasers8,9,35 and inverse-Compton sources6 due to the nature of their electron beam drivers. However, in principle, any source can be modified to handle these intricate modulations to some extent.

As this technology is developed and humans begin spreading across the solar system, it may become an appealing option for communication due to the greater bandwidth and lower power requirements available than radio or optical lasers over planetary distances. Studies have already begun on this subject and simple feasibility tests have been undertaken with X-ray tube sources36.

All of this implies that any civilization needing to communicate across interstellar distances may choose X-ray or gamma-ray beams due to low background noise, high efficiency, and high bandwidth. Searching for such extraterrestrial signals will be the focus of the remainder of this paper.

Searching for these signals would have the same issues as trying to detect incidental broadcasts, i.e., the presumed low probability of having a detector looking in the right place at the right time. It has been proposed previously that a civilization might use strong X-ray/gamma-ray sources (astrophysical sources either modulated for communication or as beacons, or as byproducts of technology such as interstellar propulsion)37-41 but it appears no one has considered intentional X-ray/gamma-ray communication beams or lasers. However, if there are many interstellar civilizations, they may be using an efficient communications scheme like this, making the odds of catching a beam passing the Earth more likely. A dedicated search may be warranted because sub-millisecond transients can be missed by default astronomy data products: many widely used imaging X-ray datasets are limited by CCD integration/frame times (e.g., 50 ms for eROSITA,3.2 s for Chandra/ACIS TE full-frame42,43), and even when γ-ray event data exist at microsecond precision (e.g., Fermi/GBM TTE at 2)44,45, routinely used continuous light-curve products are commonly binned at ≳0.064–4.096 s. As a result, sub-ms signals may be present but diluted unless event-level data and tailored pipelines are used.

Methods

This study aims to determine if it’s possible to identify artificial signals in the form of X-rays or gamma-rays, and potentially decode them. A detector’s response was simulated for both artificial signals (such as compressed binary files) and natural signals from random sources. The simulated spectra were created in time-steps equal to the detector’s rise-time/dead-time interval, during which multiple pulses could accumulate. Two individual spectra for 10,000 accumulated counts at 100keV (a binary 1), and a background spectrum (no signal) are illustrated in Figure 3. In this work, a ‘spectrum’ refers to a pulse-height histogram accumulated over a single detector integration interval. A sequence of such spectra therefore forms a time series analogous to a light curve, but retains energy-resolved information. Simulations were performed at 100 keV to reflect practical present-day X-ray sources, while the 414 keV γ-ray line is used as a conceptual example of an unambiguously artificial signal. The entropy-based analysis depends on the temporal modulation of the signal and is largely insensitive to the absolute photon energy. Both spectra were simulated using the same number of particle histories. The signal spectrum appears lower in amplitude across most energy bins because normalization redistributes probability mass toward the dominant 100 keV peak; the background spectrum, lacking such a peak, has its counts spread more uniformly and thus appears relatively higher in the off-peak bins.

Simulated pulse-height spectra for a signal interval (10,000 counts at 100 keV, blue) and a background-only interval (red), generated using the same number of particle histories. The difference in amplitude is a consequence of normalization: the dominant 100 keV peak in the signal spectrum suppresses the relative counts in all other bins.

The amplitude of the resulting spectral line indicates the total number of “ON” bits (since “OFF” bits represent the absence of counts) entering the detector during the interval. These signals are 1-D arrays of 1s and 0s, which could be random or represent binary data content, such as a JPEG image. This was simulated using a pulse-height tally in a NaI scintillator crystal using the radiation transport code OpenMC46. In OpenMC (or any radiation transport code), a tally is the mechanism for estimating physical quantities of interest by statistically accumulating results from many particle histories—such as reaction rates, flux, or energy deposition—over specified regions, materials, or energy ranges. Conceptually, it is a Monte Carlo estimator that maps stochastic particle interactions into deterministic, physically meaningful observables with associated uncertainties. A pulse-height tally records the distribution of energy deposited in a detector or material per particle event, producing a spectrum that directly corresponds to what a real radiation detector would measure rather than an integrated reaction rate.

Shannon Entropy is higher for signals containing information, which are not random and have greater surprisal. This study used Relative Entropy (or KL-divergence)47 to compute the a normalized information content. Relative entropy measures the difference in entropy between a signal and a reference signal, amplifying the differences when using the same reference signal for both cases. Normalized information content, M, is computed by scaling the Kullback–Leibler (KL)48 divergence of a measured signal, Km, relative to two reference cases: a constant (empty) signal, K0, and a maximal distribution, Kmax. In theory, ideal signals without noise would yield K0 = ∞ and Kmax = 0, but measurement noise and finite data lengths make both finite, requiring noise-adjusted definitions of K0 and Kmax. These are computed by simulating constant and maximal signals with the same number of samples, precision, and range as the measured data, and then evaluating KL divergence either in the time domain (via kernel density estimation) or frequency domain (via folded, normalized discrete fourier transforms). The information content of the measured signal is expressed as ΔS = Km − K0, normalized by ΔSmax = Kmax − K0, giving M = (Km − K0)/(Kmax − K0) on a [0,1] scale. A more detailed explanation can be found in49. The value M was computed for each detector energy bin across time intervals. In other words, each spectrum is one time-step.

The detector resolution was not arbitrary since it is comparable to sodium-iodide detectors, but it makes little difference in the analysis. Scintillator detectors can be fabricated in much larger sizes which increase detector efficiency, and thus makes them more useful in detecting potential alien signals that have a high data rate and low intensity, even though they have much lower energy resolution than high-purity germanium detectors.

Results and interpretation

To determine if a signal has any real information content, the entropy must be computed for a sequence of spectra. However, this requires that they be 1-dimensional. There are a number of ways to accomplish this, however it was decided to compute normalized information content for the energy bins of the signals, taking each spectrum at each detector interval as a time-step. Routines were written to import files and convert them to 1-D binary arrays, generate the simulated spectra for a given input array (could also be a random binary array) in Python. The analysis was done using the SciPy statistics toolbox50. For each artificial (“real”) signal, a random signal of the same length was also generated,both had their normalized information content computed and compared. Figure 4 shows a typical result for a jpeg file, with a photon energy of 100 keV.

Normalized information content M as a function of detector energy bin, for an artificial (jpeg-encoded) signal and a random signal of equal length. M is computed from the time series of counts in each bin across all detector intervals. The artificial signal shows elevated M near 100 keV, where the signal photons are concentrated.

The difference in normalized information content is useful, although it can’t be known in practice what the signal pulse duration is, and so further analysis would need to be done comparing the detected signal to simulated random noise signals of varying pulse durations.

For instance, if this analysis was conducted on radar, GPS, and TV broadcast signals, the information content of each would differ substantially based on the signal complexity, even though all would appear as narrowband radio signals, and GPS and TV broadcast signals would clearly show high information content, while radar would not.

The detector produces a sequence of spectra — one per time interval — where each spectrum is a pulse-height histogram over energy bins. M is then computed for each energy bin by treating the sequence of counts in that bin across all time intervals as a 1-D signal. Thus, Figure 4's x-axis is photon energy: each point on the curve represents the normalized information content M of the time-varying count rate in that energy bin. The artificial signal concentrates photons near 100 keV, so M peaks there; bins outside this range contain only background and show low M, consistent with a random signal. The modulation scheme does not need to be binary, as in on–off keying; any finite amplitude encoding will still be distinguishable in the analysis, regardless of the numerical base. An FM-like modulation would also be detectable, provided that enough photons are collected during each detector interval to maintain a reasonable signal-to-noise ratio. More complex modulation schemes, such as polarization or phase-based encoding, may require further detector development to be reliably identified.

It may be possible to search archival X-ray or gamma-ray telescope data to find artificial signals hiding within them. In particular, treaty-enforcement spacecraft such as the Vela series might be a good place look since those spacecraft were designed to observe the “double-flash” of an atmospheric nuclear detonation, which only lasts for about 1 ms, and thus these satellites had adequate time resolution for signal detection and observed a wider portion of the sky than a telescope51. Other instruments on past or present missions may also have the time resolution required52. It’s assumed that they should be able to detect at least 1,000 distinct pulses per second so that the received signal is broken into enough pieces to show a high relative entropy or information content.

The probability of detecting an incidental signal may be negligibly low due to the narrow beams (unless there are at least trillions of transmitters), however if this is indeed a common means of communication, other civilizations may be making extensive use of it, perhaps even contacting any planet that appears to have intelligent life on it. If this is the case, then we are far more likely to detect a beam, since at least one would be intended for us. If there are 2,500 civilizations in the galaxy, and each one was beaming signals at 1 keV to any planet that shows signs of intelligent life (assumed here to be equivalent to mammals), and the lifetime of such intelligence is 100 million years then we would expect at any given time to see around 25 beams. Of course, this estimate has quite a large uncertainty associated with it, but can be used to provide potential upper and lower bounds on the probabilities. This assumes there are 250 to 25,000 transmitting civilizations, and 125,000 to 1.25M worlds with signs of intelligence (such as mammals).

The assumption that every civilization is transmitting to every potentially habitable world is likely too optimistic, but even if each civilization was only transmitting to every other civilization, we might expect to see multiple beams per year. In any case, this should be a compelling type of signal to search for, but with the understanding that any signal may not repeat, which necessitates analysis that can identify an artificial signal without repetition.

Discussion

For a signal transmitted at 1 TW and 1,000 light-years distant, with a 10 cm beam waist, there is roughly 5.9E8 photons/second/m2 received at 414 keV. With the simple OOK modulation assumed in this paper, it is perhaps not feasible to detect discernable signals with a conventionally sized detector. So, at least a 1-meter diameter detector should be used. Plastic scintillators (surrounded by silicon photomultipliers) can be readily manufactured in this size and are not prohibitively massive (<200 kg for a cylinder 1 m diameter and 0.25 m long). Although plastic scintillators tend to have poorer energy resolution, their decay time is much faster (<1 µs)53. Such detectors could be installed on a single modest-sized spacecraft of no more than 300 kg total. To minimize the cost of such a mission, a single scintillator on an ESPA class spacecraft could be launched on a rideshare launch for about $1.95M (as of August 2025)54.

Conclusions

Free-electron lasers and other currently available X-ray and gamma ray sources raise the possibility of ultra-short wavelength high-bandwidth communication over enormous distances through space, including and especially between star-systems. In addition to the obvious practical applications for communication between space settlements within our own solar system, there is the possibility that civilizations only slightly more advanced than our own might be using it to talk to their own planetary or interstellar settlements, or other advanced civilizations. This could provide an answer to the Fermi Paradox, since such signals are a far more efficient method of communication than radio or optical lasers, but have been ignored in previous SETI searches. Although the hypothetical beams are narrow, and the odds of incidentally detecting any one beam may be small, if this method is widely used by other civilizations (and if there are indeed many other civilizations), then it may be likely that we can detect such a beam.

Using normalized information content to analyze the spectra from a simulated X-ray/gamma-ray detector, it is possible to clearly discern an artificial signal from a natural source (random signals). It may not be possible with existing technology to actually decode an artificial signal, but information theory provides the means to prove that a signal is artificial. Previous observatories lacked both the detector efficiency and analysis products required to detect such a signal, but building one is quite achievable with moderately sized (~1 m diameter) high-speed scintillator detectors. A spacecraft with adequate detector(s) could likely be launched within a small-satellite form factor.
