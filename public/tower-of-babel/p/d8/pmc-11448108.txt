Progress and scenario-based projections of health service availability and coverage towards UHC in the post-conflict and post-pandemic Iraq: a Bayesian hierarchical regression approach
Authors: Hiroko Taniguchi, Md Mizanur Rahman, Ashraf Hussain, Shuhei Nomura, Ganan Devanathan, Masahiro Hashizume
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractBackgroundTwo decades have passed since the beginning of the Iraq War in 2003. Iraq has long suffered from conflicts and instability, where the people have limited access to healthcare. The coronavirus disease (COVID-19) pandemic brought additional disruption to health service provision.ObjectivesAt the midpoint towards universal health coverage (UHC) in 2030, this study aims to gain a better understanding of the trends of UHC progress in Iraq in the context of the conflicts and the COVID-19 pandemic and to indicate possible pragmatic options.DesignThis study employed Bayesian hierarchical regression models to estimate trends and projections of health service availability and coverage indicators up to 2030. Furthermore, for health service coverage, four scenarios were defined based on the availability of health services, and projections were made for each scenario up to 2030.SettingOur approach used the yearly data from the Ministry of Health and four nationally representative household surveys between 2000 and 2020. We evaluated the subnational-level progress in three health service availability indicators and 13 health service coverage indicators in 18 governorates in Iraq from 2000 to 2030.ResultsThe findings from 2000 to 2020 revealed a lack of progress in the indicators of health facility and inpatient bed, and pronounced detrimental effects from major conflicts and the pandemic on all measured health service coverage indicators. Despite these setbacks, several health service coverage indicators demonstrated resilience and elasticity in their recovery. The projected trends for 2021 to 2030 indicated limited alternations in the health service availability. By 2030, five health service coverage indicators will achieve the designated 80% targets. A scenario-based analysis predicts improved coverage of antenatal care, and child immunisation and treatment if health service availability is bolstered to globally recommended standards. Under this scenario, several governorates—Anbar, Baghdad, Nainawa, Qadissiyah, Salahaddin, Thiqar and Wasit—presented improved health service coverage in more indicators.ConclusionStrengthened health service availability has the potential to significantly improve fragile health service coverage indicators and in more vulnerable governorates.Keywords: health policy, health services accessibility, primary health care, accident & emergency medicine, health workforce, public health

Introduction

It is now over 20 years since the coalition forces invaded in Iraq in 2003. Iraq has long suffered from wars and conflicts, sanctioned regimes and successive political upheavals with varying politics, sectarianism, ethnic issues and resource races. The country declared the end of the latest conflict, the battle against the Islamic State (IS), in December 2017 and around five million people returned home from April 2015 to December 2022. However, the social and political instability continues, and 1.2 million people were still internally displaced (as of December 2022).1 Online supplemental S-figure 2 in appendix section 2 shows the timeline of the major violent events in Iraq in 2000–2020 together with the numbers of internally displaced people (IDPs) and conflict deaths (due to state-based conflict, non-state conflict and one-sided violence). During the Iraq War, the number of conflict deaths had a sudden rise with over 8000 in 2003, followed by the resurges of conflict deaths in 2007–2008 and the constant increase in the number of IDPs from 2005 to 2008. At the height of the Battle against IS, the scale of conflict deaths was much bigger than the Iraq War with over 10 000 annual deaths in 2013–2017. The number of IDPs rapidly increased from 2013 to 2015; it took 5 years to approach the prebattle level. In these situations, people faced risks of death and disability not only because of destructive violence but also due to heavily limited access to essential healthcare.

The year 2023 is also at the midpoint towards 2030, the target year of the Sustainable Development Goals (SDGs). Universal health coverage (UHC), SDG Target 3.8, is to achieve at least 80% essential health service coverage for the entire population of the country irrespective of their economic status, gender or place of residence and 100% protection from catastrophic and impoverishing health payments for healthcare by 2030.2 3 The Iraqi Ministry of Health Iraq (MoH) developed the National Health Policy 2014–2023, where UHC and secured provision of Essential Package of Health Services (EPHS) were its core elements.4 Iraq has, however, faced challenges to achieve UHC. For example, although MoH has been a main provider of healthcare in both treatment and prevention, public health facilities are not equitably distributed across governorates and between rural and urban populations.4 The health system in Iraq has been capital intensive, hospital oriented and treatment focused.4 5 Around half of the primary healthcare centres have only one doctor, while nurses and other health workers manage the rest.6 Shortage of trained physicians and brain drain from Iraq have been major barriers towards equitable quality care.7 In the long-lasting fragile situations, the Health Cluster, an interorganisational health partnership in humanitarian emergencies, has worked on the Health Emergencies Programme and the expansion of EPHS together with Iraq MoH and other actors to fill in humanitarian needs and secure the progress towards UHC.8,10

On the fragile health system in Iraq, the pandemic of the coronavirus disease (COVID-19) hit the country. Among healthcare workers in Iraq, 47 641 COVID-19 cases and 349 deaths were confirmed as of 20 October 2022, primarily due to a lack of adequate infection prevention and control.11 12 Moreover, the healthcare workers in Iraq faced additionally heightened tensions in already violent health workplaces since the pandemic started.13 In the global pulse survey on continuity of essential health services during the pandemic in November–December 2021, Iraq reported that all services in immunisation, some services in sexual and reproductive health, maternal, newborn, child and adolescent health, communicable diseases, mental health and care for older people were still disrupted.14

In the post-conflict and post-COVID-19 era and towards 2030, it is vital for policymakers in Iraq and their partners to understand the progress of UHC, implement better strategies and strengthen the targeted health areas. To date, a number of studies on humanitarian needs and country-level UHC monitoring reports of Iraq have been developed.15,17 Country-level data could, however, mask differences and inequity at the subnational level, although subnational variation might be as wide as between-country variations.18 19 Nonetheless, no previous studies were identified which assessed the progress of Iraq towards UHC considering the key aspects of health service availability, health service coverage, conflicts and the COVID-19 pandemic at the subnational level.

This study aims to gain a better understanding of the trends of UHC progress in Iraq in the context of the conflicts and the COVID-19 pandemic and to indicate possible pragmatic options towards the post-COVID-19 era and the final phase to 2030. Accordingly, we formulated two hypotheses. Hypothesis 1: The trend of UHC progress in Iraq, namely health service availability and coverage at the subnational level, will show no changes in 2000–2020 despite the series of conflicts, political and social turmoil and COVID-19 pandemic. Hypothesis 2: The projections of health service coverage at the subnational level in Iraq will show no changes in 2021–2030 even if health service availability were strengthened. To achieve the study aims and test the hypotheses, we set three objectives. The first and second are to estimate trends and make projections in three health service availability indicators and 13 health service coverage indicators at the national and subnational levels in Iraq in 2000–2030, adjusting by the latest socioeconomic data. The last is to project health service coverage by modelling the degrees of health service availability strengthening up to 2030 in the scenario-based analysis. We presented the diagram on how to achieve the study aims in online supplemental S-figure 2 in appendix section 3. As the global community has faced an unprecedented pandemic, and also devastating armed conflicts, this study can indicate how to be resilient towards the achievement of UHC to Iraq and other countries.

Materials and methods

Data sources

For the estimation of the trends and projections of health service availability indicators in Iraq, we collected and used the governorate-level annual data of numbers of health facility of hospitals and primary healthcare centres, inpatient bed and core health workforce of physicians, nurses and midwives from the Iraqi MoH in 2000–2020 as a result of the examination of WHO Service Availability and Readiness Assessment (SARA), literature review and data availability validation.20 SARA is a standard tool for an annual monitoring system for health service availability and has the service availability indicators in three areas: health infrastructure, core health workforce and service utilisation.21 The detailed SARA indicators are described in online supplemental appendix section 4.

To estimate trends and projections of health service coverage indicators, we used the data from nationally representative cross-sectional household surveys in Multiple Indicator Cluster Surveys Iraq in 2000, 2006, 2011 and 2018.22 The surveys used a multistage cluster sampling design and high response rates. The survey descriptions are presented in online supplemental appendix section 5. Using the survey data, we made proportional data and then used them for the estimates and projections.

Measurement of UHC: health service availability and coverage indicators

Using SARA, we chose three indicators for health service availability. Based on the collected data, we first computed the density of health facilities, inpatient beds and core health workforce per 10 000 population at the subnational level. Next, we followed the SARA method to calculate the percentage score of health facility, inpatient bed and core health workforce.21 These three percentage scores were used as health service availability indicators in the present study. Table 1 shows the benchmark and the computation of each indicator. In the process of examination of health service availability indicators, we considered the association between each of three health service availability indicators and each of 13 health service coverage indicators. For example, each indicator from health service availability and coverage are not necessarily directly related to each other, such as inpatient bed and diphtheria-tetanus-pertussis (DTP3). Nonetheless, based on SARA, we selected the above three indicators to overview the trends and projections of health service availability as a whole.23 24

Following the UHC monitoring framework and selection criteria jointly developed by WHO and the World Bank and examining data availability, we chose 13 indicators including reproductive, maternal, newborn and child health indicators as well as infectious diseases indicators for the analysis of health service coverage in Iraq (table 1).15 Like health service availability indicators, these indicators and interventions benefit every individual in every country, regardless of the country’s socioeconomic development level, epidemiological circumstances and the type of health system it may have.15 These indicators are also in line with EPHS for Iraq, which covers comprehensive service components including reproductive, maternal and child health, child immunisation and water and sanitation against infectious diseases.10

Predictor variables for the analysis of health service availability and coverage

This study sought to reflect the associations between population movement, economics, severity of conflict and both health service availability and health service coverage in assessing UHC trends and projections in Iraq towards 2030. The timeframe of 2000–2030 covers the period of the COVID-19 pandemic. The study also sought to reflect the associations between health service availability and health service coverage. By assessing possible variables and their data availability, we selected six predictor variables: the subnational-level population density, national-level gross domestic product (GDP) per capita, subnational-level crude death rate (CDR) of conflict death and indicators of health facility, inpatient bed and core health workforce. The summary of data characteristics, as well as the detailed data sources and the computations of predictor variables, is described in online supplemental appendix sections 6–9. For the scenario-based analysis of the trends and projections of health service coverage, we additionally used annual subnational-level scores of health facility, inpatient bed and core health workforce in 2000–2030 as predictor variables. Where data for a given year in 2000–2020 was not available, data from the immediately following year was used as a proxy.

Statistical analysis

In this study, three steps were performed in the data analysis. First, we developed a Bayesian hierarchical or multilevel regression model to predict a covariate, namely population density, up to 2030 to be used in the trend and projection analysis of health service availability and coverage trends. Second, the Bayesian hierarchical regression model was applied to estimate the trends and projections of three health service availability indicators up to 2030. The covariates included in the projection model were the year, national-level GDP per capita, governorate-level population density and governorate-level CDR of conflict death. We employed the Bayesian approach in our study due to its two major advantages. One is that it addresses the issues of limited data points across all provenances, and therefore is used and favoured when aiming to project probabilities. Another is that it also has the ability to combine prior knowledge about parameters with the evidence from data and are favoured for the analysis of hierarchical models.25 The full description of the appropriateness of using the Bayesian approach for this study is described in online supplemental appendix section 10. Each equation of the governorate-level Bayesian models for a covariate and health service availability indicators, the model validity examination and prior sensitivity analysis are presented in online supplemental appendix section 11.1 and 11.2. Third, to assess the association between health service availability and health service coverage and to simulate the progress of health service coverage by options, we set four types of models for the projections of 13 health service coverage indicators at the subnational level in 2021–2030. In all four scenarios, we used the observed scores of health service availability (health facility, inpatient bed and core health workforce) for 2000–2020. For 2021–2030, we used the projected scores of health service availability in scenario 0 and the adjusted scores in scenarios 1, 2 and 3.

Patient and public involvement

Patients or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of our research.

Results

Health service availability: trends and projections at the subnational level

Online supplemental S-figure 3 in appendix section 13 and figure 1 show the trends and projections of the national-level and subnational-level three health service availability indicators in 2000–2030. Against the hypothesis 1, the study found perceptible changes in these trends and projections. In 2000–2020, however, the score of health facility overall did not see major changes except in Duhok, Erbil, Muthana and Thiqar. For example, Duhok maintained full scores until 2006 but decreased to 40% in 2007–2008 and stayed at 60%–70% after 2009. The lowest scores of health facility were found in Baghdad, Karbala and Wasit. Inpatient beds presented various score levels among the governorates in 2000–2020. Baghdad, Basrah, Erbil, Muthana and Sulaimaniya presented higher scores, around 60%, while Diala and Thiqar stayed at 20%–40% scores. Baghdad also marked over 70% in 2000–2001, however, it declined to 50% score in 2005 and did not increase to the level before 2001. In core health workforce, two-thirds of the governorates experienced more frequent fluctuations in 2000–2020 than health facility and inpatient bed. More than two-thirds of the governorates faced severe declines of over 20% in their score; however, they achieved sound recoveries by 2019.

This study found that Iraq observed three major trends in three phases over the past three decades (2000–2030) at both national and subnational levels: the Iraq War period (2003–2011) and post-War (2012–2013) (phase I); the battle against IS period (2013–2017) and post conflict (2018–2020) (phase II); and the COVID-19 pandemic and following period (2020–2030) (phase III). The country experienced significant declines in all health service coverage indicators in phases I and II, with a wide variation in the magnitudes of the major events’ impact, timing and recovery duration and degree. These varied between governorates as well as between health service coverage indicators. Moreover, Iraq encountered the COVID-19 pandemic before recovering health service coverage to the level of the preconflict levels. Despite the challenges, all governorates are projected to achieve 80% coverage by 2030 in institutional delivery, skilled birth attendance, Bacille Calmette-Guérin (BCG), improved water sources and adequate sanitation.

Among the indicators, at least four antenatal care visits (ANC4) in Babil, Diala, Karbala, Nainawa, Qadissiyah, Salahaddin, Thiqar and Wasit will maintain an upward trend in phase III in scenario 0. This group shared the same experience of sharp decreases but greater recovery in phase I and/or quick recovery in late phase II. Meanwhile, Anbar showed significant fall by nearly 90% in phases I and II and recovery by 60% in late phase II; however, it will follow a sharp downturn in phase III. In the beginning of phase III, Qadissiyah and Salahaddin showed roughly 10% decreases in ANC4, while the other governorates did not present major decreases.

DTP3 presented that half of the governorates (Duhok, Karbala, Kirkuk, Missan, Muthana, Nainawa, Najaf, Qadissiyah and Salahaddin) experienced large coverage declines in phases I and II and 2020. Other large declines were also found in DTP3 in Salahaddin in 2014–2015. The governorates that showed great recoveries in late phase I beyond predeterioration levels will keep upward trends towards 2030 regardless of additional decreases in phase II. Meanwhile, Baghdad, Basrah, Diala and Erbil had limited improvement in late phase I and will remain downwards towards 2030.

Oral rehydration therapy (ORT) presented overall improving trends in all governorates through phases I–III. For example, although Nainawa faced a series of coverage decreases in 2008, 2015 and 2018, it will present sound improvement towards 2030. Duhok, Kirkuk, Missan and Muthana, however, faced overall decreased trends in phase I and more serious fluctuations in 2000–2020. They showed tentative recoveries in 2015–2016; however, they will keep declining or stagnant towards 2030.

Scenario-based projections of health service coverage indicators in phase III

Overall, scenario 3 (ambitious scenario) brought higher coverage than other scenarios in ANC4, measles, DTP3, full immunisation, ORT and acute respiratory infection treatment for pneumonia (ARI treatment). It will bring the most benefit to ANC4 and measles in 14 and 13 governorates, respectively. In scenario 3, ANC4 coverage will reach 80% in Babil, Muthana, Qadissiyah and Wasit in 2030 and will be closer to 80% in Baghdad, Basrah and Nainawa, along with three governorates (Karbala, Salahaddin and Thiqar), which will achieve it in scenario 0. Scenario 3 will also improve the coverage in DTP3 and full immunisation in 13 and 12 governorates, respectively. In scenario 3 of DTP3, four governorates (Baghdad, Nainawa, Salahaddin and Qadissiyah) will achieve 80% coverage by 2030, together with six governorates in scenario 0. Scenario 3 will also see improved trends in two-thirds of governorates in ORT and ARI treatment. In scenario 3 of ORT, four governorates (Baghdad, Basrah, Erbil and Nainawa) will reach 80% coverage by 2030, in addition to two governorates in scenario 0.

Among the governorates, the more benefitted where more indicators will have largely increased coverage in scenario 3 will be Anbar, Baghdad, Nainawa, Qadissiyah, Salahaddin, Thiqar and Wasit. In Erbil, scenario 2 presented the highest coverage in 2030 in ANC4, measles, DTP3, full immunisation, ORT and ARI treatment. This may be due to Erbil having marked a high score of health service availability in 2020; therefore, continued improvement in scenario 2 may have a larger impact on health service coverage than scenario 3.

We examined the prior sensitivity of our results by altering the priors for the hyperparameters. The results exhibited no significant variation. We presented three tables as the results of prior sensitivity analyses of ANC4 (scenario 2), DTP3 (scenario 3) and improved water sources (scenario 1) as examples in online supplemental appendix section 19.

Discussion

This is the first attempt to assess the elaborate trends and projections of UHC indicators of health service availability and health service coverage in Iraq, at the national and subnational levels in 2000–2030. The trend analysis in 2000–2020 revealed stagnant trends in health facility and inpatient bed, as well as significant trend changes in all health service coverage indicators, which were negatively impacted by the major conflicts and the pandemic. The projections using Bayesian hierarchical regression model present few changes in health service availability, and 80% achievement by 2030 in five health service coverage indicators. The scenario-based analysis indicates that antenatal care, and child immunisation and treatment will benefit from health service availability strengthened to the level of global recommendation. In this ambitious scenario, Anbar, Baghdad, Nainawa, Qadissiyah, Salahaddin, Thiqar and Wasit will have improved health service coverage in more indicators.

Impact and recovery from the major health crises

Health service availability

This study found that the scores of health facility and inpatient bed showed limited changes in 2000–2030, while core health workforce reached the full score in all governorates by 2020, although most governorates experienced violent fluctuations in core health workforce in phases I and II. However, even when there was little change in health facility and inpatient bed scores, the reduced core health workforce may have affected service availability, functionality and outputs as well as workload and risks on the remaining health workers.16 23 26 In fact, the achievement of 100% score in core health workforce was due to an increase in the number of paramedical workforce (nurses and midwives) (online supplemental appendix section 14). The density increases of paramedical workforce were in line with the surge of conflicts. After 2003, responding to the growing demand of health service and shortage of health workers, Iraq adopted a nursing training strategy to train nurses to earn a diploma or college degree with a considerable expansion in the number of institutes of paramedical specialties in the public and private health sectors.27

In contrast, health facility indicator remained without major changes in all governorates through all phases except Duhok. Duhok has been prone to violence-related consequences, IDPs and refugees.28 This situation has prevented Duhok from social and economic development, which could be supported by the previous study where Duhok faced worsened catastrophic health expenditure and impoverishment.29 Nevertheless, three governorates in Kurdistan region, a wealthier region in Iraq, presented higher health facility scores than the others (regarding Duhok, it was until 2020) as the previous study indicated.30 31 The rapid decrease in Duhok from 2021 may be due to projected increased population density.

Inpatient bed indicator presented various levels of scores between the governorates among three health service availability indicators and more fluctuations than health facility. It may show inequitable access to care between the governorates and difficulty maintaining health services in unstable settings. The trends of inpatient bed could also indicate that both conflict-affected and underdeveloped areas suffered from shortage of healthcare. For example, Diala, which was heavily affected by both the Iraq War and the battle against IS, and Thiqar, which had a less direct impact from the conflicts but has been underdeveloped, present lower coverage. Furthermore, inpatient bed scores are higher than health facility scores in many governorates, except Kurdistan governorate. It may prove the hospital-oriented situation in Iraq. Baghdad marked a lower score in health facility but a higher score in inpatient bed. This could reaffirm the capital-intensive, hospital-oriented and treatment-focused health system in Iraq.4 32

Health service coverage

Phase I

Each governorate showed sharp but varying magnitudes of decline in the specific years across the indicators. For example, the declines in 2003 or 2004 are common in almost all governorates, when the death toll from the conflict soared and GDP per capita drastically dropped. The year-specific sharp declines were also observed in Diala in 2007, in Nainawa in 2004 and 2007 and in Salahaddin in 2003, 2005, 2008 and 2011. This trend is in line with the CDR trend of conflict death. Institutional delivery presented smaller decreases than the others. This may be due to the prioritisation by Iraq and its partners, as well as the nature of maternal delivery care as the last gatekeeper to save the lives of mothers and neonates.10 33 ANC4 showed continued decline or stagnant coverage. Unavoidable deprioritisation and/or abandonment might happen under the insecure situations, in the temporal shelters and/or on the move.34

Phase II

The major decline in ANC4 emerged in 2013–2014 and the larger-scale decline occurred in family planning needs satisfied (FPNS) in 2015–2016. Although FPNS had recovered to the pre-Iraq War level until 2012–2013 and contraceptive supply might have been sustained until the early phase II at home, pharmacies and/or private doctors, it might yet have been disturbed by the protracted insecurity in the following years.35 36 In the longer term, ANC4 presented swift tenacious recoveries in 2015–2020 in less conflict-affected areas. The overall worsened trend in ARI treatment in phase I was exaggerated in phase II. The improving trends were shattered in both battle-affected governorates (Anbar, Baghdad, Diala and Duhok) and socioeconomically vulnerable governorates (Muthana, Qadissiyah and Thiqar). Unlike others, Salahaddin, which had the highest CDR of conflict death in phase II, showed the same level of degradation in BCG, measles and DTP3 as reproductive and maternal healthcare.

Phase III

The decreases in health service coverage in phase III of COVID-19 pandemic were relatively small compared with the severe declines in phases I and II. However, decreases of over 20% were observed in FPNS in almost all governorates. This may trigger its continued deterioration in phase III. A similar degree of declines was also observed in measles in Kirkuk, Missan, Muthana and Najaf, in Polio3 in Duhok, Kirkuk, Missan, Muthana and Najaf, in DTP3 in Missan and Najaf and in ARI treatment in Anbar, Duhok and Kirkuk. Note that the declines in the study may be minimal because we hypothetically applied the same CDR of conflict death in 2020 to 2021–2030 and did not apply possible worsened health service availability in 2021–2022, which might be contradictory to the report by Iraq and their partners.37

In terms of the recovery from the major health crises, the present study identified two points. The first is the level of recovery. When the recovery level in phase I went beyond the level of predeterioration, it could lead to an improving trend after 2021 despite following multiple fluctuations in phase II and in 2020. In the recovery operations, Iraq and its partners targeted water and sanitation, maternal delivery care and childhood immunisations, including the continued Expanded Program on Immunisation and replacement of trivalent oral polio vaccine to bivalent oral polio vaccine.38,40 It would have brought resiliency and elasticity against a forthcoming series of deteriorations. This elasticity was found in more governorates in measles, Polio3, DTP3 and full immunisation. The second point is the time-span between the health emergencies. The short time between successive emergencies may hinder recovery from a prior emergency and make the social and health system even more vulnerable.41 42 FPNS (all governorates except Babil) and ARI treatment (all governorates except Anbar) in phases II and III could be from these cases.

Options for the future towards UHC

Overall, ANC4 had most benefit from strengthened health service availability in scenario 3 showing the increased coverage in 14 governorates. Scenario 3 brought highest health service coverage to 11 governorates in ANC4, 12 governorates in measles, 10 governorates in DTP3, 8 governorates in both full immunisation and ORT and 7 governorates in ARI treatment. This could support previous findings showing that improvement in health service availability could promote sound progress in health service coverage.23 24 43 44

In the scenario-based analysis, Anbar, Baghdad, Nainawa, Qadissiyah, Salahaddin, Thiqar and Wasit will have a greater benefit from strengthened health service availability. A much larger degree of recovery was found in the directly conflict-affected governorates (Salahaddin, Anbar, Nainawa and Baghdad). However, governorates not directly affected by conflict (Thiqar, Qadissiyah and Wasit) would have large benefits from increased health service availability in ANC4, measles, DTP3 and full immunisation. Strengthened health service availability, however, did not statistically improve the declining or stagnant trends in FPNS and ARI treatment in most governorates. The coverage in these two indicators in 2020 was the lowest in the past 20 years in almost all governorates. Nonetheless, women’s needs remain and lower respiratory infections has stayed as the 12th leading cause of deaths in Iraq; therefore, a radical policy review may be critical to stop further deterioration.45 The previous study of 183 countries indicated that FPNS was the most dominant intervention area to aim for UHC in upper-middle income countries.23

Proactive investment in the health system has not been seen in Iraq; Domestic General Government Health Expenditure in Iraq is 1.7 in 2017 against, for example, 12.0 in Palestine, 3.86 in Pakistan and 2.1 in Afghanistan.46 The national-level inpatient beds per 10 000 are 12.0 in Iraq in 2020 against 32.0 in Libya and 13.9 in Syria.46 Although the paramedical workforce has been strengthened in Iraq, the exodus of Iraqi doctors abroad continues and its density, 9.3 in 2020, remains low.47 It is lower than 20.0 in Libya, 15.5 in Palestine, 11.0 in Pakistan in 2017. To promote the UHC progress in Iraq, the country’s regulated investment on health could be a strong driver. For example, under its Universal Health Insurance Law issued in 2018, Egypt has secured national funds to achieve UHC and aimed the standardised quality—hard and soft—in public health facilities, along with health financing reforms.48 49 Türkiye introduced health transformation plan, which enhanced equity and improved the level and distribution of health services.50 In Iraq, reconstructions of health facilities continues with support from its partners, which should be maintained despite social instabilities and the pandemic.51 52 Strengthened health workforce density would be also critical, overcoming various challenges in post-conflict settings including their safety, number of doctors and equitable distribution.13 23 53 54 It is also vital to continue to monitor these investments and their impacts on the UHC progress. Due to the unprecedented pandemic and unexpected armed conflicts, the global community is under pressure to improve their health systems. Iraq as well as other countries could learn from others’ attempts to be resilient together towards the achievement of UHC.

Strengths and limitations

This study has several strengths. First, this is the first study which assessed the elaborate trends and projections of health service availability and coverage in Iraq at the subnational levels. Second, this study used a large number of population-based, household health survey data for health service coverage analysis. Third, this study applied the annual official data of health service availability, the data of number of conflict deaths, which is widely used as the armed-conflict data source and the population density in each governorate, which include data in 2020 after the COVID-19 pandemic started. Finally, this study developed a Bayesian hierarchical regression model to estimate the trends and projections by considering the hierarchy of the data set. However, this study has limitations. First, all results in this study were based on present trends and hypothetical estimations for future consideration. Second, this study was not able to make analyses of non-communicable diseases and financial risk protection indicators due to scarce data. Third, this study did not include the assessment of functionality, performance and quality of health service as well as physical access to health facilities. This study neither shows the direct impact of health service availability and coverage on health, nor their changes. Despite these limitations, this study provides a better understanding of the country’s progress across the periods of major health crises, and possible options towards enhancing health service availability and coverage.

Conclusion

The impact of two major conflicts and the COVID-19 pandemic were significant in all health service coverage indicators in almost all governorates. Despite these challenges, several health service coverage indicators have shown resilience and elasticity across the country. Comprehensive and urgent investment to support health service availability will be needed for the improvement of both health service availability and coverage. Strengthened health service availability may bring significant improvement in fragile health service coverage indicators and more vulnerable governorates. This would be of great use to Iraq in its progress towards UHC.
