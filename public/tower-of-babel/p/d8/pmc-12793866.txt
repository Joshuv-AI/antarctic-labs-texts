Vasospasm-Associated Plaque Erosion
Authors: Teruo Sekimoto, Hiroaki Tsujita, Yuki Numajiri, Hiroyoshi Mori, Hiroki Tanisawa, Kengo Suzuki, Yosuke Oishi, Yuki Takai, Taka-aki Matsuyama, Toshiro Shinke
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundPlaque erosion is a significant cause of acute coronary syndromes, representing a distinct pathophysiological entity from plaque rupture. Coronary vasospasm is a suspected trigger for erosion, though its precise mechanisms are not fully understood. Furthermore, reports of its histopathological confirmation by directional coronary atherectomy (DCA) are limited.Case PresentationWe present the case of a 56-year-old male with organic coronary stenosis and a clinical history suggestive of vasospasm. Intravascular optical coherence tomography revealed features highly suggestive of plaque erosion, including an overlying white thrombus. To obtain a definitive diagnosis, DCA was performed for tissue acquisition. Subsequent histopathological examination of the retrieved specimens clearly confirmed plaque erosion, demonstrating a thrombus adherent to an intact, smooth muscle cell-rich intimal layer without disruption of a fibrous cap.ConclusionsThis case provides DCA-confirmed pathological evidence for the clinically important potential link between coronary vasospasm and plaque erosion.

Vasospasm has been increasingly recognized as a potential trigger for plaque erosion, a significant underlying mechanism of acute coronary syndromes.1, 2, 3, 4 However, reports documenting plaque erosion confirmed by histopathological evaluation of specimens obtained via directional coronary atherectomy (DCA) remain limited, especially in the context of suspected vasospasm. Furthermore, diagnosis by advanced intravascular imaging modalities such as optical coherence tomography (OCT) can be challenging, often leaving diagnostic uncertainty.5,6 We therefore present a case of plaque erosion with suspected coronary vasospasm involvement. In this case, the diagnosis was suggested by OCT and subsequently established by definitive histopathological confirmation via DCA.Take-Home Message•Coronary vasospasm should be considered a potential contributing factor in plaque erosion.

Case Presentation

A 56-year-old male with a past medical history of dyslipidemia and atrial fibrillation presented with intermittent chest pain at rest, typically occurring in the early morning, for several months prior to catheterization. Vasospastic angina was suspected, and a calcium channel blocker was prescribed, which led to the resolution of his chest pain. However, 3 months prior to admission, he experienced syncope on exertion. Transthoracic echocardiography revealed no significant valvular disease or regional wall motion abnormalities. Subsequently, coronary computed tomography angiography was performed, which suggested severe stenosis of the left main trunk (LMT) (Figure 1). Coronary angiography revealed a 90% stenosis in the LMT with delayed antegrade flow. Suspecting vasospasm, intracoronary isosorbide dinitrate was administered, after which the LMT stenosis improved to 50% (Figure 2). The right coronary artery showed no significant stenosis. Fractional flow reserve (FFR) measurement with PressureWire X Guidewire (Abbott Vascular) was performed to assess the physiological significance of the lesion. The FFR was 0.72 in the far distal left anterior descending artery (LAD) and increased to 0.81 at the LAD ostium during pullback (Figure 3). A pressure drop of approximately 0.20 (from an assumed aortic FFR of 1.00-0.81 at the LAD ostium) was noted across the LMT lesion, indicating its physiological significance. Intracoronary imaging was performed prior to intervention. OCT using the Ultreon 1.0 Software (Abbott Vascular) revealed a plaque characterized by an irregular luminal surface with an overlying thrombus (visualized as a signal-rich, backscattering projection), without any evidence of fibrous cap rupture (Figure 4A). These findings are consistent with the established OCT criteria for plaque erosion.6 Intravascular ultrasound performed with the AltaView system (Terumo) showed a fibrous plaque with focal attenuation (Figure 4B). The LMT lesion was treated with an ATHEROCUT DCA device (NIPRO) using a large-sized device. A total of 10 cuts were performed at a maximum pressure of 5 atm. Subsequently, a Xience Sierra drug-eluting stent (4.0 × 8 mm; Abbott Vascular) was implanted. The stent was then postdilated with an NC Euphora noncompliant balloon (4.5 × 8 mm; Medtronic), achieving an excellent final result with 0% residual stenosis (Figure 5).Figure 4Co-registered OCT and IVUS Images of the LMT Lesion(A) The OCT image shows a plaque characterized by signal-rich projections (interpreted as white thrombus, indicated by yellow arrows) overlying a heterogeneous, poorly delineated region with diffuse borders and associated signal-rich bands. Discussion

Plaque erosion is pathologically distinct from plaque rupture. Rupture is characterized by the disruption of a thin fibrous cap overlying a large, lipid-rich necrotic core, typically representing an advanced atherosclerotic lesion. In contrast, plaque erosion occurs on a lesion with an intact fibrous cap, where a thrombus forms in direct contact with an intimal surface rich in smooth muscle cells and proteoglycans.7 Unlike plaque rupture, plaque erosion is known to occur at an earlier stage of atherosclerosis. Indeed, an autopsy registry of over 150 cases of plaque erosion demonstrated that the underlying lesions were typically pathological intimal thickening (16%), early fibroatheroma (50%), or late fibroatheroma (34%).8 Consistent with this pathological difference, the clinical profiles also differ: Erosion is more frequently observed in younger patients, women, and smokers, whereas plaque rupture is typically associated with older patients who have multiple traditional atherosclerotic risk factors.7 Despite these distinctions, the precise triggers leading to endothelial loss and subsequent erosion remain incompletely understood.

As highlighted in the introduction, the role of coronary vasospasm as a potential trigger for plaque erosion is gaining recognition, a concept substantially advanced by in vivo observations using OCT. Indeed, several pivotal OCT-based studies in patients with vasospastic angina have provided compelling quantitative evidence for this link. These studies have consistently reported that plaque erosion is present at the site of spasm in approximately one-quarter of patients (26%).1,2 This is in stark contrast to classic plaque rupture, which is rarely observed at these sites.2 The primary clinical implication of this high prevalence of erosion and associated thrombus is the potential therapeutic role for antiplatelet therapy in the management of vasospastic angina to prevent thrombotic complications.1,2 Furthermore, it has been reported that coronary segments prone to vasospasm may also exhibit features such as healed plaques or medial thickening on OCT, suggestive of repeated endothelial injury and abnormal vasoconstriction.9,10 The proposed pathophysiological mechanisms by which vasospasm might induce endothelial injury or detachment are considered multifaceted and complex.

A key strength of this case is the ability to directly correlate the in vivo OCT findings with the ex vivo histopathology from the same lesion. The OCT image demonstrated a signal-rich mass, interpreted as a thrombus, attached to a continuous and uninterrupted intimal surface. This corresponded precisely with the histopathological findings, which revealed a platelet-rich thrombus (confirmed by CD42b staining) adherent to an intact intimal layer predominantly composed of smooth muscle cells (confirmed by α-smooth muscle actin staining). Crucially, both OCT and histopathology confirmed the absence of fibrous cap rupture and a large underlying necrotic core, definitively distinguishing this lesion from a plaque rupture. This direct imaging-to-pathology correlation provides a clear validation of the OCT criteria6 for plaque erosion in this context and reinforces the diagnostic accuracy of OCT when interpreted carefully.

Although its use has declined, DCA retains a niche utility for its ability to provide tissue for histopathological analysis. Its clinical indications are highly selective and limited. However, for complex lesions where intravascular imaging is inconclusive and tissue confirmation is desirable, DCA might be a valuable tool for clarifying the underlying pathophysiology, as our case illustrates. The procedure does have notable limitations, including technical demands, unsuitability for certain anatomies (eg, tortuosity or calcification), and providing only superficial samples. It also carries inherent risks such as coronary perforation and distal embolization, requiring a careful risk-benefit assessment. In our patient with LMT lesion of uncertain etiology, we judged that the benefit of obtaining a definitive diagnosis outweighed these potential risks.

An important clinical consideration is whether the OCT findings alone would have been sufficient for management. In this patient, the decision to perform PCI was primarily driven by the severe and hemodynamically significant LMT stenosis, not the underlying plaque morphology. Although the OCT findings were highly suggestive of plaque erosion and likely sufficient to guide long-term secondary prevention focusing on vasospasm, the definitive histopathological confirmation via DCA was valuable for solidifying this therapeutic rationale. Regarding reproducibility, while DCA use for tissue confirmation has limited availability, the diagnostic pathway of using clinical suspicion and OCT to identify potential erosion represents a more generally applicable strategy.

A limitation of this report is the absence of a formal vasospasm provocation test. This test was deferred primarily for safety reasons, given the high risk of inducing complete occlusion of the patient's critical and hemodynamically significant LMT stenosis. Furthermore, the diagnosis of a significant vasospastic component was already well-supported by clinical history and the angiographic response to a vasodilator, making revascularization the clinical priority over further high-risk diagnostic testing.

Conclusions

This case of plaque erosion, confirmed by both OCT and histopathology, suggests an association with coronary vasospasm as a potential underlying trigger. These findings highlight the importance of considering vasospasm in the etiology of plaque erosion and the value of a multimodality diagnostic approach in select complex cases.
