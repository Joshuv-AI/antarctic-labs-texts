The influence of ultrasound on the oil–water interfacial behavior of peanut protein isolate-high methoxyl pectin with different occurrence states
Authors: Jingmin Sun, Yingxi Li, Xiaoyang Sun, Fusheng Chen, Tingwei Zhu, Yu Chen, Lifen Zhang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

An emulsion is a dispersed system composed of multiple immiscible liquids. Common stabilizers for emulsions include low molecular weight surfactants, polysaccharides, and proteins [1,2,3]. In the last few years, plant-based emulsions have increasingly replaced traditional emulsions stabilized with animal proteins. This transition has been primarily driven by their lower production costs, the absence of animal-derived ingredients, and a reduced risk of allergic reactions [4,5]. However, natural macromolecules such as proteins and polysaccharides are characterized by high molecular weights, slow interfacial adsorption rates, and a propensity for severe aggregation during emulsion formation, which could limit their functionality in industrial applications [6]. Ultrasound, as an economical and environmentally friendly high-energy emulsification technology, has been demonstrated to accelerate the migration of proteins and polysaccharides to the oil–water interface. This might enhance the interfacial performance of proteins and polysaccharides, and facilitates the stabilization of their emulsions [7,8,3].

There have been many studies on the preparation of protein-polysaccharide emulsions by ultrasound. Ultrasound cavitation enhances the surface hydrophobicity and electrostatic interactions of protein-polysaccharide complexes by exposing more hydrophobic residues and charged amino acids, thus improving the emulsifying potential of protein-polysaccharide emulsifiers [9,10]. Researches described that ultrasound could reduce the protein particle size, expose its hydrophobic groups, and enhance the protein interface adsorption capacity[8]. Simultaneously, ultrasound could decrease the viscosity of polysaccharides, facilitating the accumulation of polysaccharide at the O/W interface [6]. The behavior (such as interfacial adsorption content and structure) of proteins and polysaccharides in emulsions will also be affected by ultrasound [11,12].

Peanut protein isolate (PPI) has great potential as a functional ingredient in the food industry for its high nutritional value, low cost, low cholesterol content, and stable availability[13]. However, PPI alone is not an ideal emulsifier because of its rigid, globular structure. Recent studies have demonstrated that combining proteins with polysaccharides could significantly improve emulsion stability. For instance, Ma et al. [14], Ma et al. [15] reported that PPI forms stable complexes with cellulose nanocrystals via electrostatic and hydrophobic interactions, making it a promising and sustainable emulsifier. The non-covalent interactions (hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and electrostatic interactions) between proteins and polysaccharides are sensitive to pH [16,17]and external energy input, thus affect the occurrence states of proteins and polysaccharides [18]. Stronger protein-polysaccharide complexes could be formed and their emulsion stability enhanced by adjusting the pH [19],Wang et al. [20], Wang et al. [21]. Our previous studies reported that PPI-high methoxyl pectin (HMP) existed a soluble complex at pH 5.0, no interaction at pH 7.0, and co-soluble at pH 9.0, which required specific ultrasound conditions to achieve optimal emulsion stability. Ultrasound could strength both the hydrophobic and electrostatic interactions between PPI and HMP, and this improved the stability of emulsion [22,23]. However, it remained unclear why the optimal stability of these emulsions stabilized by PPI-HMP in different occurrence states, required specific ultrasound conditions.

HMP has been effectively utilized to enhance the functional properties of various plant proteins. Wang et al. [24] improved the emulsifying capacity of oat protein isolate (OPI) by developing an OPI-HMP complex. Similarly, Li et al. [25] formulated a highly efficient emulsion complex through the self-assembly of soy protein isolate and HMP. Moreover, the adsorbed protein and HMP content associated with the emulsion stability. The incorporation of appropriate HMP could increase the amount of protein adsorbed on droplet surfaces, thus improving the emulsion stability[26]. Wu et al. [27] reported that sonication significantly increased the adsorption of interfacial proteins, and effectively improves emulsion stability.

Based on our previous findings, the effects of ultrasound time on the interface-adsorbed contents of PPI and HMP, structural evolution of interface-adsorbed PPI, and interface-unadsorbed components were investigated, to clarify why optimal emulsion stability of PPI-HMP with different occurrence states required specific ultrasound conditions. The results will provide a theoretical basis for applying PPI-HMP and ultrasound in the food industry.

Materials and methods

Materials

Defatted peanut flour (protein content = 51.23 %, Kjeldahl nitrogen measured) was bought from Changshou Co. Ltd. (Shandong Province, Qingdao, China). Macklin Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) provided HMP (degree of esterification 55–65 %, galacturonic acid content ≥ 74.0 %, CAS number: 9000–69-5). Soybean oil was acquired from regional markets (Zhengzhou, Henan Province, China). The reagents used in the experiment were all of analytical grade (Tianjin Tianli Chemical Reagent Co., Ltd., Tianjin, China).

Peanut protein isolate preparation

The method of Wang et al. [20], Wang et al. [21] was used to extract PPI from defatted peanut flour. The ratio of defatted peanut flour and double-distilled water was 1:10 (w/v), then 1 M NaOH was used to regulate the mixture pH to 9.0. The mixture was stirred using a R30 electric stirrer (FLUKO Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China) for 2 h at 150 rpm to assure the protein was fully alkali-dissolved. The evenly stirred mixture was then centrifuged for 15 min at 4,000 × g. The supernatant was collected, and was adjusted to pH 4.5 using 1 M HCl, and then placed at 25 °C for 1 h. The precipitate was washed twice with double-distilled water after being centrifuged for another 15 min at 4,000 × g. Finally, PPI was obtained by freeze-dried the precipitate. The protein content (82.69 ± 0.14 %) was measured by Kjeldahl method.

Preparation of PPI-HMP emulsions

PPI (2.0 g, 4.0 %, w/v) and HMP (0.25 g, 0.5 %, w/v) were dissolved in 50 mL of double-distilled water, and stirred overnight. The pH of the PPI-HMP mixtures were regulated to pH 5.0, 7.0, and 9.0 according to our previous study [23]. The mixtures were oscillated for 2 h to ensure that the reactions were complete. Meanwhile, microbial contamination was prevented by adding sodium azide (0.02 %, w/v).

The PPI-HMP mixtures (48 mL) prepared above were combined with soybean oil (12 mL), and sheared for 1 min at 13,000 rpm using a FM 200 high-speed shear homogenizer (FLUKO Equipment Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). Then, the emulsions were treated using an ultrasound equipment (JY92-IIDN, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Inc., Ningbo, Zhejiang Province, China) with a 6 mm probe for 4, 8, and 10 min at a power density of 6.0 W/cm3. The ultrasound on and off times were 2 s, and the temperature was 20°C and kept using a condensate water circulation tank (DC-2006, Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Ningbo, Zhejiang Province, China).

Analysis of interface-adsorbed contents of PPI and HMP

The PPI-HMP emulsions were separated as two independent phases after centrifugation (60 min, 11,000 × g, 4°C). The cream layer and the serum phase were utilized to analyze interface-adsorbed contents of PPI and HMP, respectively [28].

PPI content

The interface-adsorbed content of PPI was determined using a Kjeldahl nitrogen analyzer (K1100, Hanon Co., Ltd., Jinan, Shandong Province, China). The results were expressed as milligrams of PPI in per gram cream layer (mg/g).

HMP content

The content of adsorbed HMP was estimated from the HMP concentration in the serum phase, and expressed relative to the original HMP concentration. To avoid the influence of protein, the content of HMP was analyzed with the phenol–sulfuric method. Transferred 0.0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8, and 1.0 mL of standard sucrose solution (0.1 mg/mL) into a colorimetric tube accurately, and then adsorbed a certain amount of double-distilled water make the whole volume 2.0 mL. After adding 1.0 mL of phenol solution (9.0 %, w/v) and 5.0 mL of sulfuric acid, the mixture was stored at 25°C for 30 min. The absorbance was determined at 490 nm with a TU-1900 Dual Beam UV–Vis Spectrophotometer (Persee Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). A standard calibration curve was drafted according to the amount of sucrose and the absorbance.

The serum phase of PPI-HMP emulsion was diluted 100 times with double-distilled water. Phenol and sulfuric acid were added into diluted sample (2.0 mL) as mentioned above. The concentration of HMP could be calculated from the standard curve. The interface-adsorbed content of HMP was determined according to equation (1).(1)P=CINI-CSERCINI×100%

where P is interface-adsorbed content of HMP (%), cINI is the original HMP concentration in the continuous phase (mg/mL), and cSER is the concentration of HMP in the serum phase calculated using the standard curve line (mg/mL).

Structure of interface-adsorbed PPI

Separation of interface-adsorbed PPI

The method interface-adsorbed PPI separation was according to Li et al. [28] with some modifications. The cream layer of the PPI-HMP emulsion was collected and mixed with pre-chilled acetone (–20°C) at a ratio of 1:5 (v/v) overnight. After storage at –20°C for 2 h, the mixture was centrifugated for 30 min (2 °C, 10,000 × g). Cold acetone was used to wash the precipitate three times, followed by air-drying at 25°C. The precipitate was redissolved in double-distilled water and freeze-dried to obtain interface-adsorbed PPI.

Fluorescence spectroscopy

The fluorescence spectroscopy of the interface-adsorbed PPI (0.1 mg/mL) was carried out using a Cary Eclipse fluorescence spectrophotometer (Agilent Technologies Inc., Palo Alto, CA, USA). 290 nm, 5 nm, and 2 nm were set as the excitation wavelength, slit width, and the sensitivity, respectively. Scanning emission spectra: 300–500 nm.

FTIR

Interface-adsorbed PPI (1 mg) was precisely weighed and pelleted with 100 mg KBr. All samples were scanned 32 times at room temperature (25 °C) using Nicolet 6700 spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Co., Ltd., Waltham, MA, USA) in the range of 4000 to 400 cm−1. The resolution was set to 4 cm−1, and KBr was used as the background. Omnic 8.2 (Nicolet Co., Ltd., Madison, WI, USA) was applied to perform baseline calibration and smoothing of the images. The relative content of secondary structure was calculated by Peakfit 4.12 (Systat Software Inc., San Jose, CA, USA).

Contact angle

The contact angle of interface-adsorbed PPI was determined by a JY-82C video contact angle meter (Dingsheng Testing Machine Testing Equipment Co., Ltd., Chengde, Hebei Province, China) as reported previously [29]. A certain amount of interface-adsorbed PPI was put into a mold and compressed using an infrared table machine. The pressed sheet was then placed on the stage and dripped with water droplets through an autosampler. Photographs of the water droplets were recorded using a digital camera, and the contact angle was measured employing the fitting method.

Structure and morphology of interface-unadsorbed components

The serum phase of the PPI-HMP emulsion, separated as in section 2.4, was gathered and freeze-dried for FTIR and SEM analysis of interface-unadsorbed components. The preparation of samples and the test method of interface-unadsorbed components for FTIR analysis were consistent with that of the interface-adsorbed PPI (section 2.5.3). The microscopic features of the interface-unadsorbed components were observed by SEM (Quanta 250 FEG, FEI Co. Ltd. Hillsboro, OR, USA). The morphological information was recorded under 3.0 kV accelerating voltage.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 20.0 software (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA) was used to analyze the data. Significant differences among samples were determined by Duncan's multiple range test (P < 0.05). All figures were drawn using Origin 2021 (OriginLab Co., Ltd., Northampton, MA, USA).

Results and discussion

Interface-adsorbed content of PPI and HMP

The interface-adsorbed contents of PPI and HMP of emulsions treated with ultrasound are displayed in Fig. 1. The introduction of ultrasound significantly improved the PPI content in the cream layer (Fig. 1A). After ultrasound treatment, the interface-adsorbed content of PPI reached 64.08 mg/g, 23.74 mg/g, and 23.73 mg/g at pH 5.0 (4 min), pH 7.0 (8 min), and pH 9.0 (10 min), respectively. This proved that ultrasound treatment caused more PPI to participate in the emulsification process [30]. Compared with that at pH 7.0, and 9.0, the interface-adsorbed content of PPI at pH 5.0 was the highest. This might be related to the occurrence states of PPI and HMP.Fig. 1Peanut protein isolate (PPI) (A) and high methoxyl pectin (HMP) (B) adsorption situation in cream layer.

Peanut protein isolate (PPI) (A) and high methoxyl pectin (HMP) (B) adsorption situation in cream layer.

The interface-adsorbed percentages of HMP in the PPI-HMP emulsions are shown in Fig. 1B. The interface-adsorbed percentage of HMP correlated positively with the ultrasound time at pH 5.0, which increased from 23.04 % to 28.92 % after ultrasound treatment for 10 min. Ultrasound treatment could expose the interaction sites of PPI [31]. The increase in the relative molecular area and interaction sites on the surface provided more opportunities for contact between PPI and HMP, which was beneficial to the formation of a PPI-HMP soluble complex [32]. Thereafter, HMP migrated to the oil–water interface with PPI in the form of a soluble complex. The interfacial adsorption of HMP increased, forming a more robust interfacial film and enhancing emulsion stability.

At pH 7.0, the interface-adsorbed percentage of HMP decreased from 22.44 % to 13.47 % after ultrasound treatment for 10 min (Fig. 1B). Ultrasound could degrade the main and side chains of HMP, and alter its distribution and quantity of surface charges [33]. Thus, electrostatic repulsion between HMP and PPI might be improved. Meanwhile, ultrasound affected the structure of PPI to strengthen its competitive adsorption with HMP, leading to the displacement of HMP by PPI at the oil–water interface.

Ultrasound had no significant effect on the interface-adsorbed percentage of HMP at pH 9.0 (Fig. 1B). PPI and HMP carry strong negative charges at pH 9.0, leading to complete dissolution in the system and a lack of interaction between them [23]. Zhao et al. [34] also revealed that the highly hydrophilic nature of HMP results in low interfacial activity, causing it to remain in the continuous phase rather than adsorb at the interface.

Structural properties of interface-adsorbed PPI

Fluorescence spectra

Aromatic amino acids (tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine) in proteins could naturally fluoresce when excited at specific wavelengths. The endogenous fluorescence of tryptophan (Trp) is commonly as a probe to obtain information about protein tertiary structure. The fluorescence spectra of interface-adsorbed PPI are shown in Fig. 2.Fig. 2Fluorescence spectrum of interface-adsorbed peanut protein isolate (PPI).

Fluorescence spectrum of interface-adsorbed peanut protein isolate (PPI).

At pH 5.0, the λmax of interface-adsorbed PPI was red shifted from 327 nm to 330, 330, and 332 nm after ultrasound treatment for 4, 8, and 10 min, respectively. Meanwhile, compared to the untreated sample, the fluorescence intensity of interface-adsorbed PPI increased significantly with ultrasound treatment, reaching the highest value at 4 min. The above changes indicated that the generation and collapse of bubbles during ultrasound stretched and loosened the tertiary structure of PPI, which gradually exposed the Trp residues embedded in the hydrophobic environment. Consequently, more available action sites appeared on the surface of the proteins, which improved the flexibility of interface-adsorbed PPI. This enhancement of protein flexibility was beneficial for its attachment on the O/W interface to form a stronger viscoelastic film to wrap oil droplets, which provided a certain stability to the emulsion system [35]. However, the fluorescence intensity of PPI was decreased with ultrasound time for 8 and 10 min. A large number of hydrophobic groups of PPI were released, and aggregates were formed through hydrophobic interactions during a long ultrasound time. Such protein aggregation leads to decreased stability of its emulsions [32].

The fluorescence spectrum of interface-adsorbed PPI from the untreated emulsion exhibited a bimodal distribution at 331 and 366 nm at pH 7.0. The disappearance of this bimodal pattern after ultrasound treatment indicated that the disordered structure of interface-adsorbed PPI became more ordered. Meanwhile, compared with that of the untreated sample, the λmax of the interface-adsorbed PPI with ultrasound treatment for 8 min blue-shifted to 327 nm. This might have been caused by the strong electrostatic repulsion that preserved the polar environment surrounding Trp residues Wang et al. [20], Wang et al. [21]. A more ordered structure allowed PPI to adsorb on the interface, thereby increasing the content of interface-adsorbed content of protein.

The λmax of interface-adsorbed PPI was red-shifted from 342 nm to 344, 346, and 347 nm after ultrasound treatment for 4, 8, and 10 min at pH 9.0, respectively. This represented a tendency of Trp residues to move outward. The fluorescence intensity of interface-adsorbed PPI at 4 min was even lower than that of the untreated sample. This was possibly caused by the hydrophobicity of the aromatic amino acid microenvironment being enhanced under low-energy ultrasound, which resulted in the accumulation of protein aggregates and structure densification [36]. The fluorescence intensity reached the peak value at 8 min, but decreased at 10 min. This phenomenon might be associated to the increased in the content of interface-adsorbed proteins and the aggregation of PPI through hydrophobic interactions.

FTIR

The secondary structure of interface-adsorbed PPI is exhibited in Fig. 3. The bands in the range of 3200 to 3400 cm−1 (amide A) represent the stretch vibrations of –NH and –OH in intermolecular hydrogen bonds. The peaks at 1600 to 1700 cm−1 (amide I) are associated to the C=O stretch vibrations, and N-H bending vibration is resulted in the adsorption peak among 1500 to 1600 cm−1 (amide II) [11]. The stretch vibrations of C-N and the bend vibrations of N-H led to the peaks presented at 1200 to 1300 cm−1 (amide III) [37]. It was found that the infrared spectra of the interface-adsorbed PPI were quite different from those of the raw material PPI at all three pHs. This indicated that the structure of PPI adsorbed on the O/W interface was special [38]. However, the adsorption peaks of the interface-adsorbed PPI separated from emulsions with the same pH did not undergo obvious red or blue shifts in the above regions, which implied that ultrasound could not change the carbon skeleton of the proteins.Fig. 3Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) of interface-adsorbed peanut protein isolate (PPI).

Contact angle

The contact angle (θ) is a vital parameter to measure the wettability of materials. A sample is hydrophilic with θ < 90°, which indicating the ability of a mixture to form O/W emulsion (Ma, Zhang, Sun, Chen, & Zhu, 2023). The contact angles and corresponding droplet photos of interface-adsorbed PPI are presented in Fig. 4.Fig. 4Contact angle of interface-adsorbed peanut protein isolate (PPI).

Contact angle of interface-adsorbed peanut protein isolate (PPI).

The θ of interface-adsorbed PPI reached a maximum of 71.57° with ultrasound treatment for 4 min at pH 5.0. The increase in θ means that the hydrophobic groups of PPI are exposed, and the structure is fully extended, which is consistent with the analysis of the fluorescence spectrum of interface-adsorbed PPI (Fig. 2A). Therefore, the oil droplets could be tightly packed by PPI, which was beneficial for the emulsion to remain stable for long hours without oil-phase leakage [39]. However, the aggregation of PPI induced by prolonged ultrasound caused the hydrophobic residues to be re-buried, and its θ decreased below 67.50°.

At pH 7.0, the interface-adsorbed PPI reached a maximum θ of 80.32° at 8 min of ultrasound. Ultrasound could promote a denser and more orderly arrangement of protein molecules on the O/W interface [40]. Regular and tightly packed interfacial proteins reduce the surface hydrophilicity, resulting in higher contact angles [41]. The θ of PPI at the interface decreased from 84.40° to 78.92° at pH 9.0. According to the content of interface-adsorbed PPI, more PPI accumulated at the interface. The accumulation of PPI caused hydrophobic residues to be buried, and thus the θ reduced.

Properties of interface-unadsorbed components

Micromorphology

The interface-unadsorbed components mainly included PPI and HMP. The microscopic morphology of the interface-unadsorbed components of the PPI-HMP emulsions are shown in Fig. 5. The appearance of the interface-unadsorbed components showed obvious differences at various pH. Some holes appeared on the surface of ultrasound-treated particles (marked by the red arrow) at pH 5.0. These changes in morphology of interface-unadsorbed components were similar at pH 7.0 and 9.0. It could be observed that a network-liked structure was attached to the particle surface of the interface-unadsorbed components of the ultrasound-treated emulsions at pH 7.0 and 9.0. Compared with the samples without ultrasound treatment (0 min), the surfaces became irregular, and the particles were widely attached to the wrinkled structure of the surface at pH 7.0 (8 min) and pH 9.0 (10 min). This phenomenon was intuitively proved the promotion of HMP network structure by ultrasound. The formation of the HMP network structure weakened the movement of oil droplets, and provided a certain viscoelasticity to the emulsion system [23].Fig. 5Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of interface-unadsorbed components.

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images of interface-unadsorbed components.

FTIR

FTIR was employed to explore the composition and interaction changes of the interface-unadsorbed components, and the results are displayed in Fig. 6. The peaks observed in the range of 1010 to 1150 cm−1 of all interface-unadsorbed components were attributed to the adsorption of pyranose in HMP. This indicated that HMP was present in the continuous phase of the emulsions. The peaks at 1500 to 1600 cm−1 corresponded to the amide I and amide II of PPI [11]. At pH 5.0, all the interface-unadsorbed samples had one peak at 1650 cm−1, while at pH 7.0 and 9.0, there were another small peak appeared at 1550 cm−1. This result might be related to the occurrence states of PPI and HMP at pH 5.0, pH 7.0, and 9.0 [23]. The broad peaks appearing at 3000 to 3600 cm−1 were related to the stretch vibrations of intramolecular hydroxyl groups (O-H) [42]. Compared with those at pH 5.0, the adsorption peaks of intramolecular hydroxyl groups moved to a smaller wavenumber at pH 7.0 and 9.0 after ultrasound treatment. This indicated that ultrasound contributed to the construction of network structure of HMP by promoting the formation of hydrogen bonds, which provided viscoelasticity and stability for the emulsions.Fig. 6Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) of interface-unadsorbed components.

Fourier transform infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) of interface-unadsorbed components.

Above all, the structural changes of interfacial proteins various with different ultrasound time at pH 5.0, 7.0, and 9.0. A relaxed tertiary structure of interfacial proteins was expected at pH 5.0 with ultrasound time for 4 min. The ordered structure of interfacial proteins was observed at pH 7.0 for 8 min, and interfacial protein tended to aggregate at pH 9.0 with ultrasound treated for 10 min. Ultrasound improved the construction of the HMP network structure in interface-unadsorbed components at pH 7.0, and 9.0 with ultrasound treated for 8 min and 10 min, respectively.

Emulsion stabilization mechanisms of PPI-HMP with different occurrence states under ultrasound

The emulsion stabilization mechanism of PPI-HMP with different occurrence states under ultrasound is illustrated in Fig. 7. The different occurrence states of PPI–HMP at various pH meant that the interfacial behavior of molecules under ultrasound were also different. Our previous study reported that the PPI formed a soluble complex with HMP at pH 5.0 [23]. This study revealed that ultrasound enhanced the flexibility of PPI, and increased the contents of interface-adsorbed PPI and HMP. These were beneficial for the formation of a stronger viscoelastic film to wrap the oil droplets, and provided a certain stability to the emulsion system.Fig. 7Emulsion stabilization mechanisms of peanut protein isolate (PPI)-high methoxyl pectin (HMP) with different occurrence states under ultrasound.

Emulsion stabilization mechanisms of peanut protein isolate (PPI)-high methoxyl pectin (HMP) with different occurrence states under ultrasound.

At pH 7.0, PPI and HMP had no interaction with each other, and they were co-soluble in a strong negatively charged environments (pH 9.0) [23]. Ultrasound improved the construction of the HMP network structure in interface-unadsorbed components, and increased of the viscoelasticity and stability of the emulsions. The content of interface-adsorbed PPI increased with ultrasound, which also benefited to the stability of the interface film. Meanwhile, there was competitive adsorption of PPI and HMP at pH 7.0. PPI was tightly arranged at the interface and also improved the stabilization of the emulsion.

Conclusions

The interfacial adsorption content for PPI and HMP demonstrated that ultrasound treatment enhanced PPI adsorption at the oil–water interface at all tested pH (5.0, 7.0, and 9.0). The interface-adsorbed percentage of HMP increased at pH 5.0. However, it decreased at pH 7.0, and showed no significant change at pH 9.0. Fluorescence spectroscopy, FTIR, and contact angle analysis of interface-adsorbed PPI indicated that PPI exposed more active binding sites after ultrasound treatment, and its structure was loosened and more prone to interact with HMP to form soluble complexes at pH 5.0. Thus, a more robust interfacial film was formed, with enhanced emulsion stability. At pH 7.0, ultrasound modified the PPI structure, induced the competitive adsorption between PPI and HMP, and led to the displacement of HMP at the interface. The SEM and FTIR analysis of the interface-unadsorbed components revealed that ultrasound promoted the formation of an HMP network in the continuous phase, which imparted viscoelasticity to the emulsion system and enhanced emulsion stability at pH 7.0 and 9.0.
