L-type pyocins inhibit the BAM complex to kill without cell entry
Authors: Fabian Munder, Matthew D Johnson, Imogen Samuels, Laura McCaughey, Oleksii Zdorevskyi, Chunxiao Wang, Ashleigh Kropp, Lauren Zavan, Erin P Price, Derek S Sarovich, Swati Varshney, Christopher A McDevitt, Hari Venugopal, Vivek Sharma, Matthew T Doyle, Francesca Short, Debnath Ghosal, James P R Connolly, Gavin J Knott, Rhys Grinter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMany antibiotics are ineffective against the Gram-negative pathogen Pseudomonas aeruginosa because of intrinsic defence mechanisms, such as the impermeable bacterial outer membrane. Here, we show that protein antibiotics called L-type pyocins kill P. aeruginosa by inhibiting the β-barrel assembly machinery (BAM) complex at the cell surface, halting outer-membrane protein assembly. Using single-particle cryo-electron microscopy, we show that L-type pyocins bind a surface-exposed region of BamA and deploy a C-terminal peptide that competitively inhibits the BAM complex, demonstrating that cell entry is not required for antibiotic activity. We combine genetics, multi-omics and cryo-electron tomography to show that BAM complex inhibition by L-type pyocins or the cyclic-peptide antibiotic, darobactin, triggers a multifaceted transcriptomic, proteomic, and morphological response. BAM inhibition ultimately leads to a catastrophic loss of membrane integrity and cell death. These results validate BAM as a target for antibiotics that do not enter the cell and define an engineerable system for their development.Subject terms: Bacteriology, Cryoelectron microscopy, Target identification, Antibiotics

Introduction

Pseudomonas aeruginosa is a high-priority Gram-negative bacterial pathogen that poses a substantial threat to human health, particularly in individuals with compromised immune systems or those with chronic lung conditions, such as bronchiectasis or chronic obstructive pulmonary disease1–4. P. aeruginosa causes a wide range of opportunistic infections, from ventilator-associated pneumonia to ear, burn, bloodstream and urinary tract infections, and is notoriously difficult to treat1. This difficulty stems from a high level of intrinsic and acquired antibiotic resistance mediated by mechanisms including efflux pumps, beta-lactamases, porin loss, and low outer membrane permeability, which acts as a barrier to many antibiotics5–8. Despite a pressing need, few new antibiotic classes with efficacy against P. aeruginosa have been developed, with no new classes approved for clinical use since the 1980s1,9. To date, only two essential outer membrane protein (OMP) targets have been identified where inhibition leads to bacterial death: the LptDE complex, responsible for inserting lipopolysaccharide (LPS) into the outer leaflet of the outer membrane, and the β-barrel assembly machinery (BAM) complex, which mediates the folding and insertion of integral OMPs10–14. These essential complexes are surface-exposed, making them attractive targets for the development of next-generation antibiotics that are not restricted by the outer membrane10.

L-type pyocins are protein antibiotics produced by P. aeruginosa to eliminate closely related competitor strains and species15–17. These molecules display potent and specific antibacterial activity and have shown therapeutic promise in preclinical infection models, including the clearance of lethal P. aeruginosa lung infections in mice18. L-type pyocins recognise specific molecules on the surface of their target cell, achieving per-molecule potencies several orders of magnitude higher than small-molecule antibiotics15,16. Their specificity also enables targeted killing of P. aeruginosa without off-target effects to the host microbiota, making them attractive candidates for biologic therapies17. Structurally, L-type pyocins are composed of N- and C-terminal β-prism lectin domains connected to an unstructured C-terminal peptide. The C-terminal β-prism domain binds to D-rhamnose in the common polysaccharide antigen (CPA) of P. aeruginosa LPS, which serves as the primary cell surface receptor15,19,20. However, CPA binding alone cannot explain the lethal activity of these proteins. Importantly, the deletion of three final residues of the C-terminal peptide abolishes their antibacterial activity, suggesting that this region plays a critical but poorly understood role in L-type pyocin function19.

Recent evidence implicates the BAM complex as a cell surface receptor for L-type pyocins, and potentially as their cytotoxic target16. The BAM complex catalyses the folding and insertion of OMPs, with β-strand 1 of the BamA β-barrel binding an incoming OMP’s C-terminal β-strand to nucleate and guide β-barrel formation21–24. The BamA β-barrel contains periplasmic turns and extracellular loops, with the sequence of loop 6 correlating with a strain’s susceptibility to L-type pyocins16. This points to an interaction between L-type pyocins and the BAM complex, yet its mechanistic basis and downstream consequences remain unresolved. Despite their promise as antimicrobials, the engineering and application of L-type pyocins have been hindered, in part, by a lack of understanding of their mechanism of bacterial killing and the downstream cellular consequences of intoxication.

In this study, we comprehensively dissect the L-type pyocin mechanism of action. We expand the L-type pyocin toolkit and establish the molecular determinants of activity across a panel of clinical P. aeruginosa isolates. We isolate the BAM complex from P. aeruginosa and determine the structural and mechanistic basis of L-type pyocin-BamA interaction using single-particle cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM) and molecular dynamics simulations. To determine the cellular consequences of the inhibition of BamA by L-type pyocins, we combine transposon-directed insertion site sequencing (TraDIS), transcriptomics, proteomics, and cryo-electron tomography (cryo-ET). We find that L-type pyocins deploy their C-terminal peptide to bind the BAM complex at β-strand 1, resulting in inhibition of OMP assembly, induction of envelope stress responses, and ultimately, bacterial cell death, resulting from a loss of integrity of the outer membrane. This process makes P. aeruginosa cells sensitive to co-treatment with vancomycin, an antibiotic that is otherwise ineffective against P. aeruginosa. These findings validate the BAM complex as a key vulnerability in Gram-negative bacteria that can be targeted by antibiotics that do not enter the bacterial cell and demonstrate the therapeutic potential of L-type pyocins targeting outer membrane biogenesis.

Results

L-type pyocins are potent inhibitors of P. aeruginosa

Previous work assessed the inhibition of P. aeruginosa by L-type pyocins in a binary growth/no-growth assay, with minimum inhibitory concentrations as low as 6.9 nM reported20. To gain a more nuanced picture of L-type pyocin activity, we performed P. aeruginosa PAO1 growth curves in liquid culture in the presence of Pyocin L1 (HG422551), ranging from 3.27 µM to 0.033 nM. Pyocin L1 affected the growth of P. aeruginosa PAO1 at concentrations higher than 0.33 nM, with a half-maximal inhibitory concentration (IC50) of 0.85 nM (Fig. 1a, b). At higher concentrations, growth slowed 2 h post inoculation, arrested by ~6 h, and subsequently declined. An optical density of at least 0.75 was obtained even at higher Pyocin L1 concentrations, which may explain the lower IC50 calculated by our experiments, compared to the earlier binary inhibition assays (Fig. 1b). These data indicate that L-type pyocins can be significantly more potent, on a per molecule basis, than antibiotics commonly used to treat infection by P. aeruginosa25–28. This potency exceeds small molecules targeting outer membrane biogenesis. Darobactin and its analogues, which inhibit BamA, exhibit MIC50s of ~1-2 μM against Escherichia coli and 8–16 μM against P. aeruginosa29,30, while the LptD inhibitor murepavadin achieves MIC50 values of ~77 nM31, indicating lower potency than Pyocin L1 on a molar basis.

In addition to Pyocin L1, previous reports identified Pyocin L2 and L3, which share 86% and 30% sequence identity with Pyocin L1, and exhibit distinct but overlapping spectra of activity15,20. To expand our repertoire of L-type pyocins for development as antibiotics targeting P. aeruginosa, we searched available microbial genomes for bacteriocins with sequence and domain similarity to known L-type pyocins, identifying four additional variants (Pyocin L2b, L3b, L4 and L5). These new L-type pyocins share between 28 and 96% sequence identity, with ‘b’ designating >90% identity to an existing protein (Fig. 1c, d).

L-type pyocins inhibit diverse pathogenic P. aeruginosa strains

We recombinantly produced our seven L-type pyocins and performed plate-based growth inhibition assays against 17 P. aeruginosa strains, including 15 recent clinical isolates32. Previous reports indicate that a C-terminal affinity tag can impact the activity of L-type pyocins20,33,34. Accordingly, for growth inhibition assays, they were purified using an N-terminal poly-histidine tag, except Pyocin L5, which could only be purified using a C-terminal His-tag (subsequently referred to as Pyocin L5C-His) (Fig. S1a). The P. aeruginosa isolates displayed variable susceptibility to the L-type pyocins in our panel. Sixteen of the seventeen tested strains were susceptible to at least one L-type pyocin, with the majority potently inhibited by multiple proteins (Figs. 1e, f and S2). Pyocin L5C-His was the least effective L-type pyocin, with weak activity observed only against one bronchiectasis-derived P. aeruginosa strain (SCHI0181.S.18). However, given that its C-terminal His-tag may affect its activity, this may not represent its true killing spectrum.

Previous work demonstrated a correlation between L-type pyocin susceptibility and the amino acid sequence of extracellular loop 6 of BamA16,35–38. We identified six BamA loop 6 types in the P. aeruginosa strains tested, which correlated with L-type pyocin susceptibility, supporting BamA as the target protein (Figs. 1f, g, S2 and S3a, and Supplementary note 1). However, this correlation is not absolute, with both susceptibility and sensitivity varying among strains sharing the same BamA loop 6 sequences (Figs. 1f and S2). For example, P. aeruginosa SCHI0181.S.42 is resistant to all tested L-type pyocins, even though its extracellular loop 6 is identical to other susceptible strains, indicating that additional factors determine susceptibility, such as the abundance of CPA O-antigen. Interestingly, P. aeruginosa PAO1 became susceptible to Pyocin L2 when the BAM complex from strain P28 was overexpressed, consistent with the susceptibility profile of this strain (Fig. S3b). To assess BamA loop 6 diversity in P. aeruginosa more broadly, we analysed sequences from 557 clinical and environmental isolates (Supplementary Data 1). The bamA gene was highly conserved (97.9% nucleic acid and 98.6% amino acid pairwise identity) with variation predominantly confined to loop 6 (Fig. S4a, b). This suggests this region is under strong diversifying selection. We identified thirteen loop 6 sequence types, including the six represented by the isolates in our susceptibility screen, which together account for 427 (76%) of the strains analysed (Fig. S4c). Notably, one of the thirteen loop 6 variants, characterised by a shorter sequence, was present in 116 strains (21%) and was not represented in the screened isolates. We generated a P. aeruginosa PAO1 strain with BamA containing this short loop 6 sequence, and found it is resistant to all L-type pyocins in our collection (Fig. S4d). Thus, while none of the L-type pyocins we identified is active against all loop 6 variants, together they target most BamA loop 6 sequence variants across a broad P. aeruginosa clinical isolate panel.

The BAM complex is the target of L-type pyocins

To test whether the correlation between BamA loop 6 and L-type pyocin susceptibility results from a direct interaction between these proteins16, we chromosomally introduced a 2xStrep-tag at the N-terminus of BamA of P. aeruginosa PAO1 and natively isolated it in detergent micelles (Fig. S1c). All five BAM subunits were present in this complex (BamA-E), as well as a significant quantity of the LPS insertase LptD (Fig. 2a). We utilised cryo-EM to resolve the structure of the P. aeruginosa PAO1 BAM complex (BAMPAO1) to 2.75 Å, in an outward open conformation (Figs. 2b, c, S5, S6 and S7, Table S1, and Supplementary Movie 1).

To determine if BAMPAO1 and Pyocin L1 interact with high affinity, we combined these proteins and performed size exclusion chromatography, demonstrating these species form a stable complex (Fig. 2d). In contrast to the growth inhibition assays, C-terminally His6-tagged Pyocin L1 (Pyocin L1C-His) was used to form this complex, as it was the purified protein available at the time of the experiment. Pyocin L1C-His retains activity against P. aeruginosa PAO1 but exhibits reduced cytotoxic potency, consistent with the reported importance of the C-terminal region for full activity (Fig. S1b)19. We performed cryo-EM on this sample, resolving the structure of the BAMPAO1-Pyocin L1C-His complex to a global resolution of 2.84 Å (Fig. S8, Table S1, and Supplementary Movie 2). In comparison to the cryo-EM maps of BAMPAO1 alone, additional density is present in these maps corresponding to the two Pyocin L1C-His β-lectin domains, and part of the C-terminal peptide (Fig. 2e). This density allowed us to model the majority of the Pyocin L1C-His polypeptide chain (3-240 of 256 amino acids) in complex with BAMPAO1 (Fig. 2f). Consistent with Pyocin L1 susceptibility profiles, this structure demonstrated that Pyocin L1C-His interacts extensively with extracellular loop 6 of BamA, clasping this exposed loop between its two β-lectin domains (Fig. 2g).

In the x-ray structure of free Pyocin L1, the protein’s C-terminal peptide is bound in a cleft between the protein’s two β-lectin domains15,19. Strikingly, in the BAMPAO1-Pyocin L1C-His structure, the C-terminal peptide dissociates from the body of Pyocin L1C-His and is deployed into the lumen of the BamA β-barrel (Fig. 2h). The release of the peptide is driven by twisting of the Pyocin L1C-His β-lectin domains relative to each other, likely resulting from loop 6 binding (Fig. 2g). The C-terminal peptide interacts with BamA extracellular loops 2, 3, and 4, stabilising them in an outward open conformation (Fig. 2f, i). No density was observed for Pyocin L1C-His residues downstream of His-240 (16 Pyocin L1 residues and 14 His-tag residues), with this region positioned to occupy the lumen of the BamA barrel. To assess the dynamics of this region, we modelled the final 16 Pyocin L1 residues as an unstructured peptide, within the lumen of the BamA β-barrel in an outer membrane lipid bilayer and performed 3 ×1µs molecular dynamics (MD) simulations. Consistent with the lack of density in our cryo-EM maps, these residues were mobile during these simulations, adopting various conformations relative to the BamA β-barrel (Supplementary Movie 3).

To assess the importance of the C-terminal peptide for Pyocin L1 activity, we generated a truncation protein missing the final 17 amino acids, termed Pyocin L1ΔC-term. This protein lacked cytotoxic activity, which was not restored by the concurrent addition of Pyocin L1ΔC-term and independently synthesised C-terminal peptides (11 and 17 amino acids), emphasising the importance of this region (Fig. S3c, d, and Supplementary Note 1). This observation, combined with our structural data, suggests that C-terminal peptide deployment into the BamA β-barrel is important for its cytotoxic mechanism, possibly involving inhibition of the BAM complex. However, the lack of stable contacts between this region and BamA in the BAMPAO1-Pyocin L1C-His structure suggests it does not represent a final inhibitory state. We hypothesise that the C-terminal His-tag prevents the C-terminal peptide of Pyocin L1C-His from reaching its final bound state to the detergent-solubilised BAM complex, and that the BAMPAO1-Pyocin L1C-His structure represents a pre-inhibition state.

To test the effect of a C-terminal tag on BAM complex interactions, we sought to determine the structure of BAM in complex with an N-terminally tagged L-type pyocin. We combined N-terminally His6-tagged Pyocin L2 with the BAM complex from P. aeruginosa P28 (Fig. S1d). Pyocin L2 and L1 share 86% sequence identity, and the sequence of their 25 C-terminal amino acids is identical, allowing for direct comparison of the interaction of their C-terminal peptides with the BAM complex. BamA from strain P28 differs from PAO1 only in the sequence of extracellular loop 6, and P28 is susceptible to Pyocin L2, whereas PAO1 is not. We anticipated that this combination would enable capture of a novel loop 6-dependent binding interaction between BAM P28 and Pyocin L2, with C-terminal peptide interactions comparable to Pyocin L1. We confirmed that Pyocin L2 and BAMP28 form a stable complex via analytical-SEC and solved the structure of the complex via Cryo-EM to a global resolution of 3.18 Å (Figs. 3a, b, S9, Table S1, and Supplementary Movie 4). In this structure, Pyocin L2 binds BamA loop 6 in the same mode as Pyocin L1 (Fig. 3c). However, sequence differences between Pyocin L1 and L2, and loop 6 of the respective BamA molecules, mean the molecular contacts that maintain this interface differ significantly (Fig. 3d). Like the BAMPAO1-Pyocin L1C-His structure, the C-terminal peptide of Pyocin L2 is deployed into the BamA lumen, where it stabilises the extracellular loops 1-4 (Fig. 3e). Strikingly, in the BAMP28-Pyocin L2 structure, the Pyocin L2 C-terminal peptide is fully resolved, with the final 16 amino acids extending to bind to β-strand 1 of BamA (Fig. 3c, f, g). Amino acids 248–256 of Pyocin L2 bind to BamA β-strand 1 via β-augmentation, mimicking the interaction of an OMP terminal β-strand, with His253 occupying the same position as the conserved C-terminal aromatic residue of the OMP β-signal22,38. To assess the stability of the BAMP28-Pyocin L2 interaction, we performed 3 ×1µs MD simulations of the complex in an outer membrane bilayer. In these simulations and those of the pre-inhibition state, Pyocin L1 and L2 maintain stable interactions with loop 6 (5.6 ± 1.6 and 5 ± 1.5 H-bonds maintained over the simulations, respectively), while in the BAMP28-Pyocin L2 simulation, the C-terminal peptide maintains a stable interaction with BamA β-strand 1. We observed no large-scale conformational changes, indicating the complex is stable in the membrane environment (Supplementary Movies 3 and 5). The interaction between the C-terminal peptide of Pyocin L1 and BamA β-strand 1 is similar to the binding of BamA bamabactins family peptides (darobactin, dynobactin, and xenorceptide)29,39–41. Bamabactins are a recently identified family of macrocyclic peptide antibiotics that inhibit the BAM complex by competitively blocking β-strand 1 (Fig. S10). Based on this similarity, it is highly probable that L-type pyocins are also competitive inhibitors of OMP binding by the BAM complex.

The BAM complex adopts distinct extracellular loop-open (outward open) and loop-closed (inward open) states, where either the extracellular loops or POTRA 5 of BamA occlude access to the interior of its transmembrane β-barrel35–38,42. In the outward open state observed in our apo-BAMPAO1 and BAMPAO1-Pyocin L1C-His structures, BamA β-strand 1 is not accessible for peptide binding from the periplasm, due to contacts between the β-barrel N/C-termini and the presence of POTRA 5 (Figs. 2c, f, and S6). Instead, functional BamA accepts nascent OMPs at β-strand 1 in the inward-open conformation before transition to the outward-open conformation for membrane insertion24,38,42,43. In the BAMP28-Pyocin L2 complex, BAM adopts an inward open conformation, where the BamA β-barrel is not occluded by POTRA 5 or the extracellular loops, leading to the formation of a continuous pore, ranging from ~6–20 Å in diameter that has not been observed for other BamA-targeting antibiotics (Fig. 3h)29,39–41. A lack of interactions between POTRA 5 and the BamA β-barrel in this structure means that the POTRA domains and associated BamB-E subunits of this complex are mobile, only allowing modelling of Pyocin L2 and the BamA β-barrel with POTRA 5 (Fig. 3c).

L-type pyocins inhibit the BAM complex via a multistage process

Our structural analysis indicates that L-type pyocins bind to loop 6 of BamA and deploy their C-terminal peptide into the BamA lumen to inhibit the BAM complex by binding β-strand 1 (Fig. 4a–c). Entry of the C-terminal peptide most likely occurs when BamA adopts the outward-open conformation (Fig. 4d, e), where the open conformation of the extracellular loops allows access to the BamA lumen. However, β-strand 1 of BamA is only accessible for binding in the inward open conformation, where entry of the C-terminal peptide into BamA is blocked by closed extracellular loops. This mutual exclusivity suggests a two-step inhibition mechanism, where initial binding and C-terminal peptide deployment occur in the outward open state, creating a primed or pre-inhibited BAM complex. Full inhibition then requires transition to the inward open state, which exposes β-strand 1 for C-terminal peptide binding (Supplementary Movie 6). Notably, our MD simulations of the BAMPAO1-Pyocin L1C-His complex did not show spontaneous transition from the outward open to inward open state (Supplementary Movies 3 and 5). A conformational change of this magnitude is unlikely to be compatible with the timescale of these simulations and may be triggered by the delivery of nascent OMPs for assembly rather than solely by interactions with Pyocin L1 (Fig. 4f)24,38,42–44. However, considering the C-terminal peptide of Pyocin L1 and L2 is identical, its binding to BamA β-strand 1, observed in our fully inhibited BAMP28-Pyocin L2 complex, likely also represents the stable thermodynamic endpoint Pyocin L1 binding to BAMPAO1. This supports a unified mechanism in which both Pyocin L1 and L2 achieve the same fully inhibited state, with differences in our structures arising from the C-terminal affinity tag on Pyocin L1 rather than intrinsic properties of the proteins. Our MD simulations of the fully inhibited BAMP28-Pyocin L2 complex also provide context for the positioning of the complex in the outer membrane bilayer (Fig. 4g, h). The position of Pyocin L1 is compatible with binding D-rhamnose moieties of CPA via its C-terminal β-lectin domain, which may further stabilise the complex15.

P. aeruginosa PAO1 responds rapidly to pyocin L1 intoxication

Having defined the structural basis of BAM binding by L-type pyocins, we next sought to identify the genetic determinants of cellular susceptibility by performing TraDIS on P. aeruginosa PAO1 treated with N-terminally 6xHis-tagged Pyocin L1. These data show that outer membrane homeostasis is critical for tolerating L-type pyocin intoxication, and that the production of CPA is an important determinant of susceptibility (Figs. S11 and S12, and Supplementary Data 2). See Supplementary Note 2 for a detailed description of these data.

Given that L-type pyocins likely inhibit BAM to drive cytotoxicity, we next defined the resulting cellular response using integrated transcriptomic (RNA-seq) and proteomic profiling of P. aeruginosa PAO1 treated with N-terminally 6xHis-tagged Pyocin L1 (Fig. 5). Transcriptomic responses were examined at 15- and 120 min post treatment (~1/2 and 4 cell divisions, respectively45), while proteomic changes were assessed at 300 min (~10 cell divisions) to consider slower changes in the proteome and to capture later-stage consequences of Pyocin L1 intoxication. Transcriptional profiling 15 min post treatment reveals a rapid and multifaceted response involving the differential expression of 771 genes (377 upregulated, 394 downregulated; Log2FC > 0.5; -log10FDR > 1.3) (Fig. 5a, b, and Supplementary Data 3). These changes reflect both direct physiological disruptions caused by Pyocin L1 and its recognition as a signal of microbial antagonism. A hallmark of the early response is the strong downregulation of genes involved in translation (118 genes) and protein export (secA,B,D,E,Y, yidC). This is likely a result of cells sensing inhibition of the BAM complex by Pyocin L1, which stalls the insertion of OMPs, leading to the repression of protein synthesis to mitigate cell envelope disruption. Concurrently, P. aeruginosa reprograms its metabolism, upregulating at least 33 genes involved in the catabolism of branched-chain amino acids and fatty acids, consistent with a shift from growth to non-replicative persistence (Fig. 5b, c). Selective changes in OMP gene expression occur, supporting a strategy to address envelope stress and community-level competition. A limited number of TonB-dependent transporters (TBDTs) are repressed, while some major porins (e.g., oprB, oprD) and proteins associated with cell envelope integrity (e.g., oprL, oprF) are upregulated, suggesting remodelling of the outer membrane to reduce vulnerability to competitor-produced bacteriocins, which often target TBDTs for cell entry46, and to maintain outer membrane homeostasis47. Notably, genes associated with the type VI secretion system (T6SS, ≥24 genes) are strongly induced, suggesting a response to Pyocin L1 as a hostile signal, triggering this contact-dependent defence mechanism48,49. This is accompanied by activation of phenazine biosynthesis, quorum sensing, and biofilm-associated genes, indicating the mounting of a coordinated, community-level defence (Fig. 5b, c, and Supplementary Data 3).

Transcriptomic and proteomic analysis indicate a rapid and targeted cellular response to Pyocin L1.

By 120 min, the transcriptome shifts toward managing accumulating protein folding and envelope stress, with robust upregulation of chaperone systems (including lon1/2, groESL, clpBX, dnaK), likely a consequence of misfolded OMPs accumulating due to BAM inhibition (Fig. 5b, d, and Supplementary Data 3). Interestingly, significant upregulation of periplasmic chaperones (surA, degP, skp, etc.) was not observed, indicating that folding stress is not directly addressed in the periplasm of the cell envelope. At least 65 secretion signal peptide-encoding genes, including outer membrane porins, are upregulated, likely in an indirect attempt to restore outer membrane function and integrity. Conversely, there is reinforced repression of 18 TBDTs. Strong translational repression and some Sec system suppression (e.g., secY, secE, yidC) persist, showing a continued adaptive response to envelope stress. T6SS gene induction remained strong, but community-level signalling (quorum sensing, phenazine production) is dampened, suggesting a shift from cooperation to self-preservation (Fig. 5a, c, and Supplementary Data 3).

Proteomic analysis at 300 min post treatment reveals significant changes in protein abundance during Pyocin L1 intoxication, with 886 proteins (441 increased and 445 decreased; Log2FC > 1; -Log10Pvalue > 1) showing altered abundance compared to untreated cells (Fig. 5e, f, and Supplementary Data 4). These changes reflect both a sustained stress response and systemic disruption of protein homeostasis and envelope integrity. Consistent with transcriptomic data, T6SS components were more abundant, and ribosomal proteins and translation factors were reduced, confirming prolonged growth suppression. Strikingly, 167 signal peptide-containing proteins were reduced in abundance, of which 37 were OMPs, including 11 TBDTs and 12 porins (Fig. 5f, g). In contrast to the transcriptomic data, OprF and OprL, which are critical for outer membrane tethering, were reduced in abundance, suggesting an impaired ability to maintain outer envelope structure. Notably, the BAM components BamA and BamE, and the LPS insertase LptD, were reduced in abundance, suggesting an impaired ability to incorporate OMPs and LPS into the outer membrane. Fatty acid biosynthesis-related proteins increased in abundance, possibly to compensate for failure to insert OMPs and LPS, by reinforcing the outer membrane with phospholipids in place of these key components. Despite transcriptional induction of chaperones, few were elevated at the protein level, suggesting an inability to effectively address folding stress. However, components of the Arn lipid A modification system (ArnA, ArnB, ArnC) were upregulated, indicating activation of resistance mechanisms against cationic antimicrobial peptides, consistent with a perceived breach in envelope integrity. Finally, a greatly increased abundance of pyoverdine biosynthesis and export proteins was observed, likely reflecting iron starvation, possibly due to TBDT repression and membrane transport dysfunction (Fig. 5f, g, and Supplementary Data 4).

Based on our omics data, which indicate that L-type pyocins compromise outer membrane integrity, we hypothesised that Pyocin L1 may sensitize P. aeruginosa to antibiotics normally too large to cross the outer membrane. To test this, we co-treated P. aeruginosa PAO1 with vancomycin at concentrations that are not normally cytotoxic to this bacterium, and either Pyocin L1 or darobactin. We saw a more rapid and pronounced decline in the optical density of cultures treated with these antibiotic combinations, indicating that both Pyocin L1 and darobactin compromise the outer membrane, allowing vancomycin to target the cell wall in the periplasm (Fig. 5h). However, this effect only becomes pronounced 4 h after antibiotic treatment, making it unclear whether it is related specifically to the pore formed by the BAM-antibiotic complex or a more general disruption of the outer membrane.

To compare how P. aeruginosa responds to different BAM inhibitors, we analysed the transcriptional and proteomic responses to darobactin under the same conditions as Pyocin L1. Both agents triggered similar envelope stress programs, indicating a shared mechanism of action, but their effects diverged over time. Pyocin L1 elicited stronger and more sustained T6SS induction and translational repression, whereas darobactin maintained upregulation of oxidative phosphorylation and metabolic realignment pathways. A detailed temporal analysis of darobactin-treated cells is provided in Supplementary Note 3 and Supplementary Data 3 and 4.

Pyocin L1 treatment leads to cell envelope degradation

While P. aeruginosa rapidly responds to Pyocin L1 by radically remodelling its transcriptome and proteome, the cells appear to ultimately succumb to a process, likely driven by an extreme loss of outer envelope proteins. To assess the point of no return in this process, we determined the number of viable P. aeruginosa PAO1 cells at 120, 300 and 420 min post treatment with N or C-terminally tagged Pyocin L1 in liquid culture. Viable cells in N-terminally-tagged Pyocin L1-treated cultures increased modestly between 120 and 300 min but declined ~3-fold at 420 min post treatment (Fig. S14). Consistent with its reduced potency, viable cell numbers in cultures treated with Pyocin L1C-His increased between 120 and 300 min at a rate comparable to untreated cultures, before also declining ~3-fold at 420-min post treatment (Fig. S14). This indicates a slow bactericidal mode of action for Pyocin L1, with significant numbers of viable cells still present at 7 h post treatment.

To assess the consequences of this process on cell morphology, we performed cryo-ET on N-terminally 6xHis-tagged Pyocin L1 and darobactin-treated cells. We explored different timepoints post-treatment, settling on 420 min. At earlier time points (e.g., 120 min post treatment), gross morphological changes were rarely evident (Figs. 1a and S15). Cryo-ET imaging of untreated cells and 3D segmentation exhibited typical cell morphology for early stationary phase, with an intact cell envelope, an abundance of ribosomes, a polar flagellum and chemotaxis arrays (Figs. 6a–e and S16, and Supplementary Movie 7). In contrast, Pyocin L1-treated cells exhibited significant loss of outer membrane integrity, characterised by large holes/gaps, or delamination leading to outer membrane blebbing and particle-filled vesicle release (Figs. 6f–k, S16 and S17, and Supplementary Movie 8). The phenotype of darobactin-treated cells was similar, with subtle differences, including multilayered intracellular vesicle formation and rupture of the outer and inner membranes, which was not observed in Pyocin L1-treated cells (Figs. 6l–p, S16 and S17, and Supplementary Movie 9). Consistent with the omics data, the ribosome content of Pyocin L1-treated cells was substantially lower than that of wild-type cells. Ribosome numbers were also reduced in darobactin-treated cells, but to a lesser extent than observed in Pyocin L1-treated cells. Also, consistent with our omics data, T6SSs were significantly more abundant in Pyocin L1-treated cells compared to either darobactin-treated or untreated cells, confirming that recognition of Pyocin L1 triggers this anti-competition response. (Figs. 6, S16 and S17, and Supplementary Movies 7–9).

Discussion

Our work reveals that L-type pyocins are potent, evolutionarily tailored protein antibiotics that kill P. aeruginosa by targeting the BAM complex, an essential system for the insertion of OMPs38. We demonstrate that L-type pyocins bind to loop 6 of BamA and insert their C-terminal peptide into BamA, binding β-strand 1 and arresting BAM activity, which inhibits OMP assembly and compromises outer membrane homeostasis. This mechanism is remarkably like that of the bamabactin family peptides and represents a striking example of convergent evolution. Both families of molecules exploit the same vulnerability in Gram-negative bacterial physiology, reinforcing the validity of the BAM complex as an antibiotic target10,16,39,41,50. Crucially, this mechanism allows L-type pyocins to kill P. aeruginosa without the majority of the protein crossing the outer membrane, abrogating one of the most formidable permeability barriers in biology, and making them immune to efflux pump-mediated resistance7,8. The extensive interactions between L-type pyocins and BamA account for their narrow spectrum of activity and underlie their remarkable potency, more than 100-fold greater than that of bamabactins20,39–41.

Beyond establishing the structural and mechanistic basis for L-type pyocin BAM inhibition, we also comprehensively profile the cellular response to L-type pyocin exposure and BAM complex inhibition more generally. Transcriptomic analysis reveals that P. aeruginosa rapidly senses and responds to Pyocin L1, dramatically reprogramming gene expression in an attempt to mitigate the threat. This response is multifaceted: translation is sharply suppressed, which pre-emptively addresses the burden of mislocalized OMPs, and metabolic pathways are restructured to prepare for a shift from growth to survival. Upregulation of genes associated with the T6SS, biofilm formation, and quorum sensing indicates that the bacterium recognises Pyocin L1 as a weapon in interbacterial competition, prompting reciprocal aggression and community-level defense. Two hours after Pyocin L1 treatment, before an observable decline in growth rate or overt morphological damage, the transcriptional response shifts. There is a marked increase in the expression of genes associated with protein folding and outer envelope stress, hallmarks of a failure to compensate for BAM inhibition51,52. While these findings are strongly consistent with BAM inhibition being the primary driver of toxicity, future in vitro biochemical assays will be required to definitively uncouple direct BAM inhibition from broader envelope stress responses triggered during pyocin intoxication.

In the longer term, BAM inhibition by L-type pyocins triggers a collapse in outer membrane integrity. Our TraDIS data (see Supplementary Note 2) highlights the critical importance of cell envelope maintenance pathways, including LPS biosynthesis, outer membrane tethering and phospholipid transport and partitioning, in conferring greater tolerance to L-type pyocin attack. This is mirrored at the proteomic level with global reductions in OMPs, including essential BAM subunits and porins. Comparison of the cellular response to L-type pyocins and darobactin reveals both shared and distinct stress signatures. While both agents elicit a core BAM-inhibition response, their downstream effects diverge. L-type pyocins trigger sustained interbacterial aggression via T6SS48,49, whereas darobactin induces a metabolic shift indicative of oxidative stress adaptation. These differences also likely reflect the ecological origin of the molecules; L-type pyocins are recognised by P. aeruginosa as intraspecies weapons, while darobactin is produced by a phylogenetically distant organism that broadly targets BAM from many bacteria17,39.

Cryo-ET reveals the terminal consequences of L-type pyocin intoxication: structural disintegration of the outer membrane, loss of ribosomes, and deployment of the T6SS. These features illustrate the broad physiological collapse and response triggered by BAM inhibition, despite a concerted response to mitigate this damage. The morphological similarities between L-type pyocin- and darobactin-treated cells further reinforce that, despite compensatory stress responses, BAM inhibition ultimately leads to lethal outer membrane disruption. The proximal cause of this disintegration is halted OMP insertion; however, the consequences are far-reaching. These include the loss of LptD-dependent LPS insertion, outer membrane tethering, and permeability via porins and TBDTs. The dramatic increase in siderophore biosynthesis at later time points may reflect iron starvation due to reduced siderophore import in cells before outer membrane disruption. The outer membrane’s biophysical properties are also likely altered; phospholipid content likely increases and compensates for the dearth of proteins and LPS, decreasing membrane rigidity and increasing fluidity, which may explain the extreme blebbing and vesicle formation observed in L-type pyocin and darobactin-treated cells.

Together, our findings establish L-type pyocins as precision-guided inhibitors of the BAM complex, with both therapeutic potential and ecological significance. While promising, challenges exist in the development of L-type pyocins as potential therapeutics. Their activity is highly strain-specific, largely determined by variation in BamA loop 6 and surface components such as LPS, which may restrict their breadth of coverage and enable resistance through sequence variation or loss of surface receptors. Consistent with this, co-expression of susceptible and resistant BamA loop 6 variants produced only partial genetic rescue, suggesting that partial BAM inhibition may be sufficient for activity, and additional strain-specific factors likely modulate susceptibility to L-type pyocins. In addition, their proteinaceous nature may pose challenges for delivery, stability, and manufacturing compared to small-molecule antibiotics. However, these limitations may be mitigated through the development of engineered variants or cocktails targeting multiple loop 6 variants, alongside advances in protein design and formulation to improve stability and delivery. These results broaden our understanding of how bacteria target essential cellular machineries and open new avenues for engineering proteins to kill Gram-negative pathogens by targeting the BAM complex without entering the bacterial cell.

Methods

Bacterial strains and culturing conditions

For cloning, E. coli DH5α was grown in Lysogeny Broth (LB) (10 g/l Tryptone, 5 g/l Yeast extract, 5 g/l NaCl, 15 g/l agar) and maintained with antibiotics where appropriate. Liquid cultures were incubated at 37 °C with orbital shaking at 180 rpm. All P. aeruginosa strains could be grown on LB plates and in LB broth at 37 °C and 180–200 rpm shaking. The clinical isolates grew with a range of bright green to yellow pigments, and some tended to clump together but could be separated by resuspension with a 1 mL pipette.

For the overexpression of L-type pyocins, E. coli BL21(DE3)C41 was transformed with expression plasmids (Table S2). After 1 h of recovery, a starter culture was inoculated with 90% of the transformed E. coli and grown in LB broth with 50 µg/mL Kanamycin (pET28a, pET29b) or 100 µg/mL Ampicillin (pET23a) at 37 °C overnight at 180 rpm on a rotary shaker. The remaining 10% of the transformation was plated on LB agar with the appropriate antibiotic. On the next day, the main cultures were inoculated to a start OD600 of 0.05 and grown until OD600 of 0.8-1 in Terrific Broth (TB) (12 g/l Tryptone, 24 g/l Yeast extract, 12.3 g/l K2HPO4, 2.52 g/l KH2PO4) at 37 °C at 200 rpm. The cultures were cooled at 4 °C for 30 min and induced with 0.3 mM IPTG for expression for ~22 h at 20 °C at 200 rpm.

For BAM overexpression, E. coli BL21(DE3)C41 or P. aeruginosa PAO1 were transformed with pScRhaB2 expression plasmids and spread on LB plates with 100 µg/mL Trimethoprim and grown overnight at 37 °C53. The next day, a starter culture was inoculated with transformed cells and grown in LB broth with 100 µg/mL Trimethoprim at 37 °C overnight at 180 rpm on a rotary shaker. The main cultures were inoculated to a start OD600 of 0.05 and grown until OD600 of 0.8–1 in LB broth with 100 µg/mL Trimethoprim at 37 °C at 200 rpm. The cultures were cooled at 4 °C for 30 min and induced with 0.2% L-Rhamnose for expression for ~24 h at 25 °C at 200 rpm. Cells could be harvested and stored at −80 °C for several weeks without loss of protein yield. For native purification of BAM, a glycerol stock of P. aeruginosa PAO1::2xStrep-BamA was streaked on LB plates and grown overnight at 37 °C. The next day, a starter culture was inoculated with several colonies and grown in LB broth at 37 °C overnight at 180 rpm. The main cultures were inoculated to a start OD600 of 0.05 and grown in LB broth at 37 °C at 200 rpm for 15 h. It was important to use high-yielding culturing flasks to ensure good aeration and growth. The best yield was achieved when cultures were harvested while bright green.

Growth curves and L-type pyocin-treatment

For P. aeruginosa PAO1 growth experiments, a glycerol stock was streaked on LB plates and grown overnight at 37 °C. The next day, a culture was inoculated with several colonies and grown in LB broth at 37 °C at 180 rpm on a rotary shaker for ~20 h. The culture should reach the stationary phase with an OD600 of ~4.8. The next day, a fresh aliquot of purified Pyocin or darobactin was thawed and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Then, the Pyocin concentration was measured at a wavelength of 280 nm with a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). The growth curve cultures were inoculated to a start OD600 of 0.05 in 125 mL non-baffled Erlenmeyer flasks with 25 mL LB broth, and appropriate amounts of Pyocin, darobactin, and vancomycin were added. The cultures were grown in a shaking incubator at 37 °C at 200 rpm. The time points were taken every hour for 8 h, making sure that cultures spent the least possible amount of time outside of the shaker.

Plate-based growth inhibition assays

For P. aeruginosa PAO1 growth experiments, a glycerol stock was streaked on LB plates and grown overnight at 37 °C. The next day, cultures were inoculated in a 20-mL scintillation vial with several colonies and grown in 5 mL LB broth at 37 °C at 180 rpm on a rotary shaker. The cultures should reach stationary phases and have a range of bright green to yellow pigments depending on the strains. Plates were prepared with 6 mL of LB agar for round plates and 20 mL for square plates and cooled until solid. For the P. aeruginosa strain overlay, 0.6% agar was dissolved in boiling water. For round plates, 6 mL of 0.6% agar (A1296, Sigma-Aldrich) was transferred to a sterile scintillation vial, and when cool to the touch but not solid, 100 µL of freshly resuspended P. aeruginosa overnight culture was added and resuspended before being poured on top of the LB agar plate. For square plates, 12 mL of 0.6% agar was mixed with 200 µL of culture and overlayed in the same way. The agar-cell overlay had to cool completely to avoid condensation. Fresh aliquots of purified L-type pyocins were thawed and centrifuged at 20,000 × g for 5 min at 4 °C. Then, the L-type pyocin concentrations were measured at a wavelength of 280 nm with a NanoDrop Spectrophotometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Each L-type pyocin was prepared in a fivefold dilution series with 6 steps in room temperature buffer (50 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl), resulting in 1 mg/mL, 0.2 mg/mL, 0.04 mg/mL, 0.008 mg/mL, 0.0016 mg/mL and 0.00032 mg/mL. With a multi-channel pipette, 1 µL samples were applied to the square plates, leaving enough room to prevent the inhibition zones from touching. Round plates were prepared with 1 µL of 1 mg/mL Pyocins, except for Pyocin L1ΔC-term, which was used at 85 mg/mL. The synthesised Pyocin L1 C-terminal peptides were applied at a concentration of 20 mg/mL. The plates were incubated at 37 °C for no more than 16 h to avoid overgrowth of low-concentration inhibition zones. A growth inhibition event was determined according to the presence of visible inhibition zones and noted, as documentation of these inhibition zones was difficult and could occasionally not be done without loss of information.

For plate-based growth inhibition of P. aeruginosa PAO1 expressing PAO1-BAM or P28-BAM, electrocompetent cells were transformed with BAM expression plasmids and streaked on LB agar plates containing 100 µg/mL Trimethoprim and grown at 37 °C. Then, multiple colonies were used to inoculate an overnight culture, grown in LB at 37 °C at 200 rpm. The next day, a fresh starter culture was inoculated and grown to OD600 1.0, and BAM expression was induced with 0.2% L-Rhamnose, grown at 25 °C at 200 rpm for ~20 h. Plate inhibition assays were performed as described before with the addition of 0.2% L-Rhamnose and 100 µg/mL Trimethoprim in the LB agar to maintain selection and BAM expression.

Post Pyocin L1 treatment cell viability assay

P. aeruginosa PAO1 cultures were grown and treated with Pyocin L1 at a concentration of 1635 nM. At 2 h, 5 h, and 7 h post Pyocin L1-treatment, 1 mL of bacterial culture was harvested and centrifuged at 30,000 × g for 1 min at room temperature. At the same time, the optical density of the cell cultures was recorded. The cell pellet was resuspended in fresh, Pyocin L1-free LB broth and centrifuged again to remove any remaining Pyocin L1. Afterwards, the cells were resuspended in 1 mL of LB broth and diluted 1:100 in LB broth. For the spotting assay, serial tenfold dilutions were prepared, and 10 µL of the sample was spotted on LB agar plates in triplicate. The plates were grown at 37 °C for 18 h, after which colony-forming units (CFU) were counted, from which the viable cell number was calculated according to each sample’s volume and dilution factor.

BamA sequence analysis

To assess the presence and conservation of bamA, 572 publicly available P. aeruginosa genomes were screened using the BLASTN screening tool, Screen Assembly (v1.2.7)54, applying cut-offs of 80% identity and 80% reference length with 441 complete bamA sequences passing this threshold. PAO1 bamA was mapped to an additional 116 whole P. aeruginosa genomes using Geneious Prime (v2024.0.7) with default parameters. A total of 557 nucleotide and translated protein sequences were used for variation analysis by Clustal Omega (v1.2.4_1)55 and MUSCLE Alignment (v3.8.115)56,57, respectively, with default parameters. Aligned unique nucleotide sequences were trimmed using Noisy (v1.5.12.1)57,58, and phylogenetic trees were constructed using the maximum likelihood approach with IQ-Tree incorporating 1,000 ultrafast bootstrap (v3.0.1)59 with no outgroup and visualised using iTol (v7.2.1)60. The conservation of translated protein sequences was mapped to the Alphafold3 model (to include the in cryo-EM structure unmodelled POTRA 1 domain) of BamA using Chimera X (1.9)61. Unique BamA loop 6 amino acid sequences were extracted from translated protein sequences, aligned, and visualised using Geneious Prime.

Cloning

Constructs were generated with conventional restriction enzyme cloning and SLiCE cloning62. For PCR, Phusion High-Fidelity PCR Master Mix with GC buffer was used (Thermo Fisher Scientific, F532L). DNA sequences were confirmed by Nanopore sequencing (Primordium Labs, formerly Plasmidsaurus).

Allelic exchange

Allelic exchange was done to chromosomally 2xStrep-tag the BamA subunit of P. aeruginosa PAO1 for purification and to chromosomally exchange the variable loop 6 region of PAO1-BamA with the loop 6 of P28-BamA. The pEX18Gm constructs were cloned as described before, and the allelic exchange method was adapted and simplified from Hmelo et al.63. To make electrocompetent P. aeruginosa PAO1, cells were streaked from a glycerol stock onto LB agar and grown at 37 °C overnight (~18 h). Then, 5 mL of LB broth in a 20 mL scintillation vial was inoculated with a single colony and grown for 20 h at 42 °C in an incubator without shaking. The next day, 4 mL of the culture was harvested in 4 sterile 1.7 mL reaction tubes by centrifugation at 20,000 × g speed at room temperature. The supernatant was discarded, and the pellet was resuspended in 1 mL of room temperature 1 mM MgSO4 and re-centrifuged. The supernatant was discarded, and the cell pellets were resuspended in another 1 mL of 1 mM MgSO4. Finally, the cells were centrifuged as before and combined in a total of 50 µL of 1 mM MgSO4.

On the same day, the electrocompetent cells were mixed with 4 µL of allelic exchange plasmid at a concentration of >2 µg/µL and incubated at room temperature for 20 min. Electroporation was done at room temperature with a MicroPulser Electroporator (Bio-Rad) at 2.2 kV with a decaying exponential waveform (25 μF capacitor, 600 Ω). Then, 1 mL of LB broth was added to the cells before recovery for 3 h in an orbital shaker at 37 °C and 200 rpm. The cells were centrifuged at 3000 × g for 5 min, after which they were resuspended in 100 µL of LB and spread on an LB plate containing 20 µg/mL Gentamycin. Within 20 h of growth at 37 °C, large positive colonies appeared, but also many smaller negative colonies. A single colony was streaked on a no-salt LB agar plate containing 15% (w/v) sucrose and grown at 30 °C overnight. The same colony could be used for an overnight culture to isolate gDNA and perform PCR to confirm the colony as merodiploid by PCR with primers binding in the chromosome and plasmid. From the plate, 8–10 colonies were each resuspended in 50 µL sterile phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), after which 3 µL of each colony was patch plated on LB agar, no-salt LB agar containing 15% (w/v) sucrose, and LB agar with 20 µg/mL Gentamycin. In this step, the cells are selected for double cross-over mutants, which means that the allelic exchange plasmid is excised from the chromosome, but the DNA sequence to be exchanged stays in the chromosome, having replaced the original DNA sequence. Positive colonies will grow on plates with LB agar and no-salt LB agar with 15% (w/v) sucrose, but not on LB agar with Gentamycin. The allelic exchange can be verified by isolating a colony’s gDNA and performing PCR with a primer binding in the chromosome and the new exchange DNA sequence.

BAM purification

For BAM purification from overexpression cultures of E. coli BL21(DE3)C41, cultures were grown as described before. The protein yield was ~30 µg/L of expression culture. Cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mg/mL lysozyme, 0.05 mg/mL DNase, 1× protease inhibitor) and homogenised with a glass homogeniser before lysis with a high-pressure cell disruptor. The lysate was centrifuged at 27,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C. Then, the supernatant was centrifuged at 100,000 × g for 1 h at 4 °C to obtain the bacterial cell membranes. The supernatant was discarded, and the dark red membrane pellet was resuspended in 30 mL solubilization buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 1% N-dodecyl-β-D-maltoside (DDM), 300 µL biotin blocking buffer (BioLock, 2-0205-050)) with a glass homogeniser. The homogenised membranes were solubilized at room temperature for 30 min on a slow orbital shaker. After solubilization, the membranes were centrifuged at 30,000 × g for 40 min, after which the supernatant was loaded twice on a Strep-Tactin XT 1-mL column (Cytiva) with an Äkta start and Äkta pure purification systems (Cytiva). The DDM detergent was exchanged with lauryl maltose neopentyl glycol (LMNG) detergent by applying 30 column volumes (CV) of detergent-exchange buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 0.025% LMNG. Bound protein was eluted by hand with a syringe containing 10 mL elution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 0.025% LMNG, 75 mM biotin) and collected in 1 mL fractions. After 4 mL, the column was incubated in elution buffer for 5 min on ice to optimise protein elution. The fractions were investigated via SDS-PAGE with Coomassie-staining. Clean fractions were pooled and concentrated in a 15-mL concentrator (Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter, 30 kDa MWCO) to a final volume of 500 µL for injection on a Superose 6 10/300 Increase column for size exclusion chromatography (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 8, 150 mM NaCl, 0.01% LMNG). The BAM complex eluted at the expected retention volume, and appropriate fractions were concentrated to the highest possible concentrations to ~200 µL volume to avoid protein loss by excessive use of the concentrator. Purified BAM could be stored in size exclusion buffer at −80 °C without disassembly or degradation of the complex.

To remove LMNG from the sample, BAM was thawed and diluted in two times in 15 mL detergent-free buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 8, 150 mM NaCl), then iteratively re-concentrated in a 15-mL concentrator and then 0.5-mL concentrator (Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filters, 100 kDa MWCO). Because of the high dilution, monomeric LMNG would pass through the filter. The LMNG concentration could be diluted to below the critical micelle concentration because LMNG has a low dissociation rate when the concentration is below the critical micelle concentration. This created favourable detergent-free conditions for the usage of purified BAM.

For the native purification of BAM from P. aeruginosa PAO1, the aforementioned protocol was applied with only a few comments. The protein yield was ~7 µg/L of bacterial cell culture. After the lysate was centrifuged at 15,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C and depending on how clear the supernatant was, it had to be centrifuged a second time at 27,000 × g for 10 min. Lastly, LptD was commonly co-purified, which was not the case for BAM purification from E. coli overexpression cultures.

L-type pyocin purification

L-type pyocins were purified from overexpression cultures of E. coli BL21(DE3)C41 as described before. The protein yield ranged from 1 to 30 mg/L of expression culture, which was highly dependent on the L-type Pyocin and the location of the His6-tag, where N-terminal His6-tags can cause a lower L-type Pyocin yield. Cell pellets were resuspended in lysis buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole, 2 mM MgCl2, 0.1 mg/mL lysozyme, 0.05 mg/mL DNase, 1× protease inhibitor) and homogenised with a glass homogeniser before lysis with a high-pressure cell disruptor. The lysate was centrifuged at 30,000 × g for 20 min at 4 °C and loaded twice on a HisTrap 5 mL column for immobilised metal affinity chromatography on Äkta start and Äkta pure purification systems (Cytiva). After loading, the column was washed with buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 500 mM NaCl, 5 mM imidazole) until the UV absorption at 280 nm read close to 0. For elution, a step gradient (5%, 10%, 25%, 50%, and 100%) of Elution buffer (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl, 1 M Imidazole) was applied, with L-type pyocins eluting mostly at 250 mM imidazole. Relevant elution fractions were investigated by SDS-PAGE and Coomassie-staining, and appropriate fractions were pooled. The protein was concentrated in a 15-mL concentrator (Amicon Ultra Centrifugal Filter, 30 kDa MWCO) to a final volume of 10 mL for subsequent injection on a Superdex 26/600 S200 pg column to perform size exclusion chromatography (20 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.4, 150 mM NaCl). To obtain BAM in complex with L-type pyocins suitable for cryo-EM sample preparation, BAM was purified as described before. Molecular dynamics simulations

Classical atomistic molecular dynamics (MD) simulations were carried out on the two cryo-EM structures: PAO1-BAM-Pyocin L1 pre-inhibited complex (2.84 Å, PDB: 9PXI), and P28-BAM-Pyocin L2 fully inhibited complex (3.18 Å, PDB: 9PXJ). To keep the system size tractable and achieve sufficient simulation sampling, the model systems comprising Pyocin (L1 or L2) and BamA subunits were constructed, in which the domains POTRA 1 to POTRA 4 (residue IDs 1 to 381 of BamA) were excluded. All titratable residues were set to their standard protonation states. The protein system was embedded into a lipid bilayer representing the composition of the outer membrane of gram-negative bacteria, with the inner leaflet consisting of 75% phosphoethanolamine (PPPE), 20% phosphoglycerol (PVPG), and 5% cardiolipin (PVCL2) lipids, and the outer leaflet carrying lipopolysaccharides (LPSA) without O antigen units (see below). To compensate for the negative charge of the lipid bilayer (LPSA: charge -10e; PVCL2: charge -2e; PVPG: charge -1e), the system was neutralised by Ca2+ ions, and solvated with 150 mM of Na+ and Cl- ions. The placement of the protein-membrane model system in a periodic water box of 15 nm × 17 nm × 21 nm resulted in a total count of ~390,000 atoms (Table S3).

For the construction and relaxation of lipid bilayers, a pure membrane system was constructed and relaxed by MD simulations. The G-OM membrane patch from gram-negative bacteria73,74 (size 8.1 nm × 8.1 nm) was replicated 8 times, yielding a larger patch of 24 nm × 24 nm. After immersing the larger membrane patch in a water box, and neutralising it with Ca2+ ions, and 150 mM of Na+ and Cl− ions, the system was minimised with a conjugate-gradient algorithm implemented in NAMD (v. 2.14)75 for 1000 steps with constraints on the phosphorous atoms (force constant 4.1 × 104 kJmol−1 nm-2). The same atoms were constrained in an additional energy minimisation in GROMACS (v. 2022.3)76 with a force constant of 2.0 × 104 kJmol−1 nm−2 and force tolerance of 250 kJmol−1 nm−1, followed by a 50-ns equilibration in an NPT ensemble. Finally, a production run of 100 ns produced a relaxed membrane patch, which was used for the construction of our protein-membrane systems (see above). Both pre-inhibited PAO1-BamA-Pyocin L1 and inhibited P28-BamA-Pyocin L2 simulations were constructed by aligning the respective BamA subunits to a BAM structure available in the OPM database (PDB: 4K3B).

In order to study the conformational dynamics of BamA-Pyocin complex, we constructed four different simulation setups based on the pre-inhibited and inhibited structures (see above), and in the presence and absence of Pyocin (Table S3). To eliminate possible steric clashes, each protein-membrane setup was energy minimised using the conjugate-gradient algorithm in NAMD (v. 2.14)75 for 1000 steps with positional harmonic restraints (force constant 4.1 × 104 kJ mol−1 nm−2 on lipid phosphorous atoms, and protein heavy atoms, excluding the modelled regions (Table S3, see also main text). This was followed by an additional energy minimization in GROMACS (v. 2022.3; force tolerance of 250 kJ mol−1 nm−1) with the same atoms constrained (force constant 2.0 × 104 kJ mol−1 nm−2). Equilibration of the constructed model system was achieved with a sequence of MD simulation steps. First, a short 5-ns equilibration with a timestep of 1 fs was performed (with the harmonic restraints on protein heavy atoms and lipid/LPS phosphorous atoms), allowing the system’s pressure and temperature to equilibrate to 1.0132 bar and 310 K (37 °C), respectively, while maintaining the structural integrity of the protein and the membrane. After that, we released the constraints from lipids (keeping restraints on the protein heavy atoms) and performed a 100-ns equilibration with a 2-fs timestep, leading to the proper relaxation of the mixed lipid bilayer. The last step involved equilibration with the protein backbone restraints (force constant 2.0 × 104 kJ mol−1 nm−2) for 15 ns, where during the last 5 ns, the restraints from the modelled regions were released. The production simulations were obtained in a set of 3 replicas, ~1 μs each (Table S3), where replicas 2 and 3 were generated via extending the second equilibration step by 1 ns and 2 ns, respectively.

CHARMM36m force field77 was used for protein, while lipids and ions were described by CHARMM3678–80. TIP3P model was applied to describe water solvent81. NBFIX corrections were applied to Na+, Cl−, and Ca2+ ions, as implemented in CHARMM36 force field78,80,82. Integration timestep of 2 fs was achieved via applying rigid constraints to all covalent bonds to hydrogen atoms with the LINCS algorithm83. Short-range non-bonded interactions were described by the Verlet84 cutoff scheme with the switching and cutoff distances of 1.0 nm and 1.2 nm, respectively, whereas long-range electrostatics was treated by the particle mesh Ewald method85. During the equilibration runs, temperature was maintained at 310 K using the V-rescale thermostat86, while a stable pressure of 1.0132 bar was achieved by the Berendsen barostat87. At the production phase, the thermostat was switched to Nose-Hoover88,89, and the barostat to Parrinello-Rahman90.

All simulation trajectories were analysed with MDAnalysis (v. 2.7.0)91 and VMD (v. 1.9.4) visualisation tool. Hydrogen bonds were calculated using the Hbonds plugin (v. 1.2) implemented in VMD92.

RNA preparation from Pyocin L1 and darobactin-treated cells

RNA for sequencing was derived from four independent experiments (n = 4; Biological replicates). An overnight culture of P. aeruginosa PAO1 was subcultured with a 1/100 dilution and grown for 2 h and 15 min at 37 °C with shaking. The culture was then aliquoted into 2 mL samples, and darobactin or Pyocin L1 (N-terminal 6xHis-tagged cleaved using TEV protease) was added at 1 µg/mL (MIC) (or no antibiotic for untreated reference). 500 µL samples were taken at 15 min and 120 min timepoints and added to RNA Protect solution (Qiagen). The samples were left for 5 min at room temperature, spun down, supernatant removed, and then frozen at −80 °C until the RNA was extracted. The untreated control was only sampled at the 15-min timepoint. The Qiagen RNAprotect Bacteria Reagent Handbook protocol 4, protocol 7 and on-column DNase digestion using the Qiagen RNase-free DNase set were followed. 15 µL of each of the three technical replicates was combined to give 1 biological replicate (12 samples in total for sequencing).

RNA sequencing and bioinformatic analysis

RNA-seq libraries were sequenced at the Wellcome Trust Sanger Institute on an Illumina HiSeq 2500 using 2 × 100 bp paired-end runs. Reads containing ambiguous bases (“N”) or lacking all four nucleotides were discarded, and the remaining reads were aligned to the P. aeruginosa PAO1 genome (GenBank GCA_000006765.1) using BWA-SW v0.7.1094. For each read, the best alignment was retained based on mapping quality, and reads with multiple equally good hits were removed. Because the library preparation method captures essentially all transcripts, including small RNAs, read lengths were highly variable. We therefore quantified expression as the total number of nucleotides mapping to each gene (using the PseudoCAP GFF3 annotation95), normalized by gene length and total genic coverage, analogous to Transcripts per million96. Differential expression was assessed using edgeR97, which uses a negative binomial model and does not assume normally distributed data. To account for multiple testing across all genes, P-values were adjusted using the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure to control the false discovery rate (FDR). Genes were considered differentially expressed where Log2FC > 0.5 and −log10FDR > 1.3.

Proteomic analysis of Pyocin L1 and darobactin-treated cells

An overnight culture of P. aeruginosa PAO1 was aliquoted and diluted into fresh lysogeny broth (LB) medium to an OD₆₀₀ of 0.1. Cultures were treated with either 3 µg/mL N-terminally 6xHis-tagged Pyocin L1 or 1 µg/mL darobactin, alongside an untreated wild-type (WT) control, with four biological replicates per condition. Cultures were incubated at 37 °C with shaking at 180 rpm for 300 min, after which cells were harvested and washed twice with PBS.

The whole cell lysates were processed for proteomic analysis using an S‑Trap (PROFIT) –based workflow. Protein samples were solubilized in 50 µL of SDS buffer containing 5% SDS and 50 mM triethylammonium bicarbonate (TEAB, pH 7.55), reduced with tris(2‑carboxyethyl)phosphine (TCEP) to a final concentration of 10 mM, and incubated at 37 °C for 45 min. Alkylation was performed in the dark with iodoacetamide (IAA) at a final concentration of 55 mM under the same temperature and duration. Samples were acidified to approximately 2.5% phosphoric acid, and S‑Trap binding buffer (90% methanol, 100 mM TEAB, pH 7.1) was added before loading onto S‑Trap columns. Bound proteins were washed three times with binding buffer, then digested on‑column overnight at 37 °C with trypsin (1:10 enzyme‑to‑protein ratio, prepared in 50 mM TEAB). Peptides were sequentially eluted with 40 µL of 50 mM TEAB, 0.2% aqueous formic acid, and 35 µL of 50% acetonitrile with 0.2% formic acid. The combined eluates were centrifuged, lyophilized, and reconstituted in 2% acetonitrile with 0.1% trifluoroacetic acid for subsequent LC‑MS/MS analysis.LC-MS/MS Analysis: Thermo Fisher Scientific Orbitrap Eclipse and Thermo Fisher Scientific Orbitrap Ascend.

For LC–MS/MS analysis, lyophilized peptide samples were analysed with a Thermo Fisher Scientific Orbitrap Eclipse Tribrid mass spectrometer coupled to an Ultimate 3000 RSLC nano-flow reversed-phase HPLC system (Dionex). The nano-LC was equipped with an Acclaim PepMap 100 C18 nano-trap column (100 Å pore size, 75 µm × 2 cm, Thermo Fisher Scientific) and an Acclaim PepMap RSLC C18 analytical column (100 Å pore size, 75 µm × 50 cm, Thermo Fisher Scientific). Mobile phases consisted of solvent A (0.1% formic acid in LC–MS–grade water) and solvent B (100% ACN with 0.1% formic acid). For each run, peptides were loaded onto the trap column at 6 µL/min in an isocratic solution of 2% ACN and 0.05% TFA for 6 min before being switched in-line with the analytical column. Peptides were separated over a 95 min gradient: 3% B at 0–6 min, increased to 4% B at 7 min, then to 25% B at 82 min, followed by a ramp to 40% B at 86 min and to 80% B at 87 min; the column was held at 80% B until 90 min, then re-equilibrated at 3% B until 95 min. The mass spectrometer was operated in positive ion mode with a spray voltage of 1900 V, ion transfer tube temperature of 275 °C, and higher-energy collisional dissociation (HCD) collision gas pressure of 1 mTorr. Data were acquired in data-independent acquisition (DIA) mode with an initial full MS1 scan in the Orbitrap (m/z 350–1400; resolution 120,000 at m/z 200; AGC target 1 × 10⁶; maximum injection time 50 ms; profile mode) followed by DIA MS2 scans in the Orbitrap at a resolution of 30,000 (m/z 200–2000; AGC target 1 × 10⁶; maximum injection time 55 ms; centroid mode) using HCD at 30% normalized collision energy. Fifty sequential quadrupole isolation windows (13.7 m/z width, 1 m/z overlap) were used to cover the precursor m/z range of 361–1033, providing ~9 points across chromatographic peaks with a total cycle time of 3 s.

Data analysis

Global proteomics raw data were processed in Spectronaut (version 19.1.240806.62635, Huggins) using default BGS factory settings. Searches were performed against the P. aeruginosa PAO1 (October 2024 release, 26,816 protein entries). DIA analysis employed maximum intensity for m/z extraction, with precursor and protein identifications filtered at a q-value threshold of 0.01 and a protein PEP cutoff of 0.75. Decoys were generated by mutation using a dynamic decoy limit strategy. Protein quantification was MS2-based with automatic and cross-run normalization, as well as interference correction. For peptide identification, the directDIA+ workflow was applied using Trypsin/P as the proteolytic enzyme, allowing up to two missed cleavages. Carbamidomethylation of cysteine was specified as a fixed modification, while oxidation of methionine and acetylation of protein N-termini were set as variable modifications. Peptide and protein identifications were controlled at a 1% FDR. Differential abundance analysis was performed using Welch’s t-test (unpaired, unequal variances, no variance filtering or group-wise correction), with p-values adjusted using q-value confidence filtering (FDR threshold of 0.05). A log₂ fold-change cutoff of 1 was applied to define differentially abundant proteins. MSMS data have been deposited to the ProteomeXchange Consortium, via the PRIDE partner repository, with the dataset identifier PXD067326.

P. aeruginosa PAO1 transposon library preparation for TraDIS

A high-density P. aeruginosa PAO1 transposon mutant library was generated using biparental conjugation with E. coli S17-1 harbouring plasmid pUT-mini-Tn5-pro. Donor and recipient strains were streaked on LB agar and LB agar supplemented with 15 μg/mL gentamicin (LBA/Gent15), respectively, and incubated overnight at 37 °C. Overnight cultures were established in LB or LB/Gent15 and diluted to OD600 = 0.1 for subculturing. After 7 h incubation at 37 °C with shaking, 200 μL of donor and 100 μL of recipient were each spread onto 35 pre-warmed 1.5% LBA or LBA/Gent15 plates using sterile glass beads and incubated overnight in humidified conditions (donor at 37 °C, recipient at 44.5 °C). The next day, VBMA (1.5% agar in Vogel-Bonner (VB) minimal medium) and VBMA/Gent30 (VBMA with 30 μg/mL gentamicin) plates were prepared by autoclaving agar in water (12 g in 720 mL), cooling, and supplementing with VBMA components (80 mL per 800 mL) and filter-sterilised gentamicin. Cells from conjugation spots were scraped, resuspended in LB, and plated onto VBMA and VBMA/Gent30 plates at multiple dilutions (10−4 to 10−12) for recovery of transconjugants. After 16 h at 37 °C, colonies were counted and pooled by conjugation set (A–D), resuspended in LB + 15% glycerol, and aliquoted into 1 mL cryovials for storage at –80 °C. CFU and OD600 measurements were recorded, and mutant numbers were estimated to exceed 2 × 106 total transformants.

Cryo-electron tomography

P. aeruginosa PAO1 cells were treated with N-terminally 6xHis-tagged Pyocin L1 and darobactin as described before. At the desired treatment time, just before grid preparation, cultures were centrifuged and resuspended in ice-cold PBS buffer, and the OD600 was adjusted to ~1. Untreated, Pyocin L1- and darobactin-treated P. aeruginosa cultures were mixed in a 4:1 ratio with 10 nm colloidal gold beads that had been precoated with 1% BSA (Sigma-Aldrich, Australia). 4 µL of culture was applied to glow-discharged copper R2/2 Quantifoil holey carbon grids (Quantifoil Micro Tools GmbH, Jena, Germany). Depending on the sample, grids were blotted for 4–5 s (under 100% humidity conditions) with a blot force of 6 and plunged into liquid ethane using a Vitrobot Mark IV, FEI (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Tilt series were collected automatically with the FEI Tomography 5 software from −60° to +60° at 2° intervals with a defocus of −6 µm and a total electron fluence of 120 e-/Å2 at a pixel size of 3.4 Å using an FEI Titan Krios G4, 300 keV FEG transmission electron microscope (Thermo Fisher Scientific), equipped with a BioQuantum K3 Imaging Filter (slit width 20 eV), and a K3 direct electron detector (Gatan) in movie mode (4 dose fractions).

Tilt series movies were motion corrected and split into odd/even using motioncor3. Odd-even tilt series were pre-processed to bin4 (13.56 Å/pix), aligned using fiducial tracking, and reconstructed by weighted-back projection in IMOD (Version 5.1). Cryo-CARE software99 was used to denoise tomograms using odd and even tomograms and a U-Net 2.5 trained model (training was done using a subset of tomograms from each data collection). 2D slice Images of 3D tomograms were generated using 3dmod (IMOD, version 5.1) using n = 4 slices. Denoised tomograms were binned further (bin8, 27.12 Å/pix) for processing by 3D segmentations. All these steps were done using the scipion3 framework. Visualisation and segmentation of tomograms were done using the Dragonfly software (https://www.theobjects.com/dragonfly/index.html). Tomograms were pre-processed using built-in histogram equalization filters. Segmentation was performed within the well-resolved regions of the tomograms, avoiding areas affected by the missing wedge. Discontinuities in the outer membrane were identified within this viewable volume and were not aligned with the missing wedge direction, supporting that these features do not arise from reconstruction artefacts. Neural network 5-class U-Net (with 2.5D input of 5 slices) was trained on tomogram slices to recognise P. aeruginosa inner and outer membranes, ribosomes, flagella, T6SS, and cytoplasmic DNA using 5 rounds of optimisation. The software’s built-in tools for exporting 2D images and creating 3D movies were utilised. Movies were annotated and edited using DaVinci Resolve software.

To assess the effect of Pyocin L1 treatment, we implemented a semi-quantitative scoring framework assessing overall cellular integrity and outer membrane condition across tomograms. Cellular condition was scored on a five-point ordinal scale: 1, intact cells with fully contained intracellular contents; 2, mild membrane perturbations with ribosomes clearly present; 3, pronounced membrane perturbations accompanied by reduced ribosome density and evident inner membrane invaginations; 4, partial loss of cellular contents; and 5, complete lysis with absence of intracellular material. Outer membrane integrity was evaluated independently using a separate five-point scale: 1, intact outer membrane; 2, evidence of membrane shedding; 3, presence of a small number of discrete outer membrane perforations; 4, fewer but larger membrane discontinuities; and 5, extensive disruption characterised by large and frequent perforations.

Bioinformatics analysis of protein sequences

Protein sequences (BAM and L-type pyocins) were aligned in MEGA11 v11.0.13 using the built-in ClustalW alignment tool (Multiple Sequence alignment: Gap Opening Penalty 10.00, Gap Extension Penalty 0.20, Divergent Cutoff 30% delayed). The software TreeViewer v2.2.0 was used for the generation of phylogenetic trees with neighbour-joining of an aligned sequence file (BLOSUM62 distance model and 1000 bootstrap replicates). The STRING webserver was used for analysis of TraDIS, RNA sequencing and proteomics dataset100.

Protein structure prediction

For the prediction of protein structures not presented in the manuscript, AlphaFold2 multimer was used on the MASSIVE M3 High-Performance Computing cluster101,102. The structure of the P. aeruginosa PAO1 BAM complex (BamA–E) was predicted using AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., Nature 630, 493–500, 2024; DOI 10.1038/s41586-024-07487-w) via the AlphaFold Server (https://alphafoldserver.com; accessed 22nd April 2025)103. Input sequences corresponded to NCBI accessions WP_003092382.1 (BamA), WP_003113803.1 (BamB), WP_003112545.1 (BamC), WP_003102590.1 (BamD) and WP_003113904.1 (BamE), with N-terminal signal/lipoprotein signal sequences removed; the exact sequences are provided as FASTA in Supplementary Data 5. The fourth-ranked model (ranking score 0.70) is presented, with per-residue pLDDT and predicted aligned error shown in Fig. S7. Atomic coordinates and the AlphaFold3 confidence outputs are provided as Supplementary Data 5.
