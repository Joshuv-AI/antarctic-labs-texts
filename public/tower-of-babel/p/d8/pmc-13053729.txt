Hypoalbuminemia induces immunosuppression through disorder in macrophages and drives resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors
Authors: Fan Yang, Yaping Long, Yao Li, Yibing Bai, Yining Liu, Dingyuan Cheng, Tao Li, Yiheng Lu, Xiao Han, Yi Hu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Hypoalbuminemia is a common clinical biomarker associated with poor outcomes in cancer patients. While it correlates with resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), the direct functional role and underlying mechanisms in driving immunotherapy resistance remain unclear. In our study, we demonstrated that hypoalbuminemia directly drives ICIs resistance by orchestrating an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment (TME) through impaired macrophage arginine metabolism. In a C57/BL model, low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia mice bearing LLC tumors showed poor response to immunotherapy. Comprehensive immune profiling revealed a suppressed TME, characterized by reduced CD8+ T cell infiltration and increased macrophage abundance. Depletion of tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs) alleviated this immunosuppression. Transcriptomic analysis of TAMs from low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia mice revealed a significant downregulation of the arginine biosynthesis pathway, which was consistent with observed reductions in systemic arginine levels. Crucially, dietary arginine supplementation successfully reversed the immunosuppressive phenotype and restored ICIs efficacy. However, this restorative effect of arginine is significantly dependent on its action on macrophages. Our findings establish low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia as a key driver of immunotherapy resistance, unveil a novel metabolic-immune circuit centered on TAM arginine metabolism, and identify arginine replenishment as a potential therapeutic strategy to reverse immune suppression in hypoalbuminemia patients.Supplementary informationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00262-026-04364-5.

Introduction

Cancer immunotherapy has emerged as a revolutionary breakthrough in the field of oncology, significantly transforming the treatment landscape for various malignancies by activating the patient’s own immune system to combat cancer. Immune checkpoint inhibitors have received approval and became standard therapy in clinical practice [1–5]. However, in real-world settings, some patients exhibit primary or acquired immune resistance leading to suboptimal response rates and substantially limiting the broader application of immunotherapy [6–8]. Therefore, it’s crucial to identify biomarkers that can predict treatment efficacy and prognosis, as well as understanding the underlying mechanisms of primary or secondary resistance.

Serum albumin (ALB), a critical marker of nutritional and inflammatory status, has consistently been identified in retrospective studies as a negative predictor of immunotherapy efficacy[9]. In research practice, composite nutritional indexes incorporate serum albumin as a central component such as the Prognostic Nutritional Index (PNI)[10] also served as prognostic biomarkers for immunotherapy. Compared with static parameters like body mass index (BMI), declining serum albumin more accurately reflects deteriorating nutritional status and is more strongly associated with unfavorable immunotherapy outcomes [10]. Our previous studies further demonstrated a significant association between baseline hypoalbuminemia and hyperprogression during immunotherapy [11]. Collectively, these findings underscore hypoalbuminemia as a clinically relevant risk factor prior to treatment initiation. Nevertheless, whether hypoalbuminemia directly contributes to immunotherapy resistance remains unresolved. Elucidating the molecular pathways by which hypoalbuminemia alters immune function and influences therapeutic tolerance is therefore essential to guide the development of targeted interventions.

In this study, we first conducted a retrospective analysis of a previously established patient cohort and reaffirmed the association between low serum albumin levels and unfavorable immunotherapy outcomes. We then developed a mouse model of low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia and administered immunotherapy in experimental groups, directly demonstrating that a low-albumin microenvironment drives treatment resistance. To further explore the mechanism by which low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia contributes to immunosuppression, we conducted experiments including RNA sequencing, immunohistochemistry, flow cytometry, cytokine detection, cell depletion, and metabolite supplementation, ultimately identifying the critical role of alterations in the arginine metabolism pathway of macrophages.

Materials and methods

Clinical patient selection

The patients who received anti-PD-1 therapy were retrospectively collected from January 2015 to January 2020 in the Chinese PLA general hospital based on the previous study of our group [11]. Patients enrolled in this study were supposed to have (a) the pathological diagnosis of patients identified as carcinoma by biopsy or surgical resection. (b) Assessable target lesions could be measured by Computer Tomography (CT) or Magnetic Resonance Imaging (MRI) scan based on Response Evaluation Criteria in Solid Tumors (RECIST) 1.1. (c) Available serum albumin level test. The study was approved by ethics committee of the PLA General Hospital following the Declaration of Helsinki and its subsequent revisions (Approval: S2023-121–01). Written consent had obtained from participants in the study.

Cell lines and cell culture

Lewis Lung cancer cell line was obtained from the Type Culture Collection of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, Shanghai, China, and authenticated by STR profiling before distribution. The cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco) supplemented with 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) and at 37 °C in a humidified atmosphere with 5% CO2.

Animal experiments

All animal experiments were performed according to the ARRIVE guidelines and approved by the ethic committee of the Chinese PLA General Hospital (No. IACUC-DWZX-2023–524). C57BL/6J mice were purchased from Beijing SPF (Beijing) Biotechnology Co., Ltd. The light cycle consisted of light for 12 h and dark for 12 h. Twenty female mice at 5 weeks were first evenly divided into two feeding groups: control diet (normal-protein diet, Beijing SPF Biotechnology) group or low-protein diet[12] (1% protein, Beijing SPF Biotechnology) group. Serum albumin levels were monitored weekly until a significant difference was observed between the two groups, indicating the successful establishment of a low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia mouse model. To prevent hepatic steatosis, mice with hypoalbuminemia were subsequently returned to a normal diet [12]. Each mouse was then injected for 1 * 106 LLC cells on the right flank subcutaneously. On day 7 post-inoculation, the mice in each feeding group were randomly assigned to either anti-PD-1 inhibitor (50 mg/kg) treatment group or an IgG (50 mg/kg) control group, and the corresponding treatments were administered on day 7 and every 3 days. IgG and Ultra-LEAFTM purified anti-mouse CD279 (PD-1) Monoclonal Antibody were purchased from BioLegend. Tumor volumes were calculated using the equation Volume = (length × width2)/2. The maximal tumor size permitted by ethics committee was within 2000 mm3, and the maximal tumor size in our study was not exceeded. Body weight and serum albumin level were tested every week. Mice were anesthetized by intraperitoneal injection of 1% sodium pentobarbital at a dose of 75 mg/kg body weight. At the endpoint of the experiment, the mice were euthanized by cervical dislocation.

For in vivo macrophage depletion assay, a total of 10 hypoalbuminemia mice were equally divided into two groups. The anti-colony-stimulating factor 1 receptor (CSF1R) inhibitor PLX3397 (MCE, HY-16749) or saline was administered orally at a dose of 50 mg/kg since D1 after inoculation and every other day until sacrifice.

For in vivo arginine supplementation experiment, a total of 15 hypoalbuminemia mice were randomly divided into three treatment groups: (a) PD-1 monoclonal antibody, (b) PD-1 monoclonal antibody + 150 μg/mL arginine, and (c) PD-1 monoclonal antibody + 300 μg/mL arginine. Serum arginine level was tested by Arg Content Assay Kit (Solarbio, BC5635). L-Arginine was administered via intraperitoneal injection, concurrently with PD-1 monoclonal antibody, starting from D7 after tumor inoculation, once every three days until sacrifice.

All experiments were repeated three times, yielding consistent results.

RNA sequence and analyses

Total RNA was extracted from tissue samples or TAM cells using Trizol. mRNA was purified with Oligo(dT) magnetic beads. Reference genomes and annotations were obtained from the ENSEMBL database. Gene expression levels were quantified using FPKM after counting reads with HTSeq. Differential expression analysis was performed with DEGseq, defining DEGs as genes with q ≤ 0.05 and |log2 ratio|≥ 1. GO and KEGG enrichment analyses were conducted using the hypergeometric test, with whole-genome genes as background.

Immunohistochemistry (IHC) assay

Tumor and spleen tissues from mice were collected, fixed in formalin, and embedded in paraffin. Sections (4–5 µm) were cut for staining. For H&E, sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin following standard dewaxing and rehydration. For IHC, after antigen retrieval and peroxidase blocking, sections were incubated with anti-CD3 or anti-CD8 primary antibodies, followed by an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Signal was visualized with DAB, and sections were counterstained with hematoxylin. All slides were examined by light microscopy.

Flow cytometry

Tumors were digested into single-cell suspensions using a Tumor Dissociation Kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130–096–730). Red blood cells were lysed, and the remaining cells were washed with PBS. For T cell function analysis, cells were stimulated with Cell Activation Cocktail (with Brefeldin A) for 4 h, stained with Zombie UV for viability, followed by surface markers (CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8) and intracellular cytokines (IFN-γ, GZMB, perforin) after fixation/permeabilization using True-Nuclear™ Buffer Set. For T cell exhaustion and Treg analysis, cells were stained with Zombie UV, surface antibodies (CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8, CD25, PD-1, TIM-3), then fixed/permeabilized and stained for FOXP3. For proliferation, cells were stained with Zombie Aqua, surface markers (CD45, CD3, CD4, CD8), then fixed/permeabilized with FOXP3 Fix/Perm Buffer and stained for Ki-67. For myeloid cells, cells were stained with Zombie NIR, surface antibodies (CD45, CD11b, F4/80, CD86, Ly-6C, Ly-6G, Gr-1), then fixed/permeabilized with Cyto-Fast™ Fix/Perm Buffer and stained for CD206. All antibodies and permeabilization buffers were purchased from BioLegend. TAM apoptosis was detected using an Annexin V-FITC kit (CA1020, Solarbio). All samples were analyzed by flow cytometry, and data were processed with FlowJo V10 software.

Enzyme-Linked Immunosorbent Assay (ELISA)

The IFN-γ, IL-2, IL-10, TGF-β levels in serum were detected using the ELISA assay kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions. The serum was added to 96-well plates and incubated with the antibody from the kit. After washing nonadherent antibodies away, reaction reagents were added, and the absorbance was measured by Microplate Reader.

Extraction of tumor-associated macrophages

For the extraction of tumor-infiltrating macrophages, subcutaneous tumor tissues were digested as mentioned above. Single-cell suspensions were treated with Anti-F4/80 MicroBeads (Miltenyi, 130–110–443). Purified tumor-infiltrating macrophages were collected for further experiment.

Statistical analysis

The clinical outcomes between the two groups were assessed using the Kaplan–Meier curve analysis and the Log-rank test. T tests or Mann–Whitney’s tests were performed for continuous variables; the χ 2 or Fisher’s exact tests were performed for categorical data. The statistically significant difference was determined by “p value < 0.05”. Statistical analysis was performed in GraphPad prism 10 or R Version 4.1.0.

Results

A total of 850 patients had available pretreatment serum ALB data. Analysis of peripheral blood AST and ALT levels showed that no statistically significant differences were observed in AST or ALT levels between the two groups (Figs. 1D-E), while serum creatinine levels were lower in the hypoalbuminemia group compared to the normal albumin group (Fig. 1F). To verify the changes in the immune microenvironment, we performed flow cytometry analysis on tumor and spleen tissues of mice. A decreased number of CD3+ T cells and CD8+ T cells was observed in low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia treatment group in tumor (Fig. 3D, E), analysis of T cell effector markers revealed significantly lower levels of IFN-γ, perforin, and granzyme B in the LP group compared to the NP group (supplementary Fig. 2A), further confirming the attenuated cytotoxic capacity of CD8⁺ T cells in this group, which was accompanied by reduced proliferation (supplementary Fig. 2B). Exhaustion of CD8⁺ T cells was less pronounced in the LP group (supplementary Fig. 2C), while no significant difference was observed in inhibitory Treg cells (supplementary Fig. 2D). Together, these data consistently demonstrate impaired CD8⁺ T cell effector function in the LP group.

Macrophages abundance was significantly upregulated in the low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia group of patients treated with anti-PD-1 agents in tumor (Fig. 3F) with a predominant increase in M2 macrophages within the tumor (Fig. 3G–I), representing an “immune-desert” phenotype. Flow cytometric analysis of MDSCs revealed no significant differences in the levels of total MDSCs, M-MDSCs, or G-MDSCs among the four experimental groups (supplementary Fig. 3). The cytokine downregulation in serum included IL-2 and IFN-γ, and upregulated cytokine included IL-10 and TGF-β (Fig. 3J) in low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia group also represents the suppressive immune microenvironment in low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia group.

To further clarify the role of macrophages, we treated mice with anti-CSF1R inhibitor PLX3397 on tumor-infiltrating macrophages, to explore the alteration of CD8+ T cell infiltration in low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia subcutaneous tumors after inhibiting polarization, cellular proliferation, and chemotaxis of intra-tumoral macrophages. Mice with low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia initially received subcutaneous injections of 1 × 106 LLC cells and were then divided into two treatment groups: PLX3397 + anti-PD-1 and saline + anti-PD-1. PLX3397 was injected on day1 and every other day until sacrifices (Fig. 4A), and mice received anti-PD-1 when macrophages were significantly inhibited on day 8 (supplementary Fig. 4A). PLX3397 treatment combined with anti-PD-1 significantly slowed the tumor growth, and the tumor volume and growth curves of all treatment groups are shown in Fig. 4B and supplementary Fig. 4B. Flow cytometry revealed that inhibition of macrophage did not affect the intra-tumoral accumulation of MDSCs (Fig. 4C-D) while significantly increased the infiltration of intra-tumoral CD3, CD4, or CD8 T cells (Fig. 4E, F). Similarly, analysis of CD8⁺ T cell effector markers demonstrated that depleting macrophages in hypoalbuminemia tumors enhanced the cytotoxic capacity of T cells when combined with anti-PD‑1 therapy, compared to anti-PD‑1 monotherapy (supplementary Fig. 4C). This enhanced efficacy was accompanied by an increase in CD8⁺ T cell exhaustion markers (supplementary Fig. 4D), while the proportion of regulatory T cells remained unchanged (supplementary Fig. 4E). To further investigate the potential mechanism of how macrophage in low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia tumor involved in immune dessert microenvironment, we performed RNA sequencing of tumor-associated macrophage (TAM) in hypoalbuminemia tumor and normal ALB tumor. Compared with the NC group, TAMs in the LC group exhibited 399 downregulated genes and 2062 upregulated genes in total. (Fig. 4G). GO and Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway enrichment analysis of the downregulated differentially expressed genes revealed that the functional alterations in TAMs of the LC group primarily included decreased response to IFN-γ, diminished antigen presentation capacity, and reduced binding capability of MHC class II complexes, further corroborating the role of TAMs in the LC group in suppressing tumor immune responses. Concurrently, we also observed downregulated arginine biosynthetic capacity in TAMs of the LC group (Fig. 4H). Arginine influences the functions of various cells within the tumor microenvironment. Sequencing results revealed that the arginine anabolic pathway in macrophages of the LC group underwent significant alterations compared to the NC group, including downregulation of the arginine synthesis gene ASS1, upregulation of the uptake gene SLC7A3, and downregulation of the metabolic genes Arg1 and Nos2 (Fig. 4I).

To further validate the importance of arginine, we first measured the peripheral blood arginine concentration in mice before tumor inoculation and found that the arginine level in the low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia group was significantly lower than that in the normal control group, suggesting that macrophages were in an arginine-depleted state during the early treatment phase (Fig. 5A). We conducted an arginine rescue experiment in mice. Hypoalbuminemia diet-fed mice were divided into groups receiving PD-1 monoclonal antibody combined with 0 μg/mL, 150 μg/mL, and 300 μg/mL arginine supplementation. The tumor growth curves demonstrated that both the 150 μg/mL and 300 μg/mL groups alleviated the immune resistance in low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia mice, with combined arginine supplementation significantly reducing the tumor growth rate (Fig. 5B, C). Measurement of peripheral blood arginine levels at the experimental endpoint indicated that arginine supplementation was effective (Fig. 5D). Supplementation with 150 µg/mL arginine significantly reduced total macrophage infiltration, with the decrease primarily in the M2 subset as indicated by a lower M2/M1 ratio (Fig. 5E,F). Discussion

In this study, we demonstrated that low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia acts as a key driver of immunotherapy resistance by remodeling TME, which resulted in a decrease in CD8+ T cells and an increase in macrophages. The underlying mechanism involves reduced circulating arginine levels, which in turn suppress arginine biosynthesis in tumor-associated macrophages, ultimately promoting immunosuppression. Importantly, arginine supplementation was shown to reverse this process in a macrophage-dependent manner. Together, these findings establish a foundation for further mechanistic exploration.

As the major plasma protein, albumin regulates fluid distribution, maintains colloidal osmotic pressure [13] and functions in binding and transporting endogenous substances as well as exogenous toxins [14, 15]. A low serum ALB level is not only a biomarker for various diseases (such as heart failure[16], liver cirrhosis[17], and kidney injury[18]), but its reduction can also directly or indirectly lead to increased oxidative stress, enhanced inflammatory responses, dysregulated drug metabolism, and multiple organ dysfunction[16, 19]. Meanwhile, albumin serves as a core component in various nutritional indices: the Geriatric Nutritional Risk Index (GNRI)[20, 21], modified Glasgow Prognostic Score (mGPS) [22] and the Albumin–Bilirubin (ALBI) grade[23, 24], and these indicators have been reported in previous studies to be associated with prognosis in cancer patients. In the pan-cancer clinical cohort of this study, hypoalbuminemia showed a significant correlation with immunotherapy outcomes, and a graded trend was observed in the survival curves, highlighting the important predictive role of albumin levels in immunotherapy.

In children, severe malnutrition significantly reduces the ability of circulating innate immune cells to respond to bacteria, leading to an immunosuppressed state [25, 26]. In inflammatory bowel disease, malnutrition-induced alterations in the gut microbiota disrupt the microbiota–immune axis, further compromising host defense [27, 28]. In the tumor microenvironment, cachexia-induced metabolic abnormalities reduce energy supply, further suppressing intra-tumoral immune responses [29]. Under metabolic stress, tumor cells can also directly inhibit T cell infiltration and function by altering the concentration of metabolites in the microenvironment[30]. By establishing a mouse model of hypoalbuminemia, we successfully developed an immune-resistant tumor model. Results from animal experiments suggest that low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia may not merely be a prognostic biomarker, but rather a functional driver of drug resistance. By further integrating RNA-seq, IHC, and flow cytometry data, we discovered that hypoalbuminemia contributes to the formation of a “cold” tumor microenvironment (characterized by reduced infiltration of effector T cells) and an “immunosuppressive” microenvironment (marked by increased macrophages and an inhibitory cytokine profile). These mechanisms collectively indicate that malnutrition disrupts the balance of the tumor immune microenvironment through multiple pathways, not only weakening anti-tumor immune responses but also potentially serving as a driving factor for immunotherapy tolerance. Targeted nutritional interventions or metabolic modulation may emerge as potential strategies to improve the tumor immune microenvironment.

Arginine is crucial for maintaining cellular functions and viability [31–34]. Albumin serve as a key regulator of amino acid homeostasis and may function as a dynamic reservoir for circulating arginine. Hypoalbuminemia could thus reduce the bioavailable pool of arginine in plasma and consequently within the tumor, limiting its uptake by tumor-associated macrophages (TAMs). Studies have shown that patients with high pretreatment arginine levels have significantly superior survival outcomes after immunotherapy compared to those with low pretreatment arginine levels [35, 36]. In the tumor microenvironment, both macrophages and T cells exhibit low expression of the arginine-synthesizing enzyme ASS1 [37], rendering them dependent on arginine uptake from the microenvironment. While arginine supplementation primarily exerts an activating effect on T cells [38, 39], its impact on macrophage polarization has been inconsistently reported in the literature. Breast cancer cell-secreted arginine can polarize intra-tumoral macrophages toward the M2 phenotype [37, 40], while supplemental circulating arginine may conversely enhance M1 macrophage polarization[41]. Arginine supplementation under hypoalbuminemia conditions alleviates immunotherapy resistance; however, concurrent macrophage depletion attenuates this restorative effect. This further demonstrates that in hypoalbuminemia environments, arginine supplementation indirectly alleviates T cell suppression by modulating macrophage function. The aforementioned results demonstrate that low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia leads to reduced circulating arginine levels, thereby causing macrophages to acquire an immunosuppressive phenotype, which subsequently impairs T cell function and ultimately contributes to the development of immunotherapy resistance. These findings suggest that in hypoalbuminemia patients prior to immunotherapy, modifying macrophages through methods such as arginine supplementation, activation of key genes in the arginine biosynthesis pathway, or targeted blockade may potentially reverse the immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and enhance patient immune responses.

The mechanistic validation in this study has certain limitations that could be further strengthened in future work. For instance, multiplex immunohistochemistry (mIHC) could be employed to investigate the spatial distribution of T cells and macrophages under hypoalbuminemia conditions and its impact on the immune microenvironment. Additionally, a more detailed exploration of key genes upstream and downstream in the arginine metabolic synthesis pathway is warranted. Furthermore, a broad panel cytokine profiling assay would help delineate the systemic and local inflammatory milieu associated with these cellular changes.

This study unveils a novel immunosuppressive pathway driven by low-protein diet-induced hypoalbuminemia and dependent on macrophage arginine metabolism, which represents a significant mechanism underlying resistance to immune checkpoint inhibitors. Our findings offer potential therapeutic strategies such as combination therapies targeting arginine metabolism or macrophages to overcome such resistance.
