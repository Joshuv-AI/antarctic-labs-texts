Association Between Cardiovascular Health and Subclinical Atherosclerosis Among Young Adults Using the American Heart Association's “Life's Essential 8” Metrics
Authors: Fangqi Guo, Xinci Chen, Steve Howland, Luis E Maldonado, Sydney Powell, W James Gauderman, Rob McConnell, Mingzhu Yan, Lora Whitfield, Yanjie Li, Theresa M Bastain, Carrie V Breton, Howard N Hodis, Shohreh F Farzan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThis study investigated the association of American Heart Association's cardiovascular health guidelines Life's Essential 8 (LE8) and Life's Simple 7 (LS7) with carotid artery outcomes among young adults.Methods and ResultsThis cross‐sectional study included 240 young adults (age 24.2±1.6 years) who underwent a carotid ultrasound between 2018 and 2022. LE8 score was calculated from 4 health factors (body mass index, non–high‐density lipoprotein cholesterol, fasting glucose, and blood pressure), and 4 health behaviors (dietary intake, physical activity, tobacco use, and sleep). LS7 was calculated from 7 metrics (all LE8 metrics, except for sleep) with a simpler algorithm. Higher LE8 and LS7 scores both indicate better health and better adherence to American Heart Association guidelines. Carotid artery outcomes included carotid artery intima‐media thickness, arterial stiffness (eg, distensibility), and echogenicity determined by grayscale median of the intima media complex. Results of linear regression analyses, adjusting for age, sex, ethnicity, and parents' highest degree, indicated that a 1‐SD increase in LE8 score was associated with 12.14 μm lower carotid artery intima‐media thickness (95% CI, −20.93 to 3.35), 1.17 (10−6×m2/N) greater distensibility (95% CI, 0.09–2.24), suggesting less arterial stiffness, and 2.66 μm greater grayscale median of the intima media complex (95% CI, 0.58–4.75), suggesting less lipid deposition. Analyses using LS7 score demonstrated comparable findings. Health factor metrics demonstrated stronger association with carotid artery outcomes, as compared with behavior metrics.ConclusionsGreater adherence to the American Heart Association's cardiovascular health guidelines is associated with lower risk for subclinical atherosclerosis in young adults. LE8 and LS7 demonstrated comparable associations with carotid artery outcomes.Keywords: carotid artery stiffness, CIMT, grayscale median of the intima media complex, Life' Essential 8, subclinical atherosclerosisSubject Categories: Lifestyle, Epidemiology, Risk Factors

Cardiovascular diseases (CVD) have continued to be one of the most prevalent chronic diseases in the United States and globally for several decades. 1 To promote improvements in cardiovascular health (CVH) at both the individual and population levels, it is important to establish metrics for measuring and monitoring CVH across all age groups. Recognizing the clinical significance of CVD prevention and management, in 2022, the American Heart Association (AHA) published a new set of CVH guidelines, named Life's Essential 8 (LE8), aimed at improving, maintaining, and mitigating the risk for cardiovascular events in the general population. 2 This innovative concept builds upon and expands the AHA's previous definition of ideal CVH—Life's Simple 7 (LS7)—by incorporating sleep health as a new metric and employing a more advanced algorithm. LE8 comprises 4 health factor metrics (body mass index [BMI], non–high‐density lipoprotein [non‐HDL] cholesterol levels, glucose, and blood pressure [BP]), and 4 health behavioral metrics (physical activity, diet, tobacco use, and sleep health). 3 In recent decades, CVD risk factors have shown a more rapid increase among younger than older adults. 4 Very likely, efforts toward CVD prevention during early life stages may benefit young adults by setting them on a healthier trajectory and in turn, may contribute to long‐term improvements in their overall well‐being. Therefore, as CVD remains a critical public health concern, it is important to understand factors that may lead to greater health resilience and better CVH profiles among young adults.

Subclinical atherosclerosis measurements such as carotid artery intima‐media thickness (CIMT) and carotid artery stiffness (CAS) are important indicators of vascular health and have been widely associated with risk of cardiovascular events and mortality. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 In addition, carotid artery wall echogenicity determined by grayscale median of the intima‐media complex (IM‐GSM) is a novel noninvasive ultrasound measurement of lipid deposition in the carotid artery wall, indicative of early stages of atherosclerosis. 10 IM‐GSM research in young adults is very limited. Unlike cardiovascular events that more commonly occur in older adulthood, subclinical atherosclerosis can be evaluated at any age, and is, therefore, an ideal tool in early life stages before development of clinically identifiable disease. 11

Previous studies have found associations of LS7 with CIMT and CAS. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 , 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 However, whether the newly released concept, LE8, has a stronger association with subclinical atherosclerosis is not known. Further, the association between CVH metrics and IM‐GSM has not been previously evaluated. The main aims of this cross‐sectional study are to (1) examine the association between LE8 and subclinical atherosclerosis measured by CIMT, CAS, and IM‐GSM among young adults, and (2) compare these subclinical atherosclerosis measurement associations between LS7 and LE8.

METHODS

Participants

The Southern California CHS is one of the largest investigations of long‐term effects of air pollution on health in children. 21 , 22 Beginning in 2003, children living in 13 southern California communities were enrolled into the youngest CHS cohort (“Cohort E”) when they were in kindergarten or the first grade, and were followed through high school in 2014. 23

A substudy focused on CVH was conducted among a subset of CHS Cohort E participants (n=737) residing in 8 southern California communities in 2008. 24 In 2018, when participants were at their transition to adulthood, this subset of Cohort E participants who had a CVH assessment in childhood were invited to participate in a follow‐up assessment, named Meta‐Air 2, 25 aimed at assessing participants' cardiometabolic profiles. As of December 2022, 240 participants had completed a follow‐up carotid artery ultrasound examination and other cardiometabolic assessments at our study clinic. We compared our Meta Air 2 study sample with the entire sample of eligible CHS individuals who participated in the childhood cardiovascular health assessment and found that overall, the demographics of our study sample are similar to those of the entire sample. All participants provided written informed consent, and protocols and study materials were approved by the University of Southern California's Institutional Review Board.

Measures

Metrics for Cardiovascular Health

In 2022, AHA released a new definition of CVH named LE8 as an improvement and extension of LS7. 3 LE8 uses a more sophisticated algorithm to quantify each metric and includes sleep health as a new metric. 2 As compared with LS7, which categorizes each metric as ideal, intermediate, or poor, LE8 scores are measured on a continuous scale from 0 to 100, which increases the sensitivity of the metrics. 26 The new CVH component metrics include 4 health behaviors (diet, physical activity, tobacco use, and sleep) and 4 health factors (BMI, non–high‐density lipoprotein [HDL] cholesterol, blood glucose, and BP). 2 Each of these components were objectively measured or obtained by questionnaire from Meta‐Air 2 participants (as specified next) and used to calculate both LE8 and LS7 scores.

Health Behaviors

Behaviors were evaluated using self‐reported questionnaires during participants' Meta‐Air 2 visits. Dietary intake was assessed using the Automated Self‐Administered 24‐hour Dietary Assessment Tool, 27 in which participants recalled every item of their food intake in the past 24 hours on 2 separate days. We then generated the Health Eating Index‐2015, a diet quality indicator measuring how closely a combination of foods adheres to the 2015 to 2020 Dietary Guidelines for Americans recommendations. Health Eating Index‐2015 scores were calculated and ranged from 0 to 100 with higher scores indicating greater diet quality. 28 Physical activity was assessed using a 3‐day physical activity scale in which participants recalled physical activities and their intensity engaged in for at least 1 half‐hour over the past 3 days. We then converted the 3‐day time of activity to weekly minutes of moderate‐ (or greater) intensity activity. The self‐administered 3‐day physical activity recall scale has been established as a valid tool, as its assessments of moderate to vigorous physical activity time per day have been shown to be comparable to those obtained from the 7‐day physical activity recall scale among youth. 29 , 30 Sleep health was assessed by self‐reported usual duration of sleep, with the question “How many hours and minutes did you usually spend sleeping during the night?” To comprehensively assess tobacco use in compliance with AHA's new definition of nicotine exposure, 2 we asked participants to report whether they are a current/former/noncigarette‐smoker, frequency of electronic vapor product (eg, e‐cigarettes) usage, and whether or not they live with a regular cigarette smoker(s).

Health Factors

Health factors were objectively measured following standard protocols. Well‐trained staff with extensive experience in data collection and anthropometric measurements obtained participants' weight and height 3 times each and the average of each of the measures were used to calculate BMI (kg/m2). Systolic BP and diastolic BP were measured 3 times at 1‐minute intervals by the same well‐trained staff using an oscillometric device (GE Carescape V100) with an appropriately sized cuff, following an initial 5‐minute rest. Average systolic BP and diastolic BP were calculated from the 3 measures.

During the study visit, a trained phlebotomist collected fasting blood samples from participants, which were then delivered to the laboratory on ice and processed within 1 hour. Whole blood samples were used to measure levels of glycated hemoglobin using a DCA Vantage Analyzer. Serum samples were delivered to the University of Southern California Metabolic Assay Core for analysis of glucose and circulating lipids. Lipids, including total cholesterol and HDL cholesterol, were analyzed by enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay.

LE8 and LS7

Algorithms for generating LE8 and LS7 scores are shown in Table S1 and Table S2. 2 , 3 In short, to assign a LE8 score to a participant, each individual received a score on a scale of 0 to 100 for each CVH metric, based on AHA‐defined scoring criteria for adults 20 years and older. Higher scores within each metric indicate a healthier lifestyle or factor. For instance, a higher score in the BP metric signifies lower systolic and diastolic BPs. The overall LE8 score was calculated by averaging the scores for all 8 metrics. Participants were also assigned an LS7 score, in which participants received a score of 0 (poor), 1 (intermediate), or 2 (ideal) for each of the 7 metrics, excluding sleep health, using AHA defined cutoffs. The LS7 score is the sum of 7 CVH metrics. The LE8 ranges from 0 to 100, and the LS7 ranges from 0 to 14. Higher scores in both algorithms indicate better overall cardiovascular health and better adherence to AHA'S CVH guidelines.

LE8 and LS7 were analyzed both continuously and categorically. Based on previous research, 2 , 26 , 31 LE8 scores of <50, 50 to 79, and ≥80 were defined as poor, intermediate, and ideal CVH, respectively. Similarly, LS7 scores of 0 to 4, 5 to 9, and 10 to 14 were defined as poor, intermediate, and ideal CVH, respectively. 32

Carotid Ultrasound Outcomes

We conducted carotid arterial ultrasound to evaluate each participant's CIMT, CAS, and IM‐GSM. High‐resolution B‐mode ultrasound images of the right carotid artery and ECG traces were simultaneously acquired with standardized methodology using a Siemens Acuson X300 (Mountain View, CA) ultrasound imaging system with a linear array VF10‐5 (8.0 MHz) transducer. 33 , 34 In short, the jugular vein and carotid artery were imaged transversely and then longitudinally with the former vessel stacked above the latter. Images contained internal anatomical landmarks for reproducing probe angulation.

CIMT is a widely used measure of subclinical atherosclerosis that primarily reflects vascular remodeling in response to hemodynamic conditions. 7 , 9 CIMT was determined as the average of 70 to 100 measurements between the intima‐lumen and media‐adventitia interfaces by computerized automated edge detection along a 1 cm segment determined by an electronic ruler just proximal to the carotid artery bulb during the same phase of the cardiac cycle. 33 , 34 Lumen diameter during peak systole and end diastole were measured by computerized automated edge detection along the same 1 cm segment as CIMT. 35 , 36

CAS is a measure of endothelial function that indicates changes in the vascular structure and its responsiveness, and is an independent CVD risk factor that is linked to the later development of clinical vascular disease. 37 This study determined 3 CAS parameters: distensibility, stiffness index beta (C‐Beta), and Young's elastic modulus (YEM) according to standard formulae (see Data S1 for details). 36 Lower values of distensibility reflect a stiffer carotid artery whereas higher C‐Beta and YEM values reflect a stiffer carotid artery.

Carotid artery wall echogenicity, determined by IM‐GSM, indicates the degree of lipid deposition, fibrosis, and calcification in the arterial wall, which is an effective tool in assessing early‐stage atherosclerosis and CVD risk compared with CIMT alone. 38 Lower IM‐GSM has been associated with a higher risk of CVD. 39 IM‐GSM was calculated as the median of the echo‐strength (based on the grayscale 0–255, unitless) determined from each pixel within the CIMT by computerized automated software. 10 , 40 Each image was automatically calibrated to account for image contrast and brightness by using standardized nearby structures. 10 , 40 Regions of the carotid artery wall that appear black (echolucent, low IM‐GSM) reflect lipid deposition and white regions (echodense, high IM‐GSM) reflect fibrous tissue and calcification.

Statistical Analysis

We calculated descriptive statistics for all variables of interest, including demographic characteristics, each CVH LE8 and LS7 component, as well as carotid artery outcomes (Table 1). Then, we categorized individuals into 3 CVH categories that indicate ideal, intermediate, and poor CVH using both LS7 and LE8 scores. A Sankey diagram was generated to depict reclassification of CVH categories based on LS7 and LE8 (Figure 1). Subsequently, we compared carotid artery outcomes across the 3 CVH categories based on both LE8 and LS7 with covariate adjustment. Further, we conducted linear regression analyses to assess the association between continuous LE8 score and each carotid artery outcome with covariate adjustment. We then repeated the same analysis using LS7 to investigate whether LE8 had a stronger association with carotid artery outcomes compared with LS7. To facilitate a direct comparison between LE8 and LS7 in terms of their association with carotid artery outcomes, we standardized both LE8 and LS7 to have a mean of 0 and an SD of 1. Finally, we examined the associations between each lifestyle or factor metric with carotid artery outcome using linear regression models.

Out of the 240 participants who underwent a carotid artery ultrasound scan, a total of 1, 13, 20, 24, and 72 had missing values for metrics of sleep health, physical activity, blood lipids, blood glucose, and dietary recall, respectively. All other metrics and carotid artery outcomes (CIMT, CAS, and IM‐GSM) had complete data with no missing values. To minimize potential bias introduced by missing data, especially in the dietary metric when calculating the overall LE8 or LS7 scores, we employed mean imputation for missing values. Although the overall LE8 and LS7 scores with imputation of metric means were used in analyses, we also conducted sensitivity analyses to investigate the associations of unimputed LE8 and LS7 scores with carotid artery outcomes among the full sample and the subsample of individuals with complete data only (n=148).

Given that the 3 measures of CAS were highly correlated (rs>0.87), and their associations with CVH metrics demonstrated same trends, in subsequent result and discussion sections, we mainly discussed distensibility as an indicator of CAS. Additional results for C‐Beta index and YEM are presented in Figure S1.

In all regression models, we adjusted for age, biological sex (female, male), ethnicity (Hispanic, non‐Hispanic), and parents' highest degree, given that these were potential risk factors for adverse CVH. 1 Parents' highest degree was used to indicate participants' family socioeconomic status because participants were interviewed in young adulthood and many did not have a stable income. We did not adjust for race as a covariate due to insufficient sample sizes for a few racial groups. Instead, we investigated the mean and SD of each subclinical atherosclerosis outcome measured across racial groups (Table S3). Race and ethnicity were self‐reported by participants during the Meta‐Air 2 visit. To minimize missing data for those who did not provide this information, their race or ethnicity was filled with baseline data that was reported by their parents. Statistical analyses were conducted in Stata BE 17.0.

RESULTS

The analytic sample had 240 individuals, aged 21.2 to 26.9 years, with a mean±SD of 24.2±1.6 years (Table 1). Our sample has a high proportion of individuals who self‐identify as Hispanic ethnicity (51.7%), more women than men (55.4% versus 44.6%), and more White individuals than all other racial groups (59.2% versus 40.8%). The distribution of LE8 and LS7 scores is displayed in Table 2. The LE8 overall score ranged from 31.9 to 94.4, with a mean±SD of 68.6±12.9. When each metric was assigned a score based on the LE8 algorithm, mean score for BP (92.4±15.2) was the highest, indicating that BP levels largely fell in the ideal range among this young adult population. Meanwhile, dietary intake (32.2±32.9) was the lowest scoring metric, indicating less than ideal dietary patterns in our sample. Similar distributions were observed for these metrics when scored using the LS7 criteria. A strong correlation between the LE8 and LS7 scores was noted (r=0.87).

Figure 1 presents a Sankey diagram illustrating the reclassification of CVH categories using LE8 and LS7 scores. Specifically, we observed a notable shift in the CVH categorization using the LE8 classification, manifesting as a decrease in the number of participants within the “ideal CVH” category and an increase within the “poor CVH” category as compared with the LS7 classification. The overall pattern of CVH reclassification is consistent with previous findings from a study using US national data of young adults. 31

Figure 2 displays carotid artery outcomes predicted by the 3 CVH categories using LE8 and LS7 scores with covariate adjustment. Overall, both LE8 and LS7 demonstrated consistent trends, that is, as CVH categories transitioned from poor to ideal, we observed lower CIMT, C‐Beta, and YEM and higher distensibility and IM‐GSM. These findings suggest a notable decrease in arterial thickness and stiffness, as well as a decrease in the lipid component within the carotid artery wall as CVH categories improved.

After adjusting for age, sex, ethnicity, and parent's highest degree, we observed statistically significant associations of both LE8 and LS7 with carotid artery outcomes (Table 3). Specifically, a 1‐SD greater LE8 score was associated with 12.14 μm lower CIMT (95% CI, −20.93 to −3.35; P value, 0.007), 1.17 (10−6×m2/N) greater distensibility (95% CI, 0.09–2.24; P value, 0.033), and 2.66 greater IM‐GSM (95% CI, 0.58–4.75; P value, 0.013). Similar results were observed using the LS7 score. We found that LS7 was significantly associated with CIMT (β, −10.91 [95% CI, −19.73 to −2.09]; P value, 0.016), distensibility (β, 1.81 [95% CI, 0.75–2.87]; P value, 0.001), and IM‐GSM (β, 2.95 [95% CI, 0.87–5.03]; P value, 0.006). In addition, the other 2 CAS measurements (C‐Beta and YEM) demonstrated consistent findings with distensibility of lower arterial stiffness with higher LE8 and LS7 scores (Table 3).

Sensitivity analyses (Table S4) using unimputed LE8 and LS7 scores in both the full sample and a subsample of individuals with complete data showed that the associations of LE8 or LS7 with carotid artery outcomes remained consistent in magnitude and direction when no imputation was applied or when the sample was restricted to individuals with no missing data.

We then evaluated each LE8 metric separately with each carotid artery outcome (Figure 3 and Table S5). For health factors, we observed that each 1‐SD increase in individual LE8 metric score for BMI was significantly associated with 20.36 μm lower CIMT (95% CI, −28.61 to −12.11; P value <0.001), 2.27 (10−6×m2/N) greater distensibility (95% CI, 1.26–3.28; P value <0.001), and 6.04 greater IM‐GSM (95% CI, 4.15–7.94; P value <0.001). A 1‐SD higher LE8 score for non‐HDL cholesterol was significantly associated with 13.54 μm lower CIMT (95% CI, −22.21 to −4.87; P value, 0.002) and 2.80 higher IM‐GSM (95% CI, 0.75–4.85; P value, 0.008) and showed a borderline association with distensibility (β, 0.99 [95% CI, −0.07 to 2.04]; P value 0.066). A 1‐SD improvement in BP score was associated with 3.00 (10−6×m2/N) greater arterial distensibility (95% CI, 1.97–4.03; P value <0.001) and 3.69 greater IM‐GSM (95% CI, 1.59–5.79; P value, 0.001). LE8 glucose score had a borderline association with CIMT (β, −8.15 [95% CI, −16.99 to 0.69]; P value, 0.070) but not with CAS or IM‐GSM. Despite the significant associations between CVH health factors and carotid artery outcomes overall, the 4 health behaviors did not demonstrate significant association with these markers. The only 2 exceptions were that higher smoking scores, which indicate less use of tobacco or vaping products or as never smoking, were associated with lower arterial distensibility (ie, stiffer artery; β −1.04 [95% CI, −2.04 to −0.04]; P value, 0.042) and LE8 scores indicative of better sleep health were associated with lower IM‐GSM (β −2.21 [95% CI, −4.20 to −0.21]; P value, 0.030).

DISCUSSION

To our knowledge, this study is the first to investigate the association between LE8 and subclinical atherosclerosis among young adults. We observed that higher LE8 score was significantly associated with lower CIMT, lower arterial stiffness, and higher IM‐GSM, which indicate healthier carotid artery structure and function in our sample of young adults (mean age 24 years). Compared with LS7, LE8 was more strongly associated with CIMT but less so with CAS. Overall, the 4 LE8 health factors had stronger associations with carotid artery outcomes than did the 4 LE8 health behaviors. The mean overall LE8 score (68.6, SD: 12.9) in our study sample of young urban adults from Southern California is comparable with the LE8 score derived from a nationally representative sample of ages 18 to 44 (mean, 69.8) 31 as well as an NHANES (National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey) young adult sample aged 20 to 39 (mean, 68.7). 26 In line with results from these national samples, 26 , 31 in our young adult participants, BP and dietary intake were the individual metrics with the highest and lowest scores (means: 92.4 versus 32.2), respectively, indicating that although most participants had favorable levels of BP, few in our study scored highly for dietary intake quality.

Most prior evidence supporting the association between CIMT and CVH has been reported from studies of older adults. 13 , 14 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 41 In our study, employing the new AHA definition of CVH among a sample of young adults, we showed that higher LE8 or LS7 is significantly associated with lower CIMT. This finding is consistent with scant findings derived from young adults or adolescents. 12 , 15 , 16 , 42 For example, Pahkala et al showed that the number of LS7 ideal CVH metrics was inversely associated with aortic CIMT among adolescents aged 15 to 19. 42 Findings from previous studies and our own consistently support the idea that maintaining an ideal CVH at a younger age benefits young adults by promoting vascular health, which may translate to a reduced risk for adverse cardiovascular effects later in life. 43 Further, although both LE8 and LS7 scores are significantly associated with CIMT, our findings suggest that LE8 may have a stronger association with CIMT given the larger effect size observed for LE8 on CIMT compared with that of LS7. As for each individual LE8 metric, BMI and non‐HDL level were strongly associated with CIMT, which is in line with previous findings that CIMT is thicker among youth with obesity and high lipid levels. 44

Our study also provides evidence of an association between better CVH and reduced CAS in young adults. 42 , 45 We showed similar results to previous studies conducted in individuals of a similar age group that measured arterial stiffness based on aorta distensibility 42 and pulse wave velocity. 45 Specifically, we found that young adults with higher LE8 and LS7 CVH scores had lower carotid artery stiffness shown by higher distensibility and lower C‐Beta index and YEM. This finding is also consistent with results derived from older adults. 13 , 20 , 46 , 47 , 48 Interestingly, we found that LS7 had a stronger association with CAS compared with LE8. Although LE8 score may be a more sensitive measure of overall CVH, LS7 was highly associated with CAS and is a useful indicator of CVH if measures of sleep are not available. Of the 8 metrics, BP demonstrated the strongest association with CAS, which is in line with the fact that CAS is largely determined by BP. 49

In addition to the widely used subclinical atherosclerosis measures of CIMT and CAS, our assessment of vascular health also involved a novel measure of arterial wall composition determined by IM‐GSM. 10 , 40 Among older adults, grayscale median has been used as a tool to determine the composition carotid plaque or vascular injury. 13 , 17 , 20 However, this approach is not applicable to young adults given that most youth do not have carotid plaque. An advantage of IM‐GSM is that it can be used to assess atherosclerosis in any individual since it is not limited to only individuals with plaque. 50 To our knowledge, noninvasive determination of composition of the arterial wall has not been well characterized in young populations. Our study begins to fill this critical research gap as we showed that among a young population, better CVH (both LE8 and LS7) is significantly associated with higher IM‐GSM reflecting less lipid deposition and hemorrhagic components associated with atherosclerosis. We also showed that higher IM‐GSM is associated with higher individual LE8 scores (indictive of better health) for the metrics of BMI, BP, and non‐HDL cholesterol among our sample of young adults. To our knowledge, this is the first investigation linking AHA's CVH scores to IM‐GSM.

Overall, the health factors included in the LE8 demonstrated stronger associations with carotid artery outcomes compared with the LE8 health behaviors. Of the 4 health factors, BMI was associated with CIMT, CAS, and IM‐GSM, which is in line with the observation that adults with obesity are more likely to have poorer vascular health compared with individuals with normal BMI. 51 , 52 , 53 , 54 , 55 Compared with the LS7 lipid metric, which takes into account all lipoprotein particles that contribute to the total cholesterol measurement, the LE8 lipid metric excludes the cholesterol contribution of HDL particles to the non‐HDL cholesterol measurement. The latter lipid metric may be a more effective predictor of vascular health given that our study found a significant association between LE8 metric of non‐HDL cholesterol and most carotid arterial outcomes, whereas these significant associations were observed on distensibility only when using the LS7 metric of total cholesterol. This finding is in line with most prior studies that found that LS7 metric of total cholesterol was not significantly associated with the carotid artery outcomes. 12 , 13

Compared with the robust associations we found between health factors and CIMT, CAS, and IM‐GSM, our findings showed relatively negligible associations between health behaviors and carotid artery outcomes. Although most health behavior metrics displayed no significant association with carotid artery outcomes, ideal smoking behavior (eg, never smoked) was interestingly associated with less arterial distensibility (ie, stiffer carotid artery). Although “smoker's paradox” has been previously reported, the underlying cause of this paradoxical relationship remains unexplained. 13 Although unmeasured confounding is a possibility, limited research has suggested that the apparently favorable effect of smoking on health has been observed only in short‐term studies among younger adults, whereas long‐term smoking has shown consistent associations with multiple adverse cardiovascular outcomes. 56 Future longitudinal studies with repeated measurements of CVH may be necessary to fully capture the complex association of smoking behavior with subclinical atherosclerosis in younger populations. Taken together, although we did not find strong associations between health behavior metrics and subclinical atherosclerosis, it is important to recognize that lifestyle behavior has long‐term implications for health. 57 This impact on subclinical atherosclerosis may not be readily apparent in young adulthood when individuals are ostensibly healthy.

Limitations and Strengths

Our study results should be interpreted with a few limitations in mind. First, given that the assessment of carotid artery outcomes and CVH occurred simultaneously, it excluded the possibility to conclude a causal relationship between carotid artery outcomes and CVH. To establish a causal relationship, future longitudinal studies that repeatedly measure carotid artery outcomes and CVH from childhood to adulthood are warranted. Second, about one third of participants did not report dietary intake, so the full effect of dietary patterns may not have been captured. However, we compared demographics between participants who did and did not provide dietary data and showed that the 2 groups did not significantly differ by sex, race, and ethnicity lessening concerns of a nonresponse bias. Third, as mentioned previously, race as a proxy for structural factors was not adjusted in analyses due to sample size issues. Nevertheless, we provided a descriptive table displaying the distribution of carotid ultrasound measures across racial groups (Table S3), which provided preliminary insights into potential differences between groups. Future studies with sufficient sample size are encouraged to further evaluate differences between racialized groups. Last, study participants were primarily recruited from Southern California. Therefore, the generalization of our study results may be limited to younger populations living in urban areas with comparable demographic characteristics.

Despite these limitations, our study had considerable strengths. First, this is the first study that linked AHA's new CVH definition, LE8, directly to measures of vascular health in young adults. This is also the first study to compare LE8 and LS7 in relation to multiple carotid artery outcomes. Furthermore, measurement of CIMT, CAS, and IM‐GSM comprehensively reflects different aspects of subclinical atherosclerosis, a significant improvement over previous studies that solely used a single carotid artery outcome, predominantly CIMT. IM‐GSM is a novel noninvasive measurement of carotid artery wall composition that has not been previously evaluated regarding its association with CVH risk factors in young adults. Third, unlike the objectively measured health factors, we relied on self‐reported measures to assess health behaviors. Although self‐reported behaviors are commonly used in epidemiological research due to their practicality and feasibility, they are subject to recall bias and social desirability bias. These biases may influence the accuracy of the reported health behaviors. Future research could benefit from incorporating objective measures to assess health behaviors, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of their associations with subclinical atherosclerosis. Lastly, this study emphasized the transition from adolescence to young adulthood, a crucial phase where health behaviors and risks are developed. Despite a rise in cardiovascular risk factors among this age group, young adults remain largely unexplored in research. Our study findings underscore the utility of LE8 for early risk assessment and primary prevention strategies in CVD among young adults. By identifying individuals with lower LE8 scores at a young age, health care providers can tailor their recommendations for behavioral and lifestyle changes and potentially implement targeted interventions to mitigate disease burden and promote long‐term CVH.

CONCLUSIONS

In our sample of healthy young adults aged from 21 to 27, higher CVH LE8 scores were associated with better vascular health (ie, lower arterial wall thickness, lower arterial stiffness, and less arterial wall lipid deposition). Overall, LE8 and LS7 scores demonstrated comparable associations with subclinical atherosclerosis measured as CIMT, CAS, and IM‐GSM. Improved understanding of lifestyle modifications and interventions in young adults that positively affect CVH are key to reducing the burden of CVD in adulthood. 58

Our results support the hypothesis that early CVH promotion in healthy young adults is a strategy to reduce CVD risk.

Participants

The Southern California CHS is one of the largest investigations of long‐term effects of air pollution on health in children. 21 , 22 Beginning in 2003, children living in 13 southern California communities were enrolled into the youngest CHS cohort (“Cohort E”) when they were in kindergarten or the first grade, and were followed through high school in 2014. 23

A substudy focused on CVH was conducted among a subset of CHS Cohort E participants (n=737) residing in 8 southern California communities in 2008. 24 In 2018, when participants were at their transition to adulthood, this subset of Cohort E participants who had a CVH assessment in childhood were invited to participate in a follow‐up assessment, named Meta‐Air 2, 25 aimed at assessing participants' cardiometabolic profiles. As of December 2022, 240 participants had completed a follow‐up carotid artery ultrasound examination and other cardiometabolic assessments at our study clinic. We compared our Meta Air 2 study sample with the entire sample of eligible CHS individuals who participated in the childhood cardiovascular health assessment and found that overall, the demographics of our study sample are similar to those of the entire sample. All participants provided written informed consent, and protocols and study materials were approved by the University of Southern California's Institutional Review Board.
