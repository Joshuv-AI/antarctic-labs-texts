Multi-level evaluation of zinc sulfate toxicity and its modulation by Helichrysum arenarium in Allium cepa
Authors: Tuğçe Kalefetoğlu Macar, Oksal Macar, Emine Yalçın, Kültiğin Çavuşoğlu, Fatih Kutluer
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractIn this study, the multifaceted toxicity caused by zinc sulfate (ZnSO4) and the protective potential of Helichrysum arenarium L. extract against this toxicity were investigated using the model organism Allium cepa. In this context, changes in growth, cytogenetic, biochemical and histological parameters, as well as DNA damage were investigated in groups of A. cepa L. exposed to H. arenarium extract (50 and 100 mg/L), ZnSO4 (140 mg/L), and their combinations, respectively. The phenolic profile of the extract was also evaluated through LC-MS/MS analysis. ZnSO4 treatment reduced growth, mitotic index (MI) and chlorophyll pigment levels. Chromosomal aberrations (CAs) and micronucleus (MN) formation as well as lipid peroxidation and antioxidant enzyme (superoxide dismutase and catalase) activities increased following the ZnSO4 exposure. DNA damage, as measured by the level of DNA fragmentation, also increased significantly in response to ZnSO4. While both doses of H. arenarium caused no harm when administered alone, when combined with ZnSO4, the toxic effects were significantly mitigated. In particular, the improvement in growth, the reduction in DNA and membrane damage, the maintenance of chromosomal stability and the recovery of chlorophyll levels suggested a protective effect of the extract. The therapeutic potential of the extract was dose-dependent. Phenolic profile analysis revealed that the extract is rich in phenolic compounds such as apigenin, chlorogenic acid, and luteolin. Given the antioxidant and antigenotoxic potential of these compounds, it is suggested that the extract’s bioprotective effect may be attributable to its phenolic content. This study suggests that H. arenarium extract can exert a multifaceted protective effect against zinc-induced toxicity, thereby offering a new perspective on the use of plant-derived natural compounds against heavy metal stress.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-54179-8.Keywords: Allium cepa, Helichrysum arenarium, Comet, Genotoxicity, Oxidative stress, Zinc sulfateSubject terms: Biochemistry, Chemical biology, Environmental sciences, Plant sciences

Introduction

Zinc (Zn), an essential micronutrient for all plant and animal species, is the most abundant trace element in cells1. Regulation of reproductive activities, fetal development and healthy growth, bone metabolism, insulin response, antioxidant defense system, brain development and behavioral response are among the metabolic processes in which zinc is involved2,3. Zn, which plays important roles in physiological processes such as DNA and protein synthesis, signal transduction and apoptosis, also acts as a cofactor for at least 300 different enzymes4. In addition to all these functions, Zn is the only element present in all enzymes belonging to the classes of oxidoreductases, transferases, hydrolases, lyases, isomerases and ligases in plants and is involved in carbohydrate metabolism, membrane regulation, maintenance of ion homeostasis, regulation of hormones, chlorophyll biosynthesis and other photosynthetic processes5.

While natural sources of Zn include factors such as weathering of rocks and soil erosion, anthropogenic activities that cause zinc accumulation in the environment include mining, metal smelting, industrial waste, wastewater discharges, fossil fuel use, and the use of Zn-containing fertilizers in agriculture6,7. Indeed, Zn pollution in ecosystems causes many potential hidden dangers. Exposure to high doses of Zn promotes a number of cellular toxicities, starting with the production of reactive oxygen species (ROS) in cells3. Excessive doses of Zn inhibit growth and development8, decrease chlorophyll content and photosynthetic activity9, cause lipid peroxidation, alter the activity of the antioxidant defense system, trigger chromosomal instability and decrease mitotic activity10 in plants. It has also been reported that elevated Zn reduces viability in both cancerous and non-cancerous cells and causes DNA strand breaks3. In addition to its destructive effects on plants, it harms microorganisms in the soil, negatively affecting the nutrient cycle and soil fertility11. The accumulation of Zn, which has the potential to affect animals through the food chain, can lead to developmental and immune problems and even death7.

The pressure of environmental pollution brought about by rapid industrialization has necessitated the use of rapid, reliable, and economical model systems that offer practical advantages, including the absence of animal ethical concerns, to assess the potential biological effects of these pollutants. Plants play a prominent role in toxicity studies due to their ease of cultivation, lack of ethical approval requirements, and ability to interact directly with contaminants in the growth medium through their root systems12. The Allium test is quite popular among plant indicators due to advantages such as the rapid growth of adventitious roots in a medium containing the test chemical and the size of the roots, which allows for easy monitoring of genotoxic effects on their chromosomes (2n = 16). The Allium assay is a widely validated plant bioassay recognized and used for evaluating the cytogenotoxic effects of hazardous chemicals13,14. As a sensitive and reliable system, the Allium test is also widely used as a comprehensive and multifaceted method that allows for the simultaneous evaluation of the toxicity potential of disruptive substances and the bioprotective effects of natural products15.

Helichrysum arenarium (L.) Moench is a medicinal plant named after the Greek words “helios” (sun) and “chrysos” (golden) due to its bright golden yellow flower heads16. This perennial herbaceous species, belonging to the Asteraceae family, has been taken under protection in Serbia and Sweden due to its endangered status and has been classified as a “species requiring care” in many other countries17. H. arenarium, also known as strawflower, sandy endless flower or immortelle, grows naturally in Asia, Europe, North Africa, and China16,18. Turkey, along with the former Soviet Union countries and Poland, is among the main suppliers of H. arenarium heads in the global market19. The leaves and flowers of the plant, which are rich in secondary metabolites such as phenolic compounds, flavonoids, and essential oils, are widely used in traditional medicine for purposes such as wound healing, treating digestive system diseases, and alleviating respiratory tract infections18. In the medical field, extracts obtained from H. arenarium plants that are frequently used for their cholagogue, choleretic, and anti-inflammatory properties have been reported to possess antimicrobial, antioxidant, neuroprotective, and antidiabetic qualities17,18. These extracts have also been reported to reduce micronucleus (MN) formation and possess an antimutagenic effect20.

This study was conducted using the A. cepa model organism to evaluate the potential bioprotective effects of H. arenarium extract against toxic damage caused by zinc sulfate (ZnSO4). For this purpose, growth, cytogenetic, biochemical and histological parameters, including weight gain, rooting percentage, root length, mitotic index (MI), DNA damage, frequencies of MN and chromosomal aberrations (CAs), activities of superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT) enzymes, levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) and chlorophyll pigments, and root meristem tissue structure were evaluated. In addition, the phenolic profile of the H. arenarium extract was characterized by LC-MS/MS to elucidate the possible mechanisms underlying its observed biological effects.

Materials and methods

A. cepa var. aggregatum onion bulbs obtained from a local market in Giresun were cleaned of dust, outer scales, and dried roots in the laboratory. The flowers of H. arenarium were purchased from a local supplier in Giresun province and originated from wild collections in the Şebinkarahisar region of Giresun (approximately 40.3–40.4° N, 38.9–39.1° E), Türkiye, where the species occurs naturally, and were taxonomically identified before analysis. The species identification of the onion bulbs and the flowers was confirmed by Prof. Dr. Zafer Türkmen, who works at the Department of Botany at Giresun University. ZnSO4 (CAS No. 7446-19-7) was purchased from Merck and used for the preparation of the test solutions. A voucher specimen was deposited in the Herbarium of the Department of Biology, under the code HA28/2026. The flowers were dried in the laboratory in the shade at room temperature. They were ground into powder using an electric grinder and extracted by mixing with 70% ethanol. The powder was left to soak in alcohol on a magnetic stirrer at room temperature for 24 h. The resulting mixture was then filtered through Whatman No. 1 filter paper, and the ethanol from the filtrate was removed at 40 °C under low pressure using a rotary evaporator. The remaining extract was then dissolved in distilled water. Experimental research on plant and plant parts, including the collection of plant material, complied with relevant institutional, national, and international guidelines and legislation.

Six A. cepa groups, each consisting of 50 bulbs (n = 50 biological replicates) were established and treated with tap water (control), 50 mg/L H. arenarium extract, 100 mg/L H. arenarium extract, 140 mg/L ZnSO4, 140 mg/L ZnSO4 + 50 mg/L H. arenarium extract and 140 mg/L ZnSO4 + 100 mg/L H. arenarium extract, respectively (Table 1). The solutions were refreshed once every 24 h. The process was continued for 72 h at room temperature to obtain roots and for 144 h to obtain shoots. The ZnSO4 concentration used in the experiment was selected based on previous studies by Akbaba21 and Üstündağ et al.10. This concentration was selected as a sublethal dose capable of inducing significant cytotoxic and genotoxic effects without completely halting growth. This allowed for the evaluation of potential protective responses. Akbaba21 reported the EC50 value of ZnSO4 for A. cepa as 70 ppm, while Üstündağ et al.10 showed that 140 mg/L ZnSO4 induced serious toxicity in A. cepa without causing lethality. As a result of preliminary experiments (25–300 mg/L) conducted to determine suitable concentrations for the A. cepa test, using root growth as the primary endpoint, two concentrations of H. arenarium extract (50 and 100 mg/L) were selected and the corresponding data are provided in the Supplementary Material (Figure S1). Among the tested doses, 100 mg/L yielded the highest recovery rate, while increasing the concentration to 200 and 300 mg/L did not result in any further improvement. Therefore, 100 mg/L was selected as the upper effective dose, representing the optimal concentration that provided the maximum protective effect without additional benefit at higher levels.

Assessment of growth parameters

The rooting percentage was calculated based on the total number of rooted individuals within each group of 50 bulbs (n = 50 biological replicates). Bulbs that formed adventitious roots at least 1 cm in length were considered rooted. The average root length of the groups (n = 10 biological replicates) was determined by measuring the length of these adventitious roots from the basal plate to the root tip using a ruler. For each group, measurements were obtained from ten independent bulbs, and the mean value was calculated from these biological replicates. The weight of the bulbs measured before the application of chemicals was considered the initial weight, while the weight measured after the 72-hour treatment period was considered the final weight. Weight gain (n = 10 biological replicates) was calculated as the difference between these two values14.

Assessment of cytogenetic parameters

The frequency of CAs, MN formation, MI alteration, and tail DNA (%) were evaluated to determine the cytotoxic and genotoxic effects of the treatments. CAs, MN and MI analyses were performed on the same microscopic preparations according to the method described by Macar et al.22. For these analyses, root tips approximately 1 cm in length were excised after the treatment period and fixed in Clarke’s solution for 120 min. The fixed root tissues were then hydrolyzed in 1 N HCl at 60 °C for 9 min. After hydrolysis, the root tips were squashed on microscope slides and stained with 1% acetocarmine for 12 h to obtain permanent preparations. All slide preparation steps, including fixation, hydrolysis, staining, and squashing, were performed under the same experimental conditions for all groups to ensure consistency. The slides were examined using a light microscope (Irmeco, IM-450 TI). CAs and MN were evaluated by scoring 100 cells from each of 10 slides (1000 cells in total for each parameter), whereas mitotic index values were determined by counting 1000 cells per slide from 10 slides (10,000 cells in total). MI was calculated by expressing the percentage of cells undergoing mitotic division relative to the total number of cells examined. The criteria outlined by Fenech23 were used to identify MN, which include smaller size, staining intensity comparable to the main nucleus, and the presence of a unique structure within the cytoplasm that is distinct from the main nucleus. Furthermore, total genotoxicity (%) in the ZN, ZNHAE1 and ZNHAE2 groups was calculated to determine the overall genotoxic impact of ZnSO4 exposure and the mitigating role of H. arenarium extract according to the Eq. In this study, DNA breaks in root nuclei were assessed using the comet assay. Slides used for the assay were first immersed in ethanol for 24 h and then dried in an oven25. To prepare a base layer, slides were coated with 100 µL of 1% normal melting point agarose (dissolved in distilled water at 50 °C). A second layer was spread using a 100 µL mixture obtained by mixing the isolated cell suspension with 1% low-melting-point agarose (1:8, v/v). The solidified slides were then incubated in alkaline electrophoresis buffer for 40 min to allow DNA unwinding. Electrophoresis was carried out at 4 °C for 20 min under an electric field strength of 0.7 V/cm. Following electrophoresis, 0.4 M Tris-HCl solution was used to neutralize the slides. DNA was stained with 100 µL of ethidium bromide, and the slides were examined under a fluorescence microscope. For each group, 1,000 nuclei were assessed using TriTek Comet Assay (ver. 2.0.0.0.38) program. The analyzed nuclei were obtained from independent root samples, ensuring biological replication across treatment groups. The head DNA percentage indicates the proportion of intact DNA retained in the nucleus, whereas the tail DNA percentage reflects the degree of DNA fragmentation and strand breakage. Tail length represents the migration distance of damaged DNA fragments, while tail moment integrates both tail length and the proportion of DNA in the tail and is considered a sensitive indicator of overall genotoxic damage. The proportion of tail DNA (%) was used as an indicator of DNA damage26. Damage levels were categorized as follows: ≤5% (no or minimal damage), 5.1–20% (low damage), 20.1–40% (moderate damage), 40.1–75% (high damage), and ≥ 75.1% (severe damage).

Assessment of biochemical parameters

The biochemical responses induced by ZnSO4 in A. cepa were assessed using the activity levels of antioxidant enzymes such as superoxide dismutase (SOD) and catalase (CAT), as well as the levels of malondialdehyde (MDA) in root tissues and chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b in leaf tissues obtained from the experimental groups.

SOD and CAT enzymes were extracted following the method of Zou et al.27, with minor modifications. Approximately 0.5 g of freshly harvested root material was homogenized in 5 mL of ice-cold monosodium phosphate buffer (50 mM, pH 7.8). The homogenate was centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 25 min to remove cellular residues. The supernatant obtained after centrifugation was used as the enzyme extract and maintained at 4 °C until the enzymatic assays were carried out.

SOD enzyme activity was evaluated by monitoring the inhibition of nitroblue tetrazolium (NBT) photoreduction. The reaction mixture consisted of phosphate buffer (0.05 M), 0.1 mL EDTA (0.1 mM), 0.3 mL NBT (750 µM), 0.3 mL methionine (130 mM), 1.0 mL distilled water, 0.3 mL riboflavin (20 µM), and insoluble polyvinylpyrrolidone (4%). The reaction was initiated by adding 0.2 mL extract to the reaction mix under intense light exposure for 12 min (two 15-W fluorescent lamps). After incubation, the tubes were transferred to darkness to terminate the reaction. The absorbance was then recorded at 560 nm. Because the assay was based on endpoint determination of NBT photoreduction inhibition after a fixed illumination period, time-course measurements at 1-minute intervals were not required. SOD activity was expressed as the amount of enzyme required to produce a 50% inhibition of NBT reduction28.

CAT activity was determined spectrophotometrically by following the decomposition of hydrogen peroxide at 240 nm. The reaction mixture contained 1.5 mL phosphate buffer (200 mM), 0.3 mL H₂O₂ (0.1 M), 1.0 mL distilled water, and 0.2 mL enzyme extract. The decrease in absorbance due to the enzymatic breakdown of H₂O₂ was monitored spectrophotometrically at 240 nm29.

Changes in lipid peroxidation levels induced by ZnSO4 in root tissues were estimated by determining the MDA content using the thiobarbituric acid (TBA) reaction. Root tip samples (0.5 g) were homogenized in 5% trichloroacetic acid (TCA) and centrifuged to obtain the supernatant. The supernatant was then mixed with TBA solution prepared in 20% TCA and incubated at 98 °C for 25 min. The reaction was stopped by cooling in an ice bath, and the solution was centrifuged again after being cleared of cellular residues. The absorbance of the resulting supernatant was measured at 532 nm30.

Chlorophyll pigments were extracted from leaf tissues using acetone. Approximately 0.3 g of fresh leaf material was homogenized with 100% acetone, and the suspension was kept in darkness at low temperature to facilitate pigment diffusion. After filtration and centrifugation, the absorbance of the extract was measured at 645 nm and 663 nm using a spectrophotometer31. Chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b concentrations were calculated according to the equations: Chlorophyll a = (11.75 × A663) − (2.35 × A645) and Chlorophyll b = (18.61 × A645) − (3.96 × A663)31.

All biochemical measurements were performed using ten independent biological replicates for each treatment group (n = 10 biological replicates).

Assessment of histological parameters

To evaluate structural changes in the root meristem, transverse sections of adventitious roots were examined microscopically. Root segments were taken manually and stained by applying 0.5 mL of 1% methylene blue solution. To ensure sampling standardization, transverse sections were consistently obtained from the same meristematic region (approximately 1–2 mm above the root cap/apical meristem) in all treatment groups. From each bulb (n = 10 biological replicates), ten cross-sections were obtained, resulting in a total of 100 technical replicate sections analyzed for each treatment group. The stained sections were observed and photographed using a light microscope (IM-450 TI). The extent of tissue damage was categorized as follows: undamaged (UD), slightly damaged (●), moderately damaged (●●), and severely damaged (●●●)32.

LC-MS/MS analysis of phenolic compounds

The phenolic composition of the H. arenarium was characterized using liquid chromatography coupled with tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) at the Hitit University Scientific and Technical Application and Research Center (HUBTUAM). The extract was prepared by dissolving 1 g of dried plant material in 20 mL of a solvent mixture of methanol and dichloromethane (4:1, v/v). The solution was filtered through a 0.45 μm syringe filter to eliminate insoluble residues. Chromatographic separation was achieved using a Thermo Scientific LC-MS/MS system fitted with an ODS Hypersil column (4.6 × 250 mm). The column temperature was maintained at 30 °C, and the mobile phase was delivered at a constant flow rate of 0.7 mL/min33. Each analytical run lasted 34 min, allowing adequate resolution of the phenolic constituents present in the extract.

A targeted analytical strategy was adopted for compound identification. Selected phenolic standards known for their antioxidant and protective biological activities were used as reference compounds. Quantification of the detected phenolics was performed using external calibration curves constructed from authentic standards. All phenolic standards were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich with a purity of ≥ 99%. Detailed LC-MS/MS methodological parameters, including the blank chromatogram (Figure S2), gradient profile (Table S1), MS/MS ion transitions (Table S2), calibration equations, R² values, and linear ranges (Table S3), are provided in the Supplementary Material.

Statistical analysis

The normality of the data was assessed using the Kolmogorov-Smirnov and Shapiro-Wilk tests, and homogeneity of variances was evaluated using Levene’s test. Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 23). Data are presented as mean ± SD. Differences among groups were first tested for compliance with parametric assumptions. Since these assumptions were met, the data were analyzed using one-way ANOVA followed by Duncan’s multiple range test. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

In the control group, the rooting percentage was 94%, the root length was 4.90 ± 1.14 cm and the weight gain was + 5.10 g (Table 2). No significant differences were observed between the HAE1 and HAE2 groups and the control group for these parameters (p > 0.05). Therefore, the selected doses of H. arenarium extract did not induce a toxic effect on the growth parameters of A. cepa. On the other hand, the rooting percentage of the ZN group dropped to 52% (Table 2). Additionally, ZnSO4 caused a significant decrease in other physiological parameters (p < 0.05). The mean root length in the ZN group was determined to be 1.50 ± 0.76 cm, and the weight gain was + 1.30 g. Compared to the control group, ZnSO₄ application reduced the root length by approximately 69% and weight gain by approximately 74%. These results suggest that ZnSO4 exerted a significant inhibitory effect on the growth of A. cepa. In the ZNHAE1 and ZNHAE2 groups exposed to ZnSO₄ together with H. arenarium extract, a noticeable recovery in the growth parameters was observed. Although the growth parameters in these groups did not reach those of the control group, they were higher than those in the ZN group. The rooting percentages of the ZNHAE1 and ZNHAE2 groups were 62% and 76%, respectively. Additionally, the root length and weight gain values of the ZNHAE1 and ZNHAE2 groups were significantly higher than those of the ZN group (p < 0.05). Indeed, the root length and weight gain values of the ZNHAE2 group were 2.47 and 2.59 times those of the ZN group, respectively. These findings reveal that H. arenarium extract partially alleviates the Zn-induced physiological growth suppression in a dose-dependent manner and exhibits a protective effect. A representative image illustrating root development across treatment groups is provided in the Supplementary Material (Figure S3) to visually support the growth parameter assessments.

Our findings support previous studies showing that high concentrations of Zn suppress plant growth. Meng et al.34 demonstrated that excessive Zn exposure decreased the root elongation and total biomass increment in Ipomoea batatas. Furthermore, Mahmoudi et al.35 showed that root length, root dry weight, leaf number, and aerial parts biomass of Ocimum basilicum decreased following the excess Zn treatment. In another study, it was revealed that root and stem dry weight, plant height, stem diameter, leaf number, leaf width and leaf length lessened under Zn stress in Cucumis sativus36.

Zn can be actively or passively absorbed by plant roots and exhibits high mobility in the xylem and phloem. At high doses, Zn application inhibits root development by blocking cell division and cell elongation in root tissues37. Inhibition of root development can lead to a reduction in the plant’s uptake of water and nutrients and, consequently, to stunted overall growth. Furthermore, it has been noted that too much Zn affects the uptake and distribution of ions like sodium, potassium, magnesium, iron, calcium, and phosphate. This is because Zn shares the same absorption and transport pathways with these elements. This causes disruption of the K+/Na+ balance in cells, leading to impaired regulation of osmotic stress, which is crucial for growth and development34. An imbalance in nutrient levels can also inhibit growth by suppressing chlorophyll synthesis and metabolic activity35. Another possible mechanism underlying the growth-inhibitory effects of Zn toxicity is that exposure to high concentrations of Zn leads to the formation of ROS38. Lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage brought on by ROS accumulation can all impede growth. Enzymes such as RNA and DNA polymerases, oxidoreductases, transphosphorylases, isomerases, and aldolases require Zn as a prosthetic group. These enzymes play key roles in protein synthesis and energy production, which are essential for plant growth37. Another consequence of overexposure to Zn may be the disruption of hormonal balance, particularly auxin metabolism. It has been reported that the biosynthesis and transport of this hormone, which plays a key role in root development and cell elongation, exhibit extreme sensitivity to zinc39.

The growth inhibition observed in A. cepa was the primary indicator of ZnSO4-induced cytotoxicity. In groups treated with H. arenarium extract mixed with ZnSO4, this effect was observed to be partially attenuated (Table 2). To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies reporting the mitigating effects of H. arenarium extract against Zn or other heavy metals in plants. Nevertheless, it is recognized that H. arenarium extract contains high levels of phenolic compounds such as coumarin, luteolin, catechin, and kaempferol, and that these compounds neutralize metal ions by chelating them40. Furthermore, Babotă et al.41 reported that the phytochemical compounds present in H. arenarium possess a radical-scavenging effect, which is closely associated with antioxidant capacity. This may have contributed to a reduction in oxidative damage under zinc stress, thereby helping to preserve cellular integrity and, consequently, improving growth parameters.

No statistically significant differences were observed in the MI, MN, and CAs parameters among the groups treated with tap water and H. arenarium extract at different concentrations (p > 0.05) (Table 3). Meanwhile, treatment with ZnSO₄ reduced the mean MI in the ZN group by 34.6% compared to the control group. In addition, while CAs were observed at negligible levels in the HAE1 and HAE2 groups, they increased significantly in the ZN group due to the application of ZnSO₄ (p < 0.05). The CA types observed in the ZN group were ranked from most frequent to least frequent as sticky chromosome (STC), fragment (FRA), vagrant chromosome (VAC), bridge (BRI), unequal distribution of chromatin (UDC), vacuolated nucleus (VAN), and disorientation (DOR) (Table 3). In the ZNHAE1 and ZNHAE2 groups, MI increased, while the frequencies of MN and CA decreased significantly (p < 0.05), compared to the ZN group (Table 3). Notably, the ameliorative effect was more pronounced in the ZNHAE2 group than in the ZNHAE1 group. Indeed, the total genotoxicity percentage, calculated based on the combined frequency of MN and CAs, peaked in the ZN group at approximately 31.9%. In the ZNHAE1 group, which was treated with 50 mg/L H. arenarium extract in combination with ZnSO₄, this value decreased to 25.7%, and in the ZNHAE2 group, which received a higher extract dose, it fell to approximately 19.1% (Fig. 1).

As a primary measure of cell proliferation, the remarkable reduction in MI and subsequent root growth inhibition in A. cepa under Zn stress suggests that the developmental retardation is closely related to the suppression of mitotic activity and the disruption of the cell cycle. Problems in DNA replication during the S phase or blocking the G1 phase are the main reasons for a decrease in cell division42. The results of this study are consistent with numerous studies showing that heavy metals, including Zn, inhibit root tip growth, a process that occurs concurrently with a reduction in MI10,24,43,44. On the other hand, MN (Fig. 2a) is a marker of genotoxic damage and may be generated from the formation of FRA and chromosome lagging during mitosis45. The MN value, which was not observed in the Control, HAE1, and HAE2 groups, reached 85.13 ± 7.16 in the ZN group (Table 3). MN, which has unstable and fragile membranes, contains genetic material that remains isolated due to delays in the anaphase stage. The DNA within the MN may exhibit abnormalities such as asynchronous replication and condensation. If this genetic material undergoes chromothripsis and is reincorporated into the primary nucleus, it can lead to further genomic instability and cancer development24. STC (Fig. 2b), the most frequent CA type in the ZN group, is an irreversible chromatid abnormality that can lead to cell death. Crosslinking of DNA with DNA or DNA with proteins may result in STC formation46. This type of anomaly can also result from fragmentation, incorrect condensation, and DNA depolymerization47. Furthermore, chromosomal condensation caused by STC prevents chromosomes from separating properly. As a result, the chromosomes, which are pulled toward the poles by the spindle fibers, break into fragments48. Consistently, FRA (Fig. 2c) was the second most frequent CA type in the ZN group (Table 3). VAC (Fig. 2d), the third most common CA type in the ZN group, is an abnormality in which chromosomes migrate earlier to the poles than they should, resulting in UDC (Fig. 2f) in daughter cells. It may be linked to disruptions of tubulin proteins or cytoskeleton proteins, as well as defects in DNA and DNA-bound proteins24. The accumulation of both VAC and UDC in the ZN group indicated that Zn is a spindle fiber toxin. Indeed, disruption of mitotic spindle fibers increases the frequency of this type of CAs, while also inhibiting cell division, which can lead to a decrease in MI (Table 3). BRI (Fig. 2e), another CA that showed significant accumulation in the ZN group, is a marker of clastogenesis characterized by the breaking and subsequent fusion of broken chromatids or chromosomes49. Indeed, chromosome stickiness, which impedes proper separation, along with the formation of BRI and FRA, are three fundamental components of genomic instability that follow each other and generally result in MN formation50. The presence of VAN (Fig. 2g) in the ZN group suggests a further nuclear defect potentially linked to the suppression of DNA synthesis. As noted by Topatan et al.51, such anomalies may be triggered by nuclear toxins and often lead to the initiation of apoptosis. Disorientation (Fig. 2h), the least common CA observed in the ZN group, may have resulted from heavy metals like Zn disrupting microtubule organization and preventing chromosomes from aligning with the equatorial plane. Consequently, the chromosomes may have been unable to migrate towards the poles and may have been oriented randomly within the cytoplasm.

In addition to cytogenetic findings, the comet test was conducted to evaluate DNA damage in more detail (Table 4). As a popular biomarker for ecotoxicological studies, it is a sensitive test system that allows measuring genotoxicity at the individual cell level52. In electrophoresis, DNA fragments, consisting of single or double-stranded breaks, move relative to the nucleus, forming a comet-like structure. The head DNA percentage indicates the portion of intact DNA, whereas the tail DNA percentage reflects the degree of DNA fragmentation53. When the percentage of tail DNA was taken into account, negligible DNA damage was observed in the first three groups (Control, HAE1 and HAE2) (Table 4). This finding indicated the genomic safety of the H. arenarium extract at the tested concentrations. The DNA damage induced by the ZnSO₄ treatment in A. cepa root tip cells was demonstrated not only by an increase in tail DNA percentage but also by dramatic changes in head DNA percentage, tail length, and tail moment (Table 4). Tail DNA (%), tail length and tail moment were interpreted as complementary indicators of DNA damage, reflecting fragmentation extent, migration distance of damaged DNA, and integrated genotoxic injury, respectively. While early research focused on tail length and tail moment as damage indicators in the comet assay, tail DNA percentage has gained popularity as a damage scale54. The proportion of tail DNA in the ZN group was 62.1 ± 2.02%, which indicates ‘high damage’ (Table 4). In the groups treated with the combination of ZnSO₄ and H. arenarium, DNA damage was also reduced, consistent with the other cytogenetic findings (Tables 3 and 4). In the ZNHAE1 and ZNHAE2 groups, tail DNA percentages were 37.4 ± 1.90 and 30.9 ± 1.52, respectively. As a result, the H. arenarium extract reduced the DNA damage caused by ZnSO4 alone to a ‘moderate’ level.

Comet analysis and cytogenetic parameters such as MN and CAs are used as complementary methods to reveal the genotoxicity of pollutants in biological systems at different levels12. The former shows early and potentially repairable DNA damage, while the latter reflects the chronic consequences of this damage at the chromosomal level. Consequently, the concurrent increase in comet tail with MN and CA frequencies in the ZN group may suggest that DNA damage is not transient and leads to permanent genetic instability, such as chromosomal breaks, and misrepair events. Our findings were in line with the study of Üstündağ et al.10, who reported that ZnSO4 administration in A. cepa triggered an increase in comet tail DNA percentage along with an accumulation in MN and CAs. In the aforementioned study, STC, FRA, VAC and BRI were among the most frequent CA types reported. Furthermore, Truta et al.55 demonstrated that increasing doses of ZnSO₄ and zinc acetate (Zn(CH₃COO)₂) stimulated mitotic activity in Hordeum vulgare, while exhibiting both clastogenic and aneugenic effects on chromosomes during the ana-telophase and metaphase stages. In another study, it was reported that treatments with 2–80 mM Zn2+ (2 h) and 2–12 mM Zn2+ (24 h) increased comet tail moment values in the root cells of Nicotiana tabacum seedlings in a dose-dependent manner. The study mentioned indicated that Zn accumulation was at higher levels in roots compared to shoots and that DNA damage was particularly evident in root tissues56.

The genotoxicity caused by Zn exposure may be associated with various mechanisms. Firstly, it has been reported that metal ions including Zn can cause oxidative stress in plants due to the formation of ROS57. Under optimum conditions, Zn, a vital cofactor for antioxidant enzymes such as SOD, contributes to oxidative balance within cells58. However, in excessive doses, it can disrupt this balance, ultimately leading to DNA damage, the formation of MN and the accumulation of CAs. ROS can lead to single- and double-strand breaks, base oxidation and the formation of abasic regions through direct interaction with DNA. Furthermore, if the cellular defense system proves inadequate against these primary damages and these defects cannot be repaired, secondary genetic damages such as chromosomal fragmentation, deletion, translocation and MN may occur. Truta et al.55 stated that the stable complexes formed by Zn with nucleic acids are also closely linked to errors occurring in the genetic information system. In this context, Zn-induced genotoxicity may arise not only from oxidative stress but also from direct interactions with genetic material.

The fact that MN, CAs, and DNA damage showed a decreasing trend in the ZNHAE1 and ZNHAE2 groups compared with the ZN group indicates that the extract mitigated genotoxicity in parallel with the selected doses (Tables 3 and 4). To the best of our knowledge, there are no studies in the literature directly demonstrating the alleviatory function of H. arenarium extract against heavy metal-induced genotoxicity. However, there are numerous reports on the protective potential of plant extracts against genotoxicity caused by heavy metals in A. cepa15,44,59. The antigenotoxic effect of H. arenarium extract may be due to its rich phenolic composition. The ability of phenolic compounds to chelate metal ions and their potential to reduce oxidative damage by scavenging free radicals41,60 may have contributed to the antigenotoxic role of the H. arenarium extract.

The levels of MDA, SOD, and CAT activities, representing oxidative stress parameters, as well as the concentrations of chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b pigments were investigated to evaluate the biochemical toxicity induced by H. arenarium extract, ZnSO₄, and H. arenarium extract and ZnSO₄ mixtures in A. cepa (Figs. 3 and 4). No statistically significant differences were observed in biochemical parameters among the control, HAE1, and HAE2 groups (p > 0.05). Meanwhile, the ZnSO₄ treatment resulted in significant differences in all biochemical parameters (p < 0.05). In the ZN group, MDA levels increased by approximately 2.21-fold compared with the control group, while SOD and CAT activities increased by approximately 2.36-fold and 2.22-fold, respectively (Fig. 3). Under optimal conditions, plants maintain a unique balance between ROS formation and neutralization through a combination of non-enzymatic antioxidant compounds and outstanding enzymes such as SOD, CAT, various peroxidases and reductases. The disruption of this balance, which is crucial to the continuity of fundamental processes such as signaling, growth and development, due to adverse conditions can lead to various consequences, including cellular damage and even programmed cell death61. Zn interacts with phospholipids in membrane structures and with the sulfhydryl groups of membrane proteins. These interactions are essential for maintaining cell membrane integrity. During Zn-induced stress, ROS such as superoxide anion and hydrogen peroxide increase. Consequently, lipid peroxidation may occur in cell membranes due to the disruption of the oxidant-antioxidant balance. Zn can also trigger the activation of NADPH oxidase in the plasma membrane and increase the activity of the lipoxygenase enzyme, thereby leading to a further increase in superoxide anion production62. During this process, the activity of the SOD enzyme, which converts superoxide into hydrogen peroxide, increases and this constitutes the initial stage of enzymatic antioxidant defense in cells63. Although Zn is a required cofactor for Cu/Zn-SOD isoenzyme localized in chloroplasts, Zn exposure at high doses may induce imbalances in SOD activity in plants64. CAT plays an active role in H₂O₂ detoxification and, due to its extraordinary speed, converts up to 26 million H₂O₂ molecules into oxygen and water per minute61. ZnSO4 administration caused a dramatic decrease in chlorophyll pigment levels compared with the control group (Fig. 3). In the ZN group, chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b concentrations decreased by 66.4% and 65.5%, respectively. Our findings have confirmed previous studies showing that Zn overexposure triggers oxidative stress in plants and reduces the content of photosynthetic pigments. Islam et al.57 demonstrated that Zn treatment in Zea mays significantly increased lipid peroxidation and hydrogen peroxide levels while reducing chlorophyll and biomass production. Additionally, in the study of Üstündağ et al.10, it was reported that MDA levels along with SOD and CAT activities increased in the roots of A. cepa plants exposed to Zn, whereas chlorophyll levels in the leaves showed a significant decrease compared to the control group. In another study, Li et al.65 reported that Zn can accumulate in both the roots and shoots of wheat and, at high concentrations, causes a significant reduction in chlorophyll content. In the aforementioned study, SOD activity also increased in roots treated with Zn, along with hydrogen peroxide and MDA levels. dos Santos et al.66 also showed that both Zn deficiency and Zn excess alter antioxidant enzyme activities, induce lipid peroxidation, and reduce photosynthetic pigments in Coffee arabica. Interestingly, the study indicated that Zn deficiency caused more severe damage than Zn excess.

The photosynthetic system in plants is one of the primary targets of Zn toxicity. Zn is thought to damage photosynthetic pigments when it reaches the chloroplasts. Therefore, to prevent Zn from damaging vital organelles such as chloroplasts once it has entered the plant cell, it has been reported that the HMA1 transporter located in the chloroplast envelope transports Zn2+ ions from the chloroplast to the cytoplasm, where they are subsequently accumulated in the vacuole64. It has been reported that Zn stress may cause damage to the stomatal opening and closing process, photosystems, the oxygen-evolving complex, the plastoquinone pool and Rubisco66,67. The fact that exposure to Zn leads to a decrease in chlorophyll concentration in A. cepa can also be attributed to disruptive effects of the element on the photosynthetic system. This may be related to Zn disturbing the balance between plant nutrients, thereby inhibiting the uptake and utilization of elements such as Mg and Fe, which are essential for chlorophyll synthesis. Furthermore, Zn stress may lead to a disorganization in thylakoid membrane structure9,68. High levels of Zn exposure may have induced not only a reduction in chlorophyll biosynthesis but also an increase in chlorophyll degradation or the inhibition of photosynthetic electron transport69.

In the ZNHAE1 and ZNHAE2 groups, co-application of H. arenarium extract with ZnSO₄ reduced MDA levels as well as SOD and CAT activities compared to the ZN group while significantly increasing chlorophyll pigment content (p < 0.05) (Figs. 3 and 4). According to the findings, H. arenarium extract was capable of partially alleviating the biochemical stress caused by Zn, and this protective effect was directly proportional to the dose of the extract. Indeed, the mean values for MDA, SOD activity, and CAT activity in the ZNHAE2 group were reduced by 37.1%, 37.0%, and 30.5%, respectively, compared with the ZN group. Additionally, the chlorophyll a and chlorophyll b levels determined in this group were approximately 1.9 and 2.1 times higher than those in the ZN group, respectively (Fig. 4). The partial reduction in biochemical toxicity observed in the ZNHAE1 and ZNHAE2 groups suggested that the H. arenarium extract alleviated the severity of oxidative stress, leading to a reduction in both membrane damage and the need for oxidative defense. On the other hand, the increase in chlorophyll levels may indicate that the H. arenarium extract mitigates Zn toxicity on the photosynthetic system, thereby helping to preserve photosynthetic functions. To the best of our knowledge, this study is the first to demonstrate the potential of H. arenarium extract to mitigate heavy metal-induced biochemical toxicity in plants. Nevertheless, the rich phenolic and flavonoid composition of the extracts may have contributed to reducing Zn-induced oxidative stress in A. cepa. Czinner et al.70 reported that the bioactive compounds found in H. arenarium flower extract can act as primary and secondary antioxidants, prevent ROS-induced diseases, and inhibit lipid peroxidation by scavenging free radicals.

Table 5; Fig. 5 demonstrate that noticeable structural alterations occurred in root meristem tissues following application of ZnSO₄. Among these changes, epidermis cell damage (Fig. 5e), flattened nucleus (Fig. 5f), and cortex cell damage (Fig. 5g) reached a severe level, accompanied by a moderate level of damage for conduction tissue thickening (Fig. 5h). In contrast, no histological abnormalities (Fig. 5a–d) were detected in the control and H. arenarium extract-only groups. Co-treatment with the extract markedly reduced the intensity of Zn-induced structural disruptions. A greater improvement was evident in the ZNHAE2 group, where structural alterations were limited to slight damage in epidermal, nuclear, and cortex tissues, and no thickening was observed in conduction tissues.

Elevated Zn exposure in plants induces oxidative stress and disrupts cytoskeletal organization through Ca2+ displacement71,72. Such Zn-induced peroxidation and ROS production likely underlie the epidermal/cortex lesions, nuclear flattening and vascular tissue thickening. In addition, the plant cell wall acts as the first barrier against heavy metal ions entering the cytoplasm. The binding of these ions to cell wall components, including pectin, lignin, cellulose, hemicellulose, and proteins, may contribute to thickening of cell wall and conduction tissues73. Notably, flattened nucleus may be associated not only with membrane and cytoskeletal disruption, but also with genotoxic damage, as similar nuclear and chromatin alterations have been reported in A. cepa under heavy metal stress43. Conversely, the flavonoid-rich H. arenarium extract alleviates the toxic effects of Zn through its antioxidant and metal-chelating properties74. These results are consistent with previous studies on protective effects of plant extracts against heavy metals, which showed that curcumin reduced Ni toxicity and that Echinacea extract provided dose-dependent protection against Hg32,75.

LC-MS/MS analysis revealed that the H. arenarium extract is rich in phenolic compounds (Table 6; Fig. 6). Among the analyzed compounds, apigenin (632.391 µg/g), which had the highest concentration, stands out as the dominant compound in the extract. Following apigenin in terms of concentration, chlorogenic acid (263.463 µg/g), luteolin (150.450 µg/g) and caffeic acid (103.925 µg/g), together with apigenin, constitute a large proportion of the phenolic composition of the H. arenarium extract. In addition, the extract contains naringenin (47.414 µg/g), luteolin-7-O-glucoside (27.373 µg/g), cinnamic acid (24.731 µg/g), taxifolin (14.662 µg/g), quercetin (13.292 µg/g), sesamol (12.771 µg/g), protocatechuic aldehyde (12.433 µg/g), and pyrogallol (10.967 µg/g). Compounds present in lower concentrations in the extract include ferulic acid (6.682), vanillin (4.465), 4-hydroxybenzoic acid (2.803), oleuropein (2.731), salicylic acid (2.070), syringic aldehyde (1.363), and galangin (0.915). In contrast, it was determined that certain phenolic compounds evaluated in the analysis could not be detected in the extract (Table 6). The phenolic compounds found in plant extracts may vary depending on the plant species, genotype, stage of development, and the environmental conditions of the region in which they are grown. Furthermore, the extraction method and the processes applied also significantly influence the profile and quantity of phenolic compounds. Uğur and Güzel60 reported that a large proportion of the phenolic composition of the methanolic extract of H. arenarium consists of quercetin, luteolin, and naringin. In another study, the extract of H. arenarium was found to contain predominantly apigenin, chlorogenic acid, luteolin-7-O-glucoside, naringenin, arzanol, and arenol74. It has been demonstrated that apigenin, the most abundant flavonoid in H. arenarium extract, suppresses CAs and DNA breaks caused by genotoxic chemicals in a dose-dependent manner76,77. In addition to its anticarcinogenic effects and its natural presence in many spices, vegetables, and fruits, apigenin also plays an important role in both conventional and alternative medicine due to its free radical scavenging properties. The antioxidant potential of apigenin, which is also known for its ability to reduce lipid peroxidation, is supported by functions such as the inhibition of oxidative enzymes, the modulation of enzymatic and non-enzymatic antioxidants, metal chelation, and the regulation of redox signaling pathways78. Chlorogenic acid, another key component of H. arenarium extract, has been widely reported in cancer studies to exhibit antioxidant and anticarcinogenic properties, which are associated with the activation of antioxidant enzymes and the suppression of ROS-induced harmful signaling pathways79. Hernandes et al.80 suggested that chlorogenic acid targets DNA methyltransferases to modulate DNA methylation. It has also been demonstrated that chlorogenic acid, when applied exogenously to Malus domestica leaves, reduces membrane damage, alters catalase activity, increases the levels of other phenolic compounds, and preserves chlorophyll content and the maximum efficiency of photosystem II81. Caffeic acid, one of the components involved in the formation of chlorogenic acid, is one of the phenolic compounds present in high concentrations in the H. arenarium extract. It has been reported to be an antioxidant compound that reduces hydrogen peroxide-induced cell death and prevents the formation of double-strand breaks in DNA69. Akishina and Voronina82 revealed that exogenously applied caffeic acid increases chlorophyll content under both normal and salt-stressed conditions. Luteolin was the third most abundant phenolic compound in H. arenarium extract (Table 6). It is a flavonoid that functions in plants in response to environmental stressors, including UV83. Additionally, luteolin helps to suppress oxidative stress by scavenging superoxide anions and hydroxyl radicals generated via the Fenton reaction and the xanthine/xanthine oxidase system, thereby minimizing ROS-induced damage to lipids and DNA84. Overall, LC-MS/MS analysis has suggested that the H. arenarium extract has a rich composition in terms of phenolic compounds. In particular, the presence of compounds such as apigenin and chlorogenic acid in high concentrations, in combination with other phenolic compounds known for their antioxidant and antigenotoxic properties, may be linked to the biological effects exhibited by this extract against Zn toxicity. Indeed, previous studies have demonstrated that plant-derived extracts such as Piper betle leaf extract85, Moringa oleifera leaf extract86, and Thymus vulgaris oil87 have the potential to alleviate heavy metal-induced toxicity in model organisms, primarily through antioxidant mechanisms. However, these studies generally focused on limited parameters such as oxidative stress markers or cytogenetic endpoints. In contrast, the present study integrates multiple biological levels, providing a more comprehensive evaluation of the protective effects. Furthermore, the present findings also allow a clearer differentiation of the underlying mechanism. While most plant-based treatments are generally explained by non-specific antioxidant activity, the protective effect of H. arenarium observed in this study appears to involve multiple coordinated processes. In addition to ROS scavenging, the concurrent reduction in CAs and DNA damage suggests a role in preserving genomic stability, while the improvement in biochemical parameters indicates mitigation of oxidative imbalance. This distinction is particularly important under Zn stress, because Zn2+ is redox-inactive and does not directly participate in Fenton chemistry. Excess Zn disrupts cellular redox homeostasis indirectly through alterations in thiol status, ROS/RNS metabolism, metal and nutrient balance, and membrane-associated functions88. Moreover, the phenolic profile identified by LC-MS/MS supports the possible phenol-related mechanisms, such as modulation of redox-sensitive signaling pathways, preservation of antioxidant defenses, metal-binding effects, interaction with membrane lipids, and attenuation of DNA damage16,74,89. This mechanistic complexity distinguishes H. arenarium from more generally characterized plant-based treatments.

Conclusion

Due to the growing risk of heavy metal pollution to human and environmental health, there is an increasing focus on research which investigates the potential of plant extracts to alleviate heavy metal toxicity. The main aim of this study was to determine whether H. arenarium extract can reduce ZnSO4-induced damage in A. cepa. The results of this study show that excessive ZnSO₄ exposure causes clear toxicity in A. cepa. Inhibition of growth, a decrease in MI, an increase in MN formation, CA accumulation and elevated meristematic tissue damage indicates a strong cytotoxic and genotoxic effect. These effects are probably linked with ZnSO4-induced oxidative stress, as also indicated by changes in MDA levels and antioxidant enzyme activities. In contrast, H. arenarium extract was not toxic to A. cepa and, when administered together, significantly alleviated ZnSO4-induced damage in a dose-dependent manner. This protective effect can be attributed to the high antioxidant capacity of H. arenarium extract, arising from its rich phenolic content. The protective effect of the extract may involve not only the scavenging of ROS but also the specific action mechanisms of the bioactive compounds in the H. arenarium extract. The present study provides a multi-level evaluation focusing on physiological, cytogenetic, biochemical, and molecular responses, thereby offering a more comprehensive and mechanistically supported perspective. The present findings also confirm that A. cepa is a suitable and effective toxicity model. This work contributes to the growing evidence that plant-derived compounds can mitigate heavy metal stress in biological systems. This study has some limitations, including the use of a single model organism under controlled laboratory conditions, possible natural variation in plant extract composition, and the use of a single ZnSO₄ concentration, which limited dose-response evaluation. Future studies should address these aspects in broader biological systems and include comprehensive dose-response analyses to better elucidate concentration-dependent effects.
