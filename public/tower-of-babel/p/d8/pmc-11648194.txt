Research on the mechanism of human-machine security collaboration of miners considering automation trust
Authors: Juan Yang, Xue Yang, Shan Chai, Likun Ni, Xiao Wang, Langxuan Pan
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Safety in the coal mining industry is a long-standing issue. With the implementation of intelligent construction in coal mines, humans–machine collaboration is critical to accident prevention. We investigate the psychological dynamics among miners during intelligent construction by developing distinct evolutionary game models for miner security collaboration mechanisms, while also considering the level of trust in automation. We explore the impact of changes in automation trust on the human–machine security collaboration behaviour among miners as well as the regulatory strategies adopted by coal mining enterprises. Evidently, the current level of automation trust among miners is insufficient to ensure safety, and the safety management systems of coal mining enterprises are yet to reach a stable state. Examination of the relevant parameters using specific examples reveals that the optimal range for the level of automation trust among miners lies between 0.7 and 0.9, indicating a favourable coal mine safety production system. These conclusions provide a scientific foundation for effectively enhancing safety management in the context of intelligent construction in coal mines.

Introduction

With the normalisation of the COVID-19 pandemic and the evolving global political landscape, coal holds a crucial position as a cornerstone of China's energy safety. As a high-risk industry in China, the coal mining sector consistently exhibits high accident rates. Approximately 80 % of coal mine accidents can be attributed to unsafe miner behaviour [1].In this context, intelligent coal mine construction has allowed China's coal industry to simultaneously and synergistically make significant strides in intelligent production and construction [2,3].

Intelligent mining has led to a substantial reduction in the size of production teams; however, the coexistence of humans and automated systems poses security cooperation challenges [4]. The role of miners in coal mine safety operations has transitioned from being mere ‘operators’ to becoming ‘supervisors’ [5], which has emerged as the primary factor influencing the subjective initiative of intelligent systems [6,7].

Given the declining number of miners and the substantial investments made in intelligent equipment, guiding safety cooperation between miners and automation systems has become a critical issue to address in the management of coal mining enterprises in China. Conceptualised human–machine collaboration encompasses the collaboration between humans and robots, and emphasizes the interaction between humans and the robotic system itself. Yet, security collaboration behaviour differs from safety behaviour [8,9].

Security collaboration behaviour entails more extensive interaction, requiring both parties to collaborate to ensure the safe operation of the entire working system [10]. In intelligent manufacturing systems, physical systems take precedence, followed by information systems, whereas humans play a dominant role [11,12]. However, Nunes discovered that the role of human beings was often neglected in most studies of cyber-physical systems. Unlike intelligent manufacturing industries, such as automobiles, the coal mining industry has not fully embraced automation and intelligence owing to its complex tasks and processes; it still relies on human–machine collaboration and subjective human initiative [13].Miners' perceptions undergo significant changes when working alongside automation systems during intelligent construction in the coal mining industry. When collaborating with humans, there is a stronger emotional response and more stable cooperative relationship. By contrast, when collaborating with machines, real-time awareness of the machine's working state may be lacking, leading to greater uncertainties in cooperation.

Currently, scholars have not extensively explored security collaboration behaviour. Most contemporary research focuses on safety behaviour within the framework of rewards, punishments, and the supervision mechanisms of coal mining enterprises, overlooking the in-depth study of the psychological state of safety. Advances in intelligent construction in coal mines have shown that miners' acceptance of automation systems [14] and their knowledge of such systems influence human–machine collaboration behaviour [15]. Some scholars argue that trust issues are the underlying cause of safety problems in human–machine collaboration within the context of intelligent manufacturing [16]. Additionally, some miners hold the belief that ‘there will be no problems with the automation system’ [17].

Despite the implementation of safety measures, accidents and injuries continue to occur, posing a serious threat to workers' lives and health. These events have necessitated improvements to safety cooperation behaviour of miners.The existing research mainly explores the importance of government supervision [18] from the reward and punishment mechanism [18]and the supervision of safety investment [19]. Differences and connections with previous literature.

The increasing prevalence of automation technology in coal mines has also brought into focus the influence of trust on miners' safety cooperation behaviour. Accidents and disasters can occur when operators ‘overtrust’ automated systems. For example, an analysis of the 2019 Boeing 737-MAX 8 aircraft crash shows that the pilot was unable to intervene in the operation because the designers had overtrusted the manoeuvre characteristic enhancement systems. On the contrary, a lack of trust in automated systems can slow down the use of systems, or risk outright rejection, owing to the perception that these systems do not solve some problems or an unwillingness to provide the data required by the system, especially in the environment of underground coal mine operations.The role of trust in coal mine safety management has also received scant attention. We aim to fill this knowledge gap by exploring the factors influencing miners' safety cooperation behaviour and the regulatory focus of coal mining enterprises in the context of trust. Accordingly, we provide guidance and decision support to coal mining enterprises to help them better manage their safety behaviour.

We thus investigate two management questions: a)In the early and middle stages of intelligent construction, whether to consider automation trust, what are the differences between coal mine safety production systems ? b)What is the difference between different levels of automated trust and the choice of regulatory strategies for coal mining enterprises to monitor the safety collaboration behaviour of miners ?To address these questions, we established an evolutionary game model of miners' safety cooperation behaviour in the presence or absence of trust. This model considers various factors such as work environment, job stress, training, and education that impact miners' safety cooperation behaviour. It also incorporates ‘trust’ to analyse the probability of miners choosing safety cooperation behaviour and the regulatory strategies of coal mining enterprises. Mathematical calculations and simulation experiments yield findings on the evolution of miners' safety cooperation behaviour, which, in turn, provide a scientific basis for the development of safety management regulations in coal mining enterprises.

This study contributes significantly to coal mine safety management by providing valuable insights and recommendations. By understanding the factors that influence miners' safety cooperation behaviour, coal mine enterprises can implement targeted solutions to improve miners’ safety awareness and behaviour. Examining the regulatory priorities regarding trust issues will help coal mine enterprises develop effective strategies as well. Addressing and resolving automation trust issues will enable coal mine enterprises to ensure a high level of safety cooperation among miners when using automated equipment.

Literature review

Behavioral safety management

Safety management is a high-profile field in safety science. Since most accidents are caused by human factors (this is the consensus of the safety science community), the recent research and practice of safety management has shown a clear behavioral trend [21]. Behavioral safety management (BBS) is a safety management method developed based on behavioral analysis theory, which combines a variety of disciplines such as behavioral science, safety management science and psychology. Its goal is to gradually correct these behaviors by observing and correcting unsafe behaviors, cultivate employees ' safety awareness and habits, and improve the safety level of the organization [22]. In the field of behavioral safety, human behavior is considered manageable because it is observable and measurable. Therefore, the behaviour pattern of behaviour safety adopts the basic principle of antecedent-behaviour-consequence.Coal mine accidents can be attributed to various factors, including human, environmental, equipment, and management. Human factors contribute to more than 96 % of major coal mine accidents [23], with unsafe human behavior [24] being the primary cause of such accidents [25]. Studies on the factors influencing miner safety behaviour have mainly focused on external organisational factors [26,27] which encompass the organisational, social, work, and environmental aspects that affect miner safety behaviour choices, such as safety culture, safety leadership, role pressure, equipment, and noise. Internal individual factors [28]—including psychological, physiological, and personal safety-related factors such as safety motivation, psychological capital, and mental wandering—have also been investigated. Indeed, individual factors contribute the most to miners' unsafe behaviour, surpassing the influence of group and organisational factors [29]. Studies on unsafe behaviour resulting from miners’ personal factors indicate that psychological factors, such as emotions, directly contribute to unsafe behaviour, whereas cognitive level, positive emotional events, and safety atmosphere can inhibit such behaviour [30].

However, with the introduction and implementation of intelligent equipment, and the establishment of intelligent coal mines, the dynamics between humans and machines have significantly changed. Human roles have shifted from machine controllers to automated collaborators [31].Miners often lack a proper understanding of automated systems. Some miners exhibit excessive trust in and dependence on automation systems, whereas others harbour doubts and reservations about their effectiveness. Miners’ emotions towards automation systems can lead to deviations in actual operations, resulting in unsafe cooperative behaviours [32,33]. Consequently, the issue of trust in mining automation has become a significant challenge in achieving effective human–machine collaboration and ensuring coal mine safety.

Security collaboration behaviour

Safety behaviour usually refers to the safety-related actions or practices taken by individuals in work or life. These actions may include, but are not limited to, compliance with safety regulations and procedures, proper use of personal protective equipment, regular safety training, timely reporting of security risks, etc. Safety behaviour focuses on the behaviour of individuals responsible for their own safety in the personal working environment [34]. For example, workers wear personal protective equipment such as helmets and seat belts, follow safety operating procedures, and conduct safety reports in a timely manner. Security collaboration behaviour focuses more on the interaction and cooperation between members of a team or group to maintain and promote overall security. This includes sharing security information, providing support and help to each other, reporting security risks in a timely manner, and cooperating to solve security problems. Security collaboration behaviour focuses on the interaction between members of the team or group, working together to maintain the overall security goals [35]. For example, in a coal mine working face, workers cooperate with each other to ensure the safety of the site, such as assisting each other in the correct use of tools, and jointly supervising the surrounding environment. Safety behaviour pays more attention to the behaviour of individuals in the personal working environment.Safety behaviour focuses on the responsibility and action of individuals for their own safety, while safety collaboration behaviour focuses on the cooperation and mutual assistance between team or group members to achieve the goal of overall safety. Safety behaviour can promote the individual 's attention and awareness of safety, establish good safety habits and behaviour patterns, so as to cultivate a healthy safety culture. The security collaboration behaviour emphasizes the cooperation and mutual assistance between the team or group members, which helps to form trust and unity between the teams. Therefore, it is found that trust plays an important role in secure collaboration behaviour.

Automated trust

The concept and evolution of automated trust can be traced back to the aviation field in the early 1950 s. At that time, people began to study how to make autonomous aircraft cooperate with human pilots. The key issue is how to ensure that pilots trust the automation system. Automated trust is an important factor affecting human-machine security collaboration. Previous studies have shown that trust evaluation is essential for collaborative behaviour between users. From the perspective of big data and artificial intelligence, collaboration, automated trust and other factors will affect the generation, behaviour and effect of communication [35].Automated trust is the basic feature of collaboration between any entity, which can help entities make decisions before establishing collaboration [36].Accurately understanding the dynamic development process of trust is essential for studying the impact of trust on the effectiveness of human–machine collaborative control. Numerous qualitative conceptual models have been proposed to describe the dynamic development of automated trust [37]. Analysis of automated trust models primarily encompasses qualitative conceptual and quantitative calculation models. In their investigation of the influence of trust on dependency automation, Lee and See Ref. [38] defined trust as the attitude of agents who believe that automation assists in achieving individual goals under conditions of uncertainty and vulnerability. The operator's trust in automation not only affects their monitoring behaviour, but also impacts their performance in subsequent tasks, thereby providing a foundation for related trust research [39]. Despite significant advancements in automated systems, these systems are rendered useless if users choose not to use them because of a lack of trust [40]. A Federal Aviation Administration survey revealed that pilots who overly rely on automation may lose their readiness to handle emergencies [41].

Analysis of Ethiopian Airlines Flight 302 operated by Boeing 737-MAX 8, which crashed in 2019, highlighted that excessive trust in the system led to the utilisation of a manoeuvrability enhancement system, preventing the pilot from intervening in the operation. Clearly, maintaining trust within a safe range, achieved through an appropriate trust calibration mechanism, can effectively prevent safety accidents [42]. Kalayci et al. [42] similarly argue that, in the context of autonomous driving, insufficient trust may result in excessive environmental attention or the underutilization of automated functions, while excessive trust may lead to dangerous driving situations, as drives pay insufficient attention to the surrounding environment. Chiou and Lee [43] suggest that future research on trust should consider the personal characteristics and the impact of automation on individuals as well as explore how individuals affect automation and how interdependent interactions influence trust levels.

Scholars have also singled out the operator's ‘trust in the automation equipment’ as a key variable that may influence the decision to use or not use automation. To ensure proper utilisation, the level of trust the operator places in the equipment must align with the level of automation capability [44]. The appropriateness of trust depends on the relationship between an individual's trust in automation and the automation's ability [37,45], which, in turn, relates to the characteristics of the automation system, operator, and environment [46]. In terms of trust factors, scholars have analyzed how trust is influenced by automation reliability [47], the content and format of automated displays, automated errors, and the consequences of omissions and false alerts [48].

Automation trust plays a crucial role in addressing ambiguous and uncertain challenges in collaborative teamwork between humans and automation systems; it allows for the optimal utilisation of their respective strengths in the intelligent era. However, most research on trust is theoretical and often focuses on specific types of automation systems, such as those in the military, medical, aviation, and automotive industries. The issue of mining trust in the context of safety behaviour in coal mines is rarely explored during the intelligent construction of such mines.Evolutionary game theory, unlike classical game theory, discards the assumption of complete rationality and recognizes that individuals exhibit bounded rationality. Game equilibrium is achieved through trial and error, making it highly practical and widely employed in the study of safety behaviour. For instance, scholar use evolutionary game theory to examine the cooperation mechanisms between independent entities [[49], [50], [51]], safety supervision in construction engineering [[52], [53], [54]], and the evolutionary process of organisational control in miners' behaviour with respect to counter-production [55,56]. Therefore, this model is well-suited for studying the automation trust of miners’ safety behaviour.

In summary, enhancing the collaboration between humans and machines by examining the automation system itself has been the primary subject of scholarship, while the significant impact of trust on safety management from a psychological perspective has been neglected. Most of these studies target the military, medical, and aviation industries, with little attention given to safety management in intelligent coal mine construction. To bridge this gap, we incorporate trust into safety management within the context of intelligent coal mining construction. An evolutionary game model is constructed to analyse cooperation between miners and coal mining enterprises in terms of safety when utilising automation systems. By comparing the evolutionary trajectories and changes in strategic trends of safety management in coal mines and the human–machine security collaboration behaviour of miners, both with and without considering trust, we determine the extent to which trust influences safety outcomes. This way, we examine miners’ personal psychological factors within the intelligent construction of coal mines and provide practical recommendations and management measures for enhancing safety management in coal mine enterprises.

Problem description and basic model

On-site safety management in coal mines involves two main entities: coal mining enterprises and miners. In this paper, we regard coal mining enterprises as specific regulators and address the game-theoretic issues from the perspective of individual regulators. Coal mining enterprises, as regulators, are likely to make decisions favoring profit maximization at the expense of safety. Miners, in the absence of active regulation by coal mining enterprises, may engage in behaviors detrimental to safety for the sake of convenience and effort reduction. However, existing research has found that when miners engage in behaviors detrimental to safety, the probability of accidents significantly increases. This situation also prompts coal mining enterprises to feel a sense of urgency and adopt proactive regulatory strategies towards miners' behaviors. Additionally, with the continuous advancement of coal mine intelligent construction, the psychological state of automation trust can influence miner behaviour. Accordingly, this study establishes models based on different stages of intelligent construction. In the early stages of intelligent construction, due to the novelty of the new entity, trust is not established. However, as the intelligent construction progresses into the middle and later stages, miners may exhibit blind trust issues, aligning with the paradox of fully automated systems. By comparing and analyzing two models, this study aims to address the new challenges posed by coal mine intelligent construction to coal mine safety management.

The stability of equilibrium points is critical to the understanding of and for the prediction of the dynamic process of individual strategy evolution in evolutionary games. This concept of stability helps us grasp the dynamics of strategies as they evolve. A stable equilibrium point represents a state of equilibrium at which, under certain conditions, the strategies in a group tend to remain unsusceptible to external perturbations. By analysing the stability of the equilibrium point, we can gain insights into the relative success and stability of various strategies in the group, and thus discern the mechanism of evolutionary games. The strategy choices of miners and coal mining enterprises will eventually reach a safe and effective Nash equilibrium state, which is characterised by a dynamic balance over time and the continuous accumulation of safety knowledge. Evolutionary stability analysis considering automated trust system

With the rapid development of artificial intelligence, intelligent robots have become integral to human life, serving various purposes in industry as well as professional and private settings. In the context of intelligent coal mining, coal mining enterprises have increasingly adopted automation systems to assist miners in their work [59].

Highly advanced automation systems can be limited by users' lack of trust and failure to utilise the systems [60]. In the fields of automation and robotics, trust is a crucial factor of users' perception of and interaction with robotic systems [37,61,62]. Automation trust—as a psychological state of miners—has a significant impact on miners’ safety cooperation behaviour. Positive trust has positive implications for the safety management of miners and coal mining enterprises.

On one hand, trust is reflected in the belief that intelligent systems can assist miners in completing tasks, thereby saving time and energy during work processes and ensuring personal safety. On the other hand, excessive trust in automation may lead to complacency among miners during working hours, potentially compromising safety benefits. Nevertheless, higher levels of trust correspond to lower costs for miners [63]. We can thus infer that, in terms of automatic trust, the absolute value of positive safety benefits (or negative safety costs) resulting from mining security collaboration behaviour is greater than the absolute value of safety costs.

Discussion

The level of trust employees have in the automation system within coal mining enterprises should be maintained within a safe range, as confirmed by Swanson [44], who suggest that extreme levels of automation trust—whether too high or too low—are not ideal. To ensure a safe and appropriate level of trust, active supervision of such trust is necessary.

Miners may initially lack trust in the automation system owing to unfamiliarity or personal biases. They may, as a result, invest more time and effort in their work, and acquire information about the automation system to handle potential system failures. With the advancement of intelligent construction in coal mines and miners’ increasing understanding and familiarity with automation systems, miners may become overly reliant on the system, assuming that such a system will prevent any safety issues. An overly high trust in the system may then lead to negligence.

From the perspective of coal mining enterprises, when miners excessively trust the automation system, they are more likely to choose active supervision to prevent safety accidents. Conversely, when miners lack trust in the automation system, coal mining enterprises may assume that miners will engage in security collaboration behaviour, which, in turn, reduces the probability of choosing active supervision.

We believe that our results address a critical gap in the literature on coal mine safety psychology and automation trust. Three points are fundamental here:

First, miners should understand automation equipment and systems in order to ensure proper use; this includes keeping abreast of latest developments and advancements, grasping the fundamental knowledge and characteristics of automation systems, effective use, and ability to enhance efficiency in coal mining operations.

Second, coal mining enterprises seeking to implement automation systems should prioritise cognitive training for miners and take proactive measures to address the neglect of security collaboration by miners. Miners should be assisted in establishing accurate perceptions of automation systems. Additionally, penalties for insecurity collaboration behaviour should be appropriately increased to regulate miners’ operational conduct, ensuring their personal safety and the overall safety interests of the enterprises.

Third, the government should continue to augment investments in the research and development of automation equipment. By constructing efficient, intelligent, and specialised intelligent coal mine equipment, and leveraging advanced technologies, a comprehensive framework of protective measures can be established. This strategy will foster the collective advancement of intelligent coal mine construction.

Conclusions

Based on the current energy structure in China, coal will continue to dominate the energy sector for a considerable period. As the country pursues high-quality development and implements supply side structural reforms, intelligent and automated coal mining will reach mainstream status in the industry. In this context, safety operations in coal mining systems are increasingly important for efficiency and productivity targets. Based on this, this study starts from the level of automation trust of miners in the process of intelligent construction of coal mines, and establishes an evolutionary game model between miners and coal mining enterprises. It is proposed that automation trust is an important new factor in the process of intelligent construction of coal mines. The change of automation trust will affect the safety strategy choice of miners, coal mining enterprises and coal mine safety production system. This finding can encourage coal mining enterprises to actively pay attention to the safety psychological state of miners and thus contribute to coal mine safety management.There are three main conclusions in this paper.(1)With the continuous advancement of the intelligent construction process of coal mines, the level of automation trust of miners is too high at this stage, resulting in the safety factor of the entire coal mine safety production system being lower than the initial stage of the intelligent construction process of coal mines.(2)In order to explore the reasons behind this, the change trend of safety production system of coal mine enterprises is explored by analyzing the change of automation trust degree. Through evolutionary game simulation analysis, it is found that when the automation trust level of miners is in the range of (0.7–0.9), the probability of miners choosing safety cooperation behaviour and the probability of coal mine enterprises choosing active supervision are relatively stable.(3)When the degree of automation trust is 0.7, the probability of miners choosing safe cooperative behaviour and the probability of coal mining enterprises choosing active supervision are higher than that of the degree of automation trust is 0.9. This is mainly because when the degree of automation trust is low (e.g., 0.7), miners may be more inclined to rely on their own judgments and actions rather than relying entirely on automated systems. Therefore, they are more likely to choose to take safe collaborative behaviour to ensure the safety of themselves and their colleagues. Conversely, when automation trust is high (e.g. 0.9), miners may be more likely to shift safety responsibility to automation systems, reducing their likelihood of proactive safety collaboration. When the level of trust in automation is low, coal mining enterprises may be aware of the limitations of the automation system, and therefore more inclined to implement active regulatory measures to make up for loopholes or errors that may exist in the automation system. Therefore, it is necessary to carry out the study and education of automation system for coal mine enterprises, closely link the psychological state of miners with the safety system, and make safety management decisions according to the changes of psychological state, so as to effectively reduce coal mine accidents and improve the level of coal mine safety production.

(3)When the degree of automation trust is 0.7, the probability of miners choosing safe cooperative behaviour and the probability of coal mining enterprises choosing active supervision are higher than that of the degree of automation trust is 0.9. This is mainly because when the degree of automation trust is low (e.g., 0.7), miners may be more inclined to rely on their own judgments and actions rather than relying entirely on automated systems. Therefore, they are more likely to choose to take safe collaborative behaviour to ensure the safety of themselves and their colleagues. Conversely, when automation trust is high (e.g. 0.9), miners may be more likely to shift safety responsibility to automation systems, reducing their likelihood of proactive safety collaboration. When the level of trust in automation is low, coal mining enterprises may be aware of the limitations of the automation system, and therefore more inclined to implement active regulatory measures to make up for loopholes or errors that may exist in the automation system. Therefore, it is necessary to carry out the study and education of automation system for coal mine enterprises, closely link the psychological state of miners with the safety system, and make safety management decisions according to the changes of psychological state, so as to effectively reduce coal mine accidents and improve the level of coal mine safety production.

However, there are some limitations in this paper. First of all, the coal mine safety production system is a multi-agent complex system, which is restricted by many factors and rules. This study needs to be supplemented in the construction of evolutionary game model, and there may be missing factors, such as dynamic punishment mechanism and safety investment of coal mining enterprises. Secondly, this paper obtains basic data for the initial value of automated trust through on-site questionnaire survey. Although it avoids the common method bias problem of self-report to a certain extent, it mainly relies on other people 's data for analysis. There will be memory bias and personal bias. In order to reduce these adverse effects, in the future, the psychological state of miners can be tested by combining big data analysis and laboratory experiments, and multivariate data can be obtained by real-time transmission to reduce the deviation of common methods. Finally, the main game mainly considers miners and coal mining enterprises, but in the actual production process, it is also supported by local government policies, such as fiscal and taxation subsidies. Future research can be further explored from the above perspectives.
