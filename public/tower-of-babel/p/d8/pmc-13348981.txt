The Molecular Basis of Ocular Aging: Mechanisms, Pathologies, and Emerging Therapeutics
Authors: Yuan Zong, Qiwei Fan, Shuang Qiu, Huang Zhang, Zhimin Cen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Age-related eye diseases (AREDs)—including age-related macular degeneration (AMD), glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy—are leading causes of permanent blindness. Current treatments manage clinical manifestations but do not halt the molecular processes that drive disease progression. This limitation has shifted attention toward “geroscience,” a strategy that targets the fundamental biology of aging rather than treating each disease in isolation. Four key hallmarks of aging—mitochondrial dysfunction, loss of proteostasis, cellular senescence, and epigenetic drift—are widely implicated in AREDs. We review evidence that these hallmarks do not act independently; instead, they form an interactive, self-reinforcing network. The way this network engages differs from tissue to tissue. In the high-energy environment of the retinal pigment epithelium, mitochondrial dysfunction dominates and drives AMD. In the mechanically stressed trabecular meshwork (TM), senescence and epigenetic drift take precedence, leading to glaucoma. In the neurovascular unit, chronic hyperglycemia routes the same network into a metabolic–epigenetic amplification loop that sustains diabetic retinopathy. The same aging mechanisms, routed through distinct tissue contexts, thus produce divergent clinical phenotypes. We also evaluate emerging therapies, including senolytics, mitochondria-targeted agents, and partial epigenetic reprogramming, and identify key intervention nodes such as NLRP3, p62, and NAD⁺ metabolism. Dismantling these pathological feedback loops offers a path beyond symptom management toward combination strategies that restore tissue resilience.

The global population is aging, and with this shift, age-related eye diseases (AREDs)—specifically age-related macular degeneration (AMD), glaucoma, and diabetic retinopathy (DR)—have become leading causes of irreversible blindness worldwide.1,2 Epidemiological projections indicate that disease burden will rise sharply through 2050 (Fig. 1). Although these conditions present with distinct clinical features, they are unified by a single dominant risk factor: chronological age.3 The societal and economic costs are large and still growing.

AREDs are not localized events but rather manifestations of systemic aging. This recognition has driven the field toward geroscience—an approach focused on the fundamental biology of aging, including chronic sterile inflammation (inflamm-aging), mitochondrial dysfunction, and macromolecular damage—rather than treating each disease in isolation.7,8 The eye lends itself naturally to studying these aging processes. Its tissues—the retinal pigment epithelium (RPE), trabecular meshwork (TM), and retinal ganglion cells (RGCs)—combine high metabolic demand with a largely post-mitotic architecture. This makes them intrinsically vulnerable to oxidative stress and proteostatic collapse. Within these tissues, four hallmarks of systemic aging—mitochondrial dysfunction, proteostatic collapse, cellular senescence, and epigenetic/metabolic drift—converge and accelerate age-related functional decline. Yet, this convergence raises a paradox: If the same aging mechanisms are at work everywhere, why do they produce such strikingly different diseases in different parts of the eye?

We argue that these four hallmarks do not act independently. Instead, they form an interactive molecular network, and the way this network is engaged differs from tissue to tissue depending on local metabolic and mechanical demands. In the energy-stressed RPE, mitochondrial DNA leakage triggers cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)–stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling and NOD-like receptor pyrin domain-containing protein 3 (NLRP3) inflammasome activation, setting off a feed-forward cascade that drives AMD pathology. In the mechanically stressed TM, senescence and senescence-associated secretory phenotype (SASP)-mediated extracellular matrix (ECM) remodeling prevail, driving glaucomatous outflow resistance. In the diabetic retina, nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide–positive (NAD⁺) depletion and histone lactylation establish a metabolic memory that sustains inflammation even after glycemic control is restored.

These distinct engagement patterns explain how a shared set of aging mechanisms produces AMD, glaucoma, and DR. The network is the same; the route it takes depends on the tissue. Viewed this way, the most effective intervention points are not single-pathway targets but nodes that coordinate multiple modules—NLRP3, p62/sequestosome 1 (SQSTM1), NAD⁺ metabolism, and DNA methyltransferase 1 (DNMT1)-mediated epigenetic regulation. Each sits at the intersection of two or more modules. This provides a rationale for combination therapies that address several age-related eye diseases simultaneously.

Core Cellular and Molecular Mechanisms of Ocular Aging

The pace of ocular aging hinges on how well ocular tissues maintain bioenergetic fidelity and proteostatic resilience. Ocular senescence is not simply a matter of passive wear and tear. It is an active, self-amplifying process: Mitochondrial reprogramming, proteostatic collapse, and epigenetic drift feed into one another and together drive tissue dysfunction.

Module I and II Crosstalk: The cGAS–STING–Autophagy Nexus in RPE

In the RPE, mitochondrial function depends on a tight balance between energy production and quality control. When this balance breaks, metabolic failure tips over into immune signaling. The trigger is mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) leaking into the cytoplasm, but this is not random spillover. It is a gated process, controlled by specific channels. The key event is voltage-dependent anion channel 1 (VDAC1) oligomerization. VDAC1, a voltage-dependent anion channel on the outer mitochondrial membrane, forms a hydrophilic pore wide enough for nucleic acid export.10 Under normal conditions, the E3 ubiquitin ligase Parkin keeps this pore shut by ubiquitinating VDAC1 at lysine 53 (K53), thereby restraining inflammation.11 With age, however, Parkin expression or activity declines. The checkpoint is lost. VDAC1 oligomerizes, mtDNA escapes into the cytosol, and the cGAS–STING pathway activates.12

Cytosolic double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) binds and activates cGAS, which produces the second messenger 2′3′-cyclic guanosine monophosphate–adenosine monophosphate (cGAMP). This drives STING to translocate from the endoplasmic reticulum (ER) to the ER–Golgi intermediate compartment (ERGIC) and Golgi. The translocation is not simply a step toward interferon production. It also initiates a form of non-canonical autophagy that helps clear the inflammatory stimulus. Activated STING directly recruits WD repeat domain, phosphoinositide interacting protein 2 (WIPI2) to STING-positive vesicles, bypassing the usual Unc-51-like autophagy activating kinase 1 (ULK1) initiation complex and driving microtubule-associated protein 1A/1B-light chain 3 (LC3) lipidation and autophagosome formation.13 At the same time, the selective autophagy receptor p62/SQSTM1 (hereafter p62) recognizes ubiquitinated STING and delivers it to the lysosome for degradation. This process depends on TANK-binding kinase 1 (TBK1). It acts as a built-in brake on innate immune signaling—a negative feedback loop that keeps the inflammatory response in check.14

In the senescent RPE, this protective coupling breaks down. As autophagic flux declines with age, p62 accumulates and undergoes liquid–liquid phase separation (LLPS), forming insoluble p62 bodies.15 Rather than suppressing inflammation, these condensates sequester PYCARD, or apoptosis-associated speck-like protein containing a CARD (ASC), which nucleates the NLRP3 inflammasome and promotes caspase-1 activation.15 This is the “p62 paradox”: A protein that normally degrades inflammatory cargo switches roles and becomes a pro-inflammatory scaffold. It explains a puzzling feature of AMD tissue: Autophagy markers such as LC3-II are often elevated, yet inflammation persists. What looks like increased autophagy is often blocked flux—p62 trapped in a pro-inflammatory state rather than cleared. The failure to remove damaged mitochondria adds further pressure, because cytosolic mtDNA continues to leak, and the cycle keeps running.

In the RPE, sirtuin 1 (SIRT1)—a key upstream activator of mitochondrial quality control—declines with age and in AMD. Studies in AMD donor RPE have linked this decline to NAD⁺ depletion.16 The role of SIRT1 in regulating mitochondrial quality control through peroxisome proliferator-activated receptor gamma coactivator 1-alpha (PGC-1α) is well established in the RPE.17 Downstream, defective Rab7-dependent mitophagic clearance has been directly observed in a mouse model of dry AMD, such that autophagosomes form but fail to fuse with lysosomes.18 This failure to complete mitophagy sustains cytosolic mtDNA release, perpetuating the inflammatory cycle.

STING signaling does not produce a single outcome. It bifurcates. The path taken determines whether the RPE cell enters senescence or dies by pyroptosis. Downstream of STING, the nuclear factor kappa B (NF-κB) branch primes the NLRP3 inflammasome and triggers gasdermin D (GSDMD)-mediated pore formation, pushing the cell toward pyroptosis.19 The interferon regulatory factor 3 (IRF3) branch takes a different route. Phosphorylated IRF3 moves to the nucleus and, independently of its role in driving type I interferon transcription, binds the retinoblastoma protein. This interaction blocks E2F-dependent cell-cycle progression and locks the cell in a non-proliferative, senescence-like state.20 The arrested RPE cells do not sit idle. They secrete a sustained battery of SASP factors (IL-6, IL-8) that degrade Bruch's membrane and promote choroidal neovascularization. The clinical picture—dry (atrophic) versus wet (neovascular) AMD—may therefore reflect, at least in part, which branch of STING signaling predominates in different regions of the aging RPE.

Whether STING activation leads to cell death or survival depends heavily on the autophagic capacity of the cell. As shown in Figure 3, the autophagy receptor p62 is the critical switch. When autophagy is functional, p62 binds ubiquitinated STING and cytosolic mtDNA and escorts them to the lysosome, shutting down the inflammatory signal. In the senescent RPE, however, autophagic flux stalls; p62 can still recognize its targets but cannot clear them. It piles up into pathological aggregates. These aggregates become platforms for NLRP3 inflammasome assembly. Instead of resolving the threat, the signal now drives pyroptosis and SASP secretion (Fig. 3, Phase III). This functional flip—from cleaner to inflammatory scaffold—suggests that restoring autophagic flux may pull RPE cells back from inflammatory degeneration.

Module II: Proteostasis Imbalance and the Unfolded Protein Response Dichotomy

Protein folding, trafficking, and degradation together keep the proteome functional, and when this machinery fails, RPE cells (and neurons more broadly) are among the first to suffer.

Functional Decline of the Autophagy–Lysosomal Pathway and Lipofuscin Accumulation

RPE cells are the main phagocytes of the retina. Each day, they ingest and degrade a substantial load of shed photoreceptor outer segments, a task that depends on an intact autophagy–lysosomal pathway. With age, lysosomal acidification becomes less efficient. Hydrolase activity drops, and lipid–protein complexes are no longer fully broken down.21,22 The residual material is sequestered as lipofuscin, a cytotoxic aggregate that takes up cytoplasmic space and, more importantly, weakens the lysosomal membrane.22,23 One key reason for this failure is transcription factor EB (TFEB) mislocalization. TFEB, the transcription factor that coordinates lysosomal biogenesis, is either transcriptionally downregulated or trapped in the cytoplasm in the aging RPE, unable to reach its target genes.24 Defects in microautophagy add to the problem. Serine/threonine kinase 38 (STK38) and its interaction with the GABA type A receptor-associated proteins (GABARAP) subfamily are needed for lysosomal membrane repair; when this machinery falters, phototoxic aggregates pile up faster.25

The Unfolded Protein Response Dichotomy.

The ER is where most secreted and membrane proteins are folded and processed. In the aging eye, chronic oxidative stress frequently overwhelms the folding capacity of the ER. Misfolded proteins accumulate, and the unfolded protein response (UPR) becomes persistently activated. Acute UPR signaling through inositol-requiring enzyme 1 (IRE1), protein kinase R-like ER kinase (PERK), and activating transcription factor 6 (ATF6) generally helps restore proteostasis.26–29 But, when the stress does not resolve, as is typical in the aging retina, the response takes a darker turn. The PERK–eukaryotic translation initiation factor 2 subunit alpha (eIF2α)–C/EBP homologous protein (CHOP) axis becomes chronically active and pushes the cell from adaptation toward programmed death.26,28 This switch is a key driver of RPE and RGC loss and has been directly implicated in the neurodegenerative component of DR.28,30

Module III: Cellular Senescence and SASP Propagation

Cellular senescence is different from both quiescence and terminal differentiation. It is a stress-triggered state in which cells stop dividing permanently but do not die. Instead, they stay metabolically active and begin to reshape their surroundings—often to the detriment of the tissue. The arrest is enforced by the DNA damage response, which upregulates cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitors, principally p53/p21CIP1 and p16INK4a/Rb.31,32 In many tissues, senescence is triggered by telomere shortening, but in the eye oxidative stress and mitochondrial dysfunction accelerate the process considerably.33,34

The paradox of senescence is that cells that have permanently exited the cell cycle do not shut down metabolically. Quite the opposite: They ramp up production and secretion of a broad array of pro-inflammatory factors, collectively termed SASP.35,36 The SASP cocktail includes IL-6, IL-8, multiple chemokines, and matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs). Through these signals, senescent cells spread their phenotype to neighboring healthy cells—a paracrine “bystander effect” that sustains a chronic, sterile inflammatory microenvironment. Over time, this degrades tissue architecture throughout the eye.37,38 In the posterior segment, SASP-driven remodeling of the ECM weakens the outer blood–retinal barrier and promotes drusen formation, a hallmark of AMD.39–42 In the anterior segment, senescence of TM cells stiffens the ECM and raises outflow resistance, and senescent glial cells contribute directly to RGC degeneration in glaucoma.43,44

Module IV: Metabolic Reprogramming and Epigenetic Drift

On top of these changes, ocular cells shift how they produce energy, and the consequences extend beyond metabolism. Under chronic oxidative stress, RPE cells move away from oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) and rely increasingly on aerobic glycolysis, a pattern known as the Warburg effect.45,46 This may start as an adaptive maneuver to keep adenosine triphosphate (ATP) flowing when the electron transport chain falters, but it comes at a cost. Glycolysis is less efficient per molecule of glucose, so the RPE consumes more glucose to meet the same energy demand. The result is lactate buildup and a relative shortage of glucose reaching the neural retina, which further stresses photoreceptors and accelerates degeneration.46,47

Closely linked to this metabolic crisis is a progressive loss of NAD⁺, a coenzyme that carries electrons in redox reactions and also provides the essential substrate for sirtuins (SIRT1–7) and poly(ADP-ribose) polymerases (PARPs).48,49 As NAD⁺ levels fall with age, SIRT1 activity declines in parallel. Because SIRT1 deacetylates and thereby regulates a wide set of targets, it promotes mitochondrial biogenesis, sustains nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)-driven antioxidant defenses, and keeps NF-κB–driven inflammation in check; its loss simultaneously weakens energy production, antioxidant capacity, and inflammatory restraint.50,51

These metabolic disruptions feed directly into “epigenetic drift,” the gradual erosion of the normal epigenetic landscape. Many chromatin-modifying enzymes depend on metabolites whose levels change with age: acetyl-CoA for histone acetylation, NAD⁺ for sirtuin-mediated deacetylation, and α-ketoglutarate for DNA and histone demethylation. As these cofactors become limiting, DNA methylation patterns become aberrant and histone modifications shift, changes that can be captured quantitatively by “aging clocks” such as the Horvath clock.52–54 Accelerated epigenetic aging, as measured by such clocks, has been associated with faster glaucoma progression.55 In RGCs, loss of epigenetic fidelity has been identified as a primary driver of age-related functional decline, a process potentially reversible through partial reprogramming strategies.56

Critical Integration: Reconciling Paradoxical Findings in Ocular Aging Networks

The network model outlined above is useful, but it does not resolve every discrepancy in the literature. Several findings seem, at first glance, to contradict one another. Making sense of them is important—not just for conceptual clarity but also because the way we interpret these paradoxes directly shapes how we design the next round of therapies.

Paradox 1: Autophagy Upregulation Versus Autophagic Flux Blockade

The role of macroautophagy in AREDs is, on the surface, contradictory. Some studies report that chronic oxidative stress suppresses autophagy through mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR) activation in the aging RPE.57 Others have found elevated LC3B in AMD donor tissue—a marker typically read as evidence of increased autophagy.58 The confusion clears when one separates autophagy initiation from autophagic flux. Early mitochondrial stress can trigger autophagosome formation, and elevated autophagic markers such as LC3-II have been documented in aged RPE.58 However, sustained reactive oxygen species (ROS) and the progressive accumulation of lipofuscin weaken the lysosomal membrane, as discussed earlier, so that degradative capacity fails to keep pace with autophagosome production. What appears as increased autophagy may therefore reflect blocked flux rather than enhanced clearance. This discrepancy is consistent with reports that autophagic markers accumulate in degenerating ocular tissues despite impaired degradative capacity.59 If this interpretation is correct, then therapeutic efforts should prioritize restoring lysosomal function. One strategy to achieve this is through activation of TFEB, a master regulator of lysosomal biogenesis and autophagy.24

Paradox 2: The p62/SQSTM1 Duality

p62/SQSTM1 presents the same kind of dual identity. Under normal conditions, it is a cargo receptor: TBK1 phosphorylates p62, which strengthens its grip on ubiquitinated proteins and delivers them to the lysosome for degradation.14 But when autophagic flux stalls, unphosphorylated p62 builds up and undergoes LLPS, forming condensates that trap the ASC adaptor protein. Instead of clearing debris, p62 now nucleates the NLRP3 inflammasome.15 Whether p62 protects or harms, therefore, hinges largely on its phosphorylation status. This shift from protective clearance to inflammatory scaffolding helps explain why p62 accumulation, rather than being protective, actively fuels sterile inflammation in AREDs.

Synthesis

The two paradoxes above illustrate a general point: The biological meaning of a molecular marker depends on the state of the network it sits in. LC3-II elevation can reflect either increased autophagic flux or blocked clearance, and p62 can serve either as a cargo receptor or as an inflammasome scaffold, depending on whether lysosomal function is intact. The same principle applies at the tissue level. The four aging hallmarks operate in every ocular tissue, but their functional consequences differ with local context—metabolic demand, mechanical load, and oxidative burden. The next three sections examine how this process produces AMD, glaucoma, and DR.

Core Mechanisms in Specific Ocular Pathologies

The fundamental hallmarks of aging described in the preceding sections—mitochondrial dysfunction, proteostasis loss, senescence, and epigenetic drift—do not operate in isolation. Instead, their pathogenic impact is modulated by the specific structural constraints and metabolic demands of individual ocular tissues. Consequently, shared molecular drivers manifest as divergent pathologies depending on the local cellular environment. This section examines how these systemic drivers precipitate distinct clinical phenotypes, primarily targeting the neurovascular and outflow tissues of the eye.

Age-Related Macular Degeneration

As the archetypal manifestation of ocular aging, AMD represents a complex convergence of genetic predisposition, environmental stress, and the cumulative failure of RPE support functions. Far from a passive degenerative process, recent investigations delineate AMD as an active cascade where SASP, autophagic blockade, and aberrant inflammatory signaling synergize to dismantle the outer blood–retinal barrier.60

RPE Senescence and Bioenergetic Collapse

AMD begins with a self-amplifying loop in the RPE: Mitochondrial failure drives senescence, and senescence further compromises mitochondrial function. Senescent RPE cells, arrested by p16/p21, secrete SASP factors that promote ECM remodeling. However, clearing senescent cells alone may not suffice to arrest pathology when the upstream network is fully engaged—a pattern consistent with the limited efficacy observed when senolytic approaches were applied in advanced diabetic macular edema (DME). This suggests that in AMD, as in DME, senescent cell removal is unlikely to be curative if the mitochondrial–proteostatic feedback loops that generate senescence continue to operate unchecked. Proteomic analyses reveal stage-dependent changes in mitochondrial protein expression in AMD: Proteins associated with mitochondrial function—including ATP synthase subunits—are upregulated in early disease, consistent with a compensatory response, but they decline sharply in late-stage AMD,61 pointing to an initial adaptive response that later gives way to bioenergetic collapse. Epigenetic changes add to the problem. Diminished histone deacetylase 3 (HDAC3) activity triggers ER stress,62 and dual specificity phosphatase 4 (DUSP4) is upregulated as a counterbalance, but this endogenous brake on NF-κB–driven inflammation ultimately fails, and chronic inflammatory signaling persists.63 p62/SQSTM1 accumulation further amplifies the breakdown, switching from an autophagic cargo receptor into a scaffold for pro-inflammatory complexes, as discussed earlier. In this way, mitochondrial failure progressively dismantles quality control, exhausting the repair capacity of the cell.

Autophagic Stagnation and Drusen Biogenesis

As discussed earlier, autophagic flux declines with age in the RPE: Autophagosomes form but fail to fuse with lysosomes, and lipofuscin accumulates. In AMD, this impairment directly contributes to drusen formation. Impaired clearance leads to lipid accumulation and basal laminar deposit formation in the sub-RPE space.64,65 Transmembrane protein 97 (TMEM97) has emerged as a novel genetic risk factor linking lipid uptake, oxidative stress, and drusen nucleation.66 This creates a vicious cycle where oxidative stress compromises lysosomal integrity through iron-catalyzed lipid peroxidation and lysosomal membrane permeabilization,67 and PGC-1α deficiency further disrupts autophagic flux, leading to an accumulation of p62 and LC3B.68 The paradoxically elevated autophagy markers in AMD tissue are consistent with blocked flux rather than enhanced clearance, as discussed earlier. This discrepancy—induction without completion—fits the network model: SASP factors from Module III feed back onto Module II, sustaining autophagosome formation while blocking degradative flux. Thus, drusen represents the geological record of network failure rather than an isolated protein deposit.

Oxidative Stress and Regulated Cell Death

In the RPE, oxidative stress triggers several forms of regulated cell death, not all of which are mutually exclusive. NRF2 depletion sensitizes cells to ferroptosis via GPX4 inactivation.69,70 Cytoplasmic mtDNA accumulation activates the absent in melanoma 2 (AIM2) inflammasome,71 and, through a parallel pathway, the NLRP3 inflammasome, leading to pyroptosis.72 Under amyloid-β stimulation, RPE cells can undergo PANoptosis—a coordinated execution of apoptosis, necroptosis, and pyroptosis—driven by Z-DNA binding protein 1 (ZBP1) activation at mitochondria–ER contact sites.73,74 Disrupting AIM2-dependent signaling—for example, with statins that inhibit prenylation—may help decouple drusen-associated danger signals from this cell death cascade.75

The Inflammatory Amplification Loop

Chronic sterile inflammation, driven in part by complement dysregulation, bridges initial cellular stress and tissue-wide degeneration. In AMD, the complement system shifts from protective sentinel to driver of pathology. Systemic levels of C3a and C5a rise, and the membrane attack complex (MAC) deposits locally in drusen.76–78 Lipid metabolism is tightly coupled to this activation,, as aberrant lipid profiles not only promote drusen formation but also directly potentiate complement cascade activity.79,80 Therapeutic inhibition of C3 (pegcetacoplan) or C5 (avacincaptad pegol) slows geographic atrophy lesion expansion, but these interventions carry a paradoxical risk of accelerating conversion to neovascular AMD.81 The inflammatory milieu is further sustained by macrophages polarizing toward the pro-inflammatory M1 phenotype, a process mediated by Spi-1 proto-oncogene (SPI1), and by mast cells that secrete tryptase and chymase and degrade Bruch's membrane.82,83

Neovascularization and Fibrosis (Wet AMD)

Progression to neovascular (wet) AMD is driven by hypoxia and inflammation, which together trigger the angiogenic switch. Hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF-1α) and oxidative stress synergistically upregulate vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), overcoming the homeostatic barriers that normally keep angiogenesis in check.84 However, resistance to anti-VEGF therapy remains a significant clinical problem, often attributed to compensatory signaling through the vascular endothelial growth factor receptor 2 (VEGFR2)–signal transducer and activator of transcription 3 (STAT3) axis and alternative angiogenic mediators such as angiopoietin-2 and galectin-1.85,86 The terminal consequence of exudative AMD is subretinal fibrosis, driven by the epithelial–mesenchymal transition of RPE cells. This process depends on Wnt5a/β-catenin signaling and metabolic reprogramming, including deficiency of the mitochondrial carrier Slc25a11.87,88 Metabolic lactylation also contributes. AlkB homolog 3, alpha-ketoglutarate-dependent dioxygenase (ALKBH3)-mediated glycolysis increases lactate production, which promotes histone lactylation at H3K18 (H3K18la). This creates a positive feedback loop that reinforces both fibrosis and angiogenesis.89 Targeting the metabolic and epigenetic regulators of epithelial–mesenchymal transition may help prevent the irreversible scarring that compromises visual recovery.

Glaucoma: The Anterior–Posterior Senescence Axis

Although classically defined by intraocular pressure (IOP) elevation and optic neuropathy, primary open-angle glaucoma (POAG) is increasingly reconceptualized as a multifaceted senescence disorder. Unlike AMD, where mitochondrial failure primarily initiates the pathogenic cascade, glaucoma represents a bi-compartmental network failure. This paradigm shift is underpinned by genome-wide association studies identifying variants at the 9p21.3 locus—proximal to the cyclin-dependent kinase inhibitor 2A (CDKN2A) gene encoding the senescence marker p16INK4a—as potent risk factors for POAG, establishing a direct genetic link between cell cycle arrest and glaucoma susceptibility.90

TM Senescence and the Fibrotic Switch

The homeostatic regulation of IOP relies on the functional integrity of the TM, a tissue that exhibits a profound susceptibility to oxidative aging. Under the cumulative burden of chronic oxidative stress and mechanical strain, the TM network entry point is strongly anchored in Module III (cellular senescence). TM cells undergo a phenotypic conversion to a senescent state, characterized by permanent cell cycle arrest, enlarged morphology, and the upregulation of p16INK4a.43 This transition is driven significantly by the depletion of antioxidant defenses, notably Prdx6, which precipitates ROS accumulation, TGF-β signaling activation, and subsequent DNA damage.91

The pathogenicity of senescent TM cells is mediated primarily through the SASP. This hyper-secretory state orchestrates a maladaptive remodeling of the ECM via the imbalance of MMPs and their tissue inhibitors (TIMPs), alongside the deposition of fibrotic mediators.92,93 Crucially, this Module III hyper-activation directly drives Module IV (epigenetic drift) through mechanical feedback. The resultant stiffening of the ECM activates the YAP/TAZ mechanotransduction pathway, creating a feed-forward loop that reinforces the fibrotic phenotype and compromises aqueous humor outflow.94,95 Additionally, cytoskeletal dysregulation, specifically the elevation of septin-9, increases TM cellular stiffness, further elevating outflow resistance.96

The causal role of this Module III burden in IOP dysregulation is substantiated by therapeutic intervention studies. Given that senescent TM cells accumulate with age and their SASP drives ECM remodeling and outflow resistance, pharmacological clearance of these cells may offer a strategy for restoring aqueous humor drainage and lowering IOP, although direct experimental validation remains to be established. Furthermore, Mendelian randomization analysis has revealed a causal relationship between epigenetic age acceleration and the risk of POAG, suggesting that glaucoma reflects a locally accelerated process of epigenetic biological aging (Module IV) rather than an independent pathological event.97

RGC Bioenergetics and the NAD⁺–Axonal Transport Axis

In the posterior segment, RGCs exhibit a unique vulnerability to aging due to their unmyelinated intraocular axons and extreme metabolic demand. Here, the neurodegeneration is fundamentally triggered by Module I (mitochondrial dysfunction). Oxidative stress, exacerbated by high IOP and vascular dysregulation, damages mtDNA and proteins, evidenced by elevated levels of the oxidation marker 8-OHdG in glaucomatous retinas.98,99

This oxidative assault profoundly disrupts mitochondrial dynamics. Notably, the precise role of autophagy in this context remains a subject of ongoing debate. Although several studies have reported an upregulation of autophagic markers (e.g., LC3-II) in glaucomatous RGCs—often interpreted as a compensatory and protective response—recent studies suggest that this accumulation may instead reflect impaired autophagic flux, where autophagosomes form but fail to be cleared.100 Under this interpretation, autophagosomes may be robustly formed but experience impaired or delayed fusion with lysosomes. This raises the possibility that prolonged oxidative stress could gradually convert a pro-survival clearance mechanism into a pathological bottleneck (Module I → Module II failure). Consistent with the idea that completing autophagic flux is more critical than merely initiating it, the ablation of mitochondrial uncoupling protein 2 (UCP2) has been found to paradoxically reduce RGC death by enhancing mitophagic flux, suggesting that restoring Module II quality control efficiency is a viable neuroprotective strategy.101

Central to this metabolic collapse is the systemic depletion of NAD⁺, a critical node linking Module I bioenergetics with Module IV epigenetic regulation. The age-related decline in NAD⁺ compromises the activity of SIRT1, a deacetylase critical for mitochondrial biogenesis and genomic stability.102 In glaucoma models, this NAD⁺ crisis directly impacts axonal transport. The functioning of kinesin and dynein motor proteins is ATP dependent; thus, mitochondrial failure halts the anterograde transport of mitochondria and the retrograde transport of neurotrophic factors (e.g., brain-derived neurotrophic factor [BDNF]).103 This transport blockade deprives the RGC soma of survival signals, precipitating apoptosis. Restoring NAD⁺ levels via nicotinamide supplementation has demonstrated efficacy in protecting RGC soma and maintaining axonal integrity, although it may not fully reverse cytoskeletal breakdown, highlighting the need for early intervention.104

Neuroinflammation and Glial Crosstalk

Superimposed on these cell-intrinsic mechanisms is a deleterious neuroinflammatory response driven by glial senescence (secondary Module III activation). Activated microglia and astrocytes release pro-inflammatory cytokines (TNF-α, IL-1β) and complement components (C1q), creating a toxic microenvironment that accelerates RGC loss.105 Notably, the “A1” neurotoxic astrocyte phenotype, induced by microglial secretion of IL-1α, TNF, and C1q, is prominent in aging and glaucoma.106 Furthermore, the mechanosensitivity of Müller glia to ECM stiffness alters their secretome, effectively translating anterior physical hardening (Module IV) into posterior biochemical neurodegeneration (Module III).107 Consequently, these findings portray glaucoma as a synchronized failure of the anterior outflow system and posterior neural support networks, unified by the intercompartmental propagation of the ocular aging network. Glaucoma thus involves two compartments. In the anterior segment, senescence and ECM remodeling (III, IV) dominate; in the posterior segment, mitochondrial compromise and NAD⁺ decline (I, IV) drive RGC loss.

DR: Accelerated Neurovascular Aging and Metabolic Memory

DR represents a systemic destabilization of the neurovascular unit (NVU). Rather than it being a simple consequence of passive glucotoxicity, recent evidence delineates a sequential, cascade-amplified network failure initiating in retinal endothelial cells (RECs). The pathogenic cascade originates with mitochondrial overload (Module I). Chronic hyperglycemia drives mitochondrial superoxide overproduction via electron transport chain overload, advanced glycation end-product (AGE) formation, and protein kinase C (PKC)-driven nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide phosphate (NADPH) oxidase activation, triggering a broad ROS burst and bioenergetic crisis within RECs.108,109 To survive this energy deficit, RECs undergo a “Warburg-like” metabolic reprogramming towards aerobic glycolysis, culminating in massive lactate accumulation.110 Far from being a mere waste product, this lactate surge acts as an epigenetic trigger, driving robust histone lactylation (Module IV). This specific modification locks the chromatin in a pro-inflammatory state, establishing a durable “metabolic memory” that perpetuates vascular damage even after strict glycemic control is achieved.111

Crucially, this metabolic–epigenetic drift directly compromises cellular protein quality control (Module II). Lactylation is not confined to histones. Researchers have identified extensive non-histone lactylation, establishing that lactate modifies diverse functional proteins throughout cells.112 Recent work in diabetic models has uncovered a concrete mechanism linking this modification to autophagic failure: lactylation of leucyl-tRNA synthetase 1 (LARS1) at a key lysine residue aberrantly activates mTORC1, stalling autophagic flux and producing a distinct form of Module II dysfunction.113 Whether this or analogous events occur in RECs under hyperglycemic stress has not yet been directly demonstrated, but the mechanistic precedent is strong. Burdened with uncleared toxic metabolites, misfolded proteins, and fragmented mitochondria, the dysfunctional RECs lose their ability to maintain homeostatic crosstalk with adjacent mural cells. This multi-modular breakdown ultimately cuts off reciprocal survival signaling, precipitating pericyte apoptosis, tight junction compromise, and the terminal disintegration of the blood-retinal barrier.114

The Ang-2/Tie2 Switch and Pericyte Detachment

Although anti-VEGF therapies remain the clinical cornerstone for managing DR and DME, a significant proportion of patients exhibit refractory edema or require intensive dosing regimens. Recent mechanistic insights suggest that VEGF blockade primarily targets the angiogenic induction but fails to promote vascular normalization. In the diabetic milieu, the upregulation of Ang-2 acts as a distinct pathogenic driver—functioning essentially as a vascular extension of the Module III (SASP) inflammatory network—that actively destabilizes the endothelial–pericyte interface, rendering vessels leaky independent of VEGF levels.115,116 Consequently, targeting VEGF alone creates a “leaky suppression” rather than true structural repair. The blockade of Ang-2 does not merely suppress angiogenesis but reactivates the Tie2 signaling axis, promoting the recruitment of pericytes and tightening of junctional complexes.117 This dual-target strategy—simultaneously inhibiting the angiogenic trigger (VEGF) and restoring vascular structural integrity (Ang-2/Tie2)—provides a mechanistic rationale for neutralizing the downstream outputs of the aging network, particularly in cases resistant to anti-VEGF monotherapy.118,119

Metabolic Reprogramming: The Warburg Effect and Lactate Generation

Underlying these signaling aberrations is a profound metabolic shift that serves as the critical bridge between Module I (bioenergetic crisis) and Module IV (epigenetic drift) within RECs. To cope with hyperglycemic flux, these cells undergo a “Warburg-like” reprogramming, prioritizing aerobic glycolysis over oxidative phosphorylation despite oxygen availability.120 This shift is coordinated by the upregulation of key glycolytic enzymes, notably 6-phosphofructo-2-kinase/fructose-2,6-bisphosphatase 3 (PFKFB3) and the dimeric form of pyruvate kinase M2 (PKM2), which divert glucose flux toward lactate production rather than the TCA cycle.121 Far from being a metabolic waste product, the accumulated lactate functions as a potent signaling metabolite. Through the action of acetyl-CoA synthetase 2 (ACSS2), lactate is converted into lactyl-CoA, providing the substrate for a novel post-translational modification: histone lactylation.122

Histone Lactylation: The Epigenetic Basis of Metabolic Memory

The concept of “metabolic memory,” where retinal pathology progresses despite glycemic normalization, finds its molecular basis in histone lactylation, representing the ultimate entrenchment of Module IV. The lactyl-CoA generated upstream by ACSS2122 serves as the substrate for the histone acetyltransferase p300, which catalyzes histone lactylation.123 This modification at H3K18 (H3K18la) has been mapped to gene promoters and functionally implicated in inflammatory gene activation. In senescent microglia, elevated H3K18la directly stimulates the NF-κB signaling pathway by increasing binding to the promoters of Rela (p65) and Nfkb1 (p50), thereby upregulating SASP components such as IL-6 and IL-8.124 In fibrotic contexts, H3K18la has been linked to the activation of profibrotic gene programs.125 This distinct epigenetic mark is deposited on actively transcribed gene promoters and, by neutralizing the positive charge of the histone tail, is thought to relax chromatin structure, facilitating sustained transcriptional activation of genes such as TGF-β1 and NF-κB targets, even after the initial hyperglycemic stimulus is removed.

Unlike acetylation, which is dynamic and transient, lactylation appears to confer a more stable, long-term epigenetic imprint. This mechanism effectively “locks” retinal cells into a pathological phenotype, explaining the persistence of neurovascular degeneration seen in clinical practice.89 Similar lactylation-dependent positive feedback loops have been identified in AMD, reinforcing the broader relevance of this mechanism.89 Consequently, interventions targeting the metabolic enzymes governing this modification (e.g., PKM2 inhibition or SIRT1-mediated delactylation) represent a frontier for erasing metabolic memory and arresting DR progression at the epigenetic level.126,127 In DR, chronic hyperglycemia routes the aging network through a metabolic–epigenetic amplification loop. The dominant burden falls on bioenergetic crisis (I) and epigenetic remodeling (IV), distinguishing its pathology from that of AMD and glaucoma.

In summary, the integration of these modules across AMD, glaucoma, and DR elucidates how the fundamental hallmarks of aging—mitochondrial dysfunction (I), proteostasis collapse (II), senescence (III), and epigenetic drift (IV)—precipitate distinct pathologies depending on the unique metabolic and structural constraints of each ocular tissue. Tissue-Specific Routing of the Shared Aging Network in Ocular Pathologies

Molecular Crosstalk: Integrating Disease-Specific Mechanisms Into a Unified Network

The preceding sections show that AMD, glaucoma, and DR do not arise from isolated aging mechanisms acting independently. Rather, they are tissue-specific outputs of a shared stress network—one in which mitochondrial dysfunction, proteostasis failure, cellular senescence, and metabolic dysregulation (Module IV) continually reinforce one another. Disease specificity, then, comes not from distinct pathogenic principles but from differences in cellular context: bioenergetic demand, mechanical load, and dependence on ECM homeostasis.

At the center of this network lie mitochondrial dysfunction and proteostatic failure. Mitochondrial decline increases reactive oxygen species, lowers ATP availability, and promotes the release of danger signals such as oxidized mtDNA.10 These changes impair lysosomal acidification, autophagic flux, and organelle turnover, thereby compromising proteostasis.59 Conversely, defective autophagy and mitophagy allow damaged mitochondria to persist, amplifying oxidative stress and deepening bioenergetic failure.68 The two are best understood as a reciprocal pathogenic unit, not separate categories. This coupling may help explain why studies reporting increased autophagy-related markers do not always indicate effective cytoprotection, as induction of autophagosome formation and completion of degradative flux are biologically distinct processes.59

When mitochondrial and proteostatic dysfunction persist, cells become more susceptible to senescence and chronic inflammatory signaling. DNA damage, redox imbalance, and incomplete organelle clearance promote a senescence-like state accompanied by SASP production, and the SASP in turn spreads stress to neighboring cells through inflammatory mediators, matrix remodeling enzymes, and altered metabolic signals.35 In this context, molecules such as p62/SQSTM1 illustrate why apparently contradictory findings recur in the literature. Depending on cellular flux status, p62 may support selective autophagic clearance and resolution of stress, or instead accumulate and participate in inflammatory scaffold formation.14,15 A similar logic applies to regulated cell death. Ferroptosis, inflammasome activation, apoptosis, and pyroptosis are not always mutually exclusive endpoints; they can be overlapping outputs of the same chronically stressed network.113

Nutrient-sensing pathways and metabolic regulators influence whether cells adapt to stress or slide toward degeneration. Among these, NAD⁺ homeostasis is a convergence point, as it coordinates mitochondrial maintenance, sirtuin activity, DNA repair, redox balance, and autophagic competence.16,50 The consequences of declining NAD⁺ availability therefore extend well beyond metabolism. Likewise, altered AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK)–mTOR signaling and age-associated epigenetic drift can stabilize maladaptive transcriptional states, allowing transient stress to become chronic dysfunction.113 This may be especially relevant in DR, where hyperglycemia leaves a durable epigenetic imprint, but the same logic also applies to aging RPE and RGCs in which metabolic insufficiency is translated into persistent inflammatory and degenerative programs.

Within this shared network, the dominant pathological phenotype depends on how stress signals are routed through specific tissues. In AMD, the RPE operates under high oxidative load, continuous phagocytic demand, and intense lipid handling; accordingly, mitochondrial injury and lysosomal insufficiency are preferentially amplified, leading to drusen accumulation, inflammasome-associated inflammation, and eventual degeneration of the RPE–photoreceptor unit. In glaucoma, the TM and RGCs occupy a different stress landscape. In the TM, chronic mechanical strain and ECM dependence favor senescence-associated secretory signaling, fibrotic remodeling, and increased outflow resistance. In RGCs, extreme energetic demand and dependence on axonal transport make mitochondrial compromise and NAD⁺ decline especially consequential, linking aging to transport failure and neurodegeneration. In DR, chronic hyperglycemia engages the same network from a metabolic entry point, where redox imbalance, mitochondrial stress, endothelial–pericyte dysfunction, and stable epigenetic reprogramming interact within the NVU to sustain leakage, inflammation, and neuronal injury. The diseases share a common network architecture; what differs is where amplification occurs.

This interpretation has therapeutic implications. It may help explain why single-pathway interventions often show incomplete benefit: Blocking one downstream effector does not necessarily interrupt the upstream loops that continue to generate oxidative stress, mitochondrial damage, or senescence-associated signaling. For this reason, therapies that target broader regulatory nodes—restoring NAD⁺ homeostasis, improving mitochondrial quality control, recovering lysosomal function, or modulating the SASP—may provide more durable benefit than inhibiting any single downstream effector. At the same time, a unified network model does not imply a uniform treatment strategy. The most effective interventions will likely have to combine shared upstream targeting with tissue- and stage-specific delivery, reflecting the fact that the same aging network produces distinct pathological outputs in the RPE, TM, RGCs, and retinal NVU.

In this view, AREDs are not separate diseases that happen to share aging features. They are different faces of a common, interacting aging network. The value of this model is not that it collapses all disease mechanisms into one pathway; rather, it is that it clarifies how common molecular stresses, routed through different ocular tissues, produce AMD, glaucoma, and DR.

Innovative Therapeutic Strategies Targeting Ocular Senescence

The transition from descriptive gerontology to interventional geroscience has yielded a new class of therapeutics designed to restore tissue resilience by targeting the fundamental drivers of aging. Moving beyond symptom management, the strategies outlined below aim to arrest or reverse the biological decay of the ocular microenvironment.

Senotherapeutics: Clearing and Modulating the Senescent Burden

Senotherapeutics target senescent cells through two distinct approaches: Senolytics induce apoptosis of senescent cells, and senomorphics suppress the deleterious SASP without killing the cell.

Senolytics: Targeting Apoptotic Resistance

Senescent cells survive by upregulating senescent cell anti-apoptotic pathways (SCAPs), effectively creating a “survival shield” against endogenous death signals. Senolytic agents function by inhibiting these SCAPs, forcing the resistant cells into apoptosis.128,129 In a mouse model of dry AMD, the senolytic combination dasatinib plus quercetin (D+Q) preserved retinal structure and function. This protection was associated with elimination of senescent RPE cells, correction of lysosomal alkalinization, and reduction of lipofuscin deposition and oxidative stress.130

However, the clinical translation of systemic senolytics is constrained by on-target toxicity, particularly thrombocytopenia and neutropenia associated with B-cell lymphoma–extra large (BCL-xL) inhibition. This has necessitated the development of “second-generation” strategies, such as UBX1325 (foselutoclax), a BCL-xL–targeted inhibitor optimized for local intravitreal delivery. In a randomized, sham-controlled clinical trial for DME, a single intravitreal injection of the senolytic agent UBX1325 demonstrated a trend toward visual acuity stabilization rather than significant improvement.131 These findings suggest that, although preclinical studies demonstrate that BCL-xL inhibition can selectively eliminate senescent vascular cells and promote retinal vascular repair,132 the clinical benefits in advanced disease may be limited by factors that are not yet fully understood. The clinical limitations of senolytics may extend beyond timing to encompass a fundamental structural constraint in post-mitotic tissues. This raises the possibility that alternative strategies preserving cellular architecture, such as senomorphics (modulating Module III) or epigenetic reprogramming (reversing Module IV), could offer distinct advantages for advanced ocular pathologies. However, given that these approaches remain predominantly in preclinical stages, head-to-head comparisons and safety evaluations are imperative before concluding their translational superiority.

Senomorphics: Attenuation of the SASP

Complete senolysis may disrupt tissue integrity in post-mitotic environments such as the retina. Senomorphic strategies offer an alternative, as they do not eliminate senescent cells but rather suppress the harmful SASP—the primary pathological output of Module III, including IL-6, IL-8, and MMPs—to halt the paracrine “bystander effect.” p38 MAPK inhibition, a prototypical senomorphic intervention, can block oxidative stress–induced RPE cell senescence through the p53–p21–Rb pathway and preserve RPE function. Mechanistically, bone morphogenetic protein 4 (BMP4) mediates this oxidative stress–triggered senescence via parallel Smad and p38 signaling cascades, and its pro-senescent effects can be abrogated by either the BMP antagonist chordin-like 1 or the p38 inhibitor SB203580.133 Metformin, widely known for its metabolic effects, also suppresses inflammatory signaling in the diabetic retina. In vitreous samples from patients with proliferative DR, metformin use was associated with lower levels of multiple inflammatory cytokines, an effect linked to AMPK activation and NF-κB inhibition.134 Whether this anti-inflammatory activity reflects bona fide SASP suppression—and thus qualifies metformin as a senomorphic in the strict sense—remains to be directly tested in ocular tissues.

Innovative biomimetic strategies have also emerged. Polysialic acid (PSA) nanoparticles utilize the Siglec-E–mediated immunomodulatory axis to dampen macrophage activation. By presenting PSA to Siglec receptors on microglia/macrophages, these nanoparticles mimic the “self-recognition” signals of healthy cells, effectively resolving the chronic inflammation associated with drusen and preventing retinal thinning in AMD models.135

Restoring Molecular Homeostasis: Autophagy and Mitochondrial Quality Control

The collapse of proteostasis (Module II) and mitochondrial fidelity (Module I) in post-mitotic ocular tissues necessitates interventions that reactivate cellular quality control mechanisms.

Reactivating Autophagic Flux

Pharmacological reactivation of autophagy centers on inhibiting the mTOR complex, a negative regulator of lysosomal biogenesis. Rapamycin relieves mTOR-mediated suppression of autophagy and protects RPE cells from lipofuscin toxicity.136 However, systemic mTOR inhibition involves complex trade-offs regarding immune suppression and metabolic regulation. Alternative, non-immunosuppressive strategies include the activation of sirtuins (e.g., SIRT1). Compounds such as resveratrol and NAD⁺ precursors enhance SIRT1-mediated deacetylation of autophagy-related proteins (ATGs) and the transcription factor Forkhead box O (FOXO), thereby synchronizing mitochondrial biogenesis (Module I) with lysosomal clearance (Module II).137 Additionally, dietary supplementation with spermidine promotes macroautophagy via the hypusination of eIF5A, preserving mitochondrial respiratory capacity and cognitive function in aging models.138

Mitochondrial Therapeutics

Because oxidative stress (Module I) plays a central role in RPE and RGC degeneration, interest has moved from generic antioxidants to mitochondria-targeted antioxidants. Compounds designed to accumulate within the mitochondrial matrix (e.g., via triphenylphosphonium lipophilic cations) can neutralize ROS at their source, preserving cardiolipin integrity and preventing cytochrome c release.139 Beyond scavenging ROS, restoring mitochondrial quality control—the balance between biogenesis, fission/fusion, and mitophagy—is critical. Interventions that re-establish glutathione (GSH) homeostasis have shown promise in countering the “metabolic memory” of hyperglycemia in DR, where GSH depletion is driven by AGE accumulation.140 Furthermore, correcting trace element imbalances (zinc, aelenium, copper) supports the enzymatic activity of SOD and glutathione peroxidase, reinforcing the intrinsic antioxidant architecture of the cell.141

Epigenetic Reprogramming: Reversing the Cellular Clock

Partial reprogramming aims to reverse the epigenetic aging clock (Module IV). Unlike traditional regenerative strategies that replace lost cells, this approach seeks to rejuvenate existing cells in situ. The transient expression of the Yamanaka factors (Oct4, Sox2, Klf4, and c-Myc [OSKM]; or the safer subset OSK) initiates a remodeling of the DNA methylation landscape. This process erases the accumulated “epigenetic noise”—the stochastic methyl groups and histone modifications acquired with age—restoring a youthful gene expression profile without forcing the cell into a pluripotent, teratoma-prone state (dedifferentiation).142,143 In pioneering studies utilizing the RGC model, adeno-associated virus (AAV)-mediated delivery of inducible OSK successfully reversed the DNA methylation age of damaged neurons and, critically, restored visual function in murine models of glaucoma and optic nerve injury.56 This finding indicates that age-related neurodegeneration is not necessarily unidirectional. Current investigations are exploring pharmacological alternatives or “chemical reprogramming” (e.g., via NAD⁺ boosters or HDAC inhibitors) to achieve similar epigenetic resetting with lower oncogenic risk and higher temporal control.144

Advanced Ocular Delivery Systems

The translation of these molecular therapies and future combinatorial network strategies is currently bottlenecked by the physiological barriers of the eye—specifically, the blood–retinal barrier (BRB) and the rapid clearance kinetics of the anterior segment.

Viral Vectors (AAVs)

AAVs remain the gold standard for delivering genetic payloads (e.g., OSKM factors or anti-senescence genes such as BMI1) to the posterior segment. Serotypes such as AAV2, AAV5, and AAV8 exhibit distinct tropisms for RPE and photoreceptors.145 The development of capsid variants capable of penetrating the inner limiting membrane following intravitreal injection—rather than the more invasive subretinal approach—represents a significant leap toward clinical feasibility.146 However, capacity limitations and the potential for immunogenic responses necessitate the continuous optimization of vector design.147

Non-Viral and Nanotechnology Platforms

To circumvent viral limitations, nanoparticle-based systems offer tunable platforms for the sustained release of hydrophobic senolytics or delicate mRNA cargoes. Liposomes, polymeric nanoparticles, and hydrogels can be engineered for enhanced mucoadhesion or specific cell-penetrating capabilities (e.g., TAT peptides). For example, peptide–nanofiber systems have demonstrated the ability to extend the intraocular half-life of anti-VEGF agents, reducing injection frequency.148 “Smart” delivery systems responsive to environmental triggers, such as ROS levels or pH changes in the senescent microenvironment, may enhance the spatial specificity of senolytics and reduce off-target effects on healthy tissue.

Challenges and Future Directions

Although the elucidation of ocular aging mechanisms has identified promising therapeutic targets, the translation of these findings into clinical reality faces multidimensional barriers. The path from bench to bedside is obstructed by the anatomical impermeability of the eye, the safety paradox of cellular plasticity, and the translational chasm between rodent models and human physiology.

Delivery Systems: Breaching the BRB

The unique anatomical isolation of the eye places fundamental constraints on emerging anti-aging interventions. The BRB, comprised of the tight junctions of the retinal capillary endothelium and the RPE, rigorously restricts the permeation of both high- and low-molecular-weight therapeutics. Although intravitreal injection remains the clinical standard for bypassing these barriers, its invasive nature entails significant burdens, including the risk of endophthalmitis and retinal detachment, which limit long-term compliance. Nanotechnology-based platforms offer ways to circumvent these limitations. Recent innovations utilizing DNA nanostructures, such as tetrahedral DNA frameworks (tetrahedral framework nucleic acid [tFNA]), have demonstrated the capacity to penetrate the ocular barrier and deliver anti-angiogenic aptamers with efficacy comparable to intravitreal antibodies.149 Similarly, surface-modified lipid nanoparticles, such as penetratin–hyaluronic acid-modified liposomes, facilitate non-invasive accumulation in the RPE, offering a topical alternative to injections.150 Furthermore, to address the need for sustained release, thermo-responsive hydrogels and implantable devices (e.g., OTX-TP) are being engineered to maintain therapeutic concentrations over extended periods, thereby reducing administration frequency and enhancing patient adherence.151

Safety Concerns: Balancing Efficacy and Toxicity in Rejuvenation

The manipulation of cellular aging processes carries inherent risks that necessitate rigorous safety profiling prior to clinical deployment. A primary concern involves senolytics, where the heterogeneity of senescent cells poses a challenge. Senescent populations are not uniform; they exhibit distinct SASP phenotypes depending on the tissue context, and non-specific clearance may inadvertently damage healthy tissues or disrupt beneficial senescent cells involved in wound healing.41 Although recent studies in non-human primates utilizing D+Q suggest a favorable safety profile with metabolic benefits,152 the potential for off-target toxicity in the distinct microenvironment of the human eye remains a critical consideration.

Partial reprogramming using OSKM factors also carries risks. Prolonged or uncontrolled expression of these pluripotency factors can lead to dedifferentiation, loss of cellular identity, and teratoma formation.153 Whether such dedifferentiation would compromise the barrier function of post-mitotic ocular tissues such as the RPE has not been directly studied, but the risk warrants caution. Consequently, the development of inducible gene expression systems is imperative. Strategies employing inducible gene expression systems—such as riboswitch-based post-transcriptional control, which has been used to regulate anti-VEGF expression in the retina154—offer a safeguard against the risks of permanent genetic modification.

Precision Tools: Omics and Aging Clocks

A significant impediment to precision medicine in ophthalmology is the lack of tools to quantify biological aging at the cellular level. Traditional bulk sequencing masks the heterogeneity of ocular tissues, obscuring specific pathogenic subsets. Single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) addresses this by resolving the transcriptomic landscape of individual cells. Comprehensive atlases of the human retina have already cataloged dozens of distinct cell types and mapped hundreds of disease-associated genes to their cellular origins.155 Building on this foundation, scRNA-seq studies in the aging mouse retina have characterized the landscape of senescent cell accumulation and revealed how SASP-associated transcriptional programs drive age-related functional decline.156

Epigenetic clocks based on DNA methylation patterns have shown promise in estimating biological age across multiple tissues. Extending this approach to develop retina-specific clocks could provide a quantitative metric to assess the biological age of ocular tissues, although this remains an active area of investigation.157 Complemented by imaging biomarkers such as lipofuscin accumulation158 and advanced OCT angiography,159 these tools may help define rigorous endpoints for future clinical trials.

The Translational Gap: From Rodents to Primates

The reliance on rodent models constitutes a major bottleneck in validating ocular therapeutics. Significant physiological discrepancies, such as the absence of a macula and distinct vascular architecture in rodents compared to humans, limit the predictive value of these models for diseases such as AMD. Furthermore, species-specific differences in immune privileges and AAV capsid tropism can lead to the failure of gene therapies that were effective in mice when transitioned to higher mammals.160,161

To bridge this chasm, the utilization of non-human primate models is increasingly critical. NHPs possess retinal anatomical features that closely mimic those of the human eye, providing a more disease-relevant platform for evaluating the efficacy and duration of action of candidate therapeutics.162 Ultimately, successful clinical translation necessitates not only superior animal models but also the refinement of clinical trial endpoints. Moving beyond visual acuity (e.g., best-corrected visual acuity), future trials must incorporate structural and functional biomarkers to detect early therapeutic effects in preventative settings.163

Conclusions and Outlook

The shift toward ocular geroscience changes how we think about age-related blinding diseases. AREDs are not simply the result of wear and tear over time. Rather, they reflect the same aging mechanisms—mitochondrial dysfunction, proteostatic failure, cellular senescence, and epigenetic drift—routed through different tissues to produce distinct pathologies.

Because these hallmarks constantly amplify one another, future therapies will likely have to be combinatorial rather than targeting single pathways. One example would be pairing acute senolytic clearance to break the inflammatory loop with sustained epigenetic reprogramming to restore cellular resilience. This kind of multipronged approach is more likely to dismantle the pathological network than any single-agent strategy.

Integrating advanced biomarkers—retinal epigenetic clocks, imaging of metabolic autofluorescence—will also be essential. These tools could identify patients during the latent molecular phase, long before irreversible structural damage occurs. Ultimately, deciphering this network serves a dual purpose. It provides a mechanistic blueprint for preserving vision, and it establishes the eye as an accessible, high-resolution window for monitoring the broader efficacy of systemic anti-aging interventions.
