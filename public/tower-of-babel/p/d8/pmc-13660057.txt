Examining the challenges faced by nursing faculty and students during wartime: a qualitative analysis
Authors: Nasrin Rezaee, Mojgan Jahantigh, Sally Pezaro, Marjan Mardani-Hamooleh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundWhilst nursing care is essential, war often leaves widespread destruction, including damage to universities, faculties, student dormitories and hospitals, as well as the loss of students, faculty members, and their families. The aim of this study was to examine nursing faculty and students’ perceptions of the recent wartime challenges in Iran.MethodsThis study employed a descriptive qualitative design. Semi-structured and individual interviews with 29 participants (16 students and 13 faculty members) were undertaken. Data collection was conducted from July 1 to August 5, 2025, following the wartime crisis and during a period of ceasefire. Content analysis was used to make sense of the data collected.ResultsThe identified challenges were (1) the lack of wartime nursing content in the curriculum, (2) structural and organizational barriers associated with war, and (3) distress resulting from encountering war. The first category included the subcategories of weak theoretical instruction and inadequate clinical training. The second category comprised the subcategories of the crisis of faculty closures and threats to faculty professional development. The third category included the subcategories of immersion in a world of fear, having a blurred vision of the future, and grappling with death anxiety.ConclusionThe findings suggest a need to consider reforms in nursing education policy, including the integration of wartime nursing concepts and potential development of specialized training programs. It is also crucial to dismantle structural barriers and prioritize the psychological well-being of faculty and students to ensure compassionate and effective education during wartime.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12912-026-05082-0.

Introduction

War is an event described by rapid changes, profound social implications, and a traumatic phenomenon with unforeseen and unavoidable crises [1]. It is associated with both physical and psychological health consequences across all age groups [2–7]. War often results in widespread destruction in the healthcare arena, including damage to universities, faculties, student dormitories and hospitals, as well as the loss of students, faculty members, and their families [8]. Moreover, the academic responsibilities and obligations of healthcare students are significantly impacted, as war places students under academic distress, for which they often lack adequate coping skills [3]. Studies conducted in Syria and Palestine indicate that, in addition to academic distress, medical students experience various psychological stressors, including depression, anxiety, and reduced resilience during wartime [9, 10]. Medical students in Ukraine similarly experienced educational difficulties during the war, such as financial strain, infrastructure damage, limited research access, and decreased motivation [11]. Whilst national and community security is threatened in a wide range of conflicts [12], evidence related to the recent conflict between Iran and Israel in June 2025 remains absent.

In addition to the limited contextual research on healthcare provision during wartime, the effects on healthcare education remain underexplored [13]. This issue is particularly concerning, as war adversely affects healthcare education at precisely the times when the need for healthcare services is greatest [14]. The negative consequences of war on educational systems in conflict-affected regions can result in significant interruptions to students’ study routines, decreased engagement in instructional activities, and a subsequent decline in motivation toward education [15–17]. Furthermore, wartime circumstances can disrupt medical and nursing education by impacting safety, limiting access to educational resources for faculty and students, and altering standard training processes. Consequently, these factors may compromise the quality of healthcare education and present significant challenges to its continuity [14]. Notably, evidence from Sudan indicates that armed conflict has caused substantial damage to the infrastructure of educational institutions. Consequently, students have reported concerns about deteriorating curriculum standards and frequently expressed a preference for pursuing their studies in more stable environments [8, 18]. Similarly, international students studying in Ukraine faced multiple challenges during the war, including insecurity, inadequate shelter, insufficient food, and difficulties returning to their home countries [13]. These findings highlight the importance of studying healthcare students’ perspectives in under-researched contexts.

Nursing, a discipline grounded in both theoretical knowledge and clinical skills [19], typically maintains a balance among education, clinical practice, and research in standard circumstances. However, during wartime, this balance is threatened [20]. In Iran, the nursing education system, which plays a fundamental role in shaping the broader healthcare system [21], has encountered ongoing issues such as a shortage of faculty staff, limited clinical infrastructure, restricted clinical learning environments, lack of educational facilities in clinical settings, and a continuing disparity between theoretical knowledge and clinical performance [22]. These pre-existing challenges, combined with the additional impact of wartime conditions on learning opportunities and the experiences of both faculty and students [23], underscore the necessity of identifying the specific challenges faced by nursing educators and students during conflict. Nursing education is essential for training capable students to deliver quality clinical care. Despite growing evidence on the impact of war on healthcare systems and education, little is known about how these challenges are experienced within nursing education in the Iranian context. Therefore, this study sought to examine nursing faculty and student perspectives on recent wartime challenges in Iran through qualitative analysis of their lived experiences.

Methods

Study design

To identify nursing faculty and students’ perceptions of wartime challenges, this study employed a descriptive qualitative design, and content analysis to make sense of the data collected. Qualitative research designs, especially those informed by naturalistic methodologies, yield comprehensive insights into participants’ experiences by capturing their perspectives and expressions in their own words. Content analysis, used in qualitative designs to examine written, verbal, or visual messages related to a specific concept is useful in particular where prior knowledge is limited [24]. The Standards for Reporting Qualitative Research checklist [25], were used to guide reporting.

Study setting

Our study settings included the nursing schools affiliated with the Tehran University of Medical Sciences and Zahedan University of Medical Sciences in Iran. The Iranian students learn the theoretical skills in the theoretical classes and practical skills in the clinical skill centers and laboratories. The clinical education is arranged from simple to difficult and takes place during patients’ care based on the nursing process. Students begin their clinical education from the second semester and this is run concurrently with theoretical courses until the end of the third year. In the fourth year, students participate in full-time clinical education. There is a “preceptorship educational model” in clinical nursing education in Iran [22]. Clinical nursing education consists of direct supervision by faculty members. In cases of faculty shortage, supervision is assigned to the clinical nurse educators and head nurses. In this regard, before entering the field of clinical education, the university provide a comprehensive course in teaching skills, covering teaching, assessment, and effective communication with nursing students. Then, the clinical nurse educators and head nurses are allowed to teach, initially under the supervision of faculty members. The collaboration of clinical nurses and head nurses in the process of clinical education is not included in their job descriptions. This collaboration is voluntary and based on rich clinical experiences [26, 27]. Each faculty member follows a diverse set of activities that include educational, research, and clinical activities in Iran. The field in which a clinical faculty member spends most of his/ her time, apart from the field of education and research, is the field of caring patients in the educational hospitals. Teaching variables and educational activities, research skills, and professional skills are the most important components of performance evaluation of clinical faculty members [28]. The faculty to student ratio is estimated to be in theory class, clinical and skills laboratory 1:25, 1:8, and 1:15. Each nursing faculty has working 25 h weekly. The monthly salary of nursing faculty varies based on work experiences and academic degree [26]. The Dean of the nursing schools and the head of departments are selected by the university chancellor based on their clinical, educational, and administrative experiences in nursing. The Dean’s duty is to oversee the performance of all faculties and staff working in the schools. The duty of the head of departments is to directly supervise the faculties’ performance within their specific departments. Faculties in nursing schools have a PhD-level in nursing education and can work in various departments (medical-surgical nursing, community health nursing, pediatric nursing, psychiatric nursing, critical care nursing, and geriatric nursing) with different academic degree (assistant professor, associate professor, and full professor) under the supervision of Dean of schools. There are several academic committees with educational and research objectives in the schools, and the number of faculty members on these committees is 5 to 7 people, but there is no specialized committee for crisis management during wartime currently [26]. All of nursing education activities in Iranian nursing schools are supervised by the Advisory Board of Nursing Education and no independent and specialized accreditation program has been notified to nursing schools in Iran [29].

Participants

Participants were selected using purposeful sampling. The corresponding author called the nursing schools of the 2 universities to screen for the potential participants through a general invitation. Participants were eligible for inclusion if they were working or studying at the listed nursing schools and had experienced wartime crises. All participants had direct or indirect exposure to wartime conditions, including disruption to education, safety concerns, or institutional changes resulting from the conflict. The corresponding author contacted administrative offices and department heads within the nursing schools to disseminate invitations via institutional communication channels, including internal messaging systems and direct contact with eligible individuals. Approximately 40–45 individuals were approached to participate in the study. Of these, 29 agreed to participate (16 students and 13 faculty members), while others either declined due to time constraints or did not respond. Participants were excluded if they declined to participate in interviews or chose to withdraw from the study at any point. There were not any known differences between participants and non- participants. Sampling aimed to achieve maximum variation across variables such as age, gender, marital status, and academic level (students only), as well as academic rank and work experience for faculty members.

Data collection

Following authorization to access the research sites, coordination took place with the heads of nursing departments, who then referred eligible participants to the research team. Data were collected through individual, semi-structured interviews conducted by the first author and the corresponding author. Both interviewers held a Ph.D. in nursing education, had expertise in qualitative research, and were themselves nursing faculty members. Each interview lasted 45 to 65 min and continued until data saturation was reached, that is, until no new information was obtained [30]. Interviews were conducted from July 1 to August 5, 2025, following the wartime crisis and during a period of ceasefire. All interviews were conducted in Persian. Those parts of the interviews that were relevant to this manuscript were translated into English by an expert translator and then the English version was translated back into Persian for verification by an independent reviewer, who is an Iranian bilingual translator. These were subsequently verified for accuracy by the interview team. Each participant was interviewed once, resulting in a total of 29 interviews. Prior to conducting each interview, interviewers coordinated with participants regarding the time and location of the session. Interview schedules were coordinated based on participants’ preferences, and all interviews were conducted in the offices of the first and corresponding authors located within the respective nursing schools. The interview questions were developed utilising the researchers’ clinical expertise in nursing education (Appendix 1). Participants were asked to describe their experiences of nursing education during the recent wartime, identify the challenges they believed nursing education faced in that period, and discuss the personal challenges they themselves encountered. Data collection occurred during an active period of geopolitical tension and ceasefire, enabling the capture of participants’ experiences in real time rather than retrospectively.

Data analysis

Data collection and analysis were conducted concurrently. There are two types of content analysis. Directed (deductive) content analysis relies on preexisting research or theories. In essence, the researcher starts with a predetermined set of categories or codes and then applies them to the data in a top-down approach to meaning-making. In this approach, the initial codes framework is derived from the theory guiding the study and is applied deductively to the data. In contrast to deductive content analysis, conventional (inductive) content analysis involves collecting and analyzing data without preconceived categories or theories. This flexibility allows the data to guide the researcher’s analysis in order to identify emerging patterns, categories, and concepts. In this approach, the initial codes are derived from the data itself, making the process inductive [24, 31]. In this study, data were analysed using a conventional content analysis approach [31]. Each interview, having been audio-recorded, transcribed, and converted into a single text document was read multiple times so that the team could familiarize themselves with the data. Meaning units were then extracted and subsequently coded (meaning unit is several words, a sentence, or a statement that represents a single notion or concept and code is a short label that describes a meaning unit). Then, a codebook was developed. This codebook helps individual researchers to approach their analysis systematically, but it can facilitate coding by multiple coders who would use the same rules outlined in the codebook to the inductive coding process. An example of codebook is presented in Table 1. Codes with similarities or differences were then grouped into subcategories, and related subcategories were combined to form overarching categories [31]. In this regard, 3 authors (NR, MJ, MMH) led the analysis of the data, and the corresponding author transcribed the interviews. Then, transcripts were reviewed multiple times to gain a global understanding of the data. Lastly, the research team engaged in discussions regarding the identification of subcategories and categories and any disagreement was resolved. A sample of data analysis is presented in Table 2. To maintain the integrity of the findings, the principles of credibility, dependability, confirmability, and transferability were employed [32].

Credibility was established through continuous engagement with the data and ongoing critical reflection. The research team contributed feedback throughout the data collection and analysis phases, and the findings were validated by selected participants as well as two PhD-level nursing experts. For example, participant feedback led to minor refinement of category labels to better reflect shared experiences of uncertainty and disruption. The educational experiences of three authors in the team (NR, MJ, and MMH), who possess extensive background educating for nursing students within the Iranian context, informed both the data collection and analysis processes. The inclusion of a third author, SP, an academic midwife with extensive qualitative research expertise but an outsider to the specific cultural context, provided methodological rigor and critical reflexivity to challenge potential insider bias during data analysis and interpretation. Also, the findings were shared with 4 of the participants for validation. Reflexivity was maintained throughout the analysis process. The research team remained aware of how their values and interpretive commitments could influence data interpretation. Ongoing critical reflection and team discussions facilitated the assessment of assumptions and confirmed that the results were grounded in the participants’ perceptions. Dependability was strengthened by the inclusion of an external auditor with expertise in both nursing education and qualitative research who was not part of the research team. This auditor independently reviewed and concurred with the results. For confirmability, the research team maintained a reflexive journal from the outset of the analysis process. The interview data were analyzed using a comparative approach to identify categories and sub-categories within the data. All data and results were reviewed with the research team to minimize the influence of researcher bias. Additionally, transferability was supported by providing a thick description of the concept, thereby allowing others to assess the relevance of the results across different settings. Thus, transferability was enhanced by recruiting a diverse sample comprising undergraduate, master’s, and doctoral nursing students alongside faculty members spanning the ranks of assistant, associate, and full professor, thereby capturing demographic variety and applicability beyond any single group.

Results

Of the participants (n = 29), 17 were women and 12 were men, whilst 16 were students and 13 were faculty members. Faculty work experience ranged from 5 to 16 years. Their ages ranged from 21 to 55. Academically, our participants represent a wide spectrum, from bachelor’s and master’s students to assistant, associate, and full professors. The group is nearly evenly divided by marital status, with 15 single members and 14 married members. Wartime-related challenges experienced by nursing faculty members and students were identified and organized into three overarching categories with seven subcategories. The identified challenges consisted of (1) the lack of wartime nursing content in the curriculum, (2) structural and organizational barriers associated with war, and (3) distress resulting from encountering war. The first category included subcategories related to weak theoretical instruction and inadequate clinical training. The second category comprised the subcategories relating to the crisis of faculty closures and threats to faculty professional development. The third category included the subcategories of immersion in a world of fear, having a blurred vision of the future, and grappling with death anxiety. Taken together, these categories reflect a layered disruption, in which curricular gaps, institutional instability, and psychological distress are not isolated challenges but interconnected dimensions of how wartime conditions reshape both educational structures and lived experiences.

Comparative analysis across participant groups (students vs. faculty) did not reveal substantial differences in the types of challenges reported. Both groups consistently emphasized curriculum gaps, structural disruptions, and psychological distress. However, faculty members more frequently highlighted concerns related to professional development and institutional policies, whereas students more commonly emphasized fear, uncertainty, and disruption to learning trajectories.

Lack of wartime nursing content in the curriculum

Participants identified a considerable deficiency in the curriculum due to the lack of war-related nursing content in both theoretical instruction and clinical training. Given that nursing education integrates both theoretical knowledge and clinical practice, participants noted a significant lack of wartime nursing content within the curriculum, impacting the comprehensiveness of instruction in both areas.

These findings suggest that the absence of wartime content is not merely a curricular gap but represents a broader misalignment between educational structures and the realities of practice in conflict settings, leaving students underprepared for the contexts in which they may be required to deliver care.

Structural and organizational barriers associated with war

Findings of this category underscore the adverse effects of war on both students and academic staff. The outbreak of war brought about significant disruptions in nursing education, compelling faculty members and students to endure these changes and adapt to the circumstances imposed by the conflict. Some organizational challenges, such as the closure of nursing schools, directly affected both faculty and students. In addition, threats to the professional development of faculty members were considered another structural and organizational barrier associated with war.

Crisis of faculty closures

With the onset of war, several organizational policy issues emerged. During wartime, the education system faces difficulties due to the slowness of the internet. Thus, the implementation of the e-learning approach and virtual final exams are associated with challenges. In this regard, the support mechanisms are still not well prepared. Participants reported experiencing the closure of nursing schools in particular, which led to the cancellation of final exams for undergraduate students:The war prompted the university to enact new educational rules and regulations aligned with wartime conditions, which I would describe as organizational concerns related to war. For example, undergraduate students’ final exams coincided with the war and were completely canceled. As a result, faculty planning for end-of-term exams was disrupted (Participant #8, Assistant professor).

However, after resolving that issue, the war began, and now it has been approximately three months since the promotion sessions for faculty have not been held due to the conflict (Participant #27, Associate professor).

Distress resulting from encountering war

The distress caused by the wartime conditions constituted a set of individual concerns that threatened the participants’ mental well-being. Participants reported experiencing this distress in three interrelated ways: immersion in a world of fear, having a blurred vision of the future, and grappling with death anxiety.

Immersion in a world of fear

Participants described living in a constant state of fear, including fear of displacement and homelessness, fear of home destruction, and fear of being trapped under rubble without anyone to rescue them. These experiences ultimately contributed to sleep disturbances:I perceived the war as an immensely ugly and malevolent phenomenon seeking to dominate our psyche. The tragedy of war brings chaos, homelessness, and destruction. My uncle’s house was largely destroyed due to wartime attacks. This calamity intertwined within me a fear of devastation, fear of home destruction, and fear of being buried under a pile of rubble (Participant #26, Master’s student).Whenever the word ‘war’ comes up, I feel intense fear. The battlefield evokes images of people killing one another. During the war, our lives were under siege, and we faced a wave of fears throughout our daily existence. Fear of explosions and being trapped under a destroyed building without anyone to rescue me has disrupted my sleep; I constantly worry that attacks could occur at any moment (Participant #13, Bachelor’s student).

Grappling with death anxiety

Participants reported increased concerns primarily linked to diminished personal safety during wartime attacks, which ultimately contributed to elevated levels of death anxiety. The conflict led participants to express perceptions of imminent mortality, feelings of victimization, and apprehension regarding the immediate and very real potential cessation of life. The sense of death all around was described as being overwhelming.When war breaks out and bombardments intensify, one sees death right before their eyes. Every time there was an attack, I became extremely anxious. Discussion

This study examined nursing faculty and student perspectives on the recent wartime challenges in Iran. A key strength of this study lies in its timing, as data were collected during an ongoing period of conflict, offering contemporaneous insight into how wartime conditions shape nursing education experiences in real time. Participants stressed that curriculum addressing wartime nursing should be responsive to societal needs during periods of conflict to ensure educational standards are upheld. Consistent with these findings, studies conducted in Sudan and Ukraine similarly reported that wartime conditions disrupted student education, compromising overall educational quality and reducing faculty and student satisfaction with the learning process [18, 33, 34]. Participants further noted that the Military Nursing master’s program in Iran is currently available at just a limited number universities in the capital and does not focus solely on nursing during wartime. Considering Iran’s geographic location in the Middle East and the requirements of its healthcare system, expanding this program to more universities in various cities could be beneficial. It was also suggested that a dedicated master’s curriculum focused on nursing during wartime be created to address the gaps identified in the existing Military Nursing program. Considering the above, policymakers and nursing education specialists within the Ministry of Health may consider prioritising this initiative. The curriculum needs to reflect developments in wartime healthcare and changing needs, to ensure key aspects of war nursing are incorporated. Such updates would enable graduates to develop the necessary competencies to provide enhanced nursing care for patients and families during wartime situations.

The outbreak of war resulted in structural and organizational challenges for nursing education, including the closure of faculties. Due to ongoing internet issues and insufficient infrastructure for virtual education, some participants also recommended postponing remaining course content to future semesters. Studies in Sudan similarly demonstrated that universities adopted online learning strategies for medical and paramedical students during periods of conflict, where students likewise expressed dissatisfaction with the quality of education and limited interaction with educators during wartime [35, 36]. In Palestine, the shift to virtual education during war also resulted in reduced quality in the education delivered, decreased critical thinking abilities, and lower academic performance among nursing students [37]. Such deficits must be addressed in order to preserve the quality of future nursing care beyond wartime in future. Structural and organizational barriers to faculty staffs’ professional development emerged as a significant concern during wartime, with the promotion and annual advancement of educators postponed for substantial periods due to the conflict. Given that effective nursing education globally depends upon supporting faculty professional growth, it is imperative to implement strategies that prioritize rank advancement even amid crises and minimize delays. University and faculty leadership should therefore review and adjust relevant policies to address the unique challenges presented by wartime conditions. Similarly studies in Ukraine reported that medical students and educators faced limitations in education during the war, resulting in disrupted educational processes [33, 34]. Such comparable findings suggest that global healthcare education may be similarly affected during wartime, and thus require joined up approaches for recovery.

Following the war, participants reported experiencing various psychological challenges. Research conducted in Ukraine has indicated that medical students are subject to significant wartime stress, manifesting as psychological strain, depression, and anxiety. These individuals frequently utilize coping strategies that have limited effectiveness, resulting in compromised mental health and subsequent declines in academic performance [33, 38, 39]. Medical students in Sudan also faced psychological distress accompanied by symptoms of depression and anxiety during wartime [35]. Furthermore, the obligatory nature of clinical practicums during periods of armed conflict, which participants evocatively likened to “entering the dragon’s mouth of fire” heightened their death anxiety. These findings indicate that academic leaders and administrators should evaluate postponing pending clinical practicums to subsequent semesters during wartime, even if this results in an extended program duration. This may help mitigate increased death anxiety and better safeguard students’ psychological well-being in conflict settings.

The results of our study indicate that addressing educational, organizational, technological, and psychological challenges during wartime is vital to establishing a renewed academic environment for nursing faculty and students. Post-war instability led to a transitional period that subjected participants to heightened stress and adverse outcomes. Nonetheless, wartime circumstances do not mark the cessation of academic or personal progression; rather, adaptive strategies and resilience are necessary to sustain and advance life throughout and beyond periods of conflict. Indeed, in high-stress situations such as war, psychological preparation and the enhancement of personal safety are crucial for overcoming the mental health challenges associated with war [40–42]. Given that crises such as war are accompanied by numerous psychological issues, including stress, depression, anxiety, and cognitive decline [43, 44], the provision of psychological support for those affected becomes essential. Such support may include interventions aimed at improving cognitive functioning, emotion regulation, and utilizing available resources to reduce distress. For example, holistic practices such as yoga have been recommended to alleviate some wartime psychological problems [44, 45]. Psychological interventions should be co-created to support nursing faculty and students’ mental health during war, with a focus on building resilience and recovery. Although no major differences were identified between participant groups, subtle distinctions emerged. Faculty members were more likely to emphasize institutional and career-related disruptions, while students expressed more immediate concerns related to safety, uncertainty, and educational continuity. These findings highlight both shared and group-specific dimensions of wartime challenges.

Limitations

Due to its focused objective of examining the perceptions of nursing faculty members and students in relation to wartime challenges, the results may not be representative of individuals in other medical disciplines. Additionally, because qualitative research is inherently context-specific, these findings are not generalizable to other environments. Our sample size is not necessarily large. Yet data saturation was achieved. Thus we are confident that our findings authentically capture contemporary experiences in this context, and may be useful in preparing future nurses for wartime practice. In addition, the process of translating interviews from Persian to English could potentially lead to nuances being lost or meanings slightly altered. In this regard, a professional translator converted relevant parts of the interviews into English. For confirmation, the English text was subsequently translated back into Persian by an independent reviewer, an Iranian bilingual translator. Transcribing data into English in this way created a single, accessible version of the data that researchers, regardless of their native language, could work with directly.

Conclusion

The findings indicate an important need to reconsider aspects of nursing education policy in the context of wartime challenges. Leadership and policymakers must implement robust measures to fortify both theoretical and clinical nursing education during crisis. The current curriculum requires a fundamental revision to incorporate wartime nursing concepts, including the potential establishment of a Master’s program to formalize this specialized knowledge. Furthermore, it is critical to address and dismantle the structural and organizational barriers that impede academic progress during conflict. A primary focus must be on prioritizing the psychological well-being of faculty and students, mitigating the effects of trauma and death anxiety. Innovative solutions, such as intensive post-crisis in-person courses and the strategic postponement of clinical placements, are necessary to ensure that education continues in a manner that upholds the humanity of all participants, preparing nurses for the unique demands of providing compassionate care amidst chaos.
