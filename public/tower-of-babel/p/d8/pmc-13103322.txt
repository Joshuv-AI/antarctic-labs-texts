Site-defined Cu-O ensembles enable hydrogen-conserving light-driven ethane upgrading
Authors: Qingqing Zhang, Cong Liu, Chang Xu, Dingnan Huang, Cheng Xie, Zhandong Wang, Xiao-Ming Cao, Jinlong Zhang, Juying Lei, Ziwei Ye, Lingzhi Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractUpgrading light alkanes to value-added olefins is a long-standing challenge, owing to the high stability of C–H bonds and the tendency for overoxidation at elevated temperatures. Here, we introduce a light-driven strategy for ethane dehydrogenation using Cu-doped TiO2, in which atomically dispersed Cu coordinated to bridging oxygen (Obr–Cu) creates well-defined [Cu–O] ensembles that orchestrate site-specific, stepwise C–H activation. Photogenerated holes localize at Obr–Cu sites to initiate the first C–H cleavage, while adjacent Cu centers mediate β–H elimination and H2 evolution. In contrast, minor β–H activation at Obr–Ti sites generates *H species that cannot desorb due to a prohibitive coupling barrier with *H on Obr–Cu, leading to Cu reduction and progressive deactivation. Co-feeding CO2 restores the active Cu coordination environment and suppresses this deactivation process without perturbing the primary reaction pathway. This cooperative design achieves a C2H4 production rate of 21.1 mmol g−1 h−1 with nearly stoichiometric H2 evolution and an apparent quantum efficiency of 6.1% under 365 nm irradiation. These findings establish a site-defined, hydrogen-conserving route for photocatalytic alkane upgrading, offering a general blueprint for selective C–H bond transformations with long-term stability.Subject terms: Energy, Photocatalysis

Introduction

With increasing demands for carbon neutrality and cleaner chemical production, the selective upgrading of light alkanes, key components of shale gas, into value-added chemicals has become a strategic goal in sustainable catalysis1–5. Among these alkanes, ethane (C2H6), the second most abundant constituent (5–20 vol%) of shale gas, represents a promising feedstock for on-demand ethylene (C2H4) production, given its availability, low cost, and the central role of C2H4 in the global petrochemical supply chain6–9. However, catalytic ethane dehydrogenation (EDH) to ethylene remains fundamentally challenging due to the high bond dissociation energy of its C–H bonds (~415 kJ/mol) and the inherent difficulty in achieving high selectivity under practical conditions10–14.

In thermocatalytic systems, C–H activation typically proceeds via either noble metal nanoparticles (e.g., Pt, Pd) or via lattice oxygen species in reducible metal oxides such as ZnO, NiO, or CeO213–17. These processes generally require elevated temperatures (600–800 °C) to surmount the kinetic barrier for C–H cleavage18–20. Such harsh conditions often induce sintering of metal particles, excessive carbon deposition from deep dehydrogenation, and more critically, difficulties in regulating the properties, reactivity, and regeneration kinetics of active lattice oxygen21–25. The resulting active oxygen species are prone to uncontrolled spillover, which not only promotes nonselective overoxidation but also accelerates the consumption of valuable H2 during O2- or CO2-assisted regeneration via H2O formation13,15,26. These issues fundamentally limit the atom- and energy-efficiency of thermocatalytic EDH (TEDH) processes and create a trade-off between activity, selectivity, and catalyst longevity.

Photocatalytic EDH (PEDH) has emerged as a promising low-temperature alternative that can harness photogenerated charge carriers to circumvent the thermodynamic and kinetic limitations of thermocatalysis12,27–38. Metal oxide systems such as LaMn1-xCuxO3 and heterojunction catalysts like PdZn/ZnO have demonstrated light-assisted C–H activation through lattice oxygen participation or heterojunction-driven charge separation31,33. These successes underscore the viability of photocatalytic strategies in achieving energy-efficient alkane conversion. However, several key challenges remain unresolved. First, most photocatalytic systems suffer from low C2H4 selectivity due to overoxidation by highly reactive oxygen species such as •OH or •O2-, which indiscriminately cleave both C–H and C–C bonds12,34,39. Second, the formation, dynamics, and reactivity of surface hydrogen species (*H) remain poorly understood, especially their role in surface poisoning and deactivation16,40. Third, the lack of atomic-level control over the geometry of active sites and their redox cooperativity hampers the precise mediation of sequential hydrogen abstraction steps without undesired side reactions.

Herein, we address these challenges with a photocatalytic platform based on Cu-doped TiO2, where atomically dispersed Cu bridged by oxygen (Obr-Cu) forms site-defined [Cu–O] ensembles that serve as the primary centers for sequential C–H activation and H2 evolution. Photogenerated holes localize at Obr-Cu to trigger the first C–H cleavage in C2H6, while the adjacent Cu center promotes β–H elimination with concurrent H2 release. In contrast, β–H activation at Ti-bonded bridging oxygen (Obr-Ti) generates *H species that are kinetically trapped in H2 evolution, leading to Cu reduction and catalyst deactivation. Co-feeding CO2 promotes the removal of *H from these non-cooperative Obr sites, preserving the active [Cu–O] ensembles without disturbing the primary dehydrogenation route. This light-driven, site-defined strategy achieves highly selective C2H4 production with minimal H2 loss, offering an atom- and energy-efficient blueprint for sustainable alkane upgrading.

Results

Photocatalyst preparation and characterizations

Figure 1a illustrates the synthetic route for anchoring single Cu atoms on TiO2 (denoted as Cu1/TiO2) via a previously reported MIL-125(Ti)-templated pre-encapsulation strategy41. Initially, MIL-125 with Ti vacancies (Tiv) was synthesized, exhibiting a smooth disc-like morphology (Fig. 1b and Supplementary Fig. 1a). Upon impregnation with Cu2+, the Tiv sites were occupied to form the Cu/MIL-125 intermediate, and a slight structural collapse occurred due to the polar solvent effect (Fig. 1c). Subsequent calcination of Cu/MIL-125 at 500 °C in air yielded the final Cu1/TiO2 catalyst (Fig. 1d), with the (101) facet of anatase TiO2 exposed (Supplementary Fig. 1b). Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) confirmed the complete removal of organic linkers, as only adsorbed water loss below 200 °C was observed with no additional weight loss up to 800 °C (Supplementary Fig. 2). More detailed synthetic procedures are provided in the Methods. Inductively coupled plasma-optical emission spectrometry determined a Cu loading of 1.4 wt% in Cu1/TiO2. Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns (Fig. 1e) confirm the formation of MOF intermediates and anatase TiO2, while the absence of Cu-related diffraction peaks in both Cu/MIL-125 and Cu1/TiO2 indicates that the MOF-based pre-encapsulation strategy effectively produces highly dispersed Cu species. Energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (EDS) mapping (Fig. 1f) further showed the uniform distribution of Cu. High-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM) image reveals isolated bright spots corresponding to single Cu atoms (Fig. 1g). Atomic intensity profiles (Fig. 1h) from two representative regions show distinct contrast between Ti and Cu, and the equidistant arrangement (ca. 0.35 nm) of Ti and Cu atoms in region 2 provides direct evidence that individual Cu atoms are anchored on TiO2 by substituting Ti sites.

The oxidation states of Cu were analyzed using X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS). As shown in Fig. 2a, a distinct satellite peak confirms the presence of Cu2+, which is further verified by electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 3)42. Deconvolution of the Cu 2p3/2 spectrum reveals two characteristic peaks at binding energy (B.E.) of 933.64 eV (62%) and 932.42 eV (38%). The former is assigned to Cu2+ and the latter to Cu+, as confirmed by the valence-sensitive Cu LMM Auger spectra. Specifically, the Cu LMM spectrum of Cu1/TiO2 exhibits a kinetic energy shift toward the Cu+ position relative to the CuO reference (Fig. 2b). Additionally, a slight increase in Ti3+, oxygen vacancies (VO), and surface hydroxyl groups (–OH) is observed after the introduction of lower-valent Cu (Supplementary Fig. 4).

X-ray absorption near-edge structure (XANES) and extended X-ray absorption fine structure (EXAFS) measurements were performed to elucidate the local coordination environment of Cu sites in Cu1/TiO2. In the Cu K-edge XANES spectrum, the white-line peak intensity of Cu1/TiO2 falls between those of CuO and Cu2O (Fig. 2c), indicating an average Cu oxidation state between +1 and +2, consistent with the XPS results. Fourier-transformed EXAFS spectra in R-space (Fig. 2d) reveal a single peak corresponding to the first-shell Cu–O scattering, with no detectable Cu–Cu or Cu–(O)–Cu contributions. Wavelet transform analysis (Fig. 2e) further confirms this assignment, showing a dominant Cu–O feature at approximately 1.5 Å (R-space) and 4.4 Å⁻¹ (k-space), distinct from the Cu–Cu signature of metallic Cu foil at ~2.1 Å and 7.1 Å⁻¹. Furthermore, the O K-edge XANES spectrum of Cu1/TiO2 retains the characteristic crystal field profile of TiO2 (Fig. 2f), exhibiting resolved absorption features at 529–536 eV and 536–548 eV regions that are assigned to electronic transitions from O 1 s to O 2p–Ti/Cu 3d and O 2p–Ti/Cu 4s4p hybridized orbitals43,44, respectively. Within the 3d–hybridization region, crystal-field splitting yields well-defined t2g and eg peaks, with the t2g/eg ratio serving as a sensitive probe of electron occupancy in 3d orbitals45,46. As evidenced in Supplementary Fig. 5a, b, Cu1/TiO2 exhibits a lower t2g/eg ratio than undoped TiO2, signifying enhanced electron accumulation in t2g orbitals. Considering the contrasting 3d electron configurations of Cu2+ (d9) and Ti4+ (d0) (Supplementary Fig. 5c), this decrease directly evidences Cu–O orbital hybridization. Concurrently, the overall attenuation of absorption intensity of Cu1/TiO2 (Fig. 2f) further confirms the incorporation of Cu into the TiO2 lattice, rather than being surface-dangling, consistent with our HAADF-STEM observations (Fig. 1g). EXAFS fitting was used to further extract the quantitative spatial configuration of Cu atoms (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Table 1). The results indicate an average Cu coordination number of 3.6, suggesting the probable presence of 60% [Cu–O4] and 40% [Cu–O3] coordination units. These ratios correspond well with the concentrations of Cu2+ and Cu+ determined by XPS analysis, respectively. The quantitative agreement between XPS and XAS spectral analyses unambiguously confirms the near 100% accessibility of Cu active sites, representing exceptional atomic utilization efficiency. Taken together, we assign [Cu–O4] as the predominant configuration for Cu2+ species, while [Cu–O3] corresponds to Cu+ sites. To further evaluate the generality of this MOF-based pre-encapsulation strategy for synthesizing single-atom catalysts, we additionally prepared Ag/TiO2 under identical synthetic conditions. HAADF-STEM images of Ag/TiO2 display isolated bright spots that can be assigned to individual Ag atoms, and the corresponding Ag K-edge EXAFS spectra show only Ag–O coordination without any detectable Ag–Ag or Ag–(O)–Ag paths (Supplementary Fig. 6). These results, together with the atomically dispersed Cu centers in Cu1/TiO2, strongly support that the present synthesis method is capable of stabilizing different metals predominantly in single-atom states.

Performance evaluation of PEDH

The PEDH experiments were carried out in a sealed quartz reactor under xenon lamp irradiation without extra heating. Infrared thermal imaging and thermocouple measurements indicated a catalyst surface temperature of approximately 108 °C. (Supplementary Fig. 7). As shown in Fig. 3a, bare TiO2 exhibited negligible activity, whereas metal deposition (Cu, Au, Ag, Pd, and Pt) significantly altered the reaction pathway. Specifically, Au- and Ag-loaded TiO2 surfaces predominantly generated C4H10 via C2H6 coupling, while Pd- and Pt-loaded surfaces produced primarily H2 through deep dehydrogenation, accompanied by carbon deposition (Supplementary Fig. 8). In striking contrast, Cu1/TiO2 demonstrated dramatically enhanced near-stoichiometric production of C2H4 and H2 with 97% C2H4 selectivity, highlighting efficient and controlled selective dehydrogenation to C2H4 at the localized [Cu–O] sites. Switching the feed gas from C2H6 to C2H4/H2 or C2H2/H2 (over-dehydrogenation product) yielded no significant products, further indicating that, under the present conditions, [Cu–O] sites show low intrinsic activity toward side reactions, including alkene hydrogenation and over-dehydrogenation (Supplementary Fig. 9). Furthermore, under a fixed coating area (~3.14 cm2), the mass-specific C2H4 and H2 yields displayed pronounced catalyst dosage dependence (Fig. 3b), arising from limited photon penetration depth that restricts effective light absorption and prevents the lower catalyst layers from being fully utilized. Given that photothermal effects generally exhibit greater penetration depth than photoexcitation effects47,48, light-induced heating was consequently excluded as the primary performance contributor, supported by dark control experiments at the same catalyst surface temperatures (Supplementary Fig. 10a). However, when an ice bath was introduced under constant light intensity to attenuate the photothermal effect, the photocatalytic yield decreased from 8.4 to 3.8 mmol g−1 h−1 while the product selectivity remained unchanged, clearly demonstrating that the light-induced thermal effect (that is, phonon) plays an important synergistic role in the reaction. Moreover, the linear correlation of C2H4 production with light intensity and wavelength-dependent PEDH performance further collectively establish the photon-driven and phonon-assisted mechanism of PEDH over Cu1/TiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 10b, c). Remarkably, this system achieved an exceptionally high C2H4 yield of 21.1 mmol g−1 h−1 (Fig. 3b) with an apparent quantum efficiency (AQY) of 6.1% at 365 nm (Supplementary Fig. 10d and Supplementary Table 2), surpassing state-of-the-art photothermal EDH systems at 650 °C and achieving the highest reported efficiency among non-O2 PEDH systems to date (Fig. 3c and Supplementary Table 3)12,31,32,34–38,49–52.

The operational stability of the catalyst was evaluated by monitoring the time-dependent cumulative C2H4 yield under prolonged reaction conditions. In a C2H6 atmosphere, a nonlinear accumulation of C2H4 over 240 min indicated gradual catalyst deactivation (Fig. 3d), despite the carbon balance remaining close to 1.0. This deactivation correlates with a macroscopic color change of the catalyst from light green to dark blue, as well as a decrease in the Cu2+ signal detected by in situ EPR (Supplementary Fig. 11a). Critically, this deactivation and the change in Cu2+ content could be effectively mitigated by CO2 co-feeding (Fig. 3d), indicating that the structural changes of the Cu active sites are reversible and that Cu2+ ([Cu-O4]) is likely a potential active center. However, the observed color change under reaction conditions cannot be simply attributed to the reduction of Cu2+ via photogenerated electron capture. As a critical control, under an Ar atmosphere, a comparable decrease in the Cu2+ signal is observed without any noticeable macroscopic color change (Supplementary Fig. 11b). This clear contrast indicates that the color evolution observed in reactive atmospheres does not arise solely from changes in the Cu valence state. The origin of these valence-state variations and their relationship to the reversible structural evolution of the Cu sites will be discussed in detail in the following sections. In addition, to clarify that the observed catalyst deactivation is not an artifact arising from the use of a sealed batch reactor, a series of supplementary control experiments were conducted. First, although a slight pressure increase is detected in the sealed system during the reaction, its magnitude consistently remains below 10% of the initial pressure, and its influence on the reaction kinetics can therefore be considered negligible (Supplementary Fig. 12). Second, the stabilizing role of CO2 and the intrinsic durability of the catalyst were further verified under continuous-flow conditions. While pronounced deactivation is observed in a C2H6/Ar atmosphere (Supplementary Fig. 13), the C2H4 production rate is maintained at over 95% of its initial value during 20 h of operation in a C2H6/CO2 atmosphere (Fig. 3e). Meanwhile, HAADF-STEM analysis of the post-reaction catalyst (Supplementary Fig. 14) confirmed the preserved atomic dispersion of Cu species without detectable aggregation. Temperature-programmed surface reaction (TPSR) studies in C2H6/CO2 and C2H6 atmospheres elucidated thermal dehydrogenation pathways. Both systems exhibited identical reaction onset temperatures around 505 °C (Fig. 3f and Supplementary Fig. 15a), consistent with TEDH activity profiles (Supplementary Fig. 15b), indicating that C2H6 activation governs reaction initiation and serves as the rate-determining step. Stability evaluation of Cu1/TiO2 for TEDH at 550 °C revealed low initial activity (ca. 1.2 mmolC2H4 g−1 h−1) with rapid deactivation to 20% of the initial activity within 10 h (Fig. 3g). Notably, compared with the aforementioned PEDH processes under continuous-flow conditions, this TEDH process exhibits higher H2 yields than C2H4 yields alongside substantial carbon deposition (Fig. 3h), signifying the predominance of deep dehydrogenation side reactions. In contrast, the light-driven selectivity of PEDH enables targeted C–H activation while suppressing deep dehydrogenation and over-oxidation, highlighting the intrinsic advantage of photocatalysis for controlled C2H6-to-C2H4 conversion.

Mechanistic investigations

A systematic Arrhenius analysis revealed a significant reduction in apparent activation energies (Ea) for photocatalytic and thermal C2H6 activation. The photocatalytic Ea was 24.94 kJ mol−1, representing a 75% decrease compared to thermal catalysis (98.41 kJ mol−1, Fig. 4a), highlighting the enhanced ability of photocatalysis to facilitate C–H bond cleavage. Active species quenching experiments were conducted to further understand the mechanism of efficient C2H6 activation under mild conditions (Fig. 4b). It should be noted that, to avoid interference arising from the quenchers themselves occupying catalyst surface sites or altering surface properties, two different quenchers were employed for each target species (h+ or e−) for cross-validation. The quenching efficiencies of all scavengers toward photogenerated electrons and holes were confirmed by photoluminescence spectroscopy (Supplementary Fig. 16). Specifically, when ferrocene (C10H10Fe) and sodium oxalate (Na2C2O4) were introduced as hole (h⁺) scavengers, the yields of C2H4 and H2 decreased in an equimolar manner, indicating that h⁺ predominantly participates in the initial step, namely the cleavage of the first C–H bond of C2H6. In contrast, when potassium persulfate (K2S2O8) and potassium dichromate (K2Cr2O7) were introduced as electron (e⁻) scavengers, the C2H4/H2 ratio markedly deviated from the theoretical value of 1.0 and increased to 4.7 and 5.0, respectively, demonstrating that e⁻ plays a crucial role in promoting the kinetics of H2 formation. Meanwhile, quenching e⁻ led to a >90% decrease in the total product yield, which likely implies that the accumulation of *H species on the catalyst surface blocks active sites and ultimately causes catalyst deactivation. To further elucidate the role of Cu sites in the reaction, NH3 was introduced into the system to coordinate with Cu, forming Cu–NH3 complexes and thereby specifically blocking the Cu sites; this blocking effect was verified by NH3–TPD measurements (Supplementary Fig. 17). The results show that the decrease in C2H4 yield is more pronounced than that of H2, and the additional decrease in C2H4 (relative to H2) is stoichiometrically correlated with the newly formed C4H10. This correspondence indicates that, once Cu sites are occupied, the reaction pathway leading to C2H4 is effectively suppressed, and a fraction of the reaction intermediates is diverted toward a C–C coupling pathway to form C4H10. Accordingly, we propose that atomically dispersed Cu sites predominantly mediate β–H activation, thereby driving the dehydrogenation of the *C2H5 intermediate to form C2H4. Once these Cu sites are blocked, the lack of accessible active sites for further dehydrogenation may lead to the accumulation and prolonged residence of *C2H5 species on the catalyst surface, which in turn is more likely to promote C–C coupling pathways and ultimately yield C4H10. Highly sensitive synchrotron-based vacuum ultraviolet-photoionization mass spectrometry (SVUV-PIMS) was employed to detect trace gaseous intermediates, observing signals at m/z = 28.031 and 29.037 under the C2H6 atmosphere (Fig. 4c). Detailed analysis of the corresponding scanning photoionization efficiency (PIE) curve revealed well-defined ionization onsets at 10.40 eV and 8.18 eV (Supplementary Fig. 18), respectively, which unambiguously identified these species as C2H4 (IE = 10.40 eV) and •C2H5 (IE = 8.24 eV) based on their characteristic ionization energy (IE) thresholds53,54, with quantitative analysis indicating that 9.5% of the signal intensity at m/z = 29.037 originated from C2H4 isotopologues.

Further experiments were employed to elucidate the interaction between photogenerated carriers and reactant molecules. First, we compared in situ femtosecond transient absorption spectra (fs-TAS) of the Cu1/TiO2 under different atmospheres using the 365 nm pump laser as the light source (Fig. 4d, e). Under Ar conditions, Cu1/TiO2 presents TiO2-like characteristic negative peaks at ca. 380 nm (Supplementary Fig. 19a), which are identified as the ground-state bleaching (GSB) signals of TiO2 and provide insights into the population of photoelectrons in its conduction band (CB)55,56. Compared to pristine TiO2, Cu1/TiO2 exhibits a weaker GSB signal, indicating fewer electrons at its CB, likely due to Cu sites acting as shallow traps that capture photoexcited e−. By analyzing the recovery kinetics of the GSB signal, two distinct lifetimes were identified. The shorter lifetime (τ1) corresponds to the trapping of photogenerated e− from the CB of TiO2 into Cu sites, while the longer lifetime (τ2) is attributed to the recombination of electrons in the CB with holes in the valence band (VB) of TiO257,58. The prolonged τ2 observed in Cu1/TiO2 compared to pristine TiO2 indicates that electron transfer to Cu sites effectively delays charge recombination with VB holes, thereby extending the charge carrier lifetime (Supplementary Fig. 19b). Transient photocurrent measurements and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) further confirm enhanced charge separation efficiency (Supplementary Fig. 20). Notably, under C2H6 exposure, Cu1/TiO2 exhibits enhanced GSB intensity with accelerated decay kinetics (Fig. 4d, e), indicating ethane’s dual role as electron donor and acceptor. Specifically, adsorbed C2H6 molecules consume photogenerated holes in the VB (i.e., injecting electrons into the VB). This process facilitates the transition from the ground to the excited state and increases the initial electron concentration in the CB, thereby enhancing the GSB signal. Meanwhile, the generated •C2H5 promotes electron consumption by forming H2 through *H species, which arise from Cu-mediated β–H bond activation, further accelerating charge carrier dynamics. Besides, in situ time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy (TRFL) was also conducted under different atmospheres. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 21 and Supplementary Table 4, the average carrier lifetime was extended from 2.09 ns to 6.07 ns after Cu loading, demonstrating effective suppression of carrier recombination. It is particularly noteworthy that upon switching the reaction atmosphere to C2H6, the carrier lifetime dramatically decreased to 4.12 ns. This observation directly verifies that the photogenerated carriers migrating to the catalyst surface undergo rapid redox reactions with C2H6 molecules. Subsequently, the distribution of photogenerated carriers under light irradiation was further investigated using in situ XPS. As shown in Supplementary Fig. 22, the Cu 2p peak is shifted toward lower B.E. while the O 1s peak is shifted toward higher B.E. under light irradiation, indicating that the e− and h+ tend to be localized in the Cu and O sites, respectively. Further theoretical analysis of the partial density of states (PDOS) for Cu1/TiO2 and TiO2 reveals that Cu1/TiO2 generates gap states originating from Cu d-orbitals compared to TiO2, which theoretically confirms that the doped Cu sites act as shallow electron-trapping centers (Fig. 4f). Based on the above mechanistic evidence, we propose the reaction mechanism as follows. The C–H bond activation of C2H6 through photogenerated holes constitutes the critical step for initiating the reaction, generating •C2H5. Subsequently, these •C2H5 undergo dehydrogenation at adjacent Cu sites, releasing C2H4 and adsorbed *H species. The accumulated *H species are then reduced via photogenerated electrons, culminating in the evolution of H2.

Notably, in the aforementioned in situ TAS and TRFL tests, the CO2-induce kinetic decay of charge carriers was negligible versus the accelerated decay driven by C2H6 (Supplementary Figs. 19, 21). Simultaneously, •C2H5 radical signals showed minimal perturbations under CO2 co-feeding (Fig. 4c), collectively confirming that C2H6 non-oxidative dehydrogenation remains dominant in the C2H6/CO2 mixture. To elucidate the role of CO2 in stabilizing Cu1/TiO2 described above, product distributions before and after CO2 introduction were analyzed. As shown in Fig. 4g, trace CO formation was detected only under C2H6/CO2 co-feeding, but was absent in the CO2-free atmosphere. Control experiments with H2/CO2 co-feeding also yielded no detectable CO, indicating that the catalyst is unable to effectively activate molecular H2 under the present conditions and thus cannot initiate a redox-mediated reverse water–gas shift (RWGS) pathway. These findings therefore rule out RWGS as the source of CO formation in the C2H6/CO2 co-feeding system. Furthermore, C18O2 isotope labeling experiments confirmed that the oxygen in CO originates from CO2 rather than from lattice oxygen (Fig. 4h). To further elucidate the CO formation pathway, we set a baseline feed of C2H6: CO2 = 1:1 (20 mL each) and systematically varied the ratio by increasing either reactant. CO production showed a strong positive correlation with C2H6 input (mirroring C2H4 yield), but remained insensitive to CO2 concentration (Fig. 4i). These findings further support that CO yield is indirectly driven by C2H6 conversion.

Subsequently, in situ near-ambient pressure XPS spectroscopy (NAP-XPS) was employed to monitor the chemical state evolution of Cu1/TiO2 under C2H6 and C2H6/CO2 atmospheres, and to elucidate the role of CO2. The results revealed that the Cu2+ content decreased significantly under pure C2H6 but was notably suppressed when CO2 was co-fed (Fig. 5a, b), corroborating prior in situ EPR observations (Supplementary Fig. 11) and further implying that Cu2+ ([Cu-O4]) species are closely associated with the catalytically active centers. However, it should be particularly noted that in a C2H6 atmosphere, the transformation of Cu2+ to Cu⁺ is accompanied by an increase in –OH content. In contrast, under CO2 co-feeding conditions, –OH is significantly consumed. Accordingly, we speculate that under C2H6 atmosphere, the deactivation of the catalyst may be attributed to the coverage of active sites by undissociated *H on lattice oxygen, which leads to the transformation of Cu2+ to Cu+. Besides, quasi-in situ Raman spectroscopy with stepwise feeding of C2H6 and CO2 revealed that the Eg mode (~144 cm−1) gradually blueshifts with prolonged reaction time in C2H6 atmosphere (Supplementary Fig. 23), indicating enhanced TiO2 unit cell expansion or strain42. Notably, this blueshift disappears upon switching the atmosphere to CO2, further confirming that CO2 can reverse the microstructural changes induced in Cu1/TiO2 under pure C2H6 conditions.

Density functional theory (DFT) calculations on the electronic structure of the Cu1/TiO2 further theoretically revealed the impact of *H adsorption on the Cu valence state. Specifically, adsorption of *H species on lattice oxygen increases the Cu–O bond length from 1.95 Å to 2.26 Å (Supplementary Fig. 24). The charge difference distribution (Supplementary Fig. 25) showed a strong interaction between *H adsorbed on lattice oxygen and the support, with electrons transferring from *H to the Cu site. Bader charge calculations further confirmed that *H coverage reduces the charge at Cu sites (Supplementary Fig. 24 and Supplementary Table 5). In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS) was employed to identify the key reaction intermediates on Cu1/TiO2 under C2H6 and C2H6/CO2 atmospheres. Before the DRIFTS measurements, C2H6 temperature-programmed desorption (TPD) experiments were carried out to assess the adsorption behavior. The TPD profiles revealed a markedly enhanced C2H6 adsorption on Cu1/TiO2 compared to bare TiO2 (Supplementary Fig. 26), confirming [Cu–O] ensembles dominate C2H6 adsorption. Under illumination in C2H6 (Supplementary Fig. 27a), the immediate decay of C2H6 adsorption peaks, accompanied by the simultaneous appearance of *CH2CH3 species and gaseous C2H4, provided experimental evidence for the stepwise dehydrogenation of C2H6 to C2H4. Concomitantly, the observed generation of –OH indicated *H accumulation on the catalyst surface (Supplementary Fig. 27b). In contrast, CO2 co-feeding led to the formation of *COOH intermediates (Supplementary Fig. 27c) and effective suppression of –OH generation (Supplementary Fig. 27d). These results indicate that CO2 promotes the removal of surface-adsorbed *H species, thereby regenerating the catalytic active sites and concurrently producing CO. This mechanistic insight further corroborates the intrinsic correlation between the CO formation rate and the ethane conversion behavior.

To further validate this mechanism, DFT simulations under the excited/ground-state were employed based on the potential active center model of [Cu–O4]. First, two potential surface bridging oxygen (Obr) hole-trapping sites were investigated, which are bonded to Cu and Ti sites respectively and denoted as Obr-Cu and Obr-Ti sites. As revealed in Supplementary Figs. 28, 29, the hole trapping energies of Obr-Cu and Obr-Ti relative to bulk self-trapped hole were -1.23 and 0.08 eV, respectively, confirming effective localization of photogenerated holes at the surface Obr-Cu site. Although Cu was previously confirmed as a shallow electron-trapping center, in subsequent simulations of Obr–Cu-initiated C–H bond activation we placed the photogenerated e− on a remote Ti site to avoid high-energy configurations arising from adjacent electron–hole localization, thereby better reflecting the realistic reaction environment. Critically, Fig. 5c demonstrates that upon hole capture at the Obr-Cu site, the C–H bond of C2H6 undergoes facile cleavage via the hydrogen atom transfer (HAT) mechanism27, with a notably low barrier of 0.46 eV and a negative free energy change (ΔG) of -0.37 eV. This barrier and ΔG are 0.76 eV and 1.16 eV lower than those for ground-state C–H activation at the same Obr-Cu site, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 30), thus serving as a prerequisite for high PEDH activity under mild conditions. This process generates a free •C2H5 radical and an adsorbed *H at the Obr-Cu site. The •C2H5 radical then undergoes two successive mild endergonic steps to transfer a *H to the Cu site via the HAT mechanism, involving adsorption at Cu site (0.29 eV) followed by β–H cleavage with a reaction energy of only 0.11 eV. In contrast, although the C–H activation ability of Obr-Ti site is also significantly enhanced after trapping photogenerated holes, with the corresponding barriers being comparable to that at Obr–Cu site, the resulting •C2H5 radical exhibits an adsorption energy of 0.58 eV at the five-coordinated Ti site (Ti5c), and the subsequent β–H bond cleavage has a reaction energy as high as 1.60 eV (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 31), both of which are significantly larger than those on the Cu site. This demonstrates that the introduction of Cu not only markedly promotes the efficient localization of photogenerated holes from the bulk to surface Obr–Cu sites, but also facilitates the •C2H5 adsorption and β–H activation capabilities. This synergistic advantage in both hole management and β–H activation underpins the high ethane activation capability and excellent ethylene selectivity of the Cu1/TiO2 catalyst. In addition, the high reaction energy for β–H cleavage at the Cu+ site in [Cu–O3] (1.06 eV) further confirms its limited activity, highlighting [Cu–O4] as the dominant active center (Supplementary Fig. 32). Finally, we calculated the generation of H2 at the [Cu–O] ensembles assisted by photogenerated e− transferred from nearby Ti sites to Cu, where *H–*H coupling energy barrier is 0.79 eV. Notably, •C2H5 is also possible to continue to activate the β–H bond via hole-trapped Obr-Ti with an energy barrier of 0.07 eV (Supplementary Fig. 33), following the first C–H bond cleavage mechanism. The resulting *H is captured by the Obr-Ti adjacent to [Cu–O] ensembles. Consequently, the H2 formation is significantly hindered at the Obr-Ti and Obr-Cu sites (Fig. 5c and Supplementary Fig. 34), with an energy barrier as high as 2.79 eV. We also considered the possibility of *H migrating from Obr-Ti or Obr-Cu to Cu sites for *H–*H coupling, yet these processes remain challenging. For migration from the Obr-Ti to the Cu site, *H could first relocate to the O3c site (three-fold coordinated lattice oxygen) with an energy barrier of 1.48 eV, then proceeds from O3c to the Cu site with a barrier of 3.12 eV (Supplementary Fig. 35), while direct migration from Obr-Cu to the Cu site requires overcoming a 2.92 eV barrier (Supplementary Fig. 36). This indicates that consecutive dehydrogenation on two lattice oxygens leads to difficult desorption of *H, ultimately leading to deactivation of the catalyst due to *H coverage on the [Cu–O4] active sites. Further theoretical calculations (Supplementary Fig. 37) reveal that these two *H species can be transformed into H2O via a low energy barrier of 0.40 eV, accompanied by a structural reconstruction from [Cu–O4] to [Cu–O3]. However, this process is still endergonic from a thermodynamic perspective, and the *H species preferentially remain adsorbed on the catalyst surface. Notably, in the presence of CO2, CO2 can cooperatively interact with •C2H5 to form a *COOH intermediate, which promotes the further transformation of the [Cu–O3] species generated during the dehydration process. This, in turn, drives the continuous consumption of the accumulated *H while simultaneously regenerating the [Cu–O4] sites.

Based on the foregoing findings, a light-driven EDH mechanism is delineated in Fig. 5d. Atomically dispersed Cu promotes the localization of photogenerated holes at directly bonded Obr-Cu sites, facilitating initial C–H activation in C2H6 with the significantly reduced energy barriers. The resulting •C2H5 species binds to Cu, where β–H elimination proceeds, and the *H generated is subsequently released as H2 through the cooperative action of site-defined [Cu–O] ensembles. This sequential dehydrogenation coupled with efficient H2 evolution accounts for the high activity and selectivity toward C2H4. In contrast, β–H activation at Obr–Ti sites adjacent to (but not coordinated with) Cu lacks Cu-mediated *H–*H coupling, preventing *H recombination and leading to *H accumulation that poisons active sites. Upon introducing CO2, a self-cleaning cycle is established that promotes further transformation of surface *H, thereby removing the *H adlayer and regenerating active sites.

Discussion

In summary, we have demonstrated a site-defined photocatalytic strategy for the selective dehydrogenation of C2H6 to C2H4 under mild conditions, enabled by atomically dispersed Cu species anchored on TiO2. The [Cu–O] ensembles act cooperatively to mediate stepwise C–H activation, with bridging oxygen (Obr) capturing photogenerated holes to initiate C2H6 activation and adjacent Cu centers promoting β–H abstraction and H2 evolution. Comprehensive mechanistic investigations, including quenching experiments, in situ spectroscopy, and excited-state DFT calculations, uncover that confining dehydrogenation to [Cu–O] ensembles suppresses overoxidation, minimizes *H accumulation, and sustains long-term activity. Co-feeding CO2 facilitates the removal of *H from Obr sites, preserving active-site integrity. This work provides atomic-level insight into photocatalytic alkane upgrading and establishes a generalizable approach for designing redox-cooperative single-atom photocatalysts with enhanced selectivity and durability.

Methods

Preparation of catalysts

Synthesis of MIL-125(Tiv)

The MIL-125 with Ti vacancies (Tiv) precursor was synthesized according to the method described in previous literature41. First, 3.0 g of terephthalic acid (99%, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) was dissolved in 54 mL of N, N-dimethylformamide (DMF, 99.9%, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) with 30 min magnetic stirring at room temperature. Subsequently, 6 mL of methanol (99.9%, Shanghai Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) was added and stirred for 5 min, followed by the addition of 1.2 mL of tetrabutyl titanate (TBOT, 99%, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) with additional 5 min stirring. The mixture was transferred to a sealed round-bottom flask and reacted at 130 °C for 20 h under Ar atmosphere using an oil bath. After cooling, the precipitate was collected by centrifugation, successively washed twice with DMF and twice with methanol, and vacuum-dried at 80 °C for 10 h to remove residual solvents, yielding MIL-125(Tiv).

Synthesis of Cu1/TiO2

150 mg of MIL-125(Tiv) was dispersed in 50 mL of water/methanol solution (1:4 v/v). Under continuous stirring, a predetermined aliquot of 0.1 M CuCl2 (99.99%, Shanghai Aladdin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd.) aqueous solution was added dropwise, followed by stirring for 10 h. The resulting mixture was centrifuged and washed thrice with water/methanol solution. After oven-drying at 80 °C, the solid was calcined in a muffle furnace at 500 °C for 4 h with a heating rate of 2 °C/min to obtain Cu1/TiO2.

Evaluation of catalytic performance

Batch-type performance test

First, the catalyst was uniformly dispersed in 1.0 mL deionized water and coated onto a glass slide (coating area: 3.14 cm2), followed by drying at 80 °C. The catalyst-coated glass slide was placed at the center of the bottom of a sealed 100 mL quartz reactor. To ensure complete replacement of residual air by ethane, the reactor was subjected to three evacuation/ethane-flushing cycles (1 atm, 99.9% C2H6 per cycle) before performance test. A 300 W full-spectrum (250–1100 nm) Xe lamp (PLS-SXE300 + ) was used for vertical irradiation (4 h per reaction) without additional external heating, and the light intensity was monitored using a PL-MW2000 optical power meter. Pressure changes (defined as the difference between the instantaneous pressure and the initial pressure) during the reaction were continuously recorded with an AS511A digital differential manometer (SMART SENSOR). The reaction products were analyzed using a Shimadzu gas chromatograph (GC-2014C) equipped with a thermal conductivity detector (TCD) and a flame ionization detector (FID). For the C18O2 isotope-labeled experiment, the gas-phase products were detected by a 7890A-5975C gas chromatography-mass spectrometer (Agilent Technologies Co., Ltd.).

The catalyst surface temperature was simultaneously monitored using a Uti160S infrared thermal imager (UNI-T) and a calibrated K-type thermocouple (diameter 0.25 mm, KAIPUSEN). The thermocouple probe maintained direct contact with the center of the catalyst bed, while the thermal imager was installed at an oblique angle above the reactor, oriented toward the top surface of the catalyst bed to capture thermal image data. The emissivity was established as ε = 0.95 based on an empirical calibration procedure performed with catalyst powder distributed uniformly on a pre-calibrated heating plate. During calibration, the heating plate temperature was systematically varied (50, 100, 150, and 200 °C) and the imager’s emissivity setting was adjusted until its temperature readings concurred with the reference values at each point, thereby determining the optimal emissivity value for all subsequent experimental measurements.

Flow-type performance test

The performance tests of flow-type PEDH and TEDH were both evaluated in a PLS-LED 100 photothermal reactor (Beijing Perfectlight) equipped with external temperature control, where the catalyst-bed temperature was monitored by a K-type thermocouple inserted into the center of the catalyst layer. For each test, 10 mg of catalyst was uniformly coated onto a heat-resistant quartz fiber filter membrane (fixed coating area: 3.14 cm2), and the flow rate of the feed gas (20 vol% C2H6/20 vol% CO2/Ar or 20 vol% C2H6/Ar) was set as 10 mL min−1. The apparent activation energy measurements were carried out in the same reactor, in which a UV light source with fixed intensity was employed to mitigate photothermal effects, and a relatively low temperature window of 60–140 °C was selected to ensure that the reaction proceeded in the kinetic regime and to minimize interference from internal and external mass transfer and thermal effects.

Characterization

The morphological characteristics of the samples were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM, Helios G4 UC), transmission electron microscopy (TEM, Talos F200X), and aberration-corrected high-angle annular dark-field scanning transmission electron microscopy (HAADF-STEM, Themis Z). Powder X-ray diffraction (XRD) patterns were acquired using a Rigaku D/MAX 2550 diffractometer with Cu Kα radiation (λ = 1.5406 Å), scanning the 2θ range from 5° to 80° at a rate of 8° min−1. X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) analysis was performed using a ESCALAB 250Xi system (Thermo Fisher Scientific) with monochromatic Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV), and the binding energies were referenced and corrected using the C 1s peak at 284.8 eV. Inductively coupled plasma optical emission spectrometry (ICP-OES) measurements were conducted on an Agilent 5800 VDV system. Thermogravimetric/differential thermal analysis (TG/DTA) was conducted using a Netzsch TGA209F1 instrument, with the temperature ranging from 50 to 800 °C. Raman spectroscopy measurements were performed using a Renishaw inVia Raman system, with a 532 nm laser serving as the excitation source. UV-Vis diffuse reflectance spectra of the dry-pressed disk samples were recorded on a Cary 500 spectrophotometer (Varian) with an integrating sphere, using BaSO4 as a reflectance reference standard. Electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) measurements were performed using a 100G-18KG/EMX-8/2.7 instrument. Transient photocurrent response and electrochemical impedance spectroscopy (EIS) measurements were conducted using a ZAHNER PP211 electrochemical workstation with a standard three-electrode configuration. The working electrode consisted of fluorine-doped tin oxide (FTO) glass coated with the sample (5 mg, coating area: 1 cm2), with a platinum counter electrode and a saturated calomel electrode as a reference electrode. Transient photocurrent tests were performed in 0.5 M aqueous Na2SO4 solution, while EIS measurements were carried out in an electrolyte system containing potassium ferricyanide. Ammonia temperature-programmed desorption (NH3-TPD) measurements were performed on a Micromeritics AutoChem II 2920 chemisorption analyzer. Following saturation with 10% NH3/He gas mixture at 50 °C, the sample was heated from 50 to 800 °C at a rate of 10 °C min−1 under a He flow, while the desorbed gases were monitored via a thermal conductivity detector (TCD). C2H6-TPD measurements followed an identical procedure to that of NH3-TPD, with catalyst saturation achieved via pulsed injection of pure C2H6 gas into the system. Temperature-programmed surface reaction (TPSR) experiments were performed on a TP5080 automatic multi-purpose adsorption analyzer, and the desorbed products were monitored in situ using a Hiden Analytical DECAR mass spectrometer during linear heating from 50 to 800 °C at 10 °C min−1. For C2H6-TPSR, the feed gas consisted of 20% C2H6 balanced with Ar; for C2H6/CO2-TPSR, the feed gas was composed of 20 vol% C2H6/20 vol% CO2 balanced with Ar.

XAFS measurement

Cu K-edge X-ray absorption spectroscopy (XAS) was collected in fluorescence mode at the XAFCA beamline of the Singapore Synchrotron Light Source (SSLS). Measurements employed a double-crystal Si (111) monochromator with energy calibration referenced to a Cu foil. O K-edge XAFS measurements were performed at the soft X-ray beamline of the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory (NSRL) (Hefei, China). The XAS data were processed using the Demeter software package (ATHENA and ARTEMIS)59.

In situ experiments

In situ synchrotron-based vacuum ultraviolet-photoionization mass spectrometry (SVUV-PIMS)

In situ SVUV-PIMS measurements were conducted at the Atomic and Molecular Physics beamline (BL09U) in the National Synchrotron Radiation Laboratory (NSRL) (Hefei, China)60. The catalyst was positioned at the center of a laminar flow reactor cell. Reaction products were sampled via molecular beam into the ionization chamber, ionized by synchrotron VUV light, and detected by a time-of-flight mass spectrometer. The experiment began by purging the reaction cell’s differential chamber (pressure stabilized at 20.0 Pa) with 50 vol% C2H6/Ar at a flow rate of 20 mL min−1 for 30 min to remove air. Illumination was provided by a 300 W xenon lamp (without external heating). Following 30 min of illumination, mass spectra were acquired at 10.5 eV with an integration time of 1000 s. Product photoionization efficiency (PIE) curves were scanned from 8.0 to 11.5 eV with an integration time of 240 s per point. The same procedure was repeated for data collection after switching the feed gas to 50 vol% C2H6/CO2 mixture.

In situ femtosecond transient absorption spectroscopy (fs-TAS)

In situ fs-TAS measurements were performed using a Helios spectrometer (Ultrafast system) under pure Ar, 50 vol% C2H6/Ar, or 50 vol% C2H6/50 vol%CO2. The amplified femtosecond Ti: sapphire laser system operated at a repetition rate of 1 kHz, and the probe light was collected via the lens of an InGaAs diode array.

In-situ time-resolved fluorescence spectroscopy (TRFL)

In situ TRFL spectra were acquired on an Edinburgh Instruments FLS 980 spectrometer. Samples were sealed in 1 mm-pathlength quartz microcuvettes and measured under pure Ar, C2H6, or CO2 atmosphere. Excitation was provided by a 360 nm light-emitting diode (LED) source.

In situ NAP-XPS

In situ NAP-XPS was performed on a Thermo Scientific ESCALAB 250Xi system with Al Kα radiation (hν = 1486.6 eV). After Ar⁺ sputtering (3 keV, 40 eV pass energy, 0.1 eV step, 1 min), XPS data under vacuum were first collected, after which 50 vol% C2H6/Ar or 50 vol% C2H6/50 vol% CO2 were continuously introduced. Spectra were recorded sequentially under dark conditions, as well as 20 and 40 min after illumination with a 300 W xenon lamp. NAP-XPS spectra were averaged over 5 scans with charge referencing to C 1s (284.80 eV).

In situ diffuse reflectance infrared Fourier transform spectroscopy (DRIFTS)

In situ DRIFTS measurements were performed on a Thermo Fisher-Nicolet iS20 infrared spectrometer equipped with a liquid nitrogen-cooled mercury-cadmium-telluride (MCT) detector, using a three-window diffuse-reflectance cell (Harrick). Before the test, samples were dehydrated at 120 °C for 1 h. Subsequently, 0.1 g of the dried catalyst was loaded into the sample cell and purged with Ar (99.9 vol%) for 30 min to eliminate surface-adsorbed species. Following Ar purging, a background spectrum was collected under an Ar atmosphere. Ar was then flushed out, and a continuous flow of reactant gas (either 20 vol% C2H6/Ar or 20 vol% C2H6/20 vol% CO2/Ar) was introduced at a flow rate of 20 mL min−1. Spectra were collected at specified time intervals after initiating light irradiation. The spectral resolution was 4 cm−1, and the scanning range was 4000–600 cm−1.

Theoretical calculations

All spin-polarized calculations were performed with the Perdew-Burke-Ernzerhof (PBE) functional within the generalized gradient approximation using the VASP 6.3.2 code61,62. The valence electronic states were expanded in plane-wave basis sets with an energy cutoff at 450 eV, and the occupancy of the one-electron states was calculated using the Gaussian smearing with SIGMA = 0.05 eV. The on-site Hubbard U term (PBE + U) was added for O2p orbitals and Ti3d orbitals with a value of 4.0 eV and 4.2 eV to facilitate the localization of trapped holes and electrons. In particular, the U correction on the O 2p orbitals helps to cancel self-interaction errors and enables a more realistic localization of photogenerated holes on surface O sites. In addition, to simplify the treatment of the photoexcited system by excluding the uncertain electron–hole interactions that depend on the specific trapping center, while simultaneously preserving the charge neutrality of the periodic slab and avoiding artificial background-charge corrections, an -OH group was introduced on the bottom surface as an electron acceptor63. To validate the accuracy of the DFT + U method64, complementary HSE06 hybrid functional calculations were systematically performed for all reaction steps, with corresponding energy values exclusively derived from the HSE0627. Structure optimizations were performed using the Quasi-Newton Broyden minimization scheme until the atomic forces on each ion were less than 0.05 eV/Å.

The transition states (TSs) were searched using a constrained optimization scheme65. In this approach, we keep the C–H bond distance fixed at preselected values, and minimize the total energy with respect to all remaining orthogonal degrees of freedom. In practice, this allows the adsorbed molecules to rotate and translate under the imposed constraint, and the TiO2 surface is fully relaxed. A transition state is identified when the forces on all atoms vanish and the total energy exhibits a maximum along the chosen reaction coordinate while remaining a minimum with respect to all other degrees of freedom.
