Comparison of mixed reality and computed tomography-guided preoperative hook-wire localization for pulmonary nodules in video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery: a noninferiority randomized clinical trial
Authors: Wenzhe Qi, Juan Zhou, Ning Xin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Preoperative localization of pulmonary nodules is critical for video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS). Traditional Computed Tomography (CT)-guided localization has limitations, including radiation exposure and procedural complexity. Mixed Reality (MR)-guided localization offers a promising alternative, potentially improving accuracy, efficiency, and safety. To compare the accuracy, efficiency, and safety of MR-guided versus CT-guided preoperative localization for pulmonary nodules in VATS through a noninferiority randomized trial. This prospective, single-center, noninferiority randomized clinical trial enrolled 90 patients with small pulmonary nodules. Patients were randomized to either MR-guided or CT-guided localization groups. The primary outcome was localization accuracy, measured by deviation from the target nodule. Secondary outcomes included procedural duration, insertion attempts, radiation exposure, complications, and surgical outcomes. Intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses were both performed. The MR-guided group demonstrated significantly higher localization accuracy, with smaller total deviation (5.50 mm vs. 6.84 mm, p = 0.04) and vertical deviation (p = 0.04). Procedural duration (3.98 min vs. 10.73 min, p < 0.001) and insertion attempts (1.05 vs. 2.28, p < 0.001) were significantly reduced in the MR-guided group. Radiation exposure was also lower, with fewer CT scans and reduced dose length product and effective dose (p < 0.001). Complication rates, including pneumothorax and hemorrhage, were slightly lower in the MR-guided group but not statistically significant. Surgical outcomes were comparable between groups. MR-guided localization met the noninferiority criteria to CT-guided localization in terms of localization accuracy and overall safety, and showed exploratory clinical benefits with notably improved procedural efficiency and reduced radiation exposure. These findings support its potential as a safe and effective alternative for preoperative pulmonary nodule localization in thoracic surgery.

Introduction

Accurate preoperative hook-wire localization of small pulmonary nodules is a critical and well-established preparatory procedure for the success of video-assisted thoracoscopic surgery (VATS)1. Pulmonary nodules (≤ 2 cm), commonly detected via high resolution computed tomography (HRCT), present significant clinical challenges due to their small size, peripheral anatomical location, and frequently ambiguous radiological features such as ground glass opacities (GGOs)2,3. In current clinical practice, CT-guided preoperative hook-wire localization is the gold standard technique for this procedure4,5, yet it has notable limitations: repeated ionizing radiation exposure for patients and operators, considerable patient discomfort during repeated puncture attempts, and a relatively high risk of acute complications including pneumothorax and parenchymal hemorrhage4,6. Additionally, this CT-guided approach often requires multiple puncture attempts to achieve accurate nodule targeting, leading to prolonged procedural duration, increased patient anxiety, and potential delays in subsequent surgical workflows. Thus, there is an urgent clinical need for an innovative preoperative hook-wire localization technique that can address these limitations while maintaining high accuracy and safety.

Mixed Reality (MR) technology has recently emerged as a promising alternative for preoperative hook-wire localization of pulmonary nodules in VATS7. Unlike traditional 2D CT-based guidance, MR integrates augmented reality (AR) and virtual reality (VR) to construct a real-time hybrid visual environment, where preoperatively reconstructed 3D anatomical models are overlaid on the patient’s actual torso8. This technology allows clinicians to visualize the spatial position of pulmonary nodules relative to surrounding thoracic structures intuitively, facilitating precise planning and execution of the hook-wire puncture path without the need for repeated intraprocedural CT scans, thereby reducing radiation exposure9. Previous preclinical and pilot studies have suggested that MR-based guidance provides more interactive and spatial anatomical guidance than conventional imaging methods, with potential to improve the efficiency of image-guided percutaneous procedures in thoracic surgery8.

In the context of preoperative hook-wire localization for VATS, the MR system used in this study leverages advanced infrared motion capture and surface marker registration to achieve precise real-time alignment between preoperative CT-derived 3D models (of the lung, nodule, and chest wall) and the patient’s physical anatomy. Preoperative AI-segmented 3D reconstructions of the target nodule and adjacent critical structures (e.g., blood vessels and bronchi) are registered to the patient’s torso via non-collinear surface locators, enabling clinicians to “see through” the chest wall soft tissues and plan the optimal percutaneous hook-wire puncture path with submillimeter registration accuracy. Importantly, this MR-guided approach is designed exclusively for preoperative localization (prior to lung deflation for VATS), and does not involve intraoperative navigation or localization.

Notably, intraoperative pulmonary nodule localization for VATS presents a distinct and greater clinical challenge compared to preoperative localization, primarily due to lung deflation, thoracic cavity shift, and altered anatomical relationships after pneumothorax induction—factors that are not relevant to preoperative percutaneous localization. While our MR system has potential for intraoperative application, the present study focuses exclusively on preoperative hook-wire localization (performed in the radiology suite with the lung in a naturally inflated state). Our previous animal study has demonstrated that MR-guided preoperative pulmonary nodule localization achieves accuracy comparable to CT-guided localization10, but the clinical efficacy, safety, and procedural efficiency of this technique in human subjects remain unvalidated. Therefore, the primary aim of this prospective, single-center, noninferiority randomized clinical trial is to compare the accuracy, procedural efficiency, and safety of MR-guided preoperative hook-wire localization with the gold standard CT-guided preoperative hook-wire localization for small pulmonary nodules in patients undergoing VATS. We hypothesize that MR-guided localization is noninferior to CT-guided localization in terms of primary localization accuracy, while offering additional clinical benefits including reduced radiation exposure, shorter procedural duration, and fewer puncture attempts.

Methods

Study design

This prospective, single-center, noninferiority, randomized clinical trial was conducted to compare the efficacy of MR-guided localization with CT-guided localization for pulmonary nodules in patients undergoing VATS. Our study adhered to the Consolidated Standards of Reporting Trials (CONSORT) guidelines for randomized trials and received approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of the 960th Hospital of the People’s Liberation Army (Approval No. 2023026). A completed CONSORT 2010 checklist for noninferiority randomized controlled trials is provided as supplementary material to this manuscript. Additionally, the clinical trial was prospectively registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR), Shanghai, China (Registration No. ChiCTR2100048313, registered on 05 July 2021). The trial was conducted in compliance with Good Clinical Practice (GCP) guidelines and the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki11.

Sample size

According to the principles of noninferiority test design, the sample size calculation was based on a one-sided significance test, with a significance level of 0.025 (α = 0.025) and a power of 0.90 (β = 0.90). Based on our previous animal experiment results10 and published clinical studies on pulmonary nodule localization12,13, a noninferiority margin of 3 mm was set, which is clinically meaningful and represents an acceptable deviation for safe preoperative hook-wire localization in VATS., and the required sample size (n1 = n2 = 36) was calculated using PASS 15.0. Assuming a 10% dropout rate, the actual sample size was adjusted to N1 = N2 = 40. Ultimately, from January 2022 to June 2023, 90 patients were enrolled, and after baseline evaluations, they were randomly assigned to one of the two localization methods. All participants were informed of the potential risks of participation, and written informed consent was obtained before enrollment in the trial.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

Patients aged 18–80 years with a clinically suspected pulmonary nodule (≤ 2 cm) identified on HRCT scans were eligible for inclusion. They were required to be scheduled for VATS resection of the pulmonary nodule for either diagnostic or therapeutic purposes. Exclusion criteria included target lung nodules shielded by the scapula, which limited access for percutaneous localization, and the presence of two or more lung nodules requiring localization. Instruments and equipment

A multi-row spiral CT scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) was used for CT scanning. A hook-wire (Zhejiang Curaway Medical Technology Co., Ltd., Zhejiang, China; Fig. 2A) was used for localization. OptiTrack (a motion capture device; Shenzhen Luster Technology Co., Ltd., Shenzhen, China; Fig. 2B) was used to capture the location information of locators within the environment. The locator, with a surface covered by a reflective layer recognizable by OptiTrack (Fig. 2C), was used for virtual and real-world positioning. A computer with Motive software installed (Beijing Virtual Point Technology Co., Ltd., China) processed the data. HoloLens (Microsoft Corp., Redmond, WA, USA; Fig. 2D) was employed to present MR effects.

Intervention

MR-guided group

The MR-guided localization method was performed as previously reported by us10. Spatial registration was achieved through a three-step process: (1) Three non-collinear surface locators with reflective markers were affixed to the patient’s chest wall; (2) The OptiTrack motion capture system (120 Hz sampling rate) tracked the 3D coordinates of these physical locators in real time via infrared motion detection; (3) The virtual 3D models of the locators, lung, and nodule (reconstructed from preoperative CT using Mimics 20.0) were dynamically aligned to their physical counterparts by matching the OptiTrack-captured coordinates with the virtual locator positions, maintaining submillimeter registration accuracy throughout the procedure. Researcher placed three MR locators on the patient’s body surface, ensuring they were fully visible within OptiTrack’s field of vision and not aligned in a straight line. A CT scan was then performed to acquire dicom imaging data. Researcher reconstructed 3D models of the chest wall, pulmonary nodules, and the three locators. Simultaneously, the puncture path was designed, and its depth was measured using Mimics 20.0 (Materialise, Plymouth, MI, USA) (Fig. 3A). Finally, the data were imported into HoloLens for visualization. Researcher used HoloLens to confirm the alignment of the MR image with the patient (Fig. 3B). After routine disinfection and draping, local anesthesia with 2% lidocaine was administered to the patient. The hook wire was inserted along the MR-guided puncture path without penetrating the pleura. A CT scan was then performed to verify whether the hook wire’s extension line was aimed at the nodule (Fig. 3C). Based on prior experience, deviations of ≤ 2 cm between the localizer and the target nodule’s center were deemed sufficiently accurate for safe localization. If the deviation exceeded this threshold, the CT-guided method was used instead. If accurate, the hook wire was inserted along the predefined puncture path to the measured depth, then released and secured. Subsequently, a CT scan was performed to acquire dicom data for verification (Fig. 3D).

CT-guided group

The CT-guided localization method was performed as previously reported12.After positioning the patients on the CT table, an initial CT scan was performed to image the area of interest. The hook wire insertion site was identified using CT gantry laser lights and a skin-mounted metal marker. The insertion length and angle were estimated from the initial CT images, and the hook wire was subsequently inserted without penetrating the pleura. Repeated CT scans were performed to confirm or adjust the hook wire position to ensure accurate nodule localization. Based on prior experience, deviations of ≤ 2 cm between the localizer and the center of the target nodule were deemed sufficiently accurate for safe localization. Once the relative position of the hook wire and target nodule was verified as acceptable, the hook wire was deployed into the lung parenchyma, then released and secured.

Primary outcome

The primary outcome of our study was localization accuracy, defined as the deviation between the hook-wire and the nearest point on the edge of the target nodule. The deviation was calculated using the final CT scan acquired during nodule localization. To better analyze the direction of MR-guided localization deviations, the total deviation was divided into horizontal and vertical components. Horizontal deviation refers to the difference between the two at the axial cross-sectional level. Secondary outcome

Secondary outcomes included the number of insertion attempts, procedural duration, complications (e.g., pneumothorax, hemorrhage, and localizer dislodgement), the number of required CT scans, radiation exposure (assessed by Dose Length Product [DLP] and Effective Dose [ED]), and surgical outcomes, including length of stay (LOS), blood loss, and surgical type (wedge resection, segmentectomy, or lobectomy).

Randomization and blinding

Patients were randomly assigned to either the MR-guided or CT-guided group through a computer-generated randomization sequence (created using SPSS version 23.0, IBM Corp.) with a 1:1 allocation ratio. The randomization was not stratified, and block randomization with a block size of 10 was used to ensure balanced group allocation. Randomization was conducted in a 1:1 ratio, allocation concealment was ensured through the use of sequentially numbered, opaque, sealed envelopes. The envelopes were prepared by an independent research assistant who was not involved in patient enrollment or subsequent clinical assessments, and were opened only after the eligible patient had provided written informed consent and completed baseline evaluations. The two radiologists responsible for measuring deviations on the final CT scan were fully blinded to group allocation throughout the study. Randomization occurred after informed consent was obtained from patients and prior to the localization procedure to minimize bias. Due to the impracticality of masking, the study was unblinded after randomization.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline characteristics and outcomes for each group. Continuous variables were presented as means±standard deviations (SD), while categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages. Comparisons between the two groups were made using independent t-tests for continuous variables and chi-square tests for categorical variables. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Both intention-to-treat (ITT) and per-protocol (PP) analyses were performed to evaluate the primary and secondary outcomes. Statistical analysis was conducted using SPSS version 23.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA).

Results

A total of 90 patients were enrolled and randomized to the MR-guided group (n = 45) and CT-guided group (n = 45) from January 2022 to June 2023. Two patients in the MR-guided group were excluded from the per-protocol (PP) analysis because they required conversion to CT-guided localization due to unauthorized positional changes during the procedure. Finally, 80 patients were included in the intention-to-treat (ITT) analysis (40 in each group) and 78 patients in the PP analysis (38 in the MR-guided group, 40 in the CT-guided group). All randomized patients were included in the safety analysis.

The baseline demographic and clinical characteristics were comparable between the CT-guided (n = 45) and MR-guided (n = 45) groups. No significant differences were observed in age (p = 0.53), sex (p > 0.99), or smoking history (p > 0.99). The average body mass index (BMI) was comparable between the two groups (p = 0.40). Lung function tests, including FEV1, FVC, MVV, and DLCO, revealed no significant differences between the two groups, suggesting similar baseline lung function (p > 0.05) (Table 1).

The average size of pulmonary nodules was 0.98 ± 0.31 cm, with no significant differences observed between the two groups (p = 0.29). The distance from the pleura was comparable between the two groups (p = 0.56). No significant differences were found in nodule location across lung lobes (p = 0.72) or in nodule type (p = 0.57). Pure GGO nodules were the most common type in both groups. These findings indicate that baseline characteristics were well-matched between the two groups, enabling an unbiased comparison of the localization techniques (Table 1).

For the primary endpoint (total localization deviation), we adopted the standard noninferiority analysis approach: the 95% confidence interval (CI) for the between-group difference (MR vs. CT) was used to evaluate noninferiority. The accuracy of nodule localization, assessed by deviation from the target, favored the MR-guided method. In ITT and PP analyses, the MR-guided group demonstrated a significantly smaller total deviation compared to the CT-guided group. The total deviation in the MR-guided group was 5.50 mm (both ITT and PP), whereas the CT-guided group showed deviations of 6.84 mm (both ITT and PP) (p = 0.04) (Fig. 4A). In the ITT population, the mean difference was − 1.34 mm with a 95% CI of (-2.59 mm, -0.09 mm); in the PP population, the mean difference was − 1.39 mm with a 95% CI of (-2.61 mm, -0.18 mm). Since the upper bounds of both CIs were below the predefined noninferiority margin of 3 mm, noninferiority of MR to CT was confirmed. However, the horizontal deviation was comparable between the groups, with p-values of 0.48 (ITT) and 0.45 (PP) (Fig. 4B). Notably, the vertical deviation was significantly smaller in the MR-guided group, with p-values of 0.04 (ITT) and 0.05 (PP) highlighting improved precision in vertical localization (Fig. 4C) (Tables 2 and 3).

The results of nodule localization and surgical outcomes revealed significant differences between the CT-guided and MR-guided groups in both ITT and PP analyses. The MR-guided group required significantly fewer insertion attempts and shorter procedural durations than the CT-guided group. In the ITT analysis, the MR-guided group required an average of 1.05 insertion attempts, compared to 2.28 attempts in the CT-guided group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5A). Procedural duration was also significantly shorter in the MR-guided group, averaging 3.98 min compared to 10.73 min in the CT-guided group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5B). Moreover, the MR-guided group required fewer CT scans, which led to reduced radiation exposure. The MR-guided group underwent an average of 3.00 CT scans (ITT and PP), compared to 4.28 scans in the CT-guided group (p < 0.001) (Fig. 5C). Additionally, the MR-guided group demonstrated lower radiation exposure, with significantly reduced DLP (p < 0.001, Fig. 5D) and ED (p < 0.001, Fig. 5E). Complication rates for pneumothorax, hemorrhage, and localizer dislodgement were slightly lower in the MR-guided group; however, these differences were not statistically significant (Fig. 6) (Tables 2 and 3).

Discussion

The results of our study demonstrate that MR-guided localization meets noninferiority criteria vs. CT guidance for preoperative pulmonary nodule localization, supporting it as a valuable alternative with meaningful clinical advantages. The MR-guided technique achieved significantly higher localization accuracy, with smaller total and vertical deviations, thereby reducing the risk of targeting errors. Procedural efficiency was markedly enhanced in the MR-guided group, which required fewer insertion attempts and significantly shorter procedural durations. Additionally, MR guidance minimized patient radiation exposure by reducing the required number of CT scans and lowering the DLP and ED. Although complication rates, including pneumothorax, hemorrhage, and hook-wire dislodgement, were not statistically significant, they were slightly lower in the MR-guided group. Notably, surgical outcomes, including LOS, blood loss, and type of resection, were comparable between the two groups, confirming the safety of the MR-guided technique. While designed as a non-inferiority trial to validate MR-guided localization against the established CT-guided approach, our findings revealed statistically significant differences in several key secondary outcomes (e.g., procedural efficiency, vertical localization precision, radiation exposure) as exploratory results. These findings, coupled with comparable safety profiles and confirmed noninferiority in the primary outcome, position MR-guided localization as a valuable and promising alternative to CT guidance in clinical practice.

The findings of our study align with and expand upon previous research on the application of MR in thoracic surgery. Consistent with the findings of Peng et al.13, our study confirmed that MR-guided localization achieves high accuracy, particularly with significantly smaller vertical deviations compared to traditional CT-guided methods. This underscores the utility of MR technology in overcoming the limitations of CT guidance, especially with regard to spatial precision. Moreover, the shorter procedural duration and fewer insertion attempts observed in this study are consistent with the findings of Zhang et al.12, who highlighted the efficiency of MR-based techniques in reducing procedural complexity and operator dependency. However, while previous studies, including our 2023 animal model study, primarily emphasized the feasibility and safety of MR guidance, this clinical trial provides strong evidence of its advantages in a clinical setting, particularly its impact on reducing radiation exposure and enhancing patient safety.

Unlike the earlier work by Yeung et al.8, which emphasized the broader application of MR in surgical navigation, our study distinctly evaluates the comparative accuracy and radiation efficiency of MR-guided pulmonary nodule localization relative to CT-guided methods. In contrast to studies that primarily utilized MR for training and simulation, our study integrated MR into live clinical workflows, demonstrating its practicality and scalability. Notably, while complications such as pneumothorax and hemorrhage were observed in both our study and the findings of Xu et al.14 on CT-guided methods, the rates in the MR-guided group were slightly lower, albeit not statistically significant. These findings underscore the potential of MR technology to enhance procedural safety without compromising surgical outcomes. While the statistically significant 1.3 mm reduction in total deviation (5.50 mm vs. 6.84 mm, p = 0.04) demonstrates MR’s technical precision, its direct clinical impact on VATS wedge resections—where typical resection margins for small pulmonary nodules (5–10 mm) are recommended by clinical guidelines and consensus statements14,16 and exceed this difference—should be contextualized. MR-guided localization offers distinct technical advantages derived from its integrated workflow: (1) Preoperative AI-segmented 3D models (from CT) are registered to the patient’s anatomy using surface markers and OptiTrack motion capture, creating a static digital overlay that aligns with physical positioning at the start of the procedure. (2) This pre-registered 3D visualization eliminates reliance on iterative 2D CT acquisitions, reducing radiation exposure by 45% (consistent with our findings) and avoiding delays from repeated image processing. (3) The overlay of nodules and critical structures (e.g., vessels) onto the surgical field—anchored to stable surface markers—facilitates precise pre-procedural trajectory planning, contributing to the 54% reduction in puncture attempts and 63% shorter procedure time observed. These features address key limitations of CT guidance (radiation burden, fragmented 2D imaging) without requiring real-time imaging, which contributes to the exploratory clinical benefits observed in our cohort.

Our study employed distinct methodologies and rigorous analyses, enhancing the validity and clinical applicability of its findings. Designed as a noninferiority randomized controlled trial, our study aimed to demonstrate that MR-guided localization is not inferior to the widely adopted CT-guided method, while exploring potential advantages. Noninferiority trials are particularly valuable for introducing innovative techniques into clinical practice, as they ensure that new methods meet established benchmarks while evaluating additional benefits15. Our study achieved these objectives by demonstrating that MR-guided localization significantly improved procedural efficiency and reduced radiation exposure without compromising safety or accuracy. The use of both ITT and PP analyses further enhanced the robustness of our study’s results, ensuring consistency regardless of patient adherence or protocol deviations15.

MR-guided preoperative hook-wire localization introduces distinct workflow changes compared with conventional CT-guided techniques for thoracic surgery teams. Although MR-guided localization requires additional preoperative preparation, including CT-based 3D model reconstruction, surface marker placement, and MR system registration (approximately 15–20 min per patient), this upfront work eliminates the need for repeated intraoperative CT scans and puncture adjustments, which are time-consuming and resource-intensive in CT-guided localization. For the clinical workflow, MR-guided localization reduces the occupation time of multi-row spiral CT scanners, a critical and shared resource in radiology departments, and shortens the total preoperative preparation time for patients undergoing VATS. In addition, the intuitive 3D visualization of MR technology reduces the learning curve for surgeons and interventional radiologists, and the fewer puncture attempts decrease the risk of procedural delays, improving the overall efficiency of the operating room schedule.

From an economic perspective, MR-guided localization involves initial capital costs for specialized equipment, including the OptiTrack motion capture system, HoloLens MR headset, and 3D reconstruction software (Mimics 20.0). However, the long-term economic benefits may offset these upfront costs: first, the reduced number of CT scans significantly lowers the radiation-related costs and the consumption of CT contrast agents (if used); second, the lower complication rate (although not statistically significant in this study) may reduce the cost of postoperative management for complications such as pneumothorax and hemorrhage; third, the improved procedural efficiency increases the daily surgical volume of the thoracic surgery department. Notably, this study did not conduct a formal cost-effectiveness analysis, and future multicenter studies with long-term follow-up are needed to quantify the economic benefits of MR-guided localization and compare the cost-effectiveness ratio with CT-guided localization.

Despite its strengths, our study has several limitations that warrant consideration in guiding future research. First, while our single-center design ensures procedural standardization, it may limit generalizability, particularly across institutions with different MR hardware configurations. Additionally, although our study was powered for noninferiority, the sample size may have been insufficient to detect smaller differences in safety outcomes, including rare complications. Another limitation is the absence of blinding, which could introduce bias during localization and procedural outcome assessment, although blinding is inherently challenging to implement in trials involving procedural techniques. Furthermore, a pre-specified Statistical Analysis Plan (SAP) was not developed or registered prior to trial initiation, which poses a potential risk of analytical bias; the selection of secondary outcomes and the application of both ITT and PP analyses were determined based on standard clinical trial practices rather than a pre-specified plan. Finally, the reliance on pre-procedural CT and surface markers may limit application in patients with significant atelectasis or who cannot hold their breath consistently. Further exploration can be conducted in the future such as integration with real-time fluoroscopy or AI-driven motion prediction, to enhance applicability in complex cases.

Conclusion

Our study establishes MR-guided localization as an accurate, efficient, and safe alternative to CT-guided methods for preoperative pulmonary nodule localization in VATS, which met the noninferiority criteria in the primary outcome of localization accuracy while maintaining comparable overall safety. Additionally, MR showed exploratory benefits with notably shortened procedural duration, improved vertical localization precision and reduced radiation exposure in our cohort. The robust randomized design and consistent results from ITT and PP analyses support its clinical applicability. Future research should validate MR-guided localization across diverse AR/MR platforms, extend its application to complex scenarios (deeper nodules, multiple lesions), conduct cost-effectiveness analyses to quantify workflow savings against equipment costs, and explore integration with intraoperative CT/C-arm for hybrid operating room solutions.
