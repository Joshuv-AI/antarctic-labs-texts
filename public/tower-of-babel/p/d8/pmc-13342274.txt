Radiomics in Gastric Cancer: Advancing Precision Medicine
Authors: Jintao He, Siwei Pan, Mengxuan Cao, Can Hu, Zhiyuan Xu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractGastric cancer (GC) is a common malignancy characterized by insidious onset and aggressive invasiveness that poses a serious threat to human health. Although medical imaging plays a critical role in cancer diagnosis and treatment, its interpretation largely relies on the expertise and experience of observers, underscoring the need for more reliable diagnostic techniques. Radiomics, through a series of standardized procedures, enables the extraction of high-throughput quantitative features from medical images across various imaging modalities using machine learning or deep learning methods, thereby reducing the influence of subjective and objective variability. This review summarizes the clinical applications of radiomics in the management of GC. To enhance predictive accuracy and model interpretability, we also examine advances in imaging multi-omics research. Furthermore, we discuss key limitations that may hinder the clinical translation of radiomics models and propose future directions to advance radiomics research in GC.Keywords: Gastric cancer, Radiomics, Precision medicine, Multi-omics

INTRODUCTION

In 2020, gastric cancer (GC) accounted for approximately 1.089 million new cases worldwide, representing 5.6% of all cancer cases and ranking as the fifth most common cancer. It caused approximately 769,000 deaths, accounting for 7.7% of all cancer-related deaths and making it the fourth leading cause of cancer mortality [1]. With the increasing implementation of GC screening programs and the advancement and diversification of therapeutic strategies, the prognosis of patients with GC has gradually improved [2]. However, early GC often presents with nonspecific or subtle symptoms and progresses rapidly. In addition, the lack of reliable hematological biomarkers for dynamic monitoring, limitations in endoscopic application, and variability in observer experience and expertise contribute to subjective and objective discrepancies in clinical diagnosis and evaluation [3]. Therefore, developing accurate and comprehensive evaluation methods for GC is essential for optimizing personalized treatment strategies and improving patient prognosis.

Medical imaging is a widely used diagnostic approach for GC. Traditional medical imaging modalities, including computed tomography (CT), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET), are commonly used as noninvasive methods to evaluate tumors and surrounding lymph nodes. Clinicians typically identify and interpret the region of interest (ROI) in these images to establish a diagnosis. However, due to interobserver variability in image interpretation, achieving consistent and standardized assessments remains challenging. Radiomics—an emerging field integrating artificial intelligence (AI) with conventional medical imaging—has attracted increasing research attention. Radiomics converts medical images into high-dimensional quantitative data, enabling high-throughput extraction of relevant imaging features that can support clinical diagnosis and decision-making [4]. Furthermore, radiomics-based multi-omics models have demonstrated potential to improve predictive accuracy (ACC) and enhance model interpretability [5]. Despite growing interest in the clinical applications of radiomics, most studies remain in the early exploratory stage. In addition, the clinical translation of radiomics models faces several limitations, highlighting the need for further investigation [6].

In this review, we first describe the radiomics workflow in detail. We then summarize current clinical applications of radiomics in GC and explore the associations between macroscopic radiomic features and the underlying microscopic biological characteristics of GC (Fig. 1). Finally, we discuss the major challenges limiting the clinical implementation of radiomics, including issues related to stability, interpretability, and practicality.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Literature search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted to identify potentially relevant studies indexed in the PubMed database from its inception through July 1, 2025. The search strategy incorporated the following keywords and Medical Subject Headings terms: “gastric cancer,” “stomach neoplasms,” “radiomics,” “early detection,” “staging,” “treatment response,” “survival,” “clinical outcome,” “postoperative complications,” “molecular subtype,” and “multi-omics.”

Study selection

Two reviewers (J.T.H. and S.W.P.) independently screened the titles and abstracts of all retrieved records. Studies were considered eligible if they applied radiomics techniques to the early diagnosis, stage prediction, clinical outcome assessment, prediction of postoperative complications, molecular subtyping, or multi-omics analysis of GC. Review articles, case reports, letters, comments, and conference abstracts were excluded. The PubMed search initially identified 239 studies, of which 31 met the inclusion criteria after screening. An additional 13 studies were identified through manual screening of the reference lists of relevant articles. Consequently, a total of 44 studies published between January 2019 and January 2025 were included in this review.

Data extraction

Two reviewers (J.T.H. and S.W.P.) independently extracted the following information from each included study: sample size, multicenter involvement, validation and deployment status (including external validation [EV] and clinical deployment), model calibration, study design, imaging modality, study endpoints, main conclusions, segmentation and modeling methods, validation strategy, model performance, and year of publication.

RADIOMICS WORKFLOW

A standard radiomics workflow consists of several sequential stages (Fig. 2). The process begins with the acquisition and preprocessing of medical images to ensure adequate data quality. Next, the ROI is accurately segmented, and a large number of quantitative features are extracted. Feature selection methods are then applied to reduce redundancy and mitigate the risk of overfitting. Finally, predictive models are constructed using the selected features and are externally validated to evaluate their performance and generalizability.

Imaging modalities and image preprocessing

Radiomics analyses rely on high-quality medical imaging data obtained from various imaging modalities. Notably, these modalities are not interchangeable and should be selected according to international guidelines and the expertise of individual medical centers [7]. Recently, multimodal models have emerged as a major research focus, particularly because most previous radiomics studies on GC relied on features extracted from a single imaging modality. For example, combining CT with MRI or CT with ultrasound (US) may compensate for the limitations of individual modalities and improve the precision of clinical decision-making [8,9].

Before feature extraction, original images are typically preprocessed to reduce external interference, improve image quality, and facilitate subsequent analysis. Image preprocessing generally includes several steps. The first step is normalization, which standardizes image features—such as gray values, entropy, and co-occurrence matrix parameters—within a defined range (e.g., mapping gray values to 0–1). This process reduces variability caused by differences in imaging devices and scanning parameters, thereby improving data comparability [10]. The second step is denoising, which removes noise from medical images using techniques such as signal filtering, ROI resampling, deep convolutional neural networks, and block-matching and 3D filtering, thereby enhancing image clarity [11,12]. The final step is image registration, which aligns images from the same patient acquired at different times or with different imaging modalities into a common coordinate system. This alignment ensures accurate correspondence of anatomical structures and facilitates the analysis of lesion changes over time [13].

ROI segmentation and feature extraction

Accurate ROI segmentation is essential for obtaining reliable feature values. Three primary segmentation approaches are currently used: manual, fully automated, and semi-automated. Manual segmentation involves delineating the ROI boundary according to lesion characteristics using image analysis software and input devices such as a mouse or electronic pen. This approach offers high ACC but is time-consuming and susceptible to interobserver variability. Fully automated segmentation is more feasible in clinical settings. In a previous study involving 326 patients with meningioma, a fully automated segmentation model achieved an ACC of 0.848 and an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) of 0.842 in the internal validation (IV) set, with an intraclass correlation coefficient of 0.848 compared with manual segmentation [14]. However, substantial errors may occur when segmenting lesions with irregular shapes or indistinct boundaries [15]. Semi-automated segmentation combines the advantages of the 2 approaches by allowing manual adjustments to algorithm-generated ROI boundaries, thereby improving segmentation efficiency and ACC [16].

Radiomics feature extraction generates a data-driven representation of a tumor by quantitatively characterizing it using shape, first-order, and texture features. These features convert tumor characteristics into high-dimensional, quantifiable data that can be used for subsequent analysis.

Feature selection and model building

After feature extraction, feature selection is performed to remove redundant or non-informative features that do not contribute to disease diagnosis and prediction. The primary objective is to reduce the number of features, thereby decreasing model complexity, facilitating training, and minimizing the risk of overfitting. Feature selection methods are generally categorized as filter, wrapper, and embedded approaches. Filter methods operate independently of machine learning (ML) models and select features based on statistical properties or their correlation with the target variable. Common techniques include analysis of variance (ANOVA) and correlation coefficient analysis [17,18,19]. In contrast, wrapper methods evaluate different feature combinations by using the predictive performance of an ML model as the selection criterion. Representative approaches include recursive feature elimination [19] and forward selection. Embedded methods integrate feature selection directly into the model training process and identify important features according to model parameters or evaluation metrics during training [20]. Common embedded techniques include the least absolute shrinkage and selection operator (LASSO), random forest (RF), and gradient boosting tree. Previous studies have suggested that ANOVA, LASSO, and minimum redundancy maximum relevance perform better than many other feature selection approaches [21].

A typical radiomics modeling workflow includes dataset partitioning, classifier selection, cross-validation, model optimization, and evaluation of feature importance [22,23]. Based on the type of classifier and optimization strategy, radiomics models are generally categorized into ML and deep learning (DL) approaches (Fig. 3). Traditional ML models—such as decision trees, support vector machines, and RF—generally have relatively simple and interpretable structures [24]. In contrast, DL models use neural network architectures, including multilayer perceptrons, convolutional neural networks (CNNs), and recurrent neural networks, which contain multiple hidden layers and complex structures. For example, CNNs automatically extract image features through convolutional, pooling, and fully connected layers, enabling more sophisticated feature learning than conventional ML models [25]. During model optimization, ML approaches typically adjust algorithm-specific parameters, whereas DL models require tuning a larger set of parameters, such as the number of layers, neurons, and learning rates. IV is essential for improving model reliability and generalizability, and EV is ideally required to further confirm model robustness [26].

POTENTIAL APPLICATIONS OF RADIOMICS IN THE CLINICAL PRACTICE OF GC

Early diagnosis of GC

Radiomics extracts subvisual pixel-level information from lesions using AI to generate quantitative imaging biomarkers. By capturing features that are not discernible to the human eye, radiomics can complement the limitations of conventional diagnostic modalities such as endoscopy and CT. Endoscopic and CT-based radiomic models have therefore demonstrated improved detection rates and diagnostic efficiency for early gastric cancer (EGC) (Table 1).

Several studies have developed endoscopic detection systems based on dual imaging modes—white light imaging and narrow band imaging—using DL frameworks. One such system employed a mask region-based convolutional neural network (Mask R-CNN) and an improved version (IMR-CNN). The Mask R-CNN model achieved an overall ACC of 0.852, sensitivity of 0.930, specificity of 0.827, and an AUC of 0.910. On the IV set, IMR-CNN achieved an ACC of 0.939, specificity of 0.925, and an intersection over union (IoU) of 0.791. Another study proposed a generative adversarial network-based lesion segmentation approach that achieved a Dice coefficient of 0.866, ACC of 0.919, specificity of 0.939, IoU of 0.763, and AUC of 0.965 on the IV set. These models demonstrated superior performance compared with experienced endoscopists and showed promise for assisting in the detection of early-stage lesions [27,28,29]. However, these studies have several limitations, including relatively small sample sizes, limited explanation of the mechanisms underlying the improved models, and single-center study designs that may introduce bias. To address these limitations, another study utilized a multicenter dataset to develop a triple-branch network incorporating spatial colocalization and channel attention mechanisms. By analyzing inter-image relationships, the model enhanced the identification and segmentation of EGC, achieving a Jaccard Similarity Index of 0.845 (95% confidence interval [CI], 0.835–0.856) on the EV set. Notably, the model demonstrated reliability and clinical feasibility superior to that of junior practitioners [30]. In parallel, other studies have focused on the automated classification of gastric lesions. For example, a parameterized attention-based feature fusion model achieved the highest overall ACC of 0.956 (95% CI, 0.946–0.966) with a relatively lightweight architecture, although its limited training dataset may affect generalizability [31]. In another study, model validation using a larger endoscopic image dataset yielded an ACC of 0.947, sensitivity of 0.941, specificity of 0.952, and an AUC of 0.979 on the IV set for the identification of GC lesions [32]. Although these approaches highlight the considerable potential of radiomics for EGC screening, current research remains largely focused on algorithmic optimization. Key clinical limitations of endoscopy—including blind spots, inability to assess lymph node metastasis (LNM), and difficulty in accurately determining tumor invasion depth—remain unresolved.

In contrast, the relatively low diagnostic ACC of CT for EGC (ACC≈0.600) limits its use as a primary diagnostic modality [35]. Nevertheless, advances in CT-based radiomics suggest that it may serve as a valuable adjunct for EGC detection. One study developed a radiomics model to differentiate benign from malignant tumors in 100 patients, achieving an AUC of 0.886 (95% CI, 0.809–0.963), which was higher than that of a physician-based subjective CT sign model (AUC, 0.806; 95% CI, 0.696–0.917) [33]. In another multicenter study using non-contrast CT images from both GC and non-cancer cohorts, the radiomics model demonstrated a 21.8% increase in sensitivity and a 14.0% increase in specificity compared with radiologists, particularly for EGC detection. Importantly, the model successfully identified GC cases that had initially been missed by radiologists, enabling earlier diagnosis during follow-up imaging for other conditions [34]. These findings highlight the potential of CT radiomics as an efficient and practical approach for opportunistic GC screening.

Surgical implications: radiomics may also serve as a proactive decision-support tool for surgeons and endoscopists. By identifying patients at high risk of malignancy, radiomic models can facilitate timely confirmatory examinations while providing objective, imaging-based evidence to guide the initial therapeutic strategy. In particular, these tools may assist clinicians in determining whether patients are more suitable for endoscopic treatment or surgical resection, thereby supporting more personalized and evidence-based clinical decision-making.

Radiomics model for predicting GC staging

The clinical tumor-node-metastasis (cTNM) system developed by the American Joint Committee on Cancer is widely used for clinical staging of GC [36]. However, because GC exhibits substantial biological and morphological heterogeneity, accurate preoperative assessment of pathological tumor-node-metastasis (pTNM) status and peritoneal metastases (PM) remains challenging. Emerging radiomics techniques offer a more comprehensive and non-invasive evaluation of both intratumoral and peritumoral characteristics. These approaches have demonstrated improved ACC in predicting pTNM stage and PM (Table 2), thereby facilitating more individualized treatment strategies.

Accurate prediction of pathological T (pT) and N (pN) stages is essential for treatment planning and prognostic evaluation [47,48,49,50]. Nevertheless, most previous radiomics studies have focused on predicting either the pT or pN stage separately. For pT staging, a CT-based radiomics DL model showed strong performance in distinguishing pT1/2 from pT3/4 tumors [37], with decision curve analysis and feature visualization enhancing both clinical utility and interpretability. Another study using diffusion-weighted MRI reported significant differences in apparent diffusion coefficient values among different pT stages [51]. For pN staging, a model based on CT-derived radiomics feature scores demonstrated significantly higher values in pN-positive compared with pN-negative patients (all P<0.001) [38]. The integration of both intratumoral and peritumoral features achieved the highest predictive performance for pN status in the training set, with an AUC of 0.779 (95% CI, 0.713–0.835). Additional studies using MRI and US radiomics also reported good agreement between predicted and actual LNM status [39,40]. In contrast to studies that evaluated pT or pN separately, one investigation developed a radiomics model capable of simultaneously predicting both pT and pN status, stratifying patients into pT1N0 and pT2–4N+ groups. This integrated approach provides clinically meaningful guidance for endoscopic resection decisions in patients with EGC, thereby improving treatment selection and clinical outcomes [41].

Distant metastases of GC commonly involve distant lymph nodes, liver, peritoneum, and lungs, among other sites. Among these, PM is associated with the poorest prognosis [52]. Although conventional imaging modalities such as CT and PET/CT can detect PM, their sensitivity and ACC remain limited [53,54], underscoring the urgent need for improved detection methods. Radiomics approaches applied to CT, dual-energy computed tomography (DECT), and PET/CT have demonstrated superior diagnostic performance compared with conventional imaging assessments. For example, a radiomics model derived from preoperative CT images for evaluating the peritoneal cancer index (PCI) demonstrated significantly better diagnostic performance than conventional CT-based PCI assessment [42]. Another study developed a DECT radiomics model based on iodine-uptake images that outperformed both clinical models and physician evaluations in predicting PM [43]. Furthermore, a PET/CT-based radiomics model achieved AUCs of 0.860 (95% CI, 0.810–0.900) and 0.850 (95% CI, 0.780–0.920) in the training and IV sets, respectively, for PM prediction [44]. Collectively, these findings highlight the clinical potential of radiomics for predicting PM.

Recent studies indicate that multifactorial integrated models centered on radiomics provide a more comprehensive assessment of tumor tissue and its microenvironment than single-modality radiomics models, resulting in improved predictive ACC and higher AUC values. For example, a DL-based radiomics nomogram incorporating CT radiomic features and clinical variables outperformed conventional radiomics methods in predicting LNM burden in advanced gastric cancer (AGC) [45]. Another study developed a multimodal fusion model integrating PET/CT radiomics, clinical parameters, and expert radiological assessment, which demonstrated excellent performance in predicting PM in GC and surpassed both clinical nomograms and radiologist evaluations [46]. These findings collectively suggest that integrating multi-source information is critical for comprehensive cTNM prediction in GC and shows promise as a non-invasive staging tool.

Surgical implications: accurate preoperative prediction of pT/pN staging provides an objective basis for selecting between endoscopic resection and radical surgery, including decisions regarding the extent of gastric resection and lymphadenectomy. In addition, reliable prediction of PM may help avoid non-therapeutic laparotomy and identify patients who are more likely to benefit from neoadjuvant intraperitoneal and systemic chemotherapy.

Previous studies have identified CT-based radiomics features as potential biomarkers for predicting response to neoadjuvant therapy in GC [55]. In particular, gray-level co-occurrence matrix contrast has been shown to vary significantly before and after neoadjuvant chemotherapy (NAC), suggesting its potential as a predictive biomarker [56]. In clinical applications, radiomics features extracted from preoperative CT images have been used to predict treatment response in AGC and to guide regimen selection for potential non-responders, achieving AUCs of 0.736 (95% CI, 0.675–0.798) in the training set and 0.679 (95% CI, 0.554–0.803) in the IV set [57]. Expanding the scope of feature extraction may further improve predictive performance. For example, a radiomics model incorporating features from both the primary tumor and lymph nodes demonstrated superior performance compared with models based solely on clinical variables or primary tumor features. The model achieved an AUC of 0.779 (95% CI, 0.705–0.842), sensitivity of 0.809, specificity of 0.670, and ACC of 0.713 in the training set, and an AUC of 0.732 (95% CI, 0.608–0.835), sensitivity of 0.805, specificity of 0.600, and ACC of 0.677 in the IV set [58]. Furthermore, a nomogram integrating 2 clinicopathological variables (Lauren classification and clinical stage) with CT radiomics features demonstrated better predictive performance than a radiomics-only model [59]. Although radiomics studies evaluating treatment response in GC have primarily focused on CT, the role of MRI-based radiomics remains less clearly defined. One study constructed both CT and MRI radiomics models and integrated them into a multimodal nomogram [60]. The results showed that CT radiomics, MRI radiomics, and the combined nomogram were all significantly associated with tumor regression grade. Moreover, MRI and CT radiomics demonstrated comparable performance in the early identification of pathological response to NAC, while the multimodal nomogram further improved predictive capability. These findings suggest that MRI radiomics may also be valuable for predicting treatment response and that integrating CT and MRI modalities could enhance predictive performance.

Radiomics can also be used to evaluate key prognostic outcomes, including overall survival (OS), recurrence-free survival (RFS), and postoperative recurrence in patients with GC. One study reported that the CT radiomics score was significantly associated with RFS, OS, and postoperative recurrence (P<0.050) and served as an independent prognostic factor (P<0.050) [65]. Higher radiomics scores were significantly associated with an increased risk of distant recurrence. Another study demonstrated that CT radiomics features used to assess postoperative recurrence could function as noninvasive biomarkers, improving diagnostic ACC by 0.100–0.180 (P<0.001), and showing a positive correlation between radiomics-assessed recurrence risk and survival benefit following NAC [61]. Integrating radiomics with additional clinical or imaging features may further enhance predictive ACC. For example, a model combining recurrence-related DL features with clinical characteristics achieved an AUC of 0.859 (95% CI, 0.806–0.912) in the EV set, indicating good generalizability [62]. Similarly, a model integrating PET/CT radiomics, cTNM staging, and clinical features yielded AUCs of 0.817 (95% CI, 0.790–0.844) and 0.707 (95% CI, 0.640–0.774) in the training and IV sets, respectively, for predicting OS [63]. In addition, an MRI radiomics study developed a joint survival model incorporating age, lymphovascular invasion, and a radiomics-derived risk score, which demonstrated strong performance in predicting 3- and 5-year OS [64]. Collectively, these findings suggest that nomograms or joint models integrating radiomics with multiple clinical and imaging variables offer improved ACC and greater clinical applicability for predicting postoperative recurrence and metastasis.

Surgical implications: predicting response to NAC can help surgeons determine the optimal timing of surgery. In addition, prognostic models for recurrence and survival may assist in planning postoperative surveillance and adjuvant therapy, thereby facilitating personalized follow-up strategies for patients with GC.

Radiomics-based models for predicting postoperative complications have recently attracted increasing research interest. At present, radiomics is primarily used indirectly by assessing body composition parameters, including visceral fat area, subcutaneous fat area, and skeletal muscle mass. For example, one study developed a radiomics-based diagnostic tool for sarcopenia and demonstrated its association with short-term postoperative complications, achieving a higher AUC than the conventional diagnostic method [68]. However, relying solely on radiomics for prediction is limited and may result in relatively modest predictive performance. Integrating radiomics with clinical variables can substantially improve model performance. For instance, one study evaluated the predictive value of CT-derived visceral and subcutaneous fat components using clinical, radiomics, and combined radiomics–clinical nomogram models for postoperative complications [69]. The combined model showed promising potential for individualized risk prediction. In another retrospective study involving patients who underwent radical gastrectomy at the First Affiliated Hospital of Nanjing Medical University, an early postoperative complication prediction model was developed by integrating perioperative clinical variables with visceral fat radiomics features [70]. The model achieved an AUC of 0.923 and ACC of 0.873 (95% CI, 0.756–0.990). These findings suggest that radiomics provides a promising and effective approach for predicting postoperative complications and may have important clinical value in improving perioperative management and treatment outcomes.

Surgical implications: preoperative identification of patients at high risk of complications—such as those with sarcopenia or increased visceral fat—may enable targeted prehabilitation strategies and inform adjustments in surgical approach or perioperative management to reduce complication risk.

Potential applications of radiomics in GC molecular subtyping: predicting molecular events

Cancer molecular subtypes in GC primarily include microsatellite instability (MSI) and the expression status of human epidermal growth factor receptor 2 (HER2) and programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1). These subtypes reflect tumor immune characteristics, disease progression, and the degree of malignancy. They may serve as critical biomarkers for the precise diagnosis of GC, selection of appropriate treatment strategies, and assessment of prognosis and recurrence risk. For example, MSI-high tumors often respond more favorably to immunotherapy because of their high mutational burden. Currently, molecular subtyping in clinical practice relies mainly on invasive approaches, such as endoscopic biopsy and postoperative pathological examination. These methods are not only invasive but also limited in their ability to dynamically monitor changes in molecular subtypes over time. Therefore, the development of noninvasive approaches capable of dynamically predicting GC molecular subtypes is needed (Table 5).

Radiomics offers a noninvasive method for real-time prediction of GC molecular subtypes, with potentially high ACC and interpretability. One study developed a model for MSI prediction using features extracted from intratumoral and peritumoral regions on CT images [71]. In the IV set, the model achieved an AUC of 0.830 (95% CI, 0.727–0.906), an ACC of 0.768, a sensitivity of 0.843, and a specificity of 0.731. Another study developed a clinical–radiomics model based on a prior clinical model and reported an AUC of 0.834 (95% CI, 0.688–0.981) in the EV set [72]. These findings suggest that radiomics models can be used to noninvasively assess MSI status in GC, demonstrating good model fit and clinical utility. Their predictive performance may further improve when combined with clinical variables, although their prognostic value may remain limited. For HER2 prediction, one study constructed a radiomics model using portal venous phase contrast-enhanced CT images, achieving AUC values of 0.715 (95% CI, 0.612–0.804) and 0.718 (95% CI, 0.554–0.849) in the training and IV sets, respectively [73]. Another study developed a hybrid model integrating tumor location, TNM stage, carcinoembryonic antigen, and carbohydrate antigen 19-9 with radiomics features from the venous phase, yielding moderate predictive performance [74]. In contrast, a multimodal model incorporating both CT and PET features achieved higher predictive performance, with AUC values of 0.950 (95% CI, 0.890–1.000) and 0.760 (95% CI, 0.520–1.000) in the training and IV sets, respectively [75]. These findings suggest that radiomics can predict HER2 status and that multimodal imaging models may further improve predictive ACC. For PD-L1 prediction, one study used the Boruta algorithm for feature selection and constructed a radiomics model that achieved AUC values of 0.786 (95% CI, 0.700–0.872) and 0.774 (95% CI, 0.640–0.907) in the training and IV sets, respectively, with demonstrated clinical benefit [76]. Another study incorporated clinical variables into a hybrid DL model, which effectively distinguished high PD-L1 expression in GC, achieving AUC values of 0.806 (95% CI, 0.736–0.875) in the training set and 0.784 (95% CI, 0.668–0.901) in the EV set [77].

Overall, radiomics models based on features extracted from CT and other imaging modalities can predict the status of key molecular biomarkers, including MSI, HER2, and PD-L1. These approaches may provide insights into tumor biology and disease progression while offering complementary information to guide individualized treatment selection.

Applications of imaging multi-omics in GC

Radiomics extracts quantitative information about lesion morphology and texture from medical images; however, it cannot directly capture underlying molecular or genetic alterations. Consequently, radiomics-based models alone may be unable to distinguish cancers that present with similar imaging features but arise from different molecular mechanisms. In contrast, genomics and proteomics reveal fundamental disease mechanisms at the microscopic and molecular levels [78,79]. The integration of imaging data with multi-omics information has been widely investigated and has demonstrated strong potential for improving prognostic prediction [80,81].

Numerous studies have explored the clinical value of imaging multi-omics in GC (Table 6). For example, radiogenomic biomarkers derived from RNA sequencing and CT images have shown superior performance compared with the TNM staging system in predicting patient survival [82]. However, the limited sample size of CT imaging datasets necessitates larger studies to further validate the associations between imaging features and genomic data. In addition, radiogenomics enables gene enrichment pathway analysis, providing insights into potential therapeutic targets [83,84]. Another study developed a combined metabolomic–radiomic model that achieved an AUC of 0.882 (95% CI, 0.802–0.948) for predicting progression-free survival in GC patients, significantly outperforming a radiomics-only model and highlighting the value of multi-omics integration [85]. Furthermore, subsequent studies have demonstrated that pathomic–radiomic and immuno–radiomic features can independently predict prognosis and treatment response following chemotherapy in patients with GC [86,87].

DISCUSSION

Radiomics demonstrates significant potential in GC research; however, its clinical translation faces several practical challenges. First, technical variability in imaging equipment, software platforms, and interobserver differences in tumor segmentation—combined with the biological heterogeneity and temporal dynamics of tumors—can compromise model performance and generalizability [88]. In addition, most existing tools are not seamlessly integrated into clinical workflows and often require manual data transfer across multiple platforms, thereby increasing clinician burden [89]. Second, DL models are often criticized for their lack of transparency. Their opaque decision-making processes and unclear underlying mechanisms make it difficult for physicians to understand the rationale behind predictions, leading to caution when incorporating these outputs into critical therapeutic decisions. Issues of accountability further complicate their clinical adoption. Finally, most current studies focus primarily on validating model predictive ACC. Prospective evidence demonstrating that radiomics-assisted decision-making improves patient survival or quality of life remains limited.

Although surgery plays a central role in GC treatment, the application of radiomics and AI in surgical decision-making remains at an early stage. This limitation largely reflects the inherent complexity of surgical planning, which requires comprehensive consideration of the patient’s overall condition, tumor–anatomy relationships, and intraoperative dynamics. At present, only a limited number of studies have explored the use of radiomics to predict surgical feasibility in pancreatic and esophageal cancers [90,91]. Future progress will require the establishment of specialized surgical databases incorporating multimodal intraoperative data, as well as the development of practical tools that directly address clinical decision-making needs.

The integration of radiomics into clinical decision-making also redefines traditional frameworks of accountability, creating ambiguity regarding responsibility among models, developers, and clinicians. In this context, radiologists should critically review and interpret AI-generated imaging analyses. Surgeons, as the final decision-makers, must carefully evaluate AI recommendations in conjunction with clinical expertise. Algorithm developers are responsible for ensuring model reliability, transparency, and explainability, while healthcare institutions should establish standardized review protocols and usage guidelines and oversee system deployment.

Despite these challenges, radiomics has demonstrated clear potential for clinical application. Standardized quantitative imaging reports can provide a more objective foundation for multidisciplinary diagnosis and treatment planning. Three-dimensional visualization technologies facilitate preoperative planning of resection margins and lymph node dissection fields, while image-based risk stratification helps identify high-risk patients and enables earlier referral to specialized care. Furthermore, integrating multimodal imaging, multi-omics data, and other relevant clinical factors—by leveraging complementary information and cross-scale correlations—can substantially enhance model predictive performance, interpretability, and translational potential. However, such integration also presents challenges, including data-source heterogeneity, high dimensionality, and significant computational demands. Conventional approaches, such as feature-level fusion and DL-based architectures, are often constrained by computational cost and potential information loss during processing. Quantum computing, through advanced optimization algorithms and feature-selection strategies, offers a theoretical pathway for efficiently processing high-dimensional data while reducing redundancy. Nevertheless, this approach remains largely at an early stage of theoretical exploration and requires further investigation.

Achieving the clinical translation of radiomics will require sustained efforts across multiple domains. Multicenter, prospective studies are essential for validating model robustness and demonstrating real-world clinical utility [92,93]. Establishing standardized radiomics workflows and developing clinically integrated systems will also be critical for successful implementation [94]. In addition, strengthened interdisciplinary collaboration among experts in radiology, surgery, oncology, AI, and medical ethics is necessary to address the associated technical, clinical, and ethical challenges. By systematically addressing these barriers, radiomics has the potential to evolve from a research-oriented methodology into a key component of precision medicine for GC.
