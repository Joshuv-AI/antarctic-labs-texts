Clinical outcomes over 12 years: a comparative analysis of oocyte cryopreservation from controlled ovarian stimulation and in vitro maturation retrieved from ovarian tissue
Authors: Xiaoping Liu, Weie Zhao, Peng Sun, Yujie Li, Yingchun Guo, Wenlong Su, Weiwei Zeng, Xiaoyan Liang, Jingjie Li
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundFertility preservation is crucial for females facing gonadotoxic treatments. Controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) followed by oocyte cryopreservation is a well-established method, while ovarian tissue-derived oocyte in vitro maturation (OTO-IVM) is an emerging technique that can be used when COS is contraindicated or time is limited. However, OTO-IVM faces challenges such as low oocyte maturation rates and limited follow-up data. This study aimed to compare the clinical outcomes of COS oocyte cryopreservation and OTO-IVM, with a focus on the impact of transportation methods and the use of gonadotropin (Gn) before oophorectomy on OTO-IVM outcomes.MethodsThis retrospective study analyzed data from 472 patients who underwent fertility preservation procedures at the Reproductive Medicine Research Center, The Sixth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University, from July 2012 to July 2024. Patients diagnosed with cancer or requiring gonadotoxic treatments were included. The study compared oocyte maturation, fertilization, and embryo transfer outcomes between COS and OTO-IVM groups. Statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test for continuous data and Chi-squared or Fisher’s exact test for categorical data, with significance defined as P < 0.05.ResultsCOS group resulted in a significantly higher oocyte maturation rate (86.11%) compared to OTO-IVM group (39.43%). Direct oocyte transport yielded a higher rate of denuded oocytes compared to ovarian tissue transport. The use of Gn before oophorectomy in OTO-IVM significantly improved the IVM maturation rate (51.90% vs. 38.00%, P < 0.001).ConclusionsCOS oocyte cryopreservation remains more effective than OTO-IVM. However, the study highlighted the importance of optimizing transportation methods and using Gn before oophorectomy to improve OTO-IVM outcomes. These findings could guide clinical practice in selecting appropriate fertility preservation strategies. Further research is needed to refine OTO-IVM techniques and enhance their clinical applicability.Keywords: Oocyte cryopreservation, Controlled ovarian stimulation, OTO-IVM, Fertility preservation, Gonadotropin

Background

For women who require fertility preservation without using partner sperm (including those without a fertility partner or declining donor sperm), the available options include ovarian tissue cryopreservation (OTC) and oocyte cryopreservation [1, 2]. For patients with primary or secondary ovarian tumors and ovarian-infiltrating hematological malignancies, future ovarian tissue transplantation may carry a risk of reintroducing cancer [3]. In such cases, oocyte cryopreservation is likely a safer approach. Controlled ovarian stimulation (COS) is often limited in these patients due to a limited treatment time window and the presence of COS contraindications [4, 5]. Moreover, while bleeding and infection are common complications of transvaginal retrieval, patients with ovarian tumors or ovarian-infiltrating malignancies face additional risks of tumor rupture and dissemination [6, 7]. For these high-risk patients, ovarian tissue-derived oocyte in vitro maturation (OTO-IVM) provides a safer alternative.

OTO-IVM can also be combined with OTC to increase the efficiency of fertility preservation and the cumulative pregnancy rate [8–10]. Although OTO-IVM has potential application value in fertility preservation, significant challenges remain. Low oocyte maturation rates and the occurrence of abnormal oocyte morphology were the limitations of OTO-IVM [11]. To date, the clinical outcomes of this appended technique have only been described in several studies [12–15] and the comprehensive outcomes data remain scarce. The primary objective of this study was to compare oocyte maturation rates between COS followed by oocyte cryopreservation and OTO-IVM, while optimizing OTO-IVM conditions to enhance clinical applicability and fertility preservation outcomes.

Methods

Patient population

This study was a retrospective study, and we collected data from female patients who underwent COS oocyte cryopreservation and OTO-IVM for fertility preservation at Reproductive Medicine Research Center, The Sixth Affiliated Hospital, Sun Yat-sen University from July 2012 to July 2024. Most of these patients were diagnosed with cancer, but patients requiring gonadotoxic treatments for non-malignant conditions were also included in the study. Informed consent was obtained from all patients or their guardians (in the case of minors). The patients were categorized based on their methods of fertility preservation and subsequent statistical analysis was conducted. This study was approved by The Sixth Affiliated Hospital of Sun Yat-sen University’s ethics committee (2024ZSLYYEC-507).

All patients were consulted by multidisciplinary experts, including primary tumor-related specialties, oncology departments and reproductive centers, to determine the indications for fertility preservation and exclude contraindications. The fertility preservation methods and protocols were finally determined according to the general conditions and wishes of the patients. All patients completed anti-Müllerian hormone (AMH) detection and gynecological ultrasound to evaluate antral follicle counts (AFC) before fertility preservation. Assignment to COS oocyte cryopreservation or OTO-IVM groups was determined through multidisciplinary consultation, based on: (1) Medical treatment urgency; (2) Medical contraindications to COS; (3) Patient age and ovarian reserve; and (4) Patient preference after counseling.

Controlled ovarian stimulation

Once the need for oocyte cryopreservation is determined, ovarian stimulation should be initiated immediately, regardless of the menstrual cycle phase. In patients undergoing ovarian stimulation during the early follicular phase (defined as the first week of the menstrual cycle with follicles < 10 mm and estradiol (E2) levels < 75 pg/mL), a daily dose of 150–300 IU of human menopausal gonadotropin (HMG; Menotropins for Injection, Livzon Pharmaceutical Group Inc., Zhuhai, China) was administered, with the dose adjusted based on individual ovarian response. To prevent premature luteinizing hormone (LH) surges, either a gonadotropin-releasing hormone antagonist (GnRH-ant) or a progesterone was employed. In the GnRH-ant protocol, 0.25 mg of ganirelix acetate (Orgalutran, MSD, Oss, The Netherlands) was administered daily once the dominant follicle reached > 14 mm or when the dominant follicle was > 12 mm accompanied by E2 levels > 200 pg/mL, continuing until the trigger day. For the progestin-primed ovarian stimulation (PPOS) protocol, patients received 300 mg of progesterone soft capsules (Utrogestan, Besins Healthcare, London, UK) daily, starting from the initiation of HMG and continuing until 7 days post-oocyte retrieval.

For patients in the late follicular phase (defined as after day 7 of menstruation with a dominant follicle >10 mm and E2 >75 pg/mL), the luteal phase stimulation (LPS) protocol was initiated following a single 0.1 mg injection of triptorelin (Decapeptyl, Ferring Pharmaceuticals Ltd., Saint-Prex, Switzerland) to induce ovulation [16]. In patients already in the luteal phase, the LPS protocol could be commenced immediately with a daily dose of 150–300 IU HMG. When serum progesterone levels were < 3 ng/mL, patients additionally received 300 mg of oral progesterone soft capsules daily to suppress LH surges and prevent menstrual onset prior to oocyte retrieval.

For patients with estrogen-sensitive tumors, letrozole (Furui, Jiangsu Hengrui Medicine Co., Ltd., Lianyungang, China) at a dose of 5 mg daily was administered in combination with HMG. During the COS process, follicular development was monitored through ultrasound and sex hormone assessments. Final maturation was induced with 0.2 mg triptorelin (Decapeptyl, Ferring Pharmaceuticals, Saint-Prex, Switzerland) or 10,000 IU human chorionic gonadotropin (HCG; Chorionic gonadotrophin for injection, Livzon Pharmaceutical Group Inc., Zhuhai, China) when at least two follicles > 18 mm or three follicles > 17 mm were present. Oocyte retrieval was performed 36 h later. Metaphase II (MII) oocytes retrieved transvaginally under ultrasound guidance were cryopreserved using vitrification.

Ovarian tissue-derived oocyte in vitro maturation

OTO-IVM, as an innovative technique, can be combined with OTC to maximize fertility preservation. This approach involves isolating immature oocytes from ovarian tissue, maturing them in vitro, and cryopreservation for future use. Patients undergoing OTO-IVM received ovarian tissue harvesting with or without short-term gonadotropin (Gn) stimulation. For patients undergoing short-term Gn stimulation, a 3-day course of HMG injection was administered prior to OTC. HMG priming was considered for patients meeting all criteria: (i) No contraindications from estrogen-sensitive malignancies; (ii) Adequate time for ovarian stimulation; (iii) Informed consent obtained after counseling on risks and potential benefits. The decision to implement HMG treatment was made following comprehensive counseling during fertility consultation, during which both patients or their guardians were thoroughly informed about the potential benefits and risks of Gn priming, ultimately leading to a treatment choice based on their informed preference.

For fertility preservation patients undergoing OTC, unilateral oophorectomy was performed under general anesthesia via laparoscopy. In cases of primary or secondary ovarian tumors, unilateral or bilateral oophorectomy was determined by oncological indications. The excised ovaries were transported to the embryo laboratory in Quinn’s Advantage™ Medium with HEPES (SAGE, Trumbull, CT, USA) supplemented with 10% serum protein substitute (SPS, SAGE) at 4 °C or directly subjected to aspiration of cumulus-oocyte complexes (COCs) in the operating room. The specific method involved using a syringe to perform puncture and aspiration on the ovarian cortex to retrieve COCs. After aspiration, ovarian tissue was washed with saline. The ovarian cortical tissue was dissected by a cutter to a thickness of 1 to 2 mm. The ovarian cortex was cut with a scalpel into tissue pieces of approximately 10 mm x 10 mm x 1 mm and cryopreserved by vitrification. In patients with established ovarian metastases before oophorectomy, ovarian tissue cryopreservation was not performed after follicle aspiration. After COCs were aspirated in the operating room, they were immersed in Quinn’s Advantage™ Medium with HEPES (SAGE, Trumbull, CT, USA) supplemented with 10% serum protein substitute (SPS, SAGE) and transported to the laboratory at 4 ° C.

After the ovarian tissue had been processed for cryopreservation, the collection dish was examined for the presence of small COCs that had been released from follicles that ruptured during the cutting process. The COCs were then transferred to a follicular wash solution, thoroughly rinsed, and subsequently cultured in pre-IVM medium for a duration of 4 to 6 h before being cultured in the IVM sequential culture medium. The compositions of pre-IVM and IVM medium were identical to those described in Guo et al.‘s study [17]. The oocytes were monitored every 12 h, with the observation period up to a maximum of 72 h. Oocytes that reached MII were vitrified for cryopreservation. The remaining immature or degenerated oocytes were recorded and discarded.

Oocyte warming and embryo transfer procedures

All patients returning to the center to attempt conceive needed to be approved by their oncologist, confirming their suitability for pregnancy. For endometrial preparation preceding embryo transfer, the hormonal replacement therapy (HRT) protocol was implemented for all cases. For oocyte cryopreservation, the oocytes were warmed by kitazato kit method [18]. All vitrified oocytes were warmed, and the surviving oocytes were fertilized by intracytoplasmic sperm injection (ICSI) 2 h after the completion of the warming process. Embryos were cultured until day 3 and selected for transfer strictly according to their morphological appearance. The remaining embryos were cultured to the blastocyst stage.

Statistical analysis

Continuous data are presented as mean ± SD and compared using Student’s t-test. Categorical data are expressed as numbers (percentages) and compared using the Chi-squared test (or Fisher’s exact test where appropriate). All analyses were performed using SPSS (version 23, Chicago, USA). Significance was defined as a P value < 0.05.

Results

Patients’ characteristics

In total, 472 patients underwent fertility preservation from July 2012 to July 2024. Of these, 233 women underwent COS oocyte cryopreservation. OTO-IVM oocyte cryopreservation was performed in 10 patients. Both OTC and OTO-IVM oocyte cryopreservation were performed in 213 patients, and 16 patients underwent OTC alone. Some patients underwent COS oocyte cryopreservation cycles more than once. During the study period, we followed up the pregnancy outcomes of patients, which include COS and OTO-IVM oocytes cryopreservation, returning to the hospital for assisted reproductive treatment (ART). Regarding OTC, the present study was limited to reporting case numbers only. Given the exceptionally small cohort of patients who proceeded with cryopreserved ovarian tissue transplantation, subsequent data regarding endocrine profiles and reproductive outcomes were not included in the current analysis.

The detailed procedure was shown in Fig. 1. Indications for fertility preservation in the two groups were analyzed, as Fig. 2 shown, 63.83% of patients in the COS oocyte cryopreservation group were due to breast cancer. In the OTO-IVM oocyte cryopreservation group, 74.32% of patients were due to thalassemia major. The choice of fertility preservation method is also related to age. For this reason, we also performed statistics of related parameters after age stratification for the two groups. As shown in Table 1, in the COS oocyte cryopreservation group, the cohort comprised 227 adult patients, accounting for 97.42% of the total, while 6 postmenarchal pediatric patients represented 2.58% of the group. The OTO-IVM group consisted of 49 adult patients (21.97%) and 174 pediatric patients under 18 years of age (78.03%).

Laboratory outcomes

We conducted a meticulous comparison of the oocyte outcomes after COS and OTO-IVM (Table 2). In total, COS produced a substantial number of 3110 oocytes, and the metaphase II (MII) oocytes cryopreservation included a significant count of 2678, accounting for 86.11%. OTO-IVM involved 223 patients, generating 2579 immature oocytes that were subjected to IVM. The longest culture time for this procedure was recorded at 72 h. The IVM maturation rate was determined to be 39.43%, which was notably lower than that of the COS oocyte cryopreservation group. Moreover, we observed that there were higher rates of immature oocytes (39.98% vs. 6.56%, P < 0.001) and degenerate oocytes (11.98% vs. 1.64%, P < 0.001) compared to the COS oocyte cryopreservation group.

We also carried out an age subgroup analysis, and it was found that the IVM maturation rate was significantly lower in adult women (34.94% vs. 86.05%, P < 0.001) than in the COS oocyte cryopreservation group. Additionally, there were higher rates of immature oocytes (34.74% vs. 6.49%, P < 0.001) and degenerate oocytes (9.04% vs. 1.59%, P < 0.001) in adult women compared to the COS oocyte cryopreservation group.

The number of pediatric patients undergoing COS oocyte cryopreservation was relatively small, with only 6 cases and 91 oocytes. The oocyte maturation rate in this specific group was an encouraging 87.91%. Additionally, a number of 174 pediatric patients underwent the OTO-IVM procedure. This resulted in the retrieval of a considerable quantity of 2081 oocytes. The IVM maturation rate was 40.51%, which was significantly lower than COS oocyte cryopreservation. Meanwhile, the rate of immature oocytes was measured at 41.23%, and the rate of degenerated oocytes accounted for 12.69%. Both were significantly higher than COS oocyte preservation.

Clinical outcomes

During the study period, a total of 456 patients underwent oocyte cryopreservation, among whom 15 returned for follow-up consultation. The return rate of the COS oocyte cryopreservation group and the OTO-IVM + OTC group were 1.72% and 4.93%, respectively. In the COS oocyte cryopreservation group, 3 patients returned for oocyte warming, with a total oocyte survival rate of 85.00% (17/20), and 3 oocytes degenerated. We performed ICSI for the 17 warming oocytes, and the 2PN rate was 70.59% (12/17), resulting in 5 transferrable embryos being obtained and transferred. The rate of transferable embryos and good quality embryos were 50.00% and 40.00%, respectively. Finally, one patient had a clinical pregnancy and live birth. In the OTO-IVM group, 1 patient returned for oocyte warming, with an oocyte survival rate of 81.82% (9/11), and 1 oocyte degenerated. We performed ICSI for the 9 warming oocytes, and the 2PN rate was 100.00% (9/9), resulting in 2 transferable embryos being obtained and transferred. The rate of transferable embryos and good quality embryos were 25.00% and 12.50%, respectively. But no clinical pregnancy was achieved (Table 3).

Different transport methods in OTO-IVM

Patients with confirmed or suspected ovarian metastases did not undergo OTC procedures. Follicles were aspirated directly from the resected ovarian cortex in the operating room and transported to the laboratory for subsequent IVM procedures, with transportation times within 1 h. Patients with cancer who are not at risk for ovarian metastasis and those undergoing fertility preservation for benign indications typically undergo OTC and OTO-IVM procedures. Embryologist transported the excised ovarian tissue directly to the laboratory, followed by OTC and OTO-IVM procedures, with transportation times also within 1 h. Given that the population directly transporting follicles mainly consisted of women of reproductive age, while the population directly transporting ovarian tissue included both children and adult women, we conducted PSM (Propensity Score Matching) to compare the oocyte outcomes of these two scenarios. As shown in Table 4, the baseline characteristics of the two groups were comparable, and the rates of IVM maturation and oocyte degeneration were similar in the two groups. The rate of denuded oocytes obtained in the operating room group was significantly higher than that in the laboratory group (4.57% vs. 1.52%, P = 0.040).

Discussion

Our study revealed significant insights into the efficacy of fertility preservation methods, particularly focusing on oocyte cryopreservation from COS and OTO-IVM. However, the OTO-IVM faced challenges with lower rates of oocyte maturation, indicating the need for further refinement in this technique.

In China, embryo cryopreservation for fertility preservation is applicable to married women, while oocyte cryopreservation is available to unmarried women. As one of the largest female fertility preservation banks in South China, our data from the past 12 years demonstrate a considerable number of 3695 MII oocytes that have been cryopreserved from 456 patients. Despite the substantial number of patients undergoing fertility preservation, our follow-up data revealed a notably low utilization rate of cryopreserved oocytes. Specifically, among women who complete COS for oocyte cryopreservation, only 3 patients (1.29%) returned for thawing and subsequent embryo transfer, preventing us from obtaining further pregnancy outcomes to assess the effectiveness of oocyte cryopreservation. A meta-analysis study reported that the live birth rates after embryo cryopreservation and oocyte vitrification for fertility preservation were 41% (95% CI: 34–48, I2: 0%) and 32% (95% CI: 26–39, I2: 0%), respectively [19]. Numerous previous studies have also confirmed the efficacy and safety of both embryo and COS oocyte cryopreservation [2, 20–22]. In the latest ESHRE guidelines, published in 2020, oocyte and embryo cryopreservation were considered as established options for fertility preservation after puberty [23]. Although the overall number of patients who returned to use their COS or OTO-IVM oocytes (two patients only) is too low to draw any firm conclusions. The survival rates after OTO-IVM oocyte warming are noticeably lower compared to those of oocyte warming following oocyte vitrification after COS in patients with cancer [24]. Other studies shown consistently low survival rates after warming of OTO-IVM oocytes, with figures ranging from 33% to 68% [25–28].

OTC is an alternative option, and no longer considered experimental according to the recent ASRM guidelines [29]. OTO-IVM, as an innovative technique, can be combined with OTC to maximize fertility preservation. OTO-IVM retrieves immature follicles from excised ovarian tissue and can be performed at any time. In our study, OTO-IVM resulted in 1017 MII oocytes, generating a considerable number of oocytes available for cryopreservation in addition to the cryopreserved ovarian tissue. The maturation rate of these oocytes was 39.43%, which is comparable to previous studies [15, 30]. Mohd Faizal, et al. ’s study included a total of 12 OTO-IVM-related studies, involving 5724 immature oocytes that underwent IVM, of which 1937 oocytes matured, resulting in a maturation rate of 33.84% [30]. Additionally, in Segers, et al. ‘s study, the overall number of COC collected from excised ovarian specimens amounted to 1220 from 77 patients. OTO-IVM resulted in 494 MII oocytes, and the average maturation rate of these oocytes was 39% [15].

Obviously, the oocyte maturation rate of OTO-IVM is lower than that of COS in our study. The lower maturation rate may be related to several factors. Firstly, the age and clinical situation of women at the time of diagnosis may influence the type of fertility preservation chosen. In our study, the majority of patients undergoing OTO-IVM were young girls requiring fertility preservation due to the need for hematopoietic stem cell transplantation for severe thalassemia. It is well recognized that there are structural and functional differences in ovaries before and after puberty. The prepubertal ovary demonstrates limited development, with reduced volume, constrained follicular reserve, and immature granulosa cell function [31]. While oocyte developmental capacity shows progressive enhancement with age, it remains below optimal level [32]. Pubertal maturation induces significant ovarian volume expansion and functional enhancement. Gn-mediated granulosa cell differentiation establishes a steroidogenic microenvironment that supports synchronized oocyte nuclear-cytoplasmic maturation through growth factor signaling [33]. Accordingly, we have statistically analyzed the OTO-IVM oocytes maturation rates across different age groups. The IVM maturation rate for adults was 34.94%, while it was 40.51% for pediatric girls. According to the literature, the average maturation rate of OTO-IVM in prepubertal ovaries is as low as 22% [15]. Indeed, pre-pubertal ovaries contain a significant number of abnormal follicles, which is comparatively higher than in adult ovaries. A considerable portion of these follicles may be inherently defective in growth, and the enclosed oocytes may be incapable of reinitiating meiosis [13, 34].

Secondly, the biological effects introduced by different transport conditions might influence OTO-IVM efficiency. The results presented in this article are derived from ovarian tissue or follicles that have been transported at 4 °C for no more than 1 h. In the past, for female patients with confirmed ovarian metastatic tumors, we would directly aspirate follicles from the excised ovarian tissue in the operating room and then transport them to the laboratory under 4 °C for IVM and vitrification procedures. Our data revealed a significant increase in the rate of denuded oocytes in these OTO-IVM cases, suggesting that the interaction between oocytes and cumulus granulosa cells may be compromised. Commencing from 2020, our protocol for OTO-IVM has incorporated the transport of ovarian tissue to the laboratory under cold temperatures, ensuring that the transit duration does not surpass 1 h. Several studies suggested that ovarian tissue can be transported at cold temperatures for several hours while still maintaining the integrity and reproductive potential of the primordial follicles within the tissue [25, 35, 36]. However, there are also studies suggesting that low temperature transportation of ovarian tissue may have a negative impact on the success rate of IVM [37, 38].

Thirdly, COCs collected via OTO-IVM originate from small antral follicles, which are less developed [39]. Studies have indicated that follicle size is correlated with the stage of oocyte development [40], and oocytes can be retrieved from follicles with a diameter greater than 6 mm and matured in vitro within 48 h [39]. Consequently, we administered short-term Gn prior to excising ovarian tissue to promote follicular development and obtain more mature oocytes. Given the wide age range of the OTO-IVM population, we performed age and AMH stratified analysis. The results suggested that compared to not using Gn before oophorectomy, the short-term use of Gn before oophorectomy significantly improved the IVM maturation rate (38.00% vs. 51.90%, P < 0.001). However, more clinical and experimental data are needed to verify the value of short-term application of Gn in OTO-IVM.

It is essential to note several other limitations of the current study. Firstly, the study was retrospective in nature, and data regarding fertility preservation have been collected over a twelve-year period. This implied that there was a greater possibility that any alterations related to products other than the IVM or vitrification medium and culture, or even environmental changes, could have influenced the results. Secondly, there was the limited sample size, mainly concerning patient return rates, which were rather low. Therefore, we couldn’t draw any firm conclusions.

Conclusions

Oocyte cryopreservation has been increasingly supported by research evidence for its efficacy, with the highest efficiency achieved through vitrification of oocytes obtained after COS. OTO-IVM oocyte cryopreservation is a promising appended technique. Our results suggested that the use of Gn before oophorectomy positively influences IVM outcomes, highlighting its importance in fertility preservation protocols. The OTO-IVM method requires refinement to enhance oocyte maturation and achieve clinical pregnancies and live births. Further research is warranted to optimize OTO-IVM techniques.
