Effect of endoscopic screening for non-cardia gastric cancer: a 12-year report of a population-based randomized trial
Authors: Mengfei Liu, Wenlei Yang, Chuanhai Guo, Zhen Liu, Fenglei Li, Anxiang Liu, Haijun Yang, Lin Shen, Qi Wu, Liping Duan, Hui Wang, Hongrui Tian, Chao Shi, Yaqi Pan, Ying Liu, Fangfang Liu, Hong Cai, Zhonghu He, Yang Ke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe benefit of endoscopy for non-cardia gastric cancer (GC) prevention lacks evidence from randomized controlled trials (RCTs). This study aimed to evaluate the effect of endoscopic screening for non-cardia GC through an RCT.MethodsWe conducted a cluster RCT in rural Hua County, northern China (ClinicalTrials.gov, NCT01688908). A total of 668 villages were randomly selected and assigned to either undergo upper gastrointestinal endoscopic screening (screening group) or no screening (control group). Permanent residents aged 45-69 years were enrolled from January 2012 to September 2016. Non-cardia GC mortality between the two groups was compared in intention-to-treat, per-protocol, and subgroup analyses. Poisson regression fitted with generalized estimating equations was used, adjusting for baseline characteristics and accounting for village clustering.ResultsThe study enrolled 17,104 participants in the screening group, of whom 15,165 (88.7%) underwent endoscopy, and 16,743 in the control group. After a maximum follow-up of 12 years, non-cardia GC mortality was 13.1 per 100,000 person-years in the screening group and 13.5 per 100,000 person-years in the control group. Intention-to-treat analysis showed a 15% lower non-cardia GC mortality in the screening group compared with the control group (adjusted rate ratio [aRR], 0.85 [95% CI: 0.49-1.50]), although the difference was statistically non-significant. In the per-protocol analysis, the aRR for non-cardia GC mortality was 0.70 (95% CI: 0.38-1.29). A subgroup analysis of the screening group restricted to participants who complied with screening protocol and received timely treatment for detected malignancies yielded similar results, with an aRR of 0.66 (95% CI: 0.35-1.22); neither of these analyses reached statistical significance.ConclusionsIn this trial, endoscopic screening was associated with a non-significant reduction in non-cardia GC mortality; larger trials in higher-incidence settings are needed to confirm a benefit.Keywords: Randomized controlled trial, Gastric cancer, Endoscopy, Population-based Screening

Background

Gastric cancer (GC) ranks fifth in both incidence and mortality worldwide [1]. The overall trend in GC incidence has been shown a decline over the past decades, yet the burden remains high in Asia, particularly in China, where nearly 40% of global new cases of and deaths from GC occur [2]. The early stage of GC is relatively asymptomatic and many cases are diagnosed at advanced stages, which leads to unsatisfactory prognosis. The 5-year relative survival of GC in China is about 35% [3].

GC is typically categorized as cardia and non-cardia subtypes, with the latter comprising 73% of total GC cases globally [4]. Chronic Helicobacter pylori (H. pylori) infection is considered one of the major risk factors for non-cardia GC, and its eradication may reduce the risk of developing GC [5]. However, the eradication of H. pylori in asymptomatic populations remains controversial because of concerns about potential antibiotic resistance and gut microbial dysbiosis [6]. Secondary prevention through endoscopic screening, which enables early detection and treatment of early-stage gastric lesions, has been considered an important strategy for reducing population mortality from GC and implemented in national screening programs in Japan and South Korea for decades; however, the evidence base for these programs has not been established through randomized controlled trials (RCTs) [7–9]. Observational studies conducted in East Asia have shown that upper gastrointestinal endoscopic screening was associated with a reduction in GC mortality risk by 20 to 60% [10–13]. To date, however, evidence from RCTs supporting the effectiveness of endoscopic screening for non-cardia GC is still lacking, which hinders the development of robust screening guidelines and broader implementation.

In January 2012, we initiated a population-based cluster RCT designed primarily evaluating the effectiveness of endoscopic screening for esophageal cancer in China (ESECC trial, ClinicalTrials.gov registration number: NCT01688908). In addition to examining the esophagus, a comprehensive endoscopic inspection of the entire stomach was performed through the upper gastrointestinal endoscopy. Using long-term follow-up data from the ESECC trial, we conducted a secondary analysis to evaluate the effect of endoscopic screening on non-cardia GC mortality.

Methods

Trial design and participants recruitment

The ESECC trial was conducted in rural Hua County, located in the Taihang Mountain region in northern China. The age-standardized incidence rate of GC in this county was 12.34/100,000 in 2018, lower than the national level [14]. The trial’s rationale, study design, and 9-year interim results for the primary outcome are detailed elsewhere [15, 16]. Briefly, 668 villages with population size ranging from 500 to 3,000 were randomly selected. Using a blocked randomization procedure stratified by village population size and employing a block size of 2, these villages were then allocated in a 1:1 ratio to either the screening or the control group. Permanent residents of the selected villages were eligible for the study if they aged 45 to 69 years, had not undergone an endoscopic examination in the past 5 years, had no history of cancer, mental disorder, or infection with HBV, HCV, or HIV, and consented to complete all study procedures.

Baseline recruitment was conducted from January 2012 to September 2016. Within each randomized village, recruitment followed an identical procedure in both groups: village leaders announced the study objectives and procedures through broadcasts and bulletin boards, and residents who met the eligibility criteria, expressed willingness to participate, and provided informed consent were enrolled. After enrollment but before any endoscopic examination, a computer-aided one-on-one questionnaire investigation was conducted for all participants by trained interviewers to collect data on potential risk factors for upper gastrointestinal cancers.

Screening procedures

After enrollment, participants in the screening group received standard upper gastrointestinal endoscopy (GIF-Q150, Olympus, Japan) under topical pharyngeal anesthesia, performed by qualified and experienced endoscopists following national screening guideline in China [17]. A comprehensive endoscopic examination was performed sequentially from the gastric fundus to the body, angle, antrum, and pylorus. All regions were photographed, and suspicious lesions were biopsied. Biopsy specimens were fixed in 10% formaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin. All biopsy slides were independently reviewed by two experienced pathologists, and discordant diagnoses were resolved by consensus. Pathologic diagnoses were categorized as dysplasia (mild, moderate, or severe), carcinoma in situ (CIS), or invasive carcinoma. Participants were eligible for endoscopic surveillance after baseline screening if they met any of the following criteria: (1) mild-to-moderate dysplasia detected in the upper gastrointestinal tract, including the esophagus, cardia, non-cardia stomach, and duodenum; (2) abnormal endoscopic findings, mainly referring to iodine-unstained lesions in the esophagus; (3) participants with malignant lesions (i.e., severe dysplasia and above [SDA]) who had not received treatment before surveillance. The surveillance examinations were centrally coordinated and arranged according to the predefined project schedule after baseline recruitment and screening. In total, 1,534 eligible participants were invited to undergo surveillance endoscopy. Of these, 998 (65.1%) completed the surveillance endoscopy, and the median interval between the baseline screening and this surveillance examination was 4.4 years. Individuals diagnosed with malignant lesions in the stomach during baseline or surveillance screening were informed of their diagnosis and received medical treatment advice from the screening provider.

In the control group, participants did not undergo endoscopic examination but received identical epidemiological surveys and follow-up procedures as those in the screening group.

Treatment data collection

The detailed treatment information for screening-detected patients was collected from the New Rural Cooperative Medical Scheme (NCMS) claims data. The NCMS is a government-run health insurance program in rural China with nearly 100% coverage in the Hua County, and it has been proved to be a reliable source for cancer diagnosis and treatment [18]. For participants without NCMS records, face-to-face interviews with a semi-structure questionnaire were conducted to confirm their therapeutic procedures [19]. Timely treatment for screening-detected malignant lesions was defined as undergoing anti-cancer therapy within six months after pathologic diagnosis.

Study outcome and follow-up

The prespecified outcome evaluated in this secondary analysis was mortality from non-cardia GC (ICD-10 C16.1 to C16.9). Deaths were identified through two approaches: active yearly door-to-door interviews conducted by village doctors in the targeted villages, and passive linkage with local electronic registry data from the Death Registry maintained by the Center for Disease Control and Prevention of Hua County. The cause of death was further ascertained through cancer diagnostic information derived from the Hua County NCMS claims data. Records of deaths and cancers from these data sources were matched using participants’ personal identifiers. When discrepancies were found between information from active interviews and the passive registry databases, or when inconsistencies existed in cancer diagnosis and cause of death, an independent team of interviewers blinded to participants’ randomization group verified the information by contacting the participant or their first-degree relatives. The completeness of this follow-up framework has been previously evaluated [18, 20].

Statistical analysis

The ESECC trial was designed to enroll 32,337 participants, providing 80.4% power to detect a 30% reduction of advanced esophageal cancer incidence over a 10-year period at a one-sided significance level of 0.025 [16]. Non-cardia GC mortality was not prespecified in the 2012 trial registration. The analytic plan for the present study was finalized after the database was locked following the last follow-up on May 31, 2025, and data analysis was subsequently conducted. Baseline characteristics were summarized using counts, percentages, medians, and interquartile ranges. In this study, time-to-event analyses were performed. Person-years were calculated from the date of enrollment to the date of death or the date of last follow-up, whichever came first. Due to practical constraints of field implementation, the timing and pace of enrollment differed between the screening and control groups; therefore, follow-up was capped at 12 years in all analyses to ensure comparability between groups.

The main analysis utilized the intention-to-treat approach, in which participants were grouped according to their assignment. Additionally, exploratory per-protocol and subgroup analyses were conducted. The per-protocol analysis examined the effectiveness of undergoing the upper gastrointestinal endoscopic screening. For this analysis, the screening group was restricted to compliers, defined as participants who completed baseline upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and obtained a valid pathological diagnosis. A subgroup analysis was performed to evaluate the effectiveness of screening without treatment refusals, restricting the screening group to compliers and those who received timely treatment for detected malignancies.

Mortality rates were calculated as events per person-years, and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were estimated using a Poisson regression model with log link and the logarithm of person-years as an offset [21]. Rate ratios (RRs) and rate differences (RDs) with 95% CIs for non-cardia GC mortality between the two groups were estimated using Poisson regression fitted with generalized estimating equations (GEE), with the logarithm of person-years as an offset [22, 23]. Village was specified as the clustering unit, and robust variance estimation used to account for within-village correlation (Additional File 1: Method S1). In the Poisson regression, baseline imbalanced risk factors were adjusted for, including age at enrollment, sex, body mass index, family history of GC, cigarette smoking, ingestion of leftover food, salted food intake, regularity of eating, and unexplained weight loss (see Additional File 1: Method S2 for details). The number needed to invite (NNI) and the number needed to screen (NNS) to prevent one non-cardia GC death were calculated as the inverse of the absolute risk difference in cumulative mortality between two groups. This absolute risk difference was derived from cumulative mortality curves estimated using the Kaplan-Meier method (calculated as one minus the survival function) and adjusted via inverse probability of treatment weighting (IPTW) [24] to account for baseline covariate imbalances. Weights were estimated from propensity scores obtained via a multivariable logistic regression model, and smoothing was applied to the curves to improve visualization of underlying trends.

To evaluate the robustness of our findings, we performed several exploratory sensitivity analyses, including a multivariable Fine-Gray competing risk model, in which competing events were not treated as censoring events; an IPTW-based weighted Poisson regression model; piecewise Poisson regression and stratified Cox regression models, with the follow-up period split at the time point at which the cumulative mortality curves for the two groups appeared to cross; and a model further adjusted for calendar year of enrollment. Fixed-timepoint follow-up analyses were conducted with follow-up capped at annual timepoints from 5 to 12 years; person-years were calculated up to each timepoint, and adjusted RRs were estimated using Poisson regression consistent with the main analysis. We additionally conducted instrumental variable (IV) analyses as sensitivity analyses, using randomization assignment as the instrument and adjusting for the same covariates as in the main analysis, to estimate the effects of completing screening and of completing screening and receiving timely treatment on non-cardia GC mortality [25]. Exploratory stratified analyses were conducted by age (45 to 59 or 60 to 69 years), sex (female or male), cigarette smoking (yes or no), and alcohol consumption (yes or no) to examine whether the associations differed across subgroups. Inter-group heterogeneity in RRs was assessed using Wald tests for interaction. Data cleaning, mortality rate estimation (stptime command), and Fine-Gray competing risk regression analyses (stcrreg command) were performed using Stata 16.0. All other statistical analyses and visualizations were conducted in R 4.5.2 using the tableone (version 0.13.2), geepack (version 1.3.13), ggsurvfit (version 1.2.0), and ggplot2 (version 4.0.0) packages.

Results

Study subjects

Figure 1 shows the trial profile. A total of 33,948 individuals (17,151 in the screening group and 16,797 in the control group) were enrolled. In accordance with prespecified criteria, participants with a pre-enrollment history of cancer identified during follow-up were excluded (47 [0.3%] in the screening group and 54 [0.3%] in the control group), resulting in 17,104 participants in the screening group and 16,743 in the control group. Baseline characteristics like age, sex, family history of GC, and cigarette smoking were well-balanced between the groups, others potential risk factors showed significant differences in distribution (Table 1). The median follow-up was 11.1 and 9.4 years for screening and control participants respectively, with complete follow-up achieved for all participants.

Among the screening participants, 15,165 (88.7%) underwent upper gastrointestinal endoscopy and had valid pathologic diagnosis at baseline. Through both baseline and surveillance screening, 15 cases of non-cardia malignant lesions were identified, with corresponding detection rates of 0.08% at baseline screening and 0.30% during surveillance, respectively. Among these 15 cases, 7 (46.7%) were diagnosed with early-stage non-cardia GC (including severe dysplasia, CIS, or intramucosal carcinoma). Of these 15 cases, 3 (20.0%) did not receive timely anti-cancer therapy within six months after receiving pathologic diagnosis. Among these 3 participants, 2 failed to have malignant lesions detected in the subsequent diagnostic test, and both remained alive until the end of follow-up. The remaining participant received anti-cancer treatment 1.8 years after screening and died from non-cardia GC 3.6 years after baseline screening.

Non-cardia GC-specific mortality

During the follow-up period, 24 participants in the screening group and 22 participants in the control group died of non-cardia GC. In the intention-to-treat analysis, the mortality rates per 100,000 person-years were 13.1 (95% CI: 8.8 to 19.5) in the screening and 13.5 (95% CI: 8.9 to 20.5) in the control group, corresponding to 1.98 fewer deaths (95% CI: -8.48 to 4.53 deaths) per 100,000 person-years in the screening group and a 15% lower risk of non-cardia GC mortality (adjusted RR, 0.85 [95% CI: 0.49 to 1.50]). Among screening compliers, the per-protocol analysis showed 4.06 fewer deaths (95% CI: -9.85 to 1.73 deaths) per 100,000 person-years and a 30% lower risk of non-cardia GC mortality (adjusted RR, 0.70 [95% CI: 0.38 to 1.29]). In the subgroup analysis restricted to compliers and those who received timely treatment for detected malignant lesions, the adjusted RD was -4.64 (95% CI: -10.18 to 0.89) deaths per 100,000 person-years and the adjusted RR was 0.66 (95% CI: 0.35 to 1.22) (Table 2). The adjusted NNI to prevent one death from non-cardia GC within 12 years was 1,190. The adjusted NNS in the per-protocol and subgroup analyses were 926 and 877, respectively (Table 2).

Sensitivity and stratified analyses

Sensitivity analyses using different analytic approaches showed consistent results and are detailed in Additional File 1: Table S1. In the intention-to-treat analyses, the relative effect estimates were as follows: competing-risk sub-hazard ratio (SHR), 0.81 (95% CI: 0.46 to 1.43); IPTW-weighted Poisson RR, 0.86 (95% CI: 0.49 to 1.52); shared frailty HR, 0.79 (95% CI: 0.44 to 1.44); piecewise Poisson overall RR, 0.79 (95% CI: 0.45 to 1.38); stratified Cox overall HR, 0.79 (95% CI: 0.45 to 1.41); and RR, 0.78 (95% CI: 0.43 to 1.40), after additional adjustment for calendar year of enrollment. In fixed-timepoint follow-up analyses, person-years were generally comparable between the two groups, particularly during shorter follow-up periods; adjusted RRs showed some variation across timepoints, possibly reflecting random fluctuation given the small number of non-cardia GC deaths at each restricted timepoint (Additional File 1: Table S2). In the IV analyses, the adjusted RRs were 0.77 (95% CI: 0.40 to 1.47) for completing screening and 0.74 (95% CI: 0.38 to 1.42) for completing screening and receiving timely treatment. Stratified analyses by age, sex, cigarette smoking, and alcohol consumption are illustrated in the Additional File 1: Figure S3, and no significant differences were observed across groups.

Discussion

Survival of non-cardia GC patients remains dismal due to the absence of asymptomatic nature of early-stage disease, which usually precludes the possibility of curative treatment at initial diagnosis. Endoscopic screening enables the detection and treatment of early-stage non-cardia lesions, and has been adopted as a nationwide screening program in countries with high incidence of GC. Several observational studies have indicated that population-level endoscopic screening could reduce non-cardia GC mortality [10–13]. Among which, two population-based cohort studies conducted in China demonstrated that participants who underwent endoscopic screening experienced approximately 60% reduction in non-cardia GC mortality, compared to those who were not screened [12, 13]. However, both studies relied on existing national screening programs not specifically designed to evaluate endoscopic screening effectiveness. Participants were non-randomly assigned but recruited from communities with differing GC risk or grouped by prior screening participation. Selection bias may have overestimated benefits, as only the screened group excluded individuals with upper gastrointestinal cancer history. Additionally, outcome assessment blinding was unreported. Therefore, large-scale population-based RCTs are essential for providing convincing evidence. In this report, by utilizing endoscopic screening and longitudinal follow-up data from the ESECC trial, we presented, for the first time, evidence from a large-scale, population-based RCT showing that the point estimate was consistent with a possible benefit of endoscopic screening for non-cardia GC, although statistical significance was not reached.

We launched a population-based RCT in a well-known high-incidence area of esophageal cancer in rural China in 2012. The interim analysis with 9-year follow-up data suggested that population-level chromoendoscopy reduced esophageal cancer mortality by approximately 20%, providing the first evidence supporting endoscopic screening for esophageal cancer prevention [16]. Given that upper gastrointestinal endoscopy simultaneously examines both the esophagus and stomach, and that all ESECC trial participants underwent identical post-screening management and follow-up protocols, we extended our analysis to evaluate the effectiveness of endoscopic screening for non-cardia GC.

In this trial, village-level cluster randomization and open-label recruitment were employed to enroll eligible participants due to the practical constraints of the study area and infeasibility of blinding for endoscopic procedures. However, this approach may have introduced selection bias, as the screening group comprised individuals who expressed interest in endoscopic examination, whereas the control group did not. Therefore, although age, sex, and cigarette smoking were balanced at the baseline between the two groups, several potential risk factors showed imbalances, including body mass index, ingestion of leftover food, salted food intake, regularity of eating, and unexplained weight loss. Consequently, multivariable analysis with adjustments for imbalanced baseline covariates were necessary [26]. In addition, the cumulative mortality risk curves were adjusted for baseline imbalances using the IPTW Kaplan-Meier method, facilitating a more balanced comparison between groups and reducing potential discrepancies with the multivariable regression models.

Based on a maximum follow-up period of 12 years, our findings showed a point estimate consistent with a possible benefit of endoscopic screening for non-cardia GC, corresponding to a 15% lower mortality, although statistical significance was not reached. Results of the adjusted per-protocol and subgroup analyses, which estimated the impact of screening if all randomly assigned participants had completed the screening and had not refused treatment, yielded larger estimated reductions; however, statistical significance was not achieved for these estimates. Given the non-significant result in the main intention-to-treat analysis, these estimates should be interpreted with caution. We further evaluated estimates across different follow-up periods; however, those at restricted timepoints were based on fewer outcome events, and any variation should be interpreted cautiously. The 12-year follow-up was retained as the main analysis to maximize available person-time and outcome events, thereby improving statistical power and robustness.

In this trial, the timing of endoscopic surveillance was primarily determined by field implementation considerations, including the coordination of endoscopic resources, participant recall, and field workload. Surveillance examinations were initiated promptly after completion of baseline enrollment and screening, which lasted more than 4.5 years. This surveillance approach may have affected the overall assessment of the screening effect. Given the important role of endoscopic surveillance in cancer prevention and early detection, future implementation of cancer screening programs should optimize surveillance strategies, particularly through more personalized and risk-based approaches, to enhance the protective effect of screening [27, 28].

In real-world settings, the effectiveness of cancer screening may be influenced by multiple factors, including variations in disease profiles, population risk, and the application of screening technologies. The efficacy of cancer screening, such as the relative reduction in disease-specific mortality, is often considered stable across populations with varying disease burdens [29]. Our findings have important implications for GC control strategies in both non-high-risk and high-risk regions. A recent multicenter, community-based cluster randomized trial conducted by Xia et al. [30]. also evaluated upper gastrointestinal endoscopic screening and reported discrepancies between high-risk and non-high-risk areas. However, several considerations should be noted when interpreting their findings. First, gastric cardia and non-cardia cancers were not distinguished; however, given these cancers are distinct disease entities [31], combining them may obscure site-specific screening effects. Our study focused exclusively on non-cardia GC, providing direct evidence for this cancer. Second, Xia et al. excluded participants with endoscopy within the previous 3 years, whereas the preventive effect of endoscopy may extend beyond 5 years; our 5-year exclusion criterion may have further reduced bias from prevalent cases. Third, the reported regional discrepancies should be interpreted cautiously, as screening strategies differed across regions and no formal heterogeneity testing was reported. In non-high-risk areas, only high-risk individuals in the screening group were offered endoscopy; thus, the regional comparison may reflect differences in both baseline risk and screening strategy rather than a direct comparison of the screening effect across regions. It should be noted that, given the invasive nature and substantial costs associated with upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, implementing population level GC screening in areas with low- or even average-risk yields limited cost-effectiveness [32]. For regions with low-to-moderate GC incidence, a risk-based screening would be a more cost-effective strategy [27].

Upper gastrointestinal endoscopic screening offers the unique advantage of concurrent detection of both esophageal and gastric cancers, and evidence from this study and previous evaluation [16] suggests its protective benefits against both cancers. However, the high-incidence areas of these two cancers often do not coincide [33], the potential screening benefit for the other malignancy should also be simultaneously considered. With the development of generalizable risk stratification models for esophageal and gastric cancers [34–36], a risk-based joint screening strategy should be established. Specifically, integrated risk stratification tools should be utilized to identify individuals at high risk for either or both cancers who would benefit most from screening. The risk stratification thresholds should be tailored according to the local cancer burden patterns, e.g., the incidence rate ratios, of esophageal and gastric cancers across different regions, ensuring an optimal balance for both screening efficiency and healthcare equity [27,37].

A few limitations of this study should be noted. First, while our analyses showed that the point estimate was consistent with a possible benefit of endoscopic screening for non-cardia GC, the observed reduction did not achieve statistical significance, likely owing to limited statistical power for this endpoint, given the relatively small number of non-cardia GC mortality events in this non-high-risk area for GC. Multicenter randomized trials conducted in areas with varying GC burden, especially in those with an extremely high burden, are still warranted. Second, the open-label, cluster-level recruitment may have contributed to baseline differences between the two groups. Although we adjusted for potential confounders in multivariable analyses, residual confounding may remain, such as unmeasured baseline H. pylori infection. In addition, the unknown H. pylori status may limit the generalizability of our findings to settings with systematic H. pylori testing and eradication. Third, the relatively long surveillance interval may have attenuated the observed effect. Future trials incorporating optimized recruitment strategies, systematic assessment of H. pylori status, and risk-based personalized surveillance strategies are warranted to further validate our findings.

Conclusions

In conclusion, leveraging a population-based RCT, our study showed that the point estimate was consistent with a possible benefit of population-level endoscopic screening in reducing mortality from non-cardia GC, although statistical significance was not reached. For upper gastrointestinal cancers detectable by a single endoscopic examination, risk-based joint screening strategies that consider disease burden and resource allocation may help improve the efficiency of screening programs and warrant further evaluation.
