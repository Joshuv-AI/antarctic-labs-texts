Eugenol nanoemulsion protects chickens against S. Typhimurium infection by improving cecal barrier function and alleviating oxidative stress and inflammation
Authors: Jun Chen, Ruigang Zhou, Shaojie Yin, Xiaotong Qin, Zihan Wu, Mingjiang Liu, Ruonan Bo, Xiaolan Chen, Jing Wang, Xiaoyuting Xie, Jingui Li, Haifeng Yang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTo address the poor water solubility and high volatility of eugenol (EU), this study developed an EU nanoemulsion (NanoEU) and evaluated its anti-Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium) activity and protective effects against S. Typhimurium infection in vitro and in vivo. NanoEU showed a uniform milky-white appearance, an average particle size of 137.53 ± 5.07 nm, and a PDI of 0.23 ± 0.03. It exhibited in vitro antibacterial activity, with MIC and MBC values of 512 μg/mL and 1024 μg/mL, respectively. No hemolysis was observed in chicken erythrocytes at 128–1024 μg/mL, indicating preliminary biosafety. NanoEU also maintained stable appearance and unchanged antibacterial activity during 30 days of storage at 4 °C and 25 °C. A chicken model of S. Typhimurium infection was established to assess the protective roles of NanoEU. The results showed that NanoEU partially alleviated impaired body weight gain, reduced bacterial loads in the cecum, liver, and spleen, and mitigated histopathological damage. In addition, NanoEU improved cecal mucus secretion, upregulated tight junction-related gene and protein expression, alleviated local oxidative stress and inflammatory responses. In conclusion, NanoEU exhibited anti-S. Typhimurium activity, preliminary biosafety, and favorable storage stability. More importantly, it exerted both anti-infective and host-protective effects in vivo, suggesting its potential application in controlling S. Typhimurium infection and reducing antibiotic use in poultry.Keywords: Salmonella Typhimurium, Eugenol nanoemulsion, Chicken, Cecal barrier, Inflammatory response

Introduction

Salmonella enterica serovar Typhimurium (S. Typhimurium) infection is a major threat to poultry production and food safety worldwide. In chickens, S. Typhimurium colonization of the intestinal tract can impair growth performance, disrupt intestinal homeostasis, and promote bacterial dissemination to internal organs such as the liver and spleen. Moreover, poultry and poultry-derived products are recognized as important reservoirs for the transmission of S. Typhimurium to humans through the food chain (Zhou et al., 2023; Damasceno et al., 2024). Therefore, effective control of S. Typhimurium infection in poultry is important for both animal health and public health.

Currently, antibiotics and conventional management strategies are still widely used to control bacterial infections in poultry production. However, increasing concerns about antibiotic resistance, drug residues, and restrictions on antibiotic use are prompting the search for safer and more sustainable alternatives (Dlamini et al., 2024). Control of S. Typhimurium infection should not be limited to reducing bacterial burden alone. Intestinal colonization is often accompanied by epithelial injury, mucus barrier disruption, oxidative imbalance, and excessive inflammatory responses (Zhao et al., 2025). These pathological changes further exacerbate intestinal dysfunction and promote disease progression. Therefore, there is growing interest in developing functional interventions that can simultaneously inhibit pathogen colonization and protect intestinal barrier function of infected chickens (Azizi et al., 2026).

Eugenol (EU) is a natural phenolic compound mainly derived from clove. It has attracted considerable attention because of its antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and antioxidant activities (Elwakil et al., 2026). Previous studies have shown that EU can inhibit the growth of various pathogenic bacteria and regulate host inflammation and redox responses, suggesting its potential value in preventing and controlling intestinal infections (Shafiq et al., 2025; Randhawa et al., 2026; Narayanan et al., 2026). However, the practical application of EU is limited by its poor water solubility, volatility, and relatively low physicochemical stability. These limitations may reduce its dispersibility and biological activity in vivo. Nanoemulsion delivery systems are considered an effective strategy to improve the dispersibility, stability, and bioavailability of hydrophobic bioactive compounds. By reducing droplet size and increasing interfacial area, nanoemulsion can improve the delivery efficiency and functional performance of lipophilic substances in biological systems (Salama et al., 2026; de Carvalho et al., 2026; Gandhi et al., 2026).

In recent years, nanoemulsions containing plant-derived active ingredients have shown promising applications in animal health (Hu et al., 2023; Movahedi et al., 2024; Ji et al., 2026). However, the protective effects of NanoEU against S. Typhimurium infection in chickens remain poorly understood. This is especially true for bacterial colonization in visceral organs, cecal mucus barrier damage, changes in tight junction-related gene expression, oxidative status, and local inflammatory responses. Therefore, evaluating cecal barrier status and redox and inflammatory responses may help to better clarify the in vivo protective effects of eugenol nanoemulsion during infection.

Therefore, this study aimed to investigate the protective effect of NanoEU in a chicken model of S. Typhimurium infection. First, the physicochemical properties, storage stability, antibacterial activity, and preliminary biosafety of NanoEU were evaluated. Subsequently, body weight changes, bacterial colonization in the cecum, liver and spleen, organ damage, histopathological changes, cecal mucus secretion, the expression of tight junction-related genes and proteins, antioxidant status, and inflammatory gene expression were systematically assessed in infected chickens. Overall, this study was designed to determine whether NanoEU could alleviate bacterial colonization in the cecum, liver and spleen and cecal barrier damage caused by S. Typhimurium infection, and to provide experimental evidence supporting the potential application of NanoEU as a novel strategy for controlling S. Typhimurium infection in poultry.

Materials and methods

Materials and strains

Eugenol (EU, purity ≥ 98.5%) was purchased from Macklin Biochemical Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Tween 80 was purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). Luria-Bertani (LB) broth, Mueller–Hinton broth (MHB) and Mueller–Hinton agar (MHA) were purchased from Qingdao Hope Bio-Technology Co., Ltd. (Qingdao, China). The primary antibodies used were anti-Claudin-1 (Invitrogen, 71-7800), anti-ZO-1 (Invitrogen, 40-2200), anti-Occludin (Invitrogen, 40-4700), and anti-β-actin. The HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody was purchased from QuanShiJin Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Beijing, China). β-actin (4970T) was obtained from Cell Signaling Technology (Danvers, MA, USA). Polyvinylidene fluoride (PVDF) membranes were purchased from Merck Millipore (Billerica, MA, USA). The enhanced chemiluminescence (ECL) kit was obtained from NCM Biotech (Suzhou, China). DMEM medium was purchased from Hyclone Laboratories (Logan, UT, USA), and fetal bovine serum (FBS) was obtained from Gibco (Grand Island, NY, USA).

The coded quality-control strain S162, designated as S. Typhimurium according to the collaborator’s information, was used in this study. It was supplied by a commercial collaborator under a confidentiality agreement. Prior to use, MALDI Biotyper analysis identified the strain at the genus level as Salmonella sp., with a best-match score of 2.347 (Supplementary Figure S1). The strain was freshly cultured in Luria–Bertani broth at 37°C before animal challenge.

Preparation of NanoEU

The preparation method of NanoEU was modified from a previously reported tea tree oil nanoemulsion preparation method (Wei et al., 2021). NanoEU was prepared as follows. EU (6%, w/w) and Tween 80 (5%, w/w) were weighed at room temperature and mixed thoroughly using a magnetic stirrer. Pure water was then slowly added dropwise to the mixture. The mixture was stirred continuously at 400 rpm for 10 min to obtain 10 mL of crude emulsion. The crude emulsion was then sonicated using a cell disruption sonication system (power, 400 W; frequency, 25 kHz; amplitude, 25%; sonication time, 15 min) to obtain NanoEU, which was stored at 4°C until use. The theoretical EU content of NanoEU was 6% (w/w), and the doses used in the animal experiment were calculated based on this eugenol content.

To select the appropriate Tween 80 concentration, NanoEU was prepared with different Tween 80 concentrations while the EU concentration and other preparation parameters were kept constant. Briefly, EU was fixed at 6% (w/w), and Tween 80 was added at 3.0%, 3.5%, 4.0%, 4.5%, 5.0%, 5.5%, or 6.0% (w/w). Pure water was added to a final volume of 10 mL, and the emulsions were prepared using the same procedures described above. Particle size and polydispersity index (PDI) were measured, with each sample tested in triplicate. A particle size below 300 nm and a PDI below 0.3 were considered acceptable criteria for NanoEU preparation in this study (Danaei et al., 2018).

Characterization of NanoEU

The NanoEU samples were diluted 10-fold with PBS to achieve adequate light transmittance and avoid interference from multiple scattering effects. The average particle size and PDI were determined using a dynamic light scattering particle size analyzer.

Transmission electron microscopy (TEM)

The NanoEU sample was diluted 10-fold, and a small amount of the diluted sample was dropped onto a copper grid. After drying at room temperature, the microstructure of the NanoEU was observed using a transmission electron microscope (Tecnai 12, Philips), and representative images were recorded.

Determination of MIC and MBC

The minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) and minimum bactericidal concentration (MBC) of NanoEU were determined according to the guidelines of the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) (Schuetz et al., 2025). Briefly, NanoEU was serially two-fold diluted in MHB in 96-well microplates to obtain 10 serial concentrations. An equal volume of bacterial suspension in the logarithmic growth phase was added to each well to yield a final inoculum of 1 × 106 CFU/mL. After incubation at 37 °C for 20 h, bacterial growth was visually assessed, and the lowest concentration with no visible growth was defined as the MIC.

For MBC determination, 10 μL aliquots from wells showing no visible growth were plated onto drug-free MHA plates and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h. The lowest concentration that resulted in no colony formation was defined as the MBC.

Hemolysis assay

To evaluate the hemocompatibility of NanoEU, a hemolysis assay was performed to determine its hemolytic effect on red blood cells (Yang et al., 2026). Fresh anticoagulated chicken blood was collected and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. After the removal of the supernatant, the red blood cells were washed three times with sterile PBS until the supernatant became clear. The washed red blood cells were then resuspended in PBS to prepare a 2% (v/v) erythrocyte suspension. NanoEU solutions at different concentrations (128, 256, 512, 1024, 2048, and 4096 μg/mL) were mixed with an equal volume of the erythrocyte suspension and incubated at 37 °C for 30 min. PBS-treated cells were used as the negative control, and 2% Triton X-100-treated cells were used as the positive control. After incubation, the samples were centrifuged, and the supernatants were collected for absorbance measurement at 576 nm.

Stability testing

The prepared NanoEU samples were stored at 4 °C and 25 °C. At 10 d intervals, the samples were observed for instability phenomena, such as phase separation and flocculation. The average particle size and PDI were measured and recorded. This evaluation was continued for 30 days.

Centrifugation stability

After 10-fold dilution of the NanoEU samples, they were centrifuged at 2000 rpm for 15 min. Equal volumes of samples were taken before and after centrifugation, and their absorbance was measured at 290 nm. In vivo experiment

Experimental design and animal treatment

One-day-old male layer chicks were obtained from the Jiangsu Institute of Poultry Science and housed in the animal facility of the College of Veterinary Medicine, Yangzhou University, China. During the experimental period, the chicks were fed a drug-free basal diet provided by the Jiangsu Institute of Poultry Science, China. The ingredients and calculated nutrient composition of the basal diet are shown in Supplementary Table 1. The chicks were raised under conventional conditions until 13 days of age, weighed, statistically analyzed, and randomly assigned to groups according to body weight using SPSS software. Ten chicks were included in each group, and a total of five groups were established: the blank control group (Con), model group (Mod), and low-, medium-, and high-dose NanoEU groups [S. Typhimurium + 12.5 mg/kg (NanoEU-L), S. Typhimurium + 25 mg/kg (NanoEU-M), and S. Typhimurium + 50 mg/kg (NanoEU-H)].

A single colony of S. Typhimurium was inoculated into LB broth and cultured at 37°C with shaking at 180 rpm for 20 h. The bacterial suspension was then centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min, washed with sterile PBS, and resuspended to a final concentration of 5 × 108 CFU/mL for subsequent use. The Con group was orally gavaged with 1 mL of normal saline daily, whereas the other groups were orally administered 1 mL of the bacterial suspension daily for 3 consecutive days. After the infection period, the chicks received the corresponding treatments for 5 consecutive days. During the experiment, the clinical symptoms and mortality of the chicks in each group were observed and recorded daily. At 21 days of age, the chicks were euthanised by cervical dislocation, and the liver, spleen, cecum, and cecal contents were collected for subsequent analyses. Average daily gain (ADG) was calculated for each chicken as follows: ADG = (final body weight - initial body weight) / experimental days. In this study, ADG was calculated using body weight on day 7 and day 0 and expressed as g/bird/day.

Histological and AB-PAS staining analysis

Liver, spleen, and cecum tissues were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for 24 h, dehydrated, paraffin-embedded, and sectioned (4 μm). Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) and observed under a light microscope.

Histopathological injury was semi-quantitatively scored (0–4) based on inflammatory infiltration, epithelial damage, tissue disorganization, congestion, hemorrhage, and necrosis (0, none; 4, severe). Five random fields per sample were evaluated, and the mean score was calculated in a blinded manner. For cecal morphometry, mucosal fold height and crypt depth were measured using ImageJ from five random fields per section.

For AB-PAS staining, cecal sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and stained with Alcian blue, periodic acid, and Schiff reagent, followed by nuclear counterstaining. Acidic mucins stained blue and neutral mucins magenta. AB-PAS-positive staining area in the cecal mucosa was quantified using ImageJ software. Five random fields were selected from each section, and the AB-PAS-positive area was calculated and used to evaluate cecal mucus barrier status.

Real-time quantitative PCR analysis

Total RNA was extracted from cecal tissues using TRIzol reagent (R401 and R701, Vazyme, Nanjing, China) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. RNA concentration and purity were determined using a microplate reader (Epoch, Biotek, USA), and the RNA was reverse-transcribed into cDNA using the HiScript IV RT SuperMix kit (R423, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Subsequently, real-time quantitative PCR (qPCR) was performed using 2 × ChamQ SYBR Green qPCR Master Mix (Q311-02, Vazyme, Nanjing, China). The primers for the target genes are listed in Table 1, with GAPDH used as the internal reference gene. All primers were synthesised by Sangon Biotech Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). The relative mRNA expression levels were calculated using the 2-ΔΔCt method.

Western blot analysis

Total protein was extracted from cecal tissues using precooled RIPA lysis buffer containing protease and phosphatase inhibitors. The protein concentration was determined using an Enhanced BCA Protein Assay Kit. Equal amounts of protein from each sample were mixed with 4 × loading buffer, boiled for denaturation, separated by 8–12% SDS-PAGE, and then transferred onto PVDF membranes. After blocking with 5% skim milk at room temperature, the membranes were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against Occludin, ZO-1, Claudin-1, and β-actin (all at 1:1000 dilution). After washing with TBST, the membranes were incubated with species-specific horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies (diluted 1:10000) at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescence reagent and quantified using ImageJ software. β-actin was used as the internal reference protein.

Measurement of oxidative stress markers in cecal tissues

Cecal tissue samples were placed on ice and homogenized with pre-cooled physiological saline at an appropriate weight-to-volume ratio. After homogenization, the samples were centrifuged at 4 °C, and the supernatant was collected for subsequent analysis. Using commercial kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, the activities of glutathione peroxidase (GSH-Px) and total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD), as well as the malondialdehyde (MDA) level, in cecal tissue were determined according to the manufacturer's instructions. All results were calculated according to the kit instructions and normalized to the tissue protein concentration.

Statistical analysis

Data are presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). The number of biological replicates is indicated in the figure legends or table footnotes. The normality of residuals was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test. Since the data met the assumptions required for parametric analysis, statistical comparisons were performed using Brown–Forsythe and Welch ANOVA, followed by Dunnett’s T3 multiple-comparisons test in GraphPad Prism 9. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Physicochemical characterization, antibacterial activity, and preliminary biosafety of NanoEU

Particle size and PDI are important indicators for evaluating emulsion stability. Smaller particle size enables droplets to better resist flocculation and aggregation caused by Brownian motion and gravity. PDI reflects the particle size distribution; a lower PDI indicates a more uniformly dispersed emulsion system and therefore better stability (Danaei et al., 2018). Fig. 1A shows the preparation procedure of NanoEU. To determine the appropriate Tween 80 concentration, NanoEU was prepared with different Tween 80 concentrations while the EU concentration and other preparation parameters were kept constant. As shown in Fig. 1B, the particle sizes of NanoEU prepared with 3.0%–6.0% Tween 80 were all below 300 nm. However, the PDI was relatively high when Tween 80 was added at 3.0%, indicating poor dispersion uniformity. When the Tween 80 concentration was increased to 5.0%, NanoEU showed a small particle size and a PDI below 0.30. Therefore, 5.0% Tween 80 was selected for subsequent experiments.

(B) Effect of Tween 80 concentration on the particle size and PDI of NanoEU. (C) Particle size distribution and PDI of NanoEU. (D) TEM image of NanoEU. (E) Representative images of chicken erythrocytes after incubation with NanoEU at different concentrations. (F) Hemolysis rates of chicken erythrocytes treated with NanoEU at different concentrations. Different letters indicate significant differences (p < 0.05).

The optimized NanoEU exhibited a homogeneous milky-white appearance without visible phase separation or precipitation. The mean particle size and PDI of NanoEU were 137.53 ± 5.07 nm and 0.23 ± 0.03, respectively (Fig. 1C). TEM observation further showed that NanoEU droplets were nanosized, approximately spherical, and relatively well dispersed (Fig. 1D). The droplet size observed by TEM was noticeably smaller than that measured by dynamic light scattering, which may be attributed to the fact that dynamic light scattering measures the hydrodynamic diameter of droplets in an aqueous dispersion, whereas TEM observation is performed on dried samples. NanoEU exhibited antibacterial activity against S. Typhimurium, with MIC and MBC values of 512 μg/mL and 1024 μg/mL, respectively (Table 2). According to the international standard ASTM F756-17, a hemolytic index of > 5% is classified as “hemolytic” (Thorarinsdottir et al., 2022). The chicken erythrocyte hemolysis assay showed that NanoEU caused less than 5% hemolysis at concentrations of 128–1024 μg/mL, indicating no hemolytic activity within this concentration range. However, the hemolysis rate increased at concentrations of 2048 and 4096 μg/mL (Fig. 1E-F). These results indicate that NanoEU possessed suitable physicochemical properties, anti-S. Typhimurium activity, and acceptable hemocompatibility at concentrations up to 1024 μg/mL.

Storage stability and antibacterial activity of NanoEU

As shown in Fig. 2A and C, NanoEU maintained a homogeneous milky-white appearance throughout 30 days of storage at both 4 °C and 25 °C, with no visible phase separation or sedimentation, indicating good physical stability. Particle size analysis showed that the Z-average diameter of NanoEU stored at 4 °C gradually increased from approximately 170 nm at day 0 to about 220 nm at day 30 (Fig. 2B). Meanwhile, the PDI increased from approximately 0.19 to 0.26, but remained below 0.30 throughout the storage period. A similar trend was observed at 25°C, with the Z-average diameter increasing from approximately 170 nm at day 0 to about 240 nm at day 30, while the PDI remained below 0.30 (Fig. 2D). These results suggest a moderate increase in droplet size and a slight broadening of the size distribution during storage, while the system still retained relatively good dispersibility.

(B) Changes in Z-average diameter and PDI of NanoEU during storage at 4 °C. (C) Appearance of NanoEU after storage at 25 °C for 0, 10, 20, and 30 days. (D) Changes in Z-average diameter and PDI of NanoEU during storage at 25 °C. (E) Changes in the centrifugal stability index (Ke) of NanoEU during storage. The centrifugal stability results further showed that the Ke value increased progressively with storage time (Fig. 2E), rising from approximately 12% at day 0 to about 16% at day 30. Significant differences were observed among several storage time points, whereas no significant difference was found between the day 20 and day 30 groups. Furthermore, the MIC and MBC of NanoEU against S. Typhimurium remained unchanged during storage at both 4 °C and 25 °C, with MIC and MBC values of 512 μg/mL and 1024 μg/mL, respectively, and no significant differences among the groups (Table 2). Collectively, although certain changes in particle size characteristics and Ke value were observed during storage, the appearance and antibacterial activity of NanoEU remained largely stable, demonstrating good storage stability at both 4 °C and 25 °C.

NanoEU partially improved growth-related responses and organ indices in S. Typhimurium-infected chickens

Fig. 3A shows the experimental design of the animal trial. In Fig. 3B, chickens in the Con group exhibited a steady increase in body weight, whereas S. Typhimurium challenge markedly impaired weight gain in the Mod group. To further describe growth-related responses during the infection and treatment period, initial body weight, final body weight, body weight gain (BWG), and average daily gain (ADG) were calculated (Supplementary Table 2). No significant difference in initial body weight was observed among groups, indicating comparable body weight before challenge. Compared with the Con group, the Mod group showed significantly reduced final body weight, BWG, and ADG. NanoEU-treated groups showed numerically higher final body weight, BWG, and ADG than the Mod group, especially in the NanoEU-H group.

(B) Changes in body weight of chickens in each group. (C–E) Spleen index, liver index, and bursa of Fabricius index. (F–H) Bacterial burdens in the cecum, spleen, and liver. Different letters indicate significant differences among groups (p < 0.05).

To further evaluate the effect of NanoEU on infection-associated organ injury, the spleen, liver, and bursa of Fabricius indices were determined. As shown in Fig. 3C-E, S. Typhimurium challenge significantly increased the spleen, liver, and bursa of Fabricius indices in the Mod group compared with the Con group. NanoEU treatment attenuated these alterations to different extents. In particular, the NanoEU-H group showed significantly lower spleen and liver indices than the Mod group, whereas the NanoEU-L and NanoEU-M groups showed only partial improvement. A decreasing trend in the bursa of Fabricius index was also observed after NanoEU treatment, although the effect was less pronounced than that observed for the spleen and liver. These results suggested that NanoEU alleviated infection-associated organ injury and showed a numerical, but not statistically significant, improvement in growth-related responses under S. Typhimurium challenge.

NanoEU reduced bacterial colonization in the cecum, spleen and liver of S. Typhimurium-infected chickens

To determine whether NanoEU affected bacterial colonization in vivo, bacterial burdens in the cecum, spleen and liver were quantified after S. Typhimurium challenge. As shown in Fig. 3F-H, the Mod group exhibited markedly higher bacterial loads in the cecum, spleen and liver than the Con group, indicating the successful establishment of S. Typhimurium infection. NanoEU treatment reduced bacterial colonization in these tissues, although the extent of reduction varied among the different dose groups. In the cecum, NanoEU-M and NanoEU-H significantly reduced bacterial burden compared with the Mod group (Fig. 3F). In the spleen, a significant decrease in bacterial burden was observed only in the NanoEU-H group compared with the Mod group (Fig. 3G). In the liver, all NanoEU-treated groups showed lower bacterial loads than the Mod group, with the strongest reduction observed in the NanoEU-H group (Fig. 3H). Overall, these results indicated that NanoEU effectively reduced S. Typhimurium colonization in infected chickens, particularly in the cecum and liver.

NanoEU alleviated organ lesions and histopathological damage in S. Typhimurium-infected chickens

H&E staining showed that the liver, cecum, and spleen in the Con group exhibited largely intact histological architecture, with no obvious pathological lesions (Fig. 4). Compared with the Con group, the Mod group showed more pronounced histopathological damage. In the liver, focal inflammatory cell infiltration and disorganization of hepatic cords were observed. In the cecum, the mucosal architecture was impaired, accompanied by increased inflammatory cell infiltration in the mucosa and lamina propria. In the spleen, focal lymphoid hyperplasia was observed. These findings indicated that S. Typhimurium infection induced marked inflammatory injury in the liver, cecum, and spleen of chickens.

After treatment with different doses of NanoEU, the pathological lesions in these tissues were alleviated to varying degrees. In the NanoEU-L group, mild inflammatory cell infiltration and local tissue disorganization were still present, but these changes were attenuated compared with those in the Mod group. In the NanoEU-M and NanoEU-H groups, tissue architecture was better preserved, and inflammatory lesions were markedly reduced. Semi-quantitative histopathological injury scores further confirmed these observations (Fig. 4B–D). Compared with the Con group, the Mod group showed significantly increased injury scores in the liver, cecum, and spleen. NanoEU treatment significantly reduced the liver and cecal injury scores in all treated groups compared with the Mod group (Fig. 4B, C). For the spleen, NanoEU-M and NanoEU-H significantly reduced the injury scores, whereas NanoEU-L showed a decreasing trend but no significant difference compared with the Mod group (Fig. 4D). Collectively, these results suggested that NanoEU treatment alleviated histopathological damage to different extents, with the NanoEU-M and NanoEU-H groups showing more pronounced overall histological improvement.

NanoEU improved cecal mucus barrier function in S. Typhimurium-infected chickens

As shown in Fig. 5A, AB-PAS staining revealed that, compared with the Con group, the Mod group exhibited shortened and disorganized cecal mucosal folds, focal disruption of epithelial continuity, and inflammatory cell infiltration. Meanwhile, AB-PAS-positive mucin staining was reduced and the staining intensity was weakened, indicating that S. Typhimurium infection impaired cecal mucus barrier integrity. In contrast, NanoEU-treated groups showed progressively improved mucosal morphology, with more intact epithelial structure, reduced inflammatory infiltration, and increased AB-PAS-positive staining, particularly in the NanoEU-M and NanoEU-H groups. Quantitative analysis further showed that the AB-PAS-positive area was significantly decreased in the Mod group compared with the Con group (Fig. 5B). NanoEU treatment increased the AB-PAS-positive area to different extents, with more evident improvement observed in the NanoEU-M and NanoEU-H groups. These findings were consistent with the histological observations, further confirming the protective effect of NanoEU on the cecal mucus barrier.

To further quantify cecal structural changes, cecal mucosal fold height, crypt depth, and the mucosal fold height/crypt depth ratio were measured (Supplementary Fig. S2). Because the cecum does not have typical villus structures like the small intestine, cecal mucosal fold height was measured instead of villus height. Compared with the Con group, S. Typhimurium challenge significantly reduced cecal mucosal fold height and the mucosal fold height/crypt depth ratio, indicating disruption of cecal mucosal architecture. NanoEU treatment increased cecal mucosal fold height to different extents, with the most pronounced improvement observed in the NanoEU-M group (Supplementary Fig. S2A). For crypt depth, no significant difference was observed between the Con and Mod groups, whereas NanoEU-L and NanoEU-M showed increased crypt depth compared with the Mod group (Supplementary Fig. S2B). The mucosal fold height/crypt depth ratio showed a recovery trend after NanoEU treatment, especially in the NanoEU-H group (Supplementary Fig. S2C). These results suggested that NanoEU improved cecal mucus barrier integrity and partially restored cecal mucosal architecture in S. Typhimurium-infected chickens.

NanoEU improved the expression of cecal tight junction–related genes and proteins in S. Typhimurium-infected chickens

To further evaluate the effect of NanoEU on cecal barrier function, the mRNA expression levels of tight junction-related genes and the protein expression levels of tight junction proteins were measured in cecal tissues. As shown in Fig. 6A–C, compared with the Con group, S. Typhimurium infection significantly reduced the mRNA expression of Occludin and ZO-1 in the Mod group, and also decreased Claudin-1 expression to some extent. This indicated that S. Typhimurium infection disrupted the expression of genes associated with the cecal epithelial barrier. After NanoEU treatment, Occludin expression increased, with a significant difference observed in the NanoEU-H group. The expression of ZO-1 gradually recovered with increasing NanoEU dose, and the improvement was more pronounced in the medium- and high-dose groups. Meanwhile, Claudin-1 showed a downward trend in the Mod group, whereas high-dose NanoEU treatment partially restored its expression.

Consistent with the qPCR results, Western blot analysis further confirmed the protective effect of NanoEU on tight junction protein expression (Fig. 6D–G). Compared with the Con group, the Mod group showed markedly reduced protein levels of Occludin, ZO-1, and Claudin-1. NanoEU treatment restored these protein levels to different extents. In particular, NanoEU-M and NanoEU-H significantly increased Occludin protein expression, while NanoEU-M significantly increased ZO-1 protein expression compared with the Mod group. Claudin-1 protein expression also showed a recovery trend after NanoEU treatment, with a more evident improvement in the NanoEU-M group. Collectively, these results suggested that NanoEU partially restored the expression of tight junction-related genes and proteins in the cecum of S. Typhimurium-infected chickens, thereby supporting its protective effect on cecal barrier function.

NanoEU improved the cecal antioxidant status in S. Typhimurium-infected chickens

As shown in Fig. 7, S. Typhimurium infection significantly disrupted the antioxidant status of the cecum. Compared with the Con group, the Mod group showed significantly reduced GSH-Px activity (Fig. 7A) and SOD activity (Fig. 7C). Simultaneously, the Mod group showed a significantly increased MDA content (Fig. 7B), indicating oxidative stress in the cecal tissue. NanoEU treatment alleviated these changes to varying degrees. For GSH-Px, the NanoEU-H group showed significantly higher activity than the Mod group, while the NanoEU-L and NanoEU-M groups showed only partial improvement (Fig. 7A). For MDA, all NanoEU treatment groups showed significantly lower MDA levels than the Mod group, with the reduction becoming more pronounced with increasing dose. Notably, there was no significant difference between the NanoEU-H group and the Con group (Fig. 7B). For SOD, only the NanoEU-H group showed a significant increase compared with the Mod group, while the NanoEU-L and NanoEU-M groups did not show significant recovery (Fig. 7C). These results suggested that NanoEU effectively alleviated S. Typhimurium-induced oxidative stress in the cecum, with the NanoEU-H group showing the strongest protective effect.

NanoEU attenuated the cecal inflammatory response in S. Typhimurium-infected chickens

As shown in Fig. 8, S. Typhimurium infection markedly altered the expression of inflammatory cytokines in the cecum. Compared with the Con group, the Mod group showed significantly lower IL-10 mRNA expression (Fig. 8A) and higher IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 mRNA expression (Fig. 8B-D), indicating a pronounced cecal inflammatory response. NanoEU treatment alleviated these changes. IL-10 expression increased after NanoEU treatment, with more evident recovery observed in the NanoEU-M and NanoEU-H groups. In contrast, IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 expression decreased to different extents, and the NanoEU-H group showed the strongest effect. These results suggested that NanoEU attenuated cecal inflammation in S. Typhimurium-infected chickens by increasing IL-10 and reducing IL-1β, TNF-α, and IL-6 expression.

Discussion

S. Typhimurium infection exerts multifaceted effects on poultry production. On the one hand, it compromises the growth performance and health status of chickens. On the other hand, S. Typhimurium can enter the food chain through poultry products, thereby posing broader and more persistent food safety risks (Thorarinsdottir et al., 2022; Shaji et al., 2023). Therefore, simple antibacterial activity alone is no longer sufficient to address the current challenges, and the development of interventions that combine antibacterial efficacy with host-protective effects has become an important direction in the control of S. Typhimurium infection in poultry (Obe et al., 2023). In the present study, NanoEU treatment partially alleviated the reduction in body weight gain in infected chickens, reduced organ indices in the liver, spleen, and bursa of Fabricius, and decreased S. Typhimurium colonization in the cecum, liver and spleen. Histopathological damage in the liver, spleen, and cecum was alleviated to different extents. Meanwhile, cecal mucus barrier function, the expression of tight junction-related genes and proteins, antioxidant status, and inflammatory responses were also improved. Taken together, these findings indicate that the effects of NanoEU are not limited to inhibiting S. Typhimurium activity, but rather involve simultaneous modulation of several interrelated pathological processes during infection, including intestinal barrier dysfunction, oxidative stress, and inflammation, thereby suggesting greater practical application value.

From the perspective of S. Typhimurium control, the reduction in bacterial burden in the cecum, liver and spleen represents one of the key findings of this study. In chicks infected with S. Typhimurium, persistent intestinal colonization followed by dissemination to visceral organs such as the liver and spleen is an important indicator of aggravated infection and increased transmission risk (Wang et al., 2024). The decreased bacterial loads in the cecum, liver, and spleen not only indicate that NanoEU reduced intestinal colonization, but also suggest that NanoEU may limit systemic dissemination of S. Typhimurium in vivo. This finding provides a reasonable explanation for the subsequent improvement in pathological changes, namely that when bacterial colonization within organs is reduced, the associated inflammation and tissue injury are correspondingly alleviated. Accordingly, the reduced organ indices and alleviated histopathological damage observed in the treated groups may be related to the inhibitory effect of NanoEU on bacterial colonization and dissemination in vivo.

With respect to intestinal protection, the present results indicate that NanoEU exerted a relatively clear protective effect on the cecum. AB-PAS staining showed that NanoEU improved cecal mucus secretion after infection. The mucus layer constitutes the first line of defense between intestinal epithelial cells and luminal pathogens. Insufficient mucus secretion or thinning of the mucus layer renders the epithelial surface more vulnerable to direct attack by pathogens and their virulence factors, thereby amplifying local tissue injury. For this reason, changes in the mucus barrier should be regarded as an important indicator for evaluating intestinal defense status (Dokladny et al., 2016; Chen et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2024). Meanwhile, the present study also showed increased expression of the tight junction-related genes ZO-1, Occludin, and Claudin-1, suggesting a protective effect of NanoEU on the cecal epithelial barrier. It should be noted, however, that the present study assessed tight junction-related gene and protein expression, but did not further evaluate their spatial distribution by immunofluorescence or immunohistochemistry. Furthermore, AB-PAS staining showed greater mucus recovery in the NanoEU-H group. At the same time, the recovery of tight junction-related gene expression was more pronounced in the NanoEU-H group. Therefore, combined with qPCR and Western blot results, the overall protective effect of NanoEU on cecal barrier function appeared to be stronger in the NanoEU-H group. Even so, the combined findings of improved mucus barrier status, improved the expression of tight junction-related genes and proteins, and reduced cecal histopathological injury collectively support a protective role of NanoEU in maintaining cecal barrier function (Shao et al., 2013). Previous studies have shown that S. Typhimurium infection can downregulate the expression of barrier-related factors such as ZO-1 and Occludin, thereby impairing intestinal mucosal barrier function, whereas interventions such as eugenol, astaxanthin, β-glucan, or probiotics have been reported to restore the expression of related genes or proteins to varying degrees and alleviate intestinal injury (Shao et al., 2013; Chen et al., 2025; Lin et al., 2025).

Another important finding of this study is that NanoEU alleviated S. Typhimurium-induced oxidative stress in the cecum. In the model group, the activities of GSH-Px and SOD were reduced, whereas MDA levels were increased, indicating that S. Typhimurium infection disrupted the balance between oxidation and antioxidation and induced lipid peroxidation damage (Li et al., 2024). This result is biologically plausible, because the intestine is a site where pathogen invasion, immune responses, and metabolic activity are highly concentrated. Under infectious conditions, the accumulation of reactive oxygen species and suppression of the antioxidant enzyme system can directly damage intestinal epithelial cells and the mucosal barrier, while also amplifying inflammatory responses and disturbing intestinal homeostasis (Li et al., 2024). Notably, NanoEU treatment improved this oxidative imbalance to different extents, with the most obvious effect observed in the high-dose group. Considering the inherent antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties of eugenol, together with the ability of nano-delivery systems to improve the dispersion and bioavailability of hydrophobic bioactive substances, the antioxidant protective effect observed in the present study is theoretically reasonable (Li et al., 2024). More importantly, these findings suggest that the role of NanoEU is not restricted to antibacterial activity alone, but also involves host protection. Under infectious conditions, oxidative stress often coexists with barrier damage and amplified inflammation. Thus, the ability of NanoEU to partially restore the oxidant and antioxidant balance may also provide a deeper explanation for its protective effect against cecal injury (Zhao et al., 2022b; Zheng et al., 2023).

The improvement in inflammatory responses by NanoEU further supports the above interpretation. During infection, inflammation has a dual role: on the one hand, an appropriate inflammatory response helps the host clear pathogens. On the other hand, excessive or persistent inflammation exacerbates epithelial damage, increases mucosal permeability, and, together with oxidative stress, impairs intestinal barrier function (Antunes et al., 2011; Ktsoyan et al., 2015; Zhang et al., 2017). In the present study, the expression of pro-inflammatory cytokines in the cecum was markedly elevated in the model group, indicating activation of the inflammatory response. After NanoEU treatment, the expression of pro-inflammatory factors showed a downward trend, suggesting that NanoEU alleviated infection-induced local inflammation (Peng et al., 2025). At the same time, the increase in IL-10 expression, particularly in the NanoEU-H group, further supported the immunomodulatory effect of NanoEU in the infected cecum.

Taken together, these findings suggest that the host-protective effect of NanoEU may be multifaceted. On the one hand, NanoEU reduced bacterial burdens in visceral organs, thereby decreasing persistent pathogen stimulation. On the other hand, it improved mucosal barrier status and oxidative imbalance, which may have reduced the amplification of inflammation. Previous studies have demonstrated that eugenol can inhibit the TLR4/MyD88/NF-κB-related inflammatory pathway and maintain the expression of tight junction proteins (Zhao et al., 2022b). Other natural bioactive substances have also been reported in chicken S. Typhimurium infection models to alleviate infection-associated tissue injury by reducing inflammation and preserving intestinal barrier integrity (Zheng et al., 2023).

From the perspective of translational application, another value of this study lies in the fact that NanoEU is not simply a natural-product additive, but rather a delivery system with defined physicochemical characteristics, preliminary biosafety, and good storage stability. Characterization results showed that this nanoemulsion possessed a relatively small and uniform particle size and exhibited clear MIC and MBC values against S. Typhimurium (Fu et al., 2022). In addition, the chicken erythrocyte hemolysis assay indicated that NanoEU caused less than 5% hemolysis within the concentration range of 128–1024 μg/mL, suggesting acceptable hemocompatibility within the effective antibacterial concentration range. Storage stability testing further showed that NanoEU maintained a homogeneous milky-white appearance without visible phase separation or precipitation during 30 days of storage at 4 °C and 25 °C. Although moderate changes in particle size characteristics and Ke value were observed over time, the MIC and MBC against S. Typhimurium remained unchanged, indicating that the antibacterial activity of NanoEU was well preserved during storage. These findings mean that the present study did not merely address whether eugenol itself is effective, but rather explored whether nano-delivered eugenol has more realistic application potential. This is important for its subsequent production application and development. Many plant-derived bioactive substances show good activity in vitro, but their further application is often limited by stability, dispersibility, and in vivo efficacy. Recent reviews and studies have also indicated that the combination of natural medicines with nano-delivery technology is gradually becoming an important direction for controlling bacterial infections, with the advantage of balancing antibacterial efficiency, bioavailability, and host tolerance (Zhang et al., 2024). From this perspective, NanoEU may overcome the limitations of free eugenol in terms of stability, dispersibility, and in vivo efficacy, thereby showing promising application potential (Kazemi, 2025).

Of course, this study still has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, although the present study evaluated the mRNA and protein expression of tight junction-related markers, their spatial distribution and structural localization were not further examined using immunofluorescence or immunohistochemistry techniques. Therefore, future studies should more comprehensively verify the effects of NanoEU on tight junction organization and epithelial barrier integrity at the tissue localization level. Second, the present study provided only limited analysis of the protective effects of NanoEU on the cecum from the perspectives of antioxidation and anti-inflammation, and deeper signaling pathways or specific modes of cell death were not further investigated. Finally, this study was conducted using an experimental infection model, and the results mainly reflect the protective effect of NanoEU in chickens under controlled conditions. However, because the present study was designed as a short-term infection-challenge experiment rather than a long-term feeding trial, and individual feed intake was not systematically recorded, the current growth-related results should be interpreted mainly as supportive evidence of infection alleviation rather than as a complete evaluation of growth-promoting efficacy. Therefore, the long-term effects of NanoEU under practical production conditions, its optimal dosage, and its influence on feed intake, feed conversion ratio, growth performance, and intestinal microbiota still require further verification. Future studies integrating deeper mechanistic investigation and microbiome analysis would help to provide a more complete understanding of how NanoEU protects the intestinal barrier and the host (Lin et al., 2025).

In conclusion, this study demonstrates that NanoEU exerts multiple protective effects in a chicken model of S. Typhimurium infection. It not only reduced bacterial loads in the cecum, liver, and spleen, and mitigated histopathological damage, but also alleviated injury in visceral organs and the cecum. At the same time, it improved the intestinal mucus barrier and the expression of tight junction-related genes and proteins, while also relieving local oxidative stress and inflammatory responses (Zheng et al., 2023). Together with its preliminary biosafety and favorable storage stability, these findings further support the practical application potential of NanoEU. Therefore, as a therapeutic system combining natural plant-derived bioactive compounds with nano-delivery technology (Fu et al., 2022), NanoEU shows promising application prospects in the control of S. Typhimurium infection and the reduction of antibiotic use in poultry, and thus deserves further in-depth investigation and development (Zhang et al., 2024).

Conclusions

This study successfully developed a eugenol nanoemulsion with a uniform particle size distribution, in vitro anti-S. Typhimurium activity and preliminary biosafety. NanoEU maintained a stable appearance during 30 days of storage at 4 °C and 25 °C, and its MIC and MBC against S. Typhimurium remained unchanged. In addition, NanoEU showed acceptable hemocompatibility within the effective antibacterial concentration range.

In a chicken model of S. Typhimurium infection, NanoEU reduced bacterial loads in the cecum, liver, and spleen, attenuated infection-associated changes in organ indices, and mitigated histopathological damage in the liver, spleen, and cecum. Further analyses showed that NanoEU improved cecal mucus barrier status, restored the expression of tight junction-related genes and proteins, alleviated local oxidative stress, and attenuated inflammatory responses. The strain-specific genetic characteristics and LD50 of S. Typhimurium S162 in chickens were not determined in the present study, which should be considered a limitation. However, the challenge dose used in this study successfully induced typical Salmonella infection-related changes, including reduced body weight gain, intestinal injury, inflammatory responses, and bacterial colonization, supporting the suitability of this model for evaluating the protective effect of NanoEU.

A free eugenol-treated group was not included in the present animal experiment; therefore, the current results cannot fully distinguish the formulation-specific advantages of NanoEU from the inherent antibacterial and anti-inflammatory activities of eugenol. Previous studies have shown that eugenol can inhibit T3SS- and type I fimbriae-related virulence genes in S. Typhimurium, reduce its pathogenicity to chickens, decrease cecal Salmonella colonization, maintain tight junction proteins, modulate cecal microbiota, and suppress inflammatory responses. These findings support the intrinsic protective potential of eugenol against S. Typhimurium infection and suggest that gut microbiota modulation and host-pathogen interactions may also contribute to the protective effects of NanoEU. Nevertheless, microbiota sequencing, transcriptomics, and metabolomics were not performed in the present study. Future studies will directly compare free eugenol and NanoEU at equivalent eugenol doses and further investigate the effects of NanoEU on gut microbiota composition and host responses using 16S rRNA gene sequencing and multi-omics approaches (Zhao et al., 2022a; Zheng et al., 2023). However, because NanoEU was administered by oral gavage in this study, its stability under drinking-water and feed-mixing conditions remains to be further evaluated.

Taken together, NanoEU exerted a relatively comprehensive protective effect against S. Typhimurium infection in chickens. It not only showed in vivo anti-infective activity, but also improved cecal barrier function and the local inflammatory microenvironment to a certain extent. These findings provide experimental evidence supporting the potential application of NanoEU in the control of S. Typhimurium infection and the reduction of antibiotic use in poultry, and indicate that it merits further development.
