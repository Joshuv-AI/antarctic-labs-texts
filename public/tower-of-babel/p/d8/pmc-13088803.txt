Inflammation mediates the association between the triglyceride-glucose index and non-target lesions progression after PCI: a cohort study
Authors: Tian-Qi Teng, Lei-Lei Bai, Jing Liu, Meng-Meng Wang, Gui-Xia Sun, Zhi-Yi Yu, Zi-Shan Liu, Jia-Chao Xu, Qing-Ying Jiao, Zhi Wang, Zhuang Chen, Hai-Chu Yu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe progression of non-target lesions (NTLs) following percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) is an important clinical issue, yet the role of triglyceride–glucose (TyG) in this process is unclear.MethodsThis study enrolled patients with coronary artery disease who underwent PCI between 2017 and 2020, and completed follow-up coronary angiography 6–24 months after the procedure. Coronary stenosis was evaluated by visual estimation and verified using quantitative coronary angiography. We used TyG values at baseline and follow-up as the primary exposures, and defined NTLs progression as the study endpoint. Patterns of TyG changes were identified with K-means clustering. Analysis focused on the association between the TyG index and NTLs progression, while mediation analysis evaluated the role of inflammation, and the robustness of the findings was assessed using NTL-related PCI as a secondary outcome.ResultsAmong the 889 patients included, 246 (27.7%) showed NTLs progression. Higher TyG levels were associated with increased risk: each 1-unit increase corresponded to a 61% higher risk (HR = 1.61, 95% CI 1.26–2.05), and patients in the highest TyG quartile (Q4) had an 86% higher risk of NTLs progression compared with those in the lowest quartile (Q1) (HR = 1.86, 95% CI 1.28–2.70). After clustering, persistently high TyG was linked to a 163% higher risk compared with persistently low levels (HR = 2.63, 95% CI 1.76–3.92). The association appeared linear according to RCS analysis. CRP mediated about 12% of this effect (95% CI 4.21%–28.6%, P = 0.002). Sensitivity analysis further showed a significant association between TyG and NTL-related PCI (HR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.39–2.63, P < 0.001).ConclusionsElevated TyG levels independently predicted NTLs progression after PCI. This finding highlights the role of metabolic and inflammatory pathways in residual atherosclerotic activity. Monitoring TyG may help identify patients at risk for future lesion progression despite successful revascularization.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12944-026-02921-6.Keywords: Triglyceride-glucose index, Non-target lesion progression, Mediation analysis, Percutaneous coronary intervention

Background

Patients with coronary artery disease (CAD) remain at risk of recurrent heart attack events after percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI), even with optimal secondary prevention including antiplatelet therapy, lipid-lowering treatment, and comprehensive risk factor control [1–5]. The progression of non-target lesions (NTLs) is an essential proximate cause of late adverse events following PCI. Supporting this, a large JACC registry study of approximately 13,000 individuals with acute coronary syndrome underwent PCI demonstrated that NTLs progression accounted for a substantial proportion of recurrent ischemic events. The results showed that more than 80% of ischaemic events that occurred over 30 days after PCI were not related to the stent-treated lesions, suggesting that the progression of NTLs plays a major role in post-procedural recurrent events [6]. These patients often had multivessel disease, but only the culprit lesion was treated. Despite regular use of secondary prevention medications and good treatment adherence, significant luminal narrowing or even acute occlusion may develop in non-intervened vessels [5, 7]. Although revascularization of the target lesion restores coronary blood flow, atherosclerotic plaques in untreated vessels may continue to progress over time, eventually resulting in recurrent myocardial infarction and other adverse cardiovascular events [8]. However, effective methods to detect patients at increased risk of NTLs progression remain limited.

The triglyceride-glucose (TyG) index is a simple and non-invasive marker derived from fasting triglyceride and blood glucose levels. It serves as an effective surrogate for insulin resistance and is closely related to metabolic abnormalities and cardiovascular disease progression [9–12]. Furthermore, the TyG index appears to be related to restenosis following stent implantation in various blood vessels, including the carotid, coronary and iliac arteries [13–15]. However, with regard to the specific pathological process of NTLs after PCI, systematic research on the function and underlying mechanisms of the TyG index is still lacking.

The current secondary prevention strategy has significantly reduced the risk of reinfarction in most patients, but some patients still have recurrent cardiovascular events, which highlights the need to identify residual risk factors [16–18]. This study investigated the relationship between TyG index and NTLs progression after PCI, aiming to support early detection and personalized management of high-risk patients.

Methods

This study enrolled patients with CAD who underwent PCI between January 1, 2017, and December 31, 2020, and completed follow-up coronary angiography within a window of 6 to 24 months. For patients requiring planned staged PCI, the follow-up period was defined to commence only after the completion of the final scheduled procedure. Initially, data from 1,092 patients were collected. According to the exclusion criteria, patients with a history of coronary artery bypass grafting (n = 35), missing TyG or glucose data (n = 97), poor-quality baseline or follow-up angiograms that precluded quantitative assessment of vascular stenosis (n = 24), or in-stent restenosis at follow-up (n = 47) were excluded (Fig. 1). Ultimately, 889 participants were included in the final analysis. The clinical study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the local institutional research ethics committee (approval No. 2022-02-053-K01). All participants provided written informed consent before participation.

Flowchart of participant recruitment

Quantitative coronary angiography

All coronary interventions were performed by experienced interventional cardiologists. Each patient underwent multi-projection coronary angiography after the intracoronary administration of nitroglycerin. Target lesions were identified by consensus between two senior interventionalists, integrating stenosis severity, anatomical complexity, and objective ischemic evidence. The PCI strategies and the pre- and post-procedural medication regimens were consistent with the recommendations of the 2016 Chinese Guidelines for Percutaneous Coronary Intervention [19]. Two blinded investigators independently performed visual assessment of coronary angiography and quantitative coronary angiography (QCA) analysis. The severity of the coronary artery stenosis was initially assessed by visual estimation. All three major coronary arteries and side branches with a reference vessel diameter greater than 1.5 mm were evaluated. Lesions with possible progression of stenosis were further quantified using dedicated QCA software. Analysis of NTLs was conducted using identical or comparable angiographic projections between the baseline and follow-up examinations. The recorded parameters included the location of lesion progression and its severity, the latter comprising the minimal lumen diameter and the degree of stenosis.

Covariates

We selected covariates potentially associated with atherosclerosis for analysis, including age, sex, height, weight, and BMI. Additional variables included blood pressure, smoking status (ever or never), diabetes, hypertension, medication use, and laboratory parameters such as Fasting blood glucose (FBG), glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), total cholesterol (TC), creatinine (Cr), and C-reactive protein (CRP), triglycerides (TG), high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). Fasting venous blood samples were collected pre-procedurally at both the baseline PCI and the follow-up angiography. Antiplatelet therapy status was documented based on adherence to professional medical advice, encompassing both dual and single antiplatelet regimens.

Identification

The definition of NTLs was as a de novo stenotic lesion that did not account for symptoms of ischaemia nor a positive functional ischaemia test [20].

NTLs progression was defined as follows: (1) Lesions with a baseline diameter stenosis greater than 50% that showed more than a 10% increase in stenosis during follow-up; (2) A vascular segment with baseline stenosis of less than 50% (including segments without visible stenosis) was defined as showing progression if luminal narrowing increased by more than 30% during the follow-up period; (3) Any NTLs that progressed to total occlusion [2, 21].

Multivessel disease was defined as a minimum of 50% stenosis in a minimum of two major coronary arteries or their main branches with a reference vessel diameter > 2.5 mm [22].

Statistical analysis

Missing values (< 5%) were imputed using multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) to ensure robustness of the analyses. Continuous data were reported as mean ± SD and compared with independent-sample t-tests, whereas categorical variables were presented as counts (n, %) and analyzed using chi-square tests. To capture how the TyG index changed over time, we used K-means clustering on values measured at baseline and follow-up. As both values were on the same scale, standardization was unnecessary. The cluster number (K = 2–6) was determined using the average silhouette width and elbow method, with clinical interpretability also taken into account. Cluster stability was further examined using bootstrap resampling (1,000 iterations), evaluating Jaccard similarity and cluster dissociation to confirm robustness.

The relationship between TyG, TG, and glucose and NTLs risk was further examined using restricted cubic splines (RCS) to assess potential nonlinearity. Event-free survival across TyG and LDL-C categories was visualized with Kaplan–Meier curves, and group differences were tested with the log-rank test. Finally, Cox proportional hazards models quantified the association between TyG levels and subsequent NTLs progression. Three models were built sequentially: Model 1 (unadjusted), Model 2 (adjusted for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, BMI, and smoking), and Model 3 (additionally adjusted for multivessel disease, CRP, LDL-C, uric acid, creatinine, and use of antiplatelet or lipid-lowering agents). Mediation analysis was performed to assess whether follow-up CRP mediated the relationship between baseline TyG and NTLs progression, with indirect effects evaluated using 1000 bootstrap resamples.

Subgroup analyses verified the robustness of the findings. To test the robustness of our findings, we repeated the analysis using revascularization of non-target lesions as the endpoint, examining whether the TyG–outcome association persisted in the Cox regression model.

All statistical analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1).

Result

Baseline characteristics of study population

A total of 889 participants were enrolled in this study, among whom 246 (27.7%) developed NTLs during follow-up. The average age of the participants was 64.77 ± 10.43 years, and 457 (51.4%) of them were male. Participants in the highest TyG quartile (Q4) had a significantly higher prevalence of diabetes compared with those in Q1 (64.0% vs. 5.4%, P < 0.001), hypertension (72.5% vs. 54.7%, P = 0.001), and obesity (16.2% vs. 13.3%, P = 0.005). In addition, Q4 participants exhibited higher inflammatory levels (CRP: 5.30 ± 10.19 mg/L vs. 2.66 ± 5.92 mg/L, P = 0.001) and increased LDL-C levels (2.80 ± 0.83 mmol/L vs. 2.57 ± 0.80 mmol/L, P = 0.036).

During follow-up, patients in the Q4 group continued to exhibit higher CRP levels (4.53 ± 9.51 mg/L vs. 2.04 ± 3.05 mg/L, P < 0.001) and higher LDL-C levels (2.30 ± 0.88 mmol/L vs. 2.05 ± 0.69 mmol/L, P = 0.032). Notably, although the overall difference in the prevalence of multivessel disease did not reach statistical significance, the prevalence of multivessel disease was evidently higher in the Q4 group (49.1%) than in the other three groups (38.7%–41.1%, P = 0.104) (Table 1).

Changes of TyG index: K-means clustering analysis

K-means clustering was applied to analyze the patterns of TyG changes between baseline and follow-up. Among clustering solutions with K = 2, 4, and 6, the average silhouette coefficients were all high and comparable. Based on both the clinical interpretability of TyG changes and the balance of sample sizes across clusters, the K = 4 solution was selected as optimal. The bootstrap analysis confirmed that all clusters were stable, with Jaccard similarity coefficients exceeding 0.60 and dissociation rates below 30%. NTLs incidence across TyG subgroups: Kaplan–Meier analysis

Kaplan–Meier analysis revealed that patients with higher TyG values (above the median) had a markedly lower event-free probability of NTLs progression during follow-up (log-rank P < 0.001). A similar gradient was observed across TyG quartiles: the Q1 group maintained the greatest event-free survival, whereas the Q4 group showed the poorest outcomes (log-rank P = 0.011). When the TyG changes were considered, significant differences emerged among clusters (log-rank P < 0.001); When stratified by patterns of TyG change, significant differences in NTLs progression emerged among the clusters (log-rank P < 0.001). Cluster 1 showed the most favorable profile, while Cluster 3 had the highest incidence of NTLs progression. Stratification by follow-up LDL-C levels demonstrated that participants with LDL-C < 1.8 mmol/L had a significantly lower event-free probability of NTLs progression, consistent with previous reports (log-rank P < 0.001) (Fig. 3).

Association between TyG and NTLs risk: Cox regression analysis

In the unadjusted Cox regression model, continuous TyG was strong associated with an increased risk of NTLs progression, with each 1-unit increase in TyG correlating with a 61% higher risk (HR = 1.61, 95% CI: 1.26–2.05, P < 0.001). After adjusting for age, sex, hypertension, diabetes, BMI, and smoking in Model 2, the risk remained 54% higher (HR = 1.54, 95% CI: 1.16–2.04, P = 0.003). Further adjustment for multivessel disease, CRP, LDL-C, UA, Cr, antiplatelets, statins, β-blockers, GLDs. GLDs, glucose-lowering drugs in Model 3 yielded consistent results (HR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.11–1.98, P = 0.007).

When TyG was dichotomized by the median value, participants in the high TyG group were 57% more likely to develop NTLs progression than those in the low TyG group (HR = 1.57, 95% CI: 1.22–2.03, P < 0.001).

After adjusting for covariates in Model 2 and Model 3, the association remained consistent (HR = 1.48, 95% CI: 1.12–1.95, P = 0.006; HR = 1.43, 95% CI: 1.09–1.88, P = 0.011).

In the quartile-based analysis of TyG, compared with the Q1 group, the risk of NTLs was not significantly different in the Q2 group (HR = 1.44, 95% CI: 0.97–2.13, P = 0.071), but was 55% higher in the Q3 group (HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.06–2.27, P = 0.023) and 86% higher in the Q4 group (HR = 1.86, 95% CI: 1.28–2.70, P = 0.001). The results remained consistent after adjustment for covariates.

When TyG was categorized based on its changes, the differences in NTLs risk became more pronounced. Relative to the persistently low TyG group (Cluster 1), the risk of NTL progression was 64% higher in Cluster 2 (HR = 1.64, 95% CI: 1.07–2.51, P = 0.023) and 85% higher in Cluster 4 (HR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.22–2.82, P = 0.004). Cluster 3 exhibited the highest risk (HR = 2.63, 95% CI: 1.76–3.92, P < 0.001). These associations remained robust after adjustment for covariates (Table 2).

Restricted cubic spline analysis of TyG, TG, and glucose with NTLs progression

Restricted cubic spline analyses with three knots (median as the reference) were used to assess linear and nonlinear relationships between TyG, TG, and glucose levels and NTLs progression. For all three markers, the risk of NTLs increased roughly linearly, with TyG (P = 0.001, P for nonlinearity = 0.85), TG (P = 0.014, P for nonlinearity = 0.209), and glucose (P = 0.003, P for nonlinearity = 0.122) showing similar patterns. The curve for glucose remained nearly flat at lower values and rose gradually at higher concentrations. The consistent linear trend observed for TyG suggests a stable association with the progression of atherosclerosis (Fig. 4).

Mediating role of follow-up CRP in the TyG–NTLs association

In the unadjusted model, follow-up CRP partially mediated the relationship between baseline TyG and the development of NTLs. The indirect effect was β = 1.56 × 10-4 (95% CI: 9.87 × 10-6–1.77 × 10-3, P = 0.002), contributing to 12.4% of the total effect (95% CI: 4.21%–28.6%). The total effect of TyG on NTLs progression risk was β = 1.26 × 10-3 (95% CI: 1.39 × 10-4–8.01 × 10-3, P < 0.001) (Fig. 3A). The findings remained robust in Model 3, showing an indirect effect of β = 7.33e − 4 (95% CI: 1.41e − 5–5.99e − 3, P < 0.001) and a total effect of β = 4.13e − 3 (95% CI: 1.7e − 4–1.27e − 2, P = 0.006), which accounted for 15.7% of the overall effect (95% CI: 4.5%–48.9%, P = 0.014) (Fig. 5).

Characteristics of progressed NTLs by TyG level

Among 246 patients with NTLs progression, 58.5% were in the high-TyG group (≥ 9.004). Compared with the low-TyG group, they had less diameter [0.66 (0.44) mm vs. 0.81 (0.48) mm, p = 0.011] and greater diameter stenosis [77.7% (14.4) vs. 72.4% (16.2), p = 0.004]. In addition, the incidence of NTL-related revascularization was higher in the high-TyG group [63.9% vs. 50.0%, P = 0.041]. However, no statistically significant differences were observed between the two groups in the distribution of progressive lesion sites, baseline lesion diameter and stenosis, or the incidence of myocardial infarction (Table 3).

Subgroup analysis

Participants were grouped according to age, sex, BMI, hypertension, diabetes, LDL cholesterol level, multivessel disease, and smoking. Results showed no significant interaction between subgroups regarding the relationship between TyG and NTLs progression. Nevertheless, it is noteworthy that the TyG-related risk was higher in individuals with LDL-c < 1.8 mmol/L than in those with LDL-c ≥ 1.8 mmol/L (Table S1).

Sensitivity analysis: TyG and risk of NTLs revascularization

For sensitivity testing, NTLs revascularization detected on follow-up angiography served as the endpoint. A total of 143 patients (16.1%) required repeat PCI, and each unit increase in TyG was associated with a 91% higher risk (HR = 1.91, 95% CI: 1.39–2.63, P < 0.001). After adjusting for covariates in Models 2 and 3, the results remained consistent (HR = 1.70, 95% CI: 1.17–2.45, P = 0.005; HR = 1.73, 95% CI: 1.18–2.54, P = 0.005).

Participants with TyG values above the median had a 79% greater risk of NTLs revascularization than those below the median (HR = 1.79, 95% CI: 1.27–2.52, P < 0.001). This association persisted after multivariable adjustment in Models 2 and 3 (HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.07–2.25, P = 0.019; HR = 1.55, 95% CI: 1.06–2.27, P = 0.021).

Across TyG quartiles, the risk of NTL-related revascularization increased from Q2 to Q4 compared with Q1. Although the rise in Q2 was not statistically significant (HR = 1.34, 95% CI: 0.77–2.32, P = 0.300), Q3 and Q4 showed substantially higher risks (HR = 1.85, 95% CI: 1.11–3.07, P = 0.018; HR = 2.29, 95% CI: 1.39–3.75, P = 0.001, respectively). Discussion

This study found a clear link between the TyG index and NTLs progression after PCI. Higher TyG values were linked to a greater chance of lesion growth. In clinical settings, the risk of non-target lesion progression after revascularization is often underestimated, partly because routine follow-up angiography is not commonly performed due to factors such as the absence of symptoms, cost, or the invasiveness of the procedure. The research indicate that TyG could be used as an independent marker to predict NTLs progression. A significant linear relationship was observed between baseline TyG levels and NTLs risk, with TyG < 8.66 (the lowest quartile) associated with the most favorable long-term outcomes. Therefore, we define TyG < 8.66 as the optimal metabolic target in this study. While follow-up changes provide supplemental risk information, a straightforward baseline assessment is more practical for clinical decision-making and large-scale implementation. For patients who are not eligible for repeated angiography, TyG changes over time may offer an easy, non-invasive way of identifying those at higher risk. Consistent with previous studies, data from the VMCUN cohort with a median follow-up of ten years showed that that TyG was an independent predictor of CAD, cerebrovascular disease and peripheral arterial disease [23].

For patients post-PCI, the primary strategy for managing non-target lesions is the sustained maintenance of extremely low LDL-C levels over the long term [24, 25]. However, when patients successfully maintain LDL-C levels below 1.8 mmol/L, residual cardiovascular risk is largely driven by other metabolic abnormalities, particularly insulin resistance, become the primary determinants of non-target lesion stability [2, 26, 27]. Our study further advances this understanding by demonstrating the heterogeneity of post-PCI treatment effects across different LDL-C levels. When LDL-C is < 1.8 mmol/L, the TyG index shows a stronger and independent association with NTLs. This suggests that under low LDL-C conditions, insulin resistance-related metabolic abnormalities may represent a key residual risk factor driving the progression of NTLs. Furthermore, part of the effect of the TyG index may be mediated through chronic inflammatory pathways. A large-scale randomized controlled trial has demonstrated that effective anti-inflammatory therapy can delay NTLs progression after PCI. Monoclonal antibody therapies targeting inflammatory pathways have been shown to slow the NTLs progression by selectively inhibiting IL-1β or IL-6 signaling pathways, without further reducing LDL-C levels [28–30]. Jing Ma et al. used a rabbit model of abdominal aortic atherosclerosis to examine non-target lesion progression with intravascular ultrasound and histopathology. Stent implantation markedly accelerated plaque growth, with plaque volume and burden increasing to 182.96 mm3 and 35.14%, both far exceeding values in the sham-operated controls. The same study further analyzed 147 PCI patients, demonstrating that postoperative serum CRP levels remained persistently elevated, suggesting that stent implantation may trigger sustained vascular and systemic inflammatory responses [31]. CRP is a systemic inflammation biomarker and is closely associated with coronary heart disease [32, 33], our study newly identified CRP as a mediator in the association between the TyG index and NTLs progression after PCI, thereby providing additional evidence for the mechanistic link between insulin resistance and atherosclerosis [34, 35].

In clinical practice, the focus of postoperative PCI is usually focused on restenosis in the stent, and the progress of NTLs will also have a significant impact on long-term results. Among survivors of acute myocardial infarction (AMI), NTLs is an important factor in the recurrence of cardiovascular events. Epidemiological data show that the recurrence rate of myocardial infarction within one year after acute myocardial infarction is 5% to 15%, and the cumulative incidence rate within five years is 8% to 12%. The risk of multiple vascular lesions or insulin resistance is particularly high [36–38]. Even if LDL-C control reaches the best level, the TyG index is still independently associated with residual cardiovascular risk, highlighting its potential as a marker for predicting NTLs progress after PCI. Addressing TyG-related metabolic abnormalities could offer a potential approach to stabilizing non-target lesions and improving long-term outcomes, especially for patients who cannot undergo regular angiographic follow-up.

Repeat angiography in this study was primarily symptom-driven, meaning our cohort likely represents a higher-risk population. Consequently, the observed rate of NTLs progression may be higher than in the general post-PCI population, and these findings should be generalized to asymptomatic or low-risk patients with caution. In addition, during the evaluation of vascular lesions, precise intravascular imaging modalities were not employed, and the characteristics of the plaques were not further investigated. Although we included most of the commonly recognized covariates, as this was a retrospective analysis, some potential confounding factors including dietary patterns, physical activity, and antiplatelet resistance may not have been fully adjusted. Furthermore, previous studies previous studies have reported that the overall prevalence of anxiety after PCI is approximately 34% [39], and that insulin resistance is strongly linked to anxiety and depression [40]. But this study did not evaluate the potential influence of psychological factors on the observed associations.

Moreover, antiplatelet resistance remains a major concern in patients after PCI. A higher TyG index has been linked to enhanced platelet reactivity and a greater likelihood of aspirin resistance, implying that insulin resistance might diminish the antiplatelet response by altering platelet function [41, 42]. Although medication use was adjusted for in the analyses, resistance to these agents was not specifically evaluated. The study relied on two-point measurements due to the limited sampling frequency. This design focuses on metabolic control levels at baseline and follow-up rather than capturing a continuous dynamic trajectory. Future longitudinal studies with higher sampling density are needed to better define these patterns. Fasting insulin levels were not collected in the present study; therefore, we were unable to directly compare the diagnostic or prognostic efficacy of the TyG index with HOMA-IR. Although the TyG index has been validated as a reliable surrogate for insulin resistance in previous literature [12], future prospective studies incorporating multiple assessment methods would provide more comprehensive validation.

Conclusions

This study reveals that TyG is an independently identifiable factor in the progression of non-target lesions after PCI. As a simple and routinely available marker of insulin resistance, the TyG index could be used as a practical tool to identify patients at a higher risk of coronary atherosclerosis progression beyond treated areas. Adding a TyG assessment to regular monitoring appointments could help identify high-risk patients earlier and enable more targeted preventive treatment. More research is needed to determine establish interventions guided by TyG levels can translate into better long-term cardiovascular outcomes.
