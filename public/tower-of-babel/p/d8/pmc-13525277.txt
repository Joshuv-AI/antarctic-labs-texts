Structural white matter alterations in borderline personality disorder: A narrative review and methodological considerations
Authors: Piotr Podwalski, Bartosz Dawidowski, Łukasz Franczak, Ernest Tyburski, Błażej Misiak, Leszek Sagan, Jerzy Samochowiec
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a severe and heterogeneous psychiatric condition characterized by emotional dysregulation, impulsivity, and interpersonal instability. Increasing evidence suggests that disruptions in white matter (WM) microstructure may contribute to its neurobiological underpinnings. Clarifying these findings is important because diffusion MRI studies in BPD remain heterogeneous in terms of age, symptom severity, comorbidity, medication status, trauma exposure, and analytic approach. This narrative review synthesizes current findings from diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) studies examining WM alterations in individuals with BPD and discusses key methodological considerations that influence the interpretation of results. Across studies, the most consistent abnormalities were observed in the corpus callosum, particularly the genu and body, which may reflect altered interhemispheric connectivity. Additional alterations were reported in fronto-limbic pathways, including the cingulum and fornix, as well as long association tracts such as the superior and inferior longitudinal fasciculi. These findings are consistent with the hypothesis that WM alterations in BPD may extend across large-scale structural networks that are anatomically connected to emotional and cognitive systems. However, results remain heterogeneous due to methodological variability, small sample sizes, and clinical heterogeneity. Differences in imaging techniques and analytical approaches further complicate comparisons across studies. Overall, WM alterations may represent one component of the neurobiological profile of BPD; however, their functional and clinical significance requires confirmation using advanced diffusion methods, longitudinal designs, and standardized protocols.

1. Introduction

Borderline personality disorder (BPD) is a complex and heterogeneous condition characterized by profound emotional instability, impulsivity, and impaired regulation of interpersonal relationships. 1 Epidemiologically, BPD is estimated to affect approximately 1% of the general population, with significantly higher prevalence rates in clinical settings, reaching up to 10% in outpatient populations and approximately 20% among psychiatric inpatients. 2 The social impact of BPD is substantial, often leading to impaired occupational functioning and severe strain on social support systems. 3 The prevalence of suicidal behavior is high, with nearly 10% of individuals with BPD dying by suicide, a rate significantly higher than that of the general population. 4 Given these risks, the clinical importance of early diagnosis and targeted intervention cannot be overstated, as early management is crucial for improving long-term prognostic outcomes and reducing mortality. 5 In this context, identifying reliable neurobiological markers may contribute to translational psychiatric research aimed at improving early detection and personalized treatment strategies.3,6

Contemporary neurobiological models of BPD emphasize dysregulation within distributed fronto-limbic and salience-related systems supporting emotion regulation, threat processing, impulse control, and interpersonal functioning.7–10 This framework is supported by structural and functional neuroimaging reviews and meta-analyses showing alterations in temporolimbic and frontomedial regions, although the direction and robustness of findings vary across tasks, samples, and analytic approaches.11–14 Findings often include reductions in volume of the amygdala and hippocampus.11–13 Functional neuroimaging indicates that individuals with BPD often exhibit dysregulated activation within the prefrontal cortex (PFC) during emotional provocation. In functional neuroimaging studies, this pattern has often been interpreted as consistent with reduced prefrontal modulation of limbic activity, although such functional interpretations should not be directly inferred from structural diffusion findings. 15

White matter (WM) microstructure provides a structural substrate for communication between distributed cortical and subcortical regions, including circuits implicated in emotion regulation, cognitive control, and interpersonal functioning.16,17 WM maturation continues throughout adolescence and early adulthood, particularly within fronto-limbic circuits involved in emotional regulation and impulse control. Disruptions to these developmental trajectories—potentially shaped by early-life adversity—may contribute to the emergence and maintenance of BPD symptoms. 16 Importantly, alterations in WM microstructure may also reflect the neurobiological consequences of early-life adversity, which is highly prevalent among individuals with BPD. 17 These findings have motivated a shift toward investigating “connectivity” models of BPD, specifically focusing on the structural pathways linking these regions.18,19

To investigate these structural pathways, diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) has emerged as a critical tool for the in vivo assessment of WM microstructure. DTI measures the diffusion of water molecules within brain tissue, which is constrained by physical barriers such as axonal membranes and myelin sheaths. 20 The primary metrics used to characterize WM integrity include fractional anisotropy (FA) and mean diffusivity (MD). FA quantifies the degree of directionality of water diffusion; higher or lower values are often interpreted as reflecting differences in WM organization, although FA is not specific to a single biological process such as myelination, axonal density, or fiber coherence. 21 Conversely, MD represents the overall magnitude of water diffusion within a voxel; increased MD may be compatible with several microstructural processes, but it does not uniquely identify edema, neurodegeneration, or cellular loss. 22 Additional diffusion metrics, such as radial diffusivity (RD) and axial diffusivity (AD), are also frequently analyzed, providing complementary information on potential demyelination processes and axonal integrity. AD measures the rate of water diffusion along the longitudinal axis of axonal fibers. In clinical research, AD is primarily used as an indicator of axonal integrity, where lower values are typically associated with axonal injury, fragmentation, or loss. RD represents the magnitude of water diffusion perpendicular to the axonal tract. RD is highly sensitive to the state of the myelin sheath; elevated RD values are widely interpreted as a marker of demyelination or reduced myelin density, which allows water to move more freely across the axonal membrane. 23 These methodological considerations are particularly relevant when interpreting DTI findings in psychiatric disorders, where subtle and heterogeneous microstructural alterations are expected. Although FA, MD, RD, and AD are widely used indices of WM microstructure, they are not biologically specific. They can be influenced by factors such as crossing fibers and partial-volume effects. Therefore, interpretations in terms of demyelination or axonal damage should be presented as hypotheses rather than direct inferences.22,24

Despite their value in characterizing WM microstructure, interpreting DTI data in the context of BPD remains complex due to the inherent sensitivity of these measures to non-pathological factors, such as crossing fibers within a single voxel. 25 Furthermore, variability in tractography algorithms and the lack of standardization across study protocols often complicate the synthesis of results for specific bundles, such as the superior longitudinal fasciculus (SLF) and the uncinate fasciculus (UF). 18

A meta-analytic synthesis of DTI studies indicates that WM alterations in BPD most consistently involve the corpus callosum (CC) and fornix, supporting the hypothesis of disrupted structural connectivity within interhemispheric and fronto-limbic systems. 19 Consistent with this synthesis, individual studies most often report FA and/or diffusivity alterations in the CC, particularly in the genu and body, although these findings should be interpreted as markers of altered microstructural organization rather than direct evidence of impaired communication.26–28 Alterations have also been reported in fronto-limbic pathways, including the cingulum, fornix, and uncinate fasciculus, which anatomically connect prefrontal, temporal, and limbic regions implicated in emotional and cognitive processes.25,28 Additional findings in long association tracts, including the inferior longitudinal fasciculus (ILF) and SLF, suggest that WM alterations in BPD may extend beyond a narrowly defined fronto-limbic model toward broader large-scale structural networks.28,29

Despite the growing body of literature, findings remain inconsistent, partly due to methodological variability and relatively small sample sizes, with many studies including only a limited number of participants. Additional ambiguities arise from substantial heterogeneity in data acquisition and analytical approaches, including voxel-based analysis (VBA), tract-based spatial statistics (TBSS), tractography, and region-of-interest (ROI)–based methods. Furthermore, clinical samples are often heterogeneous with respect to demographic characteristics, comorbid psychiatric conditions, and medication status. In some cases, attempts to increase sample homogeneity, such as restricting the sample to female participants, may also limit the generalizability of the findings. Together, these factors contribute to variability across studies and complicate the identification of reliable neurobiological markers of BPD.

Given the methodological heterogeneity of available studies, a narrative review approach may provide a more suitable framework for integrating findings and discussing methodological challenges that may influence the interpretation of DTI results. The present review aims to synthesize current evidence on WM microstructural alterations in BPD, with particular attention to methodological factors that may influence reported findings and contribute to inconsistencies across studies. In addition, the review considers these findings in the context of structural connectivity models of BPD.

2. Methodology

A narrative literature review was conducted to summarize current evidence on WM microstructural alterations in BPD as assessed using diffusion imaging. A structured literature search was conducted in PubMed/MEDLINE, Embase, Scopus, and the Web of Science Core Collection. The databases were searched from inception, with publication dates restricted to December 31, 2025. Search terms combined keywords related to BPD and white matter diffusion imaging, such as “borderline personality disorder,” “white matter,” “white matter microstructure,” “structural connectivity,” “diffusion tensor imaging,” “DTI,” “tractography,” “fractional anisotropy,” and “mean diffusivity”; database-specific controlled vocabularies were used when available, supplemented with free-text keywords in titles and abstracts. The complete and exact search strings are provided in Supplemental Materials.

Studies were eligible for inclusion if they: (1) reported original, peer-reviewed research involving human participants; (2) included individuals diagnosed with borderline personality disorder; (3) used diffusion-weighted MRI, diffusion tensor imaging, tractography, or another diffusion-based method to investigate white matter microstructure or structural connectivity; and (4) reported diffusion-related results separately for the BPD group. Studies comparing participants with BPD with healthy or clinical control groups, examining associations between diffusion measures and clinical characteristics, or assessing longitudinal diffusion-related changes were considered eligible. Only full-text articles published in English were included. Records were excluded if they did not meet the predefined eligibility criteria, did not report diffusion-derived parameters, or represented review articles, conference abstracts, commentaries, or other non-original publications. Duplicate records were removed before screening.

Following deduplication, titles and abstracts were screened for relevance to the review question. Full-text articles were retrieved for all potentially eligible records or when eligibility could not be determined from the title and abstract. Full texts were then assessed against the predefined eligibility criteria, and the primary reason for exclusion was documented. This review was designed as a narrative synthesis rather than a systematic or scoping review. To improve transparency and reporting quality, the preparation of this narrative review was informed by the Scale for the Assessment of Narrative Review Articles (SANRA). 30

3. Results

3.1. Characteristics of included studies

A total of 23 diffusion tensor imaging (DTI) studies published between 2007 and 2025 were considered in this narrative synthesis. After accounting for explicitly reported sample overlap, the studies comprised 1,163 participants, including 594 individuals with BPD and 569 healthy controls, although individual sample sizes were generally modest and varied widely, ranging from 16 to 92 participants. Several studies examined specific demographic subgroups. Ten studies were conducted exclusively in female samples,27–29,31–37 while the remaining studies included both male and female participants. In addition, five studies included adolescent participants.37–41 Regarding analytical approaches, a variety of DTI methods were used. TBSS and ROI analyses were the most commonly applied approaches. Across studies, FA was the most consistently analyzed diffusion metric, examined in 21 of the 23 studies; one study primarily quantified probabilistic structural connectivity using a tract-probability index. 36 Additionally, other diffusion measures were reported less frequently, including MD in 12 studies, RD in 9, and AD in 8. Psychiatric comorbidities were reported in most studies, often including depressive disorders, anxiety disorders, ADHD, post-traumatic stress disorder, or non-suicidal self-injury. However, the extent of comorbidity reporting varied between studies. Pharmacological treatment was also common, with many participants receiving psychotropic medications such as antidepressants, antipsychotics, or mood stabilizers, while only a minority of studies selected in the narrative synthesis considered medication-free samples. 3.1.1. Methodological heterogeneity across selected studies

In addition to the descriptive characteristics summarized in Table 1, the selected studies differed substantially in several methodological aspects that may influence the interpretation and comparability of findings. These differences concerned scanner field strength, diffusion acquisition parameters, preprocessing procedures, motion and artifact correction, statistical correction for multiple comparisons, and the choice of analytic strategy, including ROI-based analyses, TBSS, tractography, and network-level approaches. 3.2. WM abnormalities in BPD

The studies considered in this narrative review reported WM microstructural alterations across several brain regions. To provide a clearer overview of the findings, the reported abnormalities are organized by the frequency with which specific WM structures were implicated across studies. The reviewed studies reported WM alterations in several major fiber systems, including the corpus callosum, fronto-limbic pathways, long association fibers, and projection fibers.

3.2.1. Corpus callosum abnormalities

Across the reviewed studies, CC emerged as one of the most consistently implicated WM structures in BPD. Several studies reported reduced FA within different callosal segments. Specifically, decreased FA in the genu and body of the CC was reported in several studies,26,35,37,42 and more widespread reductions, involving the genu, body, and splenium were reported in others.28,40 In addition, Quattrini et al. found FA reductions predominantly in the splenium of the CC. 46 Diffusivity measures also indicated callosal abnormalities, including increased MD28,40 and RD,41,46 as well as reduced AD in the splenium. 41 In contrast to studies reporting increased MD, Sadek et al. observed lower MD in the body of the corpus callosum in adolescent females with BPD, whereas MD in the genu and splenium did not differ between groups. 37 Using probabilistic fiber tracking, Rüsch et al. reported a lower probability that a cluster in the anterior corpus callosum was structurally connected with both dorsal ACCs in BPD, whereas no group differences were found for interhemispheric medial orbitofrontal or dorsolateral prefrontal connectivity. 36

Some studies additionally linked callosal alterations with clinical features. Gan et al. reported nominal associations of CC, fornix, and ATR diffusion measures with impulsivity and affective symptoms; however, none remained significant after FDR correction. 26 Sadek et al. similarly reported negative associations between FA in the genu and body of the CC and impulsivity, while MD in the body correlated positively with motor impulsiveness and negatively with BSSI suicidal-ideation scores. 37 Quattrini et al. found a positive correlation between RD in the genu and body of the CC and trait anxiety. 46 Alterations in the CC were also associated with suicidality, reduced FA, and increased MD in the splenium in suicidal BPD, with the number of suicide attempts negatively correlated with FA in the splenium and genu and positively correlated with MD in the splenium. 27 Furthermore, RD in the body of the CC positively correlated with non-suicidal self-injury (NSSI). 40

3.2.2. Fronto-limbic WM pathways

Several studies reported microstructural abnormalities in WM pathways connecting prefrontal and limbic regions in BPD. Alterations were observed in the UF, cingulum bundle, fornix, and ATR, as well as in broader frontal WM regions. Within the UF, reduced FA was reported in adolescents with BPD 39 and in adult patients bilaterally, accompanied by increased RD. 32 Additionally, increased RD in the right UF was positively associated with non-suicidal self-injury. 41 Alterations were also identified in the fornix, where several studies reported reduced FA in BPD.25,26,38 Lower fornix FA was associated with affective instability and abandonment avoidance. 25 In the cingulum, reduced FA in anterior and posterior segments was reported in BPD,25,44,45 while Ninomiya et al. observed reduced AD. 43 Reduction of FA in the cingulum was negatively correlated with anger symptoms. 25 Finally, abnormalities were reported in the ATR, including increased RD, with correlations between ATR diffusion measures and dysphoria or impulsivity.26,33

More widespread frontal WM abnormalities were also reported. Grant et al. found reduced FA in the inferior frontal WM of a population of BPD subjects with NSSI, which correlated with cognitive performance. 31 Reduced FA in orbitofrontal and prefrontal WM was observed.35,42 Although Rüsch et al. did not detect group differences in inferior frontal WM, higher MD in this region was associated with affective dysregulation, anger–hostility, dissociation, and general psychopathology in BPD. 34

3.2.3. Long association fibers

Abnormalities were additionally identified in long association WM pathways connecting frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital cortical regions in BPD. The most frequently implicated tracts included the IFOF, SLF, and ILF. Within the IFOF and related fronto-occipital pathways, reduced FA was reported in adolescents with BPD 39 and in adult samples. 46 Additional abnormalities included increased RD in bilateral IFOF and SFOF, 38 reduced AD, 43 increased RD in right prefrontal regions involving the IFOF, 33 and increased MD in adolescents with BPD. 40 Alterations were also frequently reported in the SLF. Reduced FA in the SLF was reported in several studies.28,40,46 Maier-Hein et al. observed increased RD bilaterally and increased AD in the left SLF. 38 Podwalski et al. further found reduced FA in the left SLF I, with FA in SLF I and SLF III mediating the relationship between traumatic experiences and depressive symptom dimensions. 29 Abnormalities in WM were also identified in the ILF. Reduced FA in the ILF was reported in adolescents with BPD 39 and in adult samples,28,46 while Ninomiya et al. observed reduced AD in this tract. 43

3.2.4. Projection fibers

Alterations were also reported in projection WM pathways, particularly within the corona radiata, internal capsule, and posterior thalamic radiation. Several studies identified microstructural abnormalities in the corona radiata, including reduced FA in the right superior and anterior segments 26 and in anterior and bilateral superior regions. 40 Further outcomes revealed increased MD in the anterior, superior, and posterior corona radiata 40 as well as increased ADC and reduced AD in these regions.33,41 Increased RD in the right anterior corona radiata was also reported. 41 Abnormalities were additionally observed in the internal capsule, including increased RD, 38 reduced FA in the retrolenticular segment, 46 and increased ADC in the right anterior limb. 33 Reduced FA was also identified in the external capsule and left anterior limb of the internal capsule. 35 Further alterations were reported in the posterior thalamic radiation, where reduced FA was observed.40,46

3.2.5. Additional WM regions and network-level findings

Increased MD was reported across several anterior regions, including the insula, anterior cingulate, pre- and postcentral cortex, temporal cortex, and the amygdala–hippocampus area. 35 Diffusion measures in projection pathways were also associated with clinical features: FA in the right superior corona radiata correlated positively with self-perception, 33 while increased MD in corona radiata regions was associated with non-suicidal self-injury, impulse control, and identity disturbance. 40 Additionally, RD in the right anterior corona radiata was positively related to childhood trauma. 41

Network-level analyses further showed increased MD within WM tracts of the dorsal default mode network (DMN), anterior salience network (SN), and right executive control network (ECN), accompanied by increased RD and AD. 47 Higher MD in the anterior SN and RECN was associated with suicide attempts and self-harm, while increased MD across these networks was also related to aggressive behavior. Finally, Quattrini et al. reported increased FA following Metacognitive Interpersonal Therapy (MIT) in the bilateral anterior thalamic radiation, left inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, forceps minor, and left cingulum, with increases in FA positively associated with improvements in metacognitive abilities. Notably, this remains the only study to date examining the effects of psychotherapy—the primary treatment for BPD—on WM structure. 48

4. Discussion

The results of the present narrative review indicate that WM alterations in BPD most consistently involve the CC, with recurrent findings of reduced FA and/or increased diffusivity, particularly in the genu, body, and splenium, which may reflect altered interhemispheric connectivity. This represents the most robust finding across the reviewed literature, as callosal abnormalities were reported across different analytical approaches and in both adult and adolescent samples. At the same time, converging evidence points to abnormalities within fronto-limbic pathways, including the UF, fornix, cingulum, and ATR, consistent with models proposing altered structural connectivity in neural systems involved in emotional regulation. These fronto-limbic and fronto-thalamic findings can therefore be considered convergent but less uniform than the CC findings, because the specific tract, diffusion metric, and direction of effect varied across studies. Beyond these core circuits, additional alterations were observed in long-range association tracts (e.g., SLF and ILF) and in projection pathways, such as the corona radiata and the internal capsule; however, these findings appear less consistently replicated and may be more sensitive to developmental stage, sample composition, and analytic method. Together, these findings suggest that BPD may be associated with both focal and distributed WM alterations in tracts anatomically connected with systems implicated in emotional regulation, cognition, and behavioral control. Diffusion abnormalities should therefore be interpreted as indirect and biologically non-specific markers of WM microstructure that provide a structural context for network models of BPD, rather than as direct evidence of impaired neural function or specific cognitive or clinical deficits.6,49 A schematic overview of the main WM pathways discussed in this review and their potential links with emotional regulation, cognitive control, and interpersonal functioning is presented in Figure 2.Figure 2.Schematic overview of the main white matter pathways discussed in this review and their potential links with core functional domains relevant to BPD.

Developmental stage may be particularly important for interpreting these findings. During adolescence, WM microstructure continues to mature through ongoing myelination, axonal organization, and remodeling, with many association and fronto-limbic pathways extending their development into early adulthood.16,39 In this context, the reduced FA and altered diffusivity reported in the fornix, ILF, IFOF, UF, corpus callosum, and cortico-thalamic pathways in adolescent BPD samples may reflect deviations from age-typical developmental trajectories rather than fixed structural deficits.37–39 New et al. further suggested that adolescents with BPD may fail to reach the age-related peak in FA observed in healthy adolescents, although this interpretation remains based on cross-sectional data. 39 Because adolescence combines heightened neurodevelopmental plasticity with the typical emergence of BPD symptoms, it may represent both a period of increased vulnerability and a potentially important window for early intervention. 16 However, longitudinal studies beginning before or close to symptom onset are required to determine whether these alterations predict symptom persistence, remission, or treatment response.

The CC, as the largest commissural WM structure, constitutes a critical substrate for interhemispheric integration, with its genu primarily connecting prefrontal and orbitofrontal regions, the body linking premotor and parietal areas, and the splenium subserving occipital connectivity. 50 Converging evidence across the provided literature suggests that CC is among the most consistently implicated WM pathways in BPD, with DTI studies demonstrating reduced FA within the CC, which may reflect alterations in underlying WM microstructure, potentially related to factors such as axonal organization or myelination. Compared with other tracts, the CC therefore provides the clearest example of cross-study convergence, although its biological interpretation remains limited by the non-specificity of DTI metrics. 19 Importantly, these abnormalities appear particularly prominent in anterior callosal regions, which connect prefrontal cortices commonly discussed in relation to regulatory control. The involvement of similar callosal regions in other psychiatric conditions further suggests that these abnormalities may reflect a transdiagnostic vulnerability rather than a BPD-specific marker. 35 Microstructural alterations in the CC have been observed across various psychiatric conditions. In bipolar disorder (BD), DTI tractography studies consistently report decreased FA and fiber density in the genu, body, and splenium of the CC, with heterogeneous findings in the forceps major and minor, suggesting disrupted interhemispheric connectivity that has been proposed to contribute to mood dysregulation. 51 Comparative morphological MRI studies indicate the CC is highly changed in obsessive-compulsive disorder, selectively altered in schizophrenia, but largely unchanged in BD and major depressive disorder. 52 Furthermore, the vulnerability of the CC to environmental influences—particularly childhood trauma—provides an important etiological framework, as early adversity has been associated with disrupted callosal development and may contribute to long-term impairments in interhemispheric integration and functional lateralization. 53

Building on the CC findings, diffusion MRI studies also report microstructural alterations within fronto-limbic and fronto-thalamic pathways, including the UF, cingulum, fornix, and ATR. These tracts provide anatomical connections between prefrontal, temporal, limbic, and thalamic regions, and therefore offer a structural context for models of BPD that emphasize affective dysregulation, salience processing, and impaired behavioral control.7,32,33,54 For example, reduced UF microstructural measures may be discussed in relation to socio-emotional processing because the UF connects orbitomedial prefrontal and anterior temporal regions, including the amygdala.32,33,54 Similarly, cingulum and fornix findings may be anatomically relevant to systems involved in cognitive control, contextual learning, and affective regulation.19,25,26,55,56 Abnormalities in the ATR may also suggest involvement of fronto-thalamic circuits, although their relationship to dysphoria, impulse control, or other clinical dimensions requires further behavioral and multimodal validation.26,33,57

Alterations in long-association fibers and projection pathways further suggest that WM findings in BPD may extend beyond core fronto-limbic circuits to broader cortico-cortical and cortico-subcortical networks.58,59 Long association tracts—such as the SLF and the ILF—connect frontal, temporal, parietal, and occipital regions and are commonly implicated in higher-order cognitive functions, including attention, working memory, language, and socio-emotional processing.60,61 Across the reviewed studies, these tracts frequently showed reduced FA and/or increased diffusivity, particularly within the SLF and ILF, which may reflect alterations in fronto-parietal and fronto-temporal connectivity.28,29,38,40,46 Notably, alterations in the SLF were linked to trauma-related symptom dimensions and affective dysregulation, suggesting a possible association with systems relevant to cognitive control and emotional processing. 29 However, trauma-related associations remain inconsistent across studies and may indicate a moderating influence of early adversity rather than a uniform mechanism explaining WM alterations in BPD. Similarly, projection fibers—including the corona radiata, internal capsule, and thalamic radiations—serve as critical conduits between cortical and subcortical structures, enabling efficient cortico-thalamic and cortico-striatal communication. Additionally, alterations in the IFOF—a tract connecting the occipital, temporal, and parietal regions to the frontal cortex, involved in cognitive control and response inhibition—were described as deviations in diffusion parameters and were associated with both affective and cognitive components.33,43 Consistent abnormalities in these pathways were reported across multiple studies.33,38,40,41,46 Nevertheless, because these findings involve several different tracts and diffusion indices, they should be interpreted as evidence for possible broader network involvement rather than as isolated tract-specific biomarkers.

Such a distributed pattern of alterations is consistent with the view that BPD may involve alterations in the integration of cognitive and emotional processing within large-scale brain networks described in the “triple network” model, where microstructural alterations may be associated with patterns of functional dysregulation observed in large-scale brain networks between internal mentation (DMN), salience detection (SN), and executive control (ECN).47,62 Within this framework, the SN supports the detection of behaviorally relevant internal and external stimuli and facilitates switching between the DMN and ECN, the DMN contributes to self-referential processing, autobiographical memory, and social cognition, whereas the ECN supports goal-directed attention and cognitive control.62,63 From this perspective, abnormalities in fronto-limbic, cingulate, callosal, fronto-parietal, and projection pathways may represent potential structural substrates of altered communication among systems supporting salience processing, self-referential cognition, and executive control. However, these anatomical correspondences should not be interpreted as one-to-one mappings, because individual white matter pathways contribute to communication across multiple functional networks. This interpretation is supported indirectly by DTI-based network findings (e.g., increased MD in the anterior SN, dorsal DMN, and right ECN, more pronounced in patients with self-injurious and aggressive behaviors) as well as functional connectivity data suggesting that these alterations may manifest differently depending on the level of analysis (e.g., amygdala–occipital cortex connectivity and graph-theoretical measures within a cerebellar cluster associated with the DMN).47,64 Importantly, a network-based interpretation of tract-level findings is not equivalent to a whole-brain structural connectomic analysis. Most studies included in the present review examined predefined tracts, regions of interest, or voxel-wise white matter skeletons rather than subject-level structural connectomes.

Quattrini et al. provided the first randomized controlled trial evidence suggesting that psychotherapy may alter WM microstructure in BPD. Using DTI, the authors reported increases in FA following Metacognitive Interpersonal Therapy, primarily within fronto-parietal and fronto-limbic pathways, including the anterior thalamic radiation, IFOF, forceps minor, and cingulum. These changes were associated with improvements in metacognitive abilities, which may reflect activity-dependent changes in WM microstructure, although the underlying biological mechanisms remain uncertain. However, the reported effects were limited in scope, partly due to uncorrected statistical thresholds, and their durability over time remains unknown. 48 Importantly, because this is a single randomized study without independent replication, these findings should be considered preliminary. Multivariate models combining WM measures with clinical, developmental, and functional data should test their added predictive value for symptom course, self-harm risk, and treatment response. Repeated imaging in adequately powered randomized trials could determine whether microstructural changes precede, accompany, or follow clinical improvement, clarifying their potential role in treatment monitoring. If replicated, the MIT findings may also inform personalized psychiatry by identifying potential treatment-related neuroplasticity, biological mechanisms, or response subgroups; however, they should not be viewed as evidence of a clinically useful biomarker or a specific biological process such as remyelination.

An additional interpretive limitation concerns the contribution of childhood trauma. Early-life adversity has been associated with WM differences in several tracts also implicated in BPD, including the anterior thalamic radiation, corona radiata, uncinate fasciculus, inferior fronto-occipital fasciculus, inferior longitudinal fasciculus, cingulum, and corpus callosum, while studies of PTSD report partly overlapping abnormalities in the corpus callosum, cingulum, superior longitudinal fasciculus, and frontal WM.17,53,65,66 Within BPD samples, however, associations have been mixed. Whalley et al. found no significant relationships between childhood trauma and FA in the cingulum or fornix, Rüsch et al. found no association of inferior frontal WM integrity with sexual abuse history or current PTSD, and Gan et al. reported no trauma–DTI associations surviving correction for multiple comparisons.25,26,34 In contrast, more recent studies linked childhood trauma to increased RD in the anterior corona radiata and suggested that SLF microstructure may partially mediate associations between traumatic experiences and anxiety-related symptoms.29,41

Methodological heterogeneity across the included studies represents a major source of inconsistency. Different analytical approaches appear to yield systematically different patterns of findings, with whole-brain methods (e.g., TBSS) more frequently identifying widespread alterations, whereas ROI-based and tractography approaches tend to report more localized, tract-specific changes. First, most studies relied on relatively small, often homogeneous samples (e.g., exclusively female or adolescent cohorts), typically ranging from fewer than 20 to around 50 participants, which limits statistical power and generalizability. In several cases, the absence of clinical control groups further complicates interpretation, as reported differences may not be disorder-specific. Second, substantial variability in analytic approaches—including ROI-based methods, VBA, TBSS, and tractography—introduces inconsistencies in spatial sensitivity and reproducibility across studies. This variability is central to the interpretation of the evidence: whole-brain approaches tend to support broader network-level involvement, whereas ROI-based and tractography studies more often emphasize specific fronto-limbic or callosal pathways. Differences in preprocessing pipelines, statistical thresholds, and diffusion metrics (e.g., FA versus RD or AD) further influence the sensitivity and interpretability of findings, potentially contributing to discrepancies in both the location and biological interpretation of reported abnormalities. In addition, comorbidity, trauma exposure, and medication status likely contribute to heterogeneity, because medication-free or treatment-naïve samples increase internal validity but may be less representative of typical clinical BPD populations.

Importantly, some inconsistencies stem from DTI’s intrinsic limitations. Conventional tensor models are unable to accurately resolve crossing fibers, leading to reduced anatomical specificity and potential misinterpretation of microstructural alterations. 67 Moreover, commonly used indices such as FA are non-specific and may reflect multiple underlying biological processes (e.g., myelination, axonal density), limiting interpretability. 49 DTI metrics also cannot directly distinguish between different microstructural mechanisms, such as axonal degeneration versus changes in myelin integrity. 68 Finally, variability in acquisition parameters and sensitivity to motion or noise further reduces comparability across studies.

Future research on WM alterations in BPD should move beyond conventional DTI toward more advanced diffusion modeling techniques with standardized acquisition protocols, allowing for more precise characterization of WM microstructure and improved comparability across studies. Classical DTI metrics are limited in resolving complex fiber configurations, such as crossing fibers, which can obscure biologically meaningful alterations. Recent approaches, including fixel-based analysis (FBA), enable fiber-specific quantification of properties such as fiber density and bundle cross-section, offering a more precise characterization of WM organization. 69 Complementary approaches include neurite orientation dispersion and density imaging (NODDI), which provides indices of neurite density and orientation dispersion, and diffusion kurtosis imaging (DKI), which characterizes non-Gaussian diffusion beyond the conventional tensor model.70,71 At the analytical level, voxel-wise approaches and graph-theoretical analysis of diffusion-derived connectomes may help identify spatially distributed abnormalities and quantify network properties such as integration, segregation, centrality, and hub organization. 72 Another key direction involves longitudinal study designs. Most current findings are based on cross-sectional data, limiting conclusions about causality. Longitudinal studies, particularly in adolescents or high-risk groups, are needed to determine whether WM alterations precede symptom onset or arise from illness progression, trauma, or treatment. This is especially relevant given evidence of early abnormalities linked to developmental processes and childhood trauma. 65 Future work should also prioritize multimodal imaging approaches integrating diffusion MRI with functional modalities. Combined studies have shown spatially converging structural and functional abnormalities within fronto-limbic circuits, suggesting that these modalities may provide complementary information about BPD-related network alterations. 35 Such frameworks may better explain how WM changes translate into network-level dysfunction underlying core symptoms. Finally, more treatment-focused neuroimaging studies are needed. Preliminary evidence suggests that psychotherapy may be associated with changes in WM microstructure, potentially reflecting activity-dependent plasticity. 48 Future research should examine how different interventions modulate specific pathways and whether these changes relate to clinical improvement. Taken together, these findings suggest that WM alterations in BPD may be better conceptualized as a heterogeneous, multi-level pattern of dysconnectivity encompassing interhemispheric, fronto-limbic, and large-scale network-level alterations. This perspective moves beyond tract-specific interpretations and supports a cautious network-based framework for generating testable hypotheses about the neurobiology of BPD.

The present review should be interpreted in light of several limitations. First, because it was designed as a narrative review, study identification and selection were not based on a fully reproducible systematic review procedure. Second, the literature remains limited, with only a small number of eligible DTI studies, limiting the strength of the conclusions. Third, publication bias cannot be excluded, as studies with significant findings are more likely to be published. Finally, substantial heterogeneity in sample characteristics, medication and comorbidity profiles, acquisition protocols, preprocessing methods, diffusion metrics, and analytical approaches limits direct comparability across studies and reduces the certainty of conclusions. Accordingly, clinical associations with suicidality, self-harm, anger, affective instability, anxiety, trauma, and symptom severity should be considered promising but preliminary until replicated in larger and clinically well-characterized cohorts.

Despite these limitations, the review has several strengths. It provides an integrative overview of WM abnormalities in BPD, highlighting converging evidence across key tracts, including the corpus callosum and fronto-limbic pathways. Including studies with diverse methodologies and populations offers a broad perspective on BPD neurobiology. Additionally, it identifies key methodological gaps and future directions, supporting further development of neuroimaging research in this field.

5. Conclusion

In conclusion, current evidence suggests that BPD is associated with alterations in WM microstructure; however, these findings are best understood as a heterogeneous and method-dependent pattern rather than a single, consistent neurobiological signature. Alterations were observed particularly within the corpus callosum and fronto-limbic pathways anatomically connected with regions implicated in emotion regulation, impulse control, and self-referential processing, with the corpus callosum representing the most consistently replicated finding across the reviewed studies. These findings are broadly consistent with neurobiological models proposing altered integration between prefrontal and limbic regions. However, their interpretation is constrained by methodological variability and the limited biological specificity of diffusion metrics. Additionally, involvement of long-range association and projection tracts raises the possibility of broader network-level alterations, although their cognitive and behavioral significance remains to be established.

Differences in diffusion metrics, analytic approaches, and sample characteristics suggest that WM abnormalities in BPD may reflect a complex, potentially subtype-specific profile. Importantly, emerging links between diffusion metrics and clinical features—including suicidality, self-harm, anger, affective instability, anxiety, trauma, and symptom severity—suggest that diffusion imaging may have biomarker potential; however, these associations remain preliminary, partly isolated, and strongly dependent on sample characteristics.

Future studies should emphasize longitudinal, multimodal, and methodologically standardized approaches to disentangle true neurobiological alterations from variability introduced by study design and analytic strategies, thereby better capturing microstructural complexity and clarifying the relationship between WM microstructure, clinical features, and proposed neurobiological models of BPD.
