Medieval (arrow) weapon injuries in contemporary surgical practice: Impaled posterior thoracic wall arrowhead leading to haemo-thorax: Management protocols. Case report
Authors: Ahmed Shabhay, Zarina Shabhay, Fabian Anaclet Massaga, Amri Salim Mwami, Samwel Chugulu
Published: 2023

Introduction

Arrow injuries are rare in contemporary surgical practice [[1], [2], [3]]. Pericardial arrow injuries and survival are scarce [4,5]. The discovery of the bow and arrow mechanism more than 10,000 years back revolutionized the use of “ranged weapons” in wars and hunting [[6], [7], [8]]. Thus the bow and arrow mechanism is referred to as an ancient weapon [6,7,[9], [10], [11]]. Despite having low kinetic energy unlike gunshot wounds; arrows have relatively considerable capability to penetrate soft tissues and flat bones [1,2,6,8,9,12]. It is believed arrows have claimed more fatalities than any other weapon in the history of combat warfare [6,7,10]. Due to the characteristic silent nature of the bow and arrow mechanism, it is the preferred weapon in guerilla and certain assassination assaults [9,13].

To add to the primary lethal potential and challenges of arrow wounds management, the improvisations of modifying and additions of the arrow head with barbs, spikes and metal fragments and modifying tips shapes into crescent shapes has increased the weapons lethal potential and surgical management challenges [3,5,7,10] such as it is of the case presented were the impaled arrow head had a smooth sharp head with three spikes on either sides of its tail which led to excruciating pain to the victim upon pre-hospital attempts of removing the impaled arrow head. Improvisations of the arrow tip shapes can lead to tamponade effect which can arise due to narrowing of wound tracts around arrow shafts; with broad shaped arrow heads causing star-like gaping wounds and field tips causing sharp incision-like wounds typical to the wound seen in the case presented [1,9].

In stable patients with impaled thoracic arrow injuries, with high index of suspicion of major vessel or organ involvement advanced radiological investigations should be done such as enhanced spiral computed tomography [1]. Neurological examination should also be done to exclude neurotoxins as most arrow heads are dipped and tainted with “poisons” [2,6,12].

This work has been reported in line with the SCARE 2020 criteria [14].

Case report

An 18-year-old boy presented to the emergency department with history of being assaulted with an arrow 8-h prior admission and presented with an impaled arrowhead on his posterior thoracic wall at the level of 12th thoracic vertebrae. (Fig. 1 (a), (b) and (c)). On examination he was conscious with a Glasgow coma scale 15/15, stable vitals; BP: 132/68mmHG, PR: 92b/min, RR: 18 cycles/min, SPO2: 98 % in room air, Temp: 36.6 °C and RBG 7.0 mmol/L. Respiratory system examination revealed; 1 cm circumferential wound on posterior thoracic wall at the level of T12 on the left side, protruding tip of a tail of a metal arrow head, reduced air entry on right side of the chest with no signs of pneumo-thorax. Abdominal examination and Neurological examination were normal. Thoraco-lumbar x ray was done and revealed an embedded arrowhead trans-versing across the posterior thoracic wall without bone involvement (vertebrae). (Fig. 2). Underwater seal drainage (UWSD) was inserted on the right hemithorax and an initial 900mls of blood was drained. No bubbles were noted on the UWSD tube (Fig. 3). Decision was made to retrieve the impaled arrowhead under general anaesthesia incase an open thoracotomy would necessitate. Under general anaesthesia, 2 cm oblique incisions were made on either side of the penetration wound, following the direction of the arrowhead with blunt dissection using non toothed dissecting forceps trapezius muscle fibers trapping the arrowhead spikes were released and dissection deepened up to the smooth sharp arrowhead. The arrowhead was then removed. A 12.5 cm long arrowhead was retrieved with 3 spikes on either side of its tail. (Fig. 4). The wound tract was inspected, no bleeding was noted, irrigated, debrided, muscles fibers and subcutaneous tissue apposed, drain inserted and skin edges apposed. No bleeding was collected from the drain in 24 h. post-operatively the patient was covered with tetanus toxoid immunoglobulin and broad-spectrum antibiotics. A total of 500mls of blood was further collected in the UWSD tube in a course of 96 h before the fluid turning Serosanguineous. The patient was discharged on the sixth day post admission. His post discharge clinic was uneventful with no reported complications/recurrence of either haemo/pneumo-thorax.

Discussion

“Modern” trauma surgeons have limited exposure to arrow wounds due to decline in use of medieval weapon [6,10,11]. The incidence of arrow injuries declined after discovery firearms after the sixteenth century [6,7]. Literature on the management of arrow injuries is limited [7]. However in certain regions and in some guerilla and tribal conflicts arrow and bow mechanism are still in use among ranged weapons [4,6,9,12,13,15]. In our country, there are documented cases of members of security forces being assaulted with arrows by bandits [16,17]. Documented major conflicts were arrow fatalities were recorded included World war II and the Vietnam war [6].

The high lethal potential is attributed due to physical destruction of tissues, vital organs/vessels injuries or from infection. It has been noted for ages arrow heads are dipped/tainted with “poisons” such as decomposed poisonous snakes, or in feacal material [5,6]. The lethal potential has three avenues of killing; immediate death from physical vital organ injury, intermediate from neurological/haematological toxin contamination and late from dreadful anaerobic and aerobic bacterial local and generalized septicaemia [6]. This led to the practice of wound sucking post arrow extraction in attempts to suck out the “poison” [6].

Despite arrow injuries being classified as low velocity wounds [1,3], the lethal potential increases with injuries to vital organs or large vessels [5,8]. Severity and extent of injuries depend upon range from victim, degree of penetration and arrow tip contamination with “poisons” or bacteria [11]. Arrows cause injuries by lacerating, perforating or transecting organs. Immediate fatalities occur when direct injury to vital organ or vessel occurs [3].

In a review of 154 cases of arrow injuries, Lt Col Bill noted most common injured area was the upper extremities 29.9 %, followed by the abdomen 22.1 %, chest 18.2 %, head and neck 16.9 %, lower extremities 11.7 % and direct injury to the heart “bulls eye” 1.3 % [7]. Majority of the fatalities were due to peritonitis. This was a major reason for his adage “the arrowhead must be extracted or the patient will die” in advocating for laparotomies in impaled abdominal injuries [6]. In an observation in Nigeria, the predominant area affected was the chest 40 %, followed by the Head and Neck 26 %, upper limb 13.7 %, abdomen 11 %, lower limb 6.8 % and perineum 2.7 % [3]. In the 8 cases seen in the report there was no incidence of small bowel injury, with perhaps the mobility of the bowels playing a role [3].

It is noted arrow injuries inflicted in non-micro floral areas victims rarely suffer from infections [7], unlike the bowels in the abdominal cavity. In the 19th century abdominal and pelvic arrow injuries were mostly fatal due to haemorrhage and peritonitis due to feacal contamination [7]. Other causes of death included massive haemorrhage when major vessels are involved, tetanus from infected arrow-tips, empyema and shock [7].

Accidents, suicide attempts and hunting injuries are among the major causes of recent arrow injuries admissions in contemporary surgical practice [5,6,9]. Management protocol of arrow injuries dates back to ancient times [6]. Prominent surgeons who worked and published on management of arrow injuries include Celsus, Paulus, Paré and Brevet Lieutenant Colonel Joseph H. Bill [6]. Some ancient principles of management of arrow injuries still hold water today [6]. Lt Col Bill put forward certain principles which included removal of the arrow head as soon as possible, use of extensive incisions for search and retrieval of arrow heads, arrow heads extraction may involve pushing or pulling out of the impaled object and encouragement of wound healing by primary intention [6]. He noted an arrow causes both a punctured and incised wound [7]. Non fixed visceral organs are incised through the arrow trajectory [1,3].

Lt Col Bill in the management of arrow injuries, through and through injuries were managed by observation and inspection of signs of vital organ injuries and managed accordingly, impaled arrow heads, decision was made on whether pushing it through or pulling it out which would be the best course of action. Decision was based upon the encounter of which specific organ along the trajectory when pushed out.

This was well narrated by Celsus (Aulus Cornelius Celsus, 25 BCE–50 CE) in his publication De Medicina (published around 47 CE) on management of arrow injuries [10].

“If the missile is not deeply seated, and lies in superficial tissue, or if it is certain that it has not crossed the line of large blood vessels or sinews, there is nothing better than to pull it out by the way it entered. But if the distance it has to be withdrawn is greater than that which remains to be forced through, or if it has crossed the line of blood vessels and sinews, it is more convenient to lay open the rest of its course and so draw it out. For it will be more easily got at and more safely pulled out”. Bone involvement was assessed by gentle twisting of the arrow shaft to ascertain “mobility or non-mobility” of the arrow. Instrument devised for the extraction of the impaled arrow-heads included the wire loop, strong forceps and the crocodile forceps [7].

In a review article on impaled thoracic objects, Filho et al. noted injury pattern to be of haemothorax (43.75 %), lung laceration (40.62 %), pneumothorax (28.12 %), rib fracture, and pulmonary contusion 25 %. Injuries to the heart, esophagus, diaphragm, right bronchus, thoracic vertebrae, thoracic aorta, scapula, sternum fracture, and lung laceration with rib fracture constituted 3–15 %. 15.62 % of the cases the diaphragm was involved with abdominal organs involvement [18].

Literature on the management of impaled objects/arrows penetrating thoracic injuries is scarce as this presentation is rare. Due to unique manner of individual presentation of cases, it is thus difficult to standardize the method of treatment [12,18]. Open thoracotomy is the recommended approach in management of impaled penetrating thoracic injuries involving major vessels or organs [1,18]. Underwater seal drainage is inserted for initial management of pneumo-haemo thorax [1,12]. Penetrating injuries to lungs may lead to broncho-pleural fistula, lung collapse, hilar structures damage or broncho-trauma [2,12]. The intra-abdominal thoracic area is a challenging area for penetrating trauma assessment as injuries can span from hepatic, splenic, gastric to lower oesophageal and the diaphragm [19].

Impaled thoracic injuries cause fright even to attending medical personnel due to its lethal potential [18]. Video-thoracoscopy/thoracoscopy has diagnostic value but limitations in management of major vessel or organ involvement [1]. In stable patients, impaled objects/arrows maybe be retrieved under thoracoscopy guidance [18]. When minimal invasive techniques fails, the procedure can always be converted to the standard open thoracotomy [18].

Retrieval should be withheld until major vessel or organ involvement or proximity with barbed/spiked arrowhead is ruled out [12]. Documented case of death following retrieval in battlefield had been documented by Lt Col Bill in 1862 thus creating awareness of the blocking effect of impaled objects to vessels [18]. This solidify the importance of advanced radiological modalities such as angiography and CT scanning prior planning of impaled objects [12,18]. Filho et al. noted the cases which advanced radiological investigations were done computed tomography scanning and angiography results were consisted with post thoracotomy findings adding weight of radiological results having more consideration in decision making of conservative versus operative management [18].

Basic Roentgenography investigations such as the chest x ray are still of value in penetrating thoracic injuries as they can identify life threating conditions such as pneumo/haemo-thorax, subcutaneous emphysema, retained foreign bodies such as bullets and arrowheads and rib fractures. However, contrast enhanced CT scan is the investigation of choice as it can also outline wound tracks, pick up any accompanying oesophageal, tracheal or cardiac injuries [20].CT scanning is also capable of revealing small and medium-sized pneumo/haemo-thoraces since chest Roentgenography misses these pathologies due to patients' supine position during the investigations [20,21]. In unstable patients during the primary survey, the extended-Focused Assessment with Sonography in Trauma (eFAST) is the modality of choice [21].

Impaled barbed/spiked arrows cause more tissue damage upon retrieval [1]. This lead to Celsus to develop a surgical instrument, named the spoon of Diocles, to assist in arrow heads retrievals [7,10].

Filho et al. recommended all unstable patients to undergo an open thoracotomy. UWSD draining of more than 1500mls of blood in 24 h or continued hourly blood loss of 250 mL or more for 3 consecutive hours warrants a thoracotomy in either blunt or penetrating thoracic injury [22]. In our case, 900mls of blood were drained initially, followed by 500mls of blood over 96 h. The patient's impaled arrow was removed under general anaesthesia in theater. We opine the tip of the arrow had penetrated the parietal pleural reflections. For stable patients, CT scanning with angiography should be done, presence of other organ injuries should warrant a thoracotomy, presence of extensive parenchymal injury a thoracoscopy should be done and when there is minimal parenchymal wall injury conservative management should be employed. However, it should be noted, the direct impaled object removal should be done in settings were thoracotomy is possible as the patient is in the operating room, intubated, monitored, and prepared. Consultation to vascular and thoracic surgeons is recommended. There should be minimal handling of the impaled object and its protruding length shortened to facilitate transportation, imaging and positioning during anaesthesia intubation and surgery [2,18]. Complimentary tests such as bronchoscopy and endoscopy when in doubt of airway or gastro-intestinal involvement can be added. Infection and tetanus prevention should be addressed by institution of broad spectrum antibiotics, tetanus immunoglobulin, and local tissue debridement and irrigation [18].

Conclusion

Arrow injuries are as fatal as any high energy weapon. Prompt radiological investigations and management is critical in the early hours post injury.
