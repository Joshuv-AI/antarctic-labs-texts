High-foaming modified walnut protein enhances milk coffee quality and volatile aroma profile
Authors: Di Wang, Zelin Li, Hong Zhao, Chao Li, Bin Gao, Kunxian Wang, Jingqiu Peng, Chunxiu Peng, Jiashun Gong
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Introduction

Walnuts are a globally consumed nut. China is the world's largest walnut producer and holds an important position among economic forest tree species. The protein content of walnut meal after oil extraction ranges from 40% to 45%, which is comparable to that of soybean meal, and it contains 18 kinds of amino acids. Except for lysine, the levels of all essential amino acids meet or exceed the FAO/WHO (2013), recommended standards, indicating that walnut meal has strong potential as a source of functional proteins [1]. Walnut protein (WP) is mainly composed of glutelin, albumin, globulin, and prolamin. Glutelin accounts for 72.06% of the total protein content, which greatly affects the solubility of WP and consequently limits its comprehensive utilization [2]. To date, various modification methods have been applied to improve the functional properties of WP [3]. Zhao et al. [4] proposed an ultrasonic-assisted restriction endonuclease digestion combined with glycosylation-targeted modification technology, which significantly increased the solubility of WP by 91.63%. Yang et al. [5] reported that high-speed shearing, heat treatment, and ultrasonic treatment reduced the average particle size of WP by 55.38%, 48.31%, and 73.66%, respectively. These treatments significantly improved the gel-like properties and stability of WP, enhanced its adsorption at the emulsion droplet interface, and altered its rheological properties.

Milk coffee is a widely consumed beverage that blends the creamy sweetness of milk with the characteristic aroma and taste of coffee, and it has become one of the most important coffee products in the current market. According to iResearch (https://www.iresearch.cn/index.php), milk coffee generated significant market value globally in 2022, with the United States leading at $575 million. China, considered one of the most promising markets for milk coffee, generated a market value of 67.6 billion CNY. In recent years, increasing global awareness of greenhouse gas reduction and growing consumers' demand for product diversification have accelerated the partial or complete replacement of animal-based products with plant-based alternatives [6]. Modified plant proteins, in particular, exhibit excellent foam ability and stability, emulsifying properties, and gel characteristics, making them highly suitable for applications in the food processing industry [7], [8]. Manus et al. [9] successfully developed highly nutritious plant-based protein beverages rich in low-molecular-weight peptides by fermenting pea and rice or pea and hemp proteins with probiotics. The foaming properties of modified plant proteins are particularly important in milk coffee applications, as stable and fine‑bubbled foam not only enhances visual appeal and creamy mouthfeel but also influences the release of volatile aroma compounds during consumption [10]. Therefore, improving the foaming capacity and foam stability of WP is expected to enhance the sensory quality of plant‑based milk coffee products. Our previous studies successfully developed fibrous meat analogs by combining WP with gluten and konjac glucomannan, respectively, and demonstrated that konjac glucomannan is a key component enabling WP to form meat-like structures during high-moisture extrusion [11], [12]. To date, however, no studies have reported the application of modified walnut protein in milk coffee products.

This study aimed to modify WP using ultra-high pressure (H), ultrasound (U), alkaline protease (E), ultra-high pressure combined with alkaline protease (HE), and ultrasound combined with alkaline protease (UE), and to explore the effects of these modification methods on the physicochemical properties, structural characteristics, functional properties, and rheological behavior of WP. The selection of these five modification methods and their corresponding process parameters was based on systematic preliminary experiments that identified the optimal conditions for each treatment. Furthermore, the optimally modified WP was selected to evaluate its influence on the foam properties, stability, and flavor profile of milk coffee. The findings of this study provide both a theoretical basis and experimental foundation for the comprehensive utilization of walnut protein and the diversification of coffee products.

Materials and methods

Materials and chemicals

Walnut protein (WP, 83.80% purity) was purchased from Liaoning Changbai Xianzi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). Coomassie Brilliant Blue G250, 8-anilino-1-naphthalenesulfonic acid ammonium salt (ANS), and 2-nitrobenzoic acid (DTNB) were purchased from Shanghai Yien Chemical Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Bovine serum albumin (BSA), sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS), alkaline protease, pepsin, and trypsin were purchased from Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd. (China). Protein molecular weight marker was purchased from Bio-Rad Laboratories, Inc. (USA). The 2-methyl-3-heptanone standard was purchased from Yuan Ye Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (China). All chemicals and reagents were of analytical grade unless otherwise specified.

Preparation of modified WP samples

Before initiating all WP modifications, the optimal ultrasonic power for WP modification suitable for coffee foam milk was optimized. A fixed frequency (adjustable energy) of 40 with a treatment time of 15 min at 25°C (5% WP solution, 100 mL volume) was used. Six power levels (0, 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1000 W) were tested, with solubility and foaming capacity as response variables.

All processing conditions for modified WP were determined based on optimal parameters established through preliminary experiments. For ultra-high pressure treatment (H), 20 g of WP was vacuum-sealed in polyethylene bags and treated in an ultra-high pressure unit at 188 MPa for 14 min using tap water as the pressure-transmitting medium. For ultrasonic treatment (U), a 5% WP solution was prepared and stirred at room temperature for 30 min, followed by ultrasonic treatment at 600 W for 15 min with a fixed frequency (adjustable energy) of 40 kHz. For alkaline protease treatment (E), a 5% (w/v) WP solution was prepared, stirred at room temperature for 30 min, and then subjected to enzymatic hydrolysis at 50°C and pH 9.0 for 2 h. For ultra-high pressure-alkaline protease treatment (HE), 20 g of WP was vacuum-packed and treated at 188 MPa for 14 min, after which the sample was dispersed in water to obtain a 5% (w/v) solution stirred at room temperature for 30 min, and subsequently hydrolyzed under the same enzymatic conditions (50°C, pH 9.0, 2 h). For ultrasonic-alkaline protease treatment (UE), a 5% (w/v) WP solution was prepared and stirred at room temperature for 30 min, followed by ultrasonic treatment at 600 W for 15 min with a fixed frequency (adjustable energy) of 40 kHz. The resulting solution was then subjected to enzymatic hydrolysis at 50°C and pH 9.0 for 2 h.

Physicochemical properties detection

The ultraviolet (UV) absorption spectra, electrophoretic profiles, molecular weight distribution, X-ray diffraction patterns, particle size distribution, zeta potential, contact angle, surface hydrophobicity, free sulfhydryl content, and differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) thermograms of the samples were determined according to previous studies [13], [14].

Structural characterization of different modified WP

Environmental scanning electron microscopy (SEM) was employed to observe the surface microstructure of different vacuum freeze-dried WP samples [4]. SEM images were acquired from multiple fields of view at magnifications of 500×, 2000×, and 4000×. Fourier transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy was performed by pressing the samples into a pellet with KBr, and spectra were recorded over the wavelength range of 400–4000 cm−1. The amide I region (1600–1700 cm−1) was used for protein secondary structure analysis. Circular dichroism (CD) spectroscopy was also used to characterize the secondary structure of WP. Measurements were conducted over a wavelength range of 190 to 250 nm at a scanning rate of 100 nm/min, a temperature of 25℃, a resolution of 0.5 nm, and a sensitivity of 100 mdeg/cm.

Functional properties determination

The functional properties of the samples, including solubility, foaming capacity, foam stability, emulsifying activity, emulsion stability, water-holding capacity, and oil-holding capacity, were determined according to previously described methods [4], [15].

Rheological behavior of different modified WP

The apparent viscosity and shear stress of 20% (w/v) WP solution were measured over a shear rate range of 0.01 to 1000.00 s−1. The storage modulus (G'), loss modulus (G''), and loss tangent (tan δ) were also determined. All measurements were performed at 25℃ according to a previously described method [16].

Foam microstructure observation

The foam samples were fixed on glass slides and observed under a fluorescence inverted microscope to evaluate their microstructure. The eyepiece and objective lens magnification were set at 10 × and 4×, respectively. Foam images were captured at different time intervals within 30 min.

Determination of in vitro digestibility

Electrolyte stock solutions for simulated gastric and intestinal fluids were prepared as described in Table S1 and stored at −20°C until use. Because liquid beverages are consumed rapidly and therefore undergo minimal oral processing, this study focused primarily on the gastric and intestinal digestions. The in vitro digestion process was conducted following a previously described method [9]. The WPM groups (0%, 2%, 6%, 10%, and 14%, v/v) represented milk samples containing different proportions of modified WP solution. The concentration of modified WP solution used in the milk samples was 10% (m/v).

Sensory evaluation of WP foaming milk coffee

Based on the typical cappuccino formulation, in which the volume ratio of milk to coffee is 36:150 mL (v/v), different volumes of WP foaming milk were mixed with liquid coffee. A trained sensory panel consisting of 10 members (5 males and 5 females, average age 25 years) was recruited from Yunnan Agricultural University, China. All panelists had received formal training and were capable of evaluating the sensory characteristics of different beverages in accordance with Chinese national standards. Before the sensory evaluation, all participants were fully informed of the experimental procedures and provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Research Ethics Committee of Yunnan Agricultural University. The sensory attributes evaluated included aroma, color, taste, appearance, and overall acceptability.

Electronic tongue and electronic nose analysis

The electronic tongue and electronic nose analysis of the milk coffee samples were conducted according to the method described by Hong et al. [17].

Determination of volatile compounds

The volatile compounds in coffee liquid (C), milk coffee (WPMC0), 10% WP foaming milk (WPM 10%), and coffee containing milk and 10% WP foaming milk (WPMC 10%) were analyzed using headspace solid-phase microextraction coupled with gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (HS-SPME-GC–MS). For headspace extraction, a 5 mL sample was placed into a 20 mL headspace vial, followed by the addition of 0.50 g of sodium chloride and 64 μL of the 2-methyl-3-heptanone (10 μg/mL) internal standard solution. The sample was equilibrated at 70°C for 5 min. Separation was performed on an Rtx-5MS capillary column (30.0 m × 0.25 mm, 0.25 μm) using helium as the carrier gas at a constant flow rate of 1.2 mL/min. The oven temperature was programmed as follows: initial temperature of 40℃ (held for 3 min), increased to 180℃ at 10℃/min and held for 3 min, then increased to 200℃ at 2℃/min and held for 3 min, and finally increased to 230℃ at 5℃/min. The mass spectrometer was operated in electron ionization (EI) mode at 70 eV, with an interface temperature of 200℃, an ion source temperature of 250℃, and a mass scan range of 40 to 350 m/z. Volatile compounds were identified by comparison with the NIST mass spectral library, and quantitative analysis was performed using the peak area normalization method with an internal standard.

Statistical analysis

All experiments were conducted in biological triplicate to ensure reproducibility. Graphs were plotted using an online platform (https://www.bioinformatics.com.cn/). Foaming diameters and foam distribution were analyzed using Image J software (National Institutes of Health, USA). Curve fitting was performed using RStudio, and secondary structure composition was calculated based on the fitted data. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) followed by Duncan's post hoc test was conducted using the R statistical package. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results and discussion

Optimal ultrasonic power optimization results for WP modification

The results indicated that the untreated WP showed solubility of 9.70% and foaming capacity of 34.67%. As power increased, solubility and foaming capacity first increased and then decreased, reaching maxima at 600 W (12.15% and 49.33%, respectively; p < 0.05 vs. untreated). In contrast, foam stability decreased from 59.59% (untreated) to 53.10% after 600 W, indicating that excessive unfolding destabilizes the foam (Fig. 1). Therefore, 600 W was selected as the optimal power. Effects of different modification methods on physicochemical properties and structure of WP

Effects of different modification methods on physicochemical properties

Proteins exhibit characteristic absorption near 280 nm due to aromatic amino acids, including tryptophan, tyrosine, and phenylalanine [18]. As shown in Fig. 2A, all WP samples displayed absorption peaks around 280 nm, while the modified proteins exhibited higher peak intensities. This observation indicates that the modification treatments enhanced the exposure of aromatic amino acid residues that were previously buried within the protein structure [19]. The sodium dodecyl sulfate polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) results revealed that, compared with the control group, the H and U groups showed distinct bands at approximately 8–13 kDa, 13–20 kDa, and 30–40 kDa, while the bands in the range of 55–68 kDa were relatively faint. However, the proportion of low-molecular-weight subunits was higher, indicating that individual treatments with ultra-high pressure and ultrasound had only limited effects on the overall molecular weight distribution of WP. In contrast, the E, HE, and UE groups exhibited a significant decrease in molecular weight, with the primary subunit being 20 kDa (Fig. 2B and C). This observation can be attributed to enzymatic hydrolysis alone or in combination with high-pressure or ultrasonic pretreatment, which promotes protein cleavage followed by reaggregation of peptides with different molecular weights [19]. In addition, the modification treatment did not significant changes in the crystalline regions of WP (Fig. 2D). However, enzymatic modification resulted in a more uniform particle size distribution (approximately 100 nm), and all modification methods except the UE group could increase the absolute value of the zeta potential of WP (Fig. 2E and F).Fig. 2The effect of different modification methods on physicochemical properties of WP. The larger the contact angle increases with increasing surface hydrophobicity and is commonly used to characterize interfacial wettability thereby reflecting the hydrophilic and hydrophobic properties of proteins [15]. Modification generally reduced the contact angle of WP, with especially the enzyme-involved modification methods showing a more significant effect (p < 0.05) (Fig. 2G), indicating an overall increase in hydrophilicity. In contrast, surface hydrophobicity increased significantly in all treatment groups except U and E, with the UE group exhibiting the highest value, corresponding to an increase of 5100.67 a.u. (Fig. 2H). This effect may be attributed to the exposure of previously buried hydrophobic groups induced by structural modification, as well as the formation of transient and reversible hydrophobic interactions among exposed residues [14]. It is also important to note that contact angle measurement reflects macroscopic surface wettability, whereas surface hydrophobicity assays detect hydrophobic patches at the molecular level. Structural modification, particularly ultrasound-assisted enzymatic treatment, induces protein unfolding, which simultaneously exposes internal hydrophobic residues (increasing measured surface hydrophobicity) and polar or charged groups (decreasing contact angle) [13], [20]. This dual exposure is characteristic of ultrasound- and enzyme-modified proteins and is beneficial for foaming and emulsifying properties, as it facilitates rapid adsorption at air–water interfaces while maintaining good dispersibility [21].

The results confirmed that all modification treatments indeed increased the content of free sulfhydryl groups in WP (Fig. 2I), indicating that these modification methods promoted the exposure of reactive functional groups. Free sulfhydryl groups play a key role in the formation of intra- and inter-molecular covalent bonds, and their content is widely used as an indicator of the degree of protein denaturation. In general, an increase in free sulfhydryl groups is associated with the exposure of buried hydrophobic groups, dissociation of protein subunits, and cleavage of disulfide (S-S) bonds within the protein structure [15]. To further evaluate the applicability of modified WP in milk coffee systems, the thermal stability of different WP samples was analyzed using DSC. The denaturation temperature (Td) reflects protein thermal stability, while the enthalpy change (ΔH) reflects the extent of molecular aggregation [14]. The findings of this study demonstrated that, although most modification methods enhanced the thermal stability of WP, they generally reduced the degree of molecular aggregation to varying extents. Among all treatments, Group E exhibited the lowest ΔH value (193.80 ± 3.54 J/g) (Fig. 2J and Table S2).

The effect of different modification methods on the microstructure

The microstructure of different modified WP samples was characterized using SEM. The Control group exhibited predominantly spherical structures with a loose and porous surface morphology (Fig. 3A). Compared with the Control group, all modification methods except ultrasonic treatment induced varying degrees of protein cross-linking of WP, resulting in irregular sheet-like structures with uneven surfaces and pores. In addition, small spherical protein particles were observed protruding from the surface (Fig. 3B and D–F). These structural changes may be attributed to protein cross-linking induced by ultra-high pressure, enzymatic treatment, or their combined effects. Under pressures ranging from 100 to 1000 MPa, hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds within the tertiary and quaternary structures of proteins are disrupted, leading to protein unfolding. The unfolding exposes previously buried functional groups, including free −SH groups, hydrophobic side chains, and carbonyl and amino groups. These exposed groups subsequently act as potential cross-linking sites [13]. Alkaline protease treatment hydrolyzes proteins into short peptides enriched with exposed hydrophobic or aromatic residues, such as phenylalanine (Phe), leucine (Leu), and tyrosine (Tyr). These groups can participate in intermolecular reactions with adjacent lysine –NH2 groups, including formation of ε-(γ-glutamyl)lysine isopeptide bonds via transpeptidation/condensation reactions. They may also undergo Schiff base formation, Michael addition, or coupling reactions involving –NH2 or −SH groups, leading to Tyr-Tyr or Tyr-Lys cross-linking and ultimately resulting in peptide-peptide or peptide-parent protein soluble aggregates [22]. Ultrasonic modification largely preserved the initial spherical morphology of WP, but increased surface porosity of the protein particles (Fig. 3C), likely due to localized structural disruption induced by ultrasonic cavitation [13]. During ultrasonic and ultrasonic‑assisted enzymatic treatments, cavitation generates intense microjets, shock waves, and shear forces that physically disrupt WP aggregates and promote unfolding of the globular protein structure [13]. This loosening of the protein matrix exposes previously buried hydrophobic regions and peptide bonds, thereby increasing the surface area and accessibility of cleavage sites for alkaline protease. Subsequent enzymatic hydrolysis further cleaves specific peptide bonds, producing smaller peptides and exposing additional hydrophobic and hydrophilic functional groups without excessive degradation, because ultrasonic pretreatment enables a milder and more controlled enzymatic reaction [4]. Additionally, ultrasound may enhance enzyme kinetics by improving mass transfer and reducing enzyme‑substrate diffusion limitations [4]. Consequently, the combined treatment produces moderately hydrolyzed proteins with high molecular flexibility, balanced hydrophobic and hydrophilic characteristics, and excellent foaming properties compared with ultrasound or enzymatic treatment alone.Fig. 3Scanning electron microscope images of WP modified by different methods (magnification: 500×, 2000×, and 4000×). Effects of different modification methods on the functional properties of WP

Solubility, emulsifying properties, water-holding capacity, oil-holding capacity, foaming properties, and foam stability are important functional characteristics of proteins that determine their potential applications in food systems [25]. When dissolved protein molecules adsorb at an interface, their hydrophobic groups orient toward the oil phase or gas phase, while their hydrophilic groups orient toward the aqueous phase [26]. Through intermolecular interactions, proteins form a viscoelastic interfacial film that prevents the coalescence and aggregation of oil droplets or bubbles, thereby enhancing emulsifying activity and foaming capacity [21]. Water-holding and oil-holding capacities are primarily associated with the gel-forming and thickening properties of proteins, which indirectly contribute to emulsion and foam stability by stabilizing the aqueous phase (aqueous phase) [27]. Proteins with high water- and oil-holding capacities often form highly viscous systems, such as protein gels or concentrated protein solutions, which can reduce the rate of liquid outflow from the foam matrix and consequently improve foam stability [28]. Owing to their desirable foaming properties and foam stability, such proteins have promising applications in milk coffee beverages, including cappuccino and latte products [10].

This study demonstrated that the modification treatments generally improved the solubility and emulsifying properties of WP, while reducing its water-holding and oil-holding capacities, except in the UE group (Fig. 5A–C). These changes may be attributed to structural modifications induced by the treatments (H, U, E, HE, and UE), which promoted protein unfolding, increased molecular flexibility, and exposed internal hydrophobic and hydrophilic groups, thereby affecting the aggregation behavior, folding state, and functional properties of WP [13]. As shown in Fig. 5D, enzyme-associated modification methods involving enzymes significantly enhanced the foaming properties and foam stability of WP. Compared with the Control group, the modification treatments, except for the H group, increased the number of bubbles and reduced bubble size in the WP solution, while maintaining improved foam stability over 30 min. These findings further indicated that modification promoted unfolding and increased flexibility of the WP structure (Fig. 5E and F). The enhanced foaming and functional properties observed in the UE group may be partly attributed to the enhanced enzymatic efficiency induced by ultrasonic treatment. Ultrasonic cavitation disrupts protein aggregates and induces unfolding of the globular protein structure, thereby exposing additional cleavage sites for alkaline protease and enhancing enzymatic hydrolysis [22], [29]. In addition, the reduction in surface tension enables proteins to adsorb more rapidly at the gas–water interface, facilitating foam formation [28]. Moreover, the modification slows down film aggregation at the gas–liquid interface, thereby increasing foam stability.Fig. 5The effect of different modification methods on the functional properties of WP. Effects of different modification methods on the rheological properties of WP

The apparent viscosity of all WP samples decreased with increasing shear rate in the range of 0.01 to 1000 s−1, indicating shear-thinning behavior. The apparent viscosity of WP treated with the UE and HE groups was 4.16- and 4.83-fold higher than that of the Control group, respectively (Fig. 6A). Consistent with the results in Fig. 5C, this behavior supported the stability of subsequent WP foams. Shear stress increased with the increasing shear rate (Fig. 6B), indicating that the modification exposed internal hydrophobic groups in WP, which promoted protein aggregation and enhanced intermolecular friction during flow, thereby increasing shear stress [28]. In contrast, the H group exhibited lower apparent viscosity and shear stress than the Control group (Fig. 6A and B), which may be attributed to the reversible cross-linking and structural rearrangement exposed groups induced by ultra-high pressure treatment. The power-law model parameters, including the consistency index (K) and flow behavior index (n), further supported these findings (Fig. 6C). The storage modulus (G') mainly reflects the elastic behavior of the sample, while the loss modulus (G'') reflects its viscosity behavior. The loss tangent (tan δ = G''/G') is commonly used to evaluate damping characteristics. When tan δ > 1, the material exhibits predominantly liquid-like behavior, whereas tan δ < 1, indicates predominantly elastic behavior [31]. As shown in Fig. 6D–F, both G' and G'' of WP samples generally increased with the increase in frequency, except for the H group. Within the frequency range of 1–100 rad/s, the G' values were consistently higher than the G'' for all treatment groups except the H group. This behavior may be attributed to enhanced intermolecular interactions among the exposed protein residues following modification, including the formation of covalent bonds, such as disulfide bonds, which contribute to increased viscoelasticity of the system [31]. Fig. 6G shows the viscosity recovery behavior of WP samples subjected to different modification treatments. In the initial stage under low shear, the viscosity remained unchanged. In the second stage, as the shear rate increased, viscosity decreased sharply due to shear forces that disrupted the structural arrangement of the WP system. In the third stage, when the shear rate was reduced to its initial level, the viscosity rapidly recovered. In the first and third stages, WP solutions exhibited high viscosity at a low shear rate (0.1 s−1), with thixotropic recovery rates exceeding 70%. Among all samples, the Control and H groups exhibited the highest recovery rates, reaching 122.97% and 130.89%, respectively (Fig. 6G and H). Feng et al. [32] reported that viscosity recovery in thixotropic systems is mainly attributed to shear reduction, which allows reconstruction of the solution network structure and gradual re-establishment of molecular interactions, resulting in viscosity recovery.

Application of modified WP in milk coffee

Effects of adding modified WP on the characteristics of foaming milk

The modified WP sample from the UE group, which exhibited the best overall performance, was selected for further evaluation to evaluate the effect of modified proteins on the flavor of milk coffee. The results indicated that at a 10% addition level, the foaming capacity reached 282.67%, representing a significant increase of 106.67% compared with the Control group (p < 0.05). The foam also exhibited the best stability over a 30 min period (Fig. 7A). After standing for 15 min, foam stability also showed an initial increase followed by a decrease with increasing modified WP addition. The foam stability of the WPM 2% group reached the maximum value (95.59%), followed by the WPM 6% and WPM 10% groups. However, after standing for 30 min, the WPM 6% and WPM 10% groups exhibited the highest foam stability (Fig. 7A). Further analysis indicated that the addition of modified WP maintained bubble stability by promoting a more uniform bubble morphology and size distribution, increasing the number of bubbles, and significantly reducing bubble diameter (Fig. 7B–D). Within 0–30 min, the foams were the most uniform and exhibited the highest bubble counts (greater than 195) at 0 min. As time increased, the average bubble size gradually increased, while the number of bubbles gradually decreased, with the WPM0 group showing the greatest reduction (from 228 to 113). This effect indicated that bubbles in the WPM0 group underwent instability processes such as coalescence and drainage over time, leading to increased in bubble size and a decreased number of bubbles. In contrast, the WPM 10% group maintained the most uniform, bubble size distribution, with only a slight decrease in bubble number (from 254 to 154) (Fig. 7B and C). In addition, the average bubble diameter in the WPM0 group remained the largest throughout the storage period (860.00–1333.33 μm), while changes in the WPM 6% and WPM 10% groups were relatively slow. After 30 min, the average bubble diameter was less than 1200 μm in both the WPM 6% and WPM 10% groups (Fig. 7D). An ideal foam in coffee beverages is characterized by a uniform bubble size distribution and a dense structure, as a uniform bubble population contributes to improved foam stability [33]. Therefore, the WPM 10% group demonstrates greater application potential, which may be attributed to the ability of an appropriate level of plant protein addition to enhance the extensibility, gelation, emulsification, and rheological properties of milk proteins [34].Fig. 7The influence of adding WP on the characteristics of foaming milk. During the intestinal digestion stage, the digestion rate initially increased and then decreased with increasing WP addition. At 10% addition, the digestion rate reached a maximum value (82.72%) (Fig. 7E). Further increase in WP addition led to a decrease in digestion rate, indicating that an appropriate amount of modified WP may promote protein digestibility, whereas excessive addition may promote interactions between WP and milk proteins, forming complexes that are less susceptible to enzymatic hydrolysis and thereby reducing the in vitro digestion rate of protein digestibility [36].

Effects of adding modified WP on the appearance, particle size, and ζ −potential of milk coffee

Foams of milk coffee prepared with different amounts of modified WP showed distinct differences. Compared with the WPM0 group, all foam samples exhibited relatively small changes over a 30 min period. Among them, the WPM 2% and WPM 10% groups were the most compact, with smaller foam sizes and better stability (Fig. 8A). Samples from the WPM 10% and WPMC 10% groups, selected after comprehensive screening, were analyzed for particle size and ζ-potential. The results indicated that the particle sizes of coffee liquid, modified WP milk, and modified WP milk coffee generally followed a normal distribution. Except for C group, the distribution patterns of the other groups were similar, indicating a uniform particle distribution in their within the systems (Fig. 8B). ζ-potential is an important indicator for liquid stability, generally ranging between −10 mV and −30 mV, with values around −25 mV indicating relatively stable systems [37]. Overall, the evaluation criteria included foaming ability, foam stability, bubble size and uniformity, and in vitro digestibility, which determined that 10% was the optimal addition level of foaming milk. Foaming capacity increased with WP addition levels up to 10% (reaching 282.67%), but further increase to 14% did not yield significant additional improvement. Foam stability was optimal at 10% after 30 min, with the most uniform bubble size distribution and the highest bubble count. In vitro digestibility during intestinal digestion peaked at 10% (82.72%), while 14% addition led to a decline, likely due to excessive protein‑protein interactions forming indigestible aggregates.

Effects of modified WP addition on the flavor of milk coffee

All milk coffee samples prepared with different levels of modified WP foaming milk received sensory evaluation scores above 70 score. Among them, the WPMC 10% group achieved the highest overall score of 75.11, while maintaining the characteristic flavors of both milk and coffee and exhibiting a uniform appearance. In addition, the WPMC 10% group showed a well-balanced bitter-sweet taste of coffee and milk, and fine foam texture (Fig. 9A). Compared with other plant proteins, soy protein, despite its good foaming capacity, often imparts a persistent beany off‑flavor that negatively affects coffee flavor, and its foam stability typically decreases within 15 min in the absence of stabilizers [38]. Pea protein can provide moderate foaming properties, but it frequently requires hydrocolloids or emulsifiers to maintain foam stability in hot coffee beverages, and its grassy aftertaste remains a recognized sensory limitation [39].Fig. 9The influence of adding WP on the flavor of milk coffee. The influence of adding WP on the flavor of milk coffee. A, sensory evaluation; B-C, results of electronic tongue and electronic nose; D-E, multivariate statistical analysis; F, significantly different volatile compounds; G, heatmap of the content of volatile compounds with VIP > 1 in each group.

The electronic tongue results indicated that the addition of modified WP milk improved the balance between bitter and sweet tastes in the WPMC 10% group by reducing the bitter, astringent, and sour taste responses of coffee while enhancing sweetness (Fig. 9B). This effect may be attributed to the contribution of lactose in milk and sweet components present in modified WP, such as sweet amino acids and sweet protein molecules, to the overall flavor of milk coffee [40]. The results of electronic nose analysis indicated that, compared with group C, the addition of foaming milk significantly reduced the response intensities of the W5S, W1S, W1W, W2S, W2W, and W3S (Fig. 9C). These sensors are mainly associated with nitrogen oxides, hydrocarbons, sulfides, alcohols, aromatic compounds, and organic sulfides in coffee aroma [41]. In contrast, the response values of the W1C, W3C, and W5C sensors increased in all groups except group C, with varying response intensities among treatments (Fig. 9C). These sensors are sensitive to aromatic compounds, amines, and alkyl-aromatic compounds [41]. The observed changes in flavor and aroma compound responses may be attributed to interactions between proteins in the foaming milk and volatile flavor compounds in coffee, which can either delay or accelerate their release [42], [43].

Further GC–MS analysis identified 53 volatile compounds (Supplementary Data), which were classified into seven major categories: ketones, esters, furans, pyrazines, pyridines, pyrimidines, and alcohols. Among them, 39 volatile compounds were quantitatively analyzed (Table S3). Multivariate statistical analysis indicated that group C formed a distinct cluster separate from the other groups, while differences were also observed among the remaining samples (Fig. 9D-E). However, the WPMC 10% and WPMC0 groups partially overlapped, suggesting similar volatile compounds between the two groups and indicating that these compounds were not specifically derived from WPM0 or WPM 10% (Fig. 9D-E). Only 12 volatile compounds were quantified in pure milk coffee (WPMC0) and WP-foamed milk coffee (WPMC 10%) (Table S3). This observation may be attributed to the microemulsification effect of foaming milk, which encapsulates certain volatile compounds. In addition, weak interactions, including hydrogen bonding, van der Waals forces, and π-π interactions, may occur between proteins and volatile compounds, thereby affecting their release behavior [44], [45]. In addition, 16 volatile compounds with variable importance in projection (VIP) values greater than 1 were identified (Fig. 9F), indicating significant differences in their relative contents among the samples. Compared with the WPMC0 group, the addition of WP in the WPMC 10% group significantly promoted the release of three volatile compounds: di-tert-butyl peroxide, methyl acetate, and 2,5-dihydroxybenzaldehyde (Fig. 9G). The decreased response intensities observed by the electronic nose suggest that modified walnut protein suppresses the overall release of volatile compounds through protein‑aroma interactions, including hydrogen bonding and hydrophobic interactions [44]. However, GC‑MS analysis revealed the selective enhancement of specific volatile compounds, such as methyl acetate, which may be attributed to its relatively low boiling point (52–54°C) and favorable release at the serving temperature of foaming milk (50–60°C) [46], [47]. In addition, among the three promoted volatile compounds, methyl acetate is particularly important because of its contribution to the sensory quality of milk coffee. This compound possesses a low odor threshold and imparts sweet, fruity, and slightly ethereal aroma notes [24]. In the context of milk coffee, the enhanced release of methyl acetate helped balance the inherent bitterness of coffee while reducing the astringent and sour taste responses detected by the electronic tongue (Fig. 9B). Moreover, its fruity sweetness complements the creamy and sweet notes contributed by lactose in milk and sweet amino acids derived from modified WP, resulting in a more balanced and palatable flavor profile [48]. This selective enhancement of desirable aroma compounds, together with the suppression of less favorable volatile compounds, may explain the higher sensory preference observed for the WPMC 10% group (Fig. 9A). In this study, the electronic nose and GC‑MS results demonstrated a “global suppression with selective enhancement” phenomenon commonly observed in protein‑flavor interaction systems. These findings suggest that modified WP offers a unique combination of additive‑free foam stability, flavor compatibility, and sensory enhancement, highlighting its potential as an alternative plant‑based ingredient for coffee beverages.

Conclusion

This study investigated the effects of five modification methods on WP and their application in milk coffee systems. The results indicated that all modification treatments reduced the molecular weight and contact angle of WP while increasing the content of free sulfhydryl groups. The modifications also induced partial reversible cross-linking in WP, leading to changes in the secondary and tertiary structures, including an increase in β-turn content. The modification treatments also enhanced the functional properties of WP, including solubility, emulsifying capacity, emulsifying stability, foaming capacity, and foaming stability within a 30 min period. Moreover, the foam produced from WP solutions exhibited a more uniform bubble size distribution and rheological behavior. The UE group was further selected for application in milk coffee. The results showed that modified WP enhanced foam stability, persistence, and bubble uniformity in foamed milk, particularly at a 10% addition level. Incorporating 10% modified WP also enhanced the sweetness perception and improved the overall sensory quality of milk coffee. Although the total number of detected volatile compounds decreased, the release of coffee aroma compounds, including di-tert-butyl peroxide, methyl acetate, and 2,5-dihydroxybenzaldehyde, was increased. Overall, this study provides a foundation for value addition in the walnut processing industry and for the development of improved coffee-based beverage systems. However, the optimization was performed at laboratory scale, and further studies on long‑term storage stability, industrial scale‑up, and more advanced in vitro digestion models are required.
