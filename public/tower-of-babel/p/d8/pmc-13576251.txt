Perceived COVID-19 threat and job satisfaction among healthcare workers: the indirect role of job stress and the moderating role of coping strategies
Authors: Sirer Albayrak
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe coronavirus disease 2019 pandemic introduced unprecedented demands on healthcare systems globally. This study aimed to investigate the associations between perceived coronavirus disease 2019 threat, job stress, job satisfaction, and stress-coping strategies among healthcare workers within an integrated relational framework.MethodsUtilizing a cross-sectional relational screening design, data were collected online via convenience sampling from 448 healthcare professionals (including physicians, nurses, midwives, and technicians) working in healthcare institutions in Turkey. Data collection tools included personal information forms, the Perceived Coronavirus Disease 2019 Threat Scale, the Minnesota Job Satisfaction Survey, the Job Stress Scale, and the Coping with Stress Scale. Statistical analyses were performed using Jamovi software. Descriptive statistics, Pearson product-moment correlation, and path analysis based on ordinary least squares regression were applied. Moderation analyses were conducted to examine whether specific coping strategies interacted with job stress and job satisfaction.ResultsPath analysis indicated that job stress served as a significant potential indirect pathway between perceived threat and job satisfaction. Moderation analysis revealed significant interaction terms, demonstrating that positive evaluation and seeking professional support functioned as critical moderators in the relationship between job stress and job satisfaction. Specifically, the total association model remained significant for workers with low and medium levels of positive evaluation, where job stress demonstrated a potential partial indirect role. For seeking professional support, the total association model was significant across low, medium, and high levels. Job stress exhibited a potential partial indirect role for individuals with low and medium levels of seeking professional support, whereas a potential full indirect role was observed for those with high levels.ConclusionsThe findings underscore that job stress is an important conceptual link connecting pandemic-related threat perceptions to diminished job satisfaction. Furthermore, specific coping mechanisms, particularly positive evaluation and professional support-seeking behaviors, modify how job stress correlates with occupational satisfaction. Interventions aimed at enhancing targeted coping strategies and mitigating structural workplace stress are recommended to preserve the psychological well-being and job satisfaction of healthcare personnel during public health crises.Keywords: COVID-19 threat perception, job stress, job satisfaction, coping strategies, healthcare workers

Introduction

COVID-19, a coronavirus outbreak first identified in Wuhan, China, in late 2019, rapidly spread across the world and soon evolved into a pandemic. The emergence of this global health crisis deeply affected society, placed tremendous pressure on healthcare systems, restricted social interactions, and negatively impacted many areas, including education and working life. Since healthcare workers operate in the same environments as individuals who are highly likely to be infected or to carry the virus, they have been exposed to greater levels of stress and a higher risk of COVID-19 transmission compared to the general population [5]. COVID-19 has been associated with a substantial psychological burden among healthcare workers, including stress, anxiety, depression, and burnout, due to increased workload, risk of infection, and rapidly changing working conditions [10, 37, 40]. These psychosocial hazards appear to be closely linked not only to the mental health of healthcare workers but also to key occupational outcomes such as job satisfaction, performance, and retention [2, 12, 16, 42].

Recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses have shown that COVID-19-related job stress is strongly associated with lower job satisfaction and higher levels of burnout among healthcare workers, particularly nurses [16, 37, 42, 50]. For example, a large meta-analysis involving 54 studies reported a consistent negative relationship between job stress and job satisfaction, as well as a strong positive association between job stress and burnout; heavy workload, inadequate equipment, and fear of infection emerged as the primary driving factors of this pattern [16]. Reviews focusing on nurses and intensive care personnel similarly indicate that adverse working conditions, emotional overload, and pandemic-related demands increase burnout tendencies, reduce satisfaction, and may have important consequences for the quality of care and staff turnover [42, 50]. Cross-sectional studies conducted in different regions (e.g., Ghana/Kenya, Indonesia, and Italy) have also confirmed that stress and burnout experienced by healthcare workers during the pandemic exhibit a strong negative association with job satisfaction and other indicators of well-being [2, 12, 27]. Recent studies have highlighted the psychological and occupational challenges faced by healthcare professionals during and after the COVID-19 pandemic. For instance, Jin , [29] reported that family support plays a significant moderating role in reducing burnout and improving job satisfaction among psychiatric nurses in China. Similarly, Maniscalco et al., [36] found that job satisfaction is closely associated with lower levels of depersonalisation and reduced intention to leave among physicians and nurses in Europe. Moreover, Figueiredo , [21], through topic modelling and sentiment analysis, demonstrated that the pandemic led to substantial changes in public emotions, emphasizing the importance of mental well-being and supportive work environments in public health contexts.

Job satisfaction is generally defined as the degree of positive affect or pleasure derived from an employee’s evaluation of their job and work experiences [15]. Spector, [47] conceptualizes job satisfaction as a multidimensional construct shaped by the interaction of individual evaluations, social resources, and organizational conditions. Recent studies emphasize job satisfaction as a multidimensional evaluation of work experiences that is closely related to motivation, commitment, absenteeism, turnover intentions, and patient outcomes [2, 12]. High levels of job satisfaction are known to be associated with better mental health, lower burnout, and higher quality of care [42, 50]. In COVID-19 settings, factors such as perceived preparedness, managerial communication, organizational support, and psychological capital have emerged as critical antecedents of job satisfaction [27].

Under pandemic conditions, perceived COVID-19 threat or fear has become a major work-related source of stress. Studies involving nurses, physicians, and mixed samples of healthcare workers have demonstrated that fear of COVID-19 or risk perception is associated with higher psychological distress, lower job satisfaction, and greater turnover intentions [1, 19, 35]. A study conducted among Polish physicians likewise reported that both fear of COVID-19 and perceived stress were associated with lower job satisfaction [19]. Contemporary COVID-19 research involving healthcare workers is generally grounded in Lazarus and Folkman’s transactional model of stress and coping, which posits that stress results from the primary appraisal of threat and the secondary appraisal of coping resources, while coping strategies shape the relationship between stress and outcomes [19, 34, 49]. Perceived COVID-19 risk or fear may function as a primary appraisal, increasing perceived stress, which in turn parallels declines in job and life satisfaction [19, 20]. Similarly, resource-based approaches (e.g., conservation of resources and psychological capital) emphasize that chronic stress may deplete emotional and cognitive resources, whereas personal resources (self-efficacy, optimism, and resilience) and adaptive coping can buffer these negative associations and contribute to the maintenance of job satisfaction [9, 12, 20].

An extraordinary situation such as the COVID-19 pandemic generates stress and fear in many individuals; therefore, assessing such fear is of great importance for implementing measures aimed at preventing potential psychological problems [41]. While job stress is negatively associated with employees’ emotional, cognitive, and physical health, the management of stress remains an important research topic [14, 45, 48]. The literature reveals various approaches to coping with stress, and the present study is based on the model developed by Moos. Moos discussed the role of cognitive and behavioral approach strategies in coping with stress [39]. The Coping with Stress Scale used in this study operationalizes coping through five dimensions: problem solving, positive reappraisal, logical analysis, seeking professional support, and seeking environmental support [33, 39]. These subscales correspond to broader coping typologies documented in contemporary COVID-19 research. Under conditions of high perceived threat, more frequent use of problem-solving and logical analysis strategies may facilitate the management of workload and clinical challenges, thereby reducing the likelihood that stress will translate into dissatisfaction [9, 11, 20, 32]. Positive appraisal and meaning-focused strategies may help healthcare workers maintain a sense of purpose and professional value despite ongoing risks, thereby mitigating the adverse effects of threat and stress on satisfaction [20, 22]. Seeking professional and environmental support may provide emotional and instrumental resources, consistent with evidence that organizational and social support, clear communication, and managerial appreciation support satisfaction and buffer stress during the pandemic [12, 46]. By focusing on these five dimensions, the present study examines adaptive, approach-oriented coping strategies rather than emotion-suppressing or avoidant forms of coping.

Systematic reviews on stress and mental health during the COVID-19 period have identified healthcare workers as the occupational group experiencing the highest levels of job stress due to direct exposure to the virus, heavy workloads, changing protocols, and limited resources [10, 40]. These job demands are consistent with the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) theory, which proposes that high demands (e.g., emotional, cognitive, and physical) combined with insufficient resources lead to burnout and reduced job satisfaction [27]. The JD-R framework has been empirically supported among healthcare workers during the pandemic, demonstrating that job demands are positively associated with burnout, whereas job resources (support, autonomy, and opportunities for development) are associated with increased work engagement and job satisfaction [27].

Taken together, existing evidence indicates that COVID-19 has substantially increased the psychological burden on healthcare workers, as reflected in high levels of stress, anxiety, depression, and burnout [10, 16, 50]. Perceived COVID-19 threat/fear constitutes a prominent work-related stressor associated with decreased job satisfaction and increased turnover intentions [1, 35]. Job stress appears to function as a central mediating mechanism linking pandemic-related demands and threat perceptions to reduced job satisfaction and burnout [2, 12]. Adaptive, approach-oriented coping strategies and personal resources (e.g., self-efficacy, resilience, and psychological capital) tend to mitigate the negative effects of stress and threat on healthcare workers’ well-being and occupational outcomes, whereas avoidant coping may exacerbate these effects [32, 34, 46]. Nevertheless, studies simultaneously examining perceived COVID-19 threat, job stress, multidimensional coping strategies, and job satisfaction within a single model—particularly using Moos’s stress-coping framework and established occupational measures such as the Minnesota Job Satisfaction Scale—remain limited. Grounded in transactional stress theory, the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) framework, and Moos’s stress-coping model, the present study conceptualizes perceived COVID-19 threat as a multidimensional appraisal of infection risk, workload, and emotional burden and examines its relationship with job stress and job satisfaction. Accordingly, the study aims to test the potential indirect role of job stress in the relationship between perceived threat and job satisfaction among healthcare workers. Furthermore, the potential moderating roles of coping strategies—operationalized through problem solving, positive evaluation, logical analysis, seeking professional support, and seeking environmental support—are examined in relation to the associations of perceived threat and job stress with job satisfaction. By integrating these constructs, the study aims to provide a more comprehensive and contemporary theoretical framework for understanding job satisfaction among healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic and to establish a scientific basis for interventions that enhance adaptive coping and organizational support in order to sustain healthcare workers’ mental health and motivation during current and future health crises. Previous studies have consistently shown that increased fear of COVID-19 and work-related stress are associated with greater psychological distress, hopelessness, suicidal cognitions, lower job satisfaction, and higher turnover intention among healthcare workers, highlighting the critical role of occupational well-being in healthcare settings [35, 45].

Method

Research model

The model of this research is a relational screening model, which aims to determine the existence and/or degree of change between two or more variables [30]. The variables sought in this research are healthcare professionals’ COVID-19 threat perceptions, job satisfaction, job stress, and methods of coping with stress.

Working group

The study group consisted of 448 healthcare professionals (physicians, nurses, midwives, technicians and other healthcare personnel) working in healthcare institutions in Turkey.

Data collection tools

As data collection tools, “Personal Information Form”, “Perceived COVID-19 Threat Scale”, “Minnesota Job Satisfaction Survey”, “Job Stress Scale” and “Coping with Stress Scale “were implemented.

Personal information form

A personal information form was formed to collect some personal information from the participants within the scope of the research. In this form, questions were asked about the participants’ gender, age, marital status, employment in the COVID-19 unit and education status.

Perceived COVID-19 threat scale

This form was developed by Kavaklı et al., [31]. The form consists of seven items and a five-point Likert -type scale ranging from strongly disagree (1 point) to strongly agree (5 points). These authors stated that the form has a single-factor structure according to parallel analysis. This form measures the participants’ perceived COVID-19 threat levels; a higher total score corresponds to a higher perceived threat from the COVID-19 pandemic. In this study, the Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.75.

Minnesota job satisfaction survey

The job satisfaction scale developed by Weis et al., (1967) was adapted to Turkish by Baycan [8]. The reliability coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.77 [8]. The scale does not have any reverse scored items. The scale, consisting of 20 items, was prepared in a 5-point Likert type, ranging from “I am not satisfied (1)” to “I am very satisfied (5)”. In this study, the Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.94.

Job stress scale. the job stress scale, which was implemented by Dr

Suzanne Haynes (1994), was adapted to Turkish by Aktaş [3]. In the stress scale consisting of 10 questions; a score below 12 indicates “low stress”, a score between 12 and 30 indicates “stressed” and a score above 30 indicates “high stress” [3]. In this study, when all items of the scale were included, Cronbach’s alpha coefficient was found to be 0.46 and for this, exploratory factor analysis was performed and it was found that the factor loadings of items 2 and 5 were below 0.30 and when these items were removed, the Kaiser-Meyer- Olkin (KMO) value was 0.70 (acceptable fit) and the result of Bartlett’s test of sphericity was found to be significant (p < 0.001). These results were suitable for factor analysis of the data, and it was seen that the remaining 8 items were collected in a single dimension and explained 28.37% of the total variance. When the 2nd and 5th items were removed, the new Cronbach alpha coefficient was 0.61. This value is at an acceptable level [25].

Stress coping scale

The scale was developed by Moos [39] and adapted to Turkish by Koca-Ballı and Kılıç [33]. The 22-item scale has 5 sub-dimensions: problem solving (6 items), positive evaluation (6 items), logical analysis (6 items), seeking professional support (2 items) and seeking social support (2 items). The expressions in the scale prepared in 5-point Likert type are; 1: Never – 5: Always. In this study, the Cronbach alpha reliability coefficient of the scale was found to be 0.89.

Procedure

The data of the study were collected via the internet via Google Forms between October 1–15, 2021, through convenience sampling from healthcare professionals working in healthcare institutions in Türkiye. Ethics committee approval was obtained for the conduct of the research from the Gümüşhane University Scientific Research and Publication Ethics Committee with the decision number E-95674917-108.99-5617420 21/6 dated 29/09/2021.

Data analysis

Data were analyzed using the Jamovi (version 1.6) software package. Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages. The fact that the skewness and kurtosis coefficients of the scores obtained from the scale are within the range of ± 1 is considered as evidence for the existence of a normal distribution [26]. The fact that the skewness and kurtosis coefficients of all scales used in the study are within the range of ± 1 indicates that the scores obtained from the scale are normally distributed. According to this result, it is appropriate to use parametric tests when performing analyses with the scores obtained from the scale. Pearson product-moment correlation analysis was performed to examine the relationships between the scale scores. To evaluate the proposed conceptual model within the constraints of the cross-sectional design, path analysis based on Ordinary Least Squares (OLS) regression was utilized [24]. Specifically, the indirect association of the Perceived COVID-19 Threat Form on the Minnesota Job Satisfaction Scale through the Haynes Job Stress Scale was examined. Additionally, moderation analysis was conducted to test whether the Stress Coping Methods Scale moderated the relationships between the study variables. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Discussion

The moderate positive relationship identified in our study between perceived COVID-19 threat and job stress, as well as the moderate negative relationship between job stress and job satisfaction, is consistent with recent findings in the literature demonstrating stress-related reductions in satisfaction among healthcare workers during the pandemic [2, 16, 44]. Perceived threat and fear of infection, when combined with lack of preparedness and high job demands, reduce job satisfaction [12, 27]. Studies conducted in different countries have also shown that perceived stress and pandemic-specific job demands are associated with depression, burnout, and lower job satisfaction [7, 18, 52]. Our findings contribute a Turkish sample to this global picture and emphasize the importance of considering threat perception together with stress and satisfaction. It is well known that reductions in job satisfaction pose serious risks in terms of quality of care, turnover intentions, and burnout [27, 43]. Therefore, our findings not only explain psychological dynamics but also point to critical implications for service delivery and the sustainability of healthcare systems.

The finding that job stress constituted a significant indirect pathway between perceived COVID-19 threat and job satisfaction demonstrates that job stress is a central mechanism explaining this relationship. Similarly, numerous studies have shown that stress and burnout are key processes that weaken job satisfaction in the context of the pandemic [2, 16, 20]. The global literature has demonstrated that high stress, burnout, lack of preparedness, resource shortages, and threat perception are associated with lower job satisfaction and greater psychological risks [23, 43, 52]. Thus, it can be stated that the findings of our study are not limited to the local context but are entirely consistent with an international pattern.

The fact that perceived COVID-19 threat increased job stress suggests that threat represents the perceived harm/threat dimension during the primary appraisal stage, whereas job stress reflects the emotional and physiological consequences of this appraisal [20]. Among healthcare workers, pandemic-related threat frames job demands as dangerous and difficult-to-control situations, leading to negative emotions and psychological burden [12, 27]. The “positive evaluation” finding identified in our study reflects secondary appraisal and reappraisal processes: even when stressors remain unchanged, the manner in which individuals interpret and reframe the situation can alter the strength of the stress–satisfaction relationship [18, 20].

Within the framework of the Job Demands–Resources (JD-R) model, perceived COVID-19 threat can be considered a component of job demands. Risk of infection, workload, inadequate protective equipment, uncertainty, and procedural burden constitute the basis of these demands [2, 44, 52]. In our model, the increase in job stress resulting from threat perception is consistent with the “health impairment process” proposed by the JD-R model [7, 52]. On the other hand, positive evaluation and seeking professional support function as resources resembling both job and personal resources within the JD-R framework. Social support, organizational support, and personal resources are known to alleviate the psychological effects of demands and protect well-being by reducing anxiety, depression, and burnout [27, 38, 52]. In recent years, expanded crisis-oriented versions of JD-R theory have emphasized that resources at the individual, family, organizational, and leadership levels jointly shape the effects of stressors and that regulatory strategies modify the impact of demands [6, 17]. Our study demonstrates that positive cognitive reappraisal and seeking professional support are strategies that carry substantial weight among these multilevel resources.

In Moos’s Social Climate Model, stress processes are explained through personal systems (personality, coping styles), environmental systems (family, work environment, support), and the dynamic interaction between these systems. This framework is supported by contemporary findings suggesting that social/organizational support and the work environment are critical mediating and balancing factors in translating job-demand-related stress into job satisfaction [27, 28, 52]. In the literature, active/positive coping and positive reinterpretation strategies are identified as protective factors that enhance job satisfaction and professional fulfillment. For example, positive reinterpretation and growth-oriented strategies are known to reduce burnout while increasing empathy and professional satisfaction [51]. Positive reappraisal has been described as a coping style that weakens burnout risk and strengthens job satisfaction [4, 51].

This finding provides a rational explanation for why only the positive evaluation dimension emerged as a moderator in our study: this coping style transforms job stressors from a “threat” into a “manageable challenge,” thereby weakening the negative direction of the stress–satisfaction relationship [20, 51]. In contrast, more passive or avoidant coping styles have been associated with burnout and psychopathological symptoms and may therefore function not as protective factors but rather as risk-enhancing mechanisms for satisfaction [4, 13]. Consequently, the absence of a significant buffering effect for such passive strategies in our model is an expected finding.

Seeking professional help, on the other hand, may be regarded as a structured “job resource” at the organizational and institutional level. The literature recognizes social and organizational support as protective factors that reduce stress, burnout, anxiety, and depression while supporting well-being and satisfaction. Organizational and social support mitigate the relationship between job demands and burnout/psychological symptoms, particularly by limiting adverse effects on depression and anxiety [7, 52]. Supportive conditions in the workplace increase job satisfaction while reducing turnover intentions and burnout [2, 23, 43]. Within the context of the pandemic, professional support functions as a mechanism that reduces individuals’ feelings of isolation and burden, facilitates coping with uncertainty, and provides a basis for sharing emotional burdens [7, 12, 38]. This structure is theoretically consistent with Moos’s environmental support framework, the JD-R model’s job resources, and Lazarus and Folkman’s problem-focused and emotion-focused coping models.

The literature indicates that strategies such as avoidance, religious coping, or emotional venting alone have, in some studies, been associated with higher levels of burnout and psychopathology [13, 51]. Furthermore, certain coping styles may be associated only with specific outcome variables (e.g., life satisfaction or anxiety) and may not exhibit a buffering effect specific to job satisfaction [4, 20]. In this context, our findings make an important contribution to the literature by demonstrating that structured cognitive (positive evaluation) and professional/organizational (support-seeking) strategies are particularly effective for job satisfaction, whereas more passive, inwardly focused, or less socially structured coping styles do not play a significant moderating role in the stress–satisfaction relationship. This distinction clearly emphasizes the reality, widely recognized in the occupational coping literature, that “not all coping strategies are equally protective” [4, 43, 51].

The documented indirect pathways clearly highlight the necessity of structural stress-management interventions within healthcare organizations. During prolonged periods of crisis, institutions cannot rely solely on individual resilience to maintain occupational satisfaction; they must actively reduce systemic job stress. This includes improving daily working conditions, rebalancing clinical workloads, and establishing formalized psychological support channels. Developing targeted skills such as cognitive positive evaluation and normalizing professional, stigma-free psychological counseling are vital strategies for protecting frontline personnel.

Limitations

This study is subject to several methodological limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the cross-sectional nature of the data does not permit causal inferences, and the identified pathways should be interpreted as contemporaneous statistical associations rather than evidence of temporal or causal relationships among perceived threat, job stress, and job satisfaction. Second, the sample was limited to healthcare professionals working in Turkey and was obtained through convenience sampling. Therefore, the generalizability of the findings may be restricted by cultural, organizational, and healthcare-system characteristics that differ across countries and occupational settings.

Another limitation concerns the psychometric properties of the Job Stress Scale. The scale initially demonstrated low internal consistency (Cronbach’s α = 0.46), and although the removal of two items increased the reliability coefficient to 0.61, this value remains relatively modest. In addition, the revised scale explained only 28.37% of the total variance. Consequently, measurement error cannot be ruled out, and relationships involving the job stress variable should be interpreted with appropriate caution. In moderation and path analyses, lower reliability may attenuate effect sizes or obscure underlying associations.

Furthermore, all variables were assessed using self-report questionnaires administered at a single time point. As a result, common method variance, response bias, and social desirability effects may have influenced the observed relationships. Although established and validated instruments were employed, common method bias cannot be completely excluded.

Finally, perceptions of threat, job demands, and coping responses may vary substantially across different stages of a public health crisis. Because the data were collected during a specific phase of the COVID-19 pandemic in 2021, the findings may not fully reflect experiences during earlier outbreak periods or post-pandemic conditions. Future longitudinal and multi-wave studies are needed to examine how these relationships evolve over time and across different healthcare contexts.

Conclusions

Our findings indicate that perceived COVID-19 threat is associated with higher levels of job stress among healthcare workers and with lower job satisfaction, both directly and indirectly through job stress. These findings are consistent with transactional stress theory, the Job Demands–Resources (JD–R) model, and Moos’s stress–coping framework, highlighting the interconnected nature of threat perceptions, job demands, personal and occupational resources, and coping strategies. In particular, the finding that positive evaluation and seeking professional support weaken the negative relationship between job stress and job satisfaction suggests that not all coping strategies are equally protective, and that cognitive reframing and structured support resources may play a unique role in promoting occupational well-being.

Overall, the findings suggest that protecting healthcare workers’ mental health and job satisfaction during periods of crisis requires not only reducing job demands but also strengthening cognitive and social resources, including positive evaluation, psychological capital, social and organizational support, and professional assistance. In this regard, the present study provides an important framework for researchers and policymakers seeking to support the sustainability of the healthcare workforce during pandemics and other large-scale crises.

Nevertheless, given the cross-sectional nature of the study, these findings should be interpreted as associations rather than evidence of causal relationships.

Recommendations

For future research, multi-wave longitudinal studies and experimental/quasi-experimental designs (e.g., coping skills training or professional support program interventions) are recommended to test the causal relationships among threat perception, job stress, coping, and job satisfaction. Comparative studies conducted in different countries may help clarify the effects of cultural values, organizational structures, and differences in crisis management on threat perception, stress, coping, and job satisfaction.

As recommendations for healthcare institutions and policymakers, increasing staffing levels, improving shift scheduling, strengthening equipment provision, and reorganizing workflow may reduce job stress and enhance job satisfaction. During pandemics and other crisis periods, strengthening managerial transparency, effective communication, recognition and reward systems, counseling services, team supervision, and peer-support programs is recommended. Structured training and intervention programs for healthcare workers may strengthen cognitive reframing, problem-solving, emotional regulation, and self-care skills. Regular training in areas such as infection control, crisis management, ethical decision-making, and communication skills may both make threat perceptions more manageable and serve as a buffer against stress by increasing self-efficacy.
