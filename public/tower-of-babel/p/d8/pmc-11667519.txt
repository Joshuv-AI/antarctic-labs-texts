Comparison of brief olfactory and cognitive assessments to neuroimaging biomarkers in the prediction of cognitive decline and dementia in the MCSA cohort
Authors: Davangere P. Devanand, Seonjoo Lee, José A. Luchsinger, David Knopman, Maria Vassilaki, Jeffrey N. Motter
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONWe evaluated impaired odor identification and global cognition as simple, cost‐effective alternatives to neuroimaging biomarkers to predict cognitive decline and dementia in the Mayo Clinic Study of Aging.METHODSSix hundred forty‐seven participants (mean 8.1, standard deviation 3.4 years’ follow‐up) had the following baseline procedures: modified Blessed Information Memory Concentration Test (BIMCT), 12‐item Brief Smell Identification Test (BSIT), structural brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI), and positron emission tomography (PET) imaging with 11C‐Pittsburgh compound B (11C‐PiB) and fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG; subset).RESULTSCognitive decline developed in 102 participants and dementia in 34 participants. In survival analyses, PiB PET showed robust prediction for cognitive decline. Impaired BSIT, impaired BIMCT, MRI, and FDG measures were also significant predictors. The combination of demographics + BSIT + BIMCT showed strong predictive utility (C‐index 0.81), similar to demographics + PiB PET (C‐index 0.80). Similar but stronger results were obtained for prediction of dementia.DISCUSSIONImpairment in both odor identification test and global cognition was comparable to PiB PET for predicting cognitive decline and dementia.Highlights In 647 participants in the population‐based Mayo Clinic Study of Aging, several clinical markers and biomarkers each predicted cognitive decline or dementia during an average 8 years of follow‐up.The combination of the demographic variables of age, sex, and education with a brief odor identification test (BSIT) and a global cognitive test (Blessed Information Memory Concentration Test) showed strong predictive utility (C‐index 0.81) for cognitive decline that was similar to the demographic variables combined with Pittsburgh Compound B amyloid imaging (C‐index 0.80).Combining a brief odor identification test with a brief cognitive test needs consideration as a simple, cost‐effective option in the clinical assessment of individuals at risk of cognitive decline and dementia, as well as a potential tool to identify individuals who may benefit from disease‐modifying treatments and to screen participants for prevention trials.

INTRODUCTION

Dementia is a syndrome characterized by progressive decline in cognition and everyday functioning. Alzheimer's disease (AD) comprises the majority of cases of dementia. 1 Early detection of AD can improve the identification of patients who may benefit from disease‐modifying medications, 2 , 3 may help in identifying participants for prevention trials, and longer term can help patients and caregivers plan for their future.

In recent years, biomarkers of amyloid and tau pathology, which are the hallmarks of AD, have improved diagnostic accuracy. These biomarkers include cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) amyloid beta 42/40 ratio and tau protein levels (total tau [t‐tau], phosphorylated tau [p‐tau] 181, p‐tau 217), magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) hippocampal volume and cortical thickness (markers of neurodegeneration), and fluorodeoxyglucose (FDG) positron emission tomography (PET) and amyloid PET indices. These biomarkers show moderate to strong predictive accuracy for the diagnosis of AD, but there is limited patient willingness to undergo lumbar puncture for CSF studies, and neuroimaging is expensive. 4 Promising plasma biomarkers are in development, but currently CSF and neuroimaging biomarkers of AD remain the gold standard. 5

Studies of combinations of biomarkers as predictors of transition to mild cognitive impairment (MCI) and AD have mostly focused on brain imaging and CSF measures 6 , 7 with less attention to non‐invasive biomarkers. 8 Olfactory identification ability, which can be assessed by an inexpensive, non‐invasive test, shows impairment in AD 9 and other types of dementia, including Lewy body dementia 10 and frontotemporal dementia, 11 compared to cognitively unimpaired (CU) controls. Odor identification impairment consistently distinguishes AD from CU controls, predicts the transition from MCI to dementia, and is associated with subsequent cognitive decline in CU older adults. 12 , 13 , 14 This impairment reflects pathology in the olfactory bulb and higher‐order projection areas for olfactory processing, including the anterior olfactory nucleus, orbitofrontal cortex, piriform cortex, amygdala, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampus. 15 , 16 In a cross‐sectional study of 56 older cognitively impaired and intact individuals, impaired odor identification ability was associated with increased tau pathology based on 18F‐MK‐6240 PET imaging and CSF t‐tau and p‐tau levels, particularly in amyloid‐positive individuals. 17 This result is consistent with the neuropathological finding of tau neurofibrillary tangle pathology in olfactory brain regions in AD. 15 There has been limited investigation of the utility of olfactory deficits in relation to neuroimaging biomarkers to predict cognitive decline and dementia in longitudinal studies.

The reliable and well‐validated 12‐item Brief Smell Identification Test (BSIT), which is a subset of the 40‐item UPSIT (University of Pennsylvania Smell Identification Test), has been used in several studies, takes 5 minutes to complete, is inexpensive, and can be administered by non‐medical personnel. 18 The Mayo Clinic Study of Aging (MCSA) involved serial evaluation of a large population‐based cohort of individuals residing in Olmstead County, Minnesota. In an earlier report from the MCSA, lower BSIT scores were associated with incident amnestic MCI and with progression from amnestic MCI to dementia. 13 In 2008 and 2009, the BSIT was administered together with assessment of Pittsburgh compound B (PiB) PET for brain amyloid uptake, MRI hippocampal volume and cortical thickness, apolipoprotein E (APOE) genotype, and the Blessed Information Memory Concentration Test (BIMCT). 19 , 20 We compared the predictive utility of these variables, individually and in combination, for cognitive decline to MCI or dementia during follow‐up. FDG PET was done in a subset of participants and was evaluated in secondary analyses.

METHODS

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consent

The study was approved by the institutional review boards of the Mayo Clinic and the Olmsted Medical Center (protocol number 14‐004401). Written informed consent was obtained prior to participation in the study. The study was performed in accordance with the ethical standards as laid down in the 1964 Declaration of Helsinki and its later amendments.

Study population

The study design and protocol of the MCSA have been reported previously. 21 , 22 In brief, the Rochester Epidemiology Project (REP) resources were used to enumerate Olmsted County (Minnesota) residents (October 1, 2004). 23 An age‐ and sex‐stratified random sample of Olmsted County residents who were without dementia and not terminally ill or in hospice were invited to participate in the MCSA. 21 Stratified sampling was performed by randomly selecting subjects from within the four age and sex strata of the sampling frame. 21 The Minnesota Life Tables for 2000 were used to estimate the remaining number of years of life for subjects in each of the four cells: 6.6 years for men aged 80 to 89 years, 8.3 years for women aged 80 to 89 years, 11.9 years for men aged 70 to 79 years, and 14.6 years for women aged 70 to 79 years. These estimates were used to prioritize subjects for recruitment. Sex diversity was achieved but racial and ethnic diversity was not achieved because of the population of the county being mostly European in origin. Participants were also given the opportunity to undergo 11C‐PiB PET, MRI, and FDG‐PET conducted at specific time points during long‐term follow‐up. Clinical evaluation took place every 15 months. Data obtained at the 2008 assessment, which included BSIT administration, are counted as baseline for this report, with a maximum of nine follow‐up visits available (135 months/11.25 years). The current dataset was censored March 31, 2022. Participants were excluded from the current analysis if they were missing baseline BSIT, BIMCT, MRI, PiB PET, or APOE genotype, had dementia at baseline, had no follow‐up visits, were anosmic (BSIT score ≤ 3), or were younger than 55. 24 The study was approved by the institutional review boards of the Mayo Clinic and of Olmsted Medical Center.

Clinical evaluation and diagnostic assessment

Each participant was evaluated by a nurse or a study coordinator, and a physician, and underwent neuropsychological testing by a psychometrist. The nurse or study coordinator collected demographic information, asked questions about memory, and administered interviews to an informant. The physician's evaluation included review of medical history, administration of the Short Test of Mental Status, 25 and a neurological examination. Nine neuropsychological tests were used to assess cognitive performance in four domains: (1) memory (Logical Memory–II [delayed recall] and Visual Reproduction–II [delayed recall] from Wechsler Memory Scale‐Revised, and the Auditory Verbal Learning Test [delayed percent retention]); (2) executive function (Trail Making Test Part B, and Digit Symbol Substitution from Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale–Revised); (3) language (Boston Naming Test and Category Fluency Test); and (4) visuospatial skills (Picture Completion and Block Design from the Wechsler Adult Intelligence Scale–Revised). 21 The raw scores from each test were transformed into sex‐ and age‐adjusted scores using independent normative data and a composite score was created. At a weekly conference, the nurse or study coordinator, the physician, and a neuropsychologist reviewed all available information for each subject and made the final consensus diagnosis (i.e., CU, MCI, dementia) with subclassification of MCI and dementia diagnoses. 21 , 22 Individuals who performed in the normal range and did not meet criteria for MCI or dementia were classified as CU. APOE ε4 genotype was determined from a blood draw. Medical comorbidities were abstracted from participant medical records using the REP medical records‐linkage system.

Olfactory impairment was assessed using the BSIT version A that consists of six food‐related and six non–food‐related smells (cherry, clove, strawberry, menthol, pineapple, lemon, leather, lilac, smoke, soap, natural gas, and rose). 18 Participants had to scratch, sniff, and select one of four possible choices for each smell item. The BSIT score was calculated as the sum of the correct responses (range 0–12) for participants with no more than two missing responses. A score of 0.25 was assigned for each missing response. 13 For this report, BSIT score ≤ 3 was rated as anosmia because 3 out of 12 is the score obtained by chance alone because for each item there are four odor choices to select from within the multiple‐choice format. Scoring at or below chance indicates that the individual could not identify odors, and this represents anosmia. Based on published normative data, BSIT score ≤ 8 out of 12 was considered impaired and BSIT score ≥ 9 was considered intact. 14 , 26

Global cognition was measured at baseline using a modified version of the BIMCT. 20 This test version includes 16 items of orientation, judgment, abstract reasoning, serial subtractions, and short‐term recall of a sentence. Scores range from 0 to 20, with higher scores indicating better cognitive performance. Because there is no widely accepted cutoff for this custom version of the test, the Youden index was used to determine an optimal cutoff of 17.5 (Youden J Index = 0.395, sensitivity = 0.618, specificity = 0.777) for differentiating individuals who transitioned to dementia. This cutoff was also optimal for cognitive decliners (CU to MCI/dementia and MCI to dementia). “Intact” BIMCT performance was defined as scores 18 to 20, and “impaired” BIMCT performance was defined as scores 0 to 17. In addition, the Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) score was derived from the Short Test of Mental Status. 25 , 27

MRI and PET measures acquisition

MRI was performed at 3 Tesla. Hippocampal volume was segmented, and the cortical surface was parcellated using FreeSurfer (version 5.3). A composite thickness measure representing AD signature cortical thickness was computed to assess AD‐related neurodegeneration based on individual cortical thickness for regions of interest (ROIs) from entorhinal, inferior temporal, middle temporal, and fusiform cortex from both hemispheres. 28

PET images were acquired using a PET/computed tomography scanner operating in three‐dimensional mode. PiB and FDG PET are indicators of amyloid pathology and decreased glucose metabolism, respectively, and are associated with differences in the brain regions affected. For PiB PET, a standardized uptake value ratio (SUVR) was formed from the prefrontal, orbitofrontal, parietal, temporal, anterior cingulate, and posterior cingulate/precuneus ROIs normalized to the whole cerebellum. 29 An AD signature 18F‐FDG PET SUVR (non‐sharpened, non‐partial volume corrected) was calculated based on glucose metabolic rates from an AD signature meta‐ROI that consisted of the average bilateral angular gyri, posterior cingulate, and inferior temporal cortical ROIs from both hemispheres normalized to pons and vermis uptake. 30 , 31

The cut points for biomarker abnormality were defined such that 90% of a group of 75 clinically diagnosed AD dementia subjects from the Mayo Clinic Alzheimer's Disease Research Center and MCSA were categorized as abnormal. Abnormal hippocampal volume was defined as ≤ −0.76 cm3 (actual versus expected volume, adjusted for total intracranial volume in sex‐specific models), abnormal FDG PET meta‐ROI as ≤ 1.47, abnormal AD signature cortical thickness as < 2.68 mm, and abnormal amyloid PET as SUVR ≥ 1.48. 24 , 28 , 31 , 32

Statistical analyses

The demographic and baseline characteristics are reported for all participants as well as by baseline cognitive status (CU versus MCI) using mean with standard deviation (SD) for continuous variables and frequency with proportion for categorical variables. t tests and chi squares were used to compare CU versus MCI on demographic and baseline characteristics. Pearson correlation coefficients were calculated to evaluate associations between predictors (Table S1 in supporting information).

The primary outcome was time until cognitive decline from baseline. “Cognitive decline” was defined as transition from CU to MCI, CU to dementia, or MCI to dementia. Cox proportional hazards regression was performed to test the predictive ability of the baseline measures accounting for censoring. There were no missing values in the predictors and covariates at baseline. In Model 1, we performed Cox regressions with each predictor (BSIT impairment, BIMCT impairment, APOE ε4 positivity, PiB abnormality, hippocampal volume abnormality, cortical thickness abnormality, and FDG abnormality; total n = 647 except FDG analyses with n = 556) in separate analyses. In Model 2, we performed the analyses with all predictors in the same model concurrently (except FDG PET). In Model 3, we performed the analyses with all predictors in the same model concurrently in the subset of participants who had FDG PET available (n = 556) and added it as a predictor. All analyses in Models 1 through 3 were performed both unadjusted for covariates and adjusted for the demographics of age, sex, and education due to the association of these predictors with risk for dementia. 33 We then repeated the same Cox regression analyses for transition to dementia as the outcome. Hazard ratios, their 95% confidence intervals, and P values are reported.

We compared the prediction performance between hierarchical models using the Harrell C‐index. 34 While the C‐index has technical limitations in handling ties and recurrent events, 35 the method is the most standard and acceptable way to evaluate model prediction across time. Hazard ratios of each predictor in Cox regression analyses provide complementary information.

The C‐index is a measure of the probability that the predicted event times of two randomly selected individuals have the same relative order as their true event times; C‐index ranges from 0.5 (no difference) to 1.0 (perfect discrimination). The C‐indices 95% confidence intervals were computed using bootstrapping with 1000 re‐samplings. The following models were compared: (1) demographics (age, sex, education) only, (2) BIMCT impairment with demographics, (3) BSIT impairment with demographics, (4) APOE ε4 positivity with demographics, (5) PIB abnormality with demographics, (6) BIMCT + PIB abnormality with demographics, (7) BIMCT + BSIT impairment with demographics, (8) BIMCT + BSIT impairment + PIB abnormality with demographics, (9) BIMCT + BSIT impairment + PIB abnormality + hippocampal volume abnormality with demographics, (10) BIMCT + BSIT impairment + PIB abnormality + thickness abnormality with demographics, (11) BIMCT + BSIT impairment + PIB abnormality + thickness abnormality + hippocampal volume abnormality with demographics, (12) BIMCT + BSIT impairment + APOE ε4 positivity + PIB abnormality + thickness abnormality + hippocampal volume abnormality with demographics. Second, C‐indices for each combination were also computed without demographics. Because only a subset of participants had FDG PET measures, we did not compare FDG PET's predictive utility for the primary analysis.

RESULTS

Baseline demographics and follow‐up

The derivation of the analytic sample is reported in Figure 1. A total of 864 participants had baseline BSIT, BIMCT, PiB PET, and MRI. Of these 864 participants, exclusion criteria for this report were age < 55 years (n = 114), a baseline diagnosis of dementia (n = 8), absence of APOE genotype information (n = 4), anosmia (BSIT ≤ 3, n = 26), and no follow‐up (n = 65), leading to a final sample of 647 participants. Of the 647 participants, 584 were CU and 63 were diagnosed with MCI at baseline by MSCA methods. 21 Sample demographic and clinical characteristics are presented by baseline cognitive impairment status in Table 1. The average age was 74.0 (SD 8.1) years and 54.4% were male. The participants were predominantly White (98.4%) and exclusively non‐Hispanic. Compared to participants with CU, participants with MCI were older, more likely to be male, had fewer years of education, were more likely to be APOE ε4 positive, and more likely to have hypertension and stroke, but not diabetes (Table 1). The MCI group showed worse scores in the cognitive measures and greater abnormality on all neuroimaging biomarkers of AD than the CU group. At baseline, Pearson r correlation coefficients among BSIT, BIMCT, PiB, and MRI measures ranged from −0.2 to 0.3, indicating limited shared common variance.

All 647 participants completed 1.5 and 3 years of follow‐up (100%). Follow‐up completion rates remained high at 4.5 years (89%), 6 years (79%), and 7.5 years (62%) with lower rates at 9 years (38%), 10.5 years (30%), 12 years (22%), 13.5 years (14%), and 15 years (4%). We compared the baseline characteristics between 65 participants who were not followed to those included in the study (n = 647). After multiple comparison corrections, the excluded participants were older, had lower education, and had lower BSIT and BIMCT (ps ≤ 0.01). All imaging and genetic markers did not show significant differences after multiple comparison corrections. 36 After controlling for age and education, BSIT no longer significantly differed while BIMCT was lower in the participants who were not followed (p < 0.01).

Association with cognitive decline—survival analyses

In survival models using Cox proportional hazards regression with cognitive decline as the outcome (102 of 647 participants cognitively declined from CU to MCI, CU to dementia, or MCI to dementia), several predictors were each significantly associated with cognitive decline when examined individually for their independent effect when unadjusted for demographic covariates. Impairment on the BSIT was significantly associated with cognitive decline (hazard ratio [HR] 3.46, 95% confidence interval [CI] = 2.34–5.11, p < 0.001). Other significant predictors included impaired BIMCT (HR = 3.63, 95% CI 2.46–5.36, p < 0.0001), APOE ε4 positivity (HR = 2.05, 95% CI = 1.39–3.03, p < 0.0001), abnormal PiB (HR = 5.05, 95% CI = 3.32–7.69, p < 0.0001), abnormal hippocampal volume (HR = 3.71, 95% CI = 2.51–5.48, p < 0.0001), abnormal cortical thickness (HR = 3.50, 95% CI = 2.24–5.46, p < 0.0001), and abnormal FDG (HR = 2.94, 95% CI = 1.97–4.38, p < 0.0001; Table 2, Model 1). After adjusting for age, sex, and education, all predictors remained significantly associated with cognitive decline, albeit with lower HRs (Table 2).

We next repeated the analyses with all variables in the same model concurrently (except FDG, because only 556 of 647 participants had FDG PET). In the covariate‐unadjusted and covariate‐adjusted models, all predictors including impaired BSIT and BIMCT, but not APOE ε4 positivity, remained significantly associated with cognitive decline (Table 2, Model 2). Finally, we repeated the analyses with all predictors including FDG PET (in the subset of n = 556 participants who had FDG PET) and found the same pattern of results as Model 2, with FDG PET also being significantly associated with cognitive decline (Table 2, Model 3). Adjusting for age, sex, and education attenuated these estimates (Table 2). The findings remained significant after adjusting for multiple comparisons (Table 2). 36

Association with dementia—survival analyses

In similar survival models using Cox proportional hazards regression with dementia transition as the outcome (34 of 647 participants transitioned to dementia), all predictors were each significantly associated with dementia transition in both covariate‐unadjusted and covariate‐adjusted models (Table 3, Model 1). HRs for BSIT, BIMCT, PiB, FDG, and MRI measures generally were greater than observed for the outcome of cognitive decline, although the wide CIs for some estimates suggest lower precision possibly due to lower number of events. When predictors were examined concurrently within the same model, all predictors remained significantly associated with transition to dementia except for APOE ε4 positivity and abnormal cortical thickness (Table 3, Model 2). Finally, with all predictors including FDG PET added in the model (n = 556), FDG PET was associated with transition to dementia (Table 3, Model 3). Figure 3 shows the C‐indices with bootstrapped 95% CIs of the C‐index models with different combinations of predictors for the outcome of cognitive decline, adjusted for age, sex, and years of education. The C‐index for the combination of demographics + BIMCT + BSIT (C‐index 0.81, 95% CI 0.77– 0.85) was significantly better than those from the demographics + BIMCT and from the demographics + BSIT models (Table S2 in supporting information). The C‐index for the combination of demographics + BIMCT + BSIT was not significantly different from the C‐index of demographics + PiB (C‐index 0.80, 95% CI 0.70–0.84). In the sample, 50 participants were deceased within 3.5 years of the last follow‐up. During the follow‐up, 19.6% (20/102) of the cognitive decliners died, while only 5.5% of non‐decliners (30/545) died; 23.5% (8/34) of participants who transitioned to dementia died, while 6.9% (42/613) of non‐converters died. The literature suggests that death and worsening health conditions commonly contribute to missing data. These variables are often highly correlated with cognitive performance at baseline and demographics such as age, sex, and education. All analyses were performed by including cognitive performance and demographic variables. As a sensitivity analysis, we performed competing risk analysis with death. Across all models, each predictor remained significantly associated with cognitive decline and transition to dementia after accounting for the competing risk of mortality. Therefore, it is likely that non‐informative censoring was minimal.

DISCUSSION

BSIT and BIMCT each showed significant predictive utility, which we have shown in other cohorts 14 , 37 and in the previous MCSA study, 13 as did the MRI measures and PET measures. The results also show that among the biomarkers evaluated individually, amyloid imaging with PiB PET showed robust predictive utility for cognitive decline and dementia during follow‐up. This effect is consistent with other studies in the literature. 38 For clinical relevance, we examined the combination of BSIT with BIMCT, which showed strong predictive utility with a C‐index that was similar to amyloid imaging with PiB PET. The C‐index of these three measures were in turn enhanced by the addition of APOE ε4 and MRI measures of neurodegeneration. The findings in the current report indicate that cognitive, olfactory, and neuroimaging biomarkers each have predictive utility for cognitive decline and dementia, and that no single measure is robust enough to be used as the sole predictor. 4 , 8 , 39

We previously showed in other community‐based cohorts, Washington Heights/Inwood Columbia Aging Project and Health, Aging, and Body Composition study, which were racially diverse in contrast to the MSCA, that combining measures of odor identification and brief global cognitive tests yielded strong predictive utility for dementia. 14 , 37 In those cohorts, unimpaired performance on both the BSIT and a brief global cognitive test was associated with extremely low to absent transition to dementia in study participants who were 55 to 75 years old, though this advantage, while still present, diminished in magnitude for individuals > 75 years of age. 14 , 37 Increased age and less education are associated with greater risk of dementia; incorporating these demographic measures and sex in the survival models improved predictive utility for each variable and combinations of variables examined.

In AD, there is a hypothesized progression in brain pathology with initial increase in amyloid followed by tau pathology and neurodegeneration. 39 Impairment in odor identification in AD is primarily a manifestation of tau pathology in the olfactory bulb and tract and projection areas that include the anterior olfactory nucleus, orbitofrontal cortex, piriform cortex, amygdala, entorhinal cortex, and hippocampus. 15 , 16 Odor identification deficits show strong predictive power for longitudinal tau progression and recent evidence suggests that tau spreads from the medial temporal lobe toward the olfactory system rather than from the olfactory pathways to medial temporal lobe. 40 Odor identification is the end product of odor detection, odor naming, and odor memory, and pathology in regions involved in the processing of one or more of these abilities can manifest as impaired odor identification. Alpha‐synuclein pathology in Lewy body disease is also strongly associated with olfactory deficits and represents another neurodegenerative condition that may have been contributory to the dementia diagnosis in the MCSA cohort. 10 There is growing evidence that AD, Lewy body dementia, and combined AD and Lewy body dementia are each common in neuropathological studies of clinically diagnosed patients with dementia. 41 In the MCSA cohort, the degree of association between olfactory and cognitive deficits and neuroimaging indices in older adults, including those with MCI, was modest. Combining these measures that shared limited common variance improved predictive utility at a relatively early stage of disease progression (CU or MCI stage). 8 , 12 Hypothetical causal pathways clearly are complex, and there is much‐needed knowledge to be generated to clarify their role in disease progression.

One limitation is that brief global cognitive tests like the BIMCT lack the robust predictive utility for cognitive decline and dementia of more comprehensive cognitive assessments including neuropsychological testing. 42 , 43 Nonetheless, such tests have considerable practicality because they are brief and easy to administer by a variety of trained personnel. Another limitation is that these results may not be generalizable to non‐White persons, who have a higher risk of dementia than White persons. 1 The study was conducted in Olmsted County, Minnesota, which is mostly of European descent, and showed a relatively low rate of transition to dementia during follow‐up. Of note, participants were initially ineligible for the MCSA cohort if they had a prior diagnosis of dementia or a diagnosis of dementia based on in‐person clinical assessment that included detailed neuropsychological evaluation. 21 The thresholds for abnormality were derived from within the cohort and may need to be adjusted in other populations. The neuropathological changes of AD typically develop slowly over time and the availability of long‐term follow‐up strengthened the analyses of baseline variables in predicting outcome. 24

In summary, in the MCSA cohort, amyloid PET imaging was a robust individual biomarker predictor of cognitive decline. The BSIT was also a significant predictor, as were the MRI biomarkers. The combination of the BSIT with a brief global cognitive test showed similar predictive utility to PiB PET, both when compared directly without demographics and compared alongside age, sex, and education. This finding suggests that combining the BSIT, which is brief and simple to administer, with a brief global cognitive test like the BIMCT or MMSE or Montreal Cognitive Assessment (MoCA), is a simple, brief, cost‐effective alternative with strong predictive utility for cognitive decline and dementia comparable to PET amyloid imaging that may have limited availability. Combining the BSIT with a brief cognitive test needs consideration as a simple, quick, cost‐effective option in the clinical assessment of individuals at risk of cognitive decline and dementia, as well as a potential tool to identify individuals who may benefit from disease‐modifying treatments and to screen participants for prevention trials. The advent of plasma biomarkers, which continue to develop rapidly, may broaden the range of predictors available to predict cognitive decline and dementia, and their relative predictive utility compared to the BSIT, brief global cognitive tests, and amyloid imaging needs to be examined in future research, which may include examination of non‐linear effects for continuous measures. 5
