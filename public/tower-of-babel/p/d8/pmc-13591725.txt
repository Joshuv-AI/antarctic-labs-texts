Mental healthcare expenditure in the Netherlands by urbanicity and neighbourhood socioeconomic status and interactions with age over the period 2015–2020
Authors: L Dijkstra, S Gülöksüz, A Batalla, J van Os
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundWe previously reported that young adults and young women in particular account for an increasing share of mental healthcare expenditure in the Netherlands. Here we aim to explore temporal patterns of mental healthcare expenditure over the period 2015–2020 by urbanicity and neighbourhood socioeconomic status (SES), as well as their interaction with age.MethodsWe used comprehensive data from health insurers in the Netherlands at the 4-number postal code level. We used postal-code-clustered weighted linear regression to examine temporal patterns of mental healthcare expenditure by urbanicity and SES. Additionally, we analysed interactions with age group (18–34 vs 35–65) by adding the interaction between age, year, and either urbanicity or neighbourhood SES to the model. All models were adjusted for urbanicity, neighbourhood SES, sex, and age.ResultsWe found a gradient in mental healthcare costs by urbanicity and neighbourhood SES in the Netherlands, which was stable over time. The standardized regression coefficient for transformed total mental healthcare costs in the highest urbanicity group was 0.51 (95%-CI = 0.47;0.56) and -0.58 (95%-CI = -0.61;-0.54) in the highest neighbourhood SES group. The association younger age (18–34) with mental healthcare costs was less strong in urban areas and neighbourhoods with lower SES.ConclusionsTo understand mental healthcare usage the intersection of sociodemographic factors such as age, urbanicity and SES should be taken into account. This can aid initiatives for prevention and improving access to care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s13690-026-01991-3.Keywords: Epidemiology, Urbanicity, Health Economics, Mental Health, Social and Political Issues

Introduction

Mental health disorders are a major contributor to the global burden of disease [1, 2]. Thus, patterns of mental healthcare consumption are of interest for research and policy purposes to provide insight into both the distribution of mental health needs and the accessibility of services. In the Netherlands specifically, there have been increasing concerns about the accessibility and increased demand for mental healthcare [3–6].

Mental health is known to be associated with both urbanicity and (neighbourhood) socioeconomic status (SES) [7–12]. Urbanicity may influence mental healthcare use through difference in need between areas of higher and low urbanicity and through differences in access to care. Specialized mental healthcare might for instance be more easily accessible in urban areas compared to rural areas. For the Netherlands specifically, it is known that there is a strong association between socioeconomic deprivation of the neighbourhood and the consumption of mental healthcare [13]. However, few studies have examined whether this association changes over time and with type of mental healthcare.

We aim to examine the relationship of mental healthcare costs over time with urbanicity and neighbourhood-level SES using health insurance data from 2015–2020. We hypothesized that the strength of the association of mental healthcare costs with urbanicity would be stable over time for all types of care, but that the strength of the association between mental healthcare costs and neighbourhood SES would increase over time, as income inequality is known to have risen in the Netherlands over the observed period, with a 5% increase in the Gini-coefficient for expendable household income over the period 2011–2021 [14]. We hypothesized this would be most prominent for specialized mental healthcare as it is known in the Netherlands that deprived neighbourhoods are linked to more use of specialized mental healthcare [13].

Furthermore, we previously reported on an increasing association between mental health care expenditure and young age in the Netherlands, especially in young women [15]. It is conceivable that SES and the level of urbanicity of the neighbourhood in which young people live impact mental health and the need for mental healthcare differentially compared to older adults. Earlier reports have discussed the interplay between young people and the characteristics of their neighbourhoods [16–20]. However, these have mostly focussed on adolescents rather than younger adults and have not examined how interactions with the environment influence the use of mental healthcare. To provide a better understanding of the interaction between neighbourhood-level SES, urbanicity and young adulthood in the consumption of mental healthcare, we aimed to examine whether the association between young age and costs differed by urbanicity and SES. We hypothesized that the association with age would be weaker in urban areas and in areas with a lower SES, as urban living and low SES are risk factors for mental ill-health regardless of age. Higher baseline costs across all age-groups in these areas would this attenuate the correlation with age.

Methods

Dataset and measures

We used data from Vektis, which is a registry of all health insurance costs nationally [21]. We previously reported on this dataset and a similar dataset at the 3-number postal code level [15, 22]. In short, the unit of analysis was the first 4-number postal code area (PC-4) out of the total 6-digit postal code area subdivided by age into age groups (18–20 years and 5-year groups from there on) and sex. Units of fewer than 10 people were excluded from the dataset for privacy reasons, and we excluded people over 65 years to prevent age-specific effects of neuropsychiatric conditions from obscuring the results. Costs for mental healthcare for those under the age of 18 are not paid for by health insurers but by municipalities and are thus not included in the dataset. Costs associated with long-term (> 1 year) clinical stays, forensic care, and social care were not included in this dataset, as they are funded differently. For analysis, age was dichotomized at 35-years in line with previous work on mental health epidemiology in the Netherlands [6]. Data from the Central Bureau for Statistics (CBS) was used to determine urbanicity for each postal code, which was defined as the density of addresses per square kilometre and divided into five categories ranging from low to high urbanicity [23]. For the neighbourhood-level SES we used a coefficient from the CBS based on a multiple correspondence analysis of income, occupational and educational data, which was divided into equal sized quintiles for the purpose of this analysis [24, 25].

As in our previous analysis of this dataset, the main outcome of interest was the average mental healthcare expenditure per insured person [15]. Mental healthcare expenditures were subdivided into specialist mental healthcare, which focusses on more severe or more complex cases (SPECIALIST-MHC), basic mental healthcare for non-complex common mental disorders (BASIC-MHC), and mental healthcare provided by general practitioners (GP-MHC). Urbanicity and neighbourhood SES were the main exposures of interest and were additionally examined as effect modifiers of the previously reported association between age and costs.

Analysis

Analyses were conducted using Stata version 18 [26]. The methodology used for descriptive statistics and to assess for potential confounding was previously described [15, 22]. A zero-skewness-log-transformation was applied to mental healthcare costs to address right-skewness in the expenditure distribution. The Stata command ‘lnskew0’ was used for the transformation, which chooses a constant to bring the skewness of the newly created variable as close to zero as possible. The transformed values were then centred and expressed in standard deviations to facilitate comparison of coefficients.

We have previously described the methodology used for analysis of the main outcomes in both this dataset and a similar dataset at the 3-number postal code level [15, 22]. Given the hierarchical structure of the data with observations in postal codes over time we used linear regression with cluster-robust standard errors at the PC-4 level to account for within-cluster correlation. Models were weighted by the number of insured person-years per unit to account for differential precision of estimates. All analysis were done using complete cases only, given the low percentages of missing data. Discussion

As expected, we found that there was a gradient in mental healthcare costs by urbanicity and neighbourhood SES in the Netherlands, with high urbanicity neighbourhoods and low SES neighbourhoods having the highest costs. We also found that the association of mental health care costs with urbanicity and neighbourhood SES was stable over the observed period, except for the relationship between urbanicity and GP-MHC costs, where the association decreased in strength, meaning that for GP-MHC urbanicity became a less important predicting factor for costs over the observed period. This was contrary to our hypothesis that the association would increase for neighbourhood SES. The examining of the interaction of urbanicity and neighbourhood SES with age was novel, and thus adds to the understanding of how these factors intersect. Looking at the interaction with age, our findings were in line with our hypothesis that the association with age would be weaker in urban areas and in areas with low SES, but it was unexpected that the difference for the highest urbanicity and lowest neighbourhood SES group was so marked for total and SPECIALIST-MHC costs and that the association appeared absent for GP-MHC costs and urbanicity.

Our hypothesis that the association between neighbourhood SES and mental healthcare use would increase over time in parallel with rising inequality was not supported by our analyses. One possible explanation is that national-level increases in inequality may not be reflected in neighbourhood-level SES gradients if the effects of inequality operate primarily at higher geographic levels or through individual-level mechanisms. One study from the Netherlands, for example, found that inequality at the municipal level had a larger association with psychological distress than inequality at the neighbourhood level [29]. Meta-analyses have also found associations between mental health and inequality at different levels [30, 31]. Additionally, it could be that the effect of exposure to a neighbourhood with low SES is delayed or only relevant in certain developmental phases, such as adolescence, meaning an effect would only be observed in more recent and future data, since our dataset only included adults. This theory is supported by a body of work demonstrating the importance of neighbourhoods for mental health outcomes in children and adolescents [20, 32]. Moreover, a systematic review from 2020 suggested that the neighbourhood social environment has a stronger association with mental health outcomes than neighbourhood SES, suggesting that the variable we used in this study might not have been sensitive to measure association with mental healthcare use [20]. Finally, it could be that increasing inequality by increasing mental distress increases demand for healthcare, but that this balanced out by an increase in avoidance of care by others. This theory is supported by reports showing that in Europe, inequality is linked to distrust in public institutions, which can include healthcare systems [33, 34].To better understand our null-finding regarding the hypothesis that the association between neighbourhood SES and mental healthcare use would increase over time, further research could include measures of inequality at multiple levels (national, municipal, neighbourhood, individual), longer periods of observation to account for a possible delayed effect, a wider variety of variables relating to the neighbourhood environment and measures of healthcare seeking behaviour.

Our findings regarding the changing association between urbanicity and neighbourhood-level GP-MHC costs might be due to increased familiarity with GP-MHC in rural areas over the observed period. This is in line with a report that observed that over the period 2016–2020, there was an overall increase in the number of contacts with GP-MHC per 1000 registered patients [35]. However, the percentage of GP practices that employ a GP-MHC worker has decreased from 87% in 2015 to 78% in 2021 [36, 37]. In 2016, practices in highly urbanized areas more often employed a GP-MHC worker compared to other practices, but it is not known how this developed over time [38]. It could be speculated that the decrease in the overall percentage of practices that employed a GP-MHC worker was mostly due to urban practices employing fewer GP-MHC workers over the observed period leading to the decrease in the strength of the association we observed. This is in line with a report from 2023 that showed practices in the capital were the least likely to employ a GP-MHC-worker [39]. However, as we did not analyse worker employment data ourselves, we cannot definitively confirm this hypothesis and other causes, such as potential higher levels of freely accessible teletherapy uptake in urbanized areas could also be at play.

The finding that there was a marked difference in the strength of the association between SPECIALIST-MHC—and consequently total MHC costs- and age for the highest category of urbanicity compared to all other categories of urbanicity was unexpected. BASIC-MHC showed a more gradient-like pattern, but no obvious pattern emerged for GP-MHC.. This pattern was similar for neighbourhood SES, though there was an apparent mild association for the lowest SES areas in GP-MHC costs. These patterns could partly be explained by other risk factors for poor mental health that are more prevalent in urban and low SES areas, which we did not correct for in our analysis and which would contribute to a smaller coefficient for age in those areas [40–42]. It might also be due to the higher proportion of people with severe mental illnesses, such as schizophrenia spectrum disorders and mood disorders, in urban areas [10, 43]. Severe mental illness often requires lifelong support, which makes age a less relevant factor [44]. In the Netherlands, there is also evidence that there is a higher rate of acute involuntary admissions in poorer neighbourhoods, which might conceivably lead to higher costs independent of age [45]. However, in the highest category of urbanicity, young age still went from not having any association with transformed SPECIALIST-MHC costs in 2015 to a marginally predicted regression coefficient of 0.16 (95%-CI = 0.09;0.14) in 2020, which shows that over time even in highly urbanized areas young adults represented an increasingly large share of mental healthcare costs.

The absence of a clear interaction effect of urbanicity on the association between age and GP-MHC costs and the relatively mild interaction effect of neighbourhood SES might reflect the equitable nature of Dutch primary mental health care. GP-MHC is easily accessible, embedded in local general practice, and free at the point of use. In addition, standardized referral procedures and reasonably uniform service capacity across regions may somewhat limit socioeconomic or geographic variation, while the similarity of problems treated in GP-MHC across age groups could also attenuate age-related differences in costs.

Strengths and limitations

We have previously described the limitations of this dataset [15]. These are the lack of the most recent data, the ecological nature of the study, costs not being the same as disease burden or patient numbers, the exclusion of marginalized groups that fall outside the system of insurance, and the exclusion of costs associated with forensic care, social care, and long-term clinical stay. The ecological nature of the study may be particularly relevant for SES, as individual SES does not necessarily correspond to neighbourhood level SES for all residents within a postal code area. As a result, the observed associations should be interpreted at the neighbourhood level rather than as individual level relationships, to avoid ecological fallacy [46, 47]. However, neighbourhood-level and individual-level effects address different but complementary research questions, and both are meaningful depending on the level of inference of interest. Evidence regarding the effect on mental health of individual SES, neighbourhood SES and inequality at the neighbourhood level is mixed, making it hard to extrapolate conclusions from our study to individual SES [29, 30, 48]. It should also be noted that missingness in neighbourhood SES and urbanicity was associated with total mental healthcare costs and this may therefore represent a possible source of bias. However, given the very low percentages of missing data and the fact that multiple imputation did not substantially change the results, we expect this to have little implication for the estimates. Dichotomising age at 35 is another limitation, as this may obscure important heterogeneity within the 18–34 and 35–65 age groups, although repeating the analysis with age as a continuous variable did not substantially change results. An additional limitation was our inability to distinguish between need for care, help-seeking behaviour, and service availability as drivers of the expenditure differences. Finally there is probably a high degree of residual confounding in our results, due to factors such as individual level SES, environmental exposures, or health behaviours. This together with the ecological nature of our study means our estimates should not be interpreted as causal.

The main strengths of our study were the comprehensiveness of our sample, which comprised nearly the entire Dutch population, and the fact that we had very little missing data.

Conclusions

Over the period of 2015–2020, total mental healthcare expenditure in the Netherlands for the age groups 18–65 had a stable positive association with urbanicity and a stable negative association with neighbourhood SES. The association between of younger age and total mental healthcare expenditure was weaker in urban and low SES neighbourhoods, although this pattern was not consistent in nature across sectors of the mental healthcare system. Our results highlight the complexity of factors contributing to mental healthcare use and point to the importance of taking into account the intersection of different sociodemographic factors to understand mental healthcare usage. Future research could improve the understanding of these factors by incorporating multi-level indicators of SES, by investigating the mediating effects of the supply-side and by linking expenditure patterns to clinical data. Policymakers could take the intersection of sociodemographic factors such as age, urbanicity and neighbourhood SES into account when developing initiatives for prevention of mental health problems or to improve access to care.

As no individual-level data but only postal code-level data were used, no ethical clearance or informed consent was required. This is in line with Dutch law, which only requires ethical clearance for human research and not for aggregated data that cannot be traced to an individual [49]. The involved researchers had no access to identifying data from individuals.
