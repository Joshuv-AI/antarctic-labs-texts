Investigation of the Order of Mixing the Materials and Alternative Water-soluble Contrast Materials for Preparing N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate-Lipiodol-iodinated Contrast Material Mixtures
Authors: Nobuyuki Higashino, Tetsuo Sonomura, Nobuyuki Kawai, Kodai Fukuda, Hirotatsu Sato, Akira Ikoma, Hiroki Minamiguchi
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Purpose: To investigate the optimal order of mixing N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate-Lipiodol-iodine contrast materials (N-butyl cyanoacrylate-Lipiodol-iodinated contrast material).Materials and Methods: Lipiodol was used as an oil-based contrast material. Three types of water-soluble iodinated contrast materials were used: iopamidol, iohexol, and iomeprol. The materials were mixed in three different orders: N-butyl cyanoacrylate-Lipiodol-iodinated contrast material, in which N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate was first mixed with Lipiodol and then with iodinated contrast materials; N-butyl cyanoacrylate mixed first with an iodinated contrast material and then with Lipiodol, in which N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate was first mixed with iodinated contrast material and then with Lipiodol; and Lipiodol mixed first with an iodinated contrast material and then with N-butyl cyanoacrylate, in which Lipiodol was first mixed with iodinated contrast materials and then with N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate. N-butyl cyanoacrylate-Lipiodol-iodinated contrast material was prepared at two ratios: 2:3:1 (N-butyl cyanoacrylate-Lipiodol-iodinated contrast material 231) and 1:4:1 (N-butyl cyanoacrylate-Lipiodol-iodinated contrast material 141). The particle sizes and injection pressures of the mixtures were measured, and their adhesiveness was evaluated.Results: N-butyl cyanoacrylate mixed first with Lipiodol and then with an iodinated contrast material and Lipiodol mixed first with an iodinated contrast material and then with NBCA could be prepared, but N-butyl cyanoacrylate mixed first with Lipiodol and then with an iodinated contrast material could not because of immediate polymerization between N-butyl-2-cyanoacrylate and iodinated contrast material. N-butyl cyanoacrylate mixed first with Lipiodol and then with an iodinated contrast material mixtures had large, irregular particles (33.1-126.5 μm) with non-uniform distribution. Lipiodol mixed first with an iodinated contrast material and then with N-butyl cyanoacrylate mixtures yielded significantly smaller, uniformly distributed particles (1.6-3.3 μm) irrespective of contrast material type. Both Lipiodol mixed first with an iodinated contrast material and then with NBCA and NBCA mixed first with Lipiodol and then with an iodinated contrast material mixtures showed no catheter adhesiveness.Conclusions: Lipiodol mixed first with an iodinated contrast material and then with NBCA is an appropriate mixing order because of its uniform particle sizes irrespective of contrast materials, and low adhesiveness compared with other mixtures.

Introduction

N-butyl cyanoacrylate (NBCA) is a liquid embolic material that is used to treat bleeding, aneurysms, and vascular malformations [1, 2] and for embolization in patients with coagulopathy [3]. Embolization using NBCA has a high success rate and low rebleeding rate [4]. However, NBCA is a cyanoacrylate glue with strong adhesiveness. Therefore, the catheter used to inject NBCA may adhere to blood vessels, making it impossible to remove the catheter from the body [5]; NBCA-Lipiodol-iopamidol mixture (NLI), a less adhesive material, was developed to overcome this adhesiveness [6, 7]. NLI is prepared by mixing NBCA-Lipiodol mixture (NL) with iopamidol [6, 7]. It is believed that by mixing NL with iopamidol, the adhesion is reduced because polymerization is initiated in advance within the syringe [6, 7]. Therefore, NLI is more viscose than NL and requires the injection using a 1 mL syringe [6-8]. In clinical cases, although no problems occur when injecting NLI using a 1 mL syringe [9], one report showed that when NLI was injected using a 3 mL syringe, three microcatheters were needed because of occlusion of the catheter lumen [10]. The optimal ratios of NLI were reported to be 2:3:1 (NLI 231) and 1:4:1 (NLI 141) [7]. However, no detailed studies have investigated the order of mixing of the individual materials in NLI or the use of other water-soluble contrast materials in this mixture. An emulsion is created by mixing Lipiodol, an oil-based contrast material, with a water-soluble contrast material, and is used in transcatheter arterial chemoembolization of hepatocellular carcinoma [11, 12]. It has been reported that the particle sizes and viscosity of the emulsion vary according to the ratio of Lipiodol and water-soluble contrast material [13]. From this evidence, it may be inferred that NLI forms an emulsion, and it is important to investigate the appropriate order of mixing of the materials. Particle size optimization is expected to increase the reproducibility of catheter passability and consistency of embolization outcomes, thereby improving procedural stability and safety in clinical practice.

The purpose of this laboratory study was to investigate how properties such as the particle sizes and viscosity of NLI 231 and NLI 141 are affected by the order of mixing of the materials and the use of different water-soluble contrast materials, and hence to determine the optimal order of mixing.

Materials and Methods

An institutional review board approval for this study was not required because no human or animal subjects were involved.

Contrast materials

Lipiodol (L; Ultra-Fluide, Guerbet, Roissy, France) was used as the oil-based contrast material. Iopamirone (370 mg iopamidol/mL; Bayer, Leverkusen, Germany), Omnipaque (350 mg iohexol/mL; GE Healthcare Pharma, Tokyo, Japan), and Iomeron (350 mg iomeprol/mL; Bracco Japan, Tokyo, Japan) were used as water-soluble iodinated contrast materials (i).

Preparation of Li emulsions

Li emulsions were prepared at two ratios (3:1 and 4:1) by pumping the materials using a 2.5 mL syringe and a three-way stopcock. The pumping frequency was one reciprocation per second. The viscosity of the Li emulsion was measured after 5, 10, 15, 20, 25, and 30 s of mixing using an SV-1A tuning fork vibration viscometer (A & A Co., Ltd., Tokyo, Japan). The Li emulsion was also poured into 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate solution, and the water and oil-in-water emulsion (O/W) were removed. After collecting the precipitated water-in-oil emulsion (W/O), the W/O ratio of the Li emulsion was measured. The viscosity and W/O ratio were measured 10 times for each Li emulsion.

Order of mixing of the materials to prepare NLi

The histoacryl used in this study was NBCA (N; Histoacryl, B. Braun, Melsungen, Germany). The NLi mixtures were prepared at ratios of 2:3:1 and 1:4:1 and mixed by pumping the materials using a three-way stopcock. The three materials were mixed in the following orders: NL-i, in which N was first mixed with L and then with i; Ni-L, in which N was first mixed with i and then with L; and Li-N, in which L was first mixed with i and then with N (Figure 1). The mixing times were determined on the basis of the times required to prepare the complete Li emulsion.

Measurement of particle size in Li emulsions and NLi mixtures

On preparation and 30 min after preparation, the particle sizes of the Li emulsion and NLi mixtures were measured using a laser diffraction particle analyzer (SALD-3100, SHIMAZU, Kyoto, Japan). The particle sizes were measured 10 times for each mixture.

Evaluation of polymerized materials

The prepared NLi was injected into saline. The polymerized materials were evaluated microscopically.

Evaluation of adhesiveness

A microcatheter or microballoon catheter was placed on an incised edible swine blood vessel. Next, a 3-cm catheter tip was coated with NLI, and swine blood (Cosmo Bio Company, Limited, Tokyo, Japan) was dripped onto it. After complete polymerization, a nylon suture (4-0) was wrapped around the hub body of the microcatheter and the hook portion of the handheld spring scale, tied with a surgical knot, and then secured with three simple knots. The handled spring scale was zeroed before each test to ensure that static loading of the suture was eliminated. Axial traction (parallel to the catheter shaft) was achieved by pulling the handheld spring scale. The catheter tip was gently retracted with constant manual traction (approximately 0.5 N, measured by handheld spring scale). The static weight of the suture is only a few milligrams and is therefore negligible compared with the target traction of 0.5 N. The absence of vessel movement or residual glue was assessed as “non-adhesive,” whereas vessel movement or visible glue on the catheter tip was assessed as “adhesive.” The adhesiveness between the catheter and swine blood vessel was evaluated five times using five different catheters and swine blood vessels for each NLi mixture.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as the median (interquartile range). Comparisons of two groups were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test. Comparisons of three groups were performed using the Kruskal-Wallis test, and if the main effect of factor was significant, multiple comparisons were performed using the Steel-Dwass method. p < .05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using JMP Pro 14.1 (SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC, USA).

Particle size distribution (Tables 3 and 4)

The particle sizes of the Li emulsion prepared by pumping for 20 s showed a normal distribution for all the contrast materials. The median diameter of the Li emulsion with a ratio of 3:1 ranged from 3.1 to 3.3 μm, and that of the emulsion with a ratio of 4:1 ranged from 1.6 to 1.8 μm. The particle sizes of the Li emulsions were not significantly different among the three contrast materials. However, the particle sizes of the Li emulsion with a ratio of 3:1 were significantly larger than that of the emulsion with a ratio of 4:1 (p <.001; Table 3).

For NL-i 231, large particles with diameter of approximately 100 μm were observed for all three contrast materials. Immediately after preparation, the median diameter of NL-i 231 (iohexol) particles was significantly larger than that of NL-i 231 (iomeprol) (p = .025). The median diameter of NL-i 231 (iohexol) particles at 30 min after preparation was significantly larger than that of NL-i 231 (iopamidol) and NL-i 231 (iomeprol) (both p = .011). The median diameters did not change significantly between 0 and 30 min after mixing. For NL-i 141, large particles with diameters of approximately 50 μm were observed, and the median diameters of any contrast material did not differ significantly between 0 and 30 min after mixing. The particle sizes of NL-i 231 and NL-i 141 did not show normal distributions (Figure 2a and b). By comparison, Li-N 312 contained small particles with diameters ranging from 3.2 to 3.3 μm, and there were no significant changes in the particle sizes between 0 and 30 min or among the three contrast materials. Li-N 411 contained small particles with diameters ranging from 1.6 to 1.8 μm, and there were no significant changes in the particle sizes between 0 and 30 min or among the three contrast materials. In addition, the particle sizes of Li-N 312 and Li-N 411 were uniform and showed a normal distribution (Figure 2c and d). For all three contrast materials, the median diameters of Li-N were significantly smaller than those of NL-i (p < .001; Table 4).

Evaluation of adhesiveness

None of the NLi mixtures caused the microcatheters or microballoon catheters to adhere to the swine blood vessels.

Discussion

Given NLi is a mixture of oil-soluble and water-soluble substances, it is deemed an emulsion. When preparing an Li emulsion using a three-way stopcock, W/O emulsions of approximately 17% and 70% are formed at ratios of Lipiodol to the water-soluble contrast material of 1:1 and 2:1, respectively [13, 14]. In this study, the Li emulsion formed W/O emulsions of 92.0%-92.5% and 96.5%-97.0% at ratios of 3:1 and 4:1, respectively, when the materials were pumped 20 times for 20 s using the three-way stopcock. In addition, the particle sizes of the Li emulsions prepared at each ratio were uniform with normal distributions. When the Lipiodol ratio is low, it is preferable to use an emulsifying connector to create a uniform W/O emulsion [13, 15]. However, the emulsions described in these studies contained epirubicin, which likely altered interfacial properties (surface tension and interfacial activity) compared with the epirubicin-free emulsions used in the present study. Because epirubicin was not included in this study, it was considered that when the Lipiodol ratio was high, a W/O emulsion could be adequately prepared by pumping with a three-way stopcock.

Of the three mixing orders, Ni-L could not be created. NBCA rapidly polymerizes on direct contact with water-soluble contrast materials, making it difficult to create Ni-L, which NBCA is mixed first with the contrast material. Although NL-i was the mixing order used in the past, the particle size distribution was not uniform, and the mixture formed large, irregularly shaped particles. By contrast, the Li-N formed small, uniformly sized particles, similar to those of Li emulsion. NL-i forms an emulsion when NL is mixed with i. However, it was believed that the polymerization started unevenly by the time the emulsion was created, producing non-uniformly sized particles. For Li-N, the Li emulsion was mixed with NBCA after forming a uniform Li emulsion, allowing uniform polymerization within the oil phase, producing a homogeneous particle size distribution. This uniformity could generate predictable embolization performance and improve therapeutic consistency during intravascular administration. Consequently, we speculate that the use of Li-N, with its uniform particles, might improve procedural effectiveness and safety. However, actual injection behavior and long-term clinical outcomes were not assessed in this study. These factors need to be evaluated in future animal studies and clinical trials. Previous clinical reports indicated variability in passability during NL-i injection [9, 10]. However, the uniform particle sizes of Li-N might improve the reproducibility of injection techniques and reduce variability between clinicians. Moreover, our results showed that the particle sizes of the NL-i varied depending on the type of water-soluble contrast material used, with notably larger particle sizes observed when iohexol was used. In contrast, the Li-N yielded consistently small and uniform particle sizes irrespective of the type of contrast material. This suggests that the Li-N could be less dependent on the choice of contrast materials, potentially enhancing its versatility and convenience in clinical practice. Furthermore, although the NL-i has previously been reported as having low adhesiveness, the present study indicated, for what we believe is the first time, that the newly proposed Li-N also showed no adhesiveness between the microcatheter or microballoon catheter and vessel tissues. This finding suggests that the Li-N, similarly to the NL-i, is unlikely to cause clinical complications related to catheter adhesion and difficult catheter removal.

Emulsion-templated polymer particles form microscale pores after the removal of the dispersed phase [16]. Therefore, NLi, which is presumed to form an emulsion, might also have a porous structure. Sasaki et al. [17] reported scanning electron microscopy (SEM) images showing that NL mixtures form a three-dimensional honeycomb-like polymer network at higher concentrations, whereas they exhibit a two-dimensional folded-sheet structure at lower concentrations. In contrast, NBCA-Lipiodol-ethanol mixtures consistently show a two-dimensional folded-sheet matrix with interconnected microvoid networks, correlating with reduced mechanical strength and decreased adhesiveness compared with NL. On the basis of these findings, structural evaluation using SEM will be important in future research that seeks to understand the underlying mechanism of the reduced adhesiveness of NLi. There are three limitations in this study. First, this was an in vitro study, and the properties of the mixtures were not evaluated in vivo. Future research should include animal models and clinical trials to validate these findings, further establishing the efficacy and safety of the Li-N. Second, only two types of NLi mixtures that were useful and non-adhesive in past reports were evaluated in this study; other ratios of NLi mixtures were not examined. Finally, the three contrast materials used in this study were limited to those with iodine concentrations of 350-370 mg/mL. Therefore, other contrast materials and iodine concentrations may yield different results.

In conclusion, Li-N is an appropriate mixing order because of its uniform particle sizes irrespective of contrast materials, and low adhesiveness compared with other mixtures.
