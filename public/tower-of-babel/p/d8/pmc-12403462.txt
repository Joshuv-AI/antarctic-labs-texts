A concept reflecting relative visual acuity insufficiency among low and moderate myopia students in east of China: Yicheng Myopia Study
Authors: Jiaxing Chi, Chang Liu, Peng Wang, Xiang Zhang, Haoxin Guo, Xiaorong Li, Wenbo Li, Bojie Hu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundMyopia has shown a sharp upward trend worldwide and has become a global public health concern. However, there were insufficient correction rates all over the world. This study aimed to investigate the prevalence of myopia and spectacle-wearing rate in primary and high school students in Yicheng, as well as analyze the risk factors of myopia onset and development. Additionally, we introduced a novel concept to reflect Relative Visual Acuity Insufficiency (RVAI) in myopia students.MethodsA school-based cross-sectional study was conducted in 2022, involving 37,908 students in Yicheng. The refractive error was measured using an autorefractor without cycloplegia and recorded as a spherical equivalent refraction (SER). Visual acuity was recorded as uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA). Only worse SER and UCVA values between the two eyes were analyzed. Myopia was defined as SER < -0.50 D and UCVA > 0.2 using the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution. Multivariance logistic regression identified risk factors for myopia. Correction rate (CR) referred to the proportion of myopic students wearing spectacles. Required Visual Acuity (RVA) and Actual Visual Acuity (AVA) were proposed to calculate the ratio of RVA to AVA (RAR) to assess RVAI.ResultsA total of 37,908 primary and high school students were included (16,826 girls, mean age of 11.68 ± 3.04 years). The overall myopia prevalence was 41.6%, with 5.4% classified as high myopia. Older age, girls, and schooling in urban areas were risk factors for myopia. CR increased with grade (13.0% in Grade 1 to 85.8% in Grade 11) but remained low overall (48.2% uncorrected). RAR analysis revealed RVAI of myopia students in Yicheng.ConclusionsThe myopia and high myopia prevalence was high among the students in Yicheng. The proportion of students with uncorrected myopia was high. Further research should be conducted to identify specific socioeconomic factors that contribute to myopia and to lower the incidence of myopia by lowering the exposure to these factors. The RAR metric highlights unmet visual needs even in low and moderate myopia, offering a novel tool for prioritizing interventions.Trial registrationNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12889-025-24064-6.

Background

Myopia has become a global public health concern, as it has exhibited a sharp upward trend in incidence worldwide [1, 2] The increasing prevalence of myopia not only elevates the risk of complications of high myopia among children, but also increases the national economic burden.

Myopia results when light converges in front of the retina. The most common method to correct myopia is wearing spectacles [3]. Children who failed vision screening had significantly lower academic achievement [4]. However, insufficient rates of correction are a significant issue among all developed and developing countries [5, 6]. Only 33.2% of students in the United States were wearing spectacles [7] and 29.4% in India [8]. Lower uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) affects correction rate (CR) [4]. However, students with similar UCVA may manifest diverse compliance of wearing spectacles in the previous study. Wang et al. have found the wearing spectacles prevalence between the two group of children with similar UCVA differed widely [9]. We discovered this situation among the low and moderate myopia children also in our study. When with the similar UCVA, the compliance of wearing spectacles may be determined by the actual visual acuity need in their daily tasks [10]. And the deviation between the UCVA and visual acuity need was the relative visual acuity insufficiency (RVAI). However, RVAI was hardly available, for varying vision demands among individuals. Some indicators may reflect this relative insufficiency. A concept named “Effective refractive error coverage” (eERC) was proposed by McCormick et al. [11]. eERC was calculated by correction condition, UCVA and visual acuity under their own correction. It unveiled the potential RVAI of persons. However, eERC couldn’t interpret the relationship between RVAI and correction condition of students. Thus, we proposed a novel concept to explain this situation. In our study, we aimed to present the screening data in Yicheng Myopia Study and outline the calculation progress of the novel concept to reflect RVAI.

Methods

The characteristics of the Yicheng Myopia Study

Zaozhuang City is located in Shandong Province, China. The Yicheng District is an administrative district within Zaozhuang City. Yicheng District had a total population of 422,400 and a gross domestic product of USD 2.396 billion in 2021 (ranking 134th among 136 counties and cities in Shandong Province). It has 5 administrative units, 65 schools, and approximately 60,000 students.

The Yicheng Myopia Study (YMS), conducted in 2022, from June 2022 to August 2022, was a refractive screening project that included all primary and high school students in Yicheng. YMS collected the characteristics, refractive status and correction condition of school children in Yicheng. For data collection, YMS established a data cloud platform. When screening, examiners transmit data of students on it simultaneously. Each student would have a screening record. The data are accurate and comprehensive and were collected systematically with a high degree of standardization. Thus, YMS provides a reliable database that can be used to formulate long-term plans and policies for the prevention and control of myopia within China.

Study design

The information of our study was from the first screening of YMS. YMS enrolled all the primary and high school students in Yicheng. There were 40,147 students enrolled the screening. The children were excluded who refused to participate in the screening, wore orthokeratology lenses within one week before screening, were with ocular trauma, eye diseases affecting vision, missing data. And there were 37,908 students conducted in the analysis in the end. The survey comprised both objective examinations and a subjective questionnaire. Objective examinations included the measurement of uncorrected visual acuity (UCVA) and refractive error. The subjective questionnaire was designed mainly to collect the baseline information of students. The questionnaire included personal information such as sex, age, school location, and whether any refractive corrections were used. The detailed questionnaire is shown in the S1(the questionnaire of students’ information shown in the Supplementary files).

Screening

The screening flow is as follows. First, identify screening schedules with schools and number each student by ID number and generate identity barcodes. Second, notify the school students to stop wearing orthokeratology lenses one week before the screening. Third, distribute students with questionnaires of individual information with barcodes. Forth, conduct screening and transmit characteristic information and screening data.

UCVA was measured using a standard E-chart (GB/T 11533) in a room with an illumination of more than 300 lx. Each school child was assessed without any refractive correction. The examination distance was 5 m. Right eye was tested firstly, then the left. Students were induced to recognize as many charts as possible. And the results were recorded using the logarithm of the minimum angle of resolution (logMAR) for visual acuity.

Refractive error was measured using an autorefractor (ARK-510 A, Japan) without cycloplegia. To reduce errors, the examiners were trained by ophthalmologists and optometrists. The right eye was measured first, followed by the left eye. Each eye was measured three times and the average of the results were calculated and recorded. The refractive error was recorded as a spherical equivalent refraction (SER). SER is equal to the number of spherical refractions plus half the number of cylindrical refractions.

Definitions

School grades were coded as “1” to “11” from the first grade of primary school to the second grade of senior high school. Considering the impact of accommodation, myopia was defined as SER < −0.50 D and UCVA > 0.2 (in logMAR visual acuity) [12, 13]. Myopia was categorized as either low (−3.00 D < SER < −0.50 D), moderate (−6.00 D < SER ≤ −3.00 D), or high (SER ≤ −6.00 D) [14, 15]. SER was demonstrated as mean ± standard deviation. UCVA was demonstrated as the median for its skewness distribution. Only the worse eye, with a lower SER, was used for the analysis. We used the school grade instead of student age in our analysis, because grade and age were strongly correlated in our study (Pearson correlation coefficient of 0.973, p < 0.001). And grade had a narrower range than age.

In our study some terms were introduced. The term “Actual uncorrected visual acuity” (AVA) was equal to the UCVA and measured in the visual acuity test. The term “Required Visual Acuity” (RVA) was defined the visual acuity that a student learns and lives required practically in the daily life. Thus, students must have diverse RVA for varying workload. So, the RVA almost wasn’t available in reality. In our study, CR, referred to the percentage of individuals with myopia wearing spectacles and any type of refractive correction, was used to calculate the RVA of students. We defined the QCR as the ratio of the CR of the higher grade to the CR of the lower grade in two adjacent grades. And the RVA was calculated as the ratio of the RVA of the last grade and the QCR. The following formulas utilize the letter “n” to represented the value of grade, with “n” being an integer ranging from 2 to 11. It is important to note that the RVA of Grade 1 is defined as equal to the AVA of Grade 1. For example, the RVA of grade 2 was calculated as the ratio of RVA of grade 1 and the QCR. QCR here was CR of grade 2 divided by CR of grade 1 (QCR here = 0.094/0.086 ≈ 1.093). And the RVA of grade 2 was 0.37 (RVA of grade 2 = 0.4/1.093 ≈ 0.37).

Relative insufficient visual acuity was defined as the deficiency of the AVA compared to the RVA. To determine the relative insufficient visual acuity, the ratio of RVA to AVA (RAR) was calculated. Additionally, AVA was divided by RVA to determine RAR. Relative visual acuity insufficiency represented by RAR, manifested the quotient between RVA and AVA. Statistical analysis

All data were entered into Microsoft Excel and analyzed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, N.Y., USA). An independent-sample t-test was used to compare SER between different sexes and locations. Comparisons of SER across the levels of each risk factor were performed using analysis of variance. Comparisons of myopia, high myopia, and vision impairment prevalence rates across the levels of each risk factor were performed using the chi-squared test. Logistic regression was used to explore the association between myopia and potential risk factors. Variables (grade, sex, and school location) were included in the model based on their theoretical relevance [6, 16] and preliminary univariate Logistic analyses (p < 0.01). Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Demographic characteristics of the sample

This study included 37,908 primary and high school students; 16,826 (44.4%) were girls, and 20,796 (54.9%) students’ schools were located in urban areas. The mean age was 11.68 ± 3.04 years, ranging from 6 to 20 years. S2(characteristic table shown in the Supplementary files) presents the characteristics of the study participants.

Discussion

This study investigated the prevalence of myopia and CR in primary and high school students in Yicheng. It also analyzed the influence of school grade, sex, and urban versus non-urban areas on myopia.

The overall prevalence of myopia in this study was lower than that reported among similar age groups in Tianjin (mean age = 11.2 ± 2.8 years, prevalence = 78.2%) [16], Yiwu (mean age = 12.3 ± 3.8 years, prevalence = 63.1%) [9], and Shantou (mean age = 11.5 ± 3.1 years, prevalence = 51.8%) [6]. This may be attributed to the relatively low economic level of Yicheng. Indeed, a previous study has demonstrated that the level of economic development affects myopia development [17, 18].

The high prevalence and changing trend of high myopia in our study were similar as the study conducted in Yiwu [9]. Higher grades is a risk factors of high myopia onset [6, 12]. The phase change coincides with the stage of China’s school system. Grade 1 to Grade 6 was the primary school stage. And Grade 10 to Grade 11 was the senior high school stage with much more workload for schoolchildren. The workload in these three stages gradually increases, especially in senior high stage. The sharp rise of high myopia prevalence was attributed to steep increase of workload. Wang et al. reported higher workload caused much more SER [9]. The mean SER of myopic students decreased from grade 1 to grade 11; a period of sharp decrease was observed between grades 8 and 11. This myopic shift was observed in other studies also. In Shantou Myopia Study, the mean SER of grade 1 students was a quarter of that of grade 12 [6].

There was a significant difference in the prevalence and development of myopia between students in urban and non-urban areas. The result was similar as the previous studies [6, 19]. The reasons for this difference were likely multifactorial. First, there were more junior and senior high schools in urban areas in YMS. Thus, the mean age of children in urban areas (12.59 ± 3.24 years) was greater than that of children in non-urban areas (10.57 ± 2.32 years) in YMS. In addition to age, economic development level and education burden may also have been relevant factors [20, 21]. Due to the increasing pace of urbanization in China, more attention should be paid to myopia prevention among children living in urban areas [19].

The Yicheng Myopia Study revealed the refractive correction need that was inadequate in Yicheng. The prevalence of uncorrected myopia in urban areas was also high and similar to the proportion of urban migrant children who need spectacles in China [22]. Proper correction is integral for rectifying visual acuity impairment. A low rate of spectacle-wearing may be related to low levels of economic development, insufficient attention from parents, parental misconceptions regarding myopia correction, and low compliance with spectacles among children [4].

The CR of students schooling in the urban areas is significantly higher than students schooling in the non-urban. Socioeconomic factors contribute to the difference by affecting myopia onset and spectacles wearing compliance. Socioeconomic factors, like living environment affected the onset of myopia in the previous study [21]. Economic development in a region has multiple effects on eye health in children and adolescents. For example, economic development has a large influence on dietary structure, housing characteristics, modes of transportation, and parental awareness of myopia [18]. These factors can directly or indirectly increase a child’s engagement with school work at a near distance and reduce their outdoor time. This may explain why two groups of students with similar average ages in Shantou (GDP is 301,744 billion yuan in 2022) and Yicheng (GDP is 203,904 billion yuan in 2022) had a significantly different prevalence of myopia. In developed areas, children spend more time working at near distances. Thus, the main objective of myopia control is to encourage children to exercise outdoors, instead of working or studying alone. The Shanghai Time Outside to Reduce Myopia trial (STORM) recommends a 40- to 80-min outdoor activity every day for schoolchildren [23]. While children in less-developed areas tend to have a lower school workload, they are also more likely to be unaware of uncorrected refractive errors. Therefore, another primary objective of myopia control is to improve the awareness of myopia. Education should include potential outcomes of high myopia, appropriate lifestyles, and proper measures for myopia correction [24]. The annual cost of myopia prevention and treatment in China is USD 69 per person. This cost has previously been related to the average family annual income in a city [25]. As socioeconomic factors have resulted in major differences in myopia prevalence and correction between urban and non-urban regions in Yicheng, there is a need to adopt more personalized and tailored strategies for myopia prevention and control.

The primary determinants of students’ satisfaction with wearing spectacles were low AVA and VA improvement with them [4]. However, we discovered that low and moderate myopia children in different grades with similar median UCVA had varying CR, which was shown in the Tables 2 and 4. This implies that children of different grades have diverse requirements for correction or improved vision. The children in lower grades struggle less from the burden of near work [26], and their actual visual acuity need was low [10]. RVA and RVAI were hardly available in reality. Thus, we proposed the concept RAR to reflect RVAI of students. When RAR increasing, AVA exceeds requirements (no urgent compliance for correction). When RAR decreasing, AVA insufficient for daily tasks (correction needed). This concept bridges clinical measurements (AVA) and functional needs (RVA), explaining why students with similar UCVA may exhibit different correction behaviors. For example, in grade 7, despite the AVA of 0.5, the RAR dropped to 0.29 due to elevated RVA demands (Table 5), highlighting unmet visual needs even in low myopia. What’s more, RAR was a concept reflecting, not presenting directly RVAI. Because there were assumptions and estimates in the calculating process of RVA. And the next step of study was to standardize and quantify the RVA for calculating more accurate RVAI. In summary, The RAR was a novel and valuable concept reflecting RVAI of low and moderate myopia students to some extends. And we need to pay more attention to the children with the high RAR who do not complain about vision loss in their daily lives.

The YMS was a standardized, scientific screening project. Our study had two main strengths. First was the sample scale. The YMS included 37,809 students in a region with lower economic development. As a local screening study, it covered 61.5% of schoolchildren in Yicheng. And as a local study, our data may lack representativeness. However, our study focused on a specific population in a certain area, aiming to control confounding factors as much as possible, making the conclusions drawn more generalizable. Next, we will conduct collaborative cross-sectional studies across multiple cities to further verify and refine the conclusions. Second was the concept “RAR” proposed to interpret the RVAI of myopia students, which was hardly available in reality. RAR was calculated by the CR and UCVA which was easily measured in the screening.

Limitations

Although we aimed to investigate the refractive status of all students in Yicheng, the Yicheng Myopia Study covered 61.5% of the students in this district. The reason for the incomplete coverage was that screening only started after the students in grade 12 had graduated. Additionally, some schools (mainly vocational schools) were not included in the screening. Due to the high average age of the excluded students, the overall prevalence of myopia in this study may have been underestimated. Future screening studies should aim to provide more representative data by including all students in Yicheng, including those in kindergartens.

In this study, we evaluated a limited number of risk factors for myopia that included school grade, sex, and school location. To evaluate a more comprehensive set of risk factors for myopia occurrence and development, future survey questionnaires should aim to collect data on as many factors as possible, such as family annual income, the annual cost of myopia prevention and treatment for children, the ratio of the cost and income and so on.

The classification of myopia in our study was inconsistent with the World Health Organization’s (WHO) updated classification system for myopia [14]. Myopia was defined as the SER ≤ −0.50D of one eye with cycloplegia [14]. Refraction with cycloplegia is considered the most accurate method of detecting refractive errors for excluding the influence of accommodation. In our study, the refraction of the students was measured without cycloplegia. Thus, the myopia prevalence and the mean SER reported in our study could be overestimated. To better fit the results of refraction with cycloplegia, we defined myopia as a combination of refractive error and visual acuity. SER < −0.50 D increases the sensitivity of myopia detection, but decreases specificity, which is corrected to some extent by the combination of UCVA > 0.2. Although this definition and classification may still result in a bias toward myopia, an overestimated myopia prevalence would improve awareness of this type of visual impairment among the general population [6].

Conclusions

The prevalence of myopia among students in Yicheng was 41.6%, and high myopia prevalence was 5.4%. The correction rater was 51.8% among the myopia students. This study showed the dual burden of myopia prevalence and inadequate CR in Yicheng. Thus, different strategies that account for socioeconomic status should be adopted for the prevention and control of myopia development. The novel RAR metric bridged clinical measurements (UCVA) and functional demands and reflected RVAI of students. Future research should quantify the RVA and validate the utility of RAR in diverse populations.
