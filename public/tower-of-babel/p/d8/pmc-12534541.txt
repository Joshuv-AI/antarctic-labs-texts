Enhancing virtual physically unclonable function security through neuron-criticality analysis and lightweight encryption
Authors: Raviha Khan, Hani Saleh, Brahim Mefgouda, Omar Alhussein, Sami Muhaidat
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Physically unclonable functions (PUFs) have long been a key component of hardware-based device authentication. They rely on intrinsic manufacturing variability to give unique and tamper-resistant Identifiers for each silicon device. On the other hand, they have significant limitations, including high hardware overhead, aging degradation, and vulnerability to modeling and side-channel attacks. To address these restrictions, we previously presented virtual physically unclonable functions (VPUFs), a software-based solution that uses neural networks and split learning to improve scalability, flexibility, and deployment feasibility in resource-constrained Internet of Things (IoT) environments. Despite these advancements, VPUFs remain vulnerable to physical extraction and reverse engineering of the deployed model. In this paper, we present a lightweight, neuron-criticality-aware encryption framework that significantly enhances VPUF security. By conducting detailed ablation analysis, we identify the most critical neurons and selectively apply XOR-based encryption, minimizing computational overhead and preserving authentication accuracy. Coupled with a dynamic key generation mechanism based on Rayleigh fading through Jake’s model, our approach achieves up to 99.4% added security with microsecond-scale latency.

Introduction

Integrated circuits (ICs) are increasingly exposed to risks such as hardware Trojan insertion, counterfeiting, reverse engineering, and intellectual property (IP) theft, where untrusted foundries may overbuild and illegally sell ICs, insert malicious circuits without the designer’s knowledge, or steal and claim ownership of the IP1. Logic locking, shown in Fig. 1, protects ICs from piracy, reverse engineering, and malicious tampering by embedding key-controlled logic elements, referred to as key gates, into the circuit design2. These key gates require a secret key to enable the circuit to function correctly. Without the key, the circuit returns erroneous outputs, effectively deterring unauthorized use and hardware-level attacks3. It has emerged as one of the most accepted hardware security approaches and has proven crucial in reducing these concerns. By obscuring the intended logic, logic locking ensures that only trusted parties possessing the correct key can access the true behavior of the circuit. Compared to other techniques such as IC camouflaging and split manufacturing, logic locking offers a more comprehensive and scalable solution for securing ICs throughout their lifecycle4. All elementary logic locking mechanisms hinge on XOR-based locking because of its simple yet effective nature. XOR-based logic locking is implemented at the gate level, and the XOR gates are placed at randomly selected points in the circuit5.

As edge device computing performance and storage capacity improve, Neural Network (NN)-based deep learning systems are being utilized more frequently in edge devices such as IoT nodes. These NN models are often confidential. Because NN models represent the IP of model owners, they should be secured to maintain their competitive advantages. Furthermore, accessing NN models can help adversaries launch more powerful attacks6.Fig. 1Comparison between an original circuit and a logic locked circuit.

Comparison between an original circuit and a logic locked circuit.

NN models employed on edge devices are more physically accessible, making them subject to physical access-based attacks7. Therefore, as illustrated in Fig. 2, encrypting the neural network is essential to protect it against unauthorized physical access. By selectively encrypting the weights and/or biases of specific neurons or layers, the model remains protected even if an attacker gains access to the node storing it, since, without the correct decryption key, the encrypted portions render the network non-functional or severely degraded. One basic yet effective encryption approach is XOR encryption8. XOR encryption is a lightweight, symmetric method ideal for resource-constrained systems due to its low computational and memory demands. However, its security is weak if the key is short, reused, or predictable, making strong key management essential.Fig. 2Comparison between an original model and an encrypted model.

Comparison between an original model and an encrypted model.

PUFs are a viable approach for device authentication because they use unique physical device attributes to produce identities and form Challenge-Response Pairs (CRPs) by mapping bit-vector challenges9. Each silicon device has a unique PUF, which uses variables such as route delays and transistor threshold voltages to generate unique cryptographic keys10. Hardware-based PUFs rely on manufacturing differences or random physical processes, which frequently necessitate specialized components such as ring oscillators, similar to establishing a fingerprint for each device. However, PUFs have disadvantages, such as high hardware costs and susceptibility to aging effects, which compromise the authentication process’s dependability and accuracy. Furthermore, they are subject to sophisticated assaults, including side-channel and modeling attacks, which pose concerns during CRP transmission in IoT networks11.

To overcome the limitations of traditional hardware-based security measures, we previously proposed a VPUF architecture based on a split learning-enabled encoder–decoder model, offering a software-based alternative to conventional physical PUFs12. This architecture enhances security, reliability, and scalability while reducing dependency on specialized hardware, making it particularly suited for resource-constrained IoT environments. As shown in Fig. 3, the VPUF uses an encoder–decoder structure in which only latent representations are transmitted between the client and server, reducing the risk of sensitive data exposure. The design assumes that an adversary would require infeasible computational effort to exhaustively itemize all Challenge–Response Pairs (CRPs) and incorporates periodic model updates to maintain security.Fig. 3Representation of the latent space utilizing an encoder-decoder architecture12.

Representation of the latent space utilizing an encoder-decoder architecture12.

Although this framework effectively mitigates threats such as eavesdropping and replay attacks, a significant vulnerability persists when an attacker gains physical access to the device and extracts the deployed model code. To address this gap in the VPUF and further strengthen their resistance to physical attacks, we propose a novel model encryption technique that selectively protects critical components of the neural network.

This encryption method targets the critical layers and neurons within the VPUF encoder model, which play a central role in the VPUF’s functionality. By encrypting only the most crucial neurons based on an ablation study of the encoder, we can significantly improve the security of the system while maintaining its computational efficiency. Specifically, we identify key neurons and layers that impact the authentication rate the most and apply a lightweight yet robust encryption mechanism, XOR encryption, to safeguard these elements. In parallel, we suggest a dynamic key generation method based on Rayleigh fading using Jake’s model, where the fading envelope acts as the decryption key, ensuring that the encryption key is tied to the communication environment, further strengthening security. This paper extends the VPUF architecture by incorporating these encryption techniques, focusing on the balance between maintaining high security and minimizing computational overhead. We present a detailed analysis of the impact of neuron and layer ablation on the performance of the encoder, identifying critical neurons and regions that should be encrypted for maximum protection. Additionally, we explore the trade-offs between encryption complexity, execution time, and added security, with an emphasis on ensuring the system remains efficient enough for deployment in resource-constrained environments. The main motivation of this paper is to introduce an encryption scheme that (i) addresses a vulnerability in software-based VPUFs that is traditionally mitigated in hardware PUFs using logic locking; (ii) enhances the security of the VPUF system without compromising computational efficiency; (iii) protects the most critical components (weights and biases) of the encoder model from reverse engineering or tampering; and (iv) ties encryption keys to physical communication environments using Rayleigh fading (via Jake’s model) for additional robustness.

Background and literature review

PUFs: hardware vs. software approaches

PUFs are a promising approach for device authentication, as they use each device’s unique physical properties to generate a unique identification12–14. PUFs create CRPs by mapping a bit-vector challenge to a unique response10,15,16. This is their primary feature. Each silicon device possesses a unique PUF that leverages inherent manufacturing variations to generate distinct responses. Path delays, transistor threshold voltages, and voltage gains all contribute to the distinct variances, resulting in unique cryptographic keys for each integrated circuit. Additionally, hardware-based PUFs use manufacturing discrepancies or random physical processes to provide a unique identifier. These PUFs frequently necessitate the use of dedicated hardware components, such as ring oscillators or delay lines, to acquire and analyze physical features. PUF-based authentication involves producing a unique fingerprint for each silicone device, as shown in Fig. 417–19.Fig. 4Cryptographic security through a PUF-based unique device identifier.

Cryptographic security through a PUF-based unique device identifier.

However, PUFs have their own set of constraints. One notable disadvantage is the hardware costs associated with establishing PUFs. Strong PUFs, with their huge number of CRPs, frequently demand additional on-chip area and specialized components, increasing manufacturing costs18,20,21. This presents a difficulty for wider adoption, particularly in resource-constrained IoT devices that prioritize cost-effectiveness and power economy. Another significant disadvantage of PUFs is their susceptibility to aging, where physical attributes change over time owing to environmental variables, usage, and wear22. These variances can cause irregularities in PUF responses, jeopardizing the authentication process.

Software-based PUFs offer a promising alternative to hardware-based PUFs, addressing their drawbacks23. The goal of software-based PUFs is to develop virtual representations of PUFs that closely approximate the unique device characteristics that hardware-based PUFs have typically used. By utilizing ML techniques such as deep learning and neural networks, software-based PUFs can learn and imitate the unique device characteristics that hardware-based PUFs have traditionally possessed. Ablation-based model interpretation and protection

Ablation analysis can be used to investigate the relationship between overall NN classification performance and the contribution of individual neurons to that performance24. This strategy entails methodically blocking or deleting neurons to determine their relevance by monitoring the consequent change in network output or accuracy, as shown in Fig. 5. Although deleting single neurons frequently has a modest effect on overall classification performance, due to the network’s inherent redundancy and scattered representation, removing small clusters of the most informative neurons can lead to a significant decrease in accuracy25–27. This suggests that critical information is often concentrated in a relatively small subset of neurons within a layer, despite the apparent robustness of the overall model. Prior work has shown that these key neurons tend to encode high-level semantic or discriminative features that are vital for classification tasks24,26.

Ablating neurons in a NN to determine their relevance.

Moreover, the sensitivity of neural networks to such ablations may vary across different layers, with deeper layers often exhibiting higher vulnerability due to their role in representing more abstract features28,29. This property makes ablation analysis not only a diagnostic tool for interpretability but also a powerful mechanism for identifying which components of a model are most crucial to its function, information that can be leveraged for model pruning, compression, or targeted encryption in security-sensitive applications.

Security challenges and model encryption for neural networks in edge devices

Edge computing has emerged as a key paradigm for deploying machine learning (ML)-based intelligent systems in resource-constrained environments30–33. It offers several advantages, including reduced power consumption, lower latency, and enhanced data privacy by limiting cloud communication and keeping sensitive data local34. However, the decentralized and resource-limited nature of edge devices introduces significant security challenges, as these systems are more vulnerable to both software and physical attacks and lack the capacity for complex protection mechanisms35,36. To enhance security, various decentralized trust measurement systems have been proposed37, yet securing the ML pipeline from training to inference remains an open problem.

Embedded ML models at the edge face a dual attack surface: algorithmic threats and physical threats, including side-channel and fault injection attacks. These vulnerabilities not only risk intellectual property theft through model extraction but also increase the adversary’s access level, potentially turning black-box attacks into near white-box attacks34. In particular, physical Side-Channel Attacks (SCAs) have emerged as a serious threat in embedded environments where attackers can physically access the device. SCAs, shown in Fig. 6, can leak sensitive model information, such as architectural details, trained parameters, and even input or training data38. This is especially problematic in industrial applications, where model weights and architecture are proprietary assets meant to protect intellectual property.Fig. 6Side-channel attack: attacker exploits physical leakages to extract sensitive data.

Side-channel attack: attacker exploits physical leakages to extract sensitive data.

Recent studies show that SCAs, including power and electromagnetic analysis, can reveal confidential parameters such as activation functions, the number of layers, and per-layer neuron configurations, even without prior knowledge of the model39–41. As this line of research grows, however, effective countermeasures remain scarce. For example, MaskedNet is the first binarized neural network implemented on an FPGA with built-in power-based countermeasures. It applies a masking technique to split secret-dependent operations, thereby protecting critical components like the adder tree and activation function42. Other proposed techniques, such as shuffling neuron computations, implementing ReLU functions in constant time, and shuffling input pixels, can help mitigate timing and power analysis attacks. However, these methods often incur high performance overheads and have not been extensively validated on modern edge hardware40,41.

Despite growing efforts in privacy-preserving ML, most existing work focuses on protecting data during training or inference through encryption or obfuscation. Comparatively little attention has been paid to directly encrypting the neural network model itself, particularly its structure, weights, and specific neurons or layers, to prevent reverse engineering and unauthorized access in edge deployments.

Recently, several studies have explored weight protection mechanisms aimed at preserving model confidentiality. TBNet43 and MirrorNet44 utilize trusted execution environments (TEEs) to isolate critical model components, with TBNet employing a two-branch framework and MirrorNet deploying a lightweight mirror subnetwork to degrade accuracy outside the TEE. ObfuNAS45 takes a different approach by using neural architecture search to generate misleading “mask” models that frustrate architecture recovery. Building on the idea of selective obfuscation, NNSplitter46 perturbs a minimal set of critical weights (~0.001%) while storing the restoration secrets securely, achieving strong degradation for unauthorized users without retraining. Similarly, Tempo47 protects both model and input confidentiality in distributed cloud training by combining TEE-based coordination with GPU acceleration, introducing a matrix obfuscation algorithm to blind weights and inputs while optimizing training efficiency.

Key generation from wireless channel properties

Secure device-to-device (D2D) communication has grown in significance as a result of the rapid deployment of IoT devices, allowing functions like data sharing, synchronization, and file transfer. Establishing a secure cryptographic key agreement is a critical prerequisite for maintaining confidentiality in these interactions48. Radio communications not only facilitate information exchange between devices but also serve as a medium for secret key generation. Over the past few decades, key generation leveraging radio signals has attracted significant research interest49,50.

In cryptography, the randomness of a key sequence is of paramount importance49. Cryptographic keys must be derived from a truly random source, and their lengths can be flexibly adjusted based on the size of the encrypted data. Physical-layer key generation techniques are theoretically grounded in the concept of a common source of randomness. Wireless channel characteristics, in particular, have been shown to offer an abundant source of the randomness required for secret key generation, drawing significant attention in recent years49. Two essential properties of the wireless channel, randomness, and reciprocity, are especially critical for effective key generation51. The channel’s inherent randomness arises from the time-varying nature of its parameters, while reciprocity ensures that the wireless channel is symmetric, meaning the channels observed by legitimate parties are effectively identical52.

Key generation methods can leverage various wireless channel characteristics, such as received signal strength (RSS) and channel state information (CSI)53. These techniques are considered lightweight, requiring no assistance from external users, thus offering low computational complexity49. Several studies have explored RSS-based key extraction, including the works in54–57. Despite its simplicity, RSS, being a single-dimensional metric with only one measurement per packet, limits the key generation rate. Furthermore, RSS-based methods may be vulnerable to predicted channel attacks, raising concerns about their ability to ensure complete security.

Recently, CSI-based key generation methods have gained attention. Techniques utilizing the channel phase, as proposed in Ref.58, have demonstrated significantly higher key generation speeds compared to RSS-based approaches. Authors in Ref.59 propose the use of Additive White Gaussian Noise and Rayleigh channels to generate dynamic keys that assure randomness and security. However, Rayleigh fading channels ensure that the amplitude and phase of the channel response are independent of each other, which is beneficial for the randomness of the generated keys60.

Novelty

To the best of the authors’ knowledge, the use of XOR-based neural network encryption combined with Rayleigh fading channel-based key management, as proposed in this study, has not been previously explored, particularly in the context of VPUFs or other software-based PUF systems. While recent studies have explored Trusted Execution TEE-based protection and neural architecture obfuscation, these approaches rely on hardware isolation or surrogate model masking. In contrast, our method provides a hardware-agnostic, lightweight encryption scheme that selectively targets the most critical neurons and layers, identified through ablation analysis, and encrypts them using a dynamic, non-persistent key derived from Rayleigh fading.

The novelty of this work lies in the integration of three distinct research domains: (1) split learning for encoder–decoder-based VPUFs; (2) ablation-based neuron criticality analysis; and (3) lightweight encryption tied to dynamic wireless-channel-based key generation, into a unified and practical security framework for IoT edge devices. By fusing these techniques, this work presents a novel end-to-end pipeline for training, securing, and deploying VPUF models with fine-grained protection, no reliance on secure enclaves, and minimal performance or computational overhead.

Proposed scheme

This research builds upon our previously developed split learning-based VPUF system, illustrated in Fig. 7, and introduces a dedicated training and encryption server to enhance its security. The original VPUF system employed a split learning-enabled encoder–decoder model, serving as a software-based alternative to traditional physical PUFs. As shown in Fig. 3, this architecture improves security, reliability, and scalability, making it especially suitable for resource-constrained IoT environments. By transmitting only the latent response (step 2) after receiving challenges from the server (step 1), the system minimizes the exposure of sensitive data during communication between the client and server. However, despite these benefits, the earlier design remains vulnerable to physical attacks where an adversary with access to the node could potentially extract the model code.Fig. 8Proposed VPUF scheme with an added encryption server.

Proposed VPUF scheme with an added encryption server.

To address this limitation, we propose an enhanced architecture, illustrated in Fig. 8, which integrates a dedicated training and encryption server. As shown in step 1, this server trains the encoder model and conducts detailed layer- and neuron-level ablation analyses to identify the most critical components of the network. It then selectively encrypts the corresponding weights and biases using a lightweight XOR-based encryption scheme. This targeted encryption strategy protects the core functionality of the VPUF while minimizing computational and memory overhead, preserving both security and system efficiency.

A context-aware encryption key is dynamically generated using Rayleigh fading, modeled through Jake’s method to simulate realistic multipath propagation in wireless environments. This physically grounded key is quantized and normalized to match the shape of the encrypted model parameters, replacing synthetic randomness with a source of natural entropy. The key is securely shared between the training server, the edge node, and the authentication server, offering strong resistance to key extraction or reuse by adversaries. While the model is typically trained and encrypted once, the key can be periodically refreshed to enhance security.

In step 2, the encrypted model and its associated key are transmitted to the node. When accessed by a legitimate client or the authentication server with the correct decryption key, as shown in step 3, the encrypted model is decrypted locally at the node in step 4, allowing it to generate the appropriate response. This response is then sent to the authentication server for verification, illustrated in step 5.

Impact of neuron and layer ablation on model performance

Neuron ablation is a fundamental technique in model interpretability that involves deliberately disabling individual neurons, groups of neurons, or even entire layers within a neural network. This can be done by zeroing out their outputs. The goal is to observe the resulting changes in the network’s output and performance, such as authentication rate. In the context of VPUF-based systems, where robustness and security are paramount, ablation studies serve as a diagnostic tool to identify vulnerabilities and critical computation zones within the encoder architecture.Fig. 9Understanding the structure of the encoder model.

Understanding the structure of the encoder model.

Layer ablation is a coarse-grained variant of this approach, where entire layers are deactivated. While this can give a high-level view of importance, it often leads to severe performance degradation, making it less suitable for identifying specific weak points. Neuron ablation, in contrast, provides a finer granularity of insight. By ablating individual neurons or contiguous neuron groups, one can map the contribution of specific subcomponents of the model to the overall performance. To understand the effects of layer ablation on model performance, we must first understand the structure of our encoder. Figure 9 displays the layers in the encoder. The encoder is a feedforward neural network that maps 32-dimensional input challenges to a 128-dimensional latent response through stacked layers of learned linear transformations, each followed by a rectified linear unit (ReLU) activation. The architecture consists of four fully-connected layers with decreasing sizes (1024, 512, 256, and 128 units), each followed by a ReLU activation function to introduce nonlinearity. This hierarchical structure allows the model to transform the challenges into responses while systematically changing the dimensionality of the output. By first expanding the initial 32-dimensional input challenge to 1024 units and then gradually compressing it, the encoder’s design follows a funnel-like topology, where each successive layer captures higher-level patterns by building upon the lower-level features extracted in previous layers, ultimately producing a compact latent response.

To analyze the impact of each encoder layer, we sequentially ablate (deactivate) individual layers and observe the corresponding change in the VPUF’s authentication rate. This process provides insight into the relative importance of each layer and establishes a quantifiable measure for comparison. Table 1 summarizes the authentication rate drops observed when each layer is disabled. The results indicate that all linear layers significantly affect VPUF performance. Among them, layer 4 exhibits the greatest impact, followed by layers 0, 2, and 6, respectively.

In the following subsections, we conduct a detailed analysis of neuron ablation for each layer. Ablation experiments were conducted to evaluate the importance of individual and group neurons for model performance. In these experiments, either single neurons or contiguous groups of neurons were systematically disabled by setting them to zero output, and the corresponding decrease in authentication rate was measured. A higher drop in authentication rate upon ablation suggests that the neuron(s) are more critical for the model’s decision-making performance. Each subsection presents the two ablation configurations that resulted in the largest decreases in authentication rate, for varying group sizes of n neurons removed from each layer. For the sake of simplicity, only the top two authentication rate drops are reported, as the remaining cases exhibit negligible decreases and offer limited additional insight.

Impact of neuron ablation in layer 0 on model performance

There are 1024 neurons in Layer 0, and the ablation analysis results for this layer are shown in Table 2. We sequentially ablate one neuron at a time and monitor the change in authentication rate to evaluate the effects of individual neurons. With the biggest decline occurring at just 0.27% after ablating neuron 581, the results show that ablating a single neuron does not significantly affect authentication results. Neuron 621 causes a 0.17% reduction, while other prominent neurons, including neurons 259, 362, and 996, produce a 0.20% drop.

When groups of 8 or 16 neurons are ablated simultaneously, similar trends are observed, although with slightly more pronounced drops in authentication rate. Table 2 shows that the largest drop after ablating 8 neurons (neurons 576–583) is 0.40%. Similarly, the table shows that ablating sixteen neurons (neurons 32–47) results in a maximum drop of 0.67%. The neuron groups centered around indices 576–623 appear repeatedly among the top drops, suggesting their relative importance compared to other regions of the layer.

Ablating larger groups of 32 and 64 neurons reveals more noticeable drops. Ablating neurons 608–639 causes a 1.87% drop. Similarly, the table shows that ablating neurons 576–639 results in a 4.20% drop. This suggests that the region around neurons 576–639 holds a relatively higher concentration of important neurons, but even so, the absolute drop is still not extremely significant in terms of overall authentication performance.

More substantial effects begin to emerge after ablating groups of 128 neurons, corresponding to 12.5% of the layer. Ablating neurons 512–639 leads to a significant 13.20% drop in authentication rate. Groups covering indices 0–127 and 768–895 also exhibit notable drops exceeding 12%. This indicates that certain regions, particularly around neuron indices 512–639, are critical to maintaining high authentication rates.

Ablating 256 neurons (25% of the layer) further amplifies the impact. Ablating neurons 512–767 leads to a dramatic drop of 40.90%. Other groups covering indices 0–255 and 768–1023 also show severe authentication degradation. XOR-based model encryption for VPUF

A classic hardware security method for defending integrated circuits (ICs) from hardware attacks, reverse engineering, and unwanted access is logic locking. It entails adding more logic gates to a circuit for which a secret key is necessary for correct execution. If the wrong key is used, the circuit produces incorrect outputs, making it futile for attackers. This technique prevents piracy, protects against hardware Trojans, and ensures that only authorized users can access the correct logic. In our software VPUF architecture, we aim to implement a similar approach to logic locking by utilizing model encryption. By encrypting the VPUF’s encoder, we mimic the functionality of logic locking in a software context. To prevent reverse engineering and unwanted access, the code can only be viewed or executed by authorized individuals who possess the proper decryption key. This method offers improved defense against malware insertion, tampering, and piracy in the VPUF by incorporating the benefits of logic locking in hardware with the flexibility of model encryption.

By using data from a secure communication channel, this method lowers the risks involved in key management and makes the key less predictable. This drastically increases the encryption scheme’s immunity to unauthorized decryption attempts by ensuring that, even if an attacker manages to obtain the encrypted data, they would also require access to the secure channel to recover the key.

Encryption method for layer 6 in the VPUF encoder

Since the previous results were obtained by systematically encrypting neurons, we now aim to randomly select neurons from the 6th layer for encryption and decryption. Additionally, instead of encrypting a fixed number of neuron weights and biases, we vary the combinations of selected neuron weights and biases to investigate the effects on achieved security, computational complexity, and processing time.

Key generation and bootstrap synchronization protocol

Fig. 11Simulated Rayleigh distribution fading envelope using Jake’s model.

Simulated Rayleigh distribution fading envelope using Jake’s model.

Rayleigh fading is a prominent phenomenon in wireless communication. It represents signal degradation caused by multipath propagation, in which transmitted signals travel several pathways to the receiver due to reflection, scattering, and diffraction. This causes both constructive and destructive interference, which causes the signal’s phase and amplitude to fluctuate quickly. A common statistical method for simulating this phenomenon is Jake’s Model, which generates realistic fading waveforms. This study simulates Rayleigh fading using Jake’s Model. Jake’s Model generates a Rayleigh fading envelope by approximating the fading process with a finite number of sinusoids. User-defined parameters include the number of samples, maximum Doppler frequency, and several sinusoids. It computes the fading envelope, which has a Rayleigh distribution, by combining the in-phase and quadrature components that are created by trigonometric functions modulated by Doppler shifts and random beginning phases. The simulation parameters, which include a Doppler frequency of 5 Hz and 35,250 samples, are designed to mimic a particular communication scenario. Figure 11 shows the resulting fading envelope and the amplitude changes over time as a result of the multipath effects.

This Rayleigh fading envelope is then used to generate the encryption key. The envelope, which is stored in a DataFrame, effectively acts as a unique key for the encryption and decryption processes. The key is standardized and resized to match the dimensions of the data being encrypted, ensuring consistency and alignment.

To enable secure synchronization between the server and the edge node, we propose a lightweight key management bootstrap protocol based on mutual channel estimation, shown in Fig. 12. During a secure pairing phase, both the server and the node independently measure the reciprocal wireless channel between them. Due to channel reciprocity, the Rayleigh fading envelope observed by both parties is statistically identical, allowing them to independently generate the same encryption key without any key exchange over the network.

To derive a consistent key, both parties quantize and normalize the measured fading sequence and apply a secure hash function, if needed, to enhance entropy. This ensures consistency and alignment even in the presence of minor quantization noise. Furthermore, to avoid transmitting a neuron selection map, we use a pseudorandom number generator (PRNG) seeded by the shared key to deterministically select which neurons to encrypt. This ensures that both the server and the client derive the same neuron selection layout without explicit coordination.Fig. 12Proposed bootstrap synchronization protocol between the server and the node.

Encryption and decryption implementation details

In this research, we first load the VPUF encoder and decoder model weights and biases from saved files, ensuring that the models are in evaluation mode for inference. Then, the system evaluates the initial authentication rate of the model before any encryption is applied.

For the encryption process, a previously generated key is used to encrypt the selected weights and biases using XOR operations. The system randomly selects neurons to encrypt from the final layer of the encoder by choosing a subset of neurons for both weights and biases separately. The key is applied using XOR encryption to the selected neurons’ weights and biases. The number of XOR operations required for both encryption and decryption is calculated, which represents the computational complexity of the process. The encryption time is measured, with the system running the XOR encryption for 1000 iterations to estimate the average time per operation and the variance of these times.

After encryption, the corresponding weights and biases are modified by adding “bogus” values that closely resemble the distribution of the original weights and biases. This approach is preferred over zeroing out the encrypted values to prevent making it obvious to the attacker which values have been encrypted. A new authentication rate is evaluated with these bogus values to observe the effect on the system’s performance and verify the security added through encryption.

For the decryption process, the encrypted weights and biases are then decrypted using the corresponding XOR operation, and the decrypted values are restored to the encoder. The number of XOR operations for decryption is calculated, and the decryption time is also measured similarly to the encryption time. The authentication rate is evaluated once again after decryption, allowing us to compare the performance of the model before and after encryption and decryption. This helps assess how the encryption scheme affects the model’s ability to perform its task while adding security. Ideally, the encryption and decryption should not affect the authentication rate of the VPUF.

Different security levels allow us to assess how varying degrees of neuron encryption affect the performance, complexity, and security of the system. These combinations of encrypted neurons (weights and biases) are tested to determine the optimal trade-off between security and efficiency. Threat model

In this work, we assume a semi-honest adversary model in which the attacker has physical access to the device and can extract the deployed model binary from non-volatile storage such as flash memory. The attacker may also probe unprotected RAM and bus lines during idle operation, but cannot perform run-time side-channel or fault injection attacks. Specifically, the attacker can:Read the full encoder model weights and biases from flash or static memory dumps.Probe RAM and buses during model loading or inference, assuming no active runtime protections.Extract software binaries and attempt reverse engineering.However, we exclude runtime key extraction attacks from the scope of this paper, as our architecture assumes that the encryption key is never stored or transmitted. Instead, it is dynamically and independently derived by both the server and the node using the Rayleigh fading envelope of their shared wireless channel. This channel is inherently secret, reciprocal, and time-varying, making it infeasible for the attacker to reproduce or measure after deployment. Furthermore, the model can only be decrypted in two controlled environments: either on the encryption server itself or momentarily during the secure connection between the node and the server, neither of which the attacker is presumed to have access to. Because the key and the decrypted model are both transient and limited to secure contexts, the attacker cannot recover the decryption key or intercept the encrypted model parameters, even if they have complete physical access to the device.

The goal of our proposed encryption framework is to ensure that even with full access to the encrypted model parameters, an adversary is unable to reconstruct a functional VPUF without access to the corresponding decryption key, derived solely from physical-layer randomness and never stored persistently on the device. The present work focuses on defending against adversaries with full physical access to the device and model storage. Other attack vectors, such as model extraction or gradient-based model inversion, are not explicitly addressed in our framework. Defending against such attacks may require orthogonal strategies.

Results

The results of the encryption and decryption processes for various neuron weight and bias combinations are presented below. These results are analyzed in terms of the drop in authentication rate when encrypted values are replaced by bogus values, the security achieved, quantified as the difference between the original authentication rate and the authentication rate with bogus values, and the authentication rate after decryption to ensure the preservation of VPUF functionality. Moreover, the security of the scheme is further quantified through the entropy of the encryption key, which reflects its unpredictability and randomness. The brute-force attack cost is also measured, which estimates the number of operations an adversary would require to exhaustively search the keyspace. Additionally, the complexity of the process is assessed based on the number of XOR operations required for encrypting and decrypting each weight and bias combination. The time overhead is measured as the delay (in seconds) introduced by encrypting and decrypting each weight and bias combination. This delay is calculated as the average time across 1000 iterations to ensure stability and accuracy in reflecting the added delay to the original VPUF. Additionally, the variance between the times across these 1000 iterations is also measured to ensure confidence in the consistency and reliability of the time values.

Unless otherwise specified, all evaluations are performed on a MacBook Pro (2023) with an Apple M2 chip, featuring an 8-core CPU, 10-core GPU, and 24 GB of unified memory, running macOS Ventura 13.5.

Summary of results

We have measured the authentication rate drops, added security, computational complexity, and execution times for various bias and weight combinations. Based on these metrics, we now aim to determine an appropriate trade-off threshold among security, computational overhead, and execution time. This threshold will guide the selection of optimal configurations for deploying the VPUF in resource-constrained or lightweight environments, ensuring robust protection without compromising efficiency. To ensure a high level of added security, only bias and weight combinations yielding an added security of 90% or higher are considered.

While all the configurations shown in Table 14 offer excellent security, there are opportunities to make trade-offs in terms of security to optimize for computational complexity and execution time. Multiple layer encryption

Although this paper targets the final encoder layer for encryption, it must be highlighted that this scheme can be extended to encrypt more than one layer at a time. Encrypting multiple layers involves selectively applying the encryption process to the weights and biases of each chosen layer. This extension requires careful identification of the proportion of neurons to encrypt in each layer, balancing security with computational overhead. Moreover, to maintain model functionality during training and inference, the encryption and decryption routines must be integrated for each layer involved, ensuring synchronized key management and proper restoration of parameters. Conclusion and future work

This paper presents a comprehensive framework for enhancing the security of VPUF through selective model encryption, grounded in a rigorous analysis of neuron and layer criticality. Building upon the split learning-based encoder–decoder architecture, we introduced a novel mechanism that encrypts only the most impactful portions of the encoder, identified via targeted ablation studies, thereby achieving a balance between robust protection and lightweight implementation. Our use of XOR-based encryption, combined with dynamically generated keys from Rayleigh fading profiles, demonstrated a secure and computationally efficient approach tailored for resource-constrained environments such as IoT nodes and embedded devices.

Experimental results validate the effectiveness of our method, showing that encrypting a strategically chosen subset of weights and biases could significantly improve security while maintaining high authentication accuracy and minimal computational overhead. The proposed trade-off analysis further identified an optimal encryption configuration that offers near-maximum security with low complexity and execution time.

By addressing the model-level vulnerabilities, our approach provides a practical and scalable path forward for securing neural network-based authentication systems. Future work will focus on further enhancing the bootstrap problem through autonomous key distribution protocols and expanding the encryption methodology to support adversarial robustness against emerging attack models, and implementing the proposed framework on constrained edge platforms to provide deeper insights into real-world performance, integration challenges, and hardware-specific optimizations needed for secure deployment in practical IoT environments.
