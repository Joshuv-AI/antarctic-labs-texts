Right Ventricular–Dominant Cardiac Sarcoidosis

Sarcoidosis is a systemic inflammatory disease characterized by the formation of noncaseating epithelioid granulomas with cardiac involvement in approximately 23% of cases in Japan.1 Cardiac sarcoidosis (CS) often presents with conduction defects, ventricular arrhythmias, and left ventricular dysfunction, making diagnosis difficult because of its nonspecific and varied clinical presentations.2 Although CS most commonly involves the left ventricle, there have been several recent reports of right ventricle (RV)-dominant CS in recent years.3, 4, 5, 6 Differentiating RV-dominant CS from arrhythmogenic right ventricular cardiomyopathy (ARVC) is challenging because both conditions share overlapping clinical and imaging features. To date, few reports have comprehensively described the use of a multimodal approach to differentiate RV-dominant CS from ARVC and guide appropriate treatment. This report describes a case of RV-dominant CS diagnosed through detailed evaluation of cardiac morphology and histology using multimodal diagnostic techniques.

History of Presentation

A 55-year-old man with no notable medical or family history of cardiac disease presented to a clinic with palpitations. Electrocardiography (ECG) revealed premature ventricular contractions (PVCs), and Holter monitoring revealed PVC that accounted for 5.3% of the total heartbeats per day and nonsustained ventricular tachycardia (NSVT). Although beta-blocker therapy was initiated, the symptoms did not improve, and the patient was referred to our hospital for further evaluation.

ECG showed bifascicular block (right bundle branch block, left anterior fascicular block), first-degree atrioventricular block, PVC of left bundle branch block morphology, negative T waves in the V1 to V5 leads, and epsilon waves in the V1 to V3 leads (Figure 1A). Blood tests showed elevated brain natriuretic peptide (55.9 pg/mL, normal range <18.4 pg/mL) and high-sensitivity troponin T levels (0.029 ng/mL, normal range <0.014 ng/mL), while the levels of angiotensin-converting enzyme (14.5 U/I, normal range 8.3-21.4 U/I) and soluble interleukin-2 receptor (589 U/mL, normal range 121-613 U/mL) were within the normal range. Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated no significant abnormalities in the left ventricular diameter, wall thickness, or systolic function, whereas impaired RV contractility (right ventricular fractional area change; 25%, normal range >35%), RV enlargement, and focal RV wall thickening were observed (Figures 1B and 1C, Video 1).

Differential Diagnosis

On the basis of these ECG and echocardiographic abnormalities, ARVC was initially suspected. In addition, ischemic heart disease and CS were also considered in the differential diagnosis.

Investigations

Cardiac computed tomography (CT) revealed neither coronary artery stenosis nor fatty degeneration of the myocardium but identified bilateral hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy (Figures 2A and 2B). In addition, late iodine enhancement (LIE) imaging demonstrated dilatation and focal thickening of the RV and extensive LIE on the thickened RV free wall and epicardial side of the ventricular septum (Figures 2C to 2E), raising the possibility of systemic sarcoidosis with cardiac involvement. Cardiac magnetic resonance imaging (CMR) revealed RV enlargement and focal thickening of the free wall with extensive late gadolinium enhancement (LGE), corresponding to the same region of LIE observed on cardiac CT (Figure 2F, Video 2). Due to the high clinical suspicion of sarcoidosis, an 18F-fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography (FDG-PET) scan was performed after a 24-hour fasting period, which showed abnormal accumulation in the heart, primarily in the thickened RV wall and base of the ventricular septum, as well as in the mediastinal, hilar, and right supraclavicular fossa lymph nodes (Figures 2G and 3A, and Video 3). A subendocardial myocardial biopsy performed on the right ventricular septum revealed the formation of an epithelioid cell granuloma, resulting in the diagnosis of sarcoidosis (Figures 4A and 4B).

Management

As these findings were consistent with systemic sarcoidosis with active cardiac involvement, oral prednisolone was initiated at a dose of 30 mg/day (0.5 mg/kg/day) and tapered off at a rate of 5 mg/day every 4 weeks. After the initiation of prednisolone, the frequency of PVCs increased. A Holter ECG revealed that PVCs accounted for 7.5% of total beats, along with episodes of NSVT. Therefore, the patient was discharged home with a wearable cardioverter-defibrillator.

Outcome and Follow-Up

Despite normalization of brain natriuretic peptide and high-sensitivity troponin T levels, and the resolution of cardiac hyperaccumulation on FDG-PET performed 4 months after prednisolone initiation, with oral prednisolone at 15 mg/day (Figure 3B, Video 4), the frequency of PVCs and NSVT during exertion had significantly increased (Figure 5). Given the presence of conduction disturbances and concerns about the progression of these abnormalities, the initiation of antiarrhythmic drug therapy was deemed challenging. Although cardiac inflammation resolved with steroid therapy, the patient experienced a worsening of ventricular arrhythmias. This was believed to result from structural remodeling rather than ongoing inflammation. Therefore, additional immunosuppressive agents were not introduced. Instead, an implantable cardioverter-defibrillator (ICD) was implanted for primary prevention 8 months after the initiation of prednisolone therapy.

Discussion

Patients with CS often present a wide range of clinical and imaging findings. While left ventricular involvement is most common, RV-dominant presentations—including focal wall thickening, RV dysfunction, and dilatation—are uncommon and often mimic ARVC, as seen in our case. Although multimodal imaging is increasingly applied in the diagnosis of CS, cases of RV-dominant involvement remain rare and diagnostically challenging. This case highlights the diagnostic complexity and utility of a multimodal approach for the diagnosis and appropriate management of RV-dominant CS. To our knowledge, this is one of the few reported cases of RV-dominant CS with comprehensive imaging, biopsy confirmation, and documentation of the clinical course.

The diagnostic challenges in this patient were compounded by the overlapping of features with ARVC, such as epsilon waves, RV dilatation, and dysfunction observed on echocardiography and CMR. Furthermore, the ECG findings of PVCs of left bundle branch block morphology and NSVT strengthened the initial suspicion of ARVC. However, the incidental discovery of bilateral hilar and mediastinal lymphadenopathy and epicardial LIE on cardiac CT, followed by FDG-PET and subendocardial myocardial biopsy, confirmed the diagnosis of RV-dominant CS. To further clarify the diagnosis, we demonstrated spatial concordance among cardiac CT, CMR, and FDG-PET using identical short-axis views. Specifically, the areas of wall thickening and late enhancement seen on CT and CMR precisely matched the regions of FDG uptake on PET (Figures 2E to 2G). This multimodal overlap strongly supports the presence of active inflammation superimposed on structural remodeling in the RV. These findings highlight the critical importance of a multimodal imaging- and biopsy-driven approach to differentiate these 2 conditions, which share overlapping phenotypes but require distinct management strategies.

Clinical features that differentiate RV-dominant CS from ARVC have been studied and reported. Gasperetti et al7 compared patients who initially met the diagnostic criteria for ARVC and were later confirmed to have RV-dominant CS with those who were definitively diagnosed with ARVC. Their findings revealed that a longer PR interval and QRS duration on ECG and decreased left ventricular ejection fraction on echocardiography were more common in patients with CS. In the present case, several findings including a prolonged PR interval, RV dysfunction, and mediastinal lymphadenopathy suggested RV-dominant CS rather than ARVC. This underscores the importance of recognizing subtle ECG and echocardiographic differences to guide appropriate diagnostic and therapeutic strategies.

An important clinical implication of this case is the progression of ventricular arrhythmias necessitating ICD implantation despite the resolution of inflammatory activity with corticosteroid therapy, which is consistent with previous case reports on RV-dominant CS.5,8 This suggests that inflammation control may not fully mitigate arrhythmogenic risks in RV-dominant CS, possibly because of persistent fibrosis or structural remodeling in the myocardium. Indeed, LGE in CMR may reflect myocardial fibrosis, and it has been reported that LGE was found in many CSs that required an ICD.9,10 These findings underscore the need for vigilant arrhythmia monitoring and the timely consideration of device therapies in similar cases. Thus, early recognition and tailored management, including vigilant arrhythmia monitoring and timely device therapy, are crucial for improving outcomes in these rare cases.

Conclusions

This case underscores the diagnostic challenge of differentiating ARVC from CS given their overlapping clinical and imaging characteristics. It emphasizes the essential role of multimodal imaging, including FDG-PET, and histological confirmation in establishing a definitive diagnosis. Moreover, as ventricular arrhythmias may deteriorate even after corticosteroid initiation, careful rhythm monitoring and management are warranted. Early identification and implementation of tailored therapeutic strategies are crucial for improving outcomes in such complex cases.
