Broken Beath Pin Tip During Arthroscopic PCL Reconstruction: A Rare Complication
Authors: Pujan Bishwakarma, Amartya Dahal, Prashesh Dhakal, Sudeep Khanal
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Instrument breakage during arthroscopic procedures is rare but can pose notable challenges. We present a case of Beath pin breakage during posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) tunnel preparation, requiring a separate incision for retrieval. A 38-year-old man with a multiligamentous right knee injury (Schenck KDIIIL) and common peroneal nerve palsy underwent arthroscopic anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) and PCL reconstruction with lateral collateral ligament (LCL) reconstruction. During PCL tibial tunnel preparation, the Beath pin tip broke and could not be retrieved arthroscopically. Fluoroscopic imaging confirmed the fragment's extracapsular location posterior to the tibial plateau. The patient was counseled, and a second procedure was done through an open posterior approach under fluoroscopic guidance. The fragment was successfully removed, and the patient had an uneventful recovery. At 6-week follow-up, he had full knee range of motion and improved ankle dorsiflexion. This case highlights an uncommon complication of PCL reconstruction, emphasizing the importance of intraoperative vigilance, careful trajectory assessment, and appropriate retrieval strategies.

Arthroscopic posterior cruciate ligament (PCL) reconstruction is a well-established surgical technique but with potential complications such as neurovascular injury and fracture.1 It has a higher chance of complication than anterior cruciate ligament (ACL) reconstruction.2 Instrument breakage during orthopaedic procedures is a rare but notable complication.3,4 In the early days of arthroscopy, instrument breakage was one of the common complications with incidence ranging up to 16.8%; however, recent reports of instrument breakage are sparse.5-7 The overall rate of instrument breakage in orthopaedic procedure is as low as 0.18%, with most breakages involving drill bits, followed by Kirschner wires.3,8 The Beath pin is commonly used for guidewire placement in ligament reconstructions, and its breakage, in particular, can disrupt surgical workflow and necessitate additional interventions. To our knowledge, there are no previous reports in the literature documenting Beath pin breakage during PCL reconstruction. This case highlights the management of such a complication and discusses potential preventive strategies.

Case Report

A 38-year-old man presented with persistent pain and inability to bear weight on his right leg after an anterior knee dislocation, sustained after a fall from height while working in a terrace farming field. Radiographic evaluation revealed no evidence of fracture. Initial closed reduction was done, and the knee was immobilized in a long knee brace. The patient also had an associated common peroneal nerve (CPN) injury, with absent dorsiflexion of the right ankle, although vascular examination was intact.

On examination, the patient had mild knee effusion, tenderness over the medial joint line, and positive varus stress test at 0° and 30°. Lachman and anterior drawer tests were positive, indicating ACL injury. A posterior sag sign and positive posterior drawer test confirmed PCL involvement. Radiographs showed a reduced knee joint with normal alignment, and MRI revealed complete ACL and PCL tears, a lateral collateral ligament (LCL) tear, and a lateral meniscus root tear.

The patient was diagnosed with a multiligamentous knee injury (Schenck KDIIIL) with CPN palsy and was planned for arthroscopic ACL/PCL/LCL reconstruction and CPN decompression. A single bundle, transportal technique using autografts was used for the reconstruction.

Quadriceps autograft for PCL reconstruction, peroneus longus autograft for ACL reconstruction, and hamstring autograft for LCL/PLC (posterolateral corner) reconstruction were harvested. Arthroscopic examination of the knee joint was done. During PCL tibial tunnel preparation, resistance was felt. On withdrawal, the Beath pin tip was noted to be broken. Immediate arthroscopic retrieval attempts were unsuccessful. Fluoroscopy confirmed an extracapsular fragment posterior to the tibial plateau. Since the fragment was distant from the tunnel trajectory, the procedure was continued. Femoral and tibial tunnels for PCL and ACL reconstruction were prepared. The harvested autografts were then passed and tensioned. Finally, LCL/PLC reconstruction was completed using a semitendinosus autograft through the modified Larson method.9 The wound was closed because the tourniquet time had exceeded two and half hours, and the patient was counseled for staged retrieval. Postoperatively, the radiograph of the right knee was done, which showed a fragment posterior to the medial condyle of femur (Figure 1).

Radiograph of the right knee (anterior-posterior and lateral) showing tip of broken Beath pin posterior to the medial condyle of femur [indicated with red arrows].

A week later, under spinal anesthesia, the patient was placed in the prone position. Under fluoroscopic guidance, the broken pin was localized (Figure 2). A small posteromedial incision over the popliteus fossa was made. Under fluoroscopic guidance, careful dissection was done to localize the broken pin just medial to the semimembranosus and inferior to the medial head of gastrocnemius (Figure 3). The broken fragment was successfully removed (Figure 4), and the wound was closed in layers. Postoperative recovery was uneventful, with progressive physiotherapy. At 6 weeks, the patient had full knee range of motion, improved dorsiflexion (4/5), and no effusion (Figure 5). At 3 months, the patient achieved full-strength (5/5) dorsiflexion.

Discussion

The overall complication rate during arthroscopic knee surgeries is reported to be 4.7%, with PCL reconstruction exhibiting the highest rate of 20.1%.2 Owing to the light manual demands of orthopaedic procedures, occasional breakage of instruments during operations remain inevitable.10 Instrument-related complication in all orthopaedic procedures is rare, with reported incidences ranging from 0.18% to 0.35%.3,8 Most case involved breakage of drill bits.3,8 In arthroscopic knee surgeries, documented cases involved breakage of knife, bioscrew.7,11

Factors contributing to instrument breakage include instrument-related (fragile design, metal fatigue, and instrument reuse), nonideal trajectory, small space for maneuvering, excessive force.5,7,12 In our case, the breakage of Beath pin tip likely occurred because of resistance encountered at the tibial tunnel site. Immediate retrieval attempts were unsuccessful because of the fragment's extracapsular position, necessitating a secondary procedure.

Recommendations regarding removal of broken instruments may vary. Price et al8 recommended mainly record keeping and reporting of such surgical complications because harm of instrument breakage itself to the patient is negligible. However, Pichler et al3 recommended broken instrument removal when it lies near or in a joint. We believe that the decision to remove a broken fragment during the initial operation or through a secondary retrieval surgery depends on the primary intent of the procedure, the location the fragment, the difficulty of its removal, and the potential for associated complications. In a study by Sen et al,13 retrieval surgery was done at a later date than the primary surgery despite the breakage being identified intraoperatively.

If arthroscopic retrieval fails, fluoroscopic guidance and open removal through a posterior approach can be effective, as demonstrated in our case. Similar to our approach, Milankov et al7 opted for fluoroscopy and classic arthrotomy for discovery and removal of broken instrument (knife), respectively.

Preventive strategies include ensuring proper guidewire trajectory, avoiding excessive drilling force, and using reinforced Beath pins. In addition, maintaining intraoperative vigilance and pausing the procedure when breakage is suspected, can facilitate timely management.

Conclusion

Beath pin breakage during PCL reconstruction is an extremely rare complication. This case signifies the importance of intraoperative vigilance, fluoroscopic confirmation, and a stepwise approach to retrieval. Awareness of risk factors and adherence to preventive measures can minimize instrument breakage and optimize patient outcomes.
