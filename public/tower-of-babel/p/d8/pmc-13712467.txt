A Self-Inflicted Intracardiac Foreign Body Traversing the Ventricular Septum
Authors: Arezoo Hajiali, Reza Hali, Saba Mohammadzadeh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Intracardiac foreign body, Echocardiography, Cardiac computed tomography, Left ventricle, Needle injury

Introduction

Intracardiac foreign bodies are rare clinical entities that encompass a broad spectrum of etiologies. The vast majority are iatrogenic in origin, typically resulting from complications of cardiovascular interventions such as fractured catheters, retained guidewires, embolized pacemaker leads, or dislodged vascular coils.1 Less commonly, foreign bodies may reach the heart via intravascular migration from distant sites, such as peripheral veins or the gastrointestinal tract, posing unique diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.2

In contrast, self-inflicted intracardiac foreign bodies represent an exceptionally rare subset, with only a limited number of cases documented in the literature over recent decades. These cases are predominantly associated with underlying psychiatric disorders, severe self-harm behaviors, or specific sociocultural contexts and most commonly involve sewing or acupuncture needles deliberately introduced through the chest wall. Unlike iatrogenic objects that are often discovered incidentally or during routine device follow-up, self-inflicted intracardiac foreign bodies frequently pose significant diagnostic challenges due to unreliable patient history, delayed presentation, and nonspecific symptoms that may mimic acute coronary syndromes or other cardiopulmonary conditions.3,4 Moreover, their clinical course is complicated by the potential for intracardiac migration, chronic inflammatory encapsulation, thromboembolism, infective endocarditis, or life-threatening hemorrhage and cardiac perforation.4,5

Multimodality imaging plays a pivotal role in the detection and characterization of intracardiac foreign bodies. Transthoracic echocardiography (TTE) enables real-time assessment of intracardiac structures and associated complications, while cardiac computed tomography (CCT) provides precise anatomic localization and aids in surgical planning.

We report a rare case of left ventricular penetration by a needle traversing the interventricular septum (IVS), in which collateral history and early imaging were crucial for diagnosis and successful surgical management.

Case Presentation

A 30-year-old man presented to the emergency department with a 2-day history of acute, pleuritic chest pain. The patient's medical history was significant for prior psychiatric evaluation, and a healed linear scar was noted on the anterior chest wall during the initial physical assessment. When questioned about the scar, the patient denied any knowledge of the reason for the previous surgery and provided no helpful information regarding the prior cardiac intervention. The patient was hemodynamically stable. Physical examination was otherwise unremarkable, with no audible murmurs or rubs. Initial laboratory data, including serial cardiac troponins, were negative, and the electrocardiogram showed no acute changes.

The diagnostic turning point occurred with chest radiography, which revealed a linear metallic density over the cardiac silhouette and, notably, multiple sternal wires (Figure 1).On the lateral view, the object was oriented obliquely with its midportion within the cardiac shadow, suggesting IVS involvement, while the posteroanterior view showed its long axis aligned along the mediastinum. Notably, sternal wires were visualized, confirming a prior median sternotomy. These findings—which were inconsistent with the patient's initial inability to provide a surgical history—prompted targeted questioning of the family. Collateral history then disclosed a prior surgical procedure 1 year earlier for the removal of a different needle from the heart, explaining both the wires and the scar.

Chest radiography, posteroanterior (A) and lateral (B) views, demonstrates normal lung fields without pneumothorax or mediastinal widening, evidence of prior median sternotomy, and an intracardiac metallic foreign body (arrows) with an oblique trajectory across the mediastinum and cardiac silhouette.

Transthoracic echocardiography demonstrated normal left ventricular size with normal systolic function (ejection fraction, 50%-55%), no regional wall motion abnormalities, and no pericardial effusion. A thin, immobile linear echodensity was visualized extending from the midmuscular portion of the IVS into the left ventricle (LV), consistent with an intracardiac foreign object (Figure 2, Video 1). A small irregular echogenic particle was also detected on TTE images attached to the object, which demonstrated independent mobility and was later confirmed to represent fibroconnective tissue on histopathology (Figure 3, Video 2). Given its echocardiographic appearance, thrombus or infective vegetation was considered in the differential diagnosis; however, the absence of systemic signs of infection or valvular involvement made a chronic inflammatory reaction more likely. Anticoagulation was intentionally deferred due to the planned urgent surgical intervention but would have been initiated had conservative management been pursued. No ventricular septal defect (VSD) or intracardiac shunt was detected on color-flow Doppler imaging. Valvular assessment revealed mild mitral regurgitation and mild tricuspid regurgitation with normal estimated right ventricular systolic pressure.

Contrast-enhanced computed tomography (CT) of the chest (nongated) revealed a 50 mm linear metallic foreign body traversing the mid-IVS. The proximal portion was located in the right ventricle, while the distal segment extended into the LV. Additionally, a smaller metallic fragment was identified within the left pectoralis muscle, consistent with a remnant of a fractured needle. Using maximum intensity projection and multiplanar reconstructions, the trajectory of the needle was delineated, suggesting no direct involvement of the major coronary arteries or valvular structures, which was vital for preoperative planning (Figure 4, Videos 3, 4, 5).

Contrast-enhanced CT scan of the chest, multiplanar reconstruction, axial (A), coronal (B), and sagittal (C) displays, demonstrates the transseptal trajectory of the foreign body (arrows) with its proximal end in the right ventricle (RV) and the distal tip in the LV.

The patient underwent emergency redo median sternotomy under cardiopulmonary bypass (CPB). Due to the complexity of reentry, the femoral artery and vein were initially explored for potential peripheral cannulation. After a challenging sternotomy and extensive lysis of adhesions between the heart, pleura, and pericardium, the 50 mm metallic needle was identified traversing the IVS. The needle's tip, which protruded into the LV and was associated with a small mobile mass, was successfully retrieved along its trajectory.

The resulting VSD and left ventricular entry site were primarily repaired with pledgeted sutures. The patient's postoperative course was uneventful, and they were successfully extubated within 6 hours and transferred to the cardiac ward on postoperative day 1. A predischarge TTE confirmed preserved biventricular systolic function with no residual VSD or valvular dysfunction. The patient was discharged home on postoperative day 5 in stable condition, with multidisciplinary follow-up arranged with both cardiology and psychiatry departments.

The excised small irregular mass attached to the needle was sent for histopathological examination. The final pathology report confirmed benign fibroconnective tissue, consistent with a chronic inflammatory reaction, effectively ruling out thrombus or endocarditis.

Discussion

Intracardiac foreign bodies, particularly needles, present a complex diagnostic challenge, especially when the patient's medical history is unreliable. This sequence underscores the diagnostic value of imaging in patients with unexplained chest pain and unreliable or withheld clinical history. As demonstrated in this case, an inaccurate history can delay diagnosis, so a high level of clinical suspicion and the early use of multimodality imaging are necessary to prevent potentially fatal complications.5

In patients with psychiatric comorbidities or self-harm behaviors, penetrating cardiac injuries may initially mimic acute coronary syndrome or remain clinically silent, underscoring the need for rapid radiographic screening to avoid delayed life-threatening complications such as tamponade or systemic embolization.3

While chest radiography serves as an initial screening tool, CCT is indispensable for definitive surgical planning. It provides superior spatial resolution and a three-dimensional reconstruction of the needle's trajectory, allowing the surgical team to visualize its relationship with vital structures—such as coronary arteries and valves—and to determine the precise depth of myocardial penetration before intervention.

In our patient, CT directly dictated the operative approach by confirming the transseptal trajectory and ruling out coronary or valvular entanglement, thereby mandating a controlled redo sternotomy with CPB rather than a percutaneous or off-pump strategy.

While TTE provided initial functional assessment, other modalities were carefully considered. Transesophageal echocardiography was not performed preoperatively as the TTE windows were sufficiently diagnostic and the clinical priority was rapid progression to CT for surgical mapping. Additionally, cardiovascular magnetic resonance imaging was strictly avoided, as it is contraindicated in cases of suspected ferromagnetic foreign bodies due to the potential risk for thermal injury, metallic fragmentation, and potential embolization induced by the magnetic field. Thus, CCT remained the reference standard for definitive anatomical localization in this emergency scenario.

As highlighted in the literature, CT plays a key role in the preprocedural evaluation of cardiovascular surgeries, providing a comprehensive anatomical roadmap essential for defining the operative strategy and assessing potential risks.2

A critical factor in management is the chronicity of the injury. When a foreign body remains in the heart for a prolonged period, as was the case with our patient over a 1-month interval, the development of fibroconnective tissue is a characteristic pathological finding. Although this encapsulation may stabilize the foreign body, it should not be mistaken for benign. Rather, it creates an environment conducive to thrombus formation.8

Recognizing the chronic nature of the injury directly informed perioperative management: It allowed for controlled surgical scheduling rather than emergent crash intervention, prepared the team for dense mediastinal adhesions and tissue fibrosis during redo sternotomy, and justified meticulous dissection to avoid disrupting the encapsulated tract, which could have precipitated acute hemorrhage or embolization.

Consistent with reports of long-standing intramyocardial needles, chronic foreign bodies may become corroded and encapsulated by fibroconnective tissue, causing persistent symptoms; in such cases, surgical retrieval is recommended to alleviate discomfort and prevent late complications, even in the absence of acute hemodynamic compromise or embolic risk.6

In our case, the mobile echogenic structure observed on TTE corresponded pathologically to fibroconnective tissue, highlighting the importance of correlating imaging findings with histopathology. The surgical retrieval strategy is dictated by anatomical complexity. Off-pump extraction has been successfully performed for superficial epicardial or accessible right ventricular objects.6,9 Real-time guidance with TTE or transesophageal echocardiography has enabled minimally invasive, off-pump removal of myocardial or right-sided foreign bodies in selected stable cases, avoiding sternotomy and CPB.7 However, for anatomically complex injuries, particularly those involving transseptal trajectories or left ventricular penetration, the safety profile of CPB is significantly better.

While minimally invasive or real-time echocardiography-guided off-pump techniques have been reported for myocardial or right-sided foreign bodies, left-sided intracavitary penetration carries an unacceptably high risk of systemic air embolism, uncontrolled hemorrhage, and thromboembolic events if attempted on a beating heart.4,7

In this case, the needle passed through the IVS and entered the LV. Attempting extraction on a beating heart in this multichamber configuration would pose significant risks, including uncontrolled hemorrhaging, septal disruption, acute ventricular dysfunction, and systemic air embolisms originating from the LV. Therefore, total CPB was mandatory to provide a bloodless and motionless operative field, enabling precise localization, safe extraction, and definitive myocardial repair.

The successful management of this case underscores the synergistic value of multimodality imaging. Chest radiography provided the initial red flag for a metallic density, prompting targeted history taking. Transthoracic echocardiography offered real-time functional assessment, confirming hemodynamic stability, ruling out intracardiac shunting, and characterizing the mobility of the perineedle mass. Cardiac CT then provided the definitive three-dimensional anatomical roadmap, precisely mapping the transseptal trajectory and excluding vascular involvement. Together, these modalities created a comprehensive diagnostic cascade that guided the transition from emergency evaluation to a controlled, image-informed surgical intervention.

Additionally, the absence of a hemodynamically significant VSD, despite septal penetration, can be attributed to the small caliber of the needle and the tamponade effect of the surrounding fibrosis.10 This aligns with reports indicating that even when conservative surveillance is initially chosen due to psychiatric or surgical risk, delayed thromboembolic complications remain a leading cause of mortality in left-sided intracardiac foreign bodies.4 The management of intracardiac foreign bodies varies significantly based on the object's nature, including migrating pacing wires or ballistic fragments, which present distinct diagnostic challenges compared to the self-inflicted needles observed in our case.11, 12, 13

Conclusion

Intracardiac needles are rare but life-threatening emergencies that require a high index of suspicion in patients with unexplained chest pain and psychiatric backgrounds. This case highlights the importance of multimodality imaging; while echocardiography assesses the functional impact and associated masses, CCT serves as the cornerstone for precise anatomical localization and surgical planning.

Early surgical intervention is strongly recommended for symptomatic or left-sided intracavitary foreign bodies, as conservative management—even with serial imaging—carries a documented risk of delayed thromboembolism and fatal outcomes. For complex, transseptal injuries involving the LV, surgical retrieval under CPB remains the reference standard to ensure procedural safety, prevent systemic embolic events, and allow for meticulous myocardial repair.
