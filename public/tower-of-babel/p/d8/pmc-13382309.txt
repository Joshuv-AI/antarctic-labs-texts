Clonal dissemination of linezolid-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis ST22: a retrospective analysis of 24 cases (2017–2021)
Authors: Katrine Snorradottir Steen Gudmundsson, Karen Leth Nielsen, Helle Boelsmand Bak, Rasmus L Marvig, Andreas Petersen, Anna Emilie Henius, Jenny Dahl Knudsen, Frederik Boëtius Hertz
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryBackgroundLinezolid-resistant Staphylococcus epidermidis (LRSE) outbreaks are increasingly recognized in hospital settings. Observing a rise in LRSE cases, the authors instituted surveillance to detect clonal spread, and evaluated the relationship between linezolid consumption and emergence of resistant. We illustrated transmission dynamics of LRESE by visualising patient movement and overlapMethodsClinical isolates of S. epidermidis were obtained from patient samples at the Department of Clinical Microbiology, Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen, Denmark. Species identification was performed using matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry, and linezolid resistance was determined by disc diffusion and gradient diffusion testing. All isolates underwent whole-genome sequencing (WGS) to assess genetic relatedness. Patient admission histories were mapped using EpiLinx, a digital platform for visualizing patient movement and overlap within the hospital. Linezolid consumption data were extracted from electronic health records and quantified in defined daily doses (DDD).FindingsIn total, 24 patients with at least one clinical LRSE isolate were identified. WGS analysis confirmed that all isolates belonged to a single clone, exhibiting one to 50 single-nucleotide polymorphism differences over a 46-month period. EpiLinx revealed overlapping hospital admissions, facilitating direct transmission. All patients had invasive devices. The Department of Surgical Gastroenterology exhibited notably high linezolid usage than the hospital average at 144.3 DDD/month, accounting for 20% of total hospital consumption.ConclusionThe outbreak was characterized by clonal dissemination of LRSE, strongly associated with elevated linezolid usage in the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology. These findings underscore the critical impact of departmental antibiotic consumption on the emergence and spread of resistant organisms, and highlight the value of real-time genomic surveillance and digital epidemiological tools in outbreak management.Keywords: Linezolid resistance, Staphylococcus epidermidis ST22, Genomic epidemiology, Hospital-acquired infection, Molecular clock analysis, Antimicrobial stewardship

Introduction

Linezolid is a drug of last resort, and is classified as a critically important antibiotic by the World Health Organization as it is effective against otherwise difficult-to-treat Gram-positive bacteria, such as vancomycin-resistant enterococci, meticillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus and multi-drug-resistant tuberculosis [1,2]. Linezolid susceptibility among coagulase-negative staphylococci (CoNS) is very high, with susceptibility rates of 98–100% [3,4].

The most common CoNS, Staphylococcus epidermidis, is a member of the human skin microbiota, and accounts for >75% of CoNS found in clinical specimens. Although considered a commensal and beneficial skin colonizer, it is known to cause clinically significant infections in immunocompromised patients and those with prosthetic/medical devices [[5], [6], [7], [8], [9], [10], [11]].

Numerous genetic markers have been associated with linezolid resistance through two main mechanisms. The first mechanism is point mutations in chromosomal 23S rRNA genes (varying number of gene copies) and ribosomal proteins, where targets of antibiotic binding sites are changed [2]. As these genes are located on the chromosomes, they are only transferred vertically after the point mutations. The second mechanism is the formation of protective proteins or enzymes to avoid antibiotic binding at the binding site [3,12,13]. The latter mechanisms can be transferred both vertically and horizontally, often by mobile genetic elements such as plasmids. This second group can spread across isolates, species and even genera (e.g. the cfr gene can cause interspecies transfer of linezolid resistance) [14].

It has been reported that exposure to linezolid contributes to the dissemination of linezolid-resistant S. epidermidis (LRSE) [15,16], not only through selection pressure, but also due to the acceleration of linezolid-dependent growth when LRSE is exposed to linezolid [17]. Dissemination of resistance is therefore likely to be fuelled by high linezolid consumption, and whole-genome sequencing (WGS) is the most important tool in the surveillance of LRSE to differentiate between clonal expansion and de-novo formation.

This paper presents a detailed account of an outbreak of LRSE, combining genomic analysis and visualization of patient movements. The investigation identified a strong correlation between the emergence of LRSE and exceptionally high linezolid consumption in a single hospital department, underscoring the critical need for department-specific antibiotic stewardship measures.

Presentation of outbreak

In December 2017, the first clinical isolate of LRSE was found at Rigshospitalet, Copenhagen, Denmark. Over the following 4 years, 23 isolates were found. This retrospective study demonstrates how the combination of WGS and visualization of patient encounters facilitated relevant infection control measures. Furthermore, the results indicate correlation between high linezolid consumption and the emergence of LRSE.

LRSE was detected at various times during hospitalization, and all patients had a history of linezolid exposure within the previous 6 months.

Methods

Setting

Denmark uses a comprehensive civil registration system, assigning each citizen a unique and permanent identification number at birth or immigration. Thus, in Denmark, every Danish citizen has a unique and permanent civil registration number, which allows for individual-level linkage of nationwide registers [17,18]. Rigshospitalet is a tertiary hospital with referrals from the whole of Denmark. It has 1300 beds, and admitted 314,633 patients in 2023. The Department of Surgical Gastroenterology is a highly specialized department at Rigshospitalet, with patients referred for highly specialized treatments including organ transplantations, trauma and paediatric surgery.

Epidemiological investigation

Clinical isolates of S. epidermidis that showed decreased susceptibility to linezolid were collected at the Department of Clinical Microbiology from December 2017 to October 2021. Over this period, Rigshospitalet observed an increase in isolates with reduced susceptibility towards linezolid.

All isolates were identified through routine diagnostics, including matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry analyses (Bruker, Billerica, MA, USA). Susceptibility testing was performed using EUCAST guidelines and interpreted with EUCAST breakpoints. Disc diffusion antibiotic susceptibility tests (Oxoid, Basingstoke, UK) were performed, and gradient strip tests (ETEST Linezolid; bioMérieux, Marcy l’Etoile, France) were performed routinely on isolates with a zone diameter <21 mm (EUCAST Clinical Breakpoints Table v. 14.0, valid from 1 January 2024) for linezolid discs. If minimum inhibitory concentrations ≥ 4 mg/L were observed, the isolates were subject to WGS. The outbreak investigation included two rounds of thorough environmental swabbing: the first in 2020 with 10 swabs, and the second in 2021 with 40 swabs. Swabs were taken on fixed and loose inventory, keyboards, beds and bathrooms of any suspected source for the outbreak.

EpiLinx, a tool to visualize patient admission data in outbreak situations, created by Statens Serum Institute [19], was used to track patient networks in the hospital. The tool requires the following input data: patients in a cluster based on WGS data; patient admission data; microbial sample data; and death. The tool aims to identify similarities in patient presence and exposures within the hospital.

Patient histories of linezolid consumption were reviewed using their digital medical records (Epic Systems, Verona, WI, USA). Overall consumption in the hospital was visualized with the tool SlicerDicer (Epic Systems), a self-service reporting tool to examine trends. Total consumption of linezolid in the hospital was compared with total consumption of linezolid in the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology, in terms of DDD [20] and the average percentage of patients treated with linezolid, for the study period.

Sequencing and bioinformatics

DNA was purified using a Blood and Tissue Extraction Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany) followed by library preparation with Nextera XT (Illumina, San Diego, USA), and sequenced on a Miseq or NextSeq platform (Illumina), generating 250 or 150 base-pair-end sequencing reads, respectively. The genomes were processed by an in-house pipeline which trims the reads with bbmap/36.49, and assembled the genomes using shovill/1.0.4 applying Spades/3.14.0. Assembled contigs were screened for the presence of acquired antimicrobial resistance genes using abricate/0.9.8 on the Resfinder 4.4.1 database, and MLST was determined (Seemann T, mlst Github https://github.com/tseemann/mlst) using the PubMLST database [4]. The single-nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) analysis and phylogenetic tree were performed with Bacdist (https://github.com/MigleSur/BacDist), and the tree was finalized in CLC Genomics Workbench. Sites with variation within 23S were identified by read mapping and variant calling in Geneious Prime using a variant call threshold of 15%, ensuring identification of variants that were only present in one of six 23S copies, using NC_002976 as the reference for the read mapping (23S bp position: 107,551–110,472 (Table II).

MinION library preparation and sequencing

In order to investigate genetic changes of gene content during the outbreak, eight isolates were selected from across the phylogenetic tree and time period of the outbreak. Total DNA was purified using Circulomics protocol Nanobind UHMW DNA Extraction Gram-Positive Bacteria Protocol (document ID; EXT-GPU-001) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, and DNA quantity was assessed using a Qubit fluorometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, Waltham, MA, USA). Sequencing library preparation was carried out with a Rapid Barcoding Sequencing Kit (SQK-RBK004; Oxford Nanopore Technologies, Oxford, UK) with the native barcoding expansion kit (EXP-NBD103) in accordance with the manufacturer's instructions, including a purification step using AMPure XP beads. The sequencing run lasted for 24 h.

A pipeline developed by the Department of Genomic Medicine called ‘Nanogem’ was used to combine the Illumina data with the Nanopore data. Nanogem assembles the genome with Unicycler (https://github.com/rrwick/Unicycler) applying Spades V.3.13.0, followed by bridging over contigs with long reads. The genome is finished with 10 rounds of polishing using Pilon. Nanogem then performs BLAST in order to confirm the species, annotates the genome with Prokka V. 1.14.0, runs abricate V.0.9.8 with Resfinder database, and finally visualizes the genome assembly with Bandage, a bioinformatic tool for creating interactive visualizations of assembly graphs, using default parameters (https://github.com/rrwick/Bandage). Hybrid assembly sequencing results were compared in a heatmap of the accessory genome, created with GenAPI (https://github.com/MigleSur/GenAPI) with default settings, and gene variation was investigated further in Genious Prime and using BLAST.

Results

Outbreak isolates

Between December 2017 and October 2021, 24 isolates of LRSE from 24 different patients were found in various clinical samples (drain fluids, N = 10; blood cultures, N = 5; pus from drain, N = 3; medical device, N = 3; tissues, N = 2; skin infection, N = 1) (Table I). All isolates were subject to WGS (Figure 1) and EpiLinx (Figure 2). None of the 10 environmental swabs from 2020 or the 40 environmental swabs from 2021 were positive for LRSE. Fifteen of 24 patients were admitted to the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology, indicating that there could be a common source in this department. However, relevant swabbing of the environment did not identify this. All LRSE were susceptible to vancomycin, the first-line treatment in Denmark.

Genetic characterization

WGS confirmed that LRSE from all 24 patients belonged to the same clone, ST22, with only one to 50 SNP differences (Figure 1). The isolates carried the following resistance genes: ant(6)-Ia, blaZ, aph(3′)-III, fosB, aac(6′)-aph(2″) and mecA. In addition, 23 of the isolates carried fusB, four isolates carried mph(C), and four isolates carried msr(A). None of these resistance genes confer resistance towards linezolid. Neither AMRfinder+ nor Resfinder include linR resistance mutations in S. epidermidis in the database, so the exact mechanism of resistance was not detected with a validated tool. Read mapping of raw reads towards reference genome RP62A (GenBankID: NC_002976) revealed 11 different polymorphisms across the 2922 bp 23S rRNA gene (Table II), of which two have previously been associated with linezolid resistance: C2524T and T2504A [2,5]. The genetic analyses of the accessory genome revealed that there were three major genetic events during the outbreak: (i) part of an antibiotic resistance island (similar to GenBank JK808725, query coverage of 52.7% and 98.6% identical sites) was present in the majority and most recent isolates containing genes, such as fusB, encoding fusidic acid resistance; (ii) a part of the chromosome containing metabolism genes (similar to GenBank CP127678 position 35923–45884 bp, query coverage 100% and 99.9% identical sites) which was present in the more recent isolates; and (iii) a complete plasmid which was lost during the outbreak carrying genes including antiseptic resistance (qacA), copper resistance (cop genes) and erythromycin resistance (msrA) (Figure 3).

Linezolid consumption

The percentage of patients receiving linezolid during hospitalization at Rigshospitalet varied slightly between 2017 and 2021, ranging from 0.13% to 0.17% with an average of 0.15% per year. In the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology, the average percentage of patients treated with linezolid was 2.77% per year, which was 18 times higher than the general average of the hospital. Overall, linezolid consumption in the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology was high (average 144.3 DDD/month), accounting for 20% of the overall linezolid DDD at Rigshospitalet.

Only three of the 24 patients had not been treated with linezolid during the last 6 months before an LRSE isolate was identified in a clinical sample, indicating a relevant selection pressure within the patient for the vast majority of the cases in this outbreak.

Hygiene interventions and epidemiological investigations

Fifteen of 24 patients were admitted to the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology, and all 24 patients had direct admission overlaps (Figure 2), either in this department or elsewhere in the hospital. However, environmental swabbing failed to identify the source of the outbreak.

Visualization of admission data with EpiLinx illustrated that 15 of the 24 patients were admitted to the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology during the outbreak period, and all 24 patients had direct admission overlaps, either in this department or elsewhere in the hospital (Figure 2).

Discussion

This study describes a 46-month outbreak of LRSE at Rigshospitalet from December 2017 to October 2021. The outbreak involved 24 isolates from 24 different patients, primarily found in drain fluids, blood cultures, and other clinical samples. Fifteen patients were admitted to the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology, and all had direct admission overlaps, either in this department or elsewhere in the hospital. All LRSE isolates belonged to the same clone (ST22), with minimal genetic variation of one to 50 SNPs indicating transmission. The narrow genetic diversity (1–50 SNPs) suggests sustained clonal transmission within the facility rather than multiple de-novo resistance events. ST22 has previously been associated with hospital outbreaks in France, Spain, Germany and Greece [21], and this clone has also been identified in Asia (particularly China and India), North America and Oceania [21].

The investigation of SNPs in 23S which may have caused the observed resistant phenotype identified only two SNPs which have previously been reported to cause resistance towards linezolid, and which are located in or near the binding pocket of 23S: C2524T and T2504A [2,5,6]. This suggests that these variants are causing the resistant phenotype. However, this should be investigated further. These mutations have been described previously in combination with clinical isolates of ST22 from Greece and France [5,7]. The impact of the other genetic variants is unknown.

The major genetic adaptations during the outbreak included loss of a plasmid with several resistance genes and antiseptic resistance genes, including qacA which has previously been associated with lower susceptibility towards chlorhexidine in Staphylococcus spp. [8]. This could be a fitness trade-off, as one would expect these mechanisms to be beneficial in a hospital environment. This could be studied further in vitro by competition assays comparing the growth rate of isolates with and without this plasmid. In contrast, part of an antibiotic island appeared to be acquired in later isolates in the outbreak, indicating that this may have a smaller fitness burden than the lost plasmid for the cells.

A notable finding was the high linezolid consumption within the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology, where usage was 18 times higher than the hospital average. Despite negative environmental swabs, the dense network of patient overlaps visualized via EpiLinx suggests that transmission likely occurred through transient healthcare-associated contacts or unmapped reservoirs. The failure of conventional environmental sampling underscores the limitations of reactive swabbing. This experience suggests that integrating real-time WGS with digital tools such as EpiLinx can provide earlier detection of transmission clusters, potentially allowing for more timely interventions than traditional methods alone.

Surveillance of oxazolidinone resistance is monitored closely, and several tools are used at Rigshospitalet to contribute to this surveillance, identify sources of a possible outbreak, and complete interventions to break the chain of infection. Here, the SNP analyses clearly revealed genetic relatedness of these isolates, concluding that the same S. epidermidis infected patients over a period of 4 years. This was obvious when looking at the WGS analysis in isolation, but as the patients were admitted to more than one department at the hospital, the outbreak investigation was complicated.

At Rigshospitalet, the Department of Infection Control has several measures in place to stop outbreaks, and this study tested whether the use of EpiLinx could assist in the visualization of patient encounters. Infection control measures, including patient isolation, were only implemented after LRSE carriage was identified. This highlights the value of proactive WGS and digital epidemiology tools such as EpiLinx in outbreak scenarios, enabling earlier detection of clonal transmission and more timely intervention [22].

According to linezolid consumption records, patients at the Department of Surgical Gastroenterology were treated with linezolid 18 times more frequently compared with the hospital average, but the duration seemed shorter. This may be due to frequent oral follow-up treatment with linezolid (data cannot be accessed). The consistent prior exposure to linezolid among the study cohort suggests that antibiotic pressure likely facilitated the selection of LRSE.

The authors have previously demonstrated a clear link between linezolid use and the development of resistance in Enterococcus faecium, and seen that patients who developed linezolid-resistant isolates were significantly more likely to have been exposed to linezolid treatment compared with those with related linezolid-resistant isolates [9]. This observation supports the concept that linezolid resistance can emerge within individual patients as a direct result of antibiotic exposure, but the previous study also found clusters of closely related linezolid-resistant strains, indicating nosocomial transmission of linezolid-resistant E. faecium (LREF). This suggests that once resistance develops, it can spread between patients in hospital settings, and this seems to be true for both LREF and LRSE. These findings underscore the potential benefit of targeted antimicrobial stewardship. It is speculated that high linezolid consumption in specific hospital departments, as seen in this LRSE outbreak at Rigshospitalet, can contribute significantly to the selection and spread of resistant organisms. This study emphasizes the value of combining monitoring of linezolid consumption and WGS for effective surveillance of antibiotic resistance with professional outbreak control initiatives, and adding digital visualization of patient encounters to assist the Department of Infection Control, despite the failure of conventional environmental sampling to identify the source.

In conclusion, this LRSE outbreak emphasizes the ongoing challenge of antibiotic resistance in healthcare settings. It underscores the need for vigilant monitoring, especially in departments with high antibiotic usage, and the importance of advanced molecular and epidemiological tools in outbreak management. Future research should focus on developing strategies to prevent and control the spread of resistant organisms in high-risk clinical areas. Since interventions were made in 2021, only a few sporadic cases have been registered.
