Recombinant haemostatic protein for therapeutic substitution of platelet function via tripartite haemostatic mechanisms in thrombocytopenic male mice
Authors: Chul-Gyun Lim, Joonha Lee, Gyeongseo Suk, Jong-Min Kim, Hyunbo Shim, Hae Woong Choi, Ji-Young Park, Soon Jun Hong, Chungho Kim
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Platelets play a pivotal role in haemostasis during both external trauma and spontaneous vascular injury. Thrombocytopenia, an abnormally low platelet count caused by conditions such as autoimmune disease or chemotherapy, can lead to excessive bleeding and potentially serious medical complications. Although platelet transfusion remains the most direct and conventional treatment for thrombocytopenia, its clinical use is limited by donor scarcity and safety concerns. In this study, we developed a recombinant Fc-fusion protein composed of fibrinogen-, thrombin-, and collagen-binding modules—representing a tripartite haemostatic mechanism—derived from endogenous proteins or single-chain variable fragments. Intravenous administration of this recombinant protein significantly reduced bleeding in thrombocytopenic male mice subjected to liver laceration by targeting the injury site and promoting fibrin network formation. Importantly, the protein did not induce detectable pulmonary microthrombi and suppressed spontaneous intestinal bleeding in a chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenic male mouse model. Taken together, these findings suggest that the recombinant protein can recruit fibrinogen and thrombin to collagen-exposed wound sites, facilitating fibrin gel formation even in the absence of platelets. Thus, it may serve as a promising alternative to conventional platelet transfusion therapy in thrombocytopenic conditions.

Introduction

Haemostasis is a tightly regulated process that prevents bleeding and preserves vascular integrity following blood vessel injury. Platelets play a central role in this process by recognising exposed collagen at the injury site binding to fibrinogen to recruit additional platelets and form platelet aggregates, and initiating fibrin gel formation through activation of the coagulation proteolytic cascade1. Given the critical role of platelets in haemostasis a reduced platelets count, or thrombocytopenia, poses a serious risk of bleeding during external injury or surgical intervention. Even in the absence of overt trauma thrombocytopenic conditions often fail to prevent spontaneous bleeding2, which may manifest in the skin or mucous membranes and, more seriously, as an increased risk of intracranial haemorrhage3,4.

Immune thrombocytopenia is a representative example of such a condition which results from the production of platelet-specific autoantibodies, leading to Fc-mediated phagocytic clearance of autoantibody-bound platelets in the spleen5. Various treatments have been developed for this disease. One strategy involves immunosuppressive therapy using corticosteroids6,7 or rituximab8. Another approach aims to inhibit platelet destruction in the spleen either through intravenous immunoglobulin, which competes with platelets for Fc receptor binding and inhibits phagocytosis9, or via surgical removal of the spleen10. Thrombopoietin receptor agonists have also been introduced to stimulate platelet production11.

Chemotherapy in patients with cancer represents another major cause of thrombocytopenia primarily due to the proliferation-inhibitory effects of chemotherapeutic agents on bone marrow stem cells or megakaryocytes12,13. In cancer patients with thrombocytopenia typical management strategies include dose reduction during chemotherapy or switching to alternative agents, though both may compromise tumour remission rates14. Thrombopoietin-receptor agonists have also been used in this context15.

Although multiple treatment options exist platelet transfusion remains the most effective strategy for managing thrombocytopenia16. However despite its clinical importance—particularly for thrombocytopenic patients undergoing cancer therapy or surgery—platelet transfusion is significantly limited by short shelf life, donor dependency, and the risk of alloimmunization. Therefore a functionally active, intravenously injectable and manufacturable haemostatic agent that can operate independently of endogenous platelets would represent a compelling therapeutic advancement. Nevertheless restoring haemostasis under thrombocytopenic conditions remains highly challenging. Unlike bleeding disorders caused by mutations or deficiencies in specific coagulation factors which can often be managed by factor supplementation17, such haemostatic agent must replicate the multiple and complex functions of natural platelets. In addition although several haemostatic agents have demonstrated impressive efficacy in accelerating clot formation in the presence of platelets18, truly functional agents must maintain haemostatic activity even when platelet counts are severely reduced. More importantly they must not induce unwanted thrombus formation, which could result in life-threatening complications. Recently several groups have developed platelet substitutes using engineered biomaterials, for examples, ultrasoft microgel-based platelet-like particles that interact with fibrin networks19 and plasmin-responsive liposomal nanoparticles that expose phosphatidylserine to promote coagulation at injury sites20.

In this study we adopted a different approach and present a recombinant protein-based haemostatic agent engineered by fusing human-derived endogenous protein domains or single-chain variable fragments (scFv) capable of binding to fibrinogen, thrombin or collagen—three key components involved in haemostatic plug formation—to the Fc fragment of immunoglobulin G. We demonstrate that these recombinant proteins when administered intravenously in a thrombocytopenic mouse model, were effectively recruited to injury sites, enhanced fibrinogen deposition, and significantly reduced bleeding without inducing pathological thrombus formation. In vitro mechanistic studies suggest that their haemostatic activity is attributable to fibrinogen binding and crosslinking as well as their ability to increase the local concentration of thrombin at collagen-exposed wound sites. Furthermore administration of these recombinant proteins significantly reduced spontaneous intestinal bleeding. Collectively these findings indicate that our recombinant haemostatic proteins, through their combined functional properties, can support haemostasis under thrombocytopenic conditions, and may offer a potential transfusion-independent platform for managing thrombocytopenia.

Results

Design of an endogenous protein domain-based haemostatic protein

During haemostasis platelets facilitate fibrin gel formation at injury sites by recognising exposed collagen, recruiting fibrinogen and initiating thrombin-mediated fibrin polymerisation (Fig. 1a, upper panel). We hypothesised that a recombinant protein with binding affinities for collagen and prothrombin could locally concentrate prothrombin at the lesion which would then be activated into α-thrombin by factor Xa through the extrinsic coagulation pathway21. In addition if the protein were equipped with fibrinogen-binding activity, the localised thrombin could convert the recruited fibrinogen into fibrin, exposing additional fibrin-binding sites and enabling further recruitment of fibrinogen. To test this hypothesis we first evaluated whether localising such activities could generate a fibrin gel with sufficient mechanical resistance to reduce leakage under defined pressure in the absence of other platelet-derived functions, such as their physical mass or contractile activity22,23. To this end we applied 200 μL of fibrinogen solution at its physiological concentration (5 mg/mL) through filters coated with varying concentrations of α-thrombin (Fig. 1b), or conversely, passed α-thrombin solutions at various concentrations through fibrinogen-coated filters (Fig. 1c). Centrifugation at 650 × g was used to simulate a blood pressure of ~133 mmHg for 5 min. In both scenarios leakage through the filter gradually decreased in a thrombin concentration-dependent manner, suggesting that sufficient fibrin network formation may limit fluid passage.

Next we generated a recombinant protein with binding affinities for collagen, prothrombin and fibrinogen by assembling three endogenous protein domains with these functions. For fibrinogen binding we used the N-terminal region of the fibrinogen α-chain, here referred to as FA. This region includes the signal peptide fibrinopeptide A, and the Gly-Pro-Arg (GPR) motif, which is exposed upon thrombin cleavage and mediates binding to other fibrinogen molecules24. It also contains residues that interact with thrombin exosite I a substrate docking site that facilitates efficient cleavage25. Because FA cannot bind fibrinogen until it is cleaved by thrombin activated at the injury site we anticipated that this region could act as a 'safety lock', minimising potential non-specific fibrinogen aggregation.

To enable thrombin interaction we incorporated the Apple 1 domain of coagulation factor XI, known to mediate direct binding to prothrombin26. This domain was designated as PT. Finally to confer targeting specificity for damaged vessels, we included the collagen-binding A1 domain of von Willebrand factor27. These three functional domains were fused to the Fc fragment to enhance avidity and simplify purification resulting in the recombinant construct FA-PT-A1 (Fig. 1d and Supplementary Fig. 1a), whose structure was predicted using AlphaFold228. The recombinant FA-PT-A1 protein was expressed in 293-F cells and purified by protein A affinity chromatography followed by pH elution (Fig. 1e). Sodium dodecyl sulphate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) analysis confirmed Fc-mediated dimerisation of FA-PT-A1 (Fig. 1f). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) confirmed binding of FA-PT-A1 to prothrombin and collagen (Fig. 1g, h). As expected binding to fibrinogen in the absence of thrombin was minimal, but became evident upon thrombin activation (Fig. 1i). Subsequent surface plasmon resonance (SPR) analysis showed binding affinity (KD) 674.3 nM for prothrombin, 8.48 μM for collagen, 160 nM for fibrinogen (Supplementary Fig. 1b).

Sequencing of positive clones identified eight unique collagen binders 10 fibrinogen binders and 18 prothrombin binders (Fig. 2b, d, f; clone names indicated in bold). Three scFv clones—T17 (thrombin-binding), C6 (collagen-binding) and F7 (fibrinogen-binding)—were selected based on clone abundance, binding affinity, compatibility with fibrin gel formation and expression level (Supplementary Fig. 2). These scFvs were linked via Gly-Ser flexible linkers31 to allow each module to function independently so that their activities are not dependent on domain order, and the construct was fused with a signal peptide and twin-Strep (TST) tag32 at the N-terminus and an Fc domain at the C-terminus, resulting in the construct T17-C6-F7 (Fig. 2g and Supplementary Fig. 3a). The purified protein T17-C6-F7 (Fig. 2g, h), exhibited binding to prothrombin, collagen and fibrinogen, as measured by ELISA (Fig. 2i–k). SPR analysis further determined the binding affinity to be 490.7 nM for prothrombin 210.3 nM for collagen and 2.57 μM for fibrinogen, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 3b).

Haemostatic effects of FA-PT-A1 and T17-C6-F7 in thrombocytopenic mice

To evaluate haemostatic efficacy we first determined the plasma half-lives of FA-PT-A1 and T17-C6-F7 in mice using sandwich ELISA. To minimise background signals from anti-Fc detection in serum we inserted a 2B8 tag33 immediately after the signal peptide, allowing specific capture via an anti-2B8-coated surface. ELISA on blood samples (50 μL) collected from the left jugular vein at 1, 6, 12, 26.5, 36, 51 and 73 h post-injection of the recombinant proteins (5 mg/kg) revealed plasma half-lives of 41.47 ± 2.12 h for FA-PT-A1 and 27.79 ± 1.16 h for T17-C6-F7 (Fig. 3a), suggesting a practical time window of ~24 h for efficacy evaluation. At 48 h after injection the recombinant proteins were detected predominantly in the liver (Supplementary Fig. 4), consistent with clearance pathways commonly observed for biologics34. In vitro ELISA suggested that the recombinant proteins minimally bind to plasma proteins such as albumin (Supplementary Fig. 5a) and can retain their intended interaction with fibrinogen in a plasma environment (Supplementary Fig. 5b, c). Next to assess the haemostatic efficacy of the recombinant proteins, thrombocytopenia was induced using an anti-CD41 antibody35. Platelet counts decreased to 30–60 × 10³/μL which is less than 10% of the normal level (~573 × 10³/μL), by day 1 post-injection of anti-CD41 antibodies (Fig. 3b), indicating severe thrombocytopenia36. As expected the reduced platelet count was accompanied by decreases in ATP, ADP and thromboxane A2 levels, factors derived from platelets, while plasma thrombin level was not changed significantly (Supplementary Fig. 5f–i). Administration of FA-PT-A1 or T17-C6-F7 did not significantly alter these parameters under non-injured condition (Supplementary Fig. 5f–i). For the bleeding assay thrombocytopenic mice generated by anti-CD41 injection were then subjected to a liver laceration model37 to quantitatively measure blood loss (Fig. 3c). For comparison with the recombinant proteins an Fc-only protein was used as the appropriate control, because FA-PT-A1 and T17-C6-F7 are Fc-fusion constructs. As expected Fc control-injected thrombocytopenic mice exhibited significantly increased bleeding compared to healthy controls (Fig. 3d, e, grey vs. blue lines). In contrast mice pretreated with FA-PT-A1 or T17-C6-F7 (5 mg/kg) at 30 min before liver laceration showed a 59.5% and 59.4% reduction in blood loss, respectively, based on total blood loss measured at 600 s (Fig. 3d, e), indicating their efficacy as platelet substitutes under thrombocytopenic conditions. Histological staining of liver sections using anti-Fc antibodies (Fig. 3f) confirmed wound-specific accumulation of both proteins compared to the Fc control-injected groups (Fig. 3g, top and bottom panels), while platelet staining was observed only at the wound site in the Fc control-injected groups (Supplementary Fig. 6). Fibrinogen staining which was minimal in Fc-treated thrombocytopenic mice, was restored in mice treated with the recombinant proteins and resembled that of healthy controls (Fig. 3g, middle panels). Quantification confirmed significant increases in fibrinogen staining near the wound in mice injected with recombinant proteins (Fig. 3h). Furthermore topical application of the recombinant proteins in 1% (w/v) sodium alginate hydrogel (Fig. 3i) also reduced bleeding from lacerated livers in thrombocytopenic mice (Fig. 3j, Supplementary Movies 1, 2 and Supplementary Fig. 7), further supporting their haemostatic potential. Nonetheless the absence of other platelet functions may not allow complete and sustained haemostasis. Indeed even after the recombinant proteins induced initial haemostasis at the injured tail, rebleeding was observed in thrombocytopenic mice (Supplementary Fig. 8). Taken together these data suggest that the recombinant proteins mitigate bleeding under thrombocytopenic conditions by targeting wounds and possibly promoting fibrin network formation, although they do not fully substitute for the diverse functional roles of platelets.

Safety evaluation of the recombinant haemostatic proteins

To evaluate potential side effects we administered the recombinant haemostatic proteins to mice three times at 4-day intervals and assessed their safety profiles (Fig. 4a). Throughout the dosing period no significant changes in body weight were observed across the groups (Supplementary Fig. 9a). Core body temperature also remained stable and comparable among all groups (Supplementary Fig. 9b). Plasma obtained on day 11 showed non-detectable levels of antigen-specific total IgE in all groups (Supplementary Fig. 9c) indicating the absence of IgE-mediated hypersensitivity. The stable body-temperature profiles were consistent with this absence of allergic responses. Peripheral blood collected on days 2 and 11 was also analysed for immune-cell composition. Flow cytometry revealed no significant or minimal alterations in CD3⁺ T cells CD19⁺ B cells, CD45⁺ leucocytes, or Ly6C⁺ inflammatory cells in FA-PT-A1 or T17-C6-F7-treated mice relative to controls (Fig. 4b, c and Supplementary Fig. 9d, e). These results indicate that repeated administration of the recombinant proteins does not induce meaningful changes in the major immune-cell populations in peripheral blood. Furthermore to evaluate potential organ-level toxicity, kidneys from all treatment groups were collected for H&E-based histopathological examination (Supplementary Fig. 9f). Independent pathological assessment demonstrated well-preserved glomerular architecture without evidence of mesangial expansion tuft retraction, or capillary loop distortion. Likewise the renal tubules and collecting ducts exhibited normal epithelial morphology with no signs of cellular degeneration, luminal obstruction, or inflammatory infiltrates. To evaluate potential prothrombotic side effects lung tissues from mice after repeated dosing were analysed histologically (Fig. 4a). Pulmonary microthrombi frequently occur in pathological conditions due to the lung’s dense capillary network which readily traps circulating thrombi38. A 10-min mucin injection served as a prothrombotic control39. Anti-CD41/DAPI staining revealed that clustered staining patterns—defined as particles larger than 30 µm² approximately the area of a single spread platelet and used here as indicative of microthrombi40—were prevalent in mucin-injected mice, whereas such patterns were barely detected in the recombinant protein-injected groups and in Fc control-injected groups (Fig. 4d). To quantify platelet staining we converted the microscopy images into a binary format and performed particle analysis to measure the stained areas (Supplementary Fig. 10). Mucin-injected mice showed a substantial increase in both the total area occupied by such particles (Fig. 4e) and the number of microthrombi (Fig. 4f) per microscopic field. In contrast neither FA-PT-A1 nor T17-C6-F7 induced such features indicative of pulmonary microthrombus formation. These results suggest that intravenous injection of recombinant proteins may not trigger a prothrombotic response.

To mimic the haemostatic process in vitro hFA‑PT‑A1 was applied to a collagen-coated surface and incubated with thrombin, followed by assessment of surface thrombin activity using the thrombin-specific fluorogenic substrate Z-GGR-AMC (Fig. 5g, inset). As expected deletion of either the prothrombin-binding PT domain or the collagen-binding A1 domain significantly reduced thrombin activity on the collagen surface, whereas deletion of the fibrinogen-binding FA domain had minimal effect, highlighting the importance of PT and A1 in thrombin localisation. We also evaluated fibrin network formation by measuring turbidity at 350 nm. Addition of hFA-PT-A1 to a fibrinogen solution at a 1:10 molar ratio accelerated fibrin polymerisation as indicated by a shortened time to maximal turbidity and an increased final turbidity value (Fig. 5h), suggesting enhanced fibrin network density and maturation. Confocal microscopy further revealed that hFA-PT-A1 promoted denser fibrin gel structures compared to control (Fig. 5i, j). Notably deletion of the FA domain, hPT-A1(ΔFA), markedly reduced this effect, whereas deletion of either the PT or A1 domain, hFA-A1(ΔPT) or hFA-PT (ΔA1), had effects nearly equivalent to that of hFA-PT-A1 (Fig. 5i, j). This enhancement is likely due to the Fc-mediated dimeric nature of hFA-PT-A1 which provides two GPR motifs (knob A) capable of binding to the hole a region in the fibrinogen γ chain42.

Typically knob A–hole a interactions occur between two fibrin molecules simultaneously, yielding a functionality (f) of 2 based on the Flory–Stockmeyer gelation theory43, which leads to linear protofibril formation44 (Supplementary Fig. 12a). Occasionally trimolecular junctions also form, contributing branch points to the fibrin network45 (Supplementary Fig. 12a). In contrast hFA-PT-A1 forms an Fc-mediated dimer that presents two knob A motifs but no hole a. Thus when two hFA-PT-A1 dimers bind independently to the two native hole a sites at each end of a protofibril, only one knob A motif from each dimer is engaged, leaving two unused knob A motifs at each end. As a result a total of four knob A motifs remain exposed, effectively increasing the protofibril functionality to f = 4 (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Computational simulations based on the Flory–Stockmeyer theory and the Carr–Hermans turbidity model46 predict that this increased functionality promotes enhanced branching (Supplementary Fig. 12c) and results in denser fibrin gels (Supplementary Fig. 12d) which aligns with our experimental observations of increased fibrin gel turbidity (Fig. 5h).

The haemostatic efficacy of hFA-PT-A1 was next evaluated using the same liver-laceration model described in Fig. 3c where it exhibited a comparable reduction in blood loss (Fig. 6d). Notably extending the observation period to 20 min revealed a plateau in cumulative blood loss, suggesting that bleeding was not only reduced but largely arrested. To further assess efficacy in an external injury context we employed a standardised mouse tail bleeding assay47. Following administration of hFA-PT-A1 or control Fc in anti-CD41 antibody-induced thrombocytopenic mice the tail vein was transected, guided on an aluminium bracket to generate a consistent injury depth of approximately 1 mm (Fig. 6e). Thrombocytopenic mice treated with hFA-PT-A1 exhibited a significant reduction in both total blood loss (Fig. 6f) and bleeding duration (Fig. 6g) compared with the Fc control-treated controls indicating that hFA-PT-A1 administration enhances haemostatic responses in vascular trauma under thrombocytopenic conditions.

Finally we evaluated the therapeutic potential of hFA‑PT‑A1 in a model of spontaneous intestinal haemorrhage. Bleeding was quantified by measuring haemoglobin content in faecal samples (Fig. 6h). To mimic chemotherapy-induced thrombocytopenia we co-administered carboplatin and anti-CD41 antibody. These mice exhibited a marked increase in intestinal bleeding compared with Fc-treated normal controls (Fig. 6i, bar graphs 1 and 2). Importantly injection of hFA-PT-A1 significantly reduced this bleeding (Fig. 6i, bar graphs 2 and 3), despite continued severe platelet depletion (Fig. 6j). These findings indicate that hFA-PT-A1 confers protective haemostatic effects against spontaneous intestinal bleeding in a setting of severe thrombocytopenia, reinforcing its potential as a therapeutic option for managing bleeding complications in thrombocytopenic patients.

Discussion

Recent advances in artificial platelet design have introduced promising strategies for replicating key platelet functions using recombinant or synthetic constructs. Notably several research groups have developed platelet-mimetic particles that can target sites of vascular injury and facilitate localised haemostasis—for example, lipid-based nanoparticles conjugated with von Willebrand factor-binding peptides and fibrinogen-mimetic ligands, which enable platelet recruitment and adhesion at the wound site48. Similarly functionalized nanoparticles incorporating Arg-Gly-Asp motifs have demonstrated the ability to enhance fibrin formation and accelerate clot maturation in preclinical bleeding models49. However these approaches depend on the presence of functional endogenous platelets to achieve haemostatic efficacy, which limits their applicability in conditions of severe thrombocytopenia.

To address this challenge we developed a recombinant haemostatic proteins composed of chimeric fusion proteins that integrate key endogenous domains responsible for fibrinogen binding, thrombin anchoring and vascular injury targeting. Anchoring thrombin activity at the site of injury appears to be critical for effective haemostasis. Thrombin when activated through the tissue factor-mediated extrinsic pathway and subsequent prothrombinase activity50, amplifies local coagulation through positive feedback in the coagulation cascade51. Consequently localised and sustained thrombin activity facilitates dense fibrin network formation precisely at the injury site, which may be sufficient to halt bleeding even in the absence of functional platelets under controlled conditions (Fig. 1b, c).

Another distinguishing feature of our recombinant haemostatic proteins is its ability to circulate safely in the bloodstream without inducing off-target thrombosis. This is achieved through the intrinsic 'safety lock' mechanism encoded in the N-terminal region of the fibrinogen α-chain52 which prevents premature fibrin polymerisation in the absence of thrombin activation53. By incorporating this thrombin-sensitive domain into the chimeric protein design FA-PT-A1 remains inert under physiological conditions and become selectively activated at sites of vascular injury. This design enables systemic administration without increasing the risk of ectopic clot formation (Fig. 4d).

The dimeric configuration of FA-PT-A1 may further contribute to its haemostatic efficacy by enabling simultaneous interactions with multiple coagulation components particularly fibrin molecules. Each monomer within the dimer contains a fibrinogen α-chain-derived GPR motif and the bivalent nature of the construct allows it to capture two fibrin/fibrinogen molecules simultaneously. This dual engagement likely facilitates cross-linking or local clustering of fibrin strands at the wound site thereby promoting the formation of a denser and more stable fibrin network (Supplementary Fig. 12b). Moreover by increasing avidity toward thrombin, the dimeric FA-PT-A1 construct may enhance polymerisation kinetics. This multivalent interaction model provides a mechanistic explanation for the accelerated fibrin assembly observed in both in vitro and in vivo experiments following FA-PT-A1 administration (Figs. 3g and 5i, j).

The features embedded in these recombinant haemostatic proteins—injury-site specific thrombin anchoring, a built-in safety lock that prevents premature activation, and multivalent interaction that enhances fibrin network formation—appear to confer haemostatic activity while minimising uncontrolled thrombosis. Despite these advantages several limitations remain. Because the strategy lacks platelet-specific functions such as clot retraction and amplification mechanisms driven by platelet secretion, their ability to sustain long-term haemostasis is inherently incomplete, as reflected by occasional rebleeding in the tail-laceration assay (Supplementary Fig. 8). Moreover although repeated-dose evaluation did not reveal increased pulmonary microthrombi (Fig. 4d), delayed adverse events cannot be fully excluded and will require continued evaluation in future studies.

Several innovative platelet-mimetic systems have recently been reported each offering valuable insights into how key platelet functions may be reconstructed synthetically. Ultrasoft platelet-like particles for example introduce deformable microgel structures that interact with fibrin networks and partially emulate platelet contractile behaviour19. Similarly plasmin-responsive procoagulant nanoparticles provide an elegant strategy for generating a localised phosphatidylserine-rich surface to support thrombin generation20. While promising questions remain regarding their anchorage at wound sites, potential dependence on endogenous platelets, and/or the long-term behaviour of those particles under physiological degradation. Relative to these particle-based systems our recombinant protein-based haemostatic agents have intrinsic advantages. They directly anchor to collagen exposed at vascular injuries and localise thrombin and fibrinogen through defined molecular domains. In addition because their functional domains are encoded at the amino-acid level, they do not require complex post-synthetic surface engineering, rely only on standard antibody-generation platforms for production, and allow reliable batch-to-batch reproducibility.

Taken together our findings indicate that this recombinant protein-based haemostatic agent may offer a viable therapeutic strategy. These constructs could be employed as bridge therapy during platelet transfusion shortages or as an adjunct to reduce the transfusion burden thereby addressing a critical unmet need in the management of bleeding complications in thrombocytopenic patients.

Methods

Animal and ethics statement

Male BALB/c mice (8 weeks old) were used throughout the study for surgical procedures and antibody-induced immune thrombocytopenia. All animal experiments were conducted in compliance with the National Institutes of Health Guide for the Care and Use of Laboratory Animals and all experimental procedures in this study were approved by the Korea University Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (KUIACUC-2024-0086). Animals were maintained under controlled temperature (23 °C) and humidity conditions (50%) with a 12-h light-dark cycle (light on at 7 AM). Food and water were available ad libitum.

Construction of expression vectors and protein purification

DNA sequence for FA-PT-A1 and hFA-PT-A1 was synthesised and purchased from Integrated DNA Technologies (IDT, IA, USA). For FA-PT-A1 and hFA-PT-A1 each synthesized sequence was PCR-amplified and introduced into pFUSE-hIgG1 (InvivoGen, USA) vector using AgeI and XhoI restriction enzymes. For T17-C6-F7 each selected prothrombin, collagen and fibrinogen-binding scFv clone was PCR-amplified and introduced into the pFUSE-hIgG1 vector using EcoRI, NcoI, EcoRV, XbaI, HindIII and XhoI restriction enzymes. Each scFv was connected by tandem GGGGS linker. When necessary sequences encoding the pre-protrypsin signal peptide, twin-strep tag, or 2B8 tag were inserted. The resulting plasmids were transiently transfected into FreeStyleTM 293-F cell suspension cultures (Thermo Fisher Scientific) using linear poly(ethyleneimine) (MW 25,000, Polysciences). Ten days after transfection the clarified culture media were subjected to fast protein liquid chromatography using an NGC Quest10 Plus system (Bio-Rad) equipped with a 5 mL MabSelectSURE prepacked resin column (Cytiva). Bound proteins were eluted with PierceTM IgG elution buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), neutralised to pH 7.4, mixed with glycerol to a final concentration of 10%, and filtered through Spin-X centrifuge tube filters (Corning, USA). The purified proteins were quantified flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen, and stored at –80 °C for further use.

Phage display library screening

Immunoplate strips (SPL Life Science) were coated with 1 μg of the target protein and blocked with 3% bovine serum albumin (BSA) in Tris-buffered saline (TBS). A phage pool from a single-chain variable fragment library27 was added to the wells and incubated in the wells for 2 h. After washing with TBS containing 0.5% Tween-20 bound phages were eluted with 100 mM glycine-HCl (pH 2.2), immediately neutralised, and amplified by infecting E. coli strain ER2738 in the presence of VSCM13 helper phage overnight. The amplified phages were precipitated using polyethylene glycol and resuspended in TBS containing 1% BSA, and used as the input pool for subsequent rounds. To calculate the output/input ratio both the initial phage pool and eluted phages were titrated by counting the colony-forming units. For phage binding assays periplasmic fractions were prepared27 and applied to Immunoplate strips coated with the target protein. Bound scFvs tagged with an N-terminal HA tag, were detected using an anti-HA antibody conjugated with horseradish peroxidase (HRP), followed by reaction with tetramethylbenzidine substrate (Thermo Fisher Scientific).

Mouse liver laceration model

Male BALB/c mice (8 weeks old) were intravenously injected with anti-CD41 antibody (MWReg30, Biolegend; 0.75 mg/kg) to induce thrombocytopenia. After 24 h FA-PT-A1, T17-C6-F7 or hFA-PT-A1 was administered intravenously at a dose of 5 mg/kg. Thirty minutes later mice were anaesthetised with a constant flow of isoflurane. A 3-mm horizontal cut was made on the left liver lobe using a surgical blade (#20). Immediately after laceration a pre-weighed sterile cotton gauze was placed on the wound to absorb blood. At 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, 210, 240, 300, 360, 420, 480, 540 and 600 s, the gauze was removed and reweighed to quantify blood loss. For hFA-PT-A1 experiment at 10, 20, 30, 60, 90, 120, 150, 180, 210, 240, 300, 360, 420, 480, 540, 600, 660, 720, 780, 840, 900, 960, 1020, 1080, 1140 and 1200 s, the gauze was removed and reweighed to quantify blood loss. For topical treatment FA-PT-A1 or T17-C6-F7 was mixed with 1% (w/v) sodium alginate (Sigma-Aldrich) at a final concentration of 500 nM. Immediately after laceration 100 μL of the premixed haemostatic hydrogel was applied directly to the wound site. To initiate gelation 4 μL of 50 mM CaCl2 was added and mixed with the hydrogel. The liver laceration procedure was video-recorded and time-lapse images were extracted at 30-s intervals. Bleeding time was defined as the time point at which image subtraction from preceding frames showed no detectable difference. Platelet counts were measured using a haematology analyser (BC-2800 Vet, Mindray).

Mouse pulmonary microthrombi formation assay

Male mice were intravenously injected with saline mucin (0.5 mg/kg), FA-PT-A1 (5 mg/kg) or T17-C6-F7 (5 mg/kg). After euthanasia lungs were surgically harvested and fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde. Lungs were inflated by injecting 50% O.C.T compound (Tissue-Tek) in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) dehydrated in 30% sucrose, embedded in O.C.T compound, and stored at –80 °C. Cryosectioning was performed using a cryostat (CM-1850, Leica) at a thickness of 10 μm. Tissue sections were stained with goat anti-human IgG Fc (1:100; Invitrogen) and mouse anti-fibrinogen α-chain antibody (UC45, 1:100).

Mouse tail-vein laceration assay

Tails of anti CD41-induced thrombocytopenic mice were pre-soaked in a 37 °C water bath for 5 min placed on a tail laceration guidance bracket44, and cut at the vein using a surgical knife (blade #20) guided by the bracket’s horizontal groove. Wounded tails were immersed in pre-warmed saline to collect shed blood. Red blood cells were pelleted by centrifugation and lysed in 1 mL of Drabkin’s reagent (Sigma-Aldrich, USA). Each sample was diluted to 1:250 in Drabkin’s reagent incubated for 15 min, and analysed using a spectrometer. Total blood loss was calculated in haemoglobin (Hb) nmol units based on a standard curve generated using purified haemoglobin (Merck).

Mouse spontaneous intestinal bleeding model

Mice were intraperitoneally injected with carboplatin (Sigma-Aldrich) at a dose of 50 mg/kg administered twice at 24-h intervals, followed by intravenous injection of anti-CD41 antibody. Mice were then treated with hFA-PT-A1 (5 mg/kg) and housed in separate cages for 24 h. Faecal samples were collected and lysed by incubation in 20 mL of 0.53 M potassium hydroxide at 37 °C for 1 h. The lysates were centrifuged at 17,000 × g at 4 °C, and 5 μL of the supernatant was mixed with 95 μL of luminol solution (0.53 M KOH, 1.5% H2O2, 0.04% luminol). Chemiluminescence was measured immediately at 415 nm using a microplate reader (SpectraMax i3x, San Jose, CA, USA).

Surface plasmon resonance

SPR was performed on a Biacore T200 at 25 °C. Ligands were immobilised on a CM5 chip via amine coupling: the surface was activated with 1:1 0.4 M EDC/0.1 M NHS, followed by ligand injection (5 µL/min) in 10 mM sodium acetate (pH 4.5–5.5) to target RU, and blocked with 1 M ethanolamine-HCl (pH 8.5). A reference cell was prepared identically without the ligand. Binding assays utilised HBS-EP+ running buffer (10 mM HEPES, 150 mM NaCl, 3 mM EDTA, 0.05% P20, pH 7.4). Series of diluted analytes were injected at 30 µL/min for a 180 s association and 180 s dissociation phase. The surface was regenerated after each cycle using 10 mM glycine-HCl (pH 2.5) or 5 M NaCl for 30 s at 30 µL/min, followed by a 60 s stabilisation period. Sensorgrams were processed with Biacore T200 evaluation software using reference cell subtraction. Kinetic data were globally fitted to a 1:1 Langmuir model to determine ka, kd, and KD; steady-state affinity was calculated by plotting Req against concentration for experiments reaching equilibrium.

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM), unless otherwise noted. One-way and two-way analyses of variance (ANOVA), followed by Dunnett’s method for multiple comparisons, were used to compare experimental groups unless otherwise specified. A p-value less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant. Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (La Jolla, CA, USA).
