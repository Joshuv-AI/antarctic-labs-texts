Tunneled Hemodialysis Catheters: Six Current Questions
Authors: Zong Yi Chin, Bien Soo Tan, Nanda Venkatanarasimha, Kun Da Zhuang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis review examines six key questions about tunneled hemodialysis catheters: material selection, tip design, insertion site, optimal tip positioning, fibrin sheath management, and infection prevention. Most modern catheters use polyurethane-polycarbonate copolymers that resist material failure. Symmetric-tip designs reduce dysfunction rates compared to other designs. While the right internal jugular vein is preferred, patient-specific factors should guide insertion site selection. Left-sided catheters benefit from right atrial tip positioning, whereas right-sided catheter tip location is less critical. Fibrin sheaths commonly cause catheter dysfunction, and multiple treatment methods are available, with no clear single best approach. Infection risk is multifactorial, with evidence supporting targeted preventive measures. While catheter materials and designs continue to evolve, individualized management remains essential.Keywords: hemodialysis, tunneled catheter, vascular access, end-stage renal failure

Introduction

Tunneled hemodialysis catheters are widely used today by patients with end-stage renal failure, either as a bridge prior to creation of a more permanent dialysis access or as the preferred mode of long-term hemodialysis.

The evolution of hemodialysis vascular catheters began in 1961 when Shaldon et al. [1] first introduced percutaneous femoral vessel cannulation using polytetrafluoroethylene (TeflonⓇ) tubing for repeated hemodialysis. The development of the Brescia-Cimino-Appel-Hurwich radio-cephalic AVF in the mid to late 1960s, where a period of time was required for the AVF to mature before use, reinforced the need for catheter hemodialysis in the interim [2]. In 1969, subclavian vein catheterization for hemodialysis was described, with two single-lumen catheters inserted [3]. The introduction of dual-lumen catheters in the early 1980s marked another significant advancement [3], reducing vessel cannulation time by half and improving procedural efficiency. By the 1990s, dual-lumen catheters were being widely used as a viable long-term solution for patients in whom hemodialysis access could not be created [4].

The growing complexity of the dialysis population―characterized by advanced patient age, increased comorbidities such as peripheral vascular disease and diabetes, and delayed nephrology referrals―has led to an increasing dependence on tunneled hemodialysis catheters [5].

While many aspects of tunneled hemodialysis catheters are well established, advancements in materials and design continue, and several technical questions remain. This review addresses six key questions regarding tunneled hemodialysis catheters: (1) material composition; (2) tip design; (3) optimal insertion site and laterality; (4) distal tip positioning; (5) fibrin sheath management; and (6) catheter-related infections.

What Is the Ideal Material for a Hemodialysis Catheter?

A hemodialysis catheter must be durable yet flexible, resistant to infection and thrombosis, and capable of providing efficient dialysis. Catheter materials have transitioned from silicone to polyurethane, utilizing polyurethane's superior tensile strength to maximize internal lumen size without increasing the outer diameter [6]. This advancement has proved so effective that the polyurethane tubing itself rarely fails; instead, the external hubs and clamps have become the primary points of failure [7]. Currently, most venous catheters are made of polyurethane-polycarbonate copolymers of varying proportions, in which the polycarbonate component increases mechanical strength and chemical resistance [8, 9]. Recent research revealed the potential of these copolymers to inhibit biofilm formation [10]. However, proprietary industry policies often make it difficult to determine the exact material composition of specific commercially available catheters.

Enhanced collaboration between academic researchers and industry partners to systematically evaluate the distinct properties of various poly(carbonate)urethane copolymers could potentially accelerate material innovation and yield further clinical breakthroughs.

Surface modifications with antimicrobial/antithrombotic coatings have long been hypothesized to reduce thrombogenicity and infection risk. Current antimicrobial and antithrombotic surface modifications for hemodialysis catheters provide modest clinical benefit but are not definitive solutions. Heparin-based antithrombotic coatings and selected antimicrobial strategies (including antibiotic/antiseptic-impregnated catheters and antimicrobial barrier caps) can reduce catheter-related bloodstream infections (CRBSIs), with barrier caps showing the most consistent real-world benefit, while catheter patency remains largely unchanged. However, broader adoption is limited by antibiotic resistance, cytotoxicity, durability, and mixed hemodialysis-specific trial results. There also remains a notable paucity of robust cost-benefit and cost-effectiveness analyses to justify routine use [11, 12]. Consequently, current research is shifting toward antibiotic-sparing, non-leaching, and non-fouling multifunctional surfaces, including emerging technologies such as zwitterionic polymers, nanotechnology, and biogels. Although these currently remain in preclinical development, they represent promising areas for continued monitoring as they progress toward clinical application [13-15].

While polyurethane and its copolymer variants have established themselves as the material standard for modern hemodialysis catheters, it is envisaged that newer catheter materials and coatings will emerge. Given the current evidence, catheter care and adherence to clinical protocols continue to exert a greater and more consistent impact on clinical outcomes than catheter material alone.

What Is the Optimal Tip Design?

Three primary hemodialysis catheter tip designs dominate current practice: step-tip, split-tip, and symmetric (rounded) tip configurations (Figure 1) [16]. Meta-analyses from 2019 and 2024 directly compared these configurations, finding no significant differences in secondary patency, infection rates, CRBSI, or all-cause mortality; all three designs delivered adequate blood flow for hemodialysis [17, 18]. However, symmetric-tip catheters demonstrated significantly lower thrombotic event rates compared to step-tip and split-tip designs [18]. A large cohort study of 4,722 patients reinforced these performance differences, demonstrating that symmetric-tip catheters significantly outperformed step-tip catheters in preventing dysfunction requiring removal, while split-tip catheters show promise but need further study [19].

Based on this evidence, symmetric-tip catheters have become the preferred design due to reduced dysfunction rates. Recent head-to-head comparisons between different symmetric-tip catheter variants have not revealed significant differences in patency or dialysis efficiency, suggesting that the general tip design is more important than specific manufacturer variations [20, 21].

The evidence now supports preferential use of symmetric-tip designs where available, with split-tip as an acceptable alternative, and step-tip as the least desirable due to higher dysfunction risk and mechanical complications. The lack of significant differences between manufacturer variants of symmetrical tips suggests that there is currently no superior symmetric-tip design.

Which Is the Best Insertion Site?

The subclavian vein is traditionally avoided because of its significantly higher risk of central venous stenosis (42% compared to 10% for jugular approaches), which can compromise future vascular access options [22].

The right internal jugular vein is the preferred site for tunneled hemodialysis catheter placement due to its straight anatomical pathway to the superior vena cava and right atrium, which optimizes catheter function and minimizes central venous stenosis risk [23-25]. Recent evidence demonstrates that left-sided catheters can achieve comparable long-term performance when an optimal placement technique is employed [26, 27]. This suggests a patient-first approach to laterality, with consideration of the patient's anatomy, comorbidities, future management plans, and catheter care requirements, among other factors, rather than always prioritizing the right internal jugular vein.

When the internal jugular vein is unavailable, the external jugular vein serves as a viable alternative [28, 29], with one study even suggesting right external jugular access may be superior to left internal jugular placement [30]. Femoral vein catheters have shorter primary patency and higher thrombotic complications, but nearly similar infection rates compared to internal jugular catheters, and should be reserved for salvage situations in patients who have exhausted all other vascular access options [31-33]. Although the underlying cause remains unclear, this may be attributed to the femoral site's distance from the heart, positional flow disturbances from hip flexion, or patient-specific comorbidities in a population in which neck insertions are precluded.

Systematic review evidence also indicates that catheter laterality does not significantly impact AVF maturation rates when concurrent catheter and AVF creation is required, with vein-specific (e.g., size, quality of the vein) and patient-related factors proving more influential in access site selection [34]. A decade-long retrospective analysis has dispelled concerns about catheter placement near pacemakers or defibrillators, demonstrating that fears of device dislodgment, battery site complications, or venous obstruction are largely unfounded [35].

For patients who have exhausted conventional access routes, alternative approaches, including the translumbar approach (Figure 2) and the transhepatic approach, provide viable options [36-38]. When even these approaches fail, rarer alternatives, such as the transrenal approach through the renal parenchyma or surgical right atrial catheter placement via a right anterior mini-thoracotomy, may be considered [39-41]. More recently, sharp recanalization techniques such as the REcanalisation and Balloon-Oriented Puncture for Re-Insertion of Dialysis Catheter in Nonpatent Central Veins (REBORN) technique, utilizing transfemoral balloon-targeted puncture, and the Balloon-targeted Extra-anatomic Sharp recanalization (BEST) technique for nonpatent internal jugular veins, can facilitate access via the jugular approach [42-45].

The evolution of catheter insertion site selection has moved beyond the traditional dogma of mandatory right internal jugular preference toward a more nuanced, patient-centered approach that balances anatomical considerations, individual risk factors, and long-term access planning.

Where Is the Optimal Tip Position?

Optimal catheter tip positioning varies by access route and catheter design, with placement targeting the cavoatrial junction or mid-right atrium to maximize blood flow and minimize recirculation [23, 24]. In one study, mid to deep right atrial positioning of the tip for left-sided catheters reduced dysfunction and infection rates, while superior vena cava or peri-cavoatrial junction placement increased complication risk [23, 46]. Hence, for left-sided catheters, advancing the tip into the right atrium is recommended to increase longevity and function.

Conversely, another study suggested that right-sided step-tip catheters perform better with superior vena cava positioning compared to cavoatrial junction or deep right atrial placement [47]. Over-insertion, with the tip in the right atrium, carries risks of cardiac arrhythmias or, rarely, perforation [48].

Given that tip malposition represents a well-documented cause of early catheter failure and increased complications, post-procedure imaging verification through chest radiography or fluoroscopy remains essential to confirm appropriate tip location and prevent subsequent dysfunction [49]. The use of sonography has more recently been described as an adjunct to accurately identify the level of the right atrium and for final confirmation after insertion of the catheter [50]. It remains to be seen whether this can be widely adopted.

The evidence underscores that the tip of left-sided jugular tunneled hemodialysis catheters should be advanced into the right atrium, while for right-sided neck catheters, as the catheter pathway is straight, the tip position may not be as critical.

How do We Manage Fibrin Sheaths?

Fibrin sheath formation represents an inevitable complication of long-term central venous catheter placement, substantially contributing to catheter dysfunction and serving as a significant clinical challenge in vascular access management. This accounts for 13%-57% of hemodialysis catheter malfunctions, primarily manifesting as persistent withdrawal occlusion that compromises dialysis adequacy [51]. From the moment a catheter is inserted into a vein, the body's response to the foreign surface triggers deposition of fibrin and proteins along the catheter exterior. Within hours to days, a thin coating of fibrin and platelets (termed a “fibrin sleeve”) develops, classically originating at the insertion site or around the Dacron cuff and extending along the catheter, toward the tip. Although one study describes thrombosis and fibrin sheaths as different entities related to catheter dysfunction [52], there is likely significant overlap, as studies of retrieved fibrin sheath specimens have shown association with thrombus [53]. Evidence demonstrates that fibrin sheath development occurs in most catheters within 2-3 weeks of insertion, with autopsy studies revealing sheath presence in over 50% of chronic dialysis catheters. The fibrin sheath can interfere with dialysis flow and act as a scaffold for bacterial biofilm [52].

Prevention efforts focus on pharmacological and material interventions, with complete prevention remaining elusive. Lock solutions containing heparin, citrate, or low-dose urokinase can delay sheath formation but cannot eliminate its occurrence entirely [54, 55]. Similarly, specialized catheter designs incorporating heparin or hydrophilic polymer coatings may decelerate fibrin accumulation without providing complete protection against sheath development [56]. The routine scheduled replacement of tunneled hemodialysis catheters (e.g., exchanging them at fixed time intervals while still functioning and uninfected) is not supported by recent evidence or guidelines, and there is no literature advocating this as a preventive strategy. The 2024 nationwide cohort/target trial emulation, the REDUcing the burden of dialysis Catheter ComplicaTIOns (REDUCCTION) trial secondary analysis, found that in the context of mechanical failure of catheters, there was no significant difference in complication-free catheter survival (infection or dysfunction) between exchange and new-tunnel replacement over one to 12 months, supporting exchange as a reasonable strategy when a catheter is already failing rather than as routine prophylactic replacement [57].

Clinical suspicion for fibrin sheath formation should arise in patients presenting with recurrent catheter flow difficulties. Definitive diagnosis requires venographic evaluation, which characteristically reveals the pathognomonic “ghost" sheath appearance surrounding the catheter [58].

The evidence supporting definitive treatment of the sheath remains unclear. A retrospective study found that simple catheter exchange, balloon disruption of the fibrin sheath, and transfemoral fibrin sheath stripping were equivalent in terms of immediate technical success, complication rates, and durability of catheter function during follow-up [59]. Another study suggested that failed tunneled hemodialysis catheters treated with simple exchange were significantly more likely to remain patent for up to 4 months than those treated by means of fibrin sheath stripping [60]. Yet another study demonstrated superior patency with fibrin sheath stripping and thrombolytic infusion compared to catheter exchange, and an increased rate of infection after catheter exchange [61]. This supports the postulation that simple catheter exchange alone is not sufficient, because the fibrin sleeve can remain in place attached to the vein wall, impeding the new catheter's flow. If a catheter is removed and a fibrin sheath is left behind in the vein, that sheath can persist for months and even serve as a nidus for catheter dysfunction, infection, or central vein stenosis [62].

Various techniques have been developed to strip or obliterate the fibrin sheath:

•Thrombolytic instillation: Either alteplase or urokinase can be instilled into both ports, with both agents demonstrating excellent safety profiles and effectiveness for immediate restoration of catheter function [63, 64].

•Snare Stripping (Figure 3A-D): A gooseneck snare can be introduced via a transfemoral approach to latch onto the fibrin sheath at the cavoatrial junction and peel it off. This technique has a high success rate in restoring catheter function [60].

As there is no clear advantage of any single technique, fibrin sheath screening and treatment should be individualized based on patient characteristics, catheter status, and local cost considerations.

Are There Proven Catheter-Related Infection Prevention Strategies?

A recent comprehensive systematic review and meta-analysis evaluated risk factors for tunneled hemodialysis catheter infection, synthesizing data from 30 studies and 71 candidate variables, with quantitative meta-analysis performed for 26 factors [67]. Most identified risk factors were non-modifiable, including coronary artery disease (OR: 2.16), peripheral artery disease (OR: 2.28), and a prior history of sepsis (OR: 2.79). However, several clinically important modifiable risk factors were identified, notably diabetes mellitus (OR: 1.96), nasal Staphylococcus aureus carriage (OR: 10.32), methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus colonization (OR: 5.06), repeated dressing changes (OR: 3.14), catheter exchange over a guidewire (OR: 3.79), and prolonged catheter dwell time. Collectively, these findings support clinical strategies focused on optimizing glycemic control, screening for and decolonizing Staphylococcus aureus (particularly methicillin-resistant Staphylococcus aureus), minimizing catheter manipulation (including unnecessary dressing changes), and expediting transition to definitive surgical vascular access or kidney transplantation. One interesting recommendation the authors made, on the basis of a hypothesized risk of subclinical colonization of the prior catheter track, was that guidewire exchange of tunneled hemodialysis catheters should generally be avoided unless no alternative safe vascular access can be established. It was recommended that a new tunnel and exit site be created to reduce the likelihood of pathogen transfer from the pre-existing track. This point was supported by the REDUCCTION cohort study (2016-2020), which observed that 97% of 380 infected tunneled hemodialysis catheters were managed this way, reflecting standard practice due to concerns over persistent infection risk with guidewire exchange [57]. Similarly, the Kidney Disease Outcomes Quality Initiative guidelines recommend a case-by-case approach, but favor new-site replacement for severe infections [25].

A significant randomized, double-blind trial published in 2023 (LOCK-IT-100) evaluated the prophylactic use of taurolidine-heparin lock solution for the prevention of catheter-related infections [68]. In this phase three study involving 795 patients with hemodialysis catheters, a taurolidine/heparin lock solution (taurolidine 13.5 mg/mL, heparin 1,000 U/mL) was associated with a significantly lower incidence of CRBSI than heparin alone (0.13 vs 0.46 events per 1,000 catheter-days), corresponding to CRBSI rates of 2% versus 8%, respectively (p < 0.001). Safety profiles were similar between the two groups. When considered alongside an earlier randomized trial demonstrating cost savings with taurolidine-based catheter lock solutions [69], these findings provide compelling evidence that taurolidine-based catheter locks represent an effective and modifiable strategy for reducing tunneled hemodialysis catheter-related infections.

Collectively, these data suggest that tunneled hemodialysis catheter infection rates can be meaningfully reduced by adopting measures such as glycemic optimization, Staphylococcus aureus decolonization, early definitive access, using a new catheter track after removing infected catheters, and routine use of taurolidine-based lock solutions.

Conclusions

The evolution of tunneled hemodialysis catheters has transformed from rudimentary polytetrafluoroethylene tubings in 1961 to sophisticated polyurethane-based devices that have virtually eliminated material failure. This review has discussed six current questions related to these catheters. In summary, these devices will undoubtedly continue to evolve in material composition and design. Unless new designs are conceptually different, techniques for insertion are unlikely to change further. The site of catheter insertion and tip position should be tailored to the individual patient, with the jugular approach remaining the preferred route of insertion. There are a number of techniques described to manage fibrin sheaths, with no clear evidence of the superiority of any one technique. A multifactorial strategy addressing modifiable risks and using taurolidine lock solutions can significantly lower tunneled hemodialysis catheter-related infection rates. There remain opportunities for well-designed prospective randomized trials to definitively address some of the above questions discussed in this review.
