Launch of a dedicated After‐Hours Care Unit cultivates significant improvements in key areas required by the new Australian Medical Council National Framework for prevocational doctors
Authors: Catherine Lunter, Natalie McLean, Edward Berry, Cheryl Power, Aleks Atrens, Matthew Lumchee, Nigel Fellows, Georga Cooke, Jack Lockett
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPrevocational doctors face many challenges working in the after‐hours inpatient environment. We surveyed prevocational doctors after completing an after‐hours term as part of a new After‐Hours Care Unit (AHCU) who had previously worked after‐hours on an ad hoc basis under the ‘traditional’ after‐hours model of care. Significant improvements were reported in key requirements of the new Australian Medical Council National Framework for prevocational training including education, orientation and supervision, highlighting the advantages of establishing a dedicated AHCU on the prevocational doctor experience.

The Australian Medical Council (AMC) National Framework for prevocational medical training provides standards for accredited programmes and strengthens focus on doctor health and well‐being, supervision and access to formal education and orientation programmes, including out‐of‐hours terms. 1 The after‐hours clinical environment is fraught with challenges for prevocational doctors: reduced workforce, loss of team cohesion and decreased training opportunities. 2 It is also a risk factor for poorer staff health and well‐being, 3 with reduced access to social activities, higher workload and decreased supervision, 4 factors the Framework aims to address. Dedicated after‐hours teams may help mitigate these challenges, but there is limited research in this area, particularly in the Australian context.

In 2024, a new After‐Hours Care Unit (AHCU) was established at a public adult tertiary referral hospital in Brisbane, Queensland, which is responsible for the co‐ordination of medical care delivery and the training and support of prevocational doctors outside of standard business hours.

AHCU consists of leadership and support staff (Unit Director and Term Supervisor, Medical Education Registrar, and Administrative Coordinator) and clinical staff (Medical Registrars and prevocational doctors). AHCU works closely with an Intensive Care Unit (ICU) Outreach Service—experienced ICU nurses with critical care post‐graduate qualifications—who attend all Medical Emergency Team (MET) and Code Blue emergencies on the hospital wards (two‐tiered response system). AHCU doctors provide emergent and ongoing care to all 757 inpatient beds out of hours (medical, surgical, mental health), with minimum staffing including two junior medical officers (JMOs) on weekend day shifts, three on night shifts and five on evening shifts. They are supported by a dedicated medical registrar on the wards, with two additional medical registrars admitting patients from the emergency department who can assist on the wards if simultaneous MET calls or Code Blues occur. The AHCU works in collaboration with the on‐call speciality medical and surgical teams, escalating care as required after the initial medical review. A medical registrar and two JMOs attend MET calls with an ICU outreach nurse; Code Blue emergencies are also attended by an ICU senior registrar and a second ICU outreach nurse. The MET members are allocated at the start of the shift and receive a MET pager to notify them of MET calls and Code Blue emergencies. Weekday evening shifts commence with protected, multi‐modal education that involves the JMOs, registrars and ICU outreach nursing staff who are rostered on shift that evening with interdisciplinary facilitation. The sessions focus on relevant clinical topics, as well as the practical application of crisis‐resource management principles, including teamwork, communication and situational awareness. The new model of care enables a regular term orientation, individual start‐of‐term discussions and opportunities for completion of entrustable professional activity reviews. Staff attended an average of 364 MET calls and 19 Code Blue calls, and received 8340 task requests, via Medtasker (a mobile communication/task‐management platform), per month.

We invited all 42 JMOs who had completed an AHCU term between 22 January and 19 May 2024 to complete an anonymous evaluation survey comprised of Likert scale and free‐text response questions. The surveys were sent out on 22 May to the eligible JMOs. All participants had previously completed after‐hours evening ward‐call shifts at this hospital in 2023 as part of a ‘traditional’ after‐hours model of care. These shifts followed their regular day shift as rostered overtime on an ad hoc basis and did not include formal teaching sessions prior to the shift commencement. Survey questions covered themes of general after‐hours experience, well‐being, education and training opportunities. Prior to AHCU implementation, the Medical Education Unit collected term evaluation surveys from JMOs completing night ward‐call terms and after individual evening ward‐call shifts. Relevant questions were included in the new AHCU survey for comparison before and after AHCU implementation. Descriptive statistics are reported; Likert scales were analysed using the Mann–Whitney U test and categorical responses using χ 2 or Fisher exact test where appropriate. This project was exempt from full Human Research Ethics Committee review (EX/2024/QMS/106627).

Eighteen JMOs (43%) responded to the AHCU survey. Results were compared with 143 ward‐call evaluation surveys (7% response rate) and 36 nights term evaluation surveys (60% response rate) from 2023. The proportions of PGY3+ versus PGY2 between the AHCU and prior cohorts (χ 2 = 0.63835, df = 1, P = 0.42) were similar, ensuring valid comparisons. Significant improvements were seen in key components of prevocational training, including the ability to access rostered education sessions and career guidance (Table 1). The JMOs reported a better‐quality term orientation and constructive feedback, culminating in significantly more JMOs stating they would recommend this term to colleagues. Fewer well‐being issues were reported but did not reach statistical significance.

JMOs were more likely to seek help from other staff, including each other, compared with previously (P <0.001). Most respondents strongly agreed (71%) or agreed (21%) that a benefit of AHCU was feeling part of a team and, importantly, the unit promoted multidisciplinary collaboration (strongly agreed 50%, agreed 36%). No significant differences were perceived in workload (P = 0.225). Despite this, 100% of AHCU JMOs finished within 30 min of the rostered time vs 84.1% previously, suggesting improved workflow efficiency.

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first Australian report of the benefits of a dedicated unit on JMO experience of after‐hours ward work. Our findings demonstrate that the introduction of a dedicated AHCU resulted in significant improvements in key areas of the AMC National Framework for prevocational doctors working after hours, including access to education, orientation and careers advice and support. The reported increase in help‐seeking behaviours is likely a product of feeling part of a supportive and collaborative unit that promotes teamwork as key to safe patient care. Interprofessional learning, such as that delivered to the AHCU team, facilitates collaboration in patient care and competency development, while fostering a sense of relatedness/belonging. 5 Qualitative literature suggests that teamwork education programmes, specifically including high‐fidelity simulations, should be encouraged to all staff in acute hospital settings to foster a positive workplace culture and demonstrates benefits to participants. 6 Furthermore, a systematic review highlights the importance of a team environment, with higher reported teamwork ratings associated with improved occupational well‐being and lower stress. 7

The majority of JMOs have previously been found to agree that the processes needed to report concerns about the workplace should be more accessible. 8 The clearly defined governance and supervision provided by the AHCU enables a rapid identification and response to workload and well‐being issues, where previously there was no formal escalation policy for such concerns. AHCU staff have a physical presence at education sessions where issues could be raised, and JMOs have opportunities for more formal feedback of their AHCU experience or any identified worries they may have during individual scheduled meetings at the start and end of each term.

There were no additional major changes in clinical staffing levels, digital health tools or treatment protocols during the study time frame that could account for the reported improvements, and reported hospital activity measures were unchanged. Limitations include a moderate response rate to the AHCU survey and a lack of data regarding the impact on patient care and experience (measurable surrogates, such as MET call rates, are impacted by many external factors, likely to dilute any effect of AHCU). The comparison group prior to the implementation of AHCU consists of combined data from the night term evaluation surveys and ward‐call evaluation surveys, which were completed after stand‐alone after‐hours evening shifts. This may limit the comparability of the findings given that the ward‐call survey responses reflect on isolated shifts rather than a whole term of after‐hours work. Well‐being concerns trended lower with AHCU, especially fatigue, but the differences did not reach statistical significance (P = 0.301). This is a positive direction but limited by a small sample size. Further investigation with larger cohorts and in other settings is warranted.

A dedicated AHCU can improve the experience for prevocational doctors working after hours, and further qualitative research is recommended on how to optimise these outcomes and understand the translated benefits for patient care and junior doctor well‐being in the after‐hours environment.
