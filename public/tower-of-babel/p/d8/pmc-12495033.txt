Pseudoaneurysm of the mitral-aortic intervalvular fibrosa and mitral valve perforation as a complication of infective endocarditis: a case report and literature review
Authors: José Alejandro Claros Ruiz, José Ignacio Larrubia Valle, Ana María González González, Daniel Gaitán Román, Ricardo Vivancos Delgado, Luis Antonio Moreno-Ruiz, Jessica Bocardo, Diana Romero, Marianna Garcia-Saldivia, Deepti Ranganathan

Introduction

Pseudoaneurysm of the mitral-aortic intervalvular fibrosa (MAIVF) is an uncommon but potentially life-threatening complication, typically associated with infective endocarditis (IE) or valve surgery. This fibrous, avascular region between the anterior mitral leaflet and the non-coronary cusp of the aortic valve is structurally vulnerable to infection-related damage.1,2

Clinical presentation varies widely, and early diagnosis is essential. Echocardiography—especially transoesophageal and 3D imaging—plays a central role in diagnosis and surgical planning.2,3

We report a case of Staphylococcus epidermidis IE in a patient with a dual-chamber pacemaker, complicated by a pseudoaneurysm of the MAIVF and severe mitral regurgitation, requiring urgent surgery.

Case presentation

A 74-year-old man presented to the emergency department with intermittent atypical chest pain and chills for 3 days. He denied fever, dyspnoea, neurological symptoms, or other systemic complaints.

His medical history included hypertension, Type 2 diabetes, and dual-chamber pacemaker implantation 5 months prior for high-grade atrioventricular block.

He was alert and stable (blood pressure 140/75 mmHg, heart rate 113 b.p.m., SpO2 95%, temperature 37.0°C), with a systolic murmur (III/VI) at the mitral area and fine basal crackles. No peripheral stigmata of endocarditis were present. Initial NEWS-2 was 3 and SOFA was 0, indicating no systemic decompensation.

Laboratory results on admission showed normal renal function (creatinine 0.89 mg/dL), mildly elevated liver enzymes (ALT 47 U/L, AST 54 U/L), elevated C-reactive protein (CRP; 134 mg/L), mild normocytic anaemia (haemoglobin 11.4 g/dL), leucocytes 10.8 × 109/L with neutrophil predominance (79.7%), normal platelet count, and a slightly prolonged INR (1.28), with normal activated Partial Thromboplastin Time and fibrinogen levels. Arterial blood gas analysis showed pH 7.44, pCO2 38 mmHg, pO2 78 mmHg, HCO3− 26 mmol/L, and lactate 1.5 mmol/L.

Electrocardiogram showed ventricular pacing with capture (Figure 1). Chest X-ray was normal.

Twelve-lead electrocardiogram showing atrial-sensed, ventricular-paced rhythm. Pacemaker capture is evident, with pacing spikes followed by wide QRS complexes with a left bundle branch block pattern, consistent with right ventricular pacing.

Differential diagnosis included pulmonary embolism, pneumonia, and acute coronary syndrome, all of which may present with chest pain and non-specific systemic symptoms. Pulmonary embolism was ruled out based on normal D-dimer levels, absence of suggestive findings on chest X-ray, and lack of hypoxaemia. Pneumonia was deemed unlikely due to the absence of leucocytosis, fever, or focal infiltrates on imaging. Acute coronary syndrome was excluded after serial troponin measurements were normal and electrocardiogram showed no ischaemic changes. The presence of a new systolic murmur and pacemaker-associated risk led to early suspicion of IE.

Transthoracic echocardiography revealed preserved biventricular function (left ventricular ejection fraction 60%), a fibrocalcified mitral valve with a filiform mobile structure, and significant eccentric mitral regurgitation directed towards the interatrial septum; a fibrocalcified aortic valve was noted without significant dysfunction; no vegetation was clearly seen on transthoracic images, and pacemaker leads could not be reliably evaluated. Pulmonary artery systolic pressure was 30 mmHg, and valve gradients were normal.

Blood cultures were drawn, and no antibiotics were initially started. On Day 4 of admission, preliminary microbiological results showed gram-positive cocci in clusters in all blood culture sets; daptomycin 500 mg i.v. every 24 h (6 mg/kg/day) was started.

A transoesophageal echocardiography (TEE) was subsequently performed, revealing a subvalvular aortic structure compatible with a pseudoaneurysm of the MAIVF (Figure 2). Three-dimensional TEE demonstrated a perforation of the posterior mitral leaflet with a flail segment (Figure 3), resulting in severe eccentric mitral regurgitation with Coanda effect (Figure 4).

Later that same day, S. epidermidis was isolated in all six sets of blood cultures, confirming definite IE according to modified Duke criteria. Antibiotic therapy with daptomycin was maintained at the same dose, and the patient was evaluated for urgent surgery.

Surgical intervention was indicated due to the combination of severe mitral regurgitation with posterior leaflet destruction, and subaortic pseudoaneurysm contiguous with the aortic annulus, despite only mild aortic regurgitation.

Shortly before surgery, the patient developed acute pulmonary oedema (Figure 5) requiring non-invasive ventilation and ICU admission. Laboratory tests revealed severe acute kidney injury (creatinine 2.49 mg/dL), hepatic cytolysis (AST 1217 IU/L, ALT 792 IU/L), hyperlactataemia (lactic acid 9.1 mmol/L), elevated inflammatory markers (CRP 183 mg/L, procalcitonin 1.39 ng/mL), and metabolic acidosis (pH 7.17, bicarbonate 16.3 mmol/L). Neurologically, his Glasgow dropped to 10. The SOFA score rose to 7, and the NEWS-2 to 10, reflecting imminent risk of multi-organ failure and high short-term mortality.

Portable chest radiograph showing a pacemaker device in the left pectoral region, with leads in situ. The lung fields exhibit signs of mild pulmonary congestion and bilateral interstitial infiltrates suggestive of heart failure. No evidence of focal consolidation or pleural effusion.

Urgent surgery revealed extensive mitral valve destruction and septal involvement, prompting double valve replacement. Postoperatively, the patient developed refractory biventricular dysfunction. ECMO was considered but contraindicated due to haemorrhagic shock, and he died shortly after surgery.

Histopathological analysis confirmed active IE in both valves.

Although no vegetation was found on the pacemaker leads, their proximity to the infected valves and absence of other foci suggest a possible device-related origin of the infection, supported by the temporal relationship between implantation and clinical presentation.

Discussion

Pseudoaneurysm of the MAIVF is a rare but serious complication, often associated with IE or previous cardiac surgery. This fibrous and avascular structure is anatomically located between the anterior mitral leaflet and the non-coronary cusp of the aortic valve, making it vulnerable to infectious and mechanical injuries.1,2

IE involving the aortic or mitral valve may result in local tissue destruction, leading to abscess formation or pseudoaneurysm, both associated with high morbidity and mortality, especially when diagnosis or surgery is delayed. Diagnosis may be challenging, particularly with indwelling devices such as pacemakers and non-specific clinical signs, as in our patient.3

Clinical manifestations are variable—ranging from asymptomatic to heart failure, embolism, or tamponade.4 In this case, the pseudoaneurysm was an incidental finding during echocardiographic evaluation for atypical chest pain, followed by acute pulmonary oedema prompting emergency surgery.

Imaging is key for diagnosis. Transthoracic echocardiography has low sensitivity for MAIVF pseudoaneurysm (43%), whereas TEE reaches up to 90%, and 3D echocardiography provides additional spatial and anatomical detail.2,5 In our patient, 3D TEE allowed precise identification of the pseudoaneurysm and mitral leaflet perforation, guiding surgical planning.

Although rare cases of conservative management with long-term stability have been reported in selected asymptomatic patients,6 urgent surgery is usually required due to the high risk of rupture and haemodynamic compromise, as occurred in our case. Long-term prognosis depends on early diagnosis and timely surgery. Delayed complications, such as haemolytic anaemia years after repair7 or catastrophic rupture with bleeding,8 underline the need for close follow-up and early intervention.

Although no vegetation was seen on pacemaker leads and extracted lead cultures were negative, the proximity of the pseudoaneurysm and mitral valve damage suggests possible device-related endocarditis. The lack of other infection sources and recent device implantation support this. Device-related IE may occur without vegetations or positive cultures, especially with S. epidermidis due to its biofilm capacity. ESC guidelines allow for device removal based on clinical suspicion alone. This case underscores the need to consider device-related infection in atypical or complex IE presentations9–11

This case illustrates the value of multimodal imaging—especially 3D TEE—in diagnosing complex IE complications. While preoperative computed tomography could have added anatomical detail, echocardiography proved essential for diagnosis and surgical planning. The case also emphasizes the need for vigilance in patients with cardiac devices and atypical symptoms.

Conclusions

Pseudoaneurysm of the MAIVF is a rare but potentially life-threatening complication of IE. Early detection through transoesophageal and 3D echocardiography allows accurate diagnosis and guides surgical planning. This case underscores the importance of timely imaging and multidisciplinary management in complex cases of endocarditis.
