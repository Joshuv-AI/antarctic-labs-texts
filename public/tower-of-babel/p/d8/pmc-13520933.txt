Dogs evaluated in a home environment show decreased arterial blood pressure and stress levels
Authors: Fabiana Silva Fádel Queiroz, Felipe Gaia de Sousa, Samuel Andrade Faria, Cláudio Roberto Scabelo Mattoso, Júlio César Cambraia Veado, Jonathan de Souza Matias, Suzane Lilian Beier
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStress may influence blood pressure (BP), making home-based measurements potentially more reliable.Hypothesis/ObjectivesCompare BP, stress, and clinical laboratory results obtained from dogs in 2 environments.AnimalsForty client-owned healthy dogs.MethodsA crossover observational study evaluated 40 healthy dogs after acclimatization in hospital and home settings. The BP was measured using Doppler and oscillometry, and clinical laboratory and stress-related variables were recorded. Blood and urine samples, including for cortisol determination, were collected. One urine sample was collected by the owner without veterinary intervention (referred to as PAZ urine). Statistical hypothesis testing compared measurements between settings, and multiple regression analysis was used to estimate the associations between physiological, biochemical, and stress-related variables on urinary cortisol concentrations.ResultsThe dogs were adults, with most being female and mixed-breed. Stress scores and Doppler BP results were higher in the hospital environment (P < .001). Hospital BP results exceeded home measurements by 46 mmHg in normotensive and 48 mmHg in prehypertensive, hypertensive, and severely hypertensive groups. Most of the laboratory variables assessed, including serum and urinary cortisol concentrations, also were increased in the hospital setting (P < .001). Regression analysis indicated that urinary cortisol concentration was negatively associated with systolic BP and the urine protein-to-creatinine ratio, and positively associated with proteinuria, urine creatinine concentration, and urine cortisol-to-creatinine ratio (P < .001). Dogs evaluated in the hospital excreted an additional 135.61 units of cortisol (P < .001).Conclusions and clinical importanceThe hospital setting can influence BP, clinical laboratory results, and stress-related variables.Keywords: blood pressure measurement, cortisol, stress factors, systemic arterial hypertension

Introduction

Systemic arterial hypertension (SAH) is characterized by prolonged increase in blood pressure (BP) above reference values, affecting quality of life and life expectancy.1–4 The SAH can be classified as situational (stress- and catecholamine-mediated), secondary (underlying diseases), or idiopathic (no identifiable cause), with situational SAH the most common form.1,5–9 Stress-induced catecholamine release is mediated by activation of the hypothalamic–pituitary–adrenal (HPA) axis.3,5,10

Veterinary visits expose dogs to multiple stressors, including unfamiliar environments and interactions with people and other animals, which may affect procedures such as BP measurement.1,3,6,8,10–14 Although the complete absence of fear during veterinary visits is unlikely, “fear-free” practices should be implemented whenever possible.15 Stress can increase BP, compromising diagnostic accuracy, and BP is often overlooked or measured incorrectly, potentially leading to misdiagnosis and unnecessary treatment.7,9 Therefore, home BP monitoring is recommended because it is associated with lower BP results than hospital assessments.1,3,6,9,11,16

We hypothesized that: (1) BP and clinical and laboratory variables will differ between hospital and home environments; (2) stress will significantly influence BP measurements; and (3) home-based evaluations will provide reliable information for diagnostic and therapeutic decision-making. Therefore, we aimed to: (1) compare BP, stress, and clinical and laboratory variables in dogs assessed in hospital and home environments; and (2) investigate associations between stress-related biomarkers and clinical variables using multivariable analysis.

Materials and methods

Ethics and study design

Our prospective crossover observational study was approved by the Animal Use Ethics Committee of the Federal University of Minas Gerais under protocol number 192/2024. All procedures in the study were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles established by the National Research Council, in full compliance with its recommended guidelines for the care and use of dogs.

Animals

Forty dogs of both sexes, various breeds, and ages were selected randomly from a voluntary population for the study. Dogs were recruited from owners in Belo Horizonte, Minas Gerais/Brazil and its metropolitan region. The inclusion criteria were as follows: dogs of any breed and sex; aged ≥1 year; amenable to handling; and free from diagnosed comorbidities, including diabetes mellitus, cardiac, or renal diseases. To exclude comorbidities, all dogs underwent a screening evaluation including laboratory testing (CBC and serum biochemistry profile, including markers of renal and hepatic function), complete abdominal ultrasonography, urinalysis, cardiac assessment (echocardiography with Doppler and ECG), and thoracic radiography. Of the 40 dogs initially enrolled, only 2 had incomplete evaluations. The reasons were: (1) one female dog died after the first evaluation stage (hospital environment), and (2) one dog was withdrawn from the study during the hospital evaluation because of aggressive behavior. Therefore, 40 dogs were included in the hospital-based and 38 in the home-based analysis.

The initial exclusion criteria included aggressive or irritable animals; dogs receiving treatment for SAH; animals without appropriate medical supervision; and those receiving corticosteroids or other medications that could interfere with hepatic, renal, or cardiovascular function. Dogs receiving medications known to influence stress responses, behavioral state, or autonomic nervous system activity were excluded. Dogs exhibiting manifestations of zoonoses such as dermatophytosis, were also excluded. After initial selection, any dogs with clinical or laboratory abnormalities, or other outcomes such as death, were excluded.

During the enrollment process, owners were informed about the objectives and procedures of the study, including potential risks, and for ethical reasons, provided written informed consent. Owners participated in all stages of the study in both environments. All dogs were handled by qualified professionals employing low-stress animal welfare practices to minimize stress exposure.

Sample size

Sample size was estimated a priori using the formula n = (Zα/2 ·δ/E),2 where n, sample size; Zα/2, critical value for the desired confidence level (95%); δ, population SD of the variable, and E, standard error usually 5% of the mean.17 The SD and mean were derived from variable previously reported hospital systolic BP measurements.11 A 95% confidence level and an allowable error of 5% of the mean were adopted; the estimated sample size was 40 dogs. Each dog served as its own control, increasing statistical power and decreasing variability.

Procedures

Environments acclimatization

The acclimatization process in the hospital environment was conducted in a room (20 m2) specifically prepared for each animal upon arrival. Dogs were acclimated for 15 min in a room with controlled lighting, temperature, and noise before clinical evaluation, in the presence of their owners. In the home environment, acclimatization lasted a similar duration upon arrival of the veterinary professional, with procedures performed at a location chosen by the owners. During all evaluations, no canine pheromone analogues were used. Low-stress handling techniques were employed, including gentle restraint, minimization of environmental stimuli, and avoidance of raised voices before procedures.

Behavioral and stress assessment

Behavior and stress levels were assessed using a published behavioral stress scale.15 The scale classified animals into 5 categories (0-5) based on body posture, gaze, breathing, behavior, and vocalization. Animals were assigned to the category that best reflected their observed behavior after acclimatization. The behavioral assessment aimed to obtain more reliable measurements of the variables of interest and to evaluate the extent to which stress influenced the collected data. Assessments were conducted in hospital and home environments by 2 experienced veterinary observers.

Blood pressure measurement

For BP measurement, 7 readings were taken from the left thoracic limb, and the mean of these readings was used for analysis. Dogs were evaluated in both right lateral recumbency (recommended method1) and sitting (alternative method) positions using both BP devices. The sitting position was defined as the dog supporting its pelvic limbs and gluteal region on the ground, with the left thoracic limb extended vertically and the right thoracic limb horizontally positioned, maintaining an upright and stable posture. Measurements were performed by an experienced veterinarian using the BPScan oscillometric device (InPulse Animal Health®, Florianópolis, Brazil) and the Vascular Doppler 812B (Parks Medical, Beaverton, USA), excluding the first and last readings. Blood pressure was classified according to the American College of Veterinary Internal Medicine (ACVIM) reference values.1 All measurements were performed in a quiet environment, and headphones were used to minimize auditory stress. At home, BP measurements followed the same protocol as in the hospital environment, using both positions. Measurements were performed with the assistance of the owner, at the location chosen as the animal’s preferred resting spot.

Clinical evaluation

The clinical evaluation was conducted by a specialized and trained veterinarian. In both environments, animals underwent clinical evaluation by a qualified veterinarian to assess systemic alterations. Data were recorded on a standardized physical examination form and subsequently entered into an electronic database. At home, evaluations were conducted using the same standardized protocol, with blankets and in the presence of the owner.

Laboratory evaluation

Blood samples for hematological and biochemical analyses to assess health status were collected in both environments by a specialist veterinarian. Samples were obtained by jugular venipuncture using a 10-mL disposable syringe (Descarpack®, Luer Lock, Santa Catarina, Brazil) and an appropriately-sized needle. Collected samples were stored in 5-mL vacutainer tubes (BD Vacutainer®), as follows: tube containing EDTA (hematological analysis), tube with clot activator (biochemical analysis) and a tube containing sodium fluoride (glucose analysis). The laboratory evaluation was conducted by a specialized and trained veterinarian in a veterinary diagnostic laboratory. Blood analyses were interpreted based on reference values.18,19

For urinalysis, urine samples in female dogs were collected by ultrasound-guided cystocentesis using ultrasound systems (Vivid I/Logiq E; GE; United States). A 10-mL disposable syringe and a 25 × 0.70 mm needle (Descarpack®, Luer Lock, Santa Catarina, Brazil) were used for sample collection. In male dogs, urine was collected by urethral catheterization, and the catheter was inserted into the penile urethra and guided into the bladder. Urine samples were collected by a specialist veterinarian. Collected urine samples were placed in 10-mL syringes, refrigerated, and transported to the laboratory for processing within 30 min. Samples were subdivided for urinary cortisol measurement and standard urinalysis. Physical, chemical, and sediment analyses were performed by a specialized and trained veterinarian, following the methods and reference values described.18–20

To assess the impact of stress in blood and urine samples, serum, and urinary cortisol concentrations were measured. Serum cortisol analysis was performed using the same blood sample collected for hematological and serum biochemical analyses. Samples were processed by a specialist veterinarian and stored in EDTA-containing vacuum tubes (5 mL, BD Vacutainer®). Urinary cortisol was measured from collected urine, which was centrifuged and transferred into microtubes for analysis. Both serum and urine samples were frozen and sent to an external laboratory for analysis by a specialized and trained veterinarian. With the aim of evaluating the influence of cortisol in urine samples collected by owners and comparing them with samples obtained in hospital and home environments, owners were instructed to collect these samples without veterinary intervention during the collection process; these samples were defined as PAZ urine. The urine was collected by spontaneous voiding after the home evaluation and sampling stage. Serum cortisol concentrations were measured by radioimmunoassay, whereas urinary cortisol, including samples obtained through owner-assisted collection (PAZ urine), was assessed using the same immunoassay.18

Data analyses

Statistical analyses were performed to compare physiological differences between environments and identify predictors of urinary cortisol concentration. Normality was assessed using the Shapiro–Wilk test, and paired t-tests were used to compare home and hospital conditions within a repeated-measures design. A 1-tailed hypothesis was adopted based on the expectation of higher physiological results in the hospital environment, and no correction for multiple comparisons was applied to preserve exploratory sensitivity.

A multiple linear regression model was used to evaluate the influence of clinical and environmental variables on urinary cortisol (y1),21 including oscillometric BP (x₁), Doppler BP (x₂), UPCR (x₃), proteinuria (x₄), glucose (x5), urinary creatinine concentration (x6), UCCR (x7), serum cortisol concentration (x8), stress score (x9), heart rate (x10), and leukocyte count (x11). The environment was included as a fixed-effect variable (d; 0 = home, 1 = hospital), allowing adjustment for repeated measures.

Multicollinearity was assessed using variance inflation factor (VIF < 5), and variable selection was performed using backward elimination (P < .20 to P < .05). Model assumptions were evaluated using residual analysis and the Shapiro–Wilk test. Dogs were categorized into BP strata according to established guidelines, and differences between environments were analyzed.

Results

Demographical data

After the experimental phase, 40 dogs were selected, comprising 62.5% females (n = 25/40) and 37.5% males (n = 15/40). However, at the end of the experiment, 2 dogs were excluded because of the absence of complete data for both clinical evaluations. The mean age of the dogs was 82.5 ± 53.6 months (6.9 ± 4.5 years), and the mean body weight was 16.1 ± 10.3 kg. Mixed-breed dogs were the most frequent (52.5%; n = 21/40), followed by Shih Tzu (10%; n = 4/40), Border Collie (7.5%; n = 3/40), Poodle (5%; n = 2/40), and American Staffordshire Terrier (5%; n = 2/40). The remaining breeds were represented by 1 dog each (20%; n = 8/40; Table 1). By the end of the initial phase, 38 dogs had completed the experimental protocol. Approximately 15 days after the hospital phase, all animals were clinically healthy based on clinical and laboratory evaluation.

Behavioral and stress assessment, BP measurement, and clinical-laboratory evaluation

A 1-tailed hypothesis test for mean differences identified a significant difference in stress scale scores between environments (P < .001; Table 2). The behavioral stress assessment (Table 2), indicated a significant shift in variables between environments (n = 38). Behavioral stress assessment (Table 2) identified significantly lower stress scores in the home environment (0.65 ± 0.53; median, 1; range, 0-2) compared with the hospital environment (2.18 ± 0.89; median, 2; range, 1-4). The magnitude of this difference corresponded to a very large effect size (Rank-biserial correlation = 0.817; P < .001). For SAP measured by vascular Doppler in the sitting position, a significant difference between environments also was observed (P < .001), with a higher mean value in the hospital setting (157.3 ± 27.9 mmHg) compared to the home (134.0 ± 21.0 mmHg; Table 2). This finding indicates that the hospital mean was significantly higher than the home mean. In contrast, oscillometric SAP measured in the sitting position did not differ significantly between hospital (137.9 ± 22.7 mmHg) and home (135.2 ± 23.3 mmHg; P = .15; Table 2).

From a hematological perspective, comparison of total leukocyte counts between the hospital and home environments indicated that the hospital mean was significantly higher than the home mean (P < .001). Neutrophils (P < .001), lymphocytes (P < .001), and monocytes (P = .02) also were increased in the hospital, whereas eosinophils (P = .80), glucose (P = .97), and platelet counts (P = .90) did not differ significantly between environments (Table 2).

Urinary protein and creatinine concentrations were evaluated individually to aid interpretation of urinary ratios, particularly the UCCR, rather than for clinical assessment of proteinuria. Urinary protein concentration was significantly higher in the hospital environment than at home (P < .001), whereas urinary creatinine concentration did not differ significantly between environments (P = .58). Despite this, no significant difference was observed in UPCR results (P = .44), suggesting the absence of clinically relevant proteinuria. In contrast, UCCR results were significantly higher in the hospital environment (P < .001; Table 2).

Serum and urinary cortisol concentrations differed significantly between hospital and home settings (P = .02 and P < .001, respectively; Table 2). No significant difference was observed between urinary cortisol concentrations measured in home-collected samples and those obtained from owner-collected samples (PAZ urine; P = .57). In contrast, hospital urinary cortisol concentration was significantly higher than PAZ urine cortisol concentration (P < .001; Table 3).

Multiple regression

The multivariable regression analysis (Table 5) identified the main clinical and environmental determinants of urinary cortisol concentrations using both a full and a restricted model. Joint Wald testing supported the simultaneous exclusion of non-contributory variables (oscillometric BP—×1, glucose—×5, serum cortisol—×8, stress score—×9, heart rate—×10, and leukocyte count—×11; F = 0.46, P = .83), resulting in a more parsimonious final model without loss of explanatory power. In the restricted model, the environmental dummy variable remained a significant independent predictor of urinary cortisol concentration (β = 135.61; 95% CI, 12.08-259.14; P = .03), suggesting higher cortisol excretion in the hospital environment after adjustment for physiological covariates.

Among the clinical predictors retained in the final model, Doppler SAP (×2) showed a modest inverse association with urinary cortisol concentration (β = −2.13; 95% CI, −4.22 to −0.05; P = .04), suggesting that residual variation in BP exerts only a limited influence once environmental exposure is accounted for. Urinary variables included in the final model (UPCR [×3], proteinuria [×4], urinary creatinine [×6], and UCCR [×7]), remained significantly associated with urinary cortisol concentrations (all P < .001) and were retained in the final model.

Overall, the restricted model showed good fit (R2 = 0.86; adjusted R2 = 0.84) and a significant global F statistic (P < .001), indicating that exclusion of non-significant predictors did not compromise model performance. These findings support the robustness of the final model and the independent effect of environmental exposure on urinary cortisol variation.

Discussion

Our study demonstrates that environmental context significantly affects physiological and behavioral variables in clinically healthy dogs. Overall, hospital-based evaluations were consistently associated with higher stress scores, increased SAP measured by vascular Doppler, increased leukocyte and differential cell counts, and higher serum and urinary cortisol concentrations when compared with home-based assessments. Classification of the dogs according to the ACVIM Consensus1 indicated a clear increase in dogs classified as truly normotensive in the home environment, along with a decrease in those misclassified in the hospital setting as prehypertensive, hypertensive, or severely hypertensive. These dogs would have been misclassified based on BP results if only hospital-based measurements had been considered.

Some clinical and laboratory variables appear resilient to environmental stress, maintaining stability across hospital and home settings. Variables such as oscillometric BP, HR, glucose, platelet count, urinary creatinine concentration, and UPCR did not show differences between environments. In addition, urinary cortisol concentrations obtained at home did not differ from those obtained by PAZ sampling, supporting the feasibility and consistency of owner-assisted sampling under appropriate guidance. Overall, these findings suggest an effect of the hospital environment and the use of low-stress settings and alternative sampling strategies to improve clinical and laboratory assessment interpretability.

Behavioral stress levels were consistently lower in dogs evaluated at home compared with the hospital environment when assessed using the same visual stress scale. Although dogs evaluated at home remained predominantly within the relaxed/neutral and low stress categories, those assessed in the hospital environment more frequently reached mild, moderate, and high stress classifications. This finding suggests that the hospital environment may act as a substantial stressor capable of inducing pronounced behavioral manifestations of anxiety and fear.

The higher stress levels observed in the hospital environment were likely related to unfamiliar surroundings, exposure to strangers or other animals, and human–animal interactions, all recognized stressors in dogs.22,23 Previous studies have emphasized the need for systematic monitoring and careful management to identify behavioral changes and adapt handling protocols.22 In contrast, familiar and enriched environments with adequate space and socialization are associated with lower stress levels, which may explain the lower scores observed at home.24

Blood pressure is strongly influenced by the evaluation environment, with hospital measurements consistently higher than home assessments. This pattern was observed across all body positions using vascular Doppler, and measurements obtained in sitting and right lateral recumbency were comparable, suggesting that either position is acceptable. The increased hospital BP likely reflects stress-induced sympathetic activation. According to the ACVIM Consensus,1 a higher proportion of dogs were classified as normotensive at home, whereas dogs categorized as prehypertensive or hypertensive in the hospital environment frequently were reclassified as normotensive during home assessment. These results emphasize the risk of misdiagnosis if relying solely on hospital BP measurements and underscore the need for caution in clinical interpretation.8,11

Stress-related glucocorticoid release contributes to transient increases in BP, highlighting the impact of stress in dogs.25 In our study, SAP results measured in the home environment were 46 mmHg lower in dogs previously classified as normotensive and 48 mmHg lower in dogs classified as prehypertensive, hypertensive, or severely hypertensive. Similar findings were reported, with differences in SAP of +18.4 mmHg11 and +27.7 mmHg6 between hospital and home settings. These results are consistent with stress-related BP increases in clinical environments, potentially because of white coat effect and other stress-related mechanisms, leading to increased sympathetic activity and possible misclassification of hypertension.3,9,26–28 Increased BP in hospital settings thus may represent situational SAH (eg, white coat effect), which should be carefully considered before diagnosis and initiating antihypertensive treatment.1,3,6,7

Hematological variables show environment-related variations, suggesting that stress can influence leukocyte counts even within normal reference ranges. Total leukocyte and neutrophil were lower in the home environment, suggesting decreased stress-induced leukocyte redistribution. Stress-related hematological changes have been reported in both dogs and cats; however, evidence in dogs remains inconsistent.29–33 All results remained within species reference ranges but the observed fluctuations and differences between environments may suggest a stress leukogram. Stress leukogram does not require results outside of the reference limits, because increases relative to an individual’s baseline may indicate stress even within the reference range. These findings highlight the influence of the environment on biological sample results and reinforce the importance of interpreting these results in the context of the patient’s clinical condition.

Serum cortisol concentrations reflect environmental stress, with higher concentrations observed in hospital settings. In our study, lower serum cortisol concentrations at home suggest decreased activation of the HPA axis in a familiar environment. In contrast, hospital assessments induced a nearly 2.5-fold increase, consistent with a clinically relevant stress response associated with handling, unfamiliar surroundings, and restraint. Variability in hospital cortisol concentrations may reflect individual differences in stress reactivity, temperament, and prior experiences. Cortisol is a well-established stress biomarker, but concentrations may vary according to circadian rhythms, behavioral traits, and stress induced by blood sampling itself.10,34–36 Although salivary samples offer a less invasive method for acute stress assessment, their routine use is limited by factors such as dehydration, aggressive behavior, and poor correlation with serum cortisol concentrations.10,37–40

Urinary cortisol serves as a noninvasive biomarker of environmental stress, complementing serum cortisol concentration and providing insight into chronic stress responses.10,41,42 In our study, urinary cortisol concentrations were higher in dogs evaluated in the hospital compared with home assessments, including owner-collected PAZ samples, suggesting the influence of environmental stress on HPA axis activation. Lower concentrations in owner-collected samples demonstrate that urine collection by owners is feasible when appropriate guidance is provided. However, urinary cortisol measurements are influenced by urine concentration variability, analytical variation, and biological factors such as age, breed, developmental stage, and genetic differences in stress reactivity.34,43–46 Despite these limitations, urinary cortisol remains an integrative biomarker when interpreted alongside clinical and laboratory data.46

Regression analysis was used to explore associations between urinary cortisol concentrations and clinical laboratory variables, reinforcing its role as an integrative stress biomarker. Associations were observed between urinary cortisol concentration, clinical laboratory measurements, and stress indicators. Interestingly, SAP measured by Doppler was associated with a slight decrease in urinary cortisol concentrations, which contrasts with findings previously reported.47,48 The observation of animals with increased Doppler SAP alongside decreased urinary cortisol concentration supports the hypothesis of white coat syndrome, characterized by transient BP increase triggered by acute stress in clinical settings.6,49 This finding is relevant in the absence of laboratory or clinical evidence of endocrine disorders such as hypercortisolism or renal disease that could explain secondary hypertension.10,50

The negative association between UPCR and urinary cortisol concentration in clinically healthy animals should be interpreted cautiously, because it may reflect variability inherent to urinary biomarkers rather than renal dysfunction. Given that all animals were clinically healthy and screened for renal disease, this finding is unlikely to indicate renal dysfunction, and instead may reflect statistical interactions or natural variability of urinary biomarkers,10,51 even under stress and increased serum cortisol concentration. Positive associations between urinary protein, creatinine, and cortisol concentrations suggest concurrent changes in urine concentration rather than intrinsic renal alterations. Higher urinary protein and creatinine concentrations were positively associated with urinary cortisol concentrations, suggesting parallel increases in urinary analytes under stress-related conditions rather than changes in kidney function.

The UCCR corrects for urine dilution and provides a more robust measure of cortisol excretion, minimizing the impact of variable urine concentration. Variations in urinary protein and creatinine concentrations can be influenced by physiological factors affecting urine concentration, which in turn may affect analyte measurements.52 Consequently, higher urinary concentrations of protein and creatinine, and thus higher UCCR, may be associated with increased urinary cortisol concentrations and may reflect stress-related changes rather than intrinsic renal dysfunction. The UCCR exhibits minimal diurnal fluctuation and can be considered a reliable laboratory measure, particularly for dogs with endocrine disorders.21 It primarily reflects total urinary cortisol and its metabolites rather than biologically active cortisol.21

Differences in urinary protein concentrations between hospital and home environments appear to be driven primarily by urine concentration rather than clinically relevant proteinuria. Although absolute urinary protein concentrations varied between environments, the absence of differences in UPCR suggests that these changes reflect urine dilution rather than true alterations in protein excretion. Because UPCR corrects for urinary dilution, it remains a more reliable indicator of clinically relevant proteinuria. Previous studies have reported increased proteinuria in hospital settings, potentially related to stress and environmental factors affecting urinary measurements.53 However, we did not find evidence of a clinically relevant effect of environmental stress on proteinuria in healthy dogs.

In contrast, differences in UCCR between hospital and home environments may be influenced by environmental stressors during clinical evaluation. Similar findings were reported, with higher stress scores in hospital settings and approximately 22% (n = 8/36) of dogs being borderline proteinuric, whereas 19.4% (n = 7/36) exhibited increased UCCR in the hospital.51 Increased UCCR persisted particularly in borderline proteinuric dogs. Emotional stress has also been associated with transient changes in urinary protein excretion in humans. Additionally, UCCR has been highlighted as a noninvasive indicator of stress and welfare, especially in kennel environments.54 These findings suggest that although absolute urinary analyte concentrations vary with sampling conditions, UCCR, by accounting for urine dilution, may be a more reliable marker of stress-related physiological changes in dogs.

Home-based assessments minimize stress in dogs, but practical, financial, and logistical challenges may limit their routine implementation. Unlike the controlled hospital environment, in-home evaluations occur under variable conditions in which external stimuli, space limitations, and clinician presence can influence measurements and animal behavior. Successful implementation also depends on owner cooperation, proper handling, and adherence to instructions, which may introduce variability. Despite these challenges, animals are generally more relaxed in familiar surroundings, resulting in decreased stress-related physiological responses and more representative measurements. Strategies such as acclimation periods, low-stress handling, repeated measurements, and owner-assisted sample collection may help approximate home conditions, improving reliability and minimizing stress-related bias during hospital assessments.

In many clinical settings, including referral centers and university hospitals, in-home evaluations are not routinely feasible, and variability in measurement techniques across clinicians may further affect data reliability. Additional constraints, such as time limitations, resource availability, and case load, may also restrict the adoption of home-based protocols in routine practice. Therefore, implementing standardized strategies to minimize stress-related bias in hospital environments, including consistent handling and sampling procedures, are essential to improve reproducibility and interpretation of diagnostic and laboratory findings.

Our study had some limitations. One major limitation was the heterogeneity of the study population in terms of sex, age, weight, and breed, which may have influenced results. Additional limitations include the lack of salivary cortisol measurements, which are more suitable for detecting acute stress, and the use of a stress scale primarily validated for hospital settings. The absence of serial cortisol measurements also limits assessment of stress dynamics. The variability in owner-collected urine samples (urine PAZ), because of limited experience, potential handling errors, or delays also may have introduced bias into the results.

Environmental stress should be considered a substantial confounding factor in veterinary clinical and diagnostic evaluations, because it may lead to overestimation of clinical and laboratory variables, hindering their proper interpretation. In our study, higher stress levels, BP results, and cortisol concentrations were observed in the hospital environment, suggesting that this setting may result in overestimation of the magnitude of the obtained data. For BP assessment, acclimation and low-stress handling are essential to obtain reliable measurements. A substantial proportion of animals experienced changes in BP classification according to the environment, indicating that the setting may influence ACVIM-based categorization. However, hypertension was not confirmed during subsequent home assessments, emphasizing the importance of home BP measurements before establishing a definitive diagnosis whenever feasible.
