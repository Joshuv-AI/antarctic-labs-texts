Prevalence and risk factors of paediatric acute ischaemic stroke: a systematic review and meta-analysis protocol
Authors: Ping Wang, Shenglei Jiang, Guolian Shen, Xia Li, Yitao Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractIntroductionPaediatric acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) is a rare but severe neurological condition, often leading to long-term disability and considerable societal burden. Compared with adults, paediatric AIS exhibits distinct clinical characteristics and aetiologies, making early identification and prevention more challenging. This study aims to systematically review the prevalence and risk factors of paediatric AIS, providing a comprehensive evidence base for clinical prevention and management.Methods and analysisA systematic search will be conducted across major databases, including PubMed, Embase, Web of Science and Cochrane Library, from inception to August 2025. We will include observational studies reporting on the prevalence or risk factors of paediatric AIS. Two independent reviewers will screen the literature, extract data and assess study quality using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale. Meta-analysis will be performed using RevMan 5.4 and StataMP 16.0. Heterogeneity will be evaluated using the I² statistic, and subgroup and sensitivity analyses will be conducted as needed. The Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation system will be used to assess the quality of evidence.Ethics and disseminationBecause no patients were involved, ethical approval was not required. The final results of this research will be submitted to a peer-reviewed journal or presented at relevant conferences, and any deviations from this protocol will be recorded and explained in the final report.RegistrationThis study has been registered in the PROSPERO (PROSPERO Registration Number: CRD420251067538).Keywords: Stroke medicine, Meta-Analysis, Risk Factors

Introduction

Acute ischaemic stroke (AIS) is a significant global health concern, being one of the leading causes of disability and mortality worldwide.1 The incidence of AIS is closely associated with ageing and various vascular risk factors, which include hypertension, diabetes, hypercholesterolaemia and smoking.2 3 These risk factors contribute to the pathogenesis of ischaemic stroke by promoting atherosclerosis and other vascular changes that can lead to vessel occlusion and subsequent cerebral ischaemia.4 However, while stroke is predominantly viewed as a disease of adulthood, it is increasingly recognised that children are not immune to its devastating effects.5 Paediatric AIS, though relatively rare in absolute terms (it is estimated that, in developed countries, the incidence of stroke in children varies from 1.2 to 5.11 instances per 100 000 children each year),6 poses substantial and often lifelong consequences for affected individuals and their families, including cognitive impairment, motor dysfunction and seizures.7,9 Its hallmark is an interruption of blood supply to parts of the brain during the perinatal period or childhood, resulting in neurological dysfunction and potentially leading to long-term disability.10 The under-recognition of paediatric AIS is not merely a function of its lower incidence but also stems from its atypical presentation and the lack of standardised protocols for diagnosis and management.10 11 Children often present with non-specific symptoms—such as seizures, headaches or altered mental status—that can be mistaken for more benign conditions. Moreover, there is a striking delay between symptom onset and diagnosis in paediatric populations compared with adults, further complicating timely intervention.12 This diagnostic challenge highlights a broader knowledge gap: we still know too little about the true prevalence of paediatric AIS, the factors that increase its likelihood, and how these factors vary across regions, age groups or clinical subtypes. In light of these challenges, understanding the prevalence and risk factors for paediatric AIS is not only of scientific importance but also a critical step towards improving early detection and outcomes in this vulnerable population.

While several individual studies have examined specific cohorts or localised populations, their findings are often inconsistent or difficult to generalise due to heterogeneity in study design, population characteristics and diagnostic criteria.10 13 To provide a comprehensive review of previous studies, we summarised relevant meta-analyses. Sun et al13 conducted a population-based study on the burden of stroke in children and adolescents globally, regionally and nationally from 1990 to 2019, demonstrating that the burden of stroke in children continues to increase globally, with children with ischaemic strokes making up 70% of all strokes, especially in those younger than 5 years old. Gao et al14, who conducted a meta-analysis in 2021, found the overall incidence of AIS in children to be 2.09%, ranging from 0.9 to 7.9 per 100 000 persons per year. Looking further back, a study classified paediatric AIS by age group and the pooled AIS incidence rate was 5.6 per 100 000 children, with the highest incidence in neonates.15 Not only are all of these meta-analyses outdated and in need of updating, but there is also a continued lack of assessment of risk factors for the development of AIS in children. Our study aims to fill this gap by systematically reviewing observational studies from various regions and aggregating data to produce pooled estimates of prevalence and risk factors.

Several studies have attempted to characterise paediatric AIS using single-centre data or national registries.16 However, these studies are often limited by small sample sizes, retrospective methodologies and inconsistent definitions of stroke subtypes. Additionally, while numerous case–control or cohort studies have explored potential risk factors—such as congenital heart disease, infections, prothrombotic states or trauma—there remains no consensus on which risk factors are most consistently associated with paediatric AIS.16 Neonatal stroke, defined as occurring within the first 28 days of life, is more common than childhood stroke, which occurs between 1 month and 18 years of age.17 During the perinatal period, approximately, 80% of strokes are ischaemic, whereas this proportion decreases to around 50% after the first month of life.17 This stratified approach will enable us to identify vulnerable subgroups and generate insights that are actionable in both research and practice. In doing so, this study will provide valuable guidance for clinicians, public health officials and policymakers. Improved understanding of the frequency and determinants of paediatric AIS can facilitate earlier diagnosis, targeted prevention strategies and better-informed resource allocation. It can also contribute to the development of unified diagnostic criteria and risk assessment tools, which are currently lacking for paediatric stroke. In the long term, a more precise understanding of paediatric AIS may support the design of interventional trials and the implementation of evidence-based guidelines tailored to children.

Methods

Study registration

The protocol is reported following the reporting guideline provided in the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analysis Protocols statement18 and the Meta-Analysis of Epidemiological Observational Studies guideline (seen in onlinesupplemental appendices 12).19 PROSPERO Registration Number: CRD420251067538.

Search strategy

We will extensively search multiple databases, including the Cochrane Library, Embase, PubMed and Web of Science, to identify relevant studies published prior to August 2025. The research will employ medical subject headings (MeSH) and specific keywords (or entry terms) in its search process. For instance, the PubMed search strategy included the MeSH term “ischemic stroke”, its entry terms and the MeSH term “Pediatrics OR child”. And other keywords, such as “risk factors”. Additionally, we will manually search the references of selected studies and current field reviews to identify relevant literature. After the EndNote software eliminated duplicate studies, the remaining articles will be carefully reviewed. The initial complete search strategy examples are provided in the online supplemental appendix 3.

Eligibility criteria

Participants

We will include paediatric patients diagnosed with ischaemic stroke. Age should be defined as under 18 years old, including neonatal stroke (first 28 days) and childhood stroke (1 month–18 years).16 A physician should diagnose ischaemic stroke. It must meet stroke-related criteria (any officially published ones),17 or the author must indicate that the study subjects meet the criteria for paediatric ischaemic stroke.

Exposure

The primary outcomes will be the characteristics of the patients, including risk factors or predictors of deterioration. These factors include, but are not limited to, general demographic information (such as age, ethnicity and gender), any stroke-related factors and other health-related factors (including weight, height, blood pressure, medical history and medication history).

Types of studies

Observational studies will be included, including cross-sectional studies, cohort studies and case–control studies.

Exclusion criteria

We will exclude studies that do not focus on the health issues of paediatric ischaemic stroke and do not explore related risk factors. Studies will be rejected if they fulfil the following criteria.

Data collection and analysis

We will import all obtained results into the EndNote X20 software and remove all duplicate studies. Two independent researchers (PW and SJ) will conduct an initial screening of the studies according to our inclusion and exclusion criteria, then create new groups for these studies in Endnote and carefully review the full texts. If any disagreements arise during these two steps, they will be resolved through consultation with a third researcher (YZ) until a consensus is reached. The research selection process is outlined in the online supplemental appendix 4.

Data extraction

After completing the screening, two researchers (GS and XL) will be assigned to extract data using Excel software, focusing on key details, such as study characteristics, baseline patient information and the predefined outcomes, OR values and 95% CI of risk factors. To ensure accuracy, a third researcher (YZ) will review the extracted data, address any inconsistencies and facilitate consensus among the team.

Assessment of risk of bias

Two reviewers (Ping Wang and Shenglei Jiang) will assess the risk of bias in the included articles using the Newcastle–Ottawa scale (NOS), as outlined in online supplemental appendix 5. Should there be a conflict, the third investigator (YZ) will be referred to for the ultimate decision. Each item’s rating for quality in the research will appear in the fundamental information table of the study. The evaluated research will employ the NOS, which consists of eight factors, encompassing population selection and comparability between groups, as well as the assessment of results. Every item receives a single point, except for intergroup comparability, which is allotted two points. The maximum possible score for the NOS is 9 points, achievable with scores of 7 or higher, indicating superior quality. Scores ranging from 5 to 6 signify moderate quality. A rating of 4 or lower signifies poor quality. The research will solely focus on integrating high-quality and medium-quality studies, in contrast to literature of lower quality, which will be excluded.

Strategy for data synthesis

The statistical analysis will be conducted using StataMP (V.16) software. Dichotomous variables will be represented by OR, while continuous variables will be represented by standardised mean differences, both accompanied by 95% CIs. A p value<0.05 will be considered statistically significant. The I2 test will be used to measure heterogeneity; an I2 value greater than 50% indicates high heterogeneity, and a random-effects model will be employed. Furthermore, I2≤50% indicates lower heterogeneity, and thus, a fixed-effect model will be used. Publication bias assessment will be only performed in cases when the analysis included>7 studies.20 In assessing publication bias, a funnel plot and Egger’s test will be employed. Subgroup analyses will be conducted to explore potential sources of heterogeneity. Specifically, we plan to conduct a series of prespecified subgroup analyses based on key clinical and methodological factors. These factors include: (i) age group (neonates (0–28 days) vs children (>28 days to 18 years)); (ii) sex (male vs female); (iii) geographical region (as defined by the WHO); (iv) country income level (high-income vs low- and middle-income countries); (v) study design (cohort vs case–control studies) and (vi) ischaemic stroke subtype.

Quality of evidence and publication biases assessment

Two researchers (PW and YZ) will employ Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation for assessing the evidence’s merit. If the study’s risk factor literature exceeds 10, Egger’s test will be used to evaluate publication bias. The clear imbalance observed on either side of the funnel graph indicates a likelihood of bias in publication. A p<0.05 indicates potential publication bias.

Sensitivity analysis

The leave-one-out method for sensitivity analysis will be conducted to assess the stability. Once significant publication bias was detected, and further inferences were made using the trim-and-fill analysis.

Ethics and dissemination

As this study is a protocol for a systematic review based on publicly available data, it does not involve identifiable patient information. Therefore, ethics committee approval and informed consent are not required. The data extraction and management will strictly adhere to the standards for conducting systematic reviews to ensure transparency, accuracy and reproducibility. Findings of this systematic review will be published in international peer-reviewed scientific journals.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Discussion

Paediatric AIS is a rare but serious neurological event with long-term consequences.21 Unlike adult AIS, paediatric cases are often under-recognised due to non-specific symptoms and low clinical suspicion, leading to delays in diagnosis and treatment.10 22 As we mentioned above,3 9 14 some of the problems with the existing research are that there is currently a lack of comprehensive and systematic data on the overall prevalence and risk factors associated with paediatric AIS. This study aims to synthesise available evidence to support early recognition, prevention and management strategies.

The risk factors for paediatric AIS are multifactorial and differ significantly from those in adults. In children, AIS is frequently associated with congenital heart disease, arteriopathies (such as focal cerebral arteriopathy and Moyamoya disease), infections, trauma and prothrombotic disorders.23,26 Compared with adults, haematological disorders are more strongly associated with paediatric stroke. AIS in children is often linked to both inherited and acquired coagulopathies, as well as to sickle cell disease (SCD).27 In cases of SCD, repeated sickling of red blood cells leads to endothelial proliferation and intraluminal thrombosis, which are believed to contribute to the development of large-artery stenosis and occlusive vasculopathy.27 Moreover, some basic studies have suggested that immune and inflammatory responses may also play a significant role in vascular injury and thrombosis formation in the paediatric population. These mechanisms highlight the distinct pathophysiological processes in children compared with the atherosclerotic mechanisms commonly seen in adults. Our subgroup analyses and classification of risk factors by category (eg, cardiac, vascular and haematological) will help clarify these specific associations.

This systematic review and meta-analysis will comprehensively examine the prevalence and risk profile of paediatric AIS. The results are expected to contribute to evidence-based clinical decision-making, enhance screening strategies for high-risk populations and inform health policy planning. In addition, this study may offer insights into future research directions, including paediatric-specific diagnostic protocols and pathogenesis-oriented preventive approaches. Despite anticipated heterogeneity among studies, we will adopt rigorous methodological tools to ensure the reliability of our findings.

Strengths and limitations

To the best of our knowledge, this is the first registered meta-analysis protocol to focus exclusively on both the prevalence and risk factors of paediatric AIS, unlike previous studies that have focused only on prevalence and are in dire need of updating. This study may have several potential limitations. First, only observational studies will be included, which may introduce inherent biases and limit the ability to make causal inferences. Second, due to differences in study design, diagnostic criteria and reporting standards across included studies, the results may present substantial heterogeneity, which could affect the reliability of pooled estimates. Third, as this review will include literature published in English only, relevant studies published in other languages may be missed, potentially leading to selection bias. Finally, due to the relative rarity of paediatric AIS, the number of high-quality studies may be limited, and certain risk factors may lack sufficient data to allow for quantitative synthesis, requiring narrative description instead.
