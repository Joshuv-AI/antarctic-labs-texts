A Copula-based framework for seismic resilience assessment of civil engineering structural systems
Authors: Changling Xie, Shenghao Xu, Qingtao Zheng, Yuxin Liu, Junlong Zhou, Jie Li, Kai Zhang, Wenze Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAccurate seismic resilience assessment is critical for engineering structure design and risk management. Traditional seismic resilience assessment methods widely adopt simplified bounding assumptions for the dependencies between component failure events, most commonly presuming either complete correlation or full uncorrelation of component failures. These over-simplifications inevitably introduce substantial systematic bias, leading to pronounced overestimation or underestimation of the actual seismic performance and resilience of structural systems. To address this, this paper proposes a system-level analytical framework using Copula functions to model complex dependencies between component failure events.•The proposed method delivers a more accurate, high-probability resilience estimate within traditional theoretical bounds.•It quantifies true system resilience by capturing correlation structures overlooked by conventional methods.•This framework provides a reliable basis for performance-based seismic design and risk decision-making.Keywords: Seismic resilience evaluation, Copula function, Framework development, Civil engineering structure systems

Background

Research on bridge vulnerability primarily centers on assessing the probability of damage and its evolution under extreme events such as earthquakes, floods, and strong winds. This analysis forms a critical basis for resilience assessment. By developing vulnerability curves or probabilistic models, the extent of structural performance degradation under different intensity levels can be quantified, providing reliable support for seismic design, operation and maintenance management, and the formulation of retrofit strategies. In recent years, advancements in numerical simulation, sensor monitoring, and statistical methods have enabled a shift in bridge vulnerability analysis from empirical judgment towards comprehensive, data- and mechanism-driven assessments, significantly enhancing its central role in infrastructure resilience planning and disaster prevention and mitigation research. Ní Choine et al. [1] compared the seismic performance of integral and jointed concrete bridges through probabilistic fragility analysis, confirming the seismic superiority of integral bridges while revealing the differential effects of geometric parameters on the vulnerability of different bridge types, laying a structural performance benchmark for vulnerability-based resilience assessment. With the deepening of research, structural seismic resilience has become a cutting-edge core concept in the field of civil engineering. Bruneau et al. [2] proposed a conceptual framework for community seismic resilience, integrating three indicators (reduced failure probability, consequences, and recovery time) and four measures (robustness, rapidity, resourcefulness, redundancy) across technical, organizational, social, and economic dimensions, providing a basis for quantitative resilience assessment and enhancement. Du et al. [3] pointed out that regional seismic risk and resilience assessment requires the integration of multi-scale physical processes, uncertainty quantification, and four key modules: hazard analysis, exposure modeling, fragility assessment, and consequence evaluation. Forcellini et al. [4] proposed a seismic resilience assessment framework considering the impact of structural deterioration, confirming that deterioration not only reduces the seismic capacity of structures but also significantly delays the recovery process, emphasizing the necessity of resilience assessment from a life-cycle perspective; Mina et al. [5] conducted a study on the resilience of subsea high-pressure/high-temperature (HP/HT) pipelines under combined seismic and thermal loads, finding that resilience exhibits significant load-path dependence, and multi-hazard coupling will amplify structural damage and increase recovery difficulty, further highlighting the importance of accurate resilience assessment under complex working conditions. However, although the above studies have greatly advanced the development of resilience theory and methods, the vast majority of existing seismic resilience assessment methods still universally adopt extreme simplifying assumptions of either complete correlation (lower bound) or complete uncorrelation (upper bound) between component failure events, failing to accurately characterize the complex multidimensional dependencies between components in actual engineering. The systematic bias introduced thereby leads to significant overestimation or underestimation of resilience assessment results.

To address the complexities of these assessments, Copula functions have emerged as a powerful and increasingly applied statistical tool in civil engineering. They are particularly valuable for modeling the joint probability distributions of correlated variables, which is often essential for accurate reliability analysis. For instance, Zhang et al. [6] developed a multivariate integration framework using the Gaussian Copula to establish a time-varying failure probability model, demonstrating that neglecting variable correlations leads to an underestimation of structural reliability. Liu et al. [7] integrated multivariate Copula functions with the AHP-EW method to evaluate system reliability under multiple failure criteria, providing an effective tool for complex bridge systems. Further applications include Li et al. [8], who used a Copula-based framework to account for storm parameter interactions in deck unseating failure during hurricanes, and Liu et al. [9], who combined an optimal regular vine Copula model with other methods to analyze girder failure probability. James et al. [10] also contributed by introducing a Copula method with Rayleigh margins for evaluating correlated reliability. Supporting this approach, Lei et al. [11] proposed a probabilistic framework using Copula's joint probability density functions to characterize the strong correlations between environmental factors and structural responses in cable-stayed bridges. Similarly, Zhang et al. [12] applied Copula theory to bridge damage detection and long-term health monitoring, successfully identifying changes in modal parameters over time. Existing studies still have obvious limitations in the application of Copula functions in civil engineering, and the systematic analysis of references [[13], [14], [15], [16], [17], [18]] further confirms this research gap: Khan et al. [13] used a Bayesian belief network (BBN) model to enhance the seismic resilience of bridges, which can integrate expert knowledge and limited data, but this method cannot characterize the nonlinear and asymmetric dependence structures between components, making it difficult to accurately quantify the failure probability of complex systems. Liu et al. [14] applied the Gaussian Copula to the fatigue reliability assessment of orthotropic steel decks of the Bridge, considering the correlation between fatigue stress and stress cycle counts, but the study remained at the component level and was not extended to the entire bridge system. James et al. [15] proposed a stress-strength reliability model based on the Farlie-Gumbel-Morgenstern Copula with Rayleigh marginals, improving the reliability calculation method under the traditional independence assumption, but it was also limited to component-level analysis. Eryilmaz [16] applied multivariate Copulas to the dynamic reliability modeling of weighted-k-out-of-n systems, derived analytical expressions for the system survival function and mean time to failure, and realized system-level reliability analysis, but did not integrate the post-earthquake recovery process, which is the core element of resilience assessment. Li et al. [17] used Copula functions to calculate the system reliability index of bridge construction processes, verifying the important impact of failure mode correlation on construction safety, but the study focused on the construction stage and did not involve seismic resilience assessment during the operation stage. Zhou et al. [18] proposed a system-level seismic fragility assessment framework for highway bridges based on D-vine Copula theory, constructed a joint probabilistic seismic demand model (JPSDM) for multiple engineering demand parameters (EDPs), and confirmed the superiority of optimal vine Copulas in characterizing complex dependence structures, but this study only stopped at fragility analysis and was not further extended to system-level seismic resilience assessment.

In summary, most existing studies are limited to component-level failure probability or fragility analysis. Even the few studies involving the system level have not coupled Copula functions with the post-earthquake recovery process, and a systematic method for system-level seismic resilience assessment has not yet been formed. Compared with traditional methods, Copula functions have unique advantages in characterizing nonlinear and asymmetric dependence structures, enabling the effective construction of joint probabilistic models for multi-component performance indicators. Based on this, this study proposes a Pair-Copula function-based framework for seismic resilience assessment of civil engineering structural systems. By establishing a system-level joint failure probability model and coupling multiple types of recovery processes, it achieves quantitative assessment of the resilience performance of the overall structural system under strong earthquakes, providing a new technical approach for the seismic resilience design and evaluation of bridges and other engineering structures.

Method details

This study adopts a four-step method to achieve the expected objectives, and the technical flowchart is illustrated in Fig. 1. (1): Component-level seismic vulnerability modeling: Develops probabilistic seismic demand models and capacity models for each structural component to obtain component-level failure probabilities under different ground motion intensities. (2) Pair-Copula dependency modeling: Constructs the joint probability distribution of component failure events using hierarchical Pair-Copula constructions, including parameter estimation via maximum likelihood, optimal Copula selection via AIC, and goodness-of-fit validation via the Cramer-von Mises test. (3) System-level joint failure probability calculation: Derives the system-level failure probability based on the Pair-Copula joint distribution and the system's logical failure mode (series, parallel, or hybrid). (4) Seismic resilience assessment and parametric analysis: Couples the system failure probability with post-earthquake recovery models to calculate seismic resilience, and conducts parametric analysis of recovery functions, repair targets, and delay times.

Resilience evaluation framework

The critical step in assessing the seismic vulnerability and resilience of civil engineering structure systems, while accounting for component interdependencies, involves constructing the joint probability mass distribution of failure probabilities across structural elements using Copula Functions.

The framework adopts a decoupled modular structure: the component-level vulnerability module can be replaced with vulnerability data for any structural type (e.g., high-rise buildings, underground structures, lifeline systems), while the Pair-Copula dependency modeling and resilience calculation modules remain unchanged. The framework supports all commonly used Copula function types (Gaussian, Clayton, Gumbel, Frank, etc.) and all standard recovery function models (linear, logarithmic, trigonometric), making it adaptable to different engineering applications.

Method validation

Finite element model

The shaking table test of a scaled rigid-frame bridge model was used to validate the FEM, as illustrated in Fig. 2. The span of the bridge is 5.1 m + 9.4 m + 5.1 m. The section size of the pier is 700 mm × 400 mm × 12 mm, and the pier height is 4000 mm. Laser displacement sensors were mounted at the ends of the main girder to measure horizontal displacement, while accelerometers were used to measure the acceleration of the girder and the foundation.

Shaking table test of the scaled model of bridge.

The seismic performance of the rigid-frame bridge was investigated through a detailed nonlinear finite element model, presented in Fig. 3. This model explicitly represented the primary structural components, such as the girders and piers. The constitutive behavior of concrete was simulated with the Concrete Damaged Plasticity (CDP) model to account for stiffness degradation and plastic damage under cyclic loading. The reinforcement was modeled with a bilinear kinematic hardening law, considering the yield and post-yield behavior. Concrete components (girders and piers) were modeled using 8-node linear brick elements with reduced integration (C3D8R). Reinforcement bars were modeled using 2-node linear 3D truss elements (T3D2), and embedded in the concrete elements to ensure composite action. The Concrete Damaged Plasticity (CDP) model was adopted with the following parameters: dilation angle = 30°, eccentricity = 0.1, K = 0.667, viscosity parameter = 0.0001. The compressive strength of concrete was 30 MPa, and the tensile strength was 3.0 MPa. The element size was set to 200 mm for piers and 300 mm for girders, which was determined through a mesh convergence study. The bases of the piers were fully fixed to simulate the foundation constraints. The connections between piers and girders were modeled as rigid ties to ensure the transfer of both moments and shear forces, which is consistent with the characteristics of rigid-frame bridges.

Influence to seismic resilience of different resilience evaluation method

Figs. 6 present the comparisons of seismic resilience under three distinct dependency scenarios: complete correlation (Lower Bound), complete uncorrelation (Upper Bound), and accounting for dependency using the Copula function. Linear recovery function assumes a constant rate of recovery. This is appropriate for scenarios where resources are continuously available and repair work progresses at a steady pace. Logarithmic recovery function assumes rapid initial recovery followed by a gradual slowdown. This is the most commonly observed recovery pattern, as critical functions are typically restored first, followed by more time-consuming repairs. Trigonometric recovery function assumes an initial delay before recovery begins, followed by rapid recovery and then a gradual slowdown. This is appropriate for scenarios where there is a delay in mobilizing resources after an earthquake. Compared to the complete correlation, and complete uncorrelation method, which can only provide a broad and potentially misleading range, the Pair Copula-based assessment method delivers a single, more probable estimate of resilience that is closer to the true state by precisely quantifying inter-component correlations. This effectively resolves the inherent risk of the traditional method either overestimating or underestimating structural resilience due to its simplifying assumptions, providing a significantly more reliable basis for performance-based and risk-informed seismic design and decision-making. In essence, the value of the Copula method lies not just in “falling within the bounds,” but in its ability to dramatically reduce decision-making uncertainty, guiding engineers away from guessing between “risky” and “wasteful” options and towards a more precise and credible assessment.

Influence of repair targets to seismic resilience based on copula function

Fig. 8 reveals the influence of repair targets to seismic resilience based on copula function. Different repair targets corresponding to 80%, 85%, 90%, 95%, and 100% recovery of pre-earthquake structural capacity were considered. These were taken to represent common practical scenarios ranging from emergency temporary repairs to full permanent restoration. The results showed that lowering repair targets systematically reduces bridge seismic resilience, with varying sensitivity across recovery functions. This demonstrates the coupling effect between recovery path morphology and repair criteria, emphasizing that practical resilience management must select appropriate recovery models based on expected resource mobilization scenarios (immediate response, steady progress, or initial delays). The Pair Copula method enables quantitative assessment of these interactions, providing critical insights for developing balanced resilience strategies that integrate both safety and economic considerations. At lower ground motion intensities, the seismic resilience of bridges exhibits only minor differences across various recovery functions. As the intensity increases, the impact of the recovery functions on seismic resilience becomes progressively more pronounced. In summary, the Logarithmic recovery function leads to the highest seismic resilience, followed by the Linear function, whereas the Trigonometric function yields the lowest resilience.

Seismic resilience curves for different repair targets: (a) Linear; (b) Logarithmic; (c) Trigonometric.

Influence of target delay times to seismic resilience based on copula function

Fig. 9 illustrates the seismic resilience considering different delay times. The results reveal that delay time significantly reduces bridge seismic resilience based on copula function, with the impact intensifying as delays prolong. Among different recovery functions, the logarithmic function is most affected by delays, followed by linear, while trigonometric is the least sensitive. This study effectively quantifies the specific impact of delays on resilience through the Copula method, providing a reliable basis for accurate post-earthquake recovery assessment. This methodology not only decouples multi-factor interactions but also applies to resilience assessment of complex structural systems, multi-hazard scenario analysis, and resource allocation optimization, providing a reliable quantitative tool for resilience construction.

Conclusion

Main research findings

This study proposes a Copula-based framework for seismic resilience assessment of civil engineering structural systems, addressing the limitations of traditional methods that rely on simplified dependence assumptions. The main findings are:

(1)Traditional seismic resilience assessment methods that assume either complete correlation or complete uncorrelation between component failures lead to significant systematic biases. The Pair-Copula-based method provides a more accurate, high-probability resilience estimate that lies within the traditional theoretical bounds.

Traditional seismic resilience assessment methods that assume either complete correlation or complete uncorrelation between component failures lead to significant systematic biases. (2)The type of recovery function has a significant impact on seismic resilience assessment results. Among the three typical recovery functions studied, the logarithmic recovery function is most sensitive to recovery delays, followed by the linear function, while the trigonometric function is the least sensitive.

(3)Lowering repair targets systematically reduces structural seismic resilience, and there is a coupling effect between repair targets and recovery function types. This indicates that resilience management strategies should be tailored to specific resource mobilization scenarios.

(4)Recovery delay time is a critical factor affecting seismic resilience. The longer the delay time, the more significant the reduction in resilience. This highlights the importance of rapid post-earthquake emergency response.

Limitations

Despite its contributions, this study has certain limitations. The analysis is fundamentally a resilience assessment based on structural seismic capacity. To enhance the generalizability and practical relevance of the findings, future work should involve applications to a wider variety of structures and incorporate the influences of socio-environmental factors.
