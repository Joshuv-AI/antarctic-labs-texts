Inverse Infrared Spectral Deconvolution for Quantitative Analysis of Polymer Mixtures in Scattering Media
Authors: Proity Nayeeb Akbar, Reinhold Blümel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Infrared (IR) spectroscopy is a powerful tool for analyzing complex substances. It allows one to identify materials based on their functional groups. Nevertheless, spectral interpretation of composite materials with IR spectroscopy remains a significant challenge for analytical and forensic laboratories, especially if the samples are small, producing significant scattering that complicates the interpretation of IR spectra. Effectively tackling this challenge, we present an innovative IR-based method for nondestructively identifying the individual components within a mixture. Our study focuses on two- and multicomponent strongly scattering homogeneous mixed-composition microspheres filled with organic polymers. The results reveal that our algorithm can (1) reconstruct the pure absorption of functional groups in composite systems by eliminating scattering effects, (2) identify the number of components in a mixture, (3) determine the volume fractions of the constituents, and (4) generate pure permittivity spectra for each component in the mixture. What sets this method apart is its noninvasive natureit does not rely on expensive separation techniques such as chromatography or time-consuming calibration processes. Additionally, it may be fully automated, making it accessible for users of various levels of expertise in spectroscopy. The method addresses critical challenges in analytical and forensic chemistry, enabling previously unattainable insights, such as rapid quantification of trace chemicals in drug formulations for forensic analysis or identification of synthetic polymer blends in environmental microplastics.

Introduction

Infrared (IR) spectroscopy is a powerful tool for analyzing the composition of organic and inorganic substances. − Frequently, however, chemical, biological, and forensic laboratories are faced with the task of nondestructively analyzing the composition of microscopic samples with sizes of the order of the IR wavelength itself. Examples include the observation and analysis of individual cells in vivo, individual fibers, hair samples, and plant pollen. , These samples, which may, for instance, serve as forensic evidence in court cases, must retain their structure and are not allowed to be altered. Infrared (IR) spectroscopy is an ideal technique for analyzing such samples because it is nondestructive. However, because of the structural integrity requirement, it may not be allowed to convert the samples into thin films, the optimal format for IR spectroscopy. Furthermore, because the size of the samples may be of the same order as the IR wavelength, this leads to strong scattering effects. ,− These scattering effects pose a significant challenge for the interpretation of IR spectra. Specifically, they cause baseline and intensity variations, which complicate analysis by causing deviations from the assumptions of the Beer–Lambert law. An additional complication arises in cases where the sample contains several different substances whose IR spectra overlap. In this case, the measured IR spectrum represents the combined vibrational modes of all the substances in the sample, resulting in overlapping peaks from different compounds that make it laborious to identify the individual constituents. Consequently, to eliminate scattering and distortion effects, more sophisticated and powerful methods are necessary to study mixtures in chemical, biological, and forensic applications. −

Various approaches have been tried and tested to interpret the IR spectral properties of composite materials. , IR spectroscopy, coupled with chromatographic techniques, is one approach, where mixtures are thoroughly separated into their components and then analyzed to obtain individual IR spectra. While this technique can be beneficial, the associated sample preparation is usually complicated and time-consuming. Spectral subtraction software offers a seemingly simple solution by subtracting the IR signature of the known compounds from the mixture spectrum, simplifying the spectrum of the composite system. However, even if nonlinear effects , are neglected, this becomes impossible to accomplish in the mid-IR region due to scattering effects, which may completely change the appearance of the spectra. Other common approaches, such as library searching and mixture analysis software, fail to address spectral overlap caused by scattering and overlapping peaks. Thus, distinguishing between a pure compound and a mixture and identifying specific compounds within that mixture with high confidence, relying solely on its IR data, is a significant challenge. This is especially true if the components have similar functional groups occurring within overlapping spectral regions. However, the technical problems notwithstanding, since speed-up of the analysis processes is definitely a desirable goal, there is an ever-pressing need to develop faster and more efficient techniques for analyzing mixtures using IR spectroscopy.

IR spectroscopy of μm sized objects can be implemented using several spectroscopic techniques. Among them are Fourier-transform infrared (FT-IR) spectroscopy, ,− (spatially offset) Raman spectroscopy and holographic techniques. No matter which technique is used, all these spectroscopic techniques have to take scattering effects into account for the correct interpretation of IR spectra. In FT-IR microspectroscopy this has been recognized early on and has recently also been emphasized in connection with IR Raman spectroscopy. The reason for this is that μm sized samples are dielectric resonators whose IR internal resonances, for instance whispering gallery modes, , can be resonantly excited by the incident IR radiation which results in significant distortions of the measured IR spectra. ,,, We emphasize that not even holographic techniques are immune from this effect, since the laser used to generate the holograms, due to the geometry of the sample, will be refracted and reflected at the sample boundary and may excite shape resonances, , which, again, result in scatter distortions of measured spectra. Thus, while our method, to be described below, is focused on eliminating scattering effects in FT-IR spectroscopy, the problem of scatter distortions plagues all IR spectroscopy techniques, and suitably modified versions of our algorithm may turn out to be useful and find application in spectroscopic techniques other than FT-IR spectroscopy.

Methods

This work presents a novel approach to characterize, identify, and analyze the IR spectra of homogeneous multicomponent mixed-composition materials in the presence of strong scattering. To illustrate the feasibility and robustness of our approach, we use a set of six organic polymers enclosed in a spherical model cell with a diameter of 5 μm, which is of the order of the IR wavelength. This guarantees strong scattering as expected to occur for similarly sized microscopic samples. The six polymers chosen encompass a range of common plastic materials, including poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), polycarbonate (PC), polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), poly­(ether imide) (PEI), polyethylene terephthalate (PET), and polystyrene (PS). The experimental data for the refractive indexes of these polymers are published measurements obtained from ellipsometry and Fourier-transform IR spectroscopy studies of thin slabs prepared from each material. − This way the experimentally determined refractive indexes are the pure, innate properties of the respective materials, not distorted by scattering effects. The measured refractive indexes, ñ(ν̃), for each individual polymer are complex functions of the wavenumber ν̃, as shown in eq , n~(ν~)=n~′(ν~)+in~″(ν~) 1 where ñ′(ν̃) is its real part, ñ″(ν̃) is its imaginary part, and ν̃ = 1/λ, where λ is the vacuum wavelength of the incident IR radiation. Since our theory is based on the relative dielectric permittivity, ϵ̃ r (ν̃), we transform from ñ(ν̃) to ϵ̃ r (ν̃) according to the set of relationships in eq , ϵ~r(ν~)=[n~(ν~)]2=ϵ~r′(ν~)+iϵ~r″(ν~)ϵ~r′(ν~)=n~′(ν~)2−n~″(ν~)2ϵ~r″(ν~)=2n~′(ν~)n~″(ν~) 2 where ϵ̃ r ′ (ν̃) and ϵ̃ r ″ (ν̃) are the real and imaginary parts of the relative dielectric permittivity, ϵ̃ r (ν̃), respectively.

The composite system is described using the effective-medium approach, where the complex effective permittivity, ϵ̃eff(ν̃), of the mixture is a weighted sum of the complex permittivities of the components of the composite system (eq ), ϵ~eff(ν~)=∑j=1JVjϵ~rj(ν~)=ϵ~eff′(ν~)+iϵ~eff″(ν~) 3 where ϵ̃ r j (ν̃) are the complex permittivities of the components of the mixture, ϵ̃eff ′(ν̃) and ϵ̃eff ″ (ν̃) are the real and imaginary parts of the complex effective permittivity, V j are the volume fractions, i.e., the respective ratio at which substance number j is present in the mixture, and J denotes the total number of components in the mixture.

Given a sphere filled with a medium with complex permittivity ϵ̃eff(ν̃), a typical IR absorbance spectrum, , A ­(ν̃), is generated by illuminating the sample with IR radiation of intensity I 0(ν̃) and measuring the transmitted intensity I(ν̃) in forward direction. In this case, the absorbance spectrum is defined as shown in eq as , A(ν~)=−log10[I(ν~)I0(ν~)] 4 An alternative quantity is the extinction efficiency, Q ext(ν̃), which is related to the absorbance A(ν~) via Qext(ν~)=β[1−10−A(ν~)] 5 where β is a constant that depends on the details of the detector and the sample. Since, according to eq , Q ext(ν̃) and A(ν~) contain the same information about the missing light associated with the shape and chemical composition of the sample, it is possible to reconstruct the functional groups based on either variable. While both quantities are equally valid, our method is based on Q ext(ν̃), since it is directly related to the extinction cross section and is therefore more convenient to work with in theoretical and numerical spectral analysis.

The task at hand is now the following. Given an extinction spectrum, Q ext (given)(ν̃), we have to discover the identity of the polymers in the mixture together with their mixing ratios, entirely and exclusively based on Q ext (given)(ν̃) as input. We accomplish this in two steps. In step 1, referred to as “spectral inversion”, we use an inverse scattering method to discover the effective permittivity ϵ̃eff(ν̃) derived from Q ext (given)(ν̃). In step 2, referred to as “mixture deconvolution”, we use a multidimensional fitting method to discover the individual components that contribute to ϵ̃eff(ν̃) and thus discover the identity of the polymers in the mixture together with their mixing ratios.

Step 1: Spectral Inversion

Step 1 of our method requires a convenient parametrization of ϵ̃eff(ν̃). For this, we use the Lorentz model, which predicts that ϵ~eff′(ν̃)=∑n=1N{ϵ∞+ν̃peff(n)2(ν̃0eff(n)2−ν̃2)(ν̃0eff(n)2−ν̃2)2+(ν̃γeff(n))2} 6 ϵ~eff″(ν~)=∑n=1N{ν~peff(n)2ν~γeff(n)(ν~0eff(n)2−ν~2)2+(ν~γeff(n))2} 7 where the index n = 1, ···, N labels the different absorption bands within the composite system and N is the total number of bands. The parameters ν̃0eff (n), ν̃ p eff (n), and γeff (n) denote the position, plasma frequency, and width of absorption band number n of the mixture, respectively. The parameter ϵ ∞ in eq is a constant offset that represents the contributions of high-frequency bands beyond the mid-IR spectral region, and is therefore not included in the sums in eqs and .

The goal of step 1 is to extract ϵ̃eff(ν̃) of a multicomponent mixture from its IR spectrum, represented by Q ext (given)(ν̃). This is achieved by determining the parameters ν̃0eff (n), ν̃ p eff (n), γeff (n), n = 1, ···, N, and ϵ ∞ , such that they optimally reproduce the given or measured extinction spectrum Q ext (given)(ν̃). This is accomplished by minimizing the sum of the residuals, S, defined as S({ν~0eff(n),ν~peff(n),γeff(n)},ϵ∞)=∑l{Qext(model)[{ν~0eff(n),ν~peff(n),γeff(n)},ϵ∞;ν~l]−Qext(given)(ν~l)}2 8 with respect to the parameters ν̃0eff (n), ν̃ p eff (n), γeff (n), n = 1, ···, N, and ϵ ∞ . Here, Q ext (model)[{ν̃0eff (n), ν̃ p eff (n), γeff (n)}, ϵ ∞ ;ν̃ l ] is the extinction efficiency evaluated with the Python package “PYMIESCATT” at wavenumber ν̃ l , using the set of variables {ν̃0eff (n), ν̃ p eff (n), γeff (n)}, n = 1, ···, N, and ϵ ∞ . The optimization of S is performed using MICROSOFT EXCEL.

To start the EXCEL optimizer code, initial conditions for the variables ν̃0eff (n), ν̃ p eff (n), γeff (n), n = 1, ···, N, and ϵ ∞ are required. Since peak widths in ϵ̃ r (ν̃) of typical polymers in the mid-IR region fluctuate around 20 cm–1, we chose 20 cm–1 for the initial condition of all of the peak widths γeff (n). It turns out that the performance of our method is insensitive to this choice. Testing the sensitivity to initial conditions we used initial γeff (n) values ranging from 20 to 30 cm–1 in steps of 0.1 cm–1 and found no qualitative difference in the performance or achieved accuracy of our method. We also tested our method with an initial condition of 130 cm–1 with the same result. This shows insensitivity of our method with respect to fine-grained as well as large-scale changes of initial conditions.

In contrast to the peak widths, since the peak heights, ν̃ p eff (n), typically fluctuate over more than an order of magnitude, no simple recipe can be given for the optimal choice of initial conditions of peak heights. However, the simple choice of a constant peak height of 20 cm–1 for the initial conditions of all peak heights, ν̃ p eff (n), yielded excellent results. We found that the choice of initial conditions for peak positions ν̃0eff (n) is not critical either. Neither, it turned out, is the choice of the initial value for ϵ ∞ . We used the initial value ϵ ∞ = 2 for all of our computations, which turned out to yield excellent results.

Concerning the choice of N, we argue in the following way. Most organic polymers exhibit approximately 15 to 20 absorption bands in the IR spectrum. However, polymer mixtures or blends lead to an increased number of absorption peaks, since, in general, each component exhibits absorption resonances at different wavenumbers. This complexity must be considered in the reconstructions to preserve chemical information. To this end, for the reconstructions reported in this work, we represent the target ϵ̃eff (ν̃) choosing N = 30 in eqs and . This approach not only maintains the integrity of the reconstructions but also ensures computational efficiency. Since each term in ϵ̃eff(ν̃) is characterized by three parameters, i.e., ν̃0eff (n), ν̃ p eff (n), γeff (n), and ϵ ∞ also needs to be determined, this results in a total of 91 parameters to be optimized.

While, as mentioned above, the choice of initial values for ν̃0eff (n) is not critical, we implemented the following strategy. Since the composite material under investigation is a polymer mixture, we leverage existing knowledge of typical IR spectra for polymers to distribute 25 ν̃0eff (n) evenly spaced across the interval 500–2200 cm–1, allocate none of the ν̃0eff (n) in the “silent” region, 2200–2800 cm–1, where no absorption is expected, and distribute the remaining 5 ν̃0eff (n) equi-spaced in the region 2800–3500 cm–1, not allocating any initial ν̃0eff (n) in the region 3500–4000 cm–1, where, again, no absorption is expected to occur. These choices optimally cover the spectral region 500–4000 cm–1, where IR spectroscopy of organic materials is conventionally performed, , maximally economizing our resources and, because of the chosen equi-spaced distributions of initial ν̃0eff (n), avoiding any bias in the initial conditions. Starting the EXCEL optimizer with these initial conditions, the optimizer will run until it reaches the termination criterion, i.e., all first partial derivatives of S with respect to all 91 variational parameters are (numerically) zero. The optimizer then returns the optimal parameters ν̃0eff (n), ν̃ p eff (n), γeff (n), n = 1, ···, N, and ϵ ∞ , which we then use in eqs and to construct the step-1 predicted permittivity. We refer to this optimal permittivity, reconstructed entirely from the given extinction efficiency Q ext (given)(ν̃), as the reconstructed model permittivity ϵ̃eff (model)(ν̃). The reconstructed ϵ̃eff (model)(ν̃) then serves as input to step 2, i.e., the mixture deconvolution step.

Step 2: Mixture Deconvolution

Mixture deconvolution is the step where we discover the volume ratios V j of the polymers that make up the mixture. We accomplish this using a straightforward fitting procedure. As shown in eq , we define ϵ~eff(fit)[{Vj};ν~]=∑j=1JVjϵ~rj(ν~) 9 where J is the number of polymers included in the fit function and ϵ̃ r j (ν̃) is the permittivity of polymer number j at wavenumber ν̃. We then minimize the sum of residuals RSS({Vj})=∑l{ϵ~eff(fit)[{Vj};ν̃l]−ϵ~eff(model)(ν~l)}2 10 with respect to the variational parameters V j .

The process of fitting ϵ̃eff (fit)[{V j };ν̃] to ϵ̃eff (model)(ν̃) to determine the parameters V j , j = 1, ···, J, is performed using two different methods: grid search and gradient descent. The grid search algorithm works by creating a grid of all possible combinations of the parameters V j , evaluating the fit of ϵ̃eff (fit)[{V j };ν̃] to ϵ̃eff (model)(ν̃) for each {V j } combination, and selecting the values for V j with the best performance based on the sum of residuals defined in eq . Similarly, the gradient descent algorithm finds the optimal parameters by minimizing the sum of residuals, defined in eq , with an iterative approach that adjusts the parameters in the direction of the negative gradient.

Results and Discussion

In the following sections we present our results for spheres homogeneously filled with two- and multicomponent mixtures of organic polymers. We also demonstrate the robustness of our reconstruction algorithm (see Section “Assessment of Accuracy and Scaling of the Reconstruction Algorithm”) by analyzing the effect of varying initial conditions on the extraction of volume fractions for a given mixture.

Two-Component Mixed-Composition Homogeneous Spheres

To demonstrate the feasibility and robustness of our novel two-step reconstruction approach, we examine the 15 possible combinations of two-component homogeneous mixtures that can be formed from the six test substances, with each combination tested at three distinct mixing ratios. These 15 samples are labeled as follows: PC–PDMS, PC–PEI, PC–PET, PEI–PDMS, PEI–PET, PET–PDMS, PMMA–PC, PMMA–PDMS, PMMA–PEI, PMMA–PET, PMMA–PS, PC–PS, PDMS–PS, PEI–PS, and PET–PS, and the mixing ratios for each are specified as 10–90%, 30–70%, and 50–50%. Here, for example, a mixture labeled PC–PDMS at a mixing ratio of 10–90% indicates that the first component of the composite system is 10% polycarbonate (PC) and the second component is 90% polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS).

We assume that the mixtures are described by their distinct permittivities, namely ϵ̃ r 1 (ν̃) and ϵ̃ r 2 (ν̃), representing the chemical properties of the individual constituents in the mixture with relative concentrations or volume ratios, V 1 and V 2, respectively. According to eq , with J = 2, this generates a complex permittivity, ϵ̃eff,2(ν̃), and a given absorbance spectrum, Q ext, 2 (given)(ν̃), which we computed using the Python package “PYMIESCATT”. As a result of step 1, we obtain the reconstructed complex effective permittivity ϵ̃eff, 2 (model)(ν̃).

In each case, the top plot compares the real part of the experimental effective permittivity (ϵ̃eff ′ , solid pale green) with the reconstructed real part (ϵ̃eff ′(model), dashed dark blue). The middle plot compares the imaginary part of the experimental effective permittivity (ϵ̃eff ″ , solid khaki) with the reconstructed imaginary part (ϵ̃eff ″(model), dashed magenta). These differences are considered indistinguishable for extracting the absorption bands associated with the major functional groups in the composite system. The findings reveal that the algorithm effectively reconstructs the pure absorption (the truth) of the functional groups for a wide range of mixtures at different volume fractions by separating the scattering contribution and thus correcting for spectral distortions, and in doing so, extracts the structural and molecular properties of the model system corresponding to the mixture.

Following step 1, step 2 separates the effective permittivity, ϵ̃eff (model)(ν̃), of the mixture into the individual complex permittivities of the two constituents of the mixture, i.e., ϵ̃ r 1 (ν̃) and ϵ̃ r 2 (ν̃), along with their respective volume ratios, V 1 and V 2. This is accomplished using the search algorithms grid search and gradient descent. The grid search algorithm explores the material database of pure IR spectra by evaluating the numerous combinations for the materials, ϵ̃ r j , with varying volume ratios, V j , j = 1, 2, that range from 0 to 100% in increments of 0.02%. At each step, the objective function, RSS­(V 1, V 2), for a two-component mixed-composition sphere is calculated using eq . The gradient descent algorithm follows a structured approach similar to grid search but is more efficient. It begins with a random initial guess of values for V 1 and V 2. The algorithm then iteratively updates the parameters V 1 and V 2 based on the gradient of RSS­(V 1, V 2), with respect to the parameters V 1 and V 2, continuing until convergence is achieved.

The results of step 2 are summarized in Table 1 of the Supporting Information, which compares the reconstructed constituents and volume fractions extracted from ϵ̃eff (model)(ν̃) with two different search algorithms (grid search and gradient descent) with the individual constituents and volume fractions in the 45 mixture samples (15 combinations each analyzed at 3 volume ratios). Both grid search and gradient descent algorithms successfully identified the individual components of the mixture in all 45 cases. They predicted volume fractions that are closely aligned with the true volume fractions of the components in the mixture. Furthermore, the volume fractions predicted by both methods are nearly identical, highlighting the high precision of the reconstruction algorithm. Overall, both algorithms demonstrated effective performance in reconstructing the chemical composition of the composite systems.

Despite the accuracy of the reconstruction method, both grid search and gradient descent come with some drawbacks. While grid search systematically explores the solution space, gradient descent can sometimes get trapped in “secondary minima” that represent solutions to the RSS optimization problem that are somewhat less optimal than the best, global, optimal solution. While grid search avoids getting trapped in secondary minima, it is also computationally more expensive, which may limit its applicability for more complex mixtures involving multiple components. Thus, gradient descent is better suited for higher-dimensional data analysis. Finally, care has to be taken that the grid search algorithm operates at a resolution fine enough to capture the nuances of the material properties adequately, especially in cases of near-identical volume fractions. This, however, can always be guaranteed by increasing the grid’s resolution, i.e., by reducing the grid spacing.

We now extend the reconstruction algorithm to a multicomponent system consisting of PMMA, PC, PDMS, PET, PEI, and PS, analyzed at six different sets of volume fractions: (a) 40%–10%–5%–15%–20%–10%, (b) 30%–20%–10%–5%–15%–20%, (c) 5%–5%–65%–5%–5%–15%, (d) 0%–0%–78%–3%–7%–12%, (e) 19%–13%–41%–8%–18%–1%, and (f) 16.67%–16.67%–16.67%–16.67%–16.67%–16.67%. In these ratios, a mixture labeled as 19%–13%–41%–8%–18%–1% PMMA–PC–PDMS–PET–PEI–PS indicates that the composite is composed of 19% poly­(methyl methacrylate) (PMMA), 13% polycarbonate (PC), 41% polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), 8% polyethylene terephthalate (PET), 18% poly­(ether imide) (PEI), and 1% polystyrene (PS).

As in the two-component case, we begin by generating the given extinction spectrum, Q ext, 6 (given)(ν̃), for the multicomponent system. Spectral inversion is then applied to fit the given Q ext, 6 (given)(ν̃) to the predicted extinction efficiency, Q ext, 6 (model)(ν̃), enabling the reconstruction of the complex effective permittivity, ϵ̃eff, 6 (model)(ν̃). Next, we perform the mixture deconvolution step, where we extract the complex permittivities of the individual components of the mixture, namely ϵ̃ r 1 (ν̃) for PMMA, ϵ̃ r 2 (ν̃) for PC, ϵ̃ r 3 (ν̃) for PDMS, ϵ̃ r 4 (ν̃) for PET, ϵ̃ r 5 (ν̃) for PEI, and ϵ̃ r 6 (ν̃) for PS, along with their respective volume fractions, V 1 for PMMA, V 2 for PC, V 3 for PDMS, V 4 for PET, V 5 for PEI, and V 6 for PS. These permittivities are extracted from the reconstructed effective permittivity, ϵ̃eff, 6 (model)(ν̃), using gradient descent, following the same procedure outlined for the two-component system.

Accuracy and Scaling of the Reconstruction Algorithm

To demonstrate the robustness, accuracy, and precision of our reconstruction algorithm, we conducted 5000 reconstructions of the volume fractions corresponding to a two-component 30–70% PC–PDMS composite system and 2000 reconstructions of the volume fractions corresponding to a six-component 19%–13%–41%–8%–18%–1% PMMA–PC–PDMS–PET–PEI–PS composite system. This involved performing spectral inversion (step 1) followed by mixture deconvolution (step 2) using grid search and gradient descent. The reconstructions were performed utilizing different initial conditions for the heights, ν̃ p eff (n), randomly sampled from an exponential distribution, supported on the interval [0, ∞), with a rate parameter of λ = 10 and a distribution mean (1/λ) of 0.1. This choice of rate parameter ensures that the randomly generated height values are uncorrelated and concentrated toward smaller values, which more accurately reflects the specific absorption characteristics of the materials under investigation, particularly with respect to their typical IR spectral features.

Since the width of a peak in the dielectric function changes if the height is changed, and thus, in this sense, width and height are connected, it is sufficient to modify either parameter to illustrate their effect on the reconstruction algorithm. To this end, we set the initial widths γeff (n) to 20 cm–1 and position the ν̃0eff (n), as described in the Methods section, in regions where significant absorption is likely to occur. Finally, the predictions from the 5000 reconstructed volume fractions for a two-component mixture and the 2000 reconstructed volume fractions for a multicomponent mixture are compiled into histograms with bin widths of 0.2% and compared to the expected volume ratios of the two-component mixture, i.e., 30% PC–70% PDMS, and of the multicomponent mixture, i.e., 19%–13%–41%–8%–18%–1% PMMA–PC–PDMS–PET–PEI–PS, respectively.

The reconstructed volume fractions primarily range from 27 to 30.5%. The mean is approximately 28.6%, indicating a reconstruction error of 1.4% from the true volume fractions of 30%. Turning now to the case of the multicomponent mixture, we apply the reconstruction algorithm to a multicomponent mixture of 19% PMMA–13% PC–41% PDMS–8% PET–18% PEI–1% PS. As shown in Figure , the predictions generated using gradient descent show volume fractions primarily ranging from 14 to 24% for PMMA, 10 to 14% for PC, 39.5 to 42% for PDMS, 6 to 11% for PET, 13 to 22% for PEI, and 0 to 5% for PS. For each component, the predicted values are centered around 19.6% (PMMA), 12.6% (PC), 41.2% (PDMS), 8.2% (PET), 17.8% (PEI), and 1.4% (PS), with standard deviations of approximately 2.1, 0.8, 0.5, 0.9, 1.8, and 1.3%, respectively. The median volume fraction in each case is 19.6% (PMMA), 12.8% (PC), 41.4% (PDMS), 8.0% (PET), 18.2% (PEI), and 1.1% (PS). An overview of the histograms of the individual components in the multicomponent mixture, displayed together in a single frame across the full volume fraction spectrum, is presented in the Supporting Information. Notably, PDMS, the largest component in the mixture, exhibits the most accurate predictions with the lowest standard deviation, followed by the second, third, and fourth largest components, i.e., PMMA, PEI, and PC, which also show fairly tight distributions around their true mean volume fractions. In contrast, the two low-concentration components, PET and PS, show more variability and relatively less accurate predictions. Consistent with the pattern observed in two-component mixtures, gradient descent applied to multicomponent mixtures also tends to underestimate the components present at lower mixing ratios (such as PET and PS) and to overestimate those with larger components, where the reconstructed ratios are closely aligned with their true values.

Reconstructions of the volume ratios of a 19%–13%–41%–8%–18%–1% PMMA–PC–PDMS–PET–PEI–PS multicomponent mixture were performed for 2000 randomly chosen initial conditions for ν̃ p eff (n) using gradient descent.

Concerning the scaling properties of our algorithm, the number of substances does not enter in step 1, since even many substances in a complex mixture produce only a single (complex) effective permittivity function that is reconstructed from a single given Q ext (given)(ν̃). This means that the performance of step 1 does not depend on the composition of the mixture, and the execution time of step 1 is essentially constant and independent of the number of components of the mixture. The computational burden becomes more noticeable in Step 2 of our algorithm. However, even for a 7-component mixture, step 2 amounts to a small fraction of the total execution time of our algorithm. These observations demonstrate that our algorithm scales well with the number of substances in the mixture. All computations were performed on a standard desktop computer (Intel i5-10310U CPU, 16 GB RAM, no GPU acceleration). To give an idea of the execution time of our method, a complete reconstruction, which includes both steps 1 and 2 of Figure , required about 30 min with the accuracy parameter of the EXCEL optimizer set to 10–14. We note that relaxing this parameter to 0.001 yields the same reconstruction quality while reducing the runtime to an average of about 5 min per reconstruction.

Conclusions

The paper presents a straightforward, efficient, cost-effective, and user-friendly method for analyzing mixtures with IR spectroscopy. The method was tested on two- and multicomponent systems that contain polymers with distinct but closely overlapping spectral regions, relevant for biological, chemical, and medical applications. Our data sets comprise multiple sample mixtures, each analyzed at different volume ratios of the individual polymers. The absorption spectrum of the individual components and their volume ratios were successfully determined, regardless of the complexity of the multicomponent system, based solely on the IR spectrum of the mixture. Using an automated, noninvasive approach, the method accounts for both spectral distortions due to scattering and complications due to overlapping peaks in the composite system. These features are particularly useful in analytical chemistry, pathology, polymer research, biomedicine, forensics, and quality control applications.
