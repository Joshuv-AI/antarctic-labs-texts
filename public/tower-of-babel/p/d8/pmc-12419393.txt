The degree of vascular calcification affects endovascular recanalization in chronic intracranial and extracranial large artery occlusion: A retrospective study
Authors: Lijuan Liu, Kun Zhang, Xudong Su, Jianghua Yu, Zengpin Liu, Cunhe Zhou, Binbin Wang, Yanying Zhao, Ruomeng Chen, Zhou Zhou, Minghan Zhao, Xiaoyun Liu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Chronic cerebral artery occlusion is an important cause of cerebral ischemic events. Endovascular recanalization is an effective treatment for this condition, but its success depends on appropriate patient selection and assessment. This is a retrospective study that collected patients with chronic cerebral artery occlusion who underwent endovascular recanalization to determine how imaging features from computed tomography angiography – including the extent of internal carotid artery occlusion, the number of calcified vessels, and the degree of calcification in the occluded vessels – affect the success rate of recanalization. A total of 147 patients were included, 103 (70.1%) were successfully recanalized. Lipoprotein(a) (Lp[a]) levels were significantly higher in the failed group (38.40 ± 34.41 mg/dL) compared to the successful group (22.93 ± 22.84 mg/dL) (P = .008). Among these patients, 49 underwent preoperative CTA, and 36 (73.5%) achieved successful recanalization. There was no significant association between the number of calcified vessels and the endovascular recanalization rate (P = 1.000). However, patients with a calcification degree >50% had a lower recanalization rate (40%) than those with 50% or less calcification (82.1%, P = .014), and the same result was seen in patients with chronic anterior circulation large artery occlusion (P = .019). In patients with chronic intracranial and extracranial large artery occlusion, the degree of vascular calcification in the occluded vessels significantly influences the success of endovascular recanalization.

1. Introduction

Stroke is the second leading cause of death globally, while ischemic stroke is 4 times more common than cerebral hemorrhage.[1] Chronic cerebral artery occlusion is the leading cause of ischemic stroke in China[2] and is becoming more and more prevalent in clinical practice with the increasing elderly population and advancements in diagnostic techniques. The patient’s clinical symptoms are closely related to the compensatory capacity of the collateral circulation of the intracranial arteries.

There is currently a lack of uniform clinical assessment and treatment protocols for chronic cerebral artery occlusion. Treatment of chronic cerebral artery occlusion includes pharmacological therapy and cerebral revascularization, which consists of extracranial-intracranial (EC-IC) bypass surgery and endovascular recanalization. According to the inclusion criteria of the COSS trial and the CMOSS trial, patients undergoing cerebral revascularization therapy should meet at least the following 2 conditions: the presence of clear clinically localized symptoms and the presence of hypoperfusion in the responsible vascular supply area confirmed by relevant imaging examinations. Endovascular recanalization effectively treats chronic cerebral artery occlusion[3] but is technically challenging. The recanalization treatment for chronic internal carotid artery (ICA) occlusion (CICAO) patients includes simple carotid endarterectomy (CEA), simple interventional therapy, and a combination of CEA and interventional treatment.

The risk of ischemic stroke in chronic cerebral artery occlusion patients remains high despite pharmacological treatment.[4] The CMOSS randomized clinical trial showed that EC-IC bypass surgery showed no benefit for stroke prevention in patients with atherosclerotic occlusion of the ICA or middle cerebral artery (MCA).[5] For CEA treatment, the success rate and surgical safety are within an acceptable range, but it is only applicable to short-segment occlusion of the carotid artery. However, intervention surgery is suitable for ICA in other segments and other arteries, and many patients choose it because of its minimal trauma.

Endovascular recanalization is difficult to perform, and the recanalization rate is suboptimal. Only selecting suitable cases ensures greater patient benefit. The imaging features of the lesion are important predictors of recanalization. Digital subtraction angiography (DSA) is the gold standard for the diagnosis of chronic cerebral artery occlusion, but it is invasive and costly, limiting its clinical application. High-resolution magnetic resonance vascular wall imaging (HRMR-VWI) can display plaque components of the occluded segment, such as lipid-rich necrosis core, calcification, intra-plaque hemorrhage, and others.[6] However, HRMR-VWI requires the support of advanced equipment and professional technicians. computed tomography angiography (CTA) is second only to DSA in terms of accuracy, as it can obtain the occlusion length and the first level of compensation. It has the advantage of displaying calcification, which makes it a commonly used test for the diagnosis of chronic occlusion.

Research suggests that the calcification’s impact on cerebrovascular diseases is inconsistent,[7] as vascular calcification can predict cardiovascular events and mortality.[8] One study found that calcification of the MCA had a protective effect on symptomatic infarction.[9] However, the impact of calcification evaluated by CTA on recanalization in chronic cerebral artery occlusion is unclear. Therefore, this study conducted a retrospective analysis of patients who underwent CTA examination before recanalization to evaluate the impact of calcification on the recanalization rate.

2. Materials and methods

2.1. Participants

We retrospectively analyzed patients with chronic intracranial and extracranial artery occlusion (including MCA, ICA, external carotid artery, vertebral artery, basilar artery, brachiocephalic trunk, subclavian artery, etc) who attempted interventional recanalization at the Second Hospital of Hebei Medical University from March 2021 to September 2023. All patients underwent ultrasound, CTA, magnetic resonance angiography, or DSA imaging before surgery.

Patients undergoing cerebral revascularization therapy should meet at least the following 2 conditions: the presence of clear clinically localized symptoms. The main clinical symptoms include TIA or mild to moderate ischemic stroke related to occluded vascular supply areas that occur within 12 months, with no or only mild to moderate neurological deficits [modified Rankin Scale (mRS) score ≤3 points]. Patients who experience recurrent ischemic events in occluded vascular supply areas after optimal drug treatment are most likely to benefit from cerebral revascularization therapy; relevant imaging examinations confirm the presence of hypoperfusion in the responsible vascular supply area.

If the patient met the following criteria, they were eligible for inclusion: ipsilateral transient ischemic stroke, ischemic stroke, or amaurosis lasting more than 1 month; patients who met the diagnostic criteria for chronic intracranial and extracranial artery atherosclerotic occlusion (occlusions lasting longer than 4 weeks are generally referred to as chronic arterial occlusions based on the patient’s clinical symptoms and imaging data); responsible vessel occlusion confirmed by DSA; drug therapy failed. Exclusion criteria: patients with incomplete medical records or unclear medical history; patients with arterial occlusion duration of less than 4 weeks; arterial dissection; intracranial hemorrhagic diseases: cerebral hemorrhage, subarachnoid hemorrhage; cardioembolic embolism. This study was approved by the Ethics Committee with an exemption from informed consent.

2.2. Equipments information

Siemens DSA machine (Artis zee) was used for imaging. In the MCA, we employed the Synchro micro-guidewire and Echelon microcatheter for initial vascular access and recanalization. Post-recanalization, we utilized the Gateway balloon for gradient dilation, followed by deployment of the Enterprise stent. For carotid artery and vertebral artery interventions, we selected either the Synchro micro-guidewire or the Boston Scientific PT micro-guidewire, in conjunction with the Echelon 10 microcatheter. Balloon dilation was performed using Bard balloons with varying diameters (2.5 × 20, 3 × 20, 4 × 20, 5 × 20 mm) tailored to the vessel size, employing a multi-balloon gradient dilation technique. In carotid artery, protection was achieved using the ev3 Emboshield (size selected based on vessel diameter), and carotid stenting was performed with either Boston Scientific or ev3 stents, with model selection based on the specific case requirements. Vertebral artery stenting was conducted with either Boston Scientific bare metal stents or Sinopharm drug-eluting stents, with model selection based on the clinical scenario. In cases of longer lesions involving the V2 segment, the Enterprise stent was generally preferred.

For subclavian artery interventions, we either used a Pterobalu guidewire for initial access or a combination of Boston Scientific PT micro-guidewire and Echelon 10 microcatheter for recanalization and a multi-balloon gradient dilation technique was utilized. Alternatively, peripheral balloons guided by a 0.035-inch guidewire were employed for dilation. Subclavian artery stenting was performed with either Boston Scientific or Abbott bare metal stents, with model selection based on the specific clinical context.

2.3. Clinical baseline data collection

Baseline data for all enrolled patients were collected, including demographic information and cerebrovascular disease risk factors such as gender, age, hypertension (systolic blood pressure ≥140 mmHg or diastolic blood pressure ≥90 mmHg), coronary heart disease, diabetes (glycated hemoglobin A1c (HbA1c) ≥6.5%; or fasting blood glucose (FPG) >7.0 mmol/L; or oral glucose tolerance test 2-hour blood glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L; or random blood glucose ≥11.1 mmol/L with typical hyperglycemic symptoms or hyperglycemic crisis), previous history of cerebral infarction, personal history of smoking and alcohol consumption, etc.

2.4. Cerebral and carotid CTA

We collected cerebral and carotid CTA images from patients and categorized the occluded vessels into anterior circulation arteries (common carotid artery, ICA, MCA), posterior circulation arteries (vertebral artery, basilar artery), subclavian arteries, and brachiocephalic trunk according to the location of the lesions. The occluded vessel lesions were further categorized into intracranial segments (MCA, ICA C2-C7 segments, basilar artery, vertebral artery V4 segments), extracranial segments (common carotid artery, ICA C1 segment, vertebral artery V1-V3 segment, subclavian artery, and brachiocephalic trunk), and simultaneous intracranial and extracranial segments.

The images were then imported into the RadiAnt DICOM Viewer and vascular calcification is diagnosed based on CT values >130 HU in CTA imaging. Then the following pathological features were evaluated (Fig. 1): the extent of the occlusion, the ICA was classified based on whether the occlusion was limited to or extended beyond the C1 segment; the number of calcified vessels, calcification was classified as massive vascular calcification (calcified vessels >5) and minor vascular calcification (calcified vessels ≤5) (including the MCA, ICA, external carotid artery, common carotid artery, vertebral artery, basilar artery, subclavian artery, brachiocephalic trunk, and aortic arch); the degree of calcification at the occluded site. Calcification was classified as mild/moderate calcification (calcification area ≤50% on cross-section) or severe calcification (calcification area >50%) in the occluded segment.

All enrolled patients underwent surgery according to standard procedures and guidelines. Endovascular recanalization surgeries were completed by our hospital’s fixed personnel intervention team, with mature and unified surgical procedures and plans. At least 2 experienced intervention experts performed surgeries. Successful recanalization was defined as modified Thrombolysis in Cerebral Infarction grade 3 and residual stenosis <50%.[10,11]

2.5. Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 software (Chicago). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as count (percentage). The Student t-test, Mann–Whitney U test, chi-square test, and Fisher exact test were used to compare patients’ clinical and imaging characteristics between successful and unsuccessful endovascular recanalization. Univariate and multivariate logistic regression were applied to identify independent predictive factors, and the area under the curve was calculated to evaluate the effectiveness of predictive scores. A P-value <.05 was considered statistically significant; all P-values were 2-tailed.

3. Results

From March 2021 to September 2023, a total of 147 patients were enrolled. The mean age was 59.1 ± 9.7 years; 75.5% were male, and the overall technical success rate was 70.1% (103/147). After endovascular recanalization treatment, most patients showed no symptom changes, so there was no change in mRS scores upon discharge. There were no deaths during the perioperative period. The incidence of complications was 6.8% (10/147), including subarachnoid hemorrhage, dissection, contrast agent leakage, thrombus formation, and thrombus detachment, which had no significant impact on the clinical outcomes of patients. The baseline characteristics are shown in Table 1. Compared with the successful group, the Lp(a) levels of the failed group were significantly increased. We also found that there was no significant difference in the recanalization rate among patients with different occluded vessels (P = .572) and occluded lesion locations (P = .666).

The effect of vascular calcification on the recanalization rate.

Endovascular recanalization is widely used in patients with anterior circulation large artery occlusion. To minimize potential confounding effects from variables such as recanalization site characteristics, we performed stratified analyses to evaluate the impact of vascular calcification on recanalization rates across different locations. Our results demonstrated that vascular calcification did not significantly affect the recanalization rate in the posterior circulation large arteries, subclavian arteries, or the brachiocephalic trunk, which may be attributable to the limited sample size in these subgroups. Among 38 chronic anterior circulation large occlusion patients, compared to patients with calcified occluded vessels (68.4%), patients with non-calcification in occluded vessels (73.7%) showed a higher recanalization rate (Table 3). In addition, we found that the recanalization rate was significantly higher in patients with no or mild calcification (cross-section calcification ≤50%) (82.1%) than in patients with severe calcification (cross-section calcification >50%) (40%, P = .019) (Table 3). These results indicate that severe calcification can affect the recanalization rate. Among the baseline characteristics of 38 patients, we observed that the failed group (54.55%) had a higher incidence of hyperhomocysteinemia than the successful group (18.52%, P = .026) (Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/P877). We conducted a telephone follow-up with a median duration of 26 (15–42) months for 38 patients with anterior circulation large artery occlusion, achieving successful follow-up in 28 cases. Among the 28 patients, 19 achieved successful vascular recanalization, with no recurrent cerebrovascular events observed. Of the 9 patients with failed recanalization, 1 experienced recurrent ischemic stroke. However, this difference was not statistically significant (P = .321). The sole recurrent ischemic stroke case involved a 35-year-old male with left C1 to 6 ICA occlusion, hypertriglyceridemia, and hyperhomocysteinemia. He suffered a recurrent cerebral infarction 1 year postoperatively after being discharged, with the mRS score of 2.

ICA occlusion is one of the major causes of ischemic stroke. Our analysis revealed that, compared to CICAO patients with calcification in occluded vessels (66.7%), non-calcification CICAO patients (75%) showed a higher recanalization rate (Table 3), but this difference was not statistically significant. However, as with the results observed in patients with anterior circulation large artery occlusion, the recanalization rate was significantly higher in CICAO patients with no or mild calcification (87.5%) than severe calcification (40%, P = .026) (Table 3). We also analyzed the effect of occluded lesions’ length and location on the recanalization rate. It was found that although the recanalization rate is higher in patients with occlusion limited to the C1 segment (100%, P = .102, Table S3, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/P877), this finding was not statistically significant. There was no significant difference in the recanalization rate among the patients with intracranial segment, extracranial segment, and simultaneous intracranial and extracranial segment vascular occlusion (P = .149, Table S4, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/P877).

4. Discussion

4.1. Lp(a) and vascular calcification

In patients with chronic intracranial and extracranial artery occlusion, our study found that Lp(a) levels of the successful group were significantly lower than those of the failed group through univariate analysis. But there was no significant correlation between Lp(a) levels and the degree of calcification as reflected by the number of calcified vessels. In Table S2, Supplemental Digital Content, https://links.lww.com/MD/P877, we evaluated factors particularly associated with recanalization in anterior circulation large artery occlusion patients. Here, no significant correlation was observed between Lp(a) levels and successful recanalization. This difference may be due to differences in sample size and subgroup characteristics, or it may reflect different potential mechanisms that affect the success of recanalization in different patient subgroups.

High levels of Lp(a) can increase the risk of cerebrovascular events[12] and cardiovascular disease.[13] Additionally, Lp(a) is associated with coronary artery calcification[14] and calcific aortic valve disease.[15] In this study, higher Lp(a) levels were associated with recanalization failure and difficulty, possibly because higher Lp(a) correlated with the degree of atherosclerosis or vascular calcification. In addition to Lp(a), blood lipid levels are a risk factor for atherosclerosis. There was no significant difference in total cholesterol, low-density cholesterol, and triglycerides between the successful and failed groups, which may be related to most patients’ use of lipid-lowering drugs that do not significantly affect serum Lp(a) levels.

4.2. Age and vascular calcification

Among patients who underwent CTA examination, patients with massive calcification were relatively older than those with minor calcification and were more likely to have hypertension and coronary heart disease, both of which showed statistically significant differences. This was consistent with other studies.[16,17] Furthermore, age emerged as an independent risk factor for massive calcification, similar to the finding in coronary artery calcification patients.[18] However, there was no significant difference in the number of calcified vessels and recanalization rate. Due to the small sample sizes, the statistically insignificant result does not necessarily mean that there is no clinically relevant association. Further studies with larger sample sizes are needed to clarify the relationship between them and to better understand the factors that influence recanalization.

4.3. The effect of vascular calcification on recanalization

Although we found no significant difference in the impact of the number of calcified vessels on recanalization rate, multiple studies have suggested that calcification increases the failure rate of chronic cerebral artery occlusion recanalization.[10,19] Additionally, anterior circulation large artery occlusion patients with non-calcification in occluded vessels had a higher recanalization rate than patients with calcification in our study. Many studies also have found that severe calcification is a predictor of recanalization failure in chronic coronary artery occlusion patients.[20,21] Therefore, we analyzed the impact of cross-section calcification on the recanalization and found that the failed group was more likely to have cross-section calcification in occluded vessels compared to the successful group. Still, there was no statistical significance, which may result from the small sample size and differences in other components of plaques.

Anterior circulation large artery occlusion is a major cause of cerebral ischemic events, and cerebral infarction and transient ischemic attack caused by ICA occlusion account for 10% to 15% of all intracranial ischemic events.[22] We, therefore, further analyzed the effect of calcification on endovascular recanalization in patients with chronic anterior circulation large artery occlusion and CICAO. Our research results supported the finding that severe calcification affects recanalization in chronic anterior circulation large artery occlusion. Compared with patients with severe calcification (cross-section calcification area >50%), patients with no and mild calcification on the occluded vessels had a higher recanalization rate, and the same result was observed in CICAO patients. Calcification and other hard components may hinder the guide wire from passing through the chronic occluded area. Therefore, the severity of calcification appears to have an impact on the recanalization, rather than the overall situation of cerebral artery calcification. This difference emphasizes that the location and severity of calcification in the occluded area may play a more important role in the recanalization rate than the total number of calcified vessels. Apart from calcification, this study lacks research and evidence on the relationship between other components and recanalization rate, possibly due to the difficulty in detecting other components by CTA. However, research suggests that extensive hyperintense signals (HIS) occlusion on MR-VWI is associated with successful recanalization, possibly because HIS lesions represent intra-plaque bleeding or fresh thrombi, indicating more soft tissue in the lesion and easier passage of the guide wire.[10]

4.4. The effect of other factors on recanalization

In addition to vascular calcification, we also found that in anterior circulation artery occlusion patients, the incidence of hyperhomocysteinemia was significantly higher in the failed group than the successful group. Homocysteine is a risk factor for atherosclerosis and stroke. Higher homocysteine levels were associated with calcified plaques and vascular disease.[23] Therefore, hyperhomocysteinemia may aggravate atherosclerosis and calcification, leading to difficulty in recanalization.

When analyzing the CTA imaging results of CICAO patients with, we found that the length and location of the occluded lesions did not significantly affect the recanalization rate. However, previous studies have found that the occlusion length can also affect the recanalization rate in CICAO patients and chronic coronary artery occlusion patients.[10,24,25] Thus, the recanalization rate of chronic cerebral artery occlusion is influenced by multiple factors, not only by the degree of vascular calcification. Therefore, we need to consider multiple factors when evaluating patients, but the degree of vascular calcification can still help us screen and evaluate patients to some extent.

4.5. Study limitations

This study has several limitations. It is a single-center study with a small sample size. Some analyses in our study may lack sufficient statistical power due to the small sample sizes in some subgroups. Thus, there may be an increased risk of overlooking potentially important trends. Although they may not meet statistical significance, they may still be clinically important or indicate trends that warrant further study in larger studies. Future multi-center studies with large sample sizes may uncover additional factors affecting endovascular recanalization in chronic cerebral artery occlusion. Additionally, we mainly focused on the calcification of the occluded vessel and did not analyze the impact of other components of the occlusion site (such as hemorrhage, fibrosis, etc) or other vessel characteristics (such as degree of vessel curvature, degree of collapse, morphology of the occluded end, etc) on recanalization. There are many confounding factors that affect recanalization and we referred to the CAO score to select patients with a high probability of recanalization, which partially mitigates the influence of confounding factors, but larger datasets are still needed to validate these findings and explore the impact of additional variables.

5. Conclusion

Age is an independent risk factor for increased vascular calcification, with older age associated with more extensive calcification. In chronic intracranial and extracranial artery occlusion, the number of calcified vessels does not significantly affect the recanalization rate. However, severe calcification in occluded vessels can impacts the recanalization success in chronic intracranial and extracranial large artery occlusion and chronic anterior circulation large artery occlusion patients.
