Diagnostic performance of a 250-ml net ultrafiltration challenge to identify risk of preload-dependence in critically ill patients undergoing continuous renal replacement therapy: a randomized, cross-over trial
Authors: Charlotte Biscarrat, Guillaume Deniel, Matthieu Chivot, Hodane Yonis, Louis Chauvelot, Mehdi Mezidi, Jean-Christophe Richard, Laurent Bitker
Published: 2025

Introduction

During renal replacement therapy (RRT), net fluid removal by mean of net ultrafiltration (UFNET) may help control fluid balance and prevent fluid overload. Nevertheless, uncertainty exists about the optimal rate of UFNET. Indeed, while a lower rate of fluid removal may be associated with prolonged exposure to tissue edema and organ dysfunction, a faster rate may lead to hemodynamic instability associated with renal replacement therapy (HIRRT) [1, 2]. As a consequence, HIRRT commonly leads to discontinuation of UFNET by clinicians, fueling the vicious circle of fluid overload [3].

However, HIRRT by hypovolemia may only occur if UFNET rate exceeds plasma inputs (from IV medications and interstitial transfer) in a preload-dependent patient in whom cardiac output falls. The prevalence of HIRRT associated with preload-dependence reaches 50% during continuous RRT (CRRT), suggesting that some HIRRT episodes may not be related to a state of RRT-induced hypovolemia [4, 5]. Also, preload-dependence outside a HIRRT episode is associated with the incidence of preload-dependent HIRRT [4].

On the other hand, patients who are preload-independent, although deemed at lower risk of presenting with preload-dependent HIRRT, may fall into a preload-dependent state if their heart function is situated close to the tipping point of the Frank-Starling curve [6, 7]. This situation is not clinically detectable by passive leg raising or other dynamic evaluations which increase cardiac preload, leaving the clinician blind to the patient’s risk of developing preload-dependent HIRRT.

We hence hypothesized that the fast removal of a small ultrafiltrate volume – a UFNET challenge – would stress the cardiac preload reserve, and would help detect preload-independent patients who would fall into a preload-dependent state in case of excessive fluid removal. The primary objective of our study was to evaluate the diagnostic performance of the UFNET challenge to identify de novo preload-dependence appearing after the challenge in preload-independent patients undergoing CRRT.

Methods

Study design

We conducted a single-center, randomized, open-label, cross-over, proof-of-concept trial in a 15-bed intensive care unit in Lyon, France. The study protocol (Additional File 1) complied with the Declaration of Helsinki, was approved by an ethics committee (CPP Sud-Est VI Clermont Ferrand 2021-A02939-32) and was registered on ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT05214729). Informed consent was obtained from all participants or their representative. The present report followed recommendations of the CONSORT and STARD consensus (Additional File 2) [8, 9].

Population

To be eligible, subjects had to fulfil all the following inclusion criteria: age 18 years or older, with invasive mechanical ventilation with a PEEP ≥ 5 cmH2O under deep continuous general anesthesia (defined by a Richmond Analgesia and Sedation Scale [10] ≤ −4), with stage 3 KDIGO (Kidney Disease – Improving Global Outcome) acute kidney injury [11], treated with CRRT for less than 14 days, equipped with calibrated continuous cardiac output monitoring (PiCCO®, Pulsion Medical System, Feldkirchen, Germany) mandated by acute circulatory failure, and with an expected intensive care unit length of stay of 24 h or more.

Exclusion criteria included hyperlactatemia defined by an arterial lactate concentration > 4 mmol.L−1, calibrated cardiac index (CI) < 2.0 L.min−1.m−2 or > 4.0 L.min−1.m−2, and preload-dependence assessed by a positive postural maneuver performed within the 2 h preceding eligibility evaluation and whose methodology is described below [12]. These criteria were chosen to exclude patients in whom the UFNET challenge was either deemed irrelevant (high CI, and/or preload-dependence) or at high risk of generating HIRRT (high lactate and low CI). Other exclusion criteria included extracorporeal membrane oxygenation (ECMO), active hemorrhage requiring transfusion, ischemic or hemorrhagic stroke, fulminant hepatitis, patient receiving maintenance dialysis or kidney graft recipient, passive leg raising non achievable (lower limb amputation, abdominal compartment syndrome, inferior vena cava obstruction), pregnancy or breast feeding, active decision of withdrawal of care, moribund patient, or patient already included in the study.

Interventions

The studied diagnostic test was a UFNET challenge, whose aim was to unmask the potential shift from a preload-independent state to a preload-dependent state in patients undergoing CRRT. The challenge consisted in the removal of 250 ml of ultrafiltrate (i.e. plasma water) over 15 min (fast challenge) or 30 min (slow challenge), by setting a UFNET flow rate of 990 ml/h (the highest UFNET rate applicable with our generators) or 500 ml/h on the CRRT generator, respectively (Supplemental Figure 1). A 250-ml volume was chosen as the best compromise between safety (i.e. ∽5% reduction in blood volume) and efficacy to detect a relative change in cardiac output. A maximum duration of 30 min was deemed adequate to limit the confounding effect of plasma refilling.

Enrolled patients performed both challenges, following a cross-over design, with a wash-out period of at least 24 h between them (and a maximum of 48 h). The duration of the wash-out period was empirically decided; a shorter duration was deemed at-risk of exposing the second challenge to the persisting effects of the first challenge, while a longer duration would have led to some patients being either deceased, weaned from CRRT, sedation or from the CCI monitoring device.

The order of challenge application (slow followed by fast, or fast followed by slow) was determined by randomization, using sealed envelopes. Investigators did not have access to the randomization allocation sequence. Given the interventions’ nature, blinding was not feasible. Other details regarding trial’s interventions are given in Supplemental Methods.

Study primary and secondary outcomes

The primary endpoint was the diagnostic performance of the UFNET challenge, indistinctive of its type (15 or 30 min), by quantifying the relative variation in calibrated CI measured by transpulmonary thermodilution (predictor) between immediately before and after the UFNET challenge (∆CITPTD,UFC), and assessed against the preload-dependence status adjudicated using the variation in continuous cardiac index (CCI) induced by a postural maneuver (either passive leg raising or Trendelenburg) performed immediately after the UFNET challenge (reference diagnostic test) [12, 13]. Based on the result of this postural maneuver, challenges were classified as responder if positive, and non-responder if negative.

A diagnostic fluid challenge, the conventional reference method to evaluate preload-responsiveness, was not considered in the protocol to classify UFNET challenges, as this intervention appeared clinically irrelevant, and potentially deleterious, in patients with hemodynamic stability and preload-independence at time of eligibility assessment.

Secondary endpoints evaluated the optimal threshold of ∆CITPTD,UFC during UFNET challenges (regardless of their duration) to diagnose challenge response; the respective diagnostic performance of fast and slow challenges and related optimal thresholds; the effects of UFNET challenges on systemic hemodynamics based on challenge response status (as defined by the postural maneuver performed after the UFNET challenge); and the hemodynamic tolerance of the UFNET challenge in the 8 h follow-up period.

An a posteriori defined secondary outcome consisted in the evaluation of the predictive performance of the relative change in CCI during the postural maneuver performed before UFNET challenges (∆CCIPM,PRE) to predict the occurrence of preload-dependence after the challenge, as defined above.

Hemodynamic monitoring

Cardiac output was measured by transpulmonary thermodilution (PiCCO®, Pulsion Medical System, Feldkirchen, Germany), performed by mean of a triplicate injection of 15 ml of cold saline on the medial port of the central venous line positioned in the superior vena cava territory, and indexed to body surface using Dubois’s formula.

Each challenge was preceded and followed by a postural maneuver, within a maximum timeframe of 10 min before and after the challenge. The postural maneuver consisted of a 1-min passive leg raising test in patients in the supine position (deemed positive if CCI increased by more than 10% during the test), or a Trendelenburg maneuver in those in the prone position (positive if CCI increased > 8%) [12, 13]. Also, given the potential confounding effects of the autonomous nervous system response during study procedures, enrolled patients were to have a RASS value ≤ −4 at time of enrolment. Postural maneuvers, quality checks of hemodynamic signals and deep sedation management were protocolized and are described in Supplemental Methods.

CRRT settings

Patients enrolled in the study were treated with CRRT, using either continuous veno-venous hemofiltration or hemodialysis. Indication, choice of CRRT modality, and CRRT settings were made by the clinician in charge, following international recommendations and a unit-based protocol [11, 14]. Details regarding CRRT techniques and settings are given in Supplemental Methods.

Safety

During the 8 h following each UFNET challenge, HIRRT episodes were systematically recorded hourly by the bedside nurse. Hemodynamic instability episodes were defined as follows: hypotension defined by a mean arterial pressure < 65 mmHg and requiring a therapeutic intervention (vasopressor introduction or increase, and/or fluid bolus therapy), de novo tachycardia by a heart rate > 120 min−1, new onset or extension mottles, or a drop in CCI > 15% [5]. Interventions in response to HIRRT were left at the clinician’s discretion. Any other adverse event occurring during trial interventions were reported in the case report form.

Study population

The study’s population consisted in all included challenges (intention-to-treat, ITT). We also considered a modified intention-to-treat population (mITT), consisting of all UFNET challenges, after the exclusion of those that were a posteriori identified as being preload-dependent prior to challenge application. The primary outcome and safety were evaluated in the ITT population. The primary and secondary outcomes were evaluated in the mITT population.

Sample size calculation

Assuming a prevalence of 50% of preload-dependence (vs. preload-independence) identified by the postural maneuver performed after the UFNET challenge [12], 34 challenges (irrespective of their duration – slow or fast) were to be performed to identify an AUROC of 0.80 with a 95% confidence interval of 0.65 to 0.95 (i.e. an accuracy of ± 15%). This number was increased by 15% in order to take into account enrolled patients who underwent a single challenge, resulting in a total number of 40 UFNET challenges in 20 patients.

Statistical analysis

Data collection methods are described in Supplemental Methods. Statistical analyses were performed using R software, with packages lme4, emmeans and cutpointr [15–17]. A P value below 0.05 was chosen for statistical significance. Data are presented as median [interquartile range], or count (percentage) as appropriate. Missing data was not imputed.

Between-groups comparisons (UFNET challenge type, challenge response status) of the hemodynamic parameters obtained before, during and after the ultrafiltration challenges was done using mixed effects linear regression, with the patient identification number as random intercept, to take into account the potential repetition of measurements in a given subject. Interaction terms between these explanatory variables – UFNET challenge type (fast vs. slow) and challenge response status (responders vs. non-responders) – were systematically checked for when inserted in models. In case of a significant interaction term, we performed post-hoc comparisons exploring statistical differences between fast and slow challenges, adjusted for the repetition of tests using Tukey’s method. Given the cross-over design, interaction of UFNET challenge type with the visit order (first vs. second) was also checked for to evaluate carry-over effects. P values were bootstrapped over 500 samples.

Diagnostic performance of challenges was assessed by computation of the area under the receiver-operating curve (AUROC). AUROCs, specificity, sensitivity, and positive and negative predictive values were expressed with their 95% confidence interval, determined using bootstrapping (500 samples). The Youden method was used to identify the best threshold if the AUROC was significantly different from 0.50. AUROCs of fast and slow challenges were compared using Delong’s test only if their respective AUROCs were significantly different from 0.50.

Evaluation of the primary outcome and safety in the ITT population

Irrespective of UFNET challenge type, 33% of challenges (12/36, 95% confidence interval: 19% to 51%) were associated with preload-dependence after the challenge. The AUROC of ∆CITPTD,UFC to predict challenge response was 0.74 (95% confidence interval: 0.58–0.88) and was significantly different from 0.50 (P = 0.02). The respective AUROCs of slow and fast challenges did not significantly differ from 0.50 and were not compared (Supplemental Table 1).

HIRRT episodes (52 hourly reports, over a total of 288 h of follow-up) were significantly more frequent after slow challenges, compared to fast challenges, with episodes mainly occurring during the first two hours of follow-up (Supplemental Figure 2). Challenge response status was not associated with the rate of HIRRT episodes (P = 0.62). No severe adverse event in relation with the trial was reported.

Hemodynamic effects of UFNET challenges in the mITT population

Five challenges (4 fast, 1 slow, all related to the first visit) were a posteriori identified as being preload-dependent prior to challenge start, leaving 31 challenges (in 17 patients) in the mITT population (15 fast, 16 slow, Supplemental Table 2)

In this population and irrespective of UFNET challenge type, 29% of challenges (9/31, 95% confidence interval: 15% to 48%) led to de novo preload dependence after the challenge (i.e. challenge responders). No significant difference in baseline hemodynamic parameters between responders and non-responders was observed (Supplemental Table 3).

In challenge responders, ∆CITPTD,UFC varied by −13 [−14; −9] %, vs. −2 [−7; + 2] % in non-responders (P = 0.02, Fig. 2, panel A). Diagnostic performance of UFNET challenges in the mITT population

∆CITPTD,UFC was significantly and negatively associated with the relative change in CCI during the postural maneuver performed after the challenge (Supplemental Figure 3, panel A). The AUROC of ∆CITPTD,UFC to diagnose challenge response was 0.83 (95% confidence interval: 0.66 to 0.99, P < 0.01), with an optimal threshold of −9% (95% confidence interval: −13% to −5%) (Supplemental Table 4). Slow challenges’ AUROC did not reach statistical significance, and was not compared to fast challenges’ AUROC.

Discussion

In this single-center, randomized, cross-over trial, we evaluated the diagnostic performance of a UFNET challenge removing 250 ml of ultrafiltrate in 15 or 30 min to predict preload-dependence incidence in preload-independent patients undergoing CRRT. Our results showed that 1/preload-dependence appeared in approximately a third of challenges, with no statistically significant difference in this rate between fast and slow challenges; 2/a decrease in calibrated CI > 9% induced by the removal of 250 ml diagnosed de novo preload-dependence with acceptable diagnostic performance; 3/no statistical difference in the diagnostic performance of fast vs. slow challenges was identified, in relation with comparable hemodynamic effects of both challenges; and 4/the relative variation in CCI during the postural maneuver preceding the challenge demonstrated good performance to predict de novo preload dependence after the challenge at an optimal threshold of ≥ 5%.

Prediction of HIRRT is a challenging problem in patients undergoing CRRT, and has important clinical implications as inadequate UFNET flow rates (either excessive or insufficient) are associated with worse outcome [18, 19]. A preload-dependent status outside an HIRRT episode may help predict the risk of HIRRT associated with preload-dependence [4]. On the other hand, although half of HIRRT episodes were associated with preload-dependence in the intervention arm of the GO NEUTRAL trial, only a quarter of the total number of episodes were preceded by preload-dependence at the previous systematic evaluation [5]. This implies that preload-independent patients remain at risk of switching from a preload-independent state to a preload-dependent state, which increases their HIRRT risk. Our results confirm this trend, with a third of challenges inducing preload-dependence in relation with the absolute reduction in cardiac preload.

In a non-randomized prospective study, Jacquet-Lagrèze et al. evaluated the hemodynamic impact of a 500-ml fluid removal (i.e. approx. 10% of plasma water in a 70 kg patient) by UFNET over 1 h (i.e. a fluid removal rate corresponding to slow challenges’ rate) in both preload-dependent and independent post-cardiac surgery patients undergoing CRRT [20]. In this study, 23% of patients presented with a significant drop in CI, defined by a decrease ≥ 12%, an incidence and cutoff value close to those identified in our trial. In our study, the removal of 250 ml of ultrafiltrate appears to have the potential to decrease venous return and stress the cardiac preload reserve, especially if performed over a short period of time. The ultrafiltrate volume removed in our trial corresponds to approximately 5% of plasma water volume in a 70 kg adult, which would translate into an estimated reduction in GEDVI of 40 ml.m−2 (i.e. close to the value observed in challenge responders) in the absence of plasma refilling or IV fluid input. In line with this hypothesis, we observed a significant relationship between GEDVI and CI in preload responders, supporting the relevance of the intervention to adequately evaluate the patient’s specific heart function curve. Also, knowing that a passive leg raising maneuver mobilizes an estimated volume of 300 ml and is deemed positive if the CCI increases > 10% in preload-dependent patients [21, 22], it is interesting to underline that a 250-ml UFNET challenge which induced preload-dependence decreased CI by approximately 13% in our study. Our hypothesis is that stressing the heart’s function curve leftward (i.e. decreased preload) is equivalent to stressing it rightward (with a postural maneuver) once preload has decreased in patients situated close to the tipping point of the heart function curve. Yet, we also observed that the reduction in GEDVI in challenge non-responders was non-significantly lower than that measured in challenge responders, implying that the UFNET challenge may not have sufficiently reduced blood volume and cardiac preload to evaluate its effect on cardiac index in some patients.

The duration over which the challenge is applied may be of physiological importance, as its impact on vascular stressed volume, mean systemic filling pressure and venous return will be opposed by plasma refilling inward flux [7, 23]. Challenges performed over 15 or 30 min (as in the present study) or one hour (as in the Jacquet-Lagrèze study) may consequently perform differently based on the plasma refilling rate, whose intensity will depend on the patient’s medical condition, adrenergic and neurohormonal responses, and on the hydrostatic gradient existing between intravascular and extravascular compartments. However, and against this hypothesis, we did not observe statistically significant hemodynamic variations between fast and slow challenges in our study, although there was a non-significant trend in greater reduction in CI and GEDVI with fast challenges, compared to slow challenges. Yet, we observed that, contrary to our initial hypothesis, slow challenges were significantly associated with a higher HIRRT rate after the challenge, compared to fast challenges. The absence of interaction of this association with the order in which challenges were performed (slow then fast vs. fast then slow) does not support a long-lasting/persisting negative effect of the first challenge over the second. Also, that fact that HIRRT rate was not associated with the challenge response (preload-dependence or not) suggests that this result was not related to the intervention. Yet, this safety warning mandates that future evaluations focus on the immediate and long-lasting hemodynamic tolerance of UFNET challenges.

Preload-dependence after the challenge (the reference test in our trial) was evaluated using a postural maneuver, which inherently exposes to the risk of false negative and false positive tests. Especially, the effects of the autonomous nervous system response may confound postural maneuvers conclusions, and led to our choice of performing UFNET challenges only in patients under deep sedation. In a large meta-analysis, Cherpanath et al. identified a sensitivity of 86% and a specificity of 92% of the passive leg raising test when using fast-responding CCI measurement methods, which implies that our reference test may be exposed to incorrect adjudication regarding the preload-dependence status of our patients [24]. We acknowledge that this is a potentially important limitation of our trial. Yet, although the reference evaluation of preload-dependence consists in performing a 500-ml fluid challenge, this intervention is not devoid of side effects in a population at high risk of fluid overload, and whose preload-independent status without hemodynamic instability suggested that a fluid bolus was clinically irrelevant and potentially dangerous [25].

Our study showed that the pre-challenge postural maneuver had the ability to detect preload-independent patients situated close to the tipping point of the Frank-Starling curve and who would fall into preload-dependence after the challenge, at a cutoff value of + 5%. This exploratory result, evaluated in the mITT population, supports the hypothesis that using a lower threshold of CCI change during a postural maneuver in preload-independent patients may help clinicians to detect at-risk patients and adjust their strategy in terms of fluid removal and UFNET rate setting. Given the fact that this analysis was not planned and not evaluated in the ITT population, it will require confirmation in future validation studies.

Two clinical implications based on the high negative predictive values of the presented diagnostic tests may be formulated. First, based on a pre-test clinical assessment concluding to high HIRRT risk in a preload-independent patient, a postural maneuver showing a change in CCI < 5% would suggest that de novo preload-dependence is unlikely if fluid removal is applied. This diagnostic strategy appears clinically safe and relevant because 1/it does not imply the subsequent or systematic application of the UFNET challenge to predict the switch to preload-dependence, hence not exposing the patient to inadequate fluid removal; and 2/it will prevent the application of higher UFNET rate settings if the postural maneuver is positive. Second, if the pre-test clinical assessment deems HIRRT risk as being low in a preload-independent patient, a UFNET challenge might be performed using calibrated CI values to identify preload-dependence occurrence. Yet, this intervention which stresses the cardiovascular system is not devoid of risk, as this might expose the patient to inadequate fluid removal and hypovolemia, and in some, a greater probability of presenting with HIRRT, and should hence be balanced against expected clinical benefits (mainly faster control of fluid overload). However, and especially because performing the UFNET challenge might be potentially deleterious, these two potential applications will require to be tested in future studies. Furthermore, our protocol did evaluate the optimal timing at which these diagnostic procedures would be best employed, although we may consider that they were performed relatively early after resuscitation given the clinical severity of our population.

The strength of our study is the rich documentation of hemodynamic effects of the UFNET challenge, using advanced cardiac output monitoring and systematic cardiac output calibration by transpulmonary thermodilution. The prospective and cross-over design removes the risk of bias induced by time and allowed the comparison of two different lengths of application of the challenge, the patient serving as his own control to reduce variability.

However, our study has several limitations. First, the studied population corresponds to selected patients at high HIRRT risk (being under CRRT, mechanical ventilation and frequently receiving vasopressors), all equipped with a CCI monitoring device. Second, some patients were a posteriori identified as being preload-dependent after inclusion, which led to their exclusion and the unplanned analysis of the mITT population. Consequently, the study may have lacked statistical power to detect significant differences when considering this population, given the fact that the number of challenges studied in mITT did not reach the predefined sample size. However, the mITT analysis after exclusion of these cases demonstrated similar results, compared to ITT, implying that selection bias may be limited. Third, the respective low numbers of slow and fast challenges may have impacted our ability to detect statistical differences when comparing challenge types. Also, baseline clinical characteristics may have differed between slow and fast challenges, especially in terms of fluid balance and clinical severity at challenge’s start, which may have affected our conclusions drawn from these analyses. Fourth, we made the choice to study the removal of 250 ml of ultrafiltrate of 15 or 30 min, which may be considered excessive in the context of acute circulatory failure. However, patients were continuously monitored, and were enrolled based on the absence of preload-dependence as a safety criterion. Lower volumes (e.g. 100 ml of ultrafiltrate) have never been tested but may prove insufficient to detect variations in calibrated CI, while longer duration of application (with lower UFNET flow rates) have been shown unable to detect significant changes in cardiac output [20]. Finally, the trial design did not include a parallel arm with a control group, in which a UFNET challenge was not performed. This would have been especially relevant to conclude firmly regarding the safety of the UFNET challenge in the 8 h following its realization.

Conclusions

In the specific setting of our center, a UFNET challenge removing 250 ml of ultrafiltrate allowed the uncovering of a preload-dependence state in one third of preload-independent patients undergoing CRRT. A decrease in CI of 9% or more after UFNET challenge demonstrated acceptable diagnostic performance to identify the subgroup of patients becoming preload-dependent, with no detectable difference in the diagnostic performance of fast and slow challenges. Finally, a postural maneuver inducing a CCI increase ≥ 5% showed good predictive performance to identify patients at risk of switching from preload-independence to preload-dependence at high UFNET flow rates. Future research should focus on the assessment of challenges’ hemodynamic safety and the validation of the identified diagnostic thresholds in larger cohorts.
