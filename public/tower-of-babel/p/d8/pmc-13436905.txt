A qualitative study of emergency management ability of anesthetist nurses
Authors: Zihan Jiang, Qian Song, Jie Cao, Quyun Zhang, Dongni Shi, Wenjing Jiao, Xinyan Zhu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTBackground:In China, most nurse anesthetists enter the profession from a general nursing background, often lacking specific expertise in anesthesia, procedural competencies, and Emergency management skills initially. Moreover, the absence of a uniform standardized training framework for new nurse anesthetists hinders their ability to respond to crisis and provide comprehensive anaesthesia care.Objectives:This study aims to exploring the components of anesthesia nurses’ emergency management abilities and to provide a foundation for establishing a comprehensive and systematic emergency management competency system for anesthesia nurses.Methods:A descriptive phenomenological research method was employed. Using purposive sampling, 13 anesthesia nurses and department managers from the anesthesia department of a Grade A tertiary general hospital were selected for semistructured interviews from January to February 2026. Thematic analysis was utilized for data analysis.Results:Four primary themes and thirteen subthemes were identified through the analysis. The themes include: (1) Emergency Response Knowledge Base; (2) Emergency Response Competency; (3) Teamwork and Communication Skills (4) Psychological Resilience and Reflective Practice. These themes and subthemes provide a detailed and structured overview of participants’ experiences, and primary challenges from the training.Conclusions:This study demonstrates that the emergency management competency system for anesthesia nurses should be based on a foundational framework comprising an Emergency Response Knowledge Base, Emergency Response Competency, Teamwork and Communication Skills, Psychological Resilience, and Reflective Practice. Particular emphasis should be placed on the content and implications of Emergency Response Competency as well as Psychological Resilience and Reflective Practice. This approach is intended to refine a more comprehensive and rigorous assessment system for the emergency management competency of anesthesia nurses.

Introduction

With the expansion of anesthetic services, the role of nurse anesthetists has extended beyond the preoperative and intraoperative settings to include specialized areas such as pain clinics and gastrointestinal endoscopy. Consequently, there is a growing clinical demand for nurse anesthetists in terms of both quality and quantity.[12] During anesthesia, factors such as patients’ pre-existing conditions, residual anesthetic agents, and surgical trauma increase the risk of critical incidents, thereby raising patient safety concerns. Safety-related crises in anesthesia directly impact routine clinical workflows and the nurse-patient relationship.[34] In Mainland China, the development of anesthetic nursing started relatively late and has progressed slowly.[56] Most nurse anesthetists transition from general nursing or other clinical departments, and often lack foundational theoretical knowledge and procedural skills in anesthesia during their careers, leaving them ill-prepared to manage emergencies in anesthetic practice.[7] Research on the Emergency management competencies and training curricula for nurse anesthetists in China remains limited. Existing studies have primarily focused on isolated technical skills and teaching methods,[8] nurse anesthetists widely recognized as highly stressful and challenging, often accompanied by considerable occupational and positional turnover.[910] Investigating the components and key challenges of crisis response capabilities among novice nurse anesthetists from the dual perspectives of both nurses and managers will contribute to refining the training framework for nurse anesthetists, thus supporting their professional development. Therefore, there is a clear need to update training programs for nurse anesthetists and enhance their crisis response competencies.

Therefore, this study aims to examine the experiences and major challenges faced by novice nurse anesthetists in crisis response training, explore managers’ recommendations for training implementation, and analyze issues encountered during the training process. The findings are intended to inform the further development and optimization of a structured training curriculum for nurse anesthetists.

Methods

Study design

This study employed a qualitative descriptive methodology,[11] utilizing one-on-one, semistructured interviews to explore participants’ experiences and suggestions regarding the training of new nurse anesthetists. The qualitative approach is well-suited to investigate how individuals perceive and interpret phenomena, as it facilitates an in-depth exploration of subjective experiences, which are shaped by participants’ unique backgrounds, personal interests, and perspectives.

Participants

Participants were recruited from a tertiary general hospital in Beijing, China, using a purposive sampling strategy with maximum variation.[12] This approach, suitable for exploratory qualitative research, was employed to capture a wide spectrum of perspectives and to understand perceptions, experiences, and systemic training challenges from both teaching and learning standpoints.[13] The sample comprised nurse anesthetists and anesthesiologists. Inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) anesthetists nurse with more than 5 years of clinical experience, and (2) administrators with over 10 years of professional experience. These criteria ensured the representation of diverse viewpoints across roles and experience levels. Prior to data collection, written informed consent was obtained via email, accompanied by detailed study information. The final sample consisted of 13 participants: seven junior nurse anesthetists, three anesthesia nursing educators/administrators, and four anesthesiologist educators/administrators. Data collection

This study adopted an exploratory qualitative design.[11] Data were collected through in-depth, semistructured interviews employing open-ended questions. The interview protocol was initially developed through collaborative discussions within the research team, informed by a comprehensive literature review. Prior to formal data collection, a pilot interview was conducted to refine the guide and test the recording equipment. Subsequently, the research team critically reviewed and revised the questions to enhance their relevance and clarity.

All interviews were conducted by the first author, who has received advanced training in qualitative interview methods, between March and May 2024. To accommodate participants, sessions were held either via Tencent Meetings or in person, based on individual preference and availability. Before each interview, participants were contacted to confirm their understanding of the study aims and to schedule a mutually convenient time. During the interviews, methodological rigor was maintained by adhering to a neutral stance and utilizing established qualitative techniques, including active listening, probing, and reflective responses.[14] Both verbal and nonverbal cues were documented. With participants’ consent, all interviews were audio-recorded to ensure accuracy, each lasting approximately 30–60 min to allow for a thorough exploration of the topics.

Following each interview, the researcher promptly transcribed and initially coded the content in Chinese. These transcripts were then returned to the respective participants for member checking to confirm accuracy and enhance the credibility of the data.[15] For the purpose of international dissemination, the verified Chinese transcripts were translated into English under the supervision of a bilingual coauthor with native-level proficiency in both languages and extensive international experience.

The sample size was guided by the principle of information power, which considers the study’s aim, sample specificity, theoretical framework, dialogue quality, and analysis strategy.[16] Recruitment concluded after 13 interviews, as ongoing analysis indicated that the data had reached sufficient richness and descriptive depth to provide novel and meaningful insights relevant to the research questions. Data analysis and quality control

The data were analyzed following thematic analysis method,[15] using a systematic thematic inductive approach. The analysis proceeded as follows: First, the primary researchers (the first two authors) meticulously transcribed each interview verbatim and thoroughly reviewed all transcripts to immerse themselves in the data and note initial impressions. Second, significant statements relevant to the research objectives were manually identified and extracted using inductive coding. Third, recurring patterns were iteratively coded, with coding consistency reviewed and verified by the senior researcher to ensure analytical rigor. Fourth, the research team systematically organized the coded data, synthesizing meaningful patterns and conceptual similarities to construct preliminary thematic frameworks. Fifth, these frameworks were developed into detailed themes, each supported by representative verbatim quotations from participants. Sixth, the team engaged in iterative discussions to refine the thematic structure, critically examining the data until consensus was reached on the final themes and subthemes. Finally, in the seventh step, preliminary findings were returned to participants for member checking to validate interpretations and incorporate feedback.

To ensure reliability, any discrepancies in data interpretation were resolved through team deliberation until consensus was achieved. Throughout the process, participant confidentiality was strictly maintained by anonymizing all data; audio recordings were transcribed and assigned anonymized identification codes in interview order.

Ethical considerations and participation consent

The study protocol obtained formal approval from the Capital Medical University before any data collection commenced (2024-P2-373-01). In adherence to established ethical standards, all potential participants received comprehensive written information detailing the study’s objectives, procedures, and ethical considerations. This documentation clearly articulated key principles, including data confidentiality, voluntary participation, and the right to withdraw at any stage of the interview process without providing reason or consequence.

Results

Guided by the research objectives and structured interview protocol, this study systematically analyzed and synthesized key elements essential for developing emergency management competency in primary anesthesia nurses. The analysis encompasses three core dimensions: theoretical knowledge of emergency response and crisis identification, clinical operational skills, and teamwork coordination as well as psychological adaptation.

Theme 1: Emergency response knowledge base

Professional knowledge and skills

Emergency knowledge reserve serves as the cornerstone of emergency response and the foundation for translating theory into practice. Professional expertise and skills are fundamental to addressing complex situations, requiring anesthetic nurses not only to master a solid theoretical framework but also to continuously update their knowledge base to keep pace with cutting-edge developments.

N2: The postanesthesia care unit (PACU) often admits various types of patients, and abnormal situations may arise at any time. It is essential to integrate extensive medical knowledge while also possessing clinical critical thinking skills. When abnormalities occur in patients, quick responses are required, along with analysis of the underlying causes and implementation of appropriate interventions. This significantly enhances the comprehensive capabilities of anesthesia nurses.

N5: It is crucial to master commonly used anesthesia medications, including drug compatibility, contraindications, and administration methods, to ensure patient safety.

D1: Nowadays, there are numerous guidelines and training programs available. I review the latest guidelines and create my own mind maps. These serve like a standard answer—when a patient’s condition changes, I can immediately determine the direction of my thinking, which gives me confidence.

First aid knowledge and skills

First-aid knowledge and skills are directly related to the timeliness and effectiveness of emergency rescue, emphasizing the implementation of precise and standardized procedures within the golden window of opportunity.

N1: When handing emergency equipment, such as a laryngeal mask, the doctor emphasized assessing the patient’s body size to select the correct size. Similarly, for intubation, confirming the tube size is essential; improper sizing can lead to insertion failure, delay critical resuscitation time, and waste medical supplies.

N3: Our department emphasizes emergency skills training from the start. We conduct monthly assessments on CPR, airway management, and other skills, and doctors occasionally lead case-based discussions on emergency knowledge.

N8: During a patient’s sudden ventricular fibrillation, the anesthesiologist called for a defibrillator. I passed the pregelled electrode pads almost immediately, with no delay, contributing to a successful resuscitation.

Master relevant equipment and their principles

Familiarity with professional equipment and their underlying mechanisms ensures a seamless integration of technology and human expertise. Only by truly understanding how the devices operate can one unlock their full potential in critical moments.

N1: You know, I really think we need to learn more about how anesthesia machines actually work. Once you understand the basics, you can figure out problems way faster. Like one time, the machine kept showing high airway pressure—we were all focused on the patient, but it turned out a part of the breathing tube was just pinched. Knowing how the system works helped us spot it quickly.

N10: I feel like equipment is kind of like people—you gotta check on it regularly to avoid problems. Take the oxygen sensor, for example. If you don’t replace it before it runs out, your readings might be off. And in a critical situation, that could really throw off your decisions.

N7 and D3: In the OR, it’s pretty crowded and noisy with all the devices around. That’s why it’s so important to recognize the different alarm sounds from the anesthesia machine and know what they usually mean. That way, you know exactly where to look and what to do right away.

Theme 2: Emergency response competency

Vital signs and symptom surveillance skills

Emergency response and management skills represent the crucial link in translating knowledge into actionable practice. Patient monitoring demands continuous, precise observation of subtle changes in vital signs. Establishing a holistic perspective allows for the collection and integration of multidimensional information, providing a robust data foundation for clinical judgment.

N10: First of all, everyone should be able to monitor a patient’s condition. Besides basic vital signs, we also need to keep an eye on things like the patient’s complexion, mental state, urine output, and more. It’s important to train ourselves to integrate multiple pieces of information before making a clinical decision.

N9: We should practically stick to the monitor and the patient—not only watching the numbers but also analyzing trends, keeping the big picture in mind. Is their BP fluctuating? Is it dropping too fast? How’s the peripheral circulation? What about the color and amount of urine?

Crisis identification and risk foresight capability

Crisis recognition and risk anticipation enable the prediction of evolving trends based on early signs. By identifying potential hazards early, anesthesia nurses can shift from reactive response to proactive prevention. Risk anticipation also forms the core of clinical decision-making capability, requiring the rapid integration of patient information, assessment of risks versus benefits, and evaluation of different options in complex, high-pressure environments—ultimately leading to scientific choices that best serve the patient’s interests.

D1 and D2: Anesthesia nurses are often the first to notice when a patient is in critical condition, so their ability to anticipate risks is especially important.

N1: Once during a thoracic surgery, the patient started having trouble breathing and their heart rate went up, but there was no output from the chest tube. We lowered the tube below chest level—and suddenly about 100 ml of fresh blood drained out.

N8: You really have to think ahead and anticipate risks. For example, in the PACU, if we have an elderly patient with hypertension and diabetes, I make sure to closely monitor their blood pressure and blood sugar levels.

Clinical decision-making ability

Under circumstances where information may be incomplete, time is limited, and pressure is immense, healthcare professionals are required to rapidly integrate multifaceted patient information, assess risk-benefit trade-offs, weigh different options, and make evidence-based decisions that best serve the patient’s interests. This reflects their expertise, experience, and clinical judgment.

D2: In critical moments, the initial yet crucial decisions made by the anesthesia nurse can often buy us critical time during resuscitation. For example, while the doctor is still assessing the situation, the anesthesia nurse may have already established a second IV line, adjusted the patient’s position to improve ventilation, and so on.

N3: The anesthesia nurse often needs to stay one step ahead of the doctor—anticipating and understanding their intentions while responding flexibly. Say, during a major hemorrhage case, the surgeon might be tied up with controlling bleeding on the field and won’t have time to spell everything out. If they ask for epinephrine and blood, you’ve got to make instant judgment about where the meds are, the correct dosage, how to reach the blood bank, and more.

Competency in analyzing and implementing standardized procedures

The ability to execute standardized protocols ensures systematic and reliable actions, enabling emergency response to be both rapid and orderly. This provides a robust action framework for both individual performance and team collaboration.

N2: When a patient’s condition becomes abnormal or critical, we need to quickly figure out what’s is happening and carry out a series of nursing measures. For example, if a patient has massive bleeding, first we establish IV access, then monitor their blood pressure, urine output, and level of consciousness, and finally follow the doctor’s orders for corresponding treatment. There’s a clear set of steps to follow. If we all know the procedure well, it helps with task allocation and saves precious rescue time.

N6: I remember once a patient went into ventricular fibrillation. My team and I immediately brought over the defibrillator and crash cart. Because we’d practiced and been tested on this so many times, everyone knew the drill, and teamwork went smoothly—who was doing compressions, who managed the airway, who prepared meds, who documented, and so on. You could say standardized procedures are like a navigation system in moments of chaos.

N4: Our department’s procedures are based on guidelines and refined over time based on real issues we’ve faced at work. Every adverse event leads to reflection and review, and we keep testing and adjusting our steps during drills. During an actual emergency, we stick to the procedure step by step—that’s how we stay organized and calm when things get intense.

Theme 3: Teamwork and communication skills

Implement closed-loop communication

Ensuring accurate and complete transmission of information within the team. Closed-loop communication secures that information is conveyed without distortion or omission, establishing an accurate and reliable information flow. Its core model is: “The sender delivers clear instructions → the receiver repeats and confirms → the sender verifies the accuracy of the repetition.” This approach eliminates misunderstandings arising from assumptions—such as “I thought you knew”—and serves as a critical communication discipline to prevent medical errors and ensure instructions are correctly understood and executed, especially in chaotic or high-pressure environments.

N2: During emergencies, when there might be a lot of people around, it’s crucial to clearly hear the instructions from the attending anesthesiologist, repeat them back, and then double-check with another person before administering meds or taking action. That way, you close the communication loop. And always keep the ampule vials for verification after the resuscitation ends.

N8: Now in the PACU, I’ve gotten into the habit of repeating back instructions. It can get pretty noisy sometimes, so I need that confirmation to feel at ease.

N9: When communicating, I make sure to use precise medical terms. Instead of saying “this patient’s blood pressure is a bit high,” I’ll accurately tell the doctor the patient’s specific systolic and diastolic blood pressure readings.

Organizational and coordination competency

Organizational and coordination skills focus on the overall arrangement, rationally allocating personnel and tasks, enabling the team to operate in synergy like a precision instrument.

N5: Emergencies really test how well a team works together. Anesthesia nurses often anticipate what’s needed even before the doctor gives the order—like having the bite block, the right size endotracheal tube, and everything else ready to go. Sometimes just a glance from the doctor is enough for us to react quickly. That’s what real team looks like.

N9: Usually, a senior nurse takes charge of coordinating things. Having her there makes me feel much more at ease. She tells everyone what to prepare, what to do, and assigns tasks—she’s really the backbone of our team.

N3: My role is to ensure proper staffing during emergencies, coordinate extra help if needed, and connect with external resources—like calling in a consult or notifying the ICU to reserve a bed.

Humanistic care

Humanistic care embodies the warmth within a team. Maintaining respect and empathy for patients during intense rescue efforts helps sustain team resilience and trust. Upholding compassion in high-pressure environments not only alleviates patient suffering and fosters mutual trust but is also an indispensable component of delivering high-quality care.

N6: After an emergency situation, patients can be pretty shaken up—both physically and emotionally. That’s when it’s really important to reassure them and help them stay calm. For instance, some patients might have trouble regulating their body temperature, so we offer warming. We also keep an eye on pressure points, check in with them about how they’re feeling, and ask if they need anything. These simple acts of care can really help boost their confidence and resilience.

N4: Humanistic care comes through in so many ways. Learning things like nonviolent communication or stress-relief techniques—and making sure the whole team is trained in them—benefits not just patients and their families, but also our colleagues. Using a gentle tone and calm words can really ease a patient’s fear and a family’s anxiety. Plus, our department also looks after our nurses—we’ve set up a decompression room, for example—which helps build a supportive team atmosphere. And that positive vibe naturally gets passed on to the patients, too.

D1: Before anesthesia, I try to take just an extra minute—hold the patient’s hand and say, “Don’t worry, I’ll be right here with you.” It might only be a minute, or just a few words, but it really helps bridge the gap between us and the patient. It builds trust and makes communication after surgery much smoother.

Theme 4: Psychological resilience and reflective practice

Stress resilience and emotion management

Psychological adaptation and reflective learning serve as the internal engine for the continuous evolution of emergency response capabilities and individual professional growth. Stress resilience and emotional regulation enable practitioners to maintain rationality and focus under high pressure, transforming stress into the psychological capital for composed action.

N2: When faced with an emergency, I usually take a deep breath first to calm myself down, then quickly run through the standard procedure in my head. I focus on each step of the operation—staying composed and breaking down any fear or panic into concrete tasks.

N5: After handling a critical event, I like to relieve stress through exercise and give myself some positive reinforcement. I tell myself I did the best I could and slowly recharge my energy that way.

N4: During critical moments, I try my best to stay present, coordinate the team, and keep an eye on everyone’s emotional state. Afterwards, during debriefings, I make sure to acknowledge and thank the team. I also arrange timely rest for colleagues who worked overtime due to the emergency, and I encourage staff in our department to attend psychological support sessions—whether for resilience building or post-traumatic growth.

Debriefing and lessons learned

Postevent review and reflection focus on extracting lessons and insights from every experience, driving practice to evolve from mere “occurrence” into meaningful “experience.”

N4: After a critical emergency, we log it in the special patient registry. Then we hold case analysis and discussion sessions, inviting doctors and nursing specialists to talk through the causes of the event and any issues during the response—so we can handle it better next time.

N7: We can see updates to the special incident registry in real time through our WeChat group. And during morning handover, the nurses involved in the resuscitation are invited to walk through what happened, how it unfolded, and what we should watch out for.

N5: In our department, debriefs focus on the incident, not the person. We review and reflect as a team, analyze the event and contributing factors, and then refine our procedures. That way, everyone feels comfortable sharing, and we really learn from it together.

Scenario-based simulation exercise

Through high-fidelity, immersive simulation training, complex and rare clinical crisis scenarios can be replicated. This approach helps consolidate standardized skills and teamwork procedures, promoting anesthesia nurses’ ability to respond to emergencies appropriately within the continuous cycle of: practice – reflection – enhancement,” thereby fostering greater composure during critical moments.

N6: Aside from debriefing sessions, to help everyone better absorb and master what we’ve learned, I also organize role-play simulations with the staff involved. We recreate the scene to keep it vivid and true to life, so that anesthesia nurses who weren’t present can still learn how to handle critical moments.

N4: The scenario simulation drills we run in our department are really realistic now. We incorporate a lot of interpersonal communication scenarios and sudden equipment failure responses. After each drill, we hold brainstorming sessions to develop various emergency protocols.

N5: Our department regularly organizes scenario simulation drills. Individually, we might all perform well, but working together as a team doesn’t always go smoothly. It requires each step to seamlessly follow the next—if coordination isn’t good, things can get stuck. These drills really help us continuously improve our teamwork and organizational coordination skills.

Discussion

Constructing an emergency knowledge system and safety culture to deepen crisis mitigation capability

“Emergency Knowledge Reserve” is the core of the “mitigation” phase in Emergency management. Interviews indicated that anesthesia nurses possess a profound awareness of the importance of knowledge reserve; however, its depth and systematization still require enhancement. Severe adverse events in the PACU predominantly involve critical systems such as the cardiovascular, pulmonary, and neurological systems, with missed diagnoses and delayed diagnoses being primary risk factors.[1718] This necessitates that anesthesia nurses possess advanced capabilities in risk identification and avoidance. The study found that the professional knowledge of experienced nurses has evolved from a static knowledge base into an integrated form of expertise capable of flexibly synthesizing equipment principles, pathophysiology, and real-time data for analysis.[1920] For instance, they can anticipate potential bronchospasm in a patient by detecting abnormalities in ventilator waveforms, rather than waiting for explicit alarms. Internalizing emergency knowledge into instinctive responses represents the highest form of reducing emergency incidents. Therefore, it is imperative to systematically develop a safety culture: this requires classifying and grading crisis events, studying “close-call” cases, utilizing safety warning tools, and optimizing handover procedures to deeply embed risk prevention awareness into daily practice and systemic workflows.

Strengthening clinical critical thinking and rapid clinical decision-making execution

Emergency response and management capabilities are manifested in real crises, where rapid and precise decision-making and execution are critical for saving lives. This ability presents a clear cognitive-action closed loop: Patient Condition Monitoring (information gathering) → Crisis Identification and Anticipation (understanding and predicting the situation) → Clinical Decision-Making (generating a plan) → Protocol Execution (action output). The study found that anesthesia nurses often play a crucial supportive decision-making role.[2122] In emergencies, anesthesia nurses must possess a certain degree of autonomous decision-making capacity. For example, when a physician decides to administer a specific vasopressor, the nurse must immediately make autonomous decisions regarding the most suitable venous access to establish and the appropriate concentration and infusion rate for preparing the medication. This autonomous decision-making capability directly impacts treatment outcomes. Consequently, training must extend beyond repetitive drills of isolated technical skills. Instead, it should involve repeated tempering of anesthesia nurses’ decision-making schemes under conditions of conflicting information and time pressure through high-fidelity simulation and emergency drills, ensuring they can maintain coherent and stable closed-loop thinking under stress.

Optimizing team synergy and communication mechanisms centered on a shared mental model

Effective emergency collaboration is built upon four pillars: closed-loop communication ensures accurate and unambiguous information transfer; organizational coordination achieves optimal allocation of resources and roles; and humane care maintains the team’s psychological safety and long-term resilience. During resuscitation, the implementation of closed-loop dialogue and nurses proactively preparing instruments the physician may need next exemplify the high degree of internalization of communication and coordination.[23] This profoundly demonstrates a shared understanding of the situation, objectives, and respective roles among team members. Therefore, the key to team building lies in fostering team默契 (tacit understanding). This requires interdisciplinary in situ simulation training, allowing medical and nursing staff to jointly manage complex cases, exposing and bridging cognitive gaps. This, in turn, enables seamless, often nonverbal, coordination during real crises, transforming a group from a mere “collection of individuals” into a cohesive “unit of action.”

Improving psychological recovery and systemic learning loops to drive continuous capability evolution

Psychological adjustment and reflective learning are the engines driving the continuous improvement of the entire emergency response capability system. The majority of anesthesia nurses reported experiencing negative emotions following emergency events, precisely highlighting the urgency of building psychological resilience.[24] Psychological adjustment at the individual level, such as managing emotions through mindfulness training, is an acquirable skill conducive to developing the occupational health and long-term efficacy of anesthesia nurses.[25] Reflective learning at the organizational level is even more critical: structured postevent debriefing and regular scenario simulation training together constitute a “practice-reflection-improvement” learning closed loop. Analyzing the causes of emergency events through debriefing helps identify underlying issues and shortcomings, thereby promoting patient safety management safeguards. Furthermore, establishing a nonpunitive safety event reporting system serves as the foundation for collecting learning materials and facilitating fundamental quality improvement. Nursing managers should actively utilize these tools to transform crisis experiences into optimized protocols, processes, and training programs, ultimately generating sustained momentum for the spiral escalation of nursing quality.

Limitations

While this study provides valuable insights from the Chinese context, its generalizability may be limited by the homogeneous cultural background of participants. Future research should incorporate more diverse cultural perspectives through multinational collaborations or cross-cultural comparative studies, which would significantly enhance the robustness and applicability of the findings across different healthcare systems and cultural contexts.

Conclusion

This study identifies four core components of anesthesia nurses’ emergency response capabilities: a comprehensive knowledge system, rapid clinical decision-making and execution, effective teamwork, and a reflective learning cycle. These elements form an interrelated and dynamic system essential for patient safety. The findings suggest shifting educational focus from knowledge transfer to practical training, enhancing decision-making through high-fidelity simulations, and fostering a safety culture emphasizing risk anticipation and collaboration. Future multicenter studies should examine how institutional factors influence these capabilities, with quantitative research needed to develop standardized evaluation metrics.
