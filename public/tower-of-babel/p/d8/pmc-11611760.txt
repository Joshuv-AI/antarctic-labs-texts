The novel and potent CD40 agonist KHK2840 augments the antitumor efficacy of anti‐PD‐1 antibody and paclitaxel
Authors: Chiaki Kobayashi, Minami Suzuki‐Imaizumi, Yasuko Sakaguchi, Toshihiko Ishii, Maiko Adachi, Ayumi Kaneda, Ritsuko Ebihara, Masato Saito, Takeshi Uemori, Kiyotoshi Mori
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractLack of tumor‐reactive cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs) limits the antitumor efficacy of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs). CD40 agonists have been expected to overcome this limitation by generating tumor‐reactive CTLs. However, the clinical efficacy of CD40 agonistic antibodies is not as good as in non‐clinical studies. The novel human CD40 (hCD40) agonist KHK2840 is a fully human anti‐CD40 IgG2 agonistic antibody that is Fc‐engineered to minimize complement‐dependent cytotoxicity and antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity. Compared to other hCD40 agonists, KHK2840 exhibited the most potent hCD40 agonistic signal in tumor‐bearing hCD40 transgenic mice and human peripheral blood B cells. Moreover, KHK2840 enhanced the antitumor efficacy of the antiprogrammed cell death 1 antibody and paclitaxel. Comprehensive immune profiling revealed that the antitumor immune response of the triple combination involved tumor‐draining lymph nodes in addition to tumor microenvironments. This suggests that a coordinated antitumor immune response between tumors and lymph nodes may underlie the synergistic antitumor efficacy of the triple combination therapy. Finally, a toxicology study in cynomolgus monkeys demonstrated that KHK2840 activated the CD40 signal with tolerable toxicological properties. These results indicate that KHK2840 is a novel and potent hCD40 agonistic antibody for cancer immunotherapy, which is expected to augment the antitumor efficacy of ICIs and chemotherapy.

INTRODUCTION

Since the clinical approval of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), such as antiprogrammed cell death 1 (anti‐PD‐1) antibody, patients' prognoses have dramatically improved. However, the clinical benefit is still limited to a small patient population, and a large part of the patients have shown no response to ICIs. 1 , 2 Therefore, substantial effort has been focused on seeking a new therapeutic agent that can overcome ICI‐resistant tumors. 3 , 4

The defect in the antitumor efficacy of ICIs is believed to be due to a lack of tumor‐reactive cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs), 1 therefore agents that can augment induction of tumor‐reactive CTLs and their infiltration into tumors are being investigated to overcome anti‐PD‐1 resistance. To this end, some cytotoxic treatments, such as chemotherapy and radiation therapy, have been tested in combination with anti‐PD‐1 antibody 5 because such therapies are expected to initiate T‐cell immune response against tumor by triggering immunogenic cell death. 6 In fact, these combinations improved the outcomes in some cancer patients. However, a significant number of patients still do not respond to treatment, 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 indicating the need for an additional signaling pathway to improve the effectiveness of ICIs and chemotherapy. 3

CD40 is a member of the TNF receptor superfamily. 11 It is expressed by many cell types, including antigen‐presenting cells, such as dendritic cells (DCs), monocytes, macrophages, and B cells. 11 CD40 is activated by trimerization via interaction with CD40 ligands (CD40L/CD154), which are mainly expressed on activated T cells. 11 Once CD40 is activated, its signal induces expression of immune co‐stimulatory molecules, including CD80, CD86, MHC molecules, and cytokines, such as IL‐12. 12 This results in the priming of antigen‐specific T cells 13 , 14 and differentiating B cells. 15 CD40 is thus a key component in establishing acquired immunity against infections and tumors. Accordingly, CD40 agonists are expected to enhance the antitumor effect of ICIs by inducing tumor‐reactive CTLs.

In mouse tumor models, CD40 agonists induced tumor‐specific CTLs 16 and a synergistic antitumor effect with the combination of ICIs and chemotherapy. 17 These data suggest that CD40 agonists augment the antitumor efficacy of the combination of ICIs and chemotherapy. Encouraged by these promising non‐clinical results, several CD40 agonists, such as CP‐870,893 (selicrelumab) 18 and APX‐005 M (sotigalimab), 19 have entered clinical trials. 20

We herein report the novel and potent human CD40 agonistic antibody KHK2840. We evaluated its antitumor efficacy in monotherapy and in combination with anti‐PD‐1 antibody and chemotherapy, and examined its toxicology in cynomolgus monkeys.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Ethical statements

The human blood study was approved by the Research Ethics Review Committee and was performed in accordance with the Kyowa Kirin Ethical Standard for Medical and Biological Research Involving Human Subjects and the Procedure for Sample Collection from In‐House Volunteers. All donors provided informed written consent before sampling.

All animal studies were performed in accordance with the Rule for Animal Experiments of Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd. under the approval of the company's Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee. Fuji Research Park of Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd. is fully accredited by the AAALAC international.

Cell lines

Human Burkitt's lymphoma Ramos (RA 1) cells (ATCC) were cultured in RPMI1640 supplemented with 10% FBS. Mouse colon cancer MC‐38 cells (Kerafast) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS, 1% MEM Non‐Essential Amino Acids Solution, 1% HEPES and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. Mouse melanoma B16.F10 cells (ATCC) were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin–streptomycin. All cells were maintained at 37°C under 5% CO2.

Test articles

The full‐length amino acid sequence of KHK2840 can be found in US9234044B2, 21 referred to as IgG2‐AAS(341). KHK2840, a defucosylated human antidinitrophenol (DNP) IgG1 antibody, and a human anti‐DNP IgG2 antibody (V234A, G237A, and P331S) were produced at Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd. Rituximab was purchased from R&D Systems. CP‐870,893 was made at Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd., based on US20120263732A1. 22 APX‐005M was made at Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd., based on WO2012149356A2 23 and US20180327496A1. 24 Paclitaxel (Taxol® injection, 30 mg) was purchased from Bristol–Myers Squibb. The antimouse PD‐1 antibody was purchased from BioLegend.

Surface plasmon resonance analyses

Recombinant human CD40 (hCD40) and cynomolgus monkey CD40 proteins were produced at Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd. Binding of KHK2840 to recombinant hCD40 protein, cynomolgus monkey CD40 protein, rat CD40 protein (R&D Systems Inc.), and mouse CD40 protein (Sino Biological Inc.) was measured using a Biacore T100 (Cytiva). Sensorgrams were fitted to a 1:1 binding model with single‐cycle kinetics to estimate the association rate constant (k a), dissociation rate constant (k d), and equilibrium dissociation constant (K D).

Enzyme‐linked immunosorbent assay

Recombinant FcγRIIIa 158F and 158 V proteins (His Tag) were produced by Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd. FcγRIIIa or C1q was captured on a plate. KHK2840, anti‐DNP IgG1 antibody, and anti‐DNP IgG2 antibody were added and detected by HRP‐labeled anti‐human IgG (American Qualex) or HRP‐labeled anti‐C1q antibody (Abcam). Absorbance was measured using a SpectraMax® M3 (Molecular Devices).

Human monocyte‐derived dendritic cells

Human peripheral blood CD14‐positive monocytes (Lonza and HemaCare) were differentiated in RPMI1640 containing 10% FBS, 100 ng/mL recombinant human IL‐4 (R&D Systems), and 100 ng/mL recombinant human GM‐CSF (Miltenyi Biotec) for 7 days at 37°C under 5% CO2. Then, the cells were treated with KHK2840 or anti‐DNP IgG2 antibody for 2 days at 37°C under 5% CO2. Human IL‐12p70 in the supernatant was measured using a Human IL‐12p70 Quantikine ELISA kit (R&D Systems). The CD80 and CD86 expression levels in live cells were measured by multi color flow cytometry (detailed information can be found in Data S1).

In vitro assessment of the time dependency of KHK2840‐induced CD40 agonistic signal

Ramos cells were treated with KHK2840 at 37°C under 5% CO2. Cells were washed at 0.5, 4, and 24 h after the initiation of KHK2840 treatment to replace the medium with fresh medium with or without KHK2840. After culturing for a total of 48 h, CD86 and CD95 in live cells were measured by multi‐color flow cytometry (detailed information can be found in Data S1).

Pharmacodynamics of KHK2840 in hCD40 BAC Tg mice

hCD40 bacterial artificial chromosome (BAC) transgenic (Tg) mice on the C57Bl/6J background were established by Kyowa Kirin Co., Ltd. 25 KHK2840 was administered intravenously (0.038, 0.075, 0.15, 0.3, 0.6, and 1.2 mg/kg) to female hCD40 BAC Tg mice. Blood was collected under isoflurane anesthesia at approximately 4, 24, 48, 168, and 336 h post‐dose.

CD80, CD86, and CD95 in B cells were measured by multi‐color flow cytometry (detailed information can be found in Data S1). Cytokines in plasma were quantified using a V‐PLEX Proinflammatory Panel 1 Mouse Kit (MSD) with a MESO SECTOR S600 plate reader (MSD) and the DISCOVERY WORKBENCH 4.0 Analysis software program (MSD).

In vivo antitumor study

MC‐38 cells and B16.F10 cells were subcutaneously inoculated into female hCD40 BAC Tg mice. After confirming the establishment of the tumor, KHK2840, CP‐870,893, and paclitaxel were intravenously administered, while the anti‐PD‐1 antibody was intraperitoneally administered. The tumor volume was calculated as follows:

tumor volume (mm3) = L × S 2 × 0.5 (L, long diameter; S, short diameter).

The relative tumor volumes were calculated as follows:

relative tumor volume = tumor volume on each day/tumor volume on day 0.

Blood was collected under isoflurane anesthesia. IL‐12p70 in serum was captured by antimouse IL‐12p70 antibody (BioLegend)‐coated paramagnetic beads and detected by biotin‐labeled antimouse IL‐12/23p40 antibody (BioLegend) using the digital ELISA system of a Simoa HD‐1 Analyzer (Quanterix).

Comprehensive immune profiling of tumors and axillary lymph nodes

The sample preparation is described in Data S1. The immune‐related gene expression in the tumors and the axillary lymph nodes of MC‐38 tumor‐bearing mice was analyzed using an nCounter® PanCancer Immune Profiling Panel, with 20 selected genes added by an nCounter Panel Plus (NanoString Technologies, Inc.) (Table S1). Numbers of immune cells were estimated as the cell type score based on the expression of marker genes using the nSolver Advanced Analysis 2.0 software program (NanoString Technologies, Inc.; Tables S2 and S3).

Ex vivo comparison of CD40 agonists in human peripheral blood

Human peripheral blood was obtained from healthy adult donors. Blood was treated with KHK2840, CP‐870,893, and APX‐005 M overnight at 37°C under 5% CO2. CD69, CD86, and CD95 in B cells were measured by multi‐color flow cytometry (detailed information can be found in Data S1).

GLP toxicity study in cynomolgus monkeys

A nonclinical toxicity study in accordance with Good Laboratory Practice (GLP) was conducted in cynomolgus monkeys. Vehicle and KHK2840 (0.1, 0.3, 1, and 3 mg/kg) were intravenously infused once every 2 weeks for 3 doses. Five male and five female animals were assigned to each of the vehicle, 1 and 3 mg/kg groups. Three male and three female animals were assigned to each of the 0.1 and 0.3 mg/kg groups. Clinical observations, ophthalmology, hematology, clinical chemistry, and histopathology were included in the study. Blood samples were collected to evaluate toxicokinetics, antidrug antibody (ADA) production, immunophenotyping, and cytokine release.

Data analyses

Graphs were created using GraphPad Prism (version 9.4.0). Half‐maximal effective concentrations (EC50) were estimated by fitting data to a sigmoidal dose–response curve according to the four‐parameter logistic equation of Rodbard using JMP (version 15, SAS Institute Inc.). Steel's test, clustering and regression analyses were performed using JMP (version 17, SAS Institute Inc.). Wilcoxon's rank sum test was performed using JMP (version 15 and 17, SAS Institute Inc.) and EXSUS Version 8.1.0 (CAC Croit Corp.).

RESULTS

KHK2840 is a novel CD40 agonistic antibody

We developed KHK2840, a novel fully human Fc‐engineered anti‐human CD40 (hCD40) IgG2 agonistic antibody. In addition to binding to human CD40 (Figure 1A; k a = 5.33 × 105 M−1 s−1, k d = 2.58 × 10−4 s−1, K D = 0.485 nmol/L), KHK2840 was also cross‐reactive with cynomolgus monkey CD40 (Figure 1A, k a = 6.94 × 106 M−1 s−1, k d = 1.02 × 10−2 s−1, K D = 1.46 nmol/L). However, KHK2840 did not bind to rat or mouse CD40 (Figure 1A).

KHK2840 carries amino acid substitutions in its constant region of the heavy chain of IgG2 (V234A, G237A, and P331S). IgG2 subtype has a low binding affinity to FcγRIIIa, 26 as seen in KHK2840 (Figure 1B, left and middle). In addition to this characteristic, V234A, G237A and P331S amino acid substitutions are reported to minimize the effector functions, such as C1q binding (Figure 1B, right). 27 As expected, antibody‐dependent cellular cytotoxicity (ADCC) and complement‐dependent cytotoxicity (CDC) of KHK2840 were not observed in hCD40‐expressing cells (Figure S1). In in vitro experiments using hmoDCs, KHK2840 increased CD80, CD86, and IL‐12p70 in a concentration‐dependent manner (Figure 1C and Figure S2; EC50 = 0.188, 0.072, 0.436 μg/mL, respectively; experiments were conducted using six donors). Furthermore, KHK2840 increased CD80, CD86, and CD95 in in vitro B cells of hCD40 Tg mice but not of WT mice, indicating that KHK2840 induces hCD40 specific agonistic activity (Figure S3). A concentration‐dependent CD40 agonistic signal was also observed when Ramos cells were treated with KHK2840 in vitro (Figure 1D). We performed washout experiments to remove KHK2840 from some cultured cells at 0.5, 4, and 24 h after initiating KHK2840 treatment to determine whether or not the treatment duration affected the CD40 agonistic activity of KHK2840. After a total of 48 h of culture, CD86 and CD95 increased as the concentration increased. In addition, CD86 and CD95 induction was more profound when the treatment was longer (Figure 1D). These data predict that the magnitude of the CD40 agonistic signal induced by KHK2840 depends on the treatment duration as well as the concentration. KHK2840 exhibited a CD40 agonistic signal in these in vitro experiments without IgG cross‐linking agents. These results and the lack of binding activity of KHK2840 to FcγRs suggest that KHK2840 can induce CD40 agonistic signal without being cross‐linked via FcγR‐expressing cells.

KHK2840 exerts an in vivo antitumor effect on mouse colon tumors

KHK2840 increased antigen‐specific CD8 + T cells in in vitro human PBMC (Figure S4), suggesting that KHK2840 has potential to induce tumor specific immune response. However, it remains unclear whether KHK2840 induces CD40 agonistic activity and antitumor effect in vivo, therefore we evaluated KHK2840 in mice and syngeneic mice tumor models. As KHK2840 does not bind to mouse CD40, we used hCD40 BAC Tg mice (Figures 1A and S3). We intravenously administered KHK2840 to hCD40 BAC Tg mice and then monitored B cell activation (Figure 2A) and cytokine release (Figure 2B) in peripheral blood. Dose‐dependent increases in CD80‐, CD86‐, and CD95‐positive B cells were observed (Figure 2A), and CD80‐, CD86‐, and CD95‐positive cells apparently increased with doses of 0.6 and 1.2 mg/kg after 24 h. Dose‐dependent increases in cytokines were also observed (Figure 2B). IL‐12p70, IFN‐γ, IL‐6, IL‐10, TNF‐α, IL‐1β, and CXCL1 apparently increased at 0.6 and 1.2 mg/kg at 24 h postdose. IL‐2, IL‐4, and IL‐5 did not appear to increase (Figure S5). Overall, 0.6 mg/kg is considered the minimum dose to induce a systemic CD40 agonistic signal in vivo. We also evaluated the antitumor activity of KHK2840 in MC‐38 tumor‐bearing hCD40 BAC Tg mice. We administered KHK2840 at 0.3, 0.6, and 1.2 mg/kg to evaluate the dose‐dependent increase in antitumor efficacy. As a result, slight tumor growth suppression was observed at 0.3 and 0.6 mg/kg KHK2840, and significant tumor growth suppression was seen at 1.2 mg/kg KHK2840 (Figure 3; P = 0.0128). Based on these results, we considered that a dose of 1.2 mg/kg KHK2840 should be used in combination therapy with anti‐PD‐1 antibody and paclitaxel (Figure 5).

KHK2840 is the most potent CD40 agonistic antibody

Next, we compared KHK2840 with other CD40 agonistic antibodies. We treated human fresh peripheral blood with KHK2840, CP‐870,893, and APX‐005 M ex vivo and measured the CD69, CD86, and CD95 expression of B cells (Figure 4A). These three antibodies exhibited CD40 agonistic activity, but KHK2840 showed more efficacious CD69, CD86, and CD95 induction (EC50 = 0.018, 0.015, 0.016 μg/mL, respectively) than CP‐870,893 (EC50 = 0.030, 0.020, 0.021 μg/mL, respectively) and APX‐005 M (EC50 = 0.033, 0.013, 0.014 μg/mL, respectively). In addition, we compared the CD40 agonistic activity of CP‐870,893 and KHK2840 in B16.F10 tumor‐bearing hCD40 BAC Tg mice. We measured IL‐12p70 levels in peripheral blood after a single intravenous administration of KHK2840 at doses of 0.57 and 1.2 mg/kg, and CP‐870,893 at doses of 4.3 and 8.92 mg/kg. Both KHK2840 and CP‐870,893 increased IL‐12p70 levels in peripheral blood 1 day after administration (Figure 4B). KHK2840 showed a dose‐dependent increase, while there was no marked difference between the two doses of CP‐870,893. IL‐12p70 concentrations were higher in KHK2840‐treated mice than in CP‐870,893‐treated mice. These results suggest that KHK2840 induces the most potent CD40 agonistic signal among CD40 agonistic antibodies.

KHK2840 enhances the antitumor efficacy of anti‐PD‐1 and chemotherapy

We evaluated the antitumor efficacy of KHK2840, anti‐PD‐1 antibody, and paclitaxel in combination in MC‐38 tumor‐bearing hCD40 BAC Tg mice (Figure 5A). Similar to Figure 3, KHK2840 monotherapy tended to suppress tumor growth in comparison to vehicle. In addition, the combination of anti‐PD‐1 antibody and paclitaxel tended to suppress tumor growth. When these three treatments were administered in combination, tumor growth was significantly suppressed in comparison to all other groups (vs. vehicle: P < 0.0001, vs. anti‐PD‐1 antibody + paclitaxel: P = 0.0003, vs. KHK2840: P < 0.0001). Importantly, two cures (tumor volumes reduced to 0 mm3) were observed with the combination of KHK2840, anti‐PD‐1 antibody, and paclitaxel, while no cures were observed in the other groups. These data indicate that KHK2840 has a synergistic antitumor effect with anti‐PD‐1 antibody and paclitaxel.

We investigated the mechanism underlying the synergistic antitumor effect of this combination by performing immune profiling of tumors and lymph nodes. We obtained gene expression data from samples collected 2 and 7 days after the first dose (days 2 and 7, respectively) to observe dynamic changes in the immune cell composition throughout the treatments. A hierarchical clustering analysis revealed similar gene expression profiles in the same treatment groups, especially in lymph nodes on both days and in tumors on day 2, indicating that this immune profiling system captured distinct immune responses against each treatment (Figure S6). We calculated the cell type score (CTS), which estimates immune cell abundance in the samples, using the gene expression data. We then analyzed the correlation between relative tumor volumes and CTSs to understand which immune cells most strongly influenced tumor growth. The strongest negative correlations were found in Th1 cells in lymph nodes and DCs in tumors on day 2 (Figure 5C and Table S2). Importantly, both CTSs were significantly increased in the triple‐combination group. DCs in tumors are expected to attract tumor‐reactive CTLs. 28 Accordingly, the CTS of CD8 T cells in tumors was significantly increased in the triple‐combination group in comparison to the vehicle group (Figure 5D). In addition, some tumor antigen‐loaded DCs migrate from the tumor to the lymph nodes, 28 , 29 which was seen as a significant increase in the CTS of DCs in the lymph nodes of the triple‐combination group on day 2 (Figure 5E). In lymph nodes, Th1 cells activate these DCs to generate tumor‐reactive CTLs. 30 The concomitant increase of the CTSs of Th1 cells and DCs in lymph nodes might contribute to the long‐lasting antitumor immune response, exemplified by a significant increase in CD8 T cells in tumors in the triple‐combination group in comparison to the vehicle group until day 7 (Figure 5D).

We also evaluated the antitumor efficacy of KHK2840 and anti‐PD‐1 antibody in combination in hCD40 BAC Tg mice bearing the cancer cells established from pancreatic ductal adeno carcinoma of Kras and p53 knockout mice. In this model, both KHK2840 and anti‐PD‐1 antibody monotherapies tended to suppress tumor growth, although those efficacies were not strong enough to induce tumor remissions. However, the combination of KHK2840 and anti‐PD‐1 antibody significantly increased antitumor efficacy compared to each monotherapy, noting that tumor remission was observed only in the combination group (eight out of 10 mice; Figure S7).

KHK2840 induces CD40 agonistic signaling in cynomolgus monkeys at a tolerable dose in the GLP toxicity study

Finally, we carried out a GLP toxicity study for KHK2840 using cynomolgus monkeys. KHK2840 was intravenously infused at 0.1, 0.3, 1, and 3 mg/kg once every 2 weeks for three doses. The toxicokinetic parameters are summarized in Table 1. ADA was noted from 14 days after the first dose in all animals except for two males treated with 0.1 mg/kg KHK2840. ADA appeared to affect the serum concentration of KHK2840 at subsequent doses (Table 1). No mortality or moribundity was observed in this study, therefore KHK2840 was deemed tolerable at doses of up to 3 mg/kg in this condition.

Clinical observations considered adverse, such as swollen eyelids, lethargy, and decreased activity, were observed in animals treated at 1 and 3 mg/kg. Microscopic findings considered adverse, such as decreased B cells in lymphoid organs, degeneration in the germinal center of the spleen, pigmentation and opacity in the cornea, and opacity in the lens, were observed at 1 and 3 mg/kg, therefore the no observed adverse effect level (NOAEL) in cynomolgus monkeys was 0.3 mg/kg KHK2840.

On immunophenotyping, CD86‐ and CD95‐positive B cells increased in a dose‐dependent manner, while decreases in the absolute populations of T cells (total, helper, and cytotoxic), B cells, and NK cells were noted at ≥1 mg/kg (Table 2). IL‐12/23p40 levels were increased at 0.3, 1, and 3 mg/kg (Table 2). CRP levels were increased at 1 and 3 mg/kg (Table 3). AST and ALT levels were increased at 3 mg/kg (Table 3).

DISCUSSION

We introduced a novel and potent hCD40 agonistic antibody, KHK2840. KH2840 increased CD69, CD86, and CD95 in ex vivo human peripheral blood B cells in comparison to CP‐870,893 and APX‐005 M. This potent hCD40 agonistic activity of KHK2840 was also observed in vivo in B16.F10 tumor‐bearing mice. KHK2840 enhanced the antitumor efficacy of anti‐PD‐1 antibody and paclitaxel. We also confirmed that KHK2840 was tolerable and increased activated B cells and IL‐12/23p40 in peripheral blood in cynomolgus monkeys. These results suggest that KHK2840 will be clinically beneficial for enhancing the antitumor efficacy of immunotherapy‐chemotherapy combinations.

CD40 is a TNF receptor superfamily member and is activated when clustered with its ligand. 31 CD40 agonistic antibodies thus generally need to be cross‐linked via FcγRs to activate the CD40 signal. KHK2840 is unlikely to induce an agonistic signal as its Fc is engineered to not be recognized by FcγRs‐expressing cells. However, as we demonstrated here, KHK2840 exhibited CD40 agonistic activity, indicating that KHK2840 is not dependent on cross‐linking via FcγRs‐expressing cells.

Anti‐CD40 antibody, which behaves as an antagonist in IgG1 and IgG4 format, reportedly exhibits FcγR‐independent and potent CD40 agonistic activity in the IgG2 format. 32 , 33 This implies that the IgG2 subclass underlies the most efficacious CD40 agonistic activity of KHK2840. Furthermore, due to the lack of ADCC and CDC activity, KHK2840 will not deplete any CD40‐expressing cells, such as DCs, and will not induce ADCC‐ or CDC‐mediated side effects, such as an infusion reaction. 34 , 35 Although such FcγR‐independent CD40 agonists have already been reported, 32 , 33 the toxicity has not been investigated in detail, therefore this is the first GLP toxicology study of this type of CD40 agonist in cynomolgus monkeys.

No mortality or moribundity was observed, with KHK2840 proving tolerable up to 3 mg/kg in this GLP study. However, some clinical observations and microscopic findings considered adverse were found at 1 and 3 mg/kg, therefore we determined that the NOAEL was 0.3 mg/kg. Increases in CD86‐ and CD95‐positive B cells and IL‐12/23p40 were noted at this dose. These results indicate that KHK2840 induces a CD40 agonistic signal without serious adverse events in cynomolgus monkeys.

Given that the CD40 signal is essential for acquired immunity, 15 and based on the nonclinical findings of a synergistic antitumor effect of CD40 agonists and ICIs, 17 several clinical trials have been investigating the combination of anti‐PD‐1, chemotherapy, and a CD40 agonist. Here we also demonstrated that KHK2840 had a synergistic antitumor effect with anti‐PD‐1 antibody and paclitaxel in MC‐38 tumor‐bearing hCD40 BAC Tg mice. Furthermore, comprehensive immune profiling revealed that the triple combination induced the most distinct antitumor immune response among KHK2840 monotherapy and the combination of anti‐PD‐1 antibody and paclitaxel. The triple combination most strongly increased the CTS of Th1 cells in lymph nodes and DCs in tumors, which were negatively correlated with the tumor volume. DCs in tumors are expected to recruit tumor‐reactive T cells into the tumor, 28 which is observed as an increased CTS of CD8 T cells in tumors. Another important role of DCs in tumors is to take up and carry tumor antigens to lymph nodes, which is observed as an increase in the CTS of DCs in lymph nodes. 28 , 29 In lymph nodes, antigen‐loaded DCs are licensed by Th1 cells and further generate tumor‐reactive CTLs. 30 The triple‐combination therapy increased the CTSs of both Th1 cells and DCs in lymph nodes, implying that this therapy induced a robust antitumor immune response by promoting DC licensing in lymph nodes and recruiting tumor‐reactive CTLs in tumors, observed as a significant increase in the CTS of CD8 T cells in tumors, which lasted until day 7. Given these results, KHK2840 seems to induce the coordinated antitumor immune response between tumors and lymph nodes in combination with anti‐PD‐1 antibody and paclitaxel, resulting in augmented antitumor efficacy.

The clinical outcomes of CD40 agonists have not been as good as expected based on nonclinical studies. 20 , 36 If this lack of efficacy is due to an insufficient CD40 agonist signal and/or FcγR‐dependence in CD40 agonistic activity, KHK2840 has the potential to show good efficacy because of its potent and FcγR‐independent CD40 agonistic activity. Overall, KHK2840 is anticipated to be a viable new therapeutic agent in cancer immunotherapy.
