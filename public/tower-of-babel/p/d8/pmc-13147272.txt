Discontinuation of anticoagulation therapy after atrial fibrillation ablation: the ALONE-AF and OCEAN studies
Authors: Mario Campisi, Florinda Bonanno, Piera Capranzano
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTwo randomized trials, the ALONE-AF and OCEAN, have provided valuable evidence to guide the management of oral anticoagulation therapy after ablation of atrial fibrillation. In the ALONE-AF study, amongst patients with no documented atrial fibrillation recurrence at 1 year after transcatheter ablation, the 2-year incidence of the composite endpoint of stroke, systemic embolism, and major bleeding was significantly lower in those who discontinued oral anticoagulation compared with those who continued therapy. This difference was driven by a reduction in major bleeding events, with similar rates of stroke between groups. In the OCEAN study, amongst patients without atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence during the year following transcatheter ablation, rivaroxaban 15 mg, compared with acetylsalicylic acid (ASA), did not significantly reduce the 3-year incidence of stroke, systemic embolism, or new silent embolic cerebral infarction, but was associated with an increased risk of clinically relevant non-major bleeding. The annualized incidence of thromboembolic events was low (0.3–0.6 events per 100 patient-years). Taken together, data from these two trials suggest that discontinuation of oral anticoagulation (OAC) may be a clinically reasonable option in patients without AF recurrence for at least 1 year after ablation. This strategy should be limited to patients at low thromboembolic risk, comparable to those enrolled in the two trials. Furthermore, the implementation of optimal and sustainable rhythmmonitoring strategies is essential to support decisions regarding OAC discontinuation after AF ablation.Keywords: Atrial fibrillation, Transcatheter ablation, Oral anticoagulation

Transcatheter ablation of atrial fibrillation (AF) has been shown to be more effective than medical therapy in maintaining sinus rhythm, reducing arrhythmic burden, and improving symptoms and quality of life.1,2 In contrast, the impact of ablation on the reduction of AF-related thromboembolic events remains controversial, resulting in uncertainty regarding the management of oral anticoagulation (OAC) therapy after ablation.3 Long-term continuation of OAC therapy may lead to an increased risk of bleeding events, which could outweigh the risk of embolic events that appear to be attenuated following AF ablation.4 This makes assessment of the balance between the potential risks and benefits of prolonged anticoagulant treatment essential (increased bleeding risk vs. prevention of embolic events).

Meta-analyses of observational studies have attempted to evaluate the safety of OAC discontinuation after transcatheter AF ablation; however, the results have been highly heterogeneous.5,6 Consequently, in the absence of robust evidence, current guidelines recommend continuation of OAC therapy after AF ablation based on the patient’s thromboembolic risk profile, as quantified by the CHA₂DS₂-VA score (score ≥2), rather than on the procedural success of ablation.7 This recommendation to continue OAC after ablation has been assigned a Class I indication but with a level of evidence C, reflecting the lack of data derived from randomized clinical trials.

Recently, the results of two randomized trials have been published, providing important evidence to guide the decision to continue or discontinue OAC therapy beyond the post-ablation period.8,9 The first of these trials is the ALONE-AF (Anticoagulation One Year after Ablation of Atrial Fibrillation in Patients with Atrial Fibrillation), which was designed to assess whether discontinuation vs. continuation of OAC is associated with a lower risk of embolic or bleeding events in patients without AF recurrence after ablation. The second randomized trial is the OCEAN (Optimal Anti-Coagulation for Enhanced-Risk Patients Post-Catheter Ablation for Atrial Fibrillation), which evaluated whether continued OAC with rivaroxaban is superior to acetylsalicylic acid (ASA) in preventing stroke and systemic embolism in patients who successfully underwent transcatheter AF ablation.

ALONE-AF and OCEAN studies: design and results

ALONE-AF study

ALONE-AF is a prospective, randomized, open-label trial in which patients with at least one risk factor for stroke and no documented recurrence of atrial arrhythmias for at least 1 year after transcatheter AF ablation were randomized at 18 centres in South Korea to either discontinue OAC therapy or continue treatment with direct oral anticoagulants (apixaban 5 mg twice daily or rivaroxaban 20 mg once daily). Recurrence of atrial arrhythmias was defined as any documented episode lasting ≥30 s of AF, atrial flutter, or atrial tachycardia after ablation, assessed by at least two 24–72 h Holter monitoring sessions and electrocardiographic (ECG) monitoring. At least one Holter monitoring session and ECG monitoring had to be performed within 2 months prior to enrolment.

The study population (n = 840) had a mean age of 64 ± 8 years; 24.9% were women, and 67.6% had paroxysmal AF. The mean CHA₂DS₂-VASc score was 2.1 ± 1.0, and the mean HAS-BLED score was 1.8 ± 1.1.

At 2 years, the primary study endpoint—defined as the composite of stroke, systemic embolism, and major bleeding—occurred in 1 of 417 patients (0.3%) in the no-OAC group compared with 8 of 423 patients (2.2%) in the OAC group (absolute difference, −1.9 percentage points [95% confidence interval (CI), −3.5 to −0.3]; P = 0.02). The 2-year cumulative incidence of ischaemic stroke or systemic embolism was 0.3% in the no-OAC group vs. 0.8% in the OAC group (absolute difference, −0.5 percentage points [95% CI, −1.6 to 0.6]). A transient ischaemic attack occurred in two patients (0.6%) in the no-OAC group and in no patients in the OAC group (absolute difference, 0.6 percentage points [95% CI, −0.2 to 1.3]).

The 2-year cumulative incidence of major bleeding was 0% in the no-OAC group compared with 1.4% (n = 5) in the OAC group (absolute difference, −1.4 percentage points [95% CI, −2.6 to −0.2]). Clinically relevant non-major bleeding occurred in five patients (1.4%) in the no-OAC group and in seven patients (1.9%) in the OAC group. No cases of death or myocardial infarction were observed in either group.

OCEAN study

OCEAN is a prospective, international, randomized, open-label trial conducted across 56 centres in six countries, which enrolled patients with risk factors for stroke (CHA₂DS₂-VASc score ≥1 in men and ≥2 in women) and no recurrence of atrial arrhythmias for at least 1 year following transcatheter AF ablation. Ablation was considered successful if there was no clinical evidence of atrial arrhythmias and no atrial arrhythmia lasting >30 s on at least one 24 h Holter monitoring performed between 2 and 6 months and at any time beyond 6 months after the procedure. In addition, for study inclusion, recurrence of atrial arrhythmias had to be excluded by an additional 48 h Holter monitoring performed within the 2 months preceding enrolment.

A total of 1284 patients were enrolled and, at a median of 16.4 months after ablation, were randomized to treatment with ASA (n = 643) or rivaroxaban 15 mg once daily (n = 641). All patients underwent brain magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) at baseline and at 3-year follow-up to detect the occurrence of new silent embolic strokes, defined as at least one new cerebral infarction with a diameter ≥15 mm. Trial enrolment was prematurely discontinued in 2022 for futility, owing to the high probability of failing to demonstrate a difference in outcomes between the two treatment groups, both of which were associated with a low incidence of ischaemic events.

The enrolled patients had a mean age of 66.3 ± 7.3 years and a mean CHA₂DS₂-VASc score of 2.2 ± 1.1 (31.9% had a score ≥3); 28.6% were women, and approximately two-thirds had paroxysmal AF. The primary efficacy endpoint, defined as the composite of stroke, systemic embolism, or new silent embolic stroke detected by MRI, occurred in five patients (0.8%) in the rivaroxaban group and in nine patients (1.4%) in the ASA group, with an absolute difference of −0.6 percentage points that was not statistically significant (relative risk [RR], 0.56; 95% CI, 0.19–1.65; P = 0.28). Stroke alone occurred in 0.8% of patients treated with rivaroxaban and in 1.1% of those treated with ASA (RR, 0.72; 95% CI, 0.23–2.25).

New silent embolic strokes were observed in only two patients in the ASA group, and no systemic embolic events occurred. Cerebral infarctions smaller than 15 mm were detected in 3.9% of patients in the rivaroxaban group and in 4.4% of those in the ASA group, with no significant difference between groups (RR, 0.89; 95% CI, 0.51–1.55). The incidence of the primary safety endpoint, defined as the composite of fatal or major bleeding, also did not differ significantly between groups, with 10 events (1.6%) in the rivaroxaban group and four events (0.6%) in the ASA group (hazard ratio, 2.51; 95% CI, 0.79–7.95). However, compared with ASA, rivaroxaban was associated with a significantly higher incidence of clinically relevant non-major bleeding (5.5% vs. 1.6%) and minor bleeding (11.5% vs. 3.1%).

ALONE-AF and OCEAN studies: interpretation of results and practical implications

In the ALONE-AF study, the 2-year incidence of the primary endpoint—stroke, systemic embolism, and major bleeding—amongst patients without documented AF recurrence during 1 year after transcatheter ablation was significantly lower in those who discontinued OAC compared with those who continued therapy. This difference was primarily driven by a reduction in major bleeding events, whereas the incidence of stroke was comparable between the two groups. However, the observed increase in major bleeding associated with prolonged OAC should be interpreted in light of two important aspects of the ALONE-AF trial. First, the study included exclusively Asian patients, who may have a higher bleeding risk whilst receiving OAC; therefore, the generalizability of these findings to other populations remains uncertain. Second, the trial had an open-label design in which patients were aware of whether they were receiving anticoagulant therapy (no placebo was used), raising the possibility that patients receiving OAC may have been more likely to seek medical attention or receive treatment for bleeding events. Consequently, the magnitude of the risk associated with prolonged OAC cannot be clearly defined based solely on the ALONE-AF study.

In the OCEAN study, amongst patients who underwent transcatheter AF ablation, had no recurrence during the year following the procedure, and had risk factors for stroke, treatment with rivaroxaban 15 mg compared with ASA did not significantly reduce the 3-year incidence of stroke, systemic embolism, or new silent embolic stroke, but was associated with an increased risk of clinically relevant non-major bleeding. Notably, the trial demonstrated a substantially low incidence of thromboembolic events in both treatment groups, with annualized rates of the primary efficacy endpoint of 0.31% per year with rivaroxaban and 0.66% per year with ASA. These rates are markedly lower than those predicted by the CHA₂DS₂-VASc score in a typical non-ablated AF cohort, and also below the threshold at which OAC is generally recommended.

Taken together, data from these two trials suggest that it is clinically reasonable to consider discontinuation of OAC in patients without documented AF recurrence for at least 1 year after ablation. However, successful implementation of this strategy in clinical practice requires careful consideration of the following key elements:1 identification of patients at low thromboembolic risk, comparable to those enrolled in the two trials, in whom prolonged OAC may be unnecessary or who may even benefit from OAC discontinuation because of an increased bleeding risk; and establishment of appropriate follow-up strategies to ensure accurate detection of recurrent atrial arrhythmias in patients in whom OAC is discontinued after AF ablation.

Selection of patients in whom discontinuation of oral anticoagulation after AF ablation may be considered

In both the ALONE-AF and OCEAN trials, approximately 70% of patients had a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score ≤2, and amongst the remaining patients, only about 10% had a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score ≥4. Accordingly, the results of these trials appear to be applicable primarily to patients at lower thromboembolic risk. In contrast, in patients at higher risk—defined as CHA₂DS₂-VASc ≥3 in men and ≥4 in women—continuation of OAC irrespective of the apparent success of AF ablation remains advisable, in accordance with current guideline recommendations.7 Indeed, patients with AF and higher CHA₂DS₂-VASc scores may derive benefit from prolonged OAC despite a reduced arrhythmic burden after ablation.

Evidence supporting this concept can be extrapolated from the randomized trials NOAH-AFNET (Non–Vitamin K Antagonist Oral Anticoagulants in Patients with Atrial High-Rate Episodes) and ARTESIA (Apixaban for the Reduction of Thromboembolism in Patients with Device-Detected Subclinical Atrial Fibrillation), which evaluated the impact of OAC on cerebral and systemic embolism in patients with subclinical AF.10,11 Although neither trial demonstrated a clear net clinical benefit of OAC in the overall study population, a prespecified subgroup analysis of ARTESIA showed that patients with a CHA₂DS₂-VASc score ≥4 derived benefit from OAC, with a reduction in ischaemic stroke risk that outweighed the associated bleeding risk.12 In addition, a large recent Japanese registry showed that continuation of OAC beyond 6 months after AF ablation was associated with a reduction in adjusted thromboembolic risk without an increase in bleeding events only in patients with a CHADS₂ score ≥3.4

Finally, regardless of the risk score, a history of prior stroke is a key variable to consider when deciding whether to discontinue or continue OAC after ablation. In the OCEAN trial, patients with a prior stroke accounted for 2.7% of the overall study population, whereas in ALONE-AF only 5.6% of enrolled patients had a history of stroke or transient ischaemic attack. These data suggest that the results of the two trials may not be applicable to patients identified as being at particularly high risk due to a previous stroke. Therefore, any decision to discontinue OAC after AF ablation should be preceded by a careful, individualized assessment of multiple clinical and echocardiographic variables to identify patients at the lowest thromboembolic risk, in whom prolonged OAC may not be necessary. This preliminary evaluation should also take into account concomitant bleeding risk to identify patients at higher haemorrhagic risk who might benefit from OAC discontinuation.

Follow-up management of patients who discontinue oral anticoagulation after AF ablation

The current guideline recommendation to continue OAC indefinitely after the post-ablation period based on the patient’s risk profile, irrespective of procedural success, is largely driven by the recognition that AF recurrence after ablation is common. In a study with a 10-year follow-up after AF ablation, patients who were initially free from atrial arrhythmias at 12 months after ablation had a 30% incidence of AF recurrence at 10 years.13 Moreover, in the ALONE-AF study, atrial arrhythmia recurred in 40 patients (9.6%) in the no-OAC group and in 37 patients (8.7%) in the OAC group at a median of 12 months (interquartile range, 6.0–17.4 months) after randomization. These findings underscore the need to define optimal follow-up strategies for close rhythm monitoring in patients for whom discontinuation of OAC is considered.

In ALONE-AF, patients underwent routine ECG monitoring at each follow-up visit and 24–72 h Holter monitoring at least every 6 months. Additional Holter monitoring, use of event recorders, or wearable ECG devices were recommended if patients reported symptoms suggestive of arrhythmia recurrence. Such follow-up strategies may be more difficult to implement in routine clinical practice, where continuous monitoring with an implantable loop recorder could be considered. However, these devices increase healthcare costs, whereas the use of wearable technologies—including smartwatch- or smartphone-based applications—may represent a more sustainable solution, particularly for long-term follow-up.

In this context, longer-term data on the safety of OAC discontinuation after AF ablation are needed beyond the 2− and 3-year follow-up durations reported in the ALONE-AF and OCEAN trials, respectively, especially in light of the progressive increase in AF recurrences over time and the dynamic evolution of patients’ thromboembolic risk profiles.

Conclusions

The results of the ALONE-AF and OCEAN trials suggest that discontinuation of OAC after AF ablation may be considered in selected patients who have had no documented recurrence of atrial arrhythmias for at least 1 year following the procedure. The decision to discontinue OAC should be made through shared decision-making with the patient and should be based on a careful, individualized clinical assessment aimed at identifying patients at low thromboembolic risk, comparable to those enrolled in the trials, who may not require prolonged anticoagulation treatment or who may even benefit from OAC discontinuation in the presence of an increased bleeding risk. Furthermore, the implementation of sustainable follow-up strategies capable of accurately detecting recurrent atrial arrhythmias is essential to support decisions regarding OAC discontinuation after AF ablation in routine clinical practice.
