Ethical issues in Iranian pediatrics: a qualitative study
Authors: Maryam Modabber, Shabnam Bazmi, Leila Masoudiyekta, Mina Forouzandeh
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundChildren are recognized as one of the most vulnerable groups in society, and their integration into healthcare systems involves not only medical care but also significant emotional investment and time to achieve optimal outcomes. This process is complicated by various ethical challenges that healthcare teams face daily, influenced by social, cultural, economic, and religious factors. This study aims to identify the ethical challenges and the factors influencing their emergence in pediatric care in Iran. While developed countries have established ethical guidelines to address these challenges, Iran lacks similar frameworks tailored to its cultural and social context. Developing such ethical guidelines is crucial for fostering critical thinking, building trust among stakeholders, and ultimately facilitating ethical decision-making in pediatric healthcare. Given the absence of prior research on this topic in Iran, this study seeks to fill this gap by exploring the specific ethical dilemmas faced in pediatric settings.MethodsThe present qualitative study was conducted based on a grounded theory. The participants were 39 practitioners who were interviewed after giving consent and being briefed about the study. All interviews were recorded, transcribed, and then analyzed in this study. The data were then analyzed using Strauss-Corbin (1998) method in three phases of open, axial, and selective coding.ResultsDuring analysis, 1410 initial (open) codes, 22 categories, and 6 axial codes were obtained to explain the ethical challenges. These challenges included “Insufficient collaboration of physicians with parents and children”,” Lack of support and respect for the service recipient by the physician”, “Lack of trust and mutual understanding between physician and parents”, “Lack of responsiveness and proper education to parents by the physician”. Several factors contributed to the development of ethical challenges. This presented model, the most central of them was the distorted professional identity of the treatment team.DiscussionThe factors that cause ethical challenges in the pediatric clinic include: Inadequate moral education, Weak management, Lack of moral and legal rule, Individual Capabilities and Personality Traits, popular culture, distorted professional identity, the central code of which was distorted professional identity. The process of professional identity formation is a long-term and continuous process that is influenced by several factors. This conceptual model of professional identity creates the above ethical challenges in pediatrics medicine.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12910-025-01204-6.Keywords: Ethical challenges, Professional identity, Pediatrics medicine

Introduction

The triadic physician-patient-parent relationship is critical for decision-making in children. Pediatrics is a complex and emotionally charged environment [1]. Furthermore, ethical challenges in pediatrics encompass a wide range of complex and nuanced issues that require significant attention and care. The admission of a child into the health care system, in addition to the responsibility for care and treatment, requires more emotional interactions, additional energy, and time to achieve better outcomes, which can be compounded by religious, social, cultural, and economic factors, potentially leading to ethical dilemmas and creating greater stress and uncertainty among the clinical team, especially pediatricians, and creating a distinct role for pediatric health care professionals compared to other staff [2, 3].

In current century, clinical issues have led to increasing ethical dilemmas in medicine, and a simple solution is rarely offered. This problem is particularly noticeable in ethical discussions concerning children who are incapable of decision-making. On one hand, all discussions of bioethics emphasize adult centrality, and applying these principles to children sometimes proves challenging [4]. For instance, the principle of autonomy, crucial in adults, seems less applicable and directly useful for children [5]. Similarly, the principle of beneficence requires parents or guardians to act in the child’s best interest, yet determining the child’s best interests and what standards should guide these decisions complicates matters. Another issue with the four principles approach is that parental or guardian authority does not always align clearly with these principles [6, 7]. Therefore, addressing ethical dilemmas in pediatric clinical settings solely through the four principles approach oversimplifies matters and should not be directly translated from adult clinical ethical conflicts. Fundamental reconsideration is necessary before applying solutions to children. Hence, there is a need to develop a new approach to pediatric medical ethics [8].

According to pediatric medical ethics texts in developed countries, issues raised in pediatric medicine involve general and common discussions with complex or solvable ethical conflicts within the healthcare system. Each country’s healthcare team faces these issues daily based on social, cultural, economic, religious, family-oriented systems, etc., causing tension and anxiety among those involved in tripartite decision-making between physician, patient, and family. To overcome these dilemmas and choose options that serve the best interests of the child and family, guidelines and ethical codes are necessary. An ethical code can provide a basis for decision-making options and individuals cannot judge in an ethical vacuum. These codes serve as ethical guidelines and merely act as catalysts but cannot substitute for discussions and consultations on ethical issues [6]. Currently, guidelines and protocols are being developed and implemented to resolve existing ethical conflicts in pediatric clinical settings in the United States, the United Kingdom, Canada, and most European countries [3, 8]. In Iran, daily ethical dilemmas arise in outpatient clinics and pediatric hospitals. Therefore, it is necessary to develop ethical solutions and guidelines based on the prevailing cultural, social, economic, religious, and other conditions in society. This approach should help enhance critical thinking skills, build trust between parties, and ultimately aid in ethical decision-making. Therefore, considering the multifaceted nature of ethical challenges in pediatric medicine and the influence of various factors on the occurrence of ethical problems in the clinical practice of pediatric specialists and subspecialists, it seems that a Grounded Theory-based theoretical approach is suitable for identifying and elucidating the ethical challenges in pediatric medicine that healthcare teams face daily. Additionally, the data collected during this research process is a valuable source for offering solutions to address the ethical problems prevalent in pediatric medicine in Iran.

Materials and methods

The present study was conducted qualitatively and with a “grounded theory” approach, assuming that the concepts of ethical challenges are not known in pediatric physicians. The aim of this study was to explain the nature, structure, and process of formation of ethical challenges in pediatrics and to present a theory about how behaviors related to ethical challenges are formed.

According to Strauss and Corbin, a qualitative researcher must know how participants experience a phenomenon and what they understand about their experience. They always pay attention to the fact that explaining a phenomenon and describing the process of human actions, interactions, and emotions that occur in response to it is not comprehensive enough without considering the context and context of their formation [9]. The purpose of its implementation is, rather than describing a specific phenomenon, to develop a theory about the dominant social processes underlying the formation of that phenomenon [10]. Using this approach, researchers are able to examine social processes and explain the process of formation of a phenomenon, in the real conditions of its occurrence [11].

In the present study, considering the multifactorial nature of ethical challenges and the influence of various educational, economic, social and cultural factors on its occurrence, the “context-based theory” approach was chosen to explain the process and recognize the different dimensions of this phenomenon in pediatrics.

The researcher, after obtaining ethical approval, began sampling from available participants. informed consent was obtained from all the participants in the interview. Regarding the recording of the interviewee’s voice, Verbal informed consent was obtained. The research was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and other relevant national guidelines. After explaining the objectives, study methods, and ensuring the confidentiality of information, semi-structured interviews were conducted. Prior to each interview, a demographic information questionnaire was completed by participants (Table 1). Then, based on the interview guide, questions with the following concepts were asked: What ethical challenges have you encountered during clinical practice in children? What are your clinical experiences in this field? What solutions do you think of?

Then, based on the answers provided, the researcher guided the discussion to achieve the desired responses. The interviews with parents began with the question, “Does your child’s doctor involve you in the treatment process?” This question indicates the extent of family participation in the child’s clinical decision-making and the importance given to family requests. This open question provided a suitable basis for continuing the discussions. During the interviews, notes were taken about the participants’ nonverbal messages. The duration of the interview varied from at least 45 min to 2 h.

The questionnaire included general questions. Therefore, the information obtained from the participant determined the type of questions for the next interviewee. In this type of interview, the interviewee was asked to elaborate on the answers with the questions “Why? How? Explain more?”

Discovering and explaining the underlying psychosocial process of the data plays a very important role in integrating the data and forming the final theory. In a grounded theory study, the explanation of a psychosocial process is based on a theory that is formed around a central category. Therefore, the questions were about reasons, contexts, possibilities, consequences, diversity, and conditions, which helped the researcher in extracting theoretical codes. The information was collected through in-depth individual interviews, observation methods, and note-taking of nonverbal messages. Interviews were conducted with the agreement between the researcher and the participant, in line with scientific interview principles, and were subject to change. To ensure maximum diversity, samples were selected from general practitioners in Tehran city or healthcare centers, specialized and subspecialist pediatricians with private practices or full-time university faculty members in Tehran, nurses working in educational hospitals, and parents of sick children with various socio-economic backgrounds in different government, educational, and private hospitals.

A total of 39 interviews were conducted (21 physicians, 15 parents, 3 nurses). The analyst continuously gathered, coded, and analyzed data to develop the emerging theory during the process. The researcher decided which data to collect at each research stage and which group of physicians to select as the next sample. Selection of individuals, groups, or other sampling considerations was based on the evolving theory, aiming to create a new level of insight that would support the developing theory. Subsequently, the research progressed based on theoretical sampling. In this type of sampling, the selection of each sample was carried out after initial interviews and the emergence of initial classifications. The selection of interviews included available pediatricians, general practitioners, nurses working in hospitals, and parents of sick children from different socio-economic backgrounds selected from various government-teaching and private hospitals. After completing each interview, interview transcripts and data analysis, initial (free) coding was performed, and based on the concepts derived from the categories, the researcher decided which group of physicians to select for the next interview. The study continued based on theoretical sampling. The duration of data collection for this study was from July 2014 to March 2015 and was analyzed simultaneously.

During the open coding phase of this study, after each interview, the audio file was listened to and transcribed word for word and must be analyzed before conducting the next interview. This way, each interview directed the subsequent one. Following the transcription of each interview, the audio files were reviewed again, and notes were taken regarding key points made by participants, as well as the researcher’s thoughts and interpretations of their statements, in the margins of the transcripts. Data analysis was done using a line-by-line analysis method. The transcribed texts of each interview were read multiple times, key sections were highlighted using Word software, and conceptual codes or direct quotes reflecting what participants had said were identified. The coded versions were reviewed after a few days, recoded, and compared for consistency. The results of the two coding phases were compared to ensure code consistency, data stability, and alignment. Some of the coded excerpts were shared with participants for validation, and their acceptance was confirmed. In the axial coding phase, the analyst systematically formed categories, established relationships between them and subcategories. Similar meaningful codes were grouped together in this phase to form the broadest categorization first, followed by the inclusion of subcategories in subsequent stages. After axial coding, the researcher engaged in an iterative process of breaking down, comparing, and re-categorizing, aiming to refine, complete, and develop the categories. New categories with explicit names and concepts were identified and formed as subcategories. Subsequently, in the selective coding phase of the current research, the categories were linked together to develop a theory explaining ethical challenges in pediatric medicine. The researcher continuously reviewed initial data, notes, and conducted comparative analyses to harmonize and discover relationships between primary and subcategories, refining and developing theoretical concepts and codes. These actions enabled the researcher to integrate the concepts obtained during open and axial coding stages, using them to select a central category. The researcher then proceeded with theoretical coding, aiming to transition from descriptive statements to theoretical statements and abstract thinking. All possibilities influencing the data analysis process and findings were carefully considered to move towards theoretical and abstract formulations.

Explaining the basic psychosocial process is based on a theory that is formed around the central category. Questions that can help the researcher in extracting theoretical codes include: What is the cause of these behaviors? Under what circumstances do they occur? What are their results? At this stage, the central code titled “Ethical Challenges” that was explained in the process of analysis and comparison among conceptual classes, indicated the existence of clinical ethics problems in the field of doctor-patient interaction in pediatrics.

These concepts include: (1) Insufficient cooperation of the doctor with parents and child (2) Lack of support and respect for the service recipient by the doctor (3) Lack of trust and mutual understanding between the doctor and parents (4) Lack of appropriate accountability and education to parents by the doctor.

Ethical issues in Iranian pediatrics

Results

In the 21-member medical team, consisting of 10 women and 11 men, 2 of them were general practitioners, 4 were pediatric specialists, and 15 were pediatric subspecialists. After conducting interviews, a model of ethical challenges in pediatric medicine was developed, highlighting seven conceptual categories: “Ethical Challenges,” “Compromised Professional Identity,” “Management Weaknesses,” “Legal Ethical Gaps,” “Inadequate Medical Ethics Education,” “Popular culture,” And “Individual Capabilities And Personality Traits Of Pediatric Physicians,” which can influence pediatric physicians’ motivation to adhere to professional principles in pediatric clinics, reduce ethical challenges, and shape the ethical landscape in pediatric medicine. Finally, a model illustrating the relationship between individual and environmental factors contributing to ethical challenges was presented.

Ethical challenges

Ethical challenges were the initial concept that emerged during the continuous analysis and comparison of data. It signifies the existence of clinical ethical problems in the interaction between physicians and patients in pediatric medicine. This concept was derived from the study participants’ perspectives and the researcher’s conclusions based on their experiences. These concepts encompass four groups of clinical ethical challenges in pediatric medicine:

Insufficient physician collaboration with parents and children

This category includes two concepts: “decision-making based on professional knowledge and clinical experience” and “possession of decision-making rights due to greater capability.”

Decision-making based on professional knowledge and clinical experience

This concept, as one of the fundamental challenges in professional behavior and the most important factor creating ethical challenges in the insufficient physician collaboration with parents and children, particularly in the neonatal group.

“Physicians currently do not consult with parents and from the outset tell them the child will not survive and almost act arbitrarily.” (Pediatric Subspecialist 1).

“Conditions improved after 2001, and our responsiveness also improved. We decided to save infants born before 28 weeks. Previously, I used to say due to lack of facilities we couldn’t save them and would abandon the infant.” (Pediatric Subspecialist 2).

Possession of decision-making rights

The majority of participating physicians believed that most families have varying degrees of ignorance and lack of knowledge regarding illness, treatment, and timely medical care. If necessary guidance is not provided to them, they may neglect timely treatment or follow-up for their children.

“Most parents have varying degrees of ignorance and lack of knowledge regarding illness, treatment, and timely medical care. In my opinion, based on Iranian culture, They do not give priority to the monthly checkup of children by a pediatrician.” (Pediatric Neurology Subspecialist).

“Parental involvement in giving informed consent and satisfaction in personal discharge and hospital selection.” (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist).

Lack of support and lack of respect for the service recipient by the physician

The second category of ethical challenges involves the lack of support and respect for children and parents by physicians, explained in five conceptual subcategories.

Inattention to children’s rights in clinical settings

Data from notes indicated the following: When the treating physician was present in the child’s clinical setting, a significant proportion of interactions with children above the age of 6 were very weak. Basic rights, including respect and honoring the child, introducing the clinical team (at least the physician), and informing the child about examinations and obtaining permission in a child-friendly manner, were not observed. Communication between physicians and patients, especially children, was very weak in private clinics and hospitals. Physicians primarily addressed parents, not the child, who had almost complete decision-making competence, understanding, and recognition of the treating physician. Consequently, the child’s position as a respected and honored individual was neglected. However, this type of neglect was less evident in private clinics and very rare in private hospitals. A few physicians in the study found it easier and more genuine to engage with adolescents and believed their participation in providing symptoms was useful for diagnosis and treatment.

“I often ask the child themselves (if they are of an age to understand). I try to establish communication with children aged four and older, and their parents correct them. I go alone with the child into the examination room. I only talk to them. It rarely happens that a child does not come next to me and cooperate. If you talk to them in their own language and engage them, children easily come alongside.” (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist 5).

Lack of support for parents of extremely premature infants

Some participant physicians in the neonatology group pointed out that extremely premature infants discharged from the NICU require follow-up concerning neurodevelopmental issues and complications due to prematurity, for which unfortunately, the healthcare system does not provide any support to the physician and the infant’s family.

“I do not discharge parents of children with developmental disorders due to prematurity and follow them up. Two to three years later, I realized that I personally needed to follow up on the developmental issues of this group of children. The university does not cooperate with us in following up on children, and the system is highly unethical because it is not designed to be accountable to patients but solely for accounting purposes.” (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist 2).

Lack of support for parents and children with congenital disabilities

“I believe the biggest challenge in pediatric medicine in Iran is lake of supporting extremely premature infants or children with chronic illnesses. We need to outline the difficult path families face and provide assistance. We must accompany them step by step. For example, if they require rehabilitation, we should introduce them to audiometry, visual acuity testing, etc. In Iran, this support is lacking, but in developed countries, it exists and is free. A free supportive system stands behind these families, but in our healthcare system, all financial costs fall on the parents. Economic issues underlie many ethical challenges in clinics.” (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist 2).

Failure to maintain confidentiality and privacy

“In terms of not respecting privacy, we have no choice because the rooms are crowded. In private hospitals, rooms are double or single occupancy, and similar patients are placed next to each other. In some cases, they may not want to talk, and in such instances, we respect their privacy.” (Infectious Disease Subspecialist 2).

Lack of trust and mutual Understanding between physicians and parents

The third concept elucidated in the category of ethical challenges is the lack of mutual trust between physicians and parents.

Lack of trust of parents in physicians and the public healthcare system

“Our people still respect physicians, but this respect has diminished as their trust in the medical community has decreased… due to increased fraud and decreased trust. Patients must seek the truth and see who is telling the truth. Unfortunately, we do not spend time on the patient, leading to misdiagnosis and, consequently, loss of trust. We need to dedicate time to the patient and study extensively to avoid misdiagnoses.” (PICU Fellow).

Seeking multiple physicians

“The cultural problem our families face is constantly changing their child’s physician because they have less trust in physicians. They believe if a physician prescribes a medication or a procedure for their child, it is due to financial interests rather than medical indications. Therefore, due to witnessing these behaviors from some physicians, trust in the medical community weakens, and they are willing to have another consultation with a different physician to receive a full explanation”. (Pediatric Specialist)

Lack of responsiveness and adequate education to parents by physicians

The fourth concept elucidated in the category of ethical challenges is the physician’s lack of accountability and insufficient necessary education to parents.

Being time-constrained and inadequate responsiveness to patient questions

“I think it depends on how much time physicians allocate for their patients. If a physician speaks in layman’s terms, patients understand. Unfortunately, we use medical jargon and do not have the patience to sit down and talk based on the patient’s level of understanding… I think the fault lies with us.” (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist 3).

Patients’ inability to understand medical information

One of the concepts elucidated in this category that leads to physicians not being responsive to parents is the scientific conflict between physician and patient, where not all patients have sufficient understanding of medical information.

“Despite the vast information available on the internet, all parents have varying degrees of ignorance regarding medical science.” (Pediatric Specialist).

Destroyed professional identity

Most participating physicians in the current study considered establishing a two-way relationship, with mutual trust and responsiveness for justification and clarification of concepts, as a valuable and ethical issue in healthcare. They believed that many of the current ethical challenges in clinics stem from weaknesses in these mutual relationships. While they viewed parental involvement as obligatory and as part of basic medical ethics principles, they observed that these opinions and recommendations were not implemented in practical observations. Even in private hospitals, where physicians have significant financial incentives and treat fewer critically ill children compared to governmental-educational hospitals, many participating physicians acknowledged engaging in unethical behavior in the private sector and often attempted to justify it. Their primary justification was the unethical clinical environment prevalent across all healthcare systems.

“An organization that is fundamentally unethical and operates unethically leads individuals to act unethically and believe they are doing the right thing. It doesn’t matter if you work in the private or public sector.” (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist 2).

“Some participating physicians (more experienced physicians) viewed personal gain as one of the reasons for the lack of adherence to professional ethics in clinics. This personal gain manifests in financial, legal, and moral dimensions”. (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist 2)

Management weaknesses

According to the statements of participating physicians, a compromised professional identity is mainly the product of management weaknesses, categorized into four levels: “Deficiencies in supervision and necessary performance at macro levels,” “Ineffective policies of the Ministry of Health,” “Inadequate support for the medical community by officials,” and “Lack of facilities.”

Deficiencies in supervision and necessary performance at macro levels

Most participating physicians identified deficiencies in supervision and necessary performance at macro levels. These supervisory and performance deficiencies are interpretable in several aspects, including:

Lack of continuous real supervision overall healthcare systems

“There is no real and continuous supervision over clinics, it definitely has a negative impact on me, reflected in my family and patient interactions.” (Pediatric Infectious Disease Subspecialist 2).

Decisive response to errant nursing and medical team

Some participating physicians suggested that many unethical practices in pediatric clinics stem from the lack of a decisive approach towards errant medical and nursing teams.

“Currently, clinics in Tehran earn income through non-medical channels. Unnecessary tests and injections are prevalent. In the mortality committee, a physician had prescribed five types of unnecessary antibiotics. the manager was defending him.” (PICU Fellowship).

System’s inadequate response to protests

“One of the most significant factors contributing to parental dissatisfaction with the healthcare system is the system’s inadequate response to protests and weaknesses, including crowded rooms, inadequate ventilation, insufficient facilities in departments and clinics, understaffing, unresponsiveness to parental requests, outdated equipment, and more”. (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist 4)

Inappropriate policies of the ministry of health

“Most participating physicians almost unanimously regarded one of the main factors leading to a lack of motivation to adhere to professional ethics in pediatric clinics as the greed of physicians, arising from incorrect policies such as illogical fee structures and improper referral systems. “(PICU Fellowship).

“Physicians work for two things, respect or money. If you give them money, they work well, like in America, and if you don’t, they engage in unethical practices that go against medical professionalism.” (PICU Fellowship).

Inadequate support for the medical community by officials

One of the highly influential factors shaping the compromised professional identity of physicians in this study was identified as managerial weaknesses, with the primary dissatisfaction of participating physicians from the lack of support for the medical community by officials. This lack of support has led to the decline in the dignity and reputation of the medical profession in society, causing detrimental consequences for children, parents, and physicians and ultimately tarnishing the physician-patient relationship based on mutual trust and confidence. The primary reason for the decline in the position of physicians was seen as excessive disrespect by the media towards physicians.

Lack of resources

In impoverished families where children are born unexpectedly or to parents struggling with addiction, the possibility of selling or abandoning infants near hospital entrances is quite high. Showing kindness and opening a place in the hearts of these families to accept the child is crucial, requiring a specialized support team present in the neonatal and pediatric departments to counsel and guide parents.

“Individuals from lower socio-economic backgrounds and laborers who visit these hospitals generally become parents of a fifth unplanned child, often facing the challenge of what to do with the child. Sometimes parents are addicted, and you feel they might administer opioids to the child outside the hospital or leave a disabled newborn next to a trash can outside the hospital. Therefore, a space must be opened in their hearts for the child, a duty entrusted to a team of caregivers and psychologists who can welcome the child. So, the issue of where an unwanted child, born into a poor or addicted family, belongs and where they can be accommodated, is crucial.” (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist 3).

Legal-ethical gap

Lack of moral and legal rules

In the current study, one of the factors contributing to a compromised professional identity among participating physicians is the legal ethical gap, divided into two categories: a lack of indigenous scientific-ethical resources and a deficiency in efficient laws and guidelines.

Lack of indigenous scientific-ethical resources

“ Neonates who are not viable and have a very poor prognosis, the physician is not allowed to order no resuscitation The legislator does not allow me to do such a thing, and if you want to violate Iranian law, the law does not support the physician, and you end up with a headache.” (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist 3).

It should be noted that among the pediatric subspecialists, only two had studied neonatal ethics as a reference. One of them had studied it multiple times and had a solid grasp of the concepts, while the other was somewhat familiar with the terminology.

“We don’t have a specific source of medical ethics in our country to refer to and make decisions based on. Therefore, we are forced to turn to Western sources that do not align with our conditions.” (PICU Fellowship).

Lack of efficient laws and guidelines

“In Iran, according to the legal and jurisprudential principles, it is said that one must continue resuscitation until the last moment, and the continuation of life and its preservation is mandatory. I have seen few people easily detach a patient from the device.” (PICU Fellowship).

“In Iran, you are obliged to resuscitate the newborn because the law does not support the physician. Even with a patient with numerous complications, you are compelled to resuscitate, as there is no law. The family might say leave it, but if you do and the newborn dies, they will file a complaint.” (Pediatric Neonatology Subspecialist 5).

Popular culture

In this study, the popular culture is among the internal and background factors that play a foundational role in shaping a professional identity under various conditions. The popular culture was categorized into three subgroups, including “materialism among physicians,” “the general culture of parents,” and “the culture of interaction with children.” In the researched community, consisting of clinical society, parents, and children, each group possesses its dynamic and unique culture derived from environmental and societal conditions. One of the significant reasons cited for the decline of professional ethics culture and the reinforcement of materialism among physicians was the impact of economic, cultural, and social issues. Many participating physicians expressed that no one is satisfied financially and is always thinking about higher income, leading to a culture of materialism being reinforced.

“When you go to buy fruits, if the fruit seller recognizes you as a physician, they expect you to buy the best and most expensive fruits or to have the most expensive car and so on. Families also expect you to purchase the most luxurious house with excellent facilities, which is challenging because not everyone has a billion-dollar income.” (Pediatric Specialist).

General Culture of Parents: The common aspect among most participating parents was the fear of expressing truths and objections, which was visibly manifested by fear and anxiety on their faces, silence instead of responding, providing incorrect answers, reluctance to record complaints during objections, and ultimately pursuing researchers after spending hours and days with parents to prove their authenticity and honesty in various ways. Parents considered them part of the clinical team responsible for reporting parents’ objections to authorities. The fear of parents stemmed from several factors, with the most important being economic poverty, which was considered the main axis of most family problems, attributing all issues to economic poverty.

Culture of Interaction with Children: Part of the same culture of parents is what is taught to children. All specialized and subspecialized pediatric physicians admitted that the culture of interacting with children is much more challenging than with adults. This includes the need for a quick examination before the child becomes distressed, often due to their fears. Depending on the child’s age and their level of understanding of the environment and the physician, examinations should be conducted either in the mother’s arms or in a separate examination room. Physicians must engage in entertaining and child-friendly behaviors, even starting before entering the examination room and through interactions with the receptionist, to overcome the child’s fear, mostly derived from incorrect parental upbringing. However, families with only one child, obsessive behaviors, higher literacy levels, and more stubborn children have higher expectations from physicians and expect more from their children, leading to heightened expectations in both the children and parents.

All specialized and subspecialized pediatric physicians admitted that the culture of interacting with children is much more challenging than with adults. This includes the need for a quick examination before the child becomes distressed, often due to their fears. Individual competence and personality traits of pediatric physicians

Some participating physicians believed that the individual competence and personal traits of each physician shape their psychological, social character, and they considered personal characteristics as the essential factor in establishing relationships between physicians and patients. Many physicians believed that clinical experience and medical history are the most crucial factors in the type and method of establishing relationships with parents and children, which, over time and with increased experience, become part of the personality traits of the pediatric group. Most physicians stated that with increasing medical experience, they realize the need to be patient and tolerant with parents and children and show respect to everyone.

Discussion

According to the findings of this study, ethical challenges identified in the pediatric physician group included: (1) insufficient collaboration of physicians with parents and children, (2) lack of support and respect for service recipients by physicians, (3) absence of trust and mutual understanding between physicians and parents, and (4) inadequate responsiveness and proper education to parents by physicians examined the ethical challenges posed by the group of pediatricians. Insufficient collaboration of physicians with parents/children involves decision-making based on the physician’s personal beliefs and values and the right to make decisions due to the physician’s greater ability to actively engage the parent/child/In clinical decisions and especially in the decisions of the beginning and end of life of very premature infants, the child was one of the ethical challenges posed by pediatricians in the present study.

The above results are similar to a small number of studies in East Asian regions, including Japan. In numerous studies conducted in Japan on physicians and adult patients, the Japanese have very few opportunities to participate in medical decision-making. In the same survey of cancer and noncancerous patients, 71% of patients asked for cooperation, 17% asked for a passive role, and only 12% of patients asked for an active role medical decisions were made. The dominant ideology in Japan is therefore the system of medical patriarchy, which is very different from Western countries, which are active participants in medical decision-making [12]. The study by Franco highlights that factor’s physicians in treatment decisions include the physician’s personal beliefs, values, skills, clinical type, reduced perception of hope in life, medical factors, and the type of relationship. Patient factors encompass beliefs, personal values, nationality, decision-making priorities, health-related experiences, understanding of the decision-making process, individual factors, and background factors such as nurse availability, lack of insurance, poor financial status, and geographical barriers [13]. In the current study, the decision-making model in Iran closely resembles a paternalistic model, with physicians playing a crucial role in treatment decisions while patient factors are less prominent. The study attributes the diminishing significance of patient factors, especially in public hospitals, to background factors like very unfavorable economic conditions within families and individual factors such as cultural and social poverty.

A study conducted by the WHO in 41 countries revealed that most participants prioritized dignity as the second most important aspect after immediate access to care. The quality of care received is not solely dependent on treatment but also on the care pathway provided. Lack of dignity may lead to poorer outcomes for patients and impact their health path. The research underscores the interdependence of respecting patient privacy and dignity [8, 14]. Woogara emphasizes that privacy encompasses physical, mental, emotional, and spiritual aspects [15] while in the present study, due to the lack of respect for the privacy of the sick child, the dignity of the child is also weak and neglected.

The study by Fernandes reports on the application of a self-assessment model and tools to uphold children’s rights in hospitals across 10 countries, highlighting varying levels of attention to different rights. Rights such as a child’s right to information, participation, and the lack of communication and educational skills are given less emphasis compared to preserving their physical privacy, dignity, and participation [16]. The study by Butz and colleagues indicates that traditionally, children have had significantly less participation during healthcare visits or in decisions related to health and treatment with their physicians. Typically, children’s involvement during medical visits has been limited, with an estimated 10% participation rate, and the communication framework is primarily between the physician and the parents [17]. The results of the current study demonstrate that one of the most significant ethical challenges in pediatric clinics is the development of a weak relationship between physicians and patients, leading to dissatisfaction among both physicians and parents. The lack of trust in physicians and the public system is a key factor. In the present study, the majority of parents lack trust in the public healthcare system but accept private healthcare due to a positive attitude towards it. The primary reason for parental distrust in physicians is insufficient justification provided by physicians, leading to multiple physician consultations. Due to poor quality of physician-patient communication, patient satisfaction is not achieved, resulting in a loss of trust in the patient-physician relationship. Safran’s research illustrates that the relationship between the seven defined elements of primary care (access, continuity, comprehensiveness, coordination, clinical interaction, inter-personal treatment, trust) and patient satisfaction and health status has improved. It argues that patient trust in their physician lead to enhanced quality of the physician-patient relationship [18]. The findings of the current study are completely different.

The study by Calnan shows that among 48% of respondents, 30% lacked trust in the healthcare system, with their distrust stemming from issues related to healthcare service costs, especially waiting times and the concept of cost reduction for patients. In England, there is a belief that public trust in the healthcare system has decreased. However, trust in physicians in England seems to still be stronger compared to other professions [19]. The first part of this study, which focuses on the decline in public trust in the healthcare system, somewhat aligns with the results of the current study. It is noteworthy that in the current study, parents have very high levels of trust in certain aspects of private healthcare, while their trust in the same approaches within the public sector is very weak. This conclusion resonates with a study by Sachiko Ozawa that examined and compared trust in private and public healthcare providers in Cambodia [20]. In Cambodia, people believe that Public sector health care providers are honest and sincere and not obscene, and explain the disease situation, instilling trust in their skills, abilities, and the appropriate referral system. Several other studies in low to middle-income countries, such as Sri Lanka, demonstrate organizational trust in public providers, but weak interpersonal relationships act as a barrier to complete trust, leading to increased preference for the private sector [21]. This aligns with the findings of the current study.

Alan R. Fleischman’s book is a research that examines ethical challenges in pediatrics, which identifies ethical issues related to specific conditions in hospital or healthcare and caregiving/learning environments. In hospital settings, unique challenges such as end-of-life etc.,. care are discussed, while in the healthcare sector, ethical issues relating to the physician-patient relationship, professional behavior, and the process of educating children are addressed [22]. The current study, without considering clinical situations, generally presents challenges from the perspectives of physicians and families, which aligns with the aforementioned study.

In the study by Margaret and colleagues, five ethical challenges in pediatric educational clinics, obtained through qualitative study by assistants, are categorized into five groups: 1-Enhancing the best interests of children in complex and resource-poor environments 2-Managing therapeutic alliances with parents and caregivers 3-Protecting patient privacy and confidentiality 4-Balancing dual roles of learner and healthcare provider 5-Proper use of professional discretion. These categories align with the results of the current study. The fivefold classification in Margaret and colleagues’ study delves into specific discussions within the realm of physician-patient communication. The current study also highlights four major ethical challenge groups, emphasizing the lack of proper and desired communication between physicians and patients, with categories 1, 2 and 3 overlapping with Margaret’s study. Categories 4 and 5 represent unique challenges for educational-medical institutions, focusing on comprehensive engagement with the service recipient [23], a topic not explored in the current study.

Conclusion

In other conducted studies, ethical challenges in the field of pediatrics have been explored, focusing on the communication issues between physicians and patients, along with the specific dimensions of the pediatric specialty that lack decision-making competence, leading to a triadic relationship. Challenges related to the beginning or end-of-life decisions for sick children, who lack capacity, have been elucidated. This study delves deep into the reasons for the lack of proper communication between pediatric physicians and patients, as well as the factors creating these challenges, drawn from the analyzed content provided by physicians in interviews. The primary reason for the lack of proper communication between healthcare professionals and service recipients, identified as the key challenge in this study, is a distorted professional identity. This was elucidated as the main concept in the pattern of ethical challenges. In the section on the model for addressing ethical challenges, the restoration of professional identity was proposed as the theoretical basis for an exploratory theory. By rectifying the healthcare management system at a macro level, rectifying legal ethical voids, providing effective medical ethics education in social and cultural support areas, and practical steps to prevent ethical challenges, the ultimate goal is to enhance the professional identity of physicians through ethical challenge resolution. To prevent ethical challenges in pediatrics, there is a need to reconstruct the desired professional identity, necessitating the correction of socially and psychologically disturbing factors outlined in the pattern of challenges. This reconstruction leads to the prevention of ethical challenges in clinical settings.
