Development of a deep learning model to classify choroidal melanoma risk factors based on color fundus photographs☆
Authors: Huzaifa Suri, P. Connor Lentz, David A. Leske, Mostafa Mousavi, Haley S. D’Souza, Muhammad B. Qureshi, Raymond Iezzi, Yogatheesan Varatharajah, Lauren A. Dalvin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Choroidal melanoma is the most common malignant primary intraocular tumor and can develop either de novo or from a preexisting choroidal nevus, a benign pigmented lesion. Key risk factors for the transformation of choroidal nevus into melanoma include tumor diameter > 5 mm, tumor thickness > 2 mm, orange pigment, subretinal fluid, and low internal reflectivity on ultrasound. However, the assessment of many of these risk factors requires multimodal imaging equipment and skilled subspecialists, only available at tertiary referral centers. In this study, we developed and validated a deep learning approach to identifying these risk factors based solely on fundus images of choroidal nevi. Results indicate acceptable to excellent predictive performance for detection of all five risk factors. These findings suggest that deep learning models may be valuable tools for identifying high-risk choroidal nevi, particularly in resource-limited settings.

Introduction

Choroidal melanoma is the most common primary intraocular malignancy among adults, occurring at a rate of around five cases per million in the United States.1 Nearly half of those diagnosed with choroidal melanoma will experience metastasis within a decade, with survival following metastasis typically ranging from 6 to 12 months.2 Prompt detection of choroidal melanoma, when small, is crucial to achieve effective tumor management and improved patient prognosis.

Choroidal melanoma arises either de novo or from a precursor lesion called choroidal nevus.3 The latter is a benign tumor with a prevalence estimated as high as 5 % of the general population.4 However, distinguishing between a benign choroidal nevus and a small malignant choroidal melanoma is challenging, as they share many similar features. To aid in appropriate referral of choroidal melanocytic lesions, different risk factors systems have been developed. One such system, the MOLES scoring system, is intended for use by non-specialists, and stratifies the risk of malignancy in melanocytic lesions based on five criteria: Mushroom shape, Orange pigment, Large size, Enlargement, and Subretinal fluid.5 Each feature is scored from 0 to 2, with the cumulative score guiding whether observation, referral, or treatment is warranted.

Another risk factors system was introduced by Shields et al. with the mnemonic “To Find Small Ocular Melanoma Doing Imaging (TFSOM-DIM)”, which highlights key risk factors linked to the malignant transformation of choroidal nevus.6 These risk factors include basal diameter > 5 mm, thickness > 2 mm, orange pigment, the presence of subretinal fluid, visual symptoms, and melanoma acoustic hollowness (low internal reflectivity). Accurate identification of these risk factors typically requires multimodal imaging and extensive specialized training.7

Despite increased appropriate subspecialist referrals following introduction of aids such as the MOLES scoring system,8,9 challenges remain in identification of more subtle risk factors that could aid in early choroidal melanoma detection, particularly in underserved settings. In these contexts, limited access to trained eye care providers, variability in clinical interpretation, and inconsistent image quality can lead to unnecessary referrals or delays in detecting malignant transformation. Artificial intelligence (AI) tools have the potential to assist both community eye care providers and non-specialists by offering reassurance for low-risk lesions and facilitating early detection of high-risk choroidal nevus and small choroidal melanoma. Such tools would rely on readily accessible equipment, such as fundus photography, which is available in most eye care settings. Herein we evaluate the feasibility of an AI-based approach for the detection of risk factors for malignant transformation in choroidal nevus using color fundus photography (CFP).

Methods

This study was approved by the Mayo Clinic Institutional Board Review (IRB) and was conducted in accordance with the tenants of the Declaration of Helsinki.

Study participants:

Patients with choroidal nevi in either or both eyes previously enrolled in the Prospective Ocular Tumor Study at the Mayo Clinic, Rochester between 2019 and 2023 were included in the present study. A total of 404 affected eyes from 384 participants were identified. We identified 40 control participants who had a normal eye examination and no diagnosis of retinal disease (diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, congenital hypertrophy of the retinal pigmented epithelium, retinal hemorrhage, choroidal nevus, or choroidal melanoma). Demographics and clinical examination results were obtained for each patient by reviewing electronic health records.

Study dataset:

CFP images from patients with choroidal nevi (n = 18,000, Topcon TRC-50DX, Topcon, Tokyo, Japan) were reviewed manually by ophthalmologists and ophthalmology residents to ensure that selected images contained a full view of the nevus and were devoid of significant noise, motion blur, or lighting artifacts. Prior to choosing images, reviewers were trained under a supervising ocular oncologist (LAD) and vitreoretinal surgeon (RI) in identifying presence of choroidal nevus, retinal pathology, and high-quality images. Between two and six CFP images were included per eye (n = 404) and included images from multiple follow-up visits. While most images were centered on the lesion, images including the optic disc, macula, or other parts of the retina were also included if the full nevus was in view. A total of 1540 images were identified and hereafter will be referred to as the “nevus dataset.”

Two control datasets were also constructed. Control Dataset I consisted of 4195 CFP images from the same larger set of 18,000 images that did not contain any part of the nevus. These included images from the nevus eye with the lesion entirely out of frame, as well as from the contralateral eye. Included images were from multiple follow-up visits and determined to contain no retinal pathology. Control Dataset II consisted of 250 CFP images from the 40 control participants. These images were also obtained from multiple visits and included views of the optic nerve, macula, and peripheral retina. All images were resized from 2392 × 2048 pixels to 224 × 224 pixels for further analysis. The nevus dataset was used for training, evaluation, and testing of the model, whereas control datasets I and II were used exclusively for inference only.

Risk factor classification:

The following risk factors were chosen for classification: nevus diameter > 5 mm, nevus thickness > 2 mm, presence of orange pigment, presence of subretinal fluid, and low internal reflectivity on A-scan ultrasound. Although the TFSOM-DIM mnemonic also includes visual symptoms, we excluded this risk factor from the model due to uncertainty about whether visual acuity at the time of imaging may be attributable to refractive error or other unrelated ocular pathology. Additionally, while acoustic hollowness and subretinal fluid are typically assessed using multimodal imaging, these features were included in the model under the premise that AI may be capable of detecting them on CFP images by recognizing patterns not visible to the human eye. A board-certified ocular oncology specialist (LAD) recorded risk factors at the time of initial diagnosis based on examination and review of multimodal imaging features. Internal reflectivity was measured by A-scan through the center of the lesion.

Machine learning models:

Due to the limited size of our dataset, we utilized the RETFound pretrained model, a vision transformer (ViT) foundation model specifically developed for retinal images, as the backbone architecture.10 RETFound serves as a strong initialization point for further, more specialized tasks, consistently outperforming several comparison models in the diagnosis and prognosis of common eye diseases, as well as incident prediction of complex systemic disorders.10 As part of our model architecture, we utilized RETFound’s pretrained encoder to extract robust retinal features, which were then fed to individual classification layers tailored to identify distinct risk factors. Each model was fine-tuned to adapt to the specific task of detecting clinically relevant features within fundus images, such as nevus size and pigmentation.

Experimental setup & evaluation:

Our overall model training and evaluation scheme is illustrated in Fig. 1. We employed a stratified 5-fold cross-validation, including 70 % training set, 10 % validation set, and 20 % test set. In addition, since there were multiple images from each affected eye, we ensured that images of the same affected eye were not shared between training, validation, and testing sets. During each cross-validation, we inspected the learning curves and utilized the model checkpoint with the best validation set loss to perform inference on the test set. During model development, we noticed that classifiers struggled to generalize when there was a severe class imbalance due to a low number of images positive for a specific risk factor. To address this issue, we included additional images within the same eye captured from different fields of view during training. We kept all images from a single eye within the same fold to avoid data leakage, as they were not independent samples. In addition, we also utilized randomized artificial data augmentations (e.g., crops, rotations, zoom) to increase data variability during training.

For model training, we used the AdamW optimizer with a batch size of 16 and a learning rate of 5e-3. Each model underwent a training process spanning 200 epochs to achieve convergence. Training was performed using a high-performance computing cluster containing A100 and V100 GPUs. We used the average Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) across all cross-validation folds as the primary evaluation metric, considering values ≥ 0.70 to be indicative of acceptable performance, values 0.80–0.90 to be good, and values ≥ 0.90 to be excellent.11,12 We also computed sensitivity and specificity values using optimal thresholds determined using the Youden’s index method.13 In addition to the evaluation of predicting melanoma risk factors, we analyzed whether the deep learning (DL) models demonstrated robustness when presented with unseen images not containing nevi. We computed model predictions for control set I and II using the trained classifiers and analyzed whether the model predictions reflected the fact that these images did not contain any risk factors. A separate subset analysis was also performed on patients with documented malignant transformation of choroidal nevus during follow-up. Finally, we utilized the Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM)14 to generate saliency maps for qualitative inspection of the image regions contributing to model predictions.

Results

Participant and data characteristics

Patient demographics and risk factors present for all datasets are outlined in Table 1. The primary dataset, comprising the nevus dataset and Control Dataset I, included 384 patients (404 eyes) with a mean age of 61.8 years. The majority of patients were White (99 %) and female (61 %). Control dataset II, consisting of 40 eyes from 40 patients, had similar demographic characteristics but represented a younger population, with a mean age of 38.6 years. Notably, there was substantial clinical heterogeneity in the risk factors present among the nevi in the primary dataset. Among the 404 nevus eyes, the most common risk factor was a diameter > 5 mm (195 eyes), while the least common was the presence of subretinal fluid (13 eyes).

Diagnostic accuracy of the deep learning approach in detecting melanoma risk factors

ROC curves (Fig. 2A) and average area under ROC curve (AUROC) metrics (Table 2) for each of the five risk factors indicate excellent performance for detection of diameter > 5 mm (AUROC = 0.93), good performance for models predicting the risk factors thickness > 2 mm (AUROC = 0.84) and low internal reflectivity (AUROC = 0.82), and acceptable performance for detection of subretinal fluid (AUROC = 0.77) and presence of orange pigment (AUROC = 0.70). Sensitivity and specificity values at optimal cut points in the ROC curve for sensitivity ranged from 0.70 to 0.91 and specificity ranged from 0.51 to 0.85 (Table 2). Furthermore, model performance of each classifier in the training, validation, and testing sets (Fig. 2B) was similar between training, validation, and testing sets suggesting that models likely did not overfit training sets.

Robustness of the AI approach in control datasets

Fig. 3 shows the distributions of the probabilities predicted by the DL algorithms for class 1 examples (i.e., risk factor is present), class 0 examples (i.e., risk factor is absent), control set I (i.e., images of nevus patients not containing nevi), and control set II (i.e., normal images of control participants without nevi). The violin / swarm plots illustrate the overall distributions and individual samples, respectively. The range of probabilities predicted for the control images was similar to the probabilities predicted for class 0 images. Moreover, the predicted probabilities for the control images were highly concentrated with lesser variability compared class 0 or class 1 images, indicating a strong degree of robustness of the DL models.

Preliminary validation in patients with confirmed malignant transformation

Of the 404 eyes included in this study, 12 were diagnosed with transformation to choroidal melanoma. The number of risk factors noted from clinical examination are compared with the number reported by the AI model for each of these 12 nevi (Fig. 4). While the AI model correctly assessed the number of risk factors for many of the nevi, it overestimated the number of risk factors in at least three of 12 cases. Predicted probabilities for each risk factor in this subset of 12 cases is shown in Supplementary Figure 1. The most over-predicted risk factors were diameter > 5 mm, followed by low internal reflectivity and thickness > 2 mm.

Interpreting model predictions using saliency maps

We utilized Gradient-weighted Class Activation Mapping (Grad-CAM) to generate saliency maps and inspect regions in the input images that contributed to model predictions. Several input images and their corresponding saliency maps for each of the risk factor classifications are shown in Fig. 5. While the majority of saliency maps were centered on the visible nevus, some maps, particularly in the diameter and thickness categories, highlighted regions surrounding the nevus, and in many images with subretinal fluid, the areas of activation extended well beyond the nevus itself. It is important to note that Grad-CAM provides coarse, qualitative localization. It may highlight contextual features used for size estimation or diffuse changes associated with risk factors, such as subretinal fluid. As a result, some heatmaps may not align precisely with the nevus margins.

Discussion

In the present study, we created and validated machine learning models to recognize risk factors in fundus photographs previously determined to be important for predicting progression of choroidal nevi to malignant melanoma. Our models were able to reliably detect tumors > 5 mm in diameter, tumors > 2 mm thick, tumors with low internal reflectivity, subretinal fluid, and orange pigment.

Significant advancements have been made in applying AI within ophthalmology, particularly in glaucoma and retinal diseases.15,16 In the field of retinal diseases, application of DL models to clinical interpretation of fundus imaging has primarily focused on diagnosis of diabetic retinopathy, age-related macular degeneration, and retinopathy of prematurity.17,18 Currently, at least three AI systems for the diagnosis of diabetic retinopathy have been approved by the FDA.19–21 Studies evaluating DL models for the assessment of diabetic retinopathy show sensitivities ranging from 73 % to 100 % and specificities ranging from 53 % to 92 %.19,22–27 Likewise, age-related macular degeneration DL model studies have shown sensitivities ranging from 84 % to 100 % and specificities from 88 % to 95 %.27–31

Despite the widespread application of DL models to various retinal diseases, studies involving DL models for diagnosis of ocular oncologic diseases remain limited. Zabor et al. proposed a DL model to predict whether a patient has a choroidal nevus or melanoma based on clinical characteristics of the tumor reported in the patient chart.32 Like our study, they analyzed several risk factors for malignancy, including tumor dimensions, presence of subretinal fluid, and orange pigment. Their model also incorporated additional features such as drusen, specific tumor location, RPE atrophy, and growth of the tumor over time. The DL model achieved an AUROC of 0.86 for predicting growth of a small choroidal melanocytic tumor within 24 months. Subretinal fluid, tumor thickness, distance to the optic disc, and orange pigment were identified as the strongest predictors of melanoma in their study. While their study demonstrated a DL approach to classify melanoma risk based on risk factors already quantified by clinical experts, the goal of our study was to evaluate whether those risk factors could be identified based solely on standard color fundus images prior to specialist evaluation.

Other groups have similarly developed DL models to distinguish choroidal nevus from choroidal melanoma. Sabazade et al. trained a model utilizing a custom U-Net architecture based on standard and wide field nevus and melanoma images from a multicenter dataset.33 Their model achieved an AUC of 0.88 and outperformed both ophthalmology residents and consultants, with results similar to ocular oncologists. Similarly, Hoffman et al. developed a DL model based on ResNet50 to differentiate between choroidal nevus, untreated choroidal melanoma, and irradiated melanoma.34 Their model achieved an AUC of 0.96 when differentiating between the three image types and 0.99 when differentiating between benign and malignant lesions.

Dadzie et al. introduced a unique approach to develop a DL model from a DenseNet121 architecture to differentiate normal, nevus, and melanoma eyes through augmentation with color fusion of red, green, and blue channels.35 They found that the most effective approach incorporated intermediate fusion, where each color channel was a separate input, and the features of each input were combined before classifying the image. This approach yielded an AUC of 0.98. Future studies involving color fusion strategies to classify malignant transformation risk factors may show improved diagnostic accuracy.

Jackson et al. fine-tuned the RETFound foundation model to differentiate choroidal nevi and choroidal melanomas using both color and autofluorescence fundus images.36 Their results showed an AUROC of 0.90 for differentiating nevus from melanoma and an AUROC of 0.92 for differentiating nevus, melanoma, and healthy eyes. Our study repurposes RETFound for risk stratification of choroidal nevi rather than simple diagnosis. Our analysis of RETFound’s attention maps verify its clinical utility and potential as an interpretable triage tool.

Tailor et al. developed multiple DL models using tabular data extracted from multimodal imaging data, including OCT, autofluorescence, and ultrasound, to predict the transformation of choroidal nevi into melanoma.37 Their SAINTS model in particular demonstrated excellent performance (AUROC = 0.93). Similar to Zabor et al., Tailor’s models relied on structured clinical data derived from expert evaluations instead of the raw imaging data. While our dataset included only a small subset of 12 patients whose nevi ultimately transformed into melanoma, our model identified a greater number of risk factors in these nevi compared to nevi that remained stable. Interestingly, our findings suggest that the model may tend to over-diagnose risk factors in lesions that eventually transform. It remains unclear whether this reflects a limitation in specificity or reflects the model’s ability to detect subtle, clinically imperceptible features associated with early malignant change. Nonetheless, our results suggest it may be feasible to develop a model that predicts which nevi will undergo malignant transformation based on fundus imaging alone.

A recent preprint by Iddir et al. also explored the use of a DL algorithm applied to multimodal imaging in patients with choroidal nevi and uveal melanoma.38 Their study incorporated B-scan ultrasound images in addition to ultra-widefield fundus images. The malignant transformation risk factors analyzed in this study varied slightly from those in our study; for example, the study included proximity of the lesion to the optic disc and presence of drusen. Their DL models achieved high AUROC values, notably, 0.98 for thickness prediction and 0.96 for subretinal fluid prediction. Furthermore, the study also evaluated a DL approach to differentiate choroidal nevus from melanoma. The findings provide strong evidence that DL applied to fundus images can be useful in identifying risk factors for melanoma progression.

Our study provided a DL approach to classify individual choroidal melanoma risk factors based only on standard CFP images of choroidal nevi. We trained and evaluated DL algorithms on a labeled dataset of CFP images for the classification of five risk factors. The DL algorithms performed well in all classifications, achieving AUROC values greater than 0.70 for all five risk factors. Sensitivities ranged from 70 % to 91 % and specificities ranged from 51 % to 85 %, similar to those achieved in prior ocular oncology DL models trained on fundus images (Jackson, Sabade, Iddir). The DL algorithms had the lowest performance in the classification of orange pigment. Orange pigment is a phenomenon highly specific to choroidal lesions that may not be represented substantially in general CFP data. Therefore, it is likely that the RETFound backbone model was not exposed to sufficient data containing orange pigment. Despite performing finetuning using our moderate size dataset, the AI model did not learn generalizable features representing orange pigment in CFP images, potentially because of the class imbalance in the orange pigment risk factor. These observations highlight the need for further refinement of the AI models to handle more subtle and complex indicators of choroidal nevus transformation.

By incorporating two types of control sets – one from the same study sample (control set I) and another from an out-of-sample population (control set II) – we evaluated whether the models demonstrate robustness when presented with images not containing nevi. The probabilities assigned by the proposed DL models to both sets of control images were similar to the probabilities assigned to class 0 images (i.e., risk factor is absent).

To further interpret model predictions, we generated saliency maps to identify which regions of the input images contributed most significantly to the DL model predictions (Fig. 5). Our findings revealed that the DL models focused primarily on features located within or around the nevus, which is consistent with prior studies.39 This observation was further supported by the models’ robust performance on control set I, which consisted of CFP images from patients with choroidal nevi without a visible nevus. While risk factors such as diameter, thickness, and orange pigment are visually appreciable on CFP images, the image features contributing to predictions of other risk factors such as subretinal fluid and internal reflectivity are currently unknown. Despite the acceptable to excellent predictive performance of the DL models, how the salient features of the images help reach such performance for the latter set of risk factors requires further investigation.

Overall, our results demonstrate that it is feasible to identify melanoma risk factors in fundus images of choroidal nevi using DL methods, specifically, prominent risk factors such as nevus thickness and diameter. Thus, it may be feasible to identify individuals who require subspecialty referral even in community care settings and potentially reduce the workload on healthcare providers by allowing them to focus only on cases that necessitate specialized attention, improving overall efficiency and enhancing patient outcomes. While our results are promising, there are several limitations in our study that require further investigation. First, the dataset was comprised of images from a single institution, which may not represent the demographic and clinical diversity seen across different geographic locations. Second, all images in this study were true color images captured with a Topcon camera, which may restrict the applicability of the DL model to other types of fundus images or camera models. A key priority for future work is prospective, multi-center validation across diverse populations and imaging platforms to increase generalizability. Finally, certain risk factors were significantly underrepresented in our dataset, leading to severe class imbalance. For instance, subretinal fluid was observed in only 2.8 % of the patients. Such imbalance limits the generality of the model’s performance for these specific risk factors. Future iterations utilizing larger study cohorts from multiple institutions could help assess the reliability and generalizability of the proposed approach.

Conclusion

In this study, we investigated the potential use of AI to improve the detection of risk factors associated with the progression of choroidal nevus to melanoma. By employing a DL approach utilizing CFP imaging, we demonstrated a viable alternative to traditional multimodal imaging techniques with subspecialty expert interpretation, which could be particularly beneficial in resource-limited settings. The models achieved acceptable to excellent predictive performance, especially in identifying critical risk factors such as nevus diameter and thickness. Although challenges such as class imbalance and the robustness of models in classifying certain risk factors remain, our findings offer a promising pathway toward more accessible, efficient, and early detection of high-risk choroidal melanocytic lesions. Future studies should focus on expanding the dataset diversity, incorporating additional imaging modalities, and enhancing model interpretability to build on the success of AI in clinical ophthalmology, potentially improving patient outcomes through earlier and more precise diagnosis.
