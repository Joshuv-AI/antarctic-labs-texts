Acute pneumocephalus following epidural procedure: An unusual presentation with hypertensive emergency and loss of consciousness
Authors: Sindu Mukesh, Sarayu Devabhaktuni, Sonum Devi, Ramit Singla, Chetan Saini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPneumocephalus, the presence of air within the intracranial space, is a rare but recognized complication following epidural procedures. It is more commonly associated with neurosurgical interventions or trauma. We report the case of a 94-year- old female who developed acute pneumocephalus during an epidural steroid injection, presenting with severe dizziness, hypertensive emergency (BP 236/137 mmHg), loss of consciousness, and subsequent spontaneous recovery. Computed tomography (CT) of the head revealed air in the suprasellar region, subarachnoid space, and third ventricle. The patient was treated conservatively with 100 % oxygen and positioning, showing marked improvement on follow-up imaging. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and management of pneumocephalus following epidural procedures, which can manifest with diverse and potentially severe symptoms. Prompt intervention typically results in a favorable prognosis, as seen in our patient, who was discharged without complications. Our case adds to the spectrum of clinical presentations by illustrating the uncommon combination of hypertensive crisis and acute pneumocephalus during an epidural procedure.Keywords: Pneumocephalus, Epidural injection, Neuraxial analgesia

Introduction

Epidural techniques are widely utilized for pain management, surgical and obstetric analgesia, and therapeutic blood patch placement [1]. Neuraxial analgesia/ anesthesia is known to help nearly 3 million patients each year in the USA [5].

While these procedures provide symptomatic relief and are valuable for diagnostic and therapeutic purposes, they are also associated with a range of potential adverse effects. These complications range from infection, bleeding, and direct needle injury to the more rare complications like pneumocephalus [2]. Pneumocephalus is defined as the presence of air in the intracranial space and it is frequently observed in neurosurgery, after trauma or infection with gas-producing organisms, and it is also an infrequent complication of epidural procedures when air enters into the subdural or subarachnoid space [3,5]. Currently, pneumocephalus as a complication of spinal anesthesia is seen in 47 % of cases, diagnostic lumbar puncture is 12 %, epidural anesthesia or injection is 11 %, continuous spinal anesthesia is 4 % and other dural procedures is 9 % [6,8]. Over the years, several mechanisms have been proposed to explain the pathophysiology of pneumocephalus following epidural procedures, these include the ball-valve mechanism and inverted soda bottle effect or vacuum effect [4]. The symptoms of pneumocephalus vary depending on the amount and location of air/saline introduced for the procedure. Not only should anesthesiologists performing the procedure be aware of the most common acute neurological presentations—such as post-puncture headache, unstable vital signs, confusion, vomiting, seizures, and increased intracranial pressure (ICP) with concerns for non-communicating hydrocephalus, which occurs when air enters the ventricles, blocks the foramina, disrupts normal cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) flow, and compresses brain structures, leading to neurological deterioration [7] but emergency physicians and neurologists should also be aware.

The growing evidence indicates the onset of pneumocephalus following epidural procedures as 1 in 67 [9]. However, there remains a significant gap in understanding the neurological presentations and their timing following these procedures. Early detection is crucial as the prognosis is favorable and about 85 % of the cases resolve with prompt recognition [11,12].

Here we report a case of a 94-year-old female who presented with non-specific neurological symptoms due to acute pneumocephalus while undergoing an epidural procedure.

Case report

Our patient, a 94-year-old female with a history of anemia, arthritis, hypertension, hyperlipidemia, atrial fibrillation, Paget's disease, cervical spondylosis, and cervical radiculitis was recently seen at a pain clinic due to increased pain, in her neck area. During an epidural steroid injection procedure at the clinic, the patient experienced severe dizziness, numbness, and tingling sensations across her entire body. Her blood pressure was noted to be 236/137 mmHg, and she subsequently lost consciousness for a minute as reported to us before regaining consciousness.

By the time she reached the Emergency Department her BP normalized to 127/65 mmHg without any intervention, she was alert and awake with no focal neurological deficits. However, a CT scan of the head was performed to rule out any intracranial injuries, such as a stroke, mass, or bleed, due to her recent loss of consciousness, which revealed several rounded areas of very low signal in the suprasellar region, subarachnoid space, and within the third ventricle, consistent with pneumocephalus (Fig. 1).

Non-contrast CT scan head Axial View demonstrating air embolism in the third ventricular, and subdural space. Regions of air are noted with white arrows.

The Neurology team was consulted, and on examination, the patient's NIHSS score was 0. She was alert, oriented to time, place, and person, with no focal neurological deficits, moving all extremities symmetrically. Given the CT findings, her normal neurological examination, and her recent epidural procedure, a diagnosis of pneumocephalus secondary to the epidural procedure was made. The patient was treated with 100 % oxygen via a non-rebreather mask and placed in Fowler's position at 30 degrees. She remained under observation overnight, and a repeat CT scan the following day showed significant improvement in the pneumocephalus (Fig. 2). The patient was subsequently discharged home without any complications.

Discussion

Pneumocephalus, defined as the presence of air within intracranial space, can occur in various intracranial areas such as the ventricles, brain tissue, and surrounding spaces. It is most commonly associated with brain surgery or head trauma but can also arise, after procedures like lumbar punctures or epidural procedures [11].

Pneumocephalus after spinal or combined spinal/epidural anesthesia usually occurs when air is accidentally injected into the space around the spinal cord while trying to locate the epidural space using the “loss of resistance” technique [11]. A few documented cases also describe pneumocephalus following accidental puncture during epidural anesthesia, typically involving patients who were seated during the procedure and developed an immediate headache that got relieved when lying down [11].

Pneumocephalus can develop due to two main theories: the “ball-valve” and “inverted bottle” mechanisms. “Ball valve theory” suggests air enters through a dural defect which acts as a one-way valve, allowing air in but preventing it from escaping, causing increased ICP that forces the brain to block entry sites. “Inverted bottle theory” suggests that CSF leakage from a dural puncture can create a negative ICP, drawing air into the intracranial space to replace the lost fluid volume due to pressure differences [4,5]. The amount of air doesn't depend on the size of the skull defect, and smaller defects often get sealed naturally, allowing the air to be gradually absorbed and the condition to resolve on its own [5].

Pneumocephalus can be categorized by timing into acute pneumocephalus that happens within 72 h of a dural puncture, and delayed pneumocephalus occurs after 72 h. It can be also classified by severity into simple pneumocephalus, which is a harmless buildup of air in the skull without serious pressure or brain damage, and tension pneumocephalus, a dangerous condition where air buildup causes high pressure in the skull, leading to brain herniation. Most cases of pneumocephalus after an epidural puncture are usually simple and not life-threatening [13]. It can also be classified by location into extra-axial, including epidural, subdural, and subarachnoid areas, and intra-axial, involving the brain tissue, ventricles, and blood vessels [14].

Jumah et al., conducted a review and reported several common symptoms associated with pneumocephalus, including headaches that worsen with movement, nausea, vomiting, tonic clonic seizures, encephalopathy, hemiparesis or disturbance of vision. Cranial nerve palsy is a rare but reported complication as well [17].

Our patient was a 94 years old woman who received cervical epidural steroid injection, and during the procedure she had an unusual presentation with severe dizziness and numbness and tingling sensation all over her body and hypertensive emergency with BP at 236/137 associated with loss of consciousness episode with sudden regaining of consciousness. Given the unclear and complex presentation, a CT head was obtained which revealed pneumocephalus in multiple ventricular spaces intracranially without any evidence of mass effect and herniation thereby simple pneumocephalus.

CT scans are very effective at detecting air, even as little as 0.5 cc, due to the distinct way air appears compared to surrounding tissues. It's crucial to distinguish between simple and tension pneumocephalus because tension pneumocephalus is a neurosurgical emergency [15].

Treatment for post epidural procedure pneumocephalus mainly involves symptomatological management [14]. The air in the brain often reabsorbs on its own, and conservative care includes keeping the patient at a 30° angle, avoiding straining, managing pain and fever, and using osmotic diuretics if necessary. In 85 % of cases, the air reabsorbs within 2–3 weeks [14]. If not, hyperbaric oxygen therapy may be needed. For tension pneumocephalus, more urgent treatments like burr holes, needle aspiration, or repairing the dural defect are necessary [14].

Our patient was placed in the Fowler position at 30–60 degrees and was given 100 % oxygen through a non-rebreather mask, which she tolerated well. A follow-up CT scan showed significant improvement in her pneumocephalus (Fig. 2), and she remained free of any neurological symptoms. She was discharged the following day.

There are several steps that can be taken to minimize the risk of developing a pneumocephalus following epidural procedures. Limiting multiple attempts and using a fine needle, preferably with needle gauges—— can decrease the incidence of damage to the dura. Being vigilant regarding avoiding air entry into the epidural space while using the “loss of resistance” technique may further limit the risk of developing a pneumocephalus as well [16]. There's no evidence on whether air or saline is better for this method, but using air can lead to complications like trapped air in the brain, spinal cord compression, or air bubbles in various body parts, which don't occur with saline. So, instead of air, use of saline to identify the epidural space can avoid complications. If air must be used, its volume should be kept minimum [10].

Conclusion

This case provides insights and emphasizes the importance of recognizing and managing pneumocephalus following epidural procedures. The resulting complications can vary from mild, like headaches, to more serious conditions such as tension pneumocephalus. Our case adds hypertension emergency with loss of consciousness while undergoing epidural procedure to the spectrum of clinical presentation of acute pneumocephalus post epidural procedure which makes it unique. Despite varied presentations and overall carrying a good prognosis, clinicians especially ED physicians and Neurologists should maintain a high level of clinical suspicion.
