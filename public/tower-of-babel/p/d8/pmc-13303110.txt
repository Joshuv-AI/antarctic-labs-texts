Exploring the Impact of the Relationship Among Nurses' Resilience, Work Environment Satisfaction, and Intention to Stay After the Local COVID‐19 Outbreak Situation Changed in Taiwan: A Pre‐ and Post‐Survey Study
Authors: Ru‐Wen Liao, Chu‐Hsuan Cheng, I‐Shiang Tzeng
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo investigate the impact on nurses' resilience, work environment satisfaction and intention to stay during the COVID‐19 pandemic, we explored the relationships among these three characteristics.Study DesignA pre‐ and post‐survey study design.MethodsTwo questionnaire surveys were conducted with nurses at a teaching hospital during the COVID‐19 pandemic: the first survey was conducted during the outbreak period when the hospital's response mechanisms were initiated, and the second survey was conducted during the mitigation period when the hospital's response mechanisms were stopped.Reporting MethodSTROBE checklist.ResultsWhen the COVID‐19 outbreak transitioned to the mitigation period, work environment satisfaction decreased (p < 0.05), intention to stay remained the same, and resilience increased (p < 0.001). However, all three characteristics showed mild‐to‐moderate and significant positive correlations in both surveys (p < 0.001). Furthermore, structural equation modelling (SEM) with bootstrapping showed a significant indirect effect of resilience on intention to stay through work environment satisfaction in both survey waves (p < 0.001).ConclusionOur results revealed the relationships between resilience, work environment satisfaction, and intention to stay when the COVID‐19 situation and the hospital's response mechanisms changed. The impact of these changes and the relationship between these three characteristics differed from what was expected. Work environment satisfaction may mediate the association between resilience and intention to stay.ImpactThis study demonstrated the impact and relationship between changes caused by the COVID‐19 pandemic and changes in hospitals' response mechanisms on nurses' resilience, work environment satisfaction, and intention to stay. However, this differs from the models used in previous studies and necessitates further research.Patient or Public ContributionChanges in COVID‐19 conditions affect nurses' satisfaction with their work environments. Therefore, improving nurses' resilience, providing sufficient support, and adjusting hospital response mechanisms to mitigate the impact on their intention to leave are needed.Keywords: COVID‐19, nurses' resilience, nursing management, turnover intention, work environment

Introduction

The 2019 coronavirus pandemic (COVID‐19), caused by severe acute respiratory syndrome coronavirus 2 (SARS‐CoV‐2), has spread rapidly (Ciotti et al. 2020). After its discovery in December 2019, it quickly became a global pandemic. Due to the rapid spread of this disease, many patients required isolation and treatment, leading to overwhelmed medical facilities (Ranney et al. 2020; Sen‐Crowe et al. 2021) after local outbreaks in many countries. For medical staff, such as nurses who need to care for patients, the influx of COVID‐19 patients also affected their working conditions and environments (Hoogendoorn et al. 2021; Nagel et al. 2022; Ranney et al. 2020; Sen‐Crowe et al. 2021). According to a report by Nagel et al. (2022), the impact of COVID‐19 on hospitals caused poor working conditions and a compromised work environment, contributing to mental health problems among nurses, worsening their physical well‐being, and increasing their intention to leave their jobs.

In Taiwan, after the local COVID‐19 outbreak started in mid‐May 2021, several COVID‐19 response mechanisms in hospitals, which followed the guidelines of the Ministry of Health and Welfare (2021), were activated to cope with COVID‐19 and mitigate its impact on medical facilities until the outbreak was controlled. The response mechanisms included the temporary deployment of personnel and management of unit resources to ensure that hospitals could cope with the massive number of COVID‐19 patients and prevent further spread. However, these COVID‐19 response mechanisms also affected nurses' working conditions, thereby affecting their work environment. It remains unclear whether these effects are beneficial or detrimental to nurses. Additionally, the impact of both events and changes (such as the massive influx of COVID‐19 patients and hospitals' response mechanisms) on nurses remains uncertain.

Thus, individual resilience was important for nurses during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Individual resilience is the ability to adapt to and overcome adversity, such as the challenges posed by the COVID‐19 pandemic. Individuals with high resilience are likely to mitigate the impact of adversity or maintain positive adaptation when faced with challenges (Cooper et al. 2020; Hartmann et al. 2020; Sisto et al. 2019). In the field of occupational safety and health, individual resilience can reduce the negative effects of occupations (Hartmann et al. 2020), increase work performance (Hartmann et al. 2020), and, most importantly, reduce staff intent to leave (Cooper et al. 2021; Gensimore et al. 2020; Hartmann et al. 2020; Labrague and de los Santos 2021). Moreover, individual resilience can be improved through interventions such as training and education (Baker et al. 2021; Brassington and Lomas 2020). This ability helps alleviate high‐stress and shortage problems in occupations such as nursing (Cooper et al. 2020).

However, the work environment can also influence individual resilience (Cooper et al. 2020; Tabakakis et al. 2019; Yu and Lee 2018; Yu et al. 2019). Working in unfamiliar environments (Cooper et al. 2020; Fisher et al. 2019) and under poor working conditions (Cooper et al. 2020; Tabakakis et al. 2019) may affect nurses' resilience. Drawing on the integration of job demands–resources (JD‐R) theory and conservation of resources (COR) theory suggested by Hartmann et al. (2020), resource‐limited or unsatisfactory work environments may constrain nurses' ability to maintain and develop resilience, which may further contribute to the depletion of their core resources. As a result, nurses may have lower intention to stay as a way to avoid further resource loss. Accordingly, nurses' resilience may mediate the relationship between satisfaction with the work environment and intention to stay. This mediating relationship was also reported by Yu and Lee (2018) in a study of newly graduated registered nurses. Therefore, changes in satisfaction with the work environment may influence both resilience and intention to stay.

Since most nurses have never worked during an emerging infectious disease outbreak like COVID‐19, their working conditions and environment were affected by the disease and the hospital's response mechanisms, impacting resilience. Changes in nurses' work environments and resilience due to COVID‐19 could influence their turnover and intention to stay. Most studies have employed cross‐sectional methods to investigate COVID‐19's impact on nurses but have not considered the influence of hospitals' response mechanisms and the evolving dynamics during the pandemic.

Aim

This study aimed to examine the impact of sudden changes in work conditions on the relationship between nurses' resilience, satisfaction with their work environment, and intention to stay in their jobs. We utilised the changes in the COVID‐19 situation and hospital response mechanisms to explore how alterations in work conditions affect nurses' work environment satisfaction, resilience, and intention to stay. We conducted surveys during two time periods: the pre‐survey during the local outbreak of COVID‐19 and the post‐survey during the mitigation period when the hospital's response mechanisms were suspended.

Background

COVID‐19 Response Mechanisms of Taiwan Medical Facilities and Hospitals

Following the COVID‐19 outbreak in Taiwan, several response mechanisms have been activated in hospitals to cope with the pandemic, including: (1) temporarily scheduling some medical staff, mostly nurses, to support quarantine and isolation efforts; (2) closing all outpatient clinics; (3) no longer admitting non‐acute or non‐serious patients to the wards and discharging those who are already hospitalized; (4) postponing or cancelling non‐emergency surgeries; (5) implementing strict precautionary measures in all units of the hospital to prevent nosocomial infection (before the outbreak, these measures were only active in COVID‐19 dedicated wards, which are the wards that hospitalised immigrant COVID‐19 patients); and (6) transforming some wards into respiratory isolation wards to accommodate more COVID‐19 patients.

Some of the response mechanisms ceased when the outbreak entered the mitigation period. During this period, the hospital reopened its outpatient clinics, readmitted non‐acute or non‐serious patients, and resumed non‐emergency surgeries. However, wards transformed into respiratory isolation wards were not reverted to their original use, and strict precautionary measures remained in place throughout the hospital. The COVID‐19 response mechanisms followed the guidelines of the Ministry of Health and Welfare (2021) to prevent nosocomial infections, lack of medical resources and overwhelming situations.

Work Environment Satisfaction in Nurses

Nurses' work environment satisfaction reflects how well the organization supports nursing practice (Lake et al. 2019). This support serves as an indicator for nurses to provide safety and better healthcare and also benefits them by creating a healthier workplace (Wei et al. 2018).

When an organization provides strong support for nursing practice and maintains a reasonable workload, nurses are likely to be more satisfied with their work environment. This leads to higher job satisfaction (Al‐Hamdan et al. 2017; Al Sabei et al. 2020), better work performance (Lake et al. 2019; Yosiana et al. 2020), lower levels of burnout (Al Sabei et al. 2020; Phillips 2020), and a stronger intention to stay in their jobs (or lower turnover intention) (Labrague and de los Santos 2021). Some studies have also reported that the work environment is an antecedent of resilience, indicating that nurses working in positive environments tend to have higher resilience (Cooper et al. 2020; Gensimore et al. 2020; Hartmann et al. 2020; Tabakakis et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2019).

Many instruments have been developed to evaluate work environments (Gu and Zhang 2014). Cox et al. (2006) developed an instrument to assess work environments (Cox et al. 2006; Teasley et al. 2007), evaluating nurse‐perceived satisfaction with management, units, and peer support, as well as workload and intention to stay—characteristics and features of the work environment that nurses consider important (Cox et al. 2006; Lin et al. 2011). Our study considered manager support, peer support, unit support and workload as indicators of nurses' satisfaction with their work environment, while the intention to stay served as the reverse indicator of turnover intention.

Nurses' Resilience

Individual resilience is a complex concept that helps individuals adapt positively to challenges such as the negative effects of occupations (Hartmann et al. 2020). Resilience encompasses characteristics such as personality traits that enable individuals to quickly adapt to adversity (Oshio et al. 2018). Resilience exhibits domain‐specific characteristics, meaning it can change with alterations in work conditions or the environment (Hartmann et al. 2020). Some studies have reported plasticity in resilience, indicating it can improve (Baker et al. 2021; Brassington and Lomas 2020). Recent studies suggest that resilience is more akin to progress (Hartmann et al. 2020; Leys et al. 2020). Hartmann et al. (2020) emphasized that adversity, resilience mechanisms, resilience‐promoting factors and resilience outcomes are important elements of the resilience process. They also suggested that individuals' perceived ability and self‐efficacy to handle workplace situations may serve as resilience‐promoting factors or mechanisms that support resilience (Hartmann et al. 2020). Because resilience is malleable, training and intervention can enhance it (Baker et al. 2021; Brassington and Lomas 2020). For those in high‐stress jobs, such as nurses, resilience can be considered an important ability to protect mental health and reduce turnover intention (Cooper et al. 2020).

This study focused on investigating the impact of changes in the work environment on nurses' resilience. Previous research has shown that the work environment correlates with nurses' resilience (Cooper et al. 2020; Gensimore et al. 2020; Hartmann et al. 2020) and that a supportive work environment benefits resilience (Tabakakis et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2019). A review article indicated that the work environment is an antecedent of individual resilience (Hartmann et al. 2020). However, these results were mostly based on cross‐sectional studies, and the impact of longitudinal changes in the work environment on individual resilience remains unclear.

Theoretical Framework of Work Environment Satisfaction, Resilience and Intention to Stay

Theoretical frameworks describe the relationship among work environment satisfaction, resilience and intention to stay. Hartmann et al. (2020) suggested that the integrated resource theories may explain the relationship among the antecedents, resilience and job outcomes. The integrated resource theories include the job demands–resources (JD‐R) theory and the conservation of resources (COR) theory. The JD‐R theory provides a framework for describing the relationships among work demands, resources and their outcomes, whereas the COR theory describes how resources interact with each other and how they affect individuals' intentions and behaviours (Hartmann et al. 2020).

According to the JD‐R theory (Bakker et al. 2023; Demerouti et al. 2001), work environment satisfaction can be conceptualized as a job resource because a healthy and supportive work environment helps nurses achieve work goals, buffer the impact of job demands on job outcomes, promote professional skill development, and further enhance personal resources (Bakker et al. 2023). Resilience can be conceptualized as a personal resource, referring to positive self‐evaluations regarding individuals' ability to control and successfully influence their work environment. It may be developed through supportive work environments and may also buffer the impact of job demands on job outcomes (Bakker et al. 2023). Therefore, nurses' resilience may mediate the effect of work environment satisfaction on job outcomes.

COR theory explains individuals' intentions and behaviours by proposing that people are motivated to gain resources and avoid resource loss (Hobfoll 2010; Hobfoll et al. 2018). When important resources are threatened, individuals may actively seek to avoid resource loss, such as through turnover among nurses to escape excessive job demands or a poor work environment (Hobfoll et al. 2018). Therefore, the hospital's COVID‐19 response mechanisms may affect nurses' work tasks, which may influence their work environment satisfaction and further impact their resilience and intention to stay. In addition, a previous study by Yu et al. (2019) found that resilience mediated the effect of work environment satisfaction on turnover intention among new graduate registered nurses. However, among nurses working during the COVID‐19 pandemic, the relationships among these three variables remain unclear.

Materials and Methods

Design

Given the primary objective of this study, which aimed to investigate the impact of changes during disease outbreaks and the mitigation phase on nurses' work environments and resilience, a pre‐ and post‐survey study design was used. Two surveys were conducted before and after the COVID‐19 situation and the change in the hospital's response mechanisms.

Study Setting

Pre‐ and post‐surveys were conducted after July 22, 2021, and November 15, 2021, respectively. The pre‐survey was conducted during the COVID‐19 outbreak period in Taiwan when hospitals were implementing outbreak response mechanisms. The post‐survey was conducted during the COVID‐19 mitigation period when hospitals returned to their normal operations.

Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The target population included all registered nurses working in a teaching hospital in northern Taiwan for more than 3 months. According to the hospital's report, the total population was about 800. According to the sample size calculator with default settings (95% confidence level and 5% margin of error), the sample size needs to be more than 260 (Calculator.net 2021). In addition, because this study involved mediation modelling, a formal power analysis was conducted to estimate the required sample size for the main analytical models. The analysis was performed using G*Power (version 3.1.9.7), assuming an α error probability of 0.05, a statistical power of 0.80, a medium effect size, and a maximum of nine predictors, including seven background variables and two main study variables. The results indicated that at least 114 participants were required. Furthermore, Kline (2016) noted that for analyses involving structural equation modelling (SEM), sample sizes greater than 200 are often preferable for stable estimation. Therefore, the final sample size of 260 participants was considered sufficient to satisfy all three sample size considerations.

Participants who refused to participate in the second survey or only participated in the first survey were excluded from the analysis of the differences between withdrawal and full participation. Data and questionnaires from those who refused to participate in the presurvey were immediately abandoned and destroyed and thus not included in the study. Questionnaires with more than half of the items not completed on at least one scale were also excluded.

Instrument With Validity and Reliability

This study used a questionnaire that included three sections: participants' background, work environment and resilience.

Background

The background section involved collecting data on demographic variables that could affect the work environment and intention to stay, as reported by Lin et al. (2011). These variables included age, nursing experience, sex, marital status, education level, type of work department and work shift. Additionally, the number of staff members was included to match the pre‐ and post‐survey data.

Work Environment

Work environments were measured using the Taiwan version of the Individual Workload Perception Scale—Revised (T‐IWPS‐R). The IWPS‐R, originally developed by Cox et al. (2006), was used to assess nurses' work environments. Lin et al. (2011) translated the scale into Taiwanese. The T‐IWPS‐R includes 6 subgroups and 24 items: manager support, peer support, unit support, workload and intention to stay. Manager support, peer support, and unit support reflect nurses' satisfaction with the work environment's support for their work content. Workload reflects nurses' satisfaction with their workload in the work environment, and the intention to stay reflects the probability that nurses will remain in their jobs under current work conditions (Cox et al. 2006; Lin et al. 2011). Each item in this study was assessed using a 5‐point Likert scale, where the respondents indicated the degree of conformity between their situations and the items. Scores range from 1 (strongly disagree) to 5 (strongly agree), with higher scores indicating greater satisfaction with the work environment. The total score was typically the average score for each item. For internal consistency, Cronbach's alpha for the overall T‐IWPS‐R scale was 0.88 (Lin et al. 2011), whereas in our study, it reached 0.912 for both the pre‐ and post‐surveys, indicating a high level of internal consistency.

Since our study investigated the relationships among resilience, work environment satisfaction, and intention to stay, the “intent to stay” subscale within the work environment satisfaction measure was treated as a separate outcome variable and analysed independently from work environment satisfaction. After excluding the ‘intent to stay’ subscale, the Cronbach's alpha coefficients ranged from 0.91 to 0.92 in both the pre‐ and post‐surveys, indicating a high level of internal consistency. In addition, work environment satisfaction scores with and without the ‘intent to stay’ subscale were highly correlated in both surveys (pre‐survey: r = 0.97, p < 0.001; post‐survey: r = 0.97, p < 0.001), suggesting that excluding the subscale had minimal impact on validity and reliability.

Intention to Stay

In our study, intention to stay was measured using the ‘intent to stay’ subscale of work environment satisfaction. In this subscale, higher scores indicate a higher intention to stay or a lower turnover intention. For internal consistency, the Cronbach's alpha coefficients for the pre‐ and post‐surveys were 0.78 and 0.82, indicating high internal consistency, which suggests that this scale is suitable for our participants.

Resilience

Resilience in this study was measured by the Connor‐Davidson Resilience Scale (CD‐RISC), which was developed by Connor and Davidson (2003). The CD‐RISC was initially used to measure the psychological resilience of individuals with posttraumatic stress disorder or anxiety, but it can also be used to measure the resilience of healthy individuals (Davidson 2020). The original version of the CD‐RISC is a 25‐item Likert 5‐point scale, where respondents indicate the degree of agreement between their own experiences and the items. Scores range from 0 (not true at all) to 4 (true nearly all the time), with the total score ranging from 0 to 100. Higher scores indicate greater psychological resilience. This scale has been translated into Taiwanese (Davidson 2020). For internal consistency, Cronbach's alpha was 0.90 (Dominguez‐Cancino et al. 2022), while in our study, it was 0.958 and 0.955 for the pre‐ and post‐surveys, respectively.

Process of Participants Recruitment and Data Collection

In both the pre‐ and post‐surveys, the researchers introduced the purpose of this study during unit meetings, which most nurses attended, and distributed questionnaires and incentive rewards (approximately 100 new Taiwan dollars) to all participants who wanted to participate. The remaining nurses who did not attend the meetings were informed by their head nurses. The researchers then contacted nurses who expressed interest in participating and provided them with a questionnaire and an incentive reward. After completing the questionnaire, participants were required to place it in a designated collection area within each unit, which was an opaque envelope or return it directly to the researcher. The researcher collected the questionnaires weekly, and all were completed and collected approximately 2 months after recruitment.

Data Analysis and Addressing Missing Values

Data normality was assessed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Changes in scale scores between the pre‐ and post‐surveys were analysed using either a paired t‐test or the Wilcoxon signed‐rank test, depending on whether the data met the assumption of normality. Pearson's or Spearman's correlation coefficients were used to examine relationships between scale scores, as appropriate.

For background variables, relationships between categorical variables and scale scores were analysed using independent t‐tests or one‐way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Tukey's honestly significant difference post hoc test, while continuous variables were analysed using Pearson's correlation coefficients. When the assumptions for parametric tests were not met, corresponding nonparametric analyses were applied, including the Wilcoxon rank‐sum test, the Kruskal–Wallis test with Dunn's post hoc test and Holm–Bonferroni adjustment, and Spearman's rank correlation coefficients.

For mediation and path analysis, linear regression analyses were first conducted to preliminarily examine the associations among work environment satisfaction, resilience, and intention to stay. Structural equation modelling (SEM) was then performed as the primary analytical approach to simultaneously estimate direct and indirect effects. The significance of indirect effects was evaluated using bootstrap confidence intervals based on 5000 resamples, a commonly used approach in mediation analysis (Hayes 2022; Preacher and Hayes 2008). Sex, nursing experience, and other significant background variables were included as covariates in both the regression and SEM models. Sensitivity analyses were conducted using subgroup analyses based on nursing experience (during the 2‐year on‐the‐job training program [< 2 years] vs. ≥ 2 years) and type of working department to examine the robustness of the findings. All analyses were performed in R (version 4.4.3), and SEM was conducted using the lavaan package (version 0.6.21).

Regarding missing values, we followed the approach suggested by Wu et al. (2022). For scale scores with fewer than 40% missing items within a given subscale, prorated scoring based on completed items was applied to retain participants while preserving the original scale metric. When missingness exceeded this threshold, missing data were handled using full information maximum likelihood (FIML) in the SEM analyses. For analyses involving background variables, participants with missing data on the variables required for a given analysis were excluded on an analysis‐by‐analysis basis.

Declaration of the Use of Artificial Intelligence–Generated Content

The content of this study was not generated by artificial intelligence (AI). Because the authors' native language is not English, large language models (LLMs) were used only to correct the English writing and ensure that the sentences accurately conveyed the authors' intended meaning.

Ethical Considerations

This study was approved by the Taipei Tzu Chi Hospital Institutional Review Board (IRB No. 10‐XD‐068), and the approved study period was from July 15, 2021, to July 15, 2022. Nurses who wanted to participate in the study were required to carefully read the informed consent message before completing the questionnaire. The informed consent message on the cover page of the questionnaire included the purpose of this study, details about the data to be collected (including the staff number used in matching pre‐ and post‐surveys and scales included in the questionnaire), information about the usage and security of questionnaire data, and a question regarding the participants' intention to participate. All participants were asked to read the informed consent message and answer ‘yes’ or ‘no’ questions. If the answer was ‘no’, or if there was no answer, the questionnaire was considered a refusal to participate and excluded from the study. Participation in this study was voluntary for all nurses, who were free to withdraw from the study without providing a reason.

The respective authors approved the scales used in this study.

Results

Characteristics of the Sample

A total of 639 questionnaires were sent; 509 nurses participated in the pre‐survey, and 209 nurses withdrew. According to the researcher who introduced this study at the participants' unit meetings, withdrawal by participants could be due to resignation or lack of time to fill out the questionnaire. However, since participants leaving this study were not required to provide a reason, the exact cause was unknown. Ultimately, 300 nurses participated in this study, exceeding the suggested sample size of 260, thus ensuring our results are sufficient. We analysed all hypotheses and examined the differences between withdrawals and fully participating participants. Participants' backgrounds are listed in Table 1. The participants were, on average, 28.30 ± 7.05 years old, single (82.00%), university graduates (59.00%), with an average of 6.95 ± 6.44 years of nursing experience. Most were not working in COVID‐19‐dedicated wards (75.00%) and had a three‐shit work status (52.35%).

Table 5 and Figure 1 present the results of the mediation analyses. The regression models were adjusted for sex, nursing experience, and other demographic variables that were significantly associated with the outcome variables. In Table 5, the results showed that resilience and work environment satisfaction were independent predictors of nurses' intention to stay in both the pre‐ and post‐surveys. Specifically, resilience was positively associated with intention to stay in the pre‐survey model (β = 0.01, p < 0.001) and the post‐survey model (β = 0.01, p < 0.001). Similarly, work environment satisfaction was positively associated with intention to stay in the pre‐survey model (β = 0.64, p < 0.001) and the post‐survey model (β = 0.61, p < 0.001). All four regression models were statistically significant (p < 0.001), indicating that higher resilience and greater work environment satisfaction were associated with a higher intention to stay. When both resilience and work environment satisfaction were included in the linear model predicting intention to stay, resilience was no longer a statistically significant predictor (pre‐survey: β = 0.0001, p > 0.05; post‐survey: β = 0.004, p > 0.05).

Figure 1 presents the results of the additional mediation analysis using SEM. In the pre‐survey model, the path model demonstrated good fit to the data (RMSEA = 0.042, CFI = 0.969, TLI = 0.953). The standardized total effect (β = 0.227, p < 0.05) and standardized indirect effect (β = 0.321, p < 0.001) were significant, whereas the standardized direct effect was not significant (β = −0.094, p > 0.05). These results suggested a potential mediating role of work environment satisfaction in the association between nurses' resilience and intention to stay in the pre‐survey model.

In the post‐survey model, the path model demonstrated a marginally acceptable fit to the data (RMSEA = 0.066, CFI = 0.917, TLI = 0.880), although the TLI was slightly below the conventional threshold. The standardized total effect (β = 0.221, p < 0.05) and standardized indirect effect (β = 0.177, p < 0.001) were significant, whereas the standardized direct effect was not significant (β = 0.044, p > 0.05). These results suggested a potential mediating role of work environment satisfaction in the association between nurses' resilience and intention to stay in the post‐survey model. Furthermore, the results of the sensitivity analyses are shown in the Supporting Information. Only the subgroup with less than 2 years of nursing experience did not reach statistical significance (standardized indirect effect = 0.338, p = 0.061), likely due to the small sample size (n = 36). For the remaining subgroups, the direction and overall pattern of the findings were generally consistent with the main analyses.

Discussion

This study investigated the impact of changes during the COVID‐19 pandemic on nurses' work environment satisfaction, resilience, and intention to stay. We also explored the relationships among these three characteristics. Based on the previous results, our study proposes and examines four hypotheses:

Hypothesis 1. Nurses' Resilience, Work Environment Satisfaction, and Intention to Stay Show a Significant Positive Correlation With Each Other

Our results support this hypothesis, indicating that, in both the pre‐ and post‐surveys, all three characteristics exhibited a mild‐to‐moderate and significant positive correlation with each other. This means that if nurses had a higher level of one of the three characteristics (resilience, work environment satisfaction, or intention to stay), the other two would also indicate a higher level of satisfaction. These results are consistent with the integrated resource theories, suggesting that a supportive and healthy work environment may promote nurses' resilience, which in turn may increase their intention to stay. Conversely, a harmful work environment may undermine resilience development and create a threat of resource loss, thereby decreasing nurses' intention to stay and potentially contributing to turnover as a strategy to avoid further resource loss (Bakker et al. 2023; Hartmann et al. 2020; Hobfoll et al. 2018).

Several previous studies have reported findings consistent with ours. According to past research, nurses with higher satisfaction with their work environments exhibit higher resilience (Cooper et al. 2020; Gensimore et al. 2020; Tabakakis et al. 2019; Yu and Lee 2018). Other studies indicate that higher resilience is associated with lower turnover intent or a higher intention to stay (Gensimore et al. 2020; Labrague et al. 2021; Labrague and de los Santos 2021; Yu and Lee 2018). Additionally, research has suggested that nurses with higher satisfaction with their work environment have a lower turnover intention or higher intention to stay (Gensimore et al. 2020; Mulkey and Casey 2023; Phillips 2020; Al Sabei et al. 2020; Yu and Lee 2018).

Hypotheses 2 and 3: The Transition From the Outbreak Period to the Mitigation Period of the COVID‐19 Situation Was Hypothesized to Impact Nurses' Resilience, Work Environment Satisfaction, and Intention to Stay, With the Impact Expected to Be in the Same Direction

The Job Demands–Resources (JD‐R) model indicates that the work environment may promote or constrain nurses' resilience and may further increase or decrease their intention to stay (Bakker et al. 2023; Hartmann et al. 2020). Furthermore, Conservation of Resources (COR) theory indicates that when nurses perceive their core resources to be threatened, their intention to stay may decrease as a strategy to avoid further resource loss (Hartmann et al. 2020; Hobfoll et al. 2018). Therefore, nurses' resilience, work environment satisfaction, and intention to stay were positively correlated and may move in the same direction.

However, our results did not support this hypothesis. Although nurses' work environment satisfaction decreased during the COVID‐19 outbreak mitigation period, resilience increased, and intention to stay remained unchanged. Nevertheless, our results also indicated that these three characteristics were still positively correlated in both surveys. This finding could be attributed to the fact that work environment satisfaction is not the only antecedent of individual resilience. Some studies have shown that personal resources, such as experience and self‐efficacy, may also influence resilience (Cooper et al. 2020; Gensimore et al. 2020; Hartmann et al. 2020). In addition, resilience itself has been conceptualized as a personal resource because it reflects positive self‐evaluations and individual's perceived capacity to control and successfully influence the current environment (Bakker et al. 2023; Hartmann et al. 2020). Although the changing situation of COVID‐19 negatively affected nurses' work environment satisfaction, which in turn negatively impacted their resilience, these changes could also positively affect other antecedents of nurses' resilience. This positive effect could be stronger than that of work environment satisfaction, resulting in increased resilience.

More detailed discussions of the impact of changes in the local COVID‐19 outbreak situation on these three characteristics are presented in the following subsections. First, we discuss the impact on nurses' work environment satisfaction. Second, we discuss the impact on nurses' resilience. Finally, we discuss the nurses' intentions to stay.

Impact of Local Outbreak of the COVID‐19 Situation Change on Nurses' Work Environment Satisfaction

The nursing work environment reflects how well the organization supports nursing practice (Lake et al. 2019), and when work conditions change, it affects nurses' work environment satisfaction. The impact of the COVID‐19 outbreak on nurses' work conditions was influenced by the surge in COVID‐19 patient numbers, temporary reassignments, intensified anti‐pandemic work regulations, unfamiliarity with the disease, and lack of resources to combat it (Bergman et al. 2021; Hoogendoorn et al. 2021; Nagel et al. 2022; Wu et al. 2023). Therefore, when the COVID‐19 outbreak enters the mitigation period, during which the number of patients decreases, nurses' work environments are expected to improve. Our results show that nurses' work environment satisfaction decreased after the COVID‐19 outbreak entered the mitigation period. Analysis of work environment domains revealed that manager and unit support satisfaction decreased significantly. Although the decrease in workload satisfaction was not significant, it was close to the critical value. Considering that some nurses with low satisfaction with their work environment withdrew from the study, the true difference might be more significant.

A possible reason for this is that during the COVID‐19 mitigation period, hospital response mechanisms stopped, and previously discontinued services were restarted. However, the intensified anti‐pandemic work regulations continued, and the wards transformed into COVID‐19‐dedicated isolation wards did not return to their original purpose. As reported by Hoogendoorn et al. (2021) and Nagel et al. (2022), nursing procedures became more complex owing to intensified COVID‐19 anti‐pandemic work regulations, leading to an increase in nurses' workloads. In our study, although the burden of caring for COVID‐19 patients decreased, the restart of discontinued services meant that nurses had to care for several patients under intensified anti‐pandemic work regulations. This could have resulted in an increased workload, even with a gradual decrease in the number of COVID‐19 cases. Moreover, due to the disease characteristics of COVID‐19, all patients are potentially infected with the virus. This increases nurses' perception of their risk of infection, leading to decreased work environment satisfaction (Nagel et al. 2022). Additionally, after reopening temporarily closed units, the support allocated to each department was reduced, affecting nursing practice and further decreasing work environment satisfaction.

Therefore, as the COVID‐19 period transitioned to the mitigation period and some of the hospitals' COVID‐19 response mechanisms stopped, some participants experienced an increase in their workload and a decrease in support from their units and managers, leading to a decrease in their work environment satisfaction. Furthermore, since we did not have data from before the COVID‐19 outbreak, the benefit of the hospital's COVID‐19 response mechanisms remains unclear. However, stopping some response mechanisms negatively affected nurses' satisfaction with their work environment.

Impact of Local Outbreak of the COVID‐19 Situation Change on Resilience

According to the JD‐R model, job resources, such as a supportive work environment, may foster nurses' resilience, which can be conceptualized as a personal resource (Bakker et al. 2023; Hartmann et al. 2020). Therefore, resilience may be strengthened in supportive work environments, whereas less supportive or high‐demand work environments may limit or undermine resilience.

Several studies have reported similar findings, showing that nurses with higher satisfaction with their work environment tend to have higher resilience (Gensimore et al. 2020; Tabakakis et al. 2019; Yu and Lee 2018), and that a supportive work environment can contribute to resilience (Tabakakis et al. 2019; Yu et al. 2019). Review articles have also suggested that social support benefits resilience (Cooper et al. 2020, 2021; Hartmann et al. 2020; Öksüz et al. 2019). Cooper et al. (2020) proposed that social support, including support from managers, colleagues and friends, is associated with better resilience among nurses; notably, support from managers and colleagues is also a component of work environment satisfaction (Cox et al. 2006). Hartmann et al. (2020) further suggested that workplace support and feedback are positively correlated with resilience. Therefore, when their work environment deteriorates, nurses become less satisfied, which can lead to lower resilience.

However, our results showed the opposite outcome: nurses' satisfaction with their work environment decreased, but their resilience increased when the COVID‐19 outbreak entered the mitigation period. Considering that the timing of our pre‐survey did not include the initial stage of the COVID‐19 outbreak, our participants exhibited lower resilience (58.61 ± 14.22) in the pre‐survey compared to the scores reported in the CD‐RISC manual (Davidson 2020). Our participants' scores were lower than those of individuals with generalized anxiety problems (61), as well as the range of nurses' resilience scores (ranging from 54 to 81) reported in most studies using the CD‐RISC manual (Davidson 2020). Therefore, the impact of severely adverse work environments on the participants' resilience may have occurred early during the COVID‐19 outbreak, after which their resilience gradually recovered.

According to Hartmann et al. (2020), individuals' perceived ability and self‐efficacy to handle workplace situations may serve as resilience‐promoting factors or mechanisms that support resilience. In addition, self‐efficacy, work–life balance/self‐care, humour and optimism have been identified as protective factors for nurses' resilience (Cooper et al. 2020, 2021; Sisto et al. 2019). Among our participants, after the COVID‐19 outbreak, nurses may have gradually learned how to cope with the outbreak, which may have led them to perceive that their experience had improved, thereby enhancing their self‐efficacy and, consequently, their resilience. Some studies reported findings similar to ours (Bergman et al. 2021; Sun et al. 2020). They found that nurses and caregivers felt chaotic and experienced negative emotions at the beginning of the COVID‐19 outbreak; however, over time, they gradually adapted to the pandemic and felt that their skills had improved. In addition, the post‐survey was conducted during the mitigation period, when hospital response mechanisms had stopped and the situation appeared more familiar than it had been during the chaotic initial phase. Moreover, peer support, a component of the work environment, did not decrease during the mitigation period of the COVID‐19 outbreak and is also an important antecedent of resilience (Cooper et al. 2020, 2021; Hartmann et al. 2020; Öksüz et al. 2019). Therefore, the impact of decreased work environment satisfaction may have been limited among our participants.

In summary, the impact of decreased work environment satisfaction on resilience may have been limited, while participants' experiences and relevant skills related to COVID‐19 may have increased. In addition, as the COVID‐19 outbreak entered the mitigation period, the situation became more controlled than it had been during the early stage. Consequently, these factors may have contributed to the increased resilience observed in the post‐survey.

Impact of the Local Outbreak of the COVID‐19 Situation Change on Intention to Stay

According to the JD‐R model, job resources and personal resources can mitigate the negative impact of job demands on job outcomes (Bakker et al. 2023). In addition, COR theory indicates that when resource loss is perceived as threatening core resources, individuals may increase their efforts or take active actions to prevent further resource loss (Hobfoll et al. 2018). Nurses' intention to stay, or the reverse of turnover intention, is also associated with turnover risk (Van Der Heijden et al. 2018).

Therefore, among our participants, changes in hospital response mechanisms may have led nurses to perceive reduced work environment support, representing a loss of job resources, and increased job demands, which may contribute to resource depletion. These changes may have threatened their core resources. As a result, their intention to stay may have decreased, and their turnover intention may have increased as a way to avoid further resource loss. However, our results did not support this hypothesis, as participants' intention to stay did not change significantly.

One possible explanation is that participants' resilience also increased. As a personal resource, resilience may have mitigated the negative impact of decreased job resources and increased job demands, thereby reducing the perceived threat of core resource loss. This interpretation is also consistent with previous studies. Labrague and de los Santos (2021) found that compassion fatigue, which may result from an overall lack of resources, affected nurses' turnover intention through resilience during COVID‐19. In addition, Yu and Lee (2018) showed that the work environment affects intention to stay through resilience.

Hypothesis 4. Resilience Would Mediate the Effect of the Work Environment on the Intention to Stay

Our results did not support this hypothesis. According to the results, whether in the pre‐ or post‐surveys, the indirect effect of resilience on intention to stay through work environment satisfaction was statistically significant. Additionally, when resilience was selected as the mediator, bootstrapping in the SEM analysis showed that the indirect effect was not significant, suggesting that resilience may not have a mediating effect (data not shown). These results may indicate that nurses' resilience influences their intention to stay through work environment satisfaction rather than work environment satisfaction influencing their intention to stay through resilience.

These results differ from those of previous studies. Many studies have reported that individual resilience is a mediator or moderator of the negative effects of occupation on turnover intention (Cooper et al. 2021; Gensimore et al. 2020; Hartmann et al. 2020; Labrague and de los Santos 2021). Additionally, Yu and Lee's (2018) model showed that nurses' work environment satisfaction affected their turnover intentions through resilience and work involvement.

However, these results may not contradict the integrated resource theories. According to COR theory, resources may promote the gain or development of other resources (Hobfoll et al. 2018). The JD‐R model also indicates that job resources and personal resources can influence each other; individuals with more personal resources may perceive more resources in their workplace (Bakker et al. 2023). Therefore, although nurses' resilience may be promoted by the work environment under normal circumstances, when the COVID‐19 outbreak affected their work environment, resilience may have helped them perceive and utilize more resources within the impacted work environment, thereby buffering the negative impact of the COVID‐19 outbreak on their intention to stay. This may explain why our results showed a significant indirect effect of resilience on intention to stay through work environment satisfaction.

Other reasons could explain the differences between our research and that of others. Yu and Lee (2018) used the scale developed by Yu and Kang (2016), whereas our study used the T‐IWPS‐R. Additionally, the participants in Yu and Lee's (2018) study were new graduate nurses, whereas our participants had an average nursing experience of approximately 6 years. This difference in experience may have contributed to the variations in their results compared with ours. Gu and Zhang (2014) suggested that the IWPS‐R is affected more by nurses' subjective feelings. As resilience can be seen as a protective factor for nurses' mental health (Cooper et al. 2020), it may influence how nurses perceive and respond to changes in their work environment. However, there is no evidence to support this hypothesis, and further research is required.

Differences Between Participants Who Fully Participated Throughout the Study and Those Who Participated Only in the First Survey (Withdrew)

Our results show that participants who fully participated in our study had a higher percentage of three‐shift workers than those who participated only in the first survey and also reported greater satisfaction with their work environment. According to Rhodes et al. (2025), healthcare employees who withdraw from well‐being surveys may have lower well‐being and a higher risk of turnover. Since the work environment reflects organisational support for nursing practice (Lake et al. 2019), our results may be consistent with the findings of Rhodes et al. (2025). In addition, during the hospital's response to the COVID‐19 pandemic, resources were redirected from low‐priority units to high‐priority units to cope with the impact of the pandemic (Ministry of Health and Welfare 2021). However, most fixed‐shift units were outpatient units, which were considered low‐priority units, whereas three‐shift units were more likely to be high‐priority units. Therefore, nurses in fixed‐shift units may have perceived reduced organisational support, which may have led to decreased satisfaction with their work environment and withdrawal from our study. This may help explain why withdrawn participants were more likely to be fixed‐shift workers and reported lower work environment satisfaction.

Consequently, these results indicate potential selection bias in our study. As noted by Rhodes et al. (2025), this may lead to overly optimistic survey results. In our study, because participants with lower satisfaction were more likely to withdraw, our findings may have underestimated the impact of changes in the hospital's COVID‐19 response mechanisms. This is one of the limitations of our study.

Strengths and Limitations of the Work

Strengths

The strengths of this study are its large sample size and pre‐ and post‐survey research design.

Limitations

This study had several limitations. First, the impact of the COVID‐19 outbreak may have been underestimated for two reasons: the pre‐survey was conducted during a relatively stable period compared to the beginning of the outbreak, and some participants with lower satisfaction in their work environment withdrew from the post‐survey. This could have led to an underestimation of the impact of COVID‐19 on work environments. Second, the sex ratio among participants was extremely uneven, with male participants accounting for only 4% of the total. This disparity may have led to inaccurate inferences regarding sex‐related outcomes. Third, the work environment was measured using a self‐report scale that may not reflect actual changes in the work environment. Fourth, our outcome, ‘intention to stay’, is one of the domains of the work environment scale and not a separate scale. Therefore, potential conceptual overlap and common‐method bias may still exist. Fifth, although the hypothesized pathways were based on a strong theoretical framework, the correlational nature of the data and the analytical design limit causal interpretation. Therefore, causal inferences should be interpreted with caution. Sixth, Although Holm–Bonferroni correction was applied for post hoc analyses where appropriate, the overall risk of Type I error inflation due to multiple analyses may still remain. Therefore, the findings regarded as indicative rather than conclusive. Finally, all participants were recruited from a single teaching hospital, which may have introduced selection bias. Therefore, the findings may not be generalizable to nurses working in other hospitals of medical facilities.

Summary

Our results showed that the transition from the COVID‐19 outbreak period to the mitigation period may have affected nurses' satisfaction with their work environment. However, nurses' resilience increased, possibly because accumulated nursing experience improved their ability to cope with workplace adversity. In addition, our results indicated that nurses' work environment satisfaction may have a mediating role in the association between resilience and intention to stay during the COVID‐19 pandemic. Although this finding is consistent with both the JD‐R model and COR theory, it differs from studies conducted under normal circumstances, which have suggested that resilience mediates the effect of the work environment on intention to stay.

However, because of the limitations of our study, these results should be interpreted with caution. Specifically, this study used self‐reported survey data; most participants were female, some participants withdrew from the study, and the study was conducted in only one hospital. These factors may have introduced selection bias and recall bias. In addition, causal interpretation was based only on quantitative analyses, which may differ from actual clinical situations. Furthermore, given the multiple analyses conducted in this study, the findings should be interpreted as indicative rather than conclusive.

Recommendations for Further Research

As mentioned in the limitations section, our study did not capture the impact of transitioning from the hospital's normal setting to the initial COVID‐19 outbreak setting or the initial phase of a workplace adverse event. Therefore, further research could be conducted before a workplace adverse event is initiated, fully recording the changes in the impact of work adversity on these three characteristics from beginning to end.

In addition, the instruments used to measure nurses' work environment satisfaction vary between studies. The study that provided the model where resilience mediates work environment satisfaction and intention to stay used a different instrument (Yu and Lee 2018). Therefore, future research could use a different instrument to measure these three characteristics and investigate whether there are any differences in the results.

Finally, our study focused only on the relationship between nurses' resilience, work environment satisfaction, and intention to stay, excluding other relative characteristics mentioned in other studies (Hartmann et al. 2020; Tabakakis et al. 2019; Yu and Lee 2018). Further studies could involve more relative characteristics to provide a more comprehensive understanding.

Implications for Policy and Practice

Based on our results, changes in COVID‐19 response mechanisms could create an adversity‐inclined work environment. Therefore, the hospital's process of converting COVID‐19 response mechanisms may require adjustment, or additional support should be provided to nurses to improve their work environment satisfaction and reduce the negative effects caused by changes in work conditions.

In addition, our model shows the importance of nurses' resilience and work environment satisfaction in their intentions to stay. Therefore, improving both nurses' resilience and work environment satisfaction is essential for helping nurses cope with adverse workplace events and decreasing turnover rates.

Conclusion

Our study conducted surveys to investigate the impact of the COVID‐19 situation and changes in hospitals' response mechanisms on nurses' work environment satisfaction, resilience, and intention to stay, as well as to examine the relationship between them. We found that although all three characteristics had a significant positive correlation with each other in both the pre‐ and post‐surveys, work environment satisfaction was considered to be the antecedent of resilience. After the transition from the COVID‐19 outbreak to the mitigation period and the changes in hospitals' response mechanisms, the impact on work environment satisfaction and resilience was the opposite: nurses' work environment satisfaction decreased while resilience increased. However, nurses' intention to stay was not affected. Additionally, path analyses using SEM suggested that work environment satisfaction may mediate the association between resilience and intention to stay.
