Petroleum jelly vs. Saline in tracheostomy wound care and pressure injury prevention: a randomized controlled trial
Authors: Chidchanok Chutimasaengtrakul, Sarinya Urathamakul, Kanokrat Bunnag
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundTracheostomy-related pressure injuries (TRPIs) compromise recovery and prolong hospital stay.MethodsWe conducted a prospective randomized controlled trial at a tertiary hospital to compare the efficacy of petroleum jelly application versus normal saline irrigation in preventing peristomal pressure injuries and promoting tracheostomy wound healing. This study enrolled 28 participants, aged 18–80 years, who underwent tracheostomy procedures at Vajira Hospital, Navamindradhiraj University. They were randomly assigned using a permuted-block randomization methods (block size = 4) to either the petroleum jelly application group or the sodium chloride irrigation (0.9% NaCl solution) group. The outcomes were evaluated by one physician and one nurse at first, second, and fourth weeks postoperatively using the Pressure Ulcer Scale for Healing (PUSH) Tool and Skin Integrity Score.ResultsThe petroleum jelly application group demonstrated significantly lower mean scores on the PUSH Tool at first, second, and fourth weeks than the sodium chloride irrigation group. Moreover, the petroleum jelly application group exhibited significantly higher median scores for skin integrity at first, second, and fourth weeks than the sodium chloride irrigation group. The interrater reliability between the two observers was good to excellent. The petroleum-jelly arm showed faster improvement on both scoring tools across all follow-up points.ConclusionsThe participants who received petroleum jelly application following tracheostomy demonstrated better results in preventing pressure injuries and maintaining skin integrity compared with those who received sodium chloride irrigation.Trial registrationTCTR20240806002 (Thai Clinical Trials Registry), registered on August 6, 2024. Introduction

Tracheostomy, [1–3] a surgical procedure, establishes a direct passage between the trachea and the anterior neck skin, serving various purposes, such as managing upper airway obstructions and addressing conditions, such as severe obstructive sleep apnea, prolonged mechanical ventilation, and laryngeal or head and neck cancer. Regular post tracheostomy monitoring is crucial to assess patients’ conditions and replace the tracheostomy tube as needed, with complications including bleeding, tube obstruction, pressure injuries, and wound infections.

Recent audits report Tracheostomy-related pressure injurie (TRPIs) incidence ranging from 17% in mixed ICU populations to 23% five days post-tracheostomy despite routine care. [4, 5] Medical-device-related pressure injuries (MDR-PIs) now constitute ~ 30% of all hospital pressure injuries, with a global prevalence up to 19%. [6] Recommended preventive bundles—flange off-loading, tie tension optimisation, moisture-retentive dressings—are resource-intensive and costly. Saline irrigation followed by dry gauze is still routine in many centers, whereas small trials of silver-impregnated foam, PHMB(polyhexamethylene biguanide) gauze or antibiotic ointment have shown mixed benefit at greater expense [7, 8]. Petrolatum is an affordable, widely available occlusive that has accelerated re-epithelialisation in burn and dermatologic RCTs but has not been tested prospectively in tracheostomy care. Saline and petrolatum were therefore selected to contrast ‘standard cleansing’ with a low-cost barrier strategy [9, 10]. Randomized studies in superficial burns and post-excision wounds have shown that plain petrolatum accelerates epithelial closure by ≈ 1.6 days compared with silver sulfadiazine or antibiotic ointment [9, 10].

Pressure injuries [11] represent a significant concern following tracheostomy. Preventive measures entail meticulous wound care, [12, 13] which includes cleansing the skin around the tube with saline-soaked gauze and positioning gauze under the tube to prevent direct contact between the tube and skin. This approach not only improves patients’ quality of life but also accelerates hospital discharge, leading to shorter hospital stays [12, 13]. Given that even a single TRPI can add US $700–$2,000 in extra care costs and delay decannulation by several days, any inexpensive strategy that reliably protects peristomal skin would have meaningful system-wide benefits [4].

This study aimed to compare the efficacy of petroleum jelly application and sodium chloride irrigation in preventing pressure injuries and promoting tracheostomy wound healing, with the ultimate goal of improving patients’ quality of life and accelerating hospital discharge, thereby leading to shorter hospital stays.

Materials and methods

Study design, setting and ethics

A prospective randomized controlled trial, approved by the Research Ethics Review Committee (IRB no: 163/64FB), included 28 participants aged 18–80 years who underwent tracheostomy procedures at Vajira Hospital, Navamindradhiraj University, between November 2021 and December 2022. Clinical trial number: TCTR20240806002 (Thai Clinical Trials Registry), registered on August 6, 2024. Retrospectively registered. This study adheres to the CONSORT guidelines for the reporting of randomized controlled trials. No interim analyses or early stopping guidelines were planned for this trial.

Participants and sample-size calculation

The inclusion criteria were: adults aged 18–80 years, undergoing elective tracheostomy at Vajira Hospital, receiving a synthetic tracheostomy tube made from polyvinyl chloride (PVC), and able to provide informed consent and comply with follow-up care. All tracheostomies were performed using a standardized surgical technique, (open surgical technique with horizontal incision and blunt dissection) and this standardized technique was an explicit inclusion criterion. All participants received identical size 7 PVC tracheostomy tubes. Although cuff pressures were not protocolized, they were uniformly maintained within standard clinical practice (~ 20–30 cm H₂O). Level of consciousness and patient mobility status were not explicitly recorded. The exclusion criteria were: patients with confirmed COVID-19 infection, those who underwent tracheostomy procedures outside the operating room, and those with other surgical wounds or cervical lymphadenopathy that could confound skin assessment. Patients who underwent a tracheostomy using techniques other than the standardized open surgical method described were excluded. The discontinuation criteria included death or the inability to care for the tracheostomy wound by dressing alone, such as requiring surgical debridement. The required sample was estimated with G*Power v3.1.9.7 [14] for an independent two-group, two-tailed t-test. Mean ± SD PUSH scores reported by Chuang et al. [7] (9.1 ± 1.8 vs 7.2 ± 1.6) give an effect size d = 1.14. With α = 0.05 and power = 0.80, G*Power indicated 11 participants per arm. To allow for ~ 20% attrition (decannulation, withdrawal or death), the target was increased to 14 per arm, 28 in total.

Randomization and blinding

Participants were randomized using permuted-block randomization (block size = 4) into either the petroleum jelly group or the saline (0.9% NaCl) group to compare their efficacy in preventing pressure injuries and promoting tracheostomy wound healing. The random allocation sequence was generated by a statistician independent of the trial and was concealed using sealed, opaque, sequentially numbered envelopes. These envelopes were opened by the otolaryngology resident only after the participant's enrollment and baseline assessment, ensuring that the allocation remained concealed until the intervention was assigned. Caregivers and participants were aware of group allocation; however, both independent outcome observers remained blinded throughout data collection and analysis.

Interventions

Wound care began on postoperative day 1 (the morning after the tracheostomy). In both groups the site was cleansed once daily with 10 mL sterile 0.9% saline. In the saline group, the cleansed area was covered with sterile gauze. In the petroleum-jelly group, cleansing was followed by the application of 0.5 g petrolatum measured precisely on a sterile, pre-weighed cotton-tip applicator to ensure consistency, circumferentially to the peristomal skin, after which the same gauze dressing was applied. Dressings were replaced every 24 h or sooner if wet or dislodged by trained otolaryngology residents or nurses, with caregivers continuing the identical regimen at home.

Outcome measures and follow-up

The outcomes were assessed by one physician and one nurse at first, second, and fourth weeks postoperatively using the Pressure Ulcer Scale for Healing [15, 16] (PUSH) Tool and Skin Integrity Score. [7] (Figs. 1 and 2) The PUSH Tool sums three domains—wound size, exudate amount and tissue type—each scored 0–5, for a total of 0 (best) to 17 (worst). The Skin-Integrity Score rates erythema, maceration, granulation tissue and crust on 0–2 anchors, giving 0 (worst) to 8 (best), where higher values indicate healthier peristomal skin. At each scheduled follow-up (first, second, and fourth weeks), standardized photographs of the tracheostomy site were taken using the same digital device under consistent conditions. Both exudate amount, scored 0 (none) to 3 (heavy), and tissue type were later scored directly from these photographs. A sterile 10 mm ruler was positioned beside the stoma; when this was not available, the 11 mm outer flange of the size-7 PVC tube served as an internal reference. The observer measured the longest and perpendicular wound diameters onscreen, multiplied them (cm2), and assigned the result to the corresponding PUSH size band. The “wound” comprised only the peristomal epithelial gap (ulcer or granulating ring); the circular, already-epithelialized stoma lumen was excluded from area calculations unless ulceration extended into it. All images were anonymized and randomized by an independent study coordinator before evaluation. Two trained observers — one physician and one nurse — independently reviewed the images and scored wound status using the PUSH Tool and Skin Integrity Score. Both observers were blinded to group assignment and were not involved in patient care or intervention delivery.

Inter-rater reliability was excellent, with intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) ranging from 0.85 to 0.97 across all tools and time-points. The PUSH Tool yields a total score ranging from 0 (best) to 17 (worst), with higher scores indicating worse wound status and lower scores indicating better healing. Statistical analysis

Continuous variables were first examined for normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test [17]. Variables that met the normality assumption (p > 0.05) are presented as means ± standard deviations and compared with an independent t-test. Variables that violated normality (p ≤ 0.05) are presented as medians (range) and compared with the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical data are shown as frequencies and percentages and were analyzed with Fisher’s exact test. Inter-rater reliability was assessed with the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). All analyses were performed in SPSS v26.0 with two-sided p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. No subgroup or ancillary analyses were performed.

Results

General characteristics

The 28 patients who underwent tracheostomy procedures were randomly assigned to two groups. Fourteen patients used petroleum jelly for dressing their wounds, whereas the other 14 patients used sodium chloride irrigation (0.9% NaCl solution) for dressing their wounds. The study found no differences in basic demographic information. In the petroleum jelly application group, seven were female patients and seven were male patients, with an average age of 65.07 ± 8.38 years. In the sodium chloride irrigation group, four were female patients and 10 were male patients, with an average age of 66.29 ± 12.94 years.

No significant differences in other demographic characteristics were observed between the two groups, including height, weight, body mass index, hemoglobin, hematocrit, serum albumin, serum creatinine, aspartate aminotransferase, alanine aminotransferase, diabetes mellitus, smoking history, and history of infection, including sepsis, pneumonia, and other infections. PUSH Tool

Tracheostomy wound healing was compared between the two groups using the PUSH Tool [15, 16]. In the first week, PUSH scores were significantly lower (indicating better healing) in the petroleum jelly group compared to controls (8.93 ± 1.59 vs. 10.21 ± 1.25, p = 0.025). Similarly, significantly lower PUSH scores were maintained in the petroleum jelly group at the second week (7.43 ± 1.45 vs. 9.00 ± 1.30, p = 0.006) and fourth week (6.79 ± 1.58 vs. 8.29 ± 1.20, p = 0.009). Comparable findings were consistently observed in assessments performed independently by physician and nurse observers, as well as their combined evaluations. Overall, petroleum-jelly dressings consistently yielded better PUSH scores across all observers (Table 2, Fig. 3).

Interrater reliability

The interrater reliability assessments using the ICC [18] for the PUSH Tool at first, second, and fourth weeks were 0.957 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 0.906–0.980, p < 0.001), 0.926 (95% CI: 0.841–0.966, p < 0.001), and 0.923 (95% CI: 0.834–0.964, p < 0.001), respectively.

The interrater reliability assessments using the ICC [18] for the Skin integrity Score at first, second, and fourth weeks were 0.947 (95% CI: 0.886–0.976, p < 0.001), 0.848 (95% CI: 0.671–0.930, p < 0.001), and 0.970 (95% CI: 0.935–0.986, p < 0.001), respectively.

From the assessment results, there was a high level of agreement among the observers, which was statistically significant. The reliability between observers fell within the range of 0.75–1.00, indicating good to excellent agreement among them. Discussion

Pressure injuries and wound healing following tracheostomy procedures present a significant concern and impacts the quality of life of patients. In Thailand, accessing moisture-retentive dressings, such as Mepilex Ag, [8] foam dressings, [19, 20] Drain sponge, [21] and solid skin barriers, is challenging because of their high cost and the requirement for daily changes. Recent studies, including that of Meng Yue et al. [22] have shown that moisture-retentive wound dressings significantly reduce the incidence of pressure injuries. This aligns with our findings, which demonstrate that petroleum jelly—a simple, cost-effective occlusive agent—offers similar benefits in preventing device-related pressure injuries. Petroleum jelly [23, 24] acts as a hydrophobic barrier that retains moisture, reduces transepidermal water loss, and minimizes friction between the tracheostomy tube or ties and the skin. Unlike saline, which serves only to cleanse the wound, petroleum jelly forms a semi-occlusive layer that supports re-epithelialization and prevents mechanical irritation. This contributes to its superior wound healing performance observed in our trial. These properties align with evidence summarized by Lodén [25], who reviewed the clinical benefits of moisturizers in enhancing skin barrier function and reducing irritation [26]. Petrolatum forms a hydrophobic, semi-occlusive film that holds 70–90% relative humidity at the wound surface, reduces friction and shear from the tracheostomy flange, and minimizes trans epidermal water loss. Together these effects accelerate re-epithelialization and collagen remodeling [9, 10], explaining the superior PUSH and Skin-Integrity scores recorded in our intervention group.

Furthermore, petroleum jelly is easy to use, cost-effective, and does not absorb into the bloodstream, making it suitable for most skin types. Its nonirritating nature and wide availability make it a practical alternative to expensive wound dressings in resource-limited settings [25, 26]. These features likely underlie the improved skin integrity and lower wound severity scores observed in our intervention group. Most petrolatum data involve intact skin; however, randomised trials in partial-thickness burns and post-excision wounds have shown that petrolatum shortens epithelial-closure time by 1–3 days compared with silver sulfadiazine or antibiotic ointments. [10] These open-wound findings parallel our tracheostomy results and support the concept that an inexpensive, occlusive agent can match (or exceed) the performance of costlier moisture-retentive dressings. Therefore, it is being used in this research project. Both groups received an identical prevention bundle—one-finger tie tension, daily skin inspection, and a single layer of sterile polyurethane gauze. The sole difference was the additional petrolatum around the stoma in the intervention arm; consequently, the improved outcomes can be attributed to petrolatum as an add-on rather than to variations in basic care. Therefore, the superior outcomes observed in wound healing and pressure injury prevention can be attributed to the added effect of petroleum jelly, beyond what is achieved by saline cleansing alone. This highlights petroleum jelly’s role not as a replacement, but as an enhancement to standard wound care. Petroleum jelly has also been shown to be safe in dermatologic and wound applications. It is non-toxic, non-sensitizing, and not systemically absorbed when used topically. Studies by Czarnowicki et al. [26] confirm that petrolatum supports barrier repair and promotes beneficial immune responses in skin healing. No adverse events were recorded in our trial, reinforcing its safety when used properly. To address the rare but reported risk of exogenous lipoid pneumonia from airway exposure to petroleum products—as documented in a case report by Gorospe et al. [27]—we note that in our study petroleum jelly was applied only to the external peristomal skin and never introduced into the stoma. All caregivers were instructed on proper application techniques, and no respiratory complications occurred.

The population included in this study primarily comprised males aged between 18 and 80 years who underwent tracheostomy procedures. According to the study findings, petroleum jelly was effective in preventing pressure injuries and promoting wound healing, as assessed by the PUSH [15, 16] Tool and Skin Integrity [7] Score. These assessments revealed statistically significant improvements compared with normal saline solution. The consistency of assessment results between the physician and nurse, along with statistically significant interrater reliability, further supports the effectiveness of petroleum jelly.

Each participant underwent a medical history review and physical examination, which was conducted by a single physician (the researcher). This approach was employed to minimize potential discrepancies that could arise from multiple examiners. The purpose of these procedures was to accurately classify the target groups and to explain the utilization of tools, wound care procedures, and data collection methods.

From this study, it was observed that the use of petroleum jelly was effective in preventing pressure injuries and facilitating wound healing in patients undergoing tracheostomy procedures. The results are consistent with those of Meng Yue et al., [22] who found that wound care using moist dressings can prevent and reduce the occurrence of pressure injuries.

The limitations of this study include its small sample size, which may not fully reflect the diversity of the population undergoing tracheostomy procedures. Furthermore, having only two evaluators could introduce potential biases in the assessments because different evaluators may have varying perspectives and interpretations. Moreover, the study only experimented with one type of petroleum jelly, particularly the product from the hospital’s production. This limitation restricts the generalizability of the findings to other types of petroleum jelly products available in the market. Additionally, while our study included patients with chronic conditions such as diabetes mellitus, it was not powered to assess subgroup differences based on comorbidities. Finally, because all PUSH domains were scored from standardized photographs rather than direct observation of the removed dressing, very light serous drainage or exceedingly heavy exudate may have been under- or over-estimated, respectively, introducing a potential measurement error. The small sample size and single-center design may limit the broader applicability of these results to diverse clinical populations. Additionally, we did not explicitly record certain patient characteristics such as level of consciousness (GCS or sedation state) and mobility status, which could potentially influence wound healing outcomes.

To address these limitations and further develop the research, the researchers suggest increasing the sample size to enhance the representativeness of the findings. Expanding the number of evaluators, including both physicians and nurses, can provide a more comprehensive assessment and reduce potential biases. Furthermore, future studies could explore the efficacy of different materials and compare them with petroleum jelly to determine the most effective option for preventing pressure injuries and facilitating wound healing in patients undergoing tracheostomy.

Conclusion

Participants who received petroleum jelly application following tracheostomy exhibited significantly better outcomes in preventing pressure injuries and maintaining peristomal skin integrity compared with those who received standard sodium chloride irrigation alone, suggesting that petroleum jelly should be considered as an adjunct to standard tracheostomy wound care, particularly in resource-limited healthcare settings.
