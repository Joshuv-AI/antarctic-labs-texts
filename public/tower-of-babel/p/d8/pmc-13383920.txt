Open Lateral Retinacular Lengthening During an “À La Carte” Step‐by‐Step Surgical Approach for Habitual Patellar Dislocation

ABSTRACT
AbstractHabitual patellar dislocation is rare and primarily characterized by superolateral soft‐tissue contractures. The surgical approach described focuses on a step‐by‐step, “à la carte”, release of these contractures. The algorithm and technique described here offer a reproducible surgical strategy applicable to both skeletally immature and mature patients.

Habitual patellar dislocation, or obligatory patellar dislocation, is a rare and debilitating condition primarily affecting the pediatric and adolescent populations, although its exact prevalence remains unclear. 1 , 2 Contrary to typical objective patellar instability, which occurs predominantly during knee extension, habitual dislocation is characterized by dislocation of the patella during knee flexion, spontaneously reducing upon returning to extension. 1 This atypical instability pattern is commonly attributed to excessive superolateral soft‐tissue contractures, fibrosis, or scarring, resulting in dislocation during knee flexion. 3 Some authors suggest neonatal intramuscular injections into the quadriceps as a possible explanation for these fibrotic changes; 3 however, the exact etiology remains unclear due to the limited number of studies and small patient cohorts described in the literature. 1 , 4 Surgical treatment is recommended for this condition.

This article describes a step‐by‐step surgical algorithm focusing on soft‐tissue release. Additional osseous and soft tissue procedures may be considered, depending on the individual's skeletal maturity. Given the complexity of habitual patellar dislocation and the necessity of individualized management based on skeletal anatomy, this article describes a structured, “à la carte” surgical technique.

Surgical Indications

This technique is indicated in patients with habitual patellar dislocation without advanced osteoarthritis who are eligible for surgery.

Preoperative Planning

Although habitual patellar dislocation is primarily a soft‐tissue disorder, all anatomical risk factors for patellar instability should be assessed. 2 Standard radiographic evaluation includes anteroposterior, true lateral, and axial views of the knee. Although magnetic resonance imaging is used routinely, computed tomography is appropriate for cases with clinical suspicion of rotational deformities. The current comprehensive approach to patellar dislocation is well described by Dejour et al. 2

Patient Positioning and Anesthesia

The procedure is performed under general or regional anesthesia with the patient in the supine position. A lateral post and distal foot support are used to maintain 90° knee flexion. A high‐thigh tourniquet is applied but not inflated initially, as this may alter muscle tension and affect landmarks used during quadriceps tendon lengthening. The tourniquet is only inflated if visualization is significantly impaired due to bleeding. Standard sterile preparation and draping of the entire lower extremity are performed.

Surgical Sequence

The procedure follows an “à la carte,” step‐by‐step algorithm (Figure 1). The goal of surgery is to release the lateral contractures and restore the medial patellar restraints. Each surgical step is performed sequentially until the patella shows stable tracking within the trochlear groove. After each step, patellar alignment is assessed throughout the full range of motion, from full extension to deep flexion. The endpoint of the procedure is achieved when patellar stability is maintained in full flexion, confirming that no additional steps are necessary. With the knee positioned at 90°, a longitudinal midline skin incision is made, centered over the medial aspect of the tibial tuberosity, extending from approximately 5 to10 cm proximal to the patella to the level of the tibial tubercle. Dissection is carried out through the subcutaneous tissues until the patella is exposed. A full‐thickness flap is developed to visualize the iliotibial band (ITB), vastus lateralis tendon, and quadriceps tendon. Adhesions between the iliotibial band and patella must be released, and the iliotibial band should be freed proximally from surrounding fatty tissue. This initial step alone is insufficient to stabilize the patella in habitual dislocations. Release of the lateral retinacular contracture is always required. This release is performed through a lengthening of the lateral structures by identifying and opening the plane between the capsule/synovium and the lateral retinaculum (Figures 2 and 3). This technique creates a new retinaculo‐capsular layer covering the joint, which contributes to patellar stability by preventing overcorrection and excessive medial tilt. During the lateral retinacular lengthening, an arthrotomy is performed, allowing direct assessment of intra‐articular adhesions, cartilage lesions, and trochlear dysplasia (Figure 4). After completing this critical first step, patellar tracking is reassessed through a full range of motion. If the patella remains centered and stable in full flexion, no further lateral release is necessary, and the focus can shift to medial reinforcement, which will be described later. However, if instability persists, the next step is to release the vastus lateralis (Figure 5). To mobilize the vastus lateralis, the tendon must be detached distally from the patella, medially from the rectus femoris, and laterally from the iliotibial tract adhesion. A traction suture is placed at the distal tendinous tip to facilitate handling and release. The tendon must be completely mobilized, while preserving enough tendon tissue to allow for reattachment. Once released, knee flexion is reassessed. If patellar stability is achieved, the vastus lateralis tendon is sutured back to the quadriceps tendon with the knee at 110° flexion. After suturing, patellar tracking must be verified again. If redislocation occurs, it indicates that the suture has reintroduced excessive tension; it should be removed, and the tendon reattached more proximally. If vastus lateralis release is insufficient to stabilize the patella, quadriceps lengthening is necessary. In habitual dislocations, contracture of the lateral half of the rectus femoris and the vastus intermedius is frequently observed. Conversely, the medial portion of the quadriceps tendon is usually stretched due to repeated dislocations. Several techniques have been described for quadriceps lengthening, we use a Z‐plasty quadriceps lengthening (Figure 5). The first step is the transverse sectioning of the distal lateral part of the tendon, then the flexion is re‐evaluated and, if necessary, Z‐plasty is performed. The amount of lengthening needed is determined intraoperatively and defined as the amount that allows full knee flexion without tension‐induced patellar dislocation, typically between 1 and 3 cm. The tendon is reapproximated using interrupted No. 1 or No. 2 nonabsorbable sutures. If quadriceps lengthening is performed, the vastus lateralis tendon is reattached afterward using the same suture technique. It is uncommon for patellar instability to persist after this step. If instability remains, distal realignment may be considered. In skeletally immature patients, a modified Roux‐Goldthwait procedure is recommended, which consists of a longitudinal split of the patellar tendon with medial transfer of its lateral half to the tibial tubercle. 5 Whereas in skeletally mature patients, a tibial tubercle osteotomy is generally preferred. 6

After addressing the lateral soft tissue contractures, it is time to correct medial laxity. Skeletal maturity plays a critical role in selecting the appropriate technique. In skeletally mature or near‐mature patients, a medial patellofemoral ligament reconstruction can be performed using the surgeon's preferred technique. In skeletally immature patients, a vastus medialis obliquus advancement may be considered. 7 , 8 Other procedures have also been described for this population, such as adductor magnus tendon transfer, medial quadriceps tendon transfer, or looping a graft around the adductor magnus tendon insertion. 9 , 10 The choice among these options is made according to surgeon preference. Correction of medial laxity should be performed with more tension in flexion, rather than extension, which differs from the typical approach used in cases of recurrent patellar dislocation. Care must be taken to avoid overtightening the medial structures, which could lead to excessive medial tilt or medial patellofemoral overconstraint. Layered closure is then performed, and patellar stability is reassessed at each step to ensure that no redislocation occurs during the final phases of the procedure.

Management of Associated Abnormalities

Patients with habitual patellar dislocation may present with additional anatomical abnormalities that must be addressed during surgery. In the presence of patella alta, a modified Grammont distalization procedure can be considered in skeletally immature patients, whereas a tibial tubercle distalization osteotomy may be performed in skeletally mature patients. 11 In cases of associated patella infera, when the patella remains dislocated after quadriceps lengthening and the patellar tendon is under excessive tension, patellar tendon lengthening or tibial tubercle proximalization may be necessary, depending on skeletal maturity. 12 If trochlear dysplasia Dejour type 3 is identified, defined by a supratrochlear spur ≥ 5 mm, a trochleoplasty can be considered, particularly if the patient is within 2 years of reaching skeletal maturity. 13 , 14 In cases of associated genu valgum with open physis, growth modulation may be performed. The authors recommend using a medial distal femoral 8‐plate when the deformity originates from the femur. In cases of rotational abnormalities confirmed by computed tomography after clinical suspicion, derotational osteotomies can also be appropriate. 15 Cartilage preservation procedures may also be needed when chondral lesions are present. 16

Postoperative Management

The limb is immobilized in a hinged knee brace locked in full extension until adequate quadriceps control is achieved, typically by 6 weeks postoperatively. Under supervision of a physiotherapist, the range of motion is progressively increased as follows:

Weeks 0 to 2: 0° to 30°

Weeks 2 to 4: 0° to 60°

Weeks 4 to 6: 0° to 90°

After 6 weeks, flexion can be gradually increased beyond 90°. Weight‐bearing is allowed immediately postoperatively unless quadriceps lengthening has been performed. In such cases, toe‐touch weight‐bearing is recommended for the first 6 weeks. Stationary cycling is permitted once 110° of knee flexion is achieved. Running can be introduced at approximately 4 months postoperatively, with return to pivoting sports at around 6 months.

DISCUSSION

The surgical management of habitual patellar dislocation is complex. However, following a structured, step‐by‐step approach allows for effective stabilization while avoiding unnecessary procedures. Although most patients are skeletally immature, adult cases are also possible. 11 , 17 The technique described in this article is adaptable to all ages and skeletal maturity levels. A key feature of this approach is the systematic assessment of patellar tracking in full flexion after each surgical step, with a mandatory lateral retinacular lengthening performed using a capsulo‐synovial advancement. Unlike simple lateral retinacular release, this technique preserves a tissue layer over the joint, avoiding communication between the joint space and subcutaneous tissues. Moreover, this reconstructed retinaculo‐capsular layer provides additional patellar stability and helps prevent medial overcorrection or tilt.

Alternative surgical options have been described in the literature. The Baksi procedure remains a valid approach in skeletally immature patients, using pes anserinus transposition to restore medial stability after release of lateral quadriceps contractures, with excellent outcomes and no redislocations at a mean follow‐up of 51 months. 18 , 19 Other authors have described various quadriceps lengthening techniques, including rectus femoris slide over vastus intermedius or quadriceps Z‐plasty as in the technique described by Ellsworth et al., which closely resembles ours. 17 , 20 Due to the rarity of habitual dislocations, studies feature small cohorts, limiting the ability to compare outcomes between techniques. Nevertheless, postoperative functional scores generally improve, and redislocation rates range from 8% to 18%. 4 , 21 Compared with previously reported techniques, our approach presents several advantages (Table 1). It is adaptable to skeletal maturity and incorporates adjunctive procedures tailored to an associated pathoanatomy, such as patellar height abnormalities or high‐grade trochlear dysplasia. The limitations of our technique are of a more general nature and not specific to the procedure (Table 1). Pearls and pitfalls are summarized in Table 2. Importantly, the technique is considered safe, with no major neurovascular structures at risk. Potential complications include quadriceps weakness, extensor lag, and recurrent instability. In conclusion, habitual patellar dislocation is a rare condition primarily characterized by superolateral soft tissue contractures. Surgical success relies on effective lateral release and appropriate correction of medial laxity. Our “à la carte” technique for habitual dislocation is a viable solution, applicable to patients of all ages and skeletal maturity.
