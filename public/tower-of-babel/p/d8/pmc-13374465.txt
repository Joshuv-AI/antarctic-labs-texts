Effects of targeted interventions on HIV testing, knowledge and sexual risk behaviours among long-distance truck drivers: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Cyrus Mutie, Berrick Otieno, Kawira Kithuci, John Gachohi, Grace Mbuthia
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesWe aimed to systematically summarise and quantify the empirical evidence on targeted interventions and their effects on various HIV prevention outcomes for long-distance truck drivers (LDTDs) globally.DesignA systematic review and meta-analysis.Data sourcesWe searched PubMed, PubMed Central (PMC), Cumulated Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), ProQuest Central, EBSCOhost, ScienceDirect, World Health Organisation (Global Index Medicus), Cochrane Library, Scientific Electronic Library Online (SCiELO), Japan Science and Technology Information Aggregator, Electronic (J-STAGE) and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI) up to 15th May 2026.Eligibility criteriaWe included original peer-reviewed interventional studies involving LDTDs of either gender aged above 18 years and reporting findings on HIV prevention interventions from any part of the world. We excluded non-empirical publications like systematic reviews.Data extraction and synthesisWe used a predesigned and piloted Microsoft Excel data extraction form to extract information on study details, characteristics of study participants, intervention characteristics, outcome measures and reported effect size measures. The general characteristics of the included studies were summarised using basic descriptive statistics like mean, frequencies and proportions. Where effect estimates for individual studies were missing, risk ratios (RR) were calculated from the proportions of the respective outcomes. Thereafter, we performed a random-effects meta-analysis using R. We performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding individual studies with outlier effect estimates and a subgroup analysis of studies with no high risk of bias. Where adequate data for meta-analysis were not reported, a narrative approach was used to synthesise the findings. The ROB 2 and ROBIN-1 Cochrane risk of bias assessment tools were used to assess the risk of bias for the randomised and non-randomised interventions, respectively. The certainty of evidence for the included studies was assessed using the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.ResultsOf the 2076 records identified, 24 were included. Most of the included studies were conducted in the regions of SSA (50%, n=12) and Asia and the Pacific (37.50%, n=9). The frequently reported outcomes following targeted interventions were condom use, HIV-related knowledge, HIV testing and sexual risk behaviours. The pooled effect estimates showed that interventions on condom use had the most significant effect (RR=1.25; 95% CI, 1.09 to 1.44, p=0.002), followed by HIV-related knowledge (RR=1.24, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.36, p<0.001) and sexual risk behaviour reduction (RR=0.86; 95% CI, 0.74 to 0.99, p=0.042). Interventions on HIV testing showed a modest effect at follow-up, though not statistically significant (RR=1.14; 95% CI, 0.91 to 1.42, p=0.248). Significant heterogeneity was observed, indicating that the pooled effect estimates were not due to chance but other factors, such as intervention variations and sample size. Following sensitivity and subgroup analysis, we did not observe statistically significant changes in pooled effect estimates across most intervention outcomes. Based on the GRADE assessment, the certainty of evidence was predominantly very low, with only one study providing moderate-certainty evidence and three others providing low-certainty evidence.ConclusionGenerally, most of the targeted interventions had significant effects on various HIV prevention outcomes and were randomised controlled trials, peer-led, outreach-based and adopted mobile health approaches. Thus, there is a need to upscale such interventions along transport corridors, as they had the most significant effects in promoting HIV prevention outcomes among LDTDs. However, the predominantly very low certainty of evidence based on the GRADE assessment limits confidence in the effect estimates reported in this study. Consequently, these findings should be interpreted with caution and confirmed through more rigorous, high-quality studies.PROSPERO Registration NumberCRD42024505542Keywords: INFECTIOUS DISEASES, HIV & AIDS, Public health, Nursing research, Behavior

Introduction

While there have been significant breakthroughs in the development and delivery of prevention interventions in the course of the HIV pandemic, new infections continue to occur globally. In 2024, a staggering 1.3 million new HIV infections occurred globally, beyond the projected 370,000.1 Given the current trend, the global response to the HIV pandemic may not be sufficient to end new HIV infections and AIDS related deaths by 2030. This outcome is partly driven by key population groups whose access to routine HIV prevention interventions is often hindered by widespread systemic vulnerabilities. One such population is the Long-distance truck drivers (LDTDs). Briefly, LDTDs’ constant mobility and hard-to-reach nature often limit their access to biomedical and structural HIV prevention interventions.2 Consequently, LDTDs are susceptible to poor HIV prevention outcomes. Concurrently, LDTDs endure high-risk sexual networks along their transit routes,3 making them more vulnerable to HIV infections, as evidenced by the global prevalence of 3.86%, almost six times that of the general population.4

By virtue of their chronic susceptibility, LDTDs are recommended for routine HIV prevention interventions.5 6 These may include peer-led prevention campaigns, HIV counselling and testing, sexually transmitted infections (STIs) screening and treatment and initiation to Pre-Exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). Such interventions have been implemented in India,7 China,8 Malawi,9 Tanzania,10 Morocco,11 and Kenya,12 though to a lesser extent than for other key population groups.

Nonetheless, despite the implementation of targeted interventions, their actual effects on various outcomes such as HIV testing, knowledge and sexual risk behaviours remain unknown among LDTDs. This is especially true in the developing world, where a significant majority of HIV prevention interventions are donor-funded and not sustainable in the long run.13 Furthermore, the recent freeze on HIV funding from the US President’s Emergency Plan for AIDS Relief (PEPFAR) has largely left many HIV programmes crippled with critical resource gaps. As such, it’s imperative to understand the effects of various interventions on HIV prevention outcomes, such as HIV testing and reduction of sexual risk behaviours among LDTDs. Eventually, this will inform evidence-based prevention practices in resource-limited settings where chronic funding gaps in HIV programmes exist.

Previously, only one systematic review partly assessed HIV prevention interventions in sub-Saharan Africa.13 Whereas the systematic review featured some studies on HIV prevention interventions, it also included others whose primary objective was not HIV prevention among LDTDs, limiting generalisability to other global settings.13 Thus, the current study sought to estimate the overall effects of targeted interventions on HIV prevention outcomes globally. This will inform design and implementation strategies, thus strengthening HIV prevention efforts for LDTDs.

Objectives

This review aimed to systematically: (1) describe the outcomes of various HIV prevention interventions targeted for LDTDs; and (2) establish the effects of targeted HIV interventions on various outcomes such as HIV testing, knowledge, condom use and sexual risk behaviour among LDTDs through meta-analysis.

Methods

Protocol and registration

This systematic review was written based on the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis (PRISMA) guidelines14 as given in online supplemental table 1. The protocol is registered in the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO) under registration number CRD42024505542. In our protocol development, we had anticipated the manuscript title as ‘Effectiveness of HIV prevention interventions targeting long-distance truck drivers: a systematic review of global evidence’. However, given the emerging outcomes following the meta-analysis, we refined it to “Effects of Targeted Interventions on HIV Testing, Knowledge and Sexual Risk Behaviours among Long-distance Truck Drivers: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis”.

Eligibility criteria

For potential inclusion, studies needed to meet predetermined inclusion and exclusion criteria as highlighted below.

Inclusion criteria

Studies were included if they reported findings on HIV prevention interventions among LDTDs aged 18 years or older, regardless of gender, involved a control group and reported on outcome measures such as condom use, HIV testing and knowledge on HIV prevention.

Exclusion criteria

Non-empirical research studies, including systematic reviews, scoping reviews, literature reviews, meta-analyses and protocols for RCT and quasi-experimental studies, were excluded. Studies reporting on health interventions other than HIV among long-distance truck drivers were also excluded.

Information sources

A comprehensive electronic search was conducted from PubMed, PubMed Central (PMC), Cumulated Index to Nursing and Allied Health Literature (CINAHL), ProQuest Central, EBSCOhost, ScienceDirect, World Health Organization (Global Index Medicus), Scientific Electronic Library Online (SCiELO), Japan Science and Technology Information Aggregator, Electronic (J-STAGE) and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI). More studies were manually searched from the reference lists of included studies.

Search strategy

A primary search string was developed from a comprehensive set of key search terms grouped into intervention, condition and population. Synonyms in each search group were combined using the Boolean operator “OR”. The three search groups were then combined using the Boolean operator “AND”. Our primary search strategy involved the following text words (tw) and Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) terms where suitable; (“Internet-Based Intervention” (Mesh) OR “Psychosocial Intervention” (Mesh) OR “Implementation Science” (Mesh) OR intervention*(tw) OR quasi-experimental*(tw) OR “randomised controlled trial” (tw) OR “non-randomised intervention*” (tw) OR “behaviour change intervention*” (tw) OR “risk reduction” (tw) OR “sexual partner reduction” (tw) OR “peer led education” (tw) OR “SMS campaigns” (tw) OR “community-based campaigns” (tw) OR “educational campaigns” (tw) OR “condom use promotion” (tw) OR “HIV testing” (tw) OR “sexually transmitted infection screening” (tw) OR “pre-exposure prophylaxis campaigns” (tw) OR “PrEP promotion” (tw)) AND (“HIV” (Mesh) OR “AIDS” (Mesh) OR “Sexually Transmitted Diseases” (Mesh) OR HIV(tw) OR “HIV/AIDS” (tw) OR “sexually transmitted infections” (tw) OR “sexually transmitted diseases” (tw) OR STI(tw) OR “venereal diseases” (tw)) AND (“Truck Drivers” (Mesh) OR “long-distance truck drivers” (tw) OR “long-distance truckers” (tw) OR “long-haul truckers” (tw) OR “truck drivers” (tw)). The search strategy was modified to suit the specifications of other databases. The search terms were in English or the language of the specific database, such as German, Spanish and Chinese, among others. Our last search was conducted on 15 May 2026, for all databases except for the Global Index Medicus and CINAHL. This is because the Global Index Medicus site was inactive, whereas for CINAHL, we didn’t have enough resources to meet the subscription requirements for its access. Moreover, the reference list of the included studies was searched to identify potential articles that could meet our eligibility criteria. The full search strategy for all databases is available in online supplemental file 1.

Selection process

The data management and selection process was done in three stages. Initially, the search results were uploaded in the Rayyan software, an internet-based platform that enables collaboration between reviewers during the screening process.15 Here, duplicate articles were removed. In the subsequent step, CM and BO independently screened the titles and abstracts of all records. Any conflicts were resolved through consensus or by consultation from a third author, GM. Lastly, retrieved full texts were assessed for eligibility by both CM and BO. Studies that did not meet eligibility were listed as well as the reasons for their exclusion. Data items

Where possible, the following variables were extracted: details of the retrieved articles, study details, intervention characteristics, characteristics of study participants, outcome measures and reported effect size measures. Absolute numbers, frequencies, percentages and means for specific outcomes will be extracted depending on what is reported in a particular study. The two reviewers (CM and BO) later resolved any discrepancies in the extracted data, aided by a third author, G.M., where necessary.

Study risk of bias assessment

Two reviewers, CM and BO, independently performed the risk of bias and quality assessment of the included studies. Any discordance in the risk of bias assessment between the two reviewers was resolved through discussion and consensus, aided by a third author, GM. The risk of bias for the randomised control studies was assessed using the Cochrane risk of bias for randomised trials, ROB 2.16 The tool has five domains on which the risk of bias is assessed. These include the risk of bias arising from the randomisation process, deviations from the intended intervention, missing outcome data, bias in the measurement of the outcome and bias in the selection of the reported result.16 For the other interventional studies, the Cochrane tool for assessing the risk of bias in non-randomised intervention studies, ROBINS-1, was used.17 The tool addresses seven possible causes of bias, such as confounding, selection bias, bias in measurement, classification of interventions, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias due to missing data, bias in measurement of outcomes and bias in selection of the reported result.17 Traffic Light plots were used to visualise outcomes for the risk of bias assessment on the online RobVis software.18

Data synthesis

General characteristics of the included studies were summarised using descriptive statistics, such as means, frequencies and proportions. Due to the lack of standardised reporting of effect estimates across many studies, the Risk Ratio (RR) was used as the primary outcome measure for the meta-analysis. The RR and its 95% CI upper and lower bounds were calculated from the raw data for the intervention and control arms at baseline and follow-up.

To ensure normality, all outcome measures were transformed on the log-transformed risk ratio (logRR) scale using the R statistical software version 4.5.2.19 The upper and lower bounds of the 95% CIs were equally log-transformed to derive standard errors. Where more than two studies had reported adequate data for an outcome of interest, a random-effects meta-analysis using the ‘metagen’ function from the ‘meta’ package in R was performed. Similar interventions were grouped for meta-analysis, and their pooled effect sizes were estimated using a random-effects model. This enabled an overview of the effect specific interventions had in enhancing HIV prevention outcomes among the LDTDs. Where specific studies did not report adequate data for meta-analysis, their findings were synthesised through a narrative approach.

Given the variations in study populations, designs, sample sizes, and study settings, we had anticipated heterogeneity. Therefore, we applied a random-effects model to quantify heterogeneity using the I2 statistic. In this case, I2 values of 25%, 50% and 75% indicated low, moderate, and high heterogeneity, respectively.20 21 Forest plots were used to visualise heterogeneity, effect estimates, and corresponding 95% CIs. We performed a sensitivity analysis by excluding individual studies that had outliers in effect sizes at a time to establish if they had any effect on the pooled effect estimate and heterogeneity.22 23 We also performed a subgroup analysis by excluding studies deemed to have a high risk of bias.22 This would enable us to identify how effect estimates might change if only articles deemed to have a low risk of bias were included.22 Given the few studies reporting similar interventions, a subgroup analysis by region was not possible. Publication bias was assessed and visualised using a Luis Furuya-Kanamori (LFK) index and Doi plot with asymmetry and thresholds outside (−1) and (1) indicating the presence of publication bias.24 Also, a funnel plot was used to complement evidence on publication bias in the included studies.25

Reporting bias assessment

The risk of bias due to missing results was assessed and reported narratively. This involved investigating whether individual studies clearly reported baseline and postintervention outcomes as well as data for both control and interventional arms.

Certainty assessment

To assess the certainty of evidence, we used the Grading of Recommendations Assessment, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) approach.26 Two reviewers CM and BO were independently involved in assessing the certainty of evidence. Where conflicts arose, they were resolved through consensus or by consulting a third author, GM. Given that all of the included studies were experimental, the default certainty level was set as high but downgraded depending on the risk of bias, inconsistencies, indirectness, imprecision and publication bias in individual studies.

Patient and public involvement

We did not include patients or members of the public in the development of this study.

Results

Study selection

A total of 2073 records were identified from the initial electronic search conducted from various databases: PubMed, n=123; ScienceDirect, n=322; EBSCOhost, n=80; Global Index Medicus, n=67; CINAHL, n=32; PMC, n=1072; PROQUEST CENTRAL, n=75; Cochrane Library, n=19; Scientific Electronic Library Online (SCiELO), n=14; Japan Science and Technology Information Aggregator, Electronic. (J-STAGE), n=142; and China National Knowledge Infrastructure (CNKI), n=127). An additional three records were identified from Google Scholar and the reference lists of included studies. A total of 224 duplicates were removed, after which 1852 articles were screened for eligibility. After screening based on titles and abstracts, a total of 1745 irrelevant records were excluded. After screening the remaining 107 full texts, 83 articles that had initially appeared to meet the inclusion criteria were removed, with reasons. Eventually, 24 studies were included in the review and meta-analysis. A PRISMA flow chart for the systematic review process is given in figure 1.

Study characteristics

Half of the studies were conducted in SSA countries (50%, n=12),69 10 12 27,34 followed by Asia and the Pacific (37.5%, n=9),35,43 while two from Latin America and the Caribbean,44 45 and lastly one study from North Africa.11 Only two studies had been published in the last 5 years,6 12 with a majority of the rest (70.83%, n=17) having been published more than a decade ago. Most of the studies were interventional in design with pre-test and post-test (54.17%, n=13), except for eight randomised controlled trials (RCTs)1227 30,32 34 35 46 and three quasi-experimental designs.6 28 45 The characteristics of the 24 included studies are described in detail in table 1 and online supplemental file 2.

Study population

The mean age of LDTDs ranged from 30.0236 to 41 years.28 The total sample size of LDTDs in the included studies was 24 292. Studies conducted in Asia and the Pacific comprised the highest sample size (57.04%, n=13 857), followed by sub-Saharan Africa (30.57%, n=7425) and the least from North Africa (1.80%, n=459). In individual studies, the smallest sample size was 14932 from a study done in Kenya, with the highest being 483641 from India. All of the studies involved only LDTDs of male gender. All studies involved participants from individual countries, except for one, where LDTDs were multinational.27

Risk of bias for the included studies

For the eight RCT studies, the most commonly encountered sources of bias were bias due to missing outcome data, bias in the measurement of the outcome, and bias in the selection of the reported result. The risk of bias as a result of randomisation was low in most of the studies, except for two studies where it was high.32 35 There were some concerns about the risk of bias due to missing outcome data in most of the studies, except for two.27 31 Bias in measurement of the outcome was judged to be high in one study,34 some concerns in another study,35 and low in the rest of the other studies. There were some concerns regarding the risk of bias in the selection of the reported result in two studies,35 37 a high risk of bias in one study34 and a low risk of bias in the remaining five studies. Generally, only one of the eight RCT studies was found to involve a low risk of bias,31 some concerns in four studies,12 27 30 37 and a high risk of bias in the remaining three.32 34 35 A summary of the risk of bias assessment for RCT studies is given in figure 2.

Of the 16 non-randomised interventional studies, only one of them was of high-quality rating (low risk of bias).45 Three other studies were judged to be of fair-quality rating (moderate risk of bias).9 29 33 The remaining 12 non-randomised intervention studies were judged to be of low-quality rating (high risk of bias). Intervention characteristics

Above half (58.33%, n=14) of the study interventions were community-based, with the rest being clinic or hospital-based. The follow-up duration in most (54.16%, n=13) of the included study interventions ranged from six to 12 months, except for five studies with a follow-up duration of below 6 months,9 28 31 32 44 two with a follow-up duration of 2 years,10 40 and one with the longest follow-up duration of 7 years.39 In three studies the intervention follow-up duration was not specified.11 33 42 Most of the studies had their interventions characterised by an aspect of before and after comparison for the outcome of interest, except for six studies where the same was not specified.29 32 36 36 38 44 In almost all of the studies, the type of intervention allocation was not specified except for two where the intervention allocation was in the ratio of 1:1.12 30 Almost all of the studies either used the same group for comparison before and after the intervention or had distinct control and intervention arms, except for three studies where the aspect of comparison was missing or not clear.11 34 38 None of the included studies reported the use of placebo or blinding as part of their intervention process. The various HIV prevention interventions were mainly based on the sharing of information through health education materials like posters, pamphlets and IECs,1128 29 33 41 43,45 peer-led campaigns,9 10 36 38 39 health worker initiatives,37 38 outreach-based campaigns11 45 and text-based campaigns.6 27 31 More specifically, two interventions were based on the behaviour change communication (BCC) model41 45 and three on the information motivation behavioural skills (IMB) model.6 35 40 A summary of the study interventions is provided in online supplemental file 2.

Outcomes and effects of the targeted HIV prevention interventions

The leading primary outcome in most of the interventions was condom use which was reported in 12 studies,9,1127 29 33 36 followed by HIV testing1112 27 28 30,32 41 45 and risk sexual behaviours,2729 30 33 37 41 43,45 both reported in nine studies, knowledge on HIV prevention,6 9 11 27 28 33 35 43 reported in nine studies, the incidence of STIs,29 37 reported in two studies and lastly incidence of HIV reported in one study.29 Pooled estimates and effect sizes of various interventions are detailed in the subsequent sections.

Interventions to enhance condom use

Eight of the 12 studies reporting on interventions on condom use provided sufficient data for inclusion in the meta-analysis. The pooled effect estimate showed a statistically significant improvement in condom use among LDTDs (RR=1.25; 95% CI, 1.09 to 1.44, p=0.002), figure 4. A majority of the interventions (n=6)911 33 36,38 reported significant positive effects on condom use. Whereas one study37 had shown some improvement in condom use at follow-up, the findings were not statistically significant. Nonetheless, two studies27 45 had no effect in enhancing condom use among LDTDs as evidenced by an RR of below or almost one. Overall, statistically significant heterogeneity (I2=94.5%, p<0.0001) was observed across the eight studies (figure 4). The effectiveness of condom use interventions in four studies could not be ascertained due to inadequate or missing data.10 29 43 44

Meta-analysis; Pooled effect of interventions targeting enhanced condom use among Long-distance truck drivers (both intervention and control groups at follow-up).

Interventions to improve knowledge on HIV prevention

Most (n=5) of the interventions9 11 28 33 35 aimed at enhancing knowledge of HIV prevention were deemed effective. However, only three of the studies were included in the meta-analysis. One study35 was omitted from the meta-analysis following a sensitivity analysis. The other study28 reported a significant shift in mean knowledge scores (baseline, 4.17±1.43; follow-up, 6.70±1.55; p<0.0001), making it impossible to calculate the RR for the meta-analysis. The three studies included in the meta-analysis showed a statistically significant pooled effect (RR=1.24, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.36, p<0.001), indicating that interventions aimed at enhancing HIV-related knowledge were effective. An overall heterogeneity of (I2=0.0%, p=0.41) was observed as shown in figure 5. While there was a marked improvement in HIV-related knowledge at follow-up in two studies,9 33 the outcomes were not statistically significant. There was insufficient data to warrant a conclusive judgement on the effectiveness of one study.43

Meta-analysis; Pooled effect of interventions on improving HIV-related knowledge among Long-distance truck drivers (both intervention and control groups at follow-up).

Interventions to enhance uptake of HIV testing

Four11 27 30 31 out of the nine interventions on HIV testing were judged as effective in enhancing uptake of the service among the LDTDs. One study27 was omitted from the meta-analysis because its effect size was reported as an Odds Ratio (OR=1.72; 95% CI=1.11–2.66; p=0.02). Overall, a pooled effect (RR=1.14; 95% CI, 0.91 to 1.42, p=0.248) was identified, though not statistically significant (figure 6). A statistically significant high heterogeneity (I2=95.4%, p<0.0001) was observed (figure 6). Unexpectedly, in one study, uptake of HIV testing was significantly higher in the control arm (RR=0.89; 95% CI, 0.81 to 0.97, p=0.011) than in the intervention arm at follow-up.45 Whereas the intervention in another study12 led to a notable improvement in HIV testing (RR=1.01; 95% CI, 0.78 to 1.32, p=0.94), the outcome wasn’t statistically significant. In three other studies,28 32 41 the reported information was inadequate to determine whether the intervention was effective.

Meta-analysis; Pooled effect of interventions on uptake of HIV testing among Long-distance truck drivers (both intervention and control groups at follow-up).

Interventions for sexual risk behaviour reduction

A statistically significant pooled effect (RR=0.86; 95% CI, 0.74 to 0.99, p=0.042) was established from the six studies included in the meta-analysis, figure 7. However, only two interventions27 33 had a statistically significant effect in enhancing safe sexual behaviours among the LDTDs at follow-up. In one study,30 the proportion of LDTDs reporting sexual risk behaviours in the control arm was almost equivalent to that in the intervention arm, as indicated by a risk ratio of 1.00 (RR=1; 95% CI, 0.62 to 1.61; p=0.98). Whereas there was a notable reduction in risk sexual behaviours in the other two interventions, the outcomes were not statistically significant (RR=0.94; 95% CI, 0.85 to 1.03; p=0.16),37 (RR=0.96; 95% CI, 0.91 to 1.03; p=0.37).45 Overall, a statistically significant moderate heterogeneity (I2=71.7; p<0.007) was observed, figure 7. In the remaining four studies,29 41 43 44 there was insufficient data to conclude on the effectiveness of their respective interventions.

Meta-analysis; Pooled effect of interventions for risk sexual behaviour reduction among long-distance truck drivers (both intervention and control groups at follow-up).

Interventions to reduce the incidence of sexually transmitted infections and HIV

Studies reporting on the effect of HIV interventions on STI and HIV incidence didn’t report adequate data for meta-analysis. Of the interventions targeted to reduce the incidence of HIV and STIs, only one was judged to be effective.37 Here, the study demonstrated that the intervention significantly reduced the risk of STIs by 73% (RR=0.27; 95% CI, 0.2 to 0.36; p<0.001).37 In another study,29 the available information was insufficient to assess the effects of the interventions on STI incidence among the LDTDs.

Sensitivity analysis

We excluded individual studies with outlier effect sizes to assess their influence on pooled effect estimates and heterogeneity. For the interventions promoting condom use, exclusion of outlier studies did not result in any statistically significant change in pooled effect sizes or heterogeneity. For interventions aimed at improving HIV prevention knowledge, sensitivity showed a substantial increase in heterogeneity (I2=79.2%, p=0.002) following inclusion of the study by Cornman et al., 2007.35 Although the pooled effect estimate also shifted (RR=2.31, 95% CI, 0.82 to 6.51), this change was not statistically significant. After exclusion of the same study, heterogeneity became non-significant (I2=0.0%, p=0.401), while the pooled effect estimate remained statistically significant (RR=1.24, 95% CI, 1.13 to 1.36). Similarly, there were no statistically significant changes in pooled effect sizes and heterogeneity following exclusion of outlier studies for interventions designed to enhance uptake of HIV testing. However, for interventions targeting sexual risk behaviours, exclusion of the study by Kelvin et al, 201730 resulted in a significant change in heterogeneity (I2=78.6%, p=0.007) and pooled effect estimate (RR=0.84, 95% CI, 0.71 to 0.99). A summary of the sensitivity analysis is presented in online supplemental file 3.

Sub-group analysis

We did not observe any statistically significant changes following subgroup analysis after excluding studies with a high risk of bias across all intervention outcomes. However, variations in heterogeneity were noted across the different intervention categories. For interventions promoting condom use, heterogeneity decreased following exclusion of studies with a high risk of bias (I2=88.1%, p<0.0001) compared with the original analysis (I2=94.5%, p<0.0001), although heterogeneity still remained considerably high. In contrast, for interventions aimed at improving HIV prevention knowledge, a slight increase in heterogeneity was observed after excluding studies with a high risk of bias (I2=43.9%, p<0.182) compared with the original analysis (I2=0.0%, p=0.401). For interventions designed to enhance uptake of HIV testing, exclusion of studies with a high risk of bias resulted in a reduction in heterogeneity from (I2=95.4%, p<0.0001) to (I2=82.5%, p=0.007). Nevertheless, heterogeneity still remained high despite this reduction. A summary of sub-group analysis is presented in online supplemental file 3.

Reporting biases

Of the 24 included studies, only 11 were judged to have no reporting bias.6 9 11 12 27 28 31 33 35 37 45 The remaining 13 studies were judged to have some risk of reporting bias due to missing information in the baseline, follow-up, control, or intervention arms of the study.

Publication bias

A significant publication bias was evident, as indicated by an LFK index of 2.77, which falls outside the recommended range of (−1) and (1), as well as the visual asymmetry of the Doi plot in figure 8. Certainty of evidence

The certainty of evidence in almost all of the included studies was graded as very low, except for four studies.6 12 31 45 Of the four studies, only in one31 was the certainty of evidence graded as moderate. For the other three studies,6 12 45 the certainty of evidence was graded as low. The relatively low grading was mainly attributed to the high risk of bias and imprecision across many of the included studies. A detailed summary of the GRADE assessment for certainty of evidence in the included studies is given in online supplemental table 2.

Discussion

The review systematically summarised and analysed the available evidence on the effects of HIV prevention interventions on prevention outcomes globally. The evidence demonstrates that there have been concerted efforts to fight the HIV pandemic among the hard-to-reach LDTDs from different global fronts, although with successes and failures. This coincides with the UNAIDS call for an enhanced fight against HIV with a key focus on the most at-risk and the hard-to-reach populations, like the LDTDs.47 48 Nonetheless, while the global burden of HIV among LDTDs is 3.86%,4 evidence of the effectiveness of the prevention interventions didn’t exist.

Almost all the interventions had been implemented in the regions of SSA, Asia, and the Pacific. This is expected considering that the burden of HIV among LDTDs is highest in SSA at 14.34%, followed by Asia and the Pacific at 2.12%, compared with other parts of the world.4 Only a few HIV prevention interventions had been implemented in other parts of the world, an indication that HIV may not be a priority health problem in those regions. However, given the recent UNAIDS report that HIV is on the rise in some regions like North Africa, Eastern Europe, the Middle East, and Latin America, there is a need to implement more of these interventions in those regions.

The pooled estimate showed a statistically significant effect of interventions on condom use (RR=1.25; 95% CI, 1.09 to 1.44, p=0.002). This implies that such interventions can effectively enhance condom use and safe sexual behaviours among LDTDs. Notably, most of these interventions were either outreach-based11 or peer-led.9 36 38 Peer-led approaches in condom use may enhance trust and acceptability among LDTDs and their peers, leading to enhanced safer sexual behaviours.49 Interventions implemented through outreaches for hard-to-reach populations, like LDTDs, may be best suited to their constantly disruptive trucking careers.50 Therefore, interventions seeking to enhance condom use among LDTDs should leverage peer-led and outreach approaches for effective prevention outcomes. Nonetheless, interventions on condom use were highly heterogeneous (I2=94.5%, p<0.0001). This implies that the differences in effect sizes were not due to chance but to other factors such as study design, geographical location, study population and sample size.

Whereas there was a statistically significant pooled effect (RR=1.24, 95% CI 1.13 to 1.36, p<0.001) for interventions reporting HIV-related knowledge, only three were included in the meta-analysis. As such, the studies included in the meta-analysis may limit the generalisability of the pooled effect. Thus, the results should be interpreted with caution. We note that all three studies had a significant effect in enhancing HIV-related knowledge among LDTDs. Again, two of the three studies were either outreach-based or peer-led. Therefore, implementing HIV prevention interventions through outreach may overcome the structural barrier of having to seek services from mainstream public health facilities.50 Conversely, hospital-based interventions may not address certain barriers unique to LDTDs, such as inadequate time and therefore may be less effective. Even though there was no heterogeneity in interventions on HIV-related knowledge, this was not statistically significant, most likely due to the small number of studies included in the meta-analysis. Thus, there is a need for greater detail in reporting interventions on HIV-related data among LDTDs to provide insight into the overall effect of interventions on HIV-related knowledge among LDTDs.

A modest pooled estimate (RR=1.14; 95% CI, 0.91 to 1.42, p=0.248) was observed for interventions on HIV testing among LDTDs, though it was not statistically significant. This may be explained by variations in interventions, contextual barriers or even follow-up durations. One of the interventions11 that had a significant effect on HIV testing was outreach-based. Notably, this study implemented outreach programmes led by peer educators and had a considerably longer follow-up period of 5 years, compared with most other studies.11 In the meta-analysis, the other study,30 which had a significant effect on HIV testing, was a randomised controlled trial. This is not surprising, given that RCTs are considered the gold standard for effectiveness research.51 Overall, a statistically significant heterogeneity (I2=95.4%, p<0.0001) was observed for interventions on HIV testing, indicating that the differences in effect sizes were not due to chance.

A statistically significant pooled estimate (RR=0.86; 95% CI, 0.74 to 0.99, p=0.042) was established in the six interventions for sexual risk behaviour. However, only two interventions27 33 had a statistically significant effect in enhancing safe sexual behaviours among the LDTDs at follow-up. One effective intervention27 was implemented via mobile phone short message service (SMS). Mobile phone technology has been used with fruitful outcomes in HIV prevention among other most-at-risk populations like FSWs and MSMs.52,54 Therefore, researchers implementing HIV prevention interventions should leverage technology for better outcomes. However, text-based interventions still have their fair share of challenges. As seen in one of the included studies, changes or losses in touch via phone numbers may complicate follow-up, leading to high attrition rates.41 Therefore, there is a need to verify the authenticity of phone numbers before text-based HIV interventions are rolled out.41 Moderate heterogeneity (I2=71.7; p<0.007) was observed for interventions on sexual risk behaviours. This implies that the observed effect sizes were not due to chance but to other factors, such as intervention variations and sample size.

The sensitivity and subgroup analyses showed that the overall findings were reliable despite variations in study quality and the presence of outlier studies. Exclusion of studies with outlier effect sizes and those with a high risk of bias did not produce statistically significant changes in pooled effect estimates across most intervention outcomes. This outcome suggests that there was some consistency in the direction of intervention effects in the original analyses. However, we observed fluctuations in heterogeneity, especially for interventions targeting HIV prevention knowledge, condom use, HIV testing and sexual risk behaviours. The substantial reduction in heterogeneity following exclusion of studies such as Cornman et al. (2017)35 and Kelvin et al. (2017)30 indicates that certain individual studies may have contributed significantly to between-study variability due to intervention characteristics or other contextual factors. Nevertheless, heterogeneity remained significantly high even after excluding studies with a high risk of bias, implying the existence of other underlying diversities across the included studies. As such, caution should be taken when interpreting the pooled effect estimates reported in this study.

Studies reporting on the effects of targeted interventions on STI and HIV incidence didn’t report adequate data for meta-analysis. Only one intervention had a significant effect in reducing the risk of STIs by 73% among LDTDs at follow-up (RR=0.27; 95% CI, 0.2 to 0.36; p<0.001).37 Notably, this was an RCT study, which affirms the methodological rigour of this type of intervention and thus its effectiveness in HIV prevention outcomes.

The GRADE assessment indicated that the certainty of evidence was predominantly very low across most of the included studies. The low certainty of evidence was primarily driven by high risk of bias, wide CIs on effect estimates and inconsistencies in outcome reporting. The findings underscore the need for more rigorous and high-quality experimental studies on HIV interventions for LDTDs.55 This would lead to enhanced certainty of evidence on the effectiveness of HIV interventions and inform stronger evidence-based recommendations for HIV prevention among LDTDs.55 Therefore, the estimated effects of the interventions reported in this study should be interpreted with caution.

Lastly, a significant publication bias was observed in the interventions included in the meta-analysis. This may imply that studies with negative findings were underreported. Therefore, the pooled effect estimates may have been overestimated and should be interpreted with caution.

Strengths and limitations

The meta-analysis enabled pooled effect estimates across studies with diverse designs, interventions, sample sizes and follow-up durations, which enhanced the statistical power of the overall analysis. Nonetheless, several limitations should be considered in the context of the current study. First, only 12 out of the 24 studies included in the review were included in the meta-analysis. Indeed, almost half of the included studies had missing information, and their effects on various HIV prevention outcomes could not be gauged. Also, most of the interventions included in the meta-analysis were highly heterogeneous. Notably, a significant publication bias was observed in the interventions included in the meta-analysis. Besides, the certainty of evidence was rated as low to very low across many of the included studies, mainly due to risk of bias, indirectness of interventions and imprecision due to small sample size studies and missing data. As such, these limitations imply that we may have overestimated or underestimated the pooled effect estimates of the various interventions.

Conclusion

There have been mixed outcomes following the implementation of targeted HIV interventions among LDTDs. Targeted interventions on condom use had the most significant effect (RR=1.25; 95% CI, 1.09 to 1.44, p=0.002), followed by HIV-related knowledge (RR=1.24; 95% CI, 1.13 to 1.36, p<0.001) and lastly, sexual risk behaviour reduction (RR=0.86; 95% CI, 0.74 to 0.99, p=0.042). While interventions on HIV testing showed some effect on enhancing uptake at follow-up, the outcome was not statistically significant (RR=1.14; 95% CI, 0.91 to 1.42, p=0.248). Generally, interventions with significant effects on various HIV prevention outcomes were randomised controlled trials, peer-led, outreach-based and adopted modern technologies like text messaging. Therefore, the study recommends upscaling of peer-led, outreach-based and technology-based interventions along transport corridors, as they had the most significant effects in promoting HIV prevention outcomes among LDTDs. Given the predominantly very low certainty of evidence in the included studies, the findings should be interpreted with caution. As such, more rigourous studies are needed to strengthen evidence-based practices on HIV prevention among LDTDs.
