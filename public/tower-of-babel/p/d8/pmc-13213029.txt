Marginal causal effect of fungicide seed treatments on soybean yield and uncertain profitability in the US Midwest
Authors: Paul D. Esker, Denis A. Shah, Spyridon Mourtzinis, Patricio Grassini, Tatiane Severo Silva, Shawn P. Conley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Fungicide seed treatments (FSTs) are widely used in Midwest soybean production due to perceived disease risk. While some studies report significant yield increases, overall economic and environmental benefits remain unclear. This study utilized randomized controlled trial (RCT) and observational data to estimate the causal FST effect on soybean yield. Analysis of the RCT data revealed a modest average yield increase of 22.2 kg/ha attributable to FST. Observational data also indicated a small average yield gain of approximately 36 kg/ha. Monte Carlo simulations showed that yield gains often do not offset the seed treatment costs, with financial benefit likely only under low FST costs and high soybean prices. Given the limited economic return and concerns about the potential negative impacts of widespread FST use on soil microbes and non-target organisms, our research suggests that FSTs may not be necessary in Midwest soybean production, and growers should carefully evaluate their use based on individual farm gate economics as well as ecological considerations.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-47390-0.

Introduction

Soybean production practices in the Midwest United States (US) have changed significantly over the past two decades. First, a notable shift has occurred towards conservation or reduced tillage for promoting soil health in terms of water retention, better drainage, reduced erosion and organic matter improvement1. Currently, about 75% of US soybean fields are managed through conservation practices2. Second, sowing date recommendations have been updated to reflect significant, sometimes dramatic, yield increases with earlier crop establishment3–8. For example, sowing late April to early May can lead to a 55% increase in yield compared with sowing after the end of May9. Improved cultivars also benefit from earlier sowing10. These changes in production practices, particularly earlier sowing into cool, wet soils and reduced tillage, have increased the potential risk of soilborne and seedborne diseases11–13. The main diseases are caused by a complex of pathogenic fungal and oomycete species14.

Soybean growers rapidly adopted fungicide seed treatments (FSTs) in response to the perceived risk of soilborne and seedborne disease challenges. In 1996, about 8% of soybean seed was treated with a seed-applied fungicide, which rose to 30% by 200815, partly due to the commercialization of seed-applied protectants in 200516. By 2015, 60–75% of soybean seed was fungicide treated17,18. FSTs grew rapidly in popularity for several reasons. They were marketed as an insurance policy, as the likelihood of soilborne and seedling diseases is unknown at the time of seed purchase. Adoption was also driven by the successful packaging of FSTs with other seed treatments11,19 and by research indicating large responses in some environments13,20. The almost doubling of soybean seed cost over the 10-year period 2007–2017 also contributed17, as growers sought to protect their investments.

Numerous randomized controlled field trials (RCTs) have been done on the efficacy of FSTs in response to soybean growers’ interest in this management option. The results of these trials from the 1980s to the early 2020s have been varied and contradictory. Initial investigations focused primarily on plant establishment and yield21–25; however, subsequent studies expanded to evaluate multi-location, multi-component treatments and cost-effectiveness implications8,26–29. Observed positive effects were frequently contingent upon specific environmental conditions, such as elevated rainfall or high pathogen populations20,27. Contemporary research efforts are directed towards targeting specific pathogens and utilizing host resistance17,30. Nevertheless, the evidence for the overall impact of FSTs remains ambiguous, attributable to limitations inherent in field-based RCTs and the context-dependent nature of the findings31–34. Unfortunately, agronomic RCTs are costly, time-consuming, and their limited scope may not reflect real-world FST adoption decisions. Observational data can complement RCTs and fill knowledge gaps by offering larger datasets, more factor combinations, broader geographic coverage, and field-scale data31,35–38.

The current research was motivated by the rapid and widespread adoption of FSTs, the discordant findings among individual RCTs regarding the economic advantages of FSTs in Midwestern soybean cultivation, and the latent ecological ramifications of ubiquitous FST utilization. We employed both RCT and observational data to ascertain the causal effect of FSTs on soybean yield within the Midwest. We demonstrate that estimations of the FST effect derived from singular RCTs may be inflated without adequate contextualization of the site-specific conditions that affected the trial. Utilizing a comprehensive, previously amassed, multi-state, multi-location RCT-based dataset39, we derived causal estimates of the average treatment effect (ATE) attributable to FST use. An observational dataset6 was analyzed within a potential outcomes causal paradigm to estimate the ATE from a different perspective40. Our study culminates in a partial economic evaluation of the financial returns to growers resulting from their decision to utilize fungicide-treated soybean seed.

Methods

All coding was done within the R programming environment (version 4.4.2). See the Supplementary Methods online for further details on packages used and for which specific tasks. All yield data used in this study were converted to SI units. Yields originally reported in US customary units (bushels per acre) were converted to kilograms per hectare (1 bushels per acre = 67.19 kg per ha). All analyses and results are reported in metric units (kg/ha or Mg/ha).

RCT-based data

The literature on randomized controlled trials (RCTs) evaluating fungicide seed treatments in soybean is narrow and limited. Consequently, we conducted a targeted systematic review rather than a broad PRISMA-style review. This approach was appropriate because the specialized nature of the topic meant relevant studies were concentrated in a small number of journals and our objective was to synthesize quantitative treatment effects from available, published RCTs. The iterative search strategy was completed in January 2025. Google Scholar was used as the primary search engine because of its broad coverage of citation networks. The search began with combinations of keywords including ‘soybean’, ‘seed treatment’, ‘yield’, and ‘randomized controlled trial’. The initial set of key articles so identified, including those by the current article’s authors and collaborators, was used in performing backward citation tracking (examining their reference lists) and forward citation tracking (identifying recent papers that cited them), sometimes called a ‘snowballing’ approach41. Studies were included if they (1) were randomized controlled trials, (2) were conducted in the US Midwest, and (3) reported extractable plot-level or treatment-mean yield data for both fungicide-treated seed (FST) and an untreated control (UTC). Studies that only reported non-extractable summaries (e.g., ANOVA tables without means, or only p values) were excluded. The search was restricted to English-language publications. This process produced the 11 articles (ranging from 1989 to 2023) listed in the Supplementary Appendix online, from which 410 observations were extracted. We denote this as the RCT1 dataset.

We also extracted the yield data from untreated control plots and from those sown to fungicide-treated seed from another, larger dataset39, which included results from numerous RCTs done across several US states. We further filtered the data to align with the observational dataset (described in the next section). First, we selected trials from Midwest states only (IA, IL, IN, KS, MI, MN, MO, NE, ND, OH, SD, WI). This resulted in 176 unique studies. Second, we used the irrigation status (a yes/no binary factor) recorded for each trial to exclude 33 irrigated studies, leaving the final 143 non-irrigated (rainfed) studies. This final RCT2 dataset contained 1692 plot-level observations and was 4.1 times larger than the RCT1 dataset. Unlike the RCT1 dataset, which was a synthesis of disparate published articles, the RCT2 dataset represented a curated collection of trial-year observations (individual locations within years) conducted by university researchers using consistent experimental protocols. This centralized curation minimized the variability in reporting and methodology often found in literature-based syntheses. Each “observation” in these two datasets (RCT1 and RCT2) represented a single, plot-level yield comparison (treated vs. untreated), and effectively served as the unit of replication for our synthesis.

For each observation (i) in the dataset, two effect measures were calculated: the causal ratio (Ri = FSTi/UTCi) and the causal difference (Di = FSTi - UTCi). The causal ratio, R, represents the relative effect of fungicide-treated seed (FST) on soybean yield compared to the untreated control (UTC), calculated as the ratio of their respective yields. This metric quantifies the proportional change in yield due to FST. The causal difference, D, represents the absolute difference in yield between the FST and UTC treatments. Confidence intervals (CIs) for the means of R and D for both the RCT1 and RCT2 datasets were estimated using a clustered bootstrap to account for the non-independence of plots within studies. We did 10,000 bootstrap draws, in which we first resampled the studies (11 for RCT1, 143 for RCT2) with replacement. For each draw, we then calculated the mean of all associated plot-level observations from that resampled cohort of studies. This method weights each study equally at the sampling stage. The 95% CIs were determined using the percentile method (i.e., from the 2.5% and 97.5% sample quantiles) from the resulting 10,000 mean estimates. The mean of D is an estimate of the population-level causal average FST (treatment) effect, called the ATE. We denote the ATE obtained for the two RCT datasets as ATERCT(1) and ATERCT(2), respectively, and the corresponding mean causal ratios as RRCT(1) and RRCT(2). No observations were removed as outliers. Extreme values were retained in the analysis as they represented valid, high-impact scenarios (e.g., severe disease pressure) and were of direct interest.

Observational data

The third dataset was an observational product derived from self-reported information collected from Midwest soybean growers concerning their individual-field production practices6. Data were gathered across three growing seasons (2014–2016) and 10 Midwest states (IA, IL, IN, KS, MI, MN, ND, NE, OH, WI). We restricted the dataset to non-irrigated fields as only 16.5% of fields were irrigated and of these 88% were in NE. Data were further filtered to fields reporting yield and whether or not they were sown with fungicide-treated seed (FST+) or untreated seed (FST−). Other relevant recorded data included covariates related to field characteristics, phenologically-linked environmental conditions37, management practices and agricultural inputs. The final dataset utilized for analysis comprised soybean yield (Supplementary Fig. S1 online) from 3614 individual fields (Supplementary Fig. S2 online), FST usage (FST+ or FST−, a binary variable) and 21 pre-treatment covariates (Supplementary Table S1 online). All numeric covariates were centered and scaled before analysis (Supplementary Table S1 online).

We excluded post-treatment management variables from the methods used to estimate the ATE, thereby avoiding post-treatment bias. Foliar fungicide (FF) application, for example, is one such post-treatment variable. Foliar fungicides target pathogens that emerge later in the season and do not protect against the early-season soilborne pathogens targeted by FST. As FFs do not serve as compensatory interventions (if FST was skipped), adjusting for FF use would be biologically and statistically inappropriate.

Causal analysis

We estimated the ATE of FST on soybean yield from the observational data. No differentiation was made between specific fungicide products, application rates, or targeted pathogens, because growers are aware that seeds have been treated with fungicide(s) but generally don’t have the information (recorded or recalled) of which products or formulations were used42. Consequently, we defined FST+ as representing the generic application of a fungicidal seed treatment, potentially involving one or more active ingredients and targeting one or more seedborne or soilborne fungal or zoosporic pathogens within the genera Phytophthora, Phytopythium, Pythium, Rhizoctonia, Fusarium, and Phomopsis. We do note that our definition is a potential violation of the consistency assumption (no multiple versions of treatment) necessary to obtain a causal ATE43. However, our definition of FST+ assumes the irrelevance of treatment variations. In other words, we investigated the effect of FST as a policy44, assuming that different forms of the treatment do not affect the outcome differently45.

We started with a simple difference-in-means estimator to determine the ATE46, with a heteroscedasticity-consistent (HC2) standard error estimator for the variance to produce a more reliable confidence interval for the ATE. The rest of our causal analysis was centered on doubly-robust methodologies within a potential outcomes framework47, incorporating both linear modeling and machine learning approaches48. The causal estimation techniques we used included augmented inverse-probability-of-treatment weighting (AIPTW40, Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation (TMLE49, causal forests (nonparametric50 and Bayesian51, and Bayesian Additive Regression Trees (BART52. Three versions of AIPTW were modeled, reflecting progressive relaxation of the linearity assumptions within the model for yield, contingent upon covariates, and for the propensity scores (ps), representing the probability of a field being sown with fungicide-treated seed: (i) parametric - utilizing a linear model for yield and a generalized linear model (glm) for ps; (ii) semiparametric - employing a machine learning model (xgboost) for yield and a glm for ps; (iii) SuperLearner53 models for both yield and ps. Given that distinct estimation methods entail unique underlying assumptions, we refrained from reliance on any singular ATE estimation method. Instead, we employed various parametric and Bayesian methods to allow us to assess the consistency and robustness of the ATE estimates across different estimation approaches38,54.

Sensitivity analysis

Sensitivity analyses (SA) were done to determine the magnitude required of an unobserved confounder to nullify or invert the calculated ATE attributable to FST. These assessments included a tipping point analysis within a linear modeling approach55 and a Bayesian sensitivity analysis56.

Heterogeneity in FST effects

This analysis was motivated by the heterogeneity in the individual conditional average treatment (CATE) values (Supplementary Fig. S3 online), and to determine if FST was largely beneficial in certain soybean production scenarios. The heterogeneous treatment effects (HTE) analysis was done using a causal forest implementation with the grf package57. After fitting a causal forest, a global test for heterogeneity was done using the test_calibration function. Next, the dataset was randomly partitioned into training (70%) and evaluation (30%) sets. A causal forest model was fit using a subset of the pre-treatment covariates (“Planting date”, “Topsoil (0–30 cm) pH”, “Snow water equivalent from the beginning of the year to sowing”, “Seeding rate”, “Topsoil organic matter content”, “Row spacing”) on the training data. The subset targeted the most important continuous covariates (based on a variable importance measure as returned by the variable_importance function), recognizing the sparsity in categorical variables, and to reduce the dimension of the covariate space relative to the number of observations so as to increase the power of detecting HTE57. This model was then used to predict CATEs for the evaluation set. Observations in the evaluation set were subsequently stratified into quartiles based on their predicted CATE. To characterize these groups, we calculated and plotted the mean and 95% confidence intervals for the CATEs themselves, for pre-treatment covariates (“Planting date”, “Topsoil pH”), and a relevant post-treatment moderator (“Sowing to emergence precipitation”) for each quartile58. This two-stage approach allowed for the descriptive analysis of the post-treatment variable in relation to causal treatment effect estimates.

Partial economic analysis of FST benefit

Soybean price (soy.price) was modeled by a three-component Gaussian mixture distribution, fitted to empirical data from 2004 to 2024 (https://www.nass.usda.gov/). The 20-year temporal window was selected to represent the most recent fluctuations in the soybean market (Supplementary Fig. S4 online).

Soybean seed is typically sold commercially in units of 140,000 seeds. Soybean seeding rate was defined as the number of units sown per ha. Seeding rates in the full observational data (n = 5184 observations6 were used to fit a beta distribution, scaled between the observed minimum and maximum seeding rates (Supplementary Fig. S5 online). Specifically, a Beta(6.3, 6.2) distribution, scaled to the range of 1.57–3.97 units/ha, was used to model the distribution of seeding rate (seed.rate; Supplementary Fig. S6 online).

The per-unit cost of FST (FST.unit.cost) was modeled by a beta distribution, scaled between US$5.00 and US$15.00 per unit (Supplementary Fig. S7 online). The mode was set to US$7.50, and the mean slightly higher than the mode at US$8.00. The alpha and beta shape parameters of the beta distribution were derived analytically from the scaled mean and mode; the explicit equations are provided in the code repository associated with this article.

The CATE values from the nonparametric causal forest (NCF) and the Bayesian causal forest (BCF) models (Supplementary Fig. S3 online) were fitted to distributions that were then used to draw simulated estimates of the individual-field yield response (i.e., yield gain or loss) due to FST. The NCF-simulated CATE values represented a more conservative, low-variance scenario, whereas simulated values based on the BCF CATE distribution represented a high-variance scenario (Supplementary Fig. S8 online). A non-standardized t-distribution was used to simulate CATEs from the NCF model, and a two-component Gaussian mixture distribution was used for the simulation of CATEs from the BCF model. Simulated values drawn from the fitted distributions represented yield return due to FST (yield.return).

Given soy.price, seed.rate, FST.unit.cost and yield.return, the value of the yield gain (loss) due to FST (yield.value) was estimated as follows: yield.value (US$/ha) = yield.return (Mg/ha) × soy.price (US$/Mg). The cost of FST per ha (FST.cost) was calculated as: FST.cost (US$/ha) = seed.rate (unit/ha) × FST.unit.cost (US$/unit). Finally, the net benefit (loss) of FST use (net.benefit) was determined by net.benefit (US$/ha) = yield.value (US$/ha) - FST.cost (US$/ha).

The distribution of net.benefit was estimated via Monte Carlo simulation, in which 50,000 values were drawn from each of the previously described distributions for soy.price, seed.rate, FST.unit.cost and yield.return. The net.benefit was then computed by element-wise application of the aforementioned equations.

The entire data analysis pipeline is visualized in Supplementary Fig. S9 online.

Results and discussion

FST effect derived from RCTs

Analysis of the RCT1 dataset, using a clustered bootstrap, revealed a mean fungicide seed treatment to untreated control (FST/UTC) yield ratio (RRCT(1)) of 1.15 (95% CI 1.01–1.33), suggesting a potential yield increase of up to 33% with FST use. The highest individual yield ratios (greater than two) were observed in trials done in environments with known high populations of soilborne pathogens or with above-average rainfall within a few weeks of sowing (Fig. 1a). The distribution of the individual causal yield differences (Di) also exhibited a right skew (Fig. 1b). The estimated average treatment effect, ATERCT(1), was 84.8 kg/ha (95% CI 14.1–179 kg/ha), indicating an overall positive (yet statistically imprecise and uncertain given the wide CI) effect of FST on soybean yield.

However, the generalizability of RCTs performed under specific, potentially extreme conditions is limited. Manipulations such as selecting fields with a history of disease, artificially elevating pathogen populations via inoculation, or imposing factors like irrigation may not accurately represent typical soybean production environments. The skewed distributions of the Ri and Di, with their large upper values, likely reflect an exaggeration of the FST effect under these specific conditions34. Many field-based RCTs may suffer from low signal-to-noise ratios59, a factor known to bias reported effect sizes upwards60. Consequently, the ATERCT(1) estimate derived from the RCT1 dataset is likely positively biased, as it may overstate the average benefit of FST across a broader range of typical field conditions. This effect size exaggeration was addressed here not by standard meta-analytic tools (which were inappropriate for our data structure), but by countering it with the larger more representative RCT2 dataset.

Analysis of the RCT2 dataset revealed a more modest effect of FST on soybean yield, with a mean FST/UTC ratio of 1.01 (95% CI 1.01–1.02) and ATERCT(2) of 22.2 kg/ha (95% CI 0.55–43.3 kg/ha). Compared to RCT1, RCT2 included more data points representing a broader range of experienced environments. The restriction of the RCT2 dataset to rainfed conditions and exclusion of artificial inoculations was reflected in fewer extreme values in both yield ratio and difference distributions (Fig. 2), in spite of an increase in heterogeneity compared to their distributions in the RCT1 dataset. For these reasons, we believe the analysis of the RCT2 dataset to be less biased than that of the RCT1 dataset and the findings more generalizable. While RCT1 was heavily weighted toward “efficacy” trials where conditions were optimized for disease (e.g., inoculation or irrigation), RCT2 captured the performance of FSTs across a broader, more typical range of rainfed production environments in the Midwest. Comparing the two estimates provides a quantitative measure of this bias: the ATERCT(1) of 84.8 kg/ha is 3.8-fold larger than the ATERCT(2) of 22.2 kg/ha, which highlights the severe impact of selection bias in the non-representative RCT1 sample.

FST effect derived from observational data

The observational data from the Midwest indicated that 71% of fields were sown with fungicide-treated seed during the 2014–2016 period; given the trends, that number is likely to be even higher now. Figure 3 presents the main ATE estimates derived from the N = 3614 observational fields. The consensus among the various doubly-robust causal analysis methods applied to the observational dataset was a 36 kg/ha yield gain due to FST, generally not exceeding 100 kg/ha (Fig. 3). However, there was considerable uncertainty in the estimates. Five estimation methods indicated that the ATE could plausibly be zero. The nonparametric causal forest stood out as a method with a very narrow confidence interval, in contrast to the Bayesian causal forest. Despite their similar names, NCF and BCF methodologies are quite different58. The NCF algorithm is based on generalized random forests and uses specialized splitting criteria to partition the covariate space, directly targeting heterogeneity in the estimated CATEs57. In contrast, BCF is a fully Bayesian, sum-of-trees (BART-based) model that additively decomposes the outcome into separate prognostic (predictive) and treatment effect functions, uses regularization priors on the treatment effects to estimate CATEs, and incorporates the propensity scores as a covariate to help reduce confounding bias51. As noted in the Fig. 3 caption, the ATE estimate from the RCT2 analysis (from the Fig. 2 data) is included as the blue shaded region for direct comparison. Overall, the ATERCT(2) estimate correlated with the ATE estimates derived from the causal analysis of the observational data, thereby establishing congruity and complementarity between the ATE estimates from two distinct data sources (Fig. 3).

Defining FST as a binary treatment aggregates various fungicide active ingredients, formulations, and application rates. In causal inference, this variability in treatment “versions” can complicate the interpretation of the ATE. However, evaluating FST as a broad management policy reflects the practical reality for soybean growers, for whom seed is typically marketed as a pre-treated package. While specific formulations may differ in efficacy against certain pathogen complexes, our results provide an estimate of the average benefit of adopting the FST strategy across the diversity of products used in Midwest production.

The causal interpretation in the observational data analysis relies on the standard assumptions of exchangeability, positivity, and consistency47. To mitigate the risks associated with violation of these assumptions, we: (i) restricted covariates to strictly pre-treatment factors to avoid bias, (ii) verified covariate balance to ensure sufficient overlap (positivity), and (iii) triangulated our findings against the independent RCT2 dataset. The convergence of multiple doubly-robust estimators (Fig. 3) further supports the validity of the estimated average treatment effect.

A difference-in-means estimator of the FST effect gave an ATE of 189 kg/ha (Fig. 3). This estimate was 5.2 times higher than the 36 kg/ha average over the doubly-robust estimates, and 8.5 times higher than the 22.2 kg/ha ATE estimate derived from the RCT2 dataset. The rudimentary difference-in-means estimator ignores the disparity in the number of FST+ and FST− fields, as well as covariates potentially influencing the decision to use fungicide-treated seed or not46,61. For these reasons, the difference-in-means estimator is not suitable for observational data (it can be used with RCTs) as it leads to biased estimates46, as seen in the result here, and is presented only for illustrative purposes.

Sensitivity analysis

With observational data, there is no guarantee that all possible confounders have been measured or included in the data. Sensitivity analysis from both the linear and Bayesian perspectives indicated that an unmeasured confounder would have to be rather strong to reverse the FST causal effect or bring it to zero (Supplementary Figs. S10 and S11 online). From the Bayesian perspective, an unmeasured confounder would have to have an effect at least as large as that of the covariate with the strongest marginal effect to reduce the FST effect to zero (Supplementary Fig. S11 online).

Heterogeneous treatment effects

The global test for heterogeneity confirmed the presence of significant CATE variability (p < 0.001). Stratifying the evaluation data into quartiles based on predicted CATEs revealed significant treatment effect heterogeneity (Fig. 4). The lowest-benefit quartile (Q1) exhibited a negative mean treatment effect (− 9.4 kg/ha), with a 95% confidence interval entirely below zero. Conversely, the highest-benefit quartile (Q4) showed a large positive mean effect (20.8 kg/ha). The intermediate quartiles (Q2 and Q3) showed mean effects of 5.5 and 12.8 kg/ha, respectively. We then profiled the mean covariate values for each quartile. For sowing date, a negative association was observed. This means that early sowing was associated with a positive FST benefit that decreased steadily as sowing was delayed. Soil pH within the 6.6–6.7 range was associated with positive CATEs. Positive FST effects were associated with higher precipitation from sowing to emergence, which is congruent with results from RCTs in which there was frequent rain or irrigation after sowing (Fig. 1). We do caution that these HTE results are exploratory, but do generate testable hypotheses. A more robust generalizabilty and identification of situation-specific effects will depend on a larger and higher resolution (in terms of covariates) observational dataset.

Financial benefit of FST

The results from 50,000 Monte Carlo simulations to estimate the net financial benefit of FST on soybean production in the high-variance scenario are shown in Fig. 5. The results are faceted by FST cost and soybean price, visualizing how these factors influence net benefit. Furthermore, our economic analysis explicitly accounted for FST product heterogeneity by incorporating a wide distribution of treatment costs (US$5.00–US$15.00 per unit), thereby accounting for price differences between varied FST formulations. The conditional means for all simulation inputs and outputs are provided in Supplementary Table S2 online. The causal gain in soybean yield due to FST is not likely to be large enough to offset the cost of treatment, and hence not likely to be worthwhile (> US$5 per ha), except under low (US$5.10–US$7.26 per unit) or moderate (US$7.26–US$8.52 per unit) FST cost per seed unit in conjunction with soybean prices being at or near the last 20-year highs (US$410–US$689 per Mg). In the more conservative, low-variance scenario (FST yield return represented by the CATE distribution from the NCF model), there was no or only mediocre net financial benefit of FST under any combination of soybean price and FST cost (Supplementary Fig. S12 online).

Conclusions

The primary novelty of this study is its use of a causal inference framework to address data from both controlled trials and large-scale, on-farm observational data. For investigating soybean seed fungicide treatments, this is the first analysis to move beyond statistical significance comparisons of yield within small-plot RCTs to quantification of the marginal causal effect and uncertain profitability of FSTs in large-scale soybean production in the Midwest. Although there is a yield benefit on average, the gain is often not large enough to offset the cost of treatment and may be worthwhile only under low FST cost combined with high soybean prices. We found that large FST effects reported in some published literature are likely biased by context-dependent atypical scenarios (e.g., irrigation, high rainfall shortly after sowing, or artificial augmentation of soilborne pathogen populations) not representative of the average field. Heterogeneous treatment effects analysis of the observational data suggested that FST could be most beneficial when sowing early into fields that may be at risk to above-average rainfall before emergence; confirmation of this benefit will require more extensive data. Although our datasets span the last two decades, the results are still relevant to the current time. The core soilborne pathogen complex has remained consistent, and the agronomic decision to use FST is evaluated here as a management policy rather than a test of specific active ingredients. Furthermore, the observational data captures a unique window where adoption was widespread but not yet saturated, providing the necessary mix of fields sown to treated and untreated seed for causal inference. This condition will be difficult to replicate in the current landscape of near-universal seed treatment adoption62.

Our analysis, which shows a small average yield gain (22 to 36 kg/ha) that often fails to offset treatment costs, adds to the growing body of evidence that FST as a component of a high-input soybean production system does not provide consistent and reliable protection against downside yield loss risk27,63. For producers, our findings argue against using FST as a wide-scale insurance policy. A re-evaluation of the routine, prophylactic use of FSTs is encouraged. This may be a difficult task as seed treatments and seed purchase are often bundled together, and the trend is to multiple fungicide chemistries being integrated in a seed treatment package64–66. Ideally, growers should adhere to Integrated Pest Management (IPM) principles, using FSTs only in specific, high-risk situations or after verifying a positive return-on-investment through on-farm strip trials. Not every soybean field is at risk to soilborne disease, nor every part of a field for that matter, owing to the heterogeneous distribution of soilborne pathogens within a field67.

The widespread use of FSTs raises questions about their potential harmful effects on seed-associated endophytic fungi, the soil microbiome, and non-target organisms68. Furthermore, the continuous prophylactic use of these fungicides exerts selection pressure that can lead to the emergence of resistant pathogen populations, compromising the long-term efficacy of chemical control69. Seeds have a unique microbiome that benefits germination and seedling development70,71. Any negative environmental effects of FSTs are especially worrying given that an estimated 525 Mg of seed-applied fungicide was used on soybeans in the northern US between 2006 and 201472, and this amount is likely increasing as FSTs become even more widely integrated into Midwest soybean production. Given the marginal economic benefit and the known ecological risks, including non-target effects on the soil microbiome and the selection pressure for fungicide resistance, policymakers should prioritize support for research and extension programs that help growers identify the specific conditions where FSTs are likely to be profitable. Supporting the development of field-specific risk assessment tools would be a far more sustainable and economical approach than the current widespread, prophylactic use.
