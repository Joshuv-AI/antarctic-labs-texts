Germline determinants of toxicity and efficacy in patients with large B-cell lymphoma treated with CAR T-cell therapy
Authors: Paolo Strati, Amanda Brandt, Anath C Lionel, Jared Henderson, Jason R Westin, Sherry Adkins, Elizabeth J Shpall, Partow Kebriaei, Jeremy Ramdial, Neeraj Saini, Sairah Ahmed, Christopher Flowers, Sattva S Neelapu, Michelle A T Hildebrandt
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundRecent data have suggested that germline genetic aberrations can affect outcomes in patients with large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL) treated with chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy (CART). However, a comprehensive analysis of germline determinants of response and toxicity after CART has not yet been described.MethodsGenome-wide genotyping was performed in 170 patients with LBCL treated with standard of care axicabtagene ciloleucel. Polygenic risk score instruments for blood cell traits and inflammatory markers were obtained from the PGS Catalog and analyzed using PRSice-2. Exploratory gene-based and genome-wide association study analyses were performed. Genetic ancestry of the patients with LBCL was estimated using ADMIXTURE. Analysis was conducted to identify genetic risk of toxicity and efficacy endpoints.ResultsIncreasing PRS for monocyte count was associated with increased risk of cytokine release syndrome of any grade (OR 2.49, 95% CI 1.18 to 5.25, p=0.016). Similarly, genetically predicted interleukin (IL)-1Rα and (IL)-27 levels were decreased (p=0.002) and increased (p=0.012) in patients with G3-4 day 30 cytopenia, respectively. The latter was also associated with variation in the hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis-related gene RAB27A (p=0.041). Genome-wide significant (p<5×10−8) variants were identified in association with progression-free and overall survival, including SPOCK1, SLC28A2-AS1 and DUOX1. No significant differences in outcomes were observed based on ancestry, except for a decrease in risk for D30 G3-4 cytopenia for patients of European ancestry (p=0.026).ConclusionGermline genetic aberrations relevant to myeloid cell biology can predict toxicity and efficacy of CART in patients with LBCL. Elucidating such intrinsic determinants may help improve patient selection and develop strategies to enhance the therapeutic index of CART.Keywords: Genetic, Lymphoma, Chimeric antigen receptor - CAR, Macrophage, Cytokine release syndrome

Introduction

Increasing data have demonstrated that acquired genetic aberrations can impact the efficacy and/or toxicity of anti-CD19 autologous chimeric antigen receptor T-cell therapy (CART) in patients with relapsed or refractory large B-cell lymphoma (LBCL). In a single-center retrospective analysis including 153 patients with LBCL treated with CART, TP53 alterations (mutations and/or copy number alterations) in tumor cells were common (37%), and independently associated with inferior complete response (CR) and overall survival (OS) rates.1 In addition, our group has previously shown that clonal hematopoiesis mutations, especially those associated with inflammation (DNMT3A, TET2, and ASXL1), occur in up to 36% of patients and are associated with increased severe neurotoxicity in a cohort of 114 CART-treated LBCL individuals.2

Interestingly, recent data have suggested that germline genetic aberrations can also affect outcomes in these patients. Performing whole genome sequencing and using a deep learning variant characterization framework to identify germline variants in 17 hemophagocytic lymphohistiocytosis (HLH)-associated genes in 110 patients with LBCL treated in the ZUMA-1 pivotal trial, deleterious germline variants in STXBP2 were enriched in toxicity cases relative to control patients.3 In addition, our group has previously genotyped 132 patients with LBCL treated with CART and observed that polymorphisms in genes involved in drug metabolism and macrophage biology, such as ABCB1, MISP and CPVL, were associated with lymphocyte kinetics in response to conditioning chemotherapy.4 However, a comprehensive analysis of germline determinants of response and toxicity after CART in this patient population has not yet been described.

Polygenic risk scores (PRS) analyses are approaches that move beyond investigation of a single variant, and summarize genetic predisposition based on multiple variants on the endpoint of interest. PRS instruments are developed in large populations, such as the UK Biobank and other population-based cohorts, to identify a panel of variants significantly associated with a trait.5 For circulating biomarkers, PRS instruments represent the intrinsic, genetically predicted levels of this biomarker in the general population. These instruments can then be applied to a study population in downstream analyses to assess if their genetic predisposition to higher (represented by increased PRS scores) or lower (represented by decreased PRS scores) levels of the biomarker result in differences in risk for an endpoint of interest. Several PRS instruments of interest to the CART patient population related to blood cell traits and inflammatory markers have been developed, which served as the instruments for this current study.6 7

Methods

Patient selection

This is a cohort analysis of 170 consecutive patients with relapsed or refractory LBCL treated with standard-of-care axicabtagene ciloleucel (axi-cel) at our institution, The University of Texas MD Anderson Cancer Center, between January 2018 and October 2021. Standard of care was defined as administration of commercial product outside of a clinical trial. Patients received conditioning chemotherapy (CCT) with cyclophosphamide (500 mg/m2) and fludarabine (30 mg/m2) administered intravenously on days −5 to –4 and −3, followed by axi-cel infusion (2×106 cells/kg) on day 0.

Toxicity and response assessment

Clinical characteristics and laboratory features before CCT (day −5) were confirmed by review of the medical records. Cytokine release syndrome (CRS) and immune cell-associated neurotoxicity syndrome (ICANS) were prospectively graded for up to 30 days after axi-cel infusion, according to the CARTOX grading system from January 2018 to April 2019, and according to the American Society for Transplantation and Cellular Therapy criteria from May 2019 onward.8 9 Cytopenia at day 30 was graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events V.5. Performance status was defined according to the Eastern Cooperative Oncology Group (ECOG).10 The International Prognostic Index (IPI) was calculated as previously described.11 Response status was determined by the Lugano 2014 classification.12

Single nucleotide polymorphism analysis

Germline DNA was isolated in all patients from banked peripheral blood samples using standard procedures. Genotyping was conducted on the Illumina Genome Screening Array (V.2.0) with inclusion of 2% replicates for quality control and repeated for samples with call rates less than 95%. Following the removal of duplicate samples, imputation was performed to TOPMed r3v1.0.0 using Minimac implemented in the Michigan Imputation Server.13,15 Variants with minor allele frequencies <1% and imputation scores <0.60 were excluded from further analysis. Additional quality control parameters based on Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium and heterozygosity were applied.

Statistical methods

Differences between categorical variables were evaluated using χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. The difference in a continuous variable between patient groups was evaluated by the Mann-Whitney test. PRS instruments for blood cell traits and inflammatory markers (online supplemental table 1) were obtained from the PGS Catalog and analyzed using PRSice-2.6 7 16 17 Differences in the PRS values by toxicity and efficacy endpoints were tested using the Student’s t-test with bootstrap resampling of 1,000 iterations for p value generation. Significantly different PRS scores (continuous) were further analyzed using multivariable logistic regression with inclusion of age at time of CART (continuous), gender, use of bridging therapy, principal components (PCs) for population substructure. IPI score (≥3) was also included in analyses of efficacy endpoints. Gene-based analysis was performed for candidate genes (online supplemental table 2) related to HLH, apoptosis, interferon (IFN)-γ signaling pathway, and macrophage function using MAGMA, with a nominal gene-based p value of 0.05. In the exploratory investigation, genome-wide analyses were conducted with the standard Bonferroni-corrected genome-wide association threshold of 5×10–8 used for significance. ADMIXTURE was applied to estimate proportions of global genetic ancestry.18 K was set to four to represent the continental populations in the study population (African (AFR), European (EUR), Asian (ASN), and Admixed (ADM)/Indigenous American) with proportions of AFR, EUR, ASN, and ADM ancestry assigned for each individual. Progression-free survival (PFS) was defined as the time from the start of axi-cel infusion to progression of disease, death, or last follow-up (whichever occurred first). OS was defined as the time from the start of axi-cel infusion to death or last follow-up. Cox proportional hazards models with adjustment for age at time of CART (continuous), gender, bridging therapy, PCs, and IPI score were used to assess risk of progression and death.

Results

Toxicity and efficacy outcomes

170 patients were included in the analysis. Self-reported race/ethnicity included white (N=114; 67.1%), Hispanic (N=28; 16.5%), black (N=9; 5.3%), ASN (N=9; 5.3%) and other (N=10; 5.9%) patients. The other baseline characteristics collected before initiation of CCT are shown in table 1. Overall, 82 (48%) patients had grade (G) 2–4 CRS, 65 (38%) had G3-4 ICANS, and 98 (58%) had D30 G3-4 cytopenia. Of 153 patients evaluable for response on D90, CR was achieved in 89 (52%) patients. After a median follow-up of 26 months (95% CI 24 to 28 months), 116 (68%) patients progressed and/or died, and median PFS was 6 months (95% CI 4 to 8 months). At the most recent follow-up, 94 (55%) patients had died, and median OS was 17 months (95% CI 10 to 24 months).

Germline determinants of toxicity: CRS and ICANS

PRS values for blood cell traits and inflammatory markers were compared between patients who experienced a toxicity event and those who did not (table 2). In the analysis of genetic susceptibility to CRS of any grade, the distribution of PRS for monocyte count (p=0.063), white blood cell count (p=0.081), interleukin (IL)-6RA levels (p=0.080), and IL-8 levels (p=0.099) reached borderline significance in those who experienced CRS as compared with those who did not. For monocyte count, this difference conferred a 2.49-fold increase in risk of CRS (OR 2.49; 95% CI 1.18 to 5.25; p=0.016) (figure 1). Furthermore, a trend for lower genetically predicted reticulocyte count among those who experienced G2-4 CRS was observed (p=0.078) (table 2).

Germline determinants of toxicity: D30 G3-4 cytopenia

A total of 120 patients were evaluable for G3-4 cytopenia at D30 after removing those who had baseline G3-4 cytopenia (table 2). Levels of genetically predicted IL-1Rα were lower among patients with D30 G3-4 cytopenia (p=0.002). This difference conferred a 56% decrease in risk of D30 G3-4 cytopenia (OR 0.56; 95% CI 0.37 to 0.86; p=0.007) (figure 2A). In contrast, genetically predicted levels of IL-27 were significantly higher among patients with D30 G3-4 cytopenia compared with those without (p=0.012), and the difference conferred a 1.55-fold increase in risk of D30 G3-4 cytopenia (95% CI 1.06 to 2.26; p=0.022) (figure 2B). Gene-based analysis of a panel of HLH genes was performed for D30 G3-4 cytopenia (candidate genes are indicated in online supplemental table 2). A significant relationship between D30 G3-4 cytopenia and variation within RAB27A was identified (p=0.041). Of interest, no association between STXBP2 and D30 G3-4 cytopenia was observed (p=0.16).

Germline determinants of efficacy

Significant intrinsic differences in genetically predicted levels of monocyte counts (p=0.045) were observed for D90 CR, with suggestive findings for PRS values of IL-6 levels (p=0.061) (table 2). In gene-based analyses of members of the IFN-γ pathway, ALCAM was significantly associated with D90 CR (p=0.021), while no significant associations were observed for macrophage and apoptosis-related genes. However, in exploratory genome-wide analysis of time to event for PFS and OS, 13 variants for each endpoint reached GWAS significance (p<5×10−8), respectively (online supplemental tables 3,4). Three of these variants with minor allele frequencies of 0.015 were significant for both endpoints, representing two distinct loci: one on chromosome 5 (encompassing SPOCK1) and another on chromosome 15 (encompassing SLC28A2-AS1 and DUOX1) (table 3). The variants on chromosome 15 were in high, long-range linkage disequilibrium (r2 and LD=1.0) over an 82-kilobase region.

Genetic ancestry analysis

Due to the genetic diversity of our patient population (33% non-white race/ethnicity), we conducted ADMIXTURE analysis to investigate potential relationships between toxicity and efficacy endpoints with germline genetic ancestry (figure 3). No significant differences in risk of these endpoints by AFR, ASN, and ADM ancestries were identified. However, a significant decrease in risk for D30 G3-4 cytopenia was observed for patients of EUR ancestry (OR 0.23; 95% CI 0.06 to 0.84; p=0.026).

Discussion

We present here the first comprehensive analysis of intrinsic determinants of toxicity and efficacy in patients with LBCL treated with autologous anti-CD19 CART. While in our analysis, due to sample size, the overall risk conferred by germline genetic factors was limited, our findings provide insight into the potential genetic contribution to CART outcomes and help generate hypothesis on potential biological pathways and novel predictive factors.

In our study, multiple genetic determinants relevant to macrophage biology, including genetically predicted monocyte count, IL-6 and CRP levels, had significant or highly suggestive associations with risk of either CRS or ICANS. It is currently well known that cytokines responsible for CART-related toxicities, including IL-6 and IL-1β, are mainly secreted by host macrophages on stimulation from CAR T-cells-secreted IFN-γ.19 20 In this regard, both in vitro and in vivo models have demonstrated that tissue accumulation of macrophages is associated with the onset of severe CRS and/or ICANS. In contrast, macrophage depletion and/or targeting can result in their complete resolution.21,23 This is also supported by translational studies, which show associations between CART-related toxicities and macrophage-related parameters, including macrophage-derived cytokines, monocyte count, and concentration of monocytic populations in tissue or in the infusion product.24,28 The PRS associations identified are in line with these prior studies and further underscore the key role of host macrophages in CART-related toxicities. Identification of these intrinsic differences in circulating levels of macrophage-related factors may help to better define the profile of those who develop these toxicities.

Of interest, these same genetic determinants for monocyte count and IL-6 were also associated with CART efficacy. To this regard, in preclinical models, macrophage depletion resulted in decreased CAR T-cell amplification,21 29 suggesting that biologically rational targeting of their phenotype and function may result in better outcomes.23 This is also supported by translational studies, showing associations between CAR T-cell kinetics and macrophage-related parameters, including peripheral monocytes and tissue macrophage content.4 30 31 While agents targeting macrophage-secreted cytokines, including tocilizumab, siltuximab and anakinra, are currently extensively used for the management of CRS and ICANS, our data support the hypothesis that further exploration of myeloid cell biology may help not only mitigate CART-related toxicities but also improve CART-related outcomes.32 33 Although the GWAS analysis in this study was highly exploratory due to the limited sample size, it is notable that the three variants consistently and significantly associated with OS and PRS, located within SPOCK1, SLC28A2 (also known as CTN2), DUOX1 and DUOX2, all playing a critical role in the innate immunity and inflammatory response.34,37

Intrinsic differences relevant to T-cell biology and HLH, including IL-1Rα, IL-27 and RAB27A, were associated with risk of D30 G3-4 cytopenia. Recent studies have demonstrated that an oligoclonal CD8+T cell population with upregulated IFN-γ-associated pathways is enriched in the bone marrow of patients with LBCL with persistent severe cytopenia after CART.38 39 A similar biological mechanism has been reported for primary HLH, a genetic condition in which natural killer cells and/or CD8+T cells present with defective feedback regulation, resulting in elevated levels of IFN-γ and uncontrolled macrophage activation.40 IFN-γ-driven cytopenia can be managed using thrombopoietin agonists or IFN-γ-neutralizing antibodies, without compromising CAR T-cell function.2041,43 Together, these findings present a potential approach to the identification of those patients at elevated risk while offering a potential therapeutic strategy for this common and severe CART-related toxicity.

Finally, no difference in CART-related outcomes based on genetic ancestry was observed in our study, similar to previous studies focusing on race and ethnicity.44 These initial lack of findings of differences across ancestries and race/ethnicities is encouraging in reinforcing the value of CART for the LBCL population overall. However, inclusion of under-represented populations and minorities in CART trials remains limited and disparities in CART access have been well described.45,47 As the number of individuals of diverse ancestries receiving CART increases, we will be better positioned to truly evaluate potential differences in CART toxicities and efficacy as there is some evidence that disparities may exist. For example, individuals of EUR descent experienced a lower risk of persistent severe cytopenia, a condition known to be associated with a shorter survival and limits access to potentially life-saving clinical trials.38 48

A limitation in the development of PRS instruments is the requirement for large, well-characterized populations with linked genome-wide genetic data and circulating biomarker levels. The UK Biobank is widely used for PRS development due to the availability of 500,000+study participants.49 However, a challenge is that the UK population is that it is limited in the number of individuals of non-EUR ancestry. The PRS instruments selected for this study were developed in the UK Biobank, which may limit their ability to represent intrinsic genetically determined circulating biomarker levels in more diverse populations. There is a lack of similar, robust PRS instruments developed in non-EUR populations. As more diverse populations are characterized, the likelihood for population-specific PRS instrument development increases. These would be of great interest to apply to our non-EUR CART patients. Finally, these PRS instruments included in this analysis were developed in the general population and would not represent the circulating levels of these biomarkers in patient with cancer during the treatment period.

In conclusion, we took several approaches to define the impact of germline genetic factors on CART efficacy and toxicity as a tool to identify predictive and prognostic factors, while also gaining insight into CART biology. We acknowledge several limitations of our study, including its single-center and retrospective nature, the lack of a validation cohort, and its limited power to assess PRS associations in case of low incidence toxicities (such as G2-4 CRS) or to perform a robust genome-wide analysis. It is also important to highlight that causality implications of associations observed in this study remain to be confirmed, and findings should not be overinterpreted in this regard. Larger studies are warranted to confirm these observations and better understand how relevant germline genetic determinants may influence efficacy and toxicity after CART. Elucidating such determinants may help in improving patient selection and in developing strategies to enhance the therapeutic index of CART.
