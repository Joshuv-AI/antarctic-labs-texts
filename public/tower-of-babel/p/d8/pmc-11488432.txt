Deciphering the hydrodynamics of lipid-coated microbubble sonoluminescence for sonodynamic therapy
Authors: Priyankan Datta, Sreejesh Moolayadukkam, Rakesh Prasad Sahu, Ranjan Ganguly, Swarnendu Sen, Ishwar K Puri
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
Graphical abstractKeywords: Sonodynamic therapy, Glioblastoma, Lipid-coated microbubble, Ultrasound, Sonoluminescence

Introduction

Sonodynamic therapy (SDT) is a targeted, minimally invasive treatment [1], [2], [3], [4], [5] that destroys deep tissue solid tumors such as glioblastoma (GBM), which is the most aggressive, infiltrative, and recurrent brain tumor with a low five-year survival rate of 6.9% [1]. The SDT mechanism that neutralizes solid deep-tissue tumor cells is not yet fully understood [6]. The most accepted hypothesis attributes the efficacy of SDT to the cytotoxic effect of reactive oxygen species (ROS), such as singlet oxygen and free radicals, which are produced when low-intensity pulsed ultrasound (<10W/cm2), or LIPU[6], [7], is applied to activate specific sonosensitizer drugs [6]. These sonosensitizers are nontoxic, metabolize rapidly, and remain inactivated without ultrasound application [8]. Two sonosensitive drugs, 5-aminolevulinic acid (5-ALA) [9] and hematoporphyrin [10], are currently used in clinical trials to treat GBM.

For selective accumulation within tumor cells, sonosensitizers are typically administered exogenously before LIPU is applied [11]. Upon LIPU application, endogenous nuclei, or microbubbles, within the biological fluid undergo stable or inertial acoustic cavitation, producing visible light emission called sonoluminescence.

As they absorb sonoluminescence energy, sonosensitizers become activated to their singlet excited state. During de-excitation from the singlet state, these sensitizers interact either directly with molecular oxygen or undergo an interstate crossing to their triplet-excited state and transfer energy. This process generates cytotoxic singlet oxygen and free radicals, which produce tumor cell death [6], [12]. A schematic representation of the SDT mechanism for glioblastoma treatment is presented in Fig. 1.

Schematic representation of the SDT mechanism during glioblastoma treatment. Upon ultrasound application, lipid-coated microbubble oscillation enables transient opening of the blood–brain barrier, which facilitates sonosensitizer crossing. Sonosensitizers accumulate selectively within the glioblastoma tumor cells. Sonoluminescence occurs through bubble collapse and excites the sonosensitizer to its singlet state. During de-excitation from the singlet to triplet state, reactive oxygen species (ROS) are generated, which cause further oxidative stress, leading to cell death.

SDT therapy is challenged by the abnormal vasculature and lymphatic dysfunctions of solid tumors. The rapid proliferation of tumor cells leads to a hypoxic tumor microenvironment and higher interstitial fluid pressure (∼30 mm Hg) compared to the tin normal tissues (1–3 mm Hg) [13], [14]. This higher pressure at the tumor site acts as a barrier towards sonosensitizer accumulation, reducing sensitizer efficacy for ROS generation and thereby diminishing SDT effectiveness. To overcome this challenge, microbubbles, which act as acoustic cavitation nuclei, are administrated exogenously [6], [15] to improve SDT efficiency. Choices include contrast agents for ultrasound radiography [16], [17] such as perfluoro gas-filled microbubbles (1-8µm dia.) encapsulated within lipid, protein, or polymer shells.

Microbubbles injected through intravenous infusion [18] have four advantages for SDT. First, the administrated microbubbles exhibit hydrodynamic characteristics similar to those of an endogenous gas microbubble in the presence of ultrasound [19], leading to a synergistic therapeutic effect. Second, sonosensitizers can be tethered to the microbubbles, making precise delivery to the target site feasible. Third, sufficient cavitation can induce transient opening of the blood–brain barrier [6], [20], enhancing SDT efficiency for high-grade gliomas. Fourth, the use of an imaging contrast agent can facilitate MRI-guided SDT [20].

The literature reports three types of U.S. Food and Drug Administration (FDA)-approved microbubbles (Sono Vue® / Lumason®, Optison®, Definity®) used as ultrasound contrast agents.[20] Phase 1/2a [18] clinical trials have shown that applying pulsed ultrasound with systematic microbubble (Sono Vue) injection opens the blood–brain barrier for patients with recurrent GBM, helping optimize the subsequent chemotherapy dose. During Phase I clinical trials for pancreatic cancer [21], Sono Vue injection was combined with chemotherapy and ultrasound stimulation to improve solid tumor treatment.

In addition to conventional perfluoro gas-filled coated microbubbles, the preclinical focus has also considered O2 gas-filled microbubbles in SDT [22], [23] for pancreatic cancer treatment. The rationale for using these microbubbles is that oxygen is the primary component for ROS generation and is thereby expected to improve SDT efficacy. Besides, O2 toxicity is lower than that of perfluoro gas [24].

Predictions of the onset of sonoluminescence for lipid-coated O2 microbubbles are not yet available in the literature. Although exogenously injected microbubbles can play an important role in improving the efficacy of deep-tissue tumor treatment, their use for SDT is still in the pre-clinical stage [22], [23], [25], [26], [27]. Further investigation is required to understand the bubble dynamics, including the onset of sonoluminescence, the primary route for ROS-mediated tumor cell death with SDT.

Previous investigations [28], [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34] focused on the effect of ultrasound pressure variations on bubble oscillation. However, sonoluminescence onset, which depends on the maximum temperature rise due to gas compression when a bubble compresses to a minimum volume, and its effect on lipid-coated oxygen microbubbles influenced by low-intensity ultrasound remain unexamined. Identifying the ultrasound parameters relevant to SDT, particularly the frequency and pressure at which the onset of sonoluminescence occurs, is important to further facilitate lipid-coated oxygen microbubble-assisted SDT therapy for clinical applications.

We address these gaps by proposing a physically plausible maximum temperature rise criterion that lends to the estimation of the minimum bubble contraction factor and the pertinent parameters conducive to the onset of sonoluminescence. Here, we investigate sonoluminescence from lipid-coated microbubbles in the presence of ultrasound. We consider the hydrodynamics of the FDA-approved ultrasound contrast agent Lumason® and phospholipid-coated oxygen microbubbles to identify the optimal pressure and frequency ranges for sonoluminescence. A regime map is also constructed for sonoluminescence events in the therapeutic ultrasound range based on the maximum temperature rise criterion for bubble collapse.

Theoretical model

If a spherical gas bubble in an incompressible liquid is subjected to ultrasound, it absorbs energy from the pulsating pressure field, growing to a maximum size and then collapsing to a minimum. As the gas inside the bubble experiences rapid (and hence nearly adiabatic) expansion and contraction, it experiences large temperature sweeps; sonoluminescence occurs when the bubble temperature exceeds a typical value as it is compressed. The hydrodynamics of a pulsating gas bubble in a sound field can be derived from the energy conservation principle, often referred to as the Rayleigh-Plesset (R-P) equation [35], [36]. Here we use an extended form of the R-P equation to account for the acoustic radiation energy per unit volume, which becomes significant near bubble collapse due to the high acceleration R˙c1 of the bubble wall [37]. In our study, three states [28] are considered for capturing the change in the lipid shell structure under the ultrasound field, i.e., buckled, ruptured, and elastic.

Buckled state: This state arises near the end of the bubble collapse. In this state, the effect of surface tension is vanishingly small because of the fast compression of the lipid monolayer; lipid molecules are expelled from the interface into the surrounding liquid. Hence, the area at the buckled state is very small and primarily dependent on the number of net molecules present and the molecular area (∼ 0.4 nm2).

Rupture state: During its fast expansion under the negative cycle of the ultrasound, the lipid shell can break, resulting in the gas being exposed to the surrounding liquid. In this state, the effective surface tension is reckoned the same as that of the liquid.

Sonoluminescence onset criteria and modeling assumptions

Experimental evidence of the maximum temperature rise during a sonoluminescence event from a collapsing bubble in water or body fluid, such as blood, is unavailable. Previous experiments [39] reported a minimum temperature of 15,200 ± 1,900 K for sonoluminescence during a single argon bubble collapse in an 85% H2SO4 (aqueous) medium. Therefore, we consider a minimum temperature rise of 15,000 K as the lower threshold for the onset of single-bubble sonoluminescence. This assumption is supported by the hot-spot model, which considers a spatially uniform temperature distribution within the collapsing bubble that provides a maximum temperature rise of the order of 104 K [40].

With this minimum temperature criteria for the onset of sonoluminescence Tmin=15,000K and an initial temperature T0=298K set for an initial bubble diameter range (1.25 μm≤R0≤2.5μm), the minimum value of the bubble contraction factor βmin for sonoluminescence is evaluated and discussed in the following section.

Numerical method and physical properties considered for simulation

Equations (1–7) are solved implicitly using the ordinary differential equation solver ODE 23s of MATLAB 2023a. The numerical methods reported in the literature [35], [42], dedicated to single bubble sonoluminescence and modeling of the lipid-coated microbubble hydrodynamics in the liquid, used the Runge-Kutta method, which is a non-stiff solver. However, in our study, we used the implicit solver, ODE23s, that can handle stiff source terms that often arise before the bubble collapse ends and result in a large bubble wall velocity R˙c1 in the case of sonoluminescence onset. Results and discussion

Validation

Validation is performed to assess the reliability of our numerical code. Based on reported data [36], we consider a 4.3 μ m initial radius xenon bubble in water subjected to an ultrasound field with a maximum amplitude (Pa) of 1.45 atm. Fig. 2 shows that the bubble begins to expand during the negative portion of the ultrasound stimulation cycle until its internal pressure exceeds the outside fluid pressure. Even after the ultrasound pressure passes its zero crossing, the bubble continues to expand due to its inertia up to 37.14 μ m maximum radius (Rm), ∼ 9 times larger than its initial value. Thereon, the bubble starts collapsing to a minimum value at a more rapid rate. The bubble wall velocity at its first minimum is 2280 m/s, after which the bubble radius oscillates and finally settles at its initial radius.

Comparison between the simulated temporal variation of a xenon bubble radius in water with previous experimental measurements (inferred from photomultiplier tube voltage), when a bubble with a 4.3 μm initial radius is subjected to an ultrasound field with a maximum pressure of 1.45 atm. The surface tension is considered to be 0.05 N/m. The simulation and measurements are in agreement over a complete ultrasound pressure cycle, confirming code accuracy for these conditions.

The simulations are compared against the previous measurements using a photomultiplier tube (PMT), where the bubble radius was assumed to be proportional to the square root of the PMT voltage for a sonoluminescing xenon bubble in water [36]. There is a relatively small (0.56%) difference in the maximum bubble radius between our simulation and the measurements. For the first after-bounce, this difference is 34% for the maximum radius. A possible source for the differences is the resolution of the PMT, which may not accurately record relatively small after-bounce magnitudes. An acousto-optic modulator would, therefore, be required for capturing after-bounces [36]. An additional validation with a different gas is also performed to assess code versatility (supplementary sections S4 and S5).

Validation of temperature rise during sonoluminescence

The most acceptable theory for sonoluminescence onset is that the gas inside the bubble is weakly ionized during bubble compression. While temperatures in the 10,000 K −20,000 K range have been reported to cause either electron-atom bremsstrahlung or electron–ion bremsstrahlung, resulting in light emission (see supplementary section S6) during single bubble sonoluminescence [35], [40], accurate measurements of the maximum temperature during sonoluminescence are unavailable. The lifetime of a sonoluminescence event is of the order of ∼100 ps [43]. It is a reported fact that the direct measurement of the maximum temperature rise is challenging since the volume of the hot zone is very small (<1 µm radius), and the phenomenon is highly transient in nature [44]. Therefore, a few indirect approaches have been attempted, such as determining the relative rate constants of chemical reactions (e.g., methyl radical recombination method, MRR) or using a molecular emission approach [44]. These indirect measurement approaches have uncertainties. For instance, MRR relies on measuring the reaction products, such as ethylene and ethane. However, the reaction rate constant for ethylene formation is temperature-dependent, whereas that for ethane formation is temperature-independent. This leads to uncertainty in the temperature measurement since it relies on the ratio between the two reaction rate constants based on the measurement of the two products. Furthermore, the experimentally measured temperatures are time and volume-averaged, which could lead to inaccuracy in predicting the instantaneous maximum temperature rise during a sonoluminescence event.

Therefore, we simulate the maximum temperature rise during sonoluminescence as a bubble collapses and compare the results with a previously reported simulation [35] that offers a benchmark theoretical analysis of the temperature rise during single-bubble sonoluminescence. Fig. 3 shows good agreement between our simulation and the reported simulation data, confirming code accuracy for predicting the maximum temperature during sonoluminescence.

Regime map for argon bubble

A regime map is constructed to predict sonoluminescence onset for a single R0=2.5 μm argon bubble in water. The microbubble is subjected to an ultrasound field with pressure and frequency ranges of 0.5 – 10 bar and 20 kHz – 1 MHz, respectively. The frequency range is selected based on values for in vivo and in vitro experiments for glioblastoma SDT treatment [2].

Sonoluminescence occurs within a pressure and frequency window. Fig. 5a presents pressure contours to identify the minimum bubble contraction factor for sonoluminescence to occur, where sonoluminescence onset is determined according to the criterion defined in Eq. (9). For the lowest ∼20kHz frequency, the lower and upper threshold pressures for sonoluminescence are 1.35 bar and 6.225 bar, respectively. As the driving frequency increases, so does the lower threshold pressure. At the highest driving 1 MHz frequency, the lower threshold rises to 9.3 bar.

Previous experimental measurements [48] (white and green data points represent lower and upper-pressure thresholds, respectively, for the onset of sonoluminescence) lie largely within the predicted sonoluminescence region. The anomaly for the lowest threshold pressure, where the single experimentally observed sonoluminescence event is observed in the simulated no-event zone in Fig. 5a, is attributed to a conservative assumption for the minimum temperature rise for sonoluminescence onset based on a prior experimental measurement [39]. The dashed white line in Fig. 5b represents the FFDA-approved limit of the mechanical index (Pa/f = 1.9) that must not be exceeded during diagnostic imaging [50].

During a complete ultrasound cycle, a bubble experiencing a relatively low driving frequency has sufficient time to reach its first minimum radius after reaching a maximum value [46], [47]. With increasing driving frequency, the time available for the bubble to reach its first minimum decreases, after which it undergoes expansion due to the net pressure difference across the gas–liquid interface.

Previous experimental measurements [48] overlaid on the Pa-f contours of Fig. 5a, lie within the sonoluminescence regime obtained from our simulation. Contours for the maximum temperature increase corresponding to the minimum bubble contraction factor are shown in Fig. 5b. For βmin≤0.15, there is a minimum temperature rise of 15,000 K, which also supports sonoluminescence onset predictions from our model. The anomaly at the lowest threshold pressure, where the single experimentally observed sonoluminescence event lies in the simulated “no event zone” in Fig. 5a is attributed to our assumption of the minimum temperature rise required for sonoluminescence onset. The experiments do not report the corresponding temperature.

The SDT efficacy improves at higher Pa and lower f, as shown in Fig. 5b where for a specified f the maximum temperature increases with increasing Pa. The temperature rise becomes steeper with increasing Pa for lower f values. Such a temperature increase enhances ionization (through KBT), increasing the power emitted from a sonoluminescing bubble,[35], [49] which is in accord with previous time-correlated photon counting experiments that tracked the intensity and spatial spread of sonoluminescing light flashes [35]. Parametric variation

Lumason® microbubble in the water

For the Lumason® (SF6 gas) microbubble in water, the initial bubble radius, phospholipid-coated surface elasticity (χ) and surface dilatational viscosity (ks) are 1.25 μm, 0.5N/m, and 7.5 × 10-9 kg/s, respectively [38]. Within the pressure-frequency range considered, Fig. 6a shows the minimum bubble contraction factor βmin ∼ 0.25. The maximum temperature rise shown in Fig. 6b for this case is ∼1150 K. For the same ultrasound driving pressure, the lipid monolayer elasticity and viscosity dampen the bubble oscillations for Lumason®-microbubbles more than for the uncoated bubble. This results in a higher minimum contraction factor and lower temperature rise for the Lumason® microbubble. This observation reveals that Lumason® collapse could possibly lead to a weak sonoluminescence event. Experimental evidence [19] supports our finding that the SonoVue microbubble, which contains an SF6 gas core, has weak sonoluminescence intensity.

Predictions for sonoluminescence onset for a R0=1.25 μm Lumason® (SF6 gas) microbubble during compression in water for (a) minimum bubble contraction factor and (b) maximum temperature. A weak sonoluminescence is observed for Lumason® as the maximum temperature is ∼ 1150 K at the lowest operating frequency (20 kHz).

Lipid-coated oxygen microbubble in the water

The initial radius and lipid shell parameters (viz., χ,ks, σ0, σr etc.) of the oxygen microbubble are considered similar to those for bubbles with Lumason®. The van der Waals hard core radius (h) in Eqs. (2), (3), and 5) is calculated (see the supplementary section S7) based on the density (1.291 kg/m3), molecular weight (32 kg/kmol), and molecular kinetic diameter (346 pm) of oxygen. The minimum bubble contraction factor (Fig. 7a) and the maximum temperature rise (Fig. 7b) in this case are 0.1 and ∼50,000 K, respectively. Thus, for the same initial radius and ultrasound pressure and frequency ranges, an oxygen microbubble exhibits stronger sonoluminescence in comparison to the one with Lumason® (Fig. 6a-b), which is filled with SF6 gas.

Predictions for sonoluminescence onset for a R0=1.25 μm phospholipid coated O2 microbubble during compression in water for (a) minimum bubble contraction factor and (b) maximum temperature. Our simulation shows that an O2 microbubble sonoluminescence is stronger in comparison to one of the same radius that has Lumason®. The threshold pressures at the lowest (20 kHz) and highest (1 MHz) ultrasound frequencies for sonoluminescence onset are 1.6 and 3.7 bar, respectively.

Our finding also supports the gas ionization theory for the onset of sonoluminescence. The first ionization energy for oxygen is 13.6 eV, which is lower in comparison to that for SF6 (15.3 eV) [52]. This results in a higher temperature rise when the oxygen bubble is compressed as compared to the SF6 for the same ultrasound frequency and pressure ranges.

Effect of physiological conditions on lipid-coated microbubble hydrodynamics

While the foregoing analysis is done in an aqueous medium, it is logical to extend the same to physiologically pertinent media as well. An in vitro cell culture complete medium for culturing glioblastoma cell lines such as U-87 MG contains the basal medium e.g., Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM) supplemented by 10% (v/v) Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS). Therefore, to mimic an in vitro environment, we conducted simulations to investigate the lipid-coated microbubble hydrodynamics (both for Lumason® and O2 microbubble) in a complete DMEM medium [53], considering the initial surrounding fluid temperature remains at 37°C. The sonoluminescence onset is predicted in terms of βmin (see supplementary section S8). Observation reveals (Fig. S4) that no significant change is observed for sonoluminescence onset for the applied ultrasound field compared to the water for both Lumason® and O2-coated microbubbles.

Sonoluminescence onset is also predicted in blood for a phospholipid-coated oxygen microbubble (supplementary section S9), keeping the lipid shell parameters (ks,χ) and initial bubble radius (R0) identical to those for water. The minimum bubble compression factor βmin is higher for blood than for water due to the higher viscosity and density of blood, which impose greater inertial and viscous impedances to changes in bubble size. A higher βmin results in a lower maximum temperature rise at the lowest frequency (20 kHz). The threshold pressure for sonoluminescence in blood at the highest ultrasound frequency (1MHz) increases to 4.8 bar (see supplementary section S9) compared to 3.7 bar for water (Fig. 7a). This implies a 1.29 times increase in the threshold pressure in blood as compared to the water.

An additional investigation is also carried out to study the effect of viscosity and density variation of the surrounding fluid on the hydrodynamics and sonoluminescence onset for a lipid-coated O2 microbubble (see supplementary section S10). We observed that at a specific liquid density, an increased viscosity elicits higher damping force, which in turn yields a smaller extent of bubble shrinkage that is insufficient for sonoluminescence onset. However, changing the surrounding fluid density has a negligible effect on βmin.

Conclusions

We numerically simulate the onset of sonoluminescence during microbubble collapse in the presence of ultrasound. The ultrasound driving frequency is specified from conditions relevant to clinical trials of SDT-based glioblastoma treatment. The results provide a pressure-frequency map for sonoluminescence onset during SDT for two types of lipid-coated microbubbles, facilitating appropriate choices of operating parameters while designing an SDT procedure. Contours of the maximum temperature rise within the selected ultrasound pressure and frequency ranges are also predicted. Sonoluminescence occurs within a certain ultrasound pressure-frequency window. The FDA-approved ultrasound contrast agent Lumason® exhibits weak sonoluminescence Tmax=1150K. Stronger sonoluminescence from a lipid-coated oxygen microbubble of the same initial radius could be used to enhance SDT efficacy.

In summary, (1) the threshold pressures for the onset of sonoluminescence for a coated O2 microbubble in water at 20 kHz and 1 MHz frequencies are 1.7 bar and 3.7 bar, respectively, and (2) the sonoluminescence is weaker for the Lumason® microbubble compared to that from an O2 bubble. (3) No significant change is observed for sonoluminescence onset in an in vitro cell culture medium compared to the water for both Lumason® and O2-coated microbubbles. (4) The threshold pressure for sonoluminescence at the highest ultrasound frequency (1MHz) increases by 1.29× in blood in comparison to water for lipid-coated O2 bubble. (5) Sonoluminescence is not expected for a highly viscous liquid.
