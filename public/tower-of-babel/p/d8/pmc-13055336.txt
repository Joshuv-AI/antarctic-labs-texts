Adverse childhood experiences and cognitive outcomes among older adults in low-income and middle-income countries: a systematic review
Authors: Che Henry Ngwa, Sawsan Abdulrahim, Rim Nehme, Meiya Saad, Mayssan Kabalan, Maria Rita Lteif, Yara Yazbek, Monique Chaaya, Martine Elbejjani
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectivesWhile adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) have been consistently linked to poorer cognitive outcomes in later life, far less is known about ACEs’ contribution to dementia and cognitive ageing risk in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), despite their growing and disproportionate share of global Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) burden. This study aimed to systematically review existing evidence on the association between individual ACEs and cognitive outcomes among adults aged 40 years and older in LMIC settings.DesignSystematic review and narrative synthesis.Data sourceWe searched Medline, Embase, PsycINFO and CINAHL from the inception of each database to January 2026.Eligibility criteriaObservational studies examining the association between individual ACEs and cognitive outcomes among adults aged 40 and older in LMICs.Data extraction and synthesisTwo reviewers independently screened each record, assessed risk of bias using the Joanna Briggs Institute critical appraisal tool and extracted data. Results were illustrated using descriptive forest plots and a narrative synthesis.ResultsOur systematic review included 14 studies, primarily from upper-middle-income countries, that assessed the relationship between individual ACEs and cognitive impairment (n=4) and function (n=10). 10 studies reported significant associations between ACEs and poorer cognitive function and increased risk of cognitive impairment. No study assessed the association between ACEs and ADRD. Parental death, neglect and mental health issues in the family showed consistent associations with cognitive outcomes, whereas experiencing hunger and poor health in childhood showed inconsistent associations.ConclusionsOur review corroborated evidence from high-income countries that exposure to ACEs is associated with a long-term risk of poorer cognitive outcomes among older populations in LMICs. However, the available literature remains limited, with most studies originating from upper-middle-income countries, few examining cognitive impairment and none assessing ADRD. These findings underscore substantial gaps in knowledge and highlight the need for research across diverse LMIC settings to clarify the role of broader psychosocial and contextual childhood experiences in shaping cognitive ageing and dementia risk.PROSPERO registration numberCRD42024501816.

Introduction

With the rapid ageing of the population worldwide, understanding the burden and risk factors of Alzheimer’s disease and related dementias (ADRD) is a global public health priority. ADRD, a class of neurodegenerative disorders characterised by cognitive decline, memory loss and impaired daily functioning, is among the leading causes of disability and death among older adults.1 2 Several social, behavioural and health factors have been associated with ADRD risk, including low education, alcohol use, depression, smoking and other cardiovascular risk factors.3 Growing evidence also suggests a role for early-life adversity,4 5 highlighting that ADRD risk may be shaped in the earliest life stages and offering opportunities for early and lifecourse prevention approaches to sustain cognitive health.

Adverse childhood experiences (ACEs) are stressful, negative experiences occurring within the family or a child’s social environment, that usually occur before age 18 and manifest in varying severity and chronicity, and that pose harm or threaten a child’s health and development.6 A large body of evidence exists on the detrimental impacts of ACEs on a range of health outcomes that manifest in older age,7 8 including increased risk of cardiovascular diseases, cancer and depression. Exposure to ACEs has also been associated with a higher risk of adopting negative health behaviours across the lifecourse, such as smoking, drug and alcohol use, obesity and physical inactivity,7 8 further highlighting their role in initiating a cascade of adverse health outcomes and underlining their potential as a target for early prevention and intervention. Furthermore, evidence from several prospective studies and systematic reviews indicates a significant association between ACEs and poorer cognitive function and ADRD risk in later life.911

Several direct and indirect mechanisms have been proposed to explain how ACEs influence cognition throughout the lifecourse. ACEs may directly influence the structure and function of key brain regions such as the amygdala, prefrontal cortex and hippocampus, which can persist, resulting in poorer cognitive outcomes later in life.12 13 ACEs may influence cognitive development trajectories and ADRD risk through psychosocial pathways as well by affecting both access to and years of education and participation in cognitively stimulating activities and opportunities across the lifecourse, which are essential for the formation of cognitive reserve.14 15 ACEs also operate through indirect pathways by increasing the risk of adverse health behaviours and risk factors, chronic disorders and depression, which have been linked to a higher risk of neurocognitive outcomes and faster cognitive decline.16 17

Examining the specific characteristics of ACEs, such as their type, frequency, severity, timing and duration, can offer important insights into how early-life adversity influences health and ADRD risk across the lifecourse.18 19 For example, repeated exposures to abuse and financial difficulties in childhood, compared with a single exposure, have been shown to be associated with a higher risk of cardiovascular disease, obesity and diabetes.19 20 Moreover, results from a neuroimaging study highlight differential effects of the timing, type and severity of ACE on amygdala and hippocampal volume,21 suggesting a heightened vulnerability of these brain regions to the severity of neglect during preadolescence. Some research found a dose-response relationship between ACEs and poorer cognitive function,22 23 which is in line with the cumulative stress hypothesis that accumulated exposure to stress leads to higher risk of disease.24 In contrast, studies indicating a timing-specific impact of ACEs on cognitive function25 and on stress-sensitive brain regions involved in cognition21 align with the critical period theory, which suggests that adverse exposures at particular periods during development have more detrimental effects on health over the lifespan.26

Empirical evidence on the association between ACEs and cognitive outcomes in older age has been primarily derived from research in high-income countries.159 Little is known about these associations in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs), which currently bear the greatest share of global ADRD burden and are projected to experience the fastest rates of population ageing and ADRD prevalence in the coming decades2729; synthesising the evidence on modifiable early-life risk factors such as ACEs is therefore particularly critical in these settings. Importantly, the social and structural conditions that shape exposure to and recovery from ACEs differ substantially between LMICs and high-income countries. Exposure to ACEs in LMICs decades ago often occurred in settings characterised by pervasive poverty, limited educational opportunities and constrained upward mobility, which may reduce access to protective resources during early development and resilience across the lifecourse, and amplify the long-term impact of early-life adversity on cognitive outcomes in older age. Moreover, social protection systems, such as child welfare services and social and economic safety nets, that can buffer the effects of child abuse or neglect and other ACEs, have historically been limited in many LMICs.30 These challenges remain relevant today, with three in four individuals in low-income countries and one in two individuals in LMICs still lacking any form of social protection.31 These vulnerabilities are further compounded by under-resourced healthcare systems, with fewer opportunities for early identification and intervention among children exposed to adversity, limiting prospects to long-term health consequences and promoting recovery from early-life stressors.30 32

In this study, we conducted a systematic review of studies that examined the associations between individual ACEs and cognitive outcomes, including cognitive function, impairment and ADRD, among adults aged 40 and older in LMICs. We described the studied populations, ACE characteristics and reported associations between ACEs and cognitive outcomes.

Methods

This systematic review followed the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-analyses guidelines.33 The review protocol was registered on the International Prospective Register of Systematic Reviews (PROSPERO-CRD42024501816).

Search strategy

We performed a comprehensive search in August 2024 across four databases to identify published articles from the inception of each database to the date of the search: Medline (Ovid:1946-present), Embase(1947-present), APA PsycInfo (EBSCOhost:1887-present) and CINAHL ultimate (EBSCOhost:1937-present). We updated the search in January 2026, across the four databases, using the same search strategy to identify potentially eligible articles published between 2024 and January 2026.

In close coordination with a medical librarian, we developed the search strategy in Medline using keywords and medical subject headings (MeSH) for each component of the Population (older adults), Exposure (ACE), and Outcomes (cognitive function, cognitive impairment, dementia and Alzheimer’s) in LMICs (based on World Bank classification),34 and combined them using appropriate Boolean operators.

First, we identified important keywords and broad categories for the exposure and outcome, such as “childhood adversity,” “childhood trauma,” “abuse,” “neglect,” “violence,” “cognition,” “cognitive decline,” “cognitive impairment,” “cognitive function,” “Alzheimer’s disease,” and “dementia” from the literature. Using this information, we identified relevant MeSH terms and additional keywords that were combined to form the search strategy in Medline. This search strategy was then translated to the other databases. See online supplemental table 1 for the complete search strategy for all databases. We opted to employ a broad search strategy to match the broad definition of ACEs and to increase the chances of identifying all relevant articles.

Eligibility criteria

The definition of LMICs was based on the World Bank classification as of 2024, and economies that did not meet this definition were excluded from the search. For example, Hong Kong and Macao special administrative regions of China were not included in the search since they are classified as high-income economies. Studies from the China Longitudinal Ageing Social Survey (CLASS) and the China Health and Retirement Longitudinal Study (CHARLS) were conducted among the adult population in mainland China, excluding Hong Kong and Macao,3537 and were therefore included in our study. None of the identified study settings/countries transitioned to an HIC classification during the review period (up to January 2026).

We included cross-sectional, case–control and cohort studies reporting an association between any ACE (measured before age 18) and any of the cognitive outcomes of interest (cognitive function, cognitive impairment, dementia and Alzheimer’s disease) among middle-aged and older adults (40 years or older) in LMICs. While we focused on adults aged 50 and above, our search identified two articles including participants aged 40+, which we did not exclude because they were relevant to our search. We also included studies that examined continuous (cognitive function scores) and binary/categorical outcomes (cognitive impairment and dementia). Because different ACEs may have differential impacts on cognition and because we were interested in mapping the ACEs studied in LMIC contexts, we focused on studies reporting the association between individual ACEs (such as physical neglect or violence, parental death, childhood poverty, childhood experience of hunger and childhood illness) and outcome. We excluded studies that only assessed aggregate or cumulative scores of multiple adversities. Studies based on a single wave of data from an existing cohort study were included as cross-sectional. Studies in any language were included.

Screening and data extraction

The screening of titles and abstracts of all articles obtained through the search was independently performed by two co-authors using Rayyan based on a predetermined screening checklist. Then, two co-authors independently screened the full text of all included studies using the full-text screening option in Rayyan. A hand search of the reference list of all included articles was performed to identify potential studies that might have been missed through the original search strategy. The first author was involved in all screening steps. All discordant decisions during the screening process were resolved through discussion between the team members.

Data extraction was performed independently by three researchers; a data extraction trial on four articles was performed to ensure a consistent process. A standard data extraction form included the following indicators: First author, year, country of data collection, study design, age of participants, percentage of females in the sample, ACE measure(s), cognitive outcome, covariates, and effect size and 95% CI of the association between ACE and outcome. Moreover, when available, we extracted data on the characteristics of ACE like type, definition/scope, age at exposure, frequency and severity. In cases where a single study reported the independent association between more than one ACE and outcome, we extracted multiple effect sizes and their corresponding 95% CIs. In the case of multiple articles published from the same study (eg, using the same data and reporting on the same association), we only included the article reporting the more comprehensive results with the largest sample size or the article reporting results based on multiple waves of data.

Quality assessment

We assessed the quality of the included studies using the Joanna Briggs Institute (JBI) critical appraisal tool for cohort, case–control and cross-sectional studies. This tool was developed to assess the methodological quality of studies and to determine the extent to which authors have addressed the possibility of bias in the design, conduct and analysis of their studies.38 Two coauthors independently assessed the methodological quality of each included study and rated each item as ‘yes’ indicating that the authors addressed possible sources of bias, ‘no’, ‘unclear’ or ‘absent’. Disagreement in the rating of JBI items was resolved through discussion between two co-authors.

Analysis

We synthesised information about the characteristics of each included study (eg, country, study design, sample size), ACE and cognitive measures used, and covariates adjusted for in the analysis. Given the substantial variation in definitions and measures of both ACEs and cognitive outcomes across the studies and the lack of reporting of standardised effect sizes or summary measures of the outcomes, we were not able to conduct a meta-analysis and present pooled estimates. Using data extracted on the independent association between each ACE and cognitive outcome, we generated descriptive forest plots for a visual summary of the results. This was done separately for studies that assessed continuous cognitive function, with the beta coefficient as the effect estimate, and for studies that assessed the presence of cognitive impairment as a binary outcome, with the OR as the effect estimate. We also summarised information about the characteristics of the ACE measure used in each study. We indicated whether each study considered any ACE characteristic, including how often the adversity occurred in childhood (frequency), how severe the adversity was (severity), over what age range it occurred (time window of exposure), and if the question posed to participants clearly indicated what ACE is measured (scope).

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, or conduct, or reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Results

The systematic search was conducted in August 2024 and updated in January 2026, yielding one additional article included in the final synthesis. Our combined search yielded 12 067 records. After removing duplicates, the titles and abstracts of 8770 articles were screened. We employed a broad search strategy, searching keywords across all fields to identify all relevant articles. As a result, a substantial number of the initial search results were expectedly outside the scope of this review (eg, studies focused on childhood cognitive development, non-cognitive outcomes, younger populations or high-income settings).

Following initial screening, 104 studies were deemed eligible and retained for full-text screening review. Of those, a total of 90 records were excluded, including articles in which the outcome was not cognition or the predictor was not an ACE (n=33), studies conducted in countries that are not LMICs (n=14), wrong publication type such as reviews and editorials (n=20), articles among children and young adults below the age of inclusion in this study (n=12), studies (n=8) that investigated the same ACEs as those reported in one comprehensive article,23 or studies that had no extractable data (n=3). Ultimately, 14 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the final synthesis (figure 1).

Table 1 presents characteristics of the 14 identified studies,233951 including publication year, country; study design; sample size (% female); age; and ACE and cognitive measures. Most studies (n=10) were conducted in China, while one study was conducted in each of South Africa, Indonesia, India and Vietnam. Seven of the 14 studies were cross-sectional, six cohort and one was a case–control study. Sample sizes ranged from 137 in the case–control study to 32 540 in one of the cohort studies. All studies were published between 2010 and 2025.

Among the 14 studies, 13 were based on large population cohorts, including the CHARLS, CLASS, Health and Ageing in Africa: Longitudinal Studies in South Africa, Longitudinal Ageing Study in India, Vietnam Health and Ageing Study, Guangzhou Biobank Cohort Study (GBCS) and the Chinese Longitudinal Health Longevity Survey. All studies included both males and females, with the percentage of females ranging from 48.3% in CLASS42 to 74.3% in the GBCS.48 The age range of participants in the included studies also ranged from ‘40 years and above’, ‘50 years and above’ to ‘85 years and above’.

The ACEs assessed encompassed various aspects of childhood adversities, including childhood experiences of hunger, death in the family (parental or sibling), experiences of violence (domestic violence, physical abuse, peer bullying), neglect (emotional neglect, lack of care/support and maltreatment), poor self-reported childhood health status, familial mental health or substance use, familial separation (parental separation and incarceration) and other adversities (subjective SES, parental disability, household dysfunction and unsafe neighbourhood). The most examined ACEs were parental death (n=4), neglect (n=4), poor childhood health status (self-reported) (n=3) and childhood hunger experience (n=3). Nine studies examined one ACE only, while the others examined multiple adversities. In terms of outcomes, 10 studies focused on continuous cognitive scores.234044 46 48 50 51 Among these, the most used cognitive assessment tool was the Mini–Mental State Examination (MMSE), followed by an adapted version of the Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS). Five studies used composite measures that summed scores from different tests for specific domains. Cognitive impairment as a binary outcome was assessed in four studies.39 45 47 49 None of the identified studies examined ADRD as an outcome.

Table 2 presents more detailed information about the characteristics of the ACE measures used in each study. Most studies (n=10) analysed ACE as a binary variable, with no information about its frequency or severity. Only one study measured experiencing hunger during childhood in three categories (never, once, or multiple times)41; one study measured subjective childhood SES along a 3-point Likert scale of good, average/fair or poor44; and two studies measured ACE as one parent or both parents deceased versus both parents alive.39 48 There are also differences in the time window of assessment of ACEs, ranging between ‘up to 10 years’42 and ‘up to 18 years’.23 48 Only two studies did not clearly define the age range over which the exposure to adversity was measured.40 45 Almost all studies (n=12) adjusted for age, gender and education; six adjusted for lifestyle factors such as physical activity and smoking; and seven adjusted for health-related conditions such as diabetes, stroke and heart disease. Four studies adjusted for maternal and paternal education and paternal occupation, and five studies adjusted for place of residence.

Risk of bias

Online supplemental table 2 presents the risk-of-bias assessment for cohort, cross-sectional and case–control studies. Overall, the included studies were of moderate to good methodological quality. Among cohort studies, the most frequently unclear items related to whether participants were free of the outcome at baseline and whether appropriate strategies were used to address incomplete follow-up. For cross-sectional studies, uncertainty most commonly concerned the use of objective or standardised outcome measurement criteria, while for case–control studies, it related primarily to whether the exposure period was sufficiently long to be meaningful.

ACEs and cognitive function

Figure 2 presents a summary of reported associations of ACEs and continuous cognitive function scores (figure 2). The most frequently studied ACEs in relation to cognitive function were parental/sibling death (n=3), neglect (n=3), poor self-reported childhood health status (n=3) and childhood hunger experience (n=3).

Descriptive forest plot for cognitive function. *Multiple estimates corresponding to different levels of an adversity from the same study. MMSE, Mini–Mental State Examination; SES, socioeconomic status, SPMSQ, Short Portable Mental Status Questionnaire.

Experiences of childhood hunger were assessed in three studies: Korinek et al found that persons exposed to hunger once (beta=−0.33) and multiple times (beta=−0.10) reported lower MMSE scores and Maharani reported lower TICS scores among those exposed to hunger in childhood.41 43 In both studies, however, associations were not statistically significant. On the other hand, Li et al51 reported a marginally significant relationship between childhood hunger experience and lower MMSE scores (beta=−13). Significant relationships were observed for parental death and lower cognitive function later in life: Li and Chen reported a significant association with lower short portable mental status questionnaire (SPMSQ) scores,42 and Zhang et al and Zhang et al reported significant associations with lower TICS (beta=−0.45) and MMSE (beta=−0.61) scores, respectively.23 48

Violence-related ACEs, including domestic violence (beta=−0.02), physical abuse (beta=−0.10) and peer-bullying (beta=−0.05), were not significantly associated with lower TICS scores.23 The results for neglect were mixed. Wang et al46 and Zhang et al reported a significant relationship between emotional neglect (beta=−0.68) and lack of care in childhood (beta=−0.44), respectively, with lower MMSE scores.46 48 In contrast, Zhang T. reported no significant relationship between neglect (beta=−0.11) and cognitive function.23

Results on the association between self-reported childhood health status and cognitive function were mixed: Kobayashi et al reported a significant association between poor childhood health (beta=−0.30) and lower composite cognitive scores40; Muhammad et al on the other hand, showed that reporting fair childhood health (beta=−60), but not poor health (beta=0.29), was significantly associated with lower composite cognitive scores.44 Zhang et al reported no significant association between poor childhood health and cognitive function.50 Only one study reported a significant association between mental health problems (beta=−0.67) and substance use (beta=−0.43) by a household member with lower TICS scores.23 Parental separation (beta=−0.75) was significantly associated with lower TICS scores, whereas incarceration of a household member was not (beta=−0.03).23 Similarly, Zhang et al showed a significant relationship between separation from the mother for more than a year and lower MMSE scores.48 Zhang et al reported a significant relationship between unsafe neighbourhood (beta=−0.54) but not for parental disability (beta=−0.13) with lower TICS scores. Muhammad et al reported lower cognitive scores among participants whose family’s socioeconomic status during their childhood was average (beta=−0.11, statistically insignificant) and poor (beta=−0.81, statistically significant), compared with well-off.44

The four studies assessing the relationship between childhood adversity and cognitive impairment included sample sizes ranging from 137 to 15 444 participants (table 1). The ACEs assessed across these studies included parental death, physical neglect, emotional abuse, maltreatment, household dysfunction and childhood hunger experience.39 45 47 49 All studies measured cognitive impairment using the MMSE. All investigated ACEs had a trend of higher odds for cognitive impairment, except for emotional abuse in Wang et al.45 Significant associations were observed for physical neglect (OR=1.23, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.41),45 maltreatment (OR=1.37, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.68).47 Childhood hunger experience was significantly associated with cognitive impairment among women (OR=1.27, 95% CI 1.12 to 1.44) but not men.49

Discussion

Our study examined the evidence on the association between individual ACEs and cognitive outcomes among older adults in LMICs. Our review revealed several major knowledge limitations and gaps. A key limitation concerns the populations studied, with the majority of available studies (n=12) coming from upper-middle-income countries such as China, South Africa and Indonesia, while evidence from low-income countries remains lacking. Another major gap was the absence of studies investigating the association of ACEs with ADRD risk in LMICs. For cognitive function and cognitive impairment in LMICs, our findings, for the most part, align with the overall pattern in the literature on HICs, indicating that exposure to ACEs is associated with a long-term risk of poorer cognitive outcomes. Our findings also highlight significant variability in the assessment of exposures and outcomes across studies. Moreover, we found that most studies (n=9) in LMICs do not examine key ACE characteristics, such as frequency or severity of exposure.

Regarding the individual ACEs, we found that among the 14 different types of ACEs investigated, ten encompassing different types of adversities showed associations with poorer cognitive scores or higher odds for cognitive impairment. Only five ACEs (childhood hunger experience, parental death, parental separation, poor health in childhood and emotional neglect) were reported in more than one study and two ACEs (childhood hunger experience and self-reported childhood health status) in more than one population. This highlights the need for reproducibility and for more investigations of local/contextual ACEs across populations and subgroups.

Different ACEs showed differential associations with cognitive outcomes. For example, violence-related ACEs (domestic violence, peer bullying and physical abuse) were not related to cognitive function, whereas parental death or separation and familial mental health/substance use problems showed more consistent associations with poorer cognitive function scores. Of note, because all the violence-related adversities came from one study, the findings may be characteristic of that sample and not representative.23 A previous study reported that violence-related ACEs may be more associated with physical and functional disabilities in later life, while parental death and neglect have a more detrimental impact on cognitive function.52 ACEs related to neglect were varying in their definitions across identified studies (encompassing emotional neglect, physical neglect, abuse/maltreatment); nonetheless, they showed a pattern of associations with poorer cognitive function and higher risk of cognitive impairment. Other ACEs such as childhood hunger experience and poor childhood health had more varying associations: poor childhood health was significantly associated with lower cognitive scores in two out of three studies; childhood hunger experience was marginally associated with cognitive function in one out of three studies but associated with higher odds of cognitive impairment in the women subsample in one study.49 These findings highlight the importance of investigating gender, age and socioeconomic subgroup differences in future studies.

Together, these results suggest that specific ACEs may have more important impact on cognitive health in older age, underscoring the value of investigating individual adverse exposures for a better understanding of risk factors and mechanisms. The relation of parental death in childhood with poorer cognitive outcomes may reflect the longer-term effects of psychological stress and financial repercussions that accompany the loss of a parent in childhood.53 This is particularly relevant in low-resource settings with weak or absent social protection systems. Relatedly, associations of ACEs indicating an absence or failure in meeting a child’s emotional and physical needs, such as parental separation, experiences of neglect and familial dysfunction and mental health/substance problems further reflect the importance of a nurturing and supportive environment in childhood in LMICs. Attention and responsiveness to children’s emotional needs facilitate the development of cognitive skills and learning coping strategies to deal with stress throughout their lifecourse.54 Again, this is particularly salient in resource-limited contexts that often lack comprehensive policies and infrastructure for child protection and social support. The relation of poorer subjective childhood SES with poorer cognitive function reported by Muhammad et al,44 also highlights the long-term impact of financial strains in childhood globally.55

Other explanations for the differential results of different ACEs and cognitive outcomes include methodological differences across studies, ranging from sample size to the definitions and assessments of ACEs and cognitive outcomes. However, we note that within the same studies, such as Zhang et al, Yuan et al and Wang et al, some ACEs were related to cognitive outcomes whereas others were not.23 45 47

Results of this review are largely consistent with the current body of knowledge, showing that ACEs are associated with poor cognitive function and higher odds of cognitive impairment.9 10 Similar to a previous systematic review of studies from HICs, deprivation-type adversities, including neglect and parental death, in our review showed a consistent association with poorer cognitive function.10 This consistency in findings across settings reinforces the life course relevance of early-life adversity for cognitive ageing globally. Our review also extended current evidence, identifying ACEs that are contextually salient in LMICs, such as childhood hunger experiences, which have not been previously reported in HIC studies and which emerged as one of the most assessed ACEs in LMIC studies.10 In addition, focusing the synthesis on LMICs underscored major gaps in the literature, including limited evidence from low-income countries and a lack of studies examining ADRD risk and longitudinal cognitive changes.

A key finding in our review is the lack of studies on the associations between ACEs and ADRD in LMICs, which contrasts with the large body of evidence from high-income countries.56 57 This could be potentially explained by the general scarcity and limitations in data on ADRD in LMICs, as well as methodological limitations in the availability of established ADRD cut-offs and diagnostic approaches in these populations, particularly in the context of observational studies. Other studies that have investigated associations with ADRD risk focused on cumulative ACEs or the presence of several adversities, for example, three or more ACEs when assessing the relationship between ACE and dementia, and not individual ACEs.9 58

Key ACE characteristics, such as severity and frequency of exposure, were not considered in most studies in LMICs, hindering investigations of lifecourse effects and mechanisms. Further, the variety in ACE and outcome measures and the lack of standardised reporting limited comparisons between ACEs, across countries and with other HICs. Finally, one study examining unsafe neighbourhoods in childhood showed a significant negative association with cognitive scores, highlighting the value of investigating broader environmental ACEs.23 This is particularly relevant for LMIC settings where periods of crises and instability can have important repercussions on neighbourhood safety and child development.

Our systematic review has several limitations. All studies included in this review utilised retrospective self-reports to measure ACEs. Though this is the typical approach in studies on ageing, it is prone to measurement and recall errors, particularly for exposures occurring decades ago. These errors may also be more frequent among older adults with possible cognitive and memory problems or due to social desirability bias, leading to inaccurate or under-reporting of adverse familial and childhood circumstances. Other study limitations concern the substantial heterogeneity in the measurement of cognitive outcomes and predictors, as well as the lack of reporting of standardised effect sizes in most studies measuring cognitive outcome as a continuous measure and key descriptive statistics. This posed significant limitations for comparing magnitudes of associations and for pooling effect sizes. Although included studies were generally of moderate quality, risk-of-bias assessment identified frequent uncertainty in key domains, largely due to incomplete reporting. This limited our ability to systematically assess the potential influence of attrition, baseline cognitive status and outcome measurement validity across studies. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the strength of observed associations. Finally, most of the studies included are based on research on ageing in China, an upper-middle-income country, which largely limits generalisability to diverse LMIC settings. The findings may therefore be more reflective of China’s reality than those of poorer nations, as contextual differences could influence the type and severity of the adverse experiences. This also constrains ability to assess potential heterogeneity by cultural or racial/ethnic backgrounds. We note that studies on adversities such as childhood hunger experience and childhood health assessed across different LMIC contexts showed results consistent with those reported in Chinese populations. Our findings are also consistent with global data on the effects of ACEs on health outcomes, including cognitive function.9 10

Despite the limitations, our systematic review adds to the current body of knowledge by providing a much-needed, although descriptive, synthesis of the evidence on the association between ACEs and cognition among older adults in LMICs. The consistency of our findings with the broader scholarship strengthens the current understanding that ACEs influence developmental processes and that their effects persist into adulthood, leading to poor cognitive function. Overall, our systematic review extended prior work by identifying consistent associations and highlighting the role of contextual ACEs, while underscoring future directions for rigorous and representative evidence from LMICs.

Recommendations and future directions

Research on ACE and cognition has received increasing attention in the last decade. However, our review shows that this topic remains largely understudied in LMICs. Results of reviewed studies indicate broader associations with several ACEs and poorer cognitive function, higher risk of cognitive impairment, and identify important ACEs showing consistent patterns of associations. The review also identified important gaps and future directions for improving both understanding and prevention of neurocognitive disorders. First, there is a pressing need for more investigations on ACEs and neurocognitive outcomes across different populations and subgroups, particularly underrepresented populations and lower-income countries. This would facilitate reproducibility of findings, comparisons across different settings (within LMICs and between LMICs and HICs), and identification of higher-risk groups. Second, future research would benefit from moving beyond binary definitions of adverse exposures to incorporating more detailed information on ACEs, such as their severity, frequency, duration and timing. Third, there is a need for more investment in LMICs in longitudinal study design and lifecourse approaches to delineate the mechanisms through which early adversity shapes cognitive health in older age and provide comprehensive assessments of the critical period and cumulative risk trajectories. There is also an important gap in improving the scope and assessments of cognitive outcomes. Investigating the associations of ACEs with ADRD risk, longitudinal cognitive changes and decline, and standardised and harmonised definitions of cognitive assessments across studies is critical for more robust and comprehensive evidence and for enhancing pooling and comparability of results across countries and populations. More detailed and improved reporting of both ACEs and cognitive outcomes investigated and analyses are also crucial to achieve these goals. Finally, examining environmental and contextual adversities, including experiences of conflict, displacement and societal crises, is particularly relevant for improving risk factor identification and informing targeted and successful interventions for cognitive health of older adults in LMICs.

Conclusions

This work provides a comprehensive review of the current evidence on the relation of individual ACEs and cognitive outcomes among older adults in LMICs. Identified studies suggest an association between ACEs and an increased risk of poorer cognitive function and impairment among older adults in LMICs, consistent with findings from high-income countries. However, the evidence remains scarce and heterogeneous. Importantly, most studies were conducted in upper-middle-income countries, and evidence from lower-income countries and certain world regions, such as the Middle East and North Africa or Latin America, remains limited. Moreover, the available findings suggest that specific ACEs, such as parental death and separation, neglect and familial challenges, may be more important for cognitive health in older age, whereas childhood hunger and violence experiences showed inconsistent or no associations. These results highlight the importance of broadening the populations studied and refining the scope, definitions and characteristics of ACEs studied to improve knowledge on early determinants of neurocognitive outcomes and their repercussions on lifecourse risk trajectories both locally and globally.
