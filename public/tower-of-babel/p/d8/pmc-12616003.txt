Sex-specific phosphoproteome responses to calorie restriction and insulin in skeletal muscle from older rats
Authors: Haiyan Wang, Søren Madsen, Elise J Needham, Sean J Humphrey, Amy Zheng, Edward B Arias, Jacqueline Stöckli, Harry B Cutler, David E James, Gregory D Cartee, Gustavo Duque
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractCalorie restriction (CR; calorie intake reduced by ∼20%-40% below ad libitum, AL, intake) potentiates skeletal muscle insulin sensitivity during old age by incompletely understood mechanisms. We aimed to identify CR-induced changes in muscle insulin signaling that may explain this enhanced sensitivity. We examined how CR (65% of AL intake for 8-weeks) alters muscle insulin action and signaling in aged rats (24-month old) of both sexes. We assessed insulin-stimulated glucose uptake (ISGU) in muscle together with deep phosphoproteomic profiling. CR enhanced ISGU in both sexes, with higher ISGU in females regardless of diet. We identified 590 diet-responsive phosphosites, indicating extensive CR-induced remodelling of muscle phosphorylation, particularly within structural and contractile pathways. Strikingly, 70% of these sites were sex-specific. Numerous insulin-responsive sites were identified (193 in females; 107 in males) with 60 overlapping sites. The magnitude of the insulin-effects among all significantly regulated sites correlated between sexes. S1443 phosphorylation on EH domain-binding protein 1-like protein-1 (Ehbp1l1; a potential regulator of Rab proteins that control GLUT4 glucose transporter trafficking) was insulin-responsive in both sexes but only associated to ISGU in females. Personalized phosphoproteomic analysis also identified insulin-responsive sites on Leiomodin-1 (Lmod1) that correlated with ISGU across individuals. Both Lmod1 and Ehbp1l1 have strong genetic association with glycemic traits in humans, reinforcing their translational relevance. This study revealed sex-dependent and sex-independent phosphosignaling mechanisms that associate with muscle insulin responsiveness as well as hundreds of sex-specific, CR-responsive phosphosites. These findings provide a rich resource for future research on CR and insulin sensitivity.Keywords: Glucose transport, Insulin resistance, mTOR, Ehpb1l1, Lmod1

Introduction

Moderate calorie restriction (CR; ∼20%-40% reduction in calorie intake compared to ad libitum, AL, intake) has been shown to influence many health-related processes in humans and other species.1–3 In particular, CR improves insulin sensitivity, a notable effect given that insulin resistance is a major risk factor for age-related diseases such as type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease. Because skeletal muscle plays a central role in whole-body insulin sensitivity, it is likely crucial for CR’s metabolic benefits. Indeed, increased insulin-stimulated glucose uptake (ISGU) in skeletal muscle is a hallmark of CR.4–7

Protein phosphorylation is a crucial regulatory mechanism in muscle insulin action, and several studies used immunoblotting to show that CR leads to changes in site-selective phosphorylation of proteins in insulin-stimulated muscle.4,8–12 However, these studies were limited in scope, assessing only 4-12 phosphosites, representing a narrow view, given that the proteome harbors thousands of phosphorylation events, with many hundreds modulated by insulin. Two recent studies used mass spectrometry-based phosphoproteomics analysis to assess thousands of phosphosites in muscle from C57BL/6J mice undergoing CR.13,14 While these studies observed CR-induced changes in the muscle phosphoproteome, insulin’s effect on global protein phosphorylation was not evaluated.

To unravel the mechanisms underlying improved insulin sensitivity following CR, we performed unbiased phosphoproteomic analyses in muscle from AL and CR rats in basal and insulin-stimulated conditions. Given that approximately 73% of Americans aged 65-years or older have diabetes or prediabetes,15 and a major health-related benefit of CR is improved insulin sensitivity in muscle,9 our central goal was to interrogate changes in insulin-dependent protein phosphorylation in muscle from both sexes with either CR or AL diets. Because Akt is a key, insulin-regulated kinase crucial for the full effects of CR on insulin sensitivity,11,16 muscles were also incubated with or without a selective Akt inhibitor. To gain novel functional insights, each muscle was analyzed for both ISGU and deep molecular phenotype by phosphoproteomics. We hypothesized that the muscle phosphoproteome from rats of each sex would be highly responsive to insulin and CR.

Methods

Materials

Chemicals used for muscle incubations were from Sigma-Aldrich (St Louis, MO) or Fisher Scientific (Hanover Park, IL) unless otherwise noted. [3H]-2-Deoxy-D-glucose ([3H]-2-DG), D-[14C]-mannitol, and liquid scintillation cocktail were from PerkinElmer (Boston, MA).

Animal treatment

Animal care procedures were approved by the University of Michigan Committee on Use and Care of Animals. Female (n = 16) and male (n = 16) Fischer-344 Brown Norway rats were obtained from the National Institute of Aging (NIA) at 22-month old. Animals were individually housed in specific pathogen-free conditions, maintained on a 12:12 h light-dark cycle (lights out at 1700 h). Rats had AL access to food (NIH 31 chow, Richmond, IN) and water for a one-week acclimation period with food intake determined daily. Following acclimation, animals were randomly assigned to AL (n = 8 per sex) or CR (n = 8 per sex) groups. AL rats had unlimited access to the NIH31 chow. CR rats were provided with NIH31/NIA fortified chow (Charles River Laboratories, Wilmington, MA). Fortified chow contained sufficient vitamin supplementation for CR rats to ingest vitamins to levels comparable to AL controls. CR rats within each sex were restricted to 65% of the daily intake of the sex-matched AL group for 8-weeks. Animals were provided their daily food allotment between 1530 h and 1630 h with food intake and body mass determined weekly. After 8-weeks, rats (24-month old) were euthanized, and the muscle incubation experiment was performed. A male rat in the AL group was euthanized at week-6 of the dietary protocol after losing ∼140 g of body mass. The remaining rats completed the dietary protocol, and their tissues were analyzed.

Tissue dissection and muscle incubation

Food was removed from the cages of all rats at 1900 h on the evening before the muscle experiment. While rats were deeply anesthetized (intraperitoneal injection of 50 mg/kg ketamine; 5 mg/kg xylazine), their epitrochlearis muscles were dissected out. The epitrochlearis muscle was chosen for study because its flat, thin structure makes it ideal for ex vivo incubation to assess insulin-stimulated glucose uptake and protein phosphorylation. Additionally, its fiber type profile closely resembles that of overall rat skeletal muscle,17,18 and CR produces similar effects on glucose uptake in the epitrochlearis as in the widely studied gastrocnemius muscle.9 A retroperitoneal fat pad was dissected and weighed. Each muscle was longitudinally split into two similarly sized strips that were placed in vials, including appropriate media, shaken (45-revolutions/minute) and gassed (95% O2-5% CO2) in a heated (35 °C) water bath. Muscles were incubated in vials containing 2 mL of Krebs-Henseleit buffer (KHB) plus BSA (0.1%), 2 mM sodium pyruvate, 6 mM mannitol, and either dimethyl sulfoxide (vehicle) or MK-2206 (Akt inhibitor, AKTi; 0.5 μM) for 20-min. Muscles were transferred to a vial containing identical buffer as the preceding step, with or without insulin (1.2 nM) for 20-min. Muscles were transferred to a vial containing 2 mL KHB/BSA, the same concentration of AKTi and insulin as the previous step, 2 mM sodium pyruvate, 0.1 mM 2-DG (including [3H]-2-DG), and 6 mM mannitol (including [14C]-mannitol) for 20-min. After this step, muscles were rapidly blotted, trimmed, freeze-clamped, cooled in liquid nitrogen, and stored (−80 °C) until processed.

Muscle sample preparation and phosphopeptide enrichment

Frozen muscles were processed as described.19 Briefly, frozen muscles were ground on liquid nitrogen with a mortar and pestle, lysed in 200 μL 6 M GdmCl/100 mM Tris pH 8.5 and sonicated (5-min, 90% amplitude, 2/5-s pulses, 4 °C) in a Q800R2 sonicator (QSonica). Lysates were boiled immediately (95 °C, 5-min), and further sonicated for 1-min (90% amplitude, 5/5-s pulses, 4 °C), then, centrifuged (20 000 g for 5-min). Lysate supernatants were reduced and alkylated using 10 mM TCEP/40 mM CAA (5-min, 45 °C followed by 40-min at room temperature). Protein was precipitated using chloroform/methanol (1.25 volumes of 1:4). Upon protein precipitation, equal volumes of the upper aqueous phase were collected and stored for subsequent determination of 2-deoxyglucose and mannitol (below). Protein pellets were washed with methanol, and resolubilized with 6 M GdmCl/100 mM Tris pH 8.5 by sonication. Samples were diluted 1:1 with water, and protein was re-precipitated with four-volumes of acetone (−30 °C overnight). Precipitated protein was resuspended in SDC buffer (4% SDC/100 mM Tris pH 8.5), and an aliquot was taken to determine protein content by BCA assay. All samples were diluted to equal protein amounts (700 μg in 300 µL SDC buffer) and processed according to the EasyPhos workflow.20 Briefly, proteins were digested with Lys-C and trypsin (1:100 w/w enzyme:protein) overnight at 37 °C with shaking (1500 rpm). To the digested peptides, 400 µL of isopropanol was added and mixed thoroughly (1500 rpm, 30 s). 100 µL EP enrichment buffer (48% TFA/8 mM potassium dihydrogen phosphate) was added to each sample and mixed thoroughly (1500 rpm, 30 s). Samples were centrifuged (2000 × g, 15 min) and supernatants transferred to a 2 mL deep-well plate. Phosphopeptides were enriched in parallel in 96-well plate format by the addition of 8 mg TiO2 beads (Titansphere Phos-TiO bulk, 10 µm, GL Sciences) resuspended in 8 µL of EP loading buffer (6% trifluoracetic acid, 80% acetonitrile), and incubated at 40 °C for 5 min with shaking (2000 rpm). Beads were pelleted (2000 × g, 1 min) and supernatant containing non-phosphopeptides was removed and discarded. Beads were washed five times with 1 mL EP wash buffer (5% trifluoracetic acid, 60% isopropanol) with mixing (2000 rpm, 30 s), pelleting after each wash (2000 × g, 1 min) and discarding the supernatant wash. After the final wash beads were resuspended in 75 µL EP transfer buffer (0.1% trifluoracetic acid, 60% isopropanol) and transferred to the top of a C8 StageTips.21 A further 75 µL of EP transfer buffer was added to each well and transferred to the C8 StageTip to ensure all beads were captured. Using an in-house 3D printed 96-well StageTip centrifuge device,22 each StageTip was spun through to dryness (1500 × g, 8 min). Phosphopeptides were subsequently eluted from the beads retained on C8 StageTips by the sequential addition of 2 × 60 µL EP elution buffer (200 µL 25% ammonia solution added to 800 µL of 40% acetonitrile) with centrifugation to dryness after each (1500 × g, 3 min), and phosphopeptides were collected into clean PCR strip-tubes. Immediately, the phosphopeptides in PCR tubes were lyophilized using a centrifuge vacuum concentrator (Eppendorf) at 45 °C for 30 min, ensuring samples were not completely dry (∼10 µL volume remained). To the partially-dried samples, 100 µL of styrenedivinylbenzene-reverse phase sulfonated (SDB-RPS) loading buffer (1% trifluoracetic acid in isopropanol) was added, and each sample transferred directly to a SDB-RPS StageTip for desalting.21 StageTips were centrifuged at 1500 × g for 3 min, and washed with 100 µL of each 1% trifluoracetic acid in ethylacetate, followed by 1% trifluoracetic acid in isopropanol, and then, 0.2% trifluoracetic acid/5% acetonitrile. Phosphopeptides were finally eluted with 60 µL of 5% ammonium hydroxide/80% acetonitrile, dried by vacuum concentration, and resuspended in MS loading buffer (2% acetonitrile, 0.3% trifluoracetic acid) prior to analysis by LC-MS/MS.

2-Deoxyglucose uptake calculations

After sample processing, the aqueous phase underwent scintillation counting (Tri-Carb 2810R Liquid Scintillation Counter; PerkinElmer) to determine [3H]-2-deoxyglucose (2-DG) and [14C]-mannitol counts. The [3H]-2-DG dpm values were adjusted for estimated loss due to quenching from sample processing, extracellular volume (using [14C]-mannitol), and volume counted, followed by normalization to muscle weight. Intracellular [3H]-2-DG dpm/mg values, referred to hereafter as glucose uptake (GU), were used for further analysis. Delta-insulin GU was calculated (GU of muscles incubated with insulin minus GU of paired muscles incubated without insulin).

LC-MS/MS measures

Enriched phosphopeptides were resuspended in 5 µL MS loading buffer (2% ACN, 0.3% TFA) and loaded onto in-house-fabricated, 50-cm columns with a 75-μm I.D, packed with 1.9 μM C18 ReproSil Pur AQ particles. Peptides were separated using a Dionex U3000 HPLC coupled to an Orbitrap Exploris 480 mass spectrometer (Thermo Fisher Scientific), with column temperature maintained at 60 °C using a column oven (Sonation). Peptides were eluted with a binary buffer system comprising 0.1% formic acid (buffer A) and 80% ACN plus 0.1% formic (buffer B) with a flow rate of 400 nL min−1, over a gradient of 3%-19% buffer B in 40-min, followed by 19%-41% buffer B over 20-min (1-h gradient elution time). Peptides were analyzed in Data Independent Acquisition (DIA) mode, with one MS1 full-scan (350-1400 m/z, R = 120 000) at a target of 3 × 106 ions, followed by 48 DIA MS/MS scans (350-1022 m/z) with HCD (target 3 × 106 ions, maximum injection time 22-ms, isolation window 14 m/z, 1 m/z window overlap, NCE 25%). MS2 fragment ions were detected in the Orbitrap (R = 15 000).

RAW data analysis

Raw data were analyzed using Spectronaut (15.4.210913.50606). Data were searched with directDIA using the Rat UniProt Reference Proteome database (UP000002494_10116, March 2022 release), with default settings (precursor and protein Q value cutoffs 0.01, Q value filtering, MS2 quantification). For post-translational modifications, “PTM localization” was enabled with the Phospho (STY) search option. The PTM “Probability cutoff” was set to 0 and localization filtering was performed during downstream analysis, where only class I phosphosites (localization probability > 0.75) were retained for analysis. Peptide collapse was performed within Spectronaut (with PTM consolidation “Sum”).

Statistical analysis

Body mass, fat pad mass, food intake, and GU analysis

Body mass, fat pad mass, and food intake of AL and CR groups were compared for each sex using Student’s t-test. For GU, multilevel mixed-effects linear regression analysis was performed with Stata/SE 18.0 statistical software (Stata Corporation) because the results derived for paired muscles from the same rat are inherently correlated. Two-way analysis of variance with main effects of Diet and Sex was used for comparisons of delta-insulin GU. A two-tailed Student's t-test was used for two-group comparisons. Values are presented as means ±SD. p-values < .05 were considered statistically significant.

Phosphoproteome analysis

Phosphopeptide intensity values were log2 transformed and median normalized. Imputation was performed separately on the female and male experiments, and within diet groups. Phosphopeptides were filtered for sufficient missingness consistent with abundances being lower than the limit of detection, rather than missingness at random. Block imputation was performed, imputing if quantifications of a phosphopeptide was missing in the basal, basal + AKTi or insulin + AKTi condition but present in the insulin condition, or if quantifications of a phosphopeptide were missing in the insulin condition but present in any other condition. Present was defined as ≥70% samples with a quantified value in that condition, and absent was quantified as completely missing, or if phosphopeptides were quantified in fewer than three samples and had a lower mean than the site in the other conditions. These conditions were selected because imputation was performed for a down-shifted distribution assuming missingness that was not-at-random. Values were downshift imputed by randomly sampling from a normal distribution with a mean = minimum (site value) − 1.6*standard deviation (site value) and standard deviation = 0.6*standard deviation (site value).

For statistical analysis, phosphopeptides were filtered for at least three rats in each diet group with fully quantified values in the basal (neither insulin nor AKTi), insulin and insulin + AKTi conditions. A multilevel linear model was calculated, modelling the rat and experimental day as a random effect, insulin as a within-subject factor and diet as a between-subject factor, and the interaction between insulin and diet with the nlme R package (https://cran.r-project.org/package=nlme). To find phosphopeptides sensitive for Akt inhibition, we compared sites that were different between muscles incubated with insulin alone and muscles incubated with insulin + AKTi, using a paired t-test in both males and females.

Phosphopeptide values were correlated with ISGU measures. Correlations were performed on insulin-stimulated samples, separately for females and males. As values were not normally distributed, Kendall’s rank correlation was performed. Only sites that showed a suggestive main effect of insulin or diet, or a suggestive interaction effect (defined as unadjusted p < .01) were included for correlation analysis to ISGU. To combine the phosphoproteome from males and females, sex was regressed out using lm base function in R and correlating the residuals to ISGU. p-values were adjusted for multiple hypothesis testing with the q-value method.23

Kinase enrichment was performed with ksea R package.24 All enrichment was done on human phosphosites as the human phoshoproteome is well annotated for substrate/kinase relationships. Rat-to-human phosphosites orthologs conversion was done by PTMoreR.25 Kinase substrate annotation was collated from PhosphositePlus26 acquired through the R package PhosR27 with the function data (PhosphoSitePlus). Annotations for Akt kinase isoforms (Akt1, Akt2 and Akt3) and catalytic AMPK subunit (Prkaa1 and Prkaa2) were merged. Enrichment of phosphopeptides on proteins were done with ClusterProfiler28 with Gene Ontology (GO) terms. Insulin proteins were defined as all genes contained within the GO term “cellular response to insulin stimulus (GO: 0032869).” We utilized Synteny29 to look up human genetic evidence on rat proteins where the phosphosites correlated with ISGU. When visualizing phosphopeptide intensities, we used “removeBatchEffect” from the limma R package30 to remove batch effects purely for visualization purposes.

Results

To understand how CR influences muscle insulin sensitivity, we measured ex vivo ISGU and performed phosphoproteomics in muscle from old rats (22-months) after 8-weeks of either AL or CR feeding (Figure 1). Rats were randomly assigned to AL or CR groups before the 8-week CR period. Because the serine/threonine-kinase Akt is a core component of the insulin signaling pathway,31 we included a condition in which muscles were incubated with an Akt inhibitor (AKTi).

Experimental design. Animals (females and males Fischer-344 Brown Norway rats; initially 22-month old) were randomly assigned to the ad libitum (AL) or calorie restriction (CR; 65% of AL food intake for 8 weeks) diet groups. Each of the two epitrochlearis muscles from each rat were longitudinally bisected into two strips, yielding 4 muscle strips per rat. For each rat, one muscle strip was incubated under each of these 4 conditions: without AKTi or insulin; with AKTi and without insulin; without AKTi and with insulin; with AKTi and with insulin. Muscles underwent a 3-step incubation (60 min total duration) with [3H]-2-Deoxy-D-glucose ([3H]-2DG) included in the final incubation step. Muscles were subsequently processed and analyzed for 2DG uptake and phosphoproteomics. AKTi, Akt inhibitor; BSA, bovine serum albumin; FBN, Fischer-344 Brown Norway; KHB, Krebs Henseleit Buffer. The figure was created with BioRender.com.

Body mass, fat pad mass, epitrochlearis mass, and food intake

The mean daily food intake of CR rats during the 8-week CR period was ∼65% of the value for AL rats of each sex (Figure 2A). There were no significant differences in initial body mass between the rats randomly assigned to the different diet groups, for either females (AL =251.7 ± 22.4 g vs CR = 253.5 ± 18.5 g) or males (AL = 487.5 ± 29.3 g vs CR = 506.1 ±28.9 g). As expected, final body mass and retroperitoneal fat pad mass were lower for CR versus AL rats of each sex (Figure 2A). The epitrochlearis mass was significantly greater (p < .05) in AL rats than in CR rats for males (AL = 146.1 ± 17.4 mg vs CR = 125.8 ± 12.8 mg), and there was a statistically non-significant (p = .21) CR-related trend in females (AL = 87.9 ± 15.6 mg vs CR = 78.4 ±13.4 mg).

CR and AKT inhibition affect muscle ISGU

Insulin led to the expected increase in GU versus paired unstimulated muscles, and ISGU was greater for CR versus AL rats of each sex (p < .05; Figure 2B and C). Consistent with earlier studies,5,11 the AKTi resulted in reduced ISGU regardless of diet or sex (p < .05; Figure 2B and C). These results revealed that, irrespective of sex, there was a CR-induced increase in delta-insulin GU, and irrespective of diet, delta-insulin GU was greater for females versus males (Figure 2D).

Skeletal muscle phosphoproteomics

We used the EasyPhos workflow20 in the same muscle as used for ISGU to explore the molecular underpinning of CR on insulin sensitivity. We quantified 8762 phosphopeptides across all samples, corresponding to 6827 unique phosphosites from 1296 phosphoproteins (Figure 3A). For robust comparisons across conditions, we retained phosphopeptides that were quantified in at least three samples within any given condition, resulting in a high-confidence dataset of 7430 phosphopeptides (hereafter referred to as phosphosites; Figure 3A). Dimension reduction revealed that the data clustered by sex and by experimental day (Figure S1A). Within these clusters, individual rats tended to group together more so than by treatment (Figure 3B).

To explore the insulin response at a molecular level, we fitted a linear mixed-effects model taking advantage of the paired nature of our experimental setup, while correcting for experimental day, and tested for main effect of insulin (q-value < 0.05). This resulted in 240 insulin-regulated phosphosites on 92 proteins across males and females. Females had more significantly regulated sites than males with 60 insulin-responsive sites shared in both sexes (Figure 3C). Insulin-regulated sites were enriched with sites found on canonical insulin signaling proteins (Figure 3D) and the majority of these (65% in females and 91% in males) were increased with insulin (Figure 3C). This included a range of key intermediates in the insulin action cascade: Akt2-S474, Tbc1d4-S350 (AS160), Tbc1d4-S579, Akt1s1-T247, Flnc-S2237 (filamin C; a striated muscle-specific protein that cross-links F-actin), Irs1-S325, and Irs1-S343 (Figure 3E and F). Insulin-responsive sites showed remarkably similar behavior between diets within the same sex, with highly correlated magnitudes of change (r = 0.92, p = 3.2 e − 54 in females and r = 0.86, p = 9.7 e − 15 in males, Figure S1B). Although many sites were only significantly regulated in one sex, the magnitude of change of these sites was significantly correlated between sexes (r = 0.51, p = 2.68 e − 13, Figure 3G). The absolute fold-change of insulin-responsive sites, either uniquely regulated in one sex or shared between sexes, was significantly greater in female muscle compared to male, which may help explain the greater ISGU observed in females (Figure 3H; Figure S1C). Several sites on canonical insulin proteins were only significantly regulated in females, such as Tbc1d4 (S600, S609, T761), Tsc2 (tuberin sclerosis complex 2; S1130, S1132, T1466), and Irs1 (S340, S325, S522, T346; Figure 3E and F). Insulin also increased phosphorylation on both catalytic subunit isoforms of AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) only in females: Prkaa1 (AMPK catalytic subunit α1; S496), Prkaa2 (AMPK catalytic subunit α2; S377). Insulin was also recently reported to increase phosphorylation of these AMPK sites in muscles from young men.19

Some insulin-responsive phosphosites that were only significantly regulated in males included well-known insulin-responsive proteins: Gys1 (Y636, S649), Tbc1d4 (S353, S675), and Akt1s1 (S212, S213). Insulin also increased phosphorylation of Nos1 (nitric oxide synthase 1; S280), which catalyzes the production of nitric oxide.

AKTi treatment significantly inhibited ISGU (Figure 2B and C), and as we previously reported,32,33 the phosphoproteomic changes reflected the specific involvement of Akt in the insulin signaling network. Phosphorylation of Akt2 (S474/S478) was reduced by ∼25-fold, and phosphorylation of canonical Akt substrates including Akt1s1-T247 (∼3-fold) and Tbc1d4-S350/S579 (∼50%) was also reduced, confirming suppression of Akt signaling (Figure S1D). In total, 70 sites were significantly different between insulin and insulin + AKTi, with ∼90% decreased in the AKTi condition. These sites showed no further enrichment on canonical insulin signaling proteins compared to all insulin-responsive sites (Figure 3D), and the majority (∼75%) were changed by ≤50%. Of the 240 insulin-responsive sites identified across sexes, 61 were also AKTi-sensitive (25%), consistent with our previous findings in adipocytes (∼21%).32 Thus, while Akt is required for the insulin effect on glucose uptake, only a subset of insulin responsive phosphorylation events in skeletal muscle are directly Akt-dependent.

Effects of CR on the phosphoproteome

To determine how CR influences signaling in muscle, we quantified the main effect of diet on phosphoproteomic profiles in males and females. In total, 590 sites on 189 proteins were significantly regulated by diet. Males had significantly more diet-responsive sites (387 sites) than females (293 sites; Fisher’s exact test, p = .0003) and only 30% of the sites were regulated in both sexes (Figure 4A). Unlike insulin-regulated sites, diet-responsive sites were not enriched for canonical insulin signaling proteins (Figure 4B). Instead, they were predominantly enriched in pathways associated with muscle fiber structure and contraction, indicating that CR substantially remodels the muscle phosphoproteome in ways closely tied to muscle physiology (Figure 4C).

Although the overlap of diet-responsive sites between the sexes was modest, the magnitude of change (CR vs AL) of all responsive sites was significantly correlated (r = 0.58, p = 2.28 e− 54; Figure 4D). Notably, the direction of the diet-effect (ie, increased or decreased in CR) was consistent across sexes for all significant diet-responsive phosphosites. Among these, the most strongly CR-decreased sites included Neb-T6489 (nebulin, an actin-stabilizing protein) and multiple sites on Hrc (S125, S131, S141, S152, S538, S601, S605), a regulator of calcium dynamics via the sarcoplasmic reticulum.34 The most prominent CR-increased shared sites included Myom2-Y1401 (myomesin 2, which stabilizes the M-band) and Actn2-S431 (α-actinin 2, which anchors actin and titin to Z-discs).

The diet-responsive, female-specific sites with the greatest changes included Neb-T3079 and T3565, while the top male-specific increase was observed on Hadh-S13, a mitochondrial β-oxidation enzyme. CR in males also led to increased phosphorylation of Sod1-S108 and S112, a key antioxidant enzyme. We next examined the predicted upstream kinases associated with diet-mediated phosphorylation. In males, the diet-responsive phosphoproteome showed negative enrichment for CDK5 and positive enrichment for PRKACA (Figure 4E). CDK5 is a proline-directed serine/threonine-kinase often linked to stress responses and impaired insulin signaling in metabolic tissues.35 Its negative enrichment suggests that CR may suppress detrimental signaling pathways in aged male muscle. In contrast, PRKACA, the catalytic subunit of protein kinase A, supports energy metabolism and mitochondrial function,36 and its positive enrichment may reflect improved nutrient sensing and muscle adaptation. In females, CR led to strong positive enrichment of AKT and SGK1, which are both linked to insulin signaling. These results suggest that while CR-induced phosphoproteome remodeling is enriched for core muscle-related proteins rather than canonical insulin signaling components, it nonetheless enhances insulin-related pathways in aged female muscle. Together, these findings suggest that CR activates distinct kinase-driven pathways in male and female muscle, potentially reflecting sex-specific adaptations to metabolic stress.

Notably, only four diet-responsive sites were also sensitive to acute Akt inhibition, all of which were observed in male muscle. These diet-responsive sites included insulin receptor substrate 1 (Irs1) S343 and S352, Maf1-S75 (a transcriptional repressor involved in RNA polymerase III regulation), and Xirp2-S2897 (a muscle-enriched protein associated with actin remodeling; Figure 4F). Although limited in number, these overlapping sites may represent points of convergence between nutrient sensing and insulin signaling.

Overall, these findings suggest that CR remodels the muscle phosphoproteome in a manner that is largely distinct from acute insulin signaling, with sex-specific patterns and a strong emphasis on structural and contractile proteins.

Paired analysis of the phosphoproteome and ISGU

Because CR enhanced ISGU in both sexes, we next sought to determine how it modulates the insulin-regulated phosphoproteome. Specifically, we quantified the interactive effects of diet and insulin through the mixed linear model. Surprisingly, only two phosphosites in female muscle exhibited a statistically significant interaction (q < 0.1): Akap2 S122 and Ktn1 S77 (Figure S2A and B), however, the pattern did not reflect the CR-enhanced ISGU. No significant interaction sites were detected in males. Although few sites showed significant interactions, kinase enrichment analysis on the nominal fold-changes between insulin-stimulated samples revealed greater Akt activity with CR compared to AL (Figure S2C). These findings indicate that while global interaction effects on the phosphoproteome are modest, CR may prime or enhance specific aspects of insulin signaling through increased Akt pathway activity.

Given the limited number of sites showing significant interaction between discrete groups, we applied our recently described personalized phosphoproteomics approach19 to better capture individual-level signaling variation associated with ISGU. This approach leverages individual variability in signaling and ISGU to identify phosphosites linked to insulin sensitivity. It enables discovery of functionally relevant sites that are missed by standard group comparisons. This analysis revealed that 31 sites on 18 proteins correlated significantly with ISGU (25 of 31 were also insulin responsive in at least one sex) and 87% of these had a positive association (Figure 5A and B). Only 4 of these sites, Tbc1d4 S350 and Tbc1d4 S353 on female muscle; Akt2 S474 and Akt2 S478 in males, were on canonical insulin signaling proteins. Furthermore, only three sites, Akt1s1-T247, Nek9-T332, and Lmod1 S511, correlated significantly with ISGU in both females (Figure S2D) and males (Figure S2E) with the latter two also insulin-responsive in females (Figure 3E). Akt1s1-T247 is an Akt substrate, and phosphorylation on this site induces Akt1s1’s dissociation from mTORC1, thereby leading to greater mTORC1 activity.37 Nek9 (NIMA-related kinase 9) is a serine/threonine kinase with multiple functions, including regulation of mitosis. Lmod1 (Leiomodin-1) is an actin-binding protein primarily involved in the regulation of actin filament nucleation and organization in muscle cells.38

The remaining 28 of 31 sites only showed significant correlation in one sex. This included phosphorylation at S1443 on Ehbp1l1 (epidermal growth factor receptor substrate 15 homology domain-binding protein 1-like 1), which was significantly increased in response to insulin in both sexes but significantly correlated with ISGU only in females (females: τ = 0.46, q = 0.018; males: τ = 0.24, q = 0.235; Figure 5A and B). Members of the Ehbp family are proposed to be Rab effector proteins that can regulate vesicle trafficking with high specificity for Rab8 family members.39 Given that females exhibit a greater response to insulin in terms of ISGU, this target is of relevance as a sex-specific regulator.

Phosphorylation of Jph2 (junctophilin 2) on either S462 or T453 was positively correlated with ISGU in females (τ = 0.49, q = 0.014; Figure 5A). Although neither of these sites was significantly correlated with ISGU in males (τ = 0.20, q = 0.14), another site on Jph2 (T230) was among the top 10 most insulin-responsive sites that were uniquely identified in AL-males. Notably, Jph2 tethers the sarcoplasmic reticulum to the sarcolemma and is implicated in normal transverse tubule structure and calcium flux in striated muscle.40 Jph2 can bind and be phosphorylated by Speg, a serine/threonine-kinase, but it is unclear if either S462 or T453 is phosphorylated by Speg. Other sites that were insulin-responsive and correlated with ISGU only in females included Tacc2-S2529 (females: τ = −0.45, q = 0.021; males: τ = −0.11, q = 0.44) and Gys1-S645 (females: τ = 0.47, q = 0.014; males: τ = 0.11, q = 0.44). Notably, two of the five phosphosites with negative correlations only in males were also located on Gys1, namely S641 (males: τ = −0.42, q = 0.029; females: τ = −0.20, q = 0. 26, and S649 (males: τ = −0.49, q = 0.01; femlaes: τ = −0.16, q = 0. 33; Figure 5A). This finding is exciting as Gys1 is the rate-limiting enzyme for glycogen synthesis in skeletal muscle and Gys1 activity is negatively regulated by phosphorylation of these two sites by glycogen synthase kinase (GSK3).41 Site-directed mutagenesis experiments indicated that phosphorylation on S641 (also known as site 3a) strongly inhibits Gys1 activity, and that phosphorylation on S649 (also known as site 3c) facilitates S641 phosphorylation.41,42

To further explore upstream signaling differences associated ISGU, we performed kinase enrichment analysis using the correlation between phosphosite abundance and ISGU. This analysis revealed sex-specific enrichment patterns: in females, SGK1, RPS6KA1, MAPK14, MAPK3, MAPK12, mTOR, and PRKCA were positively enriched, suggesting coordinated activation of signaling pathways that support glucose metabolism and muscle remodeling (Figure 5C). Notably, mTOR, a central regulator of nutrient sensing and anabolic signaling, was specifically enriched in females. This observation may reflect enhanced anabolic signaling pathways that accompany the greater ISGU observed in females and aligns with prior reports that mTOR signaling is impaired in insulin resistance.43 Consistent with this idea, mTOR enrichment did not reach significance in males, which also had lower ISGU, further supporting a link between mTOR activity and insulin responsiveness in skeletal muscle. In contrast, male muscles showed positive enrichment of Akt and SGK1, but also a negative enrichment of CDK1, a kinase often associated with stress and cell cycle regulation (Figure 5C). These findings point to divergent upstream signaling mechanisms that may underlie sex-specific metabolic responses to insulin in muscle.

To increase power for detecting phosphosites associated with ISGU, we combined data from males and females after correcting the phosphoproteome for sex-effects using a linear model (Figure S2F). This integrated analysis found 25 of the 31 sites identified in the sex-specific analysis and an additional 24 sites associated to ISGU, where 50% of all significant sites were sensitive to AKTi (Figure 5D). Kinase enrichment analysis reflected the integrated nature of this approach, with substrates of Akt and mTOR significantly enriched among ISGU-associated sites (Figure 5E).

Given that many phosphosites displayed sex-specific correlations with ISGU and that ISGU was markedly higher in females than males, we next determined whether these sites show significant sex-differences. We performed a two-way ANOVA analysis on the 55 phosphosites correlated with ISGU. This analysis was restricted to the insulin-stimulated muscles to identify sex-dependent differences in insulin signaling independent of baseline variation. We identified nine sites with a significant main effect of sex, indicating consistent differences between males and females in insulin-stimulated muscles. Notably, several of these sites, such as Akt1s1 T247, Akt2 S474/S478, and Ehbp1l1 S1443, reside on canonical insulin signaling components or proteins implicated in insulin action. Additionally, the sites mentioned above on Jph2 (S462, T453) and its kinase Speg (T2495), also displayed sex-specific phosphorylation differences in the insulin-stimulated state. These findings point to a coordinated, sex-dependent regulation of this signaling axis during insulin stimulation and may provide key mechanistic insight into the enhanced insulin responsiveness observed in female muscle.

Integrating candidate genes from genome-wide association studies of relevant phenotypes with phosphoproteomics data has proven successful in prioritizing key targets.43 We utilized data from Association to Function Knowledge Portal,44 an open access database of genetic and functional data. This database utilizes a Human Genetic Evidence (HuGE) score that ranks genes by the strength of their genetic association. Of all proteins that had sites correlating with ISGU, LMOD1 had very strong genetic association to Hemoglobin A1C, a strong association to Type 2-diabetes and a moderate association to fasting C-peptide levels (Figure 5F). Furthermore, NOS1 and NEB showed strong associations with T2D, PLEC with HbA1c, and EHBP1L1 with T2D adjusted for BMI (T2DadjBMI) (Figure 5F). These findings illustrate the power of integrating personalized phosphoproteomics and candidates from human genetics studies to uncover novel, functionally relevant mediators of insulin sensitivity. The robust genetic links of LMOD1 and EHBP1L1 to glycemic traits emphasized their potential as important targets in muscle insulin sensitivity.

Together, these findings highlight the value of integrating personalized phosphoproteomics with human genetic and physiological data to uncover conserved, functionally relevant regulators of ISGU. Lmod1 stands out as a genetically linked, muscle-enriched candidate with strong associations to glycemic traits and relevance in human skeletal muscle. Ehbp1l1 also emerges as a promising target, showing insulin-responsive phosphorylation and a conserved association with glucose uptake following insulin-sensitising perturbation in a sex-specific manner at least in rat muscle. These insights point to new molecular mediators of insulin sensitivity in skeletal muscle and provide a foundation for future mechanistic studies.

Discussion

CR profoundly rewired the muscle phosphoproteome under basal and insulin-stimulated conditions. Most notably, the changes observed under these two conditions were distinct indicating that these two states describe unique physiological outputs. CR-modulated sites were enriched on structural muscle proteins, suggesting that CR may substantially influence muscle function. In response to insulin, CR-modified sites were enriched on canonical signaling proteins, such as Akt. Notably, insulin-stimulated phosphoproteome exhibited a significant sex-effect, with females showing both a greater number of sites and absolute fold-changes. CR also induced phosphosite changes enriched for Akt activity specifically in females. These findings may contribute to the greater ISGU seen in females. Taking advantage of the marked variation in insulin sensitivity across groups, we next used our recently described personalised phosphoproteomics19 and identified numerous phosphosites that were significantly correlated with insulin action. Intriguingly, genes encoding several of these proteins are significantly associated with human metabolic disease traits.

This study revealed 240 insulin-regulated phosphosites in rat muscle, many of which overlap with those found in human muscle.43 The insulin-responsive sites included proteins known to be part of the canonical insulin signaling pathway (eg, IRS1 and Akt, as well as Akt substrates) that plays an integral role in ISGU (Tbc1d4) and protein synthesis (Akt1s1). The muscle phosphoproteome was profoundly affected by CR. Most notably, the CR induced changes in the phosphoproteome were completely distinct from those observed during insulin stimulation. Whereas the insulin-regulated phosphoproteome was enriched in sites on canonical insulin signaling proteins, this was not the case for the basal phosphoproteome. In the latter case, we observed a marked modification of phosphorylation of proteins involved in actin biology, including Nebulin and alpha-actinin. These data suggest that, in addition to modulating insulin sensitivity, CR likely alters muscle structure and/or contractile function.

Because CR was imposed over weeks rather than minutes, we also considered whether the observed diet-responsive phosphoproteome might simply reflect altered protein abundance. If phosphorylation changes were primarily driven by differences in protein levels, one would expect coordinated regulation across all sites within a given protein. Instead, we observed heterogeneous site-specific behavior; for example, 136 sites were detected on Speg, varying by ∼2000-fold in signal intensity across individuals in the basal state. Moreover, across multiple CR studies, protein abundance was largely stable or changed only modestly (∼10%-20%), far less than the magnitude of many phosphorylation changes.5,8–10,45,46 Importantly, studies directly comparing phosphopeptide and protein levels show that although protein abundance can influence phosphopeptide signals, it does not fully account for them, as distinct sites within the same protein often behave differently.14 Collectively, these findings indicate that while protein expression changes may contribute in part, they are unlikely to explain the bulk of CR-induced alterations in the muscle phosphoproteome.

To explore links to muscle insulin sensitivity, we leveraged inter-individual variation in ISGU and applied our personalized phosphoproteomics approach. Phosphorylation of Akt1s1-T247 was significantly correlated to ISGU in each sex. This site is homologous to Akt1s1-T246 in humans, which has previously been associated with insulin action in clamp studies.19 Experimental modulation of Akt1s1 levels in primary human myotubes supports a causal role for this protein in ISGU.47 Moreover, phosphorylation of Akt1s1-T247 promotes mTORC1 activity, suggesting that increased mTORC1 signaling may enhance insulin sensitivity. Supporting this idea, multiple insulin-responsive phosphosites predicted to be mTORC1 targets, including Nos1-S847, Tsc2-S1130, Irs1-S325 (females), and Speg-S2458 (males), were also positively correlated with ISGU, which may further suggest a potential role for mTORC1 in enhanced insulin sensitivity. Notably, the Akt1s1-T247 site showed significant sex-effects and may play a role in the enhanced insulin-sensitivity observed in females in addition to a role in CR-mediated ISGU improvements. While this finding may contradict the traditional view of mTORC1 as a negative regulator of insulin sensitivity, several recent human studies43,48 also support a positive role for mTORC1 in muscle insulin sensitivity. This mounting evidence warrants further investigation of this potential role of mTORC1 and the mechanisms surrounding these observations.

There is evidence that with advancing age, chronic overactivation of mTORC1 has harmful effects on skeletal muscle.49 Some research suggests that CR may reduce mTORC1 activity in the skeletal muscle of older animals.14 However, in the present study, there was no indication that CR reduced mTORC1 activity.

Akt was also enriched among kinases linked to ISGU-correlated phosphosites. Intriguingly, only a subset of Akt substrates exhibited correlation with ISGU, highlighting substrate specificity in Akt signaling. We have observed a similar dichotomy in the behaviour of specific Akt substrates in response to exercise.33,43 As to whether this involves spatial regulation at the level of individual cells or subcellular variation in Akt signaling warrants further investigation.

To prioritize phosphosites of potential translational relevance, we cross-referenced ISGU-correlated sites with human genetic association data. This revealed several proteins implicated in metabolic traits (T2D, HbA1C, and BMI) including Lmod1, Nos1, Neb, Plec, and Ehbp1l1. Several sites on both Lmod1 and Ehbp1l1 have previously been observed to be regulated in humans by a single session of one-legged, aerobic exercise,43 another intervention with shared health benefits to CR. Lmod1 is a potent actin nucleator that has been implicated in a range of processes including cancer and muscle function,50 while Ehbp proteins can regulate Rab8 and Rab13, GTPases that control trafficking of the insulin-regulated glucose transporter GLUT4 to the cell surface trafficking in skeletal muscle51 underscoring a potential mechanistic link to GU.

The similar effects of CR and exercise on Lmod1 and Ehbp1l1 suggested there might be broader overlap between CR and exercise in regulating phosphorylation of skeletal muscle proteins. To explore this, we referred to a recent study that examined how endurance, sprint, and resistance exercise affect the skeletal muscle phosphoproteome.52 That study identified a core set of phosphosites consistently regulated by all three exercise types. When we compared the phosphosites regulated by CR to this core exercise-regulated phosphoproteome, we found little overlap between the phosphosites affected by exercise and those affected by CR.

Most phosphosites that responded to either diet or insulin were detected only in one sex. Previous studies have also shown that males and females can have different molecular responses to physiological interventions.53,54 In adults, these sex differences are primarily due to the effects of gonadal hormones (such as estrogen and testosterone) and/or genetic differences related to sex chromosomes (XX and XY).55 In AL-fed female rats, estrous cycles typically stop between 20 and 24 months of age, and their serum estrogen levels at this age are much lower than the peak levels seen in regularly cycling 4- to 5-month-old females.56 Similarly, serum testosterone in 25-month-old male AL rats is substantially lower than in 5-month-old males.57 The decline in gonadal hormone levels with age in AL rats means that differences in circulating sex hormones become less pronounced in older animals. As a result, the influence of sex chromosomes may become a more important factor in determining sex-specific molecular outcomes in older rats.

In summary, despite CR-increased ISGU in male and female rats, the underlying phosphoproteomic responses were largely sex-specific. Nonetheless, the subset of shared phosphosites exhibited highly concordant regulation in both sexes, revealing novel candidates for further investigation. Our study identifies key CR- and insulin-responsive phosphosites that correlate with ISGU, some with known links to human disease, and others newly implicated. These findings offer rich opportunities to understand how CR enhances insulin sensitivity and may inform strategies to harness its benefits.
