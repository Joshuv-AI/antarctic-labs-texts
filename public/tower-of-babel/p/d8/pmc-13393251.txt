Correlates of antenatal care visits among expectant mothers in Malawi: application of generalised estimating equations and likelihood-based poisson and negative binomial mixed models
Authors: Harry Chalistus Milala, Tsirizani Mwalimu Kaombe
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Despite recent improvements in antenatal care (ANC) coverage in sub-Saharan Africa, the timing and frequency of ANC visits remain inconsistent. This contributes to increased pregnancy-related complications among mothers in the region. This study utilised Poisson and Negative Binomial mixed regression with maximum likelihood estimation and generalized estimating equations (GEE) to analyse the factors associated with ANC visits among mothers in Malawi. Secondary data for 7,125 mothers from the individual recode file of the 2024 Malawi Demographic and Health Survey were used. All the analyses were carried out using R software version 4.5.2. The results indicated substantial between-cluster variation of ANC visits, indicating higher chances of observing similar ANC outcomes for mothers staying in the same cluster. The likelihood-based estimation methods yielded efficient estimates with smaller standard errors, p-values, and narrower confidence intervals than the GEE. However, both methods produced estimates with similar directions and magnitudes. On average, mothers in Malawi attend four ANC visits during their pregnancies. Higher ANC attendance rates were observed among mothers aged 20 years and above, those with tertiary education, and those with educated husbands. Conversely, lower rates were found in mothers with at least five children, those from middle wealth households, and those exposed to newspapers. Using alternative statistical estimation methods can enhance the reliability of findings in public health research. The health policy interventions that can utilise shared community cultural values, norms and networks at cluster level, as well as spouse involvement may improve ANC programs in sub-Saharan Africa.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12978-026-02372-3.

Introduction

Antenatal care (ANC) refers to the medical care provided by skilled healthcare professionals to pregnant women and adolescent girls [1, 2]. The goal of ANC is to ensure optimal health conditions for both the mother and the fetus during pregnancy. It is a key intervention for improving maternal health outcomes, such as reducing pregnancy-related complications and mortality, while also fostering a positive relationship between pregnant women and healthcare providers [3, 4]. ANC services include screening for potential pregnancy issues and assessing risks, such as pre-eclampsia, anemia, infections, and fetal growth restrictions [5, 6]. These services also involve addressing problems that may arise during pregnancy, administering medications that can enhance pregnancy outcomes, offering information to expectant mothers, and preparing them both physically and psychologically for childbirth and parenthood [7–9]. Additionally, ANC plays a vital role in conducting essential testing, counseling, and treatment for conditions like HIV, malaria prophylaxis, and other infections [10]. This comprehensive care ultimately helps reduce mother-to-child transmission of infections and protects the health of mothers [11].

The World Health Organization (WHO) recommends that antenatal care (ANC) should begin within the first 12 weeks of pregnancy, with follow-up visits scheduled at 20, 26, 30, 34, 36, 38, and 40 weeks [12]. This guideline suggests that a minimum of eight contacts is essential for enhancing pregnancy outcomes and improving the care experience for women [13, 14]. However, adherence to these antenatal care guidelines poses a significant challenge in sub-Saharan Africa, including Malawi [15–17]. Compliance with both the timing and frequency of ANC visits is alarmingly low, largely due to gaps in knowledge and financial constraints [18–21]. These ANC data challenges have implications for analysis, potentially affecting policy decisions. For example, important community behaviours among women may not be taken into account in policy-making due to a lack of clear evidence [22, 23]. This study applies a mixed-effects Poisson and Negative Binomial regression models, using generalized estimating equations (GEE) and maximum likelihood estimation methods, to examine the factors that are associated with the frequency of antenatal care visits in Malawi based on national survey data.

In statistical regression, the parameters can be estimated through maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) or method of moments estimation (MME), among other techniques. With MLE method, the estimates are found by maximising the log-likelihood function [24]. While MME approach minimises the quadratic form of the conditional expectation of some function of the response variable conditional on the independent variables and given a weighting matrix [25, 26]. The generalised estimating equations (GEE) is an example of the MME method [27]. Depending on whether the data at hand fulfill assumptions of the MLE method, the MLE technique can provide more efficient estimates than the MME [27, 28]. Otherwise, when most assumptions of the MLE are violated by the data at hand, the MME approach gives more reliable estimates [29].

Analysing the factors that are associated with the frequency of antenatal care (ANC) visits by mothers in Malawi, utilising advanced statistical techniques and the most recent data, will help identify prevailing patterns in ANC utilisation by mothers. This will provide valuable insights that can inform policymakers in improving reproductive health policies. Further, accounting for cluster random effects arising from shared community characteristics such as geographic accessibility, cultural practices, and availability of health facilities will even guide proper policy direction [30].

The next section of the article, i.e. Methods section presents the data and statistical methods used. Results section gives the results. While Discussion section gives the discussion of findings. Finally, the conclusion is presented in Conclusion section.

Methods

Data and variables

The study used secondary data from the individual recodes file of the 2024 Malawi Demographic and Health Survey (MDHS). The survey used two-stage stratified cluster sampling, where 769 enumeration areas (or clusters), each containing an average of 215 households, were randomly sampled from across the country at the first stage using rural and urban stratification. Thereafter, 30 households were randomly selected per each sampled cluster, which resulted into selecting 23,000 occupied households that formed the sample at the second stage [31]. This yielded a national sample of 20,849 women aged between 15 and 49 years. Amongst these, 7125 women had a live birth or stillbirth in the five years preceding the survey, which formed the study sample in this research.

The dependent variable in this study was the number of antenatal care (ANC) visits a woman attended during her most recent pregnancy. The following independent variables were included in the modelling based on literature: age of mother, her education level, working status, husband’s education level, household wealth, media exposure, history of family planning, and the number of children ever born [32–34]. Other variables included the distance to a health facility (perceived as a barrier or not), place of residence, and region of stay [35–37]. The 2024 MDHS data are publicly available for users through the following link: https://www.dhsprogram.com/data/dataset/Malawi_Standard-DHS_2024.cfm?flag=1. The data were cleaned using the STATA software version 17.0.

Maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) method for regression parameters

Based on the assumed Poisson probability distribution for the ANC count variable \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$Y_{ij}$$\end{document} given in Eq. (1) and based on the independence assumption for ANC visits, the national mean \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\lambda _{ij}$$\end{document} can be estimated using the joint probabilities of all women in all clusters. That is the product of the probabilities called likelihood function. The random effects can be treated as part of the penalty term during the estimation [24], or by integrating them out in a marginal likelihood function [25]. In this study, a joint marginal likelihood function was used, and it given by:5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} L(\beta , \sigma ^2_u \mid Y_{ij}, X_{ij},Z_{ij})= & \prod _{j=1}^{J} \int _{-\infty }^{\infty } \left[ \prod _{i=1}^{n_j} \frac{e^{-\lambda _{ij}} \lambda _{ij}^{y_{ij}}}{y_{ij}!} \right] f(u_j; 0, \sigma ^2_u) \, du_j \nonumber \\= & \prod _{j=1}^{J} \int _{-\infty }^{\infty } \left[ \prod _{i=1}^{n_j} \frac{ \mathrm{exp}\!\left\{ -\mathrm{exp}\!\left( X_{ij}^{T}\beta + Z_{j}^{T}u_{j}\right) \right\} \mathrm{exp}\!\left\{ y_{ij} \left( X_{ij}^{T}\beta + Z_{j}^{T}u_{j}\right) \right\} }{y_{ij}!} \right] \nonumber \\ & \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma _u^2}} \mathrm{exp}\!\left( -\frac{u_j^2}{2\sigma _u^2}\right) \, du_j, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$u_j$$\end{document} were assumed unity across clusters and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$f(u_j; 0, \sigma _u^2)$$\end{document} denoted their normal probability density functions.

The corresponding log-likelihood function is given by:6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \ell (\boldsymbol{\beta }, \sigma ^2_u) = \sum \limits _{j=1}^{J} \log \left\{ \int _{-\infty }^{\infty } \left[ \prod _{i=1}^{n_j} \frac{\mathrm{exp}\!\left\{ -\mathrm{exp}\!\left( X_{ij}^{T}\beta + Z_{j}^{T}u_{j}\right) \right\} \mathrm{exp}\!\left\{ y_{ij} \left( X_{ij}^{T}\beta + Z_{j}^{T}u_{j}\right) \right\} }{y_{ij}!} \right] \times \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi \sigma _u^2}} \mathrm{exp}\!\left( -\frac{u_j^2}{2\sigma _u^2} \right) \, du_j \right\} . \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The estimation proceeded by taking partial derivatives of the log-likelihood function in Eq. (6) with respect to each parameter and equate them to zero to solve for the values of the parameters. Such equations had no closed forms, hence parameter estimation was completed numerically [25].

The estimation proceeded in a similar manner as in standard Poisson mixed model. Thus, the log-likelihood and score functions were computed from the likelihood function. Then, the solutions of fixed effects were numerically solved from the zero equations of the score functions and variance of random effects and over-dispersion statistic were in turn computed from the updated likelihood function. The computations for both standard Poisson and Negative Binomial mixed models were done using R package lme4 in the R software version 4.5.2 [42]. The R code used is provided in the Appendix.

Generalized estimating equations (GEE) method for regression parameters

All the interpretations of the estimates were done at 95% confidence level.

Model comparison methods

For the model estimated by maximum likelihood, information criteria penalise model complexity while rewarding goodness of fit [45]. Hence, the Akaike Information Criterion (AIC) given below was used to select the best model between Negative Binomial and Standard Poisson mixed-effects models:10\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \mathrm{AIC} = -2\ell (\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta }}) + 2k, \end{aligned}$$\end{document}where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\ell (\widehat{\boldsymbol{\theta }})$$\end{document} is the maximised log-likelihood, \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$k$$\end{document} the number of estimated parameters, and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$n$$\end{document} the sample size. Lower AIC indicated a preferred model.

Goodness-of-fit diagnostics were also applied to evaluate how closely the predicted counts matched the observed counts. The deviance statistic was used for the MLE and Pearson residual for the GEE models, respectively given by:11\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} d \;=\; 2\sum \limits _{i,j} \left\{ y_{ij}\log \!\left( \frac{y_{ij}}{\widehat{\lambda }_{ij}}\right) - (y_{ij}-\widehat{\lambda }_{ij}) \right\} , \end{aligned}$$\end{document}and12\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\begin{aligned} \chi ^2 \;=\; \sum \limits _{i,j} \frac{(y_{ij}-\widehat{\lambda }_{ij})^2}{\widehat{\lambda }_{ij}}. \end{aligned}$$\end{document}

The ratios \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$D/\nu$$\end{document} and \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\chi ^2/\nu$$\end{document}, where \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\nu =n-k$$\end{document} denoted residual degrees of freedom, were used to analyse the model fit through observing the degree of dispersion of the data. The ratio values close to 1 indicated adequate fit under the Poisson assumption, while values substantially \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$>1$$\end{document} indicated overdispersion, and suggested alternative specifications might be appropriate, such as negative-binomial, or inclusion of additional random effects [46].

Finally, performance of the two MLE models and the GEE were compared using the direction, sizes, and p-values of the fixed effects. In addition, size of the standard errors, and width of the confidence intervals of the fixed effects were used to compare the models. Smaller standard errors and narrower confidence intervals of te fixed effects indicated a better model fit. The combined use of these diagnostics provided a principled and transparent basis for assessing the estimation framework that best described the antenatal care count data [47].

Model estimates results: comparison of maximum likelihood estimation (MLE) and generalised estimating equations (GEE) Poisson and negative binomial mixed models

The results in Table 2 showed that the likelihood-based count data models fitted the ANC data better, with smaller ratio of deviance or Pearson residual to degrees of freedom (i.e., 1.563 or 2.427 for Poisson; 0.677 or 1.367 of NB model), compared to the generalized estimating equations (GEE) (i.e., 2.29 or 7.88). The variance of between-cluster random effects for the model’s intercept was significantly different from zero in each case (i.e., 0.086 for Poisson and 0.067 for NB model). This suggested that the probability was high that ANC measurements of mothers from the same cluster will show similar patterns. Amongst the likelihood-based models, the negative binomial mixed model fitted the data better as it has lower AIC value than a standard Poisson model. Moreover, the negative binomial mixed model showed that the ANC data were highly over-dispersed with a dispersion estimate of 1.37.

However, the likelihood-based estimates had smaller standard errors and p-values, along with narrower confidence intervals compared to those from the Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) method. Without considering the covariates, both approaches indicated that the average number of antenatal care (ANC) visits per woman in the country was approximately four. Discussion

This article analysed the factors associated with antenatal care (ANC) visits among mothers in Malawi by applying mixed-effects Poisson and negative binomial regression, which accounted for the within-cluster dependencies of women’s ANC visits. The factors were estimated using two approaches: generalized estimating equations (GEE) and maximum likelihood estimation (MLE). The findings revealed a significant between-cluster variation of ANC data, suggesting that the chances were high that ANC measurements of mothers from the same cluster or community would give similar patterns. The model’s deviance or Pearson residuals indicated that the likelihood-based Poisson and negative binomial mixed model provided a more efficient representation of the clustered ANC data. It showed smaller dispersion and standard errors, as well as narrower confidence intervals for fixed effects, compared to the GEE-based model. Nevertheless, both estimation methods produced estimates with similar directions and magnitudes. These findings confirmed previous research, which observed that method of moments estimators (MME), such as GEE, typically yield estimates with larger bias and standard errors than maximum likelihood estimation methods, while still being consistent [25, 26]. Based on the AIC statistic, the negative binomial mixed model fitted the ANC data better than did the standard Poisson mixed model. This was expected since the data showed presence of over-dispersion, which usually penalises the standard Poisson regression estimates due to violation of the equi-dispersion assumption [28].

When evaluated at the 5% significance level, the likelihood-based Poisson and negative binomial mixed-effects regression models identified a wider range of statistically significant factors associated with antenatal care (ANC) utilisation compared to the Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) Poisson model. ANC visits were found to be higher among women aged 20 years and above, those with higher education, and those with husbands who had at least a primary education. Older women are generally more likely to utilise maternal health services due to their accumulated reproductive experience, greater awareness of pregnancy-related risks, and increased autonomy in healthcare decision-making [34]. High awareness levels among educated mothers and their husbands contribute to increased ANC visits, the spouse support and encouragement becomes meaningful in this regard [48].

On the other hand, the incidence rates of antenatal care (ANC) visits were significantly lower among mothers with at least five children, those who lived in middle income household, and those exposed to newspaper. Women with high parity are known to start ANC late, which decreases the total number of expected ANC visits during their pregnancy [48, 49]. Financial stability in wealthier families helps to mitigate geographic barriers to accessing ANC services, thereby improving attendance rates [50, 51]. The finding of low ANC attendance in middle wealth families therefore does not align with observations made in previous stuies. Similarly, newspaper exposure is expected to improve awareness and induce safe motherhood behaviour [36, 52], which is contrary to what was observed in this study.

While significant associations between antenatal care (ANC) utilisation and various determinants were identified, it is difficult to establish the temporal ordering of some variables. For instance, factors such as family planning use, employment status, and media exposure can change over time and may have occurred either before or after the pregnancy for which ANC visits were reported. Therefore, these results should be interpreted as associative rather than causal [53]. Additionally, the analysis relied on self-reported data, which may be affected by recall bias and social desirability bias. Women were asked to report the number of ANC visits for births that occurred up to five years before the survey, which could lead to under-reporting or over-reporting of these visits. Such measurement errors may weaken the estimated associations, especially among older women and those with a higher number of births [54].

The Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE), while robust against errors in the specified correlation structure, does not allow for the direct estimation of cluster-level variance. As a result, it cannot effectively quantify the level of community heterogeneity in ANC (Antenatal Care) utilisation. This limitation affects its usefulness in understanding contextual influences and diminishes its interpretability for policy purposes when compared to the likelihood-based estimation approach [29]. In this study, using GEE alongside Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) methods addressed this shortcoming and enhanced the modelling process. Additionally, the study focused on a single country and survey round, which may restrict the generalisability of the statistical findings to other contexts. Health system organisation, cultural norms, and survey design can vary significantly between countries and times [55].

Conclusion

This article examined the comparative performance of likelihood-based (Maximum Likelihood Estimation, MLE) and moments-based estimation methods (Generalized Estimating Equations, GEE) used alongside mixed Poisson and negative binomial mixed regression to investigate the factors associated with antenatal care attendance among women in Malawi. The findings revealed that, on average, expectant mothers in Malawi attended four antenatal care (ANC) visits during their pregnancies. Both the MLE and GEE methods produced similar estimates regarding the direction and magnitude of the associations. However, the likelihood-based Poisson and negative binomial mixed models yielded more accurate and reliable estimates compared to the GEE model. Factors associated with a higher number of ANC visits included being an older mother aged 35–49, being educated, and having an educated husband. In contrast, having a parity of five or more children, being in middle wealth family, and being exposed to newspaper were linked to lower ANC attendance.

These findings underscore the ongoing need for strategies to enhance early initiation and proper utilisation of antenatal care (ANC) services in Malawi. To improve ANC utilisation in the studied population, it is essential to address socioeconomic disparities and improve girlchild access to education. Incorporating shared community values and cultural norms and spouse involvement into ANC interventions may enhance ANC programs within the study population. Utilising alternative regression estimation methods can help offset the limitations of a single method and increase the reliability of the results. This study demonstrated the consistency of estimates obtained using Generalized Estimating Equations (GEE) and Maximum Likelihood Estimation (MLE) methods, although GEE showed lower precision. Future research could apply these methodologies to panel survey designs to assess the replicability of estimates over time.
