Review of Peptic Ulcer Disease: Insights from Pathophysiology, Mechanisms, Pharmacotherapy, Phytotherapy, and Case Reports
Authors: Reza Pertiwi, Gofarana Wilar, Sri Adi Sumiwi, Jutti Levita
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractPeptic ulcer disease (PUD) remains a significant global health concern associated with substantial morbidity. The primary etiologies include Helicobacter pylori infection and the use of nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), both of which impair gastric mucosal defense mechanisms. This review aims to provide an updated overview of the pathogenesis, clinical manifestations, diagnostic approaches, and current management strategies of PUD, including emerging therapeutic developments. Advances in diagnostic modalities, such as endoscopy and imaging, have improved early detection and clinical outcomes. Current management focuses on H. pylori eradication, gastric acid suppression with proton pump inhibitors (PPIs), and prevention of complications. Recent advances in peptic ulcer therapy include the introduction of potassium-competitive acid blockers (P-CABs), which provide rapid and potent acid suppression and may serve as an effective alternative to PPIs in selected patients. In cases of bleeding ulcers, endoscopic therapy combined with high-dose PPI administration remains the standard of care. Case reports from multiple countries demonstrate a wide clinical spectrum, ranging from mild dyspepsia to severe complications such as perforation and upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Common risk factors include chronic NSAID use, smoking, anticoagulant therapy, and persistent H. pylori infection. Diagnosis is established through laboratory evaluation, imaging, and endoscopy, while treatment ranges from pharmacological therapy to emergency surgical intervention in severe cases. Most patients show favourable outcomes with timely management, although mortality may still occur in complicated conditions. Emerging challenges, particularly antibiotic resistance, have led to the adoption of tailored therapeutic strategies based on antimicrobial susceptibility testing. In addition, novel approaches, including phytotherapy, are being explored as potential adjuncts. An individualized and evidence-based approach is therefore essential to optimize clinical outcomes in patients with PUD.Keywords: gastric ulcer, gastric mucosal protection, Helicobacter pylori, potassium-competitive acid blockers, proton pump inhibitor

Introduction

PUD, including gastric and duodenal ulcers, is a common gastrointestinal (GI) disorder characterized by the formation of ulcerative lesions that extend through the muscularis mucosa into the submucosa or deeper layers of the GI wall. This condition affects millions of individuals worldwide and remains a significant health problem because it can lead to serious complications, such as bleeding, perforation, and gastric outlet obstruction.1 Pathophysiologically, this disease results from an imbalance between aggressive factors, such as gastric acid and pepsin secretion, and protective mucosal factors, including mucus, bicarbonate, mucosal blood flow, and epithelial regenerative capacity.2

Globally, the prevalence of PUD is estimated at 5–14% of the population, with regional, lifestyle, and individual risk factor variations.3,4 The clinical manifestations of this disease vary, ranging from mild dyspepsia to severe epigastric pain, and can develop into serious complications that increase morbidity and mortality.5 Although its incidence has declined in some countries due to advances in diagnosis, appropriate therapy, and Helicobacter pylori eradication, PUD remains a significant global health burden.6

The primary etiology of PUD involves H. pylori infection and the use of non-steroidal anti-inflammatory drugs (NSAIDs), which together account for the majority of PUD cases.7,8 H. pylori is a Gram-negative bacterium that colonizes the gastric mucosa and plays a key role in the pathogenesis of peptic ulcer disease. Transmission occurs mainly through fecal–oral and oral–oral routes, with higher prevalence observed in populations with lower socioeconomic status and poor hygiene conditions.1,5,9 H. pylori infection plays a pivotal role in ulcerogenesis by inducing persistent gastric inflammation, disrupting mucosal integrity, and engaging in complex host–pathogen interactions that modulate the immune response and perpetuate tissue injury.9–12 On the other hand, NSAIDs induce mucosal injury primarily by inhibiting cyclooxygenase (COX) enzymes, resulting in decreased synthesis of protective prostaglandins, catalyzed by COX-1 in the gastric mucosa, and subsequent impairment of gastrointestinal (GI) mucosal defense mechanisms.13–16 Furthermore, the combination of H. pylori infection and NSAID use significantly increases the risk of ulcer occurrence and its complications.1

Research over the past decade has significantly expanded the understanding of the pathophysiology of PUD. In addition to classical etiological factors, increasing evidence highlights the roles of oxidative stress, inflammatory mediators, immune system dysregulation, and complex interactions between the gut microbiota and the host in the development and progression of the disease.17–19 These advances have also driven the development of more effective therapeutic strategies. Pharmacological therapy, such as proton pump inhibitors (PPIs) and H. pylori eradication therapy, such as clarithromycin triple therapy or bismuth quadruple therapy, remains the mainstay of PUD management.2,7 Standard triple therapy consists of a proton pump inhibitor, clarithromycin, and amoxicillin or metronidazole.5 In recent years, alternative approaches such as phytotherapy have gained increasing attention, as growing evidence suggests that plant-based therapies may provide therapeutic benefits and are generally well-tolerated, with a lower incidence of adverse effects compared to conventional pharmacological treatments, although further rigorous clinical studies are still required to confirm their efficacy and safety.20–22

Therefore, this review aims to comprehensively discuss PUD, including its pathophysiology, mechanisms of ulcer formation, pharmacotherapy, phytotherapy, and case reports from the past 10 years. By integrating recent research findings, this review aims to deepen understanding and support the development of more effective therapeutic strategies in clinical practice.

Methods

This narrative review synthesizes current evidence on PUD. A structured literature search was conducted using the PubMed and Scopus databases to identify relevant studies published within the last 10 years (2016–2026). The search was restricted to studies addressing gastric peptic ulcer disease; therefore, terms related to other ulcer subtypes, including duodenal ulcer and stress ulcer, were not included in the search strategy. The search strategy employed a combination of keywords and Boolean operators, including: “peptic ulcer” OR “gastric ulcer” AND “Helicobacter pylori” OR “NSAIDs” AND “risk factors” OR “etiology” AND “treatment” OR “management” OR “therapy”.

Only articles available in full-text format and published in English were included. Titles and abstracts were initially screened, followed by full-text assessment to determine eligibility based on relevance to the topic. Although a structured search strategy was applied, this study was conducted as a narrative review; therefore, no formal systematic review protocol (eg, PRISMA guidelines) was strictly followed.

Epidemiology

PUD has undergone significant epidemiological changes in recent decades. Although PUD continues to affect millions of individuals worldwide, with an estimated annual incidence of 1.5–3%, its overall burden has gradually declined owing to advances in diagnostic techniques, H. pylori eradication therapy, and the widespread use of acid-suppressive medications.1 Studies from South Korea have reported PUD prevalence rates ranging from approximately 2.4% to 5.4%, with a higher burden observed among men and older individuals. In addition, a nationwide study of peptic ulcer bleeding (PUB) reported an incidence of 34.98 cases per 100,000 person-years and demonstrated a significant decline in incidence after 2008.23 Similar trends have been observed in the United States, where reductions in age-standardized prevalence, hospitalization rates, and mortality indicate a decreasing burden of PUD over time.3

In general, the incidence of uncomplicated PUD shows a fairly consistent downward trend in various countries. This decline is mainly associated with the success of H. pylori eradication therapy and the widespread use of PPIs in modern clinical practice.1,8 Nevertheless, despite the decrease in uncomplicated cases, the occurrence of complications such as bleeding and perforation remains a significant clinical challenge.

The incidence of complications related to PUD has not declined in parallel with the reduction in uncomplicated cases, particularly among the elderly. This trend is associated with increased life expectancy, a higher burden of comorbid conditions, and the widespread use of medications such as NSAIDs and low-dose aspirin.1,3 In addition, despite a global decline in the overall incidence of PUD, there has been a clear demographic transition toward older patient populations. The median age of patients presenting with PUD complications has increased, indicating a shift in disease burden from younger individuals to the elderly. This phenomenon suggests that modern PUD is increasingly a disease of aging, in which complications such as gastrointestinal bleeding and perforation occur more frequently and with greater severity due to age-related physiological vulnerability and comorbid conditions. As a result, incidence, mortality, and disability-adjusted disease burden rise markedly with advancing age.24–26

From an etiological perspective, H. pylori infection remains a primary cause of PUD globally. However, its prevalence has declined in recent decades, largely due to improved eradication strategies and better sanitation. Concurrently, ulcers unrelated to H. pylori are becoming increasingly common, particularly those associated with the use of NSAIDs and aspirin, which now represent a major etiological factor in many populations.25,27,28 The use of ulcerogenic medications, particularly NSAIDs, aspirin, and antithrombotic agents, represents a major risk factor for the development of PUD and its complications. This risk is especially pronounced in the elderly population, in whom age-related physiological changes, comorbidities, and polypharmacy further increase susceptibility to mucosal injury, bleeding, and perforation.13,27,29

Furthermore, the etiological profile of PUD is evolving. While the prevalence of H. pylori infection has declined in many populations, the increasing use of NSAIDs, antiplatelet agents, and anticoagulants has contributed to a growing proportion of medication-associated ulcers. This trend is particularly evident among older adults, who frequently require long-term treatment for musculoskeletal and cardiovascular disorders. Consequently, drug-induced ulcers are emerging as an increasingly important subtype of PUD and may constitute a major clinical challenge in aging populations, underscoring the need for targeted preventive strategies in high-risk groups.5,6,13,25,27–29

In addition to biological and pharmacological factors, socioeconomic factors and lifestyle also play an important role in the epidemiology of PUD. Smoking habits, excessive alcohol consumption, and low socioeconomic status are associated with an increased risk of developing this disease.24,30 Several studies have demonstrated that psychological factors, including chronic stress and depression, are independently associated with an increased risk of PUD through neuroendocrine, immune, and behavioral mechanisms, even in the absence of H. pylori infection or NSAID exposure.31

The main complications of PUD are GI bleeding and perforation, with bleeding being the most common complication. These complications are more frequent in elderly patients and those with comorbidities and are associated with significant morbidity and mortality.4,32 Complications occur in up to 20% of cases, with upper gastrointestinal bleeding representing the most common cause of hospitalization, followed by perforation and, less frequently, gastric outlet obstruction.33,34 Perforation develops in approximately 2–14% of ulcers and accounts for nearly 40% of PUD-related deaths.33,35–37 Complications of PUD continue to be associated with considerable short-term mortality, especially in perforated ulcers, which have been shown to carry a higher risk of death than bleeding ulcers.32,38

In addition, several studies have shown that the incidence of PUD and its complications varies seasonally, with fluctuations occurring at different times of the year. However, these patterns may vary depending on the population and region studied.39,40 These differences are likely related to environmental factors, patterns of drug consumption, and variations in the prevalence of H. pylori.

In terms of management, advances in endoscopic techniques and pharmacological therapy have significantly reduced the need for surgical intervention. However, surgical intervention is still required in severe cases of PUD, particularly in patients with perforation or uncontrolled bleeding.41 In addition, advances in laparoscopic techniques and improvements in gastrointestinal surgical care have been associated with reduced hospital length of stay and improved clinical outcomes, including lower morbidity and potentially reduced mortality.42–44

Nevertheless, patients with a history of gastric ulcers still have long-term risks, including an increased risk of stomach cancer, even after H. pylori eradication. Therefore, long-term monitoring remains necessary in clinical practice.8

Pathophysiology and Mechanism

The pathophysiology of PUD results from a complex interaction between aggressive factors and protective mechanisms in the gastrointestinal mucosa. Under normal conditions, the stomach and duodenal mucosa are protected by various defense systems, including mucus and bicarbonate secretion, epithelial integrity, mucosal blood flow, and prostaglandin regulation, all of which help maintain mucosal homeostasis. However, an imbalance between these protective factors and aggressive factors—such as gastric acid secretion, pepsin activity, H. pylori infection, and the use of NSAIDs—forms the primary basis for ulceration.1,2 The pathophysiology of peptic ulcer disease is characterized by an imbalance between mucosal-aggressive factors and protective mechanisms, resulting in mucosal injury and ulcer formation (Figure 1).

Pathophysiology of Peptic Ulcer Disease.

An Imbalance Between Aggressive and Protective Factors

Gastric acid and pepsin are the main factors involved in mucosal damage. Hypersecretion of acid, especially in duodenal ulcers, increases mucosal exposure to a corrosive environment. On the other hand, the mucosal defense system, which includes mucus, bicarbonate, and mucosal blood flow, functions to neutralize acid and repair epithelial damage.45

Prostaglandins, particularly prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), play a crucial role in maintaining gastric mucosal integrity through multiple protective mechanisms. These include stimulation of mucus and bicarbonate secretion, which form a protective barrier against gastric acid, as well as the maintenance of adequate mucosal blood flow, which supports epithelial repair and regeneration. In addition, prostaglandins regulate epithelial cell turnover and inhibit gastric acid secretion, further enhancing mucosal defense. Disruption of this protective system, especially due to the use of NSAIDs, leads to inhibition of COX enzymes and a subsequent decrease in prostaglandin synthesis. As a result, mucus and bicarbonate production are reduced, mucosal blood flow is impaired, and the gastric epithelium becomes more susceptible to injury from gastric acid and pepsin. This imbalance between aggressive and protective factors ultimately increases the risk of mucosal damage and peptic ulcer development.45–47

The Role of H. pylori in Molecular Mechanisms

Infection with H. pylori is one of the main etiological factors in PUD and acts through various molecular mechanisms. This bacterium can survive in an acidic environment by producing the enzyme urease, which converts urea into ammonia, thereby creating a more neutral microenvironment around it.7 In addition, H. pylori produces several virulence factors, most notably CagA (cytotoxin-associated gene A) and VacA (vacuolating cytotoxin A), which play a central role in gastric mucosal injury. Following bacterial attachment to gastric epithelial cells, CagA is translocated into host cells via a type IV secretion system, where it disrupts intracellular signaling pathways. One of the key mechanisms involves activation of the NF-κB pathway, which leads to increased transcription of proinflammatory cytokines such as IL-1β, IL-6, and TNF-α. This sustained inflammatory response promotes chronic gastritis, epithelial damage, and increased epithelial cell turnover. In addition, VacA contributes to mucosal injury by inducing cellular vacuolation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and apoptosis of epithelial cells, further compromising mucosal integrity. Persistent infection with H. pylori therefore results in a chronic inflammatory state, disruption of mucosal defense mechanisms, and an imbalance between aggressive and protective factors, ultimately predisposing to peptic ulcer formation.47,48

The VacA of H. pylori induces apoptosis in epithelial cells and disrupts tight junction integrity, leading to increased mucosal permeability and tissue damage.49,50 Furthermore, H. pylori infection promotes oxidative stress through excessive production of reactive oxygen species (ROS), which contributes to DNA damage and lipid peroxidation of cell membranes.51–53

Recent evidence suggests that the molecular pathogenesis of H. pylori extends beyond the classical actions of CagA and VacA. H. pylori has been shown to modulate several host signaling pathways, including JAK/STAT3, PI3K/Akt, and Wnt/β-catenin, which contribute to persistent inflammation, altered epithelial cell proliferation, and impaired mucosal healing. In addition, the bacterium can regulate host gene expression through epigenetic modifications and non-coding RNAs, thereby promoting chronic infection and tissue injury.10 Emerging studies have also highlighted the ability of H. pylori to evade host immune responses by modulating macrophage and T-cell functions, facilitating long-term gastric colonization.54 Furthermore, biofilm formation has been recognized as an important virulence-associated mechanism that enhances bacterial persistence and may contribute to antimicrobial resistance and treatment failure.55 These findings provide a more comprehensive understanding of the complex molecular interactions underlying H. pylori-associated peptic ulcer disease.

Mechanism of Mucosal Injury Due to NSAIDs

NSAIDs are well recognized to induce gastrointestinal mucosal injury through both systemic and topical mechanisms. Systemically, NSAIDs exert their effects by inhibiting COX enzymes, particularly COX-1, which plays a crucial role in maintaining gastric mucosal integrity.1,56 The inhibition of COX-1 results in decreased synthesis of protective prostaglandins, such as prostaglandin E2 (PGE2), leading to reduced secretion of mucus and bicarbonate, diminished mucosal blood flow, and impaired epithelial restitution.2,57 Consequently, the gastric mucosa becomes more vulnerable to luminal aggressive factors, including gastric acid and pepsin.1,57

In addition to systemic effects, NSAIDs also produce direct topical injury to the gastric epithelium. As weak acids, NSAIDs can penetrate epithelial cells in their non-ionized form, causing intracellular accumulation, mitochondrial dysfunction, and increased epithelial permeability.2,58 This disruption of the mucosal barrier facilitates back-diffusion of hydrogen ions, further exacerbating tissue injury.57,58 The combined effects of prostaglandin depletion and direct epithelial damage ultimately contribute to the development of erosions, ulcers, and gastrointestinal bleeding associated with NSAID use.1,2 Furthermore, antiplatelet agents (eg, aspirin and clopidogrel) and anticoagulants (eg, warfarin and direct oral anticoagulants) are also important contributors to peptic ulcer bleeding. These medications impair platelet aggregation and coagulation pathways, thereby increasing the risk and severity of bleeding, particularly in patients with pre-existing mucosal injury or ulceration.1,59

The Role of Oxidative Stress and Inflammation

Oxidative stress plays an important role in the pathogenesis of PUD. An imbalance between ROS production and antioxidant defenses leads to extensive cellular damage, including lipid peroxidation, protein oxidation, and DNA damage, contributing to mucosal injury and ulcer formation.60 Furthermore, Pro-inflammatory mediators, including cytokines such as interleukin-1β (IL-1β), interleukin-6 (IL-6), and tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), play a crucial role in the pathogenesis of gastric mucosal inflammation. These cytokines promote the recruitment and activation of inflammatory cells, leading to increased infiltration within the gastric mucosa and amplification of the inflammatory response. Persistent activation of these pathways contributes to epithelial damage and disruption of mucosal integrity. Moreover, chronic inflammation mediated by these cytokines has been shown to impair tissue repair mechanisms, thereby inhibiting ulcer healing and exacerbating gastric mucosal injury.61,62

Role of Microbiota and the Immune System

Recent studies have demonstrated that the GI microbiota plays a crucial role in maintaining mucosal homeostasis through dynamic interactions with epithelial cells and the host immune system. Commensal microorganisms contribute to the preservation of intestinal barrier integrity by enhancing tight junction function, producing bioactive metabolites such as short-chain fatty acids, and regulating immune responses through the induction of regulatory T cells. Furthermore, the microbiota provides colonization resistance against pathogenic organisms by competing for nutrients and ecological niches. Disruption of this balanced microbial ecosystem, known as dysbiosis, can impair epithelial barrier function, increase intestinal permeability, and facilitate the translocation of microbial components across the mucosa. This process subsequently promotes excessive immune activation and inflammatory responses, ultimately leading to mucosal damage and increased susceptibility to GI diseases.63–65

Emerging evidence indicates that the microbiota–immune axis plays a critical role in determining the outcome of H. pylori infection. Alterations in gastric and intestinal microbial communities induced by H. pylori can influence both local and systemic immune responses by modulating cytokine production, epithelial barrier integrity, and immune cell differentiation.65–67 Dysbiosis has been associated with enhanced Th1/Th17-mediated inflammation, reduced regulatory T-cell activity, and increased mucosal permeability, which collectively contribute to persistent inflammation and tissue injury.66,67 Conversely, commensal microorganisms and their metabolites, particularly short-chain fatty acids, may promote immune tolerance and mucosal homeostasis by supporting regulatory T-cell development and maintaining epithelial barrier function.65,68 These findings highlight the bidirectional interaction between microbiota and the host immune system in shaping H. pylori persistence, disease severity, and progression toward gastric pathology.65–67 Furthermore, modulation of the microbiota through probiotic or microbiota-targeted interventions has emerged as a potential adjunctive strategy to improve eradication outcomes and mitigate inflammation associated with H. pylori infection.66,68

In addition, both innate and adaptive immune systems play essential roles in the host response to H. pylori infection and its associated pathogenesis. The innate immune response is initiated when gastric epithelial cells and macrophages recognize H. pylori through pattern recognition receptors, resulting in the activation of intracellular signaling pathways and the production of pro-inflammatory cytokines such as TNF-α, IL-1β, and IL-8. These mediators promote the recruitment of immune cells to the site of infection and contribute to the initial inflammatory response. Subsequently, the adaptive immune response is activated, involving T lymphocytes—particularly T helper (Th1 and Th17) cells—which further amplify cytokine production and sustain chronic inflammation. While these immune mechanisms are important for limiting bacterial colonization and facilitating pathogen clearance, persistent immune activation also contributes to epithelial injury, mucosal damage, and the progression of gastric diseases such as chronic gastritis and peptic ulcer.66,69

Ulcer Healing Process

Ulcer healing is a complex and highly regulated process involving epithelial regeneration, angiogenesis, and tissue remodelling to restore the structural and functional integrity of the mucosa. This process includes epithelial cell proliferation and migration, re-epithelialization of the damaged surface, and the formation of new blood vessels to supply oxygen and nutrients to the healing tissue. Growth factors play a central role in coordinating these events, particularly vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), which promotes angiogenesis, and epidermal growth factor (EGF), which stimulates epithelial cell proliferation and migration.70 However, the healing process can be impaired by the persistence of aggressive factors, such as H. pylori infection and the use of NSAIDs, which disrupt mucosal defense mechanisms, prolong inflammation, and ultimately delay healing and increase the risk of ulcer recurrence.71

Diagnostic

The diagnosis of PUD involves a combination of clinical evaluation, endoscopic examination, and laboratory testing to confirm the presence of mucosal lesions and identify underlying etiologies, particularly H. pylori infection and NSAID use. Clinically, patients with PUD may present with a wide spectrum of symptoms, ranging from epigastric pain, dyspepsia, nausea, and bloating to more severe manifestations such as gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation, or obstruction. However, symptoms alone are not sufficiently specific or sensitive to establish a definitive diagnosis, as some patients may remain asymptomatic or present with atypical features.5

Upper GI endoscopy (esophagogastroduodenoscopy) remains the gold standard for diagnosing PUD, as it provides direct visualization of mucosal lesions with high diagnostic accuracy, allows assessment of ulcer size and location, and enables tissue biopsy for histopathological evaluation to exclude malignancy, particularly in gastric ulcers.72,73 This is further supported by previous and contemporary literature, which emphasizes that endoscopy not only serves as the most reliable diagnostic modality for PUD but also plays a crucial role in differentiating benign from malignant lesions and guiding subsequent clinical management.74,75 Endoscopy also plays a crucial therapeutic role, particularly in patients presenting with complications such as Gl bleeding. It allows not only identification of the bleeding source but also immediate intervention, as endoscopic hemostasis represents the mainstay of treatment and has been shown to reduce rebleeding and mortality.76

In addition to endoscopy, detecting H. pylori infection is a critical component of the diagnostic approach, particularly in patients with dyspepsia. Non-invasive tests, such as the urea breath test and stool antigen test, are widely recommended due to their high sensitivity and specificity, especially in patients without alarm features.7,8 Serological testing may also be used; however, it has limited accuracy in distinguishing active from past infection because antibodies can persist long after eradication.77–79 In patients undergoing endoscopy, invasive biopsy-based methods such as rapid urease testing, histology, and culture are considered the gold standard and provide definitive confirmation of infection.79,80

Radiologic imaging techniques, such as computed tomography (CT), are generally not first-line diagnostic tools for uncomplicated PUD but are valuable in detecting complications such as perforation, obstruction, or penetration into adjacent organs.81 In acute settings, CT imaging plays a pivotal role by delivering rapid, high-resolution diagnostic information that supports timely clinical decision-making and guides emergency management, particularly in time-critical conditions.82,83 Laboratory findings such as anemia may indicate chronic bleeding, while elevated inflammatory markers may suggest severe inflammation or complications. However, these findings are nonspecific and should be interpreted in conjunction with clinical and endoscopic findings.84

Recent advances in diagnostic technologies have introduced magnetically controlled capsule endoscopy (MCCE) as a promising non-invasive modality for gastric examination. By using an externally controlled magnetic system, the capsule can be navigated throughout the stomach, enabling comprehensive visualization of the gastric mucosa while improving patient comfort and avoiding the need for sedation. Recent developments in robotic navigation systems, image quality, and artificial intelligence-assisted lesion detection have further enhanced the clinical potential of MCCE. Although conventional upper gastrointestinal endoscopy remains the reference standard because it allows tissue biopsy and therapeutic intervention, MCCE represents an attractive alternative for gastric screening and diagnostic evaluation in selected patients.85 Overall, the diagnosis of PUD requires an integrated approach combining clinical assessment, endoscopic evaluation, and targeted testing for H. pylori. Early and accurate diagnosis is essential to guide appropriate management, prevent complications, and improve patient outcomes.

Management

PUD is primarily managed through a combination of acid suppression, eradication of H. pylori infection, and prevention of complications. Advances in pharmacotherapy and endoscopic techniques have significantly improved healing rates and reduced recurrence. Current evidence indicates that acid suppression therapy can heal approximately 80–100% of ulcers within weeks, particularly with PPIs.27 The management approach depends on etiology, severity, and presence of complications, with most cases linked to H. pylori infection or NSAIDs use.23

Non-Pharmacological Management

Non-pharmacological management of PUD is an essential adjunct to standard medical therapy, focusing on modifying risk factors, promoting mucosal healing, and preventing recurrence. Lifestyle modification remains a central component, particularly smoking cessation and reduction of alcohol consumption, as both factors have been consistently associated with impaired mucosal defense and increased risk of ulcer formation and complications.24 In addition, avoidance or minimization of NSAIDs is strongly recommended, given their well-established role in disrupting mucosal integrity and contributing to ulcer development.73

Dietary modification also plays a supportive role, although evidence for specific dietary regimens remains limited. Current evidence suggests that a balanced diet rich in fruits and vegetables, alongside avoiding irritant foods such as spicy or acidic foods, may help reduce symptom exacerbations and the risk of recurrence.25 Furthermore, emerging evidence suggests that adherence to multiple healthy lifestyle behaviors—including adequate sleep, moderate alcohol consumption, and non-smoking—has a synergistic effect in improving gastrointestinal health outcomes and reducing overall disease burden.86–88

Stress management represents an important non-pharmacological strategy. Although the direct causal relationship between psychological stress and ulcer formation remains inconclusive, recent evidence suggests that stress can negatively affect gastrointestinal function through the brain–gut axis and may exacerbate symptoms or delay healing. Furthermore, psychological interventions such as relaxation techniques, mindfulness, and behavioral therapy have demonstrated beneficial effects in reducing psychological distress and improving patient well-being, with potential implications for disease outcomes.89,90

Patient education is a critical component of PUD management, emphasizing adherence to therapy, avoidance of risk factors such as NSAID use, alcohol, and smoking, as well as prevention of recurrence.73 In addition, non-pharmacological or procedural interventions, particularly endoscopic therapy, play a key role in managing complications such as bleeding ulcers. Endoscopic hemostatic therapy is recommended as first-line treatment and, when combined with pharmacological therapy such as PPIs, has been shown to reduce rebleeding rates and improve clinical outcomes.32,59,91

Overall, while non-pharmacological strategies alone are insufficient as definitive treatment, they are integral to comprehensive PUD management by addressing modifiable risk factors and supporting long-term disease control.

Pharmacotherapy

Classification of Drugs

Pharmacological therapy for PUD encompasses several classes of drugs that target different mechanisms involved in ulcer pathogenesis, including PPIs, antibiotics for H. pylori eradication, mucosal protective agents, and H2-receptor antagonists. Evidence from multiple studies consistently indicates that PPIs, such as omeprazole, are first-line therapy due to their superior efficacy in suppressing gastric acid secretion and promoting mucosal healing.1,2,8 Furthermore, the use of antibiotics such as amoxicillin and clarithromycin in combination with PPIs is essential for the eradication of H. pylori, a major etiological factor, and has been shown to reduce ulcer recurrence rates significantly.6,7 Mucosal protective agents, such as misoprostol, also play a crucial role in enhancing mucosal defense, particularly in ulcers induced by NSAIDs.13,92,93 Meanwhile, H2-receptor antagonists, although less potent than PPIs, continue to be utilized as an alternative in mild cases or as adjunctive therapy in clinical practice.2

Acid Suppression Therapy

Acid suppression therapy remains a cornerstone of PUD management, as gastric acid plays a critical role in ulcer formation and impedes mucosal healing. PPIs remain the first-line treatment due to their potent and sustained suppression of gastric acid secretion through irreversible inhibition of the gastric H+/K+-ATPase. Compared with histamine-2 receptor antagonists (H2RAs), PPIs achieve higher ulcer healing rates, provide more effective symptom relief, and reduce the risk of ulcer recurrence and complications, particularly upper gastrointestinal bleeding. Consequently, PPIs are widely used for both acute ulcer treatment and maintenance therapy, especially in patients with NSAID-associated ulcers or those at high risk of recurrent disease.27

Recently, potassium-competitive acid blockers (P-CABs), particularly vonoprazan, have emerged as a novel class of acid-suppressive agents that may overcome several limitations associated with conventional PPIs.94–97 Unlike PPIs, which require activation in an acidic environment and may exhibit variable responses related to CYP2C19 polymorphisms, P-CABs reversibly and competitively inhibit the potassium-binding site of the gastric proton pump, producing rapid, potent, and sustained acid suppression from the first dose.94,96 Furthermore, their acid-suppressive effect is less influenced by genetic variations in drug metabolism, resulting in more predictable pharmacodynamic responses among patients.94,97 Emerging clinical evidence suggests that vonoprazan achieves ulcer-healing outcomes comparable to PPIs while providing superior intragastric pH control.95,96 In addition, maintaining a higher intragastric pH may improve the effectiveness of Helicobacter pylori eradication therapy by enhancing antibiotic stability and bacterial susceptibility, thereby leading to improved eradication rates in some clinical settings.95 Although long-term comparative efficacy and safety data are still being accumulated, current evidence indicates that P-CABs represent a promising therapeutic option that may further expand the pharmacological management of PUD and other acid-related disorders.95,97

Drug Regimen to Eradicate H. pylori

Eradication of H. pylori is a cornerstone of PUD treatment, as it addresses the underlying cause and significantly reduces recurrence rates. Standard therapy includes a combination of a PPI with two or more antibiotics (triple or quadruple therapy), such as clarithromycin triple therapy (clarithromycin 500 mg BID combined with amoxicillin 1000 mg BID, or metronidazole 500 mg TID combined with PPI BID) for 14 days, or levofloxacin triple therapy (levofloxacin 500 mg combined with amoxicillin 1000 mg BID, and PPI BID). Evidence from systematic reviews and randomized trials confirms that eradication therapy improves healing and prevents relapse.6

In recent years, dual therapy has emerged as an alternative eradication strategy. High-dose dual therapy (HDDT), consisting of a high-dose proton pump inhibitor and amoxicillin, has demonstrated eradication rates comparable to standard triple or quadruple regimens while being associated with significantly fewer adverse events. A recent systematic review and meta-analysis reported that HDDT achieved similar eradication efficacy to comparator therapies with a more favorable safety profile. In addition, vonoprazan–amoxicillin dual therapy has shown promising efficacy and tolerability, with recent evidence supporting its use as a first-line treatment option for H. pylori infection.98,99

However, increasing antibiotic resistance—especially to clarithromycin—has led to changes in guidelines, favoring tailored therapy based on susceptibility testing or the use of bismuth-based quadruple regimens.100

Mucosal Protective Agents

Mucosal protective agents, including prostaglandin analogs and cytoprotective drugs, are particularly useful in preventing NSAID-induced ulcers. These agents enhance mucosal defense mechanisms and may be used in high-risk patients requiring long-term NSAID therapy.23 Prostaglandin analogs such as misoprostol exert their protective effects by inhibiting gastric acid secretion while simultaneously increasing mucus and bicarbonate production, improving mucosal blood flow, and promoting epithelial restitution.101

In addition, misoprostol has been specifically approved to prevent NSAID-induced gastric ulcers, particularly in patients with significant risk factors, such as advanced age, a history of ulcers, or concomitant anticoagulant use.2 Evidence from systematic reviews and meta-analyses demonstrates that prostaglandin analogs significantly reduce the incidence of both gastric and duodenal ulcers in patients receiving chronic NSAID therapy, highlighting their role as effective gastroprotective agents.102

Therapeutic Approach

The management of PUD focuses on three main strategies: suppression of gastric acid secretion, eradication of H. pylori, and enhancement of mucosal protection. PPIs are used both as monotherapy in uncomplicated cases and as part of combination regimens, owing to their effectiveness in acid suppression and ulcer healing.2 First-line therapy for H. pylori infection typically consists of triple therapy, which includes a PPI and two antibiotics.7

In cases of ulcers complicated by bleeding, high-dose PPI therapy administered either orally or intravenously has been shown to effectively reduce the risk of rebleeding.103 This approach is often combined with endoscopic interventions, such as thermal coagulation or injection therapy, to achieve hemostasis.104 Surgical intervention is reserved for cases that do not respond to pharmacological and endoscopic treatment.105 In perforated ulcers, surgical management is generally required; however, in stable patients without signs of peritonitis, a conservative approach with close monitoring may be considered.104

Management of Complicated Ulcers

Complicated PUD, particularly bleeding, requires intensive management. In patients presenting with bleeding peptic ulcers, initial management should focus on hemodynamic stabilization, including assessment of airway, breathing, circulation, intravenous fluid resuscitation, and blood transfusion when indicated. Endoscopic therapy is the first-line treatment for bleeding ulcers and should be performed promptly to achieve hemostasis. High-dose PPI therapy is recommended to reduce the risk of recurrent bleeding and facilitate ulcer healing. Endoscopic hemostatic techniques may include epinephrine injection, thermal coagulation, or hemoclipping. This approach is typically combined with high-dose PPIs therapy, which has been shown to reduce the risk of rebleeding, the need for surgical intervention, and mortality. In cases where initial endoscopic hemostasis fails, repeat endoscopy or transcatheter angiographic embolization may be considered, while surgical intervention is reserved for refractory cases or patients with severe complications.105 Additionally, testing for and eradication of Helicobacter pylori is essential to reduce ulcer recurrence and rebleeding risk.106

For perforated ulcers, surgical repair remains the standard treatment, although selected stable patients may be managed conservatively under strict monitoring.107 Perforated peptic ulcer represents a surgical emergency requiring prompt diagnosis and treatment. Initial management includes aggressive fluid resuscitation, correction of electrolyte imbalances, nasogastric decompression, intravenous PPI administration, and broad-spectrum antibiotics. CT is recommended as the preferred imaging modality to confirm perforation and assess the extent of intra-abdominal contamination. Definitive treatment is generally surgical, particularly in patients with pneumoperitoneum, contrast leakage, or signs of generalized peritonitis. Laparoscopic repair is preferred in hemodynamically stable patients when expertise is available, whereas open surgery is recommended for unstable patients. Non-operative management may be considered only in highly selected cases with a sealed perforation and no evidence of sepsis or diffuse peritonitis.106

Prevention of Recurrence

Prevention of recurrence is a fundamental component of long-term management in PUD, primarily by eliminating causative factors and maintaining mucosal integrity. The eradication of H. pylori remains the most effective strategy, consistently shown to significantly reduce recurrence rates across multiple studies.6,7,23 In addition, discontinuation or modification of NSAIDs use is strongly recommended, particularly in high-risk patients, to minimize the risk of relapse.108

For patients with an increased risk of recurrence, long-term maintenance therapy with PPIs may be considered, especially in those requiring chronic NSAIDs or antithrombotic therapy.2 Furthermore, lifestyle modifications—including smoking cessation, reduction of alcohol consumption, and stress management—play an important role in improving clinical outcomes and further decreasing recurrence rates.1

Phytotherapy and Emerging Approaches

Phytotherapy has emerged as a promising complementary and alternative approach in the management of PUD, particularly due to the limitations of conventional therapies such as adverse effects, drug resistance, and recurrence. Recent studies indicate that medicinal plants and their bioactive compounds possess significant gastroprotective properties and may serve as safer therapeutic options.109

Phytotherapeutic agents exert their anti-ulcer effects through multiple mechanisms. These include antioxidant activity, inhibition of gastric acid secretion, enhancement of mucus and bicarbonate production, and improvement of mucosal blood flow. Additionally, many plant-derived compounds promote epithelial regeneration and modulate inflammatory pathways, thereby accelerating ulcer healing.110,111 Bioactive constituents such as flavonoids, tannins, saponins, and alkaloids play a central role in these protective effects. Flavonoids, in particular, demonstrate strong antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, helping neutralize free radicals and reduce oxidative stress, a key factor in mucosal injury. Furthermore, some phytochemicals have been shown to inhibit H. pylori and regulate cytokine-mediated inflammation, highlighting their potential in addressing multiple pathogenic pathways of PUD.111,112

Recent advances also highlight the development of polyherbal formulations and novel drug delivery systems that enhance the bioavailability and synergistic effects of phytochemicals. Experimental and in silico studies suggest that these compounds can interact with key receptors involved in acid secretion and inflammatory signaling, supporting their potential as therapeutic agents.111 In addition, emerging research emphasizes integrating phytotherapy with conventional treatments, offering a combined approach that may improve efficacy while reducing adverse effects.109 Despite these promising findings, the clinical application of phytotherapy remains limited by the lack of standardized formulations, variability in plant composition, and insufficient large-scale clinical trials. Therefore, further research is required to establish safety and efficacy and to determine optimal dosing strategies before widespread clinical use can be recommended.110

Furthermore, several emerging approaches have shown promise in the management of PUD. Probiotic supplementation may improve H. pylori eradication rates and reduce antibiotic-associated adverse effects.8 Recent advances in biomaterial-based therapies have highlighted the potential of hydrogel drug-delivery systems for gastric ulcer treatment. A PLGA/CMCS-based hydrogel loaded with bioactive compounds demonstrated significant gastroprotective effects by modulating the Fas/FasL apoptotic pathway and promoting gastric mucosal healing, suggesting a promising emerging therapeutic strategy for ulcer management.113 Furthermore, bioactive natural compounds, including curcumin, possess antioxidant, anti-inflammatory, and gastroprotective properties, suggesting their potential as adjunctive therapies for PUD management.114 However, further clinical studies are required to establish their long-term efficacy and safety before widespread clinical application.

Special Considerations

Certain populations require individualized treatment strategies. Patients receiving antithrombotic therapy have an increased risk of gastrointestinal bleeding and require careful balancing of risks and benefits. In addition, antibiotic resistance and patient-specific factors necessitate personalized treatment approaches.100 In pediatric and special populations, current guidelines emphasize a tailored therapeutic approach based on accurate diagnostic confirmation and antimicrobial susceptibility testing to optimize treatment outcomes. This strategy is particularly important given the rising rates of antibiotic resistance, which significantly reduce the effectiveness of empirical therapy. Evidence-based recommendations indicate that treatment regimens guided by susceptibility testing not only improve eradication success rates but also promote more rational antibiotic use and help minimize the development of resistance.115,116

Furthermore, international pediatric guidelines recommend that therapy be individualized based on patient-specific factors, including age, prior antibiotic exposure, and local resistance patterns. Invasive diagnostic methods with antimicrobial susceptibility analysis are preferred when available, as they allow selection of the most appropriate eradication regimen and enhance treatment efficacy.100,115

Another important special population is pregnant women, in whom the diagnosis and management of PUD require particular attention because of physiological changes during pregnancy and concerns regarding medication safety. PUD is relatively uncommon during pregnancy because hormonal changes may enhance mucosal protection and reduce gastric acid secretion. Management primarily includes lifestyle modification and avoidance of ulcerogenic agents. Antacids and sucralfate are considered first-line therapies due to their favorable safety profiles, whereas H2-receptor antagonists and PPIs may be used when clinically indicated. Endoscopy should be reserved for patients with alarm symptoms or suspected complications, and treatment decisions should carefully balance maternal benefits and fetal safety.117

In geriatric patients, PUD requires special consideration because the risk of complications, including gastrointestinal bleeding, perforation, hospitalization, and mortality, is higher than in younger populations. Management should focus on minimizing modifiable risk factors, particularly the use of NSAIDs, aspirin, and anticoagulants, as well as H. pylori eradication when indicated. Although PPIs remain the mainstay of therapy, long-term use should be periodically monitored and reassessed due to the potential risk of adverse effects.1,8,118

Case Reports

PUD is a pathological condition of the upper gastrointestinal tract characterized by damage to the gastric or duodenal mucosa due to an imbalance between aggressive factors, such as gastric acid and pepsin, and mucosal protective factors. Based on various analyzed case reports, the main etiology of PUD is still dominated by H. pylori infection and the use of NSAIDs, which work through different mechanisms but mutually exacerbate mucosal damage. H. pylori infection causes chronic inflammation and disruption of acid secretion regulation, while NSAIDs inhibit the synthesis of prostaglandins that play a role in protecting the gastric mucosa.119 In addition to the classic etiology, several case reports indicate rarer causes, such as non-H. pylori infections (for example, Helicobacter heilmannii), the use of certain drugs, such as oral iron supplements, and idiopathic cases with no clear cause, indicating that PUD is a disease with a wide and complex spectrum of etiologies.120–122 Table 1 summarizes case reports of PUD from various countries, highlighting clinical presentation, etiology, complications, diagnostic findings, management, and outcomes.

Clinically, PUD can present with a wide range of symptoms, from minor ailments to potentially fatal disorders. Epigastric pain, nausea, vomiting, and feeling full after eating are the most typical symptoms. However, if there are complications, the symptoms could include abrupt, severe stomach pain, peritonitis symptoms, hematemesis, melena, or shock from bleeding or perforation.129 Interestingly, some reports indicate that the clinical presentation can be atypical and resemble other conditions, such as post-surgical complications (for example, post-cesarean section), or even be disguised in patients with psychiatric disorders or complex comorbidities, leading to delayed diagnosis.119,122,124 This emphasizes that PUD often becomes a “clinically deceptive” disease and requires high vigilance in medical practice.

The diagnostic method is multimodal. Because it enables direct visualization of ulcers and biopsy to detect H. pylori or other histological alterations, upper gastrointestinal endoscopy, also known EGD, is the gold standard. Additionally, imaging tests such as abdominal CT scans are crucial for identifying complications, including perforation, wall thickening, or inflammatory spread. On the other hand, simple X-rays of the chest or abdomen may reveal free air behind the diaphragm, which could indicate a hole.125 Laboratory tests, such as leukocytosis in infection or peritonitis and anemia in bleeding, also contribute to the diagnosis. Sometimes additional tests, including intragastric pH monitoring, are utilized to assess how well the medication is working for refractory ulcers.120 Therefore, the diagnosis of PUD cannot rely on a single modality but requires integrating clinical and supporting data.

The severity and existence of problems determine how PUD is managed. Medical therapy is the primary option in uncomplicated cases. This includes using antibiotics to eradicate H. pylori, administering PPIs, and using potassium-competitive acid blockers (PCABs) such as vonoprazan in refractory cases requiring more potent acid suppression.123 In addition, misoprostol can be used as an adjunct therapy in certain cases to enhance mucosal protection.120 Conservative treatment, including intravenous fluid administration, nil per os (NPO) fasting, antibiotics, and close monitoring, can be used for stable perforation cases and has shown positive results in certain patients.125 However, in cases of serious consequences like extensive perforation or major bleeding, surgical therapy—which includes operations like omental patch repair, exploratory laparotomy, or stomach resection—remains the gold standard. Even if surgery is a successful final treatment, the death rate is still high, particularly for individuals with severe comorbidities or late diagnoses.124

PUD is a condition with a broad range of origins, clinical symptoms, and consequences, according to the case report’s overall analysis. Because this illness does not usually exhibit normal symptoms, there is a significant chance of misdiagnosis or delayed diagnosis, which raises the risk of complications and death. A thorough approach, including endoscopy, imaging, and clinical examination, is necessary for an accurate diagnosis. The prognosis largely depends on the speed of diagnosis and the patient’s clinical condition; management often yields good results when prompt and appropriate. Therefore, reducing morbidity and mortality from PUD requires a systematic diagnostic approach and greater clinical expertise.

Conclusion

PUD continues to be a clinically significant condition requiring a comprehensive and individualized management approach. Evidence from international case reports highlights the heterogeneity of clinical presentations, underlying etiologies, and treatment outcomes across different populations. Complications such as perforation and bleeding remain major contributors to morbidity and mortality, particularly in patients with risk factors such as NSAID use, advanced age, and comorbidities. While standard therapies—including PPI-based regimens, H. pylori eradication, and endoscopic interventions—remain the cornerstone of treatment, real-world evidence demonstrates the need for flexible, patient-specific management strategies. Surgical intervention remains essential in severe or refractory cases, particularly in perforated ulcers or uncontrolled bleeding. Future management of PUD should emphasize early diagnosis, risk stratification, and precision-based therapy, including antimicrobial susceptibility-guided treatment. Integration of emerging therapies and global clinical evidence will be crucial to improving patient outcomes and reducing complications associated with peptic ulcer disease.
