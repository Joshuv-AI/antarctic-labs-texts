Influence of biopsychosocial factors on pregnant women during perinatal period
Authors: Sarab N. Fadhil
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BACKGROUND:The perinatal period is shaped by biopsychosocial factors, including biological changes, psychological stressors, and social determinants like socioeconomic status and support systems. These factors collectively impact maternal and neonatal health. While individual aspects have been studied, research on their combined effects, particularly in local contexts, remains limited. This study aimed to determine the influence of biopsychosocial factors on pregnant women’s well-being during the perinatal period. This research seeks to fill a gap in current knowledge and provide insights that can improve healthcare strategies and maternal-child health outcomes.MATERIALS AND METHODS:A descriptive study was conducted from February to April with 100 pregnant women attending obstetrics clinics in Baghdad, Iraq. Data was collected through face-to-face interviews using a structured questionnaire covering demographic, reproductive, biological, psychological, and social factors. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS version 26.RESULTS:Social factors (age 25–29, education, social support, and planned pregnancy) significantly affected pregnant women. Most participants had physical issues, but these did not significantly impact adverse maternal outcomes, except for pregnancy weight gain (P = 0.000). Psychological factors showed moderate concern (64%) and were highly associated with adverse outcomes (P = 0.000).CONCLUSION:This study underscores the importance of considering biopsychosocial factors in perinatal care. The findings highlight the need for targeted healthcare interventions that address these factors to improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes.

Introduction

The perinatal period, which includes the final month of pregnancy and the early postpartum months, represents a critical phase that significantly influences maternal well-being. During this time, women often experience a combination of physiological, psychological, and social changes, which may lead to increased anxiety, fatigue, mood fluctuations, and, in some cases, postpartum depression.[1] The World Health Organization defines health not merely as the absence of disease but as a state of complete physical, mental, and social well-being.[2] A holistic approach to maternal care, grounded in the biopsychosocial model, allows for the integration of biological, psychological, and social determinants that collectively influence health outcomes during the perinatal period.[3] Physiological changes such as hormonal fluctuations, weight gain, increased blood volume, and metabolic shifts are inherent to pregnancy and affect a woman’s physical condition.[45] In parallel, social factors—including socioeconomic status, access to healthcare, and the availability of social support—play a pivotal role in shaping both maternal and neonatal outcomes.[1] Psychological aspects, such as stress, mood swings, ambivalence, anxiety, and depressive symptoms, are also prominent during this stage and demand careful attention.[6] Despite the recognition of these domains individually, there remains limited research exploring their combined influence, particularly within specific sociocultural contexts such as Iraq. Therefore, applying a biopsychosocial framework to understand and address the multidimensional challenges faced by pregnant women can inform more comprehensive and effective perinatal care strategies.

Materials and Methods

Study design and setting

A descriptive Analytical study design is conducted to evaluate biopsychosocial factors during pregnancy for pregnant women and to determine the influence of biopsychosocial factors on pregnant women’s well-being during the perinatal period from February 1 to April 30 at Al-Elwyia Maternity Teaching Hospital and Al-Yarmook Teaching Hospital in Baghdad City, Iraq.

Study participants and sampling

The study included a non-probability (purposive) sample method and 100 pregnant women. The researcher and the pregnant woman interviewed the sample before giving birth. In addition, communication was performed after birth. The inclusion criteria for the study were singleton pregnancy, multiple and primiparas, and no medical or obstetric complications during pregnancy. The exclusion criteria for the study were women with medical or obstetric complications during pregnancy and women who declined participation.

Data collection tools and technique

The data was collected daily during the official working days. The questionnaire lasted for 10–15 minutes for each woman. Women were informed that they could interrupt the interview and withdraw whenever they wanted to answer or felt it was challenging to complete the questionnaire. The interview approach was used for data collection. The authors constructed a questionnaire. It mainly consists of five parts:

Ethical considerations

Data collection was started after getting project approval from the University of Baghdad, the scientific affairs department, with code No. S.A.3982, also from the College of Nursing authority, and official approval. Ethical approval was obtained from the Scientific Research Ethical Committee of the College of Nursing Authority, University of Baghdad. In addition, oral approval was obtained from each participant who agreed to share in this study. Before data collection, participants were asked to sign an informed consent form indicating that they had been informed about the study, its objectives, and their right to refuse or accept participation. Confidentiality of the participants’ information was respected, and only the researchers and the ethics committees had the right to access the data and report it as group data. The questionnaire was anonymous, and the data were analyzed group-wise, so there was no link between the results of this study and any of the individual participants. The privacy of the respondents was maintained by preparing and setting a specific place and time for data collection. Ethics, values, cultural background, and beliefs were respected.

The validity of instrument

The face validity of the study instrument was determined by a panel of nine experts. They were all faculty members from the College of Nursing/University of Baghdad. They reviewed the content of the questionnaire for clarity and adequacy.

Data analysis

Descriptive and inferential statistics were used to analyze and assess the study’s results by applying the Statistical Package for Social Sciences Version 26.0. The statistical measures were in the form of frequencies, percentages, arithmetic mean, and standard deviation. In addition, the Likert scale was used to evaluate the scores. The graphic presentation included pie and bar charts.

Discussion

Women play a crucial role in the overall health of families, and ensuring their health, particularly during pregnancy, is fundamental to improving public health outcomes. According to the World Health Organization, social determinants of health refer to the conditions in which people are born, live, learn, and work, as well as the broader set of forces and systems shaping daily life. These determinants significantly contribute to health disparities, which can have profound implications for the health outcomes of pregnant individuals.[7]

The primary aim of this study was to evaluate the factors influencing maternal outcomes during the perinatal period. The findings indicate that social factors, such as maternal age (25–29 years), educational attainment (bachelor’s degree), social support, and planned pregnancy, notably impact maternal health during this critical period. The overall level of adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes was predominantly moderate (63.0%), encompassing various conditions such as maternal distress (66%), cesarean delivery (59%), dystocia (32%), elevated blood pressure (25%), prolonged labor (31%), puerperal infection (27%), preterm delivery (19%), and fetal distress (8%). Additionally, 36.0% of outcomes were classified as low, while only 1.0% were identified as high.

These results align with previous studies indicating that childbirth at a young age (≤19 years) or advanced maternal age (≥35 years) is associated with higher risks of adverse maternal and perinatal outcomes, including postpartum hemorrhage, eclampsia, and cephalopelvic disproportion. Furthermore, infant outcomes such as preterm birth, poor fetal growth, low birth weight, and neonatal mortality are more common in these age groups.[8]

Studies conducted in Iran, including a comparative descriptive analysis and a systematic review and meta-analysis, further corroborate the association between social factors—such as insufficient antenatal care, poor mental health, and housing instability—and the prevalence of adverse pregnancy outcomes.[9101112] Similarly, a study among Afghan women in 2020 found that over half of the participants experienced at least one adverse pregnancy outcome, with preterm labor, preeclampsia, gestational hypertension, and diabetes being notably higher in this population.[13]

From a biological perspective, the study found that most participants experienced physical challenges during pregnancy. These included weight gain (81%), breast enlargement (69%), brittle nails and hair loss (62%), stretch marks (62%), circulatory system changes (e.g., elevated blood pressure, dizziness, and fainting) in 66% of participants, and respiratory and metabolic changes (e.g., body temperature fluctuations and dehydration) in 61%. While these physical changes were prevalent, they did not significantly influence adverse maternal or perinatal outcomes, except for gestational weight gain, which showed a statistically significant association with maternal–fetal complications (P = 0.000). These findings are consistent with a cross-sectional study in Morocco, which reported that excessive weight gain during pregnancy (>16 kg) was significantly associated with gestational hypertension, macrosomia, dystocia, and cesarean delivery.[14] Similarly, a meta-analysis from European, North American, and Australian cohorts highlighted the independent effects of gestational weight gain on pregnancy outcomes.[15] A cohort study in Iran also emphasized the need for closer monitoring of maternal weight throughout pregnancy due to its impact on fetal health.[16]

Psychological factors emerged as significant determinants of maternal and neonatal outcomes during the perinatal period. The study found that psychological stressors, including pregnancy-related worries and stress, were reported at a moderate level by 64% of participants. These stressors were significantly associated with adverse perinatal outcomes (P = 0.000). Anxiety and uncertainty about pregnancy—such as concerns about the baby’s health, labor and delivery, and the transition to motherhood—were identified as major sources of distress.

Evidence from the United States emphasizes the link between poor birth outcomes, perinatal risk factors, and mental health issues later in pregnancy. Conditions like the baby blues and postpartum depression affect many women, with risk factors including prenatal depression, childcare stress, and a history of depression. Early identification of these factors can help prevent adverse pregnancy outcomes.[17] Studies conducted in London (2008) and in the United States (2017) have demonstrated that maternal prenatal stress is strongly associated with preterm birth.[1819] Additionally, a review in 2020 underlined the association between psychological distress—such as stress and anxiety—during pregnancy and adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including spontaneous preterm birth, preeclampsia, neonatal morbidity, and low birth weight.[20] A descriptive study in Serbia (2004) also found that pregnancy can be a highly stressful event, marked by mood changes, fatigue, depression, and exhaustion, which significantly influence maternal and neonatal functioning.[6]

Limitations and recommendations

This study has several limitations. First, the sample size was limited to 100 participants from two teaching hospitals in Baghdad, which may not represent the entire population of pregnant women in Iraq. Second, the self-reported nature of the psychological assessment could be influenced by social desirability bias. Future studies with larger, randomized samples across multiple regions are recommended. In addition, longitudinal studies are needed to establish causal relationships between biopsychosocial factors and maternal-perinatal outcomes

Conclusion

This study reveals that biopsychosocial factors play a crucial role in shaping maternal and perinatal outcomes. Psychological stress, weight gain during pregnancy, and social support were identified as significant influences on maternal and neonatal health. Addressing these factors through targeted interventions could improve perinatal outcomes. Future research should focus on exploring strategies to mitigate these factors and enhance the overall well-being of pregnant women and their infants.
