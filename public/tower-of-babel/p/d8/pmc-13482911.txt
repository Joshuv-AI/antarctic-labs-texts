Life's Essential 8™ and incident dementia in an urban cohort: Findings from the Northern Manhattan Study
Authors: Erin‐Leigh Gallop, Pooja Kondeti, Hannah Gardener, Tatjana Rundek, Jose Gutierrez, Mitchell S. V. Elkind, Clinton B. Wright, Bonnie Levin, Lily Wang, Christian Agudelo, Stephanie Assuras, Vanessa Guzman, Tali Elfassy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractINTRODUCTIONThe American Heart Association's Life's Essential 8™ (LE8) defines cardiovascular health (CVH) using eight behavioral and clinical metrics. We examined the association between CVH and incident dementia.METHODSWe used data from 920 dementia‐free participants of the Northern Manhattan Study. LE8 scores (range: 0–100) were calculated from information on clinical and behavioral characteristics. Dementia was adjudicated by expert clinicians using neuropsychological assessments and clinical records. We used Cox proportional hazards models to determine associations adjusting for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, and insurance.RESULTSMean age was 64 years, 60% of participants were female, 65% were Hispanic, and mean LE8 score was 60. Over 9 years, 175 participants developed dementia. Each 10‐unit decrement in LE8 score was associated with a greater risk of dementia (hazard ratio [HR]: 1.33; 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.17–1.51).DISCUSSIONCVH is important for brain health, and our results support interventions aimed at improving CVH for prevention of dementia.

INTRODUCTION

Despite ongoing initiatives, dementia remains a public health concern for older adults and constitutes the seventh leading cause of death in the United States. 1 Among U.S. adults 65 years of age or older, the prevalence of dementia is ≈10%, 2 with a disproportionately high burden among females and race and ethnic minorities. With the aging of the U.S. population, those 65 years of age or older are projected to rise from 16% in 2020 to 23% by 2060, thereby increasing the burden of dementia 3 and exacerbating the need for scalable prevention strategies. 3 Poor cardiovascular health (CVH) has been recognized increasingly as an important and preventable contributor to dementia. 4 Not surprisingly, promoting CVH throughout the lifespan is considered to be a crucial strategy for sustaining cognitive health and mitigating the risk of dementia. 4

The American Heart Association's (AHA) Life's Essential 8™ (LE8) is a comprehensive framework for defining, monitoring, and promoting CVH. 5 In contrast to focusing on individual level risk factors, LE8 captures the cumulative influence of multiple behaviors and clinical factors that guide cardiovascular prevention efforts. Optimal LE8 is associated with lower risk of cardiovascular disease (CVD) 6 and better scores on AHA's Life's Simple 7 (LS7) metric, an earlier convention used to define CVH, have previously been associated with a reduced risk of cognitive decline 7 and incident dementia. 8 However, research utilizing the updated LE8 measure, which now includes sleep health as a component of CVH, is still emerging. 9 , 10 , 11 Our study aim was to determine whether LE8 is associated with incident dementia. We hypothesized that lower LE8 score would be associated with a higher risk of incident dementia in the Northern Manhattan Study (NOMAS), a multi‐ethnic, population‐based cohort of urban‐dwelling adults.

METHODS

Study population

NOMAS is a community‐based longitudinal cohort study of 3298 urban‐dwelling adults 40 years of age or older with a household telephone at the baseline visit. 12 Clinically stroke‐free participants living in Northern Manhattan at enrollment were recruited between 1993 and 2001 using random digit dialing. A baseline comprehensive health examination was conducted during in‐person interviews in English and Spanish by trained study coordinators and physicians. From 2003 until 2008, a total of 1290 NOMAS participants (including 199 household members) were recruited into a magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) sub‐study during annual follow‐up. 13 To be eligible for the MRI sub‐study, participants needed to be (1) without clinical stroke, (2) age 50 years or older, (3) without MRI contraindications, and (4) able to provide written informed consent. Participants of the MRI sub‐study underwent comprehensive neuropsychological assessment in English or Spanish. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants, and the institutional review boards approved the study at the University of Miami and Columbia University. NOMAS data are publicly available per the National Institutes of Health (NIH) Data Sharing Policy: https://northernmanhattanstudy.org/. Participants included in the present analysis (n = 920) completed the first MRI sub‐study neuropsychological assessment, were free of dementia (defined below), and had complete information on LE8 (defined below), Figure S1.

Measurement of Life's Essential 8 components

Diet intake was assessed using a modified food frequency questionnaire. 14 A diet score was calculated based on caloric intake and intake of various beneficial and detrimental food groups to score adherence to the Dietary Approaches to Stop Hypertension (DASH) diet. 14 Physical activity was assessed using a modified National Health Interview Survey (NHIS) questionnaire. 15 The survey details the frequency and duration of physical activity in 14 activities over the previous 2 weeks, converted into min per week of moderate or vigorous physical activity. Nicotine exposure was self‐reported based on current smoking status. Sleep health was calculated from self‐reported average sleep hours per night. Body mass index (BMI) in kg/m2 was derived from measured weight and height, wearing light clothing. Blood lipids were obtained after a minimum 8‐hour fast and measured in plasma. An automated spectrometer measured total cholesterol using enzymatic procedures (Hitachi 705; Boehringer, Mannheim, Germany). Plasma high‐density lipoprotein (HDL) cholesterol was measured after precipitation of apolipoprotein B–containing lipoproteins by phosphotungstic acid. Blood lipids (non‐HDL cholesterol in mg/dL) were calculated as the difference between total and HDL cholesterol. Blood glucose was obtained after a minimum 8‐hour fast and assessed through laboratory measurement of fasting blood glucose (FBG, in mg/dL). Because our cohort did not collect glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c), we used FPG and converted it to estimated HbA1c using validated equations. 16 Blood pressure was measured twice on the right arm after a 5‐min rest period in a seated position using a calibrated, random‐zero sphygmomanometer and averaged. 6

Life's Essential 8 and cardiovascular health

Each LE8 component was scored on a scale from 0 to 100 using the methodology recommended by AHA, 12 and shown in Table S1. The total LE8 score was calculated as the unweighted average of the eight component scores, resulting in a final CVH score ranging from 0 to 100. CVH was categorized 6 as low (LE8 score <50), moderate (LE8 score 50 to <80), or high (LE8 score ≥80) based on AHA guidance.

Dementia

Beginning in 2003, corresponding to 7 ± 2 years after the baseline examination (1993–2001), participants of the MRI sub‐study were interviewed by trained research assistants. This first assessment interview included: questionnaires, a comprehensive neuropsychological battery, and rated cognitive and functional status based on structured interviews. The Mini‐Mental State Examination (MMSE) and Cognitive Failures Questionnaire (CFQ) were administered along with the neuropsychological battery, which included tests of four cognitive domains (episodic memory, executive function, processing speed, and semantic memory), as described elsewhere. 17 Each cognitive test score was standardized against its normative value, and cognitive domain–specific Z scores were obtained by taking the average of the standardized test scores within each specific cognitive domain. 17 Depressive symptoms were measured using the Center for Epidemiologic Studies Depression scale (CES‐D). 18 On average, the second and third assessments MRI sub‐study neuropsychological assessments, used to assess incident dementia status, took place 6 ± 2 and 14 ± 2 years after the first assessment, respectively. During the second and third assessments, in addition to the tests listed, the Informant Questionnaire of Cognitive Decline in the Elderly (IQCODE) 19 was also administered to a family member or friend of each participant.

Dementia status was assessed utilizing information derived from each neuropsychological assessment by consensus of a neurologist and a neuropsychologist using criteria from the National Institute on Aging–Alzheimer's Association (NIA‐AA) 20 and the Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders, Fifth Edition (DSM‐5). 1 , 21 , 22 Prevalent dementia was adjudicated using all available data from the first assessment including: neuropsychological testing, self‐reported Clinical Dementia Rating (CDR), medical records, medication use, and the CFQ. 17 A total of 16 prevalent dementia cases were identified from the first assessment and excluded from incidence analysis (Figure S1). Assessment of prevalent dementia did not include informant functional assessment data, and we could therefore not rule out the possibility of including some individuals with dementia, a limitation. However, participants at the first assessment were able to consent and travel to the center for MRI, so taken together with the other available data it is unlikely anyone was misclassified. At the second and third neuropsychological assessments, informant‐based functional assessment data (e.g., IQCODE), were added to the dementia adjudication criteria. Participants were not required to complete all neuropsychological assessments. For participants who did not return for later assessments, dementia adjudication incorporated all available data sources, including annual follow‐up data, medical charts, and Telephone Interview for Cognitive Status (TICS) assessments when available. Individuals identified as eligible for dementia adjudication met the following criteria: missing at least 1 of the 11 neuropsychological battery tests, 2 or more age and education normalized test Z scores <1.5; or 1 or more domain Z scores ← 1.5. 17 Incident dementia was defined as a new dementia case identified after the first neuropsychological assessment using all available data, including IQCODE at the second and third neuropsychological assessments, with rule‐based criteria (any domain Z‐score below –1.5 and an IQCODE score 3.6 or greater) guiding dementia status determination. 17 Time from the baseline examination (1993–2001) to the incident dementia adjudication date or censor was recorded in years.

Other variables

At the initial study visits, age, sex, educational attainment (eighth grade or less, some high school, completed high school, some college, or college graduate or more) and insurance status (Medicaid, Medicare, private insurance, or no insurance) were ascertained through interviews. Race and ethnicity were self‐reported by participants at the baseline interview (Non‐Hispanic White, Non‐Hispanic Black, Hispanic, and Non‐Hispanic Other).

Statistical analysis

We described the demographic and socio‐economic characteristics of the sample overall and according to sex. We described the mean LE8 score overall and the distribution of low, moderate, and high CVH overall, stratified by sex, race, and ethnicity. We determined whether there were differences in the mean LE8 score and distribution of low, moderate, and high CVH health across sex and racial and ethnic groups using analyses of variance (ANOVAs) and chi‐square tests. Next, we estimated the mean LE8 score overall and according to sex, race, and ethnicity among those who did and did not develop dementia and tested differences using t‐tests. We graphically depicted age‐adjusted dementia‐free survival time (in years) across CVH categories using a Kaplan–Meier curve. Each participant began accruing person‐time from the date of the first neuropsychological visit (2003–2008) until the date of incident dementia diagnosis (typically at the second or third neuropsychological assessment), or was censored at the time of their last available cognitive assessment or death, whichever came first. To evaluate the independent association between LE8 score and low, moderate, and high CVH with risk of dementia, we used multivariable‐adjusted Cox proportional hazards regression models after verifying that the proportional hazards assumption was met. All models were adjusted for age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, and insurance status (unless stratified by sex or race and ethnicity). As a sensitivity analysis, inverse probability weights were derived from a logistic regression model predicting inclusion in the analytic sample using age, sex, race/ethnicity, education, and insurance status. Weighted Cox proportional hazards models were then fit to evaluate the potential impact of selection bias. In addition, to assess the potential impact of missing covariates, in another sensitivity analysis, we utilized multiple imputation and re‐ran our analyses in a sample of 1270, imputing LE8 for those with missing exposure data. All analyses were conducted in SAS V9.4 (Cary, North Carolina).

In multivariable‐adjusted Cox proportional regression models, each 10‐point decrement in LE8 score was associated with a 33% greater risk of dementia (hazard ratio [HR] = 1.33, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.17–1.51; Table 2). The interactions between LE8 score and sex (p = 0.27) and race and ethnicity (p = 0.62) were not significant. In stratified analyses, the associations between LE8 score and dementia were significant among females (HR: 1.41, 95% CI: 1.21–1.63) but not males (HR: 1.12, 95% CI: 0.84–1.48), and among non‐Hispanic Black (HR: 1.52, 95% CI: 1.03–2.27) and Hispanic (1.35, 95% CI: 1.16–1.57) participants but not among non‐Hispanic White participants (HR: 1.29, 95% CI: 0.85–1.97). Likewise, compared with moderate CVH, the risk of dementia was higher with low (HR: 2.51, 95% CI: 1.78–3.56) but not with high CVH (HR: 0.71, 95% CI: 0.31–1.64) in the entire sample.

DISCUSSION

In a community‐based cohort of adults free from dementia, only 1 in 20 adults had high CVH. Every 10‐point decrement in the LE8 score corresponded to a 33% greater risk of developing dementia, and those with low compared with moderate CVH had over two times the risk. These results exemplify the importance of CVH as a risk factor for subsequent dementia in later life, suggesting that higher CVH health in mid‐life may help prevent the onset of dementia.

Compared with national estimates from the National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey (NHANES), which reported 20% of U.S. adults with high CVH, 23 NOMAS participants had lower LE8 scores, likely reflecting cohort specific differences—older age and lower socioeconomic status. We also found that women were more likely to be categorized into both low and high CVH when compared with men, consistent with NHANES findings. 23 In addition, non‐Hispanic White participants had a higher mean CVH compared with Hispanic and non‐Hispanic Black participants, who had similar scores. These findings differ from earlier NHANES results showing lower composite LS7 scores in non‐Hispanic Black than Hispanic adults, likely due to demographic differences, as the Hispanic sample in NOMAS is primarily Dominican and urban‐dwelling, whereas NHANES includes a broader U.S. Hispanic population. 23

Prior research LS7‐based studies, such as the Hispanic Community Health Study/Study of Latinos 24 and the Coronary Artery Risk Development in Young Adults (CARDIA), 25 demonstrated that higher CVH is associated with better neurocognitive performance. Among participants of the Reasons for Geographic and Racial Differences in Stroke (REGARDS) study, higher LS7 scores were associated with a lower incidence of cognitive decline. 26 Likewise, results from the Atherosclerosis Risk in Communities (ARIC) study showed that a higher mid‐life LS7 score was associated with a lower risk of dementia in late life. 27 Research employing LE8 in relation with either cognitive decline 10 , 28 or a dementia risk in populations 5 , 29 , 30 , 31 is still emerging. The current study is consistent with findings from the UK Biobank, a population‐based cohort study in England, Wales, and Scotland, which found that higher LE8 scores were associated with decreased risk of mild cognitive impairment, all‐cause dementia, vascular dementia, and non‐Alzheimer's non‐vascular dementia. 32 Likewise, a study of participants of the Framingham Heart Study demonstrated that each 0.16 SD higher increment in LE8 score was associated with a 35% lower risk of developing Alzheimer's disease. 33 We extend this evidence and show that lower LE8 scores are associated with greater dementia risk in a diverse, community‐based urban U.S. cohort. Of note, the NOMAS cohort differs substantially from many prior studies of CVH and dementia. NOMAS comprises predominantly racial and ethnic minority participants and individuals of lower educational attainment. Despite these demographic differences, the magnitude and strength of the associations observed in NOMAS were generally consistent with other cohorts, suggesting that the relationship between CVH and dementia may be robust across diverse populations and support applicability of the LE8 framework.

Females are at greater risk than males for Alzheimer's disease and related dementias (ADRD). 1 Although biological and hormonal factors contribute, disparities in healthcare access, behavior, and social determinants may also play roles. 34 Although CVH‐dementia associations did not differ by sex, females were more likely to have low CVH, which may partly explain high dementia rates. Low CVH was associated with increased dementia risk among Hispanic and non‐Hispanic Black participants, but not among non‐Hispanic Whites, although small sample size limits inference, as only 15% of our cohort were non‐Hispanic Whites. Of note, interactions between LE8 and both sex and race/ethnicity were not significant, and subgroup analyses were limited by modest sample sizes and small number of dementia events. Therefore, these subgroup findings should be considered exploratory and interpreted with caution. In addition, although NOMAS participants show racial and ethnic disparities in cognitive outcomes, 17 consistent with prior NOMAS research, 35 there were no racial/ethnic differences in vascular risk–cognition associations. However, other LS7 studies such as CARDIA, have shown heterogeneity by race. 25 Future research should examine whether such differences persist using LE8, particularly since it includes sleep health.

The mechanisms linking CVH and dementia include vascular and neurodegenerative pathways. Hypertension contributes to hypoperfusion, white matter damage, and neuroinflammation. 36 The Systolic Blood Pressure Intervention Trial Memory and Cognition in Decreased Hypertension (SPRINT‐MIND) trial showed that intensive blood pressure control to systolic target <120 mmHg reduced the risk of mild cognitive impairment/dementia compared to a standard target of <140 mmHg. 37 Diabetes also contributes to dementia 1 via insulin resistance, small vessel disease, and inflammation, 38 highlighting the need for glucose control. Behavioral LE8 factors, such as physical activity and a healthy diet, may protect against neurodegeneration 39 , 40 by reducing oxidative stress, inflammation, and improving vascular health. For example, healthier cerebral vessels may enhance blood–brain barrier integrity, support cerebral perfusion, and improves the brain's ability to clear neurotoxins and amyloid beta through drainage pathways. 41 Sleep health—recently in LE8—is also critical; disorders such as sleep apnea and insomnia are associated with CVD 42 , 43 and dementia 44 , 45 through mechanisms such as chronic hypoxia, oxidative stress, and impaired glymphatic clearance, which disrupt the brain's ability to remove waste and maintain cognitive function. 27 From a clinical and public health perspective, LE8 may offer advantages over focusing on individual level risk factors. It is simple, provides a standardized framework, and can be used to identify opportunities for intervention. In fact, the multidimensional LE8 framework aligns with findings from the Finnish Geriatric Intervention Study to Prevent Cognitive Impairment and Disability (FINGER), 46 which demonstrated that a 2‐year multidomain lifestyle intervention (including diet, exercise, and vascular risk monitoring) improved or maintained cognitive performance in at‐risk older adults, and the U.S. Study of Protect Brain Health Through Lifestyle Intervention to Reduce Risk (US POINTER), 47 which tested a similar lifestyle‐based approach in a more diverse U.S. population.

This study is not without limitations. First, although prevalent dementia was approximated using neuropsychological performance, self‐reported functional data, and available clinical records, informant interviews (IQCODE) were not part of the first neuropsychological assessment. Therefore, we cannot rule out the possibility that some prevalent dementia cases may have been included in this study. Next, CVH was measured only once in mid‐life, despite its changing over the life course, and one‐time assessments may not reflect cumulative exposure or variability across the life course. We were unable to evaluate whether improvements or declines in CVH over time influenced dementia risk. However, prior studies, including within the NOMAS cohort, 48 suggest that midlife CVH has a strong association with late‐life cognitive outcomes, supporting our research methodology. Although participants with high CVH generally exhibited lower dementia risk than those with moderate CVH, only six dementia events occurred in the high CVH category, resulting in imprecise estimates for this category. Similarly, our ability to detect subgroup associations by sex, race, and ethnicity was limited given our sample size. Future studies with more extensive, nationally representative cohorts are warranted to confirm our results, particularly in subgroup analyses. Although LE8 provides a validated and structured framework, not all components may fully capture the complex and nuanced relationships between CVH and neurodegenerative processes. Residual confounding cannot be discounted because unmeasured lifestyle, environmental, and genetic factors could have influenced the observed associations. Likewise, education, an important factor contributing to dementia risk, may not be measured consistently across race and ethnic groups, 49 especially among immigrants, and we could not fully account for potential differences in quality of education. Furthermore, because HbA1c was not collected in our cohort, we used fasting plasma glucose levels and converted them to estimated HbA1c values using validated formulas for CVH scoring purposes. Despite these limitations, the study has some noteworthy strengths. First and foremost, dementia status was adjudicated from comprehensive neuropsychological assessments by an expert panel of neuropsychologists and neurologists. Our study also benefits from including a diverse cohort, which enhances generalizability compared to studies relying solely on clinic‐based or homogenous populations. Finally, we utilized the AHA (LE8) metric, to characterize CVH, using standardized measurements for each individual LE8 component.

In summary, we found that CVH was sub‐optimal for most of the study population and that lower CVH was associated with a greater risk of dementia. Our findings reinforce the importance of CVH promotion as a strategy for preventing dementia. Clinicians and public health professionals should consider integrating LE8‐based interventions to mitigate both cardiovascular and cognitive disease risk, particularly in populations with historically lower CVH.
