CAR-T TEMRA and CAR-Tregs in vivo expansion are both associated with a poor response after CD19.CAR-T therapy
Authors: Luigi Carriero, Lucia Di Re, Francesco Guardalupi, Raffaella Giancola, Fabrizia Colasante, Giulia Corradi, Francesca Troiano, Noemi Michetti, Giuseppina Ricciuti, Luana Schiattone, Annalisa Natale, Nicole Santoro, Matteo Emidio Dragani, Corrado Colasante, Ida Villanova, Antonello Brattelli, Valentina De Thomasis, Ornella Iuliani, Cecilia Passeri, Stella Santarone, Paola Lanuti, Elsa Pennese, Mauro Di Ianni
Published: 2026

To the Editor,

Chimeric antigen receptor T-cell (CAR-T) therapy has proven significant efficacy in B-cell malignancies with a subset of patients that fails to respond [1–3]. Several mechanisms have been proposed to account for the failure of CAR-T cell therapy, including T cell exhaustion and apoptosis [4], antigen loss [5] and resistance of cancer cells to apoptosis [6]. Furthermore, a recent study has demonstrated how the higher composition of the infusion product in terms of CD8 with memory features correlates with a favorable clinical response 3 months after the infusion [7]. A subsequent study showed, using single-cell transcriptome analysis performed at day 7 post infusion, that the expansion of memory-like CD8+CAR-T cells occurred in patients responsive to the treatment [8]. In our study, we longitudinally monitored the expansion and exhaustion profiles of anti-CD19 CAR-T cells. In particular, we studied CD4+CD19.CAR-T (CD4+CAR-T) and CD8+CD19.CAR-T (CD8+CAR-T) lymphocytes for up to two years following CAR-T infusion and investigated their possible correlation with the clinical response. Thirty-seven patients with relapsed or refractory B-cell lymphomas treated with CD19-directed CAR T-cell therapy were included. The cohort consisted of 18 patients with diffuse large B-cell lymphoma (DLBCL), 7 with primary mediastinal B-cell lymphoma (PMBCL), 4 with transformed follicular lymphoma (tFL), 1 with transformed lymphoplasmocytic lymphoma (tLPL) and 7 with mantle cell lymphoma (MCL). Patients received axicabtagene ciloleucel (n = 23), tisagenlecleucel (n = 7), or brexucabtagene autoleucel (n = 7). The median age at CAR-T infusion was 62 years (interquartile range, 52–68), and 56.8% of patients were male. Most patients presented advanced-stage disease (stage III–IV, 86.5%). Bridging therapy was administered in 78.4% of cases before CAR-T infusion. The study was approved by the local institutional ethics committee (RA/0404367) and conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki. Written informed consent was obtained from all patients before sample collection and analysis. Patients were classified as responders (complete or partial response) and non-responders (stable or progressive disease) based on clinical evaluation at the first response assessment after CAR-T infusion. Clinical outcomes and baseline characteristics of the cohort are summarized in Supplementary Table 1. Peripheral blood (PB) samples were analyzed by multiparametric flow cytometry to assess the frequency and phenotype of circulating CAR-T cells and their subsets, as already reported [9]. The flow cytometry reagent list is shown in Supplementary Table 2. In particular, CAR-T cells were identified as CD3⁺CAR⁺ lymphocytes. Within this population, CD4⁺ (CD3⁺CAR⁺CD4⁺) and CD8⁺ (CD3⁺CAR⁺CD8⁺) CAR-T subsets were quantified and expressed as percentage of CD3⁺CAR⁺ cells. The CD8⁺TEMRA CAR-T subset was defined as CD3⁺CAR⁺CD8⁺CD45RA⁺CCR7⁻ and expressed as percentage of CD3⁺CAR⁺CD8⁺ cells, while the frequency of total CD8⁺T cells was calculated within the CD3⁺CAR⁺ lymphocyte gate. Regulatory CAR-T cells (CAR-Tregs) were identified as CD3+CAR+CD4+CD25+CD127dim/−FOXP3+ lymphocytes. In addition, the expression of PD-1, LAG3, and TIM3 was evaluated within CD4⁺ and CD8⁺CAR-T cell subsets.

Longitudinal analysis of circulating CD19.CAR-T cells showed dynamic changes in the relative distribution of CD4⁺CAR-T and CD8+CAR-T subsets after infusion (Fig. 1a, upper panel). An inversion of the CD4/CD8 ratio was observed from day +7, driven by the preferential expansion of CD8⁺CAR-T cells during the early post-infusion phase. Thereafter, CAR-T cell levels progressively contracted and, from approximately 3 months onward, CD4⁺ and CD8⁺ frequencies stabilized at comparable levels. Given the established role of early CAR-T cell expansion in treatment efficacy, we investigated whether CD8⁺CAR-T cell kinetics differed according to clinical response. Responding patients, evaluated as complete response (CR) and partial response (PR), displayed a significantly greater expansion of CD8⁺CAR-T cells compared with non-responders, evaluated as stable disease (SD) and progressive disease (PD), particularly at day +21 (P = 0.0032) (Fig. 1a, lower panel). We then confirmed the prognostic role of CD8⁺CAR-T cell expansion. Indeed, we identified a threshold of 22% (frequency of CD8⁺CAR-T cells), which predicted response significantly stratifying patients into two groups: patients with CD8⁺CAR-T cell frequency >22% showed a better outcome, while patients with CD8⁺CAR-T cell frequency <22% showed a worse outcome, with a lower PFS (HR:5.9; CI:0.84–23.80; P < 0.0001), and OS (HR: 4.5; CI:0.87–20.71; P = 0.0057; Fig. 1a lower panel and Supplementary Fig. 1A, B). Interestingly, this threshold corresponded with the mean frequency of CD8⁺CAR-T cells in non-responders patients. To investigate potential determinants of this expansion, we analyzed the phenotype of the infusion product. Non-responders exhibited a higher proportion of CD8⁺CAR-T cells with a terminal effector memory phenotype (TEMRA) in the infusion product compared with responders (P = 0.0404; Fig. 1c). Consistently, the median frequency of circulating CD8⁺CAR-T cells inversely correlated with the proportion of CD8⁺CAR⁺TEMRA cells in the infusion product (Pearson r = −0.57; P = 0.0315; Fig. 1d), indicating that enrichment in terminally differentiated cells may be associated with reduced CD8⁺CAR-T cell expansion. No significant differences in the expression of exhaustion markers PD-1, TIM-3, and LAG-3 were observed between responders and non-responders, either in the infusion product or at day +14 post-infusion, suggesting that baseline or early expression of these markers may not be a determinant of treatment response in this cohort (data not shown). Interestingly, when we analyzed the frequency of CD8⁺TEMRA CAR-T cells from day 1 to day 21 post infusion (expansion period) responders showed value significantly lower than non-responders (Fig. 1e). We also evaluated the frequency of CAR-Tregs in the infusion product and after infusion up to day +21. The frequency of Tregs in the pre-infusion product did not correlate with clinical response (Fig. 2a). Instead, differences became evident post-infusion, as CAR-Tregs expanded more prominently in non-responders than in responders (Fig. 2b). Our study demonstrated that the in vivo expansion of CAR-T cells is characterized by an inversion of the CD4/CD8 ratio. This inversion appears significant in patients responding to treatment. Immunophenotypic characteristics allowed us to identify two dynamic markers correlated with clinical response. One of these is the number of CD8+TEMRA CAR-T cells present in the infusion product; a high count of these cells is associated with a lack of treatment response and reduced in vivo expansion of CAR-T cells. Consistently, Deng et al. found that patients with 3-fold higher frequencies of CAR-T CD8+ CM (central memory) signatures achieved a complete response by PET/CT at their 3-month follow-up [7]. While CM T cells represent a source of long-lived effector cells essential for both anti-neoplastic and anti-infective activity [10], TEMRA are a subset of T cells [10] that carry higher levels of exhaustion-related genes, which can limit their durability in the body [10]. Manufacturing studies aim to limit TEMRA and effector memory populations, prioritizing stem cell memory or central memory phenotypes to enhance the persistence of CAR-T cells in vivo should be developed. We also found that the dynamic change of the CAR-Treg frequency after infusion is clinically relevant. In contrast to Haradhvala et al. [8], the percentage of CAR-Tregs in the infusion product did not differ between responders and non-responders; however, in vivo CAR-Tregs expanded preferentially in non-responding patients. CAR-Treg, identified with CD4/Helios double positivity by using single-cell proteomic analysis, were already described as expanded in non-responders CAR-T patients [11]. In our study, CAR-Tregs are defined as CD4/FOXP3 double-positive cells as identified by flow cytometric analysis. FOXP3 represents the master regulator gene of the Treg population [12] while Helios-positive cells represent 70–80% of FOXP3+ cells [13]. Developing methods for CD4/CD25 cell depletion from the apheresis product could enable the production of more potent CAR-T cells. Taken together, these data demonstrate how the identification of simple flow cytometric markers (failure of CD8+CAR-T expansion associated with the increase of CD8+CAR-T TEMRA and CAR-Tregs) could allow for the rapid and early identification of patients who will not respond to CAR-T therapy. If validated in a larger cohort, this could enable the rapid scheduling of new therapies. Interestingly, the frequency of Tregs in the infusion product does not correlate with clinical response. On the other hand, it is known that the infusion of Tregs leads to their in vivo expansion within the lymphodepleted and pro-inflammatory environment of patients undergoing allogeneic transplantation [14]. Furthermore, it has been demonstrated in heart transplant models that Treg infusion is associated with an expansion of TEMRA cells [15]. The combination of the potent immunosuppressive effect of Tregs on CD4 and CD8 memory clones, alongside the expansion of TEMRAs—which exhibit a shorter half-life and exhaustion features—could therefore create the conditions for a reduced response to CAR-T therapy. In conclusion, our study identified two dynamic biomarkers (CAR-TEMRA and CAR-Tregs), both of which are predictive of a poor treatment response. Simultaneously, the consistent presence of these two cell populations in the infusion product highlights the need for a double purging of these cells from the starting material to achieve more potent anti-tumor killing activity.
