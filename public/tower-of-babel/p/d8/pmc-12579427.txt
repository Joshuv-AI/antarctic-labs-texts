A machine learning model including pentraxin-3 as predictor of outcomes in community-acquired pneumonia
Authors: Antonio Voza, Stefano Aliberti, Fabrizio Bonelli, Paolo Ingallinella, Elisa Ghezzi, Chiara Mauro, Clara Rossini, Claudio Mastronardo, Sonia Felotti, Barbara Bottazzi, Cecilia Garlanda, Claudia Zierold, Giorgio Ghignoni, Alberto Mantovani
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundThe clinical diagnosis, severity assessment, and outcome prognostication of community-acquired pneumonia (CAP) remain challenging due to the complex disease pathophysiology. Accurate outcome prediction is crucial for optimizing patient management, reducing mortality, and minimizing hospital and ICU admissions.MethodsIn this prospective observational cohort study, 228 CAP patients with varying degrees of disease severity were assessed. Clinical and demographic data, along with multiple biomarker measurements, including pentraxin-3 (PTX3), were analysed longitudinally. The primary outcome was clinical failure.ResultsAmong the single parameters evaluated, the oxygen saturation to fraction of inspired oxygen ratio (SpO2/FiO2), PTX3, and mid-regional pro-adrenomedullin (MRproADM) demonstrated the strongest predictive performance, with areas under the curve (AUC) of 0.799, 0.709, and 0.647, respectively. Machine learning (ML) experiments integrating multiple features identified the optimal algorithm for outcome prediction, combining these stand-alone markers at baseline and 72 h. The optimal ML model achieved an AUC of 0.950 (95% CI 0.83–0.96), recall of 92.6%, accuracy of 92.0%, and precision of 86.6%, representing a > 15% AUC improvement over any individual biomarker.ConclusionsWhile SpO2/FiO2 remains the most reliable stand-alone prognostic marker, PTX3 demonstrated significant independent outcome predictive value. When integrated with other biomarkers using ML-based models, outcome prediction significantly improved, underscoring its potential for CAP patient management.Trial registration nNCT06491004 (ClinicalTrials.gov).Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12967-025-07142-6.

Introduction

Pneumonia remains one of the leading infectious causes of mortality worldwide [1]. Severe cases often require intensive care due to respiratory failure and multiorgan involvement. Clinical diagnosis and accurate assessment of disease severity are challenging due to the complex pathophysiology of pneumonia. Stratifying patients with community acquired pneumonia (CAP) is particularly difficult when developing triage strategies or interventions to reduce mortality, hospitalizations, and intensive care unit (ICU) occupancy[1].

The management of adult patients with pneumonia largely depends on disease severity assessments outlined in the American Thoracic Society guidelines [2, 3]. These guidelines include major criteria, such as septic shock and respiratory failure requiring mechanical ventilation, and minor criteria, such as extensive radiographic infiltrates, increased respiratory rates, and hypotension [2]. Additionally, scoring systems like the Pneumonia Severity Index (PSI), CURB-65 and Sequential Organ Failure Assessment (SOFA) [4–7] are commonly used to predict severity and mortality risk. However, given the limitations of traditional assessment tools, biomarkers have been explored for their prognostic potential in CAP.

Various biomarkers have been evaluated for their ability to predict CAP severity and outcomes, including procalcitonin (PCT),C-reactive protein (CRP), and interleukin-6 (IL-6) [1, 8–16], as well as mid-regional proadrenomedullin (MRproADM) and N-terminal prohormone of brain natriuretic peptide (NTproBNP) [1, 10, 17]. Pentraxin-3 (PTX3) has garnered attention as a key component of innate immunity. Produced by myeloid cells, endothelial cells, and respiratory epithelial cells, PTX3 plays a critical role in pathogen resistance and inflammation regulation [18–23]. Elevated PTX3 concentrations have been observed in bacterial and viral infections [24–26], sepsis [27–31], ICU-admitted patients [32], and cardiovascular diseases [33, 34]. In addition, autoantibodies directed against PTX3 have been identified in autoimmune diseases and have a potential diagnostic and prognostic role [35].

Previous studies suggest that PTX3 may serve as a prognostic marker in CAP, given its strong association with inflammatory conditions [21, 24, 36]. However, the inherent limitations of single biomarkers necessitate a more integrative approach. Machine learning (ML) has emerged as a promising tool to enhance predictive accuracy by combining multiple biomarkers with clinical features [2, 37, 38]. This study prospectively evaluates PTX3 as a novel CAP biomarker and leverages ML to develop a prognostic algorithm incorporating PTX3, clinical parameters, and existing biomarkers.

Material and methods

Study design and subjects

This prospective, observational, cohort study was conducted at the IRCCS Humanitas Research Hospital (Rozzano, Italy), and Humanitas Gavazzeni Hospital (Bergamo, Italy), from October 2021 to April 2023. Adults (≥ 18 years) diagnosed with CAP in the Emergency Room were enrolled. Exclusion criteria included inability to provide informed consent, or pregnancy. CAP diagnosis was based on new pulmonary infiltrate detected by thoracic imaging, along with at least one of the following criteria: (i) new or worsening cough with or without sputum production and/or purulent respiratory secretions; (ii) fever (documented rectal or oral temperature ≥ 37.8 °C) or hypothermia (documented rectal or oral temperature < 36 °C); (iii) evidence of systemic inflammation, such as abnormal white blood cell count (leucocytosis > 10,000/mm3, leukopenia < 4000/mm3), or elevated CRP or PCT levels exceeding the local upper limit. CAP was defined as pneumonia acquired outside healthcare settings.

The study was conducted in accordance with the ethical principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki and local regulations. All participants provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Humanitas Research Hospital, approval n° 2085/2021. Data were collected in a certified electronic case report form, with regular monitoring for accuracy and compliance. The trial is registered at ClinicalTrials.gov (NCT06491004).

Study procedures

Upon enrolment, patients underwent plasma sample collection within 24 h of CAP diagnosis for biomarker analysis [4–6]. Baseline assessments included SOFA, PSI, and the CURB-65 scores. Patient management adhered to standard operating procedures and international guidelines, including early antibiotic administration and mechanical ventilation when necessary [39]. Hospitalized patients were monitored at day 3 (72 h), day 7, and at discharge, while outpatients received follow-up calls at 30 days. Adverse events were recorded according to Good Clinical Practice guidelines.

Study outcome and study groups

The primary outcome was clinical failure, defined as the occurrence of at least one of the following post-antibiotic initiation: (i) oxygen escalation, (ii) ICU transfer, or (iii) 28-day all-cause mortality. Patients were categorized into Group A (clinical failure) and Group B (stable outcome).

Biomarkers

A range of biomarkers was evaluated for prognostic significance, including PTX3, which was quantified using a novel automated quantitative sandwich chemiluminescence immunoassay developed for this study (Supplementary Fig. 1). The reference range in a healthy population was determined to be 2.16 ng/mL (2.01–2.30 ng/mL, 95% CI).

Other biomarkers, including NTproBNP, CRP, PCT, IL-6, MRproADM, 1,25 dihydroxyvitamin D, were measured using commercial assays. CRP and PCT were tested at the clinical sites, while other biomarkers were analysed at a centralized laboratory (DiaSorin SpA, Bresso, Italy). Samples were coded, and the technician performing the assays was blinded to patient information. After testing, demographic, clinical, laboratory, and outcome data were extracted from the Case Report Forms for analysis.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using MedCalc v20. Clinical outcomes were categorized as either clinical failure (death, ICU transfer, or escalation of ventilation) or stable status. Chi-square (χ2) tests assessed differences in clinical and demographic frequencies between groups. The nonparametric Mann–Whitney and Wilcoxon rank tests were used to assess significant differences in biomarker levels between various groups. The diagnostic performance of individual biomarkers in discriminating patients with clinical failure from those with clinical stability was assessed using receiver operating characteristics (ROC) analysis, with the area under the curve (AUC) as the summary measure. The corresponding optimal criterion value (Youden Index) was determined for each biomarker. Significant p-values are in bold in the tables.

Machine learning

Multiple ML models were evaluated for prognostic performance including RandomForest, LightGBM, Decision Tree, Logistic Regression, Gaussian NaiveBayes, SVC, LinearSVC, and Kneighbors Classifiers. XGBoost (eXtreme Gradient Boosting) [40] demonstrated the highest predictive accuracy and robustness, and was therefore selected for the main analysis.

Dataset preparation and splitting. The feature set included demographic and clinical information collected via CRF, severity scores (SOFA, PSI, CURB-65), complete blood count variables (white/red blood cells, platelets, neutrophils, lymphocytes, monocytes, hemoglobin, mean corpuscular volume, neutrophil/lymphocyte ratio, monocyte/lymphocyte ratio), and the selected biomarkers (PTX3, MRproADM, IL-6, SpO2/FiO2 ratio). For the main experiments, the dataset was randomly divided into training (67%) and test (33%) subsets using train_test_split function, with stratification to preserve the same ratio of clinical stability vs. clinical failure outcomes across sets. A fixed random seed was applied for reproducibility.

Handling of missing data. Variables with missing values were imputed using group-specific means when appropriate. Variables with variables with insufficiently observed data were excluded from both training and test sets to maintain consistency.

Class imbalance correction. Class imbalance was addressed by applying class weights {0:1, 1:3} and by tuning the XGBoost scale_pos_weight parameter in XGBoost, which adjusts the relative importance of each class during training. Since the clinical failure outcome was underrepresented, these steps improved sensitivity for detecting the minority class.

Model training and evaluation. Model development involved four steps: (i) dataset loading and preprocessing, (ii) random division into training (67%) and test (33%) sets, (iii) model training with XGBoost (n_estimators = 100, scale_pos_weight as above), where n_estimators specifies the number of sequential decision trees, each improving prediction by correcting errors from the previous one, and (iv) evaluation on the held-out test set. Performance was assessed with accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, confusion matrix, and ROC-AUC. ROC curves were computed from predicted probabilities, and AUC was used as the primary performance metric.

Bootstrapping for confidence intervals. Uncertainty in performance (95% CI) was quantified using bootstrapping with 100 and 1000 iterations [37]. For each bootstrap iteration: (i) resamples of the training set were created with replacement, (ii) a separate XGBoost model was trained, (iii) predictions were generated on the fixed test set, and (iv) the mean and standard deviation of the resulting AUCs were used to construct 95% confidence intervals.

Comparison with alternative classifiers. The same evaluation pipeline, including bootstrapping, was applied to additional classifiers (logistic regression, random forest, SVM, LightGBM). Both XGBoost and LightGBM achieved the highest performance. LightGBM was faster and competitive on larger resampled datasets, but XGBoost provided narrower confidence intervals and greater stability on the present dataset, justifying its choice for the main analysis.

Cross-validation. Cross-validation served as a complementary robustness check beyond the single train/test split. Stratified fivefold cross-validation was performed on the full dataset. In each fold, the model was trained on 80% of the data and tested on 20%, preserving class balance. Performance metrics (accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, ROC-AUC) were averaged across folds, with mean and standard deviation reported.

Model interpretation. Feature importance and interpretability were assessed with SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) [38] values. SHAP quantified the marginal contribution of each feature to model predictions. Global importance was visualized with bar plots, and local interpretability with summary and decision plots, including analyses of misclassified cases. This highlighted the roles of biomarkers such as PTX3 (baseline and 72 h), MRproADM (baseline and 72 h), and SpO2/FiO2 (72 h) in shaping predictions.

Final feature and algorithm selection. The optimal configuration combined PTX3 (baseline and 72 h), MRproADM (baseline and 72 h), and SpO2/FiO2 (72 h) as features, with XGBoost as the classifier. This setup offered the most stable and interpretable predictive performance, balancing accuracy, calibration, and interpretability.

Results

A total of 228 CAP patients (63% were male; median age: 74 years (IQR 63–83)) were enrolled during the study period (Table 1). Among them, 47 (20.6%) experienced clinical failure (Group A), including 17 (7.4%) patients who died, 11 (4.8%) patients who were transferred to the ICU during hospitalization and 31 (13.6%) who required oxygen escalation (Fig. 1 and Table 1). Significant differences in clinical failure rates were observed based on sex and pneumonia classification. Male patients had a higher clinical failure rate (25.2%) compared to females (12.9%). While most clinical blood parameters did not differ significantly between the two study groups, biomarkers and oxygen saturation to fraction of inspired oxygen ratio (SpO2/FiO2) measurements showed significant differences. Baseline PCT, CRP, and IL-6 levels were not significantly different between the two study groups, but all biomarkers except NTproBNP exhibited highly significant differences between baseline and 72 h post-enrolment (Table 2). Paired 72 h samples were not available for all patients, explaining slight baseline differences between Tables 1 and 2. Although all biomarker values showed a decline within the first 3 days (Fig. 2, Table 2), the degree of decline was somewhat lower in patients with clinical failure (5–31% less decline). SpO2/FiO2 values decreased in case of clinical failure.

ROC analysis was performed for each biomarker (Supplementary Fig. 2). Among the biomarkers tested, SpO2/FiO2 (AUC = 0.799) showed the highest predictive value, followed by PTX3 (AUC = 0.709) and MRproADM (AUC = 0.647). At the associated criterion to the Youden index (Table 3), sensitivity and specificity were 74.4% (95% CI 58.8–86.5%) and 72.3% (95% CI 64.8–78.9%) for SpO2/FiO2, and 76.7% (95% CI 61.4–88.2%) and 63.3% (95% CI 55.4–70.6%) for PTX3, respectively. The prevalence of clinical failure was 20.6%, yielding a positive predictive value of 41.1% (95% CI 34.0–48.5%) and a negative predictive value of 91.6% (95% CI 86.6–94.8%) for SpO2/FiO2, and a positive predictive value of 35.1% (95% CI 29.5–41.3%) and a negative predictive value of 91.3% (95% CI 85.7–94.8%) for PTX3. The negative predictive value was particularly important, as only 8.7% of patients classified as stable progressed to clinical failure.

ML analysis was explored to enhance prediction capabilities beyond single-feature performance. While all features were evaluated across multiple ML models, the best-performing model (Fig. 3A) integrated PTX3 and MRproADM at baseline and 72 h, along with SpO2/FiO2 at 72 h (reference model). This combination improved AUC by 15% and 25% compared to SpO2/FiO2 or PTX3 alone, respectively. A two-step ML approach was tested, where baseline PTX3 and MRproADM were used for an initial triage of low-risk patients before incorporating 72 h biomarker values. However, the simpler one-step approach was preferred due to comparable predictive performance. Robustness was checked by stratified fivefold cross-validation (Supplementary Table 1). Consistent with the single-split experiments, the reference model achieved the highest and most stable performance across folds. Performance parameters were slightly lower as expected when testing across multiple partitions. Additional ML experiments were carried out to evaluate the contribution of specific features: (1) replacing PTX3 with CRP or PCT, (2) replacing MRproADM with NTproBNP, and (3) removing SpO2/FiO2. Additional analysis included adding IL-6 and NTproBNP to explore whether they enhanced outcome prediction. Performance metrics based on 75 patients in the testing set, following training of the algorithm on 153 patients, are presented in Table 4. Given the imbalanced dataset, recall was prioritized to maximize detection of clinical failure and reduce false negatives. Recall is particularly important when the cost of false negatives (patients progressing to clinical failure without proper intervention) is high. Omission of SpO2/FiO2 resulted in a 28.1% decrease in recall, reinforcing its critical role. SHAP analysis confirmed SpO2/FiO2 as the most influential feature in predicting clinical failure (Fig. 3B). Recall reductions for other models ranged from 3.9 to 12.1%, demonstrating that PTX3 and MRproADM were the most effective biomarkers for predicting outcomes. The inclusion of IL-6 and NTproBNP improved model performance by providing additional predictive information.

Discussion

This study identified key predictors of clinical failure in CAP patients and demonstrated the enhanced predictive power of a ML model incorporating PTX3. The main findings are: (1) the SpO2/FiO2 ratio, PTX3, and MRproADM were the most effective individual predictors of clinical failure, with areas under the ROC curve (AUCs) of 0.799, 0.709, and 0.643, respectively. (2) A ML model incorporating PTX3 significantly outperformed single-biomarker models, achieving an AUC of 0.950 (95% CI 0.83–0.96), recall of 92.6%, accuracy of 92.0%, and precision of 86.6%. 3) PTX3 emerged as a novel and valuable biomarker for CAP prognosis, particularly when integrated into a multi-parameter ML model. These results highlight the potential of ML-driven approaches in CAP management and reinforce the importance of biomarker-based precision medicine.

Advancements in artificial intelligence are transforming healthcare, particularly in diagnostics, through the integration of multiple parameters using ML tools [41]. Concurrently, precision medicine is being increasingly applied to pneumonia management, with recent studies emphasizing its potential to improve outcomes by identifying specific biomarkers and tailoring therapies [42]. In this study, the predictive ability of PTX3 was examined both independently and in combination with other biomarkers to predict clinical failure in CAP patients. Optimal outcome prediction, with a recall of 92.6%, was achieved by integrating three key variables into a ML model. Several methods were tested (see Materials and Methods), with XGBoost providing the highest predictive accuracy. Initially, all available clinical and demographic variables, as well as biomarkers, were evaluated and ranked for importance using SHAP values (> 25 features), but biomarker combinations proved superior in predicting outcome. As expected in a condition with complex pathophysiology, combining parameters outperformed the use of any single biomarker, achieving an AUC of 0.95 and ML performance metrics (recall and accuracy) exceeding 92%. It is important to note that the ML model does not rely on fixed cutoffs. Instead, it integrates continuous biomarker values and generates calibrated probabilities, allowing decision thresholds to be adapted across hospital or laboratory settings. This approach avoids the loss of discriminative power associated with arbitrary dichotomization, and is supported by SHAP analysis, which highlights each variable’s contribution without imposing predefined thresholds. The cut-offs derived from ROC analysis reported in Table 3 provide a population-level reference for comparing single biomarker and may vary in different settings. The contribution of the selected variables was validated by both SHAP values (Fig. 3B) and the decrease in algorithm performance when these variables are either replaced or removed from the model (Table 4). For example, substituting PTX3 with CRP or PCT and replacing MRproADM with NTproBNP reduced model performance, two markers that were most correlated (Supplementary Fig. 3). This outcome aligns with the standard statistical analysis, as the same variables identified as strong individual predictors of clinical failure were also the most significant in the ML model. One can hypothesize that each variable incorporated into the final model captures a distinct dimension of CAP pathophysiology. The SpO2/FiO2 ratio reflects respiratory function and oxygenation impairment. PTX3 represents the host’s innate immune response and plays a critical role in pathogen resistance and regulation of inflammation. MRproADM serves as a proxy for adrenomedullin activity, a peptide involved in maintaining vascular tone and endothelial integrity, thereby reflecting the vascular dysfunction commonly seen in severe infections [43]. Together, these biomarkers likely provide complementary information on respiratory compromise, immune dysregulation, and endothelial dysfunction, responsible for driving CAP progression and clinical failure. The inclusion of longitudinal measurements over the first 72 h further improved outcome prediction, highlighting the value of longitudinal risk assessment. The results of the current study are consistent with previous studies, where ensemble ML models such as Random Forest and XGBoost have shown high accuracy in pneumonia outcome prediction, with reported values of 92% and 90.8%, respectively, in a cohort of 535 patients [44].

A novel aspect of this study was the use of a prototype PTX3 assay developed on the LIAISON® automated platform, which demonstrated strong alignment with previously published reference range values for healthy individuals obtained with previous methods [45]. The other biomarkers were measured using commercially available in vitro diagnostic assays.

The sample size was a limitation in this study, as ML algorithms typically benefit from larger datasets for optimal training and testing, often requiring 2–3 times the number of observations used here. While this cohort of 228 patients is substantial for a two-center study, the sample size may have restricted the ability of the algorithm to fully capture complex patterns in the data, potentially affecting predictive performance. To mitigate this, careful feature selection was followed by robust validation techniques; nonetheless, future studies with larger, multi-center cohorts are warranted to confirm these findings and improve the generalizability of the models. The study population was restricted to Northern Italy. This relative homogeneity likely reduced potential confounding factors and made the use of mean imputation for missing data, to preserve statistical power, more acceptable, but it may also limit the generalizability of the findings to populations with different demographics, genetic backgrounds, or healthcare systems. Additionally, while model performance was evaluated on a separate test set, external validation in an independent cohort was not performed, as this was beyond the scope of the present study. Future studies involving larger and more diverse cohorts will be important to confirm the broader applicability of the predictive model.

Finally, Peri et al. demonstrated that PTX3 levels peak much earlier (around 20 h) than CRP (around 50 h), likely due to PTX3’s rapid release from tissue cells and circulating leukocytes, whereas CRP requires hepatic synthesis in response to IL-6 [19, 46]. Given this difference, future studies should assess PTX3 at earlier time points (12–24 h) to capture peak levels more accurately. Additionally, evaluating PTX3 dynamics in relation to symptom onset could enhance its prognostic value in CAP.

The findings reported here are consistent with prior work in sepsis, where PTX3, particularly when integrated into ML models, outperformed traditional biomarkers (e.g. CRP, PCT, cytokines) and clinical scoring systems like SOFA [28, 47, 48]. Further comparative studies in CAP are needed to establish the relative performance of PTX3-based tools. In addition, the potential clinical and economic impact of such a prognostic tool warrants further evaluation. A more accurate prognostic evaluation early in the disesase course could improve patient outcomes by enabling earlier intervention, guiding escalation of antibiotics and oxygen therapy when needed, and supporting timely transfer to higher-level care. These benefits could translate into fewer ICU admissions, shorter hospital stays, saved lives, and reduced costs, likely offsetting the modest costs of a biomarker testing and ML implementation. Although direct cost-effectiveness data for CAP are lacking, evidence from related conditions is encouraging with biomarker-guided antibiotic therapy improving outcomes in pneumonia [49], and ML-based sepsis detection reducing ICU costs and improving survival in a Swedish study [50]. These findings suggest that integrating PTX3 with ML could provide both clinical and economic value in CAP, meriting further evaluation.

Taken together, these considerations strengthen the relevance of the present study, which establishes PTX3 as a valuable prognostic biomarker in CAP and demonstrates that a ML model incorporating SpO2/FiO2, MRproADM, and PTX3 significantly improves outcome prediction. These findings highlight the potential of ML-driven decision making in CAP management and support further research to validate PTX3-based prognostication in larger, more diverse populations.
