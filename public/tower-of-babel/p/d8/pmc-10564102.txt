Prenatal and Early Postnatal Exposure to Persistent Organic Pollutants (POPs): What Is the Correlation between Dioxins and Long-Chain Per- and Polyfluorinated Alkyl Substances (PFAS)?
Authors: Klaus Abraham
Published: 2023

Introduction

Persistent organic pollutants (POPs) are a heterogeneous group of ubiquitous environmental pollutants with a high persistence accumulating in the food chain. Despite external background exposures being relatively low, the long biological half-lives of these compounds in humans lead to considerable accumulation, with measurable levels of internal exposure in the general population. With respect to possible effects, fetal exposure may be especially critical (expected highest susceptibility), and postnatal exposure from breastfeeding, in the case of a long duration, is expected to be the highest exposure period over a lifetime.

In this context, two groups of POPs are in the focus of scientific and public concern, namely dioxins and per- and polyfluorinated alkyl substances (PFAS). One possible effect of exposure is an impact on the male reproductive system in fetal life and/or early childhood resulting in reduced semen quality in adulthood. In the case of dioxins, evidence comes not only from animal experiments,1,2 but also from high-dose exposure to tetrachlorodibenzo-p-dioxin (TCDD) following the Seveso accident in 1976.3 The European Food Safety Authority (EFSA) used data from a study of high ambient exposure in a Russian industrial city4 to lower the tolerable weekly intake (TWI) in 2018.1 Investigations into the effect of early PFAS exposure on male semen quality in laboratory animals are missing, but epidemiological support comes from a study of 864 participants,5 which focused on the exposure to PFAS in early pregnancy only.

With respect to the latter study, the question of the correlation of dioxins and PFAS was raised in a comment,6 showing data of dioxin international toxicity equivalents (I-TEq) and perfluorooctanoic acid (PFOA) in 11-month-old children predominantly breastfed for at least 4 months,7 with a Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient of r=0.77. In their response,8 the authors argued that they expect low correlations of dioxins and PFAS with respect to prenatal exposure (i.e., concentrations in the mothers). Data on the correlations are available from our study,7 not only for the children,6 but also for the mothers, the latter of which are presented in this article.

Methods

Study details were published elsewhere.7 In brief, the study protocol was approved by the ethics committee of the Virchow-Klinikum of the Free University Berlin in November 1995. The study extension (additional analysis and evaluation of PFAS) was approved by the ethics committee of the Charité in 2019 (No. EA2/090/19). The study was performed from 1997 to 1999 with focus on dioxin exposure and involved 101 children either formula-fed (n=21) or breastfed for at least 4 months (n=80). Written informed consent was obtained from all parents. Eleven months after birth, blood was drawn in the children and their mothers. Dioxins and polychlorinated biphenyls (PCBs) were measured in whole blood using an optimized isotope-dilution method with quantification on high resolution gas chromatography-mass spectrometry. Dioxins were calculated as I-TEq based on blood fat. PFOA, perfluorooctane sulfonate (PFOS), perfluorohexane sulfonic acid (PFHxS), and perfluorononanoic acid (PFNA) were measured in plasma using an isotope-dilution method and an online extraction liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry (LC-MS/MS) system with a limit of quantification (LOQ) of 0.25μg/L. In the case of PFNA only, n=25 were <LOQ; these were set to half of the LOQ.

A number of breastfeeding mothers and children (n=27) lived in a dioxin hotspot resulting in a higher dioxin exposure without higher PFAS background exposure. As this evaluation focuses on the correlation of plasma levels of dioxin and PFAS resulting from background exposure, these pairs were excluded from data analysis. From mothers who did not breastfeed, individual dioxin measurements were available for five participants only, which were randomly selected (pool analyses of the other samples were not considered here). This results in a final number of 58 mothers included in the correlation analysis, which was done using Spearman’s rank correlation. IBM SPSS software version 26 was used for statistical analysis.

Results

Levels measured in the mothers (n=58) at the end of the 1990s revealed typical mean values (±standard deviation) for this period: dioxin I-TEq (9.6±3.3 pg/g blood fat), sum of nondioxin-like PCBs No. 138, 153, and 180 (220±114 ng/g blood fat), PFOA (3.4±1.4μg/L), PFOS (14.4±4.9μg/L), PFHxS (1.1±0.6μg/L), and PFNA (0.3±0.1μg/L). Spearman’s rank correlation coefficients for dioxin I-TEq and PFAS were found to be 0.58, 0.47, 0.43, and 0.55 for PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS and PFNA, respectively (p<0.01, n=58). The sum of nondioxin-like PCBs No. 138, 153, and 180 highly correlating with dioxin I-TEq (r=0.76) revealed correlation coefficients of 0.45 (p=0.0004), 0.24 (p=0.73), 0.32 (p=0.015), and 0.28 (p=0.037) for PFOA, PFOS, PFHxS, and PFNA, respectively. Discussion

The data demonstrate a moderate correlation of dioxin I-TEq and the four PFAS in maternal blood drawn 11 months after delivery. Compared to the corresponding analysis in the children,6 the correlation coefficients in the mothers were lower, as the data field in children had a much wider range. This is attributed to formula-feeding on the one side vs. long duration of breastfeeding on the other side (resulting in lower vs. higher PFOA/dioxin levels, respectively, compared to those of the mothers7) In the response of Hærvig et al.,8 the authors argued against a relevant correlation in the mothers, pointing to data of PCB153 and PFOS from Poland and Ukraine9 with low correlation coefficients of 0.20 and 0.23, respectively. Indeed, our data confirmed a low correlation of the sum of nondioxin-like PCBs No. 138, 153, and 180 and PFOS (r=0.24, the same number was obtained for PCB-153 only and PFOS), but obviously the correlation of these two compounds does not represent the correlations of dioxin I-TEq and the four main PFAS found in human plasma.

The observed correlations of plasma levels of dioxins and PFAS make simple association analyses of biological parameters and single compound groups difficult with respect to generating hypotheses of causal relationships, if not all compound groups can be analyzed in parallel. The latter especially is difficult for dioxin analysis, which is expensive and requires a large amount of blood.

These considerations are in particular important for the discussion of possible effects on parameters of semen quality in adulthood after prenatal and early postnatal exposure to POPs. As summed up in the introduction, evidence for a causal relationship comes from animal and epidemiological data in the case of dioxins, but in the case of PFAS, it is mainly the associations observed in the Danish study5 that support the hypothesis. To get better insights, both compound groups have to be measured allowing a comparison of the respective impacts on parameters of semen quality in adulthood. Taking all together, this complex issue of co-exposure of different possible agents in a mixture with different doses, different possible windows of exposure, and the long latency time between exposure and possible effects requires cautious interpretation of the epidemiological data with respect to causality. Diminished semen quality especially in Western countries—scientifically and publicly discussed for decades—may moreover be attributable to other chemicals and/or a variety of life-style factors, like alcohol and tobacco consumption, use of recreational drugs, obesity, and/or metabolic syndrome.10
