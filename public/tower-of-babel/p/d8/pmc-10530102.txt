Genotype-specific precision tumor therapy using mitochondrial DNA mutation-induced drug release system
Authors: Yanan Li, Ru Xu, Yonghua Wu, Jialing Guo, Fenglei Quan, Yiran Pei, Di Huang, Xiu Zhao, Hua Gao, Junjie Liu, Zhenzhong Zhang, Jinjin Shi, Kaixiang Zhang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Precise killing of tumor cells without affecting surrounding normal cells is a challenge. Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) mutations, a common genetic variant in cancer, can directly affect metabolic homeostasis, serving as an ideal regulatory switch for precise tumor therapy. Here, we designed a mutation-induced drug release system (MIDRS), using the single-nucleotide variation (SNV) recognition ability and trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a to convert tumor-specific mtDNA mutations into a regulatory switch for intracellular drug release, realizing precise tumor cell killing. Using Ce6 as a model drug, MIDRS enabled organelle-level photodynamic therapy, triggering innate and adaptive immunity simultaneously. In vivo evaluation showed that MIDRSMT could identify tumor tissue carrying SNVs in mtDNA in unilateral, bilateral, and heterogeneous tumor models, producing an excellent antitumor effect (~82.6%) without affecting normal cells and thus resulting in a stronger systemic antitumor immune response. Additionally, MIDRS was suitable for genotype-specific precision drug release of chemotherapeutic drugs. This strategy holds promise for mutation-specific personalized tumor treatment approaches.

INTRODUCTION

Precise tumor cell killing without affecting normal cells is challenging due to the shared characteristics between tumors and normal tissues (1, 2). Genomic instability and mutations are important features of cancer (3, 4). Either directly or indirectly, driver mutations in the somatic cancer genome provide malignant tumors with a selective developmental advantage (5). Given their high stability and specificity, characteristic genetic mutations have become ideal tumor-specific therapeutic targets, providing a genotype-specific approach for precision tumor therapy (6, 7).

Several intelligent strategies have been designed to target tumor-specific genetic mutation to cause highly specific damage to tumor cells (8, 9). As a widely used gene editing system, the use of the clustered regularly interspaced short palindromic repeats (CRISPR) system has led to huge progress in this field (10, 11). For example, Koo et al. (12) showed the ability of the Cas9 protein to discriminate cancer mutants from wild-type epidermal growth factor receptor (EGFR) alleles to precisely eliminate oncogenic mutant EGFR alleles, resulting in precision treatment of mutated EGFR-mediated lung cancer. Nevertheless, targeting a single key mutation is not extensively applicable, as it is difficult to identify key mutations that are widely present in most cancer cells. Kwon et al. (13) used Cas9 targeting of multiple cancer-specific mutations to induce targeted tumor cell death by the simultaneous generation of multiple DNA double-strand breaks (DSBs). However, the induction of multiple DNA DSBs inevitably leads to off-target problems due to difficulties in the simultaneous delivery of multiple single-guide RNAs (sgRNAs). Therefore, the targeting of a tumor-specific multicopy mutant loci and triggering a responsive drug release could be key to improving the effectiveness and specificity of gene-specific cancer treatment.

Mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) is a ring-shaped double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) that ranges from hundreds to thousands of copies depending on the cell type and controls the biogenesis of the oxidative phosphorylation (OXPHOS) system (14). Mutations in mtDNA are one of the most common genetic events in tumors, related to the progression and metastasis of various cancer types, and can directly affect metabolic homeostasis (15–17). With the development of sequencing (18), single-nucleotide variations (SNVs) in mtDNA have recently been recognized as important molecular features in both tumorigenesis and tumor progression (19, 20), and can thus serve as ideal targets for genotype-specific tumor therapy (21). Besides, mitochondria are the energy factories of cells, performing a variety of functions related to cellular metabolism (22, 23), leading to their recent use as effective targets in anticancer therapy (24–26). Therefore, the targeting of SNVs in mtDNA to generate multiple DSBs, in combination with mtDNA mutation-triggered drug release, would be expected to enhance the killing of tumor cells.

To convert SNVs in mtDNA into a switch for mtDNA mutation-targeted drug release, it was necessary to design an innovative tool for both molecular recognition and signal conversion. Cas12a is a type II CRISPR system with efficient sequence-targeted binding and single-base resolution specificity with engineered CRISPR RNA (crRNA) (27). Its nonspecific cleavage of single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) initiated by target DNA has been widely used for the recognition and sensing of nucleic acid information in vitro and in vivo (28–30), enabling mtDNA-responsive drug release (31). However, mitochondrial-targeted delivery of Cas12a/crRNA complexes and efficient activation of its intracellular trans-cleavage activity are the main bottlenecks for controlled intracellular drug release by the Cas12a system.

Here, we synthesized an mtDNA mutation-induced drug release system (MIDRS) consisting of programmable DNA/Mg2+ hybrid nanoflowers (DNF) capable of mitochondrially targeted delivery via the cytochrome C aptamer (Cyt C apt), caging of the photosensitizer [BHQ3-quenched Ce6 (Ce6-Q)], and Cas12a/crRNA complexes capable of recognizing tumor cell–specific SNVs in mtDNA (Fig. 1A). We have proved that, mediated by Cyt C apt, MIDRS can co-introduce the Cas12a/crRNA complex, Ce6-Q, and Mg2+ into the mitochondria, which can then activate Cas12a trans-cleavage activity by recognizing tumor cell–specific SNVs in the mtDNA. Using MIDRS, we successfully turned tumor cell–specific SNVs in mtDNA into photodynamic therapy (PDT) switches and methodically characterized their capacity to regulate tumor cell death by precision targeting of mutant mitochondria both in vitro and in vivo (Fig. 1B).

The mechanism underlying MIDRS mediation of precisely induced cell death was further investigated using Ce6 as a model drug (Fig. 1C). After targeting the mitochondria, MIDRS recognized and cleaved tumor cell–specific mtDNA, with the ensuing Cas12a trans-cleavage activity degrading the nucleic acid nanocarrier and thus releasing Ce6 photosensitizing activity. MIDRS could specifically recognize SNVs in mtDNA in mitochondrial to trigger in situ burst of reactive oxygen species (ROS) and thus induce tumor cell apoptosis without photodynamic toxicity to normal cells. Furthermore, the robust innate immune response induced by oxidative stress from the release of mtDNA into the cytoplasm combined with the adaptive immune response induced by PDT to release damage-associated molecular patterns (DAMPs), which could substantially promote dendritic cell (DC) maturation and increase infiltration of cytotoxic T lymphocytes (CTLs). Benefiting from the highly specific molecular recognition and trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a to convert tumor tissue-specific mtDNA mutations into regulatory switches for drug intracellular release, MIDRS achieved impressive therapeutic efficacy in cancers harboring characteristic mtDNA mutations with minimal side effects in vivo (Fig. 1D). In addition, we further explored the applicability of MIDRS in chemotherapy drugs and its inhibitory effect on lung metastasis of tumors. This research explores the kinetics of drug release triggered by characteristic gene mutations in vivo, elucidates specific principles and mechanisms of controlled drug release, and expands the range of gene mutation–specific individualized tumor treatment approaches.

RESULTS

Design and characterization of MIDRS

The process of MIDRS synthesis is shown in Fig. 2A. I: A multi-element DNA template for rolling circle amplification (RCA) for the generation of the DNF was designed. The template DNA complement featured three main elements: (i) the mitochondria-targeting aptamer (Cyt C apt) (32), (ii) the Ce6-Q–binding element, and (iii) the Cas12a/crRNA-binding element. The DNA primer strand hybridized on the DNA template strand can be fully extended using Phi 29 DNA polymerase, followed by wrapping around the magnesium pyrophosphate produced during DNA synthesis (33). This resulted in the formation of a nanoflower structure with a diameter of about 300 nm and a potential of about −25 mV (Fig. 2, B and C, and fig. S1, A and B). Notably, the magnesium pyrophosphate core of MIDRS would react with H+ and release large amounts of Mg2+ ions in an acidic environment (fig. S1, C and D) (34, 35). II: The 5′-end Ce6-modified ssDNA [Ce6-ssDNA, 35 nucleotides (nt)] and the 3′-end BHQ3-modified ssDNA (ssDNA-BHQ3, 15 nt) were slowly annealed to obtain Ce6-Q (fig. S1, E and F). Successful loading was achieved by complementing the free 3′-terminus with the second functional domain of the DNF with a loading efficiency of 61.1%, which was confirmed by ultraviolet-visible spectroscopy (UV-vis) and fluorometric assays (Fig. 2D and fig. S1G). III: After incubation with a 5′-terminal extension of 20 nt of crRNA, the bulge-containing stem-loop within the crRNA was anchored on the cleft of the Cas12a recognition (REC) lobe to preassemble the Cas12a RNP complex (36). The 5′-terminal extension of the crRNA allowed complementary pairing with DNF to achieve efficient (~95.68%) loading of the Cas12a/crRNA complex (Fig. 2, E and F, and fig. S1H). Analysis of the colocalization of Ce6-Q (blue for Ce6-Q), Cy5-N-hydroxysuccinimide ester (NHS)–labeled Cas12a/crRNA (red for Cas12a), and GelRed-labeled DNF (green for DNF) with confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) identified the intricate decoration of Ce6-Q and Cas12a/crRNA onto MIDRS (Fig. 2G and fig. S1I).

We then investigated drug release by MIDRS in response to mtDNA. First, it was verified that Ce6-Q could act as a substrate of Cas12a in response to the trans-cleavage activity activated by dsDNAND5, thereby generating ROS (fig. S1J). Analysis by polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE) showed the self-degradation of the MIDRS response to target dsDNA (fig. S1K). Then, ROS production induced by MIDRS was measured after laser irradiation (Fig. 2, H and I). After recognition of the target 50 nM dsDNA, the 1O2 yield of 50 nM MIDRS increased with time, showing an 88.9% release rate of Ce6 within 60 min. These results suggested that MIDRS could recognize specific DNA, leading to cleavage of Ce6-Q, release of the photosensitizer activity, and ROS generation.

To further examine the stability of MIDRS delivery in vivo, we evaluated the stability of MIDRS in different physiological environments. No obvious band downshifts or tailing was observed (fig. S2, A to C), indicating that MIDRS remained stable for extended periods in physiological environments. On the basis of this, we examined whether MIDRS increased the stability of Ce6-Q and Cas12a. Compared to Ce6-Q, which leads to rapid ROS production in 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS) (3 hours), MIDRS maintained a photosensitive inhibitory state for 36 hours and could rapidly respond to target activation by inducing ROS bursts (Fig. 2J and fig. S2D). In contrast, free Cas12a rapidly (12 hours) lost its target-activated lateral cleavage activity in 10% FBS (fig. S2E). In addition, MIDRS notably improved the stability of Cas12a/crRNA complexes in acidic environments (pH 5.4, fig. S2, F and G). Considering the storage conditions of Cas12a and photosensitizers, we further evaluated the long-term stability of MIDRS stored at 4°C, protected from light. As shown in fig. S2 (H to J), the hydrodynamic dimensions, zeta potential, and responsive singlet oxygen yield of MIDRS did not change notably within 1 week. The improved stability of MIDRS could be attributed to their condensed structures, implying the favorable suitability for in vivo applications.

MIDRS identifies mtDNA to induce cancer cell death

To evaluate the properties of MIDRS associated with its recognition of mtDNA to induce cancer cell death, its capacities for endocytosis, lysosome escape, and mitochondrial targeting were systemically studied. Flow cytometry showed that MIDRS was efficiently taken up by B16 cells (fig. S3A). MIDRS notably increased Mg2+ concentrations in B16 cells due to the reaction of magnesium pyrophosphate with H+ ions (fig. S3B), which favored the intracellular activation of Cas12a trans-cleavage activity (37, 38). Confocal micrographs showed that MIDRS could disrupt lysosomal membrane permeability through proton sponge effect (fig. S3C), leading to lysosomal escape (fig. S3D) (39) and colocalized well with the mitochondria [Pearson’s correlation coefficient (PCC) = 0.75] (fig. S4A).

Because of the necessity of mitochondria-targeted delivery of Cas12a RNP for achieving mtDNA-responsive drug release, we systematically evaluated the effectiveness of MIDRS in delivering Cas12a RNP to mitochondria. As shown in Fig. 3 (A and B), the Mito-Tracker fluorescence (red) of mitochondria colocalized well with the fluorescence of crRNA (green) and Cas12a (purple). Notably, the PCC of Cas12a to the nucleus was around −0.2 with or without the mitochondrial targeting motif (fig. S4B). This indicates that MIDRS cannot deliver Cas12a/crRNA complexes into the nucleus due to the lack of nucleus-targeting motifs, substantially improving the specificity of identifying mutations in mtDNA and reducing the occurrence of genomic DNA off-target events. SDS-PAGE was used for further identification of the Cas12a protein in mitochondria after MIDRSCyt C apt+ treatment (fig. S4C). We then performed protease protection assays to confirm the successful delivery of the Cas12 protein into the mitochondria after treatment with MIDRSCyt C apt+. As shown in Fig. 3C, the cytoplasmic protein glyceraldehyde-3-phosphate dehydrogenase (GAPDH) and mitochondrial outer membrane protein MFN2 (used as negative controls) were completely digested by proteinase K, while HSP60 (positive control for mitochondrial inner membrane proteins) was protected from proteinase K digestion by the mitochondrial membrane. The Cas12a protein bands (around 140 kDa) were clear in MIDRSCyt C apt+ groups with or without proteinase K treatment, suggesting that a large fraction of the Cas12a protein was delivered into mitochondria by MIDRSCyt C apt+. In contrast, the Cas12a protein was digested by proteinase K in the MIDRSCyt C apt- group, showing the importance of Cyt C aptamer for mitochondria-targeted Cas12a delivery.

Further analytical methods based on RCA and Cas13a from Leptotrichia wadei (LwaCas13a) also demonstrated the localization of crRNA in the mitochondria (fig. S4, D to G). Flow cytometry was then used to quantify the proportions of fluorescein isothiocyanate (FITC)-labeled crRNA and Cy5-labeled Cas12a-positive mitochondria in the total mitochondrial extract. As shown in fig. S4H, the proportion of mitochondria labeled with both FITC and Cy5 was 29.6% higher than in the control group, indicating that the Cyt C apt in MIDRS notably promoted the mitochondrially targeted delivery of Cas12a/crRNA complexes. It should be noted that the mitochondrial membrane potential (MMP, Δϕm) was reduced to some extent after MIDRSCyt C apt+ treatment (fig. S4I), indicating slight damage to the mitochondrial bilayer membrane structure. To further investigate the mechanism of Cyt C apt–mediated MIDRS incorporation into mitochondria, we examined its effect on mitochondrial permeability. As shown in fig. S3 (J to L), in the presence of Cyt C apt, MIDRS substantially reduced the MMP and promoted the opening of permeability transition pore. Together, these results indicated that MIDRS could effectively target the mitochondria of B16 cells to deliver the Cas12a protein and crRNA through disruption of mitochondrial homeostasis.

To test whether MIDRS could induce cell death through its recognition of mtDNA, we selected a specific site (20 nt) on the ND5 gene in mtDNA to construct MIDRSND5. In vitro fluorescence analysis showed that MIDRSND5 could specifically recognize the ND5 gene, leading to ROS generation (Fig. 3D). Quantitative polymerase chain reaction (qPCR) findings showed that MIDRSND5 could recognize and cut the mtDNA ND5 gene (editing efficiency: 58.5 ± 7.6%), with the ROS generated by laser irradiation further reducing the ND5 copy numbers (editing efficiency: 85.9 ± 1.1%) (fig. S5A). Similar results were found by Western blotting (fig. S5B), indicating that MIDRSND5 could identify specific mitochondrial gene loci in B16 cells with excellent gene-editing ability. As shown in Fig. 3E, the green fluorescence of ROS was detected in MIDRSND5-treated B16 cells, showing a similar intensity to that observed in the DNF/Ce6 group (positive control) and thus indicating that the trans-cleavage activity stimulated by ND5 recognition could efficiently cleave Ce6-Q in B16 cells, maximizing the release of its photosensitizing activity (fig. S5C). Furthermore, the red fluorescence of JC-1 in the MIDRSND5 + hʋ group was found to be reduced while the green fluorescence increased notably (Fig. 3F), indicating decreased MMP (fig. S5D). Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK8) assay experiments showed that the viability of B16 cells was reduced by about 86.4% after treatment with 50 nM MIDRSND5 + hʋ (Fig. 3G). These results suggest that the MIDRS-mediated in situ ROS burst disrupted mitochondrial homeostasis, thereby substantially inhibiting cell proliferation.

MIDRS achieved genotype-specific killing of tumor cells

SNVs in mtDNA are considered to be important molecular signatures in tumorigenesis and tumor progression (20). Encouraged by the results on the ability of MIDRS to target mtDNA and induce cell death, we next investigated the ability of MIDRS to respond to SNVs in mtDNA specific to tumor cells. Using the mouse B16 melanoma cell line and the mouse RAW 264.7 macrophage cell line as model cells, we designed crRNA targeting the mutated ND2 gene (mt.5465A>G), which was based on the mtDNA sequencing results of both cell lines, to construct MIDRSMT (Fig. 4A). We modified the crRNA by adding mismatches near the SNV site to improve the mt.5465A>G recognition specificity of the CRISPR-Cas12a. As shown in fig. S6A, the unmodified fully matched crRNA (crRNA0) was sufficient to distinguish between wild-type and mutant mtDNA at mt.5465A>G. We then investigated whether the mutant mtDNA-specific nuclease had off-target effects. We used the CAS-OFFinder algorithm (http://www.rgenome.net/cas-offinder/) to screen the potential off-target sites and identify five sites that differed from the on-target site by up to 3 nt in the mouse genome. Compared to possible off-target sites, only the ND2 gene can be recognized by Cas12a/crRNA0, resulting in a fluorescent signal (fig. S6B). Next-generation sequencing results showed that the mutation frequency of all five potential off-target sites were nearly negligible, indicating that MIDRS did not have a notable off-target effect on genomic DNA (fig. S6C). The transmission electron microscopy (TEM) results showed that MIDRSMT was digested after treatment with B16-derived dsDNA-MT, while RAW 264.7-derived dsDNA-WT did not cause obvious morphological changes in MIDRSMT (Fig. 4B). The dsDNA-MT increased the rate of ROS production induced by MIDRSMT significantly (Fig. 4C), further indicating that MIDRSMT can recognize the SNVs in mtDNA to initiate Cas12a trans-cleavage activity, leading to ROS generation.

To better compare the recognition of SNVs in tumor cell mtDNA by MIDRS, MIDRSWT that targeted the wild-type ND5 gene was used as a control. After examining MIDRS uptake by RAW 264.7 cells (fig. S6D), we investigated the drug release behavior of MIDRSMT and MIDRSWT at the cellular level. Both qPCR (fig. S6, E and F) and Western blotting (fig. S6, G to K) results showed that MIDRSWT down-regulated ND5 gene expression in both cell lines, whereas MIDRSMT down-regulated ND2 gene expression only in the MT cell line. The DCFH-DA fluorescent probe was then used to examine the promotion of ROS production by MIDRSMT in WT and MT cell lines. The different intensities of green fluorescence in the two cell lines indicated that MIDRSMT promotes ROS generation only in tumor cells containing mutant mtDNA (Fig. 4, D to F).

We then investigated its precise killing effects on tumor cells. A coculture model was constructed using B16 and RAW 264.7 cells (Fig. 4G). We first examined the heterogeneity of the mt.5465 locus of cocultured cells (fig. S7). Pyrosequencing results showed that MIDRSMT brought the heterogeneity of the coculture model to the same level as RAW 264.7 cells, which was not altered by MIDRSWT (Fig. 4H). Unlike the indistinguishable colocalization of green (ROS), purple (DiI-B16), and red (DiD-RAW 264.7) fluorescence in the MIDRSWT-treated group, the green fluorescence appeared only in conjunction with the purple fluorescence of B16 cells after MIDRSMT treatment (Fig. 4I). These results indicate that MIDRS can identify tumor cells carrying SNVs in mtDNA at a cellular resolution level to activate Cas12a trans-cleavage activity and induce in situ ROS bursts.

We then investigated the precision killing of tumor cells by MIDRS. As shown in Fig. 4, J and K, MIDRSMT prompted a significant decrease in the MMP in mutant tumor cells, whereas MIDRSWT affected the MMP in both cell lines. The results of live and dead cell staining (Fig. 4L) and CCK8 assays (Fig. 4M) showed that MIDRS had SNV-responsive tumor cell–killing ability. As shown in Fig. 3N, MIDRSWT induced substantial apoptosis in both B16 (86.41%) and RAW 264.7 (71.9%) cells, while MIDRSMT induced only 81.09% apoptosis in B16 cells with no obvious effects on wild-type cells. In addition, we selected two additional cell lines, the mouse colon cancer HCT116 and mouse umbilical vein endothelial cell (MUVEC) cell lines to investigate the generalizability of MIDRSMT for the precision killing of tumor cells (fig. S8). These results collectively indicated that MIDRS can identify tumor cells carrying SNVs in mtDNA to achieve precise PDT at cellular resolution.

MIDRS damages mitochondria to activate cytoplasmic DNA sensors

To investigate the specific mechanism underlying the killing of tumor cells by MIDRSMT, we used transcriptomic analysis to identify differentially expressed genes in MIDRSMT-treated B16 cells. As shown in Fig. 5A, several genes associated with cytoplasmic DNA sensors (cGAS, irf3, dDX58, and IFNβ1) were significantly up-regulated after MIDRSMT treatment. When these sensors are triggered (evolved to defend against viruses), they would boost the generation of type I interferons (IFNs) and related cytokines, which activate immune cells and contribute to the antiviral process (40). mtDNA is known to be an activator of the innate immune system, affecting both antimicrobial responsiveness and inflammatory pathology (41). Encouraged by the results showing induction of ROS bursts by MIDRS in mitochondria in response to SNV, we next examined mitochondrial homeostasis and mtDNA stress. The effect of MIDRSMT on the activities of the mitochondrial respiratory chain complexes I and II was first evaluated (Fig. 5, B and C). Compared with the MIDRSWT treatment group, MIDRSMT only inhibited mitochondrial respiratory chain activity in B16 cells, where the activity of complexes I and II decreased by ~60.0% and ~69.1%, respectively. Subsequently, Calcein AM was used to assess changes in mitochondrial permeability after MIDRS treatment (Fig. 5, D and E). The observed reduction in the green fluorescence signal after MIDRSWT treatment indicated increased mitochondrial permeability in both cell lines, while MIDRSMT only had such an effect in B16 cells.

As previously reported, altered mitochondrial permeability, seen in apoptosis caused by damaged mitochondria (Fig. 4N) and the opening of mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) (Fig. 5D), allows for easier release of oxidatively damaged mtDNA into the cytoplasm (42–44). To date, several pathways including BAX/BAK macropore (43), mPTP (42), and N-terminal gasdermin D (GSDMD-N) (45) have been reported to be involved in the release of mtDNA. As shown in Fig. 5 (E and F), the red fluorescence signals of BAX/BAK and GSDMD-N in both B16 cells and RAW 264.7 cells were notably increased when mtDNA of specific genotypes was identified and cleavage. The results of high expression of these mtDNA efflux pathways suggest that MIDRS can contribute to the release of damaged mtDNA from the mitochondria to the cytoplasm via multiple pathways. Thus, dsDNA markers and anti-HSP60 antibodies were used to label mtDNA and mitochondria to observe the distribution of DNA in the cytoplasm using CLSM. After MIDRSWT treatment, red fluorescence (dsDNA) that does not colocalize with green (mitochondrial) and blue (nucleus) fluorescence indicates that part of the DNA is free in the cytoplasm. Notably, this phenomenon was only apparent in MIDRSMT-treated B16 cells (Fig. 5G). To further confirm the origin of these cytoplasmic DNAs, we evaluated mtDNA copy numbers in both the cytoplasm and mitochondria (Fig. 5, H and I). The cytoplasmic mtDNA copy number was significantly increased after MIDRS treatment, accompanied by a decrease in mitochondria, indicating that the cytoplasmic DNA described above originated in oxidatively stressed mitochondria. The very high differences in the mtDNA copy numbers in the cytoplasm and mitochondria between the two cell lines further demonstrated the sensitivity of MIDRSMT to SNVs in mtDNA. Collectively, MIDRS can impair mitochondrial homeostasis leading to the release of oxidatively stressed mtDNA into the cytoplasm, thereby activating cytoplasmic DNA sensors.

MIDRS activates cGAS-STING in response to SNV for enhancing immunotherapy

Liberated DSB-mtDNA fragments engage different pattern recognition receptors depending on their location (cytoplasm, endosome, or extracellular space) to trigger proinflammatory and type I IFN responses. Cytoplasmic dsDNA, as one of the important stimulators of innate immunity, can evoke IFN production via the cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) pathway (46). Therapeutic manipulation involving activation of the cGAS-STING pathway by genomic or mtDNA has been reported, which would be expected to enhance the immune response to cancer (47, 48). Therefore, we investigated the ability of MIDRS-mediated released mtDNA to activate innate immunity. RT-qPCR analysis of mRNA expression of cGAS-STING–related genes indicated that the cGAS-STING signaling pathway was activated in B16 cells harboring specific SNVs in mtDNA after MIDRSMT treatment (Fig. 6A), consistent with the transcriptomic results. In addition, Western blotting showed that MIDRSMT substantially enhanced TBK1 and IRF3 phosphorylation in B16 cells (Fig. 6B), which were 5.64-fold and 1.30-fold higher, respectively, than those in the control group (fig. S9A). Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assays demonstrated that MIDRS treatment led to both cGAMP generation and IFN-β secretion only in tumor cells carrying specific genetic mutations (Fig. 6, C and D). These data suggest that MIDRS can efficiently activate the cGAS-STING pathway to produce IFNs in response to SNVs in mtDNA.

PDT targeting of mitochondria may increase the immunogenicity of cancer cells by exposing or creating more antigens, such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and high mobility group box 1 (HMGB1) protein (49, 50). MIDRSWT treatment significantly increased the levels of both extracellular ATP and HMGB1 in both cell lines (Fig. 6, E and F). Notably, MIDRSMT only increased the ATP and HMGB1 levels in the supernatants of B16 cells by 1.41 and 2.27 times, respectively. Because DCs are essential mediators for the initiation of innate and adaptive immune responses, activating T cells through the presentation of tumor antigens, the maturity of DCs was then assessed. The percentage of DCs (CD80+CD86+) was higher after MIDRSWT treatment of both cell lines, which was 31% and 28% higher than the control group, respectively (Fig. 6, G and H). Meanwhile, the B16 culture supernatants were more likely (32.4%) to induce DC maturation after treatment with MIDRSMT with SNV responsiveness. Therefore, we believe that MIDRSMT can induce ROS bursts in situ in tumor cell mitochondria, which, in turn, trigger immunogenic cell death (ICD) to release DAMPs and activate the cGAS-STING pathway, inducing the secretion of IFN-β and the synergistic promotion of DC maturation, thereby further triggering an efficient immune response (fig. S9B).

MIDRS precisely identifies cells with tumor-specific SNVs in mtDNA in vivo

Before assessing the antitumor efficacy, we investigated the in vivo ability of MIDRS to identify cells with tumor-specific SNVs in mtDNA. We first analyzed the biodistribution of MIDRS in B16 tumor–bearing mice (fig. S10). As shown in fig. S10A, the fluorescence intensity in the tumor tissue increased with the time after MIDRS treatment, reaching a maximum value at around 8 hours. Besides, the tumor tissues of MIDRS-treated mice still had fluorescent signals 24 hours after injection (fig. S10, B to D). In addition, the compact structure together with the negative charge of MIDRS would facilitate their metabolism in the liver and kidneys (51–53).

To test the ability of MIDRS to recognize tumor cells harboring specific SNVs in mtDNA in vivo, tumor-bearing mouse models were constructed using B16 or RAW 264.7 cell lines, as their mtDNA sequences have been analyzed and targeted. To better observe the experimental phenomena, we used Cy5 as a mock drug. Mouse models bearing B16 (MT) tumors were treated with tail vein injection of saline, DNF/Cy5-Q, MIDRSNT, or MIDRSMT (Fig. 7A). Only MIDRSMT was found to produce a significant fluorescence signal in B16 tumor tissue, which gradually increased over time. In vitro tissue and organ imaging and its semiquantification showed that the fluorescence signals generated by MIDRSMT were mainly distributed in the tumor, liver, and kidney (Fig. 7, B to D). To evaluate the off-target effect of MIDRS at nontumor sites, we examined the uptake of MIDRS by liver-derived cells (NCTC 1469), kidney-derived cells [HK2 and human embryonic kidney (HEK) 293T]. As shown in fig. S11A, HK2 and HEK 293T cell lines did not uptake MIDRS. Although MIDRS can target the mitochondria of NCTC 1469 cells, it did not recognize wild-type mtDNA, thus failing to trigger the release of the photosensitizing activity of Ce6 (fig. S11B). The results of CCK8 experiments further showed that MIDRS did not substantially inhibit the cell viability of NCTC 1469 cells (fig. S11C). Therefore, the fluorescence in the liver and kidney may be caused by the metabolism of MIDRS fragments expelled from tumor tissue (54). Combined with the imaging results of tumor tissue cryosections, MIDRSMT could release drug mimics in response to tumor-specific SNVs in mtDNA in vivo. Additional animal models, such as dual-tumor models (left: B16 tumor; right: RAW 264.7-tumor) treated with tail vein injection of MIDRSWT and MIDRSMT were evaluated to confirm these results (fig. S12).

To examine the spatial resolution of MIDRSMT in the detection of tumor cells carrying signature SNVs in mtDNA, we constructed a highly heterogeneous tumor model by inoculating mice with mixed B16 and RAW 264.7 cells together with the tail vein injection of MIDRSWT or MIDRSMT. MIDRSWT produced fluorescent signals over almost the entire mouse body, whereas fluorescence derived from MIDRSMT was only seen at the tumor site (Fig. 7E). Ex vivo imaging of major tissues and organs showed similar results (Fig. 7F and fig. S13). Then, we used an anti-CD45 antibody (red) to label monocytes in tumor tissue to observe colocalization of the MIDRS-generated Cy5 fluorescent signal with B16 or RAW 264.7 cells. Immunohistochemical staining showed an obvious separation between red and green fluorescence after MIDRSMT treatment, whereas the yellow signal indicating colocalization was observed in the MIDRSWT-treated groups (Fig. 7G). To further evaluate the different drug-release behaviors of MIDRSMT in cells with different genotypes, we harvested the tumors of mice 8 hours after the injection, and the Cy5 signal of CD45+ cells (mononuclear cells, RAW 264.7) and CD45− cells (containing B16 cells) were analyzed by flow cytometry (Fig. 7H). Unlike MIDRSWT, which induced an enhanced Cy5 fluorescence signal in both types of cells, MIDRSMT resulted in only a ~25.9% increase in the Cy5 signal in CD45− cells. Overall, these results indicated that MIDRS can specifically identify tumor cells containing characteristic mutant mtDNA to activate the trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a and precisely release the drug in vivo without affecting other normal cells.

Evaluation of in vivo antitumor efficiency of MIDRS

Encouraged by the excellent in vivo tumor cell recognition ability, we speculated that MIDRS can kill tumor cells carrying mutant mtDNA precisely without affecting surrounding immune cells, thereby evoking a robust antitumor immune response. As shown in Fig. 8A, we constructed a B16 tumor xenograft model with five intravenous injections at 2-day intervals, in which the equivalent Ce6 dose of the various agents was 0.009 mg/kg. The tumors (+hʋ) were irradiated with a 660-nm laser (10 min, 0.2 W·cm−2) 8 hours after injection. During the 10-day treatment period, the tumor volumes and body weights were measured every 2 days for 10 days. Tumor growth was found to be dramatically suppressed in the MIDRSMT + hʋ–treated mice with a tumor growth inhibition rate of approximately 82.6% (Fig. 8B). The tumors in the MIDRSNT + hʋ group, which did not recognize mtDNA genes, showed a similar growth trend as those in the NC group. The MIDRSMT group without laser irradiation exhibited a slight antitumor effect due to the large number of DSBs produced by the editing of mutant mtDNA. The individual tumor growth curves also indicated effective tumor inhibition by MIDRSMT + hʋ (Fig. 8C). The immunohistochemistry results further showed that MIDRSMT + hʋ treatment resulted in the greatest levels of necrosis and apoptosis in the tumors (Fig. 8D). These data demonstrate the validity of MIDRS in antitumor therapeutic applications. MIDRSMT + hʋ did not cause notable changes in body weight and major organs (heart, lung, liver, spleen, and kidney) in mice (fig. S14, A and B), although it resulted in the highest level of tumor killing. Furthermore, MIDRSMT + hʋ produced minimal effects on kidney and liver functions (fig. S14C).

To clarify the potential antitumor mechanism of MIDRS, we assessed changes in mtDNA heterogeneity in tumor tissues (fig. S15A). After treatment with MIDRSMT, the heterogeneity of tumor tissue at mt.5465A>G was substantially reduced (from 57% to 7%), because the mutant mtDNA of tumor cells was recognized and cleaved by Cas12a. Moreover, the MIDRSMT + hʋ–treated group displayed bright red fluorescence demonstrating the photosensitivity of Ce6 released by MIDRSMT after recognition of SNVs in mtDNA in tumor tissue (Fig. 8E). The influence of PDT on the cGAS-STING pathway and ICD in vivo was then examined. Compared to the NC group, Western blotting and RT-qPCR showed that MIDRSMT induced activation of the cGAS-STING pathway in tumors at both the protein and mRNA levels (fig. S15, B to H). Besides, immunofluorescence experiments showed that MIDRS prompted notable calreticulin (CRT) exposure and HMGB1 release, indicating efficient activation of ICD (Fig. 8F). In addition, the highest levels of IFN-β in the sera of MIDRSMT + hʋ–treated mice (Fig. 8G) further indicated the activation of innate and adaptive immunity.

Next, we evaluated the effects of the precise killing of tumor cells on surrounding immune cells. The infiltration of antitumor immune cells in tumors was assessed by flow cytometry (fig. S16). Because mitochondrial induction of apoptosis affects cancer cell sensitivity to natural killer (NK) cells (55), MIDRSMT + hʋ treatment was found to recruit more NK cells into the tumor microenvironment compared with the other treatment groups (Fig. 8H). In the MIDRSMT + hʋ–treated group, infiltration of CD11c+CD80+CD86+ DCs and CD3+CD8α+ T cells in tumors was significantly increased, which may be attributed to the up-regulation of proinflammatory cytokine levels (Fig. 8, I to L). Notably, tumor-infiltrating DCs and CTLs in the MIDRSMT + hʋ group were 5.6- and 5.1-fold higher, respectively, than MIDRSNT + hʋ, implying that precise killing of tumor cells while avoiding normal cell cytotoxicity could potentially promote an antitumor immune response. Collectively, these results suggest that MIDRSMT can precisely kill tumor cells in vivo with minimum disruption to surrounding normal cells and can achieve highly effective tumor suppression by the simultaneous induction of strong innate and adaptive immune responses.

Tumor recurrence and metastasis are the main causes of mortality in cancer patients. The immune memory effect is crucial for preventing tumor metastasis. To further demonstrate the effect of MIDRS on metastasis prevention, we used a B16 metastasis tumor model treated according to the schedule shown in Fig. 9A. The mice were administered intravenously with various preparations, and the primary tumors were irradiated (660 nm, 10 min, 0.2 W cm−2) at 8 hours after injection. After the last observation, the mice were dissected and the lungs were harvested to evaluate the lung metastasis. As shown in Fig. 9 (B and C) and fig. S17A, MIDRSMT + hʋ treatment not only inhibited the primary tumor but also effectively reduced lung metastasis. Besides, in comparison with other treatment groups, the lung metastatic nodules in the MIDRSMT + hʋ group were significantly decreased (Fig. 9D). Hematoxylin-eosin (H&E) staining results showed a consistent tendency (Fig. 9E). Additionally, the body weight of mice had an insignificant change during the treatment (fig. S17B). These results suggested that MIDRSMT + hʋ exhibited high efficiency for effective repression of primary and metastatic tumors.

The results of UV absorption spectroscopy showed that DOX could be successfully loaded onto MIDRS with a loading efficiency of about 74.35%.

With the MIDRSDOX assembled, we proceeded to perform the in vitro validation of their genotypic precise responsive release of DOX. Using mutated ND2 gene (mt.5465A>G) as the target gene, we prepared MIDRSDOX-MT. Both PAGE gel and scanning electron microscopy (SEM) results showed that MIDRSDOX-MT can be disassembled after treatment with dsDNA-MT, while dsDNA-WT did not cause notable morphological changes in MIDRSDOX-MT (Fig. 10, B and C). After recognition of the target 10 nM dsDNA, the DOX release rate of 50 nM MIDRS increased with time, showing a DOX release rate of 63.3% within 120 min (Fig. 10D). These results suggest that MIDRS can recognize DNA of specific genotypes and self-degrade to release the encapsulated DOX.

After demonstration of the genotype-responsive DOX release, we then investigated the property of MIDRSDOX for precise DOX release in response to mutant mtDNA in living cells. Using B16 and RAW 264.7 cells as model cell lines, we examined the DOX subcellular organelle localization of MIDRSDOX-MT and MIDRSDOX-WT in both genotypic cell lines using a laser confocal microscope. As the shown in Fig. 10D and fig. S18 (E and F), the red fluorescence of DOX colocalized with the nucleus in both B16 and RAW 264.7 cells after MIDRSDOX-WT treatment, indicating that MIDRSDOX-WT recognizes wild-type mtDNA in both B16 and RAW 264.7 cells, thereby initiating trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a to cleave DNF and release DOX. Only B16 cells showed red fluorescence of DOX diffused in the cytoplasm and the nucleus. In contrast, in RAW 264.7 cells with wild-type loci, DOX fluorescence was well colocalized with mitochondria and had no nuclear tropism. Without release, the DOX would be trapped in DNF and holds little treatment effect. The quantification of DOX fluorescence intensity in the nucleus further indicates the results (Fig. 10E), suggesting that MIDRSDOX had the ability to identify mutant mtDNA within living cells, triggering an effective burst of chemotherapeutic drug release.

We then investigated the precision killing of tumor cells by MIDRSDOX. The results of live and dead cell staining (fig. 18G) and CCK8 assays (Fig. 10G) showed that MIDRSDOX had SNV-responsive tumor cell–killing ability. As shown in fig. 18H, MIDRSDOX-WT induced substantial apoptosis in both B16 (69.5%) and RAW 264.7 (45.8%) cells, while MIDRSDOX-MT induced only 68.8% apoptosis in B16 cells with no obvious effects on wild-type cells. These results collectively indicated that MIDRSDOX can identify tumor cells carrying SNVs in mtDNA to achieve precise PDT at cellular resolution.

Unlike PDT, chemotherapeutic drugs do not require laser irradiation, making MIDRSDOX more suitable for treatment of tumor metastasis. Therefore, we further explored the tumor-specific ablative ability of MIDRSDOX in vivo by constructing an animal model of B16 lung metastasis (Fig. 10H). As displayed in Fig. 10I, MIDRSDOX-MT treatment effectively reduced lung metastases, while MIDRSDOX-NT, which does not recognize tumor-specific mtDNA mutations, failed to inhibit lung nodule formation. MIDRSMT showed some degree of lung metastasis inhibition due to recognition of specific mtDNA mutations, but not as efficient as the MIDRSDOX-MT group. Besides, in comparison with other treatment groups, the lung metastatic nodules in the MIDRSDOX-MT group were significantly decreased (Fig. 10J). H&E staining results showed a consistent tendency (Fig. 10K). Additionally, the body weight of mice had insignificant change during the treatment (fig. S18I). In summary, these results suggested that MIDRSDOX-MT holds potential for effective suppression of tumor metastasis by recognition of mutant mtDNA and bursting of chemotherapeutic drug release.

DISCUSSION

Precision tumor therapy requires biomarkers that are completely different from molecules expressed by normal or other diseased tissues, which is hard to achieve with drug-release strategies based on molecules such as matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), glutathione (GSH), ATP, and pH (2). Given their high stability and specificity, specific genetic mutations are ideal tumor-specific therapeutic targets, providing a genotype-specific approach for human precision tumor therapy. CRISPR-Cas systems show great promise for cancer precision medicine, such as using Cas9 to specifically target genome rearrangements and mutations in tumor cells (11–13). Despite tremendous advances in sequencing technology, screening for key point mutations remains difficult. Moreover, DSBs generated by gene editing alone are very slow to induce tumor cell death. In addition, the simultaneous delivery of multiple sgRNAs and the inevitable off-target problem further hinder the application of current strategies. With the highest copy number in cells, mtDNA plays an important biological function and is considered as a cancer-driven mutation (19), which can serve as a trigger in the genotype-specific drug release system.

Herein, we reported an mtDNA MIDRS based on CRISPR-Cas12a to achieve precision tumor therapy with single-cell resolution. The multiple design of MIDRS guaranteed the specificity of gene editing in precision tumor therapy. One of the major highlights of this study is the demonstration that MIDRS can efficiently deliver Cas12a/crRNA complexes into mitochondria via the Cyt C aptamer and performs efficient gene editing, recognized as a major challenge. The sensitivity of gene editing in precision tumor therapy was ensured by Cas12a’s high-fidelity molecular recognition capabilities and efficient cis- and trans-cutting activities. Our results showed that MIDRS could identify specific mtDNA genotypes and activate the trans-cleavage activity of Cas12a to release the photosensitizer (Ce6) and in situ bursts of ROS for the efficient killing of tumor cells. Moreover, MIDRS could promote the influx of oxidatively stressed mtDNA into the cytoplasm, thereby promoting the simultaneous induction of innate and adaptive immune responses. Thus, MIDRS could identify and kill tumor cells carrying specific SNVs in mtDNA (~82.6%) with minimal damage to surrounding immune cells in vivo, resulting in a stronger antitumor immune response, which is useful for safe and efficient precision tumor therapy. Moreover, profiting from the systemic immune responses activated by MIDRS, the growth of lung metastases was effectively suppressed, which offers a long-term immune memory effect to protect from tumor metastasis. In addition, MIDRS demonstrated excellent performance in precise release of chemotherapeutic drugs in response to single base mutations in mtDNA, contributing to its promising results in personalized treatment of lung metastases.

Taking mtDNA mutations as an example, our work provides options for the rational engineering of nanomedicines with genotype specificity. However, genomic diversity and instability are intrinsic features of tumors and tumorigenesis, indicating the issues involved with using a single mutation (57). Despite being thought to be highly associated with tumor progression, KARS_G12D is found in approximately 25% of lung cancer patients, while only 15% of non-small cell lung cancer patients carry EGFR_L858R (12, 58). Although variants in mtDNA sequences are common in certain tumors, it is difficult to find a specific mutation that can act as a universal marker for multiple tumors. Thus, further development of MIDRS should focus on personalized tumor therapy, such as tailoring individual nano drug release systems to a series of characteristic mutations in a particular patient after tumor tissue sequencing. Furthermore, we hope that this study can further inspire the design and construction of other highly specific personalized drug delivery systems, based on some other important nucleic acid information, such as extrachromosomal DNA (ecDNA) or DNA/RNA methylation.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Study design

The purpose of this study was to develop an mtDNA MIDRS based on CRISPR-Cas12a to achieve precision tumor therapy with single-cell resolution. Firstly, we prepared MIDRS and characterized the single-base mutation recognition ability and responsive drug release properties of the nanosystem. Second, the performance and specific mechanisms of cellular uptake, lysosomal escape, and mitochondrial targeting delivery of MIDRS were systematically evaluated. Next, the ability to specifically identify single base mutation of mtDNA and efficiently control drug release in tumor cells with specific genotypes was evaluated using Ce6 and DOX as model drugs, respectively. Then, the ability of MIDRS to specifically identify tumor tissue carrying mtDNA in vivo was assessed using unilateral, bilateral, and heterogeneous tumor models. Finally, the performance of MIDRS for in vivo precision tumor treatment was evaluated by posttreatment tumor volume changes, activation of immune effects and the extent of lung metastasis.

Preparation and characterization of MIDRS

Preparation and characterization of DNF

RCA was used to synthesize the DNF. Specifically, equimolar ratios of 5′ phosphorylated template and primer were annealed in 1 × T4 DNA ligase buffer (40 mM tris-HCl, 10 mM MgCl2, 10 mM DTT, and 0.5 mM ATP, pH 7.8, at 25°C) to form an RCA template, which was then circularized by T4 DNA ligase. Template encodes Cyt C apt, a binding site for Ce6-Q and a binding site for the Cas12a/crRNA complex. A typical 40-μl RCA reaction system contains 20 μl of 1 μM ligation products, 4 μl of 10 × Phi 29 DNA polymerase buffer, 5 μl of 10 mM dNTP, 1 μl of 10 U/μl Phi 29 DNA polymerase, and 10 μl of diethy pyrocarbonate (DEPC)–treated H2O. After incubating at 37°C for 2 hours, the reaction mixture was denatured at 65°C for 10 min. Synthetic DNFs were collected by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min and redissolved with an appropriate amount of DEPC-treated H2O after washing twice with DEPC-treated H2O. Agarose gel electrophoresis, Zetasizer (Malvern), SEM, and TEM were used to analyze the synthesis process of DNF, zeta potential, and particle size and morphology, respectively.

Preparation of Ce6-Q

To prepare Ce6-Q, 3 μl of 100 μM 5-terminal Ce6-ssDNA, 3 μl of 100 μM 3-terminal ssDNA-BHQ3, and 3 μl of 10 × T4 DNA Ligase buffer were firstly dissolved in DEPC-treated H2O and then heated at 95°C for 5 min, followed by slowly chilling to 16°C, allowing the hybridization of Ce6-ssDNA and ssDNA-BHQ3, which were then incubated at 16°C for 20 min to form Ce6-Q. UV absorption spectroscopy and fluorescence spectroscopy were used to analyze the photosensitizing activity of Ce6-Q.

To investigate the loading efficiency of DNF for Ce6-Q, DNF was incubated with different molar ratios (1:1, 1:5, 1:10, 1:15, and 1:20) of Ce6-Q in phosphate-buffered saline (PBS) at 37°C for 30 min. After centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min, the DNF/Ce6-Q was redissolved with an equal volume of PBS. Then, fluorescence of DNF/Ce6-Q (excitation wavelength of 400 nm and emission wavelength of 660 nm) was measured by a Synergy H1 Hybrid Reader (BioTek, USA). In this experiment, we used unmodified BHQ3 ssDNA (ssDNA-nBHQ3) with the same sequence as ssDNA-BHQ3 to form Ce6-Q to facilitate the detection of its fluorescence loaded on DNF.

Preparation and characterization of MIDRS

LbCas12a (#32108, Tolo Biotech Co. Ltd) and crRNA were firstly mixed in TOLO Buffer 3 at a molar ratio of 1:1.5 and incubated at 37°C for 10 min to form the Cas12a/crRNA RNP complex. The previously prepared DNF and Ce6-Q were mixed in PBS at a molar ratio of 1:15. After incubating at 37°C for 30 min, the unbound Ce6-Q was discarded by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 20 min. The DNF/Ce6-Q was incubated with the Cas12a/crRNA complex with a 1:1 molar ratio at 37°C for another 30 min to form MIDRS. Agarose gel electrophoresis and CLSM were used to analyze the self-assembly process of MIDRS. Zetasizer was used to analyze the size and zeta potential of MIDRS. Then, the morphology and element composition of MIDRS were investigated by SEM and TEM imaging.

Ce6-Q cleavage assays

Cas12a/crRNA RNP complexes were preassembled by incubating Cas12a (50 nM) with crRNA (molar ratio = 1:1.5) in 1× TOLO Buffer 3 at 37°C for 10 min. The reaction was initiated by adding activator dsDNA (50 nM) and Ce6-Q (1 μM). After incubation of the reaction mixture at 37°C for 30 min, the fluorescence of the reaction mixture was measured by Synergy H1 Hybrid Reader (λex: 415 nm; λem: 650 nm).

SOSG assays

Cas12a/crRNA RNP complexes were preassembled by incubating Cas12a (50 nM) with crRNA (molar ratio = 1:1.5) in 1× TOLO Buffer 3 at 37°C for 10 min. The reaction was initiated by adding activator dsDNA (50 nM) and Ce6-Q (1 μM). After incubation of the reaction mixture at 37°C for 30 min, the reaction mixture was added in 20 μl of 2 μM Singlet Oxygen Sensor Green Reagent (SOSG, Molecular Probes). Then, the solution was irradiated (660 nm, 0.2 W cm−2) for 5 min. After incubation for 20 min, the fluorescence was detected by a microplate reader (λex: 490 nm; λem: 515 nm).

Preparation of DNF/DOX

DNF was prepared by RCA reaction and subsequently incubated with appropriate amount of DOX at 37°C overnight. DNF/DOX can be obtained by removing free DOX by centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 20 min).

Preparation of MIDRSDOX

LbCas12a and crRNA were firstly mixed in TOLO Buffer 3 at a molar ratio of 1:1.5 and incubated at 37°C for 10 min to form the Cas12a/crRNA RNP complex. The previously prepared DNF/DOX were incubated with the Cas12a/crRNA complex with a 1:1 molar ratio at 37°C for another 30 min to form MIDRSDOX. Agarose gel electrophoresis and UV absorption spectroscopy were used to analyze the self-assembly process of MIDRSDOX. Zetasizer was used to analyze the size and zeta potential of MIDRSDOX. Then, the morphology of MIDRSDOX was investigated by SEM imaging.

In vitro detection of mtDNA using MIDRS

Unless indicated, the standard in vitro mtDNA detection protocol using MIDRS is as follows: 5 μl of 1 μM MIDRS, 1 μl of 5 μM dsDNA, and 2 μl of 10× TOLO Buffer 3 and 12 μl of DEPC-treated H2O were mixed. The fluorescence of each group was measured (λex: 415 nm; λem: 650 nm) followed by incubation at 37°C for 30 min. To analyze Ce6 release kinetics of MIDRS initiated by target dsDNA, the above mixture was incubated at different times at 37°C, and the fluorescence of each group was measured as described above.

Analysis of self-cleavage of MIDRS

Specifically, the self-cleavage of MIDRS was analyzed as described below. Five microliters of 1 μM MIDRS, 5 μl of 1 μM dsDNA, 2 μl of 10× TOLO Buffer 3, and 8 μl of DEPC-treated H2O were mixed. After incubating at 37°C for 1 hour, the reaction mixtures were analyzed by 6% PAGE. TEM imaging was used to analyze the morphology of MIDRS before and after treatment by wild-type and mutant dsDNA.

Stability analysis of MIDRS

For analysis of the stability of MIDRS in different environments, MIDRS was separately incubated with 10% FBS, pH 7.4 PBS, and pH 6.5 PBS, respectively, at 37°C at different times (0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 hours) and then the polyacrylamide gel was used to check its integrity. Furthermore, after incubation with 10% FBS at 37°C at different times (0, 3, 6, 12, 24, and 36 hours), 3 μl of 1 μM MIDRS was mixed with equal molar target dsDNA for another 30 min. Then, the fluorescence of Ce6 in the reaction mixture (20 μl, 384-well microplate format) was measured by the Synergy H1 Hybrid Reader (BioTek, USA) using an excitation wavelength of 400 nm and an emission wavelength of 660 nm. After that, the reaction mixture was added in 20 μl of 2 μM SOSG to detect singlet oxygen generated by Ce6 irradiated by laser. The fluorescence of SOSG (λex: 490 nm; λem: 515 nm) in the reaction mixture (40 μl, 96-well microplate format) was measured followed by incubation at 37°C for 30 min.

Quantitative analysis of the Cas12a/crRNA complex in mitochondria

The first crRNA detection method based on RCA was developed. A padlock probe-based RCA method was firstly applied based on the principle described as follows. Padlock probes are hybridized to their target sequence (crRNA) and the probe ends are joined through ligation, locking the probe onto the target molecule. After ligation, the RCA is initiated by the Phi 29 DNA polymerase by turning the target molecule into a primer through 3′-5′ exonucleolysis of any 3′-end protruding beyond the padlock probe hybridization site. The padlock probe then serves as the template for DNA synthesis. The RCA product (gray) is detected through agarose gel electrophoresis. Specifically, using the backbone part of Cas12a-specific crRNA as primers, a specific RCA template was designed and named Template-crRNA. Firstly, 1 μM Template-crRNA and 2 μg of mtRNA were mixed in T4 DNA Ligase buffer to form an RCA template, then circularized by T4 DNA ligase. Then, the RCA reaction was conducted in a 20-μl mixture that contained 10 μl of 1 μM ligation products, 2 μl of 10× Phi 29 DNA polymerase buffer, 2.5 μl of 10 mM dNTP, 0.5 μl of Phi 29 DNA polymerase (10 U/μl), and 5 μl of DEPC-treated H2O. The mixture was incubated at 37°C for 2 hours and denatured at 90°C for 10 min; 6% PAGE was used to detect the RCA product.

Moreover, CRISPR-Cas13a protein was used to sensitively detect crRNA from mitochondria total RNA of B16 cells with different treatments. A CRISPR guide RNA (crRNA) complementary to 20 nt of the crRNA of Cas12a was used to direct LwaCas13a to the target sequence. Detection of the target resulted in Cas13a activation and subsequent collateral cleavage of an oligonucleotide carrying a quenched fluorophore that exhibits fluorescence when cleaved, correlating with the initial concentration of the crRNA in the total RNA sample from mito. The extraction procedure of RNA in mitochondria is described above. In detail, 1 μl of 2 μM Cas13a, 3 μl of 1 μM crRNACas12a, 0.8 μg of total RNA in mitochondria, 2 μl of 10 μM Poly U Reporter, and 2 μl of 10× Reaction buffer were mixed. Add DEPC-treated H2O to 20 μl. After incubating at 37°C for 30 min, the fluorescence of each group was measured (λex: 488 nm; λem: 520 nm).

To test intracellular Mg2+ release of MIDRS, after being treated with 50 nM MIDRS for 6 hours, the B16 cells were collected through a centrifuge at 4°C for 5 min at 600g after digested with Trypsin-EDTA solution. After counting cell numbers, the concentrations of Mg2+ in B16 cells were tested by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS) (Agilent, 7800).

One week after tail vein injection of B16 cells, the mice were randomly assigned into the following four groups (five mice in each group): (i) saline, (ii) MIDRSDOX-NT, (iii) MIDRSDOX-MT, and (iv) MIDRSMT. The mice were intravenously administered with various preparations (equivalent DOX concentration: 5 mg kg−1) via the tail vein. The mice’s body weight was monitored every 3 days. On the 12th day, the mice were euthanized and the lungs were collected for H&E staining.

Safety evaluation

To evaluate the biological safety of MIDRS, blood samples were collected from the eyeballs for routine blood testing to evaluate the liver and kidney functions; H&E staining and imaging of the main organs of the mice were performed to evaluate the systemic safety.

Flow cytometry analysis of tumor-infiltrating immune cells

Tumors were harvested and cut into 2- to 4-mm pieces on day 2 after the last dose. Tumor tissue (0.1 g) was randomly selected. After adding 2.35 ml of RPMI 1640, 100 μl of Enzyme D, 12.5 μl of Enzyme A, and 10 μl of Enzyme R, the samples were incubated at 37°C and filtered through a 70-μm filter to obtain a single-cell suspension. For DC detection, the cell suspension was stained with PerCP/Cy5.5 anti-mouse CD45 antibody, Alexa 488 anti-mouse CD11c antibody, Brilliant Violet 650 anti-mouse CD86 antibody, and PE anti-mouse CD80 antibody; for T cell detection, the cell suspension was stained with PerCP/Cy5.5 anti-mouse CD45 antibody, FITC anti-mouse CD3 antibody, and APC/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD8α antibody; for NK cell detection, the cell suspension was stained with PerCP/Cy5.5 anti-mouse CD45 antibody and APC anti-mouse CD49b antibody.

To detect the effector memory T cells, the spleens were collected on day 2 after the last treatment, placed in PBS, ground, and then filtered to obtain single-cell suspensions. Erythrocyte lysate (6 ml) was added and lysed at room temperature for 10 min. The collected cells were stained with FITC anti-mouse CD3 antibody, APC/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD8α antibody, APC anti-mouse/human CD44, and PE/cyanine7 anti-mouse CD62L for effector memory T cell detection.

To detect IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells in peripheral blood, the blood was collected 2 days after the final treatments. The red blood cells were lysed by red blood cell lysis buffer and the lymphocytes were collected by centrifugation. The cell suspension was stained with FITC anti-mouse CD3, APC/Cyanine7 anti-mouse CD8α, and APC anti-mouse IFN-γ+ antibodies for IFN-γ+CD8+ T cell detection.

Sequencing of mtDNA

Genomic DNA of indicated cell lines (B16, RAW 264.7, HCT116, MUVEC, and MCF-7 cells) used in this study was extracted using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China). PCRs were executed using Ex Taq Hot Start Versio (TaKaRa) in a BioRad T100 Thermal cycler under the following conditions: 95°C for 3 min; 34 cycles of 95°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, 72°C for 30 s, and final extension 72°C for 5 min. The PCR products were purified using the Gel Extraction Kit (CWBIO, China). Sanger sequencing was performed to analyze the mtDNA sequence.

Next-generation sequencing

To analyze the off-target effect of MIDRS, B16 cells were incubated with 50 nM MIDRSMT at 37°C for 12 hours. Genomic DNA from cells was extracted using the TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (Tiangen Biotech Co. Ltd.) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. PCR products were generated for each off-target site from ~200 ng of genomic DNA extracted from B16 cells. After precise quantification of genomic DNA using the Qubit 4.0 DNA Detection Kit (Thermo Fisher Scientific), to determine the PCR, one should incorporate the amount of DNA (20 ng) to be added. Illumina bridge PCR-compatible primers were introduced using a 2 × Hieff Robust PCR Master Mix (Yeasen), and sequenced via 150–base pair paired-end sequencing on a Nova Seq (PE150) instrument. The results were analyzed by CRISPR RGEN Tools.

Pyrosequencing

Assessment of mt.5465G>A mtDNA heteroplasmy was carried out by pyrosequencing. PCRs for pyrosequencing were prepared using 50 ng of template DNA (SanTaq PCR Kit, Sangon, China) with the following conditions: 95°C for 5 min; 35 cycles of 94°C for 30 s, 55°C for 30 s, 72°C for 50 s, and final extension 72°C for 8 min. The primers for this pyrosequencing are listed in table S1. NCBI reference sequence NC_005089.1 was used for the mouse tumor cell line (B16 and RAW 264.7) mitochondrial genomes.

Eukaryotic transcriptome sequencing

The effect of MIDRS on B16 cells was evaluated by mRNA transcriptome sequencing. B16 cells were seeded on a six-well plate at 5 × 105 cells per well for 24 hours. After incubation with 50 nM MIDRSMT for 6 hours, fresh medium was replaced and cells were irradiated with a laser (660 nm, 0.2 W cm−2) for 10 min. Then, the cells were cultured for another 24 hours and washed with ice-cold PBS, and collected again by centrifugation. The RNA extraction, reverse transcription, cDNA library construction, and sequencing were performed by Majorbio. Mapped reads were assigned to Mus_musculus using feature Counts2 and GRCm38.p6 (http://asia.ensembl.org/Mus_musculus/Info/Index). DESeq2 was used for differential gene expression analysis among subgroups; the filter thresholds are as follows: |log2FC| ≥1 and Padjust < 0.05.

Microscopy

Microscopic imaging was performed on a LEICA TCS SP8 STED inverted microscope. Images were taken using LAS AF Lite software by time-lapse microscopy. For live cell imaging, cells were cultured at 37°C and 5% CO2 in a humidified chamber. Details of the excitation and emission wavelengths of all fluorescent dyes used in this study are listed in table S2.

Image processing and analysis

Image processing was performed by IPP, Fiji, and LAS AF Lite, and all images were linearly adjusted by changing the minimum and maximum values for optimal visualization. Relative fluorescence was calculated by setting the average background fluorescent intensity in the native control. The PCC measures the pixel-wise covariance of two images on a scale of −1 to 1, with −1 indicating negative correlation and 1 indicating complete colocalization. PCC is checked by the coloc2 plugin in Fiji.

Statistical analysis

GraphPad Prism software was used for all statistical analyses. The results were expressed as means ± SD. Significant differences between different groups were determined using two-tailed unpaired Student’s t tests for two-group comparisons and one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with post-hoc Tukey’s test for multiple-group comparisons (*P < 0.05, **P < 0.01, ***P < 0.001 and ****P < 0.0001; ns: no significance).
