Predicting antenatal care dropout using machine learning and describing maternal and neonatal outcomes by dropout status in Sidama Region, Ethiopia: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Amanuel Yoseph, Lakew Mussie, Mehretu Belayneh
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract AbstractObjectivesWe aimed to develop and validate machine-learning models to predict antenatal care (ANC) dropout and describe maternal and neonatal outcomes by ANC dropout status in a low-resource setting in Northern Sidama, Ethiopia.DesignRetrospective cohort study.SettingHealth facilities across four districts in Northern Sidama, Ethiopia.Participants3855 pregnant women aged 15–49 years who initiated ANC between January 2021 and January 2025 were included, whereas women with incomplete records or those who relocated before delivery were excluded.Outcome measuresThe primary outcome was ANC dropout, defined as attending <8 recommended ANC visits. Introduction

Antenatal care (ANC) plays a central role in maternal health by offering timely opportunities to detect and manage pregnancy-related complications. The WHO recommends at least eight ANC contacts to optimise maternal and neonatal health outcomes.1 However, many women in Ethiopia do not complete the recommended visits, and ANC dropout remains a major public health challenge.2 Evidence shows that 35%–75% of women experience ANC dropout, driven by factors such as long travel distances to health facilities, low maternal education, poverty and socio-cultural influences.3,5 These persistent barriers highlight the need for data-driven approaches tailored to local contexts to improve continuity of care.

Despite its importance, relatively few studies in Ethiopia have used predictive modelling to identify features associated with the predicted probability of ANC dropout. Traditional statistical methods often assume linear relationships and may fail to capture the complex, non-linear patterns present in healthcare utilisation data, which can limit predictive accuracy.6 7 Machine learning (ML) addresses this limitation by analysing large datasets, detecting complex interactions and generating robust predictions.8

Researchers have increasingly applied ML methods in maternal health across different settings. Studies from low-income and middle-income countries have used ML models to predict ANC utilisation, maternal complications and neonatal outcomes, often achieving better predictive performance than conventional statistical approaches.9 10 Similarly, studies in high-income countries have applied ML techniques to predict obstetric complications such as postpartum haemorrhage, severe maternal morbidity and pre-eclampsia, supporting improved clinical decision-making and risk stratification.11,14 Together, these studies show the growing value of ML in modelling maternal health patterns. However, evidence on ML-based prediction of ANC dropout remains limited in sub-Saharan Africa, particularly in Ethiopia, where discontinuity of maternal care continues to pose a significant challenge.15 16

To improve predictive performance, we developed and evaluated multiple ML models, including logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting and extreme gradient boosting (XGBoost). We used logistic regression as a baseline model, while ensemble methods such as random forest and XGBoost allowed us to capture complex, non-linear relationships and interactions among features.11 12 Although previous studies have applied ML techniques to maternal health outcomes and healthcare utilisation, few have combined ANC dropout prediction with a descriptive comparison of maternal and neonatal outcomes by dropout status. By integrating predictive modelling with outcome description, this study provides complementary evidence that supports interpretation of model results in a public health context.

Understanding ANC dropout is important for strengthening health system planning and improving service delivery. Identifying women with a higher predicted probability of ANC dropout can help health systems prioritise early engagement, improve access in rural areas and support targeted follow-up strategies in resource-limited settings.13,15 These needs are particularly urgent in settings where maternal health services face structural constraints and utilisation remains suboptimal.14 The Sidama region in Southern Ethiopia presents additional contextual challenges, including diverse populations, variable health service availability and socio-cultural factors that influence healthcare use.13,17 Local analyses are therefore essential to describe regional patterns and support context-specific planning.

This study aimed to develop and validate ML models to predict ANC dropout among pregnant women in the Sidama region, identify key demographic, obstetric and healthcare-related features contributing to model predictions of ANC dropout status. By combining predictive modelling with outcome description within a single analytical framework, this study provides methodological advancement in predictive modelling and descriptive evidence on outcome differences across ANC dropout groups and practical evidence that may support maternal health planning in low-resource settings.18,20

Methods

Study design and setting

A retrospective cohort study was conducted from January 2021 to January 2025 in four districts of Northern Sidama, Ethiopia: Bilate Zuriya, Shebedino, Boricha and Hawella Lida. The northern zone is located approximately 273 km south of Addis Ababa and includes eight districts, two town administrations and 162 kebeles, which represent the smallest administrative units in Ethiopia. The region has an estimated population of 1.29 million, of whom about 23% are women of reproductive age.21 The local health system includes 144 health posts, 36 health centres, one general hospital and four primary hospitals, covering approximately 70% of the population. In 2025, regional reports showed that 83% of women attended at least one ANC visit and 71% delivered in health facilities.22 This setting provided a diverse and representative context for developing and validating a predictive model of ANC dropout.

Study population and eligibility criteria

Pregnant women aged 15–49 years who initiated ANC services at selected health facilities during the study period were included. Eligibility required at least one documented ANC visit and availability of essential demographic, obstetric and healthcare-related variables needed for prediction modelling. Conversely, women who relocated outside the study area before delivery or had missing information on key predictors or the outcome variable were excluded. These criteria ensured data completeness and supported robust predictive model development in line with TRIPOD recommendations.

Sample size and sampling procedure

A total of 3855 pregnant women were included in the final analysis. A multi-stage sampling approach was applied by first stratifying health facilities into urban and rural categories. Then facilities were randomly selected proportional to their ANC client volume, and all eligible records from the selected facilities were included. This approach ensured adequate representation of different service delivery contexts and supported stable predictive model estimation.

Outcome definition

The primary outcome was ANC dropout, defined as attending <8 ANC contacts, consistent with1 recommendations.1 This outcome was treated as a binary variable (dropout vs completion). No outcome information was used as predictors in the models to prevent data leakage and ensure valid prediction. Secondary outcomes included maternal complications (such as pre-eclampsia and antepartum haemorrhage) and delivery outcomes (such as facility-based delivery and neonatal outcomes). These were used only for descriptive comparison and were not included in model development.

Predictor variables

Predictors were selected based on prior literature, clinical relevance and availability in routine health records. These included maternal socio-demographic characteristics (age, residence and education), obstetric factors (parity and gestational age at the first ANC visit) and healthcare access variables (distance to facility and transportation availability). All predictors were measured prior to outcome occurrence. Any variables derived from or occurring after the outcome were excluded to ensure strict temporal validity for prediction modelling.

Data sources and preprocessing

Data were extracted from electronic health records and maternal health registers using a structured abstraction tool. Detailed data quality checks were then performed before analysis. Missing data patterns were assessed, and variables with excessive missingness were excluded based on predefined thresholds. For remaining variables, multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) was applied assuming missing at random. Continuous variables were standardised, and one-hot encoding was applied for categorical variables. Importantly, all pre-processing steps were performed within training folds during cross-validation to prevent information leakage and ensure unbiased performance estimates.

Model development and validation

Four predictive models were developed to estimate the probability of ANC dropout: logistic regression (as a baseline model), random forest, gradient boosting and XGBoost. Logistic regression provided a transparent linear benchmark, whereas ensemble models captured non-linear relationships and complex interactions among predictors.11 12

Model hyperparameters were tuned using grid search within cross-validation to minimise overfitting and ensure fair comparison across models. All models were internally validated using 10-fold cross-validation. In each iteration, models were trained on 90% of the data and tested on the remaining 10%, and this process was repeated across all folds. Average performance was reported across folds to provide stable and reliable estimates of predictive performance. No external validation was performed.

Model performance evaluation

Model performance was evaluated using multiple complementary metrics, including accuracy, sensitivity, specificity, F1-score and area under the receiver operating characteristic (AUC-ROC). These metrics collectively assessed classification performance and discrimination ability. Calibration was additionally assessed using calibration plots and the Brier score to evaluate agreement between predicted probabilities and observed outcomes. This ensured that predicted risks were not only accurate in classification but also reliable in probability estimation.

Model interpretation

SHapley Additive exPlanations (SHAP) were applied to quantify the contribution of each predictor to model outputs. SHAP values were interpreted strictly as measures of feature importance within the predictive framework. These values were not interpreted as causal effects or underlying epidemiological associations.

Statistical analysis

All analyses were conducted using R V.4.3.1. Participant characteristics were summarised using descriptive statistics. χ2 tests and t-tests were used only for exploratory comparisons of groups without making inferential or causal claims. The entire analytical framework was explicitly designed for prediction rather than hypothesis testing or causal inference.

Bias and model assumptions

Predictive models may be influenced by overfitting, variable selection effects and residual confounding due to unmeasured variables. However, these factors primarily affect interpretability rather than predictive performance, which was the primary objective of this study. Therefore, model coefficients or SHAP values were not interpreted as causal relationships.

Ethical considerations

Ethical approval was obtained from the Institutional Review Board of Hawassa University College of Medicine and Health Sciences (Ref: IRB/027/16). All data were fully anonymised prior to analysis, and informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective design. Data were stored securely in password-protected systems accessible only to the research team. This study adhered to national and international ethical standards for research involving human participants and followed Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) reporting principles for observational studies.

Patient and public involvement

This study did not involve patients or members of the public in its design, conduct or reporting, as it was based on retrospective health records. Future research should consider stakeholder involvement to improve implementation relevance and real-world applicability of predictive models.

Results

Socio-demographic and clinical characteristics

A total of 3855 pregnant women participated in the study, with a mean age of 27.4±5.8 years. The majority resided in rural areas (63%), and over half had either no formal education or only primary education (58%). Most participants were multiparous (62%), and 41% initiated ANC after 16 weeks of gestation. Regarding health service access, 45% of women lived >5 km from the nearest health facility, and only 38% reported having reliable transportation (table 1).

Secondary outcomes: distribution of maternal and neonatal outcomes by antenatal care dropout status

Women who dropped out of ANC or were classified as high risk by the ML models showed higher proportions of maternal complications, including pre-eclampsia (12%), antepartum haemorrhage (8%) and prolonged labour (10%), compared with those who completed the recommended visits. They were also less likely to deliver at health facilities (41% vs 87%). Machine-learning model performance in predicting antenatal care dropout

Four ML models were developed to predict ANC dropout: logistic regression, random forest, gradient boosting and XGBoost. Among these models, XGBoost achieved the best predictive performance, with an accuracy of 87%, AUC-ROC of 0.91, sensitivity of 85% and specificity of 88%. The gradient boosting model followed closely, with an accuracy of 86% and AUC-ROC of 0.90, and the random forest model achieved an accuracy of 84% and AUC-ROC of 0.88. Logistic regression showed lower predictive performance, with an accuracy of 76% and AUC-ROC of 81.0% (table 4 and figure 1). Overall, XGBoost demonstrated the highest predictive performance for ANC dropout in this dataset.

Discussion

This study applied multiple ML algorithms to predict ANC dropout in the Sidama region of Ethiopia and describe differences in maternal and neonatal outcomes by ANC dropout status. A high ANC dropout rate of 71.4% was identified, highlighting persistent challenges in completing recommended ANC visits in low-income and middle-income settings. The features that contributed most to model predictions of ANC dropout included late ANC initiation (>16 weeks), rural residence, low maternal education, long distance to health facilities, multiparity and lack of reliable transportation. Advanced ML techniques, particularly XGBoost, captured complex, non-linear relationships among variables that traditional statistical methods may not fully detect.

Among the models tested, XGBoost achieved the highest predictive performance (87% of accuracy, 0.91 of AUC-ROC), outperforming logistic regression, random forest and gradient boosting. This performance reflects the strength of gradient boosting methods in optimising predictive accuracy through iterative learning and regularisation, particularly when handling complex and structured health data. These properties make XGBoost well suited for prediction tasks involving heterogeneous maternal health datasets, where multiple social, demographic and service-related factors interact in non-linear ways.11 20 23

SHAP analysis identified the relative contribution of each feature to model predictions of ANC dropout. Late ANC initiation and rural residence showed the highest SHAP values, followed by low maternal education, long distance to health facilities, multiparity and lack of reliable transportation. These results describe how individual variables contributed to model outputs and should be interpreted strictly as feature importance within a predictive model.

Our findings align with previous ML studies in maternal health conducted in low-income and middle-income countries. In rural Bangladesh, gradient boosting models demonstrated strong performance in predicting ANC utilisation, with maternal education, residence and socio-economic status emerging as key predictive features.24 Similarly, studies in Nigeria and Uganda demonstrated that ensemble ML models such as random forest and XGBoost performed well in predicting ANC retention and skilled birth attendance, with consistent emphasis on maternal age, parity and access-related variables.25 26 Collectively, these studies show that ML methods consistently improve predictive performance in modelling maternal health service utilisation across diverse LMIC settings.

Evidence from high-income countries further supports the growing use of ML approaches in maternal health and clinical prediction tasks. Large clinical datasets have been used to develop models that predict obstetric complications such as postpartum haemorrhage and preterm birth, often achieving higher predictive accuracy than traditional regression models.27 28 These approaches capture complex interactions among demographic and clinical variables that may not be fully represented in conventional analytical frameworks. Similarly, ML models applied to healthcare utilisation data have improved the identification of individuals at risk of missed appointments and poor engagement with care.29 Together, these findings demonstrate the broad applicability of ML methods across healthcare systems.

Although interactions were explored among key variables, including rural residence and late ANC initiation, no statistically significant interaction effects were found. This indicates that these variables independently contributed to model predictions rather than modifying each other’s influence. Nevertheless, their combined presence reflects important structural barriers to ANC continuity. Rural residence often reflects limited access to health services and transportation challenges, while late ANC initiation may indicate delayed engagement with the health system. These contextual factors remain important for understanding patterns of ANC dropout in real-world settings.

Late ANC initiation consistently showed the strongest contribution to model predictions in the SHAP analysis. This finding aligns with previous studies in sub-Saharan Africa.25 26 In this study, SHAP values reflect the contribution of this variable to model predictions rather than causal influence. ML models allow the identification of non-linear patterns and threshold effects that traditional regression methods may not fully capture, particularly in complex health system data.

Beyond prediction, in this study, differences in maternal and neonatal outcomes were described by ANC dropout status. Higher proportions of maternal complications were observed among women with higher predicted probability of ANC dropout, including pre-eclampsia, antepartum haemorrhage and prolonged labour, among women who dropped out of ANC compared with those who completed recommended visits. Moreover, lower facility-based delivery rates and higher proportions of neonatal adverse outcomes, such as low birth weight and need for resuscitation, were observed in the dropout group. These findings describe outcome differences across groups and do not imply causal relationships.26 30

Importantly, this study highlights the added value of ML approaches in maternal health research. Traditional regression methods remain useful for hypothesis testing, but they often assume linear relationships and may fail to capture complex interactions. In contrast, ML models allow flexible modelling of non-linear patterns and interactions among predictors, improving predictive performance in real-world datasets. The addition of the SHAP analysis improved interpretability by quantifying the contribution of each feature to model predictions, thereby supporting transparent and clinically meaningful interpretation of results.

Our findings also suggest that predictors of ANC dropout show consistent patterns across different settings. In high-income countries, socio-economic disadvantage and barriers to healthcare access similarly influence service utilisation, although the magnitude and context of these barriers differ.31 32 This consistency highlights that while maternal health challenges are context-specific, underlying structural determinants such as education, access and timely engagement with care remain universally important.

Although we did not implement an ML-driven intervention, the models identified women at higher predicted probability of ANC dropout. The dropout group experienced higher proportions of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, suggesting potential utility for early identification of women with higher predicted probability of ANC dropout. However, these findings remain descriptive and should be interpreted in the context of a predictive modelling framework rather than causal inference. Future studies should validate these models prospectively and assess their implementation in routine health systems.

Implications for scaling predictive models in low-resource settings

This study demonstrated the potential value of scaling ML-based predictive models in low-resource settings. Accurate prediction of ANC dropout can support early identification of high-risk individuals and enable more efficient allocation of limited health resources. Integrating predictive analytics into maternal health systems may improve service delivery and support timely interventions, particularly in settings with constrained infrastructure.

Practical and policy implications

The findings have important implications for maternal health policy and practice. Strengthening access to ANC in rural areas, improving transportation support and promoting early ANC initiation remain critical priorities. Community-based education programmes can further improve awareness of ANC importance. Integrating ML tools into routine health information systems may help identify women at higher predicted risk of dropout and support targeted follow-up. Successful implementation will require training healthcare workers in digital tools and ensuring appropriate interpretation of model outputs. These combined strategies can improve maternal and neonatal health outcomes in resource-limited settings.

Strengths and limitations

This study offers several important strengths. Multiple ML models were developed and compared to predict ANC dropout, and SHAP was applied to improve the transparency and interpretability of model outputs. The analysis of a relatively large cohort of pregnant women drawn from both rural and urban settings enhances the robustness and real-world relevance of the predictive models.

We recognised several limitations that should be considered when interpreting the findings. First, the retrospective design introduced potential information and measurement bias because we relied on routinely collected health records. Although we implemented rigorous data cleaning and appropriate imputation methods, missing or incomplete records may still have influenced model development.

Second, the absence of important variables particularly socio-economic, psychosocial and behavioural factors might have introduced residual confounding. However, this limitation primarily affects the interpretability of feature contributions rather than the predictive performance of the models, which remained the central objective of this study.

Third, methodological risks inherent in predictive modelling were acknowledged, including overfitting, variable selection effects and instability in feature importance estimates. These risks were mitigated through internal validation using 10-fold cross-validation; however, some degree of variability in model performance and feature ranking might still persist.

Finally, because the models were developed and internally validated using data from only four districts in the Sidama Region, external validity was not assessed. Therefore, model performance might differ in other populations because of variations in the casemix, health system structure and care-seeking behaviour. External validation in independent cohorts is essential before considering broader implementation or policy use.

Future research directions

We recommend several directions for future research based on the strengths and limitations of this study. First, future studies should conduct prospective validation to evaluate the predictive performance and generalisability of the ML models in larger and more diverse populations. This step is essential to address the limitations of the retrospective design and the restricted regional scope of the current analysis.

Second, integrating predictive models into mobile health platforms could enable real-time identification of women with a high predicted probability of ANC dropout. This integration would support timely follow-up and targeted community interventions that may improve continuity of ANC services in resource-limited settings.

Third, future research should incorporate qualitative methods to explore the social, cultural and individual factors influencing ANC adherence. These should include psychosocial barriers, cultural beliefs and perceptions of healthcare quality, which were not available in the current dataset.

Finally, future studies should use time-to-event data to support survival analysis of maternal and neonatal outcomes, providing more detailed insights into the timing and patterns of ANC dropout. Addressing these priorities will strengthen the robustness, applicability and practical value of predictive modelling in maternal health, while ensuring that future applications remain evidence-informed, scalable and context-specific.

Conclusion

This study identified a high proportion of ANC dropout among pregnant women in Northern Sidama, Ethiopia. ML models were developed and evaluated to predict ANC dropout using demographic, obstetric and healthcare-related features. Among the models tested, XGBoost demonstrated the strongest predictive performance and most accurately identified women with a high predicted probability of ANC dropout.

Differences in maternal and neonatal outcomes were described by ANC dropout status, where proportions of adverse outcomes were higher among women who did not complete the recommended ANC visits. These findings reflect descriptive group differences and should not be interpreted as causal effects.

Overall, this study shows the potential value of ML-based predictive tools for identifying women with a high predicted probability of ANC dropout. Such tools could support earlier follow-up and more targeted maternal health interventions in resource-limited settings. Future research should validate these models prospectively across diverse populations to confirm their generalisability and practical use in routine healthcare systems.
