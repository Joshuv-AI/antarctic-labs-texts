A general strategy for access intrinsically antioxidant polyolefins
Authors: Feiran Yang, Chao Li, Fuzhou Wang, Chen Zou, Changle Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The long-term stability of various polyolefins relies on antioxidants. Traditional methods such as physical blending or chemical modification often suffer from non-uniform dispersion, migration, or lack of universality, especially for ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE). Developing a general strategy to access various polyethylene types bearing intrinsically antioxidant properties (defined as polyolefins with antioxidant properties produced via polymerization, without postpolymerization antioxidant addition) remains a challenge. Here, we present a Catalyst-Enabled Antioxidation Strategy (CEAS) using phenolic hydroxy-functionalized dual-functional catalysts that both catalyze polymerization and provide antioxidant capacity, enabling the production of various types of intrinsically antioxidant polyolefins. We further develop enhancement strategies via auxiliary antioxidants and biomass-supported heterogeneous catalysis to boost antioxidant properties. This approach ensures uniform dispersion, overcomes processing limitations, retains mechanical properties, and enhances antioxidant performance. Notably, CEAS-synthesized UHMWPE demonstrates resistance to oxidation during demanding processing such as high-temperature spinning and radiation-induced crosslinking.

Introduction

Since its inception, plastic has made significant contributions to modern human society1–4. The discovery of additives has further enhanced the processability and various properties of plastic products, thereby advancing the application of polymers5–12. For example, thermal stabilizers or antioxidants are commonly introduced into plastic matrices to suppress thermo-oxidative or photo-oxidative degradation during high-temperature processing and long-term environmental exposure13,14. Polyolefins are among the most widely produced and used plastics15–20. Despite their inherent chemical stability, the addition of antioxidants is imperative for inhibiting their environmental degradation and extending their service life21–24. During commercial polyolefin production, antioxidants are added during the pelletizing step after polymerization25. During subsequent high-temperature machining processes, it is often necessary to introduce supplemental antioxidants via physical addition to maintain material stability (Fig. 1A)26,27. However, the intrinsic incompatibility between polar antioxidants and nonpolar polyolefin matrices often results in non-uniform dispersion and accelerated migration, which critically undermines a material’s long-term stability28–31. To address this limitation, several chemical modification methods for antioxidants have been developed (Fig. 1A), such as the direct synthesis of polymeric antioxidants32, immobilization onto inorganic supports (e.g., via SiO2 anchoring)33, and intercalation-mediated assembly34. Alternatively, another approach involves covalent attachment of antioxidant functional groups to polyolefin chains through strategies such as grafting35, olefin coordination copolymerization36, and post-polymerization modification37. More importantly, due to the wide variety of polyethylene types, these approaches lack general applicability. For example, the ultra-long molecular chain of ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene (UHMWPE, typically with a molecular weight exceeding 106 g/mol) results in extreme chain entanglement, making it highly resistant to melt processing38,39. Furthermore, the scarcity of reactive sites along its polymer chain makes the effective implementation of the aforementioned modification methods challenging40. Additionally, due to the poisoning of polar monomers on the catalyst, it is also difficult to directly synthesize UHMWPE containing antioxidant groups through coordination copolymerization41. In practice, it is often processed under demanding conditions like high-temperature compression molding and radiation cross-linking to enhance creep and wear resistance, with these techniques requiring stringent control of oxidative stability42. Current industrial methods relying on powder impregnation or wet mixing inevitably lead to non-uniform dispersion, thereby compromising antioxidant performance and material stability43.

Therefore, it is both necessary and challenging to develop a convenient method for synthesizing polyethylene with intrinsically antioxidant functionality that is widely applicable to various polyethylene types (branched polyethylene, polyethylene wax, bimodal polyethylene, UHMWPE, and related composites) produced by different catalyst systems. To this end, we conceived a dual-function polyolefin catalyst that integrates both catalytic polymerization and antioxidation capabilities within a single transition-metal system (Fig. 1B), contrasting with conventional catalysts that are typically limited to a single function of polymerization44–47. Moreover, using a precipitation polymerization method48,49, the residual dual-function catalyst remains embedded within the polymer matrix, thereby enabling the production of polyethylene with intrinsically antioxidant properties without the need for post-polymerization additive incorporation.

Notably, given that phenolic motifs are common structural groups in antioxidants50, we introduced such a structure into the polyolefin catalyst system and implemented the following general strategy: (1) Catalyst Enabled Antioxidation Strategy (CEAS): Phenolic substituents were introduced into various catalyst systems (including bis(phenoxy-imine) titanium catalyst51, phosphophenol nickel48,52,53, α-diimine nickel54,55, pyridine-imine nickel56 and pyridine-bis(imine) iron catalysts57,58). This general approach yields polymer granules in which the weight percentage of catalyst residues is an order of magnitude lower than that of commercial antioxidants, while achieving comparable levels of antioxidant performance. Furthermore, two strengthening strategies were explored: (2) Enhancement via auxiliary antioxidants induced cooperative effects, and (3) Enhancement via biomass antioxidant supports induced cooperative effects59,60.

Results

Synthesis of intrinsically antioxidant polyolefin

Here, the ethylene polymerization performance of various catalyst systems (Fig. 2) was evaluated. First, the classical phosphophenol nickel catalyst (Ni1) was assessed, which inherently contains a phenolic OH group. To compare the antioxidant properties introduced by different catalytic structures, Ni2 (featuring an additional –OH group at the para position) and Ni3 (designed with steric hindrance from tert-butyl groups to form a hindered phenol structure) were also screened. For these phosphophenol nickel catalysts, the polymerization conditions were systematically optimized with respect to key parameters such as solvent volume. Representative optimization data are provided in Supporting Information Table S5, and the results reported in Table 1 (entries 1–3) represent the optimal conditions identified. As shown in Table 1, nickel catalysts Ni1-Ni3 exhibited activities of up to 1.52 × 107 g mol⁻1 h⁻1, and produced UHMWPE with high molecular weights (Mw, 384–488 × 104 g mol⁻1; Mη, 460–518 × 104 g mol⁻1) and high melting temperatures (136.5 °C to 141.0 °C) (Table 1, entries 1–3). This enhanced performance was attributed to the rapid precipitation of polymer chains produced by the catalytically active centers in the n-heptane solvent, forming a precipitation polymerization system. This polymerization strategy promoted the molecular weight and polymerization activity, while also helping to control the polymer morphology48,49. To further explore the potential of these catalysts, we conducted additional ethylene polymerizations under increased pressure (30 atm). Under these conditions, the activities of the phosphophenol nickel catalysts reached 177–208 kgPE/gNi (Table 1, entries 4–6), which is on the same order of magnitude as modern MgCl2-supported TiCl₄ Ziegler–Natta catalysts (typically 100–800 kgPE/gTi, Table 1, entry 7)61–70. These results demonstrate that our catalyst systems can achieve industrially relevant productivity while maintaining the ability to produce UHMWPE with exceptionally high molecular weights (up to 5.78 × 106 g/mol, entry 6). Subsequently, the ethylene polymerization behaviors of other phenolic-functionalized catalysts and their corresponding control catalysts in different systems were investigated (Table 1, entries 8–15). The activity and molecular weights of these catalysts were not compromised by the hydroxyl functionalization. Notably, the polyethylene produced by Ni4–OH and Ti–OH exhibited a significant increase in molecular weight, which we attribute to the interaction of the phenolic -OH group with the aluminum cocatalyst that modulates chain transfer and reduces catalyst solubility in n-heptane (Table 1, entries 8–9 and 14–15). These phenolic-functionalized catalysts successfully generated polyolefins with varied structures and molecular weights, including branched polyethylene (with a branching density of 25–41/1000 C, Table 1, entries 9 and 11), polyethylene wax (molecular weight about 2 × 104 g mol⁻1, Table 1, entry 11), bimodal polyethylene (PDI = 13.63, Table 1, entry 13), and UHMWPE (molecular weight reaching 276 × 104 g mol⁻1, Table 1, entry 15). Subsequently, the two enhancement strategies were also employed to synthesize polyethylene with intrinsically antioxidant properties. First, commercially available antioxidants butylated hydroxytoluene (BHT) or tert-butylhydroquinone (TBHQ) (1 equivalent) were introduced concurrently with the phosphophenol nickel catalysts. Taking catalysts Ni1 and Ni2 as examples, the addition of these antioxidants did not significantly affect the polymerization activity (up to 1.52 × 107 g mol⁻1 h⁻1) or molecular weight (up to 487 × 104 g mol⁻1) compared with the catalysts alone (Table 1, entries 16–18 vs. entries 1 and 2). Secondly, catalyst Ni2 bearing an -OH anchoring group was supported onto a series of biomass-derived antioxidant fillers (lignin, white lignin, epigallocatechin gallate (EGCG), and tannic acid). It was then used to synthesize heterogeneous catalysts incorporating antioxidant functionalities in both the support and catalyst. These heterogeneous catalysts enabled the synthesis of UHMWPE composites with activity up to 1.6 × 107 g mol⁻1 h⁻1 (Table 1, entries 19–22), nearly twice that of Ni2 under comparable conditions. The resulting UHMWPE exhibited molecular weights slightly lower than those produced by the homogeneous catalyst Ni2 (Table 1, Entry 2), ranging from 286–326 × 104 g mol⁻1, which is attributed to mild poisoning by the abundant polar functional groups present on the biomass-derived antioxidant support.

Mechanical and antioxidant properties of UHMWPE synthesized by phosphophenol nickel catalysts Ni1-Ni3

The incorporation of antioxidants into UHMWPE is critical for ensuring its long-term performance in demanding medical and industrial applications71. However, the direct preparation of intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE by grafting modification or coordination copolymerization methods is challenging41. Here, we employed homogeneous nickel catalysts Ni1-Ni3 bearing antioxidant phenolic ligands to catalyze ethylene polymerization for the synthesis of intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE, and the mechanical and antioxidant properties of the resulting products (PE-Ni1 to PE-Ni3) were evaluated. First, the obtained UHMWPE powders were processed into thin-film samples via compression molding at 200 °C under 5 MPa pressure. These films were subsequently cut into dumbbell-shaped specimens (gauge length: 28 mm; width: 2 mm; thickness: ~0.5 mm) for evaluating the tensile properties of the UHMWPE. These properties exceed those of commercial UHMWPE (GUR-1050), indicating that the synthesized samples meet basic application requirements. To benchmark our CEAS-synthesized UHMWPE against industrially relevant antioxidant incorporation methods, we prepared control samples using solvent-based impregnation following established industrial approaches72,73. Commercial UHMWPE powder was blended with vitamin E or Irganox 1010 solutions in isopropyl alcohol, mixed thoroughly, and dried under vacuum to remove solvent (see Supporting Information Section 2.3). These samples represent current industrial practice, where the antioxidant is distributed via diffusion from solution. The residual ligand content in our synthesized UHMWPE samples was approximately 0.015 wt%. Accordingly, the control groups were prepared with antioxidant concentrations ranging from equivalent to ten times this loading (0.015–0.3 wt%). The antioxidant performance of these UHMWPE samples was primarily assessed using oxidation induction time (OIT) measurements at 200 °C (Fig. 3B). OIT analysis revealed significant variations in the intrinsically antioxidant performance between the synthesized UHMWPE samples (PE-Ni1 to PE-Ni3). Among these, PE-Ni3 demonstrated the best antioxidant performance, with an OIT value of 45 min, followed by PE-Ni2 and PE-Ni1. Furthermore, a comparative study was conducted between these intrinsically antioxidative UHMWPE samples and commercial UHMWPE. OIT tests revealed that all synthesized intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE samples exhibited superior oxidative stability compared with controls (Figs. 3B, S1). And the PE-Ni3’s OIT value even surpassed those of controls impregnated with a tenfold higher concentration (0.15–0.3 wt%) of the commercial antioxidants. These conclusions were further validated by thermogravimetric analysis (TGA) under air (Fig. S2). The initial oxidation temperature (Td₀) from TGA showed a consistent trend, with the synthesized samples exhibiting superior antioxidant stability to that of commercial UHMWPE. PE-Ni3 demonstrated a Td₀ as high as 245 °C, which was 55 °C higher than that of the control sample. The excellent antioxidant performance was attributed to the uniform dispersion and encapsulation of residual antioxidant phenolic-functionalized ligands within UHMWPE particles during precipitation polymerization. Concurrent chain growth and rapid crystallization synergistically restricted the residual antioxidant ligand’s mobility. The constrained crystallization during subsequent processing (e.g., compression molding) may have further limited migration within the final product74.

Beyond directly adding organic small-molecule additives into the polymer matrix, masterbatch processing is a common industrial approach to improve the dispersion efficiency75. Building upon this method, these intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE samples were added as antioxidant masterbatches into commercial UHMWPE resins to augment their antioxidant performance. For example, PE-Ni3 was employed as an antioxidant masterbatch and was solution-blended with commercial UHMWPE at a 1:9 mass ratio to yield a final antioxidant concentration of about 15 ppm. Remarkably, OIT measurements at 160–200 °C revealed that this ultra-low loading significantly enhanced the stability of the samples, which exhibited a 40-fold increase compared with pure commercial UHMWPE (Figs. 3C, S3). This excellent antioxidant performance and flexible processability provide a practical alternative to the introduction of additives in UHMWPE.

Investigation of the intrinsically antioxidant mechanism

To determine the origin of the intrinsically antioxidant performance of these UHMWPE samples, the intrinsic radical scavenging activity of the phosphophenol ligands (L1–L3) was evaluated using azobisisobutyronitrile (AIBN)-initiated styrene peroxidation monitored by oxygen uptake76,77. This classical method allows for the precise determination of antioxidant kinetics under controlled conditions. Figure 3D presents the uninhibited oxidation kinetics of styrene (2 M) initiated by AIBN (0.01 M) in a chlorobenzene system and the inhibited oxidation profiles in the presence of different antioxidants. The results showed that the kinetic curves of these ligands and BHT were similar, with all ligands and BHT exhibiting characteristic inhibition periods with distinct inflection points upon antioxidant depletion. Quantitative analysis (Fig. 3E) revealed the stoichiometric factor (n), representing peroxyl radicals (ROO•) terminated per molecule, approached 2.0 for all ligands. This indicated that each ligand terminated two peroxyl radical chains. The n values followed the order of L3 > L2 > L1, which may be due to the steric hindrance of these ligands and their ability to stabilize radical intermediates via resonance delocalization. This order was consistent with the antioxidant performance observed in the corresponding UHMWPE (PE-Ni3 > PE-Ni2 > PE-Ni1). The inhibition rate constant (kinh) for each ligand, which characterizes the rate of chain termination with ROO• radicals, provided a complementary kinetic parameter to characterize the intrinsically antioxidant performance and structure-activity relationships of the phosphophenol ligands. These results demonstrate that the antioxidant properties of these intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE were mainly due to the catalyst ligands.

General applicability of the CEAS to prepare intrinsically antioxidant polyolefins

As previously demonstrated, the CEAS exhibits superior performance in preserving both mechanical and antioxidant properties, as exemplified by the intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE directly synthesized with a phosphophenol nickel catalyst. Subsequently, to evaluate the generality of this strategy, its application was extended to polyolefins of various types produced by other catalyst systems. For example, in the branched polyethylene system synthesized by α-diimine nickel catalysts (Ni4 and Ni4-OH), the introduction of the OH group into the catalyst not only significantly enhanced the mechanical properties of the polymer (stress at break increasing from 2 MPa to 36 MPa, Fig. 3F, Table S1), but also improved its antioxidant capacity. For the α-diimine nickel system, the dramatic change in mechanical properties from PE-Ni4 to PE-Ni4-OH (Fig. 3F) reflects the combined effects of reduced branching density (from 84 to 25/1000 C) and increased molecular weight (from 2.2 × 105 to 8.0 × 105 g/mol), demonstrating that the phenolic –OH group can modulate polymer architecture. This was evidenced by an increase in the OIT value from 0 min to 5 min (Fig. 3G) and the Td₀ from 180 °C to 195 °C (Fig. S4). Similarly, for the low-molecular-weight branched polyethylene wax produced by pyridine-imine nickel catalysts (Ni5 vs. Ni5-OH), the hydroxylated catalyst also imparted an intrinsically antioxidant character, increasing the OIT value from 10 to 21 min (Fig. 3G). In the pyridine-bis(imine) iron and bis(phenoxy-imine) titanium catalyst systems, the use of phenolic-functionalized catalysts also imparted intrinsic antioxidant properties without compromising tensile strength (Figs. 3F, G, S5). For the titanium system, the higher molecular weight achieved with Ti-OH (2.76 × 106 g/mol vs. 1.78 × 106 g/mol) resulted in comparable tensile strength but reduced elongation at break (360% vs. 520%), reflecting increased chain entanglement density at ultra-high molecular weights. For the iron system, despite the significant difference in molecular weight and distribution (Fe: Mw = 3.33 × 106 g/mol, PDI = 21.38; Fe-OH: Mw = 8.38 × 105 g/mol, PDI = 13.63), the mechanical properties remained similar, consistent with the behavior of bimodal polyethylene where high and low molecular weight fractions contribute synergistically. OIT measurements confirmed that the phenolic -OH groups remain effective after polymerization and quenching (Fig. 3G). Notably, even with residual aluminum species present after polymerization, the resulting polyethylenes still exhibit stable antioxidant performance (Supplementary Fig. 5). Consequently, enhanced antioxidant performance was achieved without compromising mechanical properties.

These results demonstrate that the CEAS strategy can be extended to various transition metal catalyst systems, enabling the synthesis of intrinsically antioxidant polyolefins across a range of polyethylene architectures. Notably, the antioxidant performance is most pronounced in UHMWPE due to its high chain entanglement and crystallinity, which collectively enhance the physical retention of antioxidant ligands, while branched polyethylenes show moderate enhancement compared to their non-hydroxyl counterparts. Nonetheless, the uniform dispersion achieved through in situ polymerization ensures meaningful antioxidant protection across all systems, highlighting the general applicability of this approach.

Auxiliary antioxidant enhancement strategy

Furthermore, it is noteworthy that both industrial and academic studies have demonstrated synergistic stabilizing effects from the combination of two antioxidants during the processing and application of polyolefins78. It is speculated that the introduction of commercial antioxidants during polymerization may enable similar cooperative effects with the phosphophenol ligands. Therefore, equimolar amounts of BHT or TBHQ were introduced as auxiliary reagents into the Ni1/Ni2 catalytic system. Notably, the UHMWPE (PE-Ni1/BHT, PE-Ni1/TBHQ, PE-Ni2/BHT) produced by this strategy maintained excellent mechanical properties, with elongation at break values ranging from 190% to 420% and tensile strengths between 42 MPa and 53 MPa (Fig. 4A, Table S1). Interestingly, this strategy significantly enhanced the intrinsically antioxidant performance of UHMWPE samples (Figs. 4B, S6). For example, compared with pristine PE-Ni1, PE-Ni1/BHT exhibited significantly enhanced intrinsically antioxidant stability, with an OIT extended from 13 to 45 min and Td0 increased from 215 °C to 255 °C. Similarly, PE-Ni1/TBHQ and PE-Ni2/BHT exhibited significantly higher OIT values of 20 min and 33 min, and elevated Td0 values of 15 °C and 34 °C compared with those of PE-Ni1 and PE-Ni2. Biomass antioxidant support enhancement strategy

Biomass-derived supports, including lignin79,80, EGCG81, and tannic acid82 contain abundant phenolic hydroxyl groups, endowing them with antioxidant activity83. Heterogeneous catalysts prepared by anchoring Ni2 onto these biomass-derived supports leverage dual antioxidant activity from both the ligand’s phenolic moieties and the biomass supports. This enables efficient in-situ synthesis of UHMWPE composites with intrinsically antioxidant properties. The experimental results demonstrated that the supported catalysts exhibited enhanced catalytic efficiency while maintaining great product mechanical properties (Fig. 4C, Table S1). The tensile strength of composite PE-Ni2/White Lignin prepared using the white lignin-supported catalyst was 33% higher than that of PE-Ni2, increasing from 46 MPa to 61 MPa. Remarkably, all UHMWPE composites significantly outperformed PE-Ni2 in terms of oxidative stability (Figs. 4D, S7). For example, PE-Ni2/Tannic Acid achieved the highest OIT (67 min) and Td0 (267 °C), which was significantly better than that of PE-Ni2. These UHMWPE composites combined antioxidant supports with residual ligands to achieve a dual stabilization effect. Thus, these catalysts supported on biomass-derived antioxidant supports could easily synthesize UHMWPE with both intrinsically antioxidant properties and excellent mechanical properties. More importantly, these functional UHMWPE composites are difficult to obtain via traditional processing methods.

Overall, these three strategies demonstrate distinct technical advantages compared to conventional antioxidant modification methods. Particularly for the notoriously difficult-to-modify UHMWPE, the intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE synthesized in this work has a reduced loading of the antioxidant component (down to ppm level), which can achieve or exceed the antioxidant performance of traditional pathways (Fig. 4E)84–91.

Applications of intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE

Having systematically demonstrated the ability of these strategies to impart intrinsically antioxidant properties to polyolefins and their generality across different catalyst systems, we subsequently turned our attention to their practical application. Among them, for UHMWPE, a pivotal application is its use as a bearing component in artificial joints92–94, which usually require radiation crosslinking treatment before implantation to effectively reduce in vivo wear or to mitigate osteolysis caused by wear debris95,96. However, radiation-induced residual free radicals can cause oxidative degradation during storage or in vivo service. An effective solution is to introduce exogenous antioxidants to provide long-term protection to implants97. Therefore, intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE developed in this study provides a potential solution to residual free radicals in radiation-crosslinked UHMWPE applications. In this study, the synthesized UHMWPE powder was compression-molded at 200 °C, sealed, and γ-irradiated (Cobalt-60 source; dose rate: 35.92 Gy/min, total dose: 50 kGy) to yield crosslinked samples. First, the residual free radical content in the irradiated samples was evaluated using electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spectroscopy (Fig. 5A). The obvious result is that the residual free radical content of radiation-crosslinked C-PE-Ni3 was significantly lower than that of the control group (commercial UHMWPE, vitamin E content of 0.015 wt%). This demonstrates that uniformly distributed residual phenolic-functionalized catalysts in directly synthesized UHMWPE provided excellent radical quenching efficacy. Furthermore, Fig. 5B shows that although the OIT value of radiation-crosslinked C-PE-Ni3 decreased by approximately half after irradiation, attributed to the consumption of intrinsic antioxidant groups during radical scavenging, the radiation-crosslinked C-PE-Ni3 nevertheless maintained its thermo-oxidative stability, which was significantly superior to that of the control group. Another critical challenge is in the trade-off between the radical scavenging efficacy of antioxidants and their inhibitory effect on crosslinking efficiency at higher concentrations39. Fortunately, this strategy achieved excellent antioxidant properties by introducing minimal but uniformly distributed residual phenolic functionalized catalysts (0.015 wt%) during the synthesis process, preventing the inhibition of radiation crosslinking that may be caused by the usual addition of large amounts of antioxidants (>0.3 wt%). Mechanical characterization (Fig. 5C, S8) showed that the radiation-crosslinked C-PE-Ni3 maintained excellent overall mechanical properties, exhibiting a significant increase in its Young’s modulus to 1.4 GPa while maintaining a stable yield strength (22 MPa) and elongation at break (210%). Furthermore, the toughness loss of radiation-crosslinked C-PE-Ni3 was much lower than that of the commercial UHMWPE control sample with an equivalent vitamin E content (0.015 wt%) (Figs. 5C, S9). For C-PE-Ni3, the toughness after crosslinking remained 73% of its pre-irradiation value, whereas the commercial control sample retained only 39% of its pre-irradiation toughness. Notably, the radiation-crosslinked C-PE-Ni3 exhibited significantly improved creep resistance, demonstrating a low permanent creep elongation of 5.5% (Fig. 5D). These results indicate that this intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE sample is suitable for radiation crosslinking processes. It simultaneously maintained its antioxidant efficacy and mechanical performance, providing a balanced solution for radiation-crosslinked UHMWPE.

In addition, UHMWPE fibers are among fibers with the highest specific strength and modulus and are widely used in military equipment, marine engineering, safety protection, textiles, and medical implant98–100. However, during both spinning and long-term service, UHMWPE is susceptible to photo-oxidative, thermo-oxidative, and mechano-oxidative degradation when exposed to elevated temperatures and/or photo-oxidative conditions; the addition of antioxidants to UHMWPE is typically necessary101. The intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE synthesized in this work, including homogeneous catalyst-synthesized UHMWPE (PE-Ni3) and white lignin-supported catalyst-synthesized UHMWPE composites (PE-Ni2/White Lignin), exhibited exceptional solution spinnability. This enabled the production of high-performance, intrinsically antioxidant UHMWPE fibers without the need for external stabilizers. These fibers were processed via industrially-optimized wet-gel spinning, followed by being drawn multiple times. As shown in Figs. 5E, S10, all fibers achieved tensile strengths exceeding 40 cN/dtex and initial moduli above 1000 cN/dtex, as measured on monofilaments using fiber-specific test fixtures. These UHMWPE fibers also demonstrated excellent antioxidant properties (Fig. 5F), and after long-term high-temperature aging experiments (168 h, 100 °C), their tensile strength only lost about 17% of its initial value. In contrast, the control group fiber with the same amount of commercial antioxidant added via the traditional method lost 35% of its mechanical strength. This synergy between exceptional mechanical properties and intrinsically antioxidant stability demonstrates strong potential for demanding applications requiring long-term durability.

Discussion

Here, we developed a general Catalyst-Enabled Antioxidation Strategy (CEAS) and further enhanced it through two approaches: cooperative effects from auxiliary antioxidants and the use of biomass-derived antioxidant supports. These approaches are applicable to a range of transition metal catalyst systems, enabling the direct synthesis of various intrinsically antioxidant polyolefins with different structures and molecular weights, including branched polyethylene, polyethylene wax, bimodal polyethylene, UHMWPE, and related composites. By leveraging functionalities such as antioxidant properties introduced through phenolic-functionalized catalysts, compatibility with commercial antioxidants, and the ability to serve as anchoring sites for supported catalysts, these strategies endow the catalysts with dual functionality: efficient catalytic polymerization and intrinsically antioxidant activity. This dual functionality fundamentally overcame the non-uniform antioxidant distribution of physical mixing approaches and catalyst poisoning during polar monomer copolymerization. The obtained intrinsically antioxidant polyolefin has excellent mechanical and antioxidant properties with a residual antioxidant ligand content as low as 15 ppm. Moreover, for the particularly challenging case of antioxidant UHMWPE, it’s potential in demanding applications such as radiation cross-linking and high-performance fiber manufacturing has been explored. Since one of the most widely practiced synthetic methodologies to access various polymers involves metal-mediated polymerization, it is envisioned that these strategies can be easily implemented in the synthesis of many other types of polymers with intrinsically antioxidant properties.

Methods

General methods and materials

All experiments were carried out under a dry nitrogen atmosphere using standard Schlenk techniques or in a glovebox. Dichloromethane, THF, and heptane were purified in solvent purification systems. Deuterated solvents used for Liquid NMR spectroscopy were dried and distilled prior to use. Liquid NMR spectra were recorded on a JNMECZR/S1 spectrometer at ambient temperature unless otherwise stated. The chemical shifts of the 1H and 13CNMR spectra were referenced to tetramethylsilane. Coupling constants are in Hz. DSC measurements were performed on a TA Instruments DSC 250. Samples (ca. 5 mg) were annealed by heating to 160 °C at 10 °C min−1, cooled to 40 °C at 10 °C min−1, and then analyzed while being heated to 160 °C at 10 °C min−1.

Gel permeation chromatography (GPC)

Molecular weight and molecular weight distribution of the polymer were determined by gel permeation chromatography (GPC) with a PL-220 equipped with two Agilent PL gel Olexis columns at 160 °C using 1,2,4-trichlorobenzene as a solvent, and the calibration was made using polystyrene standard and are corrected for linear polyethylene by universal calibration using the Mark-Houwink parameters of Rudin: K = 1.75 × 10−2 cm3 g−1 and R = 0.67 for polystyrene, K = 5.90 × 10−2 cm3 g−1 and R = 0.69 for polyethylene and K = 40.6 × 10−2 cm3 g−1 and R = 0.725 for ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene.

Viscosity-average molecular weight

Viscosity-average molecular weight of the polymer was determined by capillary viscometers IVS800H from Hangzhou Zhongwang Technology Company at 135 °C using decahydronaphthalene as a solvent. The times of flow of a solvent and a solution of polymer at a specified concentration in that solvent are measured at 135 °C. The reduced viscosity and intrinsic viscosity are calculated from these measurements and from the known concentration of the solution. According to ISO 1628, k = 5.37 × 10−4 and α = 1.49 for ultra-high molecular weight polyethylene.

Mechanical properties

Stress/strain experiments were performed at room temperature at 10 mm/min using a universal testing machine, Suns UTM 2502 from Shenzhen Suns Technology Company. At least three specimens of each polymer were tested. Each polymer was melt-pressed at about 150–200 °C, 5 MPa for 5 min to obtain the test specimen. The dumbbell shaped test specimens had the following dimensions: gauge length, 28 mm; width, 2 mm; and thickness, about 0.5 mm.

Oxidative-induction time (OIT)

Oxidation induction time (OIT) was measured using differential scanning calorimetry (Q250, TA Instruments, USA) according to ISO 11357-6. Following ISO 293, the test sample is melt-pressed into a sheet prior to analysis to yield a consistent sample morphology and mass and cut into circular-shaped specimens for tests (diameter, 4 mm). Samples (~10 mg, n = 3 each) were placed in an uncovered aluminum pan and heated from 40 to 200 °C at 20 °C/min under a nitrogen flow of 50 ml/min. After equilibrating for 5 min, the purge was switched to pure oxygen (50 ml/min). The OIT reflecting the onset of exothermal oxidation was determined by the intersection between the baseline and the tangent line of the steepest exothermic curve.

Thermogravimetric analysis (TGA)

The thermal properties of samples were performed on a thermal analyzer (STA200, HITACHI, Japan). The samples were heated at a rate of 10 °C/min in Air from room temperature to 700 °C. In TGA under air, the initial oxidation temperature (Td0) was defined as the temperature at 0% mass variation (the inflection point of mass variation), characteristic of polyethylene oxidation onset.

Samples preparation of radiation crosslinked UHMWPE

The material was melt-pressed into sheet (thickness, approximately 0.5 mm) at 200 °C under 5 MPa for 5 min. Then, the sintered UHMWPE sheets were encapsulated in sealed bags. Thin, the materials with irradiation dose (50 kGy) were obtained by irradiation (Cobalt-60), with radiation dose rates of 35.92 Gy/min for 23 h. After irradiation, the materials are vacuum annealed at 120 °C for 6 h (Only ESR tests are without annealing).

Electron paramagnetic resonance spectroscopy (ESR)

The instrument used for ESR testing is JES-FA200 (JEOL). The microwave power is l mW, the magnetic field modulation frequency is 100 kHz, and the scanning time is 1 min. The free radical concentration in the sample was obtained through the multiple integration of ESR spectra. All ESR tests are conducted before annealing.

Creep behavior102

The tensile creep experiments were performed at room temperature on a TA Instruments DMA 850. The specimen for creep tests was 20 mm long, 2 mm wide and 0.5 mm thick. The experiments were conducted under stress levels of 10 MPa, and the stress was held for a 40 min creep period. At least three samples were tested for each processed material.

Preparation of UHMWPE fiber by gel spinning-ultradrawing103,104

In a nitrogen atmosphere, the paraffin oil was used as the spinning solvent to prepare UHMWPE solution with the concentration of 3% by mechanical stirring at 240 °C. After sufficient dissolution, cool the spinning solution to room temperature. At 150 °C, the UHMWPE spinning solution was extruded through the spinneret with the diameter of 0.5 mm and immediately quenched into gel fibers by coagulation bath. Dichloromethane was used as an extractant to separate the solvent from gel fibers, and all samples should be extracted 3 times under ultrasonication with 6 min for each time to remove solvent in gel fibers as much as possible. The gel fibers after extraction contained a large number of extractant, so they were put into fuming hood for drying for a certain time to completely remove the dichloromethane. It is an effective way to acquire high-strength and high-modulus UHMWPE fibers by three-stage hot-drawing of fibers, which were conducted at 90 °C with the drawing ratio of 10, 2, 1.5.
