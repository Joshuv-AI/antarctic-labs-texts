Management of a Chronically Infolded Self-Expanding Aortic Valve
Authors: Sofia Hu, Daniel Amponsah, Sachin Malik, Patricia Nguyen, Neha Mantri, Reem Elorbany, Amelia C Watkins, Yihan Lin, Guson Kang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundInfolding is a known complication of self-expandable transcatheter valves that typically causes hemodynamic compromise. Delayed diagnosis of infolding is rare and can result in premature leaflet degeneration, with limited treatment options.Case SummaryA 71-year-old man presented 5 years after transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) with severe intravalvular regurgitation. Transesophageal echocardiography revealed significant infolding of the valve. The patient underwent valve-in-valve TAVR, which successfully resolved the aortic insufficiency.DiscussionThis is the first reported case of a chronically infolded TAVR, which is a rare but significant complication of self-expandable valves. We demonstrate that valve-in-valve replacement can successfully overcome chronic frame distortion and endothelialization.Take-Home MessagesTranscatheter valve infolding does not necessarily cause significant hemodynamic changes, which can lead to delays in diagnosis and treatment. Chronic infolding can be visualized by 3D computed tomography and can result in premature leaflet degeneration. Structural distortion of self-expandable valves, even years after implant, can be overcome with high-pressure valvuloplasty and valve-in-valve replacement.Key words: aortic valve, stenosis, valve replacement

History of Presentation

A 71-year-old man with a history of reduced systolic function presented with fatigue and was diagnosed with low-flow, low-gradient aortic stenosis. Stress echocardiography revealed a left ventricular ejection fraction (EF) of 25%, an aortic valve mean gradient of 45 mm Hg, and valve area of 0.56 cm2, and he was diagnosed with low-flow, low-gradient aortic stenosis. Computed tomography (CT) showed valvular calcification with a maximum annular diameter of 28.7 mm (Figure 1, Video 1). The patient underwent transfemoral transcatheter placement of a 34-mm Medtronic Evolut Pro valve. No predilation or postdilation with balloon valvuloplasty was performed. He tolerated the procedure well, and postdeployment echocardiography revealed mild intravalvular and perivalvular regurgitation.

The patient was followed up with surveillance echocardiography. Initially, the patient's EF improved and left ventricular volume decreased post-transcatheter aortic valve replacement (TAVR) (Figure 2). However, after 1 year, the aortic regurgitation progressed from mild to moderate. Routine post-TAVR surveillance transthoracic echocardiograms (TTEs) demonstrated a gradual decline in EF and an increase in left ventricular volumes.

He was readmitted 5 years later after an episode of near syncope and was found to have increased somnolence and significant deconditioning of several weeks.

Past Medical History

The patient had a history of heart failure with a reduced EF, which predated his TAVR procedure and subsequently improved immediately following TAVR. Echocardiography studies obtained annually after his TAVR continued to demonstrate mild intravalvular and perivalvular regurgitation. He also had a history of right carotid artery stenosis, for which he underwent transcarotid artery revascularization at age 75.

Differential Diagnosis

Differential diagnosis included heart failure exacerbation, recurrent carotid artery stenosis, recurrent aortic stenosis, and coronary artery disease.

Investigations

Initially, workup for cerebrovascular accident was pursued, including cross-sectional imaging of the head and neck, which was negative. Cardiopulmonary causes were assessed, and TTE showed significant systolic dysfunction with an EF of 25% and worsening aortic insufficiency. Subsequently, transesophageal echocardiogram (TEE) was obtained for better visualization and showed severe intravalvular regurgitation. Significant deformation of the valve was also seen (Figure 3). This prompted a review of fluoroscopy obtained at the time of valve deployment, which confirmed infolding of at least 2 nodes (Figure 4).

Re-do TAVR options were considered, and CT TAVR was obtained for preoperative planning and coronary obstruction risk assessment. The study showed incomplete expansion of the TAVR across the commissure between the left and noncoronary cusps, resulting in focal kinking of the bottom half of the valve frame (Figure 5). The left and right coronary arteries were located at 19 mm and 17 mm, respectively, above node 0.

Reconstruction of computed tomography angiography imaging revealed a focal kink across the commissure of the the left and noncoronary cusps.

Management

The patient was recommended for and consented to undergo valve-in-valve TAVR. As the nitinol frame of the patient's existing TAVR was severely kinked, the use of predilation and a balloon-expandable valve was planned to overcome the structural deformity and enable deployment of a sufficiently large second TAVR.

The procedure began with standard transfemoral access, including venous femoral access for transvenous pacing. The bioprosthetic TAVR was first predilated with a 23-mm True Dilatation balloon at high pressure (15 atm) while pacing at 160 and then 180 beats/min (Figure 6A). TEE and fluoroscopy confirmed expansion of the valve and resolution of the frame infolding. However, there was persistent severe intravalvular regurgitation (Figure 6B).

Valvuloplasty and Valve-in-Valve TAVR

Balloon dilation (A) resolved the infolding of the Evolut valve, but TEE showed persistent perivalvular regurgitation (B). Valve-in-valve TAVR was performed with a Sapien 3 Ultra valve (C), which resulted in resolution of the regurgitation (D). TAVR = transcatheter aortic valve replacement; TEE = transesophageal echocardiography.

The balloon was removed and exchanged for the TAVR delivery system. A 26-mm Edwards SAPIEN 3 Ultra was deployed successfully while pacing at 160 and then 200 beats/min (Figure 6C). Deployment was performed at node 5 to facilitate future coronary access. Postdeployment TEE demonstrated no aortic insufficiency and mild perivalvular leak (Figure 6D).

Outcome and Follow-Up

The patient had an uneventful postprocedural recovery and endorsed resolution of symptoms. TTE obtained on postprocedural day 1 demonstrated unchanged mild perivalvular leak. He was discharged without complications to a rehabilitation facility and then home. At 1-month follow-up, TTE showed improvement in systolic function with an EF of 48% (Figure 2) and stable mild perivalvular leak.

Discussion

Infolding is a rare complication of self-expandable transcatheter valves, occurring in 1% of patients.1,2 Risk factors include significant calcification of the native valve, insufficient predilation, and deployment of larger valves.3 In this case, we suspect the infolding was caused by a combination of excessive prosthesis oversizing and bulky calcification, resulting in deformation of the frame upon initial recapture.

Commonly, the incomplete leaflet apposition caused by infolding results in severe aortic insufficiency and hemodynamic compromise.4 When recognized early, infolding can be addressed with balloon postdilation or valve recapture and replacement.3 Prior case reports of valve infolding have demonstrated management at the index procedure only.5

In contrast, the infolding presented in this case did not cause significant hemodynamic changes, and the patient tolerated the mild paravalvular leak for years. Three-dimensional CT imaging obtained 5 years after TAVR placement demonstrated how extensively the frame can infold without resulting in hemodynamic collapse. To our knowledge, this is the first reported case of a chronically infolded self-expandable valve that was not managed during the initial deployment.

When infolding is not promptly addressed, asymmetrical tension and stress on the leaflets can result in premature degeneration. Therefore, approaches that address infolding during the initial valve deployment may not be successful for chronic infolding. As evidenced here, valvuloplasty resolved the valve deformation but not paravalvular leak, indicating irreversible leaflet damage. Following his initial TAVR, the patient experienced years of clinical stability before his acute-on-chronic decline, and this chronicity suggests that progressive leaflet degeneration exacerbated the paravalvular leak.

In such cases, valve-in-valve replacement may present the only solution. Our successful experience with this approach suggests that the self-expanding Nitinol frames do not exhibit significant memory effects from chronic distortion and can rebound to its manufactured shape with valvuloplasty. In addition, the frames can be mobilized despite endothelialization in a distorted conformation and can accommodate full expansion of a balloon-expandable valve. This case illustrates the percutaneous approaches available for structural deformations of self-expandable valves.

Conclusions

Chronic infolding of self-expandable transcatheter valves can result in premature leaflet degeneration, requiring valve-in-valve replacement.
