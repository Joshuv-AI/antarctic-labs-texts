Rare blood, rare voices: a participatory approach to advancing transfusion equity in the Bedouin community
Authors: Sagit Kedem-Yemini
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundThe shortage of Jr(a−) antigen-free blood donations among semi-nomadic Bedouin communities in Southern Israel represents a critical health equity challenge that disproportionately affects marginalized populations. This study employs a participatory approach to identify socio-cultural determinants affecting blood donation practices and develop culturally appropriate strategies for increasing rare blood type donations in this underserved community.MethodsUsing a mixed-methods approach guided by participatory research principles, we conducted the research in three sequential stages: an introductory study to map the socio-cultural and structural context; qualitative research (ethnographic observations, interviews, and focus groups) to identify perceived barriers and enablers; and quantitative research (a culturally sensitive survey) administered to 100 community members to validate and measure the prevalence of the themes that emerged from the qualitative phase. Insights from each stage informed the next, ensuring that the final recommendations were firmly rooted in both community perspectives and empirical data.ResultsMultiple intersecting barriers emerged, including gender-based modesty norms, religious interpretations, institutional mistrust, and accessibility challenges. Family consent proved pivotal, particularly for individuals with personal or familial exposure to transfusion needs. The findings revealed how power dynamics between healthcare institutions and the Bedouin community significantly influence health-seeking behaviors, with an absence of culturally appropriate health communication identified as a key structural barrier.ConclusionThese findings informed the development of a transformative strategic plan centered on community-led education, culturally responsive patient management, and periodic blood drives co-designed with community members. Addressing socio-cultural determinants of health through a participatory framework, provides actionable insights for advancing health equity in marginalized populations and offers a foundation for culturally competent public health interventions that recognize community agency and resilience. This study further contributes to the global understanding of how sociocultural inequities in minority populations affect access to critical health services such as blood transfusions.

Introduction

Healthcare inequities in the Negev bedouin community

Marginalized populations worldwide often face limited access to healthcare due to structural and systemic inequalities. Socioeconomic disparities, geographic isolation, and the mismatch between the healthcare systems and patient needs create barriers to equitable healthcare access [1–5]. The challenges are particularly prevalent in Israel among the Bedouin community of the Negev. Bedouins, traditionally a nomadic Arab-Muslim minority, have specific healthcare needs that have become more challenging due to rapid urbanization, poor infrastructure, and cultural dissonance with contemporary medical frameworks [6, 7]. Understanding the current settlement patterns of this population is crucial to comprehending the depth of these healthcare challenges.

Today, the Bedouin community in the Negev is considered semi-nomadic. While historically nomadic, most Bedouins now live in permanent towns and cities that are recognized by the government (such as Rahat and Hura), as well as in “unrecognized villages.” This term refers to communities that are situated on traditional tribal lands but have not reached a formal agreement with the state regarding land ownership. As a result, these villages lack official status, are not connected to basic infrastructure (such as electricity, water, or sewage systems), and are typically excluded from municipal planning. Residents do not pay municipal taxes, and although the state is obligated to provide basic education and healthcare services, access remains limited and uneven. These disparities in infrastructure and official recognition have a profound impact on the community’s ability to receive equitable healthcare, education, and other essential services [8, 9].

As a consequence, health outcomes have not yet caught up with national averages (e.g [10–13])., despite government attempts to include this population in national health initiatives. Health equity refers to the absence of avoidable, unfair differences among populations. Thus, the severe shortage of Jr(a−) blood among the Bedouin community is not simply a logistical challenge but also reflects deeper systemic inequities in healthcare access and cultural alignment.

The Bedouin population in Israel’s Southern District is an estimated 285,200 people, accounting for 36.7% of the municipality of Be’er Sheva and 14.8% of the Arab population in Israel [14]. In the Negev desert, geographic isolation limits access to healthcare facilities, while the socio-economic disadvantages — the poverty rate is above 60% — exacerbate this access even further [15, 16]. Further barriers to healthcare utilization are posed by cultural factors such as traditional gender roles, family-oriented decision-making, and linguistic differences [17–21]. Such inequities are reflected in higher levels of infant mortality, chronic diseases (e.g., diabetes, hypertension), and lower life expectancy than that of the general Israeli population [7, 22, 23].

The primary healthcare provider for the Negev, Soroka Medical Center, shares systematic difficulties in addressing the challenges of this discrepancy. Being the only tertiary care hospital in the region, it serves a heterogeneous population of 106,000 residents, including Bedouins (Soroka Medical Center, 2022). However, the low rates of blood donation among Bedouins compound shortages of rare blood phenotypes, such as the Jr(a-) antigen, which are necessary for transfusion compatibility [24–26]. While this research focuses on the Bedouin population, similar inequities in blood supply affect other genetically distinct or marginalized communities worldwide, such as the Romani in Europe [27] and certain Japanese populations with rare phenotypes [28].

Blood donation challenges in the bedouin population

A Unique Genetic Profile for the Bedouin Community

The genetic homogeneity of the Bedouin population, resulting from tribal endogamy, is a challenge for healthcare providers. Although the Jr(a-) antigen phenotype occurs in the Bedouin population, it is exceedingly rare in most populations worldwide [24, 28–30], making accurate blood matching essential to prevent severe reactions following transfusion. As a result, Soroka Medical Center suffers from acute shortages of Jr(a-) negative blood, since the donation rate within the Bedouin community is less than 1% [31].

Bedouins and the Jr(a-) Antigen

The Bedouins are a tribal community; since every tribe descends from a common ancestor, all community members are in some way related [32]. As a result, some in the Bedouin community exhibit the Jr(a-) antigen phenotype in their blood, meaning that their blood lacks the Jr(a) antigen. This means that they would require Jr(a-) blood for transfusion compatibility. This phenomenon was discovered in the 1980 s, when two unrelated Bedouin women were hospitalized in Soroka Medical Center and diagnosed with Jr(a-) antibodies. After the two hospitalizations, an additional 152 random Bedouin Women were tested for Jr(a-) antibodies, and all tested positive [24].

Anti Jr(a) antibodies were first reported in 1970 and are most commonly found in pregnant or previously pregnant women [25]. While somewhat rare, the Jr(a-) phenotype is found in Northern European, Bedouin, Mexican, Romani, and, most commonly, Japanese populations [28, 33]. Identification of the Jr(a-) phenotype typically occurs after a Jr(a-) individual receives Jr(a+) blood, leading to alloimmunization and an Acute Hemolytic Transfusion Reaction (AHTR), a life-threatening reaction [34, 35]. Once side effects appear, identification of the antibodies is a complex process requiring large amounts of testing [36].

While the Jr(a-) phenotype is rare, in Israel it is concentrated in specific Bedouin families, mainly in the townships of Tel Sheva and Hura. Like most Bedouin towns, Tel Sheva and Hura face high resident-to-health clinic ratios. Despite relatively better healthcare infrastructure in Tel Sheva and Hura compared to unrecognized villages, according to the websites of four National Health Insurance companies (HMOs) and the Israeli Central Bureau of Statistics website, blood donation rates remain negligible. To address the need for Jr(a-) blood units, the aim of this research was to suggest a strategic plan to encourage blood donations among the Bedouin community, while taking into account their motives and potential barriers to donating [37].

Sociocultural Considerations in Blood Donation

Multiple factors contribute to the low rate of blood donation. Traditional beliefs surrounding bodily integrity, alongside religious interpretations of medical procedures, often discourage participation in blood drives [17, 38]. For instance, Quranic verses on bodily stewardship are viewed by some Bedouins as prohibiting donation, while others interpret them as encouraging it as a moral obligation to save lives [17, 39–41].

Attitudes toward donating also reflect the symbolic significance many cultures assign to blood. Although religious and cultural perspectives on transfusion have shifted over time, traditional views still persist in some communities [42]. Religion continues to shape public behavior and perception around donation practices [17, 41]. In addition to these cultural barriers, geographic isolation and poor infrastructure, including unpaved roads and limited public transportation, reduce access to donation centers [43, 44].

Barriers to Blood Donation

Obstacles to blood donation among Bedouins are complex and multifaceted, involving cultural, educational, and logistical factors. More than 70% of Bedouin donors report a general lack of awareness within the community regarding the medical importance of blood donation, pointing to systemic and health literacy gaps [45, 46]. Cultural factors further hinder participation. Modesty concerns, fear of needles, and the belief that donating weakens the body physically are all cited as deterrents [47, 48].

Logistical barriers are also significant. In unrecognized Bedouin villages, where only 20% have accessible donation centers, residents often face travel times exceeding 60 min to reach a facility. These difficulties are exacerbated by inadequate infrastructure, including unpaved roads and limited public transport options [43].

Addressing the Challenge: Cultural Competence and Intervention Strategies

A systematic review of minority blood donation identifies three key themes: personal connections to recipients encourage donation; cultural and religious identity motivate participation; and medical mistrust and misunderstanding act as barriers [49–51]. These findings highlight the need for culturally tailored interventions. Cultural competence, defined as healthcare that aligns with patients’ cultural values, is central to addressing the problem [52]. For Bedouin communities, culturally competent care requires attentiveness to modesty norms, family-based consent practices, and religious beliefs [10, 44].

Thus, community-based programs have collaborated with tribal leaders to improve health literacy and increase donation rates by providing Arabic-language materials [18, 53]. Training healthcare providers in cultural mediation—bridging between traditional beliefs and modern medicine—has proven effective in these and similar contexts. For example, anthropological insights improved rare blood donation rates among Comorian immigrants in France [54]. In Israel, healthcare providers trained to navigate Bedouin familial hierarchies and consent practices have helped foster trust in medical institutions [8, 11, 46].

Study objectives

The shortage of Jr(a-) antigen-free blood donations within semi-nomadic Bedouin communities in Southern Israel poses a significant public health challenge, particularly for individuals requiring regular transfusions to prevent severe complications or those requiring blood for emergency treatments. This study aimed to identify socio-cultural barriers to blood donation and develop a strategic plan to enhance rare blood type donations in this underserved population. Incorporating community engagement, mobile donation units, and education tailored to different cultures, this research seeks to:

Methodology

This study employed a mixed-methods approach to investigate barriers to blood donation among the Negev Bedouin community, integrating insights from cultural mediation, intersectionality, and community-based participatory research frameworks. The methodology was conducted in three interconnected stages: an introductory study to establish the socio-cultural and structural context; qualitative research to further explore the themes, perceptions, and barriers identified in the first stage; and quantitative research to measure the prevalence and strength of these themes across a broader sample. Findings from each stage directly informed the design and focus of the subsequent stage, ensuring that the final strategic plan was both evidence-based and culturally grounded.

Introductory study and literature review

The preliminary phase aimed to contextualize the study within the Bedouin community’s socio-cultural and structural realities. A systematic literature review was conducted to identify gaps in understanding blood donation barriers among marginalized populations, particularly Arab-Muslim minorities in peripheral regions [44, 56]. Key themes from the review included cultural perceptions of blood donation, logistical challenges in underserved communities, and the role of religious beliefs in healthcare behavior [17, 40].

To ground the research in local realities, artifact analysis was performed on documents from Soroka Medical Center, MDA (Magen David Adom, Israel’s national emergency medical service), and the Clalit HMO, including blood donation records, rare blood type registries, and community outreach reports. These materials highlighted systemic inefficiencies in blood supply chains and the overreliance on familial donations for rare blood types like Jr(a-) [24, 25, 57].

This study received ethics approval from Ben Gurion University of the Negev. Informed consent was obtained from all participants, with additional efforts made to ensure cultural sensitivity and participant autonomy during interviews and surveys.

Qualitative research

Observations and Focus Group

A focus group was conducted in December 2019 with stakeholders from Soroka’s Blood Bank, MDA, Clalit HMO, and the Tel Sheva town council. Observations centered on a rare-blood-type donation drive in Tel Sheva on January 26, 2020, where 26 Bedouin donors were screened for Jr(a-) antigens. The session revealed logistical challenges (e.g., mobile unit placement, language barriers) and cultural hesitancy linked to modesty norms and distrust in medical institutions [44, 56].

Motives

Importance

Interviews and questionnaires showed that nearly all participants recognize the importance of blood donation, believing it helps people and can save lives. However, no statistical significance was found between acknowledging the importance of blood donation and actually donating (r =.231, p =.255). This suggests that while most people value blood donation, this belief does not necessarily translate into action [59]. Such attitude-behavior gaps suggest that awareness campaigns alone may be insufficient; public health strategies should also address emotional, social, and logistical barriers to convert positive attitudes into actual donation behavior.

Family

Interviewees expressed that family consent, such as approval from parents or a spouse, is often needed before donating blood. The questionnaire revealed a significant relationship between actual blood donation and both exposure to donation through a family member (r =.396, p <.05) and having a family member who received a transfusion (r =.364, p <.05). Thus, family influence appears to be a critical factor in the decision to donate blood [60].

Exposure

Participants suggested increasing the use of social media platforms like Facebook, Instagram, and TikTok to promote blood donation. The questionnaire found a significant correlation between actual blood donation and exposure to information about donation (r =.461, p <.05), with the strongest effect seen for social media exposure (r =.313, p <.05). Barriers

Lack of Awareness and Publicity

The most prominent barrier identified in interviews was a lack of awareness regarding the shortage of blood donations. Many participants suggested that increased publicity, such as street campaigns, informative videos, and messages from community leaders, could effectively raise awareness [59, 61]. Tailored information for the Bedouin community may help increase blood donation rates.

Modesty

Interviewees noted that donating blood in public is challenging, especially for Muslim women, as the process requires pulling up sleeves and lying on a bed, which can conflict with modesty norms. Some stated that Islamic guidelines discourage women from lying on a bed in the presence of men, making public donation settings particularly difficult [44].

Religion

Both interviews and questionnaires indicated that Islam generally views blood donation positively, with most participants stating that helping others and saving lives is encouraged. Some believe blood is not strictly Allah’s possession since it renews itself, and community leaders noted that more religious-based publicity, especially through clerics, could further encourage donations.

Fear of Donation Procedure and Risk

Concerns about the donation procedure and potential medical risks were raised, with some preferring a doctor over a paramedic for the procedure. Questionnaire data showed a significant negative correlation between fear of the procedure and the perceived importance of blood donation, suggesting that fear may reduce willingness to donate.

Accessibility

Accessibility was another barrier, as participants reported that blood drives are not held frequently enough in their residential areas, making donation inconvenient. Many preferred scheduled donations at clinics rather than spontaneous drives, and a recent Tel Sheva blood drive revealed the community’s potential for rare blood types if accessibility improves.

Donor Preservation System

When a rare blood type is identified, Soroka’s blood bank sends a notification letter to the donor, encouraging further donations and family testing. The letter also explains the risks of receiving mismatched transfusions, such as developing Jr(a-) antibodies and experiencing acute hemolytic transfusion reactions. Despite in-depth explanations regarding the risk of receiving a blood transfusion that does not match their blood type, this letter sometimes frightened the receiver into inaction.

This study sought to examine the perceptions, motivations, and barriers associated with blood donation within the Negev Bedouin community, with a particular focus on the critical shortage of Jr(a-) blood units. The findings highlight socio-cultural factors that influence donation practices and offer significant insights for rare blood type inventory management among underserved populations. While this study focused on Bedouin communities in southern Israel, its implications extend to other minority groups with distinct genetic profiles, such as populations of Japanese descent, among whom the Jr(a-) phenotype is more prevalent [28, 29].

Key findings revealed that although most participants acknowledged the importance of blood donation, this awareness did not consistently translate into action. Structural and cultural barriers—particularly familial consent norms and modesty considerations—emerged as key barriers to participation. The observed association between the perceived importance of blood donation and fear of the procedure highlights the need for educational interventions that address both cognitive and emotional responses to medical processes.

Participants emphasized the importance of tailored awareness campaigns, with social media identified as a particularly effective platform for younger demographics. Religious endorsements by clerics or imams were also recommended as a means to address the concerns of the more conservative segments of the population. However, such efforts would need to carefully navigate Islamic teachings on modesty and bodily integrity [62].

A key operational challenge identified by this study pertains to identifying individuals with the Jr(a-) blood type. The current reliance on post-transfusion detection—often following adverse reactions—reflects a reactive rather than preventive approach. Moreover, Israel’s current frozen rare blood storage system, which allows a unit to remain viable for only 72 h after thawing, exacerbates the challenge of meeting urgent transfusion needs. These limitations underscore the necessity of periodic blood drives specifically designed to proactively identify individuals with rare blood types. These include culturally adapted blood drives and targeted outreach in areas with a high likelihood of rare phenotypes.

The study also revealed a gap in the current donor recruitment and preservation methods. The existing notification system, based on impersonal letters informing individuals of their rare blood status, fails to address their concerns or provide sufficient motivation to donate. A shift toward participatory communication models and a more interactive approach, including follow-up conversations, culturally sensitive educational sessions, and opportunities for community dialogue, may improve donor retention and long-term participation.

Although the findings are grounded in the specific context of the Bedouin population, many of the identified barriers—such as fear of the donation process, medical mistrust, limited awareness of rare blood types, and logistical barriers—are common across minority populations worldwide [49, 63, 64]. These findings contribute to the broader understanding of challenges in managing rare blood inventories and emphasize the need for localized strategies, grounded in cultural competence and participatory engagement and scaled or adapted to diverse sociocultural settings.

Limitations

This study has several limitations that should be acknowledged. First, the timing of the data collection—during the initial wave of the COVID-19 pandemic—may have influenced participant perceptions, particularly regarding access to healthcare and trust in public institutions. National lockdowns, service disruptions, and increased health-related anxiety likely shaped the lived experiences discussed in the interviews and may have amplified existing barriers, such as transportation difficulties, institutional distrust, or fear of medical procedures.

Second, the qualitative interviews were conducted in Hebrew with native Arabic-speaking participants, which may have introduced language-related limitations in the depth of communication. The questionnaire, in contrast, was administered in Arabic and later translated into Hebrew and English for analysis and reporting. While every effort was made to maintain the original meaning across languages, there is a risk that certain cultural or emotional nuances were lost in translation, potentially affecting the interpretation of the findings.

Finally, the quantitative questionnaire was distributed among the general population in the study area using voluntary participation. This may have resulted in self-selection bias. Although specific efforts were made to reach individuals with the rare Jr(a−) blood type, it cannot be assumed that all respondents were from this subgroup. These limitations should be considered when interpreting the study’s findings and their generalizability.

Conclusion

This study represents a pioneering effort to investigate the socio-cultural, structural, and logistical challenges associated with blood donation within the Negev Bedouin community, with a focus on improving the inventory of Jr(a-) blood units. The findings highlight critical gaps in donor awareness, recruitment strategies, and preservation practices, while offering evidence-based, culturally responsive recommendations. The study’s findings offer actionable and transferable strategies that can inform culturally grounded rare blood donation initiatives in other culturally distinct or underserved populations supported by transfusion services. A recent publication found similar structural, cultural, and linguistic barriers affecting Bedouin communities [65].

The shortage of Jr(a-) blood is not merely a local issue but part of a global challenge in the management of rare blood phenotypes. With only 18 known Jr(a-) individuals identified in Israel, most post-transfusion, the current reactive approach is insufficient for ensuring an adequate supply. When combined with the technical limitation that frozen blood becomes nonviable within 72 h of thawing, these gaps underscore the need for proactive identification and retention of rare blood donors. Regular, culturally tailored blood drives can serve as a cornerstone of such efforts, alongside broader strategies of trust-building and community engagement.

Broader implications and future research

The findings contribute to a growing body of literature on barriers and motivators in blood donation, focusing on the Bedouin community but also relevant for other underrepresented groups with rare blood phenotypes. The barriers identified—such as cultural resistance, institutional mistrust, and logistical limitations—are shared by marginalized communities globally. Future research should expand to include other sectors of the Bedouin community and other ethnic groups with rare blood types. Additionally, an evaluation should be conducted to examine the long-term impact of culturally grounded interventions through longitudinal, mixed-methods designs.
