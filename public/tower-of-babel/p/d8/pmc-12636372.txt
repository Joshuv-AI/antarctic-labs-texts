Acute endogenous endophthalmitis caused by Streptococcus agalactiae: A case report and review

Background

Streptococcus agalactiae, also known as Group B Streptococcus (GBS), is a significant pathogen responsible for invasive diseases worldwide. It was first classified by Rebecca Lancefield in the 1930s following its isolation from cows with bovine mastitis; however, its pathogenicity in humans was not recognized until 1938. In recent years, Streptococcus agalactiae has been established as a leading cause of neonatal sepsis and meningitis, with its prevalence among bacteremia patients also increasing. Therefore, investigating the epidemiology, molecular traits, biofilm state, and virulence factors of Streptococcus agalactiae in this patient population is of considerable importance [1]. Here, we report a case of acute endogenous endophthalmitis caused by Streptococcus agalactiae.

Case presentation

A 37-year-old man presented to the ophthalmology emergency department at a tertiary hospital with progressively worsening symptoms in his left eye. The clinical course began two days prior to admission with a febrile episode (temperature > 38 °C) that resolved after self-administered oral oseltamivir. Eight hours before presentation, the patient developed acute left eye pain and decreased vision. Before the illness developed, he had no skin infections, no chronic underlying conditions such as diabetes, no history of long-term medication use, and no history of infectious diseases, blood transfusions, surgeries, or trauma. Initial clinical assessment at a primary hospital revealed ipsilateral headache and significantly elevated intraocular pressure (61 mmHg), leading to a provisional diagnosis of glaucoma. Consequently, he was treated with intraocular pressure-lowering agents (including topical eye drops and intravenous mannitol). However, due to progressive worsening of ocular pain and blurred vision, the patient was subsequently transferred to the tertiary hospital for further management. Ophthalmological examination disclosed the following findings: visual acuity was 6/18 in the right eye and 6/300 in the left eye. Intraocular pressure was normal in the right eye but elevated to 52 mmHg in the left eye. Examination of the left anterior segment revealed eyelid swelling, conjunctival mixed congestion, corneal edema, and off-white corneal opacities with areas of keratolysis (Fig. 1). Posterior segment assessment was precluded by significant opacification of the ocular media. B-scan ultrasonography demonstrated extensive vitreous opacities with clustered high‑echo densities. Additionally, imaging studies including cardiac, abdominal, and urinary tract ultrasonography, as well as cranial magnetic resonance imaging, were unremarkable and revealed no identifiable source of infection. Following admission, the patient was closely monitored; however, he experienced recurrent episodes of fever accompanied by myalgia. As the diagnosis of acute endogenous endophthalmitis was established. Accordingly, emergent surgical intervention was performed, including anterior chamber irrigation and intravitreal injection of vancomycin and cefuroxime. During the procedure, aqueous humor samples were collected and appeared turbid and purulent. Concurrently, preliminary Gram staining of blood cultures revealed Gram-positive streptococci, and a complete blood count indicated elevated white blood cell and neutrophil counts. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing confirmed that the Streptococcus agalactiae isolate was susceptible to vancomycin, moxifloxacin, penicillin, levofloxacin, ampicillin, linezolid, and tigecycline. After the bacteremia was brought under control with systemic antibiotic therapy (moxifloxacin and penicillin), evisceration and debridement of the affected eye were performed. Intraoperatively, the ocular contents were found to be filled with purulent material. The surgeon thoroughly evacuated these purulent tissues, which were sent for pathological examination. Pathology showed extensive neutrophilic infiltration and necrosis of most intraocular tissues, including the choroid and retina, with abscess formation. Postoperatively, in addition to topical antibiotic eye drops, the patient received a full course of systemic penicillin 320,000 units intravenously every 4 h for over two weeks.

Discussions

Endophthalmitis is a serious intraocular infection that can lead to irreversible blindness in the affected eye. Exogenous form, which constitute the majority, typically arise postoperatively from procedures such as cataract surgery or intravitreal injections, or following penetrating ocular trauma. In contrast, the endogenous form refers to the hematogenous seeding of pathogens into the eye from a distant site of infection; however, as the associated bacteremia or fungemia can be transient, patients might not exhibit overt signs of systemic illness. This typically happens when bacteria, originating from a distant septic focus, cross the blood-ocular barrier during bloodstream infection. A minority of cases result from secondary neurogenic or meningeal spread following the anatomical course of the optic nerve. It is a rare condition, accounting for only 5–15 % of all endophthalmitis cases. This complication occurs in approximately 0.04 % of bacteremia and 0.05–0.4 % of fungemia cases [2], [3]. The intraocular infection typically originates in the highly vascular choroid of the posterior segment. Common presenting symptoms in patients with endogenous bacterial endophthalmitis include reduced vision (90 %), eye pain (50 %), hypopyon (35 %), and vitritis (33 %) [4]. Diagnosis is often delayed in one-third of patients because of atypical initial symptoms. In this context, reduced vision accompanied by fever should raise strong suspicion for endogenous endophthalmitis. Similar with the present case, a report by Priyadarshini et al. described endogenous endophthalmitis caused by Enterococcus casseliflavus in a healthy male in his early thirties, with no history of trauma, hospitalization, or immunodeficiency. This case highlights the pathogen's rarity in causing endophthalmitis in otherwise healthy individuals and confirms the efficacy of Teicoplanin [5]. Furthermore, the infecting bacteria commonly disseminate from distant sites like endocarditis, urinary tract infections, or liver abscesses. Recognized risk factors for this condition are older age, diabetes mellitus, concurrent infections (especially urinary tract infections), use of immunosuppressants or corticosteroids, and malignancy [6], [7]. In a reported case of pneumococcal meningitis complicated by endogenous endophthalmitis, Steinier et al. described characteristic MRI findings, including preseptal orbital cellulitis and mild T1 hyperintensity within the vitreous. They emphasized that during cerebral MRI performed for septic patients, radiologists and clinicians should conduct a meticulous inspection of the orbital anatomy to identify early signs of ocular involvement and its associated complications [8]. Furthermore, Saleh et al. documented a case of a healthy individual, without any known risk factors, who went to the emergency department for acute monocular vision loss. Subsequent CT imaging revealed multiple prostatic abscesses within the abdomen and pelvis. This finding underscores the diagnostic value of systematic imaging in evaluating endogenous endophthalmitis [9]. Besides, distinct geographical disparity exists in the causative organisms of endogenous endophthalmitis. Staphylococcus aureus and Streptococcus pneumoniae are common in the West, whereas Klebsiella pneumoniae predominates in the East. Notably, this latter pathogen is strongly associated with pyogenic liver abscesses. In a retrospective series of 18 patients with K. pneumoniae liver abscess-associated endophthalmitis, Chung et al. demonstrated that a final BCVA better than 20/60 required both very early detection and a favorable initial visual acuity, implying limited microbial seeding. This highlights the critical need for timely recognition and management to improve prognosis, independent of underlying conditions [10]. Moreover, Suo et al. also evaluated prognostic factors for visual acuity in endogenous Klebsiella pneumoniae endophthalmitis. The study determined that early vitrectomy is critical for achieving better visual outcomes. Conversely, a worse final visual prognosis was significantly associated with poorer initial visual acuity, corneal edema, and delayed timing of surgery [11]. Collective evidence from prior cases and reviews establishes that endogenous endophthalmitis caused by GBS carries a poor visual outcome, a trend exacerbated by diminished presenting vision. Endocarditis represents the most prevalent source of infection, while arthritis, cellulitis, and urinary tract infections are less common. Mirroring the pathophysiology of other endogenous types, GBS infection commonly initiates in the retinal parenchyma before subsequent dissemination to the vitreous and anterior segments [12], [13]. As an atypical causative organism for endogenous endophthalmitis, Streptococcus agalactiae is responsible for only approximately 5.2 % of endogenous endophthalmitis cases but is frequently associated with a poor visual prognosis. Several studies emphasized early identification and diagnosis are crucial in patients with streptococcal endophthalmitis. However, in this case, although the patient experienced a febrile episode two days prior to admission, the initial diagnostic workup failed to identify a source of infection. In cases where the source of infection remains undetermined, empirical antimicrobial therapy (such as topical and systemic administration of vancomycin and ceftazidime) should be initiated as soon as possible. The treatment regimen should subsequently be adjusted once the results of blood, urine, and anterior chamber paracentesis cultures and susceptibility testing become available [14], [15], [16], [17]. In addition to systemic antibiotic administration, early intravitreal injection of antimicrobial agents (e.g., ceftazidime and vancomycin as first-line therapy) or vitrectomy may help preserve visual function and improve clinical outcomes. Although there is no standardized consensus about vitrectomy for endogenous endophthalmitis, Yoshida et al. conducted a combined analysis of cases and existing literature regarding endogenous endophthalmitis resulting from GBS. The findings demonstrated that 60 % of patients experienced devastating visual outcomes, including no light perception, phthisis bulbi, ocular removal, or death. Notably, surgical intervention with vitrectomy failed to prevent complete vision loss in nearly half (7 of 15) of the operated cases [13]. In cases of irreversible, severe infection—such as those complicated by corneal melting—enucleation or evisceration may be necessary to alleviate patient suffering [18]. However, antimicrobial resistance in Streptococcus agalactiae is increasing globally, driven by widespread antibiotic overuse and the bacterium's inherent genetic diversity. In the present antibacterial susceptibility study, tetracycline resistance was observed in the vast majority of isolates (91.8 %). High resistance rates were also noted for erythromycin (86.9 %), chloramphenicol (67.2 %), clindamycin (42.6 %), levofloxacin (19.7 %), and linezolid (13.1 %). Multidrug resistance was detected in 77.0 % of the isolates. Despite reports of hypervirulent isolates, most demonstrate sustained susceptibility to penicillin and vancomycin. In addition, visual outcomes in endogenous endophthalmitis depend on multifactorial determinants. Although infection with GBS generally portends a poor visual prognosis, eyes that present with initial vision of counting fingers or hand motion typically retain the greatest potential for visual improvement. For culture-negative cases, the initiation of empiric broad-spectrum intravitreal antimicrobial therapy is indicated to reduce the risk of further visual deterioration [19].

GBS is a notable pathogen capable of causing serious ocular infections, both from external and hematogenous sources. The case offers several critical clinical insights: First, it underscores the importance of early diagnosis, particularly in otherwise healthy individuals without identifiable risk factors, where prompt sampling for microbial culture is essential. Second, it highlights the critical role of early intravitreal intervention, including empirical antibiotic injection. Should ocular inflammation progress despite systemic stability, timely vitrectomy is warranted. Third, despite aggressive treatment, GBS endophthalmitis carries a poor visual prognosis, with some patients requiring evisceration or debridement of the affected eye—making postoperative care an equally important consideration. Given the polymicrobial etiology of such infections, the scientific community continues to face the dual challenge of curbing the rise of antimicrobial resistance and identifying novel, safe, and effective therapeutic strategies.
