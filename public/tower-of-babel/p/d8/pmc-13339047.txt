Bemnifosbuvir: An HCV NS5B Inhibitor With Multiple Modes of Action
Authors: Carolin Zitzmann, Ruy M Ribeiro, Aurélien Marc, Xiao‐Jian Zhou, Alan S Perelson
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBemnifosbuvir (BEM) is a potent, pan‐genotypic inhibitor targeting the hepatitis C virus (HCV) NS5B polymerase. Its antiviral activity was evaluated in an ascending dose phase I clinical trial involving 30 patients treated once a day for 7 days. After treatment initiation, plasma HCV RNA declined in a biphasic manner with a mean reduction of 2.3 log10 IU/mL after 24 hours and 4.4 log10 IU/mL by day 7 for the highest dose. Alanine aminotransferase (ALT) also normalized in most patients. We employed a multiscale mathematical model fitted to the HCV RNA and ALT dynamics to quantify the antiviral activity and evaluate the modes of action of BEM. We found that models in which BEM only acted as a typical HCV RNA polymerase inhibitor and reduced the intracellular production of HCV RNA did not fit the data as well as models in which BEM had multiple modes of action, including suppressing viral assembly and secretion and enhancing intracellular HCV RNA degradation. BEM's effectiveness in inhibiting intracellular HCV RNA production increased with dose (150 mg/day: 88.2%, 300 mg/day: 98.8%, 600 mg/day: 99.5%), while inhibition of viral assembly and release was ~95% effective regardless of dose. We observed a dose‐dependent enhancement in the degradation of intracellular HCV RNA, with degradation rates 1.5‐fold higher in patients receiving 300 mg/day and 2.7‐fold higher in those receiving 600 mg/day than in patients receiving 150 mg/day. No significant differences in antiviral activity were detected between HCV genotypes 1b and 3 or between patients with and without compensated cirrhosis.

Study Highlights

WHAT IS THE CURRENT KNOWLEDGE ON THE TOPIC?

Direct‐acting antivirals targeting the HCV NS5B polymerase achieve high cure rates primarily by inhibiting viral RNA replication, but the full spectrum of the intracellular modes of action of the novel HCV polymerase inhibitor bemnifosbuvir has not been characterized.

WHAT QUESTION DID THIS STUDY ADDRESS?

This study asked whether the antiviral effects of bemnifosbuvir in patients with chronic HCV infection can be fully explained by inhibition of HCV RNA replication alone or whether additional mechanisms, such as inhibiting virus assembly and secretion or enhancing intracellular viral RNA degradation, contribute to its in vivo antiviral effectiveness.

WHAT DOES THIS STUDY ADD TO OUR KNOWLEDGE?

Using a multiscale viral‐host mathematical model fitted to HCV RNA and ALT kinetics obtained during a clinical trial of bemnifosbuvir, we show that bemnifosbuvir exerts multiple modes of action in vivo, including inhibition of HCV RNA replication in a dose‐dependent manner, inhibition of virus assembly and secretion, and enhanced degradation of intracellular viral RNA.

HOW MIGHT THIS CHANGE CLINICAL PHARMACOLOGY OR TRANSLATIONAL SCIENCE?

These results demonstrate how using quantitative systems pharmacology can help improve our understanding of complex antiviral actions during early‐phase clinical studies. This approach can also guide dose selection, design drug combination protocols, support the development of drugs based on their mechanisms, and improve the assessment of new antivirals for different groups of patients.

Hepatitis C virus (HCV) infection remains a major global public health concern, affecting millions worldwide and contributing to significant liver‐related morbidity and mortality. In 2022, there were 50 million people chronically infected with HCV, with 1 million new infections occurring each year. 1 If left untreated, chronic HCV can progress to cirrhosis, hepatocellular carcinoma, and liver failure, often showing no symptoms until the advanced stages of the disease. Despite being a curable disease, only an estimated 36% of individuals with hepatitis C were aware of their infection, and just 12.5 million had received treatment, leaving a significant treatment gap that resulted in 242,000 deaths in 2022. 1

The introduction of direct‐acting antivirals (DAAs) has dramatically altered the natural history of HCV, transforming it from a progressive, often fatal condition into one that is curable in most patients. 2 Central to this therapeutic success are inhibitors of NS5B, the viral RNA‐dependent RNA polymerase responsible for HCV genome replication. 3 , 4

Despite these advances, important challenges remain in clinical practice. Subgroups of individuals, such as those with cirrhosis, advanced liver disease, genotype (GT) 3 infection, prior treatment failure, or coinfections, continue to experience reduced treatment success or limited therapeutic options. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 In addition, access to safe, affordable, pan‐genotypic therapies is uneven across geographic regions. Moreover, treatment interruptions or failures pose ongoing concerns for viral resistance and disease progression. 10 , 11 , 12

There is a clear need for simplified, pan‐genotypic regimens with high resistance barriers and shorter treatment durations. Bemnifosbuvir (BEM) is a modified guanosine nucleotide prodrug inhibitor of the HCV NS5B polymerase and represents a promising new drug treatment for managing chronic hepatitis C. 13 , 14 , 15 It has shown no indication of the mitochondrial toxicity observed with other guanosine nucleotides. 13 BEM is metabolized into an active 5′ triphosphate metabolite, which inhibits successful NS5B‐dependent HCV replication by incorporating into the nascent HCV RNA, leading to chain termination. The plasma half‐life of AT‐273, a nucleoside metabolite considered a surrogate of the active intracellular 5′ triphosphate, exceeds 20 h, supporting once daily dosing. 14

To gain deeper insight into the within‐host dynamics of HCV suppression under BEM therapy, we used a mechanistic multiscale model developed previously. 16 , 17 , 18 Applied initially to HCV‐infected individuals treated with daclatasvir, an NS5A inhibitor, and telaprevir, an HCV protease inhibitor, this model provided critical insights by distinguishing the suppression of HCV RNA production from the inhibition of virus assembly and secretion from an infected cell. 16 Building on this foundation, we applied a similar multiscale modeling approach to characterize the dynamics of HCV decline in the individuals treated with BEM monotherapy in a multiple ascending dose clinical trial. This framework enables the investigation of intracellular effects of NS5B inhibition and their linkage to observed plasma viral load dynamics. Our goal was to quantify the dose‐dependent efficacy and BEM's modes of action.

METHODS

Participant population

The current analysis was based on longitudinal plasma viral load (HCV RNA) and ALT data obtained from the phase I clinical trial described below.

In a phase I multi‐part dose‐escalation clinical trial (Clinicaltrials.gov NCT03219957 14 ) a total of 30 HCV‐infected individuals were treated with BEM as a single agent administered once daily for 7 days: 6 (20%) received 150 mg, 6 (20%) received 300 mg, and 18 (60%) received 600 mg (doses are expressed as BEM salt form, with 600 mg corresponding to 553 mg free base). Dose levels were first evaluated for safety and tolerability in healthy human volunteers, and the dose levels of 150, 300, and 600 mg once daily were then chosen for study in HCV‐infected individuals. 13 , 14

Most individuals were infected with genotype (GT) 1b (21/30; 70%), followed by GT 3 (8/30; 26.7%), and GT 2 (1/30; 3.3%). Most (24/30; 80%) were non‐cirrhotic, and all cirrhotic individuals were assigned to the 600 mg/day dosing group. The median age across all dosing groups was 41 (range 29–64). Twelve participants (12/30; 40%) were female, and all participants were white (Table 1).

Baseline participant characteristics

Baseline HCV RNA showed a numerically increasing trend across dose groups (Table 1), although there was substantial overlap in the observed distributions. Among GT 1 non‐cirrhotic participants, the median baseline HCV RNA was identical in the 300 mg/day and 600 mg/day groups (6.2 log10 IU/mL), compared with 5.7 log10 IU/mL in the 150 mg/day group, suggesting that the overall trend was not consistently maintained in this subgroup.

Ethics statement

The current study is not a clinical trial and only involved re‐analyses of the previously collected de‐identified data and was deemed not human subjects research by the Los Alamos National Laboratory Institutional Review Board.

Model development and selection

We employed a previously developed multiscale model to assess the antiviral activity of BEM and to determine its modes of action.

The multiscale model, shown schematically in Figure 1 , extends the standard target‐cell‐limited model, 19 which includes uninfected target cells (T), infected cells (I), and free virus (V), by incorporating key processes such as intracellular HCV RNA (R) replication, virion assembly and secretion, and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) kinetics (A). The model has been used to study the effects of HCV polymerase, protease, and NS5A inhibitors used singly or in combination. 16 , 17 , 18 , 20 , 21

The model keeps track of the length of time a cell has been infected, denoted by the age (a) of the infected cell. As a function of a, the amount of intracellular positive‐strand viral RNA (R) changes as it is produced at the maximum rate α R. degrades at rate μ, and is assembled into virions at rate ρ. Virus is assumed to be cleared by a first‐order process at rate c. Under DAA treatment, replication complexes, or equivalently negative‐strand viral RNAs, that serve as the template for positive‐strand HCV RNA, are degraded at rate γ, which slows the production of positive‐strand viral RNA. The DAA is modeled as potentially having multiple modes of action: inhibition of the intracellular production of viral RNA (ε α), inhibition of virus assembly and release (ε s), and increase in the degradation rate of positive‐strand viral RNA by a factor κ. BEM's potential modes of action are represented by three intracellular parameters: ε α, the effectiveness of the drug in reducing the production rate of viral RNA; ε s, the effectiveness of the drug in inhibiting virus assembly and secretion; and κ, the factor by which drug therapy enhances the degradation rate of intracellular viral RNA. The constants ε α and ε s are assumed to be between 0 and 1, with 0 implying no effect and 1 being 100% effective. κ is assumed to be ≥ 1, indicating a potential for increased viral RNA degradation during therapy. We also include in the model a potential indirect effect of drug therapy in reducing the rate of production of viral RNA (parameter γ in the model). This could occur because therapy reduces the production of viral proteins essential to maintain replication complexes or due to the degradation of negative‐strand HCV RNA that acts as the replication template. 2 , 4

To capture dose effects, we tested dose dependency (i.e., dose as a statistical covariate) in the parameters defining drug effects (ε α, ε s, κ, γ). Statistical significance of these covariates was evaluated using the Wald test, with P‐values indicating significance relative to the reference group (150 mg/day).

Data fitting and statistical analysis

The analysis was based on longitudinal plasma viral load (HCV RNA) and ALT data. We simultaneously fit the predicted log10 V(t) and log10 A(t) computed by Eqs. (1) and (3) to the logarithm of the viral load and ALT data, respectively, using a mixed‐effects approach as implemented in the software Monolix R2024R1 (Lixoft SA, Antony, France). These models include both fixed effects and random effects. Fixed effects represent typical population parameters, while random effects capture inter‐individual variability. We assumed a constant and proportional error model for VL and ALT, respectively. Note that all dose levels, all individuals, and both V(t) and A(t) were fit simultaneously, corresponding to 690 data points to estimate 14 parameters in the final model, including dose as a covariate to assess potential dose‐dependent effects.

Model performance was evaluated using two statistical measures: negative log likelihood (−LL) and the corrected Bayesian Information Criterion (BICc). Lower −LL values indicate a better fit to the observed data. The BICc similarly evaluates model fit but penalizes increased model complexity (i.e., a higher number of fitted parameters), thereby identifying the simplest model adequately explaining the data. A lower BICc is preferred; however, a difference in BICc values (ΔBICc) of ≤ 2 indicates minimal evidence favoring one model over another, while a ΔBICc > 10 indicates essentially no support for the alternative model. 22 For each model, at least 25 Monolix runs were performed using different initial parameter guesses, and 95% confidence intervals of model parameters were calculated with Monolix.

The parameters α R = 40 viral RNA copies/d, ρ = 8.18/d and μ = 1/d were fixed based on previous studies. 16 , 18 , 20 , 21 All remaining model parameters including the initial viral load and ALT level were estimated from the data.

RESULTS

BEM shows multiple modes of action

We fitted 80 alternative versions of the multiscale model to the longitudinal HCV RNA and ALT measurements from the 30 clinical trial participants, including both dose‐dependent and dose‐independent drug effects, with single or multiple modes of action for the drug (see Supporting Information ). Thus, rather than simply assuming BEM, as an NS5B polymerase inhibitor, inhibited HCV RNA replication, we took an agnostic view and assumed BEM treatment could affect one or multiple processes simultaneously, including intracellular viral RNA production (via two mechanisms), virion assembly/secretion, or intracellular viral RNA degradation.

When comparing dose‐independent single mode of action models, the one assuming inhibition of virion assembly and secretion (−LL = −319, BICc = −226) provided a slightly better fit than that assuming inhibition of HCV RNA production alone (−LL = −305, BICc = −213). This finding suggests that, despite BEM being an HCV NS5B inhibitor, it exerts a strong effect on virion assembly and secretion (ε s ≠ 0). In both of these single‐mode models, drug efficacy exceeded 90% (Table 2).

For the dual‐mode of action models, the model incorporating dose‐dependent inhibition of both HCV RNA production and virion assembly/release provided a superior fit (−LL = −452, BICc = −336) and was the statistically preferred model compared with the best dual‐mode dose‐independent model (−LL = −365, BICc = −263; ΔBICc = +73). This indicates that combined effects on HCV RNA synthesis and viral assembly and secretion better capture the observed viral kinetics than either mechanism alone. We also studied whether only one of the two mechanisms might be dose‐dependent. Interestingly, the model with dose‐dependent efficacy on virus assembly/secretion (ε s) fit the data better (−LL = −442, BICc = −332) than the model with dose‐dependent efficacy on HCV RNA production (ε α) (−LL = −394, BICc = −285), with a ΔBICc of +47, thus favoring dose dependence on ε s.

Among the triple‐mode‐of‐action models, those in which drug‐induced decay of the replication template (negative‐strand HCV RNA), γ, was fixed to zero provided the best overall fits (Supporting Information: Tables S4 and S5). Moreover, in models that allowed γ to vary (Table S5), whether in a dose‐independent or dependent manner, the estimated value of γ remained small, and its 95% confidence interval could not be reliably determined. This suggests that the data do not support a meaningful drug effect on the decay of the negative‐strand HCV RNA replication template, and that including this mechanism does not improve the model fit.

We thus examined in more detail triple‐mode‐of‐action models that incorporated drug effects on HCV RNA production (ε α), virion assembly/secretion (ε s), and drug‐enhanced degradation of intracellular HCV RNA (κ). A dose‐independent model for these three effects did not adequately explain the observed viral kinetics (−LL = −434, BICc = −321), and a model in which only ε α, the primary NS5B‐mediated mechanism, was dose dependent gave an improved fit (−LL = −457, BICc = −337), but was not the best model.

Models that included dose dependence for both ε α and κ showed markedly improved performance, regardless of whether ε s was modeled as dose dependent or not. The model with dose dependence on ε α and κ but no dose dependence on ε s provided the best overall fit with the strongest statistical support (−LL = −483, BICc = −358) (Tables 2 and S4). In contrast, the model assuming dose dependence across all three mechanisms had a higher BICc (−347), yielding a ΔBICc of +11. This indicates that the added complexity did not improve model performance and suggests that the dominant dose–response effects arise from inhibition of HCV RNA production (ε α) and enhanced intracellular HCV RNA degradation (κ).

In summary, across all 80 model variations, those incorporating only a single mode of action, whether dose‐dependent or dose‐independent, showed substantially higher BICc and −LL values, indicating these models were disfavored and generated poorer fits compared with multiple mode of action models (Table 2). Among all model versions tested (see Supporting Information: Tables S1 to S5), the triple‐mode‐of‐action model with dose‐dependent inhibition of HCV RNA production (ε α), dose‐independent inhibition of virion assembly and release (ε s), and dose‐dependent enhancement of HCV RNA degradation (κ) achieved the lowest −LL and BICc, making it both the best fitting (lowest negative log likelihood) and statistically preferred (lowest BICc) model to describe the clinical data (Table 2). This model fitted the viral load trajectories (Figure 2) and ALT dynamics (Figure 3) of all participants well, including those with GT 3 infection or compensated cirrhosis. The full set of population parameters for this best‐fitting model is given in Table S6. BEM is highly potent across genotypes and cirrhosis stages

BEM, at the 300 mg/day and 600 mg/day doses, was highly effective with respect to all three modes of action, with the 600 mg dose showing the highest efficacy in blocking HCV RNA replication and enhancing intracellular HCV RNA degradation. In particular, the 300 mg/day dose had an efficacy of 0.988 [95% CI: 0.950, 0.997] in inhibiting intracellular HCV RNA production, while the 600 mg/day dose had an efficacy of 0.995 [95% CI: 0.989, 0.998]. In addition, while the 300 mg/day dose increased the intracellular HCV RNA degradation rate 1.5‐fold (relative to 150 mg/day; P = 3.9 × 10−4), the 600 mg/day dose increased the degradation rate 2.7‐fold (relative to 150 mg/day; P = 3.3 × 10−9). Further, while the model suggested that inhibition of virus assembly and secretion contributes to the antiviral effect of BEM with an efficacy ε s = 0.949 [95% CI: 0.888, 0.977], the effect was not dose‐dependent in the range of doses studied.

Examining the effects of the 600 mg/day dose in more detail, individuals with GT 3 and those with GT 1b had similar viral decline kinetics (Figure 4a). While participants with compensated cirrhosis (C, n = 6) exhibited a slightly more rapid viral decline than those without cirrhosis (NC, n = 12), the difference in viral load at day 7 was not statistically significant (P = 0.61), perhaps due to the small number of patients with cirrhosis (Figure 4b). Similarly, the viral decline response was not significantly different between males (n = 10) and females (P = 0.69) (Figure 4c). Lastly, examining the values of the best‐fit model parameters for all the individuals in the 600 mg/day dose group, we found no statistically significant differences in these parameters between the individuals with different genotypes, cirrhosis state, or gender (Figure 5). The other two dosing groups were too small and homogeneous, and thus not suitable for this type of analysis (Table 1). Overall, with the 600 mg/day dose, the viral load declined by 2.3 log10 over the first 24 h and by 4.4 log10 by day 7.

DISCUSSION

Although direct‐acting antivirals (DAAs) have transformed the treatment landscape for hepatitis C, this infection remains a major global health problem, particularly in patients with GT 3 infection, advanced liver disease, or previous treatment failure. 5 , 6 , 7 , 8 , 9 , 10 , 11 , 12 The development of novel agents such as BEM, a nucleotide analog inhibitor of the HCV NS5B polymerase, which we show has multiple modes of action, remains essential. By targeting a highly conserved region of the viral polymerase, BEM offers the potential for broad activity across genotypes and cirrhosis stages. In this study, we used modeling to evaluate the antiviral activity of BEM, focusing on its ability to inhibit different steps of the HCV life cycle. Specifically, we sought to determine whether BEM, as an NS5B polymerase inhibitor, not only effectively inhibits intracellular HCV RNA production but also impacts viral assembly and secretion and the rate of HCV RNA degradation.

We fitted multiple versions of a multiscale model differing in BEM's mode of action to the HCV RNA and ALT measurements of 30 individuals infected with GT 1b, 2, and 3, under BEM monotherapy at 150 mg, 300 mg, or 600 mg once daily doses for 7 days. The 600 mg/day group included both compensated cirrhotic and non‐cirrhotic individuals. Using population fitting in which we fit both the HCV RNA and ALT data from all 30 individuals simultaneously, we evaluated model versions that included single, dual, triple and quadruple modes of action. The modes of action we considered were directly blocking viral RNA production (ε α) or indirectly blocking it by decreasing the stability of the template for replication, for example, negative‐strand or double‐stranded HCV RNA within replication complexes (γ), inhibiting virus assembly and secretion (ε s), and/or enhancing the intracellular HCV RNA degradation rate (κ). Among the 80 tested models described in the Supplemental Information and in abbreviated form in Table 2, a model with three modes of action outperformed all the others. This best model incorporated dose‐dependent effects on inhibiting HCV RNA production and enhancing the intracellular HCV RNA degradation rate, as well as having a dose‐independent effect on inhibiting virus assembly and release. Furthermore, even though BEM is an NS5B inhibitor, inhibition of HCV RNA production alone cannot explain the observed viral kinetic and ALT data as well as our preferred model that includes potent inhibition of both HCV replication and assembly/secretion and enhanced HCV RNA degradation.

BEM demonstrated antiviral activity across all doses and the three mechanisms of direct action evaluated. At the lowest dose (150 mg/day), inhibition of HCV RNA production (ε α) reached 88.2%, increasing to 98.8% and 99.5% at 300 mg/day and 600 mg/day, respectively. This clear dose–response relationship indicates a progressively stronger suppression of HCV RNA synthesis with increasing dose. In contrast, inhibition of viral assembly and release (ε s) remained uniformly high at approximately 95% across all doses, suggesting that this pathway is maximally inhibited even at the lower concentration tested here and does not contribute to further dose‐dependent gains in antiviral activity. We also observed a 1.5‐ and 2.7‐fold increase in the degradation rate of intracellular HCV RNA at 300 mg/day and 600 mg/day, respectively, confirming that higher doses accelerate the degradation of intracellular viral RNA. Importantly, no statistically significant differences in antiviral activity were found between HCV GT 1b and 3 or between compensated cirrhotic and non‐cirrhotic patients, suggesting that BEM maintains its potency regardless of viral genotype or liver disease status.

There are limitations to this study. Most significantly, the number of patients was small, N = 30, with only six having compensated cirrhosis. This was sufficient to establish the safety and efficacy of the 600 mg/day dose, which was used in a much larger phase 2 clinical trial, but in combination with the NS5A inhibitor ruzasvir. 23 , 24 That study confirmed that BEM plus ruzasvir had high effectiveness in blocking both HCV RNA replication and viral assembly/secretion, was pan‐genotypic, and effective in patients with compensated cirrhosis. 25 Thus, while supportive of the results reported here, the trial was not designed to tease out the separate contribution of BEM and ruzasvir. A second limitation is that the biochemical mechanisms mediating the inhibition of viral assembly/secretion and the enhanced degradation of HCV RNA are not known. However, these could be secondary effects of having fewer viral RNAs due to the reduced production induced by the drug. Third, we assumed constant efficacy and did not include explicit pharmacokinetics (PK)/pharmacodynamics (PD). This is a common approach in many viral dynamics studies. 16 , 17 , 18 , 19 , 20 , 21 , 26 , 27 , 28 , 29 , 30 Finally, we did not include an explicit immune response, which again would make the model more complex, and for which we do not have commensurate data. We note that the effects of DAAs are so powerful that immune response dynamics would be difficult (or impossible) to estimate from viral load alone in these studies. This is particularly true in the current study, where our model already fits the viral load data extremely well (Figure 2). However, residual viremia and infected cells, well below the limit of detection, may need immune responses for final clearance. 31

Together, our findings suggest that BEM is an NS5B polymerase inhibitor that acts through multiple, complementary mechanisms, potently inhibiting HCV RNA production and virion assembly/secretion, while simultaneously enhancing intracellular HCV RNA degradation in a dose‐dependent manner. This combination of effects likely accelerates viral clearance, leads to ALT decreases and to its normalization in most participants. We previously reported that daclatasvir, an NS5A inhibitor, and telaprevir, an NS3/4A protease inhibitor, also worked by inhibiting both HCV RNA replication and viral assembly/secretion, 16 so having multiple modes of action per se is not unique. The current study benefits from comparing multiple drug doses, which allows a clear separation of the effect of the drug in enhancing viral RNA degradation from its other modes of action by viral dynamics modeling. We are not aware of another such study (modeling the dynamics of viral and ALT decay under different doses) for an NS5B inhibitor; thus, we believe this is the first example of an HCV polymerase inhibitor having these multiple modes of action.
