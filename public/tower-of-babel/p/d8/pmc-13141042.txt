Efficacy and Safety of Rituximab Therapy to Sustain Remission in Young Children With Childhood Nephrotic Syndrome

ABSTRACT
IntroductionThe efficacy and safety of rituximab in children aged < 6 years with frequently-relapsing and/or steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome (FRSDNS) remain controversial.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective cohort study at 16 pediatric nephrology centers from 10 countries in Asia, Europe, and North America. We first analyzed young children with FRSDNS who received their first rituximab treatment before the age of 6 years. As a secondary analysis, we matched young children aged < 6 years with older patients who received their first rituximab between the ages of 6 to 21 years.ResultsOne hundred one children (70 boys) received their first rituximab courses at the age of 4.5 (interquartile range [IQR]: 3.6–5.4) years, with an observation period of 4.6 (IQR: 3.2–6.6) years. Most children were Europeans (42%) and Southeast Asians (35%). Seventy-five children (74%) were multidrug dependent. In the primary analysis, among the 101 young children, the median relapse-free survival after the first rituximab treatment was 8.8 (95% confidence interval [CI]: 7.5–11.0) months by Kaplan-Meier analysis. Each additional month of concurrent immunosuppression post-rituximab reduced relapse risk by 8% (adjusted hazard ratio: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.89-0.96; P < 0.001). Increasing age at rituximab administration trended toward a lower relapse risk (adjusted hazard ratio: 0.76/yr; 95% CI: 0.57–1.03; P = 0.07). The annual relapse rate reduced from 3 (IQR: 2–4) to 1 (IQR: 0–1) following rituximab (P < 0.0001). Rituximab was repeated in 85 children (84%), mostly because of relapses. The leading adverse events were hypogammaglobulinemia (n = 30/53, 57%), neutropenia (n = 9/97, 9%), and infection (n = 8/115, 8%). Baseline low IgG levels were associated with hypogammaglobulinemia post-rituximab. All children except 1 had normal kidney function at the last follow-up. In the secondary analysis (70 young vs. 70 older children), although insignificant, younger children showed a trend toward earlier relapse (8.3 vs. 11.3 months; log-rank test P = 0.14), and more complications (hypogammaglobulinemia, 63% vs. 35%; P = 0.01; neutropenia, 13% vs. 7%; infection, 9% vs. 4%).ConclusionRituximab offers reasonable efficacy in young children with FRSDNS, with a trend toward a shorter relapse-free period and more potential complications. Rituximab should be reserved until established treatments are exhausted.

More than 50% of cases with steroid-sensitive NS are frequently-relapsing and/or steroid-dependent.1,2 Rituximab, a chimeric anti-CD20 monoclonal antibody, is an important therapy for FRSDNS.3, 4, 5, 6 Its efficacy in sustaining remission may be partly attributed to recent findings implicating an extrafollicular B-cell response during active disease and the presence of potential circulating antinephrin autoantibodies in ≤80% to 90% children with steroid-dependent NS.2,7,8 However, the causality of these autoantibodies is yet to be definitively established. Rituximab depletes B cells and B-cell subsets and thus may reduce potentially disease-causing autoantibodies that correlate with disease activity.7,9 It also restores T-cell immune homeostasis through crosstalk between T and B cells.9

Existing efficacy and safety data from clinical trials, however, are largely derived from older children with FRSDNS, with a median age at rituximab administration ranging from 6.9 to 13.5 years.9 Published data on the youngest patients are not available. Our previous work showed that relapse risk reduced by 8% with each year increase in age at rituximab treatment.10 A cut-off age of < 10 years was recently identified to predict patients with less favorable response to rituximab.11 Younger patients are also more susceptible to complications, notably hypogammaglobulinemia and neutropenia.10,12, 13, 14 Consequently, current recommendations do not advise to prescribe rituximab in young children aged < 7 years because of limited evidence.15, 16, 17

Nonetheless, it is well known that the youngest patients are the most challenging population to treat and often have a more complicated disease course.2,15 They suffer from repeated relapses, resulting in frequent hospitalizations and significant steroid-related side effects. At the recent Kidney Disease: Improving Global Outcomes Controversies Conference on Therapies Targeting B Cell in Immune-Mediated Kidney Diseases, it was determined that the optimal therapeutic approach for young children who do not respond to oral immunosuppression remains a significant clinical gap. In view of these knowledge gaps, the present study aimed to examine the efficacy and safety of rituximab therapy through a multinational study to determine its role in young children aged < 6 years with FRSDNS.

Methods

We conducted a multinational, multicenter, retrospective cohort study between March 1, 2020, and December 31, 2023. In this study, we used 2 previously reported international pediatric cohorts with FRSDNS, which involved 16 tertiary pediatric nephrology centers from 10 countries in Asia, Europe, and North America.10,18 We included young children with frequently-relapsing, steroid-dependent and/or multidrug-dependent NS who received their first course of rituximab before the age of 6 years between 2005 and 2022. We used standard terminologies for NS, frequently-relapsing, steroid dependent, primary and secondary steroid resistance, remission, and relapse.15 Multidrug dependence was defined as the requirement of ≥ 2 immunosuppressants (corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, and/or calcineurin inhibitor) to maintain remission.19,20

All patients were in complete remission at the time of their first rituximab administration, and rituximab was given with the aim of sustaining disease remission. The minimum observation period was 12 months following the first rituximab administration, unless a relapse occurred earlier. Subjects with a history of steroid resistance could be included if they attained remission after calcineurin inhibitor therapy and subsequently became steroid responsive, with a clinical course of FRSDNS that required rituximab therapy.21,22 Patients who received repeated rituximab at fixed intervals (e.g., every 6 months),23 regardless of clinical response and B-cell population, were excluded. Further exclusion criteria were multidrug-resistant NS, in which rituximab was used to induce remission, and secondary NS (e.g., congenital or infantile NS, genetic podocytopathy, C3 glomerulopathy, membranous nephropathy, lupus nephritis, and IgA nephropathy).

The study was divided into 2 parts. In the primary analysis, we examined the efficacy and safety of rituximab in these young children. In a secondary analysis, we further matched and compared these young patients with older children and young adults who received their first rituximab between the ages of 6 and 21 years. This matched analysis was performed to address potential confounders, enabling better understanding of age-related differences in treatment and immunological responses to rituximab.

Rituximab was prescribed according to local policy, at 1 to 4 weekly infusions of 375 mg/m2 or 1 to 2 biweekly infusions of 750 mg/m2. Dosing regimens were grouped according to the total dose per treatment course: low dose (375 mg/m2), medium dose (750 mg/m2), or high dose (1500 mg/m2). Infusions that were prescribed > 4 weeks apart were considered as 2 distinct courses. The duration of concurrent immunosuppression was defined as the use of any agent, including corticosteroids, mycophenolate mofetil, and/or calcineurin inhibitors. Maintenance immunosuppression was defined as the use of concurrent immunosuppression after rituximab for ≥6 months or until relapse if that occurred within 6 months post-rituximab. Rituximab regimens and monitoring practices followed the local policies of participating centers. We collected fully anonymized data on baseline demographics, kidney histology, previous treatments, rituximab dosing regimens, immunosuppression use, relapse, kidney outcomes, adverse events, and total B-cell counts.

This study was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki and the Declaration of Istanbul. Ethical approval was obtained from the Central Institutional Review Board of the Hong Kong Children’s Hospital as the lead study site (REC-2020-004). Participating centers obtained ethics approval in compliance to local policies where required, because some centers did not mandate such review for retrospective studies.

Primary and Secondary Outcomes

In the main analysis, the primary outcome was relapse-free survival, defined as time to relapse after rituximab administration. Children were censored at the time of additional rituximab administration or at the last follow-up, whichever occurred earlier. Secondary outcomes were B-cell depletion and repopulation, long-term remission (relapse-free for ≥ 2 years), rituximab resistance (relapse within first 3 months post-rituximab), the development of chronic kidney disease (estimated glomerular filtration rate < 60 ml/min per 1.73 m2) and/or kidney failure, and rituximab-associated adverse events. B-cell depletion and repletion were defined as CD19+ B-cell levels < and ≥ 1% of the total lymphocyte population, respectively. Adverse events included death, severe infusion reactions, infections, hypogammaglobulinemia, neutropenia, neoplasia, and complications arising from cardiovascular, respiratory, and neurological systems. Agranulocytosis and neutropenia were defined as an absolute neutrophil count < 0.5 × 109/l and < 1.5 × 109/l. Hypogammaglobulinemia was defined as an IgG level that was below the reference range for age at each individual laboratory. In the secondary analysis, we evaluated the relapse-free survival, B-cell depletion duration, and adverse events between younger and older patients.

Statistical Analyses

Descriptive data were expressed as mean (SD), median (IQR), and number (percentage), as appropriate. Between-group differences were analyzed using the Chi-square or Fisher exact tests for categorical parameters and t tests or the Mann–Whitney U tests for continuous parameters. In the primary analysis of children aged < 6 years, the Kaplan-Meier method with log-rank test was used to compare relapse-free survival after the first rituximab administration. We determined the relationship between relapse and all potential risk factors in these young children using a multivariate Cox regression model. These factors included rituximab dosing, duration of concurrent immunosuppression, age at NS diagnosis, age at rituximab treatment, sex, ethnicity, disease severity, history of steroid resistance, and previous use of immunosuppression. Multicollinearity in the model was assessed using the variance inflation factor. For missing data, no imputation was used. Statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.1.1 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with “survival,” “survminer,” “coxme,” “lme4,” and “performance” packages. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05.

In the secondary study, we conducted 1:1 propensity score–based nearest-neighbor matching without replacement using the “MatchIt” package in R version 4.5.2. The propensity score was estimated using logistic regression with age group as the exposure variable and the following baseline covariates: sex, ethnicity (Caucasians/Asians/others), rituximab dosing (low dose: 375 mg/m2; medium dose: 750 mg/m2; high dose: 1125–1500 mg/m2), number of immunosuppressive agents immediately before rituximab (1, 2, or 3), interval between NS diagnosis and first rituximab (continuous), and history of steroid resistance (yes/no). Exact matching (caliper = 0) was enforced for all categorical covariates, and the interval between NS diagnosis and first rituximab was matched within an absolute caliper of 2 years. Calipers were specified on the original measurement scale (i.e., nonstandardized). A 1:1 matching ratio was applied. Covariate balance after matching was evaluated using standardized mean differences, and the matched cohort was used for subsequent outcome analyses. Relapse-free survival between younger and older patients at the time of first rituximab administration was compared using the Kaplan-Meier method with the log-rank test.

Results

Patient Population

We included 101 young children (70 boys) who received their first course of rituximab before the age of 6 years (Supplementary Table S1). They presented with NS at a median age of 2.3 years (IQR: 1.9–3.2). Majority of the patients were Europeans (42%), Southeast Asians (35%), and South Asians (12%). Among the patients, 74% and 22% were multidrug-dependent and steroid-dependent, respectively. Calcineurin inhibitors and mycophenolate mofetil were used in 86% and 25% patients, respectively. A history of steroid resistance was reported in 31% of patients (primary, 13%; secondary, 18%); however, all had achieved complete remission with calcineurin inhibitors. Among the 58% patients who underwent kidney biopsy, the prevalent histological diagnosis was minimal change disease (76%). Patients received their first course of rituximab at a median age of 4.5 years (IQR: 3.6–5.4). Low-, median-, and high-dose regimens were given in 54%, 25%, and 21% of patients, respectively. Concurrent immunosuppression was given to 61 children (60%). Primary Outcome: Relapse-Free Survival

Eight-six children (85%) relapsed after their first rituximab administration. Kaplan-Meier survival analysis showed that the median relapse-free survival of the cohort was 8.8 months (95% CI: 7.5–11.0) (Figure 2). Relapse-free survival did not differ between children aged < 4 years and those aged from 4 to 6 years (Supplementary Figure S1). A history of steroid resistance was not associated with relapse (Supplementary Figure S2A and B). Nine children (9%) relapsed within 3 months post-rituximab and were considered resistant to rituximab. Long-term remission > 2 years was achieved in 11 patients (11%), and total B cells repopulated in all of them. Five of these patients eventually relapsed. The number of relapses significantly reduced from 3 (IQR: 2–4) to 1 (IQR: 0–1) within 1-year before and after rituximab therapy (P < 0.0001).Figure 2Relapse-free survival following the first rituximab treatment among young children (aged < 6 years) with frequently-relapsing, steroid-dependent nephrotic syndrome.

Multivariable analysis demonstrated that each additional month of concurrent immunosuppression use (adjusted hazard ratio: 0.92; 95% CI: 0.89–0.96; P < 0.001) reduced relapse risk by 8%. Older age at first rituximab administration showed a trend toward a lower relapse risk (adjusted hazard ratio: 0.76; 95% CI: 0.57–1.03; P = 0.07). Although statistically insignificant, children receiving high-dose rituximab regimen (1125–1500 mg/m2) appeared to experience a lower relapse risk than those who received low- and medium-dose regimens (Table 3). Total B-cell Depletion and Repopulation

B cells were monitored within 1 month after the first rituximab administration in 70 patients, and B-cell depletion was observed in 99% of subjects (n = 69/70). Total B cells were subsequently monitored in 58 children and repopulated at a median of 5.3 months (IQR: 4.3–6.8). Among these patients, 93% relapsed after B-cell repopulation, which had been documented a median of 2.2 months (IQR: 0.85–4.6) earlier; however, 22% of children did not relapse after B-cell repopulation.

Subsequent Courses of Rituximab

A second course of rituximab was administered to85 subjects (84%). The most frequent indication for redosing was NS relapse (n = 77), followed by total B-cell repopulation (n = 8). The rates of relapse, time to relapse, long-term remission, rituximab resistance, and duration of B-cell depletion after each rituximab course are summarized in Supplementary Table S2. Importantly, 34% and 12% children attained long-term remission (≥ 2 years) following the second and third rituximab course, respectively. Between 5% and 13% of patients had rituximab resistance (relapse-free period < 3 months) during each cycle of treatment.

Safety Profiles

Hypogammaglobulinemia was the most frequently observed side effect after the first course of rituximab. Among the 53 patients whose IgG levels were monitored, 30 children (57%) had hypogammaglobulinemia documented, with a median lowest IgG value of 289 (100–380) mg/dl. Of these patients, 21 had preexisting hypogammaglobulinemia before their first rituximab exposure. Low IgG levels at baseline, compared with normal IgG levels, were associated with hypogammaglobulinemia post-rituximab (68% vs. 27%, P = 0.005). Other factors, including age at first rituximab administration (low IgG: 4.9 [IQR: 3.9–5.5] years vs. normal IgG: 4.5 [IQR: 3.9–5.4] years) and maintenance immunosuppression use (low IgG: 73% vs. normal IgG: 65%), were not significantly associated with the development of hypogammaglobulinemia. Nine patients had very low IgG levels (< 200 mg/dl), and 4 patients required regular i.v. Ig replacement. Twenty episodes (66%) recovered over a median of 3.0 months (IQR: 1.3–6.9).

Nine patients (9%) developed neutropenia, of whom 6 had agranulocytosis. The median absolute neutrophil count was 0.08 × 109/l (IQR: 0.06–0.70). The neutropenic episodes lasted for a median of 20 (IQR: 5–49) days. Patients with agranulocytosis were treated with granulocyte-colony stimulating factor (n = 3), systemic antibiotics (n = 3), and acyclovir (n = 1) with good recovery of the neutrophil count. Eight infectious episodes were reported in 7 patients (febrile neutropenia, n = 3; herpes simplex, n = 1; pneumocystis pneumonia, n = 1; community-acquired pneumonia, n = 1; viral gastroenteritis, n = 1; acute otitis media). Most of them had concurrent hypogammaglobulinemia (n = 1) and/or neutropenia (n = 5). We did not find any associated factor among these young patients with or without neutropenia, agranulocytosis, and infection. Of note, 2 patients were receiving mycophenolate mofetil as maintenance immunosuppression, whereas another patient with agranulocytosis was receiving cotrimoxazole as prophylaxis against pneumocystis. Hypogammaglobulinemia (n = 85/173, 49%) remained the most common adverse event across all courses of rituximab evaluated in our cohort, followed by infections (n = 19/273, 7%) and neutropenia (n = 18/267, 7%) (Table 4). The rates of hypogammaglobulinemia were higher among courses of rituximab administered before the age of 6 years (54% vs. 43%, P = 0.02). A total of 42 children developed hypogammaglobulinemia before the age of 6 years. The first occurrence was observed after the first, second, and third courses of rituximab in 30, 10, and 2 patients, respectively. Twenty-nine children received further courses of rituximab therapy, and hypogammaglobulinemia recurred in 20 children (68%). Among the 18 children who received additional administrations after the age of 6 years, 13 developed hypogammaglobulinemia (72%). The rates of neutropenia (9% vs. 4%) and infection (8% vs. 5%) were also higher, although these differences did not reach statistical significance.

Chronic Kidney Disease and Kidney Failure

All 101 patients remained steroid sensitive during the observation period. Normal kidney function was reported in all children at the last follow-up, except 1 who had chronic kidney disease stage 2. The patient had multidrug-dependent NS since the age of 19 months and was treated with a calcineurin inhibitor for disease control. Rituximab was administered at 5.6 years of age, and the calcineurin inhibitor was continued as maintenance for more than 4 years at the last follow-up. Although a kidney biopsy was not performed, the impaired kidney function could be attributable to long-term calcineurin inhibitor–associated nephrotoxicity. No patients required kidney replacement therapy.

Discussion

Our multiethnic cohort provides long-term efficacy and safety data on rituximab in young children aged < 6 years. Our data demonstrate that rituximab sustains remission even in young children with a complicated disease course. Hypogammaglobulinemia is prevalent in ≤57% of patients. Concomitant infection is uncommon but could be underreported. Although our findings suggest that rituximab can be used as a third-line or even second-line agent for complicated FRSDNS in young children, the therapy appears to be less effective and associated with more complications compared with those receiving the drug at an older age.

Our cohort represented the most severe and complicated group of young children with FRSDNS. As expected, most of the patients were multidrug dependent, and the rest were steroid-dependent, with frequent relapses prior to rituximab administration. One-third of the children had a previous history of steroid resistance but regained steroid responsiveness following the use of calcineurin inhibitors, which has been associated with poorer treatment response to rituximab.24,25 Up to 86% subjects with FRSDNS continued to relapse despite calcineurin inhibitor therapy, a potent steroid-sparing agent.26 This suggests that most pediatric nephrologists reserve rituximab as a last resort for FRSDNS in young children.

Our data showed that the median relapse-free period in these young children was a median of 8.8 months after rituximab treatment. Although this efficacy is not as pronounced as that observed in older children, it is remarkable given that our cohort had a challenging disease course and had failed multiple steroid-sparing agents. To our best knowledge, there are no available data for comparison, because previous studies have enrolled mostly older children and did not perform specific subgroup analyses for young children.9 Furthermore, reported treatment outcomes of rituximab have been highly variable because of heterogeneity in patient populations and the prescription of rituximab and immunosuppression. We therefore matched our young patient cohort with older children of similar clinical and treatment characteristics to enable fair comparison and minimize potential confounders. Although the intervals from NS onset to first rituximab between groups were statistically different, a 6-month difference in rituximab initiation would be unlikely to affect overall clinical response or side effect profiles. Although statistically insignificant, older children demonstrated a trend toward a longer relapse-free period than younger children. This may be partly explained by the fact that memory B-cell levels, a more established biomarker for response than other B-cell subsets,13 were higher at baseline and reconstitute earlier in children aged < 10 years compared with those aged > 10 years receiving rituximab.11 Another potential explanation is that the natural course of FRSDNS tends to improve with age, especially around the time of puberty. In contrast, the duration of total B-cell depletion was not different between the 2 groups. Furthermore, among the young patients, each additional year of age at first rituximab administration showed a trend toward lower relapse risk.

In concordance with our previous findings,10,18 our data showed that maintenance immunosuppression was an effective measure to extend NS remission in young children. Each additional month of concurrent immunosuppression use reduced relapse risk by 8%. Although our study was not designed to evaluate the choice of maintenance therapy, mycophenolate mofetil appeared to be the preferred option, being the only immunosuppressive agent used over 6 months. In this context, a recent randomized trial showed that maintenance mycophenolate mofetil could reduce relapse risk by 80%; however, the effect disappeared after drug discontinuation.27 Our study did not evaluate the rituximab drug levels and anti-rituximab antibodies because these tests are unavailable in most clinical settings. Current evidence, however, suggests that these parameters correlate poorly with relapse and does not support regular monitoring.28

Our previous work described a progressive improvement in relapse-free period after repeated courses of rituximab,10 suggesting a potential disease-modifying effect. Similar findings were observed in the current cohort. After each cycle of rituximab therapy, 10% to 30% of children achieved sustained and prolonged remission over 2 years. Although this may, in part, be related to outgrowing the disease, many children in our cohort were prepubertal at the time of rituximab retreatment, a stage at which the disease typically remains active until puberty is reached.29 This result can be impactful if repeated rituximab treatments can ameliorate the overall clinical course. One possible postulation is that early rituximab use prevents the formation of long-lived plasma cell.30 Further clinical and translational data are warranted to confirm this hypothesis.

Rituximab appears to be generally safe, but its use is not without risk.9,18,31, 32, 33 Rare yet fatal adverse events such as viral hepatitis reactivation, lung injury, and multifocal leukoencephalopathy have been reported.34, 35, 36 Hypogammaglobulinemia, neutropenia, and agranulocytosis are the most prevalent adverse events and the incidences were reported to be 14% to 58%, 4% to 8% and 2% to 5%, respectively.10,37 Our data reported more adverse events among young children than the general pediatric population. There were significantly higher rates of hypogammaglobulinemia in younger children, than in the matched older patient cohort. Although the difference in other adverse events was not significant between groups because of relatively small sample size and event numbers, this signal should be taken seriously into any treatment consideration. Interestingly, the observed adverse events were lower when rituximab was given to the same group of children after they turned 6 years or older. This concurs with previous findings that younger age at rituximab increased the risk of developing hypogammaglobulinemia and neutropenia.10,12 IgG, IgA, and IgM levels and absolute neutrophil count should be regularly monitored, because all except one of our infective complications occurred in the presence of hypogammaglobulinemia and agranulocytosis. Furthermore, our data found that low IgG levels before rituximab was associated with hypogammaglobulinemia after rituximab therapy. The role of i.v. Ig substitution for asymptomatic numerical hypogammaglobulinemia, however, remains controversial.10,38 The risk of developing complications remains a major and valid concern when prescribing rituximab to these young children.

Another important consideration, as illustrated from our data, is that most children eventually relapse and require repeated courses of rituximab for disease control. In the short- to medium-term, there are concerns pertaining to vaccine competency and long-term immunological impairment.13,39,40 Future research is required to better understand the long-term impact, such as hematologic malignancy, of repeated rituximab treatments during an active stage of immunity development and maturation.9,41 For these reasons, we recommend to use rituximab in young children with FRSDNS only after other second-line treatment options have been exhausted or resulted in side effects. Second generation anti-CD20, obinutuzumab, is effective in rituximab-resistant FRSDNS, even among children who relapse during complete depletion of circulating B cell.42,43 Until further data become available, its use in young children should be exercised with great caution, only after balancing the risk and benefits. Obinutuzumab has, due to its mode of action, clear potentials of being more effective but also having more profound side effects than the first generation of anti-CD20 monoclonal antibodies.

Despite a unique multiethnic patient cohort, our study had the limitations of a retrospective study, including selection and reporting bias. We tried to minimize them by stringent inclusion criteria and adequate observation. Second, there were variable practices between centers, including rituximab regimen, immunosuppression use, and redose indications. These might confound treatment responses and influence the reported rates of adverse events. In particular, hypogammaglobulinemia might have been underreported because of variable practices on IgG-level monitoring. Infection is an important complication after intensive immunosuppression and may be underreported. Finally, histology was not included as an adjusted factor in the regression model because kidney biopsy is not routinely indicated in FRSDNS15; available evidence however fails to substantiate its role as predictor.5,24,25

In conclusion, rituximab appears to have reasonable efficacy in young children with complicated FRSDNS. Maintenance immunosuppression may serve as a plausible option to prevent relapse and extend remission. Compared with older children, young children may experience a shorter relapse-free period and high rates of adverse events. Rituximab should therefore be considered only after exhausting other treatment options, until prospective trials provide more robust evidence to support its earlier use. Future trials should identify the prognostic values of specific B-cell subsets, examine the role of scheduled redosing, and compare the efficacy and safety of rituximab against novel therapeutics such as obinutuzumab and chimeric antigen receptor–based therapy.
