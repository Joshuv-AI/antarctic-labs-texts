Progressive clonic slowing predicts severity of respiratory dysfunction and prolonged postictal immobility in tonic–clonic seizures
Authors: John Phamnguyen, Fred Tremayne, Lata Vadlamudi, Cecilie Lander, Stephen Walsh, Xiaohua Chen, Alice‐Ann Sullivan, Viktor Vegh, David Reutens
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveProgressive clonic slowing is a common electroclinical phenomenon observed during the clonic phase of bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (BTCS) and reflects an inhibitory phenomenon. We aimed to explore the relationship between progressive clonic slowing and biomarkers implicated in the pathophysiology of sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP), specifically postictal immobility and respiratory dysfunction.MethodsWe retrospectively reviewed video‐EEG data of 110 BTCS (24 females; median age 31.5 years) from 52 patients, evaluating the presence of progressive clonic slowing and durations of seizure phases, postictal immobility, and EEG suppression. Respiratory dysfunction was assessed using pulse oximetry data (available in 76 seizures), measuring the extent and duration of oxygen desaturation during and after seizures.ResultsThe presence of progressive clonic slowing was associated with greater oxygen desaturation (median extent of desaturation: 18.3% vs. 7.8%; p = 0.01) and longer duration of postictal immobility (median: 48.0 s vs. 20.5 s; p < 0.001). The absence of progressive clonic slowing strongly predicted that moderate to severe (negative predictive value [NPV]: 88.9%; p = 0.002) and severe desaturation (NPV: 100%; p = 0.027) would not occur. In BTCS with desaturation, the duration of postictal immobility was longer (23.0 s vs. 49.0 s; Mann–Whitney U, p < 0.001). While the presence of PGES was associated with both a higher likelihood and extent of desaturation, the median duration of PGES did not differ between groups with and without progressive clonic slowing. No independent association between progressive clonic slowing and PGES duration was identified.SignificanceProgressive clonic slowing is an electroclinical marker associated with prolonged postictal immobility and oxygen desaturation. Shared mechanisms underlying progressive clonic slowing and postictal immobility may contribute to SUDEP pathophysiology. Recognition of progressive clonic slowing as a seizure severity marker may have important implications for SUDEP risk assessment and prevention strategies.Plain Language SummaryThis study found that during some convulsive seizures, the jerking movements of the body slow down gradually before the seizure ends. INTRODUCTION

Bilateral tonic–clonic seizures (BTCS) are a severe seizure phenotype associated with an increased risk of injury 1 and sudden unexpected death in epilepsy (SUDEP). 2 , 3 BTCS are the main risk factor for SUDEP, which is the leading cause of premature death in epilepsy patients. 4 , 5 , 6 The Mortality in Epilepsy Monitoring Unit Study (MORTEMUS), which analyzed observed cases of SUDEP, showed a stereotyped pattern of cardiorespiratory dysfunction. 7 In all patients, an initial period of postictal apnea led to early terminal cardiorespiratory arrest or a transient return of normal respiration followed by delayed terminal apnea and subsequent asystole. Six out of 25 SUDEP or near‐SUDEP cases in the MORTEMUS study had a history of postictal respiratory dysfunction. Postconvulsive central apnea (PCCA) has been observed in individuals surviving near‐SUDEP or dying from probable SUDEP. 8 , 9 These observations highlight the importance of respiratory dysfunction and the potential role of inhibited respiratory arousal in the pathophysiology of SUDEP.

In addition to respiratory dysfunction, postictal immobility is characterized by the absence of movement and arousal following a BTCS, is associated with severe postictal respiratory impairment 10 , 11 and is a frequent finding in SUDEP cases, particularly among individuals found in a prone position. 7 , 12 , 13 The similarity with sudden infant death syndrome (SIDS) 14 , 15 , 16 has led to the postulate that both disorders share mechanisms relating to prone positioning, with impairment of arousal and diminished airway protective reflexes contributing to terminal apnea. 10 Another manifestation during BTCS is progressive clonic slowing, a specific and common dynamic pattern observed in the clonic phase, in which inhibitory (silent) periods gradually increase in duration, resulting in progressive deceleration of clonic jerks until seizure offset. 17 Progressive clonic slowing is associated with prolonged postictal generalized EEG suppression (PGES), a marker of seizure severity that has been inconsistently linked to elevated SUDEP risk. 18 We postulated that progressive clonic slowing is a marker of motor inhibition in BTCS and that its relationship with other inhibitory phenomena such as postictal immobility and postictal respiratory dysfunction could be ascertained from video‐EEG recordings of BTCS.

METHODS

Subjects

For this retrospective study, we ascertained cases by determining whether a BTCS had been recorded in 614 video‐EEG reports from patients evaluated at the Royal Brisbane and Women's Hospital Epilepsy Monitoring Unit between 2016 and 2022. In total, video‐EEG recordings of 110 BTCS from 52 individuals were accessible for further analysis and were included in the study. Of these, 76 BTCS had usable pulse oximetry data.

Standard protocol approvals, registrations, and patient consents

The Metro North Hospital Health Service (MNHHS) Human Research Ethics Committee approved the study and consent waiver.

Data collection and variables

Demographic data (age, gender), epilepsy characteristics (onset, duration, BTCS onset and duration, seizure frequency, epilepsy syndrome, lateralization), and details regarding antiseizure medication were extracted from electronic medical records. The patient's arousal state at seizure onset was also recorded.

Video‐EEG and cardiorespiratory analysis

All patients underwent conventional scalp EEG recordings (International 10–20 System) at a sampling rate of 500 Hz on a Compumedics Neuvo System equipped with a 64‐channel amplifier and Profusion 5 EEG software (Compumedics Ltd., Abbotsford, Australia). Peripheral capillary oxygen saturation (SpO2) and heart rate were monitored using portable pulse oximetry that was visible in the video‐EEG recordings. All seizures were reviewed by a Level 3 Australian and New Zealand Association of Neurologists (ANZAN) certified epileptologist (J.P.).

Respiratory markers included baseline SpO2, maximum desaturation (lowest SpO2 in the ictal and postictal period), nadir time (the time from seizure onset to that of lowest saturation), and desaturation duration (time taken to reach normoxemia, defined as SpO2 > 90%). The extent of desaturation was calculated as the difference from an SpO2 of 90%. Often, the SpO2 data were not clear during the clonic phase of the BTCS, with the first available reading being seen early in the postictal phase. A nadir was recorded if SpO2 values consistently declined to a nadir before recovering. If there were insufficient SpO2 measurements to observe a nadir, for example, if the oxygen probe fell off, the SpO2 data were considered missing. Desaturation was defined as SpO2 <90%. Definitions for mild (SpO2 80%–89%), moderate (SpO2 70%–79%), and severe (SpO2 < 70%) desaturation were used. 19

All nurses were trained to turn patients to the lateral position and to provide oxygen supplementation after a BTCS. We recorded nurse intervention with oxygen supplementation or repositioning during any phase of the BTCS, time of oxygen supplementation or repositioning (during tonic or clonic phase).

The durations of seizure phases were obtained from visual assessment of video and EEG data. The onset of the tonic phase was defined by the first movement into the tonic phase semiology (Figure 1) and bilateral, continuous muscle activation as evidenced by muscle artifact or low‐voltage fast activity on EEG.

Classification of tonic semiology in bilateral tonic–clonic seizures. Type 1: decerebration, bilateral arm extension; type 2: decortication, bilateral arm flexion; type 3: hemi‐decerebration, one arm in flexion and the other in extension.

Tonic phase semiology was classified based on a modified classification proposed by previous authors 20 , 21 : (1) ictal decerebration (bilateral upper limb extension), (2) ictal decortication (bilateral upper limb flexion with progression to decerebration), (3) ictal hemi‐decerebration (tonic extension of one arm and flexion of the contralateral arm without progression to decortication or decerebration) and (4) absence of ictal tonic phase. Examples of tonic phase postures are shown in Figure 1. Mouth opening and vocalization during the tonic phase were recorded.

The onset of the clonic phase was defined by the onset of repetitive bilateral synchronous bursts of muscle contraction artifact separated by periods of relative absence of muscle activity. Progressive clonic slowing was defined as the pattern of gradually increasing silent periods between clonic jerks until seizure cessation; operationally, this was defined as increasing duration of at least two consecutive silent periods as determined from muscle artifact in the EEG recordings without consideration of video data. The clonic phase had to follow sustained (>3 s) continuous muscle activity (i.e., the tonic phase). This quantitative method of evaluating progressive clonic slowing has been shown to have high interrater agreement. 18 The difference between clonic phases with and without progressive slowing is depicted in Figure 2.

EEG epoch over 30 s in hemispheric bipolar montage highlighting the different dynamics of the clonic phase in BTCS. (A) Progressive clonic slowing, (B) quasi‐rhythmic clonic phase without progressive slowing.

The duration of postictal immobility was defined as the period from electrographic seizure offset to the onset of the first nonrespiratory movement identified on the video recording. PGES was defined as the absence of EEG activity >10 μV in amplitude in the immediate postictal period, allowing for electrode, muscle, movement, or respiratory artifact. 22 The offset of PGES was defined as the time when slow wave activity returned.

Statistical analysis

Analyses were performed using IBM SPSS (version 29). Due to the nonnormal distribution of data, nonparametric tests were used: Fisher's exact test for contingency tables, Cramer's V for associations between nominal variables, Kruskal–Wallis test for independent samples, and Mann–Whitney U test for pairwise comparisons. For covariate adjustment, Quade nonparametric ANCOVA was employed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics are presented as median and interquartile range (IQR). To evaluate the risk of bias from inclusion of multiple seizures per patient, we repeated key analyses using the median values for seizures with and without progressive clonic slowing in each patient.

RESULTS

Clinical and demographic data

The cohort included 28 males (54%) and 24 females (46%), with a median age of 31.5 years (IQR 24.6–47.0). The median age of epilepsy onset was 20.0 years (IQR 11.8–25.0) and the median duration of epilepsy was 12.7 years (IQR 5.2–28.0). First BTCS occurred at a median age of 20.0 years (IQR 13–25.3) and median occurrence was within a year of epilepsy onset. At the time of video‐EEG, BTCS had been occurring for a median of 10.0 years (IQR 4.6–25.9). Most patients (92%) were on at least two antiseizure medications, with 85% on sodium channel blockers and 29% on benzodiazepines. Twenty‐six patients had a single recorded BTCS, 11 had two BTCS, nine had three BTCS, and six had four or more recorded BTCS. Of the 26 patients with multiple BTCS, more than one tonic semiology was recorded in only six. Details are provided in Appendix A, Table A1. Seventy‐two BTCS occurred from wakefulness and 48 BTCS occurred from sleep. Sleep compared to wakefulness had no effect on the distribution of desaturation extent (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.058), desaturation duration (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.928), PGES duration (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.151), and postictal immobility duration (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.133). The cohort comprised 48 patients with focal epilepsy and 4 with generalized epilepsy, which made up 100 focal to BTCS and 10 generalized tonic–clonic seizures.

Progressive clonic slowing and respiratory dysfunction

Progressive clonic slowing was observed in 100/110 recorded BTCS (91%). Progressive clonic slowing occurred in 91/100 (91%) focal‐to‐BTCS and 9/10 (90%) generalized tonic–clonic seizures. The absence of progressive clonic slowing was not specifically correlated with seizure type. Patient distribution included 45 with only progressive clonic slowing seizures, 5 with only nonprogressive clonic slowing seizures, and two patients with multiple BTCS with and without progressive clonic slowing. Comparing groups with and without progressive clonic slowing, there was no significant difference with age (Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.116), epilepsy duration (Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.716), tonic–clonic duration (Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.755), and frequency of tonic–clonic seizures (Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.790). Desaturation occurred in 61 out of 76 BTCS with usable SpO2 recording (80%). Nine out of 10 BTCS without progressive clonic slowing had usable SpO2 data (90%). Progressive clonic slowing occurred in 67 of 76 BTCS with usable SpO2 data (88%), a similar frequency to that observed in the entire group (91%). In 34 BTCS, oxygen saturation data were not available because the screen of the pulse oximeter was not visible in the video recording, or the nadir was clearly not seen.

The presence of progressive clonic slowing was not associated with the occurrence of desaturation per se (Fisher's exact test; p = 0.69), but it was associated with a significantly greater degree of desaturation. Median maximum desaturation was significantly greater in BTCS with progressive clonic slowing (18.3%) than without (7.8%; Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.01; Table 1). When patients without desaturation were included (i.e., if no desaturation present, the desaturation extent was assigned a value of zero), the presence of progressive clonic slowing was associated with a greater extent of desaturation (median: 14.4 vs. 4.4% SpO2; Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.004). Progressive clonic slowing occurred more frequently in BTCS with moderate‐to‐severe desaturation (Fisher's exact test; p = 0.002) and severe desaturation (Fisher's exact test; p = 0.021) than in those with less severe desaturation. The NPV of progressive clonic slowing for moderate‐to‐severe desaturation was 88.9% and for severe desaturation was 100%. The median duration of desaturation did not differ significantly between BTCS with and without progressive clonic slowing.

To determine whether the association between progressive clonic slowing and hypoxemia extent was independent of tonic phase duration, a nonparametric ANCOVA was performed with tonic phase duration as the covariate. No significant interaction between progressive clonic slowing and tonic phase duration was observed (F(1, 72) = 0.843; p = 0.362). Tonic phase duration did not significantly interact with hypoxemia (F(1, 72) = 0.316; p = 0.576). However, a significant main effect of progressive clonic slowing on hypoxemia was observed (F(1, 72) = 7.417; p = 0.008). These results suggest that the presence of progressive clonic slowing is related to the degree of hypoxemia, independent of tonic phase duration.

To determine whether the association between progressive clonic slowing and the extent of hypoxemia was independent of the duration of postictal immobility and PGES, ANCOVA was performed using the latter two measures as covariates. Progressive clonic slowing remained significantly associated with hypoxemia after adjusting for postictal immobility duration (F(1,72)= 4.93, p = 0.030), while postictal immobility itself was not a significant covariate (F(1,72)= 2.31, p = 0.133). The overall model was significant (F(2,72) = 4.40, p = 0.016), explaining 10.9% of the variance in hypoxemia (adjusted R 2 = 0.084). When evaluating PGES duration as a covariate, to avoid exclusion bias, PGES duration was set to 0 s in seizures, where PGES was absent (14/75 seizures). PCS remained significantly associated with hypoxemia (F(1,73) = 4.76, p = 0.032), while PGES duration was not a significant covariate (F(1,73)= 2.54, p = 0.115). The overall model was significant (F(2,73) = 4.57, p = 0.014), explaining 11.1% of the variance (adjusted R 2 = 0.087). Together, these findings support progressive clonic slowing as a marker of peri‐ictal respiratory dysfunction; the relationship between progressive clonic slowing and ictal hypoxemia is independent of postictal immobility and PGES duration (Tables 2 and 3).

Relationship between progressive clonic slowing and postictal immobility

Having demonstrated the relationship between progressive clonic slowing and respiratory dysfunction, we next assessed its association with postictal immobility. The median duration of postictal immobility was significantly greater in BTCS with progressive clonic slowing than in those without (48.0 s vs. 20.5 s; Mann–Whitney U; p < 0.001). The median duration of the clonic phase did not differ significantly between BTCS with and without progressive clonic slowing (40.1 s vs. 56.0 s; Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.160). However, median tonic phase duration was greater in BTCS with progressive clonic slowing than in those without this feature (18.0 s vs. 5.0 s; Mann–Whitney U; p < 0.001). In patients with focal epilepsy, progressive clonic slowing was associated with greater duration of postictal immobility (median: 52.0 s vs. 15.0 s; Mann–Whitney U; p < 0.001).

Tonic semiology differed between BTCS with and without progressive clonic slowing (0.593; Cramer's V; p < 0.001). Progressive clonic slowing was most commonly observed in BTCS with type 1 decerebration semiology (98.3%), followed by type 3 hemi‐decerebration (96.2%), type 2 decortication (Type 2, 84.2%), and type 4 (28.6%) tonic phase semiology. Progressive clonic slowing occurred more often in BTCS with tonic mouth opening than in BTCS without this feature (95.5% vs. 72.7%; Fisher's exact test; p = 0.004).

To determine whether the association between progressive clonic slowing and postictal immobility duration was independent of tonic phase duration, an ANCOVA was performed with tonic phase duration as the covariate. No significant interaction between progressive clonic slowing and tonic phase duration was observed [F(1, 103) = 0.080; p = 0.777]. Tonic phase duration did not significantly covary with the duration of postictal immobility [F(1, 103) = 1.250; p = 0.266]. However, a significant main effect of progressive clonic slowing on the duration of postictal immobility was observed [F(1, 103) = 4.346; p = 0.040]. These results suggest that the presence of progressive clonic slowing is related to the duration of postictal immobility, independent of tonic phase duration.

Relationship between respiratory dysfunction and other motor phenomena

The median duration of postictal immobility was greater in BTCS with desaturation than in BTCS without desaturation (49.0 s vs. 23.0 s; Mann–Whitney U; p < 0.001). Neither median tonic duration (Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.686) nor median clonic duration (Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.691) differed between BTCS with and without desaturation. Tonic semiology did not differ between BTCS with and without desaturation (0.253; Cramer's V; p = 0.181). The severity of desaturation (Kruskal–Wallis test; p = 0.120) and duration of desaturation (Kruskal–Wallis test; p = 0.204) did not differ between BTCS with different tonic semiology.

Effect of epilepsy syndrome and multiple seizures

In this study, 100 BTCS were in patients with focal epilepsy (10 in patients with generalized epilepsy). In the focal epilepsy group, the presence of progressive clonic slowing was associated with a greater extent of desaturation (median: 17.5% vs. 4.0% SpO2; Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.006) and a greater duration of postictal immobility (median: 52.0 s vs. 15.0 s; Mann–Whitney U; p < 0.001), which is similar to the main effect of the entire cohort. The generalized epilepsy group was underpowered to sufficiently analyze the effect of progressive clonic slowing.

More than one seizure occurred in 52 patients. To correct for potential bias, we also performed analyses using median values in patients with multiple seizures. In one patient with both types of seizures, the data for BTCS with and without progressive clonic slowing were included separately. In these analyses, the presence of progressive clonic slowing was also associated with a greater extent of desaturation (median: 13.0 vs. 2.0% SpO2; Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.016) and a greater duration of postictal immobility (median: 50.0 vs. 20.5 s; Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.03).

Effect of nursing intervention

Supplemental oxygen was administered in 100 (90.9%) BTCS and did not significantly affect the extent of desaturation (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.077), the duration of desaturation (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.721), or the duration of postictal immobility (Mann–Whitney U, p = 0.080). This was also the case when only early administration of supplemental oxygen was considered (extent of desaturation: Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.330; duration of desaturation: Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.808; duration of postictal immobility: Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.191). Patients were repositioned in 93 (84.5%) BTCS. Repositioning did not significantly affect the extent of desaturation (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.083), the duration of desaturation (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.307) or the duration of postictal immobility (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.319). This was also the case when only early repositioning (before or during the tonic phase) was considered (extent of desaturation: Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.331; duration of desaturation: Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.743; duration of postictal immobility: Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.137).

Postictal generalized EEG suppression (PGES)

PGES was observed in 81 BTCS (73.6%) cases overall and in 51 BTCS cases with usable respiratory recordings (67.1%). PGES was more common in BTCS with progressive clonic slowing than in those without this feature (79.0% vs. 20.0%; Fisher's exact test; p < 0.001), but the presence of progressive clonic slowing was not associated with PGES duration (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.537). Median PGES duration did not differ significantly between BTCS with and without this phenomenon (39.0 s vs. 53.5 s; Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.537). Desaturation occurred more often in BTCS with PGES than in BTCS without PGES (88.2% vs. 64.0%; Fisher's exact test; p = 0.016). The median duration of PGES did not differ between BTCS with and without desaturation (44.0 s vs. 41.0 s; Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.809). In BTCS with and without the presence of PGES, no significant difference in the extent of desaturation (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.799) and the duration of desaturation (Mann–Whitney U; p = 0.985) was observed.

DISCUSSION

Our results demonstrate a significant association between progressive clonic slowing in BTCS and both duration of postictal immobility and the severity of respiratory dysfunction as reflected by the extent of oxygen desaturation. Unlike previous studies focusing primarily on PGES duration alone, our study identified progressive clonic slowing as a robust marker of respiratory and motor inhibitory phenomena, during and after BTCS. This result was independent of the duration of the tonic phase of the BTCS and tonic phase semiology. The absence of progressive clonic slowing strongly predicted that moderate to severe oxygen desaturation would not occur, indicating a reduced risk of severe respiratory compromise. This is an important finding given that postictal respiratory dysfunction may be implicated in the pathophysiology of SUDEP, 23 with PCCA being observed in near‐SUDEP and probable SUDEP cases. 8 , 9

Our finding that progressive clonic slowing was associated with longer postictal immobility suggests that these two phenomena may share inhibitory motor mechanisms, initially manifesting clinically as gradual deceleration of clonic jerks, with persisting inhibition in the postictal period resulting in immobility. Previous studies have shown that prolonged postictal immobility was associated with severe hypoxemia and hypercapnia. 10 , 11 In the prone position, prolonged postictal immobility may increase SUDEP risk because of the associated diminished airway protective reflexes and impaired arousal. 10 The overlap between inhibitory phenomena (progressive clonic slowing and prolonged postictal immobility) and severity of respiratory dysfunction suggests shared underlying mechanisms contributing to postictal motor and respiratory arousal, which may contribute to SUDEP risk.

The semiology of tonic decerebration has been associated with a greater degree of desaturation 20 and postictal tonic posturing, with a sixfold increase in the risk of PCCA and longer postictal hypoxemia following BTCS. In our study, tonic decerebration, tonic mouth opening, and longer tonic duration were all associated with the presence of progressive clonic slowing. However, our findings also demonstrate that progressive clonic slowing is associated with both hypoxemia and postictal immobility, even after controlling for tonic phase duration. Progressive clonic slowing was related to the severity of hypoxemia after controlling for the durations of both postictal immobility and PGES. These findings suggest that progressive clonic slowing reflects a distinct electroclinical process during the clonic phase, rather than simply being a downstream effect of a prolonged tonic phase or a surrogate marker of postictal immobility and PGES.

The association between progressive clonic slowing and respiratory dysfunction suggests involvement of midbrain regions, particularly the red nucleus, which regulates motor output and modulates ventilatory responses. It has been postulated that decerebrate posturing reflects reduced rubrospinal output from the red nucleus to limb flexor muscles, resulting in unopposed vestibulospinal activation of limb extensors. 24 Both tonic decerebrate posturing and mouth opening have been shown to involve midbrain dysfunction at the intercollicular‐rubral level. 24 , 25 , 26 A plausible explanation for our observations is a pattern of increased inhibition of the red nucleus that is initially tonic in nature but which becomes phasic when progressive clonic slowing occurs. Recordings from electrodes implanted in the red nucleus in animal models have shown increased burst frequency during interclonic silent periods with increased activity continuing into the postictal period; in keeping with our findings, it is possible that this finding reflects the pattern of inhibitory synaptic activity in the red nucleus. 27

The association between progressive clonic slowing and the severity of respiratory dysfunction may be underpinned by shared inhibitory processes. The red nucleus influences ventilatory responses to hypoxia, 28 , 29 and has indirect connections to the Pre‐Bötzinger Complex, which plays an important role in regulating the respiratory rhythm. 30 The dentate nucleus of the cerebellum is a key modulator of red nucleus activity. In both animal 31 , 32 , 33 and human functional MRI studies, 34 , 35 the cerebellum has been shown to play an important role in the ventilatory response to hypoxia. 36 Furthermore, cerebellar activation has been observed during the postictal phase following BTCS in humans 37 and during extinction of convulsive seizures in animal models. 38 It is possible that these structures are secondarily involved following propagation or projection of seizure activity to the brainstem resulting in a complex dynamic interplay between excitation and inhibition.

We found an association between progressive clonic slowing and the occurrence of PGES, suggesting the potential of shared pathways. Tonic decerebrate semiology, which we observed to be associated with progressive clonic slowing, has also been associated with the occurrence of PGES by others. 20 , 21 , 25 , 39 , 40 In animal studies, the development of PGES involved extrapyramidal structures in the brainstem, 27 which when lesioned abolished PGES and reduced progressive clonic slowing. 27 , 41 Although the role of PGES as a risk marker of SUDEP has not been confirmed prospectively, prolonged PGES has often been observed in BTCS with cardiorespiratory compromise and delayed arousal. 7 , 10 , 18 , 21 , 42 , 43

Nursing intervention with oxygenation and patient repositioning did not have a statistically significant effect on the extent of desaturation, duration of desaturation, and duration of postictal immobility. However, the use of supplemental oxygen (91%) and patient repositioning (85%) occurred in a high proportion of BTCS in this study; statistical power was reduced by the imbalance in group size for BTCS with and without nursing intervention. One patient died from probable SUDEP a week after video‐EEG monitoring which captured two BTCS, both with motor inhibitory phenomena—progressive clonic slowing and prolonged postictal immobility—as well as an atypical temporal pattern of severe desaturation raising the possibility of a contribution from the interaction between excessive motor inhibition and an abnormality of ventilatory control.

Four limitations should be considered. First, our cohort from a specialized epilepsy unit primarily comprises drug‐resistant patients, who have a higher SUDEP risk than the general epilepsy population. Second, without respiratory belts or capnography, our assessment of respiratory parameters was limited, though prior studies show a strong correlation between apnea and hypoxemia. 8 Third, the incidence of progressive clonic slowing in our study was higher than in previous studies (90.9% vs. 72.2%), 18 which makes the observed reduction in respiratory compromise when this marker is absent noteworthy. The prevalence of progressive clonic slowing in other populations of people with epilepsy is not yet fully defined. Fourth, we focused on the presence of progressive clonic slowing without evaluating quantitative metrics that require surface electromyography measurements, which could be considered in future studies to determine a potential quantitative relationship between progressive clonic slowing and desaturation, apnea, and postictal immobility.

CONCLUSION

This study demonstrates that progressive clonic slowing is a distinct electroclinical marker strongly associated with prolonged postictal immobility and moderate‐to‐severe respiratory dysfunction following BTCS. Unlike prior studies that primarily focused on PGES as a seizure severity marker, our findings highlight progressive clonic slowing as a robust indicator of inhibition and respiratory dysfunction. Progressive clonic slowing may reflect an initial cascade of inhibitory motor processes beginning in the clonic phase and extending into the postictal phase. These findings offer novel insights into how progressive inhibition before seizure offset may impair postictal arousal and ventilatory recovery, and the pathophysiological processes contributing to SUDEP. The clinical implications of these findings are significant as understanding the mechanisms underlying these phenomena could offer new treatment avenues.
