Multimetric analysis uncovers time-dependent climate forcings from China’s 2.3-fold cropland reactive nitrogen emissions
Authors: Peng Xu, Zhenzhong Zeng, Huizhong Shen, Min Hu, Mingxu Liu, Cheng Gong, Yilin Chen, Chaopu Ti, Geng Li, Xuejing Wang, Yi Zheng, Si-Liang Li, Cong-Qiang Liu, Benjamin Z. Houlton
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SignificanceFood production for people and animals requires nitrogen fertilizers. A major 21st-century challenge is to reduce nitrogen fertilizers while meeting the food and nutritional demands of humanity. By comprehensively analyzing cropland Ngr emissions, their climate impact, and the underlying socioeconomic drivers, we find that China’s cropland Ngr emissions in 2017 impart net cooling (−253 Tg CO2 e) at 20-y time scale ascribed to short-lived NH3 and NOx emissions, but shift to net warming (+51 Tg CO2 e) over 100 y. This shift is driven by the influence of long-lived N2O emissions. Improving nitrogen use efficiency and reducing the nitrogen intensity of protein production for consumers can significantly reduce agricultural nitrogen emissions, supporting global efforts to combat air pollution and climate change.

Global crop production is expected to increase in the coming decades in response to a growing population and increasing food demand; to date, crop production has kept pace with this rising demand through nitrogen (N) fertilizer use (1). The global total application of N fertilizer to croplands increased from approximately 11 Tg N y−1 in 1961 to 110 Tg N y−1 by 2017 (2) and has already exceeded one estimated planetary boundary (51 Tg N y−1) (3). Global N fertilizer use could increase to 117 to 163 Tg N y−1 by 2050 (4, 5). Over 15% of the N applied to croplands (including N fertilizer, manure N, biological N fixation, atmospheric N deposition, and irrigation N) is lost to the air as reactive gaseous nitrogen (Ngr, including ammonia (NH3), nitrous oxide (N2O), and nitrogen oxides (NOx = NO + NO2)) globally (29 Tg N y−1 in 2015) (4), resulting in severe air pollution from PM2.5 (fine particulate matter with an aerodynamic diameter <2.5 μm) (6) and climate change by altering both local and global radiative forcings, as well as human health and ecosystem risks (7, 8).

China was the world’s largest contributor to cropland Ngr emissions from 2010–2017, with an annual average of 8.1 Tg N y−1, representing 23% of the global total (9). However, in 2022, China’s N use efficiency (NUE, defined as crop production per kg of fertilizer nitrogen applied) was only 0.50, whereas it was 0.56 worldwide, 0.58 in France, and 0.72 in the United States (10). To address agricultural N spillovers into the environmental system, climate change, and human health impacts, obtaining an accurate accounting of cropland Ngr emissions is critical. Several studies have estimated China’s cropland Ngr emissions since 2010 via either bottom–up or top–down approaches (2, 9–25); however, these results have relatively large uncertainties, ranging from 3.5 to 9.0 Tg N y−1 for NH3, 0.32 to 0.69 Tg N y−1 for N2O, and 0.1 to 0.4 Tg N y−1 for NOx in China (SI Appendix, Table S1). These discrepancies may arise from differences in methods, input data, and emission factors (EFs), making their results difficult to compare. In addition, such previous studies typically estimated emissions of a component for a single year or were inconsistent in estimating trends, making it challenging to conclude dynamic changes in emissions. Fewer studies have quantified the socioeconomic drivers of Ngr emissions; thus, future projections are unreliable. Ngr emissions significantly impact earth’s radiative forcing, including long-lived warming and short-lived cooling effects (26). We fill this knowledge gap by using more accurate activity data and robust EFs and by building an integrated cropland Ngr emissions-climate impact assessment framework for China.

An objective assessment of the impacts of climate change on China’s cropland Ngr emissions, which has attracted global attention, is crucial (27). In addition, simultaneous and separate quantification of the climate forcings of these three gases are needed to develop effective mitigation strategies (28, 29). A study (30) of global net climate effects of anthropogenic reactive N via a comprehensive model framework suggested that the net cooling effect from agricultural sources could be dominantly attributed to NH3 emissions. The metrics used to assess the climate impacts of Ngr emissions by aggregating them into CO2-equivalent (CO2e) emissions include the global warming potential (GWP) and global temperature potential (GTP) at 20 or 100 y (31). However, there are certain limitations in the present methods used to assess their climate impacts, such as the use of a single metric (GWP100 or GTP100) and the lack of consideration of climate-carbon feedback (32). Moreover, some studies have used process-based models to quantify Ngr-related climate impacts (e.g., the effects of NOx or NH3 on aerosols, ozone, or CH4) (33–35). However, this approach has several limitations, such as the need for climate model simulations that incur high computational costs (32) and the large intermodel differences in atmospheric chemistry and radiative physics across different climate models (36). Two previous studies (32, 37) have highlighted these gaps, emphasizing the need for a simple and robust framework via a wider spectrum of climate metrics to better capture the spatiotemporal heterogeneity of climate system responses to the variety of climate forcing agents usually at play.

Here, we use bottom–up mass flow-based approaches, activity data at the county level for 2017 and the province level from 1978–2023, and regional condition-specific EFs to examine the controls on and net forcings of China’s cropland Ngr emissions. We use the Stochastic Impacts by Regression on Population, Affluence and Technology (STIRPAT) (38) model to assess the socioeconomic drivers of China’s cropland Ngr emissions from 1978–2023. We compare our results with those of previous studies (2, 9, 11–25). We quantitatively evaluate the net climate forcing of China’s cropland Ngr emissions in 2017 at the county level, and 1978 and 2023 at the province level by using multiple metrics, including GWP20, GWP100, and GTP100, which address different spatiotemporal perspectives. We further comprehensively quantify the damage costs of China’s cropland Ngr emissions.

Results

Ngr Emission Hotspots in China in 2017.

The cropland-related Ngr emissions across China’s 2,376 counties in 2017 (Etotal) were 7.5 Tg N·y−1 (R50: the difference between the 75th and 25th quartiles, 6.5 to 8.6 Tg N·y−1); of this total, 88.8% was NH3, 5.8% was N2O and 5.4% was NOx. The county-level Ngr emissions in 2017 were transformed to a 1 km resolution via a gridded land use map. Synthetic fertilizer application was a major source, contributing 47.8% of Etotal, with the remainder from livestock manure spreading (37.2%), rural excrement (6.1%), straw returning (crop straw applied to croplands directly or after mechanical pulverization or decomposition, 5.0%), and cake fertilizer (3.8%). The average Ngr emissions per unit land area in China were 2.5 ± 7.3 (mean ± SD) Mg N·km−2·y−1 (Fig. 1). The average Ngr emissions per unit area varied significantly among regions, ranging from approximately 1.1 ± 0.6 Mg N·km−2·y−1 in Southwest China to 3.4 ± 1.8 Mg N·km−2·y−1 in East China. The highest emission densities were located in the Northeast Plain, North China Plain, Huaihe River Basin, Lianghu Plain, Sichuan Basin, and southern Guangdong and Guangxi provinces (SI Appendix, Fig. S1), where most of China’s cereal and livestock production is concentrated. The greatest Ngr emissions were found in Henan, Hebei, Shandong, Jilin, Hubei, Sichuan, Hunan, Heilongjiang, Anhui, and Yunnan provinces, which together accounted for 57.4% of Etotal. Fifty percent of the total cumulative emissions were obtained from 474 of the 2,376 counties (SI Appendix, Fig. S2); however, spatial Ngr emission hotspots (those above 474 counties) covered 15.9% of the Chinese land area. A total of 65.4% of these 474 hotspots emerged in the ten provinces mentioned above.

Trends and Drivers of China’s Emissions.

China’s total cropland Ngr emissions increased by approximately 2.3-fold from 1978 to 2023 (SI Appendix, Fig. S3A). However, the rate of increase slowed, decelerating from 0.17 (R50: 0.15 to 0.19) Tg N·y−2 before 1996 to 0.15 (R50: 0.14 to 0.17) Tg N·y−2 from 1997 to 2005 and further to −0.05 (R50: −0.04 to −0.06) Tg N·y−2 after 2005. We found a sharp decline in Ngr emissions between 2006 and 2007 because of natural disasters (e.g., flooding in the Huaihe Basin) and livestock disease. The total NH3 emissions from China’s croplands increased from 2.4 Tg N (R50: 2.1 to 2.7) in 1978 to 5.2 Tg N (R50: 4.5 to 5.9) in 2023 (Fig. 2A). Similarly, N2O emissions rose from 0.13 Tg N (R50: 0.11 to 0.15) in 1978 to 0.37 Tg N (R50: 0.32 to 0.42) in 2023 (Fig. 2B), whereas NOx emissions increased from 0.10 Tg N (R50: 0.09 to 0.12) in 1978 to 0.38 Tg N (R50: 0.33 to 0.43) in 2023 (Fig. 2C). At the provincial level in 2017, the NH3, N2O, and NOx emissions in Beijing (decreased by 20%, 36% and 22%, respectively), Tianjin (decreased by 57%, 70% and 67%, respectively), and Tibet (decreased by 9%, 76%, and 66%, respectively) decreased from their 1978 levels (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). By 2023, the NH3 emissions in Inner Mongolia and Xinjiang, the N2O emissions in Heilongjiang, Tibet, and Xinjiang, the NOx emissions in Inner Mongolia, Heilongjiang, Gansu, and Tibet increased slightly from their 2017 levels, and the emissions in other provinces have all shown downward trends. (SI Appendix, Fig. S4). Moreover, Henan (decreased by 17%) and Shandong (decreased by 12%) remain emission hotspots, even with observed reductions. From 1978 to 2023, Inner Mongolia exhibited the most rapid rise in NH3 (7-fold), N2O (18-fold) and NOx (19-fold) emissions. Details regarding spatial distribution trends of NH3, N2O, and NOx emissions at the provincial level are explained in the Supplementary Information. The cropland Ngr emissions from synthetic fertilizer increased from 1.1 to 2.7 Tg N·y−1 from 1978 to 2023. Livestock manure spreading emissions increased nearly 2.8-fold in this interval, reaching 2.2 Tg N·y−1 in 2023. Rural excrement emissions decreased slightly over the interval, whereas the proportional contribution of the rural excrement sector substantially decreased from 19.5% in 1978 to 5.2% in 2023. From 1978–2023, synthetic fertilizer accounted for an average of 43%, 74%, and 76% of the NH3, N2O, and NOx emissions, respectively, whereas livestock manure spreading contributed 36%, 12%, and 15%, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S3 b-c). The average contribution of China’s cropland Ngr emissions to the global total cropland Ngr emissions was 22.2% from 1978 to 2022, based on the global emissions estimated by EDGAR v8.1 (9) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5A).

Trends of Ngr emissions from China’s cropland over the period 1978–2023. (A) NH3; (B) N2O; (C) NOx. The orange shaded area shows the R50 of the emission estimates derived from a Monte Carlo simulation.

We quantified the contributions of six socioeconomic factors (population, GDP per capita, dietary choice, urbanization rate (the ratio of the urban population to the total population), NUE, and planting structure) to changes in Ngr emissions (SI Appendix, Table S2). Our results suggest that these six socioeconomic factors accounted for approximately 94.1% of the changes of Ngr emissions in China from 1978 to 2023. NUE and planting structure had a negative correlation with Ngr emissions. Between 1978 and 2023, the most significant contributor to increased Ngr emissions was population growth, which resulted in a 0.5% annual increase in Ngr emissions and contributed 31.7% to the annual changes in Ngr emissions. The second most important factor was dietary choice, contributing 23.2% to the annual changes in Ngr emissions. In China, diets have shifted to include more animal protein at an annual growth rate of 4.5%. Since 2008, the ratio of animal food to total food consumed has increased faster (average 17% per year) (SI Appendix, Fig. S5B). The urbanization rate and GDP per capita during this period accounted for 15.7% and 16.8% of the change in Ngr emissions in the model, respectively. During this period, the NUE decreased by 0.2% per year while the planting structure increased slightly by 0.03% per year. These changes accounted for 5.7% and 1.0% of the variation in Ngr emissions, respectively.

We further investigated the contributions of each of the six socioeconomic factors to the changes in China’s cropland Ngr emissions during different periods (Fig. 3). Before 1996, dietary choice and the urbanization rate were the main drivers of the increase in Ngr emissions, accounting for 22.2% and 22.0%, respectively (Fig. 3, orange and light blue bars). Between 1978 and 2005, the 136% increase in Chinese cropland Ngr emissions was dominated by GDP per capita (Fig. 3, dark red bars), which–in the absence of other factors–would have caused emissions to increase by 21.3% and 5.6% during the periods 1978–1996 and 1996–2005, respectively. Since 2005, NUE and planting structure have been the main drivers of the decrease in Ngr emissions, amounting to −13.7% and −8.6%, respectively, which could offset China’s cropland Ngr emissions (Fig. 3, green and red bars).

Trends and Drivers of Regional Emissions.

In 1978, East China and Northwest China contributed the largest (29.6%) and smallest (11.0%) shares of Etotal, respectively (SI Appendix, Fig. S6). Significant decreases in the emission shares were observed in Southwest China and Northwest China, from 14.9% and 11.0% in 1978 to 11.9% and 8.4% in 1996, respectively. During 1978–1996, East China contributed the largest share of Etotal on average (31.2%), followed by South Central China (23.8%). The share of emissions from East China decreased from 29.7% in 1997 to 26.4% in 2005. However, the emission shares of the remaining regions of China in 2005 fluctuated slightly relative to the values observed in 1997. In 2006, South Central China became the largest contributor, with a share of 26.7% of Etotal. Between 2006 and 2023, the share of emissions from East China continued to decrease by 14.4%. Since 2005, Northwest China’s and Northeast China’s emission shares have increased by 35.6% and 14.8%, respectively. The share of emissions from South Central China remained stable during 2006–2023 and was the largest contributor, with an average share of 27.4% of Etotal.

Regionally, population growth was the most critical factor influencing increased Ngr emissions from 1978 to 2023 except in Northeast China (dietary choice) (SI Appendix, Table S3). The proportion of animal products in the total food consumed increased in all regions, with the greatest increase in South Central China (749%) and Southwest China (747%). This factor, which was the second most important driver in Northwest China, North China, and Southwest China, contributed 25.0%, 23.2%, and 18.4% of the change in Ngr emissions, respectively. The two regions with the highest average annual Ngr emission growth rates were South Central China (2.6%) and Northeast China (2.7%). During this period, NUE negatively affected Ngr emissions growth in Northeast China, North China, and Southwest China (SI Appendix, Table S3). The region with the greatest offset resulting from an increase in NUE of 1.0% was Northeast China, where Ngr emissions decreased by 18.9% (SI Appendix, Table S3). The extensive agricultural growth in this region indicates that it has accounted for 11.2% of China’s fertilizer N consumption and 12.1% of China’s agricultural production since 1978. In addition, other factors were the strongest factors that increased China’s Ngr emissions in North China, East China, and Southwest China (SI Appendix, Table S3).

The Net Climate Effect and Damage Costs of Ngr Emissions.

The net climate change forcings of Ngr from Chinese croplands in 2017 are −253 Tg CO2e (−577 to +88 Tg CO2e) on a GWP20 basis, −15 Tg CO2e (−163 to +132 Tg CO2e) on a GWP100 basis and +51 Tg CO2e (+40 to +57 Tg CO2e) on a GTP100 basis (SI Appendix, Table S4). During 1978–2023, GWP20, GWP100, and GTP100 increased 2.2-fold, 0.9-fold, and 3.3-fold respectively (SI Appendix, Table S4). The largest negative forcing is from NH3 emissions, which is −113 Tg CO2e (−182 to −43 Tg CO2e) in 1978 and −313 Tg CO2e (−507 to −120 Tg CO2e) in 2017 on a GWP20 basis. In a short timeframe, NH3 emissions substantially contribute to cooling, which is often ignored in climate change studies (39, 40) but should be considered in future research. NOx has the second largest negative effect, with −18 Tg CO2e (−30 to −6 Tg CO2e) in 1978 and −71 Tg CO2e (−117 to −24 Tg CO2e) in 2017 on a GWP20 basis. However, these effects are short-lived, and the values on a GWP100 basis and a GTP100 basis are lower. The climate forcing analysis of cropland-related Ngr emissions included six climate factors, namely, the warming potential of N2O (CF1), the direct cooling effects of nitrate and ammonium light-scattering aerosols formed by NH3 (CF2) and NOx (CF3), the impacts of NOx on ozone and CH4 (CF4), and the CO2 and CH4 flux effects derived from N deposition in natural systems (CF5) and agricultural N fertilizers, including synthetic and organic fertilizers applied to croplands (CF6) (26). The climate impacts from CF2-CF4 are all negative from medium- (GWP100) and long-term (GTP100) perspectives. We considered the inclusion of climate-carbon feedback (CF5 and CF6) in the metric values, which has been overlooked in previous studies (23, 33, 35). The cooling effect of CO2 uptake caused by N deposition (−0.8 and −3.9 Tg CO2e) and agricultural N fertilizer applications (−26 and −85 Tg CO2e) nearly offset the warming effect of CH4 emissions caused by N deposition (+0.6 and +4.4 Tg CO2e) and agricultural N fertilizers applications (+20 and +96 Tg CO2e) for GWP20 in 1978 and 2017, respectively. For GWP100 and GTP100 in 2017, the net impacts are cooling: −2.4 Tg CO2e and −3.6 Tg CO2e of CF5, and −52 Tg CO2e and −80 Tg CO2e of CF6, respectively. GWP100 and GTP100 of CF5 and CF6 increased 1.3- and 2.6-fold from 1978–2023. It can be inferred that climate change forces N additions to shift from net CO2 sources to sinks over time, which is consistent with empirical evidence from the literature (26, 41). Therefore, the combination of metrics can adequately capture short-, medium-, and long-term climate impacts.

The net climate effect of Ngr emissions varies by region (SI Appendix, Table S5). The contribution of each county or region is a net climate cooling effect for GWP20 but a net climate cooling and warming effects for GWP100 and GTP100 (Figs. 1 and 4). South Central China contributes the most to GWP20 (28%), GWP100 (28%), and GTP100 (29%). The greatest CO2 and CH4 flux effects derived from N deposition (CF5) are located in East China, which has relatively high N deposition rates (35). Northeast China (+3.8 Tg CO2e) and Southwest China (+0.6 Tg CO2e) show a net climate warming effect for GWP100. Northwest China is the lowest contributor to GTP100 because of low agricultural production and N deposition rates, and it has a net climate cooling effect. Overall, China contributed 23%, −20%, and 12% of the current net global cropland GWP20 (−1,096 Tg CO2e, −2538 to +434 Tg CO2e), GWP100 (+75 Tg CO2e, −603 to +754 Tg CO2e), and GTP100 (+423 Tg CO2e, +373 to +451 Tg CO2e) in 2017, respectively. This contribution is the sum of two terms with opposite signs. While China accounts for 20% of the global positive radiative forcing across GWP20, GWP100, and GTP100, its share of the global negative radiative forcing is larger, at 22%, 25%, and 32% for the respective metrics. Notably, the contribution of China to global Ngr emissions from croplands (29%) is greater than its contribution to GTP100 (12%), mainly because short-lived NH3 and NOx are the major components of Ngr, determining the contribution of Chinese emissions of aerosols to climate change impacts.

Climate impacts of Ngr emissions from China’s croplands at the county level in 2017. (A) GWP20; (B) GWP100; (C) GTP100.

Ngr emissions from Chinese croplands have substantial impacts on human and ecosystem health and climate change (SI Appendix, Fig. S7 and Table S8). Additionally, the health damage costs per unit of Ngr emissions in China are approximately 0.5 to 5.7 times lower than those in Europe and the United States mainly owing to the relatively low value of a statistical life (VSL) in China. Notably, our values in China fall within the range of values reported in other studies (42, 43). The median total damage cost of Ngr in China in 2017 was calculated as $28 billion (R50 was $24 to 32 billion), accounting for approximately 3.0% of China’s agricultural GDP, among which NH3 (78%) made a greater contribution than N2O (22%) and NOx (1%). Approximately $24 billion was related to impacts on human health, and $13 billion was related to impacts on ecosystems. There was an economic benefit, namely, cooling effects (−$9 billion), due to the large proportion of Ngr lost as NH3.

Discussion

Our revised estimates of China’s cropland Ngr emissions are generally within the range of those obtained in previous studies that used bottom–up or top–down approaches for the same source mix and year (Fig. 2 and SI Appendix, Table S1). The provincial condition-specific EFs used in this study vary by year. This variability is due to factors such as interannual climate conditions, the interannual proportions of each livestock production system, the annual average provincial condition-specific N excretion for each livestock category and the manure management practices in each year. For the county-level analysis in 2017, the same provincial-level EF for that year was applied uniformly across all counties within each province (with the exception of gridded NH3 EFs from synthetic fertilizer applications and county-level NOx EFs). More details regarding the comparison with previous bottom–up studies are provided in the Supplementary Information. In addition, the robustness of our estimation was confirmed by top–down emission estimates derived from satellite inversion (17, 20, 44) and atmospheric observations (18, 19). Our estimates of NH3 emissions were 22% and 12% lower than those of Zhang et al. (17) for 2008 and Paulot et al. (18) for 2005–2008, respectively. Our estimates of N2O emissions were 37% and 28% lower than those of Saikawa et al. (19) for 2008 and Thompson et al. (22) for 2016, respectively. Our estimate of N2O emissions was highly consistent with the top–down emission estimates (three global atmospheric inversion frameworks) used by Thompson et al. (22) in terms of the magnitude and the emission trend. Our estimate of NOx emissions was 5.2 times higher than that of Qu et al. (20) for 2005–2012. Our maps present more local Ngr emission hotspots (Fig. 1), particularly northwest of the “Hu Huanyong line” (SI Appendix, Fig. S1A). Some NH3 high-emission areas (for example, the North China Plain and Tarim Basin) are coincident with the high column densities observed by the IASI satellite (44). Our identified Ngr emission hotspots were qualitatively consistent with those detected via top–down approaches. In these top–down studies, emissions were dependent on the magnitude and distribution of the prior flux estimates, which are strongly constrained to an extent by the number of atmospheric observations and the different models and retrieval algorithms used (45). To narrow the uncertainties in both emission estimation methodologies, further studies are needed.

Our study demonstrates that emission abatement approaches in China can continue to further reduce Ngr through livestock manure and enhanced-efficiency fertilizers (EEFs, e.g., urease inhibitors, nitrification inhibitors, and controlled-release fertilizers) substitution, and NUE improvement. First, only approximately 41% of livestock manure was recycled in 2017 in China, while the 40 to 60% substitution of synthetic fertilizers with livestock manure achieves the maximum cobenefits of increased crop production and reduced Ngr emissions (46–48). Our previous study revealed that China has sufficient capacity to achieve this optimal proportion (46) and that the manure input value (US$ 24 ha−1) is lower than that of synthetic fertilizers (US$310 ha−1) in 2019 (49); however, but we considered transportation costs and other possible pollutants, such as heavy metals and veterinary antibiotics (50). Additionally, extra attention should be given to the phosphorus surplus issue because livestock manure replacing synthetic fertilizer is normally recommended solely according to crop N requirements (51). Technologies that improve the quality of livestock feed could reduce phosphorus excretion by 20 to 30% and promote nutrient budgeting for crops, thus reducing the overall phosphorus losses to avoid freshwater eutrophication and ocean anoxic events (52). Second, globally, a recent study indicated that full substitution of synthetic N fertilizer with EEFs can reduce Ngr emissions from fertilizer use in maize and wheat production by 27% (53). However, different EEFs can strongly differ in their effects on the abatement of Ngr emissions. For example, nitrification inhibitors decrease direct N2O emissions by 8 to 57%, but generally increase NH3 emissions by 3 to 65% (54). EEFs offer a promising opportunity but have not been widely adopted by farmers because they are more expensive than conventional fertilizers (55). Third, reducing Ngr emissions while maintaining crop yields is a major challenge for all countries and can be effectively addressed by optimal N management strategies (7, 56). You et al. (57) revealed that the current global cropland mean NUE can increase by 30% based on metaregression models through optimal nutrient, crop, and soil management practices. Cai et al. (58) indicated that the application of an optimal N rate strategy for sustainable rice production in China can increase NUE by 30% and 36% for economic and ecological performance, respectively. By developing new techniques and leveraging AI, our previous study provides insights into effective N management strategies to lower NH3 emissions from three main staple crops and to promote sustainable agriculture (55). Possible advanced practices and techniques to improve NUE include adopting 4R nutrient stewardship (right fertilizer type, amount, placement, and time) (4), improved irrigation practices (59), precision agriculture (60), genetic technologies for cereal variety selection (61) and soil microbial community modification (62, 63). Beyond direct agronomic interventions, machine learning (55) and remote sensing (64) provide critical decision-support tools to optimize N management strategies, thereby enhancing NUE.

Our study shows that reducing Ngr emissions from croplands effectively also depends on socioeconomic drivers, such as diet shifts, an optimal cropping structure, incentives, or the role of policies and institutions in promoting sustainable agricultural practices. China’s annual animal food consumption has increased approximately sevenfold from 12 Mt in 1978 to 84 Mt in 2017 (65) and is projected to increase further to 141 Mt and 224 Mt by 2030 and 2050, respectively (66). The ratio of animal food to total food consumed is projected to increase to 25% and 34% by 2030 and 2050, respectively. Increasing animal food consumption hinders efforts to reduce Ngr emissions and has high social and climate costs (67). Therefore, a shift to a less animal-based food diet, such as bivalves, seaweeds, and plant-based protein substitutes (68, 69), is recommended. An increase in the ratio of the sown area of grain crops to the total sown area of farm crops could reduce the sown area of vegetables and fruits and hence reduce Ngr emissions. The fertilizer rates for vegetables and fruits are double those for grain crops in China, where vegetables and fruits have lower NUE than the global NUE (7, 70). Moreover, the increased sown area of potatoes in grain crops can potentially reduce Ngr emissions because potatoes grown in cold regions present lower Ngr EFs than the three staple crops do (12, 71). The N credit system can be seen as an appropriate policy to address agricultural N pollution through introducing socioeconomic trade-offs between farmers and society (4). Economic and policy assistance, such as managing farm size (72) and introducing taxation schemes (73), is also necessary to encourage the implementation of technologies and management practices, mitigate agricultural N pollution and ensure food security. One study shows that NH3 emissions from croplands decrease by, on average, 0.07% for each 1% increase in the average farm size in China (74). However, the average farm size in China is much smaller than that in Europe or the United States because of higher population density and a significantly greater proportion of agricultural workers (27). Spatial planning through relocating 10 billion animals away from southern and eastern China could tackle N pollution exposure for 90% of China’s population (50). The vast heterogeneity in agricultural management practices across China has led to profound regional disparities in health and the environment.

For farmers, consumers, and policymakers, the preferred choice of cost-effective mitigation technologies is needed. Furthermore, a region-specific mitigation strategy is needed to guide the planning and implementation of regional strategies for increasing crop yield and NUE and to mitigate Ngr emissions from croplands instead of ‘one-size-fits-all’ policies. For instance, in East China (affluent provinces, particularly high-expenditure groups), the adoption of soy to replace a red meat diet can help reduce NH3 emissions by up to 20% (75). However, in provinces such as Qinghai, Tibet, and Inner Mongolia, where diets heavily rely on dairy products and red meat due to the traditional nomadic lifestyle and limited accessibility of diverse foods in these provinces, especially in rural areas, diet shifts may not be feasible, but there is a need to improve N-management knowledge through farmer-education programs (4, 76). Dietary shifts that alter food demand can induce significant structural adjustments within the national agri-food system. The diet shifts would necessitate the global supply (in calorie content) of red meat decrease by 81%, tubers by 76% and grains by 50%, while that of legumes and nuts increase by 438%, and vegetables and fruits by 28% (76). Crop switching away from some maize and toward rice in the Northeast Plain in China can reduce the demand for nitrogen fertilizers by as much as −15.9% and increase farmer incomes by +72.3% relative to current levels. However, greenhouse gas emissions (e.g., CH4) from croplands in the Northeast Plain would increase by about 11.6% (77). The most effective way to reduce Ngr emissions at the regional and global scales is through the use of EEFs, which can reduce NH3, N2O, and NOx by 69%, 35%, and 46%, respectively, while increasing crop yield by 25% and NUE by 18% (4), particularly in regions with limited access to foods needed for dietary shifts or low NUE regions. EEFs may not be effective in all regions, as no improvement in maize NUE was observed with their substitution in the North China Plain in China (53). Governments can create policy innovations or provide subsidies and incentives to encourage the widespread adoption and implementation of best practices above in mitigating or addressing the challenge of N management (78). For example, the Chinese governmental rice subsidy was approximately US$ 106 ha−1 in 2020 to implement knowledge-based N management practices such as the use of EEFs (58). Newly built livestock farms must consider their spatial colocation with the distribution of croplands to help promote manure recycling. The Chinese government has invested more than US$3 billion in improving manure recycling with the aim of decreasing livestock pollution in more than 600 counties in recent years (79).

Our findings highlight that the net climate change impacts of Ngr from Chinese croplands are cooling at present with strong regional variations and for a relatively short period into the future. However, as the time horizon of the analysis becomes longer, the warming effects induced by Ngr emissions exert greater pressure. Furthermore, the robustness of our estimation was confirmed via the radiative forcing approach. For example, the net cooling effects ascribed to the direct aerosol effect were caused mainly by NH3 emissions (e.g., GWP20: −313 Tg CO2e, −507 to −120 Tg CO2e) (SI Appendix, Table S4), which was highly consistent with the findings from the radiative forcing approach (30). It has been estimated that N addition-induced acidification over the next 30 y (2020 to 2050) under the SSP5-8.5-Low-Ambition-N policy scenario (i.e., with a high level of mineral N inputs) could reduce topsoil soil inorganic carbon, particularly in China and India (80). However, a recent study revealed that China’s cropland soils have stabilized in recent years attributed to the decrease in N fertilizer use and advanced fertilizer management (81). This finding demonstrates that the negative trajectory is not inevitable and can be altered through proactive measures. Therefore, future work must manage carbon and N in an integrated manner to sustain these positive trends and offer effective guidance for policy-making (82). The impacts of short-lived CH4 emissions rapidly decline after a few decades (83). The climate change impacts of climate-carbon feedback (including CO2 uptake and CH4 emissions) induced by N addition shift from net CO2 sources to sinks over time. This study includes climate-carbon feedback and the combination of metrics missing from a previous study of Ngr emissions regarding Chinese croplands’ contributions to the global GWP and GTP (27); thus, this assessment is more comprehensive and adequately captures short-, medium-, and long-term climate impacts.

In addition, climate change will conflict with Ngr mitigation efforts (67). Therefore, short-lived climate forcers of NH3 and NOx have long-term effects via carbon-climate feedback (84). NH3 and NOx, as short-lived species, contribute to the health-damaging effects of PM2.5 (85). PM2.5 pollution can be cost-effectively controlled to decrease NH3 and NOx emissions globally; agricultural NH3 reduction is a particular priority in many countries, including China (43, 66). However, this reduction in these short-lived components will reduce the current cooling effects induced by nitrate and ammonium light-scattering aerosols (86), indicating the importance of considering the impact of climate change in designing and implementing air quality policies.

Our study has some uncertainties and limitations that warrant attention in future research endeavors. The main objective of this study was to update an approach and estimate China’s cropland Ngr emissions, as well as their drivers and net climate forcings, at the finest resolution available now. The currently available 5 arcmin resolution gridded datasets on N fertilizer and manure use from Tian et al. (87) and Adalibieke et al. (88) were derived from the annual country-level N fertilizer and manure application amounts for agriculture from FAOSTAT (2). These datasets estimate spatial distribution at a resolution of 5 arcmin via spatial indicators such as the grid-level crop-specific N fertilizer and manure use rates per cropland area for 2000 and the crop-specific harvested area. Owing to the general unavailability of high-resolution, crop- and fertilizer-specific N input data at the global or regional scale from ground observations, national-scale data are typically disaggregated (87, 88). Moreover, process-based (45, 89, 90) or data-driven (53, 55, 91, 92) approaches have been used to derive EFs (e.g., 5 arcmin resolution) that are dependent on climate, soil, fertilization, irrigation, and tillage, which can incorporate finer spatial heterogeneity. However, these approaches remain challenging and involve greater requirements for high-resolution datasets and an inadequate consideration of some essential environmental and agronomic factors or processes (45, 93). Further studies are needed to encompass key factors, such as fertilization schemes (e.g., type, timing, and placement) and tillage practices, to refine our understanding of Ngr emissions.

Our results showed that, in China, the rate of increase in Ngr emissions slowed after 2005 because of the implementation of policy interventions, such as the soil testing and fertilization program in 2004, the elimination of agricultural taxes on farmers in 2006, and the zero growth in fertilizer use action plans in 2015. These trends were consistent with the findings of previous studies (82, 94). To accelerate the transition, the government has continued to establish several policy measures for improving food security and NUE, such as the national Livestock and Poultry Manure Utilization Action Plan in 2017 and the Fertilizer Quantity Reduction and Efficiency Increase in 2022, accompanied by a reduction in Ngr emissions (95). From 2018 to 2023, N fertilizer use declined slightly but remained 2.7 times greater than that in 1978 (SI Appendix, Fig. S8). A study conducted in China revealed that in the business-as-usual scenario, which extrapolates current practices, compared with those in 2017, the levels of NH3 and N2O are projected to decrease by 27.7% and 28.2% in 2050, respectively (96). Therefore, future research could expand upon this study with the availability of high-resolution input data for multiple years, which remains a critical issue to address in terms of achieving a more synergistic enhancement of sustainable benefits and obtaining a comprehensive basis for decision-making. Despite these data limitations, the findings of the magnitude and spatiotemporal variation in Ngr emissions from China’s croplands are consistent with those of previous studies, especially for net climate forcings. For example, the growth in China’s Ngr emissions from croplands has been very likely to continue slowing since 2012 (12, 14, 21). At present, the combined effect of short-lived and long-term agricultural sources and some reactive N-related climate forcers is net cooling, which is consistent with a previous study using a comprehensive model framework (29). This result shows that the main findings of this study are robust and valid, although conducting research based on such limited data may introduce uncertainty. We also quantified the indirect radiative forcing of climate change through the CO2 and CH4 flux effects derived from N deposition and agricultural N fertilizers on climate-carbon feedback; however, unambiguously identifying the contributions from cropland Ngr emissions is challenging. Our total N deposition to China in 2017 (19.3 Tg N y−1) was close to that reported by Zhao et al. (97) for 2008–2012 (16.4 Tg N y−1) and Yu et al. (94) for 2011–2015 (19.6 ± 2.5 Tg N y−1). Global warming may lead to changes in spatial patterns and interannual trends in atmospheric Ngr emissions and N deposition since NH3 emissions are tightly associated with temperature (98, 99). N deposition can promote crop yields and ecosystem carbon sinks (100, 101). Furthermore, this study neglected the cumulative effects of long-lived greenhouse gases since the preindustrial era, which may also introduce some computational bias (30). On a global basis, atmospheric transport and the subsequent deposition of N have become the dominant Ngr distribution process (102). Uncertainty remains in terms of the climate impacts of the atmospheric transport of nitrogen species between regions globally, which has not yet been fully understood or quantified in this study. The development of more integrative Earth system models, including the key interactions among processes of atmospheric chemistry, climate dynamics, and radiative processes, must be a future research priority (30, 103). However, this study does not address regional differences, such as a monetized valuation of health damage cost estimates by Ngr emissions in China, due to the lack of detailed regional data. Given the spatial variabilities in marginal damage, regional selection of concentration–response functions and the VSL, for which alternative values could be assumed, may vary considerably (104), strongly affecting monetized impacts. Future research should compare the Ngr-associated impacts on air pollution, health, and climate on a spatially resolved monetized basis.

Despite these limitations, this study contributes to advancing the scientific understanding of the climate effects of cropland Ngr emissions with the finest bottom–up dataset. We provide guidance to policymakers developing comprehensive national strategies for sustainable N management and climate mitigation targets. Our findings suggest that a combination of metrics can comprehensively and adequately capture short-, medium-, and long-term climate impacts that will conspire against Ngr mitigation efforts. Moreover, our assessment technique is applicable to other emerging economies facing similar issues of rapidly increasing food demand yet with underdeveloped technology, such as Brazil, India, and South Africa. This knowledge is helpful for informing policies to achieve net-zero emission targets to meet the global 1.5 °C goal and to overcome global agricultural nitrogen challenges not only in China but also in other parts of the world.

Methods

Data Sources.

County-level data on synthetic fertilizer (5 types), livestock (8 types), crops (17 types), and rural populations in 2017 were derived from 2,376 counties from 329 municipal statistical yearbooks for mainland China, Taiwan, Macau, and Hong Kong. Annual provincial-level activity data from 1978–2023 were collected from the China Animal Industry Yearbook (1999–2023), the China Agriculture Yearbook (1980–2021), the Yearly Report of Taiwan’s Agriculture (1991–2018), the China Compendium of Statistics (1987–2024), FAOSTAT and the National Pollution Source Survey Database. The activity data for Macau and Hong Kong were equal to zero owing to the lack of agriculture. We used temporal interpolation to derive inaccessible data for counties or provinces by using the arithmetic mean of adjacent values for missing years. To assess the robustness of this assumption, we performed a sensitivity analysis of temporal interpolation. Specifically, we derived accessible data for counties or provinces by calculating the arithmetic mean of adjacent values for the same years and then compared these estimates with the actual data. The results show that temporal interpolation provides sufficiently accurate missing data estimations with high R-square values (SI Appendix, Fig. S9). The input data included the soil pH from the 1-km Harmonized World Soil Database (HWSD) v.1.2 (https://www.iiasa.ac.at/), the average daily air temperature and precipitation from the China Meteorological Data Service Centre (http://data.cma.cn/en) and the Central Weather Bureau (http://www.cwb.gov.tw). A land use map (1 km) was adapted from the Resource and Environmental Science Data Platform (https://www.resdc.cn/DOI/DOI.aspx?DOIID=54).

Ngr Emissions Inventory of Chinese Croplands.

The reference approaches are bottom–up mass-flow-based approaches that use county- or provincial-level agricultural fertilizer application multiplied by the respective EFs to calculate Ngr emissions from Chinese croplands.[1]ENgr=∑iADyij·EFij·f(ECPyijk,Ck),

where ENgr refers to the county or provincial Ngr emissions of Chinese croplands; i represents the emission source, which is synthetic and organic fertilizer applied to croplands, including chemical and compound fertilizers, livestock manure, rural excrement, cake fertilizer, and straw returning; y represents the year; j represents the region ID (county-level for 2017 and provincial-level for 1978–2023); ADyij represents the agricultural N fertilizer application amount by the corresponding croplands; EFij represents the region-specific EF; k represents an emission controlling parameter; ECPyijk represents a region-specific emission controlling parameter k; Ck is a coefficient; and f(·) is a function whose mathematical form depends on the source type i. We did not consider the indirect Ngr emissions from atmospheric N deposition and N leaching or runoff owing to their minor contribution fraction and the difficulty in separating them from other sources (11).

We used the National Ammonia Reduction Strategy Evaluation System (NARSES) model to derive monthly and gridded condition-specific NH3 EFs (1 km) from synthetic fertilizer applications based on temperature, precipitation, fertilization method, application rate, and soil pH (27). To calculate provincial condition-specific NH3 EFs from livestock manure application, we used the NUtrient flows in Food chains, Environment and Resources use (NUFER) model employing a mass balance approach (105). We improved the model by incorporating the interannual proportions of each livestock production system (including grazing, traditional, medium, and industrial production systems) (106). The NH3 EFs from rural excrement, cake fertilizer, and straw returning were obtained according to Xu et al. (27). To estimate the N2O EFs from China’s croplands, we updated the PKU-N2O model from Zhou et al. (107) by incorporating new crop-specific fertilizer information and differentiating the EFs for the nonirrigation and irrigation periods in paddy fields. The cropland soil NOx emissions from the application of agricultural fertilizers were estimated via a bottom–up spatial model that combined the temperature dependence approach of Williams et al. (108) and the YL95 scheme (109). The NOx EFs was adjusted by three soil temperature intervals for upland and paddy fields at the county or province level according to Xu et al. (27). The proportions of manure returned to fields in China were obtained from Ma et al. (105), Zhang et al. (110), and the China Pollution Sources Census Committee (111). We assume that the proportions of manure returned to fields in China remained the same for the periods 1978–1994 and 1995–2023. The year 2017 is chosen as a key analysis year for two main reasons. First, it corresponds to China’s peak fertilizer N inputs since 1978, which makes it particularly informative for assessing the upper bound of the impacts from Ngr emissions. Second, 2017 is the most recent year that we were able to prepare the county-level input data and the database of the national agricultural pollution source censuses, which underpin the robustness of our estimation. More details about the models and the parameters were described in our previous studies (27, 106).

A Monte Carlo simulation with 10,000 runs was used to estimate the overall uncertainty in cropland Ngr emissions based on the variations in the activity data and EFs. According to our previous studies (27, 106), the coefficient of variation (CV) of each activity data point is equal to the absolute value of the average difference normalized by the mean of the following three datasets: the county dataset used in this study, the provincial dataset from China, and a default international dataset (FAOSTAT) (2) for 2017. The CVs of the EFs for synthetic fertilizer application, livestock manure spreading, and rural excrement were 50%, and the remaining CVs of the EFs from straw returning and cake fertilizer were 63% (27). We assume that the CVs of the activity data for all years are equal to the CVs of the 2017 data. The activity data and EFs were assumed to have uniform and normal distributions, respectively. We use the medians and R50 values to characterize the emissions and uncertainties, respectively. We conducted a sensitivity analysis comparing the uncertainties in which 1) the 50% CV for 2017 is representative for all years (1978–2016) and 2) the 150% CV for 2017 is representative for all years (1978–2016), to the overall emissions. The uncertainties across the two sensitivity analyses were not significantly different. The results show that the sensitivity of our NH3, N2O, and NOx emission estimates to the CV is very small (SI Appendix, Fig. S10). Further details of the model uncertainties have been discussed extensively in our previous publication (106).

Emission Drivers.

We assessed the effects of socioeconomic factors on changes in Ngr emissions from croplands via the STIRPAT model (38). To address the heteroscedasticity of the model, we take the natural logarithm to obtain the following equation:[2]lnI=a+blnP+clnA+dlnT+e,

where a is a constant; b, c, and d are the coefficients of P (Population), A (Affluence), and T (Technology), respectively; and e is the error term.

Here, we selected six socioeconomic factors, namely, population (P), GDP per capita (A1), dietary choice (A2), urbanization rate (T1), NUE (T2), and planting structure (T3), and analyzed their impacts on Ngr emissions from croplands. Dietary choice was the ratio of animal food to total food consumed. NUE was the nitrogen partial factor productivity. The planting structure was the ratio of the sown area of grain crops to the total sown area of farm crops. We extended Eqs. 2 to 3 by adding these factors. We conducted ordinary least squares regression to evaluate the effects of individual factors. We evaluated the multicollinearity of these six factors via the variance inflation factor (VIF). As shown in SI Appendix, Table S6, the population, GDP per capita, dietary choice, and urbanization rate yield a VIF over 10, a problem known as multicollinearity that can lead to unreliable estimated regression coefficients (112). To account for the multicollinearity problem, we applied ridge regression to stabilize the regression coefficients.[3]lnI=a+blnP+c1lnA1+c2lnA2+d1lnT1+d2lnT2+d3lnT3+e.

Impact of climate change on Ngr and climate metrics.

The climate forcings (CFs) were calculated as follows:[4]CF=Em×GWPt,norGTP100,

where Em is the Ngr emissions, m is the form of Ngr (NH3, NOx, or N2O), t is the 20-y or 100-y timescale, n is one of the four CFs (CF1-CF4) of Ngr emissions, GWPt,n is the global warming potential (GWP) for kg CO2e per kg N at t years, and GTP100 is the global temperature potential (GTP) for kg CO2e per kg N at 100 y. GWP20, GWP100, and GTP100 represent short-, medium-, and long-term climate impacts, respectively. To quantify the radiative effects of NH3 (CF2) or NOx (CF3) on aerosols and NOx on ozone and CH4 (CF4) per unit of emissions in a simple and robust framework, we summarized these effects from the literature or derived the GWP20, GWP100, and GTP100 for NH3 and NOx. The climate change impacts are calculated as the product of emissions and GWP20, GWP100, and GTP100.

The changes in CH4 and CO2 fluxes for the effects of CF5 and CF6 were estimated as follows:[5]CF5i=D×EFDi×GWPt,iorGTP100i,[6]CF6i=AD×EFAi×GWPt,iorGTP100i,

where i is the greenhouse gas derived from N deposition or agricultural N fertilizers (CH4 or CO2); D is the N deposition of Ngr emissions to croplands (kg N); EFDi is the flux factor greenhouse gas i (CH4, kg CH4 kg−1 N−1; or CO2, kg CO2 kg−1 N−1) derived from N deposition; GWPt,i is the GWP for kg CO2e per kg i at t years; GTP100i is the GTP for kg CO2e per kg i at 100 years; AD is the agricultural N fertilizer application amount by croplands (kg N); and EFAi is the flux factor greenhouse gas i (CH4, kg CH4 kg−1 N−1; or CO2, kg CO2 kg−1 N−1) derived from agricultural N fertilizers. The details of this process can be found in Cavalett and Cherubini (37), Pinder et al. (26), and Xu et al. (27). The ranges of the GWP and GTP values based on reported values are provided in SI Appendix, Table S3. In this study, CO2 uptake factors from N deposition and agricultural fertilizer application were from Shi et al. (113) (−1.08, −1.71 to −0.45) and Pinder et al. (26) (−0.52, −0.53 to −0.51) kg C-CO2·(kg N)−1, respectively; both CH4 EFs were from Liu and Greaver (114) and Pinder et al. (26) (0.02, 0.01 to 0.03) kg C-CH4·(kg N)−1.

Ngr related to climate forcing has different radiative forcing intensities and atmospheric lifetimes (26). Metric choice can affect the relative emphasis placed on reducing long-lived N2O versus short-lived NH3 and NOx (115). No single metric can fully and simultaneously assess the impacts of different climate forcings on different aspects of climate change (37). The GWP index (116) was defined to measure the time-integrated global mean radiative forcing of a pulse emission of a specific compound relative to that of CO2. The GTP index (117) was defined as the change in global mean surface temperature at a chosen point in time in response to an emission pulse-relative to that of CO2. Following recent guidelines from the United Nations Environment Programme–Society of Environmental Toxicology and Chemistry Life-Cycle Initiative (118), we used the climate metrics GWP of CO2e at 20- and 100-y timescales (GWP20 and GWP100, respectively, targeting the effects of the rate of climate change) and the GTP of CO2e at a 100-y timescale (GTP100, targeting the potential temperature increase 100 y into the future) (32, 37). These three complementary climate metrics represent short-, medium-, and long-term climate impacts. Ngr emissions were aggregated into CO2e emissions on the basis of different emission metrics without requiring climate models, which do not differentiate climate forcers; rather, the short- and long-term impacts of CO2 are account for (119). Moreover, we acknowledge that these emission metric selections have certain limitations. We chose the median and 90% CI of the GWP and GTP values as uncertainty ranges. The ranges of GWP and GTP values were based on the results of multimodel simulations from previous studies (SI Appendix, Table S7) (26, 34, 105, 120–123). Owing to insufficient data availability, the indirect cooling effects of aerosols on clouds were not included in our analysis (55). Including this effect would likely increase the impact of NH3 and NOx on aerosols and cause a more negative GWP20 for climate effects CF2 and CF3. There would be a negligible effect on the GTP100 (26). Additional details on the data sources are available in the Supplementary Information.

To calculate the CO2 and CH4 flux effects derived from N deposition in natural systems for 1978, 2017 and 2023, we calculated the N deposition rate for croplands by multiplying the N deposition rate by the area of croplands. Atmospheric wet and dry N deposition rates were obtained from national-scale spatiotemporal datasets (124–127). The contribution proportion of Ngr deposition to the total bulk N deposition was assumed to be the contribution proportion of Ngr emissions in total Nr emissions. We used the global Ngr emissions from EDGAR v8.1 (9) to calculate China’s relative contribution to the global climate impact from agricultural fertilizer application in 2017. The global fertilizer and manure N inputs and deposition in 2017 were derived from FAOSTAT (2).

Damage Costs and Benefits of Ngr Emissions.

The damage costs of Ngr emissions in this study are defined as the sum of damage costs for ecosystems, human health, and climate impacts (79). Ecosystem damage costs in this study are regarded as the damage costs of soil acidification and water eutrophication caused by NH3 and NOx emissions. We assume that the unit NH3 and NOx damage costs to the ecosystem in the European Nitrogen Assessment can be used in China after correction for differences in the willingness to pay and purchasing power parity between China and Europe (66, 128). For human health damage costs, we evaluated the respiratory and cardiac damage induced by NH3 and NOx through PM2.5 and skin cancer and cataracts induced by N2O stratospheric O3 depletion. More detailed descriptions of the health damage attributed to PM2.5 and O3 can be found in the World Bank report and on the GBD website (http://ghdx.healthdata.org/). The measurements of human health damage costs for the exposures per unit Ngr emissions were derived according to the VSL, which is the sum of what people would pay to reduce their mortality risks by small amounts that together add up to one statistical life (79). We used the VSLs of air pollution mortality in China (0.17 US$ million) and Europe (1.36 US$ million) to translate the exposures per unit of Ngr emissions to a final health damage cost from Gu et al. (129), which connected the economic costs of mortality per unit of Ngr emissions with population density and GDP per capita. We considered the different monetary climate effects of N2O (warming) and NH3 and NOx (cooling induced by increasing light-scattering aerosols, indicating an economic benefit) (55). We quantified and monetized the total damage costs and benefits of Ngr emissions caused by agricultural fertilizer application in China. In this study, we used published coefficients to concentrate on three end points assumed to make up the greatest fraction of Ngr damage costs and benefits (SI Appendix, Table S8). The damage costs per unit of Ngr species emissions are constant throughout the country owing to the limited number of studies in this dataset (48).
