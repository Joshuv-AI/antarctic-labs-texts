Revealing improvement mechanism of yak meat quality by dietary with Codonopsis pilosula stems and leaves based on transcriptomics and metabolomics
Authors: Xue Yang, Yiheng Li, Ruheng Shen, Yufeng Duan, Rende Song, Hongmei Shi, Xiangying Kong, Yongli Hua, Wangang Zhang, Li Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe stems and leaves of Codonopsis pilosula (CPSL) are commonly treated as agricultural waste, yet they are rich in antioxidants and other bioactive compounds. In order to explore their applicability in livestock feeding, a systematic evaluation was conducted on the dose-dependent effects of CPSL on yak production traits and meat characteristics. A total of forty yaks were randomly allocated into four experimental groups, including a control group fed a basal diet and three treatment groups receiving the basal diet supplemented with 0.5%, 1.0%, or 2.0% CPSL. Results indicated that the 2.0% CPSL supplementation group exhibited the highest net meat yield while significantly improving meat processing suitability. Through transcriptomic profiling, 13 genes were found to be differentially expressed between the control and CPSL-treated groups, with functions related to myofiber formation and energy metabolic processes. A marked upregulation of insulin-sensitizing gene 1 (INSIG1) and arginase 2 (ARG2) was observed specifically in the group receiving the intermediate CPSL dose. Metabolomic profiling further revealed that 81 shared differentially abundant metabolites were primarily involved in the AMPK signaling and oxidative phosphorylation pathways. Collectively, CPSL not only fortifies yak beef production by optimizing growth and slaughter performance but also significantly enhances meat tenderness and processing adaptability through regulating core mechanisms governing redox hubs and energy metabolism. These findings offer novel insights into the high-value utilization of CPSL as a feed additive.Keywords: Transcriptomics, Untargeted metabolomics, By-product, Yak, Meat quality

Introduction

The yak (Bos grunniens), a ruminant native to the Tibetan Plateau, is a crucial element of the local livestock economy. Its meat products, valued for being high – in - protein and low - in - fat, are highly popular among consumers (Jing et al., 2022). However; the extreme environment of the plateau (low oxygen; low temperature; and seasonal shortage of pasture) leads to a long growth cycle of yaks; delayed muscle development; and generally high shear force and lack of tenderness (Yang et al., 2024). The global stock of yaks is about 17 million; 90% of which are located in the Tibetan Plateau of China; but the improvement of their meat quality traits still faces challenges such as the traditional grazing mode is difficult to meet the demand of intensive production (Li et al., 2024); the conventional nutritional control means (e.g.; supplemental feeding with concentrates) are costly and easy to destroy the ecological balance; and the metabolic adaptation mechanisms of plateau endemic species have not been fully analyzed (Yi et al., 2023). Therefore, the development of environmentally friendly feed additives and the clarification of their mechanism of action are key breakthroughs in improving yak meat quality.

Globally, about 5 billion tons of agricultural wastes are generated every year, and their improper disposal leads to waste of resources and environmental pollution (Sonu et al., 2023). Codonopsis pilosula (C. pilosula); a plant with both medicinal and food – use properties; generates by - products like stems; leaves; and fibrous roots during cultivation. These by - products constitute around 30–40% of the biomass and are replete with functional constituents such as polysaccharides; saponins; and amino acids (Yuan et al., 2024). Studies have shown that C. pilosula extracts can improve livestock and poultry performance by regulating intestinal flora and enhancing antioxidant capacity (Guo et al., 2024). However; the utilization of agricultural waste from C. pilosula is currently largely confined to composting or fuel applications; with its potential value in regulating ruminant growth performance; slaughter characteristics; and meat quality yet to be fully explored. Moreover; an increasing number of agricultural by-products are being utilized as supplements in animal diets; enhancing production performance while simultaneously improving meat quality (Zhao et al., 2024; Musati et al., 2025). Converting such wastes into dietary supplement base can not only reduce the cost of farming, but also realize the “waste to treasure” model of recycling agriculture, which is in line with the principles of responsible consumption and production in the Global Sustainable Development Goals.

The formation of muscle tenderness, water holding capacity, and other edible qualities is a result of a combination of post-slaughter energy metabolism, protease systems, and cytoskeletal degradation (Wang, Guo, et al., 2024; Zou et al., 2023). In recent research, it has been revealed that the AMPK signaling pathway affects the rate of pH decline by regulating the rate of glycolysis, while abnormal mitochondrial function may lead to an imbalance in calcium homeostasis and activation of the calpain protease system (Bai et al., 2023). Transcriptomic analyses demonstrated that the expression levels of genes associated with myofiber types; including Myosin-1 (MYH1) and Troponin T3 (TNNT3); exhibited a significant negative correlation with shear force (Chardulo et al., 2019; Malheiros et al., 2019). Meanwhile, metabolomic studies indicated that arginine metabolism contributes to vasodilation and energy supply via the NO - cGMP pathway (Hosseini et al., 2025). Yet, most current research focuses solely on the single-tissue level, lacking analyses of gene-metabolite interaction networks that integrate mechanisms regulating growth performance, slaughter performance, and meat quality. This deficiency is particularly pronounced in high-altitude ruminants.

Based on the above background, the active constituents in CPSL may not only regulate growth performance and slaughter characteristics by modulating mitochondrial energy metabolism and myofibre development pathways, but also synergistically improve postmortem meat quality in yak. The aims of this study were (1) to systematically evaluate, for the first time, the dose-response effects of adding different doses of CPSL to the diets of plateau yak on their growth performance, slaughter performance, and postmortem meat quality; (2) to utilize multi-omics (transcriptome, metabolome) technologies to decipher the cascade regulatory pathways linking feed – genes - metabolites - phenotypes; (3) to focus on cross-scale interactions between key genes (e.g., ARG2) and arginine metabolism, providing theoretical foundations for targeted nutritional interventions that simultaneously optimize growth, slaughter performance, and meat quality. This study combines transcriptomics and metabolomics techniques to compare mRNA and metabolite profiles in the diets of highland yak control and CPSL-supplemented groups. By identifying potential gene-metabolite regulatory mechanisms linked to growth, slaughter, and meat quality traits, it aims to provide valuable references for optimizing highland yak dietary formulations, enhancing nutritional regulation, and producing high-quality meat products.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

All animal-related procedures in this study, from feeding to slaughter, were carried out in accordance with ethical standards and received formal approval from the Animal Ethics Committee of Gansu Agricultural University (Reference No. 2012–2-159). The slaughter process strictly adhered to the Chinese national welfare requirements for slaughtered animals (GB/T 42304–2023).

Collection of animals and samples

For this experiment, 40 healthy male yaks (aged approximately 3 years; body weight 200 ± 50 kg) were sourced from a cooperative in Menyuan County, Qinghai Province (Huangcheng Old Shepherd Ecological Animal Husbandry Professional Co-operative Society). The animals were randomly divided into four groups of ten, consisting of a control group fed a basal diet and three treatment groups receiving the basal diet enriched with 0.5%, 1.0%, or 2.0% CPSL. Throughout the study, yaks were kept in individual pens (7.0 m × 5.5 m × 2.55 m) with controlled feeding, and no notable variation in feed consumption was detected among the groups. The stems and leaves of C. pilosula were collected in late August in Dingxi City, Gansu Province, during the field pruning stage (to promote root growth). The materials were shade-dried, then ground into powder and stored under dry, light-protected conditions until use. Dietary formulations are detailed in Table 1, with the basal ration meeting the nutritional guidelines set by China's Ministry of Agriculture (NY/T 815–2004). The experiment comprised a 7-day acclimatization phase followed by an 83-day main trial, throughout which yaks received feed twice daily (08:00 and 18:00) with unrestricted water access. Upon completion of the feeding period, six individuals from each group were randomly chosen and transferred to the slaughterhouse for processing. Before slaughter, food was withheld for 24 h and water for 12 h. Animals were stunned electrically and bled within 60 s to minimize handling stress. Within 20 min of exsanguination, longissimus thoracis et lumborum (LTL) samples were excised from the left side of each carcass. Following the removal of superficial fascia and adipose tissue, fresh specimens were subjected to immediate quality evaluation. The residual muscle tissue was flash-frozen in liquid nitrogen and preserved at −80 °C pending further analysis.

Determination of meat quality

The LTL samples of slaughtered yaks were taken, and pH 45 min, cooking loss, shear force and meat color were analyzed following the methodology outlined in Yang et al. (2024).

Determination of pH value

A portable pH meter (model PHS2–25, Shanghai, China) was employed for pH measurements. To prepare the sample, the muscle surface was first cleansed of blood stains using distilled water, then carefully dried with filter paper. The electrode was subsequently inserted into the tissue to a depth of 2–3 cm, and stable readings were obtained after 1–2 min. Each sample was measured at three distinct sites, and the arithmetic mean was adopted as the final pH value.

Determination of cooking loss

A meat sample of about 100 ± 20 g was selected for accurate weighing, the temperature of the water bath was set to 80 °C, the sample was placed in it, heated to a center temperature of 75 °C (Dynamic monitoring of the center temperature of meat samples using an insertion thermometer (BG367, Zhengzhou, China)) Following heat treatment, the sample was retrieved, chilled to ambient temperature at 4 °C, and weighed again. The cooking loss percentage was derived from the weight difference before and after cooking.

Determination of shear force

Shear force was assessed on cooked samples (from Section 2.4.2) after cooling. Cylindrical cores (1.27 cm diameter, ≥2.5 cm length) were taken parallel to muscle fibers. Using a muscle tenderness meter (C-LM4, Harbin, China), each core was sheared perpendicular to fiber orientation, and the peak force was recorded as shear force.

Determination of chroma value

Referring to the method of Yang et al. (2025). Meat color (L*, a*, b*) was measured with a CR-400 chromameter (Osaka, Japan; D65 illuminant, 8 mm aperture, 2° observer). Samples were bloomed at room temperature for 30 min before measurement. The probe was placed vertically against the muscle surface, and readings were taken at three random sites per sample. The average of the three measurements was used for analysis.

Determination of nutrients

Crude protein, fat, moisture, and ash contents of yak LTL samples were determined following Chinese national standards: Dumas combustion (GB 5009.5–2016) for protein, Soxhlet extraction (GB 5009.6–2016) for fat, direct drying (GB/T 5009.3–2010) for moisture, and dry ashing (GB 5009.4–2016) for ash. Feed nutrient composition was analyzed according to GB/T 6432–2018, GB/T 6436–2018, GB/T 6437–2018, GB/T 20806–2022 and NY/T 1459–2022. The nutritional components of CPSL were determined in accordance with GB/T 6433–2025, GB/T 6432–2018, GB/T 6434–2022, GB/T 20806–2022, NY/T 1459–2022, GB/T 6438–2007, GB/T 6436–2018, GB/T 6437–2018 and GB/T 6435–2014.

Transcriptome analysis (RNA-seq data analysis)

Total RNA was extracted from liquid nitrogen-ground yak LTL samples using TRIzol (manufacturer's protocol). RNA quantity and purity were assessed with a NanoDrop 2000 (Thermo Scientific, USA), and integrity was checked on an Agilent 2100 Bioanalyzer (Agilent Technologies, USA). Libraries were prepared using the VAHTS Universal V6 RNA-seq Kit and sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (150 bp paired-end) by Shanghai Ouyi Biotechnology Co., Ltd. Raw reads (39.45–49.62 million per sample) were cleaned with fastp, aligned to the reference genome using HISAT2, and quantified as FPKM with HTSeq-count.

LC-MS/MS metabolomic analysis

LTL samples (30 mg) were extracted with 400 μL methanol:water (4:1) containing 4 μg/mL L-2-chlorophenylalanine (internal standard) using a TissueLyser II (Qiagen, Germany). After pre-cooling (−40 °C, 2 min), grinding (45 Hz, 2 min), sonication (ice bath, 10 min), overnight incubation (−40 °C), and centrifugation (12,000 rpm, 4 °C, 20 min), 150 μL supernatant was collected. Separation used an ACQUITY UPLC HSS T3 column (100 × 2.1 mm, 1.8 μm) at 45 °C with 0.35 mL/min flow (A: 0.1% formic acid in water; B: acetonitrile). Gradient: 0–2 min, 5% B; 2–4 min, 5–30% B; 4–8 min, 30% B; 8–10 min, 30–50% B; 10–14 min, 50–80% B; 14–15.1 min, 80–100% B; 15.1–16 min, 5% B. Injection volume: 5 μL. MS analysis (m/z 70–1050) was performed in positive/negative modes (spray voltages: 3800 V/3000 V). Key settings: capillary temp 320 °C, aux gas heater 350 °C, sheath gas 35 Arb, aux gas 8 Arb, S-lens 50, full MS resolution 60,000, MS/MS resolution 15,000, NCE 10/20/40. Data were processed with Progenesis QI v3.0 (Nonlinear Dynamics, UK) with precursor tolerance 5 ppm (HMDB/LipidMaps)/10 ppm (LuMet-Animal/METLIN) and product tolerance 10 ppm/20 ppm.

2.8 Determination of insulin-induced gene 1 protein (INSIG1) and arginase 2(ARG2) levels

INSIG1 and ARG2 protein levels in yak LTL were quantified using sandwich ELISA kits. Tissue homogenates in physiological saline were centrifuged (3000 g, 10 min, 4 °C), and supernatants were collected. Samples and standards were added to antibody-precoated wells, incubated with HRP-conjugated detection antibodies, and washed. TMB substrate was added, and after color development, the reaction was stopped with acid. Absorbance at 450 nm was measured, and concentrations were calculated from standard curves.

2.9. Statistical analysis

Comparisons of meat quality traits (pH, cooking loss, shear force, and color parameters) among treatment groups were conducted via one-way ANOVA in SPSS (SPSS Inc., Chicago, IL, USA). Before analysis, we verified normality with the Shapiro–Wilk test and homogeneity of variances with Levene's test; all data satisfied these assumptions. Results are expressed as mean ± SEM, and we considered P < 0.05 statistically significant. For transcriptomic data, we identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) using DESeq2 with thresholds of P < 0.05 and fold-change >1. We then performed enrichment analysis of DEGs against GO and KEGG pathways based on the hypergeometric distribution. We generated bar charts and circle plots of enrichment results using R (version 3.2.0). To integrate multi-omics data, we applied KEGG pathway enrichment modeling to identify pathways co-enriched with both DEGs and differential metabolites (DMs). We constructed gene–metabolite correlation networks to reveal regulatory patterns and visualized these relationships using the Ouyi Online Cloud tool.

Results

Effects of supplemental feeding of CPSL on yak growth performance and slaughter performance

Table 2 summarizes the production performance of yaks subjected to different levels of CPSL supplementation. Average daily weight gain was highest in the high-dose group (0.81 ± 0.07), lowest in the control group (0.38 ± 0.07), with the low- and medium-dose groups exhibiting intermediate levels showing no significant difference from the high-dose or control groups. Feed conversion ratio was markedly reduced across all treatment groups relative to the control: 9.98 ± 0.43 (0.5% CPSL), 7.86 ± 0.52 (1.0% CPSL), and 8.02 ± 0.56 (2.0% CPSL) versus 10.17 ± 0.66 for the control, indicating that CPSL supplementation enhances feed efficiency. Among meat production metrics, meat yield showed no significant differences between groups, maintaining consistently favorable levels. Net meat yield was highest in the high-dose group, showing a 3.85% increase compared to the control group. The low- and medium-dose groups exhibited intermediate values without significant differences from the high-dose or control groups. Overall, 2.0% CPSL supplementation proved most effective in improving final body weight, ADG, feed efficiency, and net meat yield. Positive effects on some growth traits were also observed in the 0.5% and 1.0% groups.

Effects of supplemental feeding of CPSL on yak meat quality

As presented in Table 2, CPSL supplementation significantly affected yak meat quality. All treatment groups showed delayed and more balanced post-slaughter pH compared to the control (P < 0.05). As we expected, supplementation of 2.0%, 1.0% and 0.5% CPSL reduced the shear force values by 11.34%, 7.2% and 3.25%, respectively, compared to the control group. Meanwhile, this supplementation measure significantly increased the redness value of yak meat. Simultaneously, this supplementation notably enhanced the redness value of yak meat. Moreover, when considering processing suitability, the cooking loss rate of the high - dose group was markedly lower than that of the other treatment groups (P < 0.05).

The chemical composition of yak meat fed with different CPSL is shown in Table 2, and the results showed that supplementation of CPSL in the diet could increase the crude fat content of yak meat, especially when the addition amount reached 2.0%, the crude fat content was significantly increased by 21.30% compared with the control group. In addition, the ash content of yak meat decreased with the addition of CPSL, and there was no difference in moisture and crude protein content between the groups (P > 0.05).

GO analysis categorized DEG functions into biological process (BP), cellular component (CC), and molecular function (MF). For LDG vs Con, the most abundant terms were xenobiotic metabolic process (BP, 17 genes), plasma membrane (CC, 248 genes), and calcium ion binding (MF, 50 genes), while the most significant were cAMP transport (BP), plasma membrane (CC), and 15-hydroxyprostaglandin dehydrogenase activity (MF) (Fig. 2A). In MDG vs Con, signal transduction (BP, 70 genes), myofibril (CC, 12 genes), and calcium ion binding (MF, 74 genes) had the highest gene counts, but muscle contraction (BP), myosin II complex (CC), and structural constituent of muscle (MF) showed the greatest significance (Fig. 2B). For HDG vs Con, top terms by gene count included positive regulation of ERK1/ERK2 cascade (BP, 30 genes), receptor complex (CC, 39 genes), and calcium ion binding (MF, 128 genes), whereas skeletal system development (BP), myosin II complex (CC), and calcium ion binding (MF) were most significantly enriched (Fig. 2C). KEGG enrichment analysis of DEGs

Comprehensive KEGG enrichment analysis of the identified differentially expressed genes (DEGs) revealed their involvement in diverse biological pathways (Fig. 2G). These pathways spanned cellular processes, metabolism, human diseases, organismal systems, environmental information processing, and genetic information processing. Among these, the top five enriched pathways included cardiovascular disease, signal transduction, signaling molecule interactions, endocrine system regulation, and cellular motility. Further analysis of all DEGs highlighted the top 10 enriched pathways. KEGG pathway analysis revealed distinct enrichment patterns across different CPSL supplementation levels (Fig. 2H–2L). Compared to the control, the low-dose group showed significant enrichment in cAMP, IL-17, PPAR, and HIF-1 signaling pathways (Fig. 2H). The medium-dose group exhibited enrichment in PI3K-Akt, Apelin, Calcium, and MAPK signaling pathways, as well as regulation of actin cytoskeleton (Fig. 2I). For the high-dose group, enriched pathways included metabolism of xenobiotics by cytochrome P450, cAMP signaling, and Calcium signaling (Fig. 2J). Pairwise comparisons between treatment groups also yielded specific enrichment patterns. MDG vs LDG showed enrichment in cAMP signaling, Apelin signaling, and glutathione metabolism (Fig. 2K). HDG vs LDG was characterized by enrichment in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, PI3K-Akt signaling, p53 signaling, arginine and proline metabolism, and pyruvate metabolism (Fig. 2L). HDG vs MDG was enriched in glycolysis/gluconeogenesis, arginine and proline metabolism, pyruvate metabolism, and glycine, serine and threonine metabolism (Fig. 2M).

Differential metabolite analysis

Untargeted metabolomic analysis identified 1995 metabolites in yak LTL samples. The robustness of the analytical platform was validated through QC sample analysis: total ion chromatograms from four consecutive injections showed near-perfect overlap in both ionization modes (Fig. S1A, S1B), and QC samples exhibited strong correlation (Fig. S1C). PCA further demonstrated the stability of the system, with QC samples clustering tightly at the origin of the PC1–PC2 score plot (Fig. S1D), confirming data reliability. Metabolites were classified into 10 distinct categories based on the LC-MS/MS platform and an in-house database (Fig. 3A). The most represented classes were amino acids, peptides and analogs (16.09%), followed by fatty acids and fatty acid conjugates (6.02%) and fatty acid esters (5.26%). The PCA plots are shown in Fig. 3B. The total cumulative variance of the first two principal components was 70.5%, with PC1 at 54.9% and PC2 at 15.6%. Subsequent OPLS-DA analysis was performed to maximize class separation, with the cumulative variance explained by the predictive and orthogonal components (t1 and to1) ranging from 58.50% to 83.72% across pairwise comparisons. The validity of these models was assessed by permutation testing, which confirmed the absence of overfitting and supported the robustness of the PCA-based classification (Fig. S2). The samples of the control group and the CPSL feeding group were distributed in different areas. The high dose group and the other treatment groups were far away from each other, while it is noteworthy that the samples of the low and medium dose treatment groups were clustered together, which in a way indicates that the concentration gradient we set up has a significant effect on the metabolites of the meat, and there is a significant dose effect. Based on the histogram and Venn diagram analysis, 568, 577, 606, 553, 645 and 624 compounds were shared in LDG vs Con, MDG vs Con, HDG vs Con, MDG vs LDG, HDG vs LDG and HDG vs MDG, respectively (Fig. 3C). The dynamic evolution landscape of differential metabolites between groups is visualized by volcano plots (Fig. 3D-3I). Based on this, the Venn plot visualized the uniqueness or commonality of differential metabolites between the comparison groups, with 81 differential metabolites in the petal center being the key differential metabolites shared between the groups (Fig. 3J). Subsequently, intergroup differences in the levels of these key metabolites were presented by hierarchical clustered heat maps (Fig. 3K). Results showed the number of compounds with the highest expression with increasing supplemental feeding dose as 16, 2, 3, and 60 corresponding to the control, low-dose, medium-dose, and high-dose groups, respectively.

KEGG enrichment analysis of differential metabolites

An overall KEGG enrichment analysis was carried out on the identified metabolites (Fig. 4A). This analysis demonstrated that these metabolites were enriched in pathways related to cellular processes, metabolism, human diseases, organismal systems, environmental information processing, and genetic information processing. Among typical metabolic pathways, amino acid metabolism, carbohydrate metabolism, the endocrine system, metabolism of other amino acids, and signal transduction were the top five pathways with the highest number of metabolite - enriched pathways. KEGG enrichment analysis was applied to all differential metabolites, and the top 10 enriched pathways were chosen. Integrative analysis of the transcriptome and metabolome

Transcriptomics technology enables a panoramic view of gene expression dynamics in specific tissues through high-resolution sequencing, while metabolomics analysis systematically elucidates metabolic flux homeostasis imbalance and molecular network reconstruction in biological samples. A cross-omics integration strategy can be used to precisely analyze the multilevel regulatory hubs of phenotypic heterogeneity, and then construct a molecular interplay framework linking transcriptional reprogramming and metabolic adaptations. In this study, DEGs and DEMs of yak meat supplemented with different doses of CPSL were analyzed and enriched into KEGG pathway. Of interest, 97 important pathways between HDG and Con groups were identified for detailed study. Among them, 10 KEGG pathways were closely related to meat development, including Taurine and hypotaurine metabolism, Glycolysis / Gluconeogenesis, Glycine, serine and threonine metabolism, Regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, and Pyruvate metabolism (Fig. S2A). Meanwhile, 98 important pathways between MDG and Con groups were identified for detailed study (Fig. S2B). Among them, nine KEGG pathways were closely related to sarcomere development, including Arginine and proline metabolism, Calcium signaling pathway, Protein digestion and absorption, Regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes, and Glutathione metabolism. In addition, differential genes and metabolites in the HDG and LDG groups, the HDG and MDG groups, and the LDG and Con groups were co-constitutive in pathways associated with the cAMP signaling pathway, ABC transporters, and Protein digestion and absorption.

In this study, the orthogonal Two-way Partial Least Squares (O2PLS) method (Fig. S1) was used for systematic analysis, which revealed the potential regulatory pathways between the metabolome and the transcriptome data matrices by efficiently identifying the correlation variables between the two. The analysis results indicated substantial correlations between gene expression and metabolite levels within each group. Moreover, the distribution of the first two principal component loadings of the O2PLS model further substantiated the aforementioned correlation characteristics. Next, using a nine-quadrant plot (Fig. 5A-F) visualized the relationship between DEGs and DEMs of slaughtered yak meat under supplementing different doses of CPSL. The multiplicity of material differences with correlation coefficients greater than 0.8 and P-values less than 0.05 were demonstrated, indicating a positive correlation between specific genes and metabolites associated with meat quality. Furthermore, the data of two omics were analyzed for association, and the most relevant gene-metabolite relationship pairs were screened according to the intensity and direction of change, and mined and constructed a correlation network of differential metabolites/genes with the enriched KEGG pathway (Fig. 5 G-M). Glutathione S-transferase alpha-4 showed excellent correlation with Octanoic acid, Gly-Trp and NADH metabolites between HDG and Con groups. Dehydrogenase/reductase SDR family member 13 showed excellent correlation with metabolites such as Leu-Glu-Leu, Phenol glucuronide and Hydroxypropionylcarnitine between HDG and LDG groups. Between HDG and MDG groups Histone H2A type 1-C showed excellent correlation with metabolites such as 2-(3-Hydroxyphenyl) ethanol 1′-glucoside, Epigallocatechin 3-O-cinnamate and Octanoic acid. Between MDG and Con groups Selenocysteine-specific elongation factor showed excellent correlation with (10Z)-7-Hydroxyhexadecenoylcarnitine, N1-(2-Methoxy-4-methylbenzyl)-n2-(2-(5- methylpyridin-2-yl) ethyl) oxalamide and N-Acetylneuraminic acid metabolites showed excellent correlation. Between LDG and Con groups Diacylglycerol kinase beta showed excellent correlation with metabolites such as Acetyl-l-lysine, N-Acetylneuraminic acid and Succinoadenosine. Between MDG and LDG groups ATP-binding cassette sub-family C member 4 showed excellent correlation with metabolites such as 2-(p-Acetamidophenyl)-2-ethylglutarimide, Methyl-N, N-di ethylthiocarbamate and N-(p-Acetamidophenyl)-3-ethylglutarimide. Discussion

In this study, the effects of supplementing different doses of CPSL on yak growth performance and slaughter characteristics demonstrated a pronounced dose-response relationship and biological significance. Regarding growth performance, inter-group differences in initial body weight reflected baseline variations among individuals at the experiment's outset. However, changes in final body weight and average daily gain more markedly highlighted the regulatory role of supplementation. Daily average weight gain was optimal in the high-dose group, exceeding the control group by over 100%. This metabolic optimization may be driven by CPSL-derived polysaccharides and saponins, which have been reported to act as AMPK activators and enhance mitochondrial function in other models (Zeng et al., 2022). Their presence likely potentiated the activation of arginine biosynthesis and AMPK signaling; redirecting energy towards anabolism (Liu et al., 2023). Furthermore; as a precursor for nitric oxide synthesis and a nutritional signaling molecule; arginine promotes muscle protein synthesis and cell proliferation; thereby accelerating weight gain (Ding et al., 2016). Regarding feed conversion efficiency, the control group exhibited a significantly higher feed-to-weight ratio than all supplementation groups. This indicates that CPSL enhances the ‘feed-to-weight’ conversion efficiency by regulating glucose and lipid metabolism (e.g., through enhanced fatty acid β-oxidation mediated by the PPAR pathway), thereby reducing energy loss from feed to non-productive metabolic pathways. The medium-dose group exhibited the lowest feed-to-weight ratio, suggesting optimal feed utilization economics at this dosage. This provides practical guidance for balancing growth rate and feed costs in commercial production. Regarding slaughter performance, no significant differences in dressing percentage were observed across groups, though a trend towards slightly higher values in the supplemented groups may have failed to reach statistical significance due to sample size or individual variation. The differences in net meat yield were more indicative, with the high-dose group exhibiting a significantly higher net meat yield than the control group. Combined with the significant enrichment of ADIPOQ in the transcriptome of the high-dose group (involved in adipocyte differentiation and lipid metabolism), it is hypothesized that CPSL may regulate the ratio of intramuscular fat to connective tissue, optimizing both the quality and quantity of muscle development, ultimately enhancing effective meat yield. In summary, CPSL supplementation not only promotes yak growth and improves feed efficiency by regulating energy metabolism and amino acid biosynthesis, but also optimizes post-slaughter meat yield. Different dosages exhibit distinct advantages in growth rate, feed economy, and lean meat percentage, providing technical justification and practical guidance for integrating ‘agricultural waste resource utilisation (Codonopsis pilosula stems and leaves)’ with ‘targeted meat quality regulation’ in intensive yak farming on the plateau.

The observed dose-dependent improvements likely stem from the synergistic actions of bioactive compounds within CPSL. Codonopsis pilosula is renowned for its richness in functional constituents, particularly polysaccharides and saponins, which have documented anti-inflammatory, antioxidant, and metabolic-modulating properties (Yuan et al., 2022). These compounds are plausible primary agents driving the transcriptional and metabolic reprogramming observed in yak muscle.

The synergistic effects of genetic, environmental and nutritional factors are crucial in muscle development and meat quality formation in plateau yaks. In particular, the low oxygen environment of the plateau (>3000 m above sea level) poses unique challenges to yak physiology and metabolism. The energy metabolism pattern of yaks under hypoxic conditions is significantly altered by reduced mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation efficiency and increased accumulation of reactive oxygen species, which may lead to an increase in oxidative stress in muscle cells, thereby affecting water retention and tenderness of postmortem meat (Wang et al., 2023; Zhang, Jiao, et al., 2025). Studies have shown that nutritionally regulated basal diets can systematically improve tenderness, juiciness and antioxidant properties, while reducing the proportion of saturated fatty acids in muscle (Zhang, Ge, et al., 2024; Zhao et al., 2024). Recent studies highlight that supplementation of basal diets with herbs significantly improves antioxidant capacity of postmortem muscle tissues and cells while enhancing growth performance, which is a critical aspect of meat quality development (Liu et al., 2024; Zhang, Sun, et al., 2024). In addition, in order to improve the bioavailability of by-products, herbal dregs supplementation of ducks has also been carried out and found to positively affect the growth performance and meat quality of ducks (Suwignyo et al., 2022). Based on this, supplementation of the basal diet with agricultural waste-Panax C. pilosula offcuts is particularly important for the growth of ruminants and the development of postmortem meat quality.

Transcriptomics elucidated how CPSL supplementation modulates molecular pathways governing yak meat quality, identifying key genes such as AGR2 (Zhang & Zhang, 2024) heat shock protein family A (Hsp70) member 6 (HSPA6) (Xu et al., 2022); cell death activator (CIDEC) (Lu et al., 2025) and INSIG1 (Wang, Jia, et al., 2024) among others. It is noteworthy that existing studies have confirmed that Codonopsis pilosula and its active component tangshenoside I can ameliorate skeletal muscle atrophy by modulating the PI3K/Akt and SIRT1/PGC-1α pathways; suggesting that CPSL may be involved in fatty acid oxidation and thus influence the biochemical changes in postmortem muscle (Kim et al., 2022). These genes are closely related to glycolytic metabolism; energy metabolism and lipid metabolism. The final quality of yak meat quality is the result of muscle tissue biological processes involving an integrated network of genes related to the metabolic functions of muscle tissue (Huang et al., 2022).

RNA sequencing analysis of yak muscle tissues across four treatment groups revealed critical gene expression patterns linked to phenotypic traits. High-quality sequencing data (77.13 GB, Q30 > 95.94%) provided a robust foundation for functional annotation and bioinformatics exploration. Integrated KEGG pathway and bubble plot analyses identified 13 differentially expressed genes (HSPA6, Signal peptide peptidase-like 2C (SPPL2C), Homeobox protein (Hoxc9), INSIG1, Myelin P2 protein (PMP2), Serotrsnsferrin (TF), ARG2, CIDEC, 60S acidic ribosomal protein (RPLP2), Carboxypeptidase B (CPB1), Acyl-coenzyme A synthetase (ACSM1), Thrsp (Thyroid hormone-inducible hepatic protein), and ADIPOQ (Adiponectin)) were identified in this study by comprehensive analysis of KEGG and bubble map. Among them, AGR2, CIDEC and ADIPOQ were significantly enriched in Arginine biosynthesis, Regulation of lipolysis in adipocytes and PPAR signaling pathway, respectively. These three pathways together shape the multidimensional optimization effect of supplemental feeding of CPSL on yak meat quality by synergistically regulating amino acid metabolism, lipid dynamic homeostasis and energy allocation. The arginine biosynthesis pathway is known to exist in different feeding systems leading to differences in glycolipid metabolism and lipid metabolism and thus affecting muscle quality (Yang et al., 2022). In addition; the arginine biosynthesis pathway can reduce oxidative damage by activating Nrf2 pathway-induced antioxidant responses through upregulation of glutathione synthesis (Liang et al., 2018). In addition; it was found that ADIPOQ was enriched in the adipocyte lipolysis pathway and regulated adipocyte differentiation; fatty acid transport and fatty acid synthesis (Ma et al., 2021). Similarly; Zhang, Wu, et al. (2025) found that genes enriched in the PPAR signaling pathway for amino acid supplementation affecting meat quality. ADIPOQ promotes fatty acid conversion to energy rather than fat deposition by activating PPARα and upregulating mitochondrial β-oxidation-related genes (e.g.; carnitine palmitoyltransferase 1 A (CPT1A); Peroxisomal acyl-coenzyme A oxidase 1 (ACOX1)) (Mello et al., 2016). This energy metabolism preference not only delayed post-slaughter glycolytic stress; but also reduced protein denaturation by maintaining sarcoplasmic reticulum calcium pump (SERCA) activity (Nakatsu et al., 2024). This mechanism explains the higher meat yield and net meat percentage observed in the CPSL supplementary feeding group, as optimized energy allocation supports improved muscle development and postmortem degradation regulation.

This study demonstrated significant upregulation of the ARG2 gene and its associated metabolite N-acetylornithine in amino acid metabolism pathways within the high-dose treatment group. The significant upregulation of ARG2 and its metabolic product N-acetylornithine suggests a targeted enhancement of the urea cycle and arginine biosynthesis. Saponins identified in Codonopsis pilosula may regulate the AMPK/mTOR pathway (Cui et al., 2020; Daigo et al., 2013). We hypothesize that specific saponins in CPSL may act as signaling molecules that potentiate mitochondrial biogenesis and amino acid flux. This component-driven metabolic shift not only accelerated arginine synthesis but also contributed to redox homeostasis via the N-acetylornithine antioxidant activity. In this study, by detecting the ARG2 protein content in the LTL of yaks after slaughter following supplementation with different doses of CPSL, it was found that the ARG2 protein content increased with the increase in supplementation dose; however, the effect of the 0.5% supplementation dose was not significant (P > 0.05). As a key intermediate in the enzymatic conversion of L-glutamate to L-arginine, N-acetylornithine plays a critical role in arginine biosynthesis. Its elevated levels suggest enhanced flux through this pathway, potentially driving arginine-mediated regulation of postmortem meat quality traits (Majumdar et al., 2016). Up-regulation of ARG2 gene expression may drive dynamic homeostasis of arginine metabolites by enhancing ornithine-urea cycle flux in mitochondria (Chen et al., 2018). This gene-metabolite synergistic mode of regulation not only accelerated the de novo synthesis of arginine; but may also influence meat quality formation through AMPK signaling activation and myocyte redox homeostasis mechanisms (Lu et al., 2023). The accumulation of N-acetylornithine may reduce ATP depletion by inhibiting the opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (MPTP); which synergistically amplifies the ARG2-mediated elevation of the AMP/ATP ratio and amplifies the activity of the AMPK pathway (Zhang, Wei, et al., 2024). In addition; elevated levels of arginine; a precursor for NO synthesis; and the antioxidant properties of N-acetylornithine (via scavenging of hydroxyl radicals) form a superimposed effect and work together to maintain redox homeostasis in postmortem muscle (Sun et al., 2024). Interestingly; ACSM1; a key enzyme in acetyl coenzyme A synthesis; catalyzes the binding of medium-chain fatty acids (e.g.; acetic acid; propionic acid) to coenzyme A to produce acetyl coenzyme A; a process that directly connects carbohydrate catabolism to mitochondrial energy metabolism (Zhang, Zhang, et al., 2024). Notably; there is a metabolite crossover between ACSM1 and the arginine biosynthesis pathway. N-Acetylornithine as an intermediate in arginine synthesis Acetyl coenzyme A is both a product of ACSM1 and a donor of acetyl groups in the synthesis of N-acetylornithine. Adequate supply of acetyl coenzyme A in the HDG group may promote the production of N-acetylornithine; thereby accelerating the de novo synthesis of arginine. In addition; the ACSM1-driven TCA cycle reinforces the production of NADPH; a cofactor of glutathione reductase; which maintains redox homeostasis in myocytes (van der Sluis & Erasmus, 2016). In addition, this experiment detected INSIG1, a downstream apoptosis-related protein, in the LTL of yaks supplemented with different doses of CPSL. The results showed that the expression level of the INSIG1 protein in the high-dose group was significantly higher than that in the other treatment groups, while there was no significant difference between the control group and the low-dose group (P > 0.05) (Fig. S4). This mechanism, together with the arginine-NO axis, builds a two-layered network of oxidative stress defense, explaining the simultaneous improvement of meat color stability (a* value) and cooking loss (water holding capacity) in the supplemented group (Fig. 6).

Up-regulation of ARG2 gene expression may drive dynamic homeostasis of arginine metabolites by enhancing ornithine-urea cycle flux in mitochondria. This gene-metabolite synergistic regulation pattern not only accelerated the de novo synthesis of arginine, but may also affect meat quality through AMPK signaling activation and myocyte redox homeostasis mechanisms.

Multi-omics analysis integrating growth performance, slaughter performance, and meat quality data indicates that CPSL supplementation exerts a dose-dependent regulatory effect on post-slaughter meat quality in plateau yak. Key genes including carnitine palmitoyltransferase 2 (CPT2), P21-activated kinase 1 (PAK1), and FOXO6 regulate quality traits through pathways such as cAMP signaling, calcium homeostasis, glutathione metabolism, and protein digestion/absorption.

Phenotypically, the medium-dose group exhibited optimal comprehensive performance. Measures such as meat yield and net meat percentage showed no significant difference from the high-dose group, whilst exhibiting the lowest feed-to-meat ratio. This dose-dependent pattern aligns with molecular-level findings—the medium dose may have achieved an optimal equilibrium in activating pathways including arginine biosynthesis, PPAR signaling, and AMPK, without inducing metabolic overload. These findings establish a molecular framework for elucidating meat quality variations and provide actionable targets for pre-slaughter nutritional interventions to enhance meat quality in high-altitude yak populations. Furthermore, utilizing Codonopsis pilosula stems and leaves as a feed additive derived from agricultural waste not only improves yak production performance but also contributes to sustainable agricultural development through the resourceful utilization of agricultural residues. While this study establishes a strong link between CPSL supplementation and multi-omics remodeling, the precise compound-pathway-phenotype causality warrants further investigation. Future work should include the fractionation of CPSL extracts, purification of individual polysaccharides and saponins, and their direct validation in myocyte or animal models to pinpoint the exact agents responsible for activating the ARG2-AMPK-PPAR axis and improving meat quality.

Conclusion

This study reveals novel molecular mechanisms underlying the beneficial effects of CPSL supplementation on yak meat quality. CPSL, particularly at optimal doses, improved growth performance, slaughter traits, and postmortem meat quality attributes (color, tenderness, cooking loss, and processing suitability) that matter most to consumers. These effects were mediated by coordinated regulation of energy metabolism, glutathione homeostasis, and protein digestion pathways. Importantly, we identified arginine metabolism as a key player in this process. These improvements were driven by synergistic modulation of energy metabolism, glutathione homeostasis, and protein digestion pathways. Specifically, mitochondrial ARG2 upregulation and elevated arginine levels synergistically activated AMPK signaling and nitric oxide synthase - mediated redox balance, directly influencing postmortem meat quality. These findings advance our mechanistic understanding of how phytogenic dietary supplement base modulate meat quality at the molecular level. The results provide actionable insights for refining yak feeding regimens, emphasizing the potential of CPSL as a sustainable strategy to optimize meat production efficiency and improve key meat quality attributes.
