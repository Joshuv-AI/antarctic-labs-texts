Mechanical stress promotes excessive NETs and exacerbates acute lung injury via Piezo1-mediated mitochondrial dysfunction
Authors: Hangfei Jin, Yiwen Mei, Xi Gao, Wenwen Zhang, Heyue Li, Yitian Chen, Bingwei Sun
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractAcute lung injury (ALI) is a respiratory insufficiency syndrome precipitated by factors such as infection, sepsis, or systemic trauma. Excessive polymorphonuclear neutrophil (PMN) infiltration and activation represent the hallmark cellular features of early-stage ALI. However, it remains poorly understood how the pulmonary biomechanical microenvironment, remodeled by ALI-induced diffuse edema, decreased lung compliance, and mechanical ventilation, modulates PMN hyperactivation. Using a sequential model of LPS-induced lung injury and differential mechanical ventilation combined with an in vitro cell compression system, we demonstrate that PMN Piezo1 senses pathological physical strain and orchestrates pro-inflammatory responses. Single-cell RNA sequencing identified a mechanosensitive “mitochondrial-stress” PMN cluster that expands during ALI, defined by profound mitochondrial damage and elevated ROS production. Mechanistically, Piezo1 transduces mechanical stimuli into cytosolic Ca2+ overload, triggering a cascade of mitochondrial dysfunction, ROS overproduction, and mtDNA leakage, thereby driving excessive NETs and lung injury. Notably, pharmacological blockade of Piezo1 effectively suppresses NETs and alleviates ALI. Our study demonstrates that, alongside inflammatory cues, Piezo1-mediated mechanosensing is a fundamental mechanism driving pathological PMN overactivation, identifying it as a promising therapeutic target for ALI.Keywords: Acute lung injury, Piezo1, Neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), Mechanical stress, Single-cell RNA sequencing

Introduction

Acute lung injury (ALI) is a complex syndrome of respiratory insufficiency precipitated by diverse etiologies, such as pulmonary infection, sepsis, and systemic trauma. It clinically manifests as tachypnea, severe hypoxemia, and non-cardiogenic diffuse pulmonary edema [1,2]. Persistent clinical deterioration can culminate in acute respiratory distress syndrome (ARDS) [3]. Evidence indicates that the mortality rate for ARDS patients has remained stubbornly high, consistently exceeding 30% over the long term [4]. However, the underlying pathogenesis of ALI remains elusive, and targeted therapies are currently lacking. Clinical management still relies on supportive care, primarily mechanical ventilation, antibiotics, and glucocorticoids [5]. Therefore, an in-depth elucidation of the molecular mechanisms underlying ALI is imperative to decipher its pathophysiological evolution and to identify novel therapeutic entry points.

ALI transforms the lung from a uniform low-stress state into a highly heterogeneous biomechanical environment. This shift, which is characterized by diffuse edema, reduced compliance, and diminished ventilation area, generates significant stress concentrations [[6], [7], [8]]. When compounded by mechanical ventilation, these conditions subject plasma membranes to aberrant tension and strain, amplifying localized injury and coupling mechanical stimuli with the systemic inflammatory cascade [9]. While aberrant mechanical stress is a key driver of ALI exacerbation, the molecular mechanisms of its mechanotransduction remain to be elucidated [10]. Furthermore, identifying shared molecular pathways between pre-existing pathologies and mechanical ventilation-induced injury holds significant clinical value for the prevention and treatment of immune dysfunction and associated pulmonary diseases [11,12].

Mechanotransduction, the conversion of mechanical stress into biochemical signals, frequently triggers profound immune cell activation [13,14]. Notably, the excessive infiltration and activation of polymorphonuclear neutrophils (PMNs) have long been recognized as the hallmark cytological features of early-stage ALI [15]. Injured alveolar epithelial and endothelial cells secrete chemokines that drive PMN microvascular adhesion and transendothelial migration, culminating in their alveolar accumulation [16,17]. As the primary defense against bacterial and fungal pathogens, activated PMNs release various cytotoxic mediators, which include proteases, reactive oxygen species (ROS), and neutrophil extracellular traps (NETs), to mediate immune responses [18,19]. NETs are extracellular DNA scaffolds interspersed with pro-inflammatory proteins. While they effectively kill pathogens, their excessive release exacerbates tissue damage [20]. NETs directly injure lung tissues and amplify inflammatory responses, marked by leukocyte accumulation, diffuse alveolar damage (DAD), and cytokine surges [21,22]. Given the pivotal roles of PMNs and NETs in ALI, targeting neutrophil homeostasis—by limiting recruitment, oxidative stress, and NETosis—offers a promising therapeutic strategy. Importantly, infiltrated PMNs are regulated not only by biochemical and microenvironmental cues but also likely by tissue mechanical stress, through mechanisms that remain elusive. Accordingly, we hypothesize that ALI-induced pathological stress drives aberrant PMN activation and excessive NET release, thereby exacerbating pulmonary inflammation and structural tissue damage. Piezo1, a mechanosensitive ion channel, has been established as a primary mechanotransducer across a broad spectrum of physiological and pathological processes [23,24]. Piezo1 transduces mechanical stimuli, including shear stress, swelling, stretch, and pressure, into biochemical signals by mediating cation influx, particularly Ca2+ [25]. Piezo1 governs diverse cellular behaviors, including proliferation and apoptosis [26], and is indispensable for mechanical immune activation [27]. Importantly, its hyperactivation or persistent signaling can drive inflammatory tissue damage [28]. Piezo1 coordinates Ca2+ dependent macrophage polarization and stiffness sensing, with its deficiency limiting inflammatory responses and enhancing wound healing [29]. Accumulating evidence has demonstrated that Piezo1 is expressed on the plasma membrane of PMNs and is actively involved in modulating their immune functional responses [30,31]. However, the role of PMN Piezo1 in ALI, particularly its involvement in PMN-driven inflammatory injury during mechanical ventilation, remains elusive. Therefore, elucidating the role of PMN-associated Piezo1 in ALI progression will provide a novel therapeutic strategy for targeting mechanotransduction to alleviate lung injury.

In the present study, we systematically investigated the mechanisms by which mechanical stress triggers the pro-inflammatory functions of PMNs and exacerbates lung injury. By integrating differential mechanical ventilation models, single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq), and a dynamic cell compression system, the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 was identified as a critical pro-inflammatory regulator in PMN-mediated biomechanical injury. Furthermore, pharmacological blockade of Piezo1 activity in PMNs during mechanical ventilation was found to suppress PMN hyperactivation and NET release, highlighting Piezo1 as a promising and effective therapeutic strategy for ALI and related inflammatory disorders.

Results

High tidal volume ventilation exacerbates endotoxin-induced acute lung injury and promotes neutrophil infiltration

To investigate the specific impact of mechanical stress on the progression of lung injury, we established a murine ALI model using intratracheal administration of Lipopolysaccharide (LPS) as a basal inflammatory insult, with varying tidal volumes of ventilation serving as the primary independent variable (Fig. 1A). Morphological observations demonstrated that lung tissues in the LPS plus high tidal volume ventilation (LPS + HVV) group exhibited the most prominent congestion and diffuse hemorrhagic foci (Fig. 1B). H&E staining further confirmed that the LPS + HVV group sustained severe histopathological damage compared to the other groups, manifested by marked thickening of the alveolar septa, severe interstitial edema, and extensive inflammatory cell infiltration (Fig. 1C). Quantitative analysis of the lung wet-to-dry (W/D) weight ratio showed a significant increase in the LPS + HVV group compared to the LPS plus low tidal volume ventilation (LPS + LVV) group, suggesting that high tidal volume ventilation significantly exacerbates endotoxin-induced pulmonary vascular permeability (Fig. 1D).To resolve the remodeling effects of mechanical ventilation on the pulmonary immune microenvironment at the single-cell level, we performed scRNA-seq on lung tissues. Through UMAP clustering analysis, we successfully identified 12 major cell populations, encompassing both immune and stromal cell lineages (Fig. 1E and F). Cell proportion analysis revealed that HVV induced a dramatic surge in the PMN population on top of LPS challenge (Fig. 1G), providing strong evidence that mechanical stress is a central driver of excessive PMN infiltration under inflammatory conditions. Both immunofluorescence (Fig. 1I) and immunohistochemistry (Supplementary Fig. S1A) for Ly6G confirmed that PMN counts within the lung parenchyma were significantly higher in the LPS + HVV group than in the LPS + LVV group (Supplementary Fig. S1B). Additionally, ELISA analysis of bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF) revealed that the levels of neutrophil elastase (NE) and myeloperoxidase (MPO) were markedly elevated in the LPS + HVV group (Fig. 1H andJ). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that mechanical stress significantly aggravates the pathological progression of ALI by promoting both the infiltration and functional activation of PMNs.

Mechanical stress promotes neutrophil activation and sensitizes endotoxin-induced NET release

The 4D Cell confinement system was used to simulate the physical constraint-induced stress on PMNs (Fig. 2A). Since the precise application of mechanical pressure on individual cells is technically challenging to control, this system achieves quantifiable stress application by precisely modulating the physical confinement height. To identify the mechanical stress threshold and structural tolerance of PMNs, we performed a gradient analysis (1, 3, 5, and 8 μm) and evaluated PMN morphology using Giemsa staining (Supplementary Fig. S1C).

Light microscopy revealed that while heights of 8 μm and 5 μm exerted insufficient mechanical stimulus for significant deformation, extreme confinement at 1 μm led to catastrophic cellular rupture and nuclear lysis. In contrast, the 3-μm height induced pronounced longitudinal flattening and characteristic mechanosensitive nuclear deformation while maintaining cellular and nuclear integrity (Fig. 2B). Phenotypic analysis showed that mechanical stress significantly upregulated the surface expression of the activation marker CD11b and the degranulation-associated molecule CD63 in PMNs (Fig. 2C and D). Additionally, the Stressed group exhibited significantly elevated levels of secreted neutrophil elastase (NE) and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9), suggesting that mechanical stress is sufficient to directly trigger neutrophil degranulation and functional activation (Fig. 2E–F). Consistent with this hyper-activated phenotype, we observed that mechanical stress-treated PMNs exhibited a pronounced tendency toward mutual adhesion and the formation of multicellular aggregates (Supplementary Fig. S1D). This pathological aggregation, coupled with the previously observed morphological remodeling and the significant downregulation of the surface chemokine receptor CXCR2 (Supplementary Fig. S1E), likely contributed to the significantly inhibited directional chemotactic capacity of PMNs (Fig. 2G). These findings suggest that mechanical strain compromises the 'molecular compass' of neutrophils, thereby impairing their ability to navigate toward chemoattractant gradients. Concurrently, alongside the enhanced phenotypic activation, intracellular reactive oxygen species (ROS) production in the Stressed group increased substantially (Fig. 2H and I), providing a critical oxidative stress foundation for subsequent NET release. These results indicate that physical mechanical force, as an independent stimulus, is sufficient to induce a hyper-activated state in PMNs. Finally, we evaluated the regulatory effect of mechanical stress on the process of NET formation. Immunofluorescence results showed that mechanical stress alone was capable of inducing a certain degree of NETs release. More importantly, to simulate endotoxin-induced acute lung injury, we applied mechanical stress in the presence of LPS and found that mechanical loading significantly potentiated the pro-inflammatory effects of the endotoxin, triggering a massive burst of extracellular DNA filament structures (NETs) (Fig. 2J). Quantitative analysis confirmed that the percentage of NET area in the LPS + Stressed group was significantly higher than that in either the Stressed-only or LPS-only treatment groups (Fig. 2K). These findings provide robust evidence that mechanical stress not only directly promotes NET formation but also significantly sensitizes LPS-induced inflammatory signaling, driving a massive, burst-like release of NETs from neutrophils.

Mechanically Activated Neutrophils Exacerbate Alveolar Epithelial Injury through excessive NET release

To explore the intercellular communication mechanisms by which high tidal volume ventilation exacerbates lung injury, we performed ligand-receptor interaction analysis using CellChat on our scRNA-seq dataset. CellChat analysis revealed that PMNs demonstrated a significant increase in incoming signaling strength (Fig. 3A). Differential mapping further identified damaged epithelial cells as the primary signaling source, acting as central hubs for PMN recruitment (Fig. 3B). Interestingly, the absence of corresponding outgoing transcriptional changes in PMNs led us to speculate that subsequent epithelial injury might be driven by non-transcriptional mechanisms—such as NET release—rather than traditional ligand-receptor pathways. Subsequently, we established a non-contact co-culture model of PMNs and AT2 cells (Fig. 3C). CCK-8 assay results demonstrated that, compared with the Static group, stressed PMNs significantly reduced the cell viability of AT2 cells (Fig. 3E) and induced a substantial release of LDH (Fig. 3F). These findings indicate that the culture supernatant from mechanically activated PMNs exerts significant cytotoxicity toward AT2 cells. Notably, when DNase I was applied to specifically degrade NETs within the co-culture system, both the loss of AT2 cell viability and the release of LDH were significantly attenuated (Fig. 3E and F). These results demonstrate that NETs released upon mechanical stress induction serve as the core effector molecules responsible for alveolar epithelial injury. To characterize the mode of alveolar epithelial cell injury, we evaluated the expression of the pro-apoptotic marker Caspase-3. Both immunofluorescence staining (Fig. 3D and G) and Western blot analysis (Supplementary Fig. S1F and G) consistently revealed that Caspase-3 was significantly upregulated in AT2 cells co-cultured with stressed PMNs. Flow cytometric analysis further validated that mechanically activated PMNs induced robust apoptosis in AT2 cells, encompassing both early and late stages, whereas DNase I treatment effectively abolished this pro-apoptotic effect (Fig. 3H and I). Collectively, these results demonstrate that NETs driven by mechanical stress can directly induce AT2 cell apoptosis through intercellular crosstalk, thereby exacerbating lung tissue injury.

Piezo1 acts as a Pivotal Mechanosensor to Initiate Calcium Influx in Neutrophils under mechanical stress

Considering that calcium signaling is a pivotal second messenger for sensing physical stimuli, we first employed the Fluo4-AM probe to dynamically monitor the response of PMNs under mechanical stress. Real-time flow cytometric analysis revealed that at the instant of mechanical excitation, intracellular Ca2+ levels surged rapidly and remained sustained at a high level (Fig. 4A and B). These immediate calcium transients indicate the presence of mechanosensitive ion channels on the PMN membrane that can rapidly sense mechanical stress and transduce it into biochemical signals. To identify the molecular source of the aforementioned calcium influx signals, we performed deep mining of the single-cell transcriptomic data. The results showed that the mechanosensitive channel Piezo1 is characteristically expressed in the PMN population. Furthermore, Piezo1 expression abundance in PMNs was significantly higher in lung tissues from the LPS + HVV group compared to the LPS + LVV group (Fig. 4C). This bioinformatic hint suggests that Piezo1 may be a core effector protein for neutrophils in response to mechanical stress. To further validate the protein levels and subcellular localization of Piezo1, immunofluorescence imaging was performed, confirming that Piezo1 is extensively localized on the plasma membrane of PMNs (Fig. 4D). Subsequent quantitative analysis revealed that mechanical stress not only significantly increased the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of surface Piezo1 but also led to an adaptive upregulation of total Piezo1 protein expression (Fig. 4E and F). To elucidate the central role of Piezo1 in mechanosensation, we performed functional validation using pharmacological approaches. The results showed that mechanical stress, similar to the Piezo1-specific agonist Yoda1, significantly increased intracellular Ca2+ influx. Conversely, pre-treatment with the Piezo1-specific antagonist Grammostola spatulata mechanotoxin 4 (GsMTx4) significantly abrogated the mechanical stress-induced calcium burst, with the Ca2+ fluorescence ratio returning to near-baseline levels (Fig. 4G–I). Flow cytometry demonstrated that GsMTx4-mediated Piezo1 inhibition not only attenuated stress-induced Piezo1 upregulation (Supplementary Fig. S2A) but also suppressed downstream phenotypic activation. Specifically, blocking the Piezo1-Ca2+ axis markedly reduced CD63 surface expression and intracellular ROS production (Supplementary Fig. S2B and C). Collectively, these findings identify Piezo1 as a critical mechanosensor that initiates the calcium cascade required for PMN degranulation and oxidative burst under mechanical strain.

Integrated Transcriptomic Profiling Reveals an Expanded Mitochondrial-Stress Neutrophil Subpopulation and Mitochondrial Dysfunction

To further elucidate the impact of mechanical stress on PMN heterogeneity, we performed re-clustering analysis on lung PMNs, identifying three functionally distinct subpopulations (N0–N2) (Fig. 5A). High tidal volume ventilation significantly reshaped the PMN subpopulation landscape, which was characterized by a marked expansion in the proportion of the N1 cluster under pathological mechanical stress compared to the LPS + LVV group (Fig. 5B and C). Feature gene analysis (Dot Plot) showed that N1 specifically expressed mitochondrial homeostasis-related genes, including Sod2, Slc25a37, and Cox17 (Fig. 5D). GO enrichment analysis further confirmed that this cluster was highly enriched in pathways related to the response to oxygen levels, mitochondrial metabolism, and response to environmental stimuli (Fig. 5E). Furthermore, at the whole-cell level, the mitochondrial stress score (calculated based on the gene set in Supplementary Table S1) was also significantly elevated in the pathological mechanical stress group (LPS + HVV) (Fig. 5F–I). In light of these molecular signatures and functional profiles, we designated N1 as the “mitochondrial stress subpopulation. To further validate the mitochondrial damage mechanisms identified in our scRNA-seq analysis, we performed bulk RNA-seq on in vitro human neutrophils subjected to mechanical stress. Differential transcriptomic analysis revealed profound remodeling of gene expression profiles induced by mechanical loading (Fig. 5G and H). Notably, the expression of PPIF (encoding cyclophilin D), a core component of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), was significantly upregulated, whereas TFAM, a critical factor for maintaining mitochondrial DNA stability, was markedly downregulated (Fig. 5H). Functional enrichment analysis further demonstrated that these stress-responsive genes were primarily involved in reactive oxygen species (ROS) metabolism and cellular mechanosensation (Fig. 5J). In vitro validation confirmed the key markers of mitochondrial damage. RT-qPCR demonstrated that mechanical stress significantly upregulated PPIF and downregulated TFAM expression in PMNs (Fig. 5K and L). These results indicate that mechanical stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction serves as an endogenous signal driving PMN activation and phenotypic transformation.

Mechanical stress-induced mitochondrial dysfunction drives the excessive release of NETs

In view of the mitochondrial dyshomeostasis indicated by the transcriptomic data, we first examined mitochondrial morphology using transmission electron microscopy (TEM). The results demonstrated that PMNs in the mechanical stress group exhibited significant mitochondrial swelling, cristae fragmentation, and vacuolization (Fig. 6A). MitoTracker staining further confirmed that mechanical stress induced a transition of mitochondria from tubular networks to fragmented structures (Fig. 6B). Functionally, JC-1 assays showed a substantial decrease in the red-to-green fluorescence ratio in the stressed group, indicating a significant loss of mitochondrial membrane potential(ΔΨm)induced by mechanical stress; notably, the application of the Piezo1 blocker GsMTx4 significantly alleviated this damage process (Fig. 6C and D). We assessed mtROS production and mPTP status to elucidate the mechanisms of mitochondrial damage. Mechanical stress induced a robust surge in mtROS levels (Fig. 6E and F). Calcein AM + CoCl2 quenching assays further demonstrated that mechanical loading triggered abnormal mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP) opening—evidenced by the loss of mitochondrial green fluorescence (Fig. 6H)—and subsequent cytosolic leakage of mtDNA (Fig. 6G and I). Notably, these effects were significantly abrogated by pretreatment with either the Piezo1 blocker GsMTx4 or the mPTP inhibitor CsA. Finally, we evaluated the impact of this mechanism on NET formation. Results showed that mechanical stress significantly induced NET release under both basal and LPS-stimulated conditions; however, blocking the Piezo1-mediated mitochondrial damage pathway with GsMTx4 markedly reduced the percentage of NET-covered area (Fig. 6J–L). Collectively, mechanical stress signals activate the Piezo1 channel, leading to substantial Ca2+ influx and a signaling cascade characterized by mitochondrial dysfunction—marked by abnormal mPTP opening and excessive ROS generation—which ultimately drives the massive release of PMN-derived NETs.

Targeting the Piezo1 signaling pathway alleviates mechanical stress-induced acute lung injury

To evaluate the protective potential of Piezo1 inhibition in vivo, GsMTx4 was administered to mice subjected to either low tidal volume (LVV) or high tidal volume (HVV) ventilation following LPS challenge (Fig. 7A). Monitoring of respiratory mechanics showed that LPS + HVV significantly compromised lung function, evidenced by tachypnea and decreased tidal volume (TV) and minute ventilation (MV). These physiological deteriorations were effectively attenuated by GsMTx4-mediated Piezo1 inhibition (Fig. 7B, C, and E). LPS-primed HVV challenge caused profound pulmonary congestion and alveolar collapse with high injury scores, whereas GsMTx4 treatment largely mitigated these pathological changes (Fig. 7 D, F and G). Thus, Piezo1 is a central mediator of mechanical stress-induced lung injury. To assess barrier integrity, we evaluated the effect of Piezo1 inhibition on vascular permeability and cell death. GsMTx4 treatment effectively mitigated pulmonary edema induced by the LPS-primed HVV challenge, restoring it to near-control levels (Fig. 7H). Additionally, TUNEL staining showed that the HVV-triggered massive apoptosis (∼25% positive rate) was significantly abrogated by Piezo1 blockade (Fig. 7I and J). These results substantiate that Piezo1 is a key mediator of the lung's physical barrier function. Finally, we examined Piezo1's in vivo role in PMN activation. LPS-primed HVV significantly upregulated Piezo1 expression, an effect effectively reversed by GsMTx4 (Fig. 7K, L and M). Importantly, GsMTx4 treatment markedly suppressed the formation of CitH3 + MPO + NETs in lung parenchyma (Fig. 7 N) and reduced BALF levels of MPO and NE (Fig. 7 O and P). Consistent with the reduction in NETs, GsMTx4-mediated Piezo1 inhibition significantly attenuated oxidative DNA damage and cellular apoptosis in the lung tissues, as evidenced by the diminished immunohistochemical staining for 8-OHdG and Caspase-3 (Supplementary Fig. S2D–E). In summary, pharmacological inhibition of Piezo1 significantly suppresses excessive NET release and alleviates lung injury, suggesting that targeting the Piezo1 signaling pathway is a promising therapeutic strategy for ALI.

Discussion

ALI is a profound inflammatory condition triggered by diverse direct insults—such as severe pulmonary infection, embolism, and lung contusion—as well as indirect factors, including sepsis, systemic trauma, and massive blood transfusion. Despite the current clinical reliance on mechanical ventilation, glucocorticoids, and antibiotics, therapeutic efficacy remains suboptimal, with mortality rates persistently hovering around 30% [32,33]. Therefore, investigating the pathogenesis of ALI holds profound theoretical and clinical significance. While the roles of inflammatory signals, such as cytokines, in the immune-inflammatory cascade of ALI have been extensively studied, how physical mechanical stress acts as a standalone pathological stimulus to drive immune cell hyperactivation—particularly concerning how PMNs sense and respond to these biophysical alterations—remains poorly understood. In the present study, we established a mouse model involving LPS nebulization coupled with differential mechanical ventilation and integrated high-resolution single-cell RNA sequencing (scRNA-seq) to focus on the pivotal role of NETs in the progression of ALI. Our findings demonstrate that the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 serves as a pivotal regulator in translating physical cues into pro-inflammatory biochemical responses. The Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ influx overload provokes mitochondrial impairment, characterized by excessive ROS generation and the cytosolic leakage of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA), which subsequently drives PMN hyperactivation and an explosive burst of NETs. This mechanism establishes a direct mechanistic link between mechanical stress and PMN hyperactivation, which underscores a critical pathway potentially contributing to the exacerbation of pulmonary inflammation and tissue injury.

During the pathological progression of ALI, the synergy of diffuse pulmonary edema, reduced lung compliance, and mechanical ventilation-induced forces significantly alters the mechanical milieu of the lung. This environment exposes the plasma membranes of infiltrating PMNs to extraordinary mechanical tension and strain, far exceeding normal physiological limits [9]. As core effector cells of innate immunity, PMNs not only possess essential antimicrobial functions, including chemotaxis, phagocytosis, and degranulation, but also exhibit remarkable plasticity, actively adapting to diverse mechanical microenvironments [34,35]. While the mechanisms by which various biochemical stimuli, such as pathogens, nitric oxide, and pro-inflammatory cytokines, induce NETs have been extensively studied [[36], [37], [38]], whether mechanical stress can serve as a standalone regulator independent of chemical signals to activate PMNs and trigger NET formation remains to be elucidated. By employing the 4Dcell dynamic cell confinement system to exert precisely controlled mechanical loads on PMNs, we confirmed that isolated mechanical stress is sufficient to effectively induce NET formation. Furthermore, we observed that the addition of mechanical strain significantly elevated NET levels in the presence of LPS pre-stimulation at equivalent concentrations. This suggests that during the pathogenesis of ALI, physical mechanical stress not only acts as a direct inducer of NETosis but also sensitizes PMNs to biochemical signals, synergistically driving PMN hyperactivation.

Piezo1 ion channel proteins are abundantly expressed on the plasma membrane of PMNs [[39], [40], [41]]. As a novel mechanosensitive ion channel, Piezo1 transduces physical stimuli into biochemical signals by facilitating Ca2+ influx, thereby governing a wide array of physiological and pathological processes [[42], [43], [44], [45]]. Furthermore, the initial activation of Piezo1 can propagate a tight functional cascade closely coupled with downstream TRPV4 channels, thereby synergistically amplifying the massive calcium influx and driving subsequent tissue hyperpermeability [46,47]. Although Piezo1 expression and its mechanical activation have been broadly documented across various cell types—including endothelial cells and certain malignant cells—to coordinate lineage functions [[48], [49], [50], [51], [52], [53]], its precise role in governing neutrophil mechanotransduction remains to be fully elucidated. During chemotaxis to the lung, circulating neutrophils must actively navigate the restrictive microvasculature, squeezing through narrow endothelial gaps. Evidence has demonstrated that during this process, the PMN plasma membrane is subjected to profound physical confinement and elevated membrane tension, forcing extreme cellular and nuclear deformation. This intense mechanical strain directly gates the Piezo1 channel, triggering transient cytosolic calcium spikes that effectively activate PMNs and enhance their bactericidal function [9]. Conversely, our findings unveil another critical dimension: during acute lung injury, the massively infiltrating PMNs are concurrently subjected to intense biomechanical stress within the tissue microenvironment. Our study demonstrates that pulmonary edema and mechanical ventilation-induced alveolar hyper-distension directly gate and hyper-activate the PIEZO1 channels on the neutrophil surface. The extensive and sustained opening of these channels triggers a massive surge in intracellular Ca2+ levels, driving the excessive release of NETs and subsequently damaging alveolar epithelial cells—a pathological cascade that is significantly blunted upon pharmacological Piezo1 inhibition. Consequently, precisely targeting the PIEZO1 channel can effectively reduce the aberrant overproduction of NETs, thereby mitigating severe acute lung injury.

Mitochondria are highly efficient metabolic hubs that play a pivotal role in oxidative stress-mediated injury. Emerging evidence suggests that mitochondrial impairment is a critical driver of aberrant PMN activation [54,55]. Mechanical stress rapidly activates the mechanosensitive ion channel Piezo1 on the PMN plasma membrane, triggering a massive influx of extracellular Ca2+. The resulting surge in cytosolic calcium drives excessive Ca2+ entry into the mitochondria via the mitochondrial calcium uniporter (MCU), culminating in mitochondrial calcium overload. This intracellular calcium crisis serves as a potent trigger for the sustained opening of the mitochondrial permeability transition pore (mPTP), leading to the dissipation of mitochondrial membrane potential, electron transport chain decoupling, and the subsequent catastrophic leakage of both mtROS and mtDNA. Specifically, mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) activate the PAD4 enzyme, which induces histone citrullination and promotes subsequent chromatin decondensation [[56], [57], [58]]. Concurrently, mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) functions as a potent damage-associated molecular pattern (DAMP) that triggers an aberrant burst of NETs through the engagement of the cGAS-STING signaling axis [[59], [60], [61]]. Consequently, mitochondria emerge as a potential therapeutic target for mitigating the excessive formation of NET induced by mechanical stress. Crucially, this continuous mechanical insult and the early-stage mitochondrial crisis actively drive the subsequent transcriptomic polarization of neutrophils; in this study, scRNA-seq analysis identified a distinct neutrophil subpopulation (N1) driven by mechanical stimuli, which was characterized by the significant enrichment of genes such as Sod2, Slc25a37, and Cox17. The upregulation of Sod2 typically reflects severe oxidative stress within the mitochondrial matrix [62], while Slc25a37-mediated dysregulation of iron homeostasis is closely linked to alterations in mitochondrial membrane permeability [63]. Notably, these transcriptomic shifts were highly congruent with our subsequent phenotypic observations of mitochondrial swelling, membrane potential collapse, and physical rupture. Accordingly, we defined N1 as the 'mitochondrial stress subpopulation'. Gene Ontology (GO) enrichment analysis revealed that the mitochondrial stress subpopulation exhibited significantly high enrichment in biological processes related to cell activation and ROS generation. These findings further substantiate, at the transcriptomic level, the intrinsic link between mechanical stress, mitochondrial dysfunction, and the hyperactivation of PMNs. Our further experiments demonstrated that inhibiting excessive mtROS production or mtDNA leakage effectively reduced mechanical stress-induced NET formation. Similar results were obtained in PMNs pre-treated with the Piezo1 inhibitor GsMTx4. Notably, studies have found that prolonged mechanical stress or channel inhibition can trigger homeostatic feedback loops, driving compensatory transcriptional upregulation of homologous channels like Piezo2, TRPV1, and TRPV4 [64]. Under prolonged mechanical stimulation, PMNs might also initiate similar compensatory pathways. However, our scRNA-seq data demonstrate that the N1 subpopulation is prominently characterized by a signature enriched in specific mitochondrial stress markers (Sod2 and Slc25a37), a phenotype effectively rescued by targeted pharmacological inhibition of Piezo1 in vitro. Therefore, within this acute disease time window, the Piezo1-mediated pathway remains the predominant driver of physical force transduction. These findings indicate that suppressing stress-induced NETosis can decrease AT2 cell apoptosis and alleviate lung tissue injury, suggesting that NETs—triggered by mechanical strain and Piezo1 activation—play a substantial role in alveolar epithelial cell damage. Importantly, in the in vivo context, the concurrent pharmacological effects of GsMTx4 on pulmonary structural cells cannot be entirely ruled out. This is because pulmonary structural cells themselves may also actively respond to mechanical stress by upregulating Piezo1 and producing a myriad of proinflammatory cytokines, thereby further cascading and amplifying the local immune microenvironment [65]. Ultimately, targeting Piezo1-mediated mechanosensitive pathways holds significant clinical value for developing novel therapeutic strategies to prevent immune dysfunction and alleviate mechanical stress-induced pulmonary diseases (Fig. 8).

Limitation

Despite the insights gained from this study, several limitations warrant consideration. Our investigation primarily focused on the mechanosensitivity and physical activation of PMNs, while the downstream molecular pathways by which NETs trigger alveolar epithelial cell apoptosis were not characterized in detail. Although the detrimental effects of NETs on the alveolar-capillary barrier are well-documented in the literature, a detailed investigation into this process remains beyond the scope of the present study. Crucially, because pulmonary structural cells also possess intrinsic Piezo1 expression, the systemic delivery of GsMTx4 might concurrently exert pharmacological effects on both alveolar epithelial and endothelial cells. In addition, recent pharmacological studies have indicated that GsMTx4 and its short-peptide derivatives can directly cross-react with TRPV4 channels and modulate their activity, thereby inhibiting calcium influx. This multi-target mechanism of action suggests that the protective efficacy of GsMTx4 observed in our model might involve a broader network of mechanosensitive channels, rather than an absolute, isolated inhibition of Piezo1 alone [66,67]. Therefore, the lack of a lineage-specific conditional knockout model or a bone marrow chimerism model to directly validate the therapeutic efficacy of blocking neutrophil Piezo1 protein constitutes one of the major limitations of the present study, an issue we hope to thoroughly address in future investigations. Finally, while our data highlight the roles of mtDNA leakage and mtROS production, the intricate signaling cascades governing these processes are not exhaustively mapped.

Conclusion

We establish that Piezo1-mediated Ca2+ entry and mitochondrial impairment are central to mechanical stress-induced NET and subsequent ALI. The aberrant release of NETs not only exacerbates alveolar epithelial cell injury but also directly leads to the structural destruction of lung tissue, thereby providing a more comprehensive understanding of their pathological role in ALI. Furthermore, we identified pathological mechanical stress, recapitulated through differential ventilation and dynamic cell pressure models, as a primary upstream regulator of Piezo1 expression and activity, offering novel insights into molecular mechanotransduction in the pathogenesis of ALI. These findings pave the way for novel therapeutic avenues targeting mechanosensitive ion channels and mitochondrial oxidative stress in ALI and associated respiratory inflammatory diseases.

Methods

Lung injury induction and mechanical ventilation strategy

Mice were initially anesthetized with sevoflurane (4% for induction and 1.5–2% for maintenance) and subjected to an intratracheal instillation of lipopolysaccharide (LPS; 5 μg/kg, MCE, HY-D1056) or an equal volume of normal saline (NS) as a control. Following a 4-h incubation period for initial acute lung injury induction, a tracheotomy was performed, and a 20-G cannula was inserted into the trachea for connection to a small animal ventilator (RWD). The mice then received 2 h of mechanical ventilation under distinct protocols: the high tidal volume (HVV) group was subjected to injurious ventilation (PIP: 22 cmH2O; PEEP: 2 cmH2O; RR: 130 bpm), while the low tidal volume (LVV) group received protective ventilation (PIP: 16 cmH2O; PEEP: 6 cmH2O; RR: 130 bpm) [68]. Immediately following the 2-h ventilation period, pulmonary function was assessed, after which the mice were euthanized. Lung tissues were promptly harvested and fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde for subsequent immunofluorescence (IF) and immunohistochemistry (IHC) analyses to evaluate histological damage and protein localization.

Single-cell RNA sequencing and Bioinformatics Analysis

The total lung RNAs were used for RNA-seq. The RNA sequencing libraries were constructed and sequenced on the BGISEQ-500 platforms.

(1) Data Pre-processing and QC: The 10×scRNA-seq data were processed and converted into Seurat objects using the CreateSeuratObject function in Seurat (v4.3.0). Low-quality cells were filtered by retaining those with a mitochondrial gene percentage (percent.mt) < 20%, total RNA counts (nCount_RNA) > 1,000, and detected genes (nFeature_RNA) > 300. The percent.mt threshold was specifically optimized for activated neutrophils in the ALI environment. (2) Integration and Normalization: To minimize batch effects across the Control, LPS + LVV and LPS + HVV groups, the datasets were integrated using the Harmony algorithm. Data were then normalized and scaled using the NormalizeData and ScaleData functions, respectively. The top 2000 highly variable genes were identified via FindVariableFeatures. (3) Dimensionality Reduction and Clustering: Principal component analysis (PCA) was performed using RunPCA. Uniform manifold approximation and projection (UMAP) was then applied for visualization. Cell clusters were identified, and cluster-specific markers were determined using the FindAllMarkers function (min.pct = 0.25, logfc.threshold = 0.25). Cell types were annotated based on canonical markers (e.g., S100a8 and Ly6g) and validated via CellMarker 2.0 and Cell Taxonomy databases. (4) Neutrophil Subclustering and Mitochondrial Stress Scoring: Neutrophil clusters were extracted using the subset function for high-resolution re-clustering, identifying three subpopulations: N0, N2, and the mechanosensitive Mitochondrial-Stressed PMN subpopulation (N1). To quantify the organelle-level response to physical strain, a “Mitochondrial Stress Score” was calculated using the AddModuleScore function. This score was based on a gene set associated with mitochondrial oxidative stress and dysfunction (e.g., Sod2, Ppif, Slc25a37, and Cox17) curated from GeneCards and GO databases. The comprehensive list of genes used for this scoring is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Violin plots and UMAP FeaturePlots were generated to visualize the distribution of stress levels.

Extraction of human neutrophils

Peripheral blood was collected from healthy volunteers using EDTAK2 anticoagulant tubes, and PMNs were isolated from the peripheral blood using the EasySep™ direct human PMN isolation kit (Stemcell, 19666). PMNs were cultured in a 37 ◦C incubator with 5% CO2 using RPMI1640 medium (Gibco, 11875119) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (OPCEL, BS-1205) and 1% penicillin and streptomycin (Beyotime, C0222).

Mechanical stress system

To evaluate the impact of physical forces on cellular behavior, PMNs were subjected to precisely controlled mechanical stress using a 4Dcell Dynamic Cell Confiner system(4Dcell, France; World-Bio Lab Technology). The cells were seeded on a 35 mm glass-bottom dish and confined between the dish surface and a PDMS piston. The magnitude of the mechanical loading was defined by sub-micron resolution PDMS micropillars (available heights from 3 μm) fabricated on the confinement slide, ensuring a uniform distribution of pressure across the cell population.

The mechanical stimulation was managed by an AF1 programmable pressure/vacuum controller, which automated the application and release of the load. The protocol involved alternating periods of mechanical pressure for 10 min followed by 10 min of relaxation, spanning a total duration of 2 h. Following this period of dynamic stress, the confinement was fully released. The PMNs were then incubated in a quiescent state for an additional 2 h to facilitate the observation and quantification of NET. This non-destructive confinement method ensures cell viability and allows for detailed biomolecular analysis post-stimulation.

Transcriptomic analysis of isolated PMNs

(1) RNA Extraction and Quality Control: Total RNA was extracted from isolated PMNs using the TRIzol reagent (Invitrogen, USA). RNA purity and concentration were determined using the NanoDrop 2000 spectrophotometer (Thermo Scientific, USA). RNA integrity was precisely assessed using the RNA Nano 6000 Assay Kit of the Bioanalyzer 2100 system (Agilent Technologies, CA, USA). Only samples with an RNA integrity number (RIN) > 7.0 were used for library construction. (2) Library Preparation and Sequencing: Sequencing libraries were generated using the NEBNext® Ultra™ RNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® (NEB, USA) following the manufacturer's recommendations. Briefly, mRNA was purified from total RNA using poly-T oligo-attached magnetic beads. After fragmentation, first and second-strand cDNA were synthesized. The library fragments were purified with the AMPure XP system (Beckman Coulter, Beverly, USA) to select cDNA fragments of preferentially 250–300 bp in length. The libraries were then sequenced on an Illumina NovaSeq 6000 platform (Novogene Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), generating 150 bp paired-end reads. (3) Bioinformatics Analysis: Raw data in FASTQ format were processed through in-house Perl scripts to remove reads containing adapter or low-quality sequences. The clean reads were mapped to the reference genome using HISAT2 (v2.0.5). Differential expression analysis was performed using the DESeq2 R package (v1.20.0). Genes with an |log2(Fold Change)| > 1 and an adjusted P-value <0.05 were assigned as differentially expressed genes (DEGs). The specific primers used for qPCR validation of selected DEGs are listed in Supplementary Table S2.

Flow cytometry detection

PMN Activation and Piezo1 Expression. Treated PMNs were harvested and washed twice with cold PBS. For Piezo1 detection, cells were first incubated with a rabbit anti-Piezo1 primary antibody (Proteintech,15939-1-AP) at 4 °C for 1 h, followed by incubation with Alexa Fluor 488 (Biolegend,405235) for 30 min in the dark. In parallel, other aliquots were stained with directly conjugated antibodies against CD11b (BioLegend, 393102) or CD63 (BioLegend, 353017) for 30 min at 4 °C. After the final wash, fluorescence signals for each marker were acquired using a BD flow cytometer.

AT2 Cell Apoptosis. The apoptosis rate of AT2 cells was determined using an Annexin V-FITC/PI Apoptosis Kit (MULTI SCIENCES, AP101). Briefly, treated AT2 cells were harvested, washed with cold PBS, and resuspended in 100 μL of Binding Buffer. The cells were then incubated with 5 μL of Annexin V-FITC and 5 μL of Propidium Iodide (PI) for 15 min at room temperature in the dark.

Intracellular Calcium Measurement. To monitor intracellular Ca2+ levels, cells from the different treatment groups were harvested and loaded with 5 μM Fluo-4 AM (Thermofisher, F14217) in HBSS at 37 °C for 30 min in the dark. After washing three times with HBSS, the mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) of Fluo-4 was immediately acquired using a BD flow cytometer to quantify the calcium influx.

All samples were analyzed using BD flow cytometer. Data were processed using FlowJo software.

Immunostaining and microscopy

Cells were fixed in 4% paraformaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100 (Beyotime, P0096-100 ml), blocked with 5% bovine serum albumin (BSA; Beyotime, ST025-20 g) and then stained with 4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole dihydrochloride (DAPI; Beyotime, C1002),Hoechst 33258 (Beyotime, C1011), Caspase-3 (Asp175) Antibody(Cell Signaling Technology,9661),Piezo1 (extracellular domain) Polyclonal antibody(Proteintech, 15939-1-AP). Stained PMNs were observed using a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal laser scanning microscope.

Intracellular Calcium Measurements

Cells were pre-loaded with 5 μM Fluo-4 AM (ThermoFisher, F14217) in HBSS for 30 min at 37 °C. Following three washes to remove extracellular dye, cells were subjected to various treatments. For the inhibitor group, GsMTx4 was added 30 min prior to mechanical stimulation. Calcium signaling was then triggered by mechanical stress or Yoda1, and the resulting fluorescence was immediately captured via confocal microscopy or flow cytometry.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay

To evaluate PMN activation primarily triggered by mechanical stress, inflammatory mediators were quantified using commercial ELISA kits. For murine models, the concentrations of myeloperoxidase (MPO, Proteintech, KE10172) and neutrophil elastase (NE, Abcam, AB252356) were determined in bronchoalveolar lavage fluid (BALF). In human studies, PMNs were subjected to mechanical stress in vitro, and the resulting culture supernatants were harvested for the quantification of NE (Abcam, ab270204), MPO (Elabscience, E-EL-H6244), and matrix metalloproteinase-9 (MMP-9, MULTI SCIENCES, EK1M09l). All assays were performed strictly according to the manufacturers'instructions. The absorbance was recorded at 450 nm using a microplate reader, and the protein concentrations were calculated based on the respective standard curves.

Western blot

PMNs subjected to mechanical stimulation treatment were collected, while mouse lung tissues were harvested from the respective groups. Both cell and tissue samples were lysed in RIPA lysis buffer (Beyotime, P0013B). The lysates were then centrifuged to obtain the supernatants for subsequent analysis. Protein samples were separated via 10% sodium dodecyl sulfate-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (SDS-PAGE) and then transferred to Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) membranes (Beyotime, FFP22). To prevent non-specific binding, membranes were blocked in TBST containing 5% non-fat milk for 2 h. The membranes were then probed overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies: Piezo1 (Proteintech, 15939-1-AP) and GAPDH (Proteintech, 10494-1-AP). After washing with TBST, membranes were incubated with HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit lgG (AB0101) and anti-mouse (AB0102) IgG (Abways Technology) for 2 h at room temperature. The immunoreactive bands were visualized using an enhanced chemiluminescent (ECL) blotting detection system.

Real-time quantitative polymerase chain reaction

All primers used for mRNA detection were synthesized by Suzhou Genewiz Biotech. Supplementary Table S2 shows the sequences of all primers in this study. RT-qPCR was performed using AceQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme) according to the manufacturer's instructions. Each 20 μL reaction contained 5 μL of diluted cDNA, 10 μL of Master Mix, 0.4 μL each of forward and reverse primers (10 μM), and 4.2 μl of nuclease-free water. Amplification was conducted on a StepOnePlus™ Real-Time PCR System (Applied Biosystems) with the following conditions: initial denaturation at 95 °C for 5 min, followed by 40 cycles of 95 °C for 10 s and 60 °C for 30 s. Target gene expression was normalized to Gapdh and calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method.

Quantification of cytosolic mtDNA release

To quantify the release of mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) into the cytosol, 1 × 107 cells were harvested and centrifuged at 900 × g for 5 min. The cell pellet was resuspended in PBS and divided into two equal aliquots. One aliquot was stored at −80 °C and designated as the whole-cell fraction. The remaining pellet was resuspended in 500 μL digitonin lysis buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM HEPES pH 7.4, and 25 μg/mL digitonin [Sigma-Aldrich]) and incubated for 10 min at room temperature to selectively permeabilize the plasma membrane. Subsequently, the lysate was centrifuged at 150 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to pellet intact cells and nuclei. The resulting supernatant was further cleared by two consecutive centrifugations at 150 × g and a final high-speed centrifugation at 17,000 × g for 10 min at 4 °C to obtain the pure cytosolic fraction, free of mitochondrial contamination [69]. DNA extraction from both the whole-cell and cytosolic fractions was performed using DNA Extraction Kits (Genewiz Biotech, China) following the manufacturer's protocols. Mitochondrial DNA levels were quantified via qPCR using primers specific to the D-loop and Nd1 regions, while nuclear DNA (nDNA) levels were assessed using Gapdh primers. The relative abundance of cytosolic mtDNA was normalized to the nDNA content of the corresponding whole-cell fraction using the 2−ΔΔCT method to represent the degree of mtDNA leakage into the cytoplasm.

Statistical analysis

All statistical analyses were performed and graphs were prepared with GraphPad Prism 8.0 software and Adobe Illustrator. The Shapiro–Wilk test was used to test the normality of continuous variables. Data are expressed as the mean ± standard deviation (SD). For group comparisons, one-way analysis of variance was used to compare continuous variables with a normal distribution. The Kruskal–Wallis test was used to compare continuous variables with a skewed distribution. Tukey's post hoc test or Dunn's post hoc test was used for multiple comparisons. Student's t_test and the Wilcoxon rank-sum test were used to compare differences between two groups. The Pearson correlation coefficient was used for correlation analysis. Significant differences are noted by asterisks (∗P < 0.05, ∗∗P < 0.01, ∗∗∗P < 0.001, ∗∗∗∗P < 0.0001).
