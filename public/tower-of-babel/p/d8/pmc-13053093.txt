The effect of obesity on revision total knee arthroplasty: a retrospective cohort study
Authors: Mohamad Nassereddine, Richard McCalden, Lyndsay Somerville, Jackie Marsh, Edward M. Vasarhelyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Background:Obesity has been linked to higher rates of postoperative complications, delayed wound healing, reoperation, and re-revision rate following revision total knee arthroplasty (RTKA). This study examined the impact of obesity across various weight classes on RTKA outcomes: healthy weight (body mass index [BMI] < 25); overweight (BMI 25 to 29.9); class I obesity (BMI 30 to 34.9); class II obesity (BMI 35 to 39.9); class III, type 1 obesity (BMI 40 to 44.9); class III, type 2 obesity (BMI 45 to 49.9); and severe class III obesity (BMI ≥ 50).Methods:We retrospectively reviewed an institutional database of patients who underwent RTKA over a period of 15 years. Data on demographics, re-revision rate, RTKA survivorship, postoperative complications, mortality rate, readmissions, 90-day costs, and functional outcomes were collected. Comparisons among BMI groups were made with Kruskal–Wallis tests for non-normal data and χ2 and Fisher exact tests for categorical data.Results:A total of 550 patients were included in the study, categorized into 7 BMI groups. The overall re-revision rate was 17%, with the BMI 45 to 49.9 (hazard ratio [HR] 2.4, p = 0.028) and BMI ≥ 50 (HR 2.8, p = 0.007) groups experiencing the highest risk. Implant survival in these groups declined to 70% at 4 years and 40% at 10 years, which was significantly lower than in patients with a lower BMI (p = 0.001). Secondary outcomes revealed that a BMI ≥ 50 was associated with higher 90-day readmission, superficial and deep infection rates, and increased 90-day costs (all p < 0.05), whereas functional improvements following RTKA were comparable across BMI groups (all p > 0.05).Conclusion:A BMI of 45 to 49.9 and ≥ 50 are risk factors for re-revision, postoperative infection, and readmission rates and increase the 90-day costs associated with RTKA.

Osteoarthritis (OA) is the most common articular disease and a leading cause of chronic disability.1 The incidence of symptomatic knee osteoarthritis is rising, driven by the aging population and the increasing prevalence of obesity.2 The treatment of OA of the knee can involve both nonsurgical modalities (e.g., physiotherapy, cryotherapy, analgesics, intra-articular injections) and surgical modalities, which typically culminate in total knee arthroplasty (TKA).3–5 Total knee arthroplasty is indicated in patients with severe pain caused by arthritis and refractory to alternative treatments, in the absence of septic arthritis.6 The incidence of revision TKA (RTKA) is on the rise despite an increasing efficacy of primary TKA.7 Obesity and its association with OA has been an area of interest in recent years; it has been found to be associated with an increased risk of postoperative orthopedic complications, delayed wound healing, reoperation, and rerevision.8–12 A study conducted to determine the national trends of obesity rates among patients undergoing RTKA in the United States over the last decade reported that 17.37% of patients who underwent RTKA were obese (body mass index [BMI] > 30) and that the incidence of obesity in patients undergoing RTKA increased significantly from 9.74% in 2002 to 24.57% in 2012.13

In the present study, we aimed to examine the impact of various classes of obesity on RTKA. Specifically, we explored the effect of obesity on re-revision rates, RTKA survivorship, postoperative complications, readmissions, and associated costs. Additionally, we sought to quantify the influence of obesity on patients’ quality of life (QoL) following RTKA.

Methods

Study design and setting

This study was conducted at the London Health Sciences Centre and consisted of a retrospective review of our prospectively collected institutional database over a 15-year time period (2005 to 2020). Patients were included in our study if they had RTKA; patients who had undergone only first-stage procedures were excluded because most of them had a static spacer, which prevented them from moving their joint, limiting the functional outcome scores that could be measured. Patients’ BMI was calculated using patient preoperative height and weight. We defined BMI categories according to the World Health Organization classification (healthy weight < 25, overweight 25 to 29.9, class I obesity 30 to 34.9, class II obesity 35 to 39.9, and class III obesity ≥ 40) because previous studies have reported that class III obesity was associated with an increased risk of reoperation.14,15 We further classified patients with BMI ≥ 40 into 3 subgroups to investigate whether differences exist within this high-risk population: class III, type 1 40 to 44.9; class III, type 2 45 to 49.9; and severe class III ≥ 50. The subgroup with a BMI ≥ 50 had the fewest people, so we used a sample of convenience based on the number of eligible patients in this category. However, members of the other BMI groups were randomly selected from the entire eligible cohort using a random number generator in a 2:1 ratio relative to the severe class III obesity group.

All patients in the cohort shared a similar perioperative protocol. The decision to proceed with revision surgery was made by the surgeon in conjunction with the patient and/or the patient’s family. The decision generally relied on the patient’s symptoms and their impact on QoL. A workup to rule out infection was carried out in all patients, although this was not standardized across the entire cohort; there was some practice variability given the number of treating surgeons as well as the time period of data collection. The workup typically included blood work consisting of complete blood count, erythrocyte sedimentation rate, and C-reactive protein. If inflammatory markers were elevated, a knee aspiration was typically performed. Routine radiography consisted of standing anteroposterior, lateral, tunnel, and skyline views as well as 3-foot standing films. Advanced imaging (e.g., computed tomography, ultrasonography, bone scan, white blood cell scan) were carried out at the discretion of the treating surgeon, depending on the patient’s presenting signs and symptoms.

Outcome measures

Our primary outcomes were re-revision rate and survival rate of RTKA. We reviewed patient charts and electronic medical records and extracted data on age, sex, BMI, smoking status, years of follow-up, Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI) score, and American Society of Anesthesiologists (ASA) classification. The CCI predicts 10-year survival in patients with multiple comorbidities; as the score increases, survival rate declines. Patients with a CCI score of 0 have a 10-year survival rate of 98%, while those with a score of 1 have a slightly lower rate of 96%. As the score increases, survival declines more noticeably: 90% for a score of 2, 77% for a score of 3, and 53% for a score of 4. At a score of 5, the 10-year survival rate drops sharply to 21%, and for patients with a CCI score of 6 or higher, the 10-year survival rate falls to 0%.

Secondary outcomes consisted of postoperative complications and mortality rate, discharge to extended care facilities, visits to the emergency department (ED), readmissions, and 90-day costs. Ninety-day costs were calculated by summing the costs of first admission for RTKA and any subsequent ED visit or readmission within 90 days. All costs were inflation-adjusted to 2021 Canadian dollars.13

Moreover, the institutional database was reviewed to collect both preoperative and most recent postoperative patient-reported functional outcome scores. Questionnaires evaluating the impact of obesity on QoL included the 12-Item Short Form Health Survey (SF-12), Knee Society Score (KSS), and the Western Ontario and McMaster Universities Arthritis Index (WOMAC). The SF-12 measures QoL by assessing the impact of health on a patient’s life physically and mentally.17 The KSS is a clinician-rated scoring system that assesses the knee and functionality before and after arthroplasty.18 The WOMAC is an index that evaluates OA based on pain, stiffness, and physical function.19 All scores were scaled such that a higher score indicates a better outcome.

Statistical analysis

We used IBM SPSS version 26 for data analysis. We summarized demographic and clinical characteristics and outcomes with descriptive statistics (frequencies, means, and standard deviations). Categorical characteristics were compared using χ2 or Fisher exact tests. The normality of continuous variables was tested using the Shapiro–Wilk test. We used analysis of variance for normally distributed, continuous variables and the Kruskal–Wallis test for non-parametric, continuous variables. A Cox proportional hazards model was used to identify the risk factors for re-revision and calculate the survival rate of RTKA. Survival time of RTKA was calculated by subtracting the date of surgery from either the last date of follow-up or the re-revision date. Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was performed with revision for any reason as the end point. The effect of BMI and other covariates on secondary outcomes such as mortality rate, complication rates, rate of discharge to extended care facilities, ED visits within 90 days, and readmission within 90 days were evaluated using odds ratios (ORs). We considered results to be significant at p < 0.05, reported with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).

Ethics approval

The Western University Research Ethics Board (REB) approved our study protocol. The REB is registered with the US Department of Health and Human Services under the IRB registration number IRB 00000940.

Results

Our sampling method yielded 550 patients. The demographic and clinical characteristics of the study participants are summarized in Table 1. There was a statistically significant difference in age across the groups (p < 0.01). In particular, patients with a BMI < 35 were significantly older than those with a BMI ≥ 45 (p < 0.001). The proportion of female patients ranged from 45% to 64% across the groups, but there was no significant difference among the groups (p = 0.163). Groups with higher BMI (≥ 40) tended to have a higher proportion of smokers (p < 0.001). Most patients in all groups had CCI scores between 3 and 6. The proportion of patients with higher comorbidity burden (CCI > 6) ranged from 21% to 31% across BMI categories. No statistically significant differences in CCI scores were observed among the BMI groups (p = 0.081). An ASA classification of 3 predominated across all groups, whereas an ASA classification of 4 was markedly more frequent in patients with higher BMI, reaching 60% in the highest BMI group (p < 0.001), indicating that patients with higher BMI were more likely to present with greater anesthesia risk. The mean duration of surgery was 130 minutes and did not differ significantly among the BMI groups (p = 0.114).

Primary outcome

On univariate analysis, the groups with a BMI of 45 to 49 (hazard ratio [HR] 2.4, 95% CI 1.1 to 5.3, p = 0.028) and ≥ 50 (HR 2.8, 95% CI 1.3 to 6.0, p = 0.007) were the only BMI groups with a statistically significant increase in the risk of re-revision. The overall rate of re-revision was 17%. These 2 BMI groups had the highest rate of re-revision, reaching 25% and 33%, respectively (p = 0.001). Third and fourth revisions were required in 6% (p = 0.146) and 1.6% (p = 0.657) of patients, respectively.

The probability of implant survival in patients with healthy weight, overweight, class I obesity, and class II obesity did not drop below 60% over a 10-year span. However, the probability of RTKA implant survival in patients with class III obesity (all subgroups) dropped to 70% after 4 years and to about 40% after 10 years (Figure 1). The difference in survival distribution among all BMI cohorts was statistically significant (p = 0.001).

Secondary outcomes

Some of our secondary outcomes are summarized in Table 2. The overweight and class I obesity BMI groups had significantly lower mortality than the healthy weight group (OR 0.2, 95% CI 0.1 to 0.6 and OR 0.3, 95% CI 0.1 to 0.7, respectively). Moreover, a BMI ≥ 50 was associated with markedly higher risks of 90-day readmission (OR 3.6, 95% CI 1.4 to 9.0), superficial infection (OR 4.6, 95% CI 1.9 to 11.2), and deep infection (OR 4.3, 95% CI 1.7 to 10.8). In addition, patients with class I obesity (OR 0.4, 95% CI 0.2 to 0.8) and class II obesity (OR 0.1, 95% CI 0.0 to 0.4) were less likely to be discharged to extended care facilities than patients with healthy weight. Other outcomes, including 90-day ED visits, did not differ significantly across BMI groups.

In addition to clinical outcomes, we assessed the economic impact of obesity on revision TKA. The mean average costs of first admission, ED visit, and readmission were $15 708 ± $6895, $99 ± $225, and $1003 ± $4492, while the mean 90-day cost was $16 810 ± $873 (in 2021 Canadian dollars). Figure 2 shows the distribution of the surgical cost and 90-day cost across the BMI categories. The differences observed among the BMI groups in surgical consignment costs (p = 0.261), first admission costs (p = 0.063), ED costs (p = 0.855), and readmission costs (p = 0.163) did not differ significantly; however, we noted a significant difference in the 90-day costs among the groups (p = 0.011). Specifically, a BMI ≥ 50 was associated with significantly higher 90-day costs than healthy weight (p = 0.022), overweight (p = 0.038), class I obesity (p = 0.018), and class II obesity (p = 0.001). A BMI of 40 to 44.9 and 45 to 49.9 was also associated with significantly higher 90-day costs than class II obesity (p = 0.006)

Figure 3 and Figure 4 illustrate the type of RTKA performed and the primary reason for revision, respectively. Single-stage RTKA was the most common procedure (67.1%), followed by 2-stage RTKA (18.4%). All single-stage procedures were carried out for aseptic reasons. As expected, aseptic loosening accounted for most indications (59.9%), followed by infection (21.7%). No significant differences were noted in the proportions of procedure type (p = 0.342) or revision indication (p = 0.349) across the BMI groups.

Regarding functional score outcomes, 207 of the 550 patients (37.6%) included in our study completed at least 1 questionnaire evaluating the impact of obesity on QoL. Table 3 summarizes the difference between post-operative and preoperative functional outcome scores. Patients were followed up postoperatively for a mean of 3 years (range 1 to 6 years). Patients’ SF-12 physical component score (4.8, 95% CI 3.2 to 6.3), WOMAC score (18.1, 95% CI 14.9 to 21.4), and KSS score (60.3, 95% CI 51.1 to 69.4) significantly improved after RTKA. However, when stratified by BMI category, the difference in the degree of improvement across the groups was not statistically significant for SF-12 PCS (p = 0.331), WOMAC (p = 0.730), or KSS (p = 0.670).

Discussion

Obesity has become a substantial challenge for orthopedic surgeons, particularly for procedures related to OA. Our study included 550 patients who underwent RTKA in a single medical institiution in Canada. Our primary objective was to assess the impact of BMI on clinical, financial, and functional outcomes. We found that patients with a BMI of 45 to 49.9 and those with a BMI ≥ 50 had a higher risk of re-revision as well as a greater decline in implant survival. For secondary outcomes, the overweight (BMI 25 to 29.9) and class I obesity (BMI 30 to 34.9) groups had increased mortality, and the group with a BMI ≥ 50 experienced higher rates of 90-day readmission and both superficial and deep infections. In addition, 90-day costs were significantly elevated among all patients with class III obesity (BMI ≥ 40).

The increase in ASA score associated with higher BMI is not a novel finding. Mudumbai and colleagues even developed a predictive model for ASA classification, noting that higher BMI was closely associated with increased ASA classification.14 This means that BMI is an important factor in preoperative risk stratification and should prompt physicians to encourage their patients to reduce their BMI to prevent further complications and potential re-revision after RTKA.

Obesity has long been studied in relation to RTKA. A previous study of 451982 patients undergoing RTKA showed that, after adjusting for demographic and clinical factors, individuals who had their surgery in 2011 and 2012 were more than 4 times as likely to be obese than those who had their surgery in 2002.16 Our primary outcome results were similar to those of several other studies. A systematic review identified a BMI ≥ 40 as one of the modifiable risk factors for re-revision following RTKA.15 Similarly, a cohort study showed that patients with a BMI ≥ 40 had a significantly increased risk of re-revision (HR 3.8, 95% CI 1.2 to 16.5, p < 0.02).17 Moreover, Sisko and colleagues reported similar results regarding survivorship.12 Their analysis of reoperation showed a significant difference at 10 years, with a higher proportion of patients with a BMI ≥ 40 undergoing re-operation (p = 0.05).12 Likewise, the rate of re-revision at 10 years in those patients was significantly higher (p = 0.014).12 It is commonly assumed that patients with a high BMI place excessive load on the knee, leading to greater impact forces on the tibial component, which in turn may contribute to increased loosening and reduced implant longevity.18

Our finding that overweight (BMI 25 to 29.9) and class I obesity (BMI 30 to 34.9) were associated with higher mortality rates contrasts with findings from a large population-based cohort study that reported patients with a BMI > 45 had a 50% higher 10-year risk of death than those with a BMI ≤ 45 (adjusted relative risk 1.50).19 This discrepancy may be attributed to frailty, as our overweight and class I obesity groups were generally older and the increased mortality in these groups was most likely age-related. Our findings showed that postoperative complications, including both superficial and deep infections, as well as 90-day readmissions, were significantly higher in patients with a BMI ≥ 50. This finding is consistent with that of another study reporting increased complication rates of RTKA.20 Keeping in mind the potential benefits of arthroplasty for patients with obesity, managing obesity before surgery can help mitigate complications. Many surgeons recommend preoperative interventions such as weight loss programs and optimization of glycemic control. Given the increased susceptibility of patients with a BMI ≥ 50 to periprosthetic joint infection, appropriate preoperative risk stratification and preventative strategies, such as prolonged postoperative antibiotics and comprehensive wound management, should be employed.21,22

The costs of first admission in our study were similar across all BMI groups, but owing to the increased complications and readmissions in patients with a BMI ≥ 50, their total 90-day costs were significantly higher than those of patients with a BMI < 40. A similar difference in costs was also noted between the class II obesity and class III obesity groups. Thus, a root-cause analysis of readmissions should be conducted. Nevertheless, policies and insurance companies should focus on covering patients with such comorbidities to help reduce financial burden.

Our results showed better functional outcomes following surgery, although the differences among BMI groups were not significant. To our knowledge, no previous study on RTKA has specifically addressed this point. Therefore, further investigations are needed, and future studies may provide contrasting results.

Patients with a BMI ≥ 45 should be counselled about the increased likelihood of re-operation and the possibility of suboptimal results. Our findings highlight the importance of careful preoperative assessment and individualized perioperative planning, emphasizing a proactive, multidisciplinary approach to optimize outcomes and reduce complications in these populations. Future studies on RTKA with larger cohorts are needed to better determine outcomes and establish best practices.

Limitations

The limitations of this study stemmed from its retrospective design, which may have led to underestimation of results owing to lost follow-up data. The varying time intervals between when patients completed the QoL-related questionnaires may have introduced bias and affected the significance of differences among BMI groups.

Although data were prospectively collected, analysis was confined to our institution’s electronic records, restricting access to information on nonoperative management. The single-centre data set further limits external validity, as it may not represent the broader population. Future research could address these limitations by incorporating multiple databases and using alternative methodologies like systematic reviews and meta-analyses to enhance sample size and statistical power. Additionally, assessing cost-effectiveness and including frailty as a covariate in future studies may provide more insight. Prospective studies should also explore nonoperative intervention costs.

Conclusion

Severe class III obesity (BMI ≥ 50) is a risk factor for re-revision, postoperative infection, and readmission rates following RTKA. The significantly higher 90-day costs observed in patients with a BMI of 45 to 49.9 and those with a BMI ≥ 50, without corresponding improvements in functional outcomes, suggest that RTKA in these cohorts is less cost-effective than in patients with lower BMI. Thus, patients with a BMI ≥ 45 should be counselled in terms of their higher risk profile versus comparable benefits to those observed in other BMI groups. The decision to proceed with surgery remains complex, requiring careful clinical judgment to balance patient benefit with the potential for harm. Beyond surgical considerations, this process involves ethical responsibilities and resource allocation. As patients with a very high BMI increasingly present to orthopedic clinics, the development of evidence-based guidelines that integrate surgical outcomes with economic and ethical analyses is imperative. Furthermore, health care and insurance policies regarding RTKA should be reviewed carefully to ensure that patients, including those with a higher BMI, have access to coverage and financial support, rather than being excluded owing to potential risks or costs.
