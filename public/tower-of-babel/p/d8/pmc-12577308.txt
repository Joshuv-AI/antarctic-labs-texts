Digital intelligence for cardiometabolic health management in older adults: protocol for a community-based prospective cohort study
Authors: Yang Li, Fengyi Lu, Qi Wang, Qian Huang, Shengying Ling, Ning Xu, Huai Jiang, Jing Fang, Yue Liu, Liying Huang, Li Wang, Shasha Geng, Peixiao Shen, Qingqing Li, Xin Chen, Yingqian Zhu, Xiaotong Guo, Liang Zheng, Jianli Ge, Chao Meng, Ningyuan Fang, Hua Jiang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCardiovascular diseases (CVDs) represent a significant cause of morbidity and mortality among older adults in China, exerting a substantial influence on their quality of life and life expectancy. The country’s ageing population is predicted to intensify this burden, underscoring the need for the development of innovative and effective management strategies.MethodsThe prospective cohort study will recruit older patients with cardiometabolic diseases from multiple community health service centres in Shanghai. Participants will be comprehensively assessed for CVD risk factors, medications, functional status, and multi-omics data, along with continuous sign monitoring data from wearable bracelets and their electronic health records. The primary objective of the study is twofold: firstly, to explore the independent and joint predictive power of digital biomarkers and clinical indicators for cardiovascular events (new incident/recurrent), dementia, death and other adverse outcomes in older adults; and secondly, to establish a digital twin platform for precision medicine.DiscussionThe study proposes a novel integration of digital intelligence into cardiometabolic health management for older adults. The utilisation of comprehensive data collection and advanced analytical methods is anticipated to generate valuable insights into the intricate interplay of risk factors and outcomes. The development of personalised risk assessment models and intelligent alert systems has the potential to enhance the prevention and treatment of CVDs, thereby improving health outcomes and quality of life for older adults.Trial registrationChiCTR2500096478 (Chinese Clinical Trial Registry). Introduction

Background

Population aging is projected to substantially increase cardiovascular diseases (CVDs) burden in China [1], with modeling studies indicating >50% annual growth in cardiovascular events during 2010–2030 [2, 3]. Despite the downward trend in the age-standardized crude death rate associated with CVD, attributable to advancements in early diagnosis and treatment of CVD and community rehabilitation management in China, the age-specific death rate remains high among the older population and has even increased [4, 5]. This phenomenon can be attributed to the prevalence of multimorbidity among older adults [6], which often leads to impaired physiological function and increased frailty [7, 8]. Consequently, these individuals face a heightened risk of mortality and adverse outcomes, particularly death from CVD [9]. However, patients with multimorbidity are frequently excluded from randomized controlled trials [10, 11], resulting in a paucity of evidence-based guidelines for their management [12–14], specifically for CVD prevention and treatment in patients aged over 75 [15].

CVD risk assessment is a necessary tool for early screening, diagnosis, and management of CVD. However, major CVD risk assessment models [16–20] perform suboptimally in elderly populations due to the following reasons: [1] they were developed in middle-aged cohorts, with underrepresentation of older adults and overestimation of their risk [21–25]; [2] the predictive efficacy of traditional risk factors diminishes with age [26, 27], and even exhibit inverse associations [28, 29]; and [3] most CVD models using Cox proportional hazard modeling are deficient in accounting for competing risks [30]. The European clinical guidelines for CVD prevention now use the Systematic Coronary Risk Evaluation in older persons, versions 1 and 2 (SCORE O.P. and SCORE2-OP) [31, 32], while the Chinese guidelines don’t explicitly recommend CVD models in older adults.

Novel risk factors such as subclinical atherosclerosis indicators, hematologic biochemical markers (high-sensitivity cardiac troponin T (hs-cTnT), N-terminal pro B-type natriuretic peptide (NT-proBNP), and high-sensitivity C-reactive protein (hs-CRP)) [33–36], polygenic risk scores, and older adult-specific predictors such as multimorbidity, frailty, and polypharmacy [37–39] have been used to improve prediction in traditional CVD models. However, the convenience and cost of obtaining these factors must be considered in determining their feasibility in primary care settings. Furthermore, given the constrained life expectancy of older demographics, a more pragmatic approach may involve the use of 3–5 years of short-term risk or lifetime risk prediction, as opposed to 10 years of risk prediction, for the purpose of CVD risk communication and health management decisions in these populations [38]. Additionally, the susceptibility of physical function and metabolic parameters to external factors may lead to fluctuations in CVD risk due to new diagnoses or pharmacologic/non-pharmacologic treatments [40].

Digital intelligence technologies, which include artificial intelligence and Internet of Things (IoT)-enabled devices, serve as foundational tools for precision medicine [41]. These technologies enable dynamic risk stratification and personalized intervention strategies [42]. Consequently, it may be advantageous to leverage digital intelligence technologies to develop a short-term CVD risk prediction model (new incidence/recurrence). This model would effectively address personalized risk trajectories, thereby enhancing cardiometabolic health management in older adults.

Design

The present prospective observational cohort study protocol was developed in accordance with the recommendations of the SPIRIT 2013 guidelines and will be implemented accordingly [44].

Participants and eligibility criteria

This prospective observational cohort study will recruit older patients with cardiometabolic diseases from several community health service centers (CHCs) in Shanghai. Participants will be enrolled irrespective of whether or not they have previously experienced a cardiovascular event, with the exception of those who have experienced CVD within the past year. To ensure the representativeness of the sample population, this study employs a multi-stage cluster sampling method for recruitment.

Prior to December 31, 2024, the research team invited 20 community health service centers, dispersed across diverse geographic areas and socio-economic levels in Shanghai, considering participants’ readiness, the capacity of health service facilities, and the number of older residents and the stability of the service population. This approach was adopted to enhance the representativeness of the participants. The selection of the CHCs was further refined by taking into account the feasibility of implementing baseline and follow-up assessments, as well as the availability of general practitioners (GPs) to participate in the cohort study. Eligible CHCs were required to have at least three GPs willing to participate in the study.

Following the determination of the CHCs and GPs to participate in the study, the GPs will screen potential participants among older patients with cardiometabolic diseases (CMDs, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, hyperlipidemia, ischemic heart disease, and cerebrovascular disease) through the EHRs of each CHC. Program pamphlets and recruitment advertisements will be disseminated via outpatient clinics and telephone. Patients who initially express their willingness to participate will be subsequently contacted by their respective GPs, who will provide a detailed introduction to the study. Personal information will be documented on the participant screening form and reviewed by community coordinators from the cohort management office. Individuals who successfully meet the study’s eligibility criteria will be scheduled for baseline visits. At these visits, the community coordinator will request that the participants sign the informed consent form and complete the baseline data collection. During this phase, at least 50 older patients with any CMD will be recruited through GPs in each CHC.

The recruitment and eligibility screening period for study participants is scheduled between January and June of 2025. Subsequent phases entail formal enrollment and baseline data collection, which is anticipated to commence in August 2025 (taking into account potential delays that might occur during the screening period). The protocol involves a subsequent one-week screening period designed to assess adherence to bracelet wearing among study participants. Individuals who have worn the bracelet for a duration of less than three days and an average of less than 16 h per day during the screening period will be evaluated by their general practitioner (GP) to ascertain the feasibility of enhancing their adherence. Individuals who demonstrate an inability or unwillingness to adhere adequately will be withdrawn from the study and will be requested to return the bracelets. Conversely, participants who have the potential to enhance their adherence will be subjected to a further one-week screening period for reassessment. Those who have not yet met the requisite wearing requirements will also be withdrawn from the study. The enrollment period may be extended to February 2026 due to the exclusion of some participants from the screening period. Upon completion of the aforementioned steps, it is expected that a baseline sample size of at least 1,000 study cohorts will be attained.

The inclusion criteria for this study are as follows: [1] aged 60–80 years old (according to the World Health Organization (WHO) criteria) [2], permanent residence in the local community for more than 6 months [3], previous diagnosis of at least one cardiometabolic disease, including hypertension, type 2 diabetes mellitus, dyslipidemia, ischemic heart disease, and cerebrovascular disease [4], the ability to use smartphones and mobile applications (APPs) by patients themselves or with family assistance, and [5] the willingness to participate in the study and sign the informed consent forms.

Exclusion criteria include the following: [1] occurrence of cardiovascular events within the past year or hospitalization for any reason within the past six months [2], presence of comorbidity with active malignant tumors, severe autoimmune diseases, or long-term use of hormones, immunosuppressants, or biological targeted therapy [3], presence of any neurological disease or severe mental illness (such as bipolar disorder and schizophrenia) leading to dementia or aphasia, or mental retardation that prevents patients from cooperating with the investigator to complete data collection [4], long-term residence in nursing facilities [5], soft tissue skin lesions, breaks, or abnormal blood supply (vascular occlusion, thrombosis or trauma) of both arms [6], inability to wear the bracelet or use mobile APP during the screening period [7], inability to wear the bracelet for prolonged periods of time as required for any reasons, and [8] any other condition deemed inappropriate for enrollment by the investigator.

Our previous research [43] has found that more than one-third of older diabetic patients in the real world also have CVD, and these patients have a higher risk of CVD recurrence. In the context of CMD management, it is imperative to accord it equivalent consideration to the risk of new-onset CVD in older patients without CVD. The exclusion of recent (less than 1 year) CVD events eliminates the potential interference from acute-phase biomarkers (such as elevated hs-CRP) and confounding effects from unstable treatment regimens, thereby reducing reverse causality bias. Given the limited life expectancy and complex multimorbidity of individuals aged over 80 years and the potential longevity paradox, this subgroup was excluded from the study.

Participants may unconditionally refuse to participate in the study or withdraw at any time, such refusal or withdrawal being respected by the researcher without cause. Participants who withdraw cannot generally be replaced, and all participants will be included in the analysis unless they withdraw informed consent. In the event of withdrawal, the individual’s information shall be utilized exclusively in subsequent analyses. A withdrawal form should be recorded with the time and reason for the participant’s withdrawal. In instances where a reason is not provided, this information must still be documented. If the researcher is unable to reconnect with a participant at the conclusion of the follow-up period, the participant should be considered as “lost.” Conversely, if contact is re-established with the “lost” participant during the study, the participant should not be considered as “lost” and should be reassessed in accordance with the previous protocol.

Data collection and management

The study questionnaires have been disseminated via the Research electronic data capture (REDCap) platform [45]. Trained community coordinators will be responsible for completing data collection by scanning the quick response (QR) code and taking anthropometric measurements of the participants. Fasting blood and urine samples will be collected by medical staff from CHCs. Upon completion of the baseline survey, all participants will be given a multi-sign monitoring bracelet (Beijing Microchip Perception Technology) and will be required to wear a 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitor for individualized adjustment of blood pressure monitoring data. Additionally, patient self-reported information based on baseline comorbidities, medications, and other information will be corrected by capturing the patient’s EHRs from all levels of hospitals. Repeat assessments and data collection, including the aforementioned examinations, will be performed annually post-baseline for all participants, with an expected follow-up period of five years. The final duration of follow-up will depend on when recruitment is completed, from which the study will likely continue until December 2031.Some participants will also be invited to participate in nested studies. Anthropometric measurements

The patients’ height (in metres) and weight (in kilograms) will be measured by the community coordinators using an electronic height and weight measuring device, with the waist circumference (in centimetres) being measured using the tape measure. Meanwhile, bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) [46] will be performed on all participants using validated equipment to assess their muscle, fat, and water distribution (see details in Table 1). Following a five-minute period of rest, the participants’ systolic and diastolic blood pressure, as well as their heart rate, will be measured and recorded using an electronic sphygmomanometer. Grip strength (in kilograms) of both hands will be measured using a grip strength meter, and the participants will be asked to complete a 5-times sit-to-stand test (5-STS) and a 6-minute walk test (6MWT). The ultrasound imaging technology will be used to evaluate flow-mediated dilation (FMD) of the brachial artery, thereby measuring participants’ vascular endothelial function as well as their vascular aging [47, 48]. Furthermore, each participant will be required to undergo 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure monitoring, with the objective of calibrating the blood pressure data collected by the bracelet.

Questionnaires

The structured questionnaires utilised in this study will be completed with the REDCap platform, which has been uploaded in advance. These will primarily encompass socio-demographic characteristics, lifestyle factors (including smoking, alcohol consumption, physical activity, and sleep), comorbidities and their corresponding medication, quality of life, psycho-cognitive and nutritional status (see Table 1 for details). The REDCap platform will also integrate anthropometric data and laboratory indicators with the result-filling forms. The completion of all questionnaires is estimated to require approximately one hour per participant.

Wearable bracelets

Participants will be provided with a multi-signs monitoring bracelet to continuously gather multiple signs information, including body temperature, pulse rate, blood oxygen, respiratory rate, physical activity, sleep and blood pressure. Among these, continuous blood pressure monitoring will undergo a combination with 24-hour ambulatory blood pressure data, with the objective being to optimise fitting on an individual level. It is anticipated that this will serve to reduce the influence of photoplethysmogram (PPG) waveform differences on the blood pressure fitting records, due to changes in vascular function in elderly patients. In order to assess physical signs and variations in different time domains as accurately as possible, it is recommended that all participants wear the bracelet for a minimum of 21 days (with an average of at least 16 h per day, of which no less than 6 h at night) during the first month after enrolment. Thereafter, the governance of the monitoring data and the calculation of the derived metrics will be conducted by the researchers in the cohort management office, with the assistance of professional technicians.

Electronic health records

As stated in the preceding study [43, 49], the ‘Shanghai Health Cloud’ information platform is administered by the Shanghai Municipal Government and managed by the Shanghai Municipal Health Commission. The primary function of the platform is to serve as a data repository for the electronic health records of the city’s residents. It facilitates the interconnection, data sharing, and business synergy among tertiary hospitals, district hospitals, and CHCs. The platform has accumulated a substantial amount of data, including more than 30 million residents’ EHRs and 10 billion detailed electronic clinical records. Access to participants’ complete EHRs from this platform database is granted to GPs of all CHCs via a dedicated mobile application, encompassing outpatient and inpatient diagnostic records, pharmaceutical prescriptions, laboratory test and imaging results, and healthcare expenditure data.

The identification of diagnostic data for outcome indicators and common comorbidities within the cohort follow-up will be conducted using participants’ EHRs with rigorous validation measures. The standard validation method is to randomly select a 10% sample from participants annually and have two physicians with extensive professional experience independently review the participants’ EHRs. The internal validity parameters (sensitivity, specificity, and their 95% confidence intervals) and the external validity parameter (predictive value) are then calculated, as well as the inter-rater agreement using kappa values.

No financial compensation is planned for the participants, except for the free examination at all time points and the permanently given bracelet. A report containing the results of the examination (including questionnaire scale assessment, anthropometric data and laboratory findings) will be issued to participants if requested. In the event of any abnormal results, the cohort management office will promptly inform the GPs to decide on further clinical management.

Data management

The collection of all data will be conducted using structured, standardised questionnaire forms on the web-based REDCap platform (Table 2). Raw results from other data sources that are not questionnaire scales (including laboratory analytical and histological data, anthropometric data, wearable bracelet monitoring data, and their derived metrics) will be entered and consolidated by the community coordinators and converted into CSV (Comma-Separated Values) files for subsequent analyses. The validation of these data sources will be conducted, and their integration into SQL (Structured Query Language) data will ensure their availability in accordance with the stipulated requirements of the data collection service. This will guarantee the continuous backup of the complete dataset, as well as its storage on a trusted and secure server.

Study outcomes

Primary outcome

The primary outcome indicator of this cohort study is major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE), including all-cause death, ischaemic heart disease, cardiac arrhythmia (transient or continuous ventricular tachycardia, ventricular fibrillation, atrial fibrillation, atrial flutter, and second- to third-degree atrioventricular block), stroke and heart failure. With the exception of the all-cause death, CVD diagnoses may be classified as new-onset, indicating the absence of baseline CVD, or recurrent, indicating the presence of baseline CVD exceeding one year. All the aforementioned events will be followed up through telephone and EHRs review, which are tracked and judged by the GPs in CHCs.

Statistical analysis

Sample size

According to the recent data from GBD2019, the age-standardized incidence rate of CVD in the Chinese population aged 1–79 years increased from 646.2 per 100,000 person-years to 652.2 per 100,000 person-years between 1990 and 2019 [51]. When the competing risks of death not caused by CVD are not taken into account, the expected CVD incidence rate in the general population can be estimated to be approximately 3.26% in the next five years. This risk is further compounded (estimated to be at least 2-fold) in older adults with multiple CVD risk factor exposures, compared to the general population. Consequently, the expected CVD incidence p0 for the specified population (older adults with CMD) can be estimated to be approximately 6.52%. Given that the relative risk (RR) of the exposure factor under investigation is greater than or equal to 2, it can be deduced that the expected CVD incidence p1 in the exposed group is greater than 13.04%.When the two-sided test is considered with α = 0.05 and 1-β = 0.8, the minimum sample size based on simple random sampling for cohort study can be calculated as 325.

The minimum sample size required for this study can be calculated based on the product of the minimum sample size of simple random sampling and the design effect (DE), where DE = 1 + ICC*(m-1), with ICC representing the intra-cluster correlation coefficient and m denoting the average size of each cluster. Referencing a comparable study [52], the minimum sample size can be estimated as 964 when assuming ICC = 0.03 and accounting for a 20% dropout rate. The objective of this study is to recruit a minimum of 50 participants from each of the 20 CHCs by GPs, thereby achieving a total of at least 1,000 individuals. This approach is intended to attain the hypothesized statistical power.

Data analysis

Initially, the gathered data will undergo a screening process for accuracy, addressing issues of missing data, outliers, and statistical assumptions. The statistical analyses will be conducted using STATA 18.0 software. All statistical tests will be two-sided, with a significance level set at 0.05.

Descriptive analyses will be used to compare the baseline characteristics of participants in the exposed group with those in the control group. In this case, t-tests or non-parametric tests will be used for continuous variables depending on whether they obey a normal distribution. Chi-square tests or Fisher’s exact tests will be used for categorical variables. Baseline variables that are significantly different will be adjusted for in correlation analyses.

Chained-equation multiple interpolation will be used to address missing data, and these results will be compared with the analyses of the complete dataset. In the event of discrepancies between the estimates derived from these two datasets, a sensitivity analysis will be conducted by employing an alternative multiple interpolation method. This process will result in multiple datasets and multiple models run on each dataset. The amalgamation of results will be achieved through the implementation of appropriate methodologies, leading to the generation of combined parameter estimates, standard errors, and 95% confidence intervals.

Correlation analysis will be used to assess the association of exposure factors with outcome indicators. The assessment of exposure factors associated with MACE will be conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves, Log-Rank tests, Cox proportional hazard models, or Fine-Gray models, with consideration given to the impact of CHC clustering, as appropriate. For CVD events other than all-cause mortality, a stratified analysis will be conducted based on the presence or absence of CVD at baseline. This will allow for an accurate assessment of the different effects of exposure factors on the incidence/recurrence of CVD. And the selection of the most suitable analysis model, such as a generalized linear model, a Cox proportional hazard model, or a Fine-Gray model, will be determined by the nature of the secondary outcome indicators.

Subgroup analyses with pertinent variables, such as baseline age, gender, and education level, will be considered to elucidate potential disparities in exposure factor effect values and interactions across distinct subgroups. Furthermore, the employment of mediation effects modeling, structural equation modeling, and related methodologies will be undertaken to establish a causal network among exposure factors, multi-omics data, and outcome phenotypes.

Ethics and data privacy

All participants will receive documented information about the study and informed consent at the beginning. Participants who meet the eligibility criteria and are willing to participate will have their GPs arrange a scheduled visit by telephone. On the visit day, the participants will be required to sign the informed consent form at the CHCs. Community coordinators and/or GPs will be available to answer any additional questions the participants may have. The study protocol will be conducted in strict accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki (2024 version) and relevant laws and/or regulations. The protocol has received approval from the Ethics Committee of Shanghai East Hospital (Approval No. 2024YS-277 on 12/16/2024) and the Ethics Committee of Renji Hospital (Approval No. KY2024-210-B on 12/16/2024). The protocol has also been duly registered with the Chinese Clinical Trial Registry (ChiCTR2500096478, assigned on 24/01/2025).

The personal data and biospecimens of cohort participants will be anonymized for transmission, and participants will be permitted to restrict their specimens and data by modifying informed consent. All data access and collaboration requests will be transmitted after review by the cohort management office and authorization by the principal investigator. The determination of authorship and the number of authors will depend on the specific requirements of the journals concerned, and the principal investigator will be responsible for deciding on the allocation of first and corresponding authors for papers from this study.

Monitoring

It is not anticipated that there will be any additional adverse events in this study, with the exception of problems such as skin sensitization, which may result from prolonged use of the bracelets. In the event that patients experience skin irritation or other issues due to prolonged use of the bracelets, GPs from CHCs will make a definitive diagnosis and provide symptomatic treatment, and report to the cohort management office for recording.

For the baseline and follow-up surveys based on cluster sampling, there is a possibility of information bias between different data collection sites. Therefore, the cohort management office will organise uniform staff training prior to participant recruitment and implementation. It is also important to ensure that the test methods and reference intervals of the report card in each community are thoroughly understood for laboratory indicator testing. In case of discrepancies, alternative methods should be considered, such as post-collection testing by the research team’s laboratory department. In addition, blood samples for multi-omics analysis will be processed and stored in a consistent manner after collection.

The cohort management office will also conduct regular on-site monitoring and verification of data collection, with additional on-site monitoring required in the event of circumstances such as participant withdrawal or excessive deviation. Community coordinators will liaise with GPs to monitor the progress of the study and respond to queries. They will also be responsible for revising and updating the Case Report Forms (CRFs), in particular to assist GPs in locating participants to complete the Outcome Report Form.

During the follow-up period, the cohort management office will also monitor participants’ bracelet wearing and sign monitoring through the management system.For patients with low wearing rate or long time without uploading data, the office will notify their GPs to contact and supervise them. In instances of repeated or protracted anomalous sign data, the GPs will be apprised of the situation and instructed to consult with the patient initially. Once potential device-related factors (such as wearing style or specific body positioning during data collection) have been excluded, an outpatient consultation should be scheduled expeditiously.

Moreover, any significant issues identified during the study process must be reported promptly to the relevant authorities, including the research team’s ethics committee. Investigators are obligated to undergo inspections, audits, ethical reviews, and regulatory inspections related to the study and to provide raw data and relevant documentation.

Discussion

The integration of digital intelligence into cardiometabolic health management for older adults signifies a substantial advancement in addressing the escalating burden of CVDs in China. This community-based prospective cohort study is meticulously designed to comprehensively assess a range of factors affecting cardiometabolic health in older adults to refine the corresponding management strategy.

In comparison with preceding studies [53], the present study will collect a wide array of data, including traditional CVD risk factors, digital biomarkers from wearable devices, and multi-omics data. This approach will facilitate a more holistic understanding of the risk factors and their interactions, which is imperative for developing precise predictive models [54, 55]. Consequently, a digital twin platform for precision cardiometabolic medicine will be developed as a novel and innovative aspect of this study. This platform will facilitate personalised risk assessment and continuous management [56], with the potential to result in a more effective prevention and treatment strategy for both CVDs and other adverse outcomes [54]. Wearable bracelets will be utilised to provide real-time sign monitoring data. This is not only crucial for the early detection of potential health issues but also essential for high-precision simulation and dynamic prediction of the digital twin platform [57]. Furthermore, this real-time monitoring can enhance the timeliness and effectiveness of interventions.

Limitations

It is imperative to acknowledge the inherent challenges and limitations that must be given full consideration. Primarily, the integration of data from a variety of sources (encompassing the REDCap platform, wearable braclets, EHRs and multi-omics data) poses substantial technical and analytical challenges; ensuring data quality and consistency across these sources will be critical to the success of the study. Furthermore, the requirement for participants to utilise wearable devices and engage with digital platforms may present challenges in terms of adherence. The provision of timely feedback to GPs on participants’ adherence and requests for them to remind participants to keep their bracelets on and to upload data regularly is envisaged, but such intensive reminder work may impose an additional burden on GPs and engender resentment among some participants. The optimal frequency of reminders is further complicated by the heterogeneity of individual cultural contexts. Despite the multicentre design of the study, which was intended to ensure that the findings reflected the diversity of the broader communities, the study’s location in CHCs in Shanghai may limit the generalisability of the findings to other regions or populations with different healthcare resources.

In conclusion, the proposed study signifies a substantial advancement in the integration of digital intelligence into cardiometabolic health management for older adults. This research has the potential to enhance our understanding of CVD risk factors in older adults, which may facilitate the development of personalised risk assessment models based on the digital twin platform. This, in turn, may lead to a more effective prevention and treatment strategy. It is recommended that future research continues to explore the potential of digital technologies in various aspects of health management, with a focus on scalability and integration into routine clinical practice.
