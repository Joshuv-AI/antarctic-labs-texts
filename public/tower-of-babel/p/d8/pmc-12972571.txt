Ultrasound-triggered dissolving microneedle platform for cGAS-STING-mediated precision immunotherapy of melanoma
Authors: Juan Wang, Siqi Yuan, Xiuwen Zhang, Yanyan Zhang, Mengting Zhang, Jing Chen, Yan Shen, Yu Tian
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMelanoma is an aggressive skin cancer with a rapidly increasing global incidence. Immunotherapy via the cGAS-STING pathway has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance anti-tumor efficacy in melanoma, however, its clinical translation remains limited by several challenges. Herein, we reported an ultrasound (US)-controllable all-in-one dissolving microneedle (dMN) platform that integrated a ROS-cleavable prodrug, formed by conjugating the VEGF inhibitor linifanib, with ROS-responsive liposomes encapsulating the sonosensitizer MnTCPP (Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs). This system simultaneously inhibited tumor cell proliferation through ROS and linifanib while activating the cGAS–STING pathway to promote immunotherapy. Upon skin insertion, Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs dissolved in the interstitial fluid, releasing Mn@CTL-LPs. Without US exposure, the system remained biologically inert and exhibited minimal cytotoxicity, whereas US irradiation triggered ROS generation, promoted linifanib release, and induced DNA damage, collectively inducing the death of tumor cells. Meanwhile, MnTCPP released into the tumor microenvironment underwent reduction from Mn3+ to Mn2+ in the glutathione (GSH)-rich milieu, which enhanced cGAS-STING activity. Importantly, the synergistic DNA damage and Mn2+-mediated pathway activation robustly promoted dendritic cell (DC) maturation and enhanced CD8+ T-cell infiltration. This spatiotemporally controllable activation ensured high therapeutic precision and safety. Overall, this work presented a noninvasive and US-triggered strategy to potentiate sonodynamic immunotherapy for melanoma.Keywords: Dissolving microneedles, ROS-Sensitive liposomes, cGAS-STING pathway, Sonodynamic immunotherapy, Melanoma

Introduction

Melanoma is one of the most aggressive and therapy-resistant skin cancers, with its incidence rising globally [1]. Surgical resection and chemotherapy are the most common therapeutic strategies, which often face limited response rates, high systemic toxicity, and drug resistance. Over the past few years, immunotherapy, especially immune checkpoint blockade (ICB), has emerged as a novel therapeutic modality revolutionizing the melanoma treatment landscape [2]. Despite its promise, a significant proportion of patients do not receive long-lasting benefits, primarily due to an immunosuppressive tumor microenvironment and inadequate pre-existing tumor-infiltrating immune cells [3,4]. This indicates the urgent need for revealing novel approaches that can effectively boost anti-tumor immunity and circumvent resistance mechanisms (see Scheme 1).

Schematic illustration of US-triggered and controllable Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs for melanoma therapy. After administration into the dorsal skin, the dMNs rapidly dissolved in the interstitial fluid, releasing the incorporated Mn@CTL-LPs. Upon US irradiation, Mn@CTL-LPs generated abundant ROS, triggering the release of Lin and the sonosensitizer MnTCPP. The released MnTCPP was subsequently reduced from Mn3+ to Mn2+ in the GSH-rich tumor microenvironment. DNA damage induced by Lin and ROS, together with Mn2+, synergistically activated the cGAS–STING pathway, promoting DC maturation and enhancing CD8+ T-cell infiltration, ultimately eliciting potent antitumor immune responses.

The cyclic GMP-AMP synthase (cGAS)-stimulator of interferon genes (STING) signaling pathway has been identified as a pivotal sentinel of innate immunity, with the ability to enhance immunotherapy [5]. In response to double-stranded DNA (dsDNA) derived from damaged mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) or nuclear DNA, cGAS induces the production of cyclic AMP-GMP (cGAMP), which promotes the translocation of STING to Golgi apparatus, triggering the phosphorylation and activation of TBK1 and IRF3 [6]. This cascade culminates in the production of multiple cytokines, including type I interferons (IFNs) and pro-inflammatory cytokines, thereby reprogramming the TME from “cold” to “hot” and enhancing the efficacy of ICB therapy [7]. Correspondingly, several STING agonists, including the cyclic dinucleotides (CDNs) and non-nucleotides, have been developed and demonstrated promising anti-tumor efficacy [8]. However, their clinical translation is hindered by challenges such as rapid clearance, low membrane permeability, off-target toxicity, and the need for invasive intratumoral injections. Therefore, a sustained, localized, and self-reinforcing delivery platform represents a promising strategy for activating the STING pathway in antitumor immunotherapy.

Inspired by the role of dsDNA as the natural ligand for STING, we proposed that exogenous dsDNA could serve as a candidate for activating STING-mediated antitumor immunity. Recently, ultrasound (US) irradiation has emerged as a promising strategy to activate the cGAS-STING pathway [[9], [10], [11]]. For instance, metal-organic framework (MOF) nanosheets have been reported to elicit a robust immune response by generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) under US irradiation, which in turn induced mitochondrial and nuclear DNA damage, thereby triggering cGAS-STING pathway activation [9]. Of importance, evidence from preclinical and clinical studies has established manganese (Mn) ions as a potent agonist that enhances the sensitivity of the cGAS-STING pathway to dsDNA [[12], [13], [14], [15]]. Mechanistically, Mn2+ directly binds to cGAS, enhancing its sensitivity to dsDNA, even at low concentrations, and boosting its enzymatic activity, thereby promoting cGAS-STING signaling [16]. Additionally, Mn2+ strengthens the interaction between cGAMP and STING, further amplifying pathway activation [12]. Therefore, we hypothesized that the combination of US irradiation and Mn2+ can synergistically activate the cGAS-STING pathway for enhanced immunotherapy. On the other hand, the combination of immunotherapy with targeted inhibitors has emerged as a promising strategy to enhance therapeutic efficacy [17,18]. For instance, the combination of VEGF and PD-1 inhibition significantly improved antitumor responses, response durability, and memory responses in melanoma mouse models [17]. Similarly, a STING-activating nanoparticle co-delivering dsDNA and the chemotherapeutic doxorubicin has also shown promise for melanoma treatment [19].

To effectively integrate and deliver such combination therapies, microneedles (MNs) offer a versatile and minimally invasive solution [20]. Due to the sufficient length and mechanical property of their needle tips, therapeutics loaded in MNs can directly reach tumor cells in epithelial cancers, potentially facilitating the elimination of malignant cells [21]. Moreover, the epidermis and dermis are rich in immune cells, such as Langerhans cells (LCs) and dendritic cells (DCs), which can be activated upon MN insertion to mount robust immune responses, further improving the effectiveness of cancer immunotherapy [22,23]. These MNs facilitate the co-delivery of multiple therapeutic agents in a single administration, establishing an all-in-one platform with remarkable multifunctional potential [24,25].

To address these challenges, we herein developed a ROS-responsive and US-controllable all-in-one dissolving microneedles (dMNs) system designed for the synergistic combination of STING activation and targeted therapy. Specifically, a ROS-cleavable prodrug, Chol-tk-Lin, was synthesized by conjugating the VEGF inhibitor linifanib (Lin) to cholesterol, followed by the construction of ROS-responsive liposomes encapsulating the sonosensitizer MnTCPP. To achieve efficient active tumor targeting, the αvβ3 integrin–binding peptide c(RGDfK) was functionalized on the liposomal surface, yielding Mn@CTL-LPs. These Mn@CTL-LPs were subsequently incorporated into pullulan-based dMNs for transdermal administration (Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs). Importantly, this system remained biologically inert and exhibited excellent biosafety in the absence of US, while US irradiation precisely triggered its therapeutic activation in a spatiotemporally controlled manner. Upon US irradiation, Mn@CTL-LPs generated abundant ROS, which not only triggered the release of Lin but also induced mitochondrial DNA damage and the death of tumor cell. Meanwhile, the released MnTCPP underwent reduction from Mn3+ to Mn2+ within the tumor microenvironment rich in glutathione (GSH). The DNA damage caused by ROS and Lin, together with Mn2+, synergistically activated the cGAS-STING pathway, promoted DC maturation, and enhanced CD8+ T-cell infiltration. This US-triggered and controllable system effectively initiated a robust systemic antitumor immune response while minimizing off-target toxicity, offering a safe and efficient strategy for melanoma therapy.

Materials and methods

Materials

Mn (III) Meso-Tetra (4-carboxyphenyl) porphine chloride (MnTCPP), 1,2-Dimyristoyl-sn-glycero-3-phosphocholine (DMPC) and cholesterol were purchased from MedChem Express (Monmouth Junction, NJ, USA). DSPE-PEG-c(RGDfk) were obtained from Shanghai Apeptide Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China). Anti-CD45-APC, anti-CD11c-PE, anti-MHC-II-PerCP/Cy5.5, anti-CD80-PE/Cy7, anti-CD86-FITC, anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD4-PE/Cy7, anti-CD8-PE and anti-PerCP/Cy5.5- IFN-γ antibodies were purchased from Biolegend (San Diego, CA, USA). Phospho-STING (72971), Phospho-TBK1/NAK (5483), Phospho-IRF-3 (4947), and Phospho-Histone H2A.X (9718) were purchased from Cell signaling Technology. Alexa 488-conjugated antibody (A-11008) was purchased from ThermoFisher. Ki67 antibody (GB111499) was purchased from Servicebio. Mouse IFN-γ (KMC4021C), IFN-β (424001) and IL-6 (KMC0061) Elisa kits were purchased from Invitrogen. JC-1 (M8650) was purchased from Solarbio.

The mouse melanoma cell line B16F10 was purchased from the Cell Bank of Shanghai Institutes for Biological Sciences, Chinese Academy of Sciences (China). B16F10 was cultured with Dulbecco's modified Eagle medium (DMEM, Gibco) containing 10% fetal bovine serum (FBS, Gibco) and 100 U/mL penicillin-streptomycin (Gibco). Cells were maintained in 5% CO2 at 37 °C and passaged every third day.

Female C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks) were purchased from Shanghai Model organisms (Shanghai, China). All experimental procedures were approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Shanghai University.

Synthesis of ROS-cleavable cholesterol-thioketal linked Lin (Chol-tk-Lin)

Chol-tk-Lin was synthesized via a Steglich esterification reaction using N,N-dicyclohexylcarbodiimide (DCC) and 4-dimethylaminopyridine (DMAP). Briefly, anhydrous thioketal (1 eq, 3 mmol), DCC (1.2 eq, 3.6 mmol), and DMAP (0.1 eq, 0.3 mmol) were dissolved in anhydrous dichloromethane (DCM, 30 mL) at room temperature under an argon atmosphere. The reaction mixture was stirred for 2 h, after which cholesterol was added and the solution was further stirred for 8 h. The solvent was then removed by rotary evaporation to obtain the intermediate compound (Chol-tk). To conjugate Chol-tk with Lin, an esterification reaction was performed. Chol-tk (1 eq, 0.24 mmol) and Lin (1 eq, 0.24 mmol) were dissolved in anhydrous DCM (15 mL). Subsequently, 1-hydroxybenzotriazole (HOBt, 1.2 eq, 0.29 mmol) and 1-(3-dimethylaminopropyl)-3-ethylcarbodiimide (EDC, 1.2 eq, 0.29 mmol) were added, and the mixture was stirred for 2 h. Triethylamine (TEA, 3 eq, 0.7 mmol) was then introduced to maintain an alkaline environment, and the reaction was allowed to proceed under continuous stirring for 48 h at room temperature. The product Chol-tk-Lin was subsequently concentrated, precipitated, and dried under reduced pressure at room temperature. The chemical structure was confirmed by 1H NMR spectroscopy (600 MHz, CDCl3, δ).

Preparation of Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs, CTL-LPs

The ROS-cleavable Mn@CTL-LPs were prepared using the reverse-phase evaporation method. Briefly, DMPC (0.02 mol), cholesterol (0.01mol), DSPE-PEG-c(RGDfk) (0.0025 mol) and Chol-tk-Lin (0.007 mmol) were dissolved in 500 μL chloroform. The organic phase was sonicated in an ice bath at 200 W for 2 min (2 s on/2 s off). Subsequently, 2.5 mL PBS (pH 7.4) containing MnTCPP was added, and the mixture was further sonicated at 200 W for 5 min to form a water-in-oil (W/O) emulsion. The organic solvent was then removed under reduced pressure using a rotary evaporator at 35 °C. The resulting suspension was extruded through polycarbonate membranes (200 nm) using a mini-extruder to obtain Mn@CTL-LPs for further analysis. Control liposomes were prepared using the same procedure. Liposomes without ROS-cleavable Chol-tk-Lin (Mn@LPs) were formulated with DMPC, cholesterol, and DSPE-PEG-c(RGDfk) while encapsulating MnTCPP. Liposomes without MnTCPP but containing Chol-tk-Lin (CTL-LPs) were prepared using DMPC, cholesterol, DSPE-PEG-c(RGDfk), and Chol-tk-Lin.

Characterization of Mn@CTL-LPs

Size distribution, zeta potential and morphology

The particle size, polydispersity index (PDI), and zeta potential of the prepared liposomes were measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS, Zetasizer Nano ZS90, Malvern) at an appropriate concentration in PBS (pH 7.4). All measurements were performed in triplicate, and results are reported as mean ± SD. The morphology of the liposomes was examined by transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Briefly, a drop of Mn@CTL-LPs suspension was placed on a carbon-coated copper grid to form a thin film, followed by negative staining with 2% (w/v) phosphotungstic acid. After air-drying at room temperature, the samples were imaged using TEM.

Encapsulation efficiency

The encapsulation efficiency (EE) of MnTCPP was determined using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer at 462 nm. Lin loading was quantified by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC 1100 system, Agilent Technologies, USA) at 270 nm. The mobile phase consisted of a degassed mixture of sodium potassium dihydrogen phosphate solution, acetonitrile, and methanol (30:40:30, v/v/v) at pH 2.8, with a flow rate of 1.0 mL/min. Chromatographic separation was performed on a reversed-phase C18 column (150 mm × 4.6 mm i.d., 5 μm particle size) maintained at 25 °C.

Stability

Mn@CTL-LPs were incubated in PBS (pH 7.4), and their particle sizes were measured daily by DLS over a period of seven days.

Detection of ROS production by Mn@CTL-LPs and intracellular ROS detection

1,3-Diphenylisobenzofuran (DPBF) was used to evaluate the in vitro 1O2 generation capability of Mn@CTL-LPs and Mn@LPs. Liposomes (MnTCPP concentration: 10 μg/mL, 2 mL) were rapidly mixed with fresh DPBF solution (1 mg/mL, 25 μL) and kept in the dark. The mixture was then subjected to US irradiation for 0, 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5 min (1 MHz, 1.5 W/cm2, 50% duty cycle). The absorption spectra were recorded every minute using a UV–Vis spectrophotometer.

2′,7′-dichlorodihydrofluorescein diacetate (DCFH-DA) was used to evaluate intracellular ROS generation. B16F10 cells were seeded in 12-well plates at a density of 2 × 105 cells per well and incubated overnight. The medium was then replaced with 1 mL of fresh culture medium containing PBS, Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs, or CTL-LPs (MnTCPP concentration: 10 μg/mL), followed by incubation at 37 °C in 5% CO2 for 4 h. After washing three times with PBS, the cells were incubated with 1 mL DCFH-DA solution (1/1000) for 20 min. Subsequently, each well was washed twice with PBS and subjected to US irradiation (1 MHz, 1.5 W/cm2). The cells were then fixed with 4% paraformaldehyde for 10 min and washed three times with PBS. Finally, the fluorescence of DCF (λex = 488 nm, λem = 525 nm) was immediately captured using an inverted fluorescence microscope (KEYENCE, BZ-X810).

ROS-responsiveness of MnTCPP and Lin release

Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs, and CTL-LPs were dialyzed using dialysis membranes (MWCO = 3500 Da, Manufacturer) against PBS, either with or without 1 mM H2O2, at 37 °C. At predetermined time intervals, 1.0 mL of the medium was withdrawn and replaced with an equal volume of fresh medium. The release of MnTCPP was quantified by UV–Vis spectrophotometry, while Lin was analyzed using the HPLC system described above.

Antitumor activity of Mn@CTL-LPs in vitro

In vitro cytotoxicity assays

The cytotoxicity of Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs and CTL-LPs with or without US irradiation against B16F10 cells was evaluated using the Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay. Briefly, B16F10 cells (1 × 104 cells per well) were seeded into 96-well plates and incubated for 24 h. The medium was then replaced with fresh medium containing blank solvent, Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs and CTL-LPs. During incubation, US irradiation was applied at 6 h for 1 min (1 MHz, 1.5 W/cm2). After 48 h, CCK-8 solution was added to each well, followed by incubation for 4 h. The absorbance of the resulting formazan was measured at 490 nm using a microplate reader (Tecan, Groedig, Austria). Cell viability in the absence of US irradiation was assessed in parallel under the same conditions.

Live/dead cell staining of 3D tumor spheroids

The cytotoxicity in 3D tumor spheroids was assessed using the Calcein-AM/PI double-staining method. To establish the spheroids, a 2 % agarose gel was prepared in DMEM. Briefly, 100 μL of 2% agarose was added to each well of a 96-well plate, followed by ultraviolet irradiation for 60 min in a laminar flow cabinet to ensure sterilization. Subsequently, 100 μL of B16F10 cell suspension (approximately 2000 cells per well) was seeded onto the agarose. After incubation for 5 to 7 days, uniform 3D tumor spheroids were formed. Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs and CTL-LPs were then added to each well at an equivalent MnTCPP concentration of 10 μg/mL. After 6 h of incubation, part of the spheroids was exposed to US irradiation for 1 min. All spheroids were further incubated for 2 h, then washed and stained with Calcein-AM (5 μg/mL) and PI (10 μg/mL) for 30 min. Finally, the stained spheroids were imaged using confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) with z-axis scanning (KEYENCE, BZ-X810).

Detection of DNA damage

The expression of γ-H2AX was evaluated by CLSM and western blotting analysis. For CLSM, B16F10 cells (5 × 104) were seeded in 24-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C. The cells were then treated with Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs and CTL-LPs (MnTCPP 10 μg/mL) for 24 h. Following treatment, the cells were washed with PBS and fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde. Cells were subsequently blocked with 1% BSA, permeabilized with 0.1 % Triton X-100, and incubated overnight at 4 °C with γ-H2AX primary antibody. After three washes with PBS, cells were incubated with Alexa Fluor 488-conjugated secondary antibody for 2 h. Finally, the cells were washed three times with PBS, counterstained with DAPI, and imaged by CLSM.

Western blot was performed as previously reported [26]. Briefly, B16F10 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 106 cells per well and cultured for 24 h. The cells were then treated with Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs, or CTL-LPs (MnTCPP: 10 μg/mL) for 24 h. After treatment, cells were washed and lysed on ice using RIPA buffer supplemented with protease inhibitors to extract proteins. Protein concentrations were determined using a bicinchoninic acid (BCA) assay kit. Equivalent amounts of protein were separated by 10 % SDS-PAGE and transferred onto poly (vinylidene fluoride) (PVDF) membranes, which were blocked with 5 % skim milk in TBS-T for 1 h at room temperature. The membranes were then incubated overnight at 4 °C with primary antibodies against γ-H2AX, followed by incubation with horseradish peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated secondary antibodies for 1 h at room temperature. Protein bands were visualized using X-ray film, and GAPDH served as a loading control.

Detection of mitochondria damage

Mitochondrial damage was assessed using a JC-1 assay kit. B16F10 cells were seeded in 6-well plates at a density of 1 × 105 cells per well and cultured for 24 h, followed by treatment with Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs, or CTL-LPs (MnTCPP: 10 μg/mL) for another 24 h. After 6 h incubation, cells were then exposed to US irradiation or left untreated. To evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential, cells were washed twice with PBS, and incubated with JC-1 dye in serum-free medium at 37 °C for 30 min. Finally, the nuclei were counterstained with DAPI, and fluorescence images were acquired using CLSM (KEYENCE, BZ-X810).

STING in vitro and western blot analysis

STING pathway activation was evaluated by western blot analysis of P-STING, P-TBK1, and P-IRF3. B16F10 cells were treated with Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs or CTL-LPs under the same conditions as described for γ-H2AX analysis. After treatment, proteins were extracted, quantified, and subjected to SDS-PAGE and PVDF membrane transfer. Membranes were incubated with primary antibodies against P-STING, P-TBK1, or P-IRF3, followed by HRP-conjugated secondary antibodies, and visualized using X-ray films. GAPDH was served as a loading control.

Fabrication and characterization of Mn@CTL-LPs incorporated dissolving microneedles (Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs)

Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs were fabricated by micromolding technique. In brief, a 15% (w/v) pullulan solution was prepared in phosphate buffer (10 mM, pH 7.4). Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs, or CTL-LPs were diluted with the same buffer and then mixed with the pullulan solution to yield a final MnTCPP concentration of 10 μg/mL. Aliquots of 200 μL of the resulting mixture were dispensed into PDMS molds and centrifuged at 4000 rpm for 30 min at 4 °C to ensure complete filling of the microcavities. The molds were subsequently dried at 37 °C for 12 h to obtain the dissolving microneedle arrays, namely Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs, Mn@LPs dMNs, and CTL-LPs dMNs.

The morphology of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs was characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM). Samples were sputter-coated with a thin layer of gold to enhance conductivity, and images were acquired at an accelerating voltage of 15 kV.

The skin penetration ability of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs was examined ex vivo. dMN arrays were applied to mouse skin for 15 min and then removed. The treated skin was stained with 0.4 % trypan blue for 10 min to visualize penetration sites. For histological analysis, skin samples were fixed in 10 % formalin for 48 h, embedded in paraffin, sectioned into 5-μm slices, and stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E). To further assess the insertion depth, Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs were labeled with Rhodamine B (RB). After 15 min of application to mouse skin, the dMNs were removed, and the skin was imaged by CLSM with z-stack scanning from the surface down to the penetration depth.

The mechanical strength of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs was measured using a texture analyzer (TMS-PRO, FTC, USA). dMN arrays were mounted on the bottom plate, and a vertical force was applied by a cylindrical probe at a speed of 0.5 mm/min. The probe displacement and fracture force were recorded until the dMNs broke.

In vivo antitumor efficacy

The melanoma mouse model was established using female C57BL/6 mice (6-8 weeks). Briefly, 100 μL of B16F10 cell suspension (3.0 × 105 cells) was subcutaneously injected into the dorsal region. When the tumors reached a volume of 50–60 mm3, the mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5 per group): (a) control (no US), (b) blank dMNs + US, (c) Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs (no US), (d) CTL-LPs dMNs + US, (e) Mn@LPs dMNs + US, and (f) Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs + US. Treatments were administered on days 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 post-inoculation. For groups requiring US, mice were exposed to US irradiation (1 MHz, 1.5 W/cm2, 5 min) at 6 h after dMN application. Tumor volumes and body weights were measured every two days. On day 16, all mice were sacrificed, tumors were excised and weighed, and major organs were collected for further analysis.

Excised tumors and organs were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, and sectioned at 5 μm thickness. Sections were subjected to H&E staining for histopathological evaluation, as well as inflorescence staining against Ki67 staining to assess tumor cell proliferation. In addition, immunohistochemical staining was performed to evaluate expression of p-STING. Tumor tissues were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde, embedded in paraffin, sectioned, and subsequently stained with p-STING antibodies for imaging analysis.

To evaluate cytokine secretion in serum, blood was collected via the retro-orbital sinus, and serum was isolated by centrifugation. Levels of inflammatory cytokines, including IL-6 and IFN-γ, were measured using ELISA kits according to the manufacturer's instructions.

DC maturation, intratumoral infiltrating lymphocytes and IFN-γ expression analysis

To investigate DC maturation and intratumoral lymphocyte infiltration, tumors were harvested on day 16. Female C57BL/6 mice were subcutaneously implanted with B16F10 cells (3 × 105) in the dorsal region. When tumor volumes reached approximately 50 mm3, mice were randomly assigned to six groups (n = 3) and treated as previously described. Mice were sacrificed on day 16, and the tumors were collected to prepare single-cell suspensions. For immune cell analysis, the single-cell suspensions were incubated with specific antibodies. DC maturation was assessed by staining 100 μL of the suspension with anti-CD45-APC, anti-CD11c-PE, anti-MHC-II-PerCP/Cy5.5, anti-CD80-PE/Cy7, and anti-CD86-FITC antibodies, followed by incubation for 30 min at room temperature in the dark. T cell infiltration was analyzed by staining another 100 μL of the suspension with anti-CD45-APC, anti-CD3-FITC, anti-CD4-PE/Cy7, and anti-CD8-PE antibodies under the same conditions.

To evaluate intratumoral IFN-γ expression, cell suspensions were first stimulated with a cell stimulation cocktail (eBioscience, Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) for 6 h. The cells were then stained with anti-CD45-APC, anti-CD3-FITC, and anti-CD8-PE antibodies for 30 min at 4 °C in the dark. After washing, the cells were resuspended in Perm/Wash™ buffer (Thermo Fisher Scientific, USA) and stained with PerCP/Cy5.5-conjugated anti-IFN-γ antibody. Finally, all samples were analyzed using flow cytometry (Beckman).

Statistical analysis

All data are presented as mean ± SD. Experiments were performed at least three times independently. Statistical comparisons were conducted using one-way or two-way ANOVA, with p < 0.05 considered statistically significant. In all figures, significance is denoted as ∗p < 0.05, ∗∗p < 0.01, ∗∗∗p < 0.001, and ∗∗∗∗p < 0.0001. All statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism 10 (GraphPad Software, Inc., La Jolla, CA, USA).

Results and discussion

Synthesis and structure confirmation of ROS-cleavable Chol-tk-Lin as prodrug

The Lin prodrug (Chol-tk-Lin) was rationally designed by conjugating Lin to cholesterol via a ROS-cleavable thioketal linker. The synthetic route is summarized in two steps, as illustrated in Fig. 1A. In the 1H NMR spectrum of cholesterol (Fig. 1B), the signal at δ 5.25 ppm corresponded to the -CH = group on the cyclopentane ring, while δ 4.48 ppm was attributed to the hydroxyl (-OH) group. For Lin, the 1H NMR signals at δ 11.68 ppm, δ 8.45 ppm, and δ 9.21 ppm were assigned to the -NH-N = group on the diazole and the NH-CO-NH moieties, respectively. The successful synthesis of Chol-tk-Lin was confirmed by the appearance of a new signal at δ 1.53 ppm, corresponding to the methyl protons in the thioketal linker, which was absent in the spectra of both cholesterol and Lin. Additionally, the chemical shifts at δ 0.85 ppm, δ 4.48 ppm, and δ 5.25 ppm were consistent with those observed in cholesterol, and the signals at δ 8.45 ppm and δ 9.21 ppm matched those of Lin. These results collectively demonstrated that Chol-tk-Lin was successfully synthesized through conjugation of cholesterol and Lin via a thioketal bond.

Preparation and characterization of ROS-cleavable Mn@CTL-LPs

Mn@CTL-LPs were successfully prepared. The morphology of Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs, and CTL-LPs was confirmed by TEM to be uniform, spherical, and unilamellar (Fig. 1C). As shown in Fig. 1D, the average diameter of Mn@CTL-LPs was 131.1 nm, comparable to that of Mn@LPs (125.8 nm) and CTL-LPs (135.2 nm). Furthermore, Mn@CTL-LPs exhibited a relatively low PDI, consistent with a monomodal size distribution. The zeta potential of Mn@CTL-LPs was-7.86 mV (Fig. S1). Notably, MnTCPP was efficiently encapsulated in the liposomes, achieving high EE of more than 85 % in both Mn@CTL-LPs and Mn@LPs (Fig. S2). Considering that liposome leakage is closely associated with particle size increase, the stability of Mn@CTL-LPs was evaluated by monitoring changes in particle size (Fig. S3). Both the particle size and PDI of Mn@CTL-LPs remained stable over 7 days of incubation at 4 °C. Taken together, Mn@CTL-LPs demonstrated superior physicochemical properties in terms of size, EE and stability were therefore selected for subsequent in vitro and in vivo drug evaluation.

The Mn 2p3/2 spectrum from X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) was used to determine the oxidation state of Mn (Fig. 1E). The results indicated that Mn existed predominantly as Mn3+ in PBS. However, when incubated with 10 mM GSH, partial reduction occurred, resulting in the coexistence of Mn3+ and Mn2+ in this tumor-mimicking environment.

Since the drug conjugate Chol-tk-Lin is linked via a thioketal bond, it is crucial to verify whether ROS generated upon US irradiation can trigger cleavage of this bond and consequently accelerate the release of Lin and encapsulated MnTCPP. First, the 1O2 generation capability of Mn@CTL-LPs under US irradiation was assessed using the 1O2 probe DPBF, whose UV absorbance decreases upon reaction with 1O2. As shown in Fig. 1F, the characteristic DPBF peaks gradually diminished over time under US exposure, indicating a time-dependent production of 1O2 by Mn@CTL-LPs. Next, the ROS-responsiveness of Mn@CTL-LPs was evaluated using 1 mM H2O2 as a ROS trigger. Fig. 1G shows the release profiles of MnTCPP from Mn@CTL-LPs and Mn@LPs. In PBS, MnTCPP was released relatively quickly during the first 4 h, with cumulative release of 36.09 % and 31.1 % for Mn@CTL-LPs and Mn@LPs, respectively, and reached approximately 70.8 % and 64.6 % over 72 h. In the presence of 1 mM H2O2, both the rate and extent of release were significantly enhanced, with Mn@CTL-LPs releasing 89.7% of MnTCPP at 24 h and reaching 93.8% at 72 h. The release of Lin from Mn@CTL-LPs and CTL-LPs was also rapid and nearly complete (Fig. 1H). CTL-LPs released 84.8% and Mn@CTL-LPs released 73.6% within 12 h, indicating strong and fast ROS-responsiveness, and over 95% Lin was released from both formulations within 72 h.

These results suggested that Mn@CTL-LPs exhibited ROS-sensitive drug release. Specifically, US-induced 1O2 triggered cleavage of the thioketal bond in Chol-tk-Lin, promoting Lin release and simultaneously causing partial decomposition of Mn@CTL-LPs, which facilitated the release of encapsulated MnTCPP.

In vitro antitumor effects of Mn@CTL-LPs

To assess the in vitro antitumor efficacy, cellular uptakes, ROS, CCK-8 and cytotoxicity assays in 3D tumor spheroids were conducted using B16F10 cells. First of all, cellular uptake of Mn@CTL-LPs was investigated by labeling them with RB (Fig. 2A, S4A). Compared with free RB, both RB@CTL-LPs and RB@LPs exhibited significantly higher uptake, which could be attributed to the liposomal structure and their relatively small particle size. To further quantitatively evaluate intracellular delivery, the Mn content in B16F10 cells after 24 h incubation with Mn@LPs or Mn@CTL-LPs was measured by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP–MS). As shown in Fig. S5, cells treated with Mn@CTL-LPs exhibited a substantial intracellular Mn accumulation, comparable to that observed in the Mn@LPs group, confirming efficient cellular internalization of Mn-containing liposomes. These results collectively demonstrate that the liposomal formulation effectively facilitates intracellular Mn delivery, providing the basis for subsequent Mn-mediated cGAS–STING activation. To examine the effect of US irradiation in vitro, DCFH-DA was employed to detect ROS generation in B16F10 cells. Upon oxidation by ROS, DCFH-DA is converted into the highly green-fluorescent product, dichlorofluorescein (DCF). Thus, the intensity of green fluorescence under 485 nm excitation directly reflects the level of ROS in tumor cells, which was observed using CLSM. As shown in Fig. 2B and S4B, the control group displayed negligible DCF fluorescence with US irradiation. Similarly, CTL-LPs produced only weak signals due to the absence of the sonosensitizer MnTCPP. In contrast, both Mn@CTL-LPs and Mn@LPs markedly enhanced ROS production compared with CTL-LPs and the control group. Notably, Mn@CTL-LPs exhibited the strongest ROS generation, consistent with the UV–Vis absorption results shown in Fig. 1F. Having confirmed the efficient internalization of Mn@CTL-LPs and ROS generation, their cytotoxicity was further assessed via CCK-8. As shown in Fig. 2C, Mn@CTL-LPs, CTL-LPs, and Mn@LPs displayed comparable cell viabilities to the control group, indicating that these formulations were biocompatible and did not induce apoptosis in the absence of US irradiation. In contrast, upon US exposure, Mn@CTL-LPs exhibited the most pronounced cytotoxic effect, leaving only a negligible fraction of viable cells, with IC50 value less than 1 μg/mL. In comparison, CTL-LPs and Mn@LPs required concentrations of 10 μg/mL or higher to induce significant cell death. These results highlighted that the antitumor activity of Mn@CTL-LPs was strictly US-dependent, remaining safe without US while inducing over 95% cell death under US due to the synergistic inhibition effects based on ROS-responsive release of Lin and ROS generation by the MnTCPP sonosensitizer. To further investigate cytotoxicity and drug penetration, live/dead staining of 3D tumor spheroids was performed (Fig. 2D). In the absence of US, Mn@CTL-LPs induced minimal cell death, with most cells remaining viable (green). Upon US treatment, however, CTL-LPs and Mn@LPs induced increased cell death along with drug penetration to a depth of 80 μm. Notably, Mn@CTL-LPs demonstrated the strongest cytotoxicity, with extensive dead cells (red) and penetration reaching 80 μm. Interestingly, US irradiation alone also caused moderate cell death and penetration to 80 μm, likely attributed to the cavitation effect. This suggested that US not only enhanced cytotoxicity but also may facilitate deeper drug penetration. Collectively, the results from both the CCK-8 assay and 3D spheroid studies confirmed that Mn@CTL-LPs were safe without US exposure (maintaining high cell viability), while under US irradiation they exerted superior cytotoxic and penetration capabilities compared with other groups. Collectively, these findings demonstrated that Mn@CTL-LPs could effectively promote intracellular ROS production upon US irradiation.

Induced DNA damage and activated cGAS-STING pathway by Mn@CTL-LPs in vitro

Cellular oxidative stress induced by US irradiation and chemical inhibitor drugs, such as Lin, can damage DNA in both mitochondria and the nucleus, and activate the cGAS-STING pathway, thereby eliciting the anti-tumor immune response. To investigate the effects of Mn@CTL-LPs on mitochondrial membrane potential, JC-1 staining was performed. The result showed that in comparison with other groups, in the presence of US Mn@CTL-LPs treated group displayed strongest green fluorescence and weakest red fluorescence, indicative of the most serious mitochondrial dysfunction (Fig. 3A). Besides, the impact on nuclear DNA double-strand breaks was assessed via immunofluorescence staining and Western blot analysis of γ-H2AX, a DNA damage marker, in B16F10 cells following various treatments (Fig. 3B–D). CLSM images revealed that cells treated with Mn@CTL-LPs in the absence of US exhibited minimal green fluorescence, indicating negligible DNA damage without US exposure. In contrast, US irradiation markedly enhanced the fluorescence signal in cells treated with Mn@CTL-LPs, producing the highest γ-H2AX signal compared with all other treatment groups. Cells treated with Mn@LPs or CTL-LPs showed weaker fluorescence, suggesting that nuclear DNA damage resulted from the synergistic effects of ROS generation by MnTCPP under US and the chemotherapeutic action of Lin. Consistently, Western blot analysis confirmed that Mn@CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs, and CTL-LPs significantly upregulated γ-H2AX expression in B16F10 cells upon US irradiation (Fig. 3C and D). This DNA damage effect was strongly dependent on US, as γ-H2AX levels remained relatively low without US, even after Mn@CTL-LPs treatment. Notably, minor DNA damage was observed in untreated cells under US, likely due to cavitation effects, which induce pyrolysis of water molecules and dissolved oxygen, generating low levels of ROS and causing DNA damage [27,28]. In summary, Mn@CTL-LPs effectively induced severe DNA damage in both mitochondria and the nucleus, and this effect was strongly dependent on US irritation.

To further verify activation of the cGAS-STING pathway, Western blot analysis of phosphorylated proteins (P-STING, P-TBK1, and P-IRF3) was performed on B16F10 cells after various treatments. The results showed that Mn@CTL-LPs combined with US significantly promoted the upregulation of these phosphorylated proteins, confirming cGAS-STING pathway activation (Fig. 3E–H). In addition, B16F10 cells were incubated with different liposome formulations for 24 h, and the culture supernatants were collected to evaluate IFN-β and IL-6 secretion using ELISA kits. The results demonstrated that IFN-β and IL-6 levels in the Mn@CTL-LPs + US group reached 192.47 pg/mL and 45.82 pg/mL, respectively, 4.62- and 4.71-fold higher than those in the control group (Fig. 3I and J). Taken together, these findings demonstrated that Mn@CTL-LPs upon US irritation could effectively induce DNA damage, thereby activating the cGAS-STING pathway and promoting the release of interferons and proinflammatory cytokines from B16F10 cells.

Characterization of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs

The dMNs were fabricated from pullulan using a micromolding technique, as previously described [23,29]. Macroscopic imaging showed that the Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs were arranged in a regular 16 × 16 array over an area of 15 × 15 mm2, with sharp and uniform needles. SEM analysis further confirmed that the dMNs were conical in shape, with an average height of approximately 800 μm and a base diameter of 400 μm (Fig. 4A–C). The dissolution behavior of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs was then investigated (Fig. 4D). After 5 min of insertion into the skin, initial dissolution of the microneedle tips was observed, with the apexes partially dissolved while most of the shafts remained intact. At 10 min, further dissolution occurred, leaving approximately 200 μm of undissolved shafts. By 15 and 20 min, more than 80% of the dMN arrays had dissolved, with residual fragments less than 100 μm remaining on the backing layer, and no significant difference was observed between these two time points. Therefore, the complete dissolution time of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs was determined to be approximately 15 min. The mechanical strength of blank dMNs, CTL-LPs dMNs, Mn@LPs dMNs, and Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs was also evaluated (Fig. 4E). The force applied to the dMNs increased proportionally with displacement, reaching 0.42 N/needle for blank dMNs and 0.21 N/needle for Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs at a displacement of 0.9 mm. Since the minimum force required for skin penetration by a single microneedle is reported as 0.058 N [30], Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs possessed sufficient mechanical strength for skin insertion without breakage.

To further verify penetration capability, trypan blue staining was performed after administration of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs into ex vivo mouse skin (Fig. 4F). The appearance of 16 × 16 blue dots confirmed complete dMNs insertion to a depth of approximately 300 μm (Fig. 4G). CLSM along the z-axis was then used to assess penetration depth. As shown in Fig. 4H, RB fluorescence was strongest at the skin surface and gradually decreased with depth, disappearing at around 350 μm, indicating an insertion depth of at least 350 μm. This depth is sufficient to penetrate the viable epidermis and dermis beneath the stratum corneum, which are enriched with LCs and DCs, both critical for antigen presentation [31,32]. Moreover, this penetration depth is biologically relevant for cutaneous melanoma treatment. Cutaneous melanoma originates from the abnormal and rapid proliferation of melanocytes, which are primarily distributed across the epidermis and the epidermal-dermal junction [33]. It has been reported that the thickness of the epidermis is approximately 100-150 μm, while the dermis extends to several millimeters in thickness [34]. Therefore, a penetration depth of around 300-350 μm enables the microneedles to effectively reach the region where melanoma cells reside, ensuring localized drug delivery to the tumor-associated skin layers.

In addition to penetration depth, the intratumoral residence time of therapeutic agents is a critical factor influencing treatment efficacy. To evaluate the local retention behavior of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs, RB-labeled Mn@CTL-LPs were applied to tumor-bearing skin, and ex vivo fluorescence analysis was performed (Fig. S6). Notably, a discernible fluorescence signal remained within the tumor tissue at 6 h post-administration, indicating prolonged retention of Mn@CTL-LPs at the tumor site. Compared with our previous studies in which fluorescence signals delivered by dMNs into normal skin diminished rapidly within several hours [35], the extended retention observed in tumor tissue suggests that the tumor microenvironment may facilitate sustained localization of Mn@CTL-LPs. This behavior is likely attributable to enhanced cellular uptake, dense extracellular matrix, and impaired lymphatic drainage commonly associated with solid tumors [36]. Importantly, such prolonged intratumoral retention provides a sufficient therapeutic time window for ultrasound-triggered ROS generation, Mn-mediated cGAS-STING activation, and subsequent antitumor immune responses, thereby contributing to the observed therapeutic efficacy.

Collectively, these results demonstrated that Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs could completely dissolve within 15 min after insertion, while providing adequate mechanical strength and penetration depth to enable effective melanoma therapy.

In vivo antitumor activity of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs

Considering the excellent in vitro ROS generation and cytotoxic effects of Mn@CTL-LPs, their therapeutic efficacy was further evaluated in a B16F10 tumor-bearing mouse model (Fig. 5A). Once the tumor volume reached approximately 50–60 mm3, mice were randomly divided into six groups (n = 5): (a) Vehicle Control (PBS,-), (b) Blank dMNs (+), (c) Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs (−), (d) CTL-LPs dMNs (+), (e) Mn@LPs dMNs (+), and (f) Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs (+). US irradiation was applied at 1.0 MHz, 1.5 W/cm2, with a 50% duty cycle for 5 min. Treatments were repeated on days 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 post-incubation. Tumor volumes were measured every 2 days (Fig. 5B and C). In the absence of US, Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs displayed only slight tumor growth inhibition compared with the control group. Mild tumor suppression was also observed in the Blank dMNs (+) group, attributed to the weak cavitation effect of US. Upon US irradiation, CTL-LPs dMNs and Mn@LPs dMNs both produced significant antitumor effects compared to the control, mainly due to the chemotherapeutic action of Lin and the sonodynamic effect of MnTCPP, respectively. Notably, Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs under US achieved the most pronounced therapeutic efficacy. This superior effect likely stemmed from the synergistic combination of ROS overproduction by MnTCPP under US and the ROS-responsive release of Lin, leading to extensive tumor cell death. At day 16, tumors were excised and weighed (Fig. 5D and E). Mice treated with Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs (+) exhibited the smallest tumor burden, with an average tumor weight of 0.21 g, corresponding to only 0.48- and 0.45-fold that of tumors from the CTL-LPs dMNs (0.48 g) and Mn@LPs dMNs (0.45 g) groups, respectively. Body weight was monitored throughout treatment (Fig. 5F), and no significant weight loss was observed, indicating a favorable systemic safety of the dMNs delivery system.

Histological analysis further confirmed the therapeutic effects (Fig. 5G). H&E staining revealed extensive nuclear fragmentation and cytolysis in tumors from the Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs (+) group, while Ki67 staining showed markedly reduced tumor cell proliferation. Moreover, immunohistochemical staining demonstrated significant upregulation of P-STING expression in tumors treated with Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs + US, confirming in vivo activation of the STING pathway. To assess biosafety, H&E staining of major organs, including heart, liver, spleen, lungs, and kidneys was conducted (Fig. S7). No noticeable histopathological abnormalities were observed, confirming the good biocompatibility of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs, Mn@LPs dMNs, CTL-LPs dMNs, and US treatment. Consistently, in vitro cytotoxicity assays (Fig. 2C) showed that CTL-LPs, Mn@LPs, and Mn@CTL-LPs exhibited negligible cytotoxicity in the absence of US irradiation, maintaining cell viability above 90%. These results collectively demonstrated the excellent biosafety of the Mn@CTL-LPs system. Therefore, Mn@CTL-LPs can be regarded as a safe and controllable delivery platform that exhibits minimal tumoricidal activity without US exposure but exerts potent therapeutic effects upon US activation. Together, these results highlighted the potent in vivo antitumor efficacy of the Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs system.

In vivo antitumor immunological responses

To further investigate the immunomodulatory effects of Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs in vivo, tumors were harvested on day 16, and immune-related indicators were analyzed by flow cytometry. As the cGAS-STING pathway enhances cross-presentation by DCs through the production of type I interferons, thereby activating tumor-specific CD8+ T cells and promoting antitumor immunity. Therefore, DC maturation was firstly evaluated. Since DC maturation is characterized by the upregulation of major histocompatibility complex class II (MHCII), the proportion of MHCII+ cells was analyzed (Fig. 6A–E). In the Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs/US group, the proportion of MHCII+ cells increased from 7.69% (control) to 19.5%. Additionally, the expression of CD80 and CD86 in CD11c+ cells was examined as another marker of DC maturation. The Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs/US group displayed the highest proportion of CD80+CD86+ DCs in tumor tissues (10.8%), which was 4.7-fold higher than in the control group (2.3%) (Fig. 6B–F). These results demonstrated that Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs/US promoted DC maturation and enhanced antigen presentation, thereby facilitating subsequent T-cell activation.

Given that matured DCs activate adaptive immunity by stimulating T lymphocytes, we next analyzed tumor-infiltrating CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. As shown in Fig. 6C–G, H, the percentage of CD3+CD8+ cytotoxic T cells in the Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs/US group was markedly elevated, reaching 8.59-fold higher than that of the control group. In contrast, Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs without US induced only slight increases (4.39% of CD3+CD8+ T cells). Moreover, the CD4/CD8 ratio significantly decreased from 1.18 (control) to 0.43 in the Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs/US group, indicating that the combined sonodynamic effect of MnTCPP and the chemotherapeutic action of Lin under US elicited a potent CD8+ T cell-mediated antitumor response. Further analysis of IFN-γ+ CD8+ T cells (Fig. 6D–I) revealed that the Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs/US group reached 50.7%, nearly 6.95-fold higher than the PBS group (7.3%), and also higher than CTL-LPs dMNs/US (36.3%) or Mn@LPs dMNs/US (42.6%). These findings confirmed that Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs/US efficiently induced DC maturation, activated T lymphocytes, and promoted strong adaptive immune responses.

To further confirm the cGAS-STING activation, we detected the expression of downstream factors, including INF-γ and IL-6 (Fig. 6J and K). The IFN-γ concentration in the Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs/US group (110.50 pg/mL) was 1.67- and 1.73-fold higher than that in CTL-LPs dMNs/US (66.24 pg/ml) and Mn@LPs dMNs/US (64.02 pg/ml), respectively. Similarly, IL-6 reached the highest level in the Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs/US group (52.31 pg/ml). Together with the immunohistochemical analysis (Fig. 5G), these results further confirmed the successful activation of the cGAS-STING pathway (Fig. 6L).

Evaluation of the Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs-mediated antineoplastic immunotherapy mechanism via transcriptomic analysis

To globally analyze the tumor immune microenvironment following Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs exposure, we performed transcriptomic profiling on tumor samples at the end of the experiment. Venn diagram (Fig. 7A) and principal component analysis (PCA) analysis (Fig. 7B) revelated the evident difference among the transcriptomic profiles between the Blank dMNs (+) and Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs (+) groups (fold change >1.5 and P < 0.05). Gene Ontology (GO) analysis revealed that the differentially expressed genes were primarily enriched in innate immune response, immune system process, cytokine production, as well as cytokine and chemokine activity (Fig. 7C). Complementing these findings, Reactome enrichment analysis further confirmed that the majority of these genes were associated with immune-related pathways, particularly the innate immune system (Fig. 7D). At a more detailed level, Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs (+) treatment led to the upregulation of genes linked to interferon-gamma inducible proteins (e.g., IFI209, IFI204, IFI205), pro-inflammatory signaling (e.g., Il1rl2, Il1r1, Il6ra, Tlr5, Tlr7, Irf7), and leukocyte-recruiting chemokines (e.g., Cxcr2, Cxcl12, Ccl4, Ccl3), all of which are associated with STING-mediated immune activation (Fig. 7E). These transcriptional changes collectively suggested a remodeling of the tumor microenvironment toward a less immunosuppressive state. Moreover, Kyoto Encyclopedia of Genes and Genomes (KEGG) pathway analysis reinforced these observations, demonstrating significant enrichment in multiple STING-activated pathways, including the cytosolic DNA-sensing pathway, NF-kappaB signaling pathway, and chemokine signaling pathway (Fig. 7F). Taken together, these results strongly support that Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs (+) effectively activates the STING signaling pathway and promotes antitumor immunity.

Conclusions

In conclusion, a dMNs-based US-controllable all-in-one delivery system (Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs) was successfully developed for efficient chemo-immunotherapy against melanoma. Upon US irradiation, Mn@CTL-LPs effectively generated ROS, triggered the release of the VEGF inhibitor Lin, and induced mitochondrial dysfunction, leading to tumor cell damage and apoptosis. Meanwhile, the elevated oxidative stress and the conversion of Mn3+ to Mn2+ from MnTCPP collaboratively activated the cGAS-STING pathway, eliciting immune responses through DNA damage and enhancing adaptive antitumor immunity. In this study, the ROS-cleavable prodrug Chol-tk-Lin and the ROS-responsive Mn@CTL-LPs were first designed and synthesized. In vitro, Mn@CTL-LPs exhibited potent cytotoxicity and efficiently activated the cGAS-STING pathway via US-induced DNA double-strand breaks. Furthermore, pullulan-based dMNs with sufficient mechanical strength successfully penetrated the skin, enabling effective intradermal drug delivery. In vivo, Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs demonstrated remarkable antitumor efficacy in melanoma-bearing mice, where ROS and Mn2+-mediated activation of the cGAS-STING pathway boosted systemic antitumor immunity through DC maturation and T-cell activation.

Importantly, Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs exhibited excellent controllability, showing negligible tumoricidal activity without US exposure but potent therapeutic effects upon US activation. Overall, Mn@CTL-LPs dMNs provide a safe, versatile, and precisely controllable platform that synergistically integrates sonodynamic therapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy to achieve enhanced therapeutic efficacy against melanoma.
