ABSTRACT
Graphical abstract

Background

Several highly compliant gene-editing technologies have been developed for rapid and economic sequence-specific modifications of the human genome. Based on nucleases, the development of gene editing technologies has opened up the possibility of modifying genomic sequences. Gene editing has promoted the preparation of cellular and animal models with intended modifications and has begun to show potential impact in a variety of fields, ranging from basic research to applied biotechnology and biomedical research 1.

Recent progress in developing programmable nucleases, such as Zinc-Finger Nucleases (ZFNs), Transcription Activator-Like Effector Nucleases (TALENs), and Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR), has greatly enriched the progress of gene editing from concept to clinical practice. These core technologies are now most commonly used to facilitate genome editing 2.

Despite the remarkable advances in CRISPR, several limitations and concerns still exist. In spite of recent progress in CRISPR–Cas systems for genetic editing, its competence is still restricted by several aspects including; “the structure of the Cas protein, the sequence of the guide RNA, the availability of the target sequence (which is related to the modified cell type), and, the delivery method of the CRISPR–Cas system, both in-vivo and in-vitro 3.

Peptide Nucleic Acids (PNAs) are DNA/RNA analogues, in which N-2-aminoethylglycine units replace the sugar-phosphate backbone and the polyamide chain is linked to nucleobases covalently through a carboxymethyl spacer 4.

Being neither a nucleic acid nor a protein, PNAs can withstand both nucleases and proteases recognition, making them potentially resistant to enzymatic degradation and stable over a wide pH range. PNA-induced gene correction was first demonstrated in 2002.5 Recently, PNAs have been used to correct human disease-causing mutations ex-vivo and in-vivo in preclinical mouse models, resulting in clinically relevant functional protein restoration and disease improvement, with low off-target genome effects. Those impressing outcomes might indicate a strong therapeutic potential in the treatment of genetic disorders 6.

Peptide nucleic acids (PNAs) have demonstrated a remarkable ability to enhance recombination efficiency when used in conjunction with donor DNA, achieving a more than five-fold increase compared to donor DNA alone. This suggests that PNAs play a crucial role in facilitating targeted genetic modifications by stimulating recombination between the target and donor DNA.7 PNAs possess the unique capability to invade double-stranded DNA helices, selectively aligning with a single strand while displacing the complementary one. This process results in the formation of a specialized structure known as a p-loop, which has been shown to activate endogenous DNA repair mechanisms. Such interactions are particularly valuable in gene editing applications, as they enable precise modifications at the molecular level.8, 9 Moreover, when a single-stranded DNA (ssDNA) template encoding a specific sequence change is co-delivered alongside PNAs, the activated endogenous repair pathways can incorporate this ssDNA into the genome, leading to a stable and persistent genetic edit. This approach has been widely explored in genome engineering strategies, offering a promising avenue for therapeutic interventions and genetic research 10.

PNAs have emerged as a powerful tool in gene editing, particularly for correcting monogenic disorders by enhancing targeted recombination and genomic modifications. In beta-thalassemia, PNA-mediated gene editing has successfully reversed splenomegaly, reduced reticulocytosis, and restored hemoglobin levels to wild-type ranges, while intravenous nanoparticle (NP) delivery methods have demonstrated no measurable increase in inflammatory cytokines, ensuring a favourable safety profile.11 Similarly, PNA-based strategies have been explored for in-vivo correction of cystic fibrosis by facilitating precise genetic modifications of the CFTR gene, leading to partial restoration of its function in epithelial tissues.12 Beyond these applications, PNA has been investigated for treating sickle cell disease through targeted correction of beta-globin mutations,11 Huntington’s disease by inhibiting mutant HTT allele expression, and Duchenne muscular dystrophy via exon-skipping to restore dystrophin expression, highlighting its versatility as a promising therapeutic approach in molecular medicine 13.

This study seeks to validate the application of peptide nucleic acid (PNA)-mediated gene editing by introducing the IVS I.6 mutation into the normal HBB gene, replicating the most common beta-thalassemia mutation found in Egyptian and worldwide patients. Establishing this methodology will serve as a foundation for reversing the approach, enabling precise correction of beta-thalassemia mutations through targeted recombination guided by donor single-stranded DNA (ssDNA).

Materials and methods

Volunteer selection and sample withdrawal

An apparently healthy volunteer participated in this study by donating a blood sample (for genotyping) and a skin biopsy for fibroblast culture. The privacy rights of the human subject have been observed. The volunteer provided informed consent in accordance with the regulations of the Ethical Committee for Human Research at the National Research Centre and the Ethical Committee (Approval Number: 13124052021).

Before obtaining a skin biopsy, a 5 ml venous blood sample was collected from the volunteer for molecular analysis screening (DNA-0 samples) of the beta-globin (HBB) gene to confirm the absence of mutations to suit for the designed PNA sequence. After DNA extraction from the blood, PCR amplification of the beta-globin gene was performed, followed by direct sequencing in both directions using the BigDye™ Terminator v3.1 Cycle Sequencing Kit, and analyzed with the ABI 3500 Genetic Analyzer. After confirming the absence of HBB gene mutations, two punch skin biopsies, approximately 2–3 mm × 1 mm in size, were obtained from the volunteer for fibroblast cell culture.

Fibroblasts cell culture

Under sterile conditions, obtained skin biopsy was washed with PBS, cut into small pieces (1 mm each) and cultured in DMEM media supplemented with 10 % FBS (Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals), 100 U/ml penicillin, 100 μg/ml streptomycin (Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals), and 0.25 μg/ml amphotericin B (Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals) at 37.5 °C in a humidified atmosphere of 5 % CO2 and 95 % air, culture media changed twice a week. After a week in culture, fibroblast cell monolayers were formed around the tissue explants, and the fibroblast cells were cultured to confluency. Upon 90 % confluence, cells were subjected to passage by incubation in a 0.1 % trypsin and EDTA solution (Sigma-Aldrich Chemicals) for 1 min at 37 °C and were allocated to new dishes for further passaging. The cell morphology was constantly monitored under a camera-equipped inverted microscope (Olympus, Osaka, Japan; CKX53).

NPs formulation and characterization

PLGA NPs Formulation

NPs were synthesized by encapsulation of PNA and DNA oligomers in the PLGA polymer using a modified double-emulsion solvent evaporation technique.15 Two batches of NPs were prepared: combined PNA/DNA PLGA NPs (loaded NPs) and blank PLGA NPs, following the protocol published by Eltaweel et al., 2022.16 The validation of the formulated NPs was ensured by release test, loading test, encapsulation efficiency, and NPs characterization as mentioned below in details.

NPs release test

Two mg of loaded NPs were incubated in 200 μl of PBS (Gibco, Grand Island, NY) in a 37 °C shaking incubator. At indicated time points (0, 3, 6, 9, 12, 24, and 48 h), the tubes were spun down, and 5 μl of the supernatant was replaced by the same volume of fresh PBS to maintain sink conditions. The nucleic acid content in the supernatant was measured by absorbance at 260 nm using a NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific), with PBS serving as a blank. The equations (1 – 3) used to calculate the cumulative percentage of drug release are shown in Supplement S1.

Determination of the amount of nucleic acid encapsulation in NPs by loading Test

Two mg of NPs were dissolved in 1 ml of DCM at room temperature for 1 h, then, 0.5 ml of TE buffer (10 mmol/l Tris–HCl/1 mmol/l EDTA, pH 7.4) was added to the solution and vortexed for 1–2 min, then centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The aqueous phase was removed. The last steps were repeated with another 0.5 ml of TE buffer for a total aqueous extraction of 1 ml of TE buffer. Absorbance at 260 nm was measured with a NanoDrop (Thermo Scientific, Waltham, MA). The OD of the oligos was determined using equation 4 (S1). Analysis of PNA/DNA encapsulation efficiency was done using Equation 5 (S1).

NPs morphology

Microsphere morphology and shape of the particles have been characterized by scanning electron microscopy (SEM), and Transmission electron microscopy (TEM).

NP dosing and sampling scheme.

NP treatments were conducted in duplicate using two separate 12-well plates. Following six passages, cells were harvested, and initial samples for DNA and RNA extraction were collected (≥1 × 10^6 cells each), designated as DNA-0 and RNA-0, respectively. The NPs were resuspended in 500 µl of cold media, then vortexed for 1 min and subsequently sonicated in an ice water bath for 30 min or until a uniform suspension was achieved. After 24 h of passaging, the resuspended nanoparticles were introduced to adherent cells at a final concentration of 4 mg/ml, marking day 0 of the experiment. Post-treatment, the cells were maintained for five days to reach confluence.

Then, cells of 2L-wells were harvested for DNA and RNA extraction for detection of the presence of the edited β-globin gene. Both samples were referred to as DNA-1 and RNA-1, respectively. Some of the once-treated cells were seeded into a separate plate with plain fresh medium without NPs. The medium was routinely changed until the end of the experiment (1 week), and then cells were harvested to test for the reversibility of the obtained edit (DNA-1*). The remaining once-treated fibroblasts were seeded into new wells (passage 7) to prevent over-confluency, a second dose was added in the same manner as previous step d. After another 5 days, cells were harvested, and 2 samples of DNA and RNA were taken (DNA-2 and RNA-2, respectively). The blank well was treated in the same manner as the loaded well at the same time intervals, and 2 final samples for DNA and RNA were taken.

Pre- and post-treatment molecular analyses

Genomic DNA Isolation, Sequencing, and quantitative allele-specific Real-Time PCR (AS-qPCR)

Genomic DNA was isolated from DNA (negative control and treated samples) using the ZYMO Quick-DNA™ Miniprep Plus kit (ZYMO Research, USA) according to the manufacturer’s protocol for cultured cells. DNA amplification and sequencing of the negative control was done as previously mentioned. AS-PCR primers were designed to target the IVS 1.6 mutation of the HBB gene, such that the 3′ end of the reverse primer corresponds to the mutated sequence introduced by the donor DNA. Equal amounts of gDNA samples were subjected to allele-specific qPCR. qPCR was performed using a QuantStudio 5™ Real-Time PCR Cycler (Applied Biosystems). Drawing a Standard Curve:

Calculating the DNA copy number/concentration is an important step in absolute qPCR since the number of copies is required during the creation of the standard curve. The DNA copy number was calculated by using the known amount (ng) and length of DNA (bp) (equation 6) (S1). Ten-fold serial dilutions of the stock homozygous mutant sample were prepared in sterile nuclease-free water (Table S1; Supplement S3). The concentrations of gDNA needed to achieve the copy numbers of interest per reaction were calculated by dividing the mass/5, ensuring; such that 5 μl from each dilution was used for the corresponding 15 μl qPCR reaction.

Standard curve was drawn by plotting CT values of the serial dilutions against the log of the copy number of mutant alleles. Also, AS-qPCR of the treated DNA samples was performed in triplicates, CT values were obtained. The copy number for the mutant allele in each sample was calculated using the trendline equation.

Total RNA extraction and measuring HBB gene Expression

Direct-zol™ RNA ZYMO Miniprep kit was performed for each patient and reversely transcribed into cDNA. For qRT-PCR, the Heraplus SYBR Green qPCR Kit and QuantiTect HBB gene assay were used in triplicates as indicated by the manufacturer’s protocol. The Ct values were obtained and used for further calculations. Relative quantification Rq was measured using the comparative ΔΔCt method. Values were normalized against β-actin as a housekeeping gene.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were conducted using Statistical Package for the Social Sciences (SPSS) software (SPSS Inc., Chicago, USA), version 19.0. Results are expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) where appropriate. Differences in means between groups were compared using ANOVA (in case of more than 2 groups) and then confirmed by student t-test between each 2 groups. Statistical differences were considered significant at P < 0.05.

Structure of PNA and donor DNA

The structure of donor DNA included in this study (for normal HBB gene to introduce an IVS I-6 mutation) was:

5′-G*T*G*AGGCCCTGGGCAGGTTGGCATCAAGGTTACAAGACAGGTTTAA*G*G*A-3′; (* means: phosphorothioate internucleoside linkage).

The structure of PNA included in this study was: N terminus-KKK-TTGJJGTJTGAAG-OOO-GA*AGT*CTG*CCG*TT-KKK-C terminus; X* indicates mini-PEG-gamma PNA residues (contain miniPEG in the gamma position); K: lysine; J: pseudoisocytosine (a replacement for cytosine that allows pH-independent triplex formation); O: 8-amino-2,6,10-trioxaoctanoic acid linkers connecting the Hoogsteen and Watson–Crick domains of the PNA.

According to the PNA tool, we followed the recommended range for The properties of the WC fragment of the designed PNA sequence, including the purine percent (46.2 %), GC content (53.8 %), presence of purine stretches (4-base purine stretch (GAAG)) and predicted dimering formation. Discussion

Throughout the last decade, several gene editing protocols have been comprehensively explored including the PNAs, a powerful tool capable of inducing specific genomic changes at targeted locations. PNAs have been delivered using nanoparticle systems due to the inability to cross the cellular membrane independently. These nanotechnology-based carriers provide safe, simple, and rapid methods for PNA application.17 In our current study, we aim to develop and assess the efficacy of PNA-based gene editing technology for targeting mutation-specific single gene disorders through induction of the beta-thalassemia common mutation within the first intron of the beta-globin gene. The donor DNA was carefully designed to span the junction between exon 1 and intron 1, aligning with positions 551–600 on the HBB gene. Notably, this position covers three adjacent mutations: IVS I-1, IVS I-5, and IVS I-6, with IVS I-6 and IVS I-1 being the most common Egyptian mutations.18 In line with prior research, we shielded the donor DNA against nucleases by incorporating three phosphorothioate internucleoside linkages at both ends.19, 20 This modification replaces the phosphate group with a phosphorothioate group—a common strategy to enhance the in-vivo stability of oligonucleotides and protect them from nuclease degradation 21.

Researchers have modified PNA oligomers using a miniPEG-γPNA approach in the “Patent Cooperation Treaty (PCT); USA” WO2017143042A2 from 2017. In this method, the purine content was lower than 60 %, while the GC content ranged between 30 % and 80 %.19 and by substituting the γ-position of PNA with diethylene glycol (referred to as “miniPEG”), they achieved several benefits such as, miniPEG enhanced PNA solubility, reduced self-aggregation, and improved its binding affinity with the DNA target.22 The optimal length of single-stranded peptide nucleic acid (ssPNA) is crucial for effective binding to DNA or RNA, typically ranging from 12 to 16 bases.23, 24 In our custom-designed miniPEG γ-PNA, we incorporated two strands: an antiparallel Watson-Crick strand with miniPEG substitutions every 3 bases, and a parallel Hoogsteen strand (where C was replaced with J). Each strand consisted of 13 nucleobases, resulting in a total length of 26 bases. To facilitate DNA invasion in an antiparallel orientation, we introduced flexible linkers composed of three O units (8-amino-3,6-dioxaoctanoic acid). These linkers not only enhance solubility but also promote effective binding 19.

The electrophoretic mobility shift assay (EMSA) was employed to detect in-vitro interactions between peptide nucleic acids (PNAs) and DNA, resulting in an upward shift in band migration that is crucial for validating the specificity and efficiency of the gene-editing tools, in addition, it can provide quantitative data on the binding affinity and kinetics of the interactions, which is essential for optimizing gene-editing protocol 25.

Given that PLGA is one of the frequently employed biodegradable and biocompatible polymers in nanomedicine and nucleic acid formulations,26, 27 we utilized PLGA nanoparticles for PNA/DNA delivery to enhance encapsulation efficiency (EE) and prevent undesirably small particle size resulting from low molecular weight (MW). Interestingly previous studies have highlighted better solubility and slower degradation rates in lactate-rich PLGA despite in unpreferred release profile.28 To ensure reproducibility, we meticulously selected and thoroughly documented all variables 29.

Consistent with previous findings, freeze-drying or lyophilization stands out as a favored drying method for heat-sensitive and high-quality products. This process preserves the solid material in an intact state, resulting in a spongy or fluffy structure.30, 31 Interestingly, the appearance of the PLGA nanoparticles formulated in our study closely resembled those prepared by the method described by Fonte et al., 2016 32.

To assess the potential application of PNA-loaded PLGA NPs for delivery, the release profile data revealed an initial burst release within the first 2 h, followed by sustained release up to 24 h until it reached a plateau. Interestingly, similar observations were reported by other studies.33, 34 However, Sahin et al., 2017 documented an initial burst within 6 h, followed by a slower release rate over 24 h until reaching a plateau.35 However, earlier research findings indicate that nanoparticles (NPs) loaded with nucleic acids below 0.4 OD/mg/ml do not effectively edit genes in vitro36, 37, 38 and consequently, the commonly accepted loading range falls between 0.4 and 0.6 OD/mg/ml. In our study, the loading value was 0.482 OD/mg/ml (equivalent to 455 pmol of donor DNA and 970 pmol of PNA per mg/ml).

Moreover, the encapsulation efficiency (EE) in our study was approximately 94 %, aligning well with previous formulations of PLGA nanoparticles (NPs) Sun et al., 2015 and Mahboob et al., 2020 who reported EE values around 88 %.36, 37 Additionally, for silver NPs, Ihegwuagu et al., 2015 documented EE ranging from 95 % to 98 % 39.

In line with previous studies, TEM demonstrated that the size of the loaded nanoparticles (NPs) was approximately 500 nm, larger than that of the blank NPs, this observation confirms successful encapsulation.38, 40 Additionally, the surfaces of the loaded NPs appeared uneven compared to the smooth blank NPs. This unevenness could be attributed to electrostatic interactions between PLGA and both nucleic acids (DNA and PNA) 41, 42.

The dynamic light scattering (DLS) analysis indicated that the size of the blank nanoparticles (NPs) was approximately 109 nm, as observed in Fig. 5 and Table S3 (Supplement S3). In contrast, the loaded NPs had a size of 153 nm. These findings align with Tsai et al. (2021) who reported loaded PLGA NPs ranging between 150–160 nm by DLS.43 Similarly, Dinarvand et al. (2011) documented NPs with a 50:50 PLGA composition having a mean size ranging from 102 ± 4 nm to 154 ± 17 nm 28.

The difference in particle size measurements between TEM and DLS is due to sample drying causing nanoparticle aggregation during TEM preparation.40 DLS provides average size and polydispersity index (PdI) data, while, TEM tracks single particles.44 PDI values below 0.2 are acceptable for polymer-based nanoparticles, indicating a narrow size distribution.45 Nanoparticles between 20 – 200 nm are preferable for avoiding clearance by the kidneys or removal by the Reticuloendothelial System (RES).46 The PdI of loaded nanoparticles in this study was within 0.2, indicating uniformity.40 The particle size measured by DLS was similar to an efficient vehicle reported by Sahin et al., (2017), which is better taken up by the cells and has a faster release rate 35.

Interestingly, the surface charge of nanoparticles (NPs) is crucial for cellular uptake. Positively charged NPs bind tightly to the negatively charged cellular membrane, facilitating entry via endocytosis.41 Conversely, negatively charged PLGA-NPs are rapidly cleared by the Reticuloendothelial System (RES), hindering effective delivery (Table S3; Supplement S3).47 In this study, the zeta potential of the blank and loaded PLGA NPs were around 2 and 5 mV, respectively. Using PLGA/PVA, produced stable nanoparticles with a small size and a narrow PdI.48 They found that 2–5 % PVA as a stabilizer improved DNA loading and release by changing the surface charge from anionic to cationic, aiding NPs escape from endolysosomes and enhancing their efficacy.49 However, in this study, 5 % PVA was used for stabilizing PLGA NPs as described by 50.

We focused on establishing PNA technology by inducing an HBB gene mutation rather than correcting an existing one. Chin et al., (2008),51 Schleifman et al., 201152 and McNeer et al., 201153 used bisPNAs to introduce specific mutations in various cell types and achieve gene modification rates up to 1.63 %, 2.46 %, and 1 %, respectively. In this study, the choice of skin fibroblasts was due to the safety of skin biopsy procedure and their high proliferation rate. The HBB gene is expressed in skin fibroblasts, as confirmed by the Human Protein Atlas and single gene transcriptome data for human skin cells. We observed no adverse effects of the nanoparticles on fibroblast adhesion or proliferation consistent with the finding of Rasel et al., (2019) 54.

Quantitative allele-specific PCR (AS-aqPCR) with standard curve construction successfully validated the introduction of the DNA sequence, confirming the presence of the edited IVS I-6 T > C mutation. Following the initial dose, the mutant allele frequency was measured at 3.7 %, demonstrating a statistically significant difference from the DNA-0 samples (p-value = 0.015). After the second dose, the frequency declined to 2.65 %, with no significant variation between doses, yet still maintaining a statistically significant distinction from the initial sample (p-value = 0.043). This observed reduction may be attributed to diminished proliferation of the edited cells over time. These findings align with previous ex-vivo studies utilizing γPNA, which reported on-target modification frequencies ranging from 3 % to 5 %, with some instances reaching up to 9 % in the HBB gene and other genomic loci.55 While these results provide promising insights into the stability and efficiency of PNA-mediated gene editing, a large-scale study is warranted to further validate these observations and assess long-term genomic integrity. Expanding research efforts in this domain will be crucial for optimizing gene-editing strategies and advancing therapeutic applications for monogenic disorders.

In the same context, γPNA treatment yielded gene editing in human CD34 + HSCs ex-vivo at a frequency of 5 % with a single treatment.15 In-vivo γtcPNA/ssDNA NPs splicing mutation in human HBB produced up to 7 % gene editing in HSCs following intravenous injection, and Ricciardi et al., (2018) achieved HBB gene editing with a frequency of ∼ 6 % in bone marrow after a single in-utero treatment and ∼ 10 % in hematopoietic progenitor cells after a single IV treatment with NPs containing γtcPNA and donor DNA.56 It is noteworthy that both studies reported that PNA/DNA PLGA NPs did not induce inflammatory cytokine secretion in treated animals after intravenous administration.

Likewise, Fields et al., (2015) applied tcPNA-loaded PLGA NPs in-vivo in mice via intranasal administration for correction of a cystic fibrosis causing mutation in the CFTR gene. NPs were found to associate with approximately 30 % of all lung cells, however, levels of editing were limited (0.6 % in alveolar macrophages and 0.3 % in alveolar epithelial cells).57 From the same perspective, treated mice showed ∼ 18 % and 30 % correction in the CFTR gene after PNA/ssDNA and γtcPNA/ssDNA PLGA NP treatment; respectively, administered as four IV doses over 2 weeks 12.

Injection of PNA in a Duchenne Muscular Dystrophy (DMD) mouse model resulted in ∼ 3 % gene editing with dystrophin-positive fibers detected in vivo four months after treatment.58 The functionality of the edit was assessed by measuring the HBB gene expression, which decreased to about half after the first dose. This altered expression was consistent with reports for other genes like γ-globin gene and CCR5 gene).7, 15, 59 After the second dose, gene expression increased, aligning with the reduced frequency of the edited allele in the DNA-2 samples.

Following the removal of nanoparticles (NPs) from the culture medium for approximately one week (DNA-1* sample), the frequency of the mutant allele remained unchanged compared to the DNA-1 sample, indicating that the gene edit may persist independently of NP presence. This observation is consistent with findings reported by McNeer et al. (2011), where gene modifications in stem cells were shown to persist for up to 30 days in culture.53 Recent studies have further reinforced the stability of gene edits post-nanoparticle removal, particularly in CRISPR/Cas9-mediated interventions, where sustained genomic modifications have been observed even after the withdrawal of delivery vectors.60 Additionally, advancements in nanoparticle-based gene delivery systems have demonstrated improved retention of genetic modifications, suggesting that the initial integration of the edit is sufficient to maintain its presence without continuous exposure to nanoparticles.61 These findings collectively highlight the robustness of gene editing approaches and their potential for long-term therapeutic applications, emphasizing the importance of optimizing delivery mechanisms to enhance genomic stability 62.

The sustained gene editing observed in this study aligns with findings reported by Piotrowski-Daspit et al. (2022), where in-vivo administration of γPNA/ssDNA PLGA nanoparticles (NPs) in mice with a cystic fibrosis mutation demonstrated stable genetic modifications two weeks post-treatment.12, 63 This consistency between studies underscores the reliability of PNA-based gene editing approaches, particularly in maintaining genomic alterations over extended periods without requiring continuous exposure to nanoparticles. The RNA-1* sample results, which showed no significant difference from the RNA-1 sample, further reinforce the notion that gene edits induced by PNA-NPs persist independently of nanoparticle presence, mirroring the findings in cystic fibrosis models 12, 55, 64.

The ability of PNA-NPs to facilitate targeted recombination and genomic modifications with minimal off-target effects presents a significant advantage over conventional gene therapy strategies. These findings collectively highlight the promise of PNA-mediated interventions in addressing inherited disorders, emphasizing the need for further studies to optimize delivery mechanisms and assess long-term genomic stability for clinical applications.

These collective findings offer fresh insights and potential treatment targets for β-thalassemia. The pioneering adoption of PNA as a gene editing technology at the Human Genetics and Genome Research Institute, NRC, Egypt—the first instance in Africa—represents a crucial stride toward unifying efforts in providing scientific advancements to thalassemic patients.

In conclusion, the results of this study establish the efficacy of peptide nucleic acid (PNA)-mediated gene editing in precisely introducing the IVS I.6 mutation into the HBB gene, mirroring the most prevalent beta-thalassemia mutation found globally. This achievement not only showcases the precision and reliability of PNA-based approaches but also highlights their transformative potential in genetic research and therapeutic applications. The study provides compelling evidence that this technique can serve as a foundation for advanced molecular interventions, paving the way for refined genome-editing strategies aimed at addressing monogenic disorders with significant clinical impact.
