Exploring the relationship between after-hours work-related social media use and teacher job burnout
Authors: Jiajun Jian, Min Zhou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractTeaching is widely recognized as a profession characterized by high levels of occupational stress. This study investigates the impact of teachers’ after-hours work-related social media communication (WRSMU) on job burnout, as well as the mediating role of work stress within the framework of the job demands-resources (JD-R) model. Furthermore, the study examines whether teachers’ work values moderate the relationship between after-hours work-related social media use and work stress. The study targeted university teachers in Sichuan province, China, and employed a questionnaire survey to collect data. A total of 936 questionnaires were distributed, of which 842 were deemed valid. Statistical analyses were conducted using SPSS 23.0 and AMOS 24.0 to test the conceptual model. The results indicated that: (1) There is a positive relationship between work-related social media use (WRSMU) outside regular work hours and work stress. (2) Work stress plays a mediating role between WRSMU after work and job burnout. The antecedents of teacher job burnout are a topic worthy of attention in both academic and practice. This study highlights the complex relationship between teachers’ after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU), work stress, work values, and job burnout. The findings demonstrate that while Work-related public social media use after work hours can enhance communication efficiency, excessive use after work hours increases work stress and contributes to job burnout. In addition, future studies can investigate other moderating or mediating variables that affect teacher job burnout.Keywords: Job burnout, Work values, Work stress, After-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU)Subject terms: Human behaviour, Occupational health

Introduction

Previous studies have found that individuals who continuously experience excessive work stress not only experience various physical and psychological discomforts but also develop feelings of burnout in the work context1,2. Additionally, teaching is one of the professions with high work stress and job burnout, leading many teachers to experience exhaustion and even choose to quit their jobs or retire early3–5. Therefore, the antecedents of teacher job burnout are an important topic of academic and practical concern.

If employees continue to experience work stress after work hours, it becomes more difficult for them to recover from work exhaustion and may even aggravate the stress level. Just as Schaufeli & Bakker (2004)6 have emphasized, “job burnout” has its clearest antecedent in “work stress” since burnout represents the extension or consequence of individuals experiencing long-term work stress6–8. Based on the job demands-resources model6,9, we view after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) as one kind of job demand that consumes teachers’ energy and causes stress and burnout. Specifically, if teachers continue to work after their regular work hours, it may intensify their work stress and subsequently lead to job burnout. Therefore, this study aims to clarify how employee WRSMU beyond normal working hours influences teachers’ job burnout.

Further, with the rapid development of information technology, communication technology has gradually blurred or even erased the boundaries between work and personal time10. Several researchers have noticed such stress caused by technology10–14. Teachers’ work environment involves not only students but also multiple stakeholders such as school administrators, colleagues, and parents. Such multi-resource pressure needs to be explored further. Besides, since work stress is one of the key antecedents of job burnout, this study also investigates whether teachers’ after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) creates work stress and subsequently affects teacher job burnout.

We set our research context in China, considering the special characteristics of Chinese teachers. With its vast population and diverse educational landscape, China presents a complex educational environment. Teachers in China often face unique challenges, such as high student-teacher ratios, rigorous academic expectations, and technology integration into daily teaching practices. These national characteristics may amplify or modify the impact of after-work software usage on teachers’ work stress and burnout levels. Furthermore, cultural factors, such as collectivism and respect for authority, may shape teachers’ perceptions of work stress and their willingness to engage in work-related activities outside official hours. By situating the study within the Chinese context, this research aims to contribute to understanding how technological advancements intersect with cultural and educational factors that affect teachers’ well-being.

Existing research supports the relationship between work stress and burnout within the research on work stress models. However, it has been suggested that personal values, one of the individual’s intrinsic resources, can be a moderating factor. Currently, researchers are beginning to focus on the moderating role of individual differences14. They believe individual resources can directly affect not only work outcomes, but also the impact of job demands and resources on employees. Personal work values influence individual career choices and work-related behaviors15, impacting individual work attitudes and behaviors16,17. Thus, during after-work hours, if teachers frequently face work-related messages through social media, job demands would increase while resources decrease. In other words, prolonged exposure to this work stress may result in job burnout, and individual differences affect teachers’ subjective perception of work values among teachers.

Therefore, we ask the following research questions: Does off-duty social media communication by teachers ultimately lead to burnout by increasing staff work stress? Do differences in individual staff values lead to different effects of WRSMU? To answer these questions, we introduce a moderated mediation model with work values as a moderating variable to examine whether it can play a buffering role between after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) and teacher work stress.

Theory and research hypotheses

Job demands-resources model

The job demands-resources (JD-R) model posits that occupational environments encompass both challenging demands and supportive resources that collectively shape employee experiences18. Within this theoretical framework, job demands are conceptualized as “aspects of the job that require sustained physical or mental effort and are therefore associated with certain physiological and psychological costs”9. Conversely, job resources encompass “those physical, psychological, social, or organizational aspects of the job that are functional in achieving work goals, reduce job demands and the associated physiological and psychological costs, or stimulate personal growth, learning, and development”9.

This dual-pathway model provides a comprehensive lens for understanding how workplace characteristics influence employee well-being and performance outcomes. According to JD-R, job resources can not only affect individual outcomes but also influence how job demands impact employees. Accordingly, we recognize the specificity of after-work WRSMU, which can act as both demands and resources. What’s more, researchers have recently noted individual traits during the process. Thus, we introduce teachers’ work value as a key boundary to explore our theoretical framework.

The relationship between after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) and teacher job burnout

Yue (2022) defines “after-hours WRSMU” as digitally mediated work-related behaviors that employees engage in beyond regular working hours11. The activities include passive content consumption, active content generation, and interpersonal communication within professional networks. Research findings suggest that as many as 85.5% of respondents reported using instant messaging for work-related communication during both working and non-working hours10,19–21. Social communication software such as Line and WeChat has become common in the workplace10,19–21. While we place WRSMU in the context of universities and investigate university teachers’ after-work WRSMU behaviors.

Teachers often need to communicate with various parties such as supervisors, colleagues, parents, and students. Instant messaging provides a method to quickly obtain instructions from leaders or feedback from colleagues, facilitating work coordination11,13. However, this constant engagement with work-related tasks even after work hours can result in the depletion of physical and cognitive resources, leading to a decline in overall health18,22. When employees continuously face negative emotions and work-related stress, it becomes hard for them to recover from work exhaustion23. Additionally, the constant availability required can create the feeling of being constantly on-call even when employees are outside the office, leading to employee dissatisfaction and increased stress8.

Prior research often supports the JD-R model in explaining job burnout2,24,25. This model suggests that work characteristics related to job demands and job resources contribute to burnout. When employees exert excessive effort to meet job demands, it can lead to resource depletion, compromised health, and burnout due to prolonged and high demands2. Hence, job burnout can result from an unfavorable work environment characterized by high work demands and low work resources7,23. According to the JD-R theory, after-hour WRSMU can be categorized as a work demand that will bring adverse emotional and cognitive reactions to teachers. Then, teachers would have to invest a significant amount of their resources.

Continuing work-related activities after work prevents employees from fully resting and recovering the personal resources they expend during work18. Organizations’ expectations for high utility from employees can create a sense of obligation to respond to work messages and emails even during non-working time12. Research has also shown a positive relationship between the use of communication technology outside of working hours and emotional exhaustion7,11. Therefore, we believe that it is equivalent to continuing to work during non-working hours, which can lead to physiological and psychological costs and eventually job burnout. Therefore, we propose that:

Hypothesis 1: There is a positive relationship between after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) and teachers’ job burnout.

After-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) and teachers’ work stress

Teachers may experience pressure from three primary sources: colleagues, students, and parents. Prior research demonstrated that apart from colleagues, expectations of perfection from students and parents can also result in greater pressure and work exhaustion3. Research indicates that teachers experience a high work-related stress as they carry the heavy responsibility of ensuring the holistic development of students while fulfilling their duties towards educational institutions, schools, parents, and society26,27. Over 90% of teachers continue to receive messages from colleagues, parents, or students even after work hours. On average, the teachers spend around 45 min handling school-related tasks. Among them, 60% of teachers reported feeling overwhelmed by excessive administrative or urgent messages. The situation severely affected their personal lives and increased work stress5,28,29. Bakker et al. (2023)18 suggested that employees who deplete their internal resources during work should engage in leisure activities. Employees would hardly recover from exhaustion if individuals continue to deplete their resources through work-related tasks after work23.

Jo and Lee (2022)30 pointed out that remote stress out of the workplace leads employees to rely on smartphones to handle work-related matters heavily. Accordingly, it would be difficult for them to psychologically or mentally detach from work. Page et al. (2021)31 also suggested that technological advancements have become a driving force for organizations to deliver overtime work. This situation results in technological stress that affects the physical, mental, and emotional well-being of employees, ultimately reducing their sense of happiness. Sonnentag et al. (2022)23 found that continuous social media use after work hours leads to negative emotions and ultimately affects the next day’s work performance. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 2: There is a positive relationship between after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) and teacher work stress.

The mediating role of work stress

According to previous empirical research on the health depletion process of the JD-R model, “job stress” is often considered as an intermediate factor of burnout2,4,6. Potential sources of work stress, whether from within the job (such as the nature of work, social relationships at work) or external factors (such as family or economic conditions), do not automatically become actual work stress. Instead, individuals’ cognitive appraisal and evaluation are necessary for stress perception, which then leads to stress-related outcomes23. Therefore, we propose that perceived stress acts as a mediating factor between potential stressors and stress-related consequences5.

According to JD-R, job burnout and excessive resource depletion hinder individuals from effectively coping with job demands9. Job stress and job burnout are common emotional responses in modern individuals. The negative state would impair employees’ personal emotions, work atmosphere as well as organizational effectiveness. When employees strive to meet job demands, they need to put continuous physical or psychological effort. Consequently, they may experience resource depletion, health impairment, and eventually job burnout due to sustained overload7,21,23.

When employees strive to meet work demands and put in continuous physical or psychological effort, it leads to resource depletion and health damage. Similar to the findings of the present study, there are already studies demonstrating the mediating role of job stress32,33. After finishing the daily teaching mission, teachers may continue to receive messages from school stakeholders through instant messaging apps, inquiring or assigning work tasks. This constant interruption may disrupt teachers’ planned leisure or family activities after work, leading to increased stress. The key starting point during the process from pressure perception to burnout is individuals’ efforts to resolve or cope with problems. This subsequently results in highly negative evaluations and dissatisfaction with the environment or oneself. Accordingly, teachers then perceive accumulating feelings of powerlessness, a sense of worthlessness, and a lack of hope for the future. The outward symptoms align with previous studies, including emotional exhaustion, depersonalization, reduced personal accomplishment, or decreased work motivation21,34.

Therefore, after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) can be viewed as one of the sources of work stress for teachers. When teachers subjectively experience physical and psychological pressure, it leads to negative stress-related consequences, that is, job burnout. In other words, the usage of instant messaging software after work to handle work-related matters affects job burnout, which is mediated by teacher work stress. Thus, work stress serves as the mediating variable between after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) and job burnout of teachers. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 3: Teacher work stress mediates the relationship between after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) and job burnout.

The moderating role of teachers’ work values

The personal traits of the message recipient can influence how they interpret the messages and the emotions they experience, especially when work-related communication and tasks continue after work hours10–13. Teachers’ work values are defined as the internal standards or reference frameworks that influence their attitudes, orientations, and behaviors in any educational-related work or activities27,35. Self-actualization, organizational safety, and physical and mental well-being are sub-constructs of teachers’ work values, ensuring the completeness of the construct and differentiation from general work values36,37. Employees are often influenced by various factors, such as family, educational and cultural values, social environment, and economic demands. These elements shape their work values and further impact their cognition and evaluation of work. Thus, work values are motivations or indicators for choosing a job and shape attitudes and behavioral outcomes related to work38.

The “integrated work stress model” suggests that work stress is a continuous and dynamic process. During the process, individuals subjectively and actively coordinate with work environment events. Therefore, work stress is a result of the constant interaction between individuals and the environment, with individual work values potentially affecting13,23,24. Previous research has also shown that employees’ organizational commitment and work values have positive relationships with job performance. In the meantime, work values can moderate the relationship between stress and performance39,40, as well as turnover intention41,42. Several dimensions of work values have been found to moderate the effects of specific work stressors and stress outcomes42–44. According to JD-R theory, employees’ work experience is influenced by two core factors: job demands and job resources. WRSMU can be seen as an additional job demand, often leading to inadequate recovery, work-family conflicts, and higher levels of work stress due to the extension of working time boundaries, information overload, and psychological burden.

Teachers’ work values can be seen as a resource at the individual level. When teachers highly identify with their educational career, view work as meaningful, and take it as an important way to realize value, these values will provide them with psychological energy and positive motivation to help them reinterpret the meaning of WRSMU. For example, they may view after-hours teaching or student communication as part of their role responsibility and professional dedication, attenuating the negative effect of WRSMU on stress. On the contrary, for teachers with low work values, WRSMU is more likely to be seen as an encroachment on private life, lacking positive support, therefore result in feeling the resource consumption and work pressure it brings more strongly.

Therefore, under the JD-R theoretical framework, teacher work values moderate the relationship between WRSMU and work stress by providing psychological resources. Thus, we propose that:

Hypothesis 4: Teacher work values negatively moderate the relationship between after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) and teacher’ work stress.

Furthermore, regarding the interactions between individuals and their environment, personal characteristics are often viewed as intervening variables. Values and personality are critical factors influencing organizational outcomes19,39,43,44. Stronger work values enable teachers to perform work competently, hold more positive attitudes towards educational work, and engage more in innovative teaching practices27. Conversely, weaker work values may lead to negative emotions towards educational work, resulting in ineffective teaching and teacher burnout. When teachers view their educational work as a mission, such an adequate state of mind weakens the negative impact of after-work WRSMU as a job demand for employees. This research proposes that stronger work values can mitigate the relationship between teachers’ use of work-related social media after work and job burnout. Therefore, we hypothesize that:

Hypothesis 5: Teacher work values negatively moderate the relationship between after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) and teachers’ job burnout.

Method

Sample and procedure

The study was conducted in Sichuan Province of China where 139 universities are settled. We selected through a stratified random sampling method by distributing questionnaires on platform Questionnaire Star and Credamo which are widely recognized data collection platforms in China. In this study, questionnaires will be distributed within the teacher ratio. What’s more we also set up a filter item “did you use instant messaging apps or social media for work-related matters after work” to help us filter out questionnaires that do not meet the requirements. In this way, we make sure that the sample accurately reflected the diversity of university teachers in Sichuan of China. A total of 1000 questionnaires were distributed, and 936 were collected. After screening for eligibility and excluding respondents who did not use instant messaging apps or social media for work-related matters after work, 842 valid questionnaires were obtained.

Measurement

To ensure the reliability and validity of the research findings, all constructs were derived or adapted from well-established scales. We used Brislin’s (1973) back-translation approach to translate English items into Chinese.

Work-related public social media use after work hours

Was adopted from Men et al. (2020)45 and van Zoonen et al., (2017)46, referring to paper form Yue (2022) published in Journal Business Research11. The scale includes 4 dimensions, consumption (three items), contribution (three items), creation (three items) and conversation (two items). We focus on investigating the extent to which surveyed teachers engage in communication related to work using instant messaging software after work. Two selection items, “(1) Do I actively engage in communication related to work using instant messaging software after work?” and “(2) Do I receive work-related messages through instant messaging software after work?” were used to screen the validity of the questionnaires. In conclusion, the scale ranged from “Never occurs (1 point)” to “Always occurs (5 points)” and consisted of 11 items. A sample item is “After work, I still voice opinions and concerns about my organization/work.” The Cronbach’s α was 0.850.

Teacher work stress scale

Eleven items adopted form Jo and Lee (2022)30 were used to measure teachers’ work stress. Participants rate the extent to which they experience work stress from “Never bothers me (1 point)” to “Always bothers me (5 points)”, with higher scores indicating higher levels of work stress. A sample item is “The students’ behavior and educational ideas have changed, and I have to adjust my teaching methods.” The Cronbach’s α was 0.835.

Teacher work values scale

The Teacher Work Values scale was developed by Skaalvik & Skaalvik (2020)27 based on the work values classification and operational definition with 13 items included. Respondents rate their agreement with the statements on a scale ranging from “Slightly agree (1 point)” to “Completely agree (5 points)”, with higher scores indicating higher levels of work values. A sample item is “Even when facing bottlenecks in teaching work, I am still willing to break through and exceed them.” The Cronbach’s α was 0.832.

Teacher job burnout scale

The Teacher Job burnout scale was adapted from the revised Maslach Burnout Inventory for Educators (MBI-ES) by Maslach et al. (1986)47. It includes a total of 10 items, and respondents rate the frequency of experiencing job burnout from “Never (1 point)” to “Always (5 points)”, with higher scores indicating higher levels of job burnout. A sample item is “I feel lethargic when I wake up in the morning, knowing that I have to face another day of teaching work.” The Cronbach’s α was 0.871.

Result

This study utilized SPSS 23.0 and AMOS 24.0 for data processing and analysis. According to Podsakoff et al. (2003), we employed Harman’s single factor test to investigate common method variance (CMV). The results of Harman’s single-factor test indicated that the first factor accounted for 23.628% of the variance, which is below the 40% threshold, suggesting that common method bias was not a significant concern. To establish the discriminant validity among individual constructs, a four-factor confirmatory factor analysis was conducted. The model fit indices were evaluated, with χ2/df = 1.093, GFI = 0.937, AGFI = 0.921, RMSEA = 0.038, TLI = 0.952, IFI = 0.967, and CFI = 0.946, indicating a good fit of the structural model in this study. Discussion

According to the job demands-resources model, we propose a moderated mediation model that explores the relationship between teachers’ after-hour WRSMU and job burnout. With the rapid development of information and communication technology (ICT), the use of social media has become increasingly common. Employees are not only required to use social media during working hours, but are also required to continue using social media to handle work matters during non-work hours49. Using questionnaire survey data, this research indicates that teachers’ work-related social media use negatively related to job burnout. The work stress plays the mediating role between teachers’ after-hour WRSMU and burnout. Furthermore, teachers’ work value can not buffer the influence of teachers’ after-hour WRSMU on work stress.

Techers’ work value

The result shows that teachers’ work value fail to moderate the relationship between teachers’ after-hour WRSMU and burnout. We explore several potential factors: First, from the theoretical level: JD-R theory emphasizes the value of “resources” in buffering “demands”, but in the teacher groups, work values may not really play as resource. For example, high-value teachers may instead be more willing to put in additional work (including WRSMU), negating their positive buffering as a resource. In addition, teacher work values may have a more direct impact on job meaning50, professional commitment51, or engagement, rather than directly moderating the negative effects of WRSMU on work stress. Therefore, its effect may be achieved indirectly through other mediations (e.g., engagement, recovery experience) rather than direct moderation.

Secondly, from the methodological level: First of all, the current study is to apply the enterprise employee value scale to the teacher group, which may lead to the scale not fully capturing the real value orientation of teachers (such as educational mission sense and student orientation). This incomplete fit of measurement may weaken statistical significance. Secondly, in the teaching profession, “high work values” are almost normative. If the general values of teachers in the sample are high and the difference is not large (i.e., homogeneous), the moderating effect is difficult to appear.

Future researches could redefine the role of teachers’ work values, for example, as a mediating variable (influencing how teachers understand the meaning of WRSMU) rather than a moderating variable. Secondly, consider the interaction of multiple resources: combine organizational support, social support, and personal values to explore more complex moderation paths. Finally, future research can also consider redeveloping the Teachers Work Value scale that is more suitable for teachers, as well as distinguishing the dimensions of values (teacher values may be divided into “intrinsic values” (educational mission, meaning) and “extrinsic values” (treatment, status), and different dimensions may have different directions for WRSMU.”

Theorical contribution

Firstly, different from the studies by Yue (2022)11 and Derks et al. (2014)10, this study adopts another view. Regarding the researches about after-work use of instant messaging apps for work, it mainly focuses on relevant stakeholders in the educational setting, such as administrators, colleagues, parents, and students. We specially limit WRSMU of teachers, to work-related interactions with students/colleagues/administrators via social media after work, such as answering questions, urging tasks, transferring materials, and communicating for approvals. One of advantages of our research is that we uniquely focuses on teachers’ psychological experiences and shifts the research perspective from work hours to after-work periods, while incorporating characteristics specific to Chinese teachers. The present research contributes to the literature by providing additional insights into teachers’ work-related stress under current circumstances.

Furthermore, compared to previous studies on JD-R9, this study expands on other temporal work characteristic variables to understand the issues in the current educational context. Bakker and Demerouti (2017)9 reviewed the literature on the JD-R model and emphasized that researchers should explore the potential positive effects of workplace work. In recent years, more and more researchers apply JD-R to investigate social media use52,53. Technologies such as ICT and social media have resource attributes - helping employees complete work tasks and reducing labor costs. However, ICTs such as social media have double-edged sword attributes: on the one hand, they greatly improve the flexibility of teachers’ work; on the other hand, they will inevitably increase teachers’ personal working hours and workload49. Thus, After-hour WRSMU can be treated as a type of job demand49,54 which might lead to detrimental outcomes, such as turnover intention55. The results of our study show a positive relationship between after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) and teachers’ work stress. What’s more, we further identified that the demand role of the using communication apps exceeds its role of the resource, especially in the after-work context.

Most prior studies on relationships between WRSMU and burnout have emphasized individual-level moderators, such as personal values or psychological adjustment strategies, such as resilience56. In contrast, our study shows that teachers’ work values did not exert a significant moderating effect. This finding suggests that individual dispositions may not always buffer the impact of stress in academic settings. Instead, there is need to reconsider the burnout pathway, shifting scholars’ attention from individual to the organizational level. In this way, our study encourages the field to refine existing models of burnout, thereby offering a novel perspective for both theory development and practice.

Implication contribution

In today’s schools, school administrators or teachers often use instant messaging applications to assign teaching and administrative tasks or communicate with students and parents. Although using communication apps to discuss work-related matters or tasks during after-work hours can be efficient for individuals and organizations12, it can also hinder the restoration of teacher’s resources, which affects their mood and stress levels. Nevertheless, during our survey, we found that some teachers stated, “Although it is a bit tiring, these tasks are my responsibilities as a teacher. We are different from enterprise employees: we are in education and are responsible not only for students’ academic performance but also for their mental health.” This means that although WRSMU can have a negative impact on teachers, this negative effect still exists in individual differences. Teachers should find the most suitable adjustment method for themselves through continuous exploration and adjustment. In additional to utilize a boundary tactic to separate work and home, according to Zipay and Rodell (2024)57, teachers can also actively incorporate work-related elements into leisure activities.

Furthermore, given that the moderating effect of faculty work values was not found to be significant in this study, our focus necessarily shifts from the individual level to the organizational level. Therefore, we put forward several recommendations aimed at schools and educational administrations. One key suggestion is the establishment of clear “communication boundaries during non-work hours”, such as administrative requests or teaching-related messages, during evenings, weekends, and official holidays. By implementing such boundaries, teachers would be better able to separate their professional and personal time, allowing them to rest, recharge, and restore their psychological resources.

Finally, our research highlights that reducing burnout requires systemic change at the organizational level with collective norms and organizational support. In this way, it’s also necessary for education administrations to formulate policies and programs to help reduce this pressure and excessive concern, such as permission for teachers during which they are allowed to have “blackout” time. We should reach a consensus that education is not only the responsibility of teachers, but also family education is the foundation. It is not feasible to rely entirely on teachers, and the joint training of teachers, families and even society can truly do a good job in a good education system. In summary, this study emphasizes the joint reduction of the adverse effects of WRSMU on teacher well-being through institutional constraints and training support.

Research limitations and future research directions

This study provides valuable insights into the relationship between after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU), work stress, work values, and job burnout. However, several limitations need to be addressed, and these limitations open avenues for future research.

This research employed a cross-sectional design, which limits the ability to establish causal relationships among variables. Future research could adopt a longitudinal design to examine the dynamics of how after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU) influences work stress and burnout over time. Longitudinal studies would provide deeper insights into the temporal sequence and long-term impacts of these relationships. For example, repeated measurements across multiple time points would allow examination of whether increases in WRSMU predict subsequent declines in well-being or job satisfaction, or conversely, whether individuals experiencing stress or high demands at one point in time later turn to WRSMU as a coping or compensatory mechanism. Such a design would also make it possible to explore reciprocal or cyclical relationships, offering a more nuanced understanding of how WRSMU and its consequences unfold dynamically across time.

Besides, the effect of work values on teachers’ work stress is still worth exploring. Although the data analysis results of this study show that the moderating effect of teachers’ work values is not significant. Future research can explore other factors that can buffer the impact of teachers’ after-hour WMSUR on work stress.

The study focused exclusively on university teachers in Sichuan of China, which may limit the generalizability of findings to other cultural or geographic contexts. Future research could include samples from diverse countries and educational systems to explore cultural differences and similarities in how work-related communication impacts stress and burnout.

Data collection relied on self-reported questionnaires, which may be subject to biases such as social desirability or recall errors. Future studies could incorporate objective measures (e.g., actual time spent on instant messaging apps, physiological stress indicators) or mixed methods (e.g., interviews or diary studies) to enhance data accuracy and reliability.

Conclusion

This study highlights the complex relationship between teachers’ after-hours work-related social media use (WRSMU), work stress, work values, and job burnout. The findings demonstrate that while Work-related public social media use after work hours can enhance communication efficiency, excessive use after work hours increases work stress and contributes to job burnout.

The implications of these findings are significant for educational institutions, administrators, and policymakers. Providing structured support systems, promoting work-life balance, and encouraging healthy digital communication practices can help mitigate the negative consequences of technology use after work. Furthermore, future research directions outlined in this study can guide scholars in advancing the understanding of the interplay between technology, stress, and occupational health across diverse contexts and professions. By addressing these challenges, researchers and practitioners can contribute to creating healthier and more sustainable work environments.
