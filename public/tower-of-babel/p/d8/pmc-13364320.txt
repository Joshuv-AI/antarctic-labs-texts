Molecularly defined renal cell carcinomas
Authors: Petr Stránský, Jiří Kolář, Dominika Šiková, Tomáš Pitra, Jan Pernický, Ondřej Fiala, Kristýna Pivovarčíková, Ondrej Ondič, Milan Hora
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Current World Health Organization (WHO) Classification of Urinary and Male Genital Tumours (2022) has introduced minor updates in the classification of renal cell carcinomas (RCC). Among the most notable changes is the recognition of a new category: molecularly defined RCC. This group comprises several distinct tumour entities characterized by specific genetic alterations. The inclusion of this category reflects the growing importance of molecular testing in the diagnosis, prognosis and treatment planning of renal malignancies. This review summarizes the basic information, clinical data, and treatment options of these rare RCCs. Due to the low prevalence of molecularly defined RCCs, the clinical management remains challenging, and standardized, evidence-based treatment guidelines are still lacking, particularly for the systemic therapy of advanced RCCs. As part of the review, a clinical case of a female patient with metastatic TFE3-translocated RCC is also presented, illustrating the diagnostic and therapeutic challenges.

Introduction

Renal cell carcinoma (RCC) accounts for approximately 3% of all cancers, with the highest incidence in Western countries [1]. Renal cell tumours include a wide spectrum of histological entities. The most common subtypes are clear cell RCC (70%), papillary RCC (10–15%), and chromophobe RCC (5%) [2].

The 5th edition of the World Health Organization (WHO) classification of renal tumours introduced several changes, among them — a new diagnostic category called “molecularly defined renal carcinomas” [3, 4]. This category includes seven RCC entities (TFE3-rearranged RCC, TFEB-altered RCC, ELOC-mutated RCC, fumarate hydratase (FH)-deficient RCC, succinate dehydrogenase (SDH)-deficient RCC, ALK-rearranged RCC and SWI/SNF-related matrix-associated actin-dependent regulator of chromatin subfamily B member 1 (SMARCB1)-deficient renal medullary carcinoma for which molecular-genetic testing is essential to establish a diagnosis [3]. The group of “molecularly defined renal carcinomas” contains entities already known from the previous WHO classifications (some with modifications), as well as an entirely new entity (ELOC-mutated RCC). SMARCB1-deficient renal medullary carcinoma represents a conceptual change for the entity previously well-known as renal medullary carcinoma [5]. The aim of this review is to summarize the information on molecularly defined RCC.

TFE3-rearranged RCC

Compared with the previous 2016 WHO classification, the category of “MiT translocation renal carcinomas” [5] has been divided into renal carcinomas with TFE3 gene rearrangement (TFE3-rearranged RCC) and those with TFEB gene alteration (TFEB-rearranged RCC and TFEB-amplified RCC) [4].

TFE3-rearranged RCCs are characterised by a translocation of the TFE3 gene located on the Xp11 chromosome, resulting in fusion with one of many different partner genes [3, 6] (ASPSCR1, PRCC, SFPQ, NONO, RBM10, MED15, LUC7L3, FUBP1, DVL2, EWSR1, SETD1B, ZC3H4, ARID1B, CLTC, GRIPAP1, KAT6A, KHSRP, MATR3, NEAT1, PARP14, TMED6, and U2AF2 [7]). Recognition of TFE3 gene fusion partners may be key to predicting tumour behaviour, as it was shown that different fusion partners may be associated with different morphologic phenotypes, biological behaviours, and prognoses [7]. Increased expression of TFE3 fusion proteins acts as an aberrant transcription factor that activates the expression of various target genes, including those commonly activated by the transcription factor MITF [8, 9]. TFE3 fusion genes may amplify signalling pathways that subsequently promote tumorigenesis, for example, by upregulating the mTORC1 pathway, increasing TGF-β levels, or dysregulating CD40L, which helps the tumour escape the immune response [10–12].

According to some authors, TFE3-rearranged RCC accounts for approximately 40% of renal tumours in the paediatric population [13] and it is the most common type of RCC in children [14]. Approximately 15% of all RCC cases in young adults (under 45 years) are classified as TFE3 RCC, while this tumour occurs in 1.6–4% of RCC cases in the overall adult population [13]. Lu et al. [15] described in their study that Black and Asian populations have a significantly higher prevalence of translocated RCC compared with White populations (7.1% vs. 1.9% and 14.3% vs. 1.9%, respectively).

The diagnosis can be made by RNA sequencing, reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), or fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) method [16, 17], which is considered the gold standard [6, 18]. A typical diagnostic approach also includes the use of immunohistochemistry (IHC), though due to its lack of specificity, IHC cannot replace molecular techniques [16].

According to available data, outcome of patients with TFE3 RCC is significantly worse than that of patients with papillary RCC but comparable to the outcome of those with clear cell RCC [19]. Better prognosis is reported in paediatric patients [19, 20], which is explained by a lower incidence of genomic events than in adults [20]. Disease relapse can occur even after many years; for example, Denize et al. [21] describe a relapse 19 years after radical surgery, highlighting the need for long-term follow-up.

Currently, there is no recommended systemic therapy protocol for advanced-stage TFE3-rearranged RCC. Treatment with tyrosine kinase inhibitors (TKIs) and mTOR inhibitors has shown only limited therapeutic effect according to available studies [22–24]. Bakouny et al. [25] found that translocated RCC exhibit the presence of tumour infiltrating CD8+ T cells, suggesting a potential positive response to immunotherapy. Boilève et al. [26] observed in a multicenter, retrospective study of 24 patients with metastatic translocated RCC (21 TFE3 and 3 TFEB RCC) treated with immunotherapy (nivolumab, ipilimumab, and pembrolizumab + utomilumab) a median overall survival (OS) of 24 months. The partial response rate was 16.7%, observed in 4 patients, while stable disease (SD) was achieved in 3 patients (12.5%). The median progression-free survival (PFS) was 2.5 months (range: 1–40 months). The median follow-up time was 19.3 months. A non-randomized phase II clinical trial led by McGregor et al. [27] achieved an objective therapeutic response in 1 out of 5 patients with TFE3 translocation treated with a combination of atezolizumab and bevacizumab. In a retrospective, multicenter study of advanced translocated RCC (22 TFE3 RCC and 7 TFEB RCC), a higher objective response rate (ORR = 36% vs. 5.5%,) and longer OS (median OS 30.7 months vs. 17.8 months) were demonstrated in patients treated with a combination of immune checkpoint inhibitor (ICIs) with vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF)-targeted therapy compared with the combination of two ICIs (nivolumab + ipilimumab) [28].

TFEB-altered RCC

Within TFEB-altered RCC, two subunits are distinguished — TFEB-rearranged RCC and TFEB-amplified RCC [4]. In both cases, there is excessive expression of TFEB [3, 29, 30], which acts as an aberrant transcription factor activating the expression of several target genes, including those normally activated by MITF (microphthalmia-associated transcription factor) [30]. Cases with concomitant amplification and translocation of TFEB have been described; however, RCCs with both TFEB translocation and amplification did not show significant clinical or pathological differences [4, 31].

The occurrence of RCC with TFEB amplification is probably more common than TFEB translocation [32]. TFEB-rearranged RCC is defined by the translocation of the TFEB gene, most commonly leading to the fusion of MALAT1::TFEB genes [33]. TFEB-rearranged RCC occurs mostly in young patients, with an average age of 34 years [34] (median age: 31 years) [33] and with low stage [35] and is usually less aggressive than TFE3-rearranged RCC [33]. Xia et al. [36] suggested an association between the presence of necrosis and aggressive tumour behaviour. TFEB-amplified RCCs are characterized by amplification of the 6p21 locus carrying the TFEB gene, leading to overexpression of the TFEB gene [35, 37, 38]. Most of them have shown co-amplification of the VEGFA gene located in the 6p21.1 chromosomal region [39]. TFEB-amplified RCCs are characterized by aggressive behaviour [37], often with advanced stage and significant metastatic potential (with the potential to metastasize many years after the primary diagnosis) [35]. In contrast to TFEB rearranged RCC, TFEB-amplified RCCs occur more frequently in older patients [4], with a median age of 65 years [33, 35].

Macroscopically and histologically, these tumours do not have any specific appearance [3, 33]. Diagnosis is based on FISH [3, 33]. Certain ambiguities arise from not entirely clearly defined diagnostic criteria for TFEB-amplified RCC. The literature distinguishes between TFEB “low-amplified” RCCs with a low level of amplification (5–10 amplified gene copies) and TFEB “high-amplified” RCCs with a high level of amplification (> 10 amplified gene copies/nucleus) [38]. The significance of the degree of amplification is not yet fully clarified. Some authors state that regardless of the degree of amplification, all TFEB-amplified RCCs behave aggressively [31].

There is no specific recommended systemic treatment protocol for these RCCs. In animal studies, Zhang et al. [40] demonstrated that TFEB mediates immune evasion and resistance to mTOR inhibitors through the induction of programmed death-ligand 1 (PD-L1) expression. Increased PD-L1 expression has been described in TFEB-rearranged [41] and TFEB-amplified [31] RCCs, and a sustained complete response to therapy with programmed cell death protein 1 (PD-1) inhibitor nivolumab [42]. Gupta et al. [38] evaluated VEGF-targeted therapy (n = 4), with one patient with TFEB-amplified RCC showing an excellent response to treatment with sunitinib in first line, which was initiated approximately 19 months after nephrectomy. Patient subsequently received a combination therapy with temsirolimus and sorafenib, during which he survived for approximately 31 months (mean survival after nephrectomy of 31 months; range: 17–50). Despite the limited number of patients, this suggests potential therapy in this tumour group [38]. Several registered clinical trials are currently investigating therapeutic options for these carcinomas.

ELOC-mutated RCC

ELOC (formerly TCEB1)-mutated RCC is a very rare tumour (about 40 cases have been documented in the literature) [43–47] characterized by a mutation of the ELOC (TCEB1) gene at locus 8q21.11. ELOC encodes elongin C, which helps the VHL complex ubiquitinate and inactivate hypoxia-inducible factor 1 alpha (HIF-1α) [48]. Loss of elongin C function due to ELOC mutation leads to the inability to degrade HIF1-alpha and its accumulation, promoting tumorigenesis [48].

ELOC-mutated RCC shows significant morphological overlap with RCCs with fibro(leio)myomatous stroma and mTOR signalling pathway alteration (TSC1/TSC2/MTOR mutation). The definitive relationship between these two entities is unclear and under research [49].

ELOC-mutated RCC behaves less aggressively than clear cell RCC [48, 50], most ELOC-mutated RCCs have been reported to have an indolent course [48]. However, even rare metastatic disease has been reported [51]. The data for an accurate prognosis assessment are limited. ELOC-mutated RCCs typically have a low clinical stage and grade at the time of diagnosis [51, 52]. DiNatale et al. [51] published five cases of ELOC RCCs, four of which were of advanced stage (III–IV). Two patients developed distant metastases, including pleural, lung, and mediastinal lymph node involvement. One patient was treated with pazopanib, leading to disease stabilization, but he died of therapy-related complications. The second patient received a combination of atezolizumab and bevacizumab with an objective treatment response, achieved complete remission (CR) and remained disease-free for more than four years [51].

Fumarate hydratase-deficient RCC

The nomenclature for FH-deficient RCC (FH-deficient RCC) [3] has changed; in the previous WHO classification (2016), it was called “hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma-associated RCC” [5].

Fumarate hydratase deficient RCC is characterized by a mutation/loss of heterozygosity (LOH) of the FH gene [53], encoding the enzyme FH. Decreased activity or loss of activity of FH enzyme results in an increase of fumarate levels and aberrant cellular metabolism. Fumarate reacts with cysteine proteins, forming S-(2-succino)-cysteine (2SC), which disrupts oxidative phosphorylation and promotes tumorigenesis [54]. The biallelic inactivation of FH is a common event either for sporadic cases of FH deficient RCC or an autosomal dominant disorder called hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma syndrome (HLRCC), predisposing individuals to multiple cutaneous and uterine leiomyomas and the development of RCC [55]. Carriers of germline FH alteration have an estimated lifetime risk of 15% of developing RCC [56]. Demonstration of FH alteration is required for the diagnosis [3].

The real prevalence of FH-deficient RCC is unknown. It is slightly more common in men and the median age at diagnosis is 44 years (range: 21–65 years) [57]. Among children and young adults, FH-deficient RCC was present in 12% of patients [58]. Taniguchi et al. [59] reported a case of FH-deficient RCC even in a 7-year-old boy. This tumour may present with as a large unilateral solitary mass, often diagnosed at an advanced stage with distant metastases [60].

FH-deficient RCCs are considered aggressive tumours [60, 61]. Harrison et al. [62], in a study of five patients with HLRCC, stated that some FH-deficient tumours have a favourable prognosis despite nuclear atypia. Therefore, grading assessment is not suitable for these tumours [3]. In a cohort of 27 patients with FH-deficient RCC, 57% of cases were ≥ pT3 stage at diagnosis, 52% had positive lymph nodes, and 19% had distant metastases. After a mean follow-up of 27 months (range: 1 to 114 months), 39% of patients died from the disease and 26% experienced disease progression [57]. Another retrospective study of 32 patients reported that after a median follow-up of 16 months (range: 1 to 118 months), only 50% of patients were alive and 31% were alive with active disease [63]. Due to the risk of early RCC onset in FH germinal mutation carriers, regular kidney examinations, including annual abdominal MRI, are recommended [56]. Renal tumours should be surgically removed with a wide resection margin, possibly with retroperitoneal lymphadenectomy [56]. In cases of metastasis, systemic therapy within a clinical trial should be considered [56].

A retrospective study of 77 patients conducted in China showed better outcomes with combined ICI and TKI therapy compared with other first-line therapies, including bevacizumab/erlotinib combination therapy or TKI monotherapy [64]. In another multicenter retrospective study, the overall treatment response rates for different types of therapy were as follows: 50% for cabozantinib, 43% for sunitinib, 63% for other antiangiogenics (sorafenib, pazopanib and axitinib), 30% for erlotinib plus bevacizumab, 0% for mTOR inhibitors, and 18% for ICIs [including nivolumab-ipilimumab or nivolumab or other anti-PD-1/PD-L1 study drugs] [61].

Succinate dehydrogenase-deficient RCC

Succinate dehydrogenase-deficient RCC is characterized by the loss of SDH expression [3] as a result of a pathogenic mutation that can occur in any of the SDHx genes (SDHA, SDHB, SDHC, SDHD) [65, 66], but the SDHB gene is most commonly affected [66, 67]. Inactivation of the SDH complex almost always occurs in the presence of a germline mutation and a second hit — somatic mutation [66]. Mutations in the SDH genes lead to SDH-complex dysfunction and predispose patients to development of various tumours [68]. Germline mutations in SDHx subunit genes are often associated with various other tumours, such as paraganglioma, pheochromocytoma, gastrointestinal stromal tumour (GIST), and pituitary adenoma [66, 69]. The diagnosis of SDH-deficient RCC is an indication for genetic counselling and testing for germline mutations in SDH genes [3].

Gill et al. [67] estimate the incidence of SDH-deficient RCC to be 0.05–0.2% of all RCCs. The average age at diagnosis is 39.8 years (median age: 43.5 years, range: 14–76 years), with a slight male predominance [male:female (M:F) = 1.3:1] [67].

SDH-deficient RCCs are often multifocal, and with longer follow-up, bilateral tumours can be identified in up to 26% of patients [67]. Higher nuclear grade, intratumoral heterogeneity, the presence of necrosis, or sarcomatoid transformation significantly increases the likelihood of metastatic disease [67].

The prognosis of SDH-deficient RCCs is favourable in low-grade tumours but significantly worse in high-grade tumours with a high degree of nuclear atypia, presence of sarcomatoid changes, or signs of coagulative necrosis [69, 70]. Gill et al. [67] reported that 12% of patients with low-grade carcinomas developed metastases, whereas 70% of patients with high-grade carcinoma experienced metastatic spread. There are also reports in the literature of very late recurrences, up to 30 years after primary surgery [67]. Long-term follow-up with monitoring for additional SDH-deficient neoplasms is indicated in cases associated with a germline mutation [66, 70]. Currently, no specific treatment is recommended for advanced-stage disease [1]. Genetic testing and counselling are indicated for affected individuals and their family members. Specific screening and surveillance protocols are established for SDH deficiency syndrome [71]. In the systemic treatment of SDH-deficient RCC, the use of TKIs may be potentially effective due to the overlap between pathways activated by SDH deficiency and VHL loss [72].

ALK-rearranged RCC

ALK-rearranged RCCs harbour chromosomal translocations leading to gene fusions involving the ALK gene located on chromosome 2p23 [3]. The literature divides ALK-rearranged RCCs into two categories: VCL-ALK RCC and non-VCL-ALK RCC, based on fusion partner, which is associated with different clinical manifestations [73]. In non-VCL-ALK RCCs, various ALK gene fusions give rise to fusion proteins that activate signalling pathways promoting cell survival and replication (STRN, TPM3, EML4, HOOK1, PLEKHA7, CLIP1, KIF5B, and KIAA1217) [74, 75].

VCL-ALK RCCs typically occur in younger patients (Smith et al. [74] described case of 6-year-old boy) and are associated with sickle cell trait and specific histological features [73]. However, a case without sickle cell anaemia or hemoglobinopathy was reported [76]. Seven cases of VCL-ALK RCCs have been described in the literature to date [76]. In contrast, non-VCL-ALK RCCs occur across a broader age range, have diverse morphological appearances [73] and are not typically associated with sickle cell trait [73]. Diagnosis requires the demonstration of ALK gene rearrangement through FISH or sequencing methods [49].

Data on the clinical course of patients with ALK-rearranged RCCs are very limited. To date, approximately 60 cases [76–78] have been reported, with a slight male predominance (M:F = 1.14:1) [76]. Patients ranged from children to adults (age range: 3–92 years) [78]. Most ALK-rearranged RCCs appear indolent but can exhibit aggressive behaviour, including metastatic spread, which has been documented in approximately 25% of the reported cases [49]. Galea et al. [77] divided a cohort of 46 patients into RCCs with and without TPM3-ALK gene fusion and found that RCCs with TPM3-ALK fusion had a more indolent course than RCCs without TPM3-ALK fusion, with metastases developing in only one and nine patients in each group, respectively [77]. Of the 60 ALK RCC cases reported, 4 patients died, 12 were alive with disease, and 31 were disease-free (mean follow-up 41.5 months; range: 2–324 months); follow-up was unavailable for 13 cases [76].

Targeted therapy with anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) inhibitors, such as entrectinib or alectinib, appears promising in both VCL-ALK and non-VCL-ALK RCCs [76, 79–81]. ALK inhibitors specifically target abnormal ALK fusion proteins, disrupting their signalling pathways and thus inhibiting tumour cell proliferation and differentiation [80, 81]. Several clinical trials are currently investigating ALK inhibitors in various malignancies, including RCC (NCT02568267, NCT04802876, NCT03213652).

SMARCB1-deficient renal medullary carcinoma

SMARCB1-deficient renal medullary carcinoma is a high-grade carcinoma with a deficiency of SMARCB1 (SWI/SNF-related matrix-associated actin-dependent regulator of chromatin subfamily B member 1, also known as INI1) caused by inactivation of the SMARCB1 gene on chromosome 22 (most commonly by chromosome translocations or deletions), typically occurring in patients with sickle cell trait or haemoglobin SC disease [3]. Because of sickle cell anaemia, this tumour is typically associated with patients of African descent [82, 83]. A wide diagnostic age range has been reported, from childhood to older age [83–85] with a median age of 29.5 years (range: 14–66 years) [85] and a male predominance (M:F = 2,2:1) [85]. The carcinomas are usually large and poorly circumscribed [3]. These are highly aggressive tumours, with most patients (67–77%) having distant metastases at the time of diagnosis [85, 86]. The 2022 WHO classification includes SMARCB1-deficient medullary-like RCC (formerly RCC unclassified with medullary phenotype) as a subtype of renal medullary carcinoma. Approximately 13 cases have been described in the literature, usually with aggressive behaviour and poor prognosis [87].

The etiopathogenesis is not yet fully understood. As a tumour suppressor, SMARCB1 protein plays a key role in controlling cell proliferation, and its loss permits unchecked cellular growth [82]. Msaouel et al. [88] proposed that more frequent occurrence of SMARCB1-deficient renal medullary carcinoma in the right kidney, may results from lower medullary blood flow from the longer right renal artery, which may increase susceptibility to ischemia in individuals with sickle cell trait.

Loss of SMARCB1 induces replication stress, rendering tumour cells particularly vulnerable to cytotoxic agents such as platinum salts, gemcitabine, and doxorubicin [89]. Platinum-based cytotoxic chemotherapy currently represents the most effective but short-term palliative clinical effect [90]. Monotherapies targeting VEGF or mTOR inhibitors are ineffective [90]. Case studies have been published demonstrating therapeutic responses to ICIs, including nivolumab monotherapy [91, 92] and combination of pembrolizumab and lenvatinib [93]. Tang et al. [94] reported a median PFS of 12.3 months and an ORR of 50% in four patients treated with axitinib (TKI) plus sintilimab (ICI).

It is of paramount importance that SMARCB1 deficiency is not specific only for SMARCB1-deficient renal medullary carcinoma, SMARCB1 deficiency has been described in other tumours, too [95]. Other neoplasms with de novo SMARCB1 inactivation are malignant rhabdoid tumour of the kidney, which occurs predominantly in children under 3 years of age [96]. However, SMARCB1 deficiency may be present in different RCCs with non-specific histology (collecting duct-like, high-grade papillary, medullary-like without hemoglobinopathy, and undifferentiated RCC) [95] and secondary loss of SMARCB1 may be found in dedifferentiated parts of originally well-differentiated RCCs, most commonly arising from clear cell RCC, less frequently from papillary or chromophobe subtypes [95]. Secondary loss of INI1 has even been reported in various types of RCC, including TFE3-rearranged RCC [97], FH-deficient RCC [98], and SDH-deficient RCC with a pathogenic SDHB variant [99].

Case report

A 30-year-old woman with no significant family history of cancer or previous illnesses presented to a general practitioner in February 2022 with left flank pain. An abdominal CT in February 2023, followed by a fluorine-18 fluorodeoxyglucose positron emission tomography/computed tomography (18F-FDG PET/CT) of the torso, revealed a lesion in the upper pole of the left kidney measuring 30 × 20 × 20 mm, retroperitoneal lymphadenopathy causing dilatation of the left renal collecting system, and additional lymphadenopathy above and below the diaphragm, raising suspicion of metastatic disease originating from the left kidney (Fig. 2–3). A haematology-oncology examination in February 2023 ruled out lymphoma.

Histological verification was performed in April 2023 on a suspected metastasis in a left supraclavicular lymph node. Initially, histology suggested metastasis of papillary RCC, but after next-generation sequencing (NGS), the diagnosis of TFE3-translocated RCC was confirmed. Cytoreductive nephrectomy was not indicated for the patient, as the procedure would have been technically challenging due to nodular masses in the hilum area.

Systemic therapy with sunitinib 50 mg p.o. once daily, in the regimen of 2 weeks on, 1 week off, was initiated at another oncology facility in June 2023. Owing to unspecified toxicity, the dose was reduced to 37.5 mg p.o. once daily. The treatment was briefly interrupted in October 2023 due to isolated hyperbilirubinemia but resumed in November 2023. Since November 2023, treatment has continued at the oncology department of our hospital at the patient’s request.

The patient showed clinical improvement, with SD for 13 months according to RECIST 1.1 criteria [100]. However, due to overall disease progression in July 2024 (Fig. 4), treatment was switched in August 2024 to pembrolizumab 200 mg i.v. every 3 weeks and lenvatinib 20 mg p.o. once daily. Due to the progression of dilation of the left kidney’s collecting system caused by the enlargement of the lymph node bundle in the hilum area (Fig. 3), an internal ureteral stent was placed in June 2024. In February 2025, the lenvatinib dose was reduced to 10 mg p.o. once daily owing to toxicity; SD was achieved for 8 months on CT. The patient is alive with disease and continues the established treatment.

The clinical course observed in our 30-year-old patient is consistent with published evidence indicating that TFE3-rearranged RCC typically presents in young adults [13] and tends to exhibit more aggressive behaviour in this age group than in paediatric patients [19, 20]. The limited efficacy of sunitinib, with disease stabilisation achieved for 13 months, aligns with reports demonstrating a variable and often modest response to VEGF-targeted and mTOR-targeted therapies in advanced TFE3-rearranged RCC [22–24]. Subsequent stabilisation under combined immunotherapy and VEGF-targeted treatment corresponds with recent studies suggesting that ICI-based regimens, either alone or in combination with VEGF inhibition, may offer improved therapeutic benefit in translocation RCC [25–28].

Conclusions

We summarised current knowledge about the group of molecularly defined RCCs. In recent years, significant progress has been made in understanding the molecular characteristics of renal tumours resulting in more accurate diagnosis and providing new opportunities for targeted therapy. However, due to the limited number of these cases and their rarity in clinical trials, more extensive research is necessary to confirm the efficacy and safety of systemic therapeutic strategies.
