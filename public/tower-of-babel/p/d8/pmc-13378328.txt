Analysis of clinical characteristics and whole-genome sequencing of carbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii isolates from pediatric patients in Guangxi, China
Authors: Minxue Liu, Chenglan Yan, Linlin Li, Liying Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundCarbapenem-resistant Acinetobacter baumannii (CRAB) is an urgent global threat and has been designated as a priority pathogen.MethodsWe conducted a retrospective study of pediatric patients with CRAB infection at a Maternal and Child Health Hospital in Guangxi, China, from January 2019 to December 2024. The bacterial isolates were then subjected to antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) and whole-genome sequencing (WGS).ResultsA total of 132 pediatric patients with CRAB infections were included in this study (83 with positive outcomes and 49 with negative outcomes). AST revealed that all CRAB isolates were multidrug-resistant (MDR). Polymyxin B (99.2% susceptible), minocycline (68.9%), and cefoperazone-sulbactam (65.2%) demonstrated the highest susceptibility rates among the antibiotics tested. WGS revealed that the most prevalent strain was ST2Pasteur/ST208Oxford - KL2(65.9%), harboring the OXA-23 carbapenemase. Furthermore, the genetically distinct ST1208Oxford-KL24 (22.0%), characterized by NDM-1 carriage, is the second most common sequence type and an emerging cause of pediatric hospital infections. A diverse array of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes, plasmid replicons, and virulence genes was detected across the isolates. Notably, the overall genetic profile of ST1208Oxford was similar to that of ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford, but distinct from that of ST2Pasteur. Multivariable logistic regression identified prior hospitalizations (OR = 3.236; P = 0.015), presence of a central venous catheter (CVC) (OR = 2.683; P = 0.028), and decreased platelet count (OR = 0.996; P = 0.005) as independent risk factors for negative outcomes in pediatric patients with CRAB infections.ConclusionsThis study details the clinical and molecular features of CRAB infections in pediatric patients in Guangxi, China. Pediatric infection control should be highly alert to the emergence of ST1208Oxford CRAB, which produces the NDM-1 carbapenemase.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12866-026-05188-0.

Intruduction

Acinetobacter baumannii has emerged as a major nosocomial pathogen, particularly in intensive care units (ICUs), causing pneumonia, bloodstream infections, abdominal infections, wound infection, urinary tract infections, and meningitis [1, 2]. Currently, carbapenem-resistant A. baumannii (CRAB) are prevalent worldwide and poses a serious threat to infected patients by increasing mortality, prolonging hospital stays, and raising healthcare costs [2]. According to data from the China Antimicrobial Surveillance Network (CHINET; https://www.chinets.com), the rate of CRAB in China has shown a fluctuating but upward trend over the past two decades, rising from approximately 40% to over 75%. This alarming rise is underpinned by the fact that CRAB is predominantly multidrug-resistant (MDR). Due to its multidrug resistance, considerable therapeutic challenges, and high mortality rates (ranging from 45% to 70%), the World Health Organization (WHO) has classified CRAB as a critical priority pathogen [3].

Over the past two decades, the clinical prominence of A. baumannii has been largely driven by its exceptional capacity to rapidly develop resistance to nearly all classes of antibiotics. The emergence of MDR strains is attributed to a diverse arsenal of resistance mechanisms, such as enzymatic antibiotic degradation, target site modification, efflux pump upregulation, and reduced membrane permeability [4, 5]. Furthermore, the genomic plasticity of A. baumannii enables it to accumulate resistance genes efficiently, largely mediated by the horizontal transfer of plasmids [6].

The management of CRAB infections in pediatric patients demands exceptional attention. Pediatric patients have been reported in a nosocomial outbreak caused by MDR, extensively drug-resistant (XDR), and CRAB [7–9]. The challenges of developing immune systems, limited symptom communication, and constrained antibiotic options due to safety concerns in children heighten the risks of infection, delayed diagnosis, and compromised treatment. Therefore, clinicians must maintain a heightened clinical suspicion in this population and consider implementing more aggressive supportive care and closer monitoring to improve outcomes. Although considerable research has examined the risk factors and genomic analysis of drug-resistant A. baumannii, most findings originate from studies in comprehensive hospitals and on adult cohorts [10–13]. Although relevant pediatric studies have been reported from numerous regions, most are limited by small sample sizes or a lack of comprehensive genetic data [14–19]. Specifically, the clinical and genomic landscape of CRAB infections in children from Guangxi, an underdeveloped region in South China, is largely unexplored. Therefore, we conducted a retrospective study at the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region, a provincial-level tertiary hospital, to characterize the clinical features, risk factors, and molecular epidemiology of CRAB in 132 pediatric patients by employing whole-genome sequencing (WGS).

Materials and methods

Study setting and patients

We conducted a retrospective review of medical records for patients with infections who were hospitalized at the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Guangxi, China, between January 2019 and December 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Medical Ethics Committee of the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region (No.2021-3-50), with the requirement that written informed consent be obtained from the parents or guardians of all pediatric participants. A total of 132 pediatric patients with CRAB infections were included. Clinical data and CRAB strains isolated from these patients were collected for analysis (Fig. 1). CRAB infection were defined as follows [20]: (i) presence of infectious symptoms such as fever, cough, and sputum production; (ii) elevated laboratory inflammatory markers (e.g., leukocytosis, elevated C-reactive protein and serum procalcitonin); and/or (iii) radiographic evidence suggesting infection; (ⅳ) microbiological evidence, defined either by the isolation of CRAB from a sterile site specimen or by microscopic observation of the gram-negative bacilli being phagocytosed by neutrophils in a clinical sample. For the sputum specimen, the white blood cell count was > 25 per low-power field, and the squamous cell count was < 5 per low-power field. The inclusion criteria for patients were as follows: (i) a diagnosis of CRAB infection confirmed by two experienced physicians; (ii) an age of 14 years or younger; and (iii) the availability of complete clinical data. Subsequent CRAB isolates from the same patient were excluded. To explore risk factors for CRAB infection outcomes, we compared patients with positive outcomes to those with negative outcomes. A positive outcome was characterized by the resolution of clinical signs and symptoms, normalization of inflammatory biomarkers, radiological improvement, and microbiological eradication, as confirmed by negative follow-up cultures in the absence of recurrence. The negative outcome was defined by treatment failure, encompassing either mortality or treatment discontinuation attributable to a poor clinical response.

Bacterial isolates and identification

A total of 132 non-duplicate CRAB isolates were collected. CRAB was defined as a clinical strain exhibiting resistance to carbapenems (imipenem or meropenem) based on the breakpoints established by the Clinical and Laboratory Standards Institute (CLSI) guidelines M100, 35th edition. All isolates were identified using the matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry system (MALDI-TOF/MS; Antobio MS600, Zhengzhou, China). All strains were preserved at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis.

Clinical data collection

A retrospective review of the hospital’s electronic medical records was conducted to collect clinical data from pediatric patients with positive CRAB cultures during the study period. The extracted information included demographic characteristics (gender and age), underlying diseases, history of invasive procedures, prior antibiotic and corticosteroid exposures, admission wards, prior hospitalizations, laboratory results within 48 h of CRAB detection, length of hospital stay, and clinical outcomes.

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing

Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) on the isolates was conducted using DL-96 A systems (Dier, China). Results were interpreted according to the guidelines provided in the CLSI M100, 35th edition. The breakpoints for cefoperazone-sulbactam against A. baumannii are as follows: ≤16/8 µg/mL, susceptible; 32/16 µg/mL, intermediate; and ≥ 64/32 µg/mL, resistant [21]. The following antibiotics commonly used in clinical practice were tested in our study: ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, cefepime, ampicillin-sulbactam, ticarcillin-clavulanic acid, piperacillin-tazobactam, cefoperazone-sulbactam, meropenem, imipenem, amikacin, gentamicin, levofloxacin, ciprofloxacin, sulfamethoxazole, polymyxin B, and minocycline. Quality control was performed using Pseudomonas aeruginosa ATCC 27,853 and Escherichia coli ATCC 25,922, obtained from the National Center for Clinical Laboratories in Beijing, China.

Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) and gene analyses

Single colonies of each A. baumannii strain were inoculated onto Columbia Blood Agar and incubated at 35 °C for 24 h. WGS was carried out for all 132 CRAB isolates. Briefly, bacterial cells were collected and genomic DNA was extracted using the MagPure Bacterial DNA Kit (D6361-02, Magen, China) following the manufacturer’s instruction. WGS was performed on the MGI DNBSEQ-T7 (MGI Tech, Shenzhen, China) platform at Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China). De novo genome assembly was carried out using SPAdes (v3.5.0). And functional gene annotation was performed through homology-based searches against multiple specialized databases with DIAMOND (v2.0.8).

The maximum-likelihood (ML) core-genome phylogenetic tree was constructed using FastTree with 1000 bootstraps under the generalized time-reversible model and was then visualized using iTOL v6 (https://itol.embl.de/).

The sequence type (ST) of A. baumannii isolates was assigned using both the Pasteur and Oxford schemes via the PubMLST database (https://pubmlst.org/organisms/acinetobacter-baumannii) [22]. The K-locus types (KLs) and lipooligosaccharide outer core biosynthesis locus types (OCLs) of the isolates were determined with Kaptive, a capsule identification program that references a curated A. baumannii-specific database (https://kaptive-web.erc.monash.edu/) [23]. The plasmid rep genes were identified and characterized using blast analysis against the Acinetobacter Plasmid Typing database (https://github.com/MehradHamidian/AcinetobacterPlasmidTyping) [24].

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes in the assembled A. baumannii genomes were predicted using the ResFinder 4.4.2 webserver (thresholds: ≥95% identity, ≥ 60% length; http://genepi.food.dtu.dk/resfinder) and the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) from the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD; https://card.mcmaster.ca/). The RGI analysis (v6.0.2 against CARD v3.2.7), restricted to ‘Perfect and Strict hits’, utilized both homology and SNP models for resistome prediction. Virulence factors were concurrently predicted using VFanalyzer with the 2023 Acinetobacter genus dataset from the Virulence Factor Database (VFDB; thresholds: ≥95% identity, ≥ 60% length; http://www.mgc.ac.cn/cgi-bin/VFs/v5/main.cgi) [25].

Statistical analysis

Statistical analysis was performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 25.0). Normally distributed continuous variables are presented as mean ± standard (x̄ ± s) deviation, while continuous variables with non-normal distribution are expressed as median along with interquartile range (IQR). Categorical data are reported as percentages.

Comparisons of continuous variables were conducted using the Student’s t-test for normally distributed data and the Mann–Whitney U-test for non-normally distributed data. Categorical variables were compared using the chi-square test or Fisher’s exact test, as appropriate. A P-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Univariate logistic regression was employed to identify potential risk factors. Clinically relevant variables from the factors with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis were subsequently entered into a multivariable logistic regression model to identify independent risk factors.

Results

Clinical characteristics of CRAB strains

In total, 132 non-duplicated isolates were collected from the pediatric patients diagnosed with CRAB infections between January 2019 and December 2024. The majority of the isolates were obtained from the respiratory tract (115/132, 87.1%), while the remaining isolates originated from blood (9/132, 6.8%), drainage fluid (6/132, 4.5%), and cerebrospinal fluid (2/132, 1.5%). Based on clinical outcomes, the 132 pediatric patients with CRAB infections were categorized into a positive outcome group (n = 83) and a negative outcome group (n = 49).

Detailed demographic and clinical characteristics of the pediatric patients are summarized in Table 1. No significant differences were observed between the positive and negative outcome groups in terms of gender, age, department, type of infection, length of hospital stay, or corticosteroid exposure. Additionally, the following factors also showed no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05): most invasive procedures—including mechanical ventilation, indwelling urethral catheterization, drainage tube placement, parenteral nutrition, and nasogastric feeding; exposure to most antibiotics such as β-lactams, β-lactam-β-lactamase inhibitors, oxazolidinones, quinolones, tetracyclines, macrolides, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole; underlying conditions including tumor and congenital heart disease; and laboratory findings including white blood cell count (WBC), prealbumin (PA), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), and alanine aminotransferase (ALT).

In contrast, the following factors were significantly associated with outcome (P < 0.05): central intravenous catheter (CVC) placement, glycopeptide exposure, anemia, enteritis, hematonosis, and laboratory parameters including platelet count (PLT), C-reactive protein (CRP), procalcitonin (PCT), albumin (ALB), and D-dimer. Antimicrobial susceptibility testing (AST) results

The AST profiles of the 132 CRAB isolates are presented in Table 2. All CRAB strains were resistant to meropenem and ticarcillin-clavulanic acid. Exhibiting high resistance rates exceeding 90%, the isolates were resistant to cephalosporins (including ceftazidime, ceftriaxone, and cefepime), penicillin–β-lactamase inhibitor combinations (including ampicillin-sulbactam and piperacillin-tazobactam), aminoglycosides (including amikacin and gentamicin), imipenem, and trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole. Approximately 70% of the CRAB isolates were resistant to quinolones, including levofloxacin and ciprofloxacin. Resistance to cefoperazone-sulbactam and minocycline was observed in about 30% of the strains. In contrast, nearly all strains remained susceptible to polymyxin B, with a resistance rate of only 0.8%. However, no statistically significant differences were observed (P > 0.05) for any of these antibiotics between the positive and negative outcome groups.

Multi-locus sequence typing (MLST)

The MLST classified the CRAB clinical isolates under Pasteur and Oxford STs, respectively.

Using the Pasteur MLST scheme, a total of two sequence types (STs) were identified. The results revealed that ST2Pasteur, which belongs to the Global Clone 2 (GC2) lineage, predominated among the isolates, representing 75.0% (99/132) of the collection. In contrast, ST150Pasteur represented only 3.0% (4/132). Additionally, 29 isolates (22.0%) could not be assigned to a known ST under this scheme. Ultimately, the distribution of Pasteur STs showed no significant association with clinical outcomes (P > 0.05; Table S1).

Based on the Oxford MLST scheme, six STs were identified among the isolates. ST208 Oxford was the most prevalent, accounting for 66.7% (88/132) of the collection, followed by ST1208Oxford at 22.0% (29/132). The remaining STs—ST136Oxford, ST718Oxford, ST195Oxford, ST540Oxford —each constituted a minority (≤ 3.0%).

According to the Oxford scheme, the predominant ST2Pasteur isolates predominantly corresponded to ST208Oxford (88.9%, 88/99), while all 4 ST150Pasteur isolates corresponded to ST718Oxford. All isolates that were non-typeable by the Pasteur scheme were identified as ST1208Oxford.

As shown in Table S1, the distribution of Oxford STs was not significantly associated with clinical outcomes (P > 0.05).

Genes associated with KL and OCL types

The pathogenicity of A. baumannii clinical isolates is largely attributed to its capsule polysaccharide (CPS), a major virulence factor present on the cell surface [26, 27]. Epidemiological tracking of this pathogen employs two variable genetic loci involved in CPS synthesis. The biosynthesis and export of CPS are governed by the chromosomal KL locus, which is flanked by the fkpA and lldP genes. Conversely, the lipooligosaccharide is encoded by the OCL gene cluster, situated between aspS and ilvE [23].

A total of seven KL types were identified. KL2 was the most prevalent (87/132, 65.9%), followed by KL24 (29/132, 22.0%). The remaining types—KL77 (4/132, 3.0%), KL81 (4/132, 3.0%), KL116 (3/132, 2.3%), KL160 (2/132, 1.5%), and KL3 (2/132, 1.5%)—were less common.

In contrast, OCL types were highly conserved, with only three variants detected: OCL1 (99/132, 75.0%), OCL7 (29/132, 22.0%), and OCL6 (4/132, 3.0%) (Table S1).

However, no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) were observed in the distribution of KL or OCL types between the positive and negative outcome groups (Table S1).

Phylogenetic analysis

A core-genome phylogenetic tree was constructed for the 132 pediatric CRAB isolates from Guangxi (Fig. 2). The analysis revealed that ST1208Oxford and ST718Oxford/ST150Pasteur were evolutionarily distant from the predominant ST2Pasteur.

Plasmid identification

Three main plasmid families, namely Rep1, Rep3, and RepPriCT_1, have been identified in Acinetobacter [6, 24].

As shown in Fig. 3, plasmid-encoded rep genes were detected in all 132 CRAB isolates (132/132, 100%). The majority of isolates (108/132, 81.8%) carried between 2 and up to 4 types of plasmid rep genes, while the remaining 24 isolates (18.2%) harbored only a single rep gene. All three plasmid families were present among the 132 CRAB isolates, with the Rep3 family being the most prevalent, identified in all isolates (132/132, 100%; Fig. 3). Rep3 family plasmids were found to be broadly distributed across all major CRAB STs, which is consistent with prior findings [6, 28]. Within the Rep3 family, the Rep3-T3 (83/132, 62.9%) and Rep3-T4 (86/132, 65.2%) subgroups were the most predominant. Notably, a majority of ST208Oxford isolates (68/88, 77.3%) were found to co-harbor Rep3-T3 and Rep3-T4 subgroups. Additionally, Rep1-T1 and Rep3-T2 were found to coexist exclusively in ST1208Oxford isolates, indicating a specific plasmids profile in this lineage.

Antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes

A variety of antimicrobial resistance (AMR) genes associated with resistance to multiple classes of antimicrobial agents were detected in the 132 CRAB clinical strains isolated from pediatric patients. Among these, genes encoding all four Ambler classes of β-lactamases were identified (Fig. 3, Table S2).

The class A extended-spectrum β-lactamase (ESBL) gene blaTEM-1 was detected in 61 out of 132 isolates (46.2%), all of which belonged to ST2Pasteur. This finding aligns with previous reports on A. baumannii from Terengganu, Malaysia [28]. The class B metallo-β-lactamase (MBL) gene blaNDM-1 was present in 33 isolates (25.0%), all of which pertained to ST1208Oxford and ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford. The class C β-lactamase gene blaADC-73 was found in a minority of the CRAB isolates (11/132, 8.3%). Two groups of acquired class D β-lactamases were identified: blaOXA-23-like (98/132, 74.2%) and blaOXA-66-like (42/132, 31.8%), both of which were specifically associated with the ST2Pasteur lineage.

More than ten distinct aminoglycoside resistance genes were identified, spanning several functional families. The intrinsic nucleotidyltransferase gene ANT(3″)-IIa was detected in 3.8% (5/132) of isolates. In contrast, the phosphotransferase genes APH(3’)-Ia, APH(3’’)-Ib, and APH(6)-Id were co-harbored by 75.0% (99/132) of isolates, all of which belonged to ST2Pasteur. Another phosphotransferase gene, APH(3’)-VIa, was present in 22.7% (30/132) of isolates across ST1208Oxford and ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford. Analysis of acetyltransferase genes revealed varied prevalence: AAC(3)-Ia and AAC(6’)-Ib7 were predominant, found in 60.6% (80/132) and 64.4% (85/132) of isolates, respectively. Other acetyltransferase genes, including AAC(3)-IId, AAC(6’)-Ib4, AAC(6’)-Ib8, AAC(6’)-Ib9, were detected at lower frequencies (ranging from 5.3% to 24.2% ). Genotypically, AAC(3)-Ia, AAC(6’)-Ib7 were primarily associated with ST2Pasteur, whereas the remaining acetyltransferase genes were mainly linked to ST1208Oxford and ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford. Additionally, the adenylyltransferase gene aadA was identified in 5.3% (7/132) of isolates. The 16 S rRNA methylase gene armA was detected at a high frequency of 72.7% (96/132) and was exclusively identified in isolates belonging to ST2Pasteur.

Two genes conferring resistance to tetracyclines were detected among the 132 CRAB isolates. The tet(B) gene was the most prevalent, present in 103 out of 132 isolates (78.0%), followed by tet(39), which was found in 32 isolates (24.3%). The tet(A) gene was not detected.

With respect to sulfonamide resistance, two genes were identified: sul1 was detected in 121 isolates (91.7%), while sul2 was carried by 36 isolates (27.3%). Isolates harboring sul2 belonged to the sequence types ST2Pasteur/ST195Oxford, ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford, and ST1208Oxford.

For macrolide resistance, the genes msrE and mphE were detected in 128 (97.0%) and 127 (96.2%) isolates, respectively. These two genes were almost always coexistent, with the exception of isolate GXFY137, which carried only msrE.

However, no significant association was found between clinical outcomes and the spectrum of acquired resistance genes. (P > 0.05; Table S2).

The outer membrane protein A gene (ompA) was detected in only 4 out of 132 isolates (3.0%). Located on the bacterial surface, OmpA protein serves as a virulence factor in A. baumannii by mediating adhesion to and invasion of epithelial cells [29].

More than ten biofilm formation-associated genes identified in the VFDB were detected in the CRAB isolates, including the adeFGH-encoded efflux pump, the bap-encoded biofilm-associated protein, the csuA/B–csuABCDE-encoded Csu pili chaperone–usher assembly system, and the pgaABCD genes involved in the synthesis of poly-β-(1,6)-N-acetylglucosamine, an intercellular adhesin. All 132 CRAB isolates carried csuA, csuC, csuD, csuE, and pgaA genes (100%). The bap gene was present in 102 isolates (77.3%), which were distributed across multiple sequence types, with the exception of ST1208Oxford isolates. The csuB gene was detected in 33 isolates (25.0%) belonging to ST1208Oxford and ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford. Both adeF and adeH genes co-occurred in ST1208Oxford and ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford isolates (33/132, 25.0%). All 29 ST1208Oxford isolates (22.0%) carried the pgaB gene, while all four ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford isolates carried pgaC gene. The pgaD gene was detected in only 1.5% of isolates.

The phospholipases plc contribute to bacterial pathogenesis by facilitating the lysis of host cells through cleavage of phospholipids in the host cell membrane, as well as by degrading phospholipids at mucosal barriers to promote bacterial invasion. All 132 isolates in our study carried the plc gene (100%).

A total of seven immune evasion-related genes (lpsB, lpxA, lpxB, lpxC, lpxD, lpxL, and lpxM) were identified. These genes were present in all CRAB isolates.

A. baumannii produces the siderophore acinetobactin to acquire iron under iron-limited host conditions [30]. In this study, we identified multiple genes associated with iron metabolism. All 132 isolates carried the barA gene of the siderophore efflux system, while barB was present only in 33 (25.0%) isolates belonging to ST1208Oxford and ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford.

Among the eight genes comprising the acinetobactin biosynthesis cluster (basABCDFGHIJ), basB, basD, and basH were detected in all 132 (100%) CRAB isolates. basA, basC and basI co-occurred in 33 isolates (25.0%) from ST1208Oxford and ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford. The prevalence of basG, and basJ was 25% and 65.2%, respectively.

Regarding acinetobactin uptake genes (bauABCDEF), bauA, bauD, and bauE were present in all isolates (100%). bauB, bauC and bauF coexisted in the same 33 isolates (25.0%) from ST1208Oxford and ST150Pasteur/ST718Oxford.

The iron uptake-related gene entE was present in all isolates (100%), while hemO, encoding a putative haem oxygenase involved in haem uptake and utilization, was detected in 127 out of 132 isolates (96.2%).

Genes associated with quorum sensing (abaI, abaR) and biofilm regulation (bfmS) were universally present, each being identified in all 132 isolates (100%).

Serum resistance, a key virulence trait enabling A. baumannii to survive in human blood and cause severe infections [31], was also investigated. The gene pbpG, encoding a penicillin-binding protein associated with serum resistance, was found in all 132 isolates.

However, no statistically significant differences (P > 0.05) in any virulence factors were observed between the positive and negative outcome groups (Table S3).

Risk factors for negative outcome in children with CRAB infection

Univariate analysis revealed that several factors were significantly associated with negative outcomes in children with CRAB infection, including prior hospitalizations (P = 0.013), anemia (P = 0.049), enteritis (P = 0.039), hematological disorders (P = 0.039), septic shock (P = 0.012), CVC (P = 0.004), exposure to glycopeptide antibiotics (P = 0.034), decreased PLT count (P = 0.001), elevated CRP (P = 0.009), and low ALB level (P = 0.034).

Due to the limited number of negative outcomes (n = 49), we selected five clinically relevant variables (prior hospitalizations, septic shock, CVC, PLT, and CRP) from the factors with P < 0.05 in the univariate analysis for inclusion in a multivariable logistic regression model. The results of logistic regression identified three independent risk factors for poor outcomes: prior hospitalizations (OR = 3.236; P = 0.015), the presence of a central venous catheter (CVC) (OR = 2.683; P = 0.028), and decreased platelet (PLT) count (OR = 0.996; P = 0.005) (Table 3).

Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the first systematic study investigating CRAB isolates from pediatric patients in Guangxi, China. Since its discovery in 1985, CRAB has spread worldwide and become a significant public health threat. In the 2024 WHO priority pathogen list, CRAB ranked third overall—following carbapenem-resistant Klebsiella pneumoniae and third-generation cephalosporin-resistant Escherichia coli—yet it was categorized as the highest critical priority pathogen [3]. This classification reflects the substantial threat CRAB poses to public health, which is attributable to its multidrug resistance, high transmissibility, and associated high mortality. Moreover, the development of new antibiotics targeting CRAB continues to lag behind, further compounding the challenge. To better understand the epidemiology, molecular characteristics, and clinical risk factors of pediatric CRAB infections in this region, we analyzed clinical and genomic data from a local cohort. To this end, we compared the clinical and molecular features of isolates collected from the Maternal and Child Health Hospital of Guangxi Zhuang Autonomous Region between January 2019 and December 2024, all of which were characterized by whole-genome sequencing (WGS).

Our study demonstrated that CRAB infections predominantly affected young children, with an average age of 2.5 years, and boys accounted for 62.5% of the cases. Young children are uniquely vulnerable to CRAB infections due to immature immune systems and a limited ability to describe symptoms, which complicates early diagnosis. Furthermore, antibiotic choices are restricted by safety concerns. For instance, although tigecycline and eravacycline are potential treatments, they are contraindicated in children under eight due to risks of impaired bone and tooth development, critically narrowing the therapeutic arsenal.

In our study, 90.2% of CRAB infections originated in the pediatric intensive care unit (PICU), slightly higher than the proportion (76.9%) in adult ICUs [32]. Literature indicates that nosocomial CRAB infections, including outbreaks, occur predominantly in ICU settings [8, 32, 33]. This may be attributed to the critical condition of patients, frequent use of invasive procedures, overuse of antibiotics, and challenges in maintaining rigorous hygiene in medical environments. Strengthening infection prevention and control is therefore imperative. Key measures such as hand hygiene, environmental disinfection, adherence to standard operating procedures, and antimicrobial stewardship are essential.

Among the 132 CRAB isolates, all were classified as MDR, and 18 (13.6%) constituted XDR A. baumannii. No pan drug-resistant (PDR) strains were detected. The high prevalence of these resistance profiles poses a significant therapeutic challenge. This is particularly pronounced in pediatric patients, where the spectrum of suitable antibiotics is substantially limited by safety concerns. Cefoperazone-sulbactam is widely used in the treatment of CRAB infections in Guangxi.The observed resistance rate of CRAB to cefoperazone-sulbactam was 34.8% in this study, which is somewhat lower compared with reports from Jiangxi, China. Sulbactam acts not only as a competitive serine β-lactamase inhibitor but also binds to penicillin-binding protein (PBP) when administered at high doses [34]. A meta-analysis demonstrated that cefoperazone-sulbactam-based regimens were superior to alternative regimens in reducing 30-day mortality and improving clinical outcomes in patients with MDR A. baumannii infections [35]. Other therapeutic options for CRAB infections include cefiderocol, sulbactam-durlobactam, eravacycline, and tigecycline. However, cefiderocol is not yet available in the Chinese market. Sulbactam-durlobactam, although recommended by the IDSA in combination with a carbapenem as a preferred regimen [36], is relatively expensive and not routinely available in many Chinese hospitals. Tetracyclines, such as eravacycline and tigecycline, are not recommended for children under 8 years of age due to potential adverse effects on teeth and bone development. Therefore, the management of CRAB infections in pediatric patients remains a significant challenge, underscoring the urgent need for research and development of new antibiotics tailored to this vulnerable population.

Among 132 pediatric patients with CRAB infection, 37.1% experienced negative outcomes, a rate lower than the mortality reported in elderly CRAB patients [37, 38]. Possible reasons may include better hospital-acquired infection control in pediatric wards compared to adult units [39], or the fact that elderly individuals are not only more immunocompromised but also generally carry a higher burden of underlying diseases. Our study identified prior hospitalizations, central venous catheter (CVC) use, and low platelet (PLT) count as independent risk factors for negative outcomes in children with CRAB infection. The association between CVC and poor outcomes is consistent with a previous reports on mortality due to A. baumannii infection [40]. Our finding that a decreased PLT level is an independent risk factor for mortality is consistent with the results of a retrospective study by Wei et al. [41]. In contrast to prior studies, our analysis specifically identifies prior hospitalization as a novel, independent risk factor for mortality. Recognition of prior hospitalizations and low PLT count may help alert pediatricians to high-risk patients and prompt timely interventions to prevent adverse outcomes. In addition, careful management of CVC placement is essential to reduce the risk of negative outcomes.

WGS of the 132 CRAB strains revealed that the ST2Pasteur lineage, which belongs to GC2, was the most prevalent (99/132, 75%), consistent with global trends [32, 33, 42, 43]. This indicates the widespread establishment of this clone not only in general hospitals but also in children’s hospitals. Based on the Oxford MLST scheme, ST2Pasteur totally comprises 23 sequence types. Among the 99 ST2Pasteur isolates in our study, 88 (66.7%) were identified as ST208Oxford, while ST136Oxford, ST540Oxford, and ST195Oxford accounted for only a small proportion (each ≤ 3.0%). Interestingly, ST195Oxford and ST191Oxford—reported among the top three ST2Pasteur types in China [12]—were not detected in our collection. In contrast, ST1208Oxford ranked second (22.0%) among isolates in our study. ST1208Oxford has rarely been reported in A. baumannii strains from other regions [12, 14, 28]. To determine whether the WGS data indicate a clonal outbreak or persistent transmission of the NDM-carrying ST1208Oxford strain within the PICU, the isolation dates of this strain were analyzed. A total of 29 PICU patients infected with the NDM-carrying ST1208 strain were identified from February 2022 to September 2024, suggesting persistent transmission of this strain within the hospital’s PICU. Therefore, ST1208Oxford may represent a sequence type unique to CRAB that causes hospital infections in pediatric patients in the Guangxi region.

Regarding KL type distribution, previous studies had indicated that KL3 and KL2 were predominant in Terengganu, Malaysia [28], whereas KL6, KL10, and KL47 were the most common in Thailand [11], where over ten types were identified. Our study revealed a more limited diversity of only seven KL types. Notably, KL2 was the most prevalent (65.9%) and was almost exclusively associated with ST208Oxford. Consequently, the ST208Oxford–KL2 combination accounted for the majority (65.9%) of cases in our dataset. This finding aligns with reports from Terengganu [28] and with studies on A. baumannii causing bloodstream infections in China [12]. Interestingly, KL24 represented the second most prevalent type (22.0%) in our study and was exclusively associated with ST1208Oxford. The KL24 type is rarely reported in other regions. Therefore, the ST1208Oxford–KL24 lineage appears to be unique to CRAB in pediatric patients from the Guangxi area.

The distribution of OCL types revealed OCL1 as the majority (75.0%), associated with ST2Pasteur—a pattern consistent with other reports [11, 28]. More strikingly, OCL7 was the second most frequent type (22.0%) in our population, linked to ST1208Oxford, in contrast to its typically low reported prevalence in other regions.

In addition to its KL and OCL locus, ST1208Oxford also exhibited distinct major profiles of AMR genes, plasmid replicons, and virulence factors compared to other sequence types. In detail, ST1208Oxford was strongly associated with a distinct repertoire, including the resistance genes NDM-1, APH(3’)-VIa, AAC(3)-Ild, tet(39), and sul2; the plasmids R1-T1 and R3-T2; and virulence genes for some biofilm formation (adeF, adeH, csuB, pgaB) and some iron acquisition (barB, basA, basC, basG, basI, bauB, bauC, bauF). ST2Pasteur, meanwhile, was characterized by the resistance genes TEM-1, OXA-23, APH(3’)-Ia, APH(3’’)-Ib, APH(6)-Id, armA and tetB; plasmid R3-T4.

The Rep1, Rep3, and RepPriCT_1 groups constitute three major plasmid types found in Acinetobacter species. Rep1 plasmids are generally small, ranging from 1.5 to 5.9 kb in length, and primarily encode a Rep protein along with a limited number of hypothetical proteins. Consistent with prior findings, R1-type plasmids lack AMR genes [6]. Conversely, R3-type plasmids are a predominant vector for drug resistance, with carbapenem resistance genes being inconsistently detected across this group [44]. Meanwhile, plasmids of the RepPriCT_1 group, though less abundant, are represented by the RP-T1 lineage, which facilitates the dissemination of diverse AMR genes, notably those conferring carbapenem resistance [44]. In our study, we speculated that the R3-T2 plasmid might carry the NDM-1 carbapenemase gene in the ST1208Oxford strain.

Several limitations should be considered in this study. First, the research was conducted as a retrospective study at a single center, rather than a prospective, multi-center epidemiological investigation. Second, it should be noted that without susceptibility data for cefiderocal, sulbactam-durlobactam, and eravacycline, we could not determine if resistance rates to these antimicrobials in our cohort aligned with established baselines from earlier research. Finally, despite testing a variety of genomic variables (including ST, KL, and OCL types, as well as AMR, plasmid, and virulence gene profiles), statistical analysis revealed no significant association with clinical outcomes. To definitively establish the potential effects suggested by the genomic data, subsequent in vitro studies and animal experiments are required for validation.

Conclusion

Here, we provide the first report on the clinical and molecular characteristics of CRAB in pediatric patients from Guangxi, China. We found ST2Pasteur/ST208Oxford carrying the OXA-23 gene was the most prevalent strain. However, ST1208Oxford, which harbored NDM-1, was genetically distinct and represented the second most common sequence type, emerging as an important cause of pediatric hospital-onset infections in Guangxi. Notably, the genetic profile of ST1208Oxford—encompassing KL, OCL loci, AMR genes, plasmid replicons, and virulence factors—was distinct from that of other sequence types. However, statistical analysis revealed no significant association between clinical outcomes and any of the tested genetic profiles. Independent risk factors for poor outcomes in children with CRAB infections included a history of prior hospitalization, presence of a central venous catheter, and decreased PLT level. Our results provide key insights into the genomic epidemiology and clinical behavior of this pathogen, which could inform future infection control and treatment strategies.
