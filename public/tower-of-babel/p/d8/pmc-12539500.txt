Enhancing Public Health Nursing Competencies Through Game-Based Education
Authors: Monica J. Hughes
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Background:Community and public health nursing (C/PHN) needs to expand to meet evolving population health needs, yet baccalaureate nursing graduates often lack the competencies required for these roles.Purpose:The aim of this study was to enhance nursing student self-efficacy in C/PHN competencies through virtual simulation games (gamulations).Methods:Researchers developed 6 public health scenarios in collaboration with a software company and piloted gamulations with 90 senior nursing students, 3 faculty, and 1 practicing PHN. Pre- and post-assessments measured self-efficacy in C/PHN competencies.Results:Two-tailed t-testing showed significant improvement in overall self-efficacy scores from pre-assessment (M = 44.76) to post-assessment (M = 54.93) (t(47) = −7.78, P < .001). Paired competency self-efficacy scores across all 19 individual competencies showed significance (P < .001) in self-efficacy improvement. Cohen’s d ranged from 0.44 to 0.72.Conclusions:Implementation of game-based educational approaches has the potential to improve new nurse graduates’ self-efficacy in C/PHN competencies, thereby improving entry to practice in community and public health roles.

It has been well established that nurses practicing in community and public health settings play a large part in improving the health of populations through activities like case management, health education, care coordination, partnerships, and advocacy,1 yet the primary focus of nursing education at the baccalaureate level is typically on nursing in acute care roles.2 The American Association of Colleges of Nursing2 has emphasized that a shift to increased nursing practice in community settings is both necessary and inevitable and noted that nursing graduates need to be prepared to practice in 4 spheres of care, only 1 of which specifically describes acute care practice.

Community and public health nurses (PHNs) are particularly needed, in part because of the work they do to address social determinants, which can only be moved through advocacy work and policy development. Research has demonstrated that there are gaps in undergraduate nursing education related to health care policy advocacy and the necessary leadership competencies to understand and address social determinants of health.3 New methods of integrating social determinants and population health are needed in nursing programs at all levels with guided learning to give students opportunities to meet the needs of underserved populations and better understand population health.4 C and PHNs who are ready to lead interdisciplinary teams, understand the populations they serve, and practice policy development are essential for long-lasting change that impacts social determinants.

Social cognitive career theory (SCCT) is a model that explains student career decision-making, describing the interplay between the development of personal interests, learning experiences, and 3 human cognitive variables, namely self-efficacy, outcome expectations, and personal goals.5 SCCT can be effectively employed as a theoretical model for career influence among nursing students, promoting students’ belief about outcomes they can achieve in a chosen field, combined with students’ belief that they can succeed in that field.6 This project employed SCCT theory with the hope of ultimately influencing students’ career interest in community and public health nursing (C/PHN) in their future practice roles, using a focus on improved self-efficacy, or the students’ belief in their ability to succeed.

The project built on the understanding that self-efficacy can improve with exposure to learning experiences that allow students to learn vicariously, accomplish something, and be emotionally aroused.7 Previous studies have shown that emotional arousal, vicarious learning, and achievement of a goal can be accomplished effectively through simulation learning8,9; however, it remains challenging to devise simulations in public health, community health, and population health simply due to the nature of their focus on groups, data, social determinants, health equity, and modifiable health behaviors, with policy implications within the context of systems thinking. Virtual simulation is an appropriate strategy that may help overcome those challenges, and it is frequently used in online public health course delivery.10

The specific aims of the project were to: (1) Develop, implement, and evaluate virtual, unfolding C/PHN simulations, known as gamulations, based on Quad Council Coalition (QCC) C/PHN competencies.11(2) Determine changes in participant self-efficacy in QCC C/PHN competencies11 from pre- to post-gamulation.

Methods

Context

The undergraduate nursing program at the author’s educational institution admits one cohort of 100 to 150 traditional face-to-face nursing students each year. The Community and Population Health Nursing course is taken in the senior year, second semester, just prior to graduation. As a result of the positioning of the course in the final semester, most students in the program are already emotionally committed to a desired specialty in nursing before they are ever exposed to content about nursing roles in C/PHN settings. During the first half of this semester, the students are concurrently completing their final capstone in an acute care setting, and this greatly impacts the students’ engagement level in this course. Additionally, due to the need for scheduling flexibility to accommodate the students’ capstone shifts, clinical experiences for the C/PHN course in the first half of the semester are typically virtual clinical experiences, such as online trainings, modules, and electronic simulations.

The availability of appropriate clinical sites continues to be the main barrier to expanding nursing programs and advancing more nursing graduates.12 In its 2019 report on nursing education and its role in population health improvement, the Future of Nursing Campaign for Action13 reported that a full 50% of respondents to their survey on the top challenges related to teaching students about population health were the availability of appropriate clinical sites. With a current reduction in the public health workforce,14 the problem with obtaining C/PHN clinical placements for students has only worsened.

Intervention

The core intervention was the development of gamified, narrative public health nursing case studies based on QCC C/PHN competencies,11 using decision trees with varied outcomes based on player choices. These were written by the project lead, an experienced public health nurse, with the intent to simulate real-world public health nursing scenarios. Scenarios included contexts such as women’s health, population assessment, and coalition building with community-based participatory research, community health improvement planning, assessment of social determinants set in a public health clinic treating sexually transmitted infections, an infectious disease outbreak, and school nursing based on mental health among LGBTQ adolescents. Once Institutional Review Board (IRB) approval was obtained through the author’s university, purposive convenience sampling was employed to recruit PHN faculty and practicing PHNs in the author’s network to review content and give feedback to guide revisions on the scenarios as they were developed. This sampling technique was used to gather focused, expert observations quickly and inexpensively. This took place over the course of the 2022-2023 academic year.

A Health Resources and Services Administration grant received by the author supported the project costs, enabling work with a gamulation company specializing in cloud-based, gamified educational simulations to construct case studies in a virtual setting. The experienced team of designers created a diverse set of avatars, with guidance from the author on developing several non-binary avatars, to better represent a modern undergraduate nursing cohort. The author made specifications on background settings and frame set-up to accurately reflect C/PHN work settings in clinics, schools, and other community locations. This work took place in conjunction with scenario writing.

Beta Testing

In Fall 2024, the gamulations were beta tested among a different convenience sample of practicing community and PHNs and C/PHN faculty from across the United States (n = 2 practicing community and PHNs; n = 5 C/PHN faculty) recruited by email and word of mouth from among members of the project lead’s professional public health nursing network. Potential bias existed in this approach. An attempt to mitigate this was made by asking the secretary of the target organizations to send out the recruitment to all members rather than it coming from the project lead directly. Each beta tester completed an open-ended survey asking for recommendations on potential revision of gamulation content to better reflect C/PHN practice or to improve generalizability for nursing students or practicing nurses in a variety of roles. Following a round of revisions based on this feedback, the gamulations were piloted within a traditional bachelor of science in nursing (BSN) nursing program at the project lead’s institution during the spring 2024 Community and Population Health Nursing course.

Survey Development

The project lead developed a 19-item pre- and post-self-efficacy survey drawn from the 8 QCC C/PHN competency10 domains: Assessment Analytic Skills; Policy Development and Program Planning Skills; Communication Skills; Cultural Competency Skills; Community Dimensions of Practice Skills; Public Health Sciences Skills; Financial Planning and Management Skills; and Leadership and Systems Thinking Skills. The questions queried participant self-efficacy using examples of activities that captured the description of each domain plus entry-level competencies within that domain but that were written at a more general level. Implementation

Following appropriate consents, senior-level, final semester nursing students (n = 90), 1 PHN in practice (n = 1), and 3 nursing faculty members (n = 3) recruited from within the undergraduate faculty at the project lead’s university received the pre-survey and gamulation scenarios. Pilot participants had from January to May of 2024 to complete the scenarios and the surveys. Participants selected the scenarios for completion in any order. Upon finishing each scenario, participants received a downloadable certificate summarizing the C/PHN competencies practiced in that scenario and date of achievement. This was done to improve participant self-efficacy by helping reinforce what the competencies look like in practice and to provide participants with a sense of accomplishment. The post-survey opened within the gamulation software automatically upon completion of all 6 scenarios for those players who agreed to participate in the research study. If the player did not agree to participate, pre- and post-surveys did not deploy.

Data Analysis

The pre- and post-self-efficacy surveys were positioned within the gamulation software, from which the project lead could access reports on participant completion. Following the close of the pilot period, all pre- and post-test pilot participant data were downloaded and analyzed using IBM SPSS (Version 30.0.0) (Armonk, NY). Of the 94 pilot participants, all completed paired pre- and post-gamulation responses. Each individual self-efficacy competency was coded as pre and post with a key word from the competency itself (as shown in Supplemental Digital Content, Table 1, Available at: http://links.lww.com/NE/C31) with the participant’s associated Likert scale selections for that competency.

To determine changes in participant self-efficacy in C/PHN competencies from pre- to post-gamulation across all 19 individual competencies, the analysis employed a paired within-subjects design, using 2-tailed t-tests. For each individual competency self-efficacy pair, the project lead calculated the means of the pairs to determine the difference in the means from pre- and post-gamulation. Each analysis included the pair’s standard error, 95% CI, t statistic, and 2-sided P-value so the strength of the evidence could be meaningfully evaluated. The mean difference of the overall competency self-efficacy change (all pairs taken together) was also analyzed. Cohen’s d was assessed to measure the effect size so that the standard difference within the pairs could be reported. This analysis helped establish the size of the changes resulting from the intervention and whether the changes could be categorized as impactful.

Ethical considerations

IRB approval was obtained from the project lead’s university research office prior to the start of scenario creation. As this intervention was deployed within an academic setting and the project lead/scenario designer was also an administrator in the undergraduate nursing program, it was important to address any concerns regarding student coercion to participate. Recruitment emails were posted on the students’ course learning management system site, with clear assurance that student privacy and confidentiality would be maintained and that course faculty would not have access to student performance within the gamulation setting or on self-efficacy survey items. Because all data were managed within the gamulation platform, faculty had no access to any gamulation information. Students were credited clinical hours in their Community and Population Health Practicum course for time spent in the gamulations. Additionally, students were given extra points on the final course unit exam as an incentive if they elected to participate in the research aspect of the intervention. Participation in the research aspect included completion of the pre- and post-gamulation surveys and completion of all 6 scenarios, evidenced by submission of the participants’ achievement certificates for each scenario. This was highly motivational, as demonstrated by the level of student participation and gamulation completion.

Results

Two-tailed t-tests showed significant improvement in overall self-efficacy scores from pre-gamulation (M = 44.76, SD = 9.62) to post-gamulation (M = 54.93, SD = 8.57) (t(47) = − 7.78, P < .001). All 19 individual competency self-efficacy scores also displayed positive mean differences. All pairs’ 95% CI ranged between 0.24 (lower CI) and 1.52 (upper CI), indicating significant improvement across all 19 competency self-efficacy pairs. Supplemental Digital Content, Table 2, Available at: http://links.lww.com/NE/C32, reports the individual paired-sample results for the 19 self-efficacy competency items. Supplemental Digital Content, Figure 1, Available at: http://links.lww.com/NE/C30, provides a graphic representation of the mean differences with CI for the competency self-efficacy items. Participant role (faculty, community or PHN, or student) demonstrated no interaction of significance in analysis.

Cohen’s d, which assesses effect size to measure how substantial a difference is between 2 groups in practical terms, ranged from 0.44 to 0.72, falling within what is normally considered a medium-to-large effect range of approximately 0.50 to 0.80. The paired-sample evaluation shows a clear, consistent pattern of competency improvement after the intervention, with most gains in participant self-efficacy in the competencies demonstrating at least medium practical value. The largest gain was for Influence (competency 3) (Δ = 1.18, 95% CI [0.85, 1.52]), followed by Status (competency 2) (Δ = 1.00, 95% CI [0.66, 1.34]). Even the smallest increase, found in Assessment (competency 1), is considerable (Δ = 0.73, 95% CI [0.39, 1.08]). All 2-sided P-values were significant. Discussion

The intervention produced robust, statistically significant, and practically meaningful gains in self-efficacy for every competency statement. Improvements ranged from roughly 3/4 to 1 ¼ points on the original rating scale; given the narrow CIs and large t statistics, these effects are unlikely to be due to chance. Because the effect was measurably impactful across all the competencies, one may conclude that the improvements from use of the gamulations were crosscutting across the QCC C/PHN competency11 domains and that gamulation implementation would accelerate student and C/PHN growth in all competency areas. These significant gains in self-efficacy among competencies were large enough to meet the potential for real, observable improvements in student performance when given opportunities to practice what they have learned in real-life settings. The gamulations are available for free use at https://son.redmeru.com/signup/link. They can also be accessed using the QR code in Supplemental Digital Content, QR Code, Available at: http://links.lww.com/NE/C33.

Competencies with the most growth (Influence, Status, and Community) are all competencies that are related to the QCC C/PHN11 domain Assessment and Analytic Skills. The greatest improvement following use of the gamulations may be found in activities involved in assessing population health, assessing community needs and assets, and communicating to community members what factors have an influence on health. This is promising, as many students have a narrow view of assessment as being pertinent to individual patients and families but are unable to fully grasp how this aspect of the nursing process is applied to populations, requiring new methods of integrating social determinants and population health in nursing education to give students opportunities to be prepared to meet population health needs and better understand population health.4 Implementation of the gamulation intervention with such successful self-efficacy improvement outcomes across competencies has the potential to greatly impact nursing graduates’ practice readiness in spheres of care outside the acute care setting.

Because of the gaps that have been noted in health care policy advocacy and leadership competencies in undergraduate nursing education, and the urgency of improving students’ ability to understand and address social determinants of health,3 such new, easily implemented methods of integrating social determinants and population health are highly valuable in nursing programs at all levels to prepare new graduates to meet the needs of underserved populations and better grasp their responsibilities regarding population health.4 The use of the gamulations improved self-efficacy in all 8 QCC C/PHN11 domains, including Assessment and Analytic Skills; Policy Development and Program Planning Skills; Communication Skills; Cultural Competency Skills; Community Dimensions of Practice; Public Health Sciences Skills; Financial Planning, Evaluation, and Management Skills; and Leadership and Systems Thinking Skills. The wide range of important aspects of C/PHN practice that are held within these domains specifically address those areas noted as essential for long-term change to impact social determinants of health, such as working with interdisciplinary teams, understanding populations, and practicing policy development.15

SCCT’s focus on improved self-efficacy guided the development and implementation of the gamulations. With the clear improvement in self-efficacy because of the intervention, it is the hope of the project lead that students will experience greater interest in community and public health careers following achievements in the gamulation combined with realistic expectations about outcomes they can achieve in C/PHN roles. This, combined with students’ belief that they can succeed in C/PHN after having experiences that make C/PHN come to life through the gamulation scenarios, may improve nursing students’ entry into community and population health.

Limitations

The instrument used for the pre- and post-survey has not been tested for validity and reliability and should be tried in this manner before fully trusting the data it generates. To truly validate the content of the gamulation scenarios, a much more extensive review of the scenario content should be conducted by practicing community and PHNs. As mentioned previously, there were times during development and implementation of the gamulations at which bias may have been introduced as a result of the use of convenience sampling among members of the project lead’s own professional network (for expert review of the scenarios) and university (for student participation). Although attempts were made to mitigate this bias, it was impossible to fully achieve, particularly among the students, who were informed who was leading the project as part of their informed consent.

To further understand the nature of the impact of gamulation use on students’ choice of nursing specialty after entry to practice, follow-up surveys could be employed at graduation and at years 1 and 5 post-graduation. It would be informative to know the percentage of students who ultimately chose a career in a C/PHN setting after using the gamulations in their nursing program. Another future consideration for data gathering is qualitative follow-up with the students to better understand those components of the intervention that aroused students emotionally or increased their confidence so that those specific aspects could be expanded in future iterations of the gamulations. Students use reflective journaling in the course in which the pilot was conducted. With appropriate IRB approval, journal entries could be mined for terms that reflected the specific impact from the use of the gamulations. Standardized community and public health content mastery exam scores could also be compared to the same population of students before and after implementation of gamulations to evaluate differences in content mastery achievement on the exam.

Conclusions

Working toward preparing nursing students with C/PHN competencies is among the numerous responsibilities of undergraduate nursing education programs as they prepare new nurses for entry to practice. In a new world of nursing care, new nurses must be armed with competencies in population health, an understanding of social determinants of health, a broader understanding of their responsibility as future nurses to engage in policy advocacy, and the ability to apply systems thinking. The use of gamulations focusing on these and other C/PHN competencies is a feasible and practical solution that has the potential for real improvement in student readiness and entry into C/PHN roles.
