Burden and predictors of cervical cancer mortality in Sub-Saharan Africa: a systematic review and meta-analysis
Authors: Tadele Emagneneh, Chalie Mulugeta, Betelhem Ejigu, Abebaw Alamrew, Belay Susu, Nigusie Abebaw, Aynalem Yetwale Hiwot, Habtam Desse Alemayehu, Sefineh Fenta Feleke
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundCervical cancer remains a major public health concern, particularly in Sub-Saharan Africa, where limited access to vaccination, screening, and treatment contributes to high mortality. Identifying mortality patterns and key risk factors is essential for designing effective interventions. This systematic review and meta-analysis aimed to estimate cervical cancer-related mortality and identify its common predictors among women in the region.MethodsThe study followed PRISMA guidelines and was registered in PROSPERO (CRD42025639606). A systematic search of Medline/PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, HINARI, and Google Scholar identified observational studies published up to 15 August 2024 reporting cervical cancer-related mortality and predictors in Sub-Saharan Africa. Six authors independently screened titles and abstracts, while all eight reviewed full texts for eligibility; discrepancies were resolved through discussion. Study quality was assessed using a modified version of the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale. Heterogeneity was evaluated with I² and Cochran’s Q test, and random-effects models were applied when substantial heterogeneity existed. Subgroup analyses and meta-regression explored potential sources of variability.ResultsFrom 1760, articles, 23 studies including 10,040 participants were included. The pooled prevalence of cervical cancer-related mortality was 56% (95% CI: 0.48–0.64). Significant predictors included advanced age (> 60 years; OR = 1.12), rural residence (OR = 1.38), hormonal contraceptive use (OR = 1.23), history of abortion (OR = 1.33), substance use (OR = 1.21), comorbidities (OR = 1.20), anemia (OR = 1.47), HIV infection (OR = 1.17), advanced disease stage (OR = 1.76), adenocarcinoma histology (OR = 1.30), single treatment modality (OR = 1.44), and single chemotherapy cycle (OR = 1.28).ConclusionsThe burden of cervical cancer mortality in Sub-Saharan Africa remains high, affecting over half of women with the disease, particularly those with advanced age, rural residence, comorbidities, and advanced disease stage. Targeted interventions for these high-risk groups and comprehensive treatment strategies are essential to reduce mortality and improve survival.Clinical trial numberNot applicable.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13027-026-00749-4.Keywords: Burden, Cervical cancer, Mortality, Predictors, Sub-Saharan Africa

Introduction

Cervical cancer is a major global public health issue, especially in low- and middle-income countries, where it accounts for most female cancer deaths [1]. Cervical cancer develops through a well-defined pathway, beginning with persistent infection by oncogenic human papillomavirus (HPV), progressing from normal epithelium to precancerous lesions (CIN1–3), and potentially advancing to micro-invasive and invasive cancer [2].

Although cervical cancer is largely preventable, it continues to pose a major global health challenge. In 2022, approximately 660,000 new cases and 350,000 deaths were reported worldwide, nearly 94% of which occurred in LMICs, with projections suggesting that annual deaths may exceed 410,000 by 2030 if current trends continue [3]. While incidence and mortality have declined in high-income countries, the burden remains disproportionately high in sub-Saharan Africa, where mortality rates exceed the global average, reflecting profound gaps in prevention, early detection, and access to treatment [4–6]. In countries such as Eswatini, Zambia, Malawi, and Zimbabwe, the age-standardized incidence rate (ASIR) ranges from 65 to 96 per 100,000 women, compared with about 6 per 100,000 in high-income settings, representing a 10–16-fold disparity [7, 8].

Despite the availability of HPV vaccination, effective screening, and treatment of precancerous lesions, access to these interventions in sub-Saharan Africa is limited. Regular screening methods—such as Pap smears, visual inspection with acetic acid (VIA), and HPV DNA testing—allow early detection and timely treatment of premalignant lesions, preventing progression to invasive cervical cancer and reducing both incidence and mortality. The success of such programs in high-income countries highlights the preventable nature of the disease and underscores the consequences of inadequate preventive and diagnostic capacity in resource-limited settings. In response, the World Health Organization (WHO) launched the Global Strategy to Accelerate the Elimination of Cervical Cancer in 2020, setting the ambitious 90-70-90 targets: 90% of girls fully vaccinated against HPV by age 15, 70% of women screened with a high-performance test at ages 35 and 45, and 90% of women with cervical disease receiving appropriate treatment [9].

The high mortality in sub-Saharan Africa is further driven by financial and geographical barriers to care, late-stage presentation, HIV infection, nutritional deficiencies, co-morbidities, large tumor size at diagnosis, suboptimal quality of care, high loss to follow-up, and treatment non-completion due to poverty [4, 10, 11]. Consequently, cervical cancer results in a substantial loss of life years, often affecting women during their most productive years and undermining the social and economic stability of families [10], often striking during their most productive years and undermining the social and economic stability of the region [4].

Although cultural, racial, and genetic factors are recognized as important determinants of cervical cancer outcomes, evidence on the predictors of mortality in sub-Saharan Africa remains fragmented and inconclusive. No comprehensive synthesis has yet been undertaken to consolidate this knowledge across the region. This systematic review and meta-analysis seek to fill that critical gap by identifying the common predictors of cervical cancer-related mortality. Generating such evidence is essential for guiding context-specific public health strategies that strengthen prevention, promote early diagnosis, and improve treatment outcomes, with the ultimate goal of reducing the disproportionate mortality burden of cervical cancer among women in sub-Saharan Africa.

Methods

This systematic review and meta-analysis investigated cervical cancer–related mortality across sub-Saharan African countries. The study was carried out in 2024 in accordance with the PRISMA (Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses) guidelines (Fig. 1). The review protocol was prospectively registered in the PROSPERO international database under the registration number CRD42025639606.

PRISMA flow chart diagram showing the included searches for systematic reviews and meta-analyses entitled Predictors of cervical cancer-related mortality in Sub-Saharan Africa

Search strategy

A comprehensive systematic search was conducted across four international electronic databases, MEDLINE (via PubMed), Scopus, Web of Science, and HINARI, to identify relevant studies published up to 15 August 2024 (the final search date). In addition, Google Scholar was searched to identify grey literature. The search strategy combined controlled vocabulary (where applicable) and free-text terms using Boolean operators. Key search concepts included cervical cancer (e.g., “Uterine Cervical Neoplasms,” “cervical cancer,” “cervical tumor,” “cervical malignancy”), mortality outcomes (e.g., “mortality,” “death,” “death rate”), and associated factors (e.g., “predictors,” “causes,” “risk factors”), in combination with the names of Sub-Saharan African countries. No restrictions were applied to the year of publication or study design; however, searches were limited to English-language publications. The complete search strategies for all databases and the detailed database search results, including screening and exclusion outcomes, are provided in Supplementary Files 1 and 2.

In addition to database and grey literature searches, the reference lists of all eligible full-text articles were manually screened to identify any additional relevant studies. All retrieved records were imported into Mendeley reference management software, and duplicates were automatically removed. Titles and abstracts were independently screened by six reviewers to assess eligibility. Any disagreements were resolved through discussion among the reviewers, and full-text articles were independently assessed by all nine reviewers to determine final inclusion.

Population

Women diagnosed with cervical cancer in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Exposure

Sociodemographic, clinical, and health system–related predictors (e.g., Age, stage at diagnosis, chronic medical illness, HIV status, access to screening and treatment-related factors).

Comparator

Women without these predictors, or across categories of predictors.

Outcome

Cervical cancer mortality.

Study design

Observational studies (cohort, cross-sectional, case–control) reporting on mortality and predictors.

Study setting

Sub-Saharan Africa.

Eligibility criteria

Inclusion criteria

This review included observational studies (cross-sectional, case–control, and cohort designs) that reported cervical cancer–specific mortality and its associated predictors. Eligible studies were published in English and provided sufficient quantitative data for inclusion in the meta-analysis, including relevant effect estimates with corresponding measures of uncertainty. There was no restriction on the year of publication; however, studies published up to August 29, 2024 (the final search date) were considered. All included studies underwent methodological quality assessment using the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) and met the predefined quality criteria; consequently, all assessed studies were included in the review.

Exclusion criteria

Review articles, systematic reviews, and meta-analyses were excluded. Studies were also excluded if they contained incomplete or insufficient data, such as missing sample sizes, effect estimates, or confidence intervals related to cervical cancer–specific mortality and its predictors.

Quality assessment

The quality of all included studies was independently assessed by two reviewers using the Newcastle-Ottawa Quality Assessment Scale (NOS), which evaluates studies across three domains: Selection (four items), Comparability (one item), and Outcome/Exposure (three items), with disagreements resolved through discussion with a third reviewer. Studies were classified as good, average, or poor quality based on their overall NOS scores: good quality corresponded to three or four stars in Selection, one or two stars in Comparability, and two or three stars in Outcome/Exposure; average quality included two stars in Selection, one or two stars in Comparability, and two or three stars in Outcome/Exposure; and poor quality included zero or one star in Selection, zero stars in Comparability, and zero or one star in Outcome/Exposure [12]. All assessed studies met the predefined quality criteria and were therefore included in the review. Detailed quality assessment scores and ratings for each study are provided in Supplementary file 3, ensuring transparency and reproducibility.

Screening of studies

All articles retrieved from the selected databases were first imported into the Mendeley reference management software, where exact duplicates were automatically removed. The resulting library was then shared among the six reviewers. Each reviewer independently screened the titles and abstracts of the identified studies to assess their relevance. To evaluate the consistency of the screening process, Cohen’s kappa coefficient was calculated. A value greater than 0.60 was considered indicative of substantial agreement among reviewers [12]. Any discrepancies identified during the title and abstract screening were resolved through discussion until consensus was achieved. Following this, the eight reviewers independently assessed the full texts of the eligible studies to determine their final inclusion.

Data extraction form

Data from the final articles included in the study were extracted using a pre-designed checklist. This checklist recorded the authors’ names, publication year, study duration, study design, sample size, study country, prevalence of cervical cancer-related mortality, and predictors of cervical cancer-related mortality.

Statistical analysis

Heterogeneity across studies was assessed using Cochran’s Q test (p < 0.05) and the I² statistic, with values above 50% considered indicative of substantial heterogeneity. To further explore sources of variability in cervical cancer-related mortality estimates, subgroup analyses were conducted by country, study design, and sample size, selected a priori to identify potential patterns and differences across studies. In addition, a meta-regression using publication year as a covariate was conducted to examine trends over time. Publication bias was assessed visually using funnel plots and statistically with both Egger’s and Begg’s tests. For analyses with substantial heterogeneity (I² > 50%), a random-effects model with the inverse variance method was applied to account for between-study variability and reduce potential bias [13]. Otherwise, a fixed-effects model was employed. All statistical analyses were conducted using RStudio software version 2025.05.1.

Risk of bias and quality appraisal

The methodological quality of the included studies was independently evaluated by nine reviewers using a modified version of the Newcastle–Ottawa Scale (NOS) for observational studies [13]. The NOS, a widely applied tool in observational research, assesses studies across three domains: sample selection (maximum of four points), comparability (maximum of two points), and outcome assessment (maximum of three points), yielding a total score of up to nine points. Studies achieving a score of seven or higher were considered high quality and were included in the final systematic review and meta-analysis.

Results of the meta-analysis and data synthesis

Data on the prevalence of cervical cancer-related mortality were synthesized to estimate the overall burden across studies. In addition, reported predictors of cervical cancer-related mortality were categorized into five thematic domains: sociodemographic factors, obstetric and gynecological characteristics, comorbid medical conditions, disease stage at diagnosis, and treatment modalities. This approach allowed for a comprehensive synthesis of both the burden and determinants of cervical cancer-related mortality in Sub-Saharan Africa.

Outcome measurement

The primary outcomes of this systematic review and meta-analysis are: (1) the burden or prevalence of cervical cancer-related mortality among patients in Sub-Saharan Africa, and (2) the common predictors associated with this mortality. Predictor variables include demographic factors (e.g., age, socioeconomic status), clinical characteristics (e.g., cancer stage at diagnosis, histological type), healthcare-related factors (e.g., access to treatment, quality of care), and comorbidities (e.g., HIV infection, other chronic conditions). The burden of mortality is reported as proportions and numbers, while associations between predictors and mortality are expressed using odds ratios (OR) as reported in the included studies.

Results

Study selection

A total of 1,760 records were identified through database searching. After removal of duplicates and clearly irrelevant records, 194 records remained and were screened by title and abstract. Of these, 99 records were excluded for not meeting the inclusion criteria. The remaining 95 full-text articles were assessed for eligibility. Following full-text review, 72 articles were excluded for predefined reasons, including inappropriate study population, outcomes, study design, or insufficient data. Ultimately, 23 studies met all eligibility criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis. In addition, the reference lists of all included studies were manually screened to identify any potentially relevant articles; however, no additional eligible studies were found. The study selection process is illustrated in Fig. 1.

Study characteristics

A total of 23 studies, encompassing 10,040 participants, were included in the meta-analysis, which assessed the burden of cervical cancer-related mortality in Sub-Saharan Africa. These studies, published between 2013 and 2024, reported cervical cancer mortality, reflecting variations in cancer stage at presentation, access to care, and treatment modalities. Geographically, nine studies were from Ethiopia, four from Kenya, three from Ghana, two from Botswana, two from Nigeria, and one each from Côte d’Ivoire, Rwanda, and Sudan. Burden of cervical cancer-related mortality

The proportion of mortality reported in individual studies varied widely, ranging from 0.23 in Teshome et al., ([22], Ethiopia) to 0.93 in Osok et al., ([28], Kenya). Using a common-effect model, the pooled prevalence of cervical cancer-related mortality was 0.61 (95% CI: 0.60–0.62), while the random-effects model yielded a pooled prevalence of 0.56 (95% CI: 0.48–0.64), indicating substantial heterogeneity among studies (I² = 98.8%, p < 0.001) (Fig. 2). To investigate potential sources of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted based on country, study design, and sample size. These analyses revealed variations in mortality estimates across different settings and study characteristics, suggesting that these study-level characteristics may contribute to variability in reported mortality estimates.

Subgroup analysis

Given the high level of heterogeneity, subgroup analyses were conducted by country/region, study design, and sample size to explore potential sources of variability in cervical cancer–related mortality. Marked differences were observed across subgroups. The pooled prevalence ranged from 44% in Botswana to 77% in Rwanda, with comparatively higher estimates reported in Ghana, Kenya, Côte d’Ivoire, Sudan, and Rwanda than in Ethiopia and Nigeria. By study design, mortality was higher in retrospective cohort studies (60%) than in prospective cohorts (48%), while the single cross-sectional study reported a prevalence of 70%. Studies with larger sample sizes (≥ 437 participants) reported slightly higher pooled mortality estimates (60%) compared with smaller studies (54%). Despite these observed subgroup differences, substantial heterogeneity persisted across all analyses, indicating that pooled estimates should be interpreted with caution (Table 3).

Publication bias and sensitivity analysis

The funnel plot demonstrated some visual asymmetry, raising the possibility of publication bias or small-study effects. However, Egger’s regression test for funnel plot asymmetry was not statistically significant (t = 0.99, p = 0.33), indicating no strong evidence of publication bias. Considering the substantial heterogeneity observed across studies (I² = 97.6%), the apparent asymmetry is more plausibly explained by genuine between-study variability rather than selective reporting (Fig. 3). Sensitivity analysis, conducted by sequentially omitting each study, yielded highly consistent pooled estimates of cervical cancer-related mortality (ranging from 0.59 to 0.63), with negligible changes in heterogeneity (I² ≈ 98.8%). These findings confirm that no single study disproportionately influenced the overall results, supporting the robustness of the pooled estimate (Fig. 4).

Meta regression

To explore potential trends in cervical cancer mortality over time, we performed a meta-regression using publication year as a covariate. The analysis revealed no significant association (β = − 0.0064, 95% CI: − 0.0281 to 0.0152, p = 0.5601). The bubble plot (Fig. 5) showed a slight, non-significant decline in mortality, with wide confidence intervals and overlapping study distributions, indicating that publication year did not account for between-study differences. Publication year explained none of the heterogeneity (R² = 0.0%), and substantial residual heterogeneity remained (I² = 98.7%, QE = 1709.2, p < 0.001). These findings suggest that no clear temporal trend could be detected based on publication year, though this analysis is limited by residual heterogeneity and the ecological nature of meta-regression.

Predictors of cervical cancer-related mortality in sub-Saharan Africa

Several studies have identified various factors that are associated with cervical cancer-related mortality. When compiling and synthesizing these factors, three main themes emerged: socio-demographic factors (including residence, age, and occupation), obstetric and gynecologic factors (such as parity, contraceptive use, and history of obstetric complications), substance use and smoking, and co-existing health problems (comorbidities, HIV, and Anemia).

Sociodemographic factors

Numerous studies have identified several socio-demographic factors as significant predictors of cervical cancer–related mortality. Age has been consistently recognized as one such factor. Findings from this meta-analysis indicate that individuals aged over 60 years at the time of diagnosis have a significantly higher risk of cervical cancer–related mortality compared with those aged 60 years or younger (OR = 1.12, 95% CI: 1.05–1.18) (Fig. 6). Place of residence also emerged as an important socio-demographic determinant; patients residing in rural areas exhibited a 1.38-fold increased risk of cervical cancer–related mortality compared with their urban counterparts (OR = 1.38, 95% CI: 1.15–1.65) (Fig. 7).

Obstetrics and gynecologic factors

Various studies have identified numerous obstetric and gynecologic factors as predictors of cervical cancer-related mortalities. One such factor is the use of contraceptives. In this meta-analysis study, cervical cancer patients who use hormonal contraceptives had a 1.23 times greater risk of cervical cancer-related mortality as compared with their counterparts (1.23, 1.05–1.45) (Table 3). The other gynecologic factor included in this study is abortion history; cervical cancer patients who had previous abortions had a 1.33 times greater chance of cervical cancer-related mortality as compared with their counterparts (OR = 1.33, CI 1.21–1.47) (Table 3).

Behavioral factors

Various studies have identified numerous behavior-related factors as predictors of cervical cancer-related mortalities. One such factor is substance use. In this meta-analysis study, cervical cancer patients who were addicted to and practiced any substance had a 1.22 times greater risk of cervical cancer-related mortality as compared with their counterparts (OR = 1.21, CI = 1.07–1.38) (Table 3).

Co-existing medical illness-related factors

Various studies have identified numerous comorbidities as predictors of cervical cancer-related mortalities. In this meta-analysis study, cervical cancer patients who had comorbidity had a 1.20 times greater risk of cervical cancer-related mortality as compared with their counterparts (OR = 1.20, CI = 1.10–1.31) (Table 3). In this meta-analysis, cervical cancer patients with anemia had higher odds of cervical cancer–related mortality compared with non-anemic patients (OR = 1.47, 95% CI: 1.37–1.58). Most of the retained articles for this meta-analysis include the stage of the disease as the variable in their primary studies. Treatment-related predictors of cervical cancer–related mortality

Most of the included studies reported data on treatment approaches as potential predictors of cervical cancer-related mortality. Patients treated with a single treatment modality had a significantly higher risk of mortality compared with those receiving combined treatment approaches (OR = 1.44, 95% CI: 1.34–1.55) (Table 3).

The number of chemotherapy cycles is identified as an important predictor. Patients receiving only a single cycle of chemotherapy had a 28% higher risk of mortality compared with those receiving two or more cycles (OR = 1.28, 95% CI: 1.08–1.52) (Table 3). The Equivalent Dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) was further assessed. Although prior studies suggest that higher EQD2 improves tumor control and overall survival, our analysis indicated no significant difference in mortality between patients treated with EQD2 < 75 and those receiving EQD2 ≥ 75.

Discussion

This systematic review and meta-analysis demonstrate that cervical cancer mortality in sub-Saharan Africa remains unacceptably high, with more than half of diagnosed women dying from the disease. Across included studies, mortality estimates were consistently elevated, although substantial variability was observed by country, study design, and population characteristics. Despite this heterogeneity, the overall findings point to a persistently high burden of mortality throughout the region, reflecting widespread challenges in prevention, early detection, and access to effective treatment. These results underscore significant inequities in cervical cancer outcomes in sub-Saharan Africa and highlight critical gaps in cancer control efforts that require urgent attention.

In our study, cervical cancer–related mortality in sub-Saharan Africa was 56%, indicating that more than half of women diagnosed with the disease die from it. This estimate is higher than reports from Europe [37], Asia [38], Latin America and the Caribbean [39], China [40], Rusia [39]. Mortality varied substantially across individual studies, ranging from 23% in Ethiopia (Teshome et al., [22]) to 93% in Kenya (Osok et al., [28]). This variation likely reflects differences in study populations, referral patterns, cancer registry systems, and study methodologies rather than true differences in healthcare quality [41–43]. For instance, studies from Kenya primarily included advanced-stage cases managed at tertiary hospitals, whereas Ethiopian studies captured a broader spectrum of disease stages [44]. Despite this heterogeneity, the consistently high mortality across settings highlights major gaps in prevention, early detection, and access to effective treatment in the region [45–47].

Subgroup analyses demonstrated notable variability in cervical cancer–related mortality by country, study design, and sample size, with higher mortality observed in Rwanda, Sudan, and Ghana, and lower estimates in Botswana, Ethiopia, and Nigeria. Retrospective studies reported higher mortality than prospective cohorts, and larger sample sizes were associated with slightly increased estimates. However, elevated mortality persisted across all subgroups, confirming the robustness and generalizability of the findings. Meta-regression by publication year showed no significant temporal decline in mortality, indicating that cervical cancer–related deaths have remained largely unchanged over time.

These persistently high mortality rates may be driven by structural and clinical barriers, including inadequate screening and early detection despite their strong association with reduced mortality [48]. Access to comprehensive cancer care, including surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy, is severely limited in sub-Saharan Africa, and delays in initiating treatment significantly worsen outcomes. The scarcity and inconsistent availability of these essential therapies further drive high mortality across all disease stages [49, 50].

This review also identified multiple predictors of cervical cancer–related mortality in sub-Saharan Africa, spanning sociodemographic, obstetric and gynecological, behavioral, comorbidity-related, disease-specific, and treatment-related factors.

In this meta-analysis, age was identified as an important demographic factor influencing cervical cancer outcomes. Patients diagnosed at age ≥ 60 years had significantly higher cervical cancer-related mortality compared with those diagnosed before 60 years. Several factors may contribute to this disparity. With advancing age, immune function declines, reducing the body’s ability to respond effectively to cancer and its treatment. Older patients are also more likely to present with comorbidities such as diabetes and cardiovascular disease, which can limit therapeutic options and compromise tolerance to aggressive interventions. Furthermore, cervical cancer in older women may exhibit more aggressive biological behavior, potentially linked to specific tumor subtypes or genetic mutations [51]. Differences in healthcare systems, access to treatment, and cultural factors across countries may further contribute to these higher mortality rates. This finding is supported by studies conducted in Bhutan [52], China [50], India [53], and Korea [54]. However, our results are higher than those from studies in China [55] and Korea [56].

The other sociodemographic predictor of cervical cancer-related mortality in our study is rural residency. Cervical cancer patients who reside in rural areas have 38% greater cervical cancer-related mortality as compared with their counterparts. This might be because cervical cancer patients in rural areas may face higher mortality due to limited access to healthcare, delays in diagnosis and treatment, shortages of specialized medical staff and resources, and financial barriers to seeking care [57]. No study specifically addressed this finding.

Among obstetrics and gynecologic factors, cervical cancer patients who used hormonal contraceptives had a 23% increased risk of cervical cancer-related mortality as compared with those who did not use hormonal contraceptives. The higher cervical cancer-related mortality in patients using hormonal contraceptives may stem from biological and behavioral factors. Hormonal contraceptives can alter the cervical environment, increasing susceptibility to persistent HPV infections and potentially accelerating the progression of HPV-related lesions to cervical cancer [57].

Another gynecologic-related factor for cervical cancer-related mortality is the history of abortion; in our research, cervical cancer-related mortality is higher by 33% among patients who have a history of abortion as compared with those who do not have one. Patients with a history of abortion may have a higher risk of cervical cancer-related mortality due to increased HPV exposure, delays in early detection from gaps in reproductive health services, hormonal and immune changes influencing cancer progression, and socioeconomic barriers to timely treatment [58]. Our findings were lower than a study done in China [55].

The other essential predictors included in this study were behavioral factors; in our research, cervical cancer-related mortality among substance-using cervical cancer patients was more significant by 22% than that of their counterparts. Cervical cancer patients who use substances face higher mortality due to immune suppression, persistent HPV infection, and increased tumor aggressiveness from tobacco and alcohol use. Substance use is also associated with delayed diagnosis, poor treatment adherence, additional health risks, and socioeconomic barriers that restrict access to quality care [58]. This study was in line with a study done in Mexico [59].

In our study, cervical cancer patients with comorbidities had a greater chance of cervical cancer-related mortality, and anemic cervical cancer patients had a nearly 50% greater chance of cervical cancer-related mortality as compared with their counterparts. Cervical cancer patients with anemia may face worse outcomes, as anemia weakens immune function, reduces treatment tolerance, and lowers overall resilience, increasing mortality risk [60]. This study agrees with a study done in China [61], India [62], and Saudi Arabia [63].

Similarly, HIV-positive cervical cancer patients had a 17% greater chance of cervical cancer-related mortality as compared with their counterparts. This could be due to HIV weakening the immune system, reducing the body’s ability to fight infections and respond to cancer treatments. This leads to faster disease progression, higher infection risk, and less effective therapies, increasing cervical cancer-related mortality [64]. Our finding is in line with one study done in rural India [65]. However, it contradicts a study done in Pakistan [66] and the United States [67].

The other significant predictor of cervical cancer mortality in sub-Saharan Africa was disease stage and histology. In this meta-analysis, women diagnosed at advanced stages (stage III and above) had an 80% higher risk of mortality compared with those at stage II or below. Advanced disease is marked by extensive tumor growth, tissue spread, and metastasis, which reduce the effectiveness of surgery, radiotherapy, and chemotherapy. This heavier disease burden and treatment challenges contribute to the significantly higher mortality observed in late-stage diagnoses [68]. Our findings are consistent with those studies from China [55] and the Netherlands [69]. But our finding is lower than a study done in Amsterdam (Netherlands) [70], Bhutan [52], and India [53]. Our study’s findings are higher than those in Mysuru (India) [71].

In this meta-analysis, histological type emerged as an essential predictor of cervical cancer–related mortality. Even though adenocarcinoma is the second most common form of cervical cancer, its mortality was 30% higher than that of squamous cell carcinoma, the most common type. This can be explained by the difficulty of detecting adenocarcinoma early, as it develops in the endocervical canal and often remains asymptomatic until advanced stages. Moreover, it responds less effectively to radiotherapy, which is more successful in treating squamous cell carcinoma. These factors contribute to the higher mortality associated with adenocarcinoma, despite squamous cell carcinoma being more prevalent [68]. Our result was on track studies from Amsterdam (Netherlands) [70], Bhutan [52], China [55], and Korea [56]. However, our finding is higher than that of another study in the Netherlands [69].

Treatment modalities were also the other essential predictors of cervical cancer–related mortality. Patients treated with a single modality, surgery, radiotherapy, or chemotherapy alone, had a 44% higher risk of death compared with those receiving combined treatment. A single approach is often inadequate, particularly in advanced stages where disease has spread, while multimodal therapy provides a more comprehensive attack on both primary and metastatic tumors. This integrated approach improves treatment success, lowers recurrence, and enhances survival outcomes [72]. Our findings are on track with a study conducted in Pakistan [66], Saudi Arabia [73], India [53], and China [61]. However, our findings are more significant than a study done in the Netherlands [69] and Malaysia [74].

The number of chemotherapy cycles also influenced outcomes. Patients receiving only one cycle had a 28% greater risk of mortality than those receiving multiple cycles. The improved survival among patients completing more cycles may be explained by the cumulative therapeutic effect, which reduces tumor size, prevents recurrence, and enhances long-term outcomes [72]. By contrast, no significant mortality difference was observed between patients treated with Equivalent Dose in 2 Gy fractions (EQD2) < 75 Gy and those treated with ≥ 75 Gy, suggesting that total radiation dose alone may not be a decisive factor when other treatment variables are considered.

Collectively, these findings underscore the urgent need to strengthen cervical cancer control in sub-Saharan Africa. Scaling up HPV vaccination, implementing effective screening programs, and improving timely access to diagnosis and treatment could significantly reduce mortality. At the policy level, investments should prioritize healthcare infrastructure, training of healthcare professionals, and equitable delivery of evidence-based treatment. Tailored interventions that address sociodemographic disparities, comorbidities, and treatment barriers are essential to reducing the disproportionate burden of cervical cancer mortality in the region.

Strengths and limitations of the study

This systematic review and meta-analysis synthesized evidence from multiple studies conducted across Sub-Saharan Africa, increasing statistical power and providing a comprehensive overview of cervical cancer–related mortality in the region. Subgroup, sensitivity, and meta-regression analyses were performed to assess the robustness of the findings and explore potential sources of variability. However, the findings should be interpreted in light of several limitations. Very high between-study heterogeneity persisted across analyses, reflecting substantial differences in study designs, populations, settings, and outcome definitions. Although subgroup analyses partially addressed this variability, considerable residual heterogeneity remained. The uneven geographical distribution of included studies, with overrepresentation from a limited number of countries, limits the generalizability of the pooled estimates to the entire Sub-Saharan African region. Restricting inclusion to English-language studies may have led to language bias and the exclusion of relevant evidence. Finally, the observational nature of the included studies limits causal inference.

Conclusion

This systematic review and meta-analysis highlight that cervical cancer mortality in sub-Saharan Africa remains a critical public health challenge, despite the largely preventable nature of the disease. The persistently high mortality reflects broader systemic issues, including barriers to early detection, limited access to timely and comprehensive care, and persistent inequities in healthcare delivery across the region. These findings underscore the need for strengthened prevention and control strategies rather than isolated clinical interventions.

Addressing this burden will require coordinated efforts to expand HPV vaccination coverage, improve screening and diagnostic services, and ensure equitable access to effective treatment, particularly in resource-limited settings. Strengthening health systems and research capacity is essential for achieving sustained reductions in cervical cancer mortality in sub-Saharan Africa. In parallel, future research should focus on generating high-quality evidence to inform context-specific interventions and evaluate their impact on survival.
