Augmenting hemopexin-mediated heme scavenging mitigates sepsis-induced acute kidney injury in humanized sickle cell mice
Authors: Olufunke O. Falayi, Jada D. Williams, Ravi Kumar, Olufunke O. Arishe, Jeremiah M. Afolabi, Adebowale Adebiyi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Key Points•Free heme from chronic hemolysis drives septic AKI in SCD, linking exaggerated inflammation and oxidative stress to kidney dysfunction.•Therapeutic HPX restoration buffers heme, preserves renal function, and improves survival in septic hemolytic SCD.

Introduction

Sickle cell disease (SCD) is a hereditary hemoglobinopathy caused by a point mutation in the β-globin gene that results in the production of hemoglobin S (HbS). Under deoxygenated conditions, HbS polymerizes, deforming erythrocytes into rigid, sickle-shaped cells that obstruct the microvasculature and impair tissue perfusion.1 This vaso-occlusive process contributes to many acute and chronic complications of SCD, including pain crises, progressive organ injury, and increased susceptibility to infection.1, 2, 3 In parallel, sickled red blood cells are fragile and undergo recurrent intravascular hemolysis, releasing cell-free hemoglobin, heme, and iron into the circulation.1,2,4 These hemolysis-derived products promote oxidative stress, endothelial dysfunction, inflammation, and tissue injury, thereby creating a chronic proinflammatory and pro-oxidant milieu.4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9

Infections are a major clinical problem in SCD and may progress to sepsis, a life-threatening syndrome characterized by dysregulated host inflammatory responses and multiorgan dysfunction.3,10, 11, 12 The increased susceptibility of individuals with SCD to infection is multifactorial and includes impaired splenic function, complement defects, macrophage saturation by hemolysis products, micronutrient deficiencies, genetic susceptibility, and frequent health care exposure.3,10, 11, 12, 13, 14, 15 Importantly, hemolysis and infection may reinforce one another in SCD. Hemolysis can impair host defense and amplify inflammation, whereas infection and sepsis can worsen red blood cell destruction through immune-mediated hemolysis, disseminated intravascular coagulation, impaired clearance of damaged erythrocytes, and pathogen-derived hemolysins.16, 17, 18, 19, 20, 21 Thus, septic stress in SCD may occur in the setting of an already elevated heme burden and may further intensify hemolysis-driven tissue injury.

The kidney is particularly vulnerable to both sepsis- and heme-mediated injury. Sepsis is a leading cause of acute kidney injury (AKI), driven by inflammatory mediators, oxidative stress, microvascular dysfunction, and tubular stress responses.13,22, 23, 24 In SCD, these mechanisms may be compounded by chronic hemolysis, renal medullary hypoxia, endothelial dysfunction, and heme- and iron-mediated cytotoxicity.6,7,9,25, 26, 27 Although AKI is recognized as an important complication of SCD and may contribute to progression toward chronic kidney disease,28, 29, 30, 31, 32, 33, 34 the specific mechanisms by which polymicrobial sepsis aggravates kidney injury in SCD remain incompletely defined. It remains unclear whether sepsis-induced worsening of the circulating heme burden serves as a mechanistic link between hemolysis, systemic inflammation, renal inflammasome activation, oxidative injury, and loss of kidney function.

Hemopexin (HPX) is a liver-derived plasma glycoprotein and the primary circulating scavenger of free heme.5,35,36 By binding heme with high affinity, HPX limits the oxidative, inflammatory, and cytotoxic effects of cell-free heme and facilitates its clearance.35, 36, 37, 38, 39 However, HPX can become depleted during chronic hemolytic states such as SCD, potentially reducing the capacity to buffer additional heme released during acute inflammatory insults.40,41 This raises the possibility that HPX depletion may represent a critical vulnerability during sepsis in SCD, allowing free heme to amplify systemic and kidney-localized injury pathways.

In this study, we tested the hypothesis that polymicrobial sepsis exacerbates hemolysis-driven heme accumulation and AKI in SCD, and that enhancing HPX-mediated heme scavenging mitigates these injuries. Using humanized Townes SCD mice, we examined whether low-grade cecal ligation and puncture (LgCLP) induces exaggerated systemic inflammation, heme accumulation, renal inflammasome activation, oxidative stress, and kidney dysfunction. We further tested whether acute HPX supplementation or sustained liver-directed adeno-associated virus serotype 8 (AAV8)–mediated human HPX (hHPX) expression lowers heme burden and protects against sepsis-induced AKI.

Materials and methods

Animal care and use

Male mice aged 10 to 12 weeks were used in all experiments. The humanized Townes SCD model, including sickling (HbSS) and nonsickling (HbAA) mice, was obtained from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME).42,43 Mice were genotyped and maintained at the University of Tennessee Health Science Center (UTHSC) and the University of Missouri–Columbia Laboratory Animal Care Units with ad libitum access to food and water. All procedures were approved by the UTHSC Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (number 19-027.0) and the University of Missouri–Columbia Animal Care and Use Committee (number 43494.0). Animal studies were designed and conducted in accordance with Animal Research: Reporting of in vivo Experiments guidelines.

CLP

All surgeries were performed under aseptic conditions. Mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and received buprenorphine (1 mg/kg, subcutaneously) for analgesia. After placement in dorsal recumbency, a midline laparotomy was performed to expose the cecum and terminal ileum. For high-grade CLP, the cecum was exteriorized, ligated just distal to the ileocecal valve to reduce luminal diameter by ∼60%, and punctured 5 times with a 21-gauge needle.44 A small, consistent amount of fecal material was gently extruded. For LgCLP, the cecum was ligated at ∼30% of its total length and punctured once through-and-through with a 22-gauge needle.45, 46, 47 Sham-operated mice underwent cecal exteriorization without ligation or puncture. After the procedure, the cecum was returned to the abdominal cavity, the peritoneum was closed with sterile 5-0 coated absorbable sutures, and the skin was closed with 5-0 silk sutures. Mice received 1 mL of warmed saline intraperitoneally, recovered on a heated pad, and were returned to their cages for up to 18 hours. Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) was then measured over 1.5 hours by transdermal monitoring, followed by necropsy. For experiments testing exogenous HPX, mice received purified hHPX (5 mg/kg in 1 mL warmed saline, intraperitoneally) immediately after LgCLP. The dose was selected on the basis of a previous study.39

AAV8-hHPX construct, vectors, and gene transfer

To provide sustained systemic heme scavenging, we used a liver-targeted AAV8 vector expressing hHPX (AAV8-hHPX) under control of the CAG promoter, which supports robust hepatic expression.48 The AAV8 capsid was selected because of its liver tropism.49, 50, 51 The AAV8-hHPX vector was designed and synthesized by the University of Missouri Animal Modeling Core, and vector genome titers were determined by quantitative polymerase chain reaction using primers specific for the hHPX transgene. A control vector expressing enhanced green fluorescent protein (AAV8-eGFP) was generated in parallel. For gene transfer studies, HbSS mice were randomly assigned to receive AAV8-hHPX or AAV8-eGFP. Under isoflurane anesthesia, mice received a single retro-orbital injection of 1.83 × 1012 vector genomes per kg in 50 μL sterile phosphate-buffered saline. Animals were monitored daily for signs of distress or adverse effects. Two weeks after vector administration, mice underwent LgCLP or sham surgery to allow stable hepatic transgene expression. At 18 hours after LgCLP, GFR was measured over 1.5 hours, followed by necropsy and sample collection.

GFR

GFR was measured using a previously described noninvasive transdermal method.52, 53, 54 At 18 hours after LgCLP, the hair on the back of conscious mice was shaved, and an optical transdermal GFR device was attached to the skin. Fluorescein isothiocyanate–sinistrin (100 mg/kg) was injected into the tail vein, and its clearance kinetics were recorded for 1.5 hours. Data were analyzed with MediBeacon Studio 3 software according to the manufacturer’s instructions to calculate GFR.

Specimen acquisition and processing

Blood samples were obtained from the retro-orbital sinus into heparinized tubes and stored at −80°C until analysis. Urine was collected during GFR monitoring using a previously described approach.55, 56, 57 Briefly, mice were individually placed on a 96-well plate, which was positioned within a modified 5-mL pipette tip box, for ∼1.5 hours. Urine from the wells was centrifuged and used for downstream assays. After euthanasia, the kidneys were removed and sectioned transversely. Portions were fixed in 4% buffered formalin for histopathology, whereas the remaining tissue was snap-frozen and stored at −80°C for molecular analyses.

Biochemical analyses

Plasma heme, neutrophil gelatinase-associated lipocalin (NGAL), and ferritin were measured using enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay kits from Abcam (ab272534, ab199083, and ab157713, respectively) according to the manufacturers’ protocols. Plasma cystatin C was measured using a Crystal Chem kit (catalog no. 80738), and urinary isoprostane was quantified using an Oxford Biomedical Research kit (EA85). Urinary creatinine was measured independently by liquid chromatography–tandem mass spectrometry at the University of Alabama at Birmingham/University of California, San Diego O’Brien Core Center for AKI Research. Plasma blood urea nitrogen (BUN), alanine aminotransferase, alkaline phosphatase, and aspartate aminotransferase were measured in a blinded manner using a standard clinical chemistry/toxicity panel by IDEXX BioAnalytics. Plasma cytokines were quantified using multiplex bead-based assays on a Luminex Magpix system at the UTHSC Regional Biocontainment Laboratory. Cytokine-release inhibitors were not administered before euthanasia. Samples for cytokine analyses were collected at the post-LgCLP end point from surviving animals.

Histology

Formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded kidney sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin. Histopathologic evaluation was performed independently by board-certified pathologists at iHisto Inc (Salem, MA), who were blinded to mouse strain, organ weights, and gross pathology. A general histologic survey was conducted using a semiquantitative scoring system from 0 to 4, in which 0 indicates no significant change, and 1, 2, 3, and 4 indicate minimal, mild, moderate, and severe change, respectively.

Immunofluorescence

Paraffin-embedded kidney sections were deparaffinized in xylene, rehydrated through graded ethanol to distilled water, and subjected to heat-induced epitope retrieval in low-pH antigen retrieval solution using a steamer for 30 minutes. After cooling and phosphate-buffered saline rinses, sections were permeabilized with 0.2% Triton X-100 for 15 minutes and blocked for 1 hour at room temperature with Animal-Free Blocker and Diluent, R.T.U. Sections were incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies against interleukin-1β (IL-1β); nucleotide-binding domain, leucine-rich–containing family, pyrin domain–containing-3 (NLRP3); lactate dehydrogenase (LDH); 3-nitrotyrosine (3-NT); tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinase 2 (TIMP-2); or insulin-like growth factor–binding protein 7 (IGFBP7); each diluted 1:100. Sections were then washed and incubated for 1 hour at room temperature in the dark with fluorophore-conjugated secondary antibodies, followed by mounting with DAPI (4′,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole)–containing antifade medium. Images were acquired from 3 to 5 randomly selected fields per slide using a Leica Thunder fluorescence microscope. Fluorescence intensity was quantified in Fiji after channel separation and normalized to DAPI intensity.

Western blot

Total liver protein was isolated in radioimmunoprecipitation assay buffer, quantified, mixed with lithium dodecyl sulfate sample buffer containing dithiothreitol, and heat-denatured at 70°C for 10 minutes. Equal protein amounts were separated by sodium dodecyl sulfate–polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis and transferred to polyvinylidene fluoride membranes using a semidry Bio-Rad system. Membranes were blocked for 1 hour in Tris-buffered saline containing 0.05% Tween-20 and 4% bovine serum albumin, then incubated overnight at 4°C with HPX (1:300) and β-actin (1:1000) antibodies. Bands were detected by enhanced chemiluminescence after horseradish peroxidase–secondary antibody incubation.

Reagents and antibodies

Purified hHPX (HX1703-R21; Athens Research) was used. Primary antibodies included anti-NLRP3 (68102-1-Ig; Proteintech), anti–IL-1β (16806-1-AP; Proteintech), anti-LDH (ab52488, Abcam), anti–3-NT (ab110282, Abcam), anti–TIMP-2 (ab230511; Abcam), anti–β-actin (ab115777; Abcam), anti-IGFBP7 (MA5-29346; Invitrogen), and anti-HPX (MA5-35760; Invitrogen). Secondary antibodies included donkey anti–rabbit Alexa Fluor 488 (20015; Biotium), donkey anti–mouse Alexa Fluor 555 (20231; Biotium), and horseradish peroxidase–conjugated anti–rabbit immunoglobulin G (7074S; Cell Signaling Technology).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using GraphPad Prism version 10 (GraphPad, Sacramento, CA). Data are presented as violin or scatter plots and expressed as mean ± standard error of the mean. A P value < .05 was considered statistically significant. Comparisons among multiple groups were performed using 1-way analysis of variance, followed by Holm-Šídák multiple-comparisons testing. Statistical significance is indicated by asterisks (∗P < .05; ∗∗P < .01; ∗∗∗P < .001; and ∗∗∗∗P < .0001).

Results

HPX reduces circulating heme and ferritin during septic stress in SCD mice

SS mice subjected to ligation of ∼60% of the cecum and multiple punctures with 21-gauge needles (high-grade sepsis)44 exhibited 100% mortality. Hence, the protocol was modified to ligate ∼30% of the cecum and to perform a single puncture with 22-gauge needles, which we regarded as low-grade sepsis (LgCLP; Figure 1A). All AA control mice survived 18 hours of LgCLP. By contrast, only 41% of SS mice survived. At baseline, SS mice exhibited significantly higher plasma free heme and ferritin concentrations than AA control mice, consistent with chronic hemolysis and iron loading (Figure 1B-C). Although LgCLP induced minimal changes in AA mice, it triggered a robust surge in both plasma heme and ferritin in SS mice, indicating that septic stress disproportionately amplifies the heme burden in the sickle genotype. HPX mitigates the exaggerated systemic cytokine response to sepsis in SCD mice

We next characterized the systemic inflammatory landscape. LgCLP elicited a markedly exaggerated cytokine response in SS mice compared with AA mice. Plasma levels of proinflammatory (IL-1β, IL-6, IL-17A, IL-18, interferon gamma, tumor necrosis factor α) and regulatory (IL-10, IL-27) cytokines were significantly elevated in septic SS mice (Figure 2). This “cytokine storm” was substantially blunted by HPX supplementation, which restored the cytokine levels toward values in sham mice (Figure 2). Heme scavenging preserves GFR and limits renal inflammasome activation in SCD mice

Given that the kidney is a primary target of heme-mediated toxicity, we evaluated the impact of septic stress on renal function and inflammatory signaling. At baseline, SS sham mice exhibited significantly higher GFR than AA sham control mice (Figure 3A), consistent with the compensatory glomerular hyperfiltration characteristic of early-stage SCD nephropathy.34,58, 59, 60 After LgCLP, AA mice showed a modest decline in GFR, whereas SS mice experienced a more pronounced functional collapse (Figure 3A). This decline in GFR in septic SS mice was accompanied by a marked increase in renal oxidative stress, as evidenced by elevated urinary isoprostane-to-creatinine ratios and a significant rise in plasma and urinary NGAL, a sensitive biomarker of acute tubular injury (Figure 3B-D). To investigate the molecular drivers of this injury, we performed immunofluorescence mapping of the NLRP3 inflammasome pathway. Septic SS kidneys displayed robust cortical upregulation of both NLRP3 and its downstream effector, IL-1β (Figure 3E-F). Notably, acute HPX administration significantly attenuated renal NLRP3 and IL-1β expression, mitigated renal oxidative stress and tubular injury, and preserved GFR near SS sham levels despite the septic insult (Figure 3). AAV8-mediated hepatic HPX expression provides sustained protection and improves survival without hepatotoxicity in SCD mice

To evaluate the efficacy of a durable therapeutic intervention, we used liver-directed gene transfer using an AAV8 vector encoding hHPX (AAV8-hHPX; Figure 4A). Two weeks after injection, AAV8-hHPX–treated SS mice exhibited robust hepatic HPX expression, which was associated with a significant reduction in steady-state plasma heme levels compared with AAV8-eGFP control mice (Figure 4B-C). Sustained transgenic HPX expression did not alter plasma markers of hepatocellular or cholestatic injury, including alanine aminotransferase, aspartate aminotransferase, and alkaline phosphatase, confirming the safety and tolerability of the vector (Figure 4D-F). We next challenged these mice with septic stress. In AAV8-eGFP–treated SS mice, hepatic HPX remained markedly depleted under both sham and septic conditions, underscoring a persistent failure of the endogenous heme-buffering system in SCD (Figure 4G). In contrast, AAV8-hHPX treatment successfully restored hepatic HPX protein levels in both sham and septic settings, effectively blunting the surge in plasma heme and iron burden (Figure 4G-I). We further examined the expression of IGFBP7 and TIMP2, biomarkers associated with G1 cell cycle arrest and renal tubular stress. Immunofluorescence analysis revealed a robust upregulation of both IGFBP7 and TIMP2 in the kidneys of septic AAV8-eGFP mice, whereas AAV8-hHPX treatment significantly attenuated the expression of these stress markers (Figure 6F-H). Histopathological evaluation across all groups revealed no overt glomerular injury, tubular necrosis, or cast formation. The predominant structural finding was mild tubular vacuolation, which was significantly exacerbated in the AAV8-eGFP sepsis group but reduced in mice receiving AAV8-hHPX (Figure 6I-J). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that genetic augmentation of HPX provides multimodal protection against septic AKI in SCD by maintaining glomerular filtration, reducing tubular stress signaling, and preserving renal histological integrity.

Discussion

In this study, we identify cell-free heme as a central mediator of sepsis-induced AKI in SCD and show that augmenting the endogenous heme scavenger HPX, either through acute protein supplementation or liver-directed AAV8-mediated gene transfer, confers substantial renal and systemic protection in humanized sickle cell mice. Overall, these findings implicate hemolysis-derived heme as a key pathogenic signal that couples septic stress to kidney dysfunction in SCD and provide mechanistic support for HPX-centered therapeutic strategies, as rational interventions to mitigate heme-driven tissue injury.

CLP is a standard polymicrobial sepsis model that recapitulates human sepsis, including an infectious source, progressive course, peritonitis, systemic inflammation, hemodynamic changes, and multiorgan dysfunction.61,62 However, the severity and mortality associated with CLP are highly dependent on procedural parameters, including the length or level of cecal ligation, needle gauge, and number of punctures.44, 45, 46, 47,63 Consistent with work showing that longer ligation and/or larger needle caliber and multiple punctures intensify systemic inflammation and mortality,44, 45, 46, 47,63 a high-grade CLP protocol, in our study (∼60% ligation with multiple 21-gauge punctures), was uniformly lethal in homozygous SS mice. This underscores a narrow physiologic reserve in the sickle genotype during polymicrobial challenge and highlights the importance of precisely defining CLP conditions when comparing outcomes across studies and strains.44, 45, 46, 47,63 To model a clinically relevant but survivable septic insult, we implemented an LgCLP regimen (∼30% ligation with a single through-and-through 22-gauge puncture), which was uniformly survivable in nonsickling AA mice yet still produced substantial mortality and a markedly amplified cytokine response in SS mice. This heightened susceptibility parallels clinical observations that individuals with SCD experience disproportionate morbidity and mortality from infections and sepsis relative to the general population.3,10, 11, 12, 13,64

Chronic intravascular hemolysis is a defining feature of SCD and results in continuous release of hemoglobin, heme, and iron into the circulation.1,2,4,5,7, 8, 9 Under physiologic conditions, plasma scavenging systems, particularly HPX for free heme, limit heme’s oxidative, inflammatory, and cytotoxic effects by binding heme with high affinity and facilitating hepatic clearance and downstream metabolism.5,35, 36, 37, 38, 39,41 Multiple studies have reported that circulating HPX levels are reduced in SCD, consistent with chronic consumption in the setting of ongoing hemolysis.65, 66, 67 In our SS mice, profound hepatic HPX depletion coincided with elevated baseline plasma heme and ferritin, supporting the concept that SCD creates a chronic state of heme/iron stress. Importantly, sepsis imposed an additional heme and iron burden: LgCLP markedly increased circulating free heme and ferritin in SS mice but induced only modest, nonsignificant changes in AA mice. This genotype-specific amplification supports a model in which septic stress pushes an already heme-loaded system past a buffering threshold, allowing cell-free heme to act as a potent damage-associated molecular pattern and amplifier of organ injury.5,7, 8, 9,35, 36, 37, 38, 39,41

Free heme is hydrophobic, intercalates into lipid membranes, and catalyzes the generation of reactive oxygen and nitrogen species, thereby promoting oxidative injury and amplifying inflammatory signaling.5,7,9,35, 36, 37, 38, 39,41 In our model, septic SS mice developed a marked systemic cytokine response, substantially greater than that observed in AA mice. This was accompanied by robust renal induction of inflammasome-associated signals, including NLRP3 and IL-1β, increased oxidative/nitrosative injury markers, including urinary isoprostanes and 3-NT, and evidence of tissue damage, as reflected by LDH. These renal readouts are consistent with the established use of isoprostanes as in vivo indices of oxidative stress and NT as a marker of reactive nitrogen species–mediated protein modification.68,69 They are also consistent with broader evidence that oxidative stress can promote NLRP3 inflammasome activation in kidney disease.70 The absence of a significant increase in plasma cytokines in septic AA mice likely reflects the relatively mild nature of the LgCLP model used in this study, which may not have been sufficient to elicit a robust systemic inflammatory response in nonsickling control mice. However, LgCLP increased renal IL-1β and NLRP3 inflammasome activation in AA mice, although to a lesser extent than in SS mice. Thus, LgCLP did not markedly activate systemic inflammation in AA mice but was sufficient to induce a localized renal inflammatory response. Collectively, these findings support a mechanism in which septic stress in SCD, driven in part by an exaggerated heme surge, promotes renal inflammasome activation and oxidative/nitrosative injury that converge to impair kidney function.

Despite pronounced functional impairment and elevations in injury/stress biomarkers, conventional histology revealed only mild tubular vacuolation, with no overt glomerular destruction, tubular necrosis, or extensive cast formation. This dissociation between marked functional AKI and relatively limited structural injury on routine histopathology is increasingly recognized in sepsis-associated AKI, in which microvascular dysfunction, altered tubular energetics, and stress-adaptive programs may predominate over frank necrosis.13,71, 72, 73, 74 In addition, SS mice displayed baseline hyperfiltration, consistent with early SCD nephropathy observed clinically.34,58, 59, 60 Hyperfiltration may provide short-term compensation but may also increase susceptibility to subsequent acute insults, reinforcing the concept that AKI and CKD are interconnected syndromes, particularly relevant in SCD, in which renal complications strongly affect outcomes.24,29,34,58, 59, 60,75

A major finding is that restoring heme-buffering capacity via HPX is sufficient to blunt both systemic and kidney-local injury pathways in septic SCD. Acute administration of purified hHPX immediately after LgCLP reduced circulating free heme and attenuated ferritin elevations in septic SS mice, demonstrating that exogenous HPX remains functional even within the inflammatory milieu of sepsis. This aligns with the established role of HPX in high-affinity heme binding and hepatic clearance, as well as previous evidence supporting its anti-inflammatory and cytoprotective effects in hemolytic states.35, 36, 37, 38, 39,41 Mechanistically, HPX supplementation mitigated the cytokine surge, reduced renal NLRP3 and IL-1β expression, decreased oxidative stress, preserved GFR, and lowered injury biomarkers. These findings support a model in which HPX acts upstream by neutralizing circulating heme, thereby limiting activation of inflammasome pathways and oxidative/nitrosative injury programs.

To determine whether sustained heme control confers durable protection, we used liver-directed AAV8-mediated expression of hHPX. Leveraging AAV8’s strong hepatotropism, this approach enabled long-term systemic production of HPX. Consistent with previous studies in hemolytic models,76,77 AAV8-hHPX restored hepatic HPX without evidence of hepatotoxicity. It reduced baseline plasma heme and blunted sepsis-induced increases in heme and ferritin, indicating effective scavenging. These biochemical improvements translated into significant outcomes: reduced renal NLRP3 activation, decreased oxidative injury, preserved GFR, and improved kidney injury biomarkers (BUN, cystatin C, NGAL). Furthermore, tubular stress markers (IGFBP7, TIMP2) and vacuolation were reduced, and survival improved. Together, these data suggest that sustained HPX restoration mitigates sepsis-induced injury in SCD by disrupting the heme-driven cycle of inflammation and oxidative injury.

Our findings complement a growing clinical focus on HPX as a determinant of susceptibility to heme-mediated damage in hemolytic disorders.35, 36, 37, 38, 39,41 CSL889 is a plasma-derived hHPX product under development for SCD, supported by preclinical evidence for HPX as a candidate therapy and by early-phase clinical evaluation.78, 79, 80, 81, 82 Phase 1 studies have reported acceptable safety/tolerability and pharmacokinetic properties compatible with repeated dosing in adults with stable SCD and in those presenting with vaso-occlusive crisis (VOC).78,79,81,82 The RHEMEDY phase 2/3 program is designed to evaluate safety, efficacy, and pharmacokinetics in adults and adolescents during VOC.80 Although these clinical efforts have focused on VOC-related outcomes, our findings provide mechanistic evidence that HPX augmentation may also benefit infection-associated complications of SCD, particularly sepsis-associated AKI, in which hemolysis, inflammation, and organ vulnerability intersect.3,10, 11, 12, 13,64 The linkage in our study between heme reduction and suppression of renal inflammasome activation, oxidative injury, and functional AKI supports heme scavenging as a mechanism-based strategy for kidney protection during severe infection in SCD.5,7,9,35, 36, 37, 38, 39, 40, 41

This study has several limitations. First, experiments were restricted to a short-term, LgCLP model in male mice, after confirming that a higher-severity regimen was lethal in SS animals. Future studies should evaluate broader CLP severities, additional time points, and female mice to define sex- and severity-dependent relationships among heme burden, inflammation, and organ injury. Second, although AAV8 is effective for liver-directed gene delivery in mice, clinical translation will require consideration of anti-AAV immunity, dose limitations, and long-term safety. Third, we did not directly assess broader consequences of sustained HPX augmentation beyond heme scavenging, including effects on iron homeostasis, complement regulation, or immune cell phenotypes. Finally, renal immunofluorescence findings were not orthogonally validated for all injury markers. Future studies should also evaluate additional sepsis models and combination therapeutic strategies, such as HPX supplementation with complementary scavengers.

In summary, our findings support a model in which chronic heme overload and HPX depletion in SCD establish a high-risk milieu, in which septic stress triggers a heme surge that amplifies systemic inflammation, kidney-localized inflammasome activation, and oxidative/nitrosative injury, ultimately culminating in AKI. The exaggerated kidney injury observed in SS mice appears to be driven, at least in part, by increased heme burden rather than by sepsis alone. Restoration of heme-buffering capacity, achieved either acutely through HPX protein replacement or durably via hepatic gene transfer, reduces heme accumulation, attenuates inflammatory and oxidative/nitrosative responses, preserves renal function, and improves survival. Collectively, these results provide a mechanistic rationale for HPX-centered therapeutic strategies to reduce sepsis-associated kidney injury and mortality in SCD.
