Static Otolith Signals Reflect Clinical Course in Acute Vestibular Neuritis
Authors: Seo‐Young Choi, Jae‐Hwan Choi, Hyun Sung Kim, Kwang‐Dong Choi
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesTo investigate how static and dynamic utricular functions differentially correlate with dizziness symptoms and quality of life (QoL) recovery in patients with acute vestibular neuritis (VN).DesignProspective observational study with 1‐week and 1‐month follow‐up assessments.SettingTertiary referral centre.ParticipantsTwenty‐six patients diagnosed with acute VN were enrolled and evaluated at baseline, 1 week and 1 month after symptom onset.Main Outcome MeasuresStatic utricular function was assessed using subjective visual vertical (SVV), and dynamic function via ocular vestibular‐evoked myogenic potentials (oVEMP). Dizziness symptoms and QoL were evaluated using the Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI), Visual Analogue Scale (VAS) and the EQ‐5D‐5L questionnaire.ResultsPatients with normalised SVV tilt at 1 week showed significantly greater improvement in DHI scores compared to those with persistent abnormal SVV (p = 0.046). By 1 month, these patients demonstrated further significant improvement in both DHI (p = 0.012) and EQ‐5D‐5L (p = 0.035) scores. Furthermore, early improvement in SVV tilt correlated significantly with DHI improvement at 1 week (p = 0.048, Spearman's rho = 0.392). In contrast, dynamic utricular function as measured by oVEMP did not correlate with clinical recovery at any time point.ConclusionIn acute VN, static utricular function correlates with early symptom relief and QoL recovery, whereas dynamic function does not. These findings suggest that central adaptive mechanisms may act more selectively on static utricular signals or recover dynamic pathways more rapidly.Keywords: otolith, subjective visual vertical, utricle, vertigo, vestibular evoked myogenic potential, vestibular neuritis

Introduction

The utricular pathway divides into sustained (static) and transient (dynamic) systems. The sustained system, involving regularly firing vestibular afferents, detects prolonged stimuli such as head tilt and plays a key role in perceiving vertical orientation [1]. In contrast, the transient system relies on irregularly firing afferents from the striola and handles quick vestibular reflexes that are crucial for maintaining gaze stability and balance during rapid movements [1].

Clinically, static utricular function can be assessed by measuring the subjective visual vertical (SVV), while dynamic utricular function can be evaluated using ocular vestibular‐evoked myogenic potentials (oVEMP) [1, 2, 3, 4]. Although there have been advances in vestibular testing, the connection between objective vestibular measurements and subjective symptoms remains unclear, especially regarding how otolithic dysfunction influences dizziness and imbalance.

Previous studies have associated otolithic dysfunction with various symptoms [5, 6, 7], such as acute vertigo with nausea and vomiting [6], postural instability, sensations of rocking [5] and directional pulling sensations [8]. However, these studies typically included diverse patient groups with broadly described symptoms, making it challenging to clearly understand symptom severity related to otolithic dysfunction.

Therefore, this study aims to clarify the relationship between subjective dizziness, quality of life (QoL) and utricular function by separately analysing the static and dynamic components in patients diagnosed with acute vestibular neuritis (VN).

Materials and Methods

Subject

In this study conducted at a tertiary referral centre between April 2019 and April 2022, patients presenting with acute vertigo and diagnosed with acute VN were prospectively enrolled. To minimise confounding effects from central vestibular compensation—particularly those involving brainstem or cerebellar structures—only cases of acute unilateral vestibulopathy (i.e., VN) were included.

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) patients experiencing sudden onset of vertigo accompanied by nausea or vomiting, (2) patients who visited the hospital within 1 week of symptom onset and (3) the presence of a unilateral decrease in vestibulo‐ocular reflex (VOR) gain on video head impulse test (vHIT) (≤ 0.70) and spontaneous nystagmus (SN) towards the opposite side from decreased VOR gain. Patients who met these criteria underwent follow‐up examinations at 1 week and/or 1 month after their initial evaluation.

The exclusion criteria for the study were: (1) patients under 18 years of age, (2) with rapid resolution of vestibular symptoms and abnormal vHIT results within 24 h after symptoms onset, (3) with decreased VOR gain on both sides, (4) with a history of or concurrent stroke, vestibular migraine, Meniere's disease, benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, labyrinthitis or other central/peripheral vestibulopathy, (5) with other neurologic signs implicated in central neurologic disorders including skew deviation and gaze‐evoked nystagmus (GEN), (6) who were clinically diagnosed but did not undergo laboratory tests or follow‐up assessments and (7) who did not provide consent to participate in the study.

Evaluation of Scoring for Symptoms and QoL

A self‐administered questionnaire to evaluate symptoms of dizziness and the health‐related QoL was the Visual Analogue Scale for dizziness (VAS), the Dizziness Handicap Inventory (DHI) [9] and the five‐level EuroQoL five‐Dimension (EQ‐5D‐5L) [10], respectively.

Oculography and Video Head Impulse Test

Eye movements were recorded at a sampling rate of 60 Hz using video‐oculography (VOG, SLMED, Seoul, Korea). SN with or without fixation, GEN, horizontal saccades and smooth pursuit were pursued following the methods described previously in detail [11]. Head and eye movements were recorded using vvHIT (SLMED, Seoul, South Korea). The detailed methods and abnormal VOR gain from both horizontal canals (HC) were described previously (normal HC gain = 0.85–1.02) [12].

Ocular Vestibular Evoked Myogenic Potentials and Subjective Visual Vertical Tilt

The detailed methods for recording oVEMP and SVV were described previously [12, 13, 14]. For oVEMP recordings (Nicolet Biomedical, Madison, WI, USA), patients sat upright and fixated on a target affixed to a wall > 2 m from the eyes, positioned to maintain an upward gaze (> 20°) throughout stimulation. Electromyographic activities were recorded using surface electrodes placed on the infra‐orbital ridge 1 cm below the centre of the lower eyelid. The reference electrode was attached 2 cm below the active electrode, and the ground electrode was on the forehead. Bilateral oVEMP were induced by up to 60 stimuli tapping the hairline at AFz using an electric reflex hammer (VIASYS Healthcare, CA, USA). For acquisition and processing, electromyographic signals were band‐pass filtered between 1.5 and 30 Hz with a 60‐Hz notch filter. AFz taps were delivered at an approximate rate of 3 stimuli per second. To ensure reproducibility, oVEMP recordings were obtained in two separate runs and the averaged waveform was used for analysis. Traces were reviewed by the examiner, and sweeps contaminated by blink or movement‐related artefacts were manually excluded before averaging. The latency of the negative wave at around 10 ms (n1) and the amplitude between n1 and the subsequent positive wave were measured. The interaural difference ratio of the amplitude of the oVEMPs was calculated as IAD (%) [(A i − A c )/(A i + A c ) × 100], where A i and A c are the n1–p1 amplitude on the ipsilesional and contralesional sides, respectively. We compared the oVEMP of the patient group with those of 26 age‐matched controls (median age = 53, IQR 40–58, p = 0.096). Abnormal oVEMP were defined as no responses to stimulation or asymmetric amplitudes (interauricular differences, IADs) > 20% [12, 13].

The SVV tilt was measured by seating the patient in a darkroom. Participants aligned a luminous rod (80 cm × 0.3 cm) to the perceived gravitational vertical using a joystick over five trials, each starting from a randomly offset position in the clockwise or counterclockwise direction. SVV was analysed as signed tilt, with clockwise deviations recorded as positive and counterclockwise deviations as negative; the mean of the five trials was used without additional outlier trimming. Testing was performed binocularly under darkroom conditions to minimise visual orientation cues. The normal value of SVV should be an average of ±3° in both eyes (a negative value indicates a counterclockwise rotation) [12, 13, 14].

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS (version 23.0; SPSS, Chicago, IL). Quantitative values are presented as the median (interquartile range, IQR, first—third quartile), while qualitative values are presented as frequency (%, percent), and mean ± standard deviation (SD). For the comparison of patients, we used the Mann–Whitney test or Wilcoxon test for unpaired or paired continuous variables, respectively, and Chi‐squared or Fisher's exact tests for nominal variables. Spearman's rank correlation method was employed to assess the correlations between variables. The level of significance was set at p < 0.05.

Results

Patients

Out of a total of 35 patients, 26 (median age = 64, IQR 54–70, range = 44–81; men = 12; lesion side, left = 14, Table 1) were ultimately enrolled in this study. Among the nine excluded patients, two were finally diagnosed with Meniere's disease, and seven were lost to follow‐up. All patients underwent immediate testing upon visitation, followed by a two‐day course of antihistamines. Subsequently, they were administered 80 mg/day of Ginkgo Biloba for about 1 month.

All 26 patients completed the 1‐week follow‐up evaluation (7 ± 3 days). By the 1‐month follow‐up, eight patients had been lost to follow‐up, and 18 completed the 1‐month evaluation (30 ± 5 days). No significant differences were found between 1‐month completers (n = 18) and non‐completers (n = 8) with respect to age, sex, lesion side, baseline vestibular measures, or baseline symptom/QoL scores (all p > 0.05; Table S1).

Initial Examination

The median time from symptoms onset to examination was 2 days (Table 1). The median HC gain in the affected side was 0.52. Abnormal SVV was observed in all the patients, while 92% (24/26) displayed abnormal oVEMP responses. The abnormal responses in both SVV and oVEMP were in the same direction as decreased VOR gain.

One Week Follow‐Up

One week later, 14 patients (54%) still had SVV values within the abnormal range, and 20 (77%) continued to exhibit asymmetric oVEMP (Table 1). However, both the SVV tilt and oVEMP IAD values showed significant improvement (p < 0.001 and 0.046, respectively, Table 1). The SVV tilt improved to a median of 7 (IQR, 2–11; 95% CI, 3–11), while the oVEMP IAD improved to a median of 11% (IQR −6 to 17; 95% CI, −17 to 4). Ipsilesional HC (iHC) VOR gain after 1 week did not differ from the initial examination (p = 0.444, Table 1). VAS, DHI and EQ‐5D‐5L scores all demonstrated significant improvements (p < 0.001, Table 1). The median values of each score showing a decrease compared to initial measurements were 4 (IQR, 3–7; 95% CI, 3–7) for VAS, 44 (IQR, 34–55; 95% CI, 36–54) for DHI and 8 (IQR 6–10; 95% CI, 6–10) for EQ‐5D‐5L.

In the group of patients whose SVV returned to normal (n = 12), there was a significant improvement in DHI (median DHI change of normal vs. abnormal SVV, 50 [IQR 44–58] vs. 34 [IQR 30–48], p = 0.046, Figure 1A). However, there were no significant differences in symptom scores between normal and abnormal groups for oVEMP or iHC VOR gain (Figure 1B,C).

The change in SVV tilt and the variation in DHI scores demonstrated a significant correlation 1 week later (p = 0.048, Spearman's rho = 0.392). No such correlation was found with the changes in VAS or EQ‐5D‐5L score (p = 0.762 and 0.169, respectively). The changes in oVEMP IAD or iHC gain did not show any correlation with the alterations in DHI, VAS, or EQ‐5D‐5L scores (p = 0.248, 0.428 and 0.389 for oVEMP IAD, p = 0.286, 0.138 and 0.508 for iHC VOR gain, respectively).

One Month Follow‐Up

Compared to the 1‐week results, the value of SVV tilt and iHC VOR gain significantly improved, while oVEMP asymmetry remained unchanged (Table 1). The median changes were 3° (IQR, 1–6; 95% CI, 1–6) for SVV tilt, 3% (IQR, −5 to 11; 95% CI, −5 to 10) for IAD and 0.13 (IQR, 0.04–0.38; 95% CI, 0.02–0.17) for iHC VOR gain. Subjective questionnaires did not show significant differences compared to the one‐week results (Table 1). Eight patients had persistent abnormal SVV values, and 13 had abnormal oVEMP IAD.

Significant improvement in DHI and EQ‐5D‐5L scores was observed in patients who continued to exhibit abnormal SVV tilt when compared to their initial evaluation (p = 0.012 and 0.035, respectively, Figure 1D). In contrast, the oVEMP symmetricities, whether normal or abnormal, did not significantly affect symptom score changes (Figure 1E). Patients in the group that demonstrated a return to normal HC gain exhibited a significant improvement in VAS and EQ‐5D‐5L score (p = 0.008, both) (Figure 1F).

No significant correlation was found between the changes in SVV tilt or oVEMP IAD from the first visit to 1 month and the alterations in DHI, VAS, or EQ‐5D‐5L scores (p values: 0.773, 0.750, 0.651 for SVV tilt; 0.404, 0.568, 0.312 for oVEMP IAD). However, the change in iHC VOR gain showed a significant correlation with the amount of improvement in VAS 1 month later (p = 0.028, Spearman's rho = 0.503). No such correlation was found with changes in DHI or EQ‐5D‐5L (p = 0.249 and 0.129, respectively).

Discussion

This study shows a clear relationship between static utricular function, measured through SVV and improvements in both dizziness severity and QoL in acute VN patients. In contrast, dynamic utricular function assessed by oVEMP did not show such an association. Patients who had normalised SVV after 1 week showed marked improvements in their subjective symptoms. By 1 month, these patients experienced greater improvements in QoL compared to those with persistent abnormal SVV values. Additionally, we found that the extent of SVV tilt angle improvement during the first week correlated significantly with symptom improvement measured by the DHI.

Previous research has indicated that vestibular functions such as SN, SVV tilt and ocular torsion generally recover within a month after VN onset [15]. In contrast, dynamic vestibular functions like the head impulse test (HIT) and vibration‐induced nystagmus typically take longer to improve [15]. These earlier findings suggest dynamic vestibular tests primarily indicate residual vestibular function [15].

However, our study demonstrates that static otolithic function, rather than dynamic, correlates more closely with early clinical symptoms. Notably, persistent abnormal SVV significantly impacts QoL even 1 month after symptom onset. Consistent with our findings, another study reported that patients still experiencing dizziness 1 month post‐onset had more frequently abnormal SVV than symptom‐free patients [16]. Interestingly, the correlation between SVV and dizziness symptoms was significant only at 1 week but not at 1 month, suggesting that symptom recovery due to central compensation becomes more prominent with time, potentially masking the relationship [17].

Why might only static utricular dysfunction correlate strongly with vestibular symptoms? One hypothesis is that when dynamic dysfunction occurs, the brain might initiate compensatory mechanisms earlier and more effectively. Dynamic vestibular functions, particularly those from semicircular canals, are responsible for gaze and postural stability during movement, a function directly tied to survival [18, 19]. As a result, even if dynamic vestibular function does not restore initially after damage and continues to show abnormalities in laboratory tests, symptomatic recovery might be fully possible due to functional adaptations [5].

Alternatively, the static otolithic pathway may serve as a signalling system more responsive to physiological stimuli. Situations that stimulate the otoliths to move like an accelerometer, causing displacement of hair cells as in the perception of gravity are likely to be more common in everyday life [20]. In contrast, stimuli where the otoconia remain stationary, akin to a seismometer and the hair cell body moves at non‐physiologic high speeds like loud sounds or vibration stimuli in VEMP [20], could be less frequent in usual. This difference may explain why static dysfunction aligns more closely with patient symptoms. This is opposite to the angular VOR system, which detects fast and high‐frequency head movements [19].

Research has traditionally focused on angular vestibular function after VN [21]. Similar to previous studies, our findings show that many patients do not fully recover to normal iHC VOR gain by 1 month [21]. However, even incomplete gain recovery correlates with symptom improvement in some patients. While prior studies indicated symptoms typically improve within a week due to neurological adaptation [17], the precise relationship between quantified VOR and subjective symptoms remains inconclusive, requiring further investigation.

This study has limitations, including the relatively small patient number and incomplete follow‐up at 1 month. However, the absence of patients with rapid symptom relief at follow‐up likely underestimates symptom improvement rather than overestimating it, preserving the reliability of our findings. Additionally, incorporating questionnaires specifically targeting otolithic symptoms, such as falls or balance sensations, could provide more detailed insights. Future studies might benefit from developing targeted symptom assessments to further clarify otolithic dysfunction's impact [19].

Conclusion

Our findings suggest that static utricular dysfunction is associated with early symptom burden in VN. Although the relationship may be influenced by central compensation over time, early abnormalities on static utricular testing appear to correlate with subjective recovery, supporting their potential clinical relevance in the assessment of acute vestibular disorders.
