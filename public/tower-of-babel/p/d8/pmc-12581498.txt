Military checkpoint exposure and predictive factors of quality of life among nurses during the 7th of October war in Palestine: a cross-sectional observational study
Authors: Fuad Farajalla
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDuring the 7th of October war in Palestine, nurses faced extreme stress from checkpoint delays, violence, and limited access to supportive healthcare systems.ObjectiveTo assess military checkpoint exposure and predictive factors of QoL among nurses during the 7th of October war in Palestine.MethodsA cross-sectional study was conducted in May 2025 among 350 nurses in Palestinian hospitals. A self-administered questionnaire collected sociodemographic data, war-related exposures, and WHOQOL-BREF scores. Data were analyzed using SPSS version 29, with multiple linear regression identifying predictors of QoL.ResultsMore than half of the nurses (52%) reported passing through military checkpoints to reach work, and 70.6% experienced violence, predominantly psychological. Poor QoL was most prevalent in the environmental (75.4%), psychological (60.3%), and physical (60%) domains. Passing through checkpoints (β = -0.261, p < .001), experiencing violence (β = -0.224, p < .001), and having a close relative injured or killed (β = -0.246, p < .001) were significant negative predictors, whereas higher education level was a positive predictor (β = 0.227, p < .001).ConclusionNurses’ QoL was severely compromised during the conflict, particularly in environmental and psychological domains. Checkpoint exposure, violence, and personal loss emerged as the strongest negative predictors, while higher education was associated with better QoL. Addressing these factors through institutional safety measures, targeted psychosocial support, and workforce protection policies is critical to sustaining nursing capacity in politically unstable and high-risk environments.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s13031-025-00721-w.

Introduction

War is an extreme form of violence that profoundly influences individuals and societies, with extremely detrimental influences in regions of political and military instability [1]. The health impacts of war extend beyond injury and death and include chronic effects such as exacerbated fear, stress, and worsening living conditions [2].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), from 7 October 2023 to 24 February 2025, a total of 739 attacks on health were recorded in the West Bank, resulting in 25 deaths. These incidents, together with ongoing movement restrictions, created significant barriers for ambulances and healthcare workers, impeding timely access to health services [3]. Médecins Sans Frontières (MSF)’s report outlines how heightened military checkpoints since October 2023 have caused regular delays for medical staff, during which personnel were subjected to harassment, ID confiscations, and violence. These conditions are responsible for poor patient outcomes and heightened stress among healthcare providers, especially nurses [4].

Violence in this context includes verbal abuse, psychological abuse, and physical abuse that people encounter regularly at Israeli military checkpoints and traveling to hospitals regularly. Quality of Life (QoL) defines individuals’ perceptions of their entire life, which includes: their physical health, mental health state, social support, daily activities, and environment [5].

Globally, nurses and other health professionals in countries like Saudi Arabia, Ukraine and Ethiopia [6–8] have been substantially impacted by the effects of war. In Ukraine, persistent traumatic stress in war zones has mediated moral distress and worsened nurses’ QoL [7]. In Ethiopia, siege and war are responsible for widespread conditions that negatively affected the delivery of healthcare [8]. Furthermore, in the Najran province of Saudi Arabia, nurses indicated significant fatigue and low sleep quality (65.9%) largely attributable to the physical and emotional weight of providing care for war casualties emerging from the Houthi-Saudi Arabia conflict [6]. Such circumstances compromise timely care provision and heighten stress, anxiety, and burnout, ultimately reducing QoL [7–11].

While these examples demonstrate the implications conflict has on health care workers, the Palestinian context presents a unique set of circumstances brought on by the continuous state of occupation and limited mobility [12]. Palestinian nurses work in an environment characterized by acute challenges including persistent violence, poverty, personal bereavement, and limited mental health availability [1, 11, 12]. The Palestinian healthcare system lacks the training and resources to respond meaningfully to these issues, exacerbating a complex humanitarian crisis with high violence and restricted medical access [2].

A recent study by the author focused on nursing students experiencing the same conflict and outlines the extent of the crisis. It shows that 63.3% of students regularly crossed military checkpoints, and 83% of them experienced violence. More importantly it appears that exposure to violence reduced QoL scores in students [12]. Given nursing students and practicing nurses experience similar pathways to exposure through commuting to hospitals or clinical placements, it suggests that nurses too are experiencing similar, if not more detrimental, effects on their QoL.

Nonetheless, little research has explicitly examined the combined effects of violence and military checkpoints on nurses’ QoL in southern West Bank during active conflict. This study seeks to assess military checkpoint exposure and predictive factors of QoL among nurses’ during the 7th of October war in Palestine, addressing a critical yet underexplored issue in the West Bank context, where nurses face unique stressors such as checkpoint delays and exposure to violence. The results provide useful information and insight into planning support programs and safety plans to improve the mental health, safety, and resilience of nurses working in conflict zones.

Methods

Research design

A cross-sectional observational design was used given its suitability in achieving the research objectives, as it enables the assessment of multiple variables and their associations at a single point in time [13]. It is particularly advantageous in conflict settings, as it allows rapid data collection from a large, diverse sample during an ongoing crisis.

Population and sampling

The target population consisted of nurses currently working in governmental and non-governmental hospitals in the southern West Bank, Palestine. This includes registered and practical nurses in these hospitals in Hebron and Bethlehem cities. Sample size was determined using General Power Analysis Software (G*Power) 3.1 software because it is appropriate for regression. A priori power analysis was conducted for multiple linear regression (Fixed model, R2 deviation from zero) for medium effect size (f2 = 0.15), significance level of 0.05, and power of 0.95. Eleven predictors were derived from the work environment and demographic variables: Gender, Age, Years of experience, Marital status, Level of Education, Working Hospital, Income, Being exposed to violence when crossing Israeli security checkpoints, Having a close family member injured or killed due to war, Passing through Israeli checkpoints when going to work, and Witnessing violence at these checkpoints. Applying these, the minimum sample size was estimated to be 178 participants. A convenience sampling method was used, and a total of 350 were recruited during May 2025.

Inclusion and exclusion criteria

In this research, the participants were nurses currently working in governmental and non-governmental hospitals in the southern West Bank, Palestine, and who were 20 years and older. The criteria for selection helped to ensure that all the participants were legally competent to give informed consent. Nurses with a known past history of psychiatric illness were excluded to isolate the effects of war-related stressors on QoL and minimize confounding variables. Such illnesses could interfere with the assessment of QoL by introducing pre-existing mental health variables.

Setting

This study was conducted in the southern West Bank of Palestine, which has numerous government and non-government hospitals.

Instrumentation

Data were collected using a self-administered questionnaire. The questionnaire consists of two sections. The first section dealt with sociodemographic and war-related variables and included questions about age, gender, marital status, level of education, working hospital, years of experience, and place of residence. This section also evaluated participants’ experiences with Israeli security checkpoints by questioning whether they pass through any security checkpoints to reach working hospitals, whether they have been subjected to any type of violence while passing through Israeli security checkpoints, the nature of the violence (if any), whether they or any of their close family members have been injured or lost (passed away) due to the war, and whether they witnessed any citizen experiencing any type of violence at security checkpoints.

The second part included the Arabic version of the World Health Organization Quality of Life-BREF (WHOQOL-BREF), a standardized instrument designed to assess QoL. The WHOQOL-BREF is composed of 26 items designed to measure QoL at an overall level (2 items) and across four main domains: environment (8 items), social (3 items), psychological (6 items), and physical (7 items). Each item is scored on a 5-point Likert scale, ranging from 1 (very poor/very dissatisfied/never/none) to 5 (very good/very satisfied/always/extremely). A single item addressing sex life within the social domain was omitted in the current study due to cultural sensitivity, making it a 25-item tool. According to the WHO guidelines, raw domain scores [4–20] were standardized on a 0–100 scale. Standardization allows comparison and interpretation across domains [14]. To classify QoL, a 60% cutoff was used for all domain scores on the 0–100 scale. A converted score of ≥ 60% represented good QoL, and scores < 60% represented poor QoL [14].

Reliability and validity

The validity of the WHOQOL-BREF has been tested in different Arab countries [15–17]. Importantly, the Arabic version has also been validated in the Palestinian context, demonstrating strong psychometric properties in a prior cohort study of Palestinian participants [18].

The whole questionnaire’s validity was evaluated through the content validity assessment. Three academic experts in public health, mental health, and nursing research reviewed the instrument to assess the relevance, clarity, and cultural appropriateness of the items. From their feedback, some questions in demographic and war related variables section of the questionnaire were revised. The revised version was reassessed and found to be valid for application in the study context. A pilot study was then carried out among 25 nurses who were excluded from the final sample. The pilot was designed to test the instrument for simplicity, understandability, and feasibility. Internal consistency reliability of the Arabic version of the WHOQOL-BREF, culturally adapted to 25 items, was assessed by Cronbach’s alpha coefficient. Total internal consistency was high, with Cronbach’s alpha = 0.89.

Data collection process

Data were collected in May 2025 following approval from the Institutional Review Board (IRB) at Palestine Polytechnic University and the participating hospitals. The nurses were informed about the study’s purpose and their voluntary right to withdraw from participation at any time. Those who agreed signed an informed consent form and responded to the self-administered questionnaire in Arabic. To ensure no responses were lost, questionnaires were collected daily after completion by the research team. A total of 400 questionnaires were distributed and 350 completed questionnaires were returned, resulting in a response rate of 87.5%.

Data analysis

Data analysis was conducted using IBM SPSS Statistics version 29. Descriptive statistics were utilized to summarize sociodemographic characteristics, exposure to violence and WHOQOL-BREF domain scores. Raw WHOQOL-BREF domain scores were transformed to a 0–100 scale. QoL was classified using a 60% cutoff, with scores ≥ 60% indicating good QoL and scores < 60% indicating poor QoL [14]. A multiple linear regression analysis was performed to identify the key predictors. One-way ANOVA was used to compare differences in mean QoL scores based on place of residence. A p-value of less than 0.05 was considered statistically significant across analyses. Prior to the regression analysis, assumptions were evaluated. Linearity was assessed through scatterplots, homoscedasticity was assessed with the Breusch-Pagan test, and the absence of multicollinearity was established using Variance Inflation Factors (VIF) scores below 5. Given the large sample size (n = 350), normality of residuals was assumed, and robust standard errors appropriately addressed any minor deviations to ensure result reliability.

Ethical consideration

Ethical approval for the conduct of the study was obtained from the Ethical Committee of the College of Nursing, Palestine Polytechnic University, Approval no. EA/2025/51. Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their enrollment in the research. Rapport was built with the participants, and they were informed that their participation was voluntary. Confidentiality was maintained. Additionally, participants were assured that all personal information would be protected, secured, and kept confidential. The research adhered to the ethical standards outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki regarding the rights, protection, and well-being of research participants.

Results

The study included 350 nurses working in Palestinian hospitals during the October 7, 2023, war. The mean age was 30.2 years (SD ± 7.45). Females (52.9%, n = 185) were nearly equal to males (47.1%, n = 165). Most had over 10 years of experience. Regarding marital status, 62.9% (n = 220) were married. Most nurses (67.1%) lived in villages. Educationally, 56.9% held a bachelor’s degree. Income-wise, 48.6% had an income of > 3,000 NIS. Most nurses (67.1%) worked in government hospitals. Regarding checkpoint exposure, 52.0% (n = 182) reported passing through Israeli security checkpoints to reach their hospitals. Of these, 70.6% (n = 247) reported exposure to violence, primarily psychological (47.4%), followed by verbal (34.8%), and physical (17.8%). Participants selected the primary type of violence experienced, choosing only one category, making the percentages mutually exclusive among the 247 participants. Additionally, 80.9% (n = 283) reported witnessing violence toward others at checkpoints. Furthermore, 19.1% (n = 67) had a close relative injured or killed due to the war. A multiple linear regression analysis was conducted to identify predictors of QoL among nurses. The overall model was statistically significant, F(10, 339) = 18.124, p < 0.001, explaining approximately 34.8% of the variance in QoL scores (R2 = 0.348, Adjusted R2 = 0.329).

Among the demographic predictors, being male was associated with significantly better QoL (p = 0.010), indicating that male nurses reported higher QoL than female nurses. Likewise, level of education was positively related to QoL (p < 0.001), suggesting that nurses with higher educational attainment had significantly better QoL. Income was marginally significant (p = 0.055), indicating a potential trend toward higher income being associated with better QoL, which warrant further investigation.

Several conflict-related variables were negatively associated with QoL. Nurses who had been exposed to violence while crossing Israeli checkpoints had, on average, a 0.271-point lower QoL score (B = −0.271, p < 0.001). Similarly, those who had a close family member injured or killed due to the war scored 0.346 points lower on QoL (B = −0.346, p < 0.001), and those who regularly passed through Israeli checkpoints on their way to work scored 0.289 points lower (B = −0.289, p < 0.001). Other variables, including age, years of experience, marital status, working hospital, and witnessing violence at checkpoints, were not statistically significant predictors of QoL in this model. Discussion

This study examined nurses’ QoL during the October 7, 2023, war in Palestine. QoL was most impaired in the environmental, psychological, and physical domains, while social support was relatively preserved. Checkpoint exposure, violence, and personal loss were strong negative predictors, whereas higher education and male gender were linked with better QoL. Overall, structural and war-related stressors outweighed sociodemographic factors in shaping nurses’ well-being.

Violence exposure, health consequences, and social support

70.6% were victims of direct violence and 80.9% were witnesses to violence against others. In agreement with each of this study findings, Zerihun et al.[8] reported that healthcare workers in Ethiopia experienced conflict-related violence, including at checkpoints. Similarly, Farajalla et al. [12] reported that 83% of nursing students passing through checkpoints experienced violence and 92% had witnessed violence against others. These consistent results between professional nurses and nursing students suggest that experiencing checkpoint-related violence is not an isolated event, but rather a systemic and live experience in the Palestinian context.

Environmental QoL was the most affected domain in the current study, with three quarters of nurses scoring poorly. Their mean score was lower than the one reported among healthcare workers in Gaza by Younes et al. [11], reflecting severe hardships in safety, mobility, and access to resources. These environmental challenges are further compounded by systemic barriers in health care settings, where personnel stress and lack of team harmony (88%) were identified as significant obstacles to effective care delivery [19].

Findings from Farajalla et al. [12] corroborate this, as most nursing students scored poorly in the environmental domain (mean 48.2), underscoring that environmental strain is a consistent and cross-population consequence of wartime disruptions.

Psychological well-being was also significantly impacted, as 60.3% of the nurses experienced poor psychological QoL. This is also corroborated by Ramadan Elrefaey et al.[6], who indicated that 65.9% of the participants experienced poor sleep and that involvement in war trauma care was significantly related to fatigue and psychological stress. Nursing students showed a parallel pattern in Farajalla et al. [12], where 56.4% reported poor psychological QoL, confirming that both professionals and students experience significant psychological strain in wartime.

The physical health domain also showed a concerning result, 60% of the participant manifesting poor QoL. These findings are similar to those of the Ramallah Medical Complex qualitative study of Abu Abed & Etewa [20], in which nurses reported physical exhaustion and repetitive delays at checkpoint. Nursing students in the Farajalla et al. [12] study also showed notable impairment, with 55.5% reporting poor physical QoL,

The social domain had the highest scores in QoL domains, where most nurses had good QoL. This finding may be explained by robust social cohesion and peer support during times of crisis as reported by Abu Abed & Etewa [20]. Farajalla et al. [12] similarly found 68.4% of students scored well in the social domain (mean 63.5), reinforcing that social connectedness is a critical buffer against conflict-related distress. In the Palestinian context, strong social cohesion and community solidarity serve as critical resilience factors, enabling individuals and healthcare workers to cope more effectively with disruptions, such as checkpoint delays and attacks on healthcare facilities. Understanding these factors enriches the interpretation of our results and suggests avenues for culturally sensitive interventions to bolster resilience.

Predictors of QoL in conflict settings

Checkpoint-related violence was a significant predictor for poor Qol. This is supported by Younis et al.[11] who reported that checkpoints, through delays and threats contribute to emotional distress and burnout among health care providers. Similarly, Farajalla et al. [12] found that nursing students exposed to checkpoint violence had significantly lower QoL scores (M = 2.96) compared to their non-exposed peers (M = 3.81, p < 0.001). Likewise, the negative effect of having a relative killed or injured due to the war reflects the deep psychological and emotional toll that personal loss exerts on nurses’ QoL. Abu Abed & Etewa [20] similarly reported that such personal losses intensified feelings of helplessness, family disruption, and grief among nurses.

Furthermore, passing through checkpoints for work was another significant predictors of low QoL in our study. As supported by Zerihun et al. [8], nurses reported prolonged working hours, psychological humiliation, and threat to personal and family safety. Farajalla et al. [12] reinforce this, showing that students regularly passing checkpoints had significantly lower QoL (M = 3.11) compared to those who did not (M = 3.33, p < 0.001), confirming the cumulative toll of restricted mobility across nursing populations. Education level was positively and significantly related to QoL, indicating that higher education level might exert protective effects. This result is consistent with Younes et al. [11] study finding. This suggests that education might strengthen nurses’ coping strategies and resources, ultimately increasing their resilience in the face of crises.

Gender was also a significant predictor, with women reporting lower QoL. This finding is consistent with previous studies of Harbuzova et al. [21] and Amro [9], which note that women healthcare workers report more symptoms of PTSD, burnout, and compassion fatigue, particularly in conflict areas. Gender based difference in coping style, role, and social expectations can be the cause of this disparity. In Farajalla et al. [12], gender was not a significant predictor among students, suggesting that while gender disparities are evident among practicing nurses, the acute conflict environment may have overshadowed such differences in the student population.

The other variables (age, marital status, years of experience, and working hospital type) were not significant predictors of QoL in this model. These findings are in line with Qtait et al. [2], Farajalla et al. [12] and Younes et al.[11]. This indicates that structural and war-related stressors can overshadow demographic effects in times of acute conflict.

Place of residence had a significant impact on QoL, with nurses residing in cities reporting higher QoL mean than those living in villages or campuses. Qtait et al. [2] and Bdier et al. [10] both described how nurses working in rural areas, face more delays, less support systems, and more security risks, which can undermine overall well-being.

Farajalla et al. [12] confirmed this pattern among nursing students, where city residents had significantly higher QoL compared to village and campus residents. Together, these findings highlight that residence location consistently influences access to resources and resilience.

Strengths and limitations

This study targeted nurses at a period of ongoing war. It is grounded on robust research methodology. Including large sample size and utilization of validated tools (WHOQOL-BREF) scale to assess QoL. The real-time information that was produced during the war provides an insight into the direct effect of military checkpoint and violence on nurses in war-torn areas, making significant contributions to the existing literature on the subject.

However, several limitations exist for this study. As with all cross-sectional designs, causality cannot be ascertained since exposures and outcomes were measured concurrently, precluding confirmation of temporality. For instance, while checkpoint exposure may reduce QoL, it is also possible that nurses with poorer QoL find checkpoint stress more stressful. Thus, findings should be interpreted as associations, rather than causal effects. Convenience sampling is a further potential source of bias. Sampling on the basis of accessibility (as opposed to random sampling) could overrepresent certain groups (e.g., urban vs. rural nurses), undermining generalizability and possibly affecting prevalence estimates and observed associations. Self-administered questionnaires enhanced feasibility but may have constrained the richness of responses. Furthermore, the "sex life" item of the social relationships domain of the WHOQOL-BREF was omitted on cultural grounds. While appropriate in this context, this modification may have a small effect on domain scores and limit direct comparability with studies using the full scale.

Implications and recommendations

The findings carry important implications for healthcare institutions and policymakers in conflict-affected zones such as Palestine. While organizations are adopting trauma-informed policies, they also need practical operational responses. For instance, the Ministry of Health could negotiate checkpoint-free transport corridors or priority lanes for healthcare workers to reduce delays and minimize exposure to harassment. Flexibility around work arrangements for nurses having significant travel time that includes multiple checkpoints may include administrative work remote from the hospital or negotiating shift changes at the beginning or end of shifts to leave or arrive by taxi to avoid delays at checkpoints. Structural and organization changes to provide healthcare workers with mandated leave for mental health reasons, or priority access to mental health services would lessen the likelihood of further psychological damage for those experiencing loss. Offering peer-support activities led by a trained mental health professional in the workplace should be built into employee work schedule so the healthcare worker does not feel the burden of additional work.

Given the pervasive exposure to violence, military checkpoint delays, and chronic psychological stress, a coordinated national and institutional response is warranted. Responsive operational planning should include safe passage assistance during escalations of hostilities, trauma-informed debriefings, formal peer-support groups, and readily available psychological services. Such measures address not only clinical enablement but also the emotional resilience needed to withstand ongoing adversity.

Conclusion

This study provides evidence that the ongoing conflict and checkpoint-related disruptions are closely associated with diminished QoL among nurses in the West Bank. What directed the findings across the physical, psychological, and environmental domains was not demographic characteristics, but exposure to structural and war-related stressors. While education and social support appeared to buffer some of these effects, the findings underscore that the professional and personal lives of nurses are inseparable from the broader context of instability in which they work. Dealing with these realities requires more than the availability of coping strategies and individual approaches; it requires system-wide considerations of workplace safety, mobility, and psychosocial health. Future research could build upon these findings by uncovering longitudinal patterns of recovery and resilience, and by evaluating interventions designed for healthcare workers in conflict zones.
