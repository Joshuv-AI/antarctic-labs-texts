Local Sodium Tanshinone IIA Sulfonate Attenuates Periodontal Bone Loss by Modulating Inflammatory, Immune and Osteoclastogenic Signalling
Authors: Rafael Scaf de Molon, Raquel Mantuaneli Scarel‐Caminaga, François Isnaldo Dias Caldeira, Edilson Ervolino, Erica Dorigatti de Avila, Paula Aboud Barbugli, Gabriela Ezequiel Oliveira, Joao Pedro Franco‐Moura, Leticia Helena Theodoro, Joni Augusto Cirelli, Sotirios Tetradis, Alpdogan Kantarci
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAimTo investigate whether local administration of sodium tanshinone IIA sulfonate (STS) modulates periodontal inflammation, alveolar bone loss, immune response and bone remodelling–related signalling pathways in a ligature‐induced murine model of periodontitis.Materials and MethodsSixty C57BL/6J male mice were randomly assigned to four groups: control group (C), periodontitis (P), low‐dose (1 mg/kg) tanshinone (STS1) and high‐dose (5 mg/kg) tanshinone treatment (STS5). Experimental periodontitis was induced by bilateral placement of ligatures around the first maxillary molars in the P, STS1 and STS5 groups. STS or vehicle was administered locally to the gingival tissues 2 days before disease induction and continued until the end of the experiment.ResultsSTS significantly attenuated periodontal bone loss, preserved bone microarchitecture, reduced inflammatory infiltrate and maintained collagen organisation. Mechanistically, STS suppressed RANKL‐dependent osteoclastogenesis by down‐regulating RANKL, CTSK, MMP‐9, OSCAR and NFATc1 mRNA expression while restoring the RANKL/OPG balance, thereby limiting osteoclast differentiation and activity. In parallel, STS attenuated inflammatory signalling by inhibiting NF‐κB pathway activation, as reflected by reduced NF‐κB protein levels, stabilisation of its inhibitory regulator IκBα as well as suppression of IL‐6, TNF‐α and TGF‐β gene expression, followed by a trend towards reduced IFN‐γ gene levels. STS further modulated macrophage polarisation by reducing M1‐like macrophages, as shown by decreased F4/80+CD80+ signal, while CD206 immunoreactivity increased. At the protein level, STS reduced TRAP, CTSK and NFATc1 expression while restoring RUNX2.ConclusionSTS effectively suppresses periodontal inflammation and alveolar bone destruction by modulating inflammatory signalling and attenuating M1‐like macrophage polarisation, inhibiting osteoclastogenesis and partially restoring osteogenic pathways.Keywords: adjunctive therapies, aetiology, alveolar bone, osteoblast, osteoclast, pathogenesis, periodontitis, signalling pathways, tanshinone

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease affecting the tooth‐supporting tissues and remains a leading cause of tooth loss worldwide (Kinane et al. 2017). Severe forms affect approximately 10% of adults and are more prevalent in aged individuals and those with diabetes, chronic inflammatory diseases and immune dysregulation (Chen et al. 2021; Kassebaum et al. 2014; Nascimento et al. 2024; Steffens et al. 2026; Hajishengallis 2022; Hajishengallis and Chavakis 2021; Villoria et al. 2024; de Molon et al. 2019). Although initiated by a dysbiotic biofilm, periodontitis is now recognised as a host‐mediated disease in which dysregulated and non‐resolving inflammation drives connective tissue destruction and alveolar bone loss (Kinane et al. 2017; Baima et al. 2025; de Molon, Vernal, et al. 2026; Hajishengallis et al. 2020; Van Dyke et al. 2025; Van Dyke and Sima 2020). Consequently, host‐modulatory therapies have gained interest as adjunctive approaches to conventional mechanical treatment (de Molon et al. 2022; de Molon, Steffens, et al. 2026). Among these strategies, natural compounds have emerged as promising candidates because they suppress destructive inflammation while preserving protective immune responses (de Molon, Vernal, et al. 2026; Elkhouli 2011; Golub et al. 2024; Novak et al. 2008; Sulijaya et al. 2019; Anwar et al. 2025; Hajishengallis and Lamont 2014; Oliveira et al. 2024; Pavanelli et al. 2022; de Molon 2025; Fernandes et al. 2021; de Molon, Rodrigues, et al. 2026; Lucchesi et al. 2025; Nikniaz et al. 2023; Zhang et al. 2022; Zhu et al. 2025). Tanshinone IIA, a bioactive diterpene from Salvia miltiorrhiza , is known to exhibit anti‐inflammatory, antioxidant and anti‐resorptive properties (de Molon 2025; Ekeuku et al. 2021; Hu et al. 2025; Sudha et al. 2025). Its water‐soluble derivative, sodium tanshinone IIA sulfonate (STS), has demonstrated beneficial effects in inflammatory bone disorders through modulation of NF‐κB, MAPK, PI3K/Akt and Wnt/β‐catenin pathways (Panwar et al. 2024; Panwar et al. 2018; Panwar et al. 2016; Panwar et al. 2017; Pavanelli et al. 2025; Li et al. 2024; Ma et al. 2024). However, its effects on localised periodontal inflammation remain poorly understood. Therefore, we investigated whether locally delivered STS prevents periodontal inflammation and alveolar bone loss in ligature‐induced periodontitis through modulation of inflammatory, immune and bone‐related pathways.

Materials and Methods

A detailed description of the experimental procedures performed in this study is provided in Supporting Information. Experiments were conducted in compliance with the ARRIVE (du Sert et al. 2020) guidelines and were approved by the Institutional Ethical Committee (#0549/2022).

Study Design and Experimental Groups

Sixty 8‐week‐old male C57BL/6J mice were used in a ligature‐induced model of periodontitis (de Molon et al. 2018; Bertolini et al. 2022), and randomly allocated to four groups: control (C), periodontitis (P), low‐dose sodium tanshinone IIA sulfonate (STS; 1 mg/kg) and high‐dose STS (5 mg/kg). STS or vehicle was administered locally to the palatal gingival tissues starting 2 days before ligature placement and continued for 2 weeks, as a preventive model (Figure 1A).

Periodontitis Induction

Ligatures were placed around the maxillary first molars bilaterally under general anaesthesia, as described elsewhere (Pavanelli et al. 2025; Silva et al. 2025).

STS Administration

Sodium tanshinone II‐A sulfonate (SML2517, Sigma–Aldrich) was diluted in distilled water immediately before use and was administered locally by daily gingival injection around the maxillary first molars at the palatal side under inhalation anaesthesia.

Animal Euthanasia and Sample Collection

Animals were euthanised at the end of the experimental period, and maxillae and gingival tissues were harvested.

Micro‐Tomographic Analysis (Micro‐CT)

Bone samples were scanned using a high‐resolution micro‐computed tomography system (μCT; Skyscan, Bruker). Scanning parameters were selected based on previously published studies (Pavanelli et al. 2025; Da Ponte Leguizamon et al. 2022). Linear, volumetric and trabecular parameters as well as bone mineral density was quantified (de Molon et al. 2014; de Molon et al. 2016; Macari et al. 2018).

Histological Analysis

Quantitative and qualitative analyses of the inflammatory process were performed on H&E‐stained sections to quantify the relative volume density of tissue components (Pavanelli et al. 2025; Da Ponte Leguizamon et al. 2022; Nogueira et al. 2017; Nogueira et al. 2014; Souza et al. 2020).

Picrosirius Red Staining

Collagen fibre organisation was evaluated using Picrosirius Red staining. Sections were analysed under polarised light microscopy.

Immunohistochemical Analysis

Immunohistochemical analysis was performed to evaluate the expression levels of anti‐RUNX2 (ab192256), anti‐NFAT2 (ab2796), anti‐NF‐κB (ab32536), anti‐cathepsin K (ab187647) and anti‐TRAP (ab65854) (Pereira et al. 2026).

Immunofluorescence Analysis

Macrophage polarisation in periodontal tissues was evaluated. Sections were incubated with antibodies against F4/80+CD80+ (M1‐like macrophages) and F4/80+CD206+(M2‐like macrophages).

Real‐Time Quantitative Polymerase Chain Reaction

RNA was extracted from gingival and bone tissues using TRIzol, quantified spectrophotometrically and reverse‐transcribed. Real‐time quantitative polymerase chain reaction (RT‐qPCR) was performed with TaqMan assays to evaluate genes related to osteoclastogenesis, inflammation and immune regulation (Table S1).

Western Blot

Total protein was recovered after RNA extraction, solubilised in SDS buffer and quantified by BCA assay; and equal amounts (30 μg) were separated by SDS‐PAGE, transferred to nitrocellulose membranes and incubated overnight with primary antibodies against anti‐NF‐κB p65 (ab32536), anti‐IκBα (ab32518) and anti‐β‐Actin (ab8226). Proteins were quantified by densitometry.

Statistical Analysis

Analyses were performed in a blinded manner. Statistical comparisons were conducted using parametric or nonparametric tests as appropriate, with significance set at p < 0.05. Sample size calculation was performed using G*Power (Pavanelli et al. 2025; Da Ponte Leguizamon et al. 2022).

Results

Local STS Treatment Preserves Alveolar Bone Height and Bone Integrity

Ligature‐induced periodontitis resulted in a consistent and reproducible pattern of alveolar bone destruction, as demonstrated across complementary imaging and histomorphometric assessments (Figure 1B–I). Two‐dimensional coronal images showed increased cemento‐enamel junction (CEJ) to alveolar bone crust (ABC) distance in the P group compared with STS‐treated groups. The C group showed the lowest CEJ–ABC distance compared to all the other groups (Figure 1B). Quantitative micro‐CT analysis revealed a pronounced increase in the CEJ–ABC distance in the P group compared with healthy controls, confirming substantial loss of alveolar bone height. This increase was evident in both buccal (Figure 1C) and palatal (Figure 1D) regions, indicating that ligature placement triggered generalised periodontal breakdown. STS significantly mitigated this bone loss. Both STS1 and STS5 groups exhibited markedly reduced CEJ–ABC distances compared with untreated periodontitis animals, demonstrating effective preservation of the alveolar bone height (Figure 1C,D). The magnitude of protection was consistently greater in the palatal region, corresponding to the site of drug application, supporting a strong local therapeutic effect (Figure 1C,D). These findings were independently validated by histomorphometric measurements performed on decalcified sections, which revealed the same directional changes in CEJ–ABC distances across groups (Figure 1E). Histological evaluation further corroborated these findings, showing the preservation of alveolar bone height and periodontal architecture in STS‐treated animals compared to those in the P group (Figure 1F–I).

STS Preserves Bone Volume, Bone Mineral Density and Trabecular Microarchitecture

Beyond linear bone loss, ligature‐induced periodontitis markedly impaired bone volume, bone mineral density (BMD) and trabecular architecture (Figure 2A–F). The P group exhibited significant reductions in BV and BV/TV, indicating extensive loss of mineralised tissue (Figure 2A,B), along with decreased BMD, reflecting disease‐associated demineralisation (Figure 2C). STS treatment significantly counteracted these changes, increasing BV/TV and BMD compared with the P group, although the values did not fully return to control levels. Trabecular microarchitectural analysis further showed disease‐associated structural deterioration. Tb.Th and Tb.N were significantly reduced in the P group, while Tb.Sp was markedly increased, consistent with trabecular thinning and rarefaction (Figure 2D–F). STS treatment significantly improved these parameters at both doses, increasing Tb.Th and Tb.N and reducing Tb.Sp, indicating preservation of trabecular connectivity and bone quality. These quantitative improvements were corroborated by two‐ and three‐dimensional micro‐CT images, which showed better preservation of alveolar bone morphology and trabecular organisation in STS‐treated mice compared with those in the P group (Figure 2G).

STS Attenuates Periodontal Inflammation and Gingival Tissue Architecture

Structural preservation of the alveolar bone was accompanied by marked improvements in periodontal soft‐tissue integrity (Figure 3A). Histological examination of control specimens revealed intact gingival architecture, a junctional epithelium positioned close to the CEJ, dense collagen‐rich connective tissue and minimal inflammatory infiltrate (Figure 3A). In contrast, ligature‐induced periodontitis resulted in extensive inflammatory cell accumulation, epithelial disorganisation, connective tissue degradation and pronounced vascular changes, collectively reflecting an aggressive and acute inflammatory response. STS treatment substantially ameliorated these pathological features. Gingival tissues from STS‐treated groups showed reduced inflammatory infiltrates, preservation of epithelial continuity and improved connective tissue organisation. These qualitative observations were reinforced by stereometric analysis, including analysis of the proportion of inflammatory cells (Figure 3B), fibroblasts (Figure 3C), blood vessels (Figure 3D), extracellular matrix (Figure 3E) and other structural components (Figure 3F). We found a significant reduction in the proportion of inflammatory cells and blood vessels in STS‐treated tissues compared with those of the P group. In parallel, fibroblast density, which was markedly reduced in periodontitis, was restored following STS treatment, indicating stabilisation of the cellular framework responsible for extracellular matrix maintenance and repair. The preservation of fibroblast populations and reduction of inflammatory burden provide a cellular basis for the structural integrity observed in both soft and hard periodontal tissues. No statistically significant differences were observed among groups in the proportions of extracellular matrix and other structural components.

STS Preserves Collagen Content and Organisation Within the Periodontal Connective Tissue

Consistent with the histological and stereometric findings, Picrosirius Red staining revealed profound disease‐related alterations in collagen deposition and organisation (Figure 3G,H). Periodontitis induced a marked reduction in collagen staining intensity, accompanied by fibre fragmentation, disorganisation and diminished birefringence under polarised light, indicative of degradation of mature collagen fibres and compromised matrix integrity. STS treatment effectively preserved the collagen network. Both STS groups showed increased collagen density and improved fibre organisation compared with P group (Figure 3G). Under polarised light, collagen fibres in STS‐treated tissues exhibited enhanced birefringence, reflecting preservation of mature, structurally organised collagen. The higher dose produced collagen patterns closely resembling those observed in healthy controls, suggesting a dose‐responsive effect on connective tissue stabilisation (Figure 3H). These findings align with the observed reductions in inflammatory infiltrates and restoration of fibroblast populations, indicating that STS supports maintenance of the extracellular matrix by limiting inflammation‐driven collagen degradation.

STS Suppresses Osteoclastogenesis and Reprogrammes Bone Remodelling Signalling

The preservation of alveolar bone architecture observed following local STS treatment was tightly linked to a coordinated suppression of osteoclast differentiation and resorptive activity within the alveolar bone (Figure 4). Osteoclast activity was first evaluated at the protein level using established immunohistochemical markers. In the P group, intense TRAP staining (Figure 4A) and robust cathepsin K (CTSK) immunoreactivity (Figure 4B) were detected along resorbing alveolar bone surfaces, confirming the establishment of a strongly osteoclastogenic and bone‐destructive microenvironment. In contrast, both STS‐treated groups exhibited a marked reduction in TRAP‐positive multi‐nucleated osteoclasts, accompanied by substantially diminished CTSK expression (Figure 4A,B). These qualitative observations were supported by quantitative protein analyses (Figure 4C,D), which showed significant down‐regulation of osteoclast‐related markers (TRAP and CTSK) in STS‐treated animals compared with those in the P group.

To determine whether these protein‐level changes were accompanied by coordinated transcriptional reprogramming, gene‐expression analyses were performed on RNA extracted from gingival and bone tissues. Ligature‐induced periodontitis resulted in pronounced up‐regulation of RANKL expression (Figure 4E) and a concomitant increase in the RANKL/OPG ratio, reflecting strong osteoclastogenic signalling. Local STS treatment significantly suppressed RANKL transcription while simultaneously up‐regulating OPG expression (Figure 4F), leading to robust normalisation of the RANKL/OPG ratio (Figure 4G) to levels comparable to, or lower than, those observed in healthy controls. Notably, RANK mRNA levels remained unchanged across all experimental groups (Figure 4H), indicating that STS does not alter osteoclast precursor availability but rather modulates osteoclastogenic signalling downstream of receptor expression. Consistent with the protein‐level findings, transcriptional analysis of CTSK further supported a direct inhibitory effect of STS on osteoclast‐mediated bone resorption. Ligature‐induced periodontitis significantly up‐regulated CTSK mRNA expression in the P group compared with controls, reflecting enhanced osteoclast activation and matrix degradation within inflamed alveolar bone (Figure 4I). Local STS treatment at both dosages significantly down‐regulated CTSK expression, restoring transcript levels to those observed in healthy animals.

Further mechanistic insight into osteoclast differentiation and bone matrix degradation pathways was provided by analysis of key intracellular regulators within the alveolar bone (Figure 5A–E). mRNA expression of NFATc1—the master transcription factor governing osteoclast differentiation and activation—was markedly elevated in the P group and significantly suppressed following STS treatment (Figure 5A). A similar expression pattern was observed for OSCAR (Figure 5B)—an immunoreceptor that amplifies RANKL‐dependent osteoclastogenesis through ITAM‐mediated co‐stimulatory signalling—indicating enhanced osteoclast commitment during experimental periodontitis. STS treatment, independently of the dosage, significantly down‐regulated the expression level of OSCAR, similar to the levels of the C group. In parallel, MMP‐9 (Figure 5C) was robustly up‐regulated in the P group compared with controls, reflecting an aggressive proteolytic and bone‐destructive microenvironment. Importantly, both STS dosages significantly reduced MMP‐9 expression relative to the P group, restoring transcript levels towards those observed in healthy tissues (Figure 5C). These findings align with the reduced osteoclastic activity observed histologically. Importantly, STS treatment resulted in a marked and significant down‐regulation of NFATc1 protein levels, restoring its expression to levels comparable to those observed in the C group (Figure 5D,E). The coordinated down‐regulation of NFATc1, OSCAR, MMP‐9 and CTSK in STS‐treated groups indicates that STS interferes not only with upstream RANKL signalling but also with downstream transcriptional, co‐receptor and proteolytic programmes essential for osteoclast maturation, activation and bone matrix degradation.

STS Modulates Pro‐Inflammatory, Immunoregulatory and Metabolic Cytokine Signalling

In parallel with its anti‐resorptive actions, STS exerted a significant modulatory effect on inflammatory signalling in periodontal tissues (Figure 5F–L). Analysis of IL‐6 mRNA expression (Figure 5F) revealed a significant up‐regulation in the P group compared with healthy controls, confirming robust activation of pro‐inflammatory signalling. Local treatment with STS resulted in a marked suppression of IL‐6 expression at both tested dosages compared with the P group, reaching values close to those observed in the C group, effectively attenuating IL‐6 transcription. TNF‐α expression was increased in the P group relative to healthy controls, consistent with activation of pro‐inflammatory signalling during experimental periodontitis (Figure 5G). Treatment with STS, particularly at the lower dosage, resulted in significant down‐regulation of TNF‐α expression compared with the P group; however, although reduced, TNF‐α levels in the STS5 group did not reach statistical significance (Figure 5G). The lack of a clear dose‐dependent effect for TNF‐α may reflect biological variability or differential regulation of cytokine responses at higher local concentrations. Similarly, IFN‐γ expression (Figure 5H) was significantly elevated in the P group compared to the C group, and a trend towards reduction was noted in both STS‐treated groups. IL‐1β expression was significantly up‐regulated in the P group compared with controls, reflecting robust activation of pro‐inflammatory signalling. However, local STS treatment at either dosage did not significantly modulate IL‐1β expression (Figure 5I).

Expression of TGF‐β (Figure 5J) was significantly up‐regulated in the P group compared with controls, likely reflecting an attempt to counterbalance sustained inflammation and tissue destruction. Interestingly, STS treatment at both dosages significantly down‐regulated TGF‐β expression, suggesting that resolution of inflammatory burden and normalisation of bone remodelling signalling reduced the need for compensatory TGF‐β activation (Figure 5J). Analysis of IL‐10 (Figure 5K) demonstrated a biologically slight response to STS treatment. While IL‐10 expression was not significantly altered in the P group relative to controls, animals treated with STS1 exhibited significantly increased IL‐10 expression compared with those in the C group. This trend suggests that lower dose STS may promote a shift towards an anti‐inflammatory or regulatory transcriptional profile. Finally, ARG2 expression (Figure 5L)—a mitochondrial arginase implicated in immune cell metabolism and macrophage polarisation—was highly elevated in the P group compared with all other groups. STS treatment led to significant down‐regulation of ARG2 expression, indicating that STS may influence metabolic and immunoregulatory pathways associated with chronic inflammation (Figure 5L). These findings indicate that STS does not uniformly suppress all inflammatory cytokines but instead selectively modulates key immunoregulatory pathways, especially IL‐6 and TNF‐α.

Immunofluorescence analysis further demonstrated marked alterations in macrophage polarisation in periodontal tissues. Double immunofluorescence staining using F4/80 and CD80 was employed to identify M1‐like macrophages, whereas F4/80 and CD206 were used to evaluate M2‐like macrophages (Figure 6A–L). Representative confocal images revealed an increased accumulation of CD80+ and F4/80+CD80+ cells in the periodontal tissues of the P group compared with the C group, indicating enhanced M1 macrophage polarisation under inflammatory conditions (Figure 6A–F). Quantitative analysis confirmed a significant increase in CD80 fluorescence intensity in the P group (Figure 6E). Treatment with STS significantly reduced CD80 expression in both groups, which exhibited lower fluorescence intensity than the P group. Similarly, the proportion of F4/80+CD80+ cells was significantly reduced following STS treatment compared with the P group (Figure 6F). In contrast, CD206 immunoreactivity was low in the P group and significantly increased after STS5 treatment, whereas STS1 showed a non‐significant trend (Figure 6G–K). However, F4/80+CD206+ double‐positive signal was not detected in the treated groups (Figure 6L).

STS Restores Osteogenic Signalling in Alveolar Bone by Preserving RUNX2 Expression

Interestingly, RUNX2 protein expression was evaluated to assess the effects of STS on osteogenic activity (Figure 6M,N). RUNX2—a master transcription factor governing osteoblast differentiation and bone matrix formation—exhibited strong nuclear immunoreactivity in alveolar bone cells of the C group, consistent with physiological bone remodelling (Figure 6M,N). In contrast, ligature‐induced periodontitis resulted in a marked reduction in RUNX2‐positive cells, indicating suppression of osteogenic signalling in the context of sustained inflammation and active bone resorption. Local treatment with STS significantly restored RUNX2 expression in alveolar bone. Quantitative analysis (Figure 6N) showed a robust increase in RUNX2‐positive cells per area in both STS‐treated groups compared with the P group, reaching levels comparable to those observed in healthy controls. Notably, both dosages of STS were effective in rescuing RUNX2 expression. These findings indicate that, in addition to suppressing osteoclastogenesis, STS preserves and restores RUNX2 expression during experimental periodontitis. The recovery of RUNX2 aligns with the observed improvements in bone microarchitecture, mineral density and trabecular organisation, indicating a favourable shift in bone remodelling dynamics by simultaneously inhibiting resorption and preserving osteogenic potential.

STS Modulates Inflammatory Signalling Through Compartment‐Specific Regulation of NF‐κB

At the molecular level, the anti‐inflammatory and anti‐resorptive effects of STS were associated with modulation of NF‐κB signalling in both gingival and alveolar bone sections. Immunohistochemistry revealed pronounced NF‐κB activation in periodontal tissues from the P group, consistent with sustained inflammatory signalling, whereas STS treatment markedly reduced NF‐κB immunoreactivity, indicating attenuation of pathway activation at the tissue level (Figure 6O,P).

Western blot analyses further delineated these effects by assessing total NF‐κB p65 and its inhibitory regulator IκBα in gingival and alveolar bone tissues (Figure 7A–F). In gingival tissues (Figure 7A–C), periodontitis increased NF‐κB p65 protein levels, while STS treatment reduced p65 abundance without reaching statistical significance. In contrast, STS significantly increased IκBα expression in both STS1 and STS5 groups, indicating stabilisation of this inhibitory protein and functional restraint of NF‐κB signalling despite limited changes in total p65 levels. These findings suggest that, in gingival tissues, STS primarily modulates NF‐κB activity through inhibition of IκBα degradation instead of suppression of total NF‐κB expression.

In the alveolar bone (Figure 7D–F), a distinct but complementary pattern was observed. NF‐κB p65 levels were not significantly increased by periodontitis; however, STS treatment, particularly at the lower dose, was associated with increased p65 protein abundance, which may reflect altered NF‐κB signalling dynamics in the presence of increased IκBα levels. Consistent with this interpretation, IκBα expression was markedly elevated in both STS‐treated groups, with the strongest effect observed in STS1. Together, these data indicate effective suppression of NF‐κB pathway activation in alveolar bone through restoration of its inhibitory regulator. The compartment‐specific responses observed highlight the complexity of NF‐κB regulation during periodontitis and underscore STS's capacity to modulate inflammatory signalling across both soft and hard periodontal tissues (Figure 7A–F). Discussion

The present study demonstrated that local administration of STS attenuated periodontal inflammation and alveolar bone destruction through coordinated modulation of inflammatory, immune and osteoclastogenic pathways in a preventive manner. The findings indicate that local delivery of STS preserved periodontal tissue homeostasis by simultaneously regulating inflammatory signalling, macrophage polarisation and bone remodelling dynamics within the periodontal microenvironment. A central mechanistic finding was the stabilisation of the NF‐κB inhibitory protein IκBα following STS administration. Western blot analyses demonstrated increased IκBα protein levels in gingival and alveolar bone tissues despite sustained total NF‐κB p65 expression, suggesting that STS primarily modulates NF‐κB signalling by preventing IκBα degradation and indirectly limiting pathway activation. This effect was accompanied by reduced expression of NF‐κB‐dependent mediators, including IL‐6, TNF‐α, IFN‐γ and RANKL, indicating attenuation of inflammatory and osteoclastogenic signalling within periodontal tissues (Hayden and Ghosh 2008; Liu et al. 2017; Ohshima and Ichikawa 2014). Immunofluorescence analyses further showed that STS influenced local immune responses by altering macrophage polarisation. Ligature‐induced periodontitis increased CD80+ and F4/80+CD80+ cells, consistent with a pro‐inflammatory M1 phenotype, whereas STS treatment reduced M1‐associated markers and increased CD206 immunoreactivity. These findings suggest that STS promoted a less inflammatory tissue environment. This immunomodulatory effect was accompanied by suppression of osteoclastogenesis. STS reduced RANKL expression and restored the RANKL/OPG balance while suppressing NFATc1‐ and OSCAR‐related signalling pathways. Downstream, reduced expression of cathepsin K, MMP‐9 and TRAP was observed, indicating diminished osteoclast differentiation and activity (Kinane et al. 2017; Bartold and Van Dyke 2013; Bartold and Van Dyke 2017; Van Dyke et al. 2020; Danks and Takayanagi 2013; Takayanagi 2007; Takayanagi 2009; Tsukasaki and Takayanagi 2019; Lacey et al. 2012). The agreement between molecular and immunohistochemical findings supports a direct anti‐resorptive effect within periodontal tissues. In parallel, STS partially restored RUNX2 expression, suggesting preservation of osteogenic signalling and rebalancing of bone remodelling disrupted during periodontitis. Overall, the combined attenuation of inflammation, modulation of immune responses, suppression of osteoclastogenesis and preservation of osteogenic signalling provide a biological rationale for future studies evaluating STS in therapeutic intervention models of periodontitis.

Conclusion

STS attenuated periodontal inflammation, immune dysregulation and alveolar bone loss by modulating NF‐κB‐related inflammatory and osteoclastogenic pathways while preserving periodontal tissue integrity. By simultaneously targeting inflammation and bone resorption, STS demonstrates preventive efficacy in experimental periodontitis and warrants further evaluation in therapeutic intervention models that more closely mimic clinical disease management.
