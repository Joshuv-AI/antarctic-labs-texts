Introduction

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM), characterized as a glucose metabolism disorder arising during pregnancy [1], affects approximately 14% of pregnant women globally, with prevalence varying significantly depending on the region and diagnostic criteria used [2]. GDM not only exerts adverse effects on both mothers and fetuses during pregnancy but is also closely associated with a substantially increased risk of developing type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM) and cardiovascular diseases in the mother later in life [3–5]. Chronic hyperglycemia can induce remodeling of the systemic microvascular system. In the ocular context, these pathological changes are not confined to the retina [6]. Accumulating evidence suggests that the choroid—the most highly vascularized tissue in the eye and one that is highly sensitive to systemic hemodynamic and metabolic perturbations—may exhibit alterations in structural composition and perfusion characteristics that serve as detectable microcirculatory signals prior to the onset of clinically visible diabetic retinopathy [7, 8]. Therefore, an in-depth investigation into the subtle choroidal alterations in patients with GDM holds significant value for the early identification of ocular complications associated with pregnancy-related glucose metabolism disorders.

However, patients with GDM present a complex clinical picture characterized by pathological hyperglycemia superimposed on physiological pregnancy-related changes [9]. On one hand, hyperglycemia may lead to choroidal vasomotor dysfunction and tissue hypoperfusion through oxidative stress, chronic inflammation, and endothelial dysfunction [10]. On the other hand, pregnancy itself is accompanied by increased blood volume, decreased systemic vascular resistance, and dramatic hormonal fluctuations [11]. These profound physiological adaptations also drive significant remodeling of choroidal thickness (CT) and perfusion, which may, to some extent, mask microvascular lesions induced by metabolic abnormalities. Previous studies on GDM-associated choroidal changes remain limited, with assessment windows mostly focused on the macula and posterior pole. In terms of metrics, subfoveal or macular regional CT is the most commonly used [12], while a few studies have evaluated macular microvasculature and choriocapillaris perfusion [13]. Overall, conclusions regarding the direction of CT changes and microvascular endpoints vary across studies. This inconsistency may be partly attributable to the fact that CT, as a thickness metric, cannot differentiate between vascular luminal and stromal components and is susceptible to hemodynamic fluctuations during pregnancy [14], thereby limiting its sensitivity to early structural remodeling.

To more precisely dissect the structural components of the choroid, the choroidal vascularity index (CVI) was introduced. Defined as the ratio of vascular luminal area to total choroidal area [15], CVI is considered a more stable biomarker than CT and is relatively less influenced by ocular factors such as axial length (AL) and intraocular pressure (IOP) [16, 17]. However, current CVI research is subject to significant methodological limitations, primarily relying on two-dimensional analysis based on single B-scans and restricted to the posterior pole of the macula [18]. These limitations have resulted in two key knowledge gaps: First, the inability to capture potential alterations in the choroidal mid-periphery. Notably, studies [19, 20] have revealed that microvascular damage in diabetic retinopathy (DR) often preferentially affects the peripheral retina. Given that the choroidal vasculature is also subject to autonomic regulation and sensitive to ischemia, the peripheral choroid may similarly exhibit earlier occult changes. Second, the lack of holistic assessment based on three-dimensional (volumetric) data makes it difficult to authentically reflect changes in the vascular and stromal proportions across the full thickness of the choroid.

Ultra-wide-field swept-source optical coherence tomography angiography (SS-OCTA), as a new generation of non-invasive imaging technology, utilizes the deep penetration characteristics of a long-wavelength laser (1060 nm) to achieve three-dimensional visualization of the full-thickness choroid over an ultra-wide field [21]. The three-dimensional choroidal vascularity index (3D-CVI), calculated based on SS-OCTA volumetric data, utilizes information from the entire scan volume. This approach provides a more comprehensive and representative assessment of vascular luminal volume (LV) proportion and stromal composition, thereby effectively mitigating the sampling bias inherent in traditional two-dimensional choroidal vascularity index (2D-CVI) to a certain extent [22].

Given the current paucity of quantitative choroidal evidence based on ultra-wide-field SS-OCTA at the ultra-wide-field and volumetric levels in GDM literature, this study aims to bridge this gap. We utilized ultra-widefield SS-OCTA to systematically compare 3D-CVI, choriocapillaris plexus (CCP), and CT across different regions among GDM patients, healthy pregnant women, and non-pregnant healthy women, incorporating covariates such as age and axial length for adjustment. The aim of this study were: (1) to delineate the characteristic changes in choroidal microcirculation and structural components across a wide field in the context of pregnancy; (2) to elucidate whether specific microvascular pathological phenotypes exist in GDM against the background of physiological remodeling in pregnancy; (3) to evaluate the potential application value of 3D-CVI as a novel component quantification metric in the risk identification of pregnancy-associated glucose metabolism disorders.

Methods

Study design and ethics statement

This cross-sectional observational study was approved by the Ethics Committee of Quzhou Affiliated Hospital of Wenzhou Medical University (Quzhou People’s Hospital)(approval number: 2019-052) and was conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki.Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to enrollment. All pregnant participants were recruited from the obstetrics outpatient clinic of Quzhou People’s Hospital. Following screening and explanation of the study protocol, eligible participants were referred to the ophthalmology department for imaging and ophthalmic examinations.

Participants and criteria

A total of 138 participants were recruited and stratified into 3 groups based on pregnancy status and glucose metabolism: GDM, the pregnant control (PC), and the age-matched non-pregnant control (NC) group.

GDM

Inclusion criteria were pregnant women aged 23–42 years at 24–28 gestational age(GA) who met the diagnostic criteria for GDM based on the 75-g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT) (fasting plasma glucose ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour glucose ≥ 10.0 mmol/L, or 2-hour glucose ≥ 8.5 mmol/L).

PC

Inclusion criteria were healthy pregnant women matched for age and GA with the GDM group, presenting with normal OGTT results and no other pregnancy complications.

NC

Inclusion criteria were age-matched healthy non-pregnant women with normal blood glucose levels.

Exclusion criteria

The exclusion criteria for all groups were as follows: (1) pre-gestational diabetes mellitus (PGDM); (2) hypertension (blood pressure [BP] ≥ 140/90 mmHg) or preeclampsia; (3) history of autoimmune diseases or cardiovascular/cerebrovascular diseases; (4) history of ocular diseases (e.g., pathological myopic changes, glaucoma, uveitis, retinal vascular occlusion, or prior ocular surgery); (5) refractive error exceeding ± 3.0 diopters (D) or extreme axial length (AL < 21 mm or > 26 mm); and (6) poor image quality (signal strength index [SSI] < 7).

Ophthalmic and systemic examinations

All participants underwent standardized ophthalmic examinations and collection of relevant systemic parameters. Ophthalmic examinations included best-corrected visual acuity (BCVA), slit-lamp biomicroscopy, IOP measured using a non-contact tonometer (NT-510, NIDEK, Japan; average of ≥ 3 measurements per eye), AL measured using optical biometry (IOL Master 500, Carl Zeiss Meditec AG, Jena, Germany; average of ≥ 3 measurements per eye), fundus photography, and SS-OCTA imaging.

Systemic parameters included resting blood pressure (systolic [SBP] / diastolic [DBP]) measured on the examination day. Mean arterial pressure (MAP) was calculated as MAP = (SBP + 2 × DBP) / 3, and mean ocular perfusion pressure (MOPP) was derived using the formula MOPP = 2/3 × MAP - IOP. Glucose metabolism indicators temporally closest to the SS-OCTA examination were recorded: for the pregnancy groups (GDM and PC), fasting plasma glucose (FPG) and glycated hemoglobin (HbA1c) were extracted from obstetric follow-up records.

To avoid potential bias arising from inter-eye correlation, only one eye per participant was included in the statistical analysis. Although both eyes of all participants underwent examination and image acquisition, the right eye was selected by default provided it met the inclusion and quality control criteria. If the right eye failed to meet the pre-set image quality threshold (e.g., SSI < 7) or exhibited ocular abnormalities/artifacts affecting quantitative analysis, the left eye was selected.

Ultra-wide-field SS-OCTA

All participants underwent imaging using a SS-OCTA device (BM-400 K BMizar; TowardPi Medical Technology, Beijing, China). This device utilizes a VCSEL light source with a central wavelength of 1060 nm and a scanning speed of up to 400,000 A-scans per second, which enhances penetration capabilities and minimizes the impact of motion artifacts on image quality. The device offers a scan range of 24 mm × 20 mm (Fig. 1), representing an approximate 13.3-fold expansion in scanning area compared to the traditional 6 mm × 6 mm range [23]. This ultra-widefield scanning mode enables the generated SS-OCTA images to provide quantitative parametric information regarding vascular perfusion and structure over a larger field of view. The imaging depth extends to approximately 6 mm, covering laminar structures ranging from the retina to the choroid-sclera interface (Fig. 1), thereby supporting the subsequent choroidal volumetric and segmentation analyses in this study [24].

SS-OCTA image acquisition

For the SS-OCTA examination, the 24 mm × 20 mm scanning mode was selected. Each location was scanned four times, with priority given to retaining high-quality volumetric data that were artifact-free and exhibited the highest SSI for subsequent quantitative analysis. The entire scanning process was completed within 15–20 s. Upon completion of the scan, the software automatically reconstructed 1280 cross-sectional B-scan images (Fig. 1). A single 24 mm × 20 mm SS-OCTA image consists of 1280 line positions, with 1536 A-scans per line and 2560 sampling points per A-scan (Fig. 1). This results in a massive dataset, with a single volumetric dataset containing approximately 5.0 × 10^9 (about 5 billion) sampling points/voxels, constructing an extremely detailed three-dimensional image model of the fundus [25, 26].

Image analysis and algorithm validity

Image analysis was performed using the built-in automated algorithms to automatically identify Bruch’s membrane (BM) and the choroid-sclera interface (CSI), with manual review and correction at the B-scan level performed when necessary. Given that a standardized protocol for the automated calculation of 3D-CVI has not yet been universally established, we referred to previous validation studies. Relevant deep learning and automated choroidal boundary segmentation methods have demonstrated high consistency with reference segmentation results, as well as good agreement in choroidal thickness measurements [27, 28]. Furthermore, 3D-CVI derived from the built-in automated algorithms has shown excellent reproducibility in repeated measurements, thereby providing methodological support for the reliability of the automated volumetric parameters used in this study [29–31].

The built-in software first delineated the boundaries of the CCP and the choroidal Sattler’s and Haller’s layers (CSHL) within the tomographic images. The accuracy of this automatic segmentation was manually verified via B-scans when necessary (Fig. 1). The boundary of the CCP was defined as extending from BM to 29 μm below it (Fig. 2). The boundary of the CSHL extended from 29 μm below BM to the CSI (Fig. 2). Subsequently, voxel-wise segmentation was performed within the choroidal volumetric data to distinguish between the luminal compartment and the stromal compartment. Volume integration was then conducted on the segmented voxels to generate three-dimensional volumetric parameters.

The specific definitions of the quantitative parameters are as follows:

3D-CVI

Defined as the ratio of the choroidal LV to the total choroidal volume (TCV) within the analyzed volume [32].

CCP Vascular Density (CCP VD)

Defined as the percentage of the area occupied by blood flow signals within the choriocapillaris slab (the layer from BM to 29 μm below it) relative to the total scan area.

CT

Defined as the perpendicular distance from BM to the CSI.

Although the scan range covered 24 mm × 20 mm, considering that the curvature of the globe may induce geometric distortion and signal attenuation in the extreme periphery, a maximum effective region of interest (ROI) of 17 mm × 17 mm was selected for analysis to ensure the accuracy and reliability of the quantitative data by excluding interference from low-quality edge signals.

For image analysis, a 3 × 3 grid layout was applied. All images were manually inspected to ensure correct grid placement, with the fovea located in the center of the central grid and the optic disc located in the nasal grid. The acquired images were divided into nine equal rectangular regions: temporal-superior, temporal, temporal-inferior, superior, macular grid, inferior, nasal-superior, optic disc grid, and nasal-inferior. The multidimensional choroidal parameters were analyzed sequentially to obtain the 3D-CVI, CCP VD, and CT for each of the nine regions, as well as the overall mean values (Figs. 2, 3 and 4).

Image quality control

All SS-OCTA examinations were performed by a single experienced ophthalmologist to ensure operational consistency and accuracy. Images were acquired across all groups following a standardized acquisition protocol. To guarantee the reliability of the ultra-wide-field SS-OCTA quantitative analysis, only scans meeting predefined quality criteria were included: SSI ≥ 7, and the absence of significant motion artifacts, defocus, obstruction (e.g., by eyelashes or blinking), or extensive projection and shadow artifacts [33]. Scans exhibiting segmentation errors that could affect quantification were reviewed by trained graders; if necessary, subjects were rescanned or the data were excluded. These thresholds and quality control procedures were established with reference to previous OCTA quantitative studies and standard quality control protocols utilized in imaging centers [34]. To minimize the impact of diurnal variations on choroidal volume [35], all examinations for both eyes were uniformly scheduled between 1:30 PM and 5:00 PM.

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 27.0 (IBM, Chicago, IL, USA). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (SD) or median (interquartile range [IQR]) based on distribution, while categorical variables were presented as frequency (%). Normality and homogeneity were assessed using Shapiro–Wilk and Levene’s tests. Group comparisons employed one-way ANOVA, Welch’s ANOVA (with Games–Howell post hoc), or the Kruskal–Wallis H test as appropriate. Key SS-OCTA metrics were analyzed using general linear model-analysis of Covariance(GLM-ANCOVA) adjusting for age, AL, and GA; between-group differences were reported as adjusted regression coefficients (β_adj) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) and Bonferroni correction. Correlations were analyzed using Pearson or Spearman methods. All tests were two-sided, and P < 0.05 was considered significant.

Results

Demographics and clinical baseline characteristics

A total of 138 subjects were enrolled and categorized into the GDM), PC ), and NC ) groups. Only one eye per subject was included for analysis, with the right eye prioritized according to the study protocol. All subjects were female (100%). No statistically significant differences were observed in age among the three groups: 31.98 ± 4.43 years (range: 23–42) for GDM, 30.74 ± 3.66 years (24–42) for PC, and 31.00 ± 4.26 years (23–40) for NC . GA was comparable between the two pregnancy groups (GDM: 26.72 ± 1.52 weeks; PC: 26.30 ± 2.39 weeks; P = 0.308); this metric was not applicable to the NC.

Regarding hemodynamic parameters, there were no significant differences in SBP, DBP, or MAP among the three groups. SBP was 118.96 ± 7.24, 121.20 ± 8.45, and 118.67 ± 6.88 mmHg, respectively ; DBP was 77.63 ± 5.55, 78.80 ± 4.73, and 77.57 ± 3.77 mmHg, respectively ; and MAP was 91.41 ± 5.03, 92.93 ± 4.19, and 91.27 ± 3.94 mmHg, respectively .

In terms of ocular baseline parameters, no significant differences were found in IOP, MOPP, or AL across the groups. IOP was 13.76 ± 2.80, 13.93 ± 1.93, and 14.26 ± 2.13 mmHg, respectively ; MOPP was 47.18 ± 4.61, 48.03 ± 2.73, and 46.60 ± 3.10 mmHg, respectively ; and AL was 23.46 ± 0.66, 23.28 ± 0.53, and 23.35 ± 0.58 mm, respectively .

Glucose metabolism indicators were compared only between the two pregnancy groups (GDM vs. PC). FPG was 5.26 (5.21, 5.44) mmol/L in the GDM versus 4.41 ± 0.20 mmol/L in the PC (P < 0.001). HbA1c was 5.20 (5.10, 5.40)% in the GDM versus 4.84 ± 0.19% in the PC (P < 0.001) (Table 1). Furthermore, the history of pre-gestational diabetes, hypertension, and other ocular or systemic diseases was zero in all three groups. All GDM patients were managed with diet control (Table 1).

Comparison of CCP VD among the three groups

A statistically significant difference among the GDM, PC, and NC groups was observed in the macular grid (P = 0.012, Table 2), whereas no significant differences were found in other regions (all P > 0.05, Table 2). In the unadjusted comparison of the macular grid, the CCP VD (mean ± SD) for the three groups was 46.26 ± 1.41 for GDM, 46.28 ± 1.43 for PC, and 45.41 ± 1.85 for NC. Post hoc pairwise comparisons indicated that both GDM and PC were significantly higher than NC (GDM vs. NC: P = 0.011; PC vs. NC: P = 0.009), while the difference between GDM and PC was not significant (P > 0.05) (Figure S1).Considering the potential influence of AL and age, we performed ANCOVA using a GLM for the macular grid, with AL and age included as covariates and interaction terms tested. The results showed that the Group × AL interaction was not significant (P = 0.081), whereas the Group × Age interaction was significant (P < 0.001), suggesting that between-group differences varied with age levels. Consequently, in the model adjusting for both AL and age, we fixed AL at 23.36 mm and performed pairwise comparisons of conditional simple effects at the mean age ± 1 SD levels (27.10, 31.24, and 35.38 years).At Age = 27.10 years, no pairwise comparisons were significant (GDM vs. PC: P = 0.314; GDM vs. NC: P = 1.000; PC vs. NC: P = 0.094) (Fig. 5a). At Age = 31.24 years, GDM was higher than NC (β_adj = 1.005, 95% CI 0.477–1.532, P < 0.001) and PC was higher than NC (β_adj = 0.753, 95% CI 0.226–1.281, P = 0.002), while the difference between GDM and PC was not significant (P = 0.766) (Fig. 5b). At Age = 35.38 years, GDM was higher than PC (β_adj = 1.015, 95% CI 0.248–1.783, P = 0.005), GDM was higher than NC (β_adj = 1.871, 95% CI 1.160–2.582, P < 0.001), and PC was higher than NC (β_adj = 0.856, 95% CI 0.051–1.661, P = 0.033) (Fig. 5c, Table S1).To further evaluate differences within the pregnant population, we constructed a GLM for CCP VD in the macular grid using only the pregnancy groups, adjusting for age, AL, and GA, with interaction terms included. Results indicated that the main effect of Group remained significant (P = 0.007), and both Group × Age (P = 0.001) and Group × AL (P = 0.031) interactions were significant, while the main effect of GA was not significant (P = 0.521). Simple effect analyses were conducted under this model.By AL (fixing Age = 31.36 years, GA = 26.51 weeks): differences were not significant at AL = 22.76 and 23.37 mm (P = 0.516 and P = 0.184, respectively); at a longer AL of 23.98 mm, GDM was higher than PC (β_adj = 0.793, 95% CI 0.155–1.431, P = 0.015) (Fig. 6a).By Age (fixing AL = 23.37 mm, GA = 26.51 weeks): differences were not significant at Age = 27.27 and 31.36 years (P = 0.133 and P = 0.184, respectively); at Age = 35.45 years, GDM was higher than PC (β_adj = 1.054, 95% CI 0.420–1.688, P = 0.001) (Fig. 6b, Table S2).

Discussion

Based on sectoral analysis using ultra-wide-field SS-OCTA, this study systematically compared CCP VD, 3D-CVI, and CT among GDM, PC, and NC groups. Furthermore, key confounding and effect-modifying factors, such as age and AL, were evaluated within the pregnancy groups and interaction models.Overall, our observations are as follows: (1)In the macular grid, unadjusted comparisons indicated an elevation in CCP VD in the pregnancy-associated groups (GDM/PC) compared to NC. After adjusting for covariates and considering interactions, this difference exhibited a potential age-dependency, becoming more pronounced at mean and higher age levels, while remaining non-significant at lower age levels. (2)Differences in 3D-CVI among groups were not widespread but were primarily concentrated in localized regions such as the macular grid and nasal-superior sectors. After adjusting for age and AL, signals indicating lower 3D-CVI in GDM compared to NC were detectable in these regions; however, differences between GDM and PC were generally not robust. Moreover, after further adjustment for GA in the pregnancy-only sample, no significant differences were observed between GDM and PC. (3) No significant differences in CT were found among the three groups, although correlations with age and GA were noted.Taken together, these findings suggest that pregnancy-related physiological hemodynamic resetting or vasodilation may be associated with alterations in CCP flow signals. In this GDM cohort, which consisted of mild-to-moderate cases managed primarily with diet and exercise, the additional structural alterations reflected by 3D-CVI appeared to be relatively limited and focal, potentially modulated by individual background factors such as age and AL. Simultaneously, AL and blood pressure-related metrics on the examination day were comparable across the three groups, and correlation analysis failed to detect significant associations between AL or MOPP and overall CCP VD, 3D-CVI, or CT. This implies that, within this sample and under the current measurement conditions, evidence for a linear association between these factors and global metrics is limited. Therefore, the observed regional differences are unlikely to be explained solely by ocular magnification effects or transient systemic hemodynamic differences; however, the influence of residual confounding, non-linear relationships, or measurement-related factors cannot be ruled out based on these data.

Changes in choroidal and fundus microcirculation during pregnancy may not simply represent a unidirectional thickening or increased flow, but rather a staged and hierarchical vascular remodeling that evolves with gestational progression [36, 37]. From the perspective of systemic circulatory physiology, healthy pregnancy is characterized by plasma volume expansion, decreased systemic vascular resistance, and upregulated cardiac output. Cardiac output typically peaks during the second trimester and shows a slight regression near term [38], suggesting that perfusion status possesses significant time-dependent and nonlinear characteristics [39]. At the ocular microcirculatory level, Pota et al. [40] reported dynamic fluctuations in microvascular parameters across different trimesters, with inconsistent directional findings among studies. This phenomenon implies that the interpretation of OCTA flow signals requires comprehensive judgment combining gestational stage and methodological differences.Consistent with this, we observed a significant negative correlation between GA and CCP VD, as well as a weak but significant negative correlation between GA and CT. We found that within the second and third trimester windows covered by this study, CCP flow signals might be in a transitional phase from relative enhancement to physiological regression; however, this finding represents a cross-sectional association rather than a longitudinal causality. Overall, this temporal consistency not only supports viewing ocular microcirculation as a window into systemic circulatory changes but also further emphasizes the importance of fully considering factors such as GA when interpreting OCTA flow signals.In the unadjusted comparison among the three groups, differences in CCP VD were not significant in most regions. Statistical significance was observed only in the macular grid, where pairwise comparisons primarily reflected an elevation in the pregnancy-associated groups (GDM and PC) relative to NC, while the difference between GDM and PC was not significant. After including age and AL and testing for interactions, we observed a Group × Age interaction, suggesting that between-group differences were age-dependent: the difference between pregnancy-associated groups and NC remained visible at the mean age level and became more pronounced at higher age levels. Furthermore, within the internal model of the pregnancy-only sample (GDM vs. PC), the difference between GDM and PC reached statistical significance only at higher age or longer AL levels. This suggests that the CCP phenotype may be co-influenced by the physiological state of pregnancy and individual background factors (e.g., age, AL).Considering that the subjects in this study were mostly mild-to-moderate GDM cases with good glycemic control and relatively limited metabolic exposure windows, and that correlations between FPG or HbA1c and overall CCP VD, 3D-CVI, or CT did not reach statistical significance in the pregnancy groups, these results tend to suggest that pregnancy-related systemic hemodynamic adaptations may be more prominent at the global level. The additional impact of GDM might be more readily detectable only under specific susceptible backgrounds. Additionally, it should be noted that previous findings in populations with pregnancy complicated by glucose metabolism disorders or pre-existing diabetes have shown inconsistencies regarding regions, metrics, and directions of change [41, 42], prompting caution in distinguishing true flow signal changes from measurement biases. Hemodilution, changes in blood flow velocity, and alterations in optical properties caused by tissue edema during pregnancy may all affect OCTA thresholded signals [43]. Therefore, whether the additional microcirculatory damage caused by GDM can be detected by OCTA is highly dependent on the heterogeneity of the sample’s metabolic control level, disease duration, and individual vascular background [44].

Compared with CCP VD, inter-group differences in 3D-CVI were more localized. In unadjusted analyses, differences were observed in certain sectors (e.g., macular grid or nasal-superior). However, after adjusting for confounders such as age and AL, the significance in the macular grid was attenuated, manifesting more as a signal of marginal significance rather than complete disappearance, whereas the nasal-superior region retained a certain degree of differential signal. Previous studies have suggested [45] that dysregulation of glucose metabolism can lead to choroidal vascular endothelial dysfunction, alterations in basement membranes and vessel walls, inflammation, and oxidative stress. Most studies have reported evidence of decreased CVI and choroidal vascular remodeling in the context of diabetes mellitus or around the onset of diabetic retinopathy [46, 47]. However, GDM is characterized by a shorter disease duration and a limited window of metabolic exposure. In populations with good glycemic control, structural proportional alterations are more likely to be mild or focally distributed. Combined with our findings, the differences in 3D-CVI were predominantly concentrated in the comparison between GDM and NC, while stable differences between the two pregnancy groups were lacking. This suggests that metabolic stress in this cohort is more likely to manifest as a mild, localized incremental effect superimposed upon the physiological hemodynamic resetting of pregnancy.Furthermore, regional differences were primarily concentrated in the nasal-superior region and the marginal signal in the macular area, which may be related to the segmental nature of choroidal blood supply and the anatomical characteristics of the choroidal watershed zone (CWZ) [48, 49]. Choroidal blood flow is segmentally supplied by the posterior ciliary artery system; overlap between sectors of the short posterior ciliary arteries is limited, and their junctions can form the CWZ [50]. In angiography, the CWZ often manifests as a zone of delayed filling surrounding the optic nerve [51] and can exhibit radial and vertical orientations. In some individuals, its course may extend toward the macula, suggesting that this region is more prone to detectable alterations in structural perfusion ratios when the spatial heterogeneity of perfusion increases, making it statistically more susceptible to capture by proportional metrics like 3D-CVI. Concurrently, a spatial gradient exists in the posterior choroidal thickness of normal eyes, such as relative thinning toward the nasal side and a decrease from the center to the periphery. Multiple studies have shown [52, 53] that the decline in CT is more pronounced toward the nasal direction, being thinner nasally than temporally. Therefore, we interpret this merely as a directional reference for potential susceptibility in the nasal and nasal-superior directions, implying relatively limited structural reserve. Additionally, given the proximity of the nasal-superior region to the optic disc and its heightened sensitivity to AL, optic disc morphology, signal attenuation, and segmentation boundary errors [54, 55], although we have included covariates such as AL in the models, it remains necessary to further verify the robustness of differences in this region through larger samples and longitudinal designs combined with sensitivity analyses.

Notably, we observed that the direction of changes in CCP flow signals and 3D-CVI was not always consistent. This directional discrepancy may reflect the different physiological hierarchies corresponding to these two types of metrics. OCTA flow density metrics such as CCP VD are derived from motion contrast/decorrelation signals obtained via repeated scans and thresholding. They are more sensitive to the presence and coverage area of detectable perfusion signals and are therefore not equivalent to absolute blood flow, flow velocity, or vascular volume fraction [56]. In contrast, 3D-CVI reflects the ratio of LV to the total volume within a specific volumetric range, leaning more toward the proportion of vascular structure rather than instantaneous perfusion intensity [32].In the context of pregnancy-related increases in circulatory volume and hemodynamic adaptation, SS-OCTA is more likely to first capture enhanced flow signals or expanded detectable areas, manifesting as elevated CCP VD. Concurrently, if fluid retention or changes in interstitial fluid and stromal components lead to a relative increase in stromal volume, 3D-CVI might show a relative decrease or remain unchanged even if LV expands synchronously; furthermore, this alteration does not necessarily require a synchronous change in CT. In mild-to-moderate GDM populations with shorter disease duration and better metabolic control, metabolic load-related endothelial functional changes and low-grade inflammatory responses might affect perfusion regulation and interstitial fluid balance earlier [57]. This could cause flow signal changes to precede significant structural remodeling, thereby presenting a phenotype where CCP VD is more sensitive to changes while 3D-CVI is more conservative [58]. Some studies suggest that CVI and OCTA metrics should be interpreted jointly to separately characterize structural composition and perfusion status, thereby achieving a more comprehensive vascular assessment [59]. Consistent with this explanation, our results showed that CCP VD differences predominantly reflected pregnancy-related changes, whereas 3D-CVI differences were more focal. After adjusting for age and AL, 3D-CVI exhibited a hierarchy of signal strength, being more robust in the nasal-superior region and marginal in the macular grid, suggesting that stratified interpretation helps in understanding the directional differences between the two metrics more consistently.Correlation analysis provided further supportive clues for this decoupling phenomenon. In the overall cohort, age was significantly negatively correlated with 3D-CVI, CCP VD, and CT, suggesting that age-related choroidal decline serves as a common baseline background for both [60]. However, in the pregnant population, GA was significantly negatively correlated only with CCP VD and CT, while its correlation with 3D-CVI was not significant. Meanwhile, correlation analysis did not detect significant associations between AL, MOPP, FPG, or HbA1c and mean CCP VD, 3D-CVI, or CT within the pregnancy groups. This suggests that while linear evidence for these factors is limited at the correlation level, their potential influence in multivariate or specific contexts cannot be ruled out. This discrepancy implies that within the second and third trimester windows covered by this study, CCP metrics and thickness parameters related to SS-OCTA flow signals may be more prone to exhibiting staged changes with GA [40, 61, 62], whereas the remodeling process of 3D-CVI, as a structural proportional metric, is relatively robust or exhibits a temporal lag. It is worth noting that a weak-to-moderate positive correlation still exists among CCP VD, 3D-CVI, and CT overall, indicating a physiological synergy between flow signals and structural composition. However, under specific pathophysiological backgrounds such as pregnancy and fluid retention, the two may display differences in response timing or sensitivity to stressors; therefore, clinical interpretation requires combined multidimensional metrics rather than single inferences.

Previous studies on the diabetes spectrum have reported significant heterogeneity in CT findings [63–65], and this inconsistency is even more pronounced in pregnancy-associated populations. Overall, multiple studies suggest that differences in CT between GDM and healthy pregnant women are unstable and often non-significant. For instance, Benfica et al. [12] compared CT at multiple fixed macular locations in the third trimester among healthy pregnant women and those with GDM, T2DM, and type 1 diabetes mellitus(T1DM). They found that CT was thinner in women with type 1 diabetes, whereas no significant differences were observed between GDM or type 2 diabetes groups and healthy controls. Similarly, Acmaz et al. [66] observed that while CT might be thicker in pregnancy-associated groups compared to non-pregnant controls, there was still no difference between GDM and healthy pregnant women. Akpolat et al. [67] also reported no significant inter-group differences in subfoveal CT.Notably, in studies including phenotypes with higher vascular risk, such as those complicated by gestational hypertension or PGDM [13, 68], CT may show a trend toward thinning or attenuated thickening, suggesting that co-existing factors and disease burden stratification influence CT presentation. On the other hand, Kahveci et al. [69] did not observe differences in central, nasal, or temporal CT even after stratification by HbA1c, implying that in GDM populations with shorter disease duration and limited metabolic exposure windows, CT, as a global full-thickness metric, may not be sensitive to mild metabolic differences.Based on this background, the lack of stable and significant inter-group differences in CT across the nine ultra-widefield sectors in this study may be collectively attributed to several factors: the predominance of mild-to-moderate GDM cases managed primarily by diet/exercise in our cohort; the increased individual variability caused by pregnancy-related physiological remodeling, which reduces statistical sensitivity to detect the incremental effects of GDM; and the inherent sensitivity of CT to factors such as axial length, hemodynamic status, and measurement/segmentation errors in ultra-widefield sectors. It is important to emphasize that the absence of significant differences in CT among groups does not rule out proportional redistribution within the choroidal composition. When the ratio of vascular lumen to stroma changes while total thickness remains relatively stable, proportional structural parameters like 3D-CVI may present differential signals earlier in susceptible regions, capturing changes in vascular components. However, future studies combining longitudinal follow-up with multi-metric approaches are still needed to further clarify the temporal trajectory from compositional changes to thickness alterations.

This study has several limitations. First, the cross-sectional design precludes the establishment of causal relationships and limits our ability to determine whether choroidal alterations recover during late pregnancy or postpartum, or whether GDM exerts cumulative temporal effects. Future longitudinal studies covering multiple gestational stages and extending into the postpartum period are warranted to delineate the dynamic trajectories of CCP VD and 3D-CVI, and to examine their associations with glycemic control and pregnancy outcomes. Second, the GDM cohort in this study consisted primarily of mild-to-moderate cases with well-controlled blood glucose, which may underestimate the impact of GDM on choroidal structural remodeling. Subsequent studies should perform stratification or interaction analyses based on HbA1c, OGTT levels, treatment modalities, and the presence of gestational hypertension to better characterize the heterogeneous effects of metabolic load.Third, although we adjusted for age and AL in our models and tested for interactions, residual confounding and measurement variability may persist. Although correlation analyses did not detect significant associations between MOPP and major OCTA metrics, future studies are recommended to standardize data acquisition times, systematically collect and include ocular perfusion pressure and additional hemodynamic parameters, and evaluate their potential impacts within a multivariate framework. Additionally, strategies to control for multiple comparisons should be employed to minimize the risk of false positives associated with sectoral exploratory analyses. Fourth, 3D-CVI relies on specific devices and algorithms; while previous studies support the reliability of its automated measurement, cross-device and cross-software consistency requires external validation. Furthermore, OCTA segmentation and choroidal boundary identification may be subject to errors in cases of signal attenuation, peripheral sectors, or poor boundary contrast, potentially leading to inaccuracies in CCP segmentation or bias in 3D-CVI estimation.

Conclusion

In conclusion, this study identified that CCP VD in the macular grid was higher in the pregnancy groups compared to NC, a difference that was more pronounced at mean and higher age levels. Within the pregnancy groups, GDM exhibited significantly higher CCP VD than PC only at older ages or longer AL, suggesting that pregnancy-related physiological states may be associated with alterations in CCP perfusion signals, which are modulated by age and AL. Inter-group differences in 3D-CVI were primarily concentrated in the macular and nasal-superior regions, manifesting as lower values in GDM compared to NC, with the nasal-superior quadrant showing greater robustness. Given the predominance of mild-to-moderate GDM cases in our cohort, structural differences between GDM and PC may not have fully manifested. 3D-CVI may serve as an auxiliary indicator reflecting regional choroidal structural remodeling. We recommend its combined application with CCP flow signal metrics to facilitate a multi-dimensional synergistic interpretation of structure and function. This approach enables a more precise and comprehensive capture of GDM-related pathophysiological alterations in the choroid, thereby enhancing the completeness and clinical utility of the assessment. Future longitudinal studies covering multiple gestational time points and postpartum follow-up are needed to further validate its dynamic trajectory and clinical significance.
