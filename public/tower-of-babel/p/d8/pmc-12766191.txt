Heterogeneity sonata: Orchestrating distal metastasis under stress
Authors: Baiyang Liu, Yongbin Chen
Published: 2025

Tumors grow in chronically stressed microenvironments characterized by hypoxia, acidosis, nutrient limitation, immune surveillance, and therapy-induced stress. To survive and expand in such adversity, cancer cells have evolved diverse adaptations and often activate stress-response pathways that can accelerate disease progression.1 Glucose is the principal carbon and energy source supporting biosynthesis and proliferation, and its rapid consumption by malignant cells renders low-glucose conditions a pervasive metabolic stress in the tumor microenvironment.2 This stress not only limits cellular energy supply but also engages programs such as autophagy, endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress, and reorganization of vesicular trafficking.2 These processes reshape cell fate, ultimately altering both the composition and function of the intra- and extracellular microenvironments. However, there is limited understanding of whether, and how, glucose deprivation in these cells affects the broader tumor microenvironment.

In this issue of Cell, Wu et al. present a comprehensive study of how glucose restriction reshapes the tumor–host ecosystem to facilitate lung metastasis despite suppressing primary tumor growth.3 Using a low-carbohydrate diet and glycolysis-deficient tumor clones across five orthotopic mouse models (hepatoma, melanoma, breast cancer, colon adenocarcinoma, and stomach carcinoma), they consistently demonstrate that glucose deprivation establishes a lung pre-metastatic niche characterized by macrophage accumulation and NK-cell deficiency. Mechanistically, glucose deprivation induces ER stress and upregulates the E3 ligase HRD1, which catalyzes K63-linked ubiquitination of TRAIL and drives its ESCRT-dependent loading into tumor-derived exosomes; these vesicles activate NF-κB, generate PVR+ macrophages, and trigger TIGIT-dependent NK-cell exhaustion. Perturbing this axis—via macrophage depletion, inhibition of exosome release, or TIGIT blockade—restores NK-cell function and abrogates metastasis; clinically, low tumor glucose-metabolism is associated with a higher 2-year postoperative recurrence rate, and plasma exosomal TRAIL outperforms both AFP levels and tumor size in predicting early postoperative lung metastasis in hepatocellular carcinoma.

A hallmark of tumor metabolic reprogramming is the upregulation of glucose metabolism, which supports rapid tumor growth and lactate production that can compromise immune responses.2,4 Even with mechanisms that maintain glycolytic demand—including enhanced glucose transport and metabolic rerouting through gluconeogenesis and autophagy—the tumor microenvironment commonly experiences glucose depletion driven by excessive uptake and inadequate vascularization.2 Although studies suggest that low glucose levels in the tumor microenvironment help sustain tumor-cell pyrimidine synthesis and limit mitochondrial outer-membrane permeability,5 whether a low-glucose environment can actively heighten malignancy—and which state, high or low glucose metabolism, is more malignant—remain critical, unresolved questions. Wu et al. show that Glucose restriction shapes pre-metastatic innate immune landscapes in the lung through exosomal TRAIL. The results from in vivo and in vitro mixed inoculation or culture systems of glucose metabolism-deficient tumor cells with normal tumor cells, as well as the single-cell RNA sequencing data from the lung tissue of mice on a low-carbohydrate diet, indicate that low-glucose tumor cells do not enhance their own metastatic ability. Instead, they promote the dissemination of adjacent glycolysis-active neighbors via a bystander effect. Tumors exhibit marked intratumoral heterogeneity in glucose metabolism, with coexisting high- and low-glucose regions. Converging evidence indicates that formation of a pre-metastatic niche is critical for circulating tumor cells to colonize distant organs. Accordingly, neither high nor low glucose metabolism alone is sufficient to drive metastasis; rather, metastasis emerges when low-glucose subpopulations condition a permissive “soil,” enabling adjacent glycolysis-active “seeds” to establish at distant sites (Fig. 1). Conventional therapies—including chemotherapy, radiotherapy, targeted therapy, and immunotherapy—primarily inhibit tumor growth by inducing stresses such as nutrient deprivation, DNA damage, and immune-mediated cytotoxicity.6 Yet therapy-induced stress can also alter the evolutionary course of tumors, shifting from proliferation-dominant states toward more invasive, metastatic phenotypes7,8—the leading cause of cancer mortality. Unlike primary tumor growth, metastatic spread is often occult and protracted, typically requiring long-term follow-up for detection. Accordingly, dynamic, real-time monitoring of tumor evolution and metastatic risk during treatment is warranted, with timely adaptation of therapy. In this study, circulating exosomal TRAIL effectively predicts early lung metastasis, supporting the use of liquid biopsy-based markers—such as plasma exosomal TRAIL—for dynamic assessment of metastatic risk. Patients classified as high risk by such markers may benefit from tailored combination strategies, such as the anti-TIGIT regimens proposed in this study, to curb metastatic progression and improve outcomes. Overall, the study uncovers a bystander mechanism in which low-glucose tumor cells condition a permissive lung niche that enables glycolysis-active neighbors to seed metastases, providing a mechanistic rationale to combine glucose-targeting therapy with TIGIT blockade. It would be interesting to know whether other stressors—such as hypoxia, acidosis, immune surveillance, or therapy-induced stress—can similarly induce “altruistic” programs in stressed tumor cells that, while limiting their own growth, promote more aggressive phenotypes in neighboring cells.
