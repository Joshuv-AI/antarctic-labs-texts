Prevalence of malaria and associated risk factors in Loka Abaya district, Sidama regional state, Southern Ethiopia
Authors: Siratu Seyoum, Mohamed Esmael
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundMalaria remains a major public health concern in Ethiopia, despite the widespread use of insecticide-treated mosquito nets. The goal of this study was to determine the prevalence of malaria, the proportion of Plasmodium species, and the risk factors for disease transmission in the study area.MethodsA community-based cross-sectional study was conducted from April to May 2023 among 419 participants. Socio-demographic and environmental data were collected using structured questionnaires and observation checklists. Capillary blood samples were examined using rapid diagnostic tests and microscopy. Data were entered into EpiData version 4.6 and analyzed using SPSS version 26. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression analyses were performed. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsThe overall malaria prevalence was 14.6% (95% CI: 11.0–18.0). Plasmodium falciparum accounted for 59.0% of infections, Plasmodium vivax for 36.1%, and mixed infections for 4.9%. Higher prevalence was observed among symptomatic individuals (30.7%) compared to asymptomatic individuals (7.3%). Independent predictors of malaria infection included no formal education (AOR = 2.56; 95% CI: 1.10–5.94), low monthly income (AOR = 2.97; 95% CI: 1.07–8.25), lack of ITN ownership (AOR = 2.56; 95% CI: 1.14–5.75), irregular ITN use (AOR = 2.88; 95% CI: 1.13–7.32), and presence of stagnant water near households (AOR = 2.12; 95% CI: 1.14–3.99).ConclusionMalaria remains a significant public health concern in the study area. Strengthening insecticide-treated nets utilization, expanding indoor residual spraying coverage, promoting early diagnosis and prompt treatment, enhancing community awareness, and implementing effective environmental management are essential for reducing malaria transmission. Addressing socio-economic disparities is also crucial for sustainable malaria control and elimination efforts in the area.Keywords: Malaria, Prevalence, Risk factors, Loka Abaya, Ethiopia

Background

Malaria remains a major public health challenge, with over 240 million cases and 600,000 deaths reported globally in 2025. Sub-Saharan Africa bears approximately 94% of the burden, with children under five years of age and pregnant women being disproportionately affected [1].

In Ethiopia, malaria is a significant public health and economic problem. Malaria distribution and transmission in Ethiopia vary from season to season due to altitude differences, climate, and rainfall patterns in areas with relatively longer duration of transmission in lowland areas, river basins, and valleys [3, 4]. Bimodal transmission: Major transmission from September to December, following the main rainy season from June to August, and minor transmission from April to May, following a short rainy period from February to March [5]. Ethiopia is one of the few African countries where both Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax are co-endemic in significant numbers and are taken into account in malaria diagnostic and treatment guidelines [6]. Plasmodium falciparum is the most common, accounting for approximately 60% of malaria cases, followed by Plasmodium vivax (40%). Plasmodium falciparum is endemic in many parts of the country. Plasmodium malariae and Plasmodium ovale infections are rare, accounting for less than 1% of confirmed malaria cases [7].

Malaria transmission in Ethiopia is primarily mediated by Anopheles arabiensis, the primary vector, with Anopheles pharoensis, Anopheles funestus, and Anopheles nili acting as secondary vectors [8]. Recent studies have also documented the establishment of Anopheles stephensi in urban settings, raising concerns about changing malaria transmission dynamics and potentially expanding malaria risk areas [9].

Malaria transmission in the Sidama Regional State is heterogeneous and predominantly concentrated in lowland and midland districts. Loka Abaya, Boricha, Hawassa Zuria, Bilate Zuria, and Darara are characterized by very high transmission intensity, with approximately 100% of the population at risk. Districts such as Dale, Aleta Wondo, Bensa, Aroresa, Shebedino, Wondo Genet, Bursa, Gorche, Bona Zuria, Bura, and Chire are classified as high-endemic areas, where approximately 50%–75% of the population is at risk of malaria transmission. In contrast, Aleta Chuko, Dara, Malga, Hula, and Hoko show moderate or focal endemicity, with 20%–50% of the population at risk, largely influenced by altitude variation and localized ecological suitability [10].

Loka Abaya district is particularly notable, as all 26 kebeles are classified as malaria endemic, and the entire district population is considered at risk. The district’s environmental conditions, such as abundant mosquito breeding sites and intermittent rivers, help to sustain malaria transmission [11].

Understanding the local epidemiology of malaria and identifying associated risk factors is essential for developing and implementing effective control and elimination strategies. However, evidence on the current malaria prevalence and determinants in Loka Abaya district is limited. This study aimed to determine the prevalence of malaria and associated risk factors among the population of Loka Abaya district, Sidama Regional State, southern Ethiopia.

Methods

Study area and period

The study was conducted in Loka Abaya district, Sidama Regional State, Southern Ethiopia, from April through May 2023. The district is one of the 36 districts found in the Sidama region, Ethiopia. The place is located at a distance of 344km southeast of the capital of Ethiopia, Addis Ababa, and 69 km from the regional capital, Hawassa. The district is located in the Great Rift Valley of Ethiopia at 6° 42´- 6º 83´ N and 38° 01´- 38° 36´E. The total area is about 878km2. The study area is characterized by a bimodal type of rainfall in which the short rainy season occurs from March to May, whilst the main rainy season occurs from June to September. Mean annual rainfall varies from 700 to 1877 mm, and the mean annual temperature ranges from 26°C to 35°C. The area is also characterized by erratic rainfall, moisture stress, and high temperatures during the dry season. The altitude ranges from 1001 to 2000 m above sea level [12].

The population's livelihood is based on subsistence mixed farming. Enset (E. ventricosum) is one of the major crops grown in the district. Based on the census conducted in 2007, Loka Abaya district has a total population of 110,917, of which 56,566 are men, and 54,351 are women [13]. Administratively, Loka Abaya district is organized into 24 kebeles and 2 municipal towns; all kebeles are malarious. The number of households in the district was 22,917. Loka Abaya is located west of the Wolayta zone, North of Darara, northeast of Dale, South of Oromiya, East of Aleta Chuko, and southwest of Lake Abaya. Loka Abaya district has 1 Primary Hospital, 5 health centers, 24 health posts, and 3 private clinics [14].

Loka Abaya district was purposively selected for the study because of its epidemiological and ecological relevance rather than convenience. The district is one of the malaria-endemic districts in the Sidama Region, with all 26 kebeles classified as malaria-endemic and a substantial population at risk of malaria. The area is characterized by environmental and climatic conditions that favor malaria transmission, including the presence of favorable mosquito breeding sites, intermittent rivers, suitable rainfall patterns, and warm temperatures. These warmer temperatures can accelerate the development of Plasmodium parasites within mosquito vectors, thereby increasing transmission potential and contributing to a higher disease burden, particularly in low-altitude areas where malaria is already prevalent. Furthermore, reports from the district indicate that malaria remains a persistent public health challenge despite the implementation of ongoing control measures. In addition, no previous study has been conducted to determine the burden of malaria and its associated factors in the district. Therefore, Loka Abaya district represents an appropriate setting for investigating malaria prevalence and its associated risk factors.

Based on the local seasonality of malaria transmission, a specific period for the study was selected. Even though malaria is endemic throughout the year due to the presence of permanent rivers, lakes, and ponds, there are two seasonal malaria transmission peaks in the study area: from April to May (minor transmission) and from September to December (major transmission) [11] (see Fig. 1).

Study design and period

A community-based cross-sectional study was conducted among Loka Abaya District communities from April through May 2023.

Population

The study’s source population was all the community members of the Loka Abaya District. The study populations were all age groups and both genders that live in the selected kebeles and were available during the data collection period. Randomly selected individuals from selected households were the study unit. Critically ill patients and individuals with mental health problems, as well as individuals who were taking anti-malarial therapy during the data collection period or who had been treated with anti-malarial drugs within the past month before enrollment, were excluded from the study.

Sample size determination

The sample size (n = 422) was calculated using a single population proportion formula, assuming a 21.1% prevalence from the study conducted in the community of Mizan-Aman town and its catchment area in Southwest Ethiopia [15], 95% confidence level, 5% margin of error, and a design effect of 1.5. A total of 419 participants were included (99% response rate).

Sampling technique and procedure

A multistage sampling technique was used. Loka Abaya district includes 24 rural kebeles and two municipal towns, all of which are malaria-endemic. However, based on the local malaria stratification and burden classification, ten kebeles are categorized as a greater malaria-endemic (higher transmission). Three of these ten higher endemic kebeles (Dese, Doya Doa, and Aldabo) were chosen for the study by using a lottery method. The most recent demographic registrations of households available at the local kebeles administrations were used to calculate the proportional sample size for each selected kebeles and it was also used as a sample frame. According to the most recent household demographic registrations, Dese has 650 households, Doya Dao has 913 households, and Aldabo has 1,715 households. The estimated sample size was proportionally allocated to the selected three kebeles based on the number of households. At every 8th interval, 83 households from Dese, 118 households from Doya Dao, and 221 households from Aldabo were chosen. Finally, one of the selected household members was chosen at random using lottery methods. If the chosen household member was not present, a revisit was made after asking when the person would be there (see Fig. 2).

Data collection and management

Socio-demographic data collection

Healthcare workers collected data after a three-day training on the study's objectives and how to obtain informed consent from study participants.

Socio-demographic and other associated factors were collected through a face-to-face interview and visual observation using a pre-tested structured questionnaire (modified from a malaria indicator survey household questionnaire) and checklist. The questionnaire was written in English first and then translated into the local language (Sidamic language) by language experts, and finally returned to the original language. If the selected study participants were infants or children, guardians/caregivers/parents were interviewed instead. An observation checklist was used to collect data on the availability, housing conditions, and the presence of stagnant water around houses. A senior health officer examined body temperature and other clinical manifestations to identify symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals. This examination took place following the selection of individuals from households. The data collection process was supervised and assisted by two supervisors.

Blood sample collection

Following consent and assent (for the age group of 12–17 years) and enrollment, trained laboratory technicians collected capillary blood samples from each participant using a disposable sterile lancet via a finger prick (a heel prick or big toe for young children) following standard operating procedure (SOP) [8].

Following the removal of the first drop, blood samples were used to run malaria rapid diagnostic tests (RDTs) on the spot (using First Response® Malaria Ag. P.f. / P.v. Card test, which has 100% sensitivity and specificity, and can specifically detect both Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax species; and is being supplied by the Ethiopian Ministry of Health to health posts, and to prepare thick and thin blood films on the same slide for each participant, and was labeled with a unique code. The thin blood smear was fixed by carefully dropping methanol (100% or absolute methanol) with a Pasteur pipette immediately after drying. By placing the slides on a flat surface, the methanol-fixed thin smears were allowed to dry completely in the air. The prepared blood smears were transported to a nearby health facility (Hantate Primary Hospital) in a slide box for Giemsa staining and microscopic examination.

Blood film examinations

Two experienced medical laboratory technologists performed microscopic examinations of blood films. After air drying, the smears were stained with a 10% Giemsa solution for 10 min. Following the standard WHO malaria microscopy quality assurance manual [16], the stained smears were examined under a light microscope with an oil immersion (100 ×) objective to detect the presence of malaria parasites. The results were classified qualitatively as either negative (no malaria parasite seen), positive for specific Plasmodium species, or mixed infection. Before reporting a negative result, at least 200 high-power fields (100 × objectives) were examined.

Data quality control measures

Both data collectors and supervisors were trained for three days on how to approach study subjects and how to collect both socio-demographic data and blood samples. Before the actual data collection, 5% of the questionnaire was subjected to a pre-test in the Segeno kebele, which is one of the kebeles in the district and was not included in the main study.

Microscopic examination of blood films was done by medical laboratory technologists who did not perform the RDT test on the spot to prevent bias that may come from knowing the RDT result.

The trained supervisors and the principal investigator closely followed the daily completeness and appropriateness of the data collection process.

To ensure a reliable result, the quality of the Giemsa stain was checked by using known negative and positive blood smears, and the RDT kits were checked for expiry dates. Slides were rechecked by two experienced blinded laboratory technologists.

All the test procedures and the interpretation of results were accomplished based on the standard operating procedure.

External quality control was performed at Hawassa University Comprehensive Specialized Hospital, Medical Laboratory Department, and quality control case team on 10% randomly selected negative and all positive slides of the blood film slides, and there were no discrepancies in the results of the slides in accordance with WHO recommendations and the Ethiopian Ministry of Health malaria microscopy quality assurance guidelines [17, 18].

To maintain the quality of the generated data, it was checked again for completeness and cleanliness before starting the analysis.

Data analysis and interpretation

Socio-demographic and laboratory data collected from each study participant were entered and cleaned using Epi-Data Manager version 4.6 and analyzed using the Statistical Package for Social Sciences (SPSS) software version 26 (IBM Corporation, Armonk, NY, USA). Descriptive summaries were used to describe the study variables, and summaries were presented in terms of counts and percentages. Bivariate and multivariable logistic regression results were used to assess the association between the dependent and independent variables. Variables with a p-value less than 0.25 in bivariate analysis were entered into multivariable analysis. An adjusted odds ratio (AOR) with 95% CI was used, and statistical significance was defined at p < 0.05.

Prevalence of malaria

RDTs and microscopy detected Plasmodium infection in 61 (14.3%) and 55 (13.3%) of the 419 participants, respectively. Overall, 61 participants (14.6%; 95% CI: 11.0–18.0) tested positive by at least one diagnostic method. All cases identified by microscopy were also positive by RDT, while five participants were positive only by RDT and not by microscopy.

The relative proportions of Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax infection were 36 (59.0%, 95% CI: 46.3–71.7) and 22 (36.1%, 95% CI: 23.7–48.5), respectively, while the proportion of mixed (Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax) infection was 3 (4.9%, 95% CI: -0.7–10.5) (Fig. 3).

In the multivariable analysis, the odds of malaria infection were significantly higher among participants with no formal education compared to those with secondary education or higher (adjusted odds ratio [AOR]: 2.56; 95% CI: 1.10–5.94). Similarly, participants with low monthly income were nearly three times more likely to contract malaria than those with high income (AOR: 2.97; 95% CI: 1.07–8.25). Individuals without an ITN had higher odds of infection compared to those who owned one (AOR: 2.56; 95% CI: 1.14–5.75), and those who never used an ITN during sleep were almost three times more likely to be infected than regular users (AOR: 2.88; 95% CI: 1.13–7.32). Additionally, households with stagnant water around their compounds had a nearly twofold higher likelihood of malaria infection (AOR: 2.12; 95% CI: 1.14–3.99) (Table 7).

Discussion

The prevalence of malaria in the current study was 14.6% (95% CI: 11.0—18.0), which is in the range reported from several malaria endemic areas of Ethiopia. Similar prevalence rates have been reported from Bahir Dar Zuria district (14.8%) [19], Samre district (14.0%) [20], and South Gondar Zone (14.7%) [21], and systematic reviews and meta-analyses that reported an overall pooled prevalence of 11.10% [22] and 13.61% [23]. The observed similarities may be attributed to shared environmental conditions conducive to mosquito breeding and malaria transmission, as well as the endemic nature of malaria in these settings.

However, malaria prevalence in the current study was higher than that reported in Dembia district (6.7%) [24], Chagni Health Center (7.3%) [3], Guanga Town (8.3%) [25], Ethiopian lowlands (9.3%) [26], Boricha district in Sidama Region (6.1%) [27], Sherkole refugee camp (3.9% and 10.2%) [28, 29], Benna Tsemay district (6.1%) [30], Halaba Special District (9.5%) [31], 6.7% in a recent systematic review and meta-analysis [32], (3.9%) in and Debre Elias district (5%) [33]. These differences are likely related to variations in geographical and ecological characteristics, malaria transmission intensity, study periods, sample sizes, study populations, and the implementation and effectiveness of local malaria prevention and control interventions. Unlike the Dembia study, which focused only on asymptomatic adults, the current study included both symptomatic and asymptomatic individuals, potentially resulting in a higher prevalence estimate. Furthermore, the absence of indoor residual spraying in the study area during the three years preceding the study, combined with the presence of favorable mosquito breeding habitats, may have contributed to the Loka Abaya district's relatively higher malaria prevalence observed.

On the other hand, the prevalence was lower than reports from various parts of Ethiopia compared with the current study, including 18.4% in West Armachiho district [34], 29.0%in Hamusit Health Center [35], 65.9% in Northeast Ethiopia [36], 25.0% in East Shewa Zone [37], 21.1% in Mizan-Aman Town [15], 24.10% in West Guji Zone [32], 32.6% in Woreta town [38], and 21.2% in Waghemira Zone [39]. This variation may be explained by differences in study design, setting, and diagnostic methods. Health facility-based studies, which frequently include individuals seeking treatment, may overestimate prevalence when compared to community-based studies. Geographical variations, high susceptibility to malaria, and frequent exposure may all contribute to variations in prevalence. Conversely, the development of partial immunity through repeated exposure can result in fewer symptomatic cases [40]. Additionally, the timing of the current study, conducted during a period of low malaria transmission (April to May), may also have resulted in lower prevalence estimate.

The result of the current study is also lower than the studies in Africa, including Kano State, Nigeria (60.6%) [41, 42], Ibadan, Nigeria (55.0%) [42], Mozambique (60.6%) [43], Unwanna, Nigeria (54.2%) [44], and Malawi (31.1%) [45]. This may reflect national differences in the national burden. Compared to other endemic countries in Sub-Saharan Africa, Ethiopia has relatively low malaria prevalence. Nigeria, Mozambique, and Malawi have higher rates of malaria cases than Ethiopia [46]. Differences in lifestyle, socioeconomic status, and housing conditions among communities in different nations may contribute to this variation. Moreover, the diagnostic strategy employed in the current study differed from studies conducted in Kano state, Nigeria, and Malawi. These studies utilized a sensitive molecular technique for malaria detection, capable of identifying asymptomatic infections missed by microscopy and RDTs [47].

In the current study, Plasmodium falciparum was the dominant species (59.0%), followed by Plasmodium vivax (36.1%), consistent with Ethiopia's national Plasmodium parasite distribution pattern, where Plasmodium falciparum and Plasmodium vivax cause 60% and 40% of the country's malaria cases, respectively [7] and also, with previous studies conducted in different parts of Ethiopia, including Chagni Heath Center, Debre Elias district, Northeast Ethiopia, Maygaba town, Benna Tsemay district, Waghemira Zone, Kombolcha, Samre district, Sherkole refugee camp, Hamusit Health Center, Lake Tana and surrounding areas, West Armachiho district and Halaba Special District [20, 28–31, 33, 34, 39, 48–52], all reported the predominance of Plasmodium falciparum. And also, comparable with studies conducted in Africa, including Kano State, Nigeria, and Mozambique's Sussundenga District [41, 43]. Its predominance may be linked to adaptation to lowland ecology and transmission dynamics in the study area [53]. In contrast, some studies reported a lower prevalence of Plasmodium falciparum than that of Plasmodium vivax [19, 29, 32]. Although it was difficult to explain the above disparities, they might be caused by variations in topographic adaptations and intrinsic parasite factors [38].

A higher prevalence was observed among symptomatic participants (30.7%) than among asymptomatic individuals (7.3%), which could be explained by partial immunity from repeated exposure, which reduces parasite density and clinical symptoms [54, 55], which are often below the detection level of microscopy and RDTs [47, 56].

Educational status was also significantly associated with malaria infection. Participants with no formal education were nearly three times more likely to be infected with malaria than those with secondary or higher education (AOR: 2.56; 95% CI: 1.10–5.94). This finding aligns with studies conducted in various parts of Ethiopia [25, 34, 50, 57] as well as from other African nations [42, 43]. This association may be explained by the role of education in enhancing awareness of malaria transmission, promoting the adoption of preventive practices, and improving timely utilization of healthcare services, all of which contribute to a lower risk of malaria infection.

Monthly income was also significantly associated with malaria infection. Participants with low monthly income were nearly three times more likely to be infected than those with higher income (AOR: 2.97; 95% CI: 1.07–8.25). This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [13, 29, 54] and supports evidence that malaria disproportionately affects economically disadvantaged populations [54]. The association may be explained by poorer housing conditions, limited access to healthcare, and reduced access to preventive measures among low-income households, all of which increase vulnerability to malaria transmission. In addition, malaria can reduce work productivity and household income, while financial challenges may limit access to treatment and prevention, perpetuating the burden of malaria in these populations [55].

Monthly income was also another significant predictor of malaria infection. Participants with low monthly income were nearly three times more likely to contract malaria compared to those with higher income (AOR: 2.97; 95% CI: 1.07–8.25). This finding is consistent with previous studies conducted in Ethiopia [15, 33, 58] and supports evidence indicating that malaria disproportionately affects economically disadvantaged populations [58]. This association might be explained by poorer housing conditions, limited access to healthcare, and limited access to preventive measures among low-income households, all of which may lead to malaria transmission. Furthermore, malaria prevalence in Ethiopia is closely linked to agricultural seasons, as agriculture is the main source of livelihood for much of the population. Transmission often increases during planting and harvesting periods when mosquito populations rise. Malaria-related illness can reduce productivity and income, while limited financial resources may hinder access to prevention and treatment services, contributing to the continued burden of malaria among low-income populations [59].

Environmental factors also played a vital role in malaria transmission. Participants living near stagnant water were more than twice as likely to contract malaria compared to those living farther from stagnant water (AOR = 2.12; 95% CI: 1.14–3.99). This finding is consistent with studies conducted across different parts of Ethiopia [15, 24, 25, 32, 39, 50, 60]. Stagnant water provides favorable breeding sites for Anopheles mosquitoes, increasing human-vector contact and the risk of malaria transmission. This finding reveals the continuing importance of environmental management interventions as part of Ethiopia's national malaria control strategy [18].

Long-lasting insecticidal nets (LLINs) remain one of the most effective malaria prevention interventions in Ethiopia. The Federal Ministry of Health (FMOH) recognizes LLINs as critical for reducing malaria transmission, morbidity, and mortality [6]. In the current study, participants who did not regularly use ITNs were nearly three times more likely to develop malaria than regular users (AOR: 2.88; 95% CI: 1.13–7.32). Similarly, households without at least one ITN were significantly more likely to report malaria infection (AOR: 2.56; 95% CI: 1.14–5.75). These findings are consistent with previous studies conducted in different regions of Ethiopia [15, 33, 34, 39, 50, 60]. This may reflect negative attitudes toward ITN use or inadequate knowledge regarding their correct utilization, as reported by Belay et al. [25].

Although ITN ownership was relatively high (79.7%), only 44.3% of households met the Monitoring and Evaluation Reference Group and WHO recommendation of one ITN per two individuals [61, 62]. indicating a gap between ownership and effective coverage. Strengthening equitable distribution and ensuring adequate ITN access remain essential for malaria control and elimination efforts.

Strengths and limitations of the study

Because this is a community-based cross-sectional study, it was useful to evaluate asymptomatic people who were important carriers of disease in the area and enable them to receive treatment to decrease disease transmission. Furthermore, this study aided in the direct observation of the participants' housing conditions; availability, conditions, and usage of ITNs, as well as the assessment of the presence of stagnant water around the house in the study area. One of the study's limitations was the failure to use more sensitive diagnostic methods, such as molecular diagnosis using PCR, to further confirm the results obtained through RDT and microscopic examinations.

Conclusion and recommendations

In conclusion, the findings of this study indicate that malaria infection was high in the Loka Abaya district community. Plasmodium falciparum was found to be the most common Plasmodium species in the study area. ITNs were distributed to the households, but none of the houses had been sprayed by the IRS in the three years before the study period. In terms of associated factors, educational status, monthly income, ITN availability and utilization, and the presence of stagnant water around the house were all significantly associated with malaria infection.
