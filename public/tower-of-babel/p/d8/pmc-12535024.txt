Clinical insights and management outcomes of tracheoesophageal and bronchoesophageal fistulas: a 20-year case series review
Authors: Salem Algomaa Alhadid, Sakhr Alshwayyat, Hamdah Hanifa, Noor Almasri, Zaid Taimeh, Mustafa Alshwayyat, Tala Abdulsalam Alshwayyat, Alhareth M Amro, Bassam Darwish
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAbnormal connections between the trachea and esophagus, known as tracheoesophageal fistula (TEFs) and bronchoesophageal fistula (BEFs), are uncommon but potentially life-threatening conditions that can result in severe complications. This study aimed to explore the anatomical features, underlying causes of TEF and BEF and evaluate the treatment efficacy.MethodsThis retrospective study reviewed 15 cases of TEF/BEF treated at the University Hospital from 2001 to 2020. It examines the anatomical features, underlying causes, and treatment outcomes of these fistulas and evaluates various treatment modalities, such as open surgery, tracheal and esophageal stenting, and gastrostomy/jejunostomy.ResultsThis study included 15 patients (aged 15–65 years). Most cases were non-malignant (80%), mainly due to post-intubation injuries, while 20% were associated with malignancies, such as lymphoma and esophageal cancer. The fistulas were mostly located in the mid-trachea, with 40% measuring 1–3 cm. Gastrostomy/jejunostomy is the most common treatment option, particularly in malignant cases. The morbidity rate was 46.7%, with tracheal stenosis being the most frequent complication and no deaths reported. Follow-up (1–23 months) showed significant improvement in benign TEFs but no improvement in malignant TEFs.ConclusionThis study highlights the challenges in managing TEF/BEF and advocates treatment plans tailored to the specific etiology and characteristics of the fistula. These findings contribute to the limited existing literature on acquired TEF/BEF, suggesting that a multidisciplinary approach is essential for optimizing patient outcomes in these complex cases.Keywords: Bronchoesophageal fistula, Esophageal neoplasms, Morbidity, Tracheoesophageal fistula, Treatment outcome

Introduction

Tracheoesophageal fistula (TEF) and bronchoesophageal fistula (BEF) are defined as abnormal connections, respectively between the trachea and the esophagus, and the bronchi and the esophagus, leading to significant respiratory complications. TEF can be acquired or congenital, each with distinct causes and implications for management [1–4].

Acquired tracheoesophageal fistulas are rare, but serious complications that often result from iatrogenic injuries. These are especially prevalent in patients requiring prolonged mechanical ventilation or feeding tube insertion. Such injuries typically arise from cuff-related damage caused by overinflated endotracheal tubes, which can lead to pressure necrosis [2, 3, 5]. Additionally, complications from feeding tubes in critically ill patients can lead to TEFs and BEFs, as highlighted in various case reports [1]. Moreover, the risk is heightened during prolonged mechanical ventilation, sometimes resulting in undiagnosed TEFs that can cause recurrent respiratory infections and significant nutritional issues due to the continuous spillage of gastric contents into the respiratory tract [5, 6].

The management of these fistulas presents significant challenges due to various health complications. Both TEFs and BEFs often result in aspiration, leading to recurrent pneumonia and other respiratory complications, particularly in the ICU setting [3, 7]. Patients frequently experience severe weight loss and malnutrition owing to swallowing difficulties [2]. Surgical repair, while often necessary, carries risks such as anastomotic leaks, strictures, and recurrent fistulas [6]. Survivors may face long-term issues, such as gastroesophageal reflux and dysphagia, with potential risks of esophageal malignancy [6]. Effective management requires ongoing multidisciplinary support to address immediate and long-term health needs [6].

The current treatment options for TEFs and BEFs include surgical and endoscopic techniques. Endoscopic repair has emerged as a less invasive alternative with variable success rates. Techniques, such as bronchoscopic obliteration, have shown promise in managing both primary and recurrent TEFs [8–10]. For malignant TEFs and BEFs, palliative measures such as endobronchial laser debridement and esophageal stenting have been utilized to provide symptom relief and improve the quality of life [11]. Surgical approaches, such as thoracoacromial artery perforator flap, have demonstrated safety and effectiveness, with no complications reported in a small cohort [12]. Customized prostheses may be considered for patients after laryngectomy, especially when surgical closure is unsuccessful [13]. Additionally, innovative methods such as magnetic compression have been explored and have shown feasibility in animal models, suggesting their potential clinical application in humans [14].

Our study aimed to explore the anatomical features and underlying causes of TEF and BEF. It also seeks to evaluate the efficacy of various treatment approaches including surgical and endoscopic methods.

Materials and methods

Between 2001 and 2020, 15 patients were admitted to the Department of Surgery at the University Hospital, located in Damascus, with a diagnosis of TEF/BEF. Clinical data, including medical records and imaging studies, for these patients were retrospectively reviewed in this case series. By employing descriptive statistics, we analyzed the anatomical characteristics and etiologies of TEF/BEF, the management methods employed, and the treatment outcomes after follow-up. This study aimed to identify these variables and present our department’s experience in managing these rare cases. The goal was to develop a systematic approach for the treatment of tracheoesophageal and bronchoesophageal fistulas. The study was conducted with the approval of the Institutional Ethics Committee (IEC) at the Faculty of Medicine, Damascus University, which reviewed and approved the research from an ethical standpoint. This work is described in accordance with the criteria of SCARE [15].

Results

The ages of the 15 patients ranged from 15 to 65 years, with a male predominance (60%) compared to females (40%). The causes of acquired TEF and BEF varied. Malignant causes accounted for 20% of cases, including lymphoma in two patients and esophageal cancer in one patient. The remaining 80% of cases were attributed to nonmalignant causes, which included post-intubation injuries in nine patients, trauma in two patients, and iatrogenic causes in one patient (Table 1).

Profile of patient demographics

Among the traumatic TEFs and BEFs, one patient sustained penetrating chest trauma from a shrapnel injury, resulting in a BEF. The other patient experienced a cut wound to the neck, which led to both esophageal and tracheal injuries. Initially, the esophageal and tracheal damage were repaired, but a traumatic TEF subsequently developed. Additionally, an iatrogenic TEF occurred due to the presence of a stent placed in the esophagus three months prior for the treatment of esophageal stenosis.

Most fistulas measured between 1 and 3 cm in diameter (40%). Smaller fistulas, less than 1 cm, were observed in 20% of patients, while giant fistulas, greater than 5 cm, were found in 20% of patients. Most of the fistulas were in the mid-trachea (53.3%), with fewer cases in the upper trachea (33.3%), and one case each in the lower trachea (6.7%) and left bronchus (6.7%) (Table 2).

Anatomic characteristics of the TEFs

Most patients (80%) had not received prior treatment for their TEFs or BEFs. Among the cases that had undergone previous treatments, one patient had surgery via a cervical approach to repair esophageal and tracheal injuries from a cut wound, which later developed into a traumatic TEF. Additionally, two patients had previously received tracheal stenting (Table 3).

Procedures of management

Gastrostomy or jejunostomy was performed in six patients as a sole procedure. This approach was used for all three patients with malignant causes of TEF (Table 4).

Among the patients who underwent open surgery, four received tracheal management. This included tracheal foramen closure in three patients and tracheal resection and reconstruction in one patient (Table 4).

Esophageal closure was managed with direct esophageal closure in five patients and esophageal stenting in one patient. Among the cases that involved surgery, a sternocleidomastoid muscle flap was placed between the esophagus and trachea in only two patients. Notably, one of these patients developed esophageal leakage following the placement of the muscle flap (Table 4).

The follow-up period for patients ranged from 1 to 23 months, with an average duration of 6.7 months. The morbidity rate was 46.7%, with no recorded deaths. During the follow-up period, the most common complication was tracheal stenosis, occurring in four patients. Among these, one patient underwent tracheal resection, while three patients received tracheal dilation (Table 5).

Outcomes of management

At the end of the follow-up period, among the 12 patients with benign TEFs, there was an improvement in the fistula status: two patients experienced some improvement, and ten patients showed good improvement. In contrast, no improvement was observed in the three patients with malignant causes of TEF (Table 5).

Discussion

Due to the disease’s rarity limited outdated data exist on acquired esophageal fistulas and their clinical features [16, 17]. This retrospective study provides an insight on different TEF/BEF cases which could contribute to further understanding of these diseases and management of future cases. Most cases were acquired non- malignantly from post-intubation.

Over the course of 20 years, only 15 cases were with a male predominance (60%). While this finding may reflect a true demographic trend, it could also be incidental given the small sample size. This observation aligns with a recent 2020 study that showed that males were more susceptible of acquiring TEF/BEF in advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (98.2%, p = 0.035) [18]. Another 2024 paper studying the impact of sex on the management and prognosis of esophageal fistulas acquired from atrial fibrillation, where females represented only 47% of the cases which aligns with our findings [19]. Wang et al. studied acquired esophageal fistulas and considered sex as a significant prognostic factor affecting survival outcomes [20]. However, our study was not designed to investigate epidemiological associations, and no statistical tests were conducted to assess sex-based risks. Therefore, no definitive conclusion can be drawn from this observation.

Our study confirms that malignancies are rarely associated with TEF/BEF (20%). Other studies yielded similar results. A retrospective analysis of 136 esophageal squamous cell carcinoma patients treated with chemotherapy only one third (30.1%) developed esophageal fistulas [15]. Rodriguez et al. proposed that TEF/BEF are associated with late stages of esophageal and lung malignant tumors [21], Anderloni et al. confirmed that and suggested that it is acquired due to the invasion in late stages or as an adverse effect of cancer therapies mainly chemoradiotherapy [22]. This could help in better understanding and management of TEF/BEF in cancer.

The location of the fistulas could directly influence the management and treatment. Our study found that most fistulas were in the mid-trachea (53.3%). However, a 2015 study about malignant TEFs said that the most common locations were the lower trachea (34.9%) followed by mid-trachea (28.6%) [23], the difference in findings could have been influenced by the primary site of the tumor.

Treatment of TEF/BEFs varied. The most common procedures were gastrostomy/jejunostomy solely (40%), with all malignant cases undergoing this procedure (n = 3), followed by open surgery. Previous studies considered gastrostomy/jejunostomy the golden standard to treat TEF especially malignant cases [24, 25]. Hu et al. argued that gastrostomy/jejunostomy had emotional and financial difficulties and is associated with respiratory and eating-related symptom when compared to stenting [26]. Chen et al. agreed with that suggesting that esophageal stents significantly improve overall survival and may be considered the first line of treatment for patients with malignant [24]. Given the palliative intent of treatment for malignant cases, direct comparisons of outcomes with benign TEFs are not appropriate. Instead, the treatment should be tailored to the underlying pathology and clinical goals.

During the follow-up, our cohort showed a morbidity rate of 46.7%. The most frequent complication was tracheal stenosis (26.6%), which was managed with resection or dilation. Our morbidity data primarily reflected the local procedure-related complications. Broader systemic morbidities in adult surgical populations, such as atrial fibrillation, bleeding, stroke, myocardial infarction, and thromboembolic events, were not observed in our retrospective review. Owing to the rarity of acquired TEF/BEF and patients’ debilitated status, comprehensive morbidity data remain limited. These patients are often medically fragile and at high risk of multiple complications, warranting detailed prospective studies.

This research draws its strengths from an extensive two-decade analysis of TEF/BEF. This long-term perspective offers valuable insights into the treatment results and management approaches, making a substantial contribution to the scarce literature on these uncommon conditions. The inclusion of comprehensive clinical data and follow-up evaluations enhanced the reliability of our findings. Nevertheless, our study was limited by several factors. First, its retrospective nature introduces risks of selection bias and incomplete data capture. Second, the sample size of 15 patients restricted the statistical power and limited the generalizability of our findings. Furthermore, we did not include a control or comparator group, which prevented causal inferences between the treatment modalities and outcomes. Additionally, data on systemic morbidities (e.g., cardiovascular events and thromboembolism) were not available, limiting the full assessment of the postoperative risk. These factors should be considered when interpreting the results of this study. Finally, the small sample limited subgroup comparisons, and individual case analysis may offer better insight than aggregate tables.

Conclusion

Our study presents a detailed retrospective analysis of TEF/BEFs over a span of two decades, highlighting the crucial aspects of their clinical presentation, management, and outcomes. The findings underscore the predominance of non-malignant causes in the development of these fistulas, with a notable prevalence in males and significant implications for treatment strategies. Most cases were managed successfully with a combination of surgical and non-surgical interventions, indicating the efficacy of current treatment protocols in a challenging medical scenario. This study contributes to existing knowledge on these rare conditions and offers a foundation for future research and potential improvements in clinical practice.
