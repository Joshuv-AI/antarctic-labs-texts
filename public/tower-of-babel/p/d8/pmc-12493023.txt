Oral Diet Outcomes and Tracheostomy Avoidance After Submental Flap Reconstruction of Oral and Oropharyngeal Defects
Authors: Michael P. Wu, Edward S. Sim, Tessa Goldsmith, Matthew Kovoor, Derrick T. Lin, Jeremy D. Richmon, Daniel G. Deschler
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTObjectivesThe submental island flap can be used to reconstruct defects of the oral cavity and oropharynx. The objective of this study is to describe functional outcomes after this reconstruction.MethodsRetrospective chart review was performed for 82 patients who underwent submental flap reconstruction of oral cavity cancer defects and 11 with oropharyngeal cancer defects. Primary outcomes were feeding tube use at last follow‐up and time from surgery to first oral intake. The secondary outcome was the need for tracheostomy.ResultsFeeding tube use at last follow‐up was 9% in the oral cavity group and 36% in the oropharynx group. Median time to oral intake was 7 days in both groups. Tracheostomy was avoided in 87% of oral cavity patients and in 46% of oropharynx patients. In regression analysis of the entire cohort, the outcomes of persistent feeding tube use at last follow‐up and tracheostomy were associated with the following variables: prior irradiation, oropharyngeal primary site, and adjuvant treatment status. Among the oral cavity group, prior irradiation was associated with persistent feeding tube use.ConclusionThe submental flap can be used in a wide range of oral cavity and oropharyngeal defects, even in elderly patients. This flap can be performed for oral cancers with avoidance of tracheostomy in most cases and reliable resumption of oral diet.Level of Evidence4

Introduction

The submental island flap can be used for reconstruction of a variety of defects ranging from the oral cavity (OC) to the skull base [1, 2, 3, 4]. The vascular supply of the flap is the submental vessels, which arise from the facial artery and vein. At our institution, the submental flap has been used extensively as a regional flap for the reconstruction of OC defects. Reported advantages of the submental flap are the potential for short operative times and reliability of regional flap reconstruction [5, 6]. It remains unclear whether these advantages translate into favorable short‐term and long‐term functional outcomes. In this brief report, we describe oral diet outcomes and the ability to avoid tracheostomy in patients undergoing submental island flap reconstruction for a wide range of OC and oropharyngeal (OP) cancer defects.

Materials and Methods

This study was approved by the Massachusetts General Brigham IRB (protocol #2019P000744). Retrospective chart review was performed for patients > 18 years of age who underwent submental flap reconstruction of oral cavity and oropharyngeal cancer defects between April 2016 and July 2023. Primary outcomes were feeding tube use at last follow‐up and time from surgery to first oral (PO) intake. The secondary outcome was the need for tracheostomy. Variables obtained from chart review included tumor characteristics, defect subsites, previous radiation history, adjuvant treatment, flap size, operative time, length of stay, diet type recommended by speech‐language pathology (SLP), and oncologic outcomes.

Logistic regression analysis was used to identify variables associated with feeding tube use at last follow‐up and tracheostomy at the time of surgery. All patients from the OC and OP were pooled together for regression analysis. Univariate logistic regression was performed for all variables, and variables with a statistically significant association (p < 0.05) with the outcomes were then selected as the initial independent variables for backward stepwise multivariable logistic regression. Using backward stepwise regression, independent variables were then removed from the statistical model until only significant variables remained. All statistical analysis was performed using RStudio v2024.12.1 (RStudio, Boston).

At the time of this series, there were three surgeons performing submental flaps at our institution, with the highest volume surgeon performing between 11 and 22 submental flaps per year for OC and OP reconstruction. Our technique for raising the submental island flap has been previously reported [7]. For OC defects, the flap was passed through the floor of the mouth defect for inset. For OP defects, the flap was passed through the parapharyngeal space. The decision to perform tracheostomy was at the discretion of the surgeon. At our institution, when using the submental flap, we generally favor avoidance of tracheostomy for defects of the OC that do not extend posteriorly into the OP. In cases requiring segmental mandibulectomy, a mandibular reconstruction plate was used to span the bony defect, and the soft tissue defect was reconstructed with the submental flap.

Results

Ninety‐three patients underwent submental flap reconstruction, of which 82 were for OC primary tumors and 11 for OP tumors (Table 1). Overall, the mean age was 71 and there were 4 (4%) patients with a history of major perioperative cardiopulmonary events (myocardial infarction, stroke/transient ischemic attack, pulmonary complications). In the OC group, ages ranged from 44 to 93 years (mean 72), with 26 (32%) patients over 80 years. Ten (12%) patients in the OC group underwent tracheostomy at the time of ablative surgery. One patient initially did not undergo tracheostomy but had a hematoma on POD2 and underwent tracheostomy at the time of hematoma evacuation. Ten (12%) had segmental mandibulectomy defects reconstructed with the submental flap and a reconstruction bar. In the OC group, median total operative time (including cancer ablation) was 3 h and 39 min. Median time to PO intake was 7 days (Table 2). Mean follow‐up was 21.4 months and 7 (9%) patients had at least partial feeding tube dependence at last follow‐up. Fifty‐seven (70%) and 66 (80%) patients were on a diet with both solids and liquids at the time of discharge and at last follow‐up, respectively. There was 1 (1%) flap failure.

Of the 10 patients who underwent segmental mandibulectomy with submental flap reconstruction, three (30%) underwent tracheostomy (Table 3). Median total operative time (including cancer ablation) was 4 h and 50 min. Median time to PO intake was 7 days. With a mean follow‐up time of 14 months, at last follow‐up, 2 (20%) of the segmental mandibulectomy patients had at least partial feeding tube dependence. The remaining 8 (80%) patients were taking a diet of both solids and liquids at last follow‐up without feeding tube use. One patient ultimately had the reconstruction plate removed due to extrusion.

In the OP group, ages ranged from 43 to 83 years (mean 64). Eight (73%) patients in this group had been previously irradiated. Seven (64%) patients underwent tracheostomy. Median total operative time (including cancer ablation) was 5 h and 34 min. Median time to PO intake was 7 days. Mean follow‐up time was 25.7 months and 4 (36%) patients had at least partial feeding tube dependence at last follow‐up. Overall, 81 (87%) patients underwent evaluation by speech‐language pathology prior to discharge.

In the OC group, there were 13 recurrences (16%), of which 8 were local (10%), 3 regional (4%), and 2 (2%) distant. 25 patients in the OC group did not receive adjuvant treatment, and 2 (8%) had local recurrence. There were 3 (27%) recurrences in the OP group, of which 2 (18%) were local and 1 (9%) distant.

In univariate logistic regression analysis for the outcome of persistent feeding tube use at last follow‐up, OP primary site (p = 0.0052), prior irradiation (p = 0.00023), and lack of adjuvant treatment (p = 0.0042) were associated with persistent feeding tube use (Table 4). In the multivariate regression model, prior irradiation (p = 0.00014) and lack of adjuvant treatment (p = 0.0071) remained significant predictor variables (Table S1). In univariate regression, OP primary site (p = 0.00036) and prior irradiation (p = 0.015) were associated with tracheostomy (Table 5). In multivariate regression, only OP primary site (p = 0.000017) remained a significant predictor of tracheostomy (Table S2). Due to heterogeneity in the OC and OP groups, these groups were also analyzed separately. In univariate logistic regression for the outcome of feeding tube dependence using the OC group of 82 patients, prior history of irradiation was the only statistically significant associated variable (logistic regression coefficient 2.5, 95% confidence interval 0.29–4.6, p = 0.017). There were no variables associated with tracheostomy in the OC group. In the OP group, there were no variables with statistically significant associations with either feeding tube dependence or tracheostomy.

Discussion

Many defects that result from OP and OC cancers are ideally reconstructed with a pliable fasciocutaneous flap to replace the mucosal lining of the defect and separate the upper aerodigestive tract from the neck. While free flaps have become a workhorse reconstructive option, the increased cost, operative time, and the need for microvascular resources demanded by free tissue transfer make regional reconstructive options attractive in select patients [5, 8]. Patients who are elderly, frail, or with significant cardiopulmonary comorbidities may benefit from the use of the submental flap. In our OC submental flap cohort, 32% of whom were over age 80, we report short operative times with a mean of 3.5 h for both cancer ablation and reconstruction, with favorable oral diet outcomes and successful avoidance of tracheostomy in 87% of patients. Persistent feeding tube use was rare at 9%, even with large and complex defects, with our cohort including 34% T4a tumors and 15% of patients undergoing segmental mandibulectomy. Our results are consistent with other reports, some of which have compared the submental flap to the radial forearm free flap [9, 10, 11]. These reports have found the submental flap to have comparable outcomes to the radial forearm flap, with shorter operative time and hospital stays for the submental flaps [9]. In this study, we characterized outcomes after submental flap reconstruction but did not aim to directly compare submental flaps to free flaps, as we feel both can be effective reconstructions in the appropriate situation.

Among pedicled flap options, the thinness of the submental flap is a major advantage when compared to the pectoralis major flap, and this facilitates tracheostomy avoidance. The ability to safely avoid tracheostomy in most cases may be particularly beneficial for frail patients [12, 13, 14]. However, because we did not compare the submental flap to free flap reconstruction, our data cannot assess the relative likelihood of tracheostomy avoidance with different reconstruction types. It is possible that, had several patients in this series undergone free tissue transfer, tracheostomy could still have been avoided, particularly in more limited OC tumors. Regardless, our data show that tracheostomy avoidance is safe in most OC resections reconstructed with the submental flap. Furthermore, one possible rationale for tracheostomy in free flap patients aside from edema‐related airway obstruction is for ease and safety of airway management in the event of return to the operating room for microvascular revision. This concern is mitigated with a pedicled reconstruction such as the submental flap.

Among the OC cohort, the 10 patients who underwent segmental mandibulectomy with reconstruction using a submental flap and reconstruction bar highlight potential advantages in the frail, medically comorbid population. Operative times for cases involving osteocutaneous free tissue transfer are generally longer than those for soft‐tissue‐only defects [8], and this increased anesthesia time can pose a significant risk in the medically frail population. High‐volume centers have reported overall surgical times of 9–10 h for cases involving fibula and scapula free flap reconstruction of the head and neck [15, 16]. In contrast, in this series, the median total operative time including cancer ablation for cases involving segmental mandibulectomy with submental flap reconstruction was 4 h and 50 min, with acceptable oral diet outcomes. To our knowledge, this is the largest series in the literature describing outcomes using the submental flap after segmental mandibulectomy [17, 18]. Additionally, we have found the submental flap to be a reliable reconstruction for surgical salvage of OP cancers, but diet outcomes are more guarded. In the regression analysis of our cohort, oropharyngeal primary site and prior irradiation were factors associated with persistent feeding tube use. The additional association between persistent feeding tube use and lack of adjuvant treatment is counterintuitive. However, this is likely explained by confounding from the association between salvage oropharynx cases and lack of adjuvant treatment, as evidenced by the multivariate analysis.

There have been oncologic concerns regarding the submental flap in OC cancer because the submental tissue includes the level IA lymphatics. However, multiple studies have shown no increase in the local or regional failure with use of the submental flap [19, 20, 21]. Our data further support the oncologic safety of the submental flap in oral cavity reconstruction, with local recurrence rates comparable with the literature [19], and notably only TWO recurrences in patients who did not receive any adjuvant treatment.

This study is limited by its retrospective single‐institutional nature and reliance on diet and feeding tube status as functional outcomes. Standardized functional assessment tools for swallowing were not consistently recorded in practice and therefore were unavailable for retrospective analysis. Furthermore, logistic regression of the OP group was limited due to small sample size. Future multi‐institutional data with prospective comprehensive functional assessments [22] and patient‐reported outcomes may help guide clinicians on the relative benefits of different reconstructive options.

Conclusion

The submental island flap can be used in the reconstruction of a wide range of oral cavity and oropharyngeal defects. For oral cavity reconstruction, rates of persistent feeding tube use are low, and tracheostomy can be avoided in most cases. Previous irradiation and oropharyngeal primary tumors are associated with higher rates of persistent feeding tube use.
