Comparison of heart rate measurement accuracy among commercially available photoplethysmography-based wearable devices across exercise intensities
Authors: Yoonyoung Jeong, Sehwa Yoon, Jaehee Lee, Woo-Min Jeong, Min-Seong Ha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Abstract[Purpose]We compared the heart rate (HR) measurement accuracy of commercially available PPG-based wearable devices against an ECG-based reference device across different exercise intensities.[Methods]Forty healthy young adults were recruited, and data from 37 participants were analyzed. HR was simultaneously measured using Polar H10 as the ECG-based reference and four PPG-based devices: Polar Verity Sense, Polar Pacer Pro, Apple Watch SE, and Galaxy Watch 6. Participants completed low-, moderate-, and high-intensity treadmill exercise. Accuracy was assessed using mean absolute error (MAE), mean absolute percentage error (MAPE), Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC), and Bland-Altman analysis.[Results]MAE and MAPE generally decreased as intensity increased. Significant differences in MAE were observed among the devices across intensities (p = 0.002). The Polar Verity Sense and Apple Watch SE showed significantly lower MAE than the Polar Pacer Pro and Galaxy Watch 6. During low-intensity exercise, the Apple Watch SE showed lower MAE than the Polar Pacer Pro and Galaxy Watch 6, whereas during high-intensity exercise, the Polar Verity Sense showed lower MAE than the Galaxy Watch 6. CCC values increased with exercise intensity and were consistently higher for the Polar Verity Sense and Apple Watch SE. Bland-Altman analysis showed narrower limits of agreement for the Apple Watch SE and Polar Verity Sense.[Conclusion]HR measurement accuracy differed substantially among the PPG-based wearable devices. The Apple Watch SE and Polar Verity Sense showed the highest agreement with the ECG-based reference, suggesting that wearable HR data should be interpreted based on device-specific validation.Keywords: wearable devices, photoplethysmography, heart rate monitoring, exercise intensity, measurement accuracy

INTRODUCTION

Wearable devices (WDs) have emerged as a key technology for monitoring health status through the continuous collection of physiological data, enabling personalized healthcare and remote monitoring applications [1,2]. Physiological information obtained from WDs has been used for the early detection of clinical deterioration, development of treatment strategies, exercise prescription, and remote patient monitoring, thereby improving access to healthcare services without temporal or geographical constraints [3,4]. Among various physiological indicators, heart rate (HR) is one of the most widely used parameters in WD-based monitoring systems because it plays a critical role in regulating exercise intensity, assessing cardiovascular status, monitoring autonomic nervous system responses, and detecting abnormal physiological conditions [5,6].

Traditionally, electrocardiography (ECG) has been regarded as the gold standard for assessing cardiac activity. By directly measuring the electrical activity of the heart through electrodes, ECG provides a high level of accuracy and reliability [7]. However, the requirement for chest electrodes and lead wires limits its practicality during exercise and in free-living conditions. Furthermore, its application to long-term monitoring and large-scale population assessment remains challenging [8,9]. To overcome these limitations, photoplethysmography (PPG)-based wearable devices have gained widespread popularity. PPG sensors detect changes in blood volume by measuring variations in the reflection or transmission of light through the skin, enabling noninvasive and continuous monitoring of physiological signals such as HR [10]. PPG-based WD are lightweight, easy to wear, and suitable for prolonged monitoring in exercise and daily-life settings [11]. Consequently, their applications have expanded across various healthcare domains, including early screening for cardiovascular diseases, post-discharge vital sign monitoring, and long-term health surveillance [12,13].

Despite their increasing use, the accuracy of PPG-based WDs must be rigorously validated before they can be considered reliable alternatives or complements to ECG in clinical and exercise settings [14,15]. In particular, previous studies have reported that motion artifacts caused by body movement, changes in sensor-to-skin contact pressure, and trunk vibration during exercise may compromise HR measurement accuracy [16-18]. In contrast, some evidence suggests that exercise-induced increases in peripheral blood flow may enhance signal quality, thereby improving measurement accuracy at higher exercise intensities [19]. Furthermore, because PPG relies on optical sensing technology, measurement performance may be influenced by factors such as skin tone, device fit, and sensor placement.

Although a wide range of commercially available PPG-based WDs is currently available, each device operates using different sensor characteristics, signal-processing algorithms, and hardware configurations. Therefore, the accuracy of individual devices should be evaluated independently. Moreover, device placement, such as on the wrist or upper arm, may affect susceptibility to movement artifacts and skin contact quality, potentially resulting in differences in measurement performance [20]. Existing validation studies are limited by variations in device selection, reference standards, and exercise protocols, making it difficult to directly compare the relative performance of multiple devices under identical conditions [21]. Accordingly, there is a need for direct comparisons of PPG-based WDs from different manufacturers and with different wear locations under standardized testing conditions.

During exercise, HR continuously changes in response to fluctuations in exercise intensity. Consequently, HR values obtained immediately after transitions between exercise stages may be affected by transient physiological responses and sensor stabilization processes. In contrast, HR measurements obtained during the latter part of each exercise stage are more likely to reflect a stabilized cardiovascular response to the corresponding intensity level. Therefore, these measurements may provide a standardized basis for comparing the accuracy of different wearable devices under equivalent physiological conditions.

Accordingly, the purpose of this study was to compare and validate the HR measurement accuracy of PPG-based wearable devices from different manufacturers and with different wear locations under various exercise intensities, using ECG-derived HR as the criterion measure within the same participants.

METHODS

Participants

This study was conducted in healthy young adults to minimize the potential confounding effects of age- and disease-related physiological changes on HR measurements. The required sample size was estimated using G*Power, version 3.1. Based on a repeated-measures analysis of variance (ANOVA), an effect size of f = 0.25, a significance level of α = 0.05, and statistical power (1 − β) of 0.80 were assumed [22]. An effect size of f = 0.25 was selected as a medium effect size according to Cohen’s convention and was considered appropriate because previous wearable HR validation studies have reported meaningful differences in measurement errors among devices. Considering the estimated minimum sample size and the possibility of participant dropout, a total of 40 participants were recruited.

Participants were recruited through convenience sampling from students enrolled at S University. Prior to participation, all individuals received a detailed explanation of the study objectives and procedures and provided written informed consent. The study protocol was approved by the Institutional Review Board of the University of Seoul (UOS 2023-10-021-001), and all procedures were conducted in accordance with the ethical principles of the Declaration of Helsinki. Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) healthy adults aged 19-29 years and (2) the ability to perform high-intensity running exercise. The exclusion criteria were as follows: (1) a diagnosis of any chronic disease, including cardiovascular, respiratory, or metabolic disorders; (2) skin conditions or injuries that could interfere with wearable device placement; (3) musculoskeletal or respiratory limitations that prevented participation in moderate- to high-intensity running; and (4) current use of medications that could influence HR or blood flow responses.

Study Design

This study compared and validated the HR measurement accuracy of four PPG-based wearable devices against that of an ECG-based reference device. The Polar H10 (PH; Polar Electro Oy, Kempele, Finland), a chest-mounted ECG device, was used as the criterion measure. The Polar H10 was selected as the reference device because previous validation studies have demonstrated excellent agreement with standard ECG measurements under resting and exercise conditions. It has shown high accuracy in HR monitoring and has been widely used as a criterion measure in wearable sensor validation studies [23,24]. The PPG-based devices included the Polar Verity Sense (PV; Polar Electro Oy), Polar Pacer Pro (PP; Polar Electro Oy), Apple Watch SE (AW; Apple Inc., CA, USA), and Galaxy Watch 6 (GW; Samsung, Suwon, Republic of Korea). All participants completed a single exercise protocol while simultaneously wearing the PH and four PPG-based devices. To ensure consistency in measurement locations, the PH was worn on the chest, the PV on the upper arm, and the PP, AW, and GW on the wrist. To minimize the potential effects of arm dominance and physiological differences between the left and right arms, the placement of the PPG-based devices was determined using simple randomization. The characteristics of the wearable devices used in this study are listed in Table 2.

Exercise Protocol

Following a 10-min seated rest period, the participants performed three consecutive treadmill exercise stages consisting of low-intensity (4 min), moderate-intensity (4 min), and high-intensity (4 min) exercise. Exercise intensity was prescribed according to the American College of Sports Medicine (ACSM) guidelines using age-predicted maximal HR (220 − age). Target HR ranges were defined as 57- 63% of maximal HR for low-intensity exercise, 64-76% for moderate-intensity exercise, and 77-95% for high-intensity exercise. The treadmill speed was individually adjusted to ensure that participants reached the target HR range at each exercise stage. HR data were collected simultaneously from the PH, PV, PP, AW, and GW throughout the exercise protocol. Because PPG-based sensors may exhibit transient signal instability immediately following increases in exercise intensity, HR values obtained 3 min 30 s after the start of each exercise stage were analyzed to reflect stabilized cardiovascular responses. HR values at each time point were independently recorded and verified by two investigators. A study flowchart and an overview of the exercise protocol are shown in Figure 1.

Statistical Analysis

Among the 40 enrolled participants, three exhibited extreme measurement errors exceeding ± 30 beats per minute (bpm) relative to the criterion device under specific combinations of exercise intensity and wearable device. These errors were not consistently observed across all devices or exercise intensities and were therefore considered attributable to temporary signal acquisition failure or improper device placement. Specifically, one participant was excluded from the AW analysis due to excessive error during moderate- and high-intensity exercise, one participant was excluded from the PP analysis due to excessive error during low- and high-intensity exercise, and one participant was excluded from the GW analysis due to excessive error during low- and high-intensity exercise. To maintain consistency in the exclusion criteria and preserve the balance required for repeated-measures analyses, data from these three participants were excluded. Consequently, data from 37 participants were included in the final analysis. The accuracy of HR measurements obtained from the PPG-based devices was evaluated relative to the criterion device using mean absolute error (MAE) and mean absolute percentage error (MAPE).

The main effects of exercise intensity and device type, as well as their interaction, were assessed using repeated-measures ANOVA. When significant effects were identified, Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc analyses were performed. Skin type was classified according to the Fitzpatrick Skin Type Classification, and independent-samples t-tests were conducted for participants classified as skin types III and IV because these groups provided sufficient sample sizes for comparison. Agreement between each wearable device and the criterion device was assessed using Bland-Altman analysis, from which the mean bias and 95% limits of agreement (LoA) were calculated.

In addition, Lin’s concordance correlation coefficient (CCC) was calculated to further evaluate agreement between devices. CCC values were interpreted as poor (< 0.90), moderate (0.90-0.95), substantial (0.95-0.99), and almost perfect (> 0.99), according to previously proposed guidelines [25]. Descriptive statistics, MAE and MAPE calculations, repeated-measures ANOVA, and post hoc analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics for Windows, version 29.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA). Bland-Altman and CCC analyses were conducted using RStudio (Posit Software, Boston, MA, USA; R version 4.4.1), and the DescTools package was used for CCC calculations. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05 for all analyses.

Comparison of Heart Rate Measurement Accuracy Across Devices

The device × exercise intensity interaction effect on MAE was not statistically significant (F = 0.592, p = 0.653, ηp² = 0.016). Therefore, the main effect of device type was interpreted. A significant main effect of device type on MAE was observed (F = 7.606, p = 0.002, ηp² = 0.174). Bonferroni-adjusted post hoc analyses revealed that the MAE of the PV was significantly lower than those of the PP (p = 0.031) and GW (p = 0.019). Similarly, the AW demonstrated significantly lower MAE values than the PP (p = 0.016) and GW (p = 0.008) (Figure 2A).

Comparison of Heart Rate Measurement Accuracy According to Exercise Intensity

Comparison of MAE among devices across exercise intensities revealed a significant difference between devices under the low-intensity condition (F = 5.254, p = 0.008, ηp² = 0.127). Post hoc analysis showed that the MAE of the AW was significantly lower than that of the PP (p = 0.032) and GW (p = 0.009) (Figure 3A). Under the moderate-intensity condition, the main effect of device was significant (F = 3.282, p = 0.047, ηp² = 0.084), but no significant pairwise differences were observed in the post hoc analysis. Under the high-intensity condition, a significant difference between devices was also found (F = 4.901, p = 0.013, ηp² = 0.120), and post hoc analysis indicated that the MAE of the PV was significantly lower than that of the GW (p = 0.023) (Figure 3B).

Bland-Altman Analysis

Bland-Altman analyses were performed to assess agreement between each wearable device and the ECG-based criterion device (PH). Data from all exercise intensities were combined to calculate the mean bias and 95% LoA for each device (Figure 4). The AW and PV exhibited relatively narrow LoA ranges of −5.24 to 5.35 bpm and −5.05 to 5.89 bpm, respectively, indicating that approximately 95% of the measurement differences relative to the ECG were distributed within ± 5 bpm. In contrast, the PP and GW demonstrated wider LoA ranges exceeding ± 10 bpm, indicating greater variability in HR measurements relative to the criterion device. All devices exhibited positive mean bias values, indicating a tendency to slightly overestimate HR compared with the ECG reference. However, the magnitude of the bias was generally small.

Fitzpatrick Skin Type

No significant differences in MAE, MAPE, or CCC were observed between Fitzpatrick skin types III and IV across any device or exercise intensity (all p > 0.05).

DISCUSSION

This study evaluated the HR measurement accuracy of several PPG-based wearable devices using the ECG-based Polar H10 as the criterion measure. The main findings were as follows: (1) the Apple Watch SE (AW) and Polar Verity Sense (PV) demonstrated the highest accuracy and agreement with the ECG reference, whereas the Polar Pacer Pro (PP) and Galaxy Watch 6 (GW) showed comparatively lower accuracy; (2) HR measurement accuracy differed substantially among devices despite all devices using PPG technology; and (3) measurement accuracy generally improved as exercise intensity increased.

The superiority of the AW and PV was consistently observed across multiple accuracy metrics, including MAE, MAPE, Bland-Altman analysis, and CCC. These findings suggested that the accuracy of PPG-based HR monitoring cannot be explained solely by device category (e.g., wrist-worn vs. upper arm-worn) or by the use of PPG technology itself. Although the superior performance of the Polar Verity Sense may be partly related to its upper-arm placement, the present study could not disentangle the independent effect of wear location from other device-specific factors, such as sensor configuration, hardware design, signal-processing methods, and proprietary algorithms. Therefore, the observed differences should be interpreted as overall device-level differences rather than solely as an effect of wear location.

Bland-Altman analyses further supported these findings. The AW and PV demonstrated relatively narrow LoA with the ECG reference, whereas the PP and GW exhibited substantially wider LoA, indicating greater variability in HR estimation. Although all devices showed a slight tendency to overestimate HR relative to the ECG reference, the magnitude of the bias was small. Therefore, the observed differences among devices appeared to be more strongly related to measurement variability and signal stability than to systematic measurement bias. In particular, the LoA provide complementary information to mean error indices by quantifying the spread of measurement differences and the extent of individual-level variability between methods [26,27]. In the present study, the AW and PV exhibited low MAE and MAPE values and relatively narrow LoA ranges, indicating a high degree of accuracy and agreement with the ECG criterion measure.

Similarly, CCC analyses revealed consistently high agreement between the AW and PV and the ECG reference across all exercise intensities. In particular, both devices achieved CCC values exceeding 0.95 during moderate- and vigorous-intensity exercise. In contrast, the PP and GW exhibited lower agreement during low-intensity exercise, although agreement improved as exercise intensity increased. Because CCC evaluates both correlation and agreement between measurements, these findings indicate that the AW and PV not only tracked HR changes effectively but also produced values that closely matched the criterion measure [28].

Interestingly, MAE and MAPE decreased, whereas CCC increased, as exercise intensity increased across all devices. This finding contrasts with previous reports suggesting that increased movement during exercise may reduce PPG accuracy because of motion artifacts [29,30]. One possible explanation is that higher exercise intensities increase HR and peripheral blood flow, resulting in larger pulse-wave amplitudes and improved PPG signal quality. However, because neither peripheral blood flow nor raw PPG signal quality was directly assessed in the present study, this explanation should be interpreted cautiously.

Potential Advantages of Upper-Arm Placement

The high accuracy observed for the PV may be partially attributed to its upper-arm placement. During running, the wrist undergoes frequent flexion and rotational movements that may alter sensor-skin contact and increase motion artifacts, potentially degrading PPG signal quality [19]. In contrast, the upper arm is generally less affected by such movements and may provide more stable sensor-skin contact during exercise. This interpretation is supported by the findings of previous studies. Moghaddam et al. (2025) reported that upper- arm-worn devices exhibited higher agreement with reference measures than wrist-worn devices and that the same device achieved greater accuracy when worn on the upper arm rather than the wrist [20]. Similarly, in the present study, the upper-arm-worn PV demonstrated higher accuracy than the wrist-worn PP, despite both devices being manufactured by Polar. These findings suggested that HR measurement accuracy in PPG-based wearable devices is influenced not only by the underlying sensor technology but also by wear location and device design. Therefore, the performance of PPG-based HR monitors should be interpreted in the context of both anatomical placement and device-specific characteristics.

Differences in Measurement Stability among Wrist-Worn Devices

Despite being wrist-worn, the AW demonstrated accuracy comparable to that of the upper-arm-worn PV. This finding indicated that wrist placement alone does not determine measurement performance and that considerable variability may exist among wrist-worn devices.

Previous validation studies have reported high levels of agreement between the Apple Watch and ECG-derived HR measurements across various exercise conditions [31,32]. Because wrist-based PPG measurements are particularly susceptible to motion artifacts and sensor displacement during exercise, signal-quality assessment and motion-compensation algorithms are likely to play important roles in maintaining measurement accuracy [33]. Therefore, the superior performance of the AW may reflect differences in proprietary signal-processing and artifact-reduction strategies.

In contrast, the GW demonstrated the largest errors and the lowest CCC values in the present study. Although the underlying reasons cannot be determined from the current data, the wider LoA and lower concordance suggested greater variability in HR tracking during running. Collectively, these findings highlighted that substantial differences in measurement stability may exist even among devices that employ similar PPG technology and are worn at the same anatomical location.

Previous studies have similarly reported superior HR measurement accuracy for upper-arm PPG devices compared with wrist-worn devices [18]. Consistent with these findings, the PV demonstrated better performance than the PP, despite both devices being manufactured by the same company. These results suggested that wear location may influence the accuracy of PPG-based HR monitoring.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, HR accuracy was evaluated using measurements obtained at a single standardized time point (3 min 30 s) during each exercise stage. Although this approach allowed the assessment of stabilized HR responses while minimizing transitional effects, it may not fully reflect long-term tracking performance or average HR accuracy throughout the entire exercise bout. Previous studies have suggested that longer averaging periods may reduce measurement variability and error [34]. Therefore, future studies should evaluate both time-point-specific and interval-averaged accuracy.

Second, data from three participants were excluded because measurement errors exceeded 30 bpm relative to the criterion device. Although these cases were likely attributable to temporary signal acquisition failures or improper device placement, they may represent an important practical limitation of wearable devices in real-world settings. Future studies should examine the frequency and conditions of extreme measurement errors.

Third, the study population consisted exclusively of healthy young adults. Therefore, caution is warranted when generalizing these findings to older adults or individuals with cardiovascular disease, arrhythmias, or other clinical conditions.

Fourth, factors that may influence optical signal quality, including skin type, body hair, tattoos, and sensor-skin contact conditions, were not fully controlled. Previous studies have demonstrated that the optical properties and surface characteristics of the skin can influence PPG signal quality [16,35,36]. Therefore, further studies involving diverse populations are warranted.

Fifth, the wearable devices were released in different years and represent different generations of sensor and algorithmic technologies. Therefore, differences in performance may partially reflect technological advancements rather than device-specific characteristics alone.

Sixth, although exercise intensity was monitored during each stage to confirm that participants had reached the target HR zones based on the ACSM guidelines, the achieved HR values were not systematically recorded for subsequent analysis. Therefore, the actual mean HR attained during each exercise intensity could not be reported, which should be acknowledged as a limitation of the present study.

Finally, because HR accuracy was evaluated only during continuous treadmill running in a relatively small sample of healthy young adults using a single running protocol, the present findings should be interpreted as reflecting relative device performance under the specific experimental conditions examined. Therefore, the findings should not be directly generalized to other exercise modalities, such as interval training, resistance exercise, or free-living conditions, or to other populations, including older adults, individuals with cardiovascular or metabolic diseases, and clinical populations, in whom movement patterns, physiological characteristics, and motion artifacts may differ substantially.

In conclusion, HR measurement accuracy differed substantially among the PPG-based wearable devices, with the Apple Watch SE and Polar Verity Sense showing the highest agreement with the ECG-based criterion measure. These findings also suggested that increasing exercise intensity does not necessarily reduce measurement accuracy and may, in some devices, even improve it. Therefore, wearable-derived HR data should be interpreted in light of device-specific validation rather than broad assumptions regarding PPG-based devices.
