From cockpit to community: Time to stop the use of first-generation H1-antihistamines☆
Authors: Mário Morais-Almeida, Raquel Baptista-Pestana, Sandra N González-Dĺaz, Bryan L Martin, Gary WK Wong
Published: 2025

Dear Editor,

We read with great interest the recent article by Clark et al, “Diphenhydramine: It is time to say a final goodbye”,1 which convincingly revisits a debate that, for many clinicians, should have been closed years ago. The authors present a compelling synthesis of pharmacological, epidemiological, and regulatory evidence showing that diphenhydramine, and by extension other first-generation H1-antihistamines, offer no unique therapeutic advantage over second-generation alternatives, yet carry a well-documented burden of significant adverse effects. Their conclusion, that the time has come for these drugs to disappear from our clinical practice and from over-the-counter (OTC) shelves, is both timely and necessary.1

While the article primarily focused on general health risks, we wish to underscore a dimension that directly intersects with public safety: the impact of first-generation H1-antihistamines on cognitive and psychomotor performance in critical operational settings, most notably civil aviation. This is not merely a theoretical concern; it is supported by experimental and forensic evidence, which in our view deserves broader dissemination among clinicians, regulators, and the general public.

Experimental evidence of performance impairment

First-generation H1-antihistamines readily cross the blood–brain barrier, producing central nervous system effects such as sedation, slowed reaction times, impaired divided attention, and memory deficits.1 A controlled study involving licensed pilots, using a static flight simulator, quantified these effects with striking clarity.2 Eight pilots, each with more than 150 flight hours, performed standardized flight tasks under both placebo and medicated conditions. When given a common first-generation antihistamine (chlorpheniramine), their mean performance scores declined by approximately 11% overall compared to placebo.

The decrement was even more pronounced in complex tasks requiring simultaneous navigation, communication, and decision-making: performance dropped by nearly 23%.2 Such deficits are not subtle, they represent a tangible erosion of the cognitive resilience required to manage unexpected events in the cockpit, especially under difficult meteorological conditions, where workload and stress are already high.

Notably, these effects occurred at therapeutic doses and in pilots with no subjective awareness of impairment.2 This is consistent with the “silent hazard” nature of sedating antihistamines: individuals often underestimate their functional decrement, which magnifies risk in activities demanding sustained vigilance.

Forensic data linking antihistamines to fatal accidents

The experimental findings are mirrored by worrying real-world data from the United States (US) Federal Aviation Administration (FAA). In a 2018 review of fatal civil aviation accidents (September 2008 to October 2014), toxicological analysis revealed the presence of first-generation H1-antihistamines in 582 pilots, compared to only 116 cases involving second-generation agents.3 The disproportionate representation is especially concerning given the overall prescription and OTC availability landscape.

An earlier, more comprehensive FAA report analysed data from 1990 to 2005, encompassing 5383 fatal accidents with post-mortem toxicology.4 First-generation antihistamines were detected in 338 pilots, of which 103 had no other substances present. Importantly, the National Transportation Safety Board (NTSB) determined that these drugs were the probable cause in 13 accidents and a contributing factor in 50 others.

In a descriptive observational study, in which the NTSB Aviation Accident Database was searched from the period beginning January 2012 to December 2014, in 24 of 646 pilots (3.7%), the drug or drugs found were listed in the database as contributing to the cause of fatal accidents; diphenhydramine was the most common one.5

Trends over time are equally worrisome: the proportion of fatal accidents involving these agents increased from 4% in 1990 to 11% in 2004, despite long-standing awareness of their sedative properties and formal rules prohibiting their use by active pilots.4 This is not a signal lost in statistical noise. It is a repeated, identifiable pattern of pharmacological impairment contributing to loss of life.

Why this matters beyond aviation

Aviation offers a uniquely well-documented lens on the problem because of rigorous accident investigation protocols, including systematic toxicology.6 But the cognitive and psychomotor impairments demonstrated in pilots are not unique to them. They apply equally to drivers, operators of heavy machinery, heavy truck and bus drivers, emergency responders, and indeed anyone engaged in critical and non-critical activities.

When an antihistamine's sedative effect is combined with factors such as fatigue, circadian disruption, or alcohol (as frequently documented in the FAA reports), the impairment can be synergistic and profound. In real-world terms, this can mean delayed hazard recognition, inappropriate decision-making, or an inability to execute motor responses quickly enough to avert disaster.2, 3, 4, 5

Public health and regulatory implications

Following best practices in several European countries, regulators have evolved to limit or remove first-generation antihistamines from general sale, favouring drugs with a much better safety profile.1,7 The aviation sector has long prohibited their use among active pilots, yet post-mortem data demonstrate that enforcement and education remain incomplete.

This raises a broader point: if a drug is deemed unsafe for use by individuals in one safety-critical profession, should it remain freely available to the general public without restriction or adequate warning? The answer, in our view, is no. The argument is analogous to the historical phase-out of other over-the-counter agents once shown to carry disproportionate public health risks.

The persistence of first-generation antihistamines in hundreds of OTC formulations, often marketed for allergies, common colds, or “night-time” symptom relief, perpetuates exposure across all age groups. The potential for accidental impairment, whether in a pilot, bus driver, or parent driving a car with a child, is both predictable and preventable.

Clinical responsibility

As clinicians, we have an obligation to align prescribing and counselling practices with current evidence. This includes:

-Preferring second-generation H1-antihistamines for allergic symptom control in nearly all circumstances;

Preferring second-generation H1-antihistamines for allergic symptom control in nearly all circumstances;

-Explicitly warning patients about the sedative and performance-impairing effects of first-generation H1-antihistamines;

The case is especially compelling when viewed through the aviation perspective. Here we have a rare convergence of experimental, epidemiological, and forensic data all pointing to the same conclusion. First-generation H1-antihistamines can and do impair performance to a degree that causes fatal accidents.

Conclusion

The article by Clark et al1 provides a strong foundation for rethinking the place of diphenhydramine and its pharmacological peers in current medical practice. When coupled with the aviation safety literature,2, 3, 4 the argument becomes not only a matter of individual patient safety, but of collective public safety. The impairment these drugs cause is not hypothetical; it has been measured in simulators, documented in accident reports, and recorded in toxicology findings from hundreds of fatal incidents.

For all these reasons, we support the authors’ call for a “final goodbye” to first-generation H1-antihistamines, not as a nostalgic farewell to a long-serving drug class, but as an evidence-driven imperative to protect lives, both in the air and in the community.
