Ultrasonic synergistic slightly acidic electrolyzed water processing to improve postharvest storage quality of Chinese bayberry
Authors: Kui Suo, Yang Zhang, Yabin Feng, Zhenfeng Yang, Cunshan Zhou, Wei Chen, Jiancheng Wang
Published: 2023

ABSTRACT
Highlights•US + SAEW was used to bolster the postharvest storage quality of Chinese bayberry.•US + SAEW efficiently cleared pesticides, debris, and larvae from the bayberry.•US + SAEW markedly inhibited microbial proliferation and decelerated bayberry decay.•US + SAEW extended the preservation of bayberry storage quality.•US + SAEW modulated metabolic enzyme activities and curtailed the synthesis of MDA.

Introduction

Chinese bayberry (Myrica rubra Seib & Zucc.), native to China, is cherished for its vibrant color, unique sour–sweet flavor, and nutritional richness [1], [2]. However, its perishable nature - marked by susceptibility to microbial decay, mechanical injuries, physiological deterioration, and dehydration - limits its shelf life to merely 3–4 days at ambient temperatures. This brief shelf life depreciates its market value and leads to notable postharvest losses [3], [4]. Thus, innovative methods to extend its postharvest longevity and preserve its quality are crucial. While methods such as ethanol emitter [5], acetic acid [6], and methyl jasmonate [7] have traditionally been employed to maintain bayberry freshness, escalating concerns over potential detrimental effects of chemicals on both the bayberry quality and broader health and environmental implications have intensified the pursuit for safer, more sustainable alternatives [8].

Slightly acidic electrolyzed water (SAEW) is an antibacterial solution produced by the electrolysis of diluted HCl or NaCl in a membrane-free chamber. It has gained considerable attention as an effective food-cleansing method due to its cost-effectiveness, broad-spectrum bactericidal properties, and alignment with sustainable practices [9]. The primary active chlorine compound in SAEW is typically hypochlorous acid, which exhibits potent bactericidal effects within a pH range of 5.0–6.5 [10]. Consequently, SAEW has been successfully employed to sterilize and enhance the quality of various fruits and vegetables. For instance, Gao et al. [11] revealed that SAEW processing decreased microbial presence on fresh-cut apples' surfaces by 2.82 log units relative to untreated samples, thereby mitigating quality deterioration during storage. Kim, Park, & Lee [12] observed that SAEW treatments curtailed the botrytis cinerea population on cut rose flowers by 71 % compared to controls, without affecting petal color, chlorophyll, or tissue integrity. Similarly, Li et al. [13] demonstrated that SAEW treatments not only reduced microbial populations on broccoli sprouts but also boosted the accumulation of glucosinolates and sulforaphane.

In addition to the microbial challenges, bayberries are vulnerable to pesticide residues, dirt, and parasitism by drosophila larvae - factors that compromise their quality. The unique clustered structure of bayberry surfaces further complicates conventional cleaning methods. Therefore, there's a pressing need in the bayberry processing industry for efficient, non-damaging cleaning technologies.

Ultrasonics (US) serve as an eco-friendly strategy to eliminate pesticide residues, dirt, and microbial contaminants from fruit and vegetable surfaces through acoustic cavitation, without compromising quality [14]. Research by Zhu et al. [15] highlighted the superior efficacy of ultrasonics in removing pesticides from pakchoi when compared to conventional water soaking, while also minimizing physical damage and Vitamin C loss. São José et al. [16] noted the potential of ultrasonics in dislodging dirt and food residues from fruits and vegetables. Additionally, Mustapha et al. [17] reported that ultrasonic treatments reduced microbial counts (aerobic mesophiles, molds & yeasts) on cherry tomatoes by 2.26 log CFU/g, as opposed to traditional water washing. Furthermore, ultrasonics can streamline processing operations, achieving desired outcomes in minutes or even seconds, resulting in cost savings, optimal product yields, and minimal energy consumption. As such, the combined efficacy of US and SAEW offers a promising avenue for enhancing postharvest bayberry quality.

To date, there is a knowledge gap regarding the impact of combined US and SAEW processing on the postharvest storage quality of bayberries. Hence, this study aims to explore the benefits of this synergistic approach on pesticide residue elimination, dirt removal, deworming, and microbial control. We also investigate its influence on several quality indicators during storage, including decay rate, weight loss, hardness, sugar-acid ratio, color, total phenolics, anthocyanins, antioxidant capacity, and the activities of enzymes such as phenylalanine ammonia-lyase, polyphenol oxidase, superoxide dismutase, and catalase, as well as malondialdehyde content.

Materials and methods

Production of SAEW

The slightly acidic electrolyzed water (SAEW) was synthesized by electrolyzing a 0.2 mL/L HCl solution for 45 min utilizing a custom-made electrolysis device (Refer to Fig. 1A). The process operated at a current and voltage of 1.2 A and 50 V, respectively. The resulting SAEW had an effective chlorine concentration of 50 mg/L and a pH of 5.5, and was immediately used for subsequent processing.Fig. 1Illustrative diagram of the production of SAEW (A) and the processing of bayberry (B). CW: conventional water washing. US: ultrasonic processing. SAEW: slightly acidic electrolyzed water processing. US + SAEW: ultrasonic synergistic acidic electrolyzed water processing.

Illustrative diagram of the production of SAEW (A) and the processing of bayberry (B). CW: conventional water washing. US: ultrasonic processing. SAEW: slightly acidic electrolyzed water processing. US + SAEW: ultrasonic synergistic acidic electrolyzed water processing.

Raw samples

Chinese bayberries (Myrica rubra Seib & Zucc.) were manually harvested from an orchard located in Xianju County, Zhejiang Province, P. R. China. The fresh bayberries were processed within 12 h after harvest to prevent decay. Only samples with uniform size and color, showing no signs of mechanical damage, were selected for subsequent experimentation.

Processing

Four distinct processing methods were conducted on the bayberries (Fig. 1B details the procedures):•Conventional water washing (CW): Bayberries were immersed in water at 25 °C and subjected to agitation washing for 480 s. The solid to liquid ratio maintained was 1:5 (g/mL).•Ultrasonic (US) processing: Bayberries were soaked in water at 25 °C, ultrasonically treated for 30 s, and then agitated washing for 450 s. A 1:5 (g/mL) solid to liquid ratio was upheld, with an ultrasonic frequency and power density of 40 kHz and 50 W/L, respectively.•Slightly acidic electrolyzed water (SAEW) processing: This mirrored the CW procedure, but with SAEW replacing the water.•Ultrasonic synergistic slightly acidic electrolyzed water (US + SAEW) processing: This procedure emulated the US method, but SAEW substituted the water.

Following treatments, the bayberries were gently dabbed with sterile gauze to eliminate excess surface moisture. They were then stored in sanitized polypropylene containers at a temperature of 4 ± 1 °C.

Pesticide residue analysis

According to local farmers, bayberries predominantly have residues of iprodione and procymidone, prompting their analysis in this study. Pesticide levels in the samples were ascertained using a gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC–MS) system (TQ8050, Shimadzu Corporation, Kyoto, Japan). Samples (5.0 g each) were blended with 1.0 g of NaCl and 10 mL of acetonitrile, then centrifuged at 6000 rpm for 5 min. From the resultant supernatant, 1 mL was combined with 1.5 g MgSO₄ and 0.5 g PSA, mixed, and centrifuged again at 6000 rpm for 3 min. The supernatant was then passed through a 0.22 μm nylon filter and introduced into an autosampler vial for GC–MS assessment. Analysis conditions were adapted from Lai et al. [18]. The pesticide removal efficiency was denoted as the removal rate of iprodione and procymidone, calculated as per Eq. (1):(1)Pesticideremovalrate(%)=N0-N1N0×100%where N1 as the iprodione/procymidone content of the treated sample (mg/kg); N0 as the iprodione/procymidone content of the fresh sample (mg/kg).

Dirt cleaning analysis

The efficacy of the four treatments in cleaning bayberries was gauged via the turbidity of the respective solutions (as detailed in section 2.3). The turbidity values were measured using a turbidimeter (TU5200, HACH, USA), and higher turbidity values signify superior cleaning capabilities, indicating reduced dirt residues on bayberries.

Deworming analysis

For the deworming evaluation, bayberries free of drosophila larvae parasitism were chosen. Each test sample was artificially infested with 10 drosophila melanogaster larvae (DML) and then incubated for 24 h at ambient temperature. Subsequent to this, the bayberries were processed using CW, US, SAEW, and US + SAEW methods. The efficacy of each process in repelling DML was quantified as the DML repellent rate, calculated as per Eq. (2):(2)DMLrepellentrate(%)=M1M0×100%where M1 is the number of DML repelling from the sample after the processing (strips), and M0 is the total number of DML that were initially inoculated in samples (strips).

Microbial analysis

Microbial testing was executed according to the methodology described by Ding et al. [19] with minor modifications. Specifically, bayberry samples were transferred to sterilized conical flasks, each containing 200 mL of sterile saline, and shaken for 5 min. The resultant sample solutions underwent serial dilutions and were then evenly spread over plate count agar (PCA, sourced from HANGWEI Co., Hangzhou, China) and potato dextrose agar (PDA, procured from Hopebio Co., Qingdao, China). For enumerating total bacterial colonies, plates were incubated at 37 °C for 48 h, whereas mold & yeast counts were determined post incubation at 28 °C for 72 h. Microbial populations were recorded as log CFU/g of the sample.

Qualities analysis

Decay index

Every storage day, ten samples from each group were evaluated for decay intensity based on the visible decay area as detailed by Wang et al. [20]. The intensity of decay was quantified by a score of 0–––4; 0 = no visible infection; 1 = mild (<10 % infection); 2 = moderate (10 %-30 % infection); 3 = severe (30 %-70 % infection); 4 = very severe (>70 % infection). The decay index was derived using Eq. (3):(3)Decayindex(%)=∑S×NsS1×N×100%where S represents the decay score; Ns is the number of samples with the current decay score; S1 stands for the highest decay score; N indicates the total number of samples.

loss

Throughout storage, the samples' weights were registered bi-daily. Weight loss rate was calculated using Eq. (4):(4)Weightlossrate(%)=mn-m0m0×100%where mn denotes the sample weight after n days of storage (g); m0 is the sample’s initial weight (g).

Hardness

Sample hardness was determined using a physical property analyzer (TMS-Touch, FTC, USA). Specific operational parameters included TPA two-cycle compression mode, force sensing element range of 250 N, and 15 % deformation. The hardness test was repeated ten times.

Sugar-acid ratio

The juice from homogenized samples was sieved through triple-layered gauze. Total soluble solids (TSS) and total acid content (TA) were then measured. TSS was determined using an Atago digital refractometer (model PR-101, Tokyo, Japan). TA was obtained through NaOH titration, and expressed as the citric acid content of the sample[21]. The sugar-acid ratio was calculated as TSS/TA.

Color

The surface color of the samples was measured using a portable spectrophotometer (CM-26d, KONICA MINOLTA, INC. Japan), and the L, a, and b values were recorded. The color parameters test was replicated ten times. The overall color shift (ΔE) of the samples during storage was determined using Eq. (5):(5)ΔE=L0-Lt2+a0-at2+b0-bt2where Lt, at, bt are the lightness, red/green and yellow/blue values of the samples at t day of storage, respectively; L0, a0, b0 indicates the initial color values of the samples pre-processing.

Total phenolic and total anthocyanin content

Extraction: A 1.0 g portion of the crushed samples was mixed with 10 mL of an 80 % methanol solution. An ultrasonic-assisted extraction was performed at a frequency of 40 kHz and power density of 50 W/L for 10 min at 25 °C. The mixture was then centrifuged at 10,000 rpm for 5 min at 4 °C. The supernatant was collected and used for the quantification of total phenolic and anthocyanin contents.

The total phenolic content of the samples was determined by the Folin-Ciocalteu method as detailed by Feng et al. [22], and the results were expressed as gallic acid equivalent (mg/g, GAE). The total anthocyanin content of the samples was gauged using pH differential method, following the protocol of Xu et al. [23], and the results were expressed as cyanidin 3-glucoside equivalents (mg/g, C3G).

Antioxidant capacity

The antioxidant capacity of the sample was expressed as scavenging rate against DPPH (1,1-Diphenyl-2-picrylhydrazyl) radical and ABTS (2,2′-Azinobis-(3-ethylbenzthiazoline-6-sulphonate)) radical [22]. Take 0.5 mL of the supernatant (extracted as above, section 2.8.6) and blended with 2 mL 0.2 mM DPPH or 4 mL 0.1 mM ABTS solution reacted in the dark at 25 °C for 30 min. Subsequently, the absorbance was recorded at 517 nm for DPPH and at 734 nm for ABTS. The scavenging rate for the DPPH/ABTS radicals was determined using Eq. (6):(6)Radicalscavengingrate=A0-AA0×100%where A0 represents the absorbance value of “DPPH/ABTS + methanol”; A indicates the absorbance value of “DPPH/ABTS + sample supernatant”.

Enzyme activities and malondialdehyde content

The activities of the enzymes phenylalanine ammonia-lyase (PAL), polyphenol oxidase (PPO), superoxide dismutase (SOD), and catalase (CAT), as well as the content of malondialdehyde (MDA), were measured using their respective assay kits (Beijing Solarbio Science & Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing).

Statistical analysis

All tests, unless specifically noted, were carried out in triplicate. The gathered data was then subjected to one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) and Student's t-test for further analysis. Pearson's correlation analysis was conducted to statistically test the relationship between the bioactive compounds and antioxidant capacity of bayberry samples. This statistical analysis was facilitated using IBM SPSS Statistics 26 software (V.26.0, SPSS Inc, USA). The results were deemed statistically significant if the p-value was less than 0.05 (p < 0.05).

Results and discussion

The effect of processing on pesticide removal

The removal of pesticide residues from fruits and vegetables is pivotal for reducing potential health risks, particularly for fruits like bayberry that lack protective peels. As depicted in Fig. 2, the iprodione and procymidone contents in unprocessed samples stood at 9.55 mg/kg and 9.05 mg/kg, respectively. All processing methods - CW, US, SAEW, and US + SAEW - reduced the residue content of iprodione and procymidone when compared to the unprocessed samples. The US + SAEW method was the most effective, achieving reductions of 69.62 % and 72.45 %, followed by SAEW (59.16 %, 50.13 %), US (52.84 %, 46.89 %), and lastly, CW (22.13 %, 24.45 %). The capability of SAEW to remove pesticides can be attributed to its low pH and redox properties. Prior research has evidenced the efficacy of electrolyzed water in removing pesticides, including iprodione [24] and procymidone [25]. Furthermore, ultrasonication can lead to the degradation of pesticides through hydroxylation and oxidation reactions, stemming from the generation of Ḣ and HȮ free radicals [26]. Accordingly, our findings underline the superior pesticide removal potential when US and SAEW are synergistically employed compared to their standalone applications.Fig. 2Iprodione (A) and procymidone (B) contents in unprocessed and processed by CW, US, SAEW and US + SAEW samples. The effect of processing on dirt

The cleanliness of bayberry post-harvest is of utmost importance as it influences consumer purchasing decisions. As illustrated in Fig. 3, the utilization of ultrasonication (whether as US or US + SAEW) led to a significant (p < 0.05) increase in turbidity, ranging between 115.91 % and 136.18 %, compared to methods that did not employ ultrasonication (CW and SAEW). Nevertheless, no significant difference (p > 0.05) was observed between the US and US + SAEW methods or between the CW and SAEW methods. This suggests that ultrasonication was the primary factor enhancing dirt removal across all processing techniques. A direct visual representation of this can be seen in Fig. 3. While the turbidity levels in SAEW processing appeared similar to those in CW, both US and US + SAEW exhibited higher turbidity, indicating a more efficient dirt removal process from bayberries. This increased efficiency is likely attributed to the cavitation phenomenon of ultrasonication. The generated mechanical force, resulting from bubble burst, effectively removes dirt particles adhered to the material's surface [27].Fig. 3The effect on dirt cleaning ability (turbidity) of bayberry processed by CW, US, SAEW and US + SAEW. The effect of processing on deworming

Fresh bayberries are notably vulnerable to drosophila larvae parasitism, which can cause both decay and health concerns. The efficacy of CW, US, SAEW, and US + SAEW processes in repelling DML from bayberries is illustrated in Fig. 4A. The repellent rate of SAEW processing was found to be comparable to that of CW. However, introducing ultrasonication (either as US or US + SAEW) markedly improved the repellent rate compared to non-ultrasonicated methods (CW and SAEW). This observation is further corroborated by Fig. 4B, where there's a conspicuous increase in the number of DML fleeing from bayberries post ultrasonic processing (US, US + SAEW). This underscores ultrasonication's superior deworming efficacy over traditional water and SAEW methods. In summary, when evaluated across the metrics of pesticide removal, dirt elimination, and deworming, the combined US + SAEW approach emerged as the most effective. This was evident when compared against CW, standalone US, and SAEW methods. This holistic approach significantly elevates the safety standards of consumable bayberries. Storage quality

Microbial

Fig. 5A&B depict the microbial counts for total bacteria, molds & yeasts on bayberries post-processing and throughout storage. Initially, unprocessed bayberries exhibited bacterial counts and mold & yeast counts of 5.61 log CFU/g and 5.97 log CFU/g, respectively. Post-processing (via CW, US, SAEW, and US + SAEW methods), these counts observed a decline, with the US + SAEW processing resulting in the most pronounced reduction (78.18 % for bacteria and 83.09 % for mold & yeast). The descending order of efficacy was: US + SAEW, SAEW (64.93 %, 69.15 %), US (46.45 %, 55.12 %), and CW (2.16 %, 5.99 %). A parallel study by Akther et al. [29] revealed a similar trend in fresh-cut cauliflower, wherein a SAEW and ultrasonic combination substantially curbed microbial growth in comparison to standard water cleaning.Fig. 5Changes in microbial counts and decay index of bayberry processed by CW, US, SAEW and US + SAEW during processing and storage. A: total bacterial. B: mold & yeasts. C: decay index.

Changes in microbial counts and decay index of bayberry processed by CW, US, SAEW and US + SAEW during processing and storage. A: total bacterial. B: mold & yeasts. C: decay index.

Over the storage span, a gradual rise in microbial counts was evident across all processed samples. Nevertheless, US + SAEW-treated samples consistently showcased the least microbial proliferation. To elucidate, on the 8th storage day, microbial counts for US + SAEW, SAEW, US, and CW samples stood at 5.06 & 5.45 log CFU/g, 5.27 & 5.68 log CFU/g, 5.44 & 5.91 log CFU/g, and 5.74 & 6.14 log CFU/g, respectively. Li et al. [30] drew a parallel conclusion, pointing out the gradual yet low rise in microbial populace on fresh-cut eggplants treated with SAEW during storage, especially when compared against sterile distilled water. Similarly, ultrasonic washing has been credited with stymieing bacterial, mold & yeast growth, thus amplifying the shelf-life of commodities like fresh-cut red cabbages [31].

Delving into the underpinnings of the superior antimicrobial efficiency of US + SAEW, a few reasons can be postulated:(1)The slightly acidic nature of the electrolyzed water, courtesy of HClO and ClO-, dismantles bacterial cell membranes and destabilizes the electron respiration chain in their mitochondria [9];(2)The ultrasonic process spawns hydroxyl radicals with inherent antimicrobial properties, while concurrently generating immense shear forces that rupture the microbial cell wall and the enclosing membrane [14];(3)Lastly, the profound pressure induced by cavitation accentuates the infiltration of HClO, ClO-, and hydroxyl radicals into microbial cell membranes during ultrasonication [32].

Decay index

As shown in Fig. 5C, the initial storage period (0–8 days) exhibited no decay indicators across all sample types. However, post the 8-day mark, a noticeable increment in decay intensity was observed. The initiation of decay for CW, US, SAEW, and US + SAEW samples was documented at 8 days, 12 days, 12 days, and 14 days, respectively. By the end of the storage period, the corresponding decay indexes were recorded as 36.66 %, 14.33 %, 12.5 %, and 10.5 %. Notably, the implementation of the US + SAEW technique markedly curtailed bayberry decay during storage, elongating the storage duration by an additional 6 days. This was trailed by both US and SAEW methods, each extending storage by 4 days when juxtaposed against the CW-treated samples. Such observations were visually corroborated by Fig. 6, wherein the superior efficacy of US + SAEW in decelerating bayberry decay stood out. The combined might of US and SAEW in robustly neutralizing microorganisms might account for this [19].Fig. 6The physical photos of bayberry processed by CW, US, SAEW and US + SAEW during storage. Synthesizing the gathered data, it emerges that the synergistic approach of US + SAEW offers multiple advantages over conventional methods (CW) or standalone techniques (US or SAEW). Not only did US + SAEW excel in pesticide elimination, dirt purification, and deworming, but it also showcased a commendable ability to suppress microbial proliferation during storage, subsequently inhibiting bayberry decay. loss, hardness, sugar-acid ratio and color

Fig. 7A&B demonstrate that both the weight loss and hardness of the bayberry samples (CW, US + SAEW) trended in opposing directions with prolonged storage time, the former rising and the latter dwindling. This phenomenon parallels observations in lotus roots where extended storage led to weight loss and hardness decline [33]. Notably, US + SAEW processed samples exhibited a marked decrease in weight loss and enhanced hardness compared to CW, statistically significant at p < 0.05. To illustrate, by the 8th day, the weight loss rates for CW and US + SAEW processed samples were 2.70 % and 0.99 %, respectively, while their hardness values stood at 4.26 N and 4.90 N. The slower deterioration in weight and hardness observed in US + SAEW samples might be attributed to the decreased tissue respiration and transpiration induced by SAEW. This effect, which involves the curbing of respiratory intensity, metabolic activity during storage, and microbial proliferation, stems from the properties of SAEW [34]. Turning to Fig. 8C & D, the antioxidant capacity (as measured by the DPPH/ABTS radical scavenging rate) of the samples (CW, US + SAEW) exhibited a consistent decline throughout storage. However, the antioxidant capacity of US + SAEW processed samples was marginally superior to that of the CW processed samples, a variation potentially attributed to the diminished degradation of bioactive compounds in the former. Enzyme activities and malondialdehyde content

Enzymatic activity is a reliable barometer for monitoring quality alterations in agricultural commodities. Notably, PAL and PPO activities govern phenolic metabolism within fruits. While PAL is the seminal enzyme spearheading the synthesis of phenolic compounds, PPO is responsible for catalyzing phenolic oxidation [40]. As evidenced in Fig. 9A&B, samples processed with US + SAEW exhibited heightened PAL activity and suppressed PPO activity in comparison to their CW-processed counterparts throughout the storage period. This underscores that US + SAEW processing not only bolsters PAL activity but also hampers PPO activity in bayberries. Such enzymatic profiles elucidate the upward trajectory of total phenolic content, total anthocyanin content, and antioxidant capacity in US + SAEW processed samples relative to CW samples. Rao et al. [41] similarly discerned that SAEW fostered flavonoid accumulation and augmented phenylpropane metabolism in buckwheat buds by modulating PAL enzymes. The observed curtailment of PPO activity likely stems from its heightened sensitivity to the acidic milieu furnished by SAEW, resulting in its deactivation under lowered pH conditions [42]. The importance of sustaining an equilibrium in the reactive oxygen species (ROS) clearance system is paramount for harvested products. This balance prevents the undue accrual of ROS, safeguarding the cell membrane from damage [43]. Central to this ROS-clearing ensemble are enzymes like SOD and CAT [44]. As shown in Fig. 9C&D, the SOD and CAT enzyme activities of US + SAEW processed samples were significantly (p < 0.05) higher than CW processed samples during storage, suggesting that the processing fosters these ROS scavenging enzymes. This mirrors observations in longan [43] and blueberry [45], wherein acidic electrolyzed water processing amplified the activities of antioxidant enzymes like SOD, CAT, and APX. In another revelation, Xu et al. [46] concluded that ultrasonic treatments fortify both SOD and CAT activities in apples during storage, conferring enhanced preservation by curbing oxidative harm.

MDA content is conventionally harnessed as a metric to gauge the extent of lipid peroxidation in cell membranes [34]. As shown in Fig. 9E, US + SAEW processed samples manifested a notably diminished MDA content relative to CW samples. This implies that US + SAEW processing augments bayberry cell membrane stability and possesses a superior capacity to thwart bayberry membrane lipid oxidation. Corroborating this notion, Zhao et al. [47] deduced that SAEW treatments inhibit MDA accumulation, thereby circumventing senescence triggered by compromises in the structural coherence of kiwifruit cell membranes.

Conclusion

This study introduced a combined approach involving ultrasonic waves and slightly acidic electrolyzed water to enhance the postharvest quality of Chinese bayberries. When benchmarked against the CW, individual US, and SAEW processes, the combined US + SAEW processing emerged superior. It not only excelled in pesticide removal, dirt cleansing, and deworming, but also remarkably curtailed the decay rate of bayberries during storage by inactivating microbes. This led to an improved storability of the fruit. With respect to quality retention during storage, the US + SAEW application bolstered various attributes like reduced weight loss, increased hardness, improved color stability, and enriched content of total phenolic and anthocyanin. Furthermore, it stimulated the activity of PAL, SOD, and CAT enzymes, while suppressing the PPO enzyme's activity and curtailing MDA synthesis. Such enhancements collectively slowed down the deterioration in quality, ensuring the freshness of bayberries was retained for an extended period. Given these findings, US + SAEW emerges as a promising successor to conventional preservation methods, offering significant potential to uplift the postharvest quality of Chinese bayberries.
