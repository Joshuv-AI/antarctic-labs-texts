A Longitudinal Assessment of Anterior Cingulate Cortex Gamma-aminobutyric Acid in Antipsychotic Medication-naïve First Episode Psychosis Patients
Authors: Genelle D Samson, Jose O Maximo, Eric A Nelson, Nina V Kraguljac, William P Armstrong, Branden Benningfield, Seyedeh Nasim Adnani, Adil Bashir, Adrienne C Lahti
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground and HypothesisConverging evidence from animal and human studies has implicated gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA)-ergic dysfunction in the pathophysiology of psychosis spectrum disorders. However, little is known about GABA’s role in the early illness stages. Based on prior research, we hypothesized that GABA levels would already be altered in first episode psychosis patients (FEP), and that they would be associated with patients’ cognitive function and response to treatment.Study DesignWe used magnetic resonance spectroscopy with a MEGA-PRESS sequence to quantify GABA in the dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (ACC) at baseline and over the course of antipsychotic treatment with risperidone. We compared GABA levels between 79 medication-naïve FEP and 113 healthy controls (HC) longitudinally over a period of 16 weeks and examined their relationships to cognition and treatment response.Study ResultsWe found significantly lower GABA levels in FEP compared to HC at all 3 time points (baseline, 6 weeks, 16 weeks), but did not observe a significant main effect of time or an interaction of group by time. We found no significant correlations between GABA and cognitive scores. Baseline GABA levels of patients considered treatment nonresponders significantly differed from HC, whereas responders did not.ConclusionsOur findings suggest that GABA dysfunction in the ACC may be an important feature of the core pathophysiology of psychosis. This dysfunction does not appear to be attenuated by conventional antipsychotic treatment, though baseline levels may be indicative of clinical prognosis in FEP.Keywords: magnetic resonance spectroscopy, GABA, schizophrenia, cognition, treatment response, MEGA-PRESS

Introduction

Gamma-aminobutyric acid (GABA), the primary inhibitory neurotransmitter in the central nervous system, has been increasingly implicated in the pathophysiology of schizophrenia (SZ) based on animal models,1–4 postmortem studies,5,6 and a growing number of in vivo magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) studies.7,8 Postmortem studies in SZ have demonstrated differences in presynaptic GABA synthesis,6,9,10 transport and reuptake,11,12 as well as altered postsynaptic GABA receptor expression.13 Cognitive dysfunction, a core feature of SZ,14 has been linked to abnormalities in GABA neurons in different brain regions, especially in the prefrontal cortex.15 The anterior cingulate cortex (ACC), a brain region consistently implicated in the pathophysiology of SZ,16,17 plays a critical role in cognitive adjustment and control functions18 that are known to be impaired in SZ.19 However, the role of GABA in the ACC and in cognition is still not well understood.8

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy studies at 3 Tesla (T) have found alterations in ACC GABAergic neurotransmission in SZ; ACC GABA levels have been found to be elevated,20,21 reduced,22 or not different23 between patients and healthy controls (HC). These inconsistencies are perhaps unsurprising given the studies’ heterogeneity in sample size, demographics, antipsychotic medication exposure, and spectroscopy voxel placement. A recent meta-analysis taking a granular approach to assessing frontal lobe GABA in SZ reported reduced GABA in the ACC, but not other frontal cortex regions.24 Subgroup analyses suggested that findings were only significant in first episode psychosis studies.24–27 Another meta-analysis that investigated 4 subregions of the medial frontal cortex (MFC) reported lower mid- and posterior-MFC GABA in SZ.28 Findings on ACC GABA levels’ relationship to cognition are similarly varied8; studies have reported positive29 and no20 correlations between ACC GABA and cognitive assessment scores in SZ. Studies with high-field 7T MR scanners, which offer better spectral resolution than 3T, have also provided inconsistent findings.26,30 Two 7T MRS studies have evaluated ACC GABA levels in medicated first episode psychosis patients (FEP). Reid et al. observed no differences in GABA between controls and FEP but found that higher GABA levels correlated with worse cognition in FEP and not in HC.30 In a large cohort of FEP (n = 81), Wang et al. found lower GABA in FEP compared to HC, and no correlations with cognition.26

Two studies evaluated ACC GABA longitudinally in medication-naïve FEP.31,32 Bojesen et al. found lower GABA levels at baseline in patients (n = 39), and no effect of 26 weeks of treatment on ACC GABA levels. However, treatment nonresponders at week 26 (defined using Andreasen criteria33) had significantly lower baseline GABA levels compared to HC, which was not the case for responders.31 Contrastingly, de la Fuente-Sandoval et al. found elevated ACC GABA levels at baseline in FEP (n = 28) and decreased GABA levels relative to baseline following 4 weeks of treatment.32 Given these inconsistencies, there is a need for well-powered studies to elucidate the baseline and trajectory of GABA levels in medication-naïve FEP and GABA’s role in treatment response. Those answers could have broad implications for the identification of specific drug development targets and provide guidance for therapeutic intervention.

Here, we used MRS to measure dorsal ACC GABA levels in vivo in a large group of antipsychotic medication-naïve FEP and matched HC. We followed FEP longitudinally, measuring GABA at baseline and after 6 and 16 weeks of treatment with antipsychotic medication.34 Based on the literature, we hypothesized that (1) ACC GABA levels would be lower at baseline in FEP compared to HC, (2) antipsychotic medication treatment would not modulate GABA levels in FEP, and (3) GABA levels in FEP would correlate with cognition scores. Further, to examine the relationship between GABA and treatment response, we measured percent change in psychiatric symptom scores and also considered patients as treatment responders or nonresponders, based on Andreasen remission criteria.33 We hypothesized that (1) baseline GABA levels would correlate with treatment response, and (2) baseline GABA levels would significantly differ between nonresponders and HC, but not between responders and HC.

Methods

Participants

We recruited antipsychotic medication-naïve FEP from the emergency room, inpatient units, and outpatient clinics at the University of Alabama at Birmingham (UAB). HC were recruited by advertisements, matching patients on age, gender, and parental socioeconomic status. Approval for this study was obtained from the UAB Institutional Review Board. Written informed consent was obtained prior to enrollment and after subjects were deemed to have the capacity to provide consent.35

Subjects were excluded if they had major neurological or medical conditions, any history of head trauma with loss of consciousness, substance use disorders (excluding nicotine and cannabis) within 1 month of imaging, were pregnant or breastfeeding, or had magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) contraindications. Additionally, HCs with a personal history of a psychiatric illness or family history of a psychotic disorder in a first-degree relative were excluded. Patients were either antipsychotic-naïve or had no more than 5 days of lifetime antipsychotic exposure prior to study entry. Participants were further excluded following the criteria listed under data quality control.

Diagnoses were established in consensus by 2 board-certified psychiatrists (A.C.L. and N.V.K.) taking into consideration information from medical records, family reports, the Diagnostic Interview for Genetic Studies (DIGS) or Mini-International Neuropsychiatric Interview (MINI) and medical records as available as well as clinical observations over several months of follow-up (ClinicalTrials.gov Identifier: NCT02034253, NCT03442101). The Brief Psychiatric Rating Scale (BPRS) was used to assess psychosis symptom severity and treatment response.36,37 Treatment response groups were defined based on the Andreasen remission criteria, where patients who scored at or less than 3 (“mild” or less) on 7 items of the BPRS were considered “responders,” and any patients who scored greater than 3 on any of those items were considered “nonresponders.”33 Treatment response criteria were based on BPRS items assessing grandiosity (item 8), suspiciousness (11), unusual thought content (15), hallucinatory behavior (12), conceptual disorganization (4), mannerisms/posturing (7), and blunted affect (16).33 Treatment response was also considered as a continuous variable, as the percentage change on the BPRS positive subscale between baseline and week 16 of treatment, BPRSBaseline−BPRSWeek 16BPRSBaseline−4 × 100, where a greater value indicates a greater reduction in positive symptoms. Cognitive function was characterized using the Repeatable Battery for the Assessment of Neuropsychological Status (RBANS).38

First episode psychosis patients enrolled in this longitudinal study went into a 16-week trial of oral risperidone using a flexible dosing regimen. Risperidone was started at 0.5–1 mg and titrated in 1–2 mg increments; dosing was based on therapeutic and side effects. In case of excessive side effect burden, as determined by a study physician, patients were switched to aripiprazole started at 2–5 mg and titrated in 2.5–10 mg increments (3 patients switched to aripiprazole by week 6; 1 additional patient switched to aripiprazole at week 16). One patient was prescribed paliperidone palmitate. Use of concomitant medication was permitted as clinically indicated. The number of patients taking concomitant medications included: amitriptyline (1), lorazepam (1), SSRIs (citalopram [1], escitalopram [1], fluoxetine [1], sertraline [10]), propranolol (1), buprenorphine/naloxone (1), trazodone (4), valproic acid (1), and bupropion (1), with 20 patients taking more than 1 concomitant medication.

Image Acquisition

Participants were scanned on a 3T Siemens MAGNETOM Prisma MRI scanner (Siemens, Erlangen, Germany) equipped with a 20-channel head coil. A high-resolution structural T1 weighted scan (MPRAGE: TR = 2400 ms; TE = 2.22 ms; inversion time = 1000 ms; flip angle = 8°; GRAPPA factor = 2; voxel size = 0.8 mm3) was acquired for anatomical reference, to guide voxel placement, and for voxel tissue segmentation.

A spectroscopy voxel was placed in the dorsal ACC (voxel size: 30 × 40 × 20 mm3, Figure 1A), and outer volume suppression bands were placed close to the edge of the voxel above and below the voxel to reduce unwanted fat signal. After automated and manual shimming to optimize field homogeneity and water suppression, spectra were acquired using a MEGA-PRESS sequence (TR/TE = 1500/68 ms; flip angle = 90º; 44 Hz bandwidth full width at half maximum editing pulses applied at 1.9 [ON] and 1.5 ppm [OFF]; 256 averages [128 ON and 128 OFF, interleaved]), where editing pulses at 1.9 and 1.5 ppm allow for macromolecule suppression using J-difference editing. We also acquired 16 averages of unsuppressed water scans from the same voxel for reference. Data were saved as single-shot spectra.

MRS Data Preprocessing.

Analyses were performed with the Gannet Toolkit in MATLAB. GannetLoad was used to import time-domain data from the scanner and transform data into a frequency-domain GABA-edited spectrum (patient spectrum example in Figure 1B). GannetFit was used for nonlinear least-squares fitting of the spectra to integrate the edited GABA peak at 3 ppm and produce GABA concentration estimates. We quantified GABA with respect to water (Figure 1C), and corrected measures for CSF and voxel tissue concentrations using tissue correction methods as introduced by Edden et al.39,40 Here, we report water-referenced GABA levels, rather than GABA normalized to creatine (Cr). While Cr levels are used as a reference in many studies, research has found that Cr significantly differs between patients with SZ and HC, thus potentially confounding the usability of Cr values as internal reference.41 The tissue fraction values were based on voxel segmentations with GannetCoRegister and GannetSegment.

Data Quality Control.

Spectra were checked for the presence of residual water, lipid contamination, and spurious echoes. Spectral quality control thresholds were determined a priori, where spectra were excluded if (1) the spectral fit error was greater than 20%; (2) the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the metabolites was less than 3 standard deviations below the mean SNR of the HC group (at baseline); or (3) the full width at half maximum (FWHM) of the GABA peak was greater than 3 standard deviations above the mean FWHM of the HC group (at baseline). Based on these criteria, 1 HC and 3 FEP spectra were excluded at baseline; 2 HC spectra were excluded at week 6; and 3 HC and 1 FEP spectra were excluded at week 16.

Statistical Analyses

Statistical analyses were performed in SPSS (version 29). Demographic variables, spectral quality parameters, and voxel tissue composition measures were compared between groups using independent samples t-tests.

Linear Mixed Models.

To examine whether ACC GABA levels differed between FEP and HC groups over time, we used a linear mixed model. Our mixed model included subject as a random effect; group (HC or FEP), scan time point (baseline, week 6, and week 16), and group * time as fixed effects; and a first-order autoregressive covariance structure with age, gender, smoking status (measured as packs per day, PPD), and SNR as covariates of no interest. We used Bonferroni correction to adjust for multiple comparisons.

ANCOVAs.

To investigate whether differences existed before treatment, we used an ANCOVA to compare ACC GABA levels between FEP and HC at baseline, with age, gender, and PPD included as covariates.

To examine whether baseline ACC GABA levels could differentiate responders from nonresponders, we used ANCOVAs to compare HC to patients considered responders at 16 weeks, and to compare HC to patients considered nonresponders at 16 weeks, where Andreasen remission criteria defined responders versus nonresponders.

Correlation Analyses.

Partial correlations were used to assess the relationship between baseline GABA levels and cognition, as measured by RBANS Total score, controlling for age, gender, and PPD. Statistical significance was set at P < .05.

We used partial correlations to assess the relationship between baseline GABA levels and treatment response as a continuous variable calculated by percent change in BPRS positive subscale scores.

Participants per Analysis.

Because mixed models include all participants with data at any time point, our full participant count for our total data were 121 HC and 96 FEP; however, the number of participants included at each time point varied. Of the 121 HC recruited, 4 began the study before MEGA-PRESS collection, 1 could not tolerate scanning, 2 had corrupted data, and 1 was excluded per spectra quality control, resulting in 113 GABA measures at baseline. Of the 96 FEP recruited, 14 did not have baseline spectroscopy data and 3 were excluded for spectral quality, resulting in 79 GABA measures at baseline. Reasons for spectral quality data exclusion are listed in the Data quality control section. A flowchart of the number of participants per group at each time point is in Figure 2.

Results

We found no significant group differences in age, gender, or parental socioeconomic status (Table 1). As expected, groups differed significantly on smoking, and HC showed significantly higher scores on the RBANS than FEP. Patients’ psychosis symptom severity, based on BPRS scores on the Positive subscale and on Total BPRS score, decreased after 6 and 16 weeks of treatment (Table 1, P < .001 for significant differences found on the BPRS Positive subscale and Total scores between baseline versus week 6, and baseline versus week 16). The average medication dose at week 16 for those prescribed risperidone was 4.6 mg (SD = 2.28).

When comparing treatment response groups to HC, we found that baseline GABA levels were significantly lower in patients considered nonresponders at week 16 (n = 15) than HC (n = 110; F(1, 120) = 7.472, p = .007; Figure 3C), but there was no significant difference between responders (n = 46) and HC (n = 110; F(1, 151) = 0.220, p = .640; Figure 3D). Discussion

To our knowledge, this is the largest longitudinal study assessing GABA function in the dorsal ACC in vivo in antipsychotic medication-naïve FEP. We report that GABA levels in this region were significantly lower in patients compared to match HC. GABA levels were lower in patients at baseline and were not affected by treatment with risperidone. GABA levels did not correlate with cognitive function. Baseline GABA levels did differ between nonresponders and HC, whereas they did not differ between responders and HC. Baseline GABA levels did not correlate with percent change in BPRS positive symptom scores.

Our findings are in agreement with Bojesen31 who reported lower ACC GABA/Cr (GABA scaled to total Cr) in medication-naïve FEP at baseline, after 6 weeks on aripiprazole monotherapy, and after 26 weeks on “naturalistic antipsychotic treatment,” and findings of GABA/Cr levels unaffected by treatment. Together, this study and ours strongly support the proposition that GABA levels are reduced at the start of psychotic illness. Our results contrast with those of de la Fuente Sandoval, who reported higher ACC GABA levels in medication-naïve FEP at baseline, and reduction after 4 weeks of treatment.32 However, it should be noted that de la Fuente-Sandoval targeted the medial prefrontal cortex and dorsal caudate,32 and this difference in voxel location could explain the differences in GABA findings, as GABA measurements may vary by location, even within the cingulate cortex.42 Interestingly, lower ACC GABA levels were also observed in a large group of medicated FEP (as defined as within 24 months of first psychotic symptoms) in comparison with HC.26

Bojesen has now published a 2-year follow-up to her initial study.43 Interestingly in this study, FEP ACC GABA levels were lower in comparison to HC at baseline and at 6 weeks, but not anymore after 26 weeks and after 2 years. This agrees with recent meta-analyses showing reduction of ACC GABA in FEP, but not in chronic patients. On the other hand, Wang44 recently reported a 4-year longitudinal follow-up to the original cohort of medicated FEP showing reduced ACC GABA. In this follow-up study, FEP patients demonstrated a significant longitudinal decrease in ACC GABA. Thus, while a reduction in ACC GABA at illness onset appears to be well demonstrated, the trajectory of ACC GABA beyond the initial reduction is still not well established.

Intriguingly, so far, findings of GABA reduction in FEP have been limited to the ACC. The Bojesen study included 2 voxels (ACC, thalamus)7,31 and Wang’s study included 5 (ACC, thalamus, dorsolateral frontal, orbital frontal, and centrum semiovale).26 In both studies, only ACC GABA was reduced in FEP. This is also in agreement with a recent meta-analysis evaluating GABA levels in frontal lobe, where reduction in GABA was specific to the ACC and no other frontal region.24 The ACC is an important hub of the salience network45; its function is closely associated with conflict resolution and cognitive control.46 It has an established functional connectivity (FC) to the striatum47 and this connectivity is altered in medication-naïve FEP compared to HC.48 In addition, ACC GABA differentially predicted ACC-striatum FC in FEP compared to HC, where the correlation was negative in FEP and positive in HC,49 suggesting alteration through an inhibitory mechanism. Further understanding of the relationship between ACC GABA and ACC-striatal physiology may shed light on the early stages of psychosis pathophysiology.

We failed to identify a correlation between ACC GABA and cognition, as measured by RBANS subscales and total scores, in either group. This agrees with the studies of Bojesen7 and Wang,26 but contrasts with those of Rowland29 and Reid.30 Because these 4 studies used a variety of cognitive tests tapping into different cognitive functions, it is difficult to draw meaningful conclusions. Future studies should evaluate the correlation of ACC GABA with a battery of cognitive tests tailored for the assessment of ACC functions. In addition, because cognitive function is already compromised before the emergence of positive symptoms in antipsychotic-naïve populations, the likelihood of identifying correlations between GABA and cognition might be low. Furthermore, shifts in bioenergetic regulation that are affected by genetic and environmental factors could impact downstream levels of GABA.50 In future studies, the evaluation of the inhibition/excitation balance in relationship with cognition should be considered.

We did find that ACC GABA levels can differentiate treatment responders and nonresponders, agreeing with the Bojesen study.31 Bojesen compared baseline GABA levels in HC, responders, and nonresponders at 6 and 26 weeks, and found that GABA levels did not significantly differ between HC and responders, or between HC and nonresponders at 6 weeks. However, nonresponders at 26 weeks had significantly lower baseline GABA levels than HC. Similarly, we found that nonresponders at 16 weeks had significantly lower baseline GABA levels than HC, and that responders and HC did not significantly differ. This consistent finding of lower GABA levels in nonresponders could reflect the importance of appropriate GABA concentrations in the ACC. Though we did not find direct correlations between GABA and symptomatology, we speculate that decreased GABA levels in the ACC could underly the pathophysiology of psychosis spectrum disorders, as well as strongly affect treatment outcomes.

These findings, which support that ACC GABA levels at baseline are associated with long-term treatment response, are clinically relevant, especially for FEP at illness onset. Our data and those of Bojesen31 suggest different pathophysiology in responders versus nonresponders, where nonresponders show early GABA dysfunction. It is possible that early GABA dysfunction could be targeted using known GABA-modulating drugs.51,52 ACC GABA levels could serve as a biomarker for predicting treatment response. A normative reference interval based on HC GABA levels could aid in guiding treatment choices, where patients whose GABA levels suggest future nonresponse could avoid the extended process of trial and error with different medications. Future studies should examine the predictive power of GABA levels in combination with other factors associated with nonresponse, such as resting state functional connectivity,48,53 duration of untreated psychosis,54,55 symptom severity scores,56 and genetic factors,57 in order to optimize therapeutic strategies and improve patient outcomes.

Our findings should be interpreted in the context of several strengths and limitations. The major strength of this study is that this is the largest longitudinal neuroimaging study characterizing GABA levels in psychosis spectrum disorders. Further, we used MEGA-PRESS with macromolecule-suppressed editing pulses to acquire GABA, which has been suggested to be a key methodological improvement of the standard MEGA-PRESS sequence, as macromolecule-suppressed GABA measurements correlate more strongly with behavior than macromolecule-contaminated measurements, thus increasing statistical power.58–60 One limitation is that more patients’ spectra than HCs’ spectra were excluded. Similarly, HC and FEP significantly differed in smoking measures; in our sample, we did not find any correlation between GABA levels and PPD, though future studies should investigate the effects of smoking on GABA, especially by brain region and within patients with psychosis spectrum disorders. Further, treatment response was examined on the basis of sorting patients as responders or nonresponders, based on criteria defined by Andreasen et al., which defined remission by using an absolute threshold of severity of diagnostic symptoms, rather than percentage improvements from a particular baseline.33 With this consideration, the working group defined a period of 6 months as the minimum time threshold during which symptom severity criteria must be maintained to achieve remission; in this study, treatment response or nonresponse was based on measurements at 16 weeks after baseline.

Conclusion

Our findings strongly support the proposition that GABA dysfunction in the ACC is an important feature of the core pathophysiology of psychosis, as it is already present in medication-naïve FEP. This dysfunction does not appear to be attenuated by conventional antipsychotic treatment over a period of 16 weeks. Our results also point to the clinical relevance of baseline GABA levels in distinguishing patients who are likely to show a poor clinical response to treatment. It will be important to investigate pharmacological interventions that specifically target GABAergic dysfunction in FEP.
