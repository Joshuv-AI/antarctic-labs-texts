Grey–green spatial morphology relates to summer daytime and nighttime land surface temperature across local climate zones in Wuhan
Authors: Ziyi Jiang, Jing Bai, Yuanyuan Zhang, Yifei Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Urban grey and green spaces jointly shape the urban thermal environment, yet their associations with land surface temperature (LST) are spatially heterogeneous and context-dependent. Existing studies have mainly focused on the city-wide scale or on individual spatial elements, with limited attention to the differentiated mechanisms through which grey–green space affects LST across Local Climate Zone (LCZ). This study developed a multidimensional grey–green indicator system integrating two-dimensional patterns, three-dimensional structures, and morphological spatial characteristics. Multiple machine learning models were then used to examine the marginal effects and nonlinear interactions of grey–green indicators on daytime LST, nighttime LST, and composite daytime–nighttime LST difference (LST_DN) across LCZ types. The optimal model explained more than 80% of LST variation. XGBoost–SHAP analysis revealed day–night transition in the contributions of grey–green indicators. Grey-space indicators contributed more strongly to model-predicted daytime LST and LST_DN, with B_PLAND, BD, and BH contributing 45.69% on average. By contrast, green-space indicators played a stronger role at night, G_PLAND contributed 30.3% and 28.1% in open low-rise and natural zones, respectively, and each 0.5 increase in G_PLAND reduced nighttime LST by about 0.6–1.2 °C. The combined effects of grey and green spaces differed significantly across LCZs. Compact built-up zones were mainly characterized by synergistic warming among grey-space indicators, whereas in open built-up and natural zones, increasing G_PLAND above approximately 0.6 reduced the positive SHAP contribution associated with high B_PLAND. These findings provide scientific support for LCZ-based thermal governance and refined grey–green space optimization in high-density cities.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-61366-0.

Introduction

Under the combined effects of global climate change and rapid urbanization, cities have become concentrated exposure spaces for extreme heat risk1. By 2050, approximately 68%–70% of the global population is projected to live in urban areas. The expansion of built-up areas, the increase in impervious surfaces, and intensified anthropogenic heat emissions are reshaping urban land-cover structures and surface energy balance, thereby exacerbating the urban heat island effect. Meanwhile, extreme heat events are becoming increasingly frequent, and the probability that global mean temperature will exceed 1.5 °C above pre-industrial levels during 2025–2029 is estimated to be as high as 70%, indicating that urban heat risk is likely to continue rising in the future2. In high-density cities, persistent heat, especially nighttime heat retention, not only increases energy consumption and heat-related health risks, but also weakens urban ecological security and climate-adaptation capacity. Land surface temperature (LST), as a key indicator of the urban thermal environment, can sensitively reflect how urban surface characteristics and spatial organization influence thermal conditions3. Therefore, clarifying the relationships between urban spatial structure and LST is essential for supporting climate-adaptive spatial governance and sustainable urban development.

The urban thermal environment is jointly shaped by natural conditions, land cover, human activities, and spatial morphology, among which land-cover composition and its spatial organization constitute the most direct physical basis4. As two core subsystems of urban space, grey space and green space jointly shape the spatial pattern of LST. Grey space, mainly composed of buildings, roads, and other impervious surfaces, is generally associated with heat accumulation through low albedo, high heat-storage capacity, and restricted local ventilation, whereas green space can improve thermal conditions through vegetation transpiration, canopy shading, and improved air exchange5. Previous studies have examined the relationships between grey–green space and LST from multiple dimensions, including green-space coverage, landscape pattern indices, MSPA-based structural characteristics, and two- and three-dimensional indicators such as building density, building height, and canopy height6,7. These studies have shown that urban green spaces with higher aggregation and connectivity generally produce stronger cooling effects8, while large UGS core areas and corridor-like structures connecting ecological sources can also contribute to thermal regulation9.

However, urban space is a complex system jointly constituted by grey space, green space, and their interactions, and its internal characteristics exhibit pronounced spatial heterogeneity10,11. The same indicator may produce differentiated, or even opposite, thermal effects under different spatial contexts and temporal processes12. For example, building height may enhance nighttime heat retention in some high-density areas, while reducing daytime surface warming through shading. Green-space coverage generally exerts a cooling effect, but its cooling benefit may also be constrained by green-space fragmentation, spatial connectivity, and the surrounding built environment13. Although previous studies have separately revealed the effects of grey or green space on the thermal environment14, a systematic understanding is still lacking of how their spatial forms jointly influence LST, especially how these effects vary across urban spatial contexts.

Compared with administrative boundaries or land-use functional types, the LCZ framework better captures differences in local surface structure and in the background conditions under which urban thermal environments are formed, thereby providing a more targeted analytical framework for revealing spatial heterogeneity in the urban thermal environment. Previous studies have shown that both LST levels and dominant predictors differ significantly among LCZs, with compact built-up, open built-up, and natural zones often exhibiting distinct daytime and nighttime thermal characteristics15. Beyond spatial-context differences, the urban thermal environment itself also shows pronounced day-night variation. Daytime land surface temperature is mainly related to solar radiation absorption, surface heat capacity, spatial shading, and vegetation transpiration, whereas nighttime land surface temperature is more closely associated with the release of daytime heat storage, spatial enclosure, sky exposure, and local heat-exchange efficiency16. The daytime-nighttime LST difference further reflects the contrast between daytime surface warming and nighttime cooling. Although recent studies have examined spatial variations in urban thermal environments and identified the roles of urban morphology or green-space patterns, most have focused on inter-LCZ LST differences or single spatial elements17,18. Less attention has been given to how grey and green spaces, as an interacting spatial system, are associated with daytime warming, nighttime heat retention, and daytime–nighttime LST difference under different LCZ contexts17. In particular, it remains unclear whether two-dimensional patterns, three-dimensional structures, and MSPA-based morphology of grey–green space exhibit context-dependent nonlinear responses, directional shifts, and interaction patterns.

Wuhan, a typical high-density megacity in central China, experiences prolonged summer heat and high heat-exposure risk. Its main urban area contains complex built-environment types and a pronounced interweaving of grey and green spaces, making it a representative case for examining how grey–green spatial morphology is associated with urban thermal environment under different LCZ contexts19. This study focuses on the main urban area of Wuhan and develops a grey–green spatial indicator system covering two-dimensional landscape patterns, three-dimensional structures, and MSPA-based morphological characteristics. Based on LCZ characteristics, the study units were classified into four typical contexts: compact built-up, open mid-/high-rise, open low-rise, and natural green-space zones. SHAP, partial dependence plots, and interaction analysis were then combined to systematically interpret the model-predicted associations, nonlinear response patterns, and interaction patterns of grey–green spatial indicators across LCZ contexts. Specifically, this study addresses three questions. First, how do summer daytime LST (LST_D), nighttime LST (LST_N), and composite daytime–nighttime LST difference (LST_DN) vary across LCZ contexts? Second, do grey- and green-space indicators show different contribution levels and association directions across LCZ contexts and between daytime and nighttime thermal conditions? Third, do key grey–green indicators exhibit LCZ-dependent nonlinear response patterns and interactions? This study developed an interpretive framework for grey–green spatial morphology using LCZs as local climatic contexts.

Within this framework, the two-dimensional landscape patterns, three-dimensional structures, and MSPA-based morphological characteristics of grey and green spaces were incorporated into a unified analysis to reveal their differentiated associated with daytime LST, nighttime LST, and composite daytime–nighttime LST difference across LCZ contexts. By further synthesizing these associations into an LCZ-conditioned grey–green thermal response framework, this study provides scientific evidence for zoning-based thermal governance and refined grey–green space optimization in high-density cities.

Methodology

Study area

Wuhan is located in central China (29°58′–31°22′ N, 113°41′–115°05′ E) and is the capital of Hubei Province as well as one of China’s important national central cities19. The city covers approximately 8,569.15 km2 and had a permanent population of about 13.81 million in 2024. Its terrain is dominated by plains and low hills, and it has a typical humid subtropical monsoon climate, characterized by hot and humid summers and a historical extreme maximum temperature of 44.5 °C. Under the combined influence of rapid urbanization and a high-density built environment, Wuhan has become one of the megacities in China with relatively high summer heat-exposure risk.

The main urban area of Wuhan is highly representative. In recent years, rapid urban expansion has substantially altered land-cover composition and built-environment structure, resulting in complex and diverse grey–green spatial patterns within the study area20. At the same time, the area contains both high-density compact built-up environments and large-scale green spaces such as East Lake, where natural green spaces and built-up areas are strongly interwoven, producing pronounced spatial heterogeneity21. Data source and preprocessing

In this study, summer daytime LST (LST_D), nighttime LST (LST_N), and composite daytime–nighttime LST difference (LST_DN) were used as the dependent variable, while grey-space and green-space indicators were used as independent variables. Multi-source datasets, including thermal infrared remote sensing, land-use classification, building vector data, and canopy height data, were integrated to construct the thermal variables and grey–green spatial indicators. To ensure spatial consistency, all datasets were projected to the same coordinate system and aggregated to a 1 km × 1 km grid scale. This study focuses on the differentiated associations between grey–green spatial morphology and LST under Local Climate Zone (LCZ) contexts, this scale was considered appropriate for representing neighborhood-scale morphological environments. This scale is consistent with the typical size of urban blocks in Chinese cities, and is therefore suitable for representing local morphological environments at the neighborhood scale. It also reduces inconsistencies caused by integrating datasets with different spatial resolutions and data structures. The main datasets included ECOSTRESS LST products (https://earthexplorer.usgs.gov/), land-use/land-cover data derived from Sentinel-2 imagery (https://earthengine.google.com/), OpenStreetMap building vector data (http://download.geofabrik.de/), and NASA canopy height data (https://www.earthdata.nasa.gov/).

LST calculation

LST in Wuhan was derived from the ECOSTRESS Level-2 ECO2LSTE product, Atmospherically Corrected LST and Emissivity, acquired from the International Space Station. This product retrieves pixel-level LST and emissivity from thermal infrared bands at a spatial resolution of 70 m, provides both daytime and nighttime observations, and has been widely used in urban thermal environment studies22.

According to the research objectives, high-quality ECOSTRESS scenes covering Wuhan from June to August 2024 were selected as the base data, with priority given to scenes with low cloud cover, reliable quality flags, and effective coverage of the study area. The detailed acquisition information of the selected scenes is provided in Supplementary Table S1. Preprocessing included quality control, removal of clouds and cloud shadows, geometric correction, projection harmonization, and conversion of acquisition time to local time. Because ECOSTRESS observations are constrained by the orbit of the International Space Station, both acquisition date and overpass time vary among scenes. Therefore, the acquisition time of each scene was converted to Beijing Time (UTC + 8), and the local time, daytime/nighttime group, and specific time-period category of each scene were summarized in Table S1. The scenes were classified into daytime (08:00–20:00) and nighttime (20:01–07:59) groups according to local time.

To further clarify the temporal composition of the composites, the number and proportion of scenes in each daytime and nighttime sub-period were summarized in Supplementary Tables S2–S3. Median composites were then generated separately for daytime and nighttime scenes to produce summer daytime LST (LST_D) and summer nighttime LST (LST_N). Indicator calculation

Grey-space and green-space base maps provided the basis for calculating landscape pattern metrics and MSPA structural indicators. Sentinel-2 Level-2A images acquired in 2024 were obtained from Google Earth Engine (GEE) and used to derive urban green-space (UGS) and urban grey space (UBS) layers. After cloud and shadow masking, image compositing, and geometric correction, land-cover samples were obtained through stratified visual interpretation based on high-resolution basemaps23. According to the needs of urban thermal environment analysis, the original land-cover classes were further grouped into urban green space, urban grey space, and other types. UGS refers to vegetation-covered surfaces within the urban area, including park green space, affiliated green space, streetside green space, and other urban vegetation areas, whereas UBS refers to buildings, roads, squares, and other artificial impervious surfaces24. More than 1000 sample points were collected and randomly divided into training and validation subsets at a ratio of 70% and 30%, respectively. To characterize the three-dimensional structure of grey and green spaces, building vector data and canopy height data were introduced. Building data were obtained from OpenStreetMap (OSM), including building footprints and height information. Based on these data, building density (BD) and mean building height (BH) were calculated for each grid cell to represent the planar aggregation and vertical structural characteristics of the built environment, respectively. The three-dimensional characteristics of green space were represented using NASA canopy height data. After clipping, projection harmonization, and zonal statistics, mean canopy height (CH) was calculated for each 1 km grid cell to characterize the vertical structure of vegetation.

Research framework

This study aims to reveal the differentiated mechanisms through which grey–green spatial characteristics affect summer daytime, nighttime, and diurnal-difference LST in Wuhan under different LCZ contexts. The research framework consists of four steps. First, multi-source datasets, including summer ECOSTRESS LST, land-use/land-cover data, building vector data, and canopy height data, were acquired and preprocessed, and were then aggregated to a 1 km × 1 km grid scale. Three thermal-environment variables, namely LST_D, LST_N, and LST_DN, were constructed accordingly. Second, a grey–green spatial indicator system was developed. For grey space, two-dimensional landscape pattern metrics and three-dimensional building structure indicators were extracted; for green space, two-dimensional landscape pattern metrics, MSPA structural indicators, and canopy height indicators were calculated, resulting in 14 explanatory variables. Third, based on the LCZ theoretical framework, the study samples were classified into LCZ types using building density, building height, and green-space coverage characteristics, and the spatial patterns and distributions of LST_D, LST_N, and LST_DN were identified across different LCZ types. Fourth, machine learning models were developed separately under different LCZ contexts. The performance of CatBoost, Random Forest, LightGBM, AdaBoost, and XGBoost was compared for the three thermal-environment variables, and the optimal model was selected for interpretation. Finally, SHAP, marginal-effect analysis, and interaction analysis were combined to reveal the differentiated effects of grey–green spatial characteristics on daytime, nighttime, and diurnal-difference thermal environments across LCZ contexts (Fig. 2).Fig. 2Research framework.

LCZ classification

The Local Climate Zone (LCZ) framework is an important classification scheme for characterizing local built environments and underlying thermal surface conditions in cities15. Based on the Stewart and Oke27 LCZ framework and previous LCZ-based urban thermal studies, mean building density, mean building height, and green-space coverage were selected as operational classification indicators at the 1 km grid scale (Table 2)22,28. These indicators represent horizontal building compactness, vertical built structure, and green-space dominance, respectively, which correspond to the compact/open, high-/mid-/low-rise, and vegetated surface-cover dimensions of the LCZ framework. Model comparison and modeling

To systematically evaluate the effects of grey–green spatial indicators on the urban thermal environment under different LCZ contexts, machine learning models were constructed independently for the four LCZ types, with LST_D, LST_N, and LST_DN used as dependent variables and the 14 grey–green spatial indicators used as predictors. Given the complex nonlinear relationships between urban thermal conditions and spatial variables, five commonly used ensemble learning models were compared: CatBoost, Random Forest, LightGBM, AdaBoost, and XGBoost. These models have advantages in handling high-dimensional nonlinear data, variable interactions, and collinearity, and have been widely applied in studies of urban thermal-environment driving mechanisms29,30. To ensure a fair comparison, the same data-splitting strategy was applied across all contexts, with samples randomly divided into a training set and a test set at a ratio of 70% and 30%, respectively. To improve generalization and reduce the risk of overfitting, model parameters were optimized on the training set. Specifically, cross-validation combined with grid search or random search was used to tune key hyperparameters, including tree depth, learning rate, subsampling ratio, and the number of weak learners. Model performance was evaluated using the coefficient of determination (R2) and root mean square error (RMSE) (Eqs. (5-6)). R2 measures the explanatory power of the model for the variation in the dependent variable, with higher values indicating better fit, whereas RMSE measures the overall deviation between predicted and observed values, with lower values indicating smaller prediction errors31.5\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$R^{{2}} = {1 - }\frac{{\sum\nolimits_{i} {\left( {a_{i} - b_{i} } \right)^{2} } }}{{\sum\nolimits_{i} {\left( {b_{i} - \overline{b}_{i} } \right)^{2} } }}$$\end{document}6\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$RMSE = \sqrt {\frac{1}{m}\sum\nolimits_{i = 1}^{m} {\left( {a_{i} - b_{i} } \right)^{2} } }$$\end{document}where, ai and bi represent the predicted value and the true value of the i-th observation, respectively. \documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$\overline{b}$$\end{document} is the mean of all true values, and m is the sample size.

Discussion

LCZ-conditioned grey–green thermal response framework

The relationship between grey–green space and LST was not determined by a single spatial element, but was jointly shaped by local climatic context, day–night thermal conditions, and grey–green spatial configuration. Grey space is generally associated with heat accumulation through impervious-surface expansion, low albedo, and high heat-storage capacity33, whereas green space can improve thermal conditions through shading, evapotranspiration, and local heat exchange5. Recent studies on Greening–LCZ–UHI mitigation and grey–green thermal resilience have also emphasized that the cooling role of urban green space depends not only on its area, but also on its spatial form, connectivity, and surrounding built environment34,35. Consistent with this view, this study further shows that the general understanding of grey-space warming and green-space cooling cannot be uniformly applied across all LCZ contexts. Instead, grey–green thermal associations varied with local morphological background and day–night thermal conditions. Unlike previous studies that mainly used LCZ and machine learning for LST prediction or feature ranking36,37, this study used LCZ as a contextual framework to interpret how grey and green spatial morphology was differently associated with daytime and nighttime thermal conditions.

First, the positive associations of grey space indicators with LST differed significantly across LCZs38. In compact built-up zones (LCZ 1–3), BD and BH (25.4%, 27.5% respectively) contributed more strongly to model-predicted LST, indicating that planar building density and vertical structure explain local thermal variation better than the proportion of grey space alone in high-density built environments. A likely reason is that compact built-up areas are characterized by high building density, continuous impervious surfaces, and restricted ventilation, which facilitate heat accumulation during the day and heat retention at night39. By contrast, in open built-up and natural green-space contexts, B_PLAND became more important for LST_D and LST_DN (Sect. 3.3), suggesting that model-predicted LST was more sensitive to changes in impervious-surface proportion in areas with lower building compactness.

Second, the negative associations of green space indicators with LST_N showed clear nighttime sensitivity and LCZ dependence40. G_PLAND was an important predictor of nighttime LST in LCZ 6 and LCZ A, with contributions of 30.3% and 28.1%, respectively, and its high values were mainly associated with negative SHAP values (Fig. 6). PDP results further indicated that, in open low-rise and natural green-space contexts, higher G_PLAND was generally associated with lower nighttime LST, with a decrease of approximately 0.6–1.2 °C. This suggests that the low heat-storage capacity, evapotranspiration, and air-exchange functions of green space can operate more effectively in open low-rise and natural green-space contexts, which may explain the stronger negative nighttime association. In compact built-up zones, however, the contribution of green-space indicators was relatively limited, possibly because small green patches, fragmented green spaces, and surrounding heat storage from grey space constrained their cooling potential41.

Notably, the direction of some indicators varied between daytime and nighttime thermal processes, which distinguishes this study from traditional single-period LST analyses. Taking BH as an example, previous studies have often regarded building height as an important morphological factor that intensifies urban heat islands42. However, this study found that BH generally showed a negative marginal association with LST_D and LST_DN, but a positive association with LST_N. This pattern suggests that building height has a dual effect: taller buildings may be associated with lower daytime surface temperature through shading, whereas greater building volume and spatial enclosure may be associated with higher nighttime heat retention43.

Interaction analysis further showed that, in compact built-up zones, combinations of grey-space indicators such as BD and B_PLAND were associated with higher positive SHAP contributions to LST_D (Sect. 3.5). By contrast, in open built-up and natural green-space zones, a clearer trade-off emerged between B_PLAND and G_PLAND: high B_PLAND combined with low G_PLAND was generally associated with stronger positive SHAP contributions, whereas higher G_PLAND could partly reduce the positive SHAP contribution associated with grey-space indicators. This is consistent with previous findings that grey-space indicators can act synergistically to intensify warming, whereas green space can alleviate thermal conditions14. However, this study further contextualizes this relationship across different LCZs, suggesting that grey–green space optimization should not rely solely on increasing green space or reducing development intensity, but should instead identify key indicators and their combinations according to specific LCZ backgrounds.

Overall, these findings suggest an LCZ-conditioned grey–green thermal response framework. Within this framework, LCZ context acts as a background constraint that regulates the relative importance of grey-space heat storage, building-induced shading and enclosure, green-space cooling capacity, and grey–green spatial interaction. In compact built-up zones, high building density, vertical structure, and spatial enclosure increase the importance of building-related indicators, so grey-space intensity and three-dimensional built morphology become the dominant explanatory dimensions. In open built-up zones, lower compactness and greater spatial openness weaken the direct dominance of building density and make the balance between impervious-surface proportion and green-space coverage more important. In natural green-space contexts, the thermal role of green space is more strongly expressed, but local grey-space expansion can still disturb the cooling background of these areas.

This framework also explains the day–night shift observed in this study. During the daytime, surface energy input and impervious-surface heating make grey-space proportion and built-form characteristics more closely associated with LST_D and LST_DN. At night, when stored heat release, sky exposure, ventilation, and cooling diffusion become more important, green-space coverage and connectivity show stronger associations with LST_N, especially in open and green-space-dominated contexts. Therefore, the mechanism change across LCZs can be understood as the result of interactions among LCZ background conditions, day–night thermal processes, and grey–green spatial configuration. This theoretical synthesis extends the empirical finding of “grey-space daytime dominance and green-space nighttime relevance” into a more general framework for interpreting LCZ-dependent urban thermal environments.

Implications for LCZ-based grey–green space optimization

The associations between urban grey–green space and LST varied clearly across LCZ context (Sect. 3.3). Therefore, urban thermal-environment optimization should not rely on a uniform greening or impervious-surface reduction strategy, but should identify the dominant grey–green indicators and spatial configurations under different local climate contexts. Previous studies have emphasized the importance of increasing green-space coverage, enhancing green-space connectivity, or reducing impervious surface and using strategic greening approaches to mitigate urban heat islands8,44. The results of this study support this view, but further indicate that the applicability of these strategies depends on LCZ contexts. In compact built-up zones, model-predicted LST was more strongly associated with BD and BH, whereas in open built-up and natural green-space zones, the balance between B_PLAND and G_PLAND becomes more critical. Accordingly, LCZ-based grey–green optimization can provide more targeted guidance than strategies based on a single indicator.

Specifically, in compact built-up zones (LCZ 1–3), priority should be given to reducing excessive building compactness and weakening the combined positive thermal contributions of grey-space indicators (Sect. 4.3). This study found that BD and BH contributed strongly to model-predicted daytime and nighttime LST, respectively, suggesting that thermal problems in compact built-up zones may be related not only to impervious-surface expansion but also to building density, vertical structure, and spatial enclosure. Because large-scale green-space expansion is often difficult in high-density blocks, optimization should focus on improving the efficiency of green-space insertion within limited space. Possible measures include adding pocket parks, roof greening, vertical greening, ventilation corridors, optimized building spacing, and high-albedo or permeable materials. In particular, continuous high-density buildings should not be combined with large impervious paved surfaces, so as to avoid the dual pressure of daytime heat accumulation and nighttime heat retention.

In open built-up zones, the optimization focus should shift from controlling building compactness alone to balancing grey-space proportion and green-space configuration. In open mid-rise zones (LCZ 4–5), B_PLAND was the primary predictor of LST_D and LST_DN, while G_PLAND and G_AI contributed more strongly to LST_N. This suggests that thermal optimization in these zones should combine grey-space reduction with green-space enhancement. In planning practice, large continuous impervious surfaces should be reduced, green space between building clusters should be increased, and green-space continuity should be strengthened through street green corridors, community green spaces, and open-space networks45. In open low-rise zones (LCZ 6), the proportional relationship between grey and green spaces should be further optimized. The fragmented expansion of roads, parking lots, and low-density residential impervious surfaces should be limited, while continuous green patches, courtyard greening, roadside green belts, and connections among green spaces, water bodies, and open spaces should be maintained.

For natural green-space zones (LCZ A), priority should be given to preventing local grey-space encroachment and maintaining green-space integrity. Although LCZ A is dominated by natural green space, B_PLAND still showed high contributions to LST_D and LST_DN. This indicates that local construction activities within or along the edges of natural green spaces may still weaken their thermal-regulation capacity. Therefore, the expansion of construction land and the occupation of hard facilities should be strictly controlled, while large core green spaces, ecological areas around water bodies, and peripheral urban green bases should be protected46. At the same time, green-space connectivity should be enhanced, green-space fragmentation should be reduced, and the continuity among structural units such as Core, Bridge, and Branch should be strengthened. In this way, natural green spaces can function not only as isolated cooling sources, but also as networks that diffuse cooling effects into surrounding built-up areas through greenways, waterfront spaces, and ecological corridors.

Overall, the key to LCZ-based thermal governance is not simply to increase the quantity of green space, but to optimize grey-space intensity, green-space coverage, and their spatial configuration according to the dominant thermal associations in different LCZ contexts.

Limitations

Although this study identified differentiated associations of summer daytime, nighttime, and composite daytime–nighttime LST with grey–green space in Wuhan by integrating LCZ contexts, grey–green morphological indicators, XGBoost–SHAP, PDPs, and interaction analysis, several limitations remain. First, the analysis was mainly based on ECOSTRESS LST data from summer 2024, which can capture the relationship between the urban thermal environment and grey–green spatial morphology during the hot season, but does not cover multi-year or multi-seasonal variation. In addition, because ECOSTRESS overpass times are variable, LST_D and LST_N should be interpreted as summer daytime and nighttime composite indicators rather than fixed-time observations. Future studies could incorporate long-term, multi-seasonal, and more temporally consistent LST data to test the stability of the LCZ-specific differences and day–night response patterns identified in this study. Second, this study focused on the two-dimensional patterns, three-dimensional structures, and MSPA-based morphological characteristics of grey and green spaces. However, the urban thermal environment and LCZ thermal complexity are also affected by meteorological conditions, sky view factor, anthropogenic heat emissions, surface material properties, population activity intensity, and urban ventilation conditions. Future studies could further integrate meteorological observations, SVF, detailed surface-material information, energy consumption, anthropogenic heat emissions, urban ventilation indicators, and ground-based measurements to improve the representation of LCZ thermal complexity and the comprehensiveness of urban heat-risk assessment. Third, XGBoost–SHAP, PDPs, and interaction analysis were effective for identifying variable importance, marginal thresholds, and interaction relationships. However, these methods mainly reveal statistical associations and model response patterns and should not be equated with strict causal relationships. Future studies could combine ground-based microclimate observations, urban climate modeling, scenario simulation, or intervention experiments to validate the actual cooling effects of grey–green optimization strategies under different LCZ contexts.

Overall, this study provides empirical evidence for understanding how grey–green spatial morphology is associated with the urban thermal environment under LCZ contexts. Future research based on longer time series, richer variable systems, and stronger causal validation frameworks would help further improve the understanding of thermal regulation mechanisms in urban grey–green space and provide more robust scientific support for climate-adaptive spatial governance in high-density cities.

Conclusion

This study developed a multidimensional grey–green spatial indicator system covering two-dimensional patterns, three-dimensional structures, and MSPA-based morphological characteristics. By integrating LCZ-based contextual classification with SHAP-based interpretable machine learning, the differentiated associations of grey–green spatial morphology with summer daytime LST, nighttime LST, and composite daytime–nighttime LST difference under different local climate contexts were examined. The main conclusions are as follows.

1) Built-up samples in Wuhan’s main urban area were dominated by LCZ 4–5 and LCZ 6, accounting for 41.58% and 38.07%, respectively. Summer thermal conditions showed clear day–night contrasts across LCZ contexts. Compact built-up zones (LCZ 1–3) showed the highest daytime LST (42.15 °C) and a composite daytime–nighttime LST difference of approximately 14 °C. 2) Grey-space indicators contributed more strongly to model-predicted LST_D and LST_DN, with B_PLAND, BD, and BH jointly contributing 45.69% on average. By contrast, green-space indicators showed stronger negative contributions to LST_N. G_PLAND was the leading green-space predictor of LST_N in LCZ 6 and LCZ A, with contributions of 30.3% and 28.1%, respectively. 3) Grey–green spatial indicators exhibited nonlinear responses and interaction patterns in model-predicted LST. A 0.5 increase in B_PLAND was associated with an approximately 1.3–3.3 °C increase in model-predicted LST_D and a 2.5–6.0 °C increase in model-predicted LST_DN, whereas a 0.5 increase in G_PLAND was associated with an approximately 0.6–1.2 °C decrease in LST_N. BH showed a contrasting day–night model response, being associated with lower LST_D and higher LST_N. Interaction analysis further indicated that compact built-up zones were mainly characterized by strong positive SHAP contributions when multiple grey-space indicators were high, whereas in open built-up and natural green-space zones, higher G_PLAND reduced part of the positive SHAP contribution associated with B_PLAND.

Overall, this study shows that the associations between grey–green space and the urban thermal environment varied with LCZ context and day–night thermal conditions. In compact built-up zones, priority should be given to reducing excessive grey-space intensity and improving green-space insertion efficiency. In open built-up zones, B_PLAND should be controlled while G_PLAND, G_AI, and green-space connectivity are enhanced. In natural green-space zones, local grey-space expansion should be limited and green-space integrity should be strengthened. Beyond the empirical findings from Wuhan, this study further suggests that LCZ-dependent thermal differences can be interpreted through the combined roles of background morphological context, day–night thermal processes, and grey–green spatial configuration. This LCZ-conditioned grey–green thermal response framework provides a transferable perspective for interpreting urban thermal heterogeneity and supporting climate-adaptive spatial governance in high-density cities.
