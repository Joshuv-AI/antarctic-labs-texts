Accuracy of Magnetic Resonance Spectroscopy–Detected Fumarate Peak for Diagnosing Fumarate Hydratase Deficiency in Uterine Leiomyomas: A Prospective Study
Authors: Guiqin Liu, Wenxin Yu, Shihang Pan, Yuansheng Luo, Jingli Chen, Mengying Zhu, Zaoyu Wang, Yang Song, Jin Zhang, Jianrong Xu, Yan Zhou, Jun Ma, Guangyu Wu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo evaluate the diagnostic performance of magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) in discriminating fumarate hydratase-deficient (FH-d) uterine leiomyomas (ULs) from FH-preserved ULs.Materials and MethodsThis study consisted of three stages, with independent cohorts recruited for each stage: 1) sample-size estimation was retrospectively performed on UL specimens (diameter ≥3 cm; age, 20–40 years) from our database with immunohistochemistry (IHC) for 2-succinocysteine (2-SC) as the reference, without genetic testing, 2) MRS sequence optimization in confirmed FH germline mutation participants with ultrasound-detected ULs (diameter ≥3 cm), without IHC analysis, and 3) prospective diagnostic test accuracy was evaluated in consecutive participants with ultrasound-detected ULs (diameter ≥3 cm; age, 20–40 years), using IHC for 2-SC for determining the FH status and subsequent genetic testing in those with positive 2-SC results to identify whether FH mutations were germline or somatic in origin. The choline and fumarate peaks in MRS were classified as positive, negative, or technical failure (TF). TFs were analyzed separately and excluded from the primary diagnostic accuracy calculations. T1-, T2-, and diffusion-weighted images were interpreted as hyperintense or hypointense. The enhancement rate and apparent diffusion coefficient were also acquired. Diagnostic performance was compared between MRS and various magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) features.ResultsThe optimal MRS parameters for the fumarate peak were echo time (TE) = 140 ms and an average of 256. Among the 360 prospective participants, 37 were confirmed to have FH-dULs. MRS showed positive fumarate peaks in 35 of 37 FH-dULs. After excluding six TFs, the positive fumarate peak on MRS showed 94.6% (35/37) sensitivity, 99.7% (316/317) specificity, and 99.2% (351/354) accuracy, all of which were significantly superior to those of other MRI features (P ≤ 0.002).ConclusionA positive fumarate peak on MRS may be a useful imaging biomarker for diagnosing FH-dULs.Keywords: Fumarate hydratase deficiency, Uterine leiomyoma, Magnetic resonance spectroscopy, Magnetic resonance imaging, Diagnostic performance, Diagnostic test accuracy

INTRODUCTION

Fumarate hydratase (FH) is a crucial enzyme in the tricarboxylic acid cycle [1]. FH deficiency leads to fumarate accumulation, which is associated with various diseases [2]. Among these, fumarate hydratase-deficient uterine leiomyomas (FH-dULs) has particularly clinical significance due to their association with hereditary leiomyomatosis and renal cell carcinoma syndrome (HLRCC), also known as fumarate hydratase-deficient renal cell carcinoma (FHRCC). This association is critical because FHRCC is characterized by early onset, high aggressiveness, and poor prognosis [3]. Although FH-dULs account for only 0.4%–1.6% of all ULs [4,5,6], their actual incidence is likely underestimated, similar to that of FHRCC [7,8]. Therefore, accurate diagnosis of FH-dUL extends beyond UL management to prompt renal surveillance at an early stage and optimization of the monitoring of other issues related to FH deficiency.

Diagnosing FH deficiency remains challenging. Clinical manifestations and conventional imaging lack specificity [9], because FH-dULs often resemble common ULs. Pathological diagnosis shows limited accuracy [5,10]. Furthermore, immunohistochemistry (IHC) assessments for FH are unreliable for predicting the FH status because certain FH mutations can yield false-negative IHC results owing to retained protein expression [11,12,13]. Although 2-succinocysteine (2-SC) is a highly specific and reliable biomarker for FH deficiency [11,13,14], the lack of commercially available 2-SC antibodies restricts its clinical use [15]. Moreover, while genetic testing is vital for detecting FH deficiency, it still yields inconclusive results [13,16] or has technical limitations [17,18] and remains costly with limited accessibility.

Magnetic resonance spectroscopy (MRS) allows noninvasive metabolite detection [19,20,21], and shows promise for disease-risk stratification when integrated with radiomics [22]. On the basis of prior success in identifying fumarate in patients with FHRCC [23] and a case report describing its detection in FH-dULs [24], we hypothesized that MRS could achieve high diagnostic accuracy for FH-dULs. However, the diagnostic performance of MRS and its practical applications in FH deficiency have not been fully explored. Therefore, this study aimed to prospectively evaluate the diagnostic performance of MRS for discriminating FH-dULs from FH-preserved ULs.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Participants

This prospective, single-center study was approved by the Ethics and Research Committee of Renji Hospital (IRB No. LY2024-055-B), and written informed consent was obtained from all participants. The study protocol consisted of three stages: sample-size estimation, MRS sequence optimization, and a prospective study of diagnostic test accuracy (details are shown in Fig. 1), with independent cohorts recruited for each stage to fulfill specific objectives. The FH status was determined by IHC for 2-SC, since 2-SC is a highly specific and sensitive biomarker for FH-deficient tumors [11,13,14], whereas FH staining served only as an auxiliary finding because of its limited reliability [11,12,13].

Flowchart of the study. Data are presented as the number of patients. MRS = magnetic resonance spectroscopy, UL = uterine leiomyoma, IHC = immunohistochemistry, 2-SC = 2-succinocysteine, FH = fumarate hydratase, dUL = deficient uterine leiomyoma

For sample-size estimation, data from the pathology database of the Renji Hospital were analyzed to identify cases of surgically confirmed ULs from January 2019 to December 2020. Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) age 20–40 years and 2) UL diameter ≥3 cm with available tissue samples in formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) blocks. FH status was determined using IHC for 2-SC, and genetic testing was not conducted at this stage.

From January 2024 to June 2024, 10 participants with ultrasound-detected ULs ≥3 cm and confirmed FH gene germline mutations were retrospectively analyzed for sequence optimization. All were previously found to show positive fumarate peaks on uterine MRS. IHC analysis for the ULs was not conducted during this stage.

For prospective study of diagnostic test accuracy, consecutive participants between July 2024 and July 2025 from the same clinic were enrolled. Inclusion criteria were as follows: 1) ultrasound-detected ULs ≥3 cm with surgery opted due to clinical symptoms, 2) age 20–40 years at the time of consent for surgery. Exclusion criteria were as follows: 1) history of therapy for ULs before MRI, 2) refusal or inability to undergo MRI examination, 3) poor image quality, 4) incomplete pathologic results, 5) pathology confirmed but not UL, and 6) lesions with significant necrosis or cystic degeneration. FH status was determined by IHC for 2-SC. Genetic testing was performed only in cases with positive 2-SC results to identify FH mutations as germline or somatic in origin.

We adopted these inclusion criteria because they were closely associated with a population at high risk for FH deficiency. The age range of 20–40 years is a high-incidence period for FH-dULs [25]. In addition, FH-dULs are characterized by early onset, multiplicity, and large tumor sizes [15]; thus, restricting UL size to ≥3 cm increased the pre-test probability, ensuring feasibility and cost-effectiveness, and secured adequate tumor volume for reliable MRS analysis [26]. Although these surgical participants are not the ultimate diagnostic targets, they represent an appropriate validation cohort; at this preliminary stage, our research intentionally focused on a cohort most likely to benefit from MRS to establish its diagnostic potential for FH-dULs and to obtain reliable reference standards. Most importantly, the long-term goal of MRS is not for broad population imaging but rather as a targeted adjunct imaging tool for selected high-risk groups.

Sample-Size Estimation

The sample size was calculated using PASS 15.0 software (Number Cruncher Statistical System, NCSS) with parameters 1 - β = 0.9 and α = 0.05, assuming MRS diagnostic sensitivity and specificity of 85% and 88%, respectively, on the basis of a prior study [23]. The prevalence of FH-dULs in participants (age, 20–40 years) at our center was determined during the first stage.

MRS Sequence Optimization

Five participants with FH-dULs underwent MRS at varying echo times (TEs) (30, 140, and 240 ms) with an average of 256 to determine the optimal TE with the highest signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of the fumarate peak (SNRfumarate). Another group of five participants underwent examinations with the optimized TEs across different averages (128, 256, and 512) to finalize the lowest average with the highest SNRfumarate.

Imaging Examinations and Data Processing

Imaging examinations were performed using a 3T MRI system (Magnetom Prisma; Siemens Healthineers, Erlangen, Germany) with body-coil transmission and 18-channel receiver coils. In participants with multiple lesions, the largest lesion was selected for MRS assessments. All participants were scanned during the early follicular phase (days 2–6 of their cycle). The MRI protocol included T2-weighted fast spin-echo, Dixon T1 gradient-echo, diffusion-weighted imaging (DWI), and dynamic contrast enhancement. MRS was performed using point-resolved spectroscopy monovoxel acquisition (Supplementary Material 1 provides details of the sequence parameters). Fumarate (C4H2O42-) showed a characteristic peak at 6.54 ppm (range, 6.44–6.64 ppm).

MRS data analysis was performed using jMRUI software (version 6.0; MRUI Consortium, http://www.jmrui.eu). The amplitudes and phases of the spectra were reviewed before quantification. Baseline correction for the unstable spectra was performed using a cubic spline approach, choosing data points close to zero at 7.5–8.0 ppm and 2.5–3.0 ppm [27]. The SNRs for fumarate and choline (SNRfumarate, SNRcholine) were calculated as the ratio of the fumarate and choline peak amplitudes to the RMS amplitude of noise, respectively, which were measured in the flat noise baseline region (>8 ppm or <0 ppm) [27]. Choline and fumarate were scored as positive with SNR ≥2. If choline was negative, MRS was deemed a technical failure (TF), since choline should be present in metabolically active tumors. A primary observer (G.L., 9 years of MRI experience), who was blinded to the genetic and pathological results, independently examined all spectra and scored the SNRs of the fumarate and choline peaks, as described above, the results of which were used for the main analysis. On the basis of the regions of interest (ROI), the enhancement rates (ERs) in three phases (defined as ER1, ER2, and ER3) and apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) were calculated. The T1-weighted imaging (T1WI), T2-weighted imaging (T2WI), and DWI were interpreted as hyperintense or hypointense by the same observer (G.L.) (Supplementary Material 2 provides details on data processing and image evaluation) [28,29]. A secondary observer (G.W., with 12 years of MRI experience) independently evaluated 100 randomly selected participant sequences for analysis of interobserver agreement.

IHC and Genotyping

FFPE tumor sections were subjected to FH and 2-SC staining using an EnVision kit (Dako, Glostrup, Denmark). The results of IHC staining were interpreted by a single pathologist (Z.W.) who was blinded to all the clinical and imaging findings. Genetic testing was performed using next-generation sequencing.

Statistical Analysis

Statistical analyses were performed with SPSS 25.0 (IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R Studio (version 4.4.1, https://cran.r-project.org). Continuous variables were expressed as mean ± standard deviation, while categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages. One-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) with Dunnett’s post-hoc test was used for pairwise comparisons of SNRfumarate among the different TEs and averages. Differences in ADC and ER values between the FH-dUL and FH-preserved groups were assessed using independent-sample t-tests or Mann–Whitney tests. Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests were used to compare the T1/T2/DWI signal intensities and positive fumarate and choline peaks between the two groups. MRS or MRI parameters that showed differences between the FH-dUL and FH-preserved groups were selected to evaluate their diagnostic performance in identifying FH-dULs. Sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy (i.e., [(true positives + true negatives)/(true positives + false negatives + true negatives + false positives)]) were calculated and compared in a pairwise manner: MRS versus each MRI parameter. Bonferroni adjustment was applied to account for multiple comparisons, with the significance threshold adjusted for the number of MRI parameters tested (P < 0.0125 = 0.05/4 for four pairwise comparisons). The ER diagnostic threshold was selected on the basis of the maximum Youden index. Intraclass correlation coefficients (ICCs) and kappa statistics were used to assess interobserver agreement, with 95% confidence intervals calculated for ICCs. Agreement levels were categorized as follows: 0–0.20 (poor), 0.21–0.40 (fair), 0.41–0.60 (moderate), 0.61–0.80 (good), and 0.81–1.00 (excellent). P < 0.05 was considered a statistically significant difference.

RESULTS

Participant Characteristics

Of the 501 participants eligible for sample-size estimation, 36 had confirmed FH-dULs, indicating a prevalence of 7.2%; thus, at least 17 FH-dULs and 219 FH-preserved participants were required. For sequence optimization, an independent cohort of 10 consecutive participants (median age, 40 years [range, 34–48 years]) was enrolled. Finally, a third independent cohort of 360 participants (median age, 35 years [range, 31–38 years]) was recruited for the prospective study of diagnostic test accuracy (Fig. 1), of which 37 participants were diagnosed with FH-dULs (24 germline mutations, 13 somatic mutations). MRS Sequence Optimization

A scan with a TE value of 140 ms yielded the highest SNRfumarate (TE140, 5.06 ± 2.69 vs. TE30, 2.40 ± 1.62, P = 0.044; TE140, 5.06 ± 2.69 vs. TE240, 1.68 ± 0.76, P = 0.014), making it the optimal choice for further studies. Increasing the average from 128 to 256 led to a significant improvement in the SNRfumarate (average128, 1.39 ± 0.74; average256, 4.06 ± 1.76; P = 0.004), whereas no notable difference was observed between averages of 256 and 512 (average256, 4.06 ± 1.76; average512, 4.28 ± 0.68; P = 0.769). Ultimately, the best combination of parameters was TE = 140 ms and average = 256 ms (Fig. 2). The choline peaks were consistently detected across all the cases (SNRcholine range, 3.62–56.26).

Assessment of Interobserver Agreement

On the 100 scans randomly selected to evaluate interobserver agreement (10 participants from the FH-dUL group and 90 from the FH-preserved group), the ICCs (95% confidence intervals) between the two observers for calculating SNRfumarate, SNRcholine, ADC, ER1, ER2, and ER3 were 0.94 (0.92, 0.96), 0.96 (0.94, 0.97), 0.82 (0.75, 0.88), 0.96 (0.94, 0.97), 0.97 (0.96, 0.98), and 0.98 (0.96, 0.98), respectively, demonstrating excellent agreement between the two observers. In the T1WI, T2WI and DWI assessments, the k values also showed excellent agreement (k = 1.00, 0.864, and 0.893, respectively).

MRS scans were successfully acquired in 354 of 360 participants (six TF cases, all FH-preserved, see details in Supplementary Table 2), whereas conventional MRI was successful in all cases. Positive results for fumarate peaks were noted in 36 cases, including 35 true positives and one false positive. Of the 318 negative fumarate peaks cases, 316 were true negatives and two were false negatives. Representative cases are shown in Figure 3 and Supplementary Figure 1. Diagnostic Performance of MRS and Conventional MRI for Discriminating FH-dULs From FH-Preserved ULs

After excluding TFs, the sensitivity and specificity were calculated using 37 FH-dUL cases and 317 FH-preserved controls, respectively. For the diagnosis of FH-dULs, the sensitivity, specificity, and accuracy of positive fumarate peaks were 94.6% (35/37), 99.7% (316/317), and 99.2% (351/354), respectively. The corresponding DWI values were 67.6% (25/37), 67.5% (214/317), and 67.5% (239/354), respectively. The corresponding values for ER1 were 37.8% (14/37), 82.7% (262/317), and 78.0% (276/354), respectively; those for ER2 were 54.1% (20/37), 74.5% (236/317), and 72.3% (256/354), respectively; and those for ER3 were 46.0% (17/37), 83.3% (264/317), and 79.4% (281/354), respectively. DISCUSSION

Our findings indicate that the detection of fumarate peak by MRS can serve as a highly accurate and noninvasive diagnostic biomarker for FH-dULs. In the final analytical cohort of 354 participants, this technique achieved favorable sensitivity (94.6%), specificity (99.7%) and accuracy (99.2%), demonstrating improved diagnostic performance over conventional imaging. The primary clinical implication is the utility of this technique as a promising adjunct for high-risk FH deficiency.

The high accuracy of MRS for detecting FH status of ULs is expected to be highly valuable in clinical settings. First, this method provides an accessible, reliable, and noninvasive platform. With the general availability of MRI at medical centers, this approach can provide clinicians with unique insights beyond the limited overall knowledge of FH deficiency. Second, FH-dULs may serve as a precursor of HLRCC or FHRCC [9]. Considering the aggressive characteristics of these diseases [3], adjunct diagnosis can facilitate renal surveillance at an earlier stage, enabling early treatment of FHRCC. Third, due to the heritability of gene mutations in some participants, adjunct diagnosis can also facilitate the early detection of family members with these gene mutations. Additionally, with advancements in in vitro fertilization [30,31], early diagnosis of FH deficiency may help prevent genetic inheritance in the offspring, especially in patients who have not undergone surgery for ULs. Finally, FH-dULs exhibit substantial hypervascularization [32] and are prone to recurrence after myomectomy [33]. A preoperative diagnosis can alert clinicians to the risk of intraoperative hemorrhage and the need for close postoperative follow-up. On the basis of our findings, UL-MRS is recommended for individuals aged 20–40 years with ULs ≥3 cm, and a positive UL-MRS result should prompt referral for genetic counseling and annual renal MRI surveillance for both the patients and their at-risk family members. Standard gynecological surveillance and management are recommended for MRS-negative cases.

This study was an extension of a previous MRS study in FHRCCs [23]. We considered that the use of MRS would be more viable in ULs than that reported in the formal literature, and our findings demonstrated that optimized MRS findings can serve as a more practical imaging biomarker for evaluating FH-dULs than for FHRCC, with a lower TF rate and improved diagnostic performance. Moreover, two false-negative results were obtained, both of which were later confirmed to be somatic mutations. We propose that the metabolic heterogeneity of fumarate accumulation at different tumor stages is a key contributor to false-negative results. In the comparatively early phase of the disease, or in certain subregions within the UL, excess fumarate may initially undergo a chemical process known as Michael addition, wherein it binds to thiol residues of free cysteine to form 2-SC [34], buffering intracellular fumarate levels. Thus, this reaction could prevent sufficient fumarate accumulation, yielding false-negative results on MRS. However, since somatic mutations do not carry a risk of transmission to offspring and only occur in the local tissue of the UL, the long-term outcomes of false-negative results are currently unknown, and no evidence in the literature suggests that such somatic mutations lead to fatal clinical outcomes.

Optimizing the MRS parameters is challenging because of scan-time constraints; therefore, we focused on a limited set of parameters. Given the relatively long relaxation time of fumarate within leiomyomas, the SNR can be potentially increased by increasing the TE to 140 ms. We also evaluated the averages of the scans and suggested that using an average of 256 is both reliable and efficient. Using these parameters, the MRS scans can be completed within 9 min. Although the selected parameters could ensure clinical efficacy and practical application, further optimization is warranted. Promising avenues include exploring novel sequences, such as hyperpolarized carbon-13 (HP 13C) MRI [35], a technique capable of real-time metabolic tracking, and the use of an inherently clear background, which may reveal more specific biomarkers for FH deficiency.

Our study had several limitations. First, this study was conducted at a single center; given the uncertain incidence of FH deficiency across regions and races, additional multicenter studies involving high-risk populations are necessary to validate the diagnostic performance of MRS. Such efforts should also include multivendor validation to ensure the robustness and interoperability of metabolic signatures across different MRI platforms. Second, the inclusion of only surgical participants may have introduced selection bias by overrepresenting advanced cases, necessitating future studies involving a broader high-risk population, including non-surgical participants, and exploring streamlined protocols that are feasible for implementation in community hospitals. Third, the small cohort size for sequence optimization may have affected the model robustness. Finally, no alternative sequences (e.g., semi-LASER) or voxel repositioning were attempted, and the TF rate may have underestimated the clinical reality. Moreover, the specific causes of TF in this cohort remain unclear. This discrepancy highlights the need for standardized protocols and the development of a practical quality control and/or reacquisition algorithm to facilitate future clinical implementation.

In conclusion, our study demonstrated that the positive fumarate peak in MRS shows promising diagnostic performance for identifying FH-dULs. This noninvasive technique may be useful for identifying high-risk FH deficiency. To validate these findings in clinical practice, multicenter validation is essential to ensure robustness across different scanners and protocols.
