Trained immunity links hematopoietic stem cell aging to aging-associated inflammation
Authors: Wei-Chieh Mu, Marine Barthez, Yufan Feng, Yibing Chen, Chih Ling Wang, Jing-Yi Huang, Jie Li, Paul Lanhart, K Claire Tsuei, Yang Joon Kim, Eric Verdin, Mihai G Netea, Danica Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractTrained immunity is a state of heightened immune response that is initiated in hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) and mediated mainly by their myeloid progeny. Aging-associated inflammation drives many aging-related diseases, yet its biological origin is largely unknown. Here we show that SIRT3, a mitochondrial deacetylase highly expressed in HSCs but reduced during aging, suppresses the HSC response to aging that drives maladaptive trained immunity, chronic inflammation and tissue functional decline in mice. Overexpression of SIRT3 in HSCs not only ameliorates aging-associated HSC decline, but also improves the function of distant tissues, including attenuation of age-related declines in cognition and motility, via myeloid cells with modulated inflammatory programs. These findings reveal that HSC aging is a driver of aging-associated inflammation through maladaptive trained immunity and broaden the possible clinical applications of targeting HSCs from hematological diseases to include countering aging-associated physiological decline and improving healthspan.Subject terms: Ageing, Inflammation

Main

Epidemiological studies have showed a marked increase in the circulating concentration of proinflammatory cytokines in individuals of older age1. This chronic systemic inflammation has been subsequently linked to aging-related conditions and diseases2. Immune cells and senescent cells contribute to aging-associated inflammation3–6; however, the biological origin of aging-associated chronic inflammation is largely unknown. Innate immune cells retain memory of previous encounters with microbial or inflammatory challenges and launch stronger response upon subsequent related or even unrelated stimuli, a phenomenon termed ‘trained immunity’7,8. Classic trained immunity has been described in the context of cytokine production responsiveness to heterologous stimuli. Innate immune adaptations are diverse and can be both beneficial and deleterious, depending on the stimulus and functional program induced. Beneficial long-term effects on innate immune responses are influenced by the magnitude and duration of stimulation and determine specific adaptations to either enhance immune responses and induce trained immunity to protect against infections, or prevent excessive immune responses through tolerance and limit tissue damage9. Trained immunity is initiated in hematopoietic stem cells (HSCs) in the bone marrow. HSCs sense and respond to infection or inflammation with myeloid-biased differentiation and epigenetic inflammatory training10–15. Besides the immune system, nonimmune cells in diverse tissues can also develop trained immunity programs7. When appropriately induced, trained immunity is protective against infection and tumors, and reinforces tissue repair upon acute damage14,16–21, but its maladaptation leads to chronic inflammatory pathologies and hinders tissue repair after repetitive damage11,19,22–29.

HSC aging is a progressive decline in HSC function with age, marked by reduced regenerative capacity, myeloid-biased differentiation, reduced lymphopoiesis and metabolic and epigenetic dysregulation30–33. These observations raise an intriguing possibility that insults in HSCs over the lifespan are a driver of maladaptive trained immunity, resulting in aging-associated inflammation and functional decline of distant tissues. Indeed, the defining features of trained immunity are reminiscent of aspects of HSC aging, such as myeloid-biased differentiation31,32 and epigenetic reprogramming34. We proposed that HSCs could sense aging-associated intrinsic or extrinsic cellular insults and initiate epigenetic inflammatory training, resulting in heightened inflammatory response to aging-associated damage-associated molecular patterns in distant tissues.

Sirtuins are genetic regulators of aging35,36. SIRT3, a member of the sirtuin family, is a mitochondrial NAD+-dependent deacetylase. SIRT3 regulates the global acetylation landscape of mitochondrial proteins, and SIRT3-initiated metabolic adaptations enhance mitochondrial stress management37–39. Sirt3 is highly expressed in HSCs and its expression is reduced in HSCs during aging in both mice and humans30,40. Sirt3 is required for HSC self-renewal under stress conditions by suppressing reactive oxygen species (ROS) and ameliorating loss of HSC quiescence and death30.

In this study, we show that SIRT3 suppresses HSC response to classic trained immunity, acute inflammation and tissue functional decline. Furthermore, SIRT3 also suppresses HSC response to aging that drives maladaptive trained immunity, aging-associated chronic inflammation and tissue functional decline. Overexpression of SIRT3 in HSCs not only ameliorates aging-associated functional decline of HSCs but also improves the function of distant tissues by giving rise to myeloid cells with a regulatory phenotype. Our findings support the concept that maladaptive trained immunity is an important mechanism in HSC aging, driving aging-associated inflammation and physiological decline. These observations have important implications in harnessing the power of HSCs to alleviate aging-associated conditions and improve healthspan.

Results

SIRT3 suppresses HSC response to classic trained immunity, acute inflammation and tissue functional decline

To determine whether SIRT3 regulates HSC response to classic trained immunity induction, we crossed Cre-inducible Sirt3-overexpressing mice with Mx1-Cre mice to generate mice that overexpress SIRT3 in the hematopoietic system (Mx1-T3) (Extended Data Fig. 1a). 3-month-old Mx1-T3 and Mx1-Cre control mice were treated with β-glucan, a well-established inducer of trained immunity. Hallmarks of trained immunity at the HSC level include increased HSC proliferation, expansion of myeloid-biased multipotent progenitor 3 (MPP3), and myeloid-biased differentiation14,15. Compared to control mice, β-glucan-treated Mx1-T3 mice exhibited reduced ROS levels in HSCs (Fig. 1a), a signaling molecule promoting HSC proliferation41, accompanied by decreased HSC proliferation (Fig. 1b), reduced MPP3 (Lin−c-Kit+Sca1+Flt3−CD150−CD48+) frequency (Fig. 1c), and attenuated myeloid-biased differentiation in peripheral blood (Fig. 1d). Following β-glucan training and lipopolysaccharide (LPS) stimulation, Mx1-T3 mice produced lower amounts of inflammatory cytokines compared to Mx1-Cre mice (Fig. 1e,f). As acute inflammation is associated with impaired tissue function42, we assessed muscle performance. β-glucan-trained, LPS-stimulated Mx1-T3 mice performed better in an inverted screen test compared to control mice (Fig. 1g), indicating preserved muscle function.

HSC activation is associated with metabolic reprogramming toward mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation41, whereas activation of differentiated myeloid cell is associated with increased glycolysis43. This distinction raised the possibility that SIRT3, a key regulator of mitochondrial metabolism, preferentially regulates trained immunity at the HSC level. To test this, lineage-negative cells isolated from Mx1-T3 and Mx1-Cre mice treated with or without β-glucan were differentiated into myeloid cells ex vivo, treated with or without β-glucan, and subsequently stimulated with LPS (Extended Data Fig. 1b). No differences were observed between genotypes following LPS stimulation alone or β-glucan treatment of myeloid cells (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). In contrast, cytokine production was significantly reduced in cells derived from Mx1-T3 mice when β-glucan treatment occurred at the HSC stage alone or in combination with myeloid cell exposure (Extended Data Fig. 1c,d). These findings indicate that SIRT3 preferentially suppresses trained immunity response through its effect on HSCs.

To further test this in vivo, we crossed Cre-inducible Sirt3-overexpressing mice with LysM-Cre mice to generate mice that overexpress SIRT3 in myeloid cells (LysM-T3) (Extended Data Fig. 1e). LysM-T3 mice mounted inflammatory cytokine responses to β-glucan training and LPS stimulation that were largely comparable to LysM-Cre controls, with no difference in IL-6 production and a modest trend toward reduced TNF (Extended Data Fig. 1f,g), in contrast to Mx1-T3 mice, which were protected from cytokine production (Fig. 1e,f). Together, these data demonstrate that SIRT3 suppresses HSC response to classic trained immunity, acute inflammation and tissue function decline.

SIRT3 suppresses HSC response to aging, aging-associated chronic inflammation and tissue functional decline

Endogenous damage-associated molecular patterns trigger maladaptive trained immunity and inflammatory diseases26,44. Because damage-associated molecular patterns are elevated during aging45, we investigated whether SIRT3 regulates HSC response to insults over the lifespan that drive maladaptive trained immunity. Competitive bone marrow transplantation assays were performed using HSCs (Lin−c-Kit+Sca1+CD150+CD48−) isolated from 2-year-old Mx1-T3 and Mx1-Cre mice. Donor HSCs (CD45.2) were mixed with CD45.1 B6.SJL competitor bone-marrow cells and transplanted into lethally irradiated CD45.1 B6.SJL recipient mice. HSCs from aged Mx1-T3 mice exhibited significantly enhanced reconstitution compared to controls (Extended Data Fig. 2a), an advantage that persisted following secondary transplantation (Extended Data Fig. 2b). HSCs from aged Mx1-T3 mice showed reduced ROS levels (Fig. 2a), decreased proliferation (Fig. 2b), and reduced cell death (Extended Data Fig. 2c) in transplantation settings, consistent with previous reports that SIRT3 preserves HSC maintenance by limiting oxidative stress and promoting quiescence and survival30,37. Correspondingly, Mx1-T3 mice exhibited improved survival following treatment with the myeloablative drug 5-fluorouracil (Extended Data Fig. 2d).

The frequency of MPP3 was reduced in the bone marrow of aged Mx1-T3 mice (Fig. 2c). Myeloid-biased differentiation in the peripheral blood emerged around 12 months old in control mice (27% myeloid cells in 11–12-month-old Mx1-Cre mice compared to 18% myeloid cells in 8–9-month-old Mx1-Cre mice) and became progressively more prominent over time (Fig. 2d). Aging-associated myeloid skewing was significantly attenuated in Mx1-T3 mice (Fig. 2d). Complete blood count analysis indicated that this effect reflected increased lymphocyte count (Extended Data Fig. 2e).

Consistent with reduced inflammation, aged Mx1-T3 mice displayed lower circulating inflammatory cytokine concentrations (Fig. 2e,f) and fewer CD68+ macrophages in distant tissues (Extended Data Fig. 2f,g). Given the link between chronic inflammation and functional decline during aging2, we assessed systemic physiological outcomes. Aged Mx1-T3 mice showed improved muscle function, including increased endurance in treadmill exhaustion tests (Extended Data Fig. 2h,i) and increased latency in the inverted screen test (Fig. 2g), despite comparable body weights (Extended Data Fig. 2j). Collectively, these data indicate that SIRT3 alleviates HSC response to aging, aging-associated inflammation and tissue functional decline.

SIRT3 overexpression in HSCs improves the function of distant tissues

Mx1-Cre can be active in several nonhematopoietic tissues46. To test whether SIRT3-mediated amelioration of HSC response to aging contributes to systemic benefits, bone marrow from Mx1-T3 or Mx1-Cre control mice was transplanted into lethally irradiated young wild-type (WT) recipient mice to allow HSCs overexpressing SIRT3 and control HSCs to repopulate the hematopoietic system of WT recipient mice. The recipient mice were aged for up to 2 years (Fig. 3a). Aged recipients of SIRT3-overexpressing HSCs (T3 Tx) exhibited reduced numbers of CD68+ macrophages, F4/80+ tissue-resident macrophages, and CCR2high inflammatory monocytes in the tissues, compared to mice transplanted with control HSCs (Cre Tx) (Extended Data Fig. 3a–g). Not only the number of CD68+ cells, but also the percentage of CD68+ cells expressing high levels of IL-6 was reduced (Extended Data Fig. 3c), indicating diminished inflammatory activation.

Aside from compromised cognition47, poor muscle function48 and insulin resistance49, aging is associated with reduced locomotor activity to novel environment47,50 and increased alveolar size in the lung51. We therefore assessed these well-established aging-associated physiological impairments. Aged recipients of SIRT3-overexpressing HSCs demonstrated improved muscle performance, as evidenced by increased running time and distance in the treadmill exhaustion test (Fig. 3b,c) and increased latency to fall in the inverted screen test (Fig. 3d). This was accompanied by muscle transcriptional programs enriched for extracellular matrix organization, wound healing, cell and organ growth, and negative regulation of cytokine production and immune response (Fig. 3e,f). These mice showed a trend of reduced latency to the target hole and a significant increase in hole search in the target quadrant in the Barnes maze test, indicative of improved cognitive function (Fig. 3g,h). They also exhibited elevated locomotor activity to novel environment as indicated by higher traveling speed and distance in the open field test (Extended Data Fig. 4a,b) and the elevated plus maze test (Extended Data Fig. 4c,d). Glucose metabolism was improved (Extended Data Fig. 4e) and the alveolar size was reduced (Fig. 3i,j). At the molecular level, these mice exhibited decreased expression of senescence markers, including p16 (Extended Data Fig. 4f–i) and senescence-associated β-galactosidase (SA-β-gal) (Extended Data Fig. 4j–m). Together, these data demonstrate that SIRT3 overexpression in HSCs alleviates aging-associated inflammation, cellular senescence and tissue functional decline.

SIRT3 suppresses aging-associated epigenetic inflammatory training in HSCs

Mitochondrial stress accumulates in HSCs with aging30–32,41,53–55. Because many mitochondrial metabolites are cofactors for epigenetic regulators, mitochondrial stress is thought to drive aging-associated epigenetic drift in stem cells41,56. Transcriptional profiling by RNA sequencing (RNA-seq) of HSCs isolated from 2-year-old Mx1-T3 and Mx1-Cre control mice showed that SIRT3 overexpression increased expression of mitochondrial genes, including Nd4, Cytb, Nd1, Cox7a2, Ndufa6, Tomm7, Nd2 and Timm8b (Extended Data Fig. 5a). Pathway analysis revealed enrichment of ribosome, oxidative phosphorylation, cell death, cell cycle, protein targeting to mitochondria and recombinational repair (Extended Data Fig. 5b,c). Gene Set Enrichment Analysis (GSEA) showed enrichment of oxidative phosphorylation, glutathione metabolism, longevity regulating pathways, the NF-κB signaling pathway and the TNF signaling pathway (Extended Data Fig. 5d). Transcription factor binding prediction analysis for differentially expressed genes suggested that SIRT3-induced transcriptional changes in aged HSCs was influenced by transcription factors that are involved in the establishment of chromatin accessibility at inflammation-trained loci, such as NF-κB, FOS, JUN, GATA1, ATF family members, CREB1, EGR1 and STAT family members (Extended Data Fig. 5e)7,11.

To test whether SIRT3 regulates epigenetic inflammatory training in HSCs, we performed epigenetic profiling of open chromatin by assay for transposase-accessible chromatin with high throughput sequencing (ATAC-seq) using HSCs isolated from 2-year-old Mx1-T3 and Mx1-Cre control mice. GSEA of differentially accessible chromatin regions revealed enrichment in pathways related to myelopoiesis, inflammatory response and metabolism (Fig. 8a), suggesting that SIRT3 regulates myeloid-biased differentiation and inflammatory training in HSCs at the epigenetic level.

To determine the effect of SIRT3 on epigenetic regulation beyond HSCs, we performed single-nuclei ATAC-sequencing of lineage-negative cells from 2-year-old Mx1-Cre and Mx1-T3 mice using the 10x Genomics Chromium platform. The analysis of single-cell chromatin accessibility data with Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP) revealed 11 distinct cell types (Fig. 8b). These clusters were identified as known cell types in the lineage-negative bone-marrow cells based on their marker genes: HSC, common lymphoid progenitor (CLP), T cell progenitor (T cell), B cell progenitor (B cell), immature myeloid progenitor (IMP), neutrophil progenitor (Neu), monocyte progenitor (mono), basophil progenitor (Ba), eosinophil progenitor (EO), erythroid progenitor (Ery) and megakaryocyte progenitor (Mk) (Fig. 8b)57. The proportion of myeloid progenitors (Ba, IMP, Mk, mono and Neu) based on UMAP clustering was reduced, while the proportion of CLP was increased in lineage-negative bone-marrow cells from old Mx1-T3 mice (Fig. 8c), consistent with amelioration of HSC aging-associated myeloid-biased differentiation by SIRT3 (Fig. 2d). Pathway analyses of differentially accessible chromatin regions revealed enrichment in pathways related to stress response, cell cycle, and inflammatory response in HSCs and their progeny (Fig. 8d–f).

To identify the epigenetic modifications induced by SIRT3, we performed Cleavage Under Targets and Tagmentation sequencing (Cut&Tag sequencing) of HSCs isolated from 2-year-old Mx1-T3 and Mx1-Cre control mice using antibodies against H3K4me3 or H3K18la epigenetic marks associated with innate immune memory14,58. Pathway analyses of differential genomic occupancy for both epigenetic marks showed enrichment in several inflammatory pathways, including TNF superfamily cytokine production, IL-6-mediated signaling, LPS-mediated signaling, Toll-like receptor signaling, cytokine production, type II interferon production and chemokine-mediated signaling (Extended Data Fig. 6a,b). These pathways were also enriched in differentially accessible chromatin regions in HSCs regulated by SIRT3 (Fig. 8a,d). Together, these results suggest that SIRT3 regulates epigenetic inflammatory training in HSCs.

Discussion

In this study, we identify a cellular origin of aging-associated inflammation. Our results suggest that HSC response to aging is a driver of aging-associated inflammation and tissue functional decline. HSC aging is a major cause of the pathophysiological conditions in the aging hematopoietic system55,59,60. Of note, overexpression of SIRT3 in HSCs ameliorates HSC response to aging, aging-associated inflammation and tissue functional decline (Figs. 2 and 3 and Extended Data Figs. 2–4), suggesting that the impact of HSC aging is broader than previously thought. HSC aging contributes to not only degeneration of the hematopoietic system, but also aging-associated inflammation and tissue functional decline, with organismal functional endpoints (treadmill endurance, inverted screen performance, spatial memory and glucose tolerance) as indicators of physiological relevance. HSC aging impacts distant tissues by giving rise to myeloid cells with an inflammatory phenotype that go into the circulation, with a deleterious long-term effect (Figs. 6 and 7). Given that HSCs are a powerful tool currently used in clinic to treat hematological diseases, our findings give hope for using HSCs to alleviate aging-associated conditions and improve healthspan.

The concept of trained immunity poses that long-term functional changes in innate immune cells can be induced by certain challenges, which are mediated by epigenetic reprogramming of innate immune cells and their stem/progenitor cells. In this study, we demonstrate all the tenets of trained immunity modulation as a result of SIRT3 overexpression: (1) there is long-term functional reprogramming of HSCs by SIRT3 expression, as demonstrated by decreased HSC response to trained immunity programs, reduced inflammation and improved function of distant tissues (Figs. 1–3 and Extended Data Figs. 1–4); (2) this effect is mediated by myeloid cells, prototype innate immune cells (Fig. 7); and (3) these changes are accompanied by epigenetic rewiring as demonstrated by differential chromatin accessibility (Fig. 8 and Extended Data Figs. 5 and 6), while the detailed biochemical mechanism linking SIRT3’s mitochondrial activity to chromatin-level epigenetic changes in HSCs remains to be elucidated. Inappropriately induced trained immunity in HSCs by endogenous stimuli has been implicated in inflammatory diseases26,44. These observations support the notion that HSC aging initiates maladaptive trained immunity to drive aging-associated inflammation. While HSC-initiated trained immunity is essential to form long-term memory to launch more robust immune response at young age, this protective mechanism comes at a cost during the aging process when HSCs are exposed to repeated cellular damage and insults.

Methods

Mice

B6.SJL-Ptprca Pepcb/BoyJ, Mx1-Cre (C57BL/6), and LysM-Cre mice (C57BL/6) were from the Jackson Laboratory. Cre-inducible Sirt3-overexpressing mice (C57BL/6) have been described previously61. C57BL/6 mice were obtained from the National Institute on Aging. Cre recombinase activity was induced by intraperitoneal injection of 250 μg of polyinosinic:polycytidylic acid (SIGMA, P1530) three times with 2-day intervals in Mx1-Cre mice at young age. Sex-matched mice were used in each experiment. A total of 150 mg kg−1 5-fluorouracil (Calbiochem, 343922) was administered intraperitoneally once per week. Mice were housed on a 12-h light–dark cycle at 25 °C with ad libitum access to water and standard laboratory chow diet (LabDiet, Rodent Diet 5053). Mouse health status was monitored by on-site veterinarians. All animal procedures were approved by the animal care and use committee at the University of California, Berkeley.

Transplantation assays

As described30–32, for noncompetitive transplantation, 1 million bone-marrow cells were retro-orbitally injected into lethally irradiated (950 cGy) B6.SJL recipient mice. For competitive transplantation, 250 freshly sorted HSCs (CD45.2) were mixed with 5 × 105 CD45.1 B6.SJL competitor bone-marrow cells and retro-orbitally injected into lethally irradiated B6.SJL recipient mice. Donor multilineage reconstitution was assessed by flow cytometry in peripheral blood of recipient mice monthly for 4 months using the antibody mixture consisting of CD45.2, CD45.1, Mac1, Gr1, B220 and CD3 (BioLegend, 109830, 110728, 101208, 108406, 103212 and 100214). For serial transplantation assay, 1 × 106 bone-marrow cells from the recipients were transplanted into each secondary recipient30.

Adoptive transfer

As described52, splenocytes were collected by gently pushing spleens through a 100-µm strainer, and red blood cells were lysed with ACK lysing buffer (Gibco, A10492-01). Then, 3 × 106 splenocytes were injected retro-orbitally into 3-month-old recipient mice. For adoptive transfer of T and myeloid cells, ACK-lysed splenocytes and bone-marrow cells were enriched for T and myeloid cells using CD3ε and CD11b microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-094-973, 130-049-601) respectively. Then, 2 × 106 enriched T or myeloid cells were injected retro-orbitally into recipient mice. For IL-1β antibody blockade, mice were treated with 4 mg kg−1 IL-1β antibody or IgG (Bio X Cell, BE0246, BE0091) before adoptive transfer and the treatment was repeated twice a week.

Induction of trained immunity

For HSC response, mice were treated with 1 mg β-glucan (Sigma, G5011) and analyzed 24 h later. For cytokine production and tissue function, mice were primed with 1 mg β-glucan, followed by treatment of 10 µg LPS (Sigma, L5668-1ML) 7 days later. Serum was collected at 1.5 h for cytokine measurement. The inverted screen test was performed 4 h after LPS treatment.

For induction of trained immunity ex vivo, mice were treated with 1 mg β-glucan or PBS. After 7 days, bone-marrow cells were lysed with ACK buffer to remove red blood cells. Lineage-negative cells were enriched using the lineage cell depletion kit (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-110-470), differentiated in the presence of 20 ng ml−1 M-CSF (PeproTech, 315-02) for 3 days, treated with 10 µg ml−1 β-glucan or PBS for 24 h, cultured in the presence of M-CSF for 3 days, induced with 10 ng ml−1 LPS for 24 h before the medium was collected for quantification of cytokines.

Flow cytometry and cell sorting

As described30–32, for lineage differentiation in the peripheral blood, blood was collected via submandibular vein bleeding into EDTA-treated tubes. Red blood cells were lysed with 500 µl of 1× BD FACS lysing solution (BD, 349202) for 5 min at room temperature, stopped with 3 ml of PBS, and stained with the antibody mixture consisting of Mac1, Gr1, B220, and CD3 for 15 min at 4 °C, followed by flow cytometry analysis (BD, Fortessa).

For HSC analysis, bone-marrow cells were obtained by crushing the long bones with staining media (calcium- and magnesium-free PBS with 2% FBS) using mortars and pestles. Bone-marrow cells were stained with the antibody mixture (lineage cocktail (Mac1 (CD11b), Gr1 (Ly-6G/C), Ter119 (Ly-76), CD3, CD4, CD8a (Ly-2), and B220 (CD45R)), c-Kit, Sca1, CD48 and CD150 antibodies) (BioLegend, 101226, 108424, 116223, 100222, 100414, 100714, 103224, 105812, 108120, 103404 and 115904) for 20 min at 4 °C, washed and analyzed by flow cytometry. For ROS analysis, cells were stained with 10 µM H2DCFDA (Invitrogen, D399) for 30 min at 37 °C. To assess cell death and proliferation, 7AAD or Ki-67 (BioLegend, 420404 and 652409) staining was performed respectively. For MPP3 analysis, bone-marrow cells were stained with the antibody mixture (lineage cocktail, c-Kit, Flt3 (BioLegend, 103236, 100218, 100434, 100734, 108428, 101228, 116228, 105826 and 135310), Sca1, CD48 and CD150 antibodies) for 20 min at 4 °C, washed and analyzed by flow cytometry.

For HSC sorting, bone-marrow cells were lysed with ACK buffer to remove red blood cells and enriched for c-Kit+ cells with c-Kit microbeads (Miltenyi Biotec, 130-091-224). c-Kit-enriched cells were stained with the antibody mixture for HSCs for 20 min at 4 °C, washed and stained with 0.5 µg ml−1 propidium iodide (BioLegend, 421301). Live HSCs were sorted using FACSAria (BD).

Complete blood count

Peripheral blood was collected via submandibular bleeding into EDTA-treated blood collection tubes. Complete blood count analysis was conducted at the Comparative Pathology Laboratory at the University of California, Davis.

Hematoxylin and eosin

The lungs were fixed with 4% formaldehyde at 4 °C overnight, transferred to 30% sucrose in PBS, and incubated at 4 °C for 2 days for cryoprotection. The lungs were frozen in Tissue-Plus O.C.T. Compound (Fisher, 23-730-571) and 10-μm sections were cut using a cryostat. Sections were stained with hematoxylin and eosin (H&E; Abcam, ab245880), imaged with a Zeiss AxioImager M2 microscope, and quantified with Fiji ImageJ.

Immunohistochemistry

As described62, tissue sections (10 µm) were mounted on glass slides. Slides were fixed with 10% formalin. Tissue sections were incubated with primary mouse antibodies FITC anti-CD192 (1:100 dilution) (CCR2) (BioLegend, 150607), PE anti-CD11b (1:200 dilution) (BioLegend, 101208), anti-CD68 (1:200 dilution) (BioLegend, 137001), anti-F4/80 (1:200 dilution) (BioLegend, 123101), and anti-IL-6 (D5W4V) (1:200 dilution) (Cell Signaling 12912S) overnight, stained with secondary antibody (for CD68, F4/80, IL-6) (Invitrogen SA5-10018, A21434 and A11036). Nuclei were counterstained using 4,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) (Thermo Fisher, 62247). Images were taken with a Zeiss LSM710 confocal microscope and quantified using Fiji ImageJ. For IL-6high and CCR2high, a threshold was applied to quantify high-intensity signals.

Mouse serum cytokine measurements

Cytokines in mouse sera were measured with the Luminex mouse cytokine immunoassay for IL-6 and TNF (Millipore, MCYTOMAG-70K) on a Bio-Plex MAGPIX Multiplex Reader. Cytokine production after LPS induction was measured with ELISA kits for IL-6 (R&D, M6000B-1) and TNF (BioLegend, 430907).

SA-β-gal staining

SA-β-gal staining was conducted using a β-galactosidase staining detection kit (CST, 9860). Cryosections were fixed with the supplied fixative solution for 15 min at room temperature. After fixation and washing, sections were immersed in fresh β-gal staining solution and incubated at 37 °C overnight. Images were taken with a Zeiss AxioImager M2 microscope. Five fields per mouse were randomly selected for analysis, and Fiji ImageJ was used for image quantification.

Quantitative real-time PCR

As described63, total RNA was isolated with TRIzol (Invitrogen, 15596018). Complementary DNA was generated using the qScript cDNA SuperMix (Quanta, 95048). Gene expression was determined by quantitative real-time PCR using EvaGreen qPCR SuperMix kit (BioChain, K5052400) on an ABI StepOnePlus system. Primer sequences were:

Sirt3 forward: ACAGCTACATGCACGGTCTG, reverse: ACACAATGTCGGGTTTCACA

p16 forward: CGCAGGTTCTTGGTCACTGT, reverse: TGTTCACGAAAGCCAGAGCG

Actb forward: GCCTTCCTTCTTGGGTATGG, reverse: AGCACTGTGTTGGCATAGAG.

Glucose tolerance test

As described4, mice were fasted for 14–16 h. Fasting blood glucose levels were measured using CONTOUR NEXT glucometer. Then, 1.5 or 2 g D-glucose per kg body weight was injected intraperitoneally into mice. Blood glucose levels after glucose administration were recorded.

Inverted screen test

As described64, mice were habituated in the procedural room for 5–20 min. Mice were put in the center of the wire cage top, inverted and suspended 40–50 cm above a padded surface. The latency until mice fell was recorded. The test was repeated three times for each mouse to calculate average latency.

Treadmill exhaustion test

As described64, mice were acclimated to the treadmill (Columbus Instruments, Exer-6M Open Treadmill) 2–3 days before the test. For each session, food was removed 2 h before exercise. Mice were familiarized with the sound and experience of moving treadmill for two training sessions (one session per day). During the training session, mice were put on the stationary treadmill for 30 s to explore the environment. Acclimation began at a low speed of 5–8 m min−1 for 10 min on day 1 and was increased to 5–10 m min−1 for 10 min on day 2. Following training, mice were allowed to rest for 1 day before the exhaustion test.

The treadmill exhaustion test began at a rate of 12 m min−1 for the first 40 min. Then, treadmill speed was increased at a rate of 1 m min−1 every 10 min for 30 min and increased at a rate of 1 m min−1 every 5 min until mice were exhausted. Exhaustion was determined by failure to remain on the treadmill for at least 20 s. Time and distance to exhaustion were recorded.

Barnes maze test

As described64, the maze was a circular platform made of acrylic plastic (92 cm in diameter) with 20 identical holes (5 cm in diameter), one of which was the target hole with an escape box placed underneath it. The test included three phases: habituation (day 1), training trial (day 2–3) and probe trial (day 5). Mice were acclimated to the procedural room for 1 h every day before the session.

For habituation, a mouse was placed at the center of the maze under a clear beaker for 30 s while noise was played via a metronome (65 decibels, one beat per second). The mouse was slowly guided straight to the target hole using the clear beaker and was given 3 min to enter the escape box through the target hole. If the mouse failed to escape within 3 min it was placed inside the escape box. The mouse was allowed to stay in the escape box for 1 min with the noise turned off.

During the training phase, three training trials were performed on day 1, and two training trials were performed on day 2. A mouse was placed at the center of the maze under an opaque beaker for 10 s. At the end of the holding period, noise was turned on and the mouse was released from the opaque beaker to explore for 2 min. If the mouse failed to escape at the end of exploration time, it was guided to the target hole with a clear beaker and was given 3 min to escape. If the mouse failed to escape within 3 min, it was placed inside the escape box. Once the mouse escaped, the noise was turned off and the mouse was allowed to stay in the escape box for 1 min.

Probe trials were carried out on day 5. The setup was the same as the training trial, except that the escape box was removed from the maze. Mice were allowed to explore the maze for 2 min with the noise turned on. Mouse performance was recorded with a digital camera recorder and videos were analyzed with Noldus EthoVision (Noldus Information Technology).

Open field

As described65, mice were habituated in the testing room under normal light for 1 h before testing. Mice were placed in the center of a plastic chamber (50 × 50 cm) and allowed free movement for 15 min. Mouse movement was recorded with a digital camera recorder and videos were analyzed with Ethovision.

Elevated plus maze

As described65, mice were habituated in the testing room under red light for 1 h before testing. Mice were placed in the center of the maze with the head facing one of the closed arms and allowed to explore the maze consisted of open and closed arms for 10 min. Entries into closed and open arms were recorded with a digital camera recorder and the videos were analyzed with Ethovision.

Single-nuclei ATAC-sequencing

Bone-marrow cells were lysed with ACK buffer to remove red blood cells. Lineage-negative cells were enriched using the lineage cell depletion kit (Miltenyi Biotec). Lineage-negative cells were stained with the lineage antibody cocktail and propidium iodide. Live lineage-negative cells were sorted with an Aria sorter (BD). Nuclei isolation from sorted cells was performed for Single-cell Multiome (10x Genomics). Single-nucleus droplets and sequencing libraries were prepared (10x Genomics). Single-nuclei ATAC libraries were sequenced using NovaSeq SP 100PE.

Sequencing data were pre-processed using Cell Ranger ARC pipeline (v.2.0.2) provided by 10x Genomics. This step involves the demultiplexing of cells using cell barcodes, the alignment of reads to GRCm39 reference genome, removal of empty droplets, cell debris and low-quality cells. The filtered single-nuclei ATAC-sequencing peak data was imported to Seurat66 (v.4.1.0) and Signac67 (v.1.11.0) for further quality control. Cells were required to have the number of peaks detected between 2,300 and 40,000, the percentage of fragments in peaks greater than 25%, the ratio of the reads that map to the blacklist regions in genome less than 5%, the nucleosome signal <4 and transcription start site enrichment score >3. The numbers of cells retained by Cell Ranger was 7,196 and 5,538 for Mx1-Cre and Mx1-T3 respectively.

We reduced the dimensionality of the chromatin accessibility data by latent semantic indexing, by first normalizing using the term frequency-inverse document frequency (TF-IDF) normalization method, followed by a singular value decomposition using all peak features implemented in Signac. A low-dimensional visualization was conducted using the reduced dimensions of LSI components (2–50) using RunUMAP function, followed by graph-based clustering using FindNeighbors and FindClusters functions from Seurat. To convert the ‘peak’ features into ‘genes’ for interpretation of the signal, gene activities were inferred from the chromatin accessibility data using the function GeneActivity from Signac. Cell-type identification was carried out manually on clusters generated at Leiden resolution 1, using gene activity data and curated gene markers from literature57. Differential peak occupancy analysis was carried out using FindMarkers function from Seurat for the comparisons between conditions for each cell type and for the comparisons between a cell type to the others.

ATAC-sequencing

A total of 50,000 HSCs were resuspended in ice-cold cryopreservation solution (50% FBS, 40% growth medium and 10% dimethylsulfoxide (DMSO)). Growth medium contained StemSpan SFEM (Stem Cell Technologies, 09600) supplemented with 10% ES-Cult FBS (Stem Cell Technologies 06952), 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Invitrogen, 15140122), IL-3 (20 ng ml−1), IL-6 (20 ng ml−1), TPO (50 ng ml−1), Flt3L (50 ng ml−1) and SCF (100 ng ml−1) (PeproTech, 213-13, 216-16, 250-31L 315-14 and 250-03). HSCs were frozen by transferring the tubes to a prechilled ‘Mr. Frosty’ container, placed at −80 °C overnight to complete cryopreservation, and submitted to Active Motif for ATAC-sequencing. Samples were processed and put through paired-end 42-bp sequencing reads format using Illumina NovaSeq 6000. Reads were aligned to the mm10 genome using BWA (v.0.7.12)68. Peaks were called using the MACS (v3.0.0)69 algorithm at a cutoff P value of 1 × 10−7 without control file and with the –nomodel option. Peak filtering was performed by removing false peaks as defined within the ENCODE blacklist70. Differential analysis was performed with DESeq2 (ref. 71). GSEA was performed with the GSEAPY Python package (v.0.10.3).

RNA-sequencing

Total RNA from 40,000 sorted HSCs was isolated with RNeasy Plus Micro kit (QIAGEN, 74034). RNA-sequencing libraries were prepared using the QuantSeq 3’ mRNA-Seq Library Prep kit FWD (Lexogen), and sequencing was performed on a HiSeq 4000 at the UC Davis Genome Center. Unique molecular identifiers were extracted and transferred to read headers with UMI-tools (v1.1.2)72. The 16-bp sequence containing a constant 4-bp spacer followed by a 12-bp random priming sequence was removed by HEADCROP with Trimmomatic (v.0.38)73. TruSeq adaptor and low-quality tails were removed by BBMap (v.38.96)74. Trimmed reads were aligned to the UCSC mm10 genome using HISAT2 (v.2.2.1)75 and counted with featureCounts (v.2.0.1)76. Differential gene expression analysis was performed using DESeq2 (ref. 71). GSEA was performed with the GSEAPY Python package (v.0.10.3). Transcription factor binding prediction analysis of differentially expressed genes with P < 0.05 was performed using TRRUST77.

Total RNA from gastrocnemius muscle was isolated with RNeasy Mini kit (QIAGEN, 74104). The poly(A)-enriched libraries were prepared by Novogene and sequencing was performed on NovaSeq PE150. Data analysis was performed within the Galaxy public server (usegalaxy.org)78. After removing low-quality reads and adaptor sequences, data were mapped against the mouse mm10 reference genome using ‘HISAT2’ (Galaxy v.2.2.1)75. Gene-level read counts were generated using ‘HTSeq’ (Galaxy v.0.9.1)79. Differential gene expression was analyzed using ‘DESeq2’ (Galaxy v.1.34.0)71. GSEA of Gene Ontology (gseGO) was performed with ClusterProfiler (v.4.6.2).

Cut&Tag sequencing

A total of 100,000 sorted HSCs were used for CUT&Tag sequencing. Sequencing libraries were prepared using the CUT&Tag-IT assay kit (Active Motif, 53160). Sorted HSCs were immobilized with Concanavalin A beads and permeabilized with digitonin-containing antibody buffer. HSCs underwent overnight incubation with anti-H3K18la rabbit mAb (PTMBIO, PTM-1406RM, 1:50 dilution) or anti-H3K4me3 (Active Motif, 39159, 1:50 dilution) at 4 °C, and then incubation with secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. Samples were incubated with pA-Tn5 transposomes at room temperature for 1 h, followed by tagmentation at 37 °C for 1 h. The tagmented DNA was then purified and subjected to PCR amplification and SPRI bead cleanup for sequencing. Sequencing libraries were quantified by KAPA library quantification kit (Roche), and sequencing was performed on NextSeq 150 MID.

To process CUT&Tag sequencing data, the paired-end DNA-seq reads were trimmed using Trimmomatic (v.0.38)73. Trimmed reads were aligned to the mouse reference genome using bowtie2 (ref. 80). Duplicates were removed by Picard (‘Picard Toolkit’ 2019. Broad Institute, GitHub Repository. https://broadinstitute.github.io/picard/; Broad Institute). Peaks were called using MACS2 (v.2.2.7.1) with a cutoff of q value 0.01 (ref. 69). Differential peak analysis was performed using DESeq2 (ref. 71). Pathway enrichment analysis was performed using GSEApy (v.1.0.6) in Python.

Statistics and reproducibility

No statistical methods were used to predetermine sample size. The number of mice is comparable to published literature for the same assays performed. Mice were randomized to groups (simple randomization) and analysis of mice and tissue samples were performed by investigators blinded to the treatment or genetic background. Measurements were taken from distinct samples. The Grubbs’ test was used to determine outliers. Statistical analysis was performed with Student’s t-test (Excel) or two-way analysis of variance (GraphPad Prism), unless specified otherwise. Data distribution was not tested. Data are presented as means and error bars represent standard errors. *P < 0.05. **P < 0.01. ***P < 0.001. NS, not significant P > 0.05. P values are provided in the Source Data. Replication information is indicated in the figures and figure legends.
