Tuberculous Pleurisy Highlighting the Need to Consider Primary Tuberculosis in Interferon-gamma Release Assay-negative Patients
Authors: Saki Takeda, Tatsuya Nagai, Taiki Kawai, Masaki Ikeda, Koshiro Sakamoto, Nami Kuji, Sae Kawafune, Hiroki Kawakami, Soichiro Yamaji, Yuki Sato, Nao Sato, Jun Hayashi, Hikaru Ito, Hiroyuki Ito, Kei Nakashima
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
We report a case of tuberculous pleurisy in a 54-year-old man who initially presented with negative interferon-gamma release assay (IGRA) results and elevated pleural fluid adenosine deaminase (ADA) levels. Bacteriological confirmation was obtained only when the pleural fluid culture grew Mycobacterium tuberculosis one month later. The IGRA yielded positive results approximately two months after symptom onset. This case highlights the limitations of IGRAs and ADA as definitive diagnostic tools and underscores the need to consider primary tuberculosis in early IGRA negative cases. Clinicians should be aware of the time-dependent nature of immune sensitization when evaluating patients with suspected tuberculous pleurisy.

Introduction

Despite various public health measures, tuberculosis (TB) remains a leading global health challenge with persistently high morbidity and mortality rates worldwide (1). Approximately 16% of all TB cases are extrapulmonary, with tuberculous pleurisy being one of the most common manifestations (2). In patients with primary tuberculous pleurisy, symptoms such as fever and pleuritic chest pain usually last less than 30 days at the time of initial presentation, indicating an acute rather than chronic clinical course in most cases (3). Nevertheless, up to 65% of such cases progress to active pulmonary TB within two years (4). Therefore, an accurate diagnosis and prompt treatment are essential.

However, the diagnosis of tuberculous pleurisy is often challenging. Bacteriological confirmation is difficult, with pleural fluid smear positivity rates of ＜10% and culture positivity rates of approximately 45% (5). Therefore, auxiliary diagnostic markers, such as adenosine deaminase (ADA) and interferon-gamma release assays (IGRAs), are frequently used. However, these markers are supportive rather than definitive. Elevated ADA levels may also be observed in pleural effusions associated with primary effusion lymphoma, rheumatic diseases, and malignant pleural mesothelioma, making it a nonspecific finding in TB (6). IGRAs detect Mycobacterium tuberculosis infection by quantifying interferon-gamma (IFN-γ) produced by T lymphocytes sensitized to TB-specific antigens (7), and are often used as a supportive diagnostic tool. Nevertheless, it may take approximately 8 weeks after the initial TB infection (8). Consequently, false-negative results may occur in cases of early primary infection or in immunocompromised individuals.

We herein report a rare case of primary tuberculous pleurisy in which the IGRA initially showed negative results but yielded positive results approximately two months after symptom onset.

Case Report

A 54-year-old man presented to a hospital with fever (body temperature, 38.7°C) and right-sided pleuritic chest pain that worsened with inspiration, beginning 21 days before his visit to our hospital. The patient was orally started on levofloxacin. However, after 6 days of treatment, his fever persisted, and the chest pain worsened, prompting a referral to our hospital. He was diagnosed with right-sided pleuritis by the Department of General Internal Medicine and referred to our department for further evaluation.

His medical history included a prior appendectomy and an abdominal aortic aneurysm, but he had no active medical conditions and did not take any regular medications. He had allergies to oysters and bee stings but no known drug allergies. The patient's family history was unremarkable. He had never smoked but consumed alcohol occasionally. The patient worked as the hospital caregiver. A public health investigation conducted after his diagnosis did not identify a specific source of infection in occupational or private settings. Therefore, we conclude that the primary infection was likely due to incidental exposure in the community. His vital signs were stable and physical examination revealed no abnormalities, including an audible pleural friction rub. Blood tests revealed elevated C-reactive protein levels (Table). The IGRA performed at our hospital, QuantiFERON-TB Gold, showed negative (＜0.35 IU/mL) results. Immunological testing revealed no significant abnormalities. Sputum cultures showed no significant bacterial growth, and acid-fast bacilli (AFB) cultures and polymerase chain reaction (PCR) testing yielded negative results. Three consecutive sputum samples tested smear-negative for AFB. Chest radiography revealed a right-sided pleural effusion (Fig. 1). Chest computed tomography (CT) confirmed right-sided pleural effusion and revealed centrilobular nodules with a tree-in-bud pattern in the right upper lobe and pleural thickening (Fig. 2). An analysis of the pleural fluid revealed lymphocyte-predominant exudative effusion with elevated ADA levels (Table). An AFB smear and PCR of the pleural fluid yielded negative results. A cytological and cell block analysis of the pleural fluid revealed no evidence of malignancy. The markedly elevated pleural fluid ADA level prompted the consideration of differential diagnoses such as lymphoma and rheumatic diseases in addition to TB. Cytology and cell block analysis showed negative results for malignancy; chest CT revealed no lymphadenopathy; rheumatoid factor, antinuclear antibody, and antineutrophil cytoplasmic antibody were negative; and complement levels were within normal ranges (Table). Owing to the suspicion of tuberculous pleurisy with pulmonary involvement, bronchial lavage from the right B3 segment and a second set of three sputum samples were obtained; nevertheless, all were smear-negative. Given his occupation as a healthcare worker, the chest CT findings of small nodular opacities suggestive of a tree-in-bud appearance, and the lymphocyte-predominant pleural exudate with markedly elevated ADA, early initiation of anti-tuberculosis therapy was considered. However, in view of the negative interferon-γ release assay, the inability to completely exclude alternative diagnoses, and the fact that all bacteriological examinations up to that point were smear-negative, we decided to defer treatment initiation. Thoracoscopy under local anesthesia was considered for further diagnostic evaluation; however, the procedure was deferred because the scant pleural effusion precluded the establishment of an adequate intrapleural working space.

Thirty-one days after his first visit to our department, the pleural fluid culture showed positive results, and PCR confirmed the presence of M. tuberculosis complex. Based on these findings, tuberculous pleurisy was diagnosed. A standard four-drug regimen consisting of isoniazid, rifampin, pyrazinamide, and ethambutol was initiated. The repeat QuantiFERON-TB Gold test on day 36 remained negative, and the test yielded positive results on day 48. The patient completed a 180-day course of antituberculosis therapy, after which follow-up chest CT confirmed resolution of the right-sided pleural effusion and diminution of the small nodules in the right upper lobe (Fig. 3).

Discussion

This case involved a patient with right-sided pleuritis characterized by IGRA-negative negativity and elevated pleural ADA levels. Tuberculous pleurisy was diagnosed 1 month after the initial visit via a positive pleural fluid culture, and the IGRA result was subsequently converted to positive 2 months after symptom onset. This clinical course provides two key insights.

First, clinicians should consider a primary TB infection when suspecting TB in patients with negative IGRA results. The principle of IGRAs lies in quantifying IFN-γ released by T lymphocytes sensitized to M. tuberculosis-specific antigens (9). Following primary infection, systemic dissemination of sensitized T cells and sufficient IFN-γ production may take up to eight weeks (8). Early IGRA negativity with subsequent seroconversion in active TB was documented in a previous case report (10). Therefore, primary tuberculous pleurisy should be considered in suspected cases of negative IGRA results. In this case, all immunological tests showed negative results; however, the patient had an elevated pleural fluid ADA level, which raised suspicion of tuberculous pleurisy despite a negative IGRA result. The IGRA yielded positive results approximately 2 months after onset, suggesting that the disease represented an initial TB infection, and that the negative IGRA result at first presentation was due to insufficient immune sensitization.

Second, IGRAs and pleural ADA levels cannot establish a definitive diagnosis of tuberculous pleurisy and should only be used as supplementary tools. The sensitivity of pleural smear staining is ＜10% and culture positivity is approximately 45%, often necessitating thoracoscopic biopsy and histopathological examination. However, thoracoscopy is invasive and not widely available. Consequently, surrogate markers such as pleural ADA concentrations and IGRA are often used (5). However, IGRA negative tuberculous pleurisy is rare. Kim et al. reported a false-negative rate of 18.1% in confirmed cases (11), and the reported sensitivity and specificity of IGRAs for tuberculous pleurisy were 77% and 71%, respectively, indicating the possibility of false-negative results (9). Furthermore, owing to the time required for IGRA conversion, it may be negative during the early clinical course of the disease. Regarding the timing of conversion, studies on pulmonary tuberculosis and recent contacts have reported that IGRA conversion typically occurs 4-7 weeks after exposure (12). Conversely, reports specifically documenting IGRA conversion in tuberculous pleurisy are rare and are mostly limited to case reports (10). Even if the IGRA result is positive, it can be independently confirmed (13). In situations of diagnostic uncertainty, a high pleural ADA level is a significant indicator. However, high ADA levels can also be observed in conditions such as primary effusion lymphoma, leukemia, empyema, rheumatic diseases, and malignant pleural mesothelioma, making them nonspecific for diagnosing TB (6). Although multiple meta-analyses and systematic reviews have evaluated the sensitivity and specificity of ADA for tuberculous pleurisy, a definitive diagnostic cutoff has not been established (14,15). Additionally, several supplementary markers have been proposed to improve the diagnostic accuracy of tuberculous pleurisy, including the lactate dehydrogenase (LDH)/ADA ratio (16-18) and the combination of ADA ＞50 U/L with a high lymphocyte-to-neutrophil ratio (19). In the present case, these criteria were compatible with those of pleural TB. Furthermore, when our patient's findings were applied to the diagnostic flowchart proposed by Shimoda et al. (20), which incorporates pleural fluid LDH, ADA/total protein (ADA/TP) ratio, and differential cell counts, the case was classified into the tuberculous pleurisy category (based on LDH ＜825 U/L and an ADA/TP ratio ≥14). However, a striking discrepancy arose between these supportive surrogate markers and negative IGRA results. While these algorithms collectively pointed toward tuberculosis, the negative result of the blood IGRA introduced substantial diagnostic uncertainty. This conflict highlights the challenge of initiating empirical therapy solely based on surrogate markers and algorithms when key immunological tests remain non-reactive. Nonetheless, in our patient, the pleural ADA level was well above the commonly used threshold of 40 IU/L (5), providing a strong rationale for maintaining a high index of suspicion for tuberculosis until alternative diagnoses were carefully excluded. Therefore, IGRA and ADA results must be interpreted within the entire clinical context. A high ADA level in a lymphocyte-predominant exudate should be suspected and it thus warrants further investigation, such as awaiting culture results, even in the presence of an initial negative IGRA result. In this case, pleural fluid culture was positive 31 days after the first visit.

Regarding the source of the infection, nosocomial infection was considered because the patient was a hospital caregiver. However, a public health investigation conducted after the diagnosis did not identify a specific source of infection, either at the workplace or in his personal life. Therefore, primary infection likely resulted from incidental community-acquired exposure. Retrospectively, the earlier initiation of antituberculosis therapy might also have been justifiable, particularly given his occupation as a hospital caregiver and the presence of small nodular opacities on chest CT suggestive of a tree-in-bud appearance. The reduction of these nodules after treatment retrospectively supports the possibility of limited, culture-negative pulmonary involvement. However, at the time of presentation, we considered the pleural effusion findings nonspecific in the context of a negative interferon-γ release assay, spontaneous clinical improvement, and repeated negative bacteriological tests, and therefore prioritized establishing a definitive diagnosis while maintaining a close follow-up.

This study is associated with some limitations. First, the initial negative IGRA result may represent a false-negative result. However, factors associated with IGRA false negativity include end-stage renal disease, hematologic malignancies, pneumoconiosis, serum protein ＜6.0 g/dL, pleural pH ＞7.6, and ADA concentrations ＜70 IU/L (11), none of which were present in this patient. Second, although culture positivity made it possible to make a diagnosis in this case, the generally low culture yield in tuberculous pleurisy indicates that many cases remain undiagnosed unless additional methods are used. Although imperfect, IGRA and ADA results can serve as helpful adjuncts in such situations. Third, prior exposure to fluoroquinolones such as levofloxacin has been associated with delayed TB diagnosis (21) and could potentially decrease early smear positivity or delay culture conversion. In this case, the patient's fever did not resolve, chest pain worsened during levofloxacin therapy, and pleural fluid culture showed M. tuberculosis on day 31; therefore, any impact of levofloxacin on microbiologic yield was likely limited. Finally, as this was a single case, the clinical course, including IGRA seroconversion timing, may not be generalizable and it could vary depending on individual immune responses.

Conclusion

This case involved tuberculous pleurisy with a negative IGRA result and elevated ADA levels, in which the IGRA yielded positive results approximately two months after disease onset. The IGRA result may be negative in the early phase of primary TB infection, before full immune sensitization occurs. It is important to recognize that IGRAs and pleural ADA are only supportive diagnostic tools and should not be relied upon for a definitive diagnosis.
