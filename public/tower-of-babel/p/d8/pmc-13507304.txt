Significant variability in virulence factors and antibiotic resistance of uropathogenic Escherichia coli strains may be counteracted by their susceptibility to lytic bacteriophages
Authors: Wojciech Wesołowski, Grzegorz Czerwonka, Katarzyna Zegadło, Ernest Jagieła, Aleksandra Łukasiak, Łukasz Grabowski, Jagoda Mantej, Anna Dziuba, Sylwia Bloch, Gracja Topka-Bielecka, Agnieszka Necel, Daniel Kostovski, Emilia Węglińska, Magdalena Narajczyk, Grzegorz Węgrzyn, Bożena Nejman-Faleńczyk, Wioletta Adamus-Białek
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC) strains are the primary cause of urinary tract infections and pose a serious clinical problem due to their remarkable genetic variability, diverse virulence traits, and rapidly increasing antibiotic resistance. In this study, five clinical UPEC isolates (EC3, EC149, EC164, EC179, and EC256) were deeply investigated to better understand their pathogenic potential. The strains differed substantially in genome architecture, mobile genetic elements, plasmid composition, virulence factors composition, and antibiotic resistance profiles, illustrating the highly dynamic nature of UPEC populations. Of particular concern was the detection of plasmids linked to resistance against “last-resort” antibiotics, emphasizing the growing epidemiological risk associated with these pathogens. To address the limitations of conventional therapy, five lytic bacteriophages active against the analyzed UPEC strains were isolated and thoroughly characterized. Comparative genomic and phylogenetic analyses revealed that all phages represent previously undescribed species belonging to the genera Hanrivervirus, Warwickvirus, Nonavirus, Kagunavirus, and Vectrevirus. Functional assays demonstrated pronounced differences in host range and infectivity. Among them, phage vB_EcoS-149_4M emerged as the most promising candidate, combining broad antibacterial activity with high stability and a favourable safety profile, including the absence of toxin or antibiotic resistance genes and no detectable cytotoxicity toward human cell lines. Taken together, these findings highlight both the complexity and clinical threat posed by UPEC strains and the substantial potential of carefully selected bacteriophages as effective and precise antimicrobial tools. In effect, this multi-faceted approach provides a strong foundation for the development of precision phage therapy against multidrug-resistant UPEC infections.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-62793-9.

Introduction

Urinary tract infections (UTIs) are among the most common bacterial infections affecting millions of individuals worldwide each year. They constitute about 30% of all hospital-acquired infections, and more than 50% of women worldwide will experience a UTI at least once in their lives1,2. The national economic burden of uncomplicated UTIs in the US costs approximately $2 billion annually, and several billions more in complicated, recurrent, or hospital-acquired infection3.The primary causative agent of these infections is uropathogenic Escherichia coli (UPEC), a subset of strains that have evolved to thrive in the urinary tract4,5. The predominance of E. coli among other microorganisms causing UTIs stems primarily from its ability to evade the host’s immune response and invade the extremely unfavorable urinary tract environment. Their remarkable adaptability is provided by horizontal gene transfer, in which pathogenic islands play a key role. These islands carry genes involved in adhesion, enabling attachment to epithelial cells and colonization, including genes encoding type 1 (fim1), P (pap), and S (sfa) fimbriae, Dra/Afa (dra/afa) and Iha (iha) adhesins, and Antigen 43 (flu). They also harbor genes encoding secreted toxins that modulate immune responses and damage host cells, such as hemolysin A (hlyA), cytotoxic necrotizing factor 1 (cnf1), and uropathogenic-specific protein (usp), as well as genes involved in siderophore biosynthesis and transport, which facilitate efficient iron acquisition (iuc, aer, irp). A feature that particularly favors the maintenance of urinary tract infection is the extraordinary ability of UPEC to cause intracellular bacterial communities (IBCs), which determines the escape from the components of the immune response and antibiotics, while at the same time giving time to activate subsequent mechanisms related to pathogenesis and antibiotic resistance6,7. These bacteria have been shown to lose certain virulence-associated genes (VAGs) when acquiring resistance, particularly to fluoroquinolones8. It should be noted that despite the highest resistance to penicillins and fluoroquinolones among all antibiotic classes, they are still used in empirical treatment, even despite evidence of cross-resistance6,8.

All these features make UPEC seem to be the most specific in terms of its ability to cause urinary tract infection. This creates a huge inconvenience and even impairment in social and professional life and permanent loss of health, including increased risk of bladder cancer9. First-line drugs for UTI have still remained nitrofurantoin, trimethoprim-sulfamethoxazole and fosfomycin, however it depends on local resistance patterns, medical history, and type of infection. Unfortunately, in the case of chronic UTIs, no antibiotic has been proven to permanently eliminate the infection. Therefore, the search for new therapeutic solutions is extremely necessary5,6.

Among these, bacteriophage therapy has garnered significant attention as a potential solution to combat UPEC infections10–12. Bacteriophages, or shortly phages, are viruses that specifically infect and lyse bacterial cells, making them a promising tool in the fight against antibiotic-resistant bacterial pathogens. UPEC is not a monolithic group but rather consists of various strains that differ in their virulence and resistance profiles13,14. This diversity poses a significant challenge for both traditional antibiotic therapies and phage therapy, as a phage that is effective against one UPEC strain may not necessarily be effective against another. Therefore, a comprehensive analysis of multiple UPEC genomes is essential to develop a broad-spectrum phage therapy or, alternatively, to tailor phage cocktails that are effective against a wide range of UPEC strains15. In addition to the genomic analysis, this research also considers the potential ecological implications of phage therapy. The introduction of bacteriophages into the urinary tract environment, which is already a complex microbial ecosystem, could have unforeseen consequences on both the bacterial community and the host. Therefore, understanding the interaction between UPEC, its resident phages, and the therapeutic phages is crucial for developing safe and effective treatments.

In the present study, we first selected five clinically relevant UPEC isolates representing diverse antibiotic resistance profiles, virulence gene repertoires, and phylogenetic backgrounds and characterized their genomes in detail. Because this diversity may constitute an important obstacle for phage-based interventions, we subsequently isolated lytic bacteriophages using these same strains as hosts and comprehensively characterized their biological properties. Finally, we evaluated the activity of the isolated phages against a broader collection of UPEC isolates to determine whether they display narrow or broad host ranges and to assess their potential utility as precision antimicrobial agents against multidrug-resistant UPEC populations.

This study employed a combination of bioinformatic tools and genomic sequencing technologies to dissect the genomes of the novel UPEC strains and the lytic phages infecting them, which represent previously undescribed species. Comparative genomics of bacterial strains was used to identify conserved and unique genetic elements across the strains, with particular attention given to genes associated with virulence, antibiotic resistance, and phage susceptibility. Furthermore, the study examined the presence of prophages in UPEC genomes, as these endogenous phages could influence the outcome of bacteriophage therapy by conferring immunity in the host bacterium.

Materials and methods

Bacterial strains

All 37 Escherichia coli strains used in this study (EC1, EC3, EC13, EC22, EC40, EC53, EC57, EC65, EC113, EC124, EC130, EC131, EC134, EC149, EC162, EC164, EC179, EC189, EC192, EC217, EC223, EC227, EC229, EC234, EC255, EC256, EC259, EC263, EC294, EC295, EC296, EC313, EC319, EC341, EC344, EC348, EC378) were isolated from patients with UTI as described previously6 and stored at -80 °C at the Department of Medical Genetics and Laboratory Diagnostics, Faculty of Medicine, Jan Kochanowski University in Kielce, Poland. The study was approved by the Bioethics Committee at the Collegium Medicum, Jan Kochanowski University in Kielce, Poland (Resolutions: No. 23/2017 of September 11, 2017; No. 22/2024 of March 13, 2024, and No. 35/2025 of June 12, 2025) and all experiments were performed in accordance with relevant named guidelines and regulations. Informed consents were obtained from all patients. Five isolates (EC3, EC149, EC164, EC179, and EC256) were selected for detailed analysis and lytic phages isolation due to their different antibiotic resistance profiles. The characteristics of the selected five E. coli strains are presented in Table 1.

Bacterial cultivation and DNA extraction

Bacterial strains used in this study were cultured at 37 °C in liquid Luria-Bertani broth (LB, BioShop) or plated on solid LB medium supplemented with 1.5% agar (LA, BTL). For the DNA isolation procedure, the liquid cultures were grown with aeration in a shaker incubator at 200 rpm and a temperature of 37 °C. DNA was isolated using the NucleoSpin Tissue spin column-based kit (Macherey-Nagel). Isolation was performed according to the manufacturer’s protocol. The DNA concentration was determined spectrophotometrically at 260 nm.

Genome sequencing and de-novo assembly

Each bacterial isolate was sequenced commercially in the Genomed company (Warsaw, Poland) using two methods: short reads on the MiSeq platform and long reads on the MinION platform, to increase sequence reliability during genome assembly. The concentration of genomic DNA was measured using the PicoGreen (Invitrogen™) reagent. Genomic DNA was fragmented by sonication using Covaris E210, in accordance with the parameters recommended for preparing libraries for sequencing using MiSeq Illumina technology. Libraries were prepared using the NEBNext® Ultra™ kit II DNA Library Prep Kit for Illumina® according to the manufacturer’s recommendations. Sequencing was performed using paired technology 2 × 300 bp ends and Reagent Kit v3 (Illumina). Libraries for the MinION platform were prepared with the Rapid Barcoding Kit (SQK-RBK004) and next sequenced with a SpotON Flow Cell Mk I (R9.4.1).

Raw sequences were assembled by hybrid assembly of Illumina and Oxford Nanopore Technologies reads via the Unicycler program (Galaxy Version 0.5.0 + galaxy1) on default settings, with the exception that the contigs shorter than 500 bp were excluded. Assembled sequences were deposited by the BankIt tool in the GenBank database under the numbers: GCF_036282145.1 for EC3, GCF_036281895.1 for EC149, GCF_036281865.1 for EC164, GCF_036281825.1 for EC179, and GCF_036281835.1 for EC256.

Genomes annotation

Assembled genomes were subjected to annotation in RAST server16 using the ClassicRAST annotation scheme, FIGfams version 70, automatic error correction, and automatic frame shift correction17 and additionally by Bakta Web server. Subsequently, according to the submission to NCBI, genomes were annotated using the NCBI Prokaryotic Genome Annotation Pipeline (PGAP)18.

Phylogenetic analysis of bacteria

Single nucleotide polymorphism (SNP) was the basis of the analysis by CSI phylogeny platform to generate phylogenetic tree. Tree was constructed by CSI Phylogeny algorithms at default settings: minimal depth at SNP positions x10, minimal relative depth at SNP positions 10%, minimal distance between SNPs (prune) 10 bp, minimal SNP quality 30, minimal read mapping quality 25, minimal Z-score 1.96, and without ignoring heterozygous SNPs19. The Newick file was uploaded into the iTOL online tool to generate the phylogeny tree based on the SNPs20. Genetic distances were calculated by the OrthoAni program21. Dendrograms were prepared with the Oat tool21,22. Genetic distances between strains were calculated by the online Average Nucleotide Identity (OrthoANI) calculator (EZbioCloud)22,23.

Virulence-associated genes, CRISPR-cas analysis, and serotyping

The raw FASTA sequence(s) of a genome were screened against the VFDB database24 using the E. coli CFT073 UPEC genome as the reference. The obtained data were tabulated and manually analyzed for virulence-associated genes (VAGs). The assembly was additionally searched for the presence of Clustered Regularly Interspaced Short Palindromic Repeats (CRISPR) arrays using CRISPRCasFinder (online version; default settings)25. Serotypes of the strains were determined from whole-genome sequence data using the SerotypeFinder tool with default settings and the Escherichia coli reference database, based on sequence homology of O-antigen and flagellar antigen determinant genes. The analysis was performed using a minimum sequence identity threshold of 85% and a minimum target gene coverage of 60%26.

Plasmid identification

Assembled circular plasmid contigs were screened through BLAST database for identification of similar plasmid sequences. The highest coverage of sequences pointed the most similar plasmid. Plasmid replicons were searched by PlasmidFinder service (2.0.1 Server 2020-07-01, Database version: 2023-01-18)27 against the Enterobacteriales database, with 95% ientity threshold and 60% mnimum coverage.

Identification of antibiotic resistance genes

Whole-genome sequences of E. coli were screened for the presence of antibiotic resistance genes (ARGs) using the Resistance Gene Identifier (RGI) tool implemented in the Comprehensive Antibiotic Resistance Database (CARD)28. Genome assemblies were uploaded in FASTA format and analyzed using only Perfect and Strict hits. Results were used to assess the resistome composition of the analyzed E. coli genomes.

Prophage sequences identification

The materials used in this study consisted of assembled genome sequences of five E. coli strains: EC3, EC149, EC164, EC179, and EC256. The genomic sequences were analysed using the PHASTEST tool29 to identify prophage regions. Identified prophages were labelled according to the strain name, sequence number in the bacterial genome, and region number in the specific sequence. Only prophages labelled as complete were selected for further analysis. The amino acid sequences of proteins identified as belonging to the holin or endolysin family (lytic cassette proteins) by PHASTEST were transferred to the Phyre2 server30 to confirm the obtained protein annotations. Simultaneously, an analysis of antibiotic resistance genes within prophages was conducted using the CARD server28. The analysis results included only detected antibiotic resistance genes classified as perfect and strict matches.

Sequencing and in silico analyses of phage genomes

Phage genomes of vB_EcoS-149_4M, vB_EcoS-149_6D, vB_EcoS-164_2, vB_EcoS-179_3, and vB_EcoP-179_5 were sequenced using next-generation sequencing technology on an Illumina MiSeq platform. Assembly and annotation of the sequences were accomplished according to the Shen and Millard31. The quality of reads was controlled using FastQC (https://www.bioinformatics.babraham.ac.uk/projects/fastqc/). The raw data were filtered using a BBtools program. Reads subsampling was performed using Seqtk. De novo assembly was conducted using SPAdes32. Correction of the assembly was performed by Pilon33 and prediction of phage termini by PhageTermVirome34. Lastly, Pharokka35 was used to annotate phage genomes, PhaTYP36 was applied for life cycle identification, and several programs, such as ABRicate (Seemann, 2016), AMRFinderPlus37, RGI28, VirulenceFinder38, and Resfinder39, were used for identification of antimicrobial resistance or virulence genes. Assembled and annotated phage genomes were deposited in the GenBank database under the following accession numbers: PX985795 for vB_EcoS-149_4M, PX985796 for vB_EcoS-149_6D, PX985797 for vB_EcoS-164_2, PX985798 for vB_EcoS-179_3, and PX985799 for vB_EcoP-179_5. All genomic sequences are also available in the Zenodo repository under the following access link: 10.5281/zenodo.20098588. Visualization of phage genomes was made using Proksee40, and genome comparison was performed using EasyFig software41.

Taxonomic and phylogenetic analysis of phages

To establish the phylogeny relations of the analyzed phages, PhaGCN42 was initially used to assign them to putative families or genera. For phage vB_EcoS-164_2, BLAST analysis was performed to identify closely related phages, since PhaGCN was unable to assign this phage to any genus or family. The reference phages selected for vB_EcoS-164_2 shared at least 50% squence similarity. Genomic distances among all analyzed phages were calculated using VIRIDIC43. In turn, OMA software was applied to establish their common evolutionary origin based on orthologous groups of amino acid sequences of gene products. Multiple sequence alignments were generated using Clustal Omega44 and subsequently trimmed with trimAl45 using the “gappyout” option. Each alignment was evaluated for the strength of its phylogenetic signal. Alignments were excluded if they contained fewer than 10 parsimony-informative sites or if the proportion of such sites was below 5%. The remainin orthologous groups were used to construct maximum likelihood phylogenetic trees with IQ-TREE46. Tree visualization was performed using MEGA, and tree rooting was determined based on hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) using a distance matrix generated by VIRIDIC. What is more, in cases where analyzed phages formed species-level clusters, a single representative phage was selected using the medoid of the cluster method. Clustering with complete linkage was carried out using the SciPy library47.

Isolation, propagation, and stability of phages

All bacteriophages tested in this work were isolated from urban sewage (Gdansk Wastewater Treatment Plant, Poland) and propagated according to the protocol described by Topka and colleagues48. Newly isolated phages were deposited in the collection of phage strains at the Department of Molecular Biology of the University of Gdansk, Gdansk, Poland. The double overlay plaque assay was employed to determine a titer of phage suspensions. Serial 10-fold dilutions of each phage suspension were prepared in TM buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl, 10 mM MgSO4; pH 7.2). From each suspension, 5 µL was spotted onto a double agar layer. After overnight incubation of plates at 37 °C, plaques were counted, and the phage titer (expressed as PFU/mL) was calculated. Phage stability assays were performed as described previously49.

Efficiency of plating (EOP) determination

All five phages were tested against its primary UPEC host (EC149, EC164, or EC179) and thirty-six other UPEC clinical isolates listed above, for a more thorough evaluation of productive infection as defined by the efficiency of plating (EOP) method proposed by50.

Analysis of plaque and virion morphology

Plaque morphology was determined using the double-layer agar method and phage lysates serially diluted in TM buffer as described Topka48.Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) photos of the phage virions’ morphology were performed in the Bioimaging Laboratory, Faculty of Biology, University of Gdansk, Gdansk, Poland according the procedure presented by Topka-Bielecka and colleagues49.

Assessment of phage cytotoxicity

Purified phage particles were first obtained using the caesium chloride density gradient centrifugation method according to the protocol provided by51. Next, the level of endotoxins in each phage suspension was measured using the Thermo Scientific Pierce chromogenic endotoxin quant kit (Thermo Scientific). Finally, the safety and the impact of the purified phage particles on the viability of the human bladder cancer cell line, T24, and the human embryonic kidney line, HEK293, were measured, using the Neutral Red Uptake (NRU) cytotoxicity test. The NRU cytotoxicity test was performed in accordance with the following protocol. Mammalian cells were grown in McCoy’s 5 A Medium (for T24, Avantor) or Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle’s Medium (for HEK293, Gibco) containing 10% FS (Gibco), 1% pnicillin (Gibco), and 1% sreptomycin (Gibco). For the assay, mammalian cells were seeded into 96-well plates at a density of 1 × 104 cells per well and allowed to attach for 24 h at 37 °C and 5% CO2. After 24 h incubation, the medium was removed from all wells, and cells were exposed to 200 µL of the mix solution containing culture medium and selected phage particles (in the highest available concentrations, 109 PFU/mL for phage vB_EcoS-149_4M; 1010 PFU/mL for phages vB_EcoS-149_6D and vB_EcoS-179_3; 1011 PFU/mL for phages vB_EcoS-164_2 and vB_EcoP-179_5). In addition, 1% Titon X-100 (Merck) and TM buffer were used as positive and negative controls, respectively. Plates were kept overnight in an incubator (37 °C, 5% C2). Following 24 h incubation, the mixed solution was removed, and the mammalian cells were washed with 150 µL of PBS (Invitrogen) and treated with 100 uL of neutral red dye solution (0.33%, hermo Scientific), which efficiently penetrates membranes of viable cells. After 45 min. incubation in the dark at 37 °C, the solution was removed, and the cells were washed with 150 µL of PBS. Then, 100 µL solution containing 50% ehanol (Chempur) and 1% gacial acetic acid (Merck) in water was added to each well to extract the dye into the solution. The amount of released neutral red (proportional to the amount of viable cells) was measured as A540 in the Viroskanmicroplate reader. The cell viability was expressed as a percentage of the control values. The assay was performed in triplicate. The tested solution was considered cytotoxic if the cell viability was below 70%. ontrol and phage-treated T24 and HEK293 cells were also examined for morphological changes and cell growth inhibition under a light microscope at x10 magnification (OLYMPUS IX51 microscope). Cells were photographed at time zero and 24 h upon treatment with phage suspensions. Stability of phage particles during incubation with mammalian cells was quantified during this assay accordingly to the procedures described in Sect. 11 and expressed as PFU/mL.

Statistical analyses

Statistical analyses were performed using JASP software (version 0.97.1). Data are presented as means ± standard deviation (SD). Comparisons between two groups were performed using a Student’s t-test. Experiments involving more than two groups or factors were analyzed using a two-way analysis of variance (two-way ANOVA), followed by Tukey’s honestly significant difference (HSD) post hoc test when appropriate. The assumptions of ANOVA were evaluated by examining residual normality using Q–Q plots and homogeneity of variances using Levene’s test. Differences were considered statistically significant at p < 0.05.

Results

Genomes’ overview

Previously described urinary isolates of E. coli6 were selected for this study based on their diverse drug resistance profiles: highly resistant (MDR strain), highly susceptible (resistance only to ampicillin), and mixed drug resistance (resistance to penicillins, fluoroquinolones, co-trimoxazole, and others). Additionally, a diverse profiles of virulence genes (presence of the 6 previously studied VAGs, or the absence of any, or a varying number of VAGs) and phylogenetic groups (A, B2, E, D, F) were also considered. These parameters were intended to aid in the search for the largest possible number of phages for a diverse group of strains. This diversity gave us the basis to hypothesize that we will find even more interesting features that may determine relationships with phages. The genomes of 5 strains (Table 1) maintaining the above-mentioned criteria, were sequenced in this work and compared with previously performed genetic analyses (CRISPR, VAGs, and phylogenetic groups by PCR) and phenotypic analyses (drug resistance testing by the automated VITEK 2 apparatus using AST Cards, BioMerieux). All studied E. coli strains revealed small genomic differences, including GC content, genome size, number of RNAs, and number of coding sequences, according to the Rapid Annotation Subsystem Technology (RAST) server annotation (Table 2). The genome sizes ranged from approximately 4.59 Mb to 5.51 Mb, with the largest genome being EC256 and the smallest EC3. The GC content across all genomes was relatively consistent, hovering around 50.5% o 50.8%.The number of subsystems varied slightly, with EC256 showing the highest count at 611 and EC3 the lowest at 585. The number of coding sequences (CDS) also varied, from 4,361 in EC3 to 5,469 in EC164. Lastly, the number of RNAs ranged from 79 in EC3 to 118 in EC164, indicating some variation in RNA content among these genomes.

Phylogenetic analysis

The phylogeny analysis of tested strains was performed by calculation of the OrthoANI values between genome sequences and the reference genome (K-12 substr. MG1655, RefSeq: GCF_000005845.2). The comparison revealed the similarity of genomes in range from 96.96% for EC164 to 99.38% for EC3, which indicated that all of them belong to the E. coli species (Table 3). Whole-genome relatedness among the investigated Escherichia coli strains was assessed using OrthoANI values and isolates shared OrthoANI values above 96.7%, confirming their assignment to the same species (Fig. 1). However, notable differences in genomic similarity were observed among the strains. The highest similarity was detected between EC3 and the reference strain E. coli K-12 MG1655 (99.43%), indicating a very close phylogenetic relationship. Similarly, EC149 also exhibited high similarity to MG1655 (99.01%). In contrast, the remaining UPEC isolates (EC164, EC179, and EC256) showed lower similarity to MG1655, with OrthoANI values ranging from 97.04% to 97.54%. Comparison with the well-characterized uropathogenic strain CFT073 revealed a different pattern. EC164 displayed the highest genomic similarity to CFT073 (98.86%), suggesting a closer relationship to the UPEC lineage. In contrast, EC3, EC149, EC179, and EC256 showed lower similarity to CFT073, with OrthoANI values ranging from 96.75% to 97.21%. Additionally, we have constructed a phylogenetic tree for the studied UPEC strains to investigate their relationships and assess genomic diversity through SNP analysis of five UPEC genomes. The SNP-based phylogenetic analysis revealed that the UPEC isolates formed distinct lineages relative to the reference strain. Isolate EC164 clustered most closely with the well-characterized UPEC strain CFT073, suggesting a high degree of genomic relatedness. This cluster was further associated with EC256, while EC179 and EC149 occupied progressively more distant branches. EC3 formed a separate lineage, showing the greatest divergence from the remaining UPEC isolates. The high bootstrap support values (0.955–1.000) for the major nodes indicate strong confidence in the inferred phylogenetic relationships. Overall, the SNP tree demonstrates substantial genomic diversity among the analyzed UPEC strains, with EC164 exhibiting the closest evolutionary relationship to the reference UPEC strain CFT073 (Fig. 2).

Plasmid identification

Because plasmids can encode phage defense systems and influence susceptibility to bacteriophage infection, characterization of the plasmid content of UPEC strains is important for evaluating their suitability as targets for phage therapy and for identifying potential barriers to phage-mediated bacterial eradication. Genomic sequences were analyzed using the PlasmidFinder and BLAST tools, which led to the identification of 13 unique replicons among the tested UPEC E. coli strains. All tested UPEC strains carried at least one plasmid (Table 4). The plasmid pEC3-1 (41,901 bp) identified in host EC3 contains the IncI1-I(Alpha) replicon, exhibiting 100% identity and coverage with its closest match, plasmid R64 from Salmonella enterica subsp. enterica serovar Typhimurium. The pEC149-1 plasmid, with the pO111 replicon, showed 98.87% identity with the reference, suggesting a specific plasmid associated with serotype O111. Furthermore, the EC149 strain harboured three additional replicon sequences, where one was similar to the IncB/O/K/Z group (pEC149-2 plasmid), and the replicons of the two remaining plasmids were unidentified. Detection of virulence associated genes

Genes encoding adhesins

A comparative analysis of five UPEC genomes revealed the presence of several VAGs linked to adhesion, exhibiting varied distribution across strains (Table 5). Colonization factor antigen I (CFA/I) was identified exclusively in strain EC256, indicating its potential strain-specific role in adherence. Conversely, E. coli common pilus (ECP), a conserved adhesin, was prevalent across all genomes, where EC3 and EC149 displaying partial presence, reinforcing its ubiquitous role in UPEC adhesion. Similarly, HCP and upaG/ehaG, key contributors to host cell attachment, were consistently identified in all genomes, emphasizing their significance as core adhesion factors.

The presence of fimbrial adhesins varied among strains. A fimH, part of the type 1 fimbrial system, was partially represented in all genomes highlighting its fundamental role in urinary tract colonization. A pap fimbriae, linked to pyelonephritis, were detected in EC164 and EC256, albeit partially, suggesting their involvement in more severe infections. Neither sfa nor foc fimbriae genes were detected in the analyzed genomes. Other adhesion factors showed differential distribution. Gene encoding EaeH protein was common in most strains except EC3, while genes coding ELF pilus was identified in EC3, EC149, and EC256, pointing to strain-dependent roles in pathogenesis. Interestingly, pilQRW were found in EC3 and EC149. This diversity in adhesion-related VAGs underscores the adaptability of UPEC strains in urinary tract colonization and pathogenesis, driven by a repertoire of strain-specific and conserved adhesins.

Genes encoding autotransporters

We examined 10 virulence-associated autotransporter genes (Table 5). The genes analyzed included aatA, agn43, cah, cdiA, ehaB, air/eaeX, pic, sat, upaH, and vat, which are involved in adhesion, biofilm formation, tissue colonization, and host cell damage. The gene distribution varied across strains, with cah (biofilm formation) present in two genomes (EC3 and EC164) and ehaB (adhesion and biofilm development) found in three (EC164, EC179, and EC256).

Invasion and iron uptake associated genes

A comparative analysis of VAGs related to invasion and iron uptake in five UPEC genomes revealed distinct gene distributions, underscoring their potential roles in UPEC pathogenesis (Table 5). The invasion-related gene ibe was consistently present across all strains, indicating its pivotal role in facilitating bacterial entry into host cells. In contrast, tia was variably distributed, appearing in EC164, and EC256 but absent in others, suggesting strain-specific differences in invasion mechanisms. Genes associated with iron uptake, a critical function for bacterial survival in iron-limited environments like the urinary tract, exhibited considerable variability. The siderophore synthesis genes iuc, chu, ireA, sit, iro, and fyuA/irp/ybt were inconsistently distributed among strains. For instance, chu and iro were present in most strains except EC3 and EC149, whereas iuc was found only in EC179 and EC256. Similarly, ireA was uniquely present in EC164, highlighting its specific adaptation. The iron transporter sit was more widespread, detected in four of the five genomes (except EC3).

Secretion systems and toxins associated genes

A detailed analysis of VAGs linked to type III secretion system (TTSS) effectors, secretion systems, and toxins was conducted (Table 5). TTSS effector genes, including espL, espR, espX, and espY, were variably present among the strains. espL was partially identified in four strains, while espR and espX were found in four strains, with espX is present (partially) in all five strains. The espY gene was detected only in strains EC179 and EC256. These findings suggest variability in TTSS effector gene distribution among UPEC strains. Regarding secretion systems, genes associated with the T6SS systems, such as aec ACE T6SS (present in all genomes except EC149) and SCI-I T6SS (present only in genome EC164) were heterogeneously distributed. Toxin-related genes exhibited diverse profiles. The classical hemolysin operon hlyABCD was absent from all genomes, whereas hlyE/clyA was detected in four strains (except EC149). Other toxins, such as usp, cnf1, and senB, were found sporadically. These results highlight the heterogeneous distribution of VAGs among UPEC strains, reflecting their diverse pathogenic capabilities in urinary tract infections.

Capsule and LOS synthesis genes

Genes involved in capsule production, which play a critical role in immune evasion and antiphagocytic activity, were identified in selected strains. The galE gene, associated with capsule biosynthesis and immune evasion was present in EC3, EC179, and EC256, but was absent in the other strains, suggesting strain-specific utilization of this mechanism. Another capsule-related gene linked to antiphagocytic functions was identified only in EC3, highlighting its unique strategy among the analyzed genomes. In terms of LOS biosynthesis, a critical virulence factor for bacterial survival and host interaction, one gene was found in EC3. Its absence in the remaining strains points to alternative mechanisms compensating for LOS-related functions in these UPEC isolates.

Detection of antibiotic resistance genes

Analyzed genomes showed a total of 81 ARGs were detected, with various mechanisms of antibiotic resistance identified across different UPEC strains (Supplementary Table 1). The majority of identified ARGs were associated with efflux pumps, particularly the resistance-nodulation-cell division (RND) and major facilitator superfamily (MFS) types. The identified genes contribute to multidrug resistance through different mechanisms. The efflux pump genes acrB and mdtG encode transporters involved in the extrusion of a variety of toxic compounds and antibiotics from bacterial cells. In contrast, marA and hns encode global transcriptional regulators that modulate the expression of multiple resistance-associated genes, including efflux systems and membrane permeability determinants. Together, these genes may contribute to reduced susceptibility to several classes of antimicrobials, including fluoroquinolones, tetracyclines, macrolides, and other structurally diverse compounds. Several identified genes encoded β-lactamases (e.g., TEM-1, CTX-M-3, SHV-1) which inactivate β-lactam antibiotics such as penicillins and cephalosporins. Genes for target modification and protection were also detected: sul1, dfrA1, armA modify antibiotic targets, rendering them less susceptible to drugs like sulfonamides and trimethoprim. Additionally, we identified the mutations in gyrA and parC which contributes to fluoroquinolone resistance. Many ARGs confer resistance to multiple antibiotic classes. For instance, marA and acrB provide resistance to fluoroquinolones, cephalosporins, and tetracyclines. Overall, the genetic analysis indicated a high level of multidrug resistance in studied UPEC strains, driven mainly by efflux mechanisms and β-lactamase production. However, these results were not fully consistent with phenotypic studies, except EC256 strain6. This is not surprising as drug resistance is multifactorial.

Susceptibility to lytic phages testing

The search for phages capable of infecting the five selected UPEC strains showing different antibiotic resistance profiles, led to the isolation of five new lytic phages from municipal sewage. The isolation procedure was based on the one-host enrichment method in which a raw urban sewage sample was mixed with a culture of the UPEC strain. With this approach, no phages infecting the EC3 and EC256 strains were acquired, however, two phages against EC149, two phages against EC179, and one phage against the EC164 strain were successfully isolated. All of them infected host UPEC bacteria with high efficiency, giving the titer of the order from 108 to 1011 PFU/mL. The obtained phages were named according to the nomenclature principles proposed by Kropinski and collaborators52, taking into account the name of the original host strain. Thus, phages isolated using strain EC149 were named vB_EcoS-149_4M, and vB_EcoS-149_6D, whereas phages isolated on strains EC164 and EC179 were named vB_EcoS-164_2 and vB_EcoS-179_3 or vB_EcoP-179_5, respectively.

To evaluate the sensitivity of UPEC strains to the isolated phages, a more detailed analysis based on the measurement of the efficiency of plating (EOP) was performed. This method allowed for comparing the phage efficiency of infection between the primary host strain EC149, EC164, or EC179, and other UPEC clinical isolates (Fig. 3). Analysis revealed that phage vB_EcoS-149_4M presented the broadest strong activity, infecting 7 isolates with high efficiency and 3 with medium efficiency (Fig. 3). Interestingly, 3 of 7 strains infected by this phage with high efficiency (EC113, EC131, and EC134) showed EOP values above 1, which means that they are even better bacterial strains for phage propagation than the primary strain EC149 (Fig. 3). In turn, phage vB_EcoS-149_6D infected 4 isolates with high efficiency but showed the highest number of low-efficiency infections, whereas phage vB_EcoS-164_2 had high efficiency against only 2 isolates, with most other infections being sporadic (10 UPEC isolates, Fig. 3). Phages vB_EcoS-179_3 and vB_EcoP-179_5 displayed very limited activity, each infecting only one isolate (the primary EC179 host) with high efficiency and showing no medium, low, or sporadic infections among other UPEC strains (Fig. 3). Surprisingly, none of the phages isolated on EC149 was able to infect strains EC164 and EC179, and vice versa (Fig. 3). Overall, the data indicate substantial variability in phage host range and infection strength, with some phages (particularly vB_EcoS-149_4M) demonstrating broader and stronger activity against UPEC isolates compared to other tested phages, which were more selective or had predominantly sporadic infections.

Plaque and virion morphology

First, all tested phages were subjected to morphological analysis of their plaques and virions. As presented in Fig. 4, all of them formed clear plaques, typical for lytic phages, which differed in size. The smallest ones had a diameter < 1 mm and were formed by phage vB_EcoS-164_2, while the largest ones were about 2 mm and were generated by phage vB_EcoS-149_6D. In addition, for phages vB_EcoS-179_3 and vB_EcoP-179_5, barely visible haloes (turbid zones around the plaques) were observed. Their presence indicates the action of released (not associated with a tail) phage enzymes on bacterial cells. The plaques of the remaining phages did not show the presence of haloes. Although we cannot completely exclude the possibility that strain EC179 releases extracellular factors in response to phage infection, this explanation appears less likely. Plaque-associated halos are most commonly attributed to phage-encoded polysaccharide depolymerases that diffuse beyond the plaque margins and enzymatically degrade bacterial surface polysaccharides, including capsules, exopolysaccharides, and biofilm matrix components. Supporting this hypothesis, bioinformatic analyses identified a tail spike protein (YEP44078.1) in phage vB_EcoS-179_3 and a tail fiber protein (YEP44190.1) in phage vB_EcoP-179_5 as candidate depolymerases. Notably, halo formation was restricted to phages infecting strain EC179, whereas phages infecting EC149 and EC164 formed clear plaques without detectable halos. This observation may indicate that strain EC179 possesses unique surface polysaccharide structures that serve as substrates for depolymerase activity and that both phages encode depolymerase-like proteins capable of interacting with these structures. Therefore, we speculate that the halo phenotype observed for EC179-associated phages results primarily from phage-mediated enzymatic degradation of host surface polysaccharides rather than from the release of host-derived factors.

The sensitivity to external agents

The stability of the tested phages following exposure to physical and chemical agents is summarized in Fig. 5. The values are expressed as percentages of surviving phages relative to the untreated control. A two-way ANOVA demonstrated significant effects of both phage type (F(4,120) = 32.51, p < 0.001) and experimental condition (F(11,120) = 112.60, p < 0.001) on phage survival. Moreover, a significant phage × condition interaction was observed (F(44,120) = 14.94, p < 0.001), indicating that the response to external agents depended on the particular phage tested and the tested external factors affected phage survival to significantly different extents.

Overall, phage vB_EcoS-149_4M exhibited the highest stability. It retained full infectivity under most tested conditions, including exposure to pH 4 and pH 10, temperatures of − 20 °C and 40 °C, osmotic shock, and treatment with DMSO, ethanol, sarkosyl, and chloroform. A reduction in titer was observed only after exposure to high temperature (62 °C), strong acidity (pH 2), and CTAB. In contrast, vB_EcoS-149_6D displayed moderate stability, remaining fully resistant to 40 °C, pH 10, chloroform, and sarkosyl, while maintaining relatively high survival at pH 4 (59%), DMSO (83%), CTAB (67%), and under osmotic shock (87%).

Phages vB_EcoS-179_3 and vB_EcoP-179_5 remained fully stable at − 20 °C and 40 °C, as well as at pH 4 and pH 10, but showed marked reductions in titer following exposure to detergents and ethanol. Phage vB_EcoS-164_2 exhibited the lowest overall stability, with its viability being strongly reduced under most conditions. Only exposure to pH 4, pH 10, DMSO, and chloroform had little or no effect on its infectivity (Fig. 5A).

Tukey’s post hoc test further demonstrated that vB_EcoS-149_4M exhibited significantly higher overall survival than all remaining phages (all p < 0.001). Phage vB_EcoS-149_6D also showed significantly higher survival than vB_EcoP-179_5 (p < 0.001), vB_EcoS-164_2 (p < 0.001), and vB_EcoS-179_3 (p = 0.029). In contrast, no significant differences in overall survival were detected among vB_EcoP-179_5, vB_EcoS-164_2, and vB_EcoS-179_3 (all p > 0.05).

Taken together, these findings indicate that vB_EcoS-149_4M was the most resistant phage among those tested, whereas vB_EcoS-164_2, vB_EcoS-179_3, and vB_EcoP-179_5 formed a group of comparatively labile phages. Overall, a strong acid (pH = 2) completely inactivated all tested phages, and CTAB caused complete or nearly complete inactivation of most phages except vB_EcoS-149_6D. In contrast, weak acidic and alkaline conditions, as well as moderate heating (40 °C), were generally well tolerated. These differential stability profiles have practical implications for phage storage and handling.

To test the safety of phages to mammalian cells, the viability of the human bladder cancer cell line, T24, and the human embryonic kidney line, HEK293, was assessed after treatment with all the tested phages (Fig. 6). The endotoxin levels were checked in all phage solutions and most didn’t exceed the value of 1 EU/mL (Supplementary Figure S1). In addition, the stability of phage particles was tested after 24 h-long incubation with mammalian cells (Fig. 6).

The addition of phages (to a concentration of at least 108 PFU/mL) to T24 or HEK293 cultures resulted in no significant changes detected in the number of viable cells relative to the negative control, which includes non-treated cultures with viability at the level of 100% (Fig. 6A, B). Phage genome content

The complete genomes of all five phages were sequenced and subjected to detailed bioinformatic analysis. The results revealed that genome lengths ranged from 43,053 to 56,684 bp, with GC contents between 44% and 51%. Genome annotation identified multiple coding sequences (CDSs), ranging from 67 to 101 per phage genome; however, putative functions were assigned to less than half of them. Genes with assigned functions were used to generate phage genome maps, which are presented in Supplementary Figure S2A-E.

In addition, analysis using PhageTermVirome indicated that vB_EcoS-149_4M and vB_EcoS-149_6D possess headful packaging termini, whereas vB_EcoP-179_5 contains short direct terminal repeats. For vB_EcoS-164_2 and vB_EcoS-179_3, the phage termini were not determined with the applied tools (Table 8).

Taxonomic and phylogenetic analyses

Performed classification revealed that phages vB_EcoS-149_4M and vB_EcoS-149_6D belong to the family Drexlerviridae but represent two distinct genera: phage vB_EcoS-149_4M clusters within the genus Hanrivervirus (Fig. 7), whereas phage vB_EcoS-149_6D groups within the genus Warwickvirus (Fig. 8).

Hierarchical cluster analysis (HCA) based on VIRIDIC genomic distances (Fig. 7A) showed that phage vB_EcoS-149_4M forms a separate branch within the Hanrivervirus cluster and exceeds the species demarcation threshold (red dashed line), indicating substantial genomic divergence from all currently described members of this genus.

This result was further supported by the maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree (Fig. 7B), where we observe that vB_EcoS-149_4M forms a well-supported, isolated lineage relative to other Hanrivervirus representatives, including EC147, aalborv, haarsle, vojen, pSf-1, JakobBernoulli, KarlJaspers, JLBYU09, vB_EcoD_Fulton, Henu8, and aaroes. The long branch length separating vB_EcoS-149-4 M from its closest relatives indicates a considerable evolutionary distance. For phage vB_EcoS-149_4M, a total of 29 orthologous genes were identified. Interestingly, only three orthologous groups contained a sufficient number of parsimony-informative sites and were therefore retained for phylogenetic reconstruction (Supplementary Table S2).

Further taxonomic classification revealed that phage vB_EcoS-149_6D belongs to the genus Warwickvirus within the family Drexlerviridae (Fig. 8). HCA based on VIRIDIC genomic distances (Fig. 8A) demonstrated that vB_EcoS-149_6D clusters below the species demarcation threshold (red dashed line) together with phage AV105, indicating that these two phages constitute a single species. This cluster is clearly separated from other Warwickvirus representatives, including Tuinin, MikeNSara, EC167, BEK12A, and PeterMerian, supporting its distinct taxonomic position at the species level.

The maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree reconstructed from orthologous gene alignments (Fig. 8B) corroborated the clustering results. It shows that phage vB_EcoS-149_6D is separated from more distantly related Warwickvirus phages such as Tuinin, MikeNSara, and EC167, indicating a distinct evolutionary lineage within the genus. High bootstrap support values at the internal nodes further confirm the robustness of this phylogenetic placement. For phage vB_EcoS-149_6D, a total of 51 orthologous genes were identified, of which eight met the criteria for phylogenetic analysis (Supplementary Table S2).

Taken together, both the genomic distance clustering (Figs. 7A and 8A) and phylogenetic tree topology (Figs. 7B and 8B) consistently demonstrate that phage vB_EcoS-149_4M represents a distinct lineage within the genus Hanrivervirus, while phage vB_EcoS-149_6D belongs to the genus Warwickvirus and forms a separate species cluster with phage AV105. Based on these data, we propose to establish two novel species: Hanrivervirus vB_EcoS-149_4M and Warwickvirus vB_EcoS-149_6D.

Further analyses revealed that phage vB_EcoS-164_2 is related to phages currently assigned to two closely related genera that are not yet classified within any recognized family. As shown in Fig. 9, vB_EcoS-164_2 clusters with phages of the genus Nonavirus, including several closely related Sashaviruses. Interestingly, phage 9NA, although the most distantly positioned, also clusters within the Nonavirus lineage (Fig. 9), further suggesting the genetic cohesion of this group. HCA showed that the analyzed phages form three subclusters that exceed the recommended by ICTV genus demarcation threshold (blue dashed line), suggesting the presence of three potential separate genera (Fig. 9A). However, the observed clustering remains compact and the overall genomic distances among these phages are relatively small, indicating a shared evolutionary background. In addition, the maximum-likelihood phylogenetic tree based on the concatenated orthologous sequence alignments (Fig. 9B) demonstrates that all analyzed phages form a coherent monophyletic lineage, with clear internal branching corresponding to the three subclusters observed in the HCA. Interestingly, the two outermost subclusters are formed by phages (vB_EcoS PJ16, vB_EcoS AVIO78A, KarinMuster_Bas82, vB_EcoS-164_2, and 9NA) belonging to the genus Nonavirus. Besides, the overall topology and short inter-branch distances support a close evolutionary relationship among all members of the group. In respect to this an taking into account the shared orthologous gene content (of which 13 exhibited sufficient phylogenetic signal to be retained for tree reconstruction as indicated in Supplementary Table S2), and the consistent phylogenetic placement of all analyzed phages, we propose to classify all these phages within a single genus, Nonavirus as indicated by a dashed black line in Fig. 9B. Our proposal results from the fact that despite the recommendation, ICTV accepts cases in which phages showing less than 70% of sequence similarity and exceeding the genus threshold (blue dashed line) are classified as belonging to the same genus due to their close evolutionary relationship. Furthermore, as phage vB_EcoS-164_2 apparently represents a distinct species within this genus, we propose to designate this species as Nonavirus vB_EcoS-164_2.

Genomic comparison between phages

As shown in Fig. 12, all analysed phages are similar to the selected reference phages from their respective genera. In the case of phages vB_EcoS-149_4M and vB_EcoS-149_6D, a small inversion is observed at the end of the genome, which is most likely the result of differences in genome assembly approaches. Nevertheless, gene regions responsible for DNA, RNA and nucleotide metabolism, as well as, connector, head and packaging, and lysis functions, are highly similar among all pairs of phages. The most pronounced differences are observed within the tail region of the vB_EcoS-164_2, vB_EcoS-179_3, and vB_EcoP-179_5 and their reference phage genomes, potentially reflecting variation in receptor-binding proteins and depolymerase-encoding genes.

As mentioned earlier, genes predicted to encode products participating in integration and excision processes were identified in the genomes of phages vB_EcoS-164_2 and vB_EcoS-179_3; however, both bioinformatic analyses and laboratory experiments indicate that all five phages analysed by us are lytic. To further investigate this discrepancy, the PhaTYP software was used to predict the lifecycles of all phages included in the phylogenetic analysis above. As shown in Supplementary Table S3, only phages Sasha, Sergei, KarinMuster Bas82, and Solent were classified as temperate, whereas all the rest, including the tested vB_EcoS-149_4M, vB_EcoS-149_6D, vB_EcoS-164_2, vB_EcoS-179_3, and vB_EcoP-179_5, were classified as lytic. Interestingly, Sasha, Sergei, and Solent were described as lytic phages by their authors53. This inconsistency cannot be fully explained based on the available information; therefore, experimental confirmation of the bioinformatic-based predictions is required.

Nonetheless, the presence in vB_EcoS-164_2 and vB_EcoS-179_3 genomes of genes annotated as products engaged in integration and excision processes may represent remnants of a temperate ancestor or so-called moron genes that were acquired in the past and have been retained in these genomes. As confirmed by phylogenetic analysis, both phages vB_EcoS-164_2 and vB_EcoS-179_3 are relatively unique, and the limited number of closely related phages currently available makes it difficult to draw definitive conclusions at this stage.

Discussion

Urinary tract infections, despite many years of intensive research, still pose a serious medical threat54–59. The main factor causing urinary tract infections is E. coli bacterium5,60. Although the concept of a distinct evolutionary specialization of UPEC remains debated and the boundaries between pathogenic and commensal E. coli are often blurred, several studies have suggested that certain phylogenetic lineages and combinations of virulence-associated traits may confer an increased propensity to cause urinary tract infections57. Accordingly, the ability of E. coli to cause UTIs does not rely on a universal set of virulence factors but rather on specific combinations of determinants that promote adaptation to the urinary tract environment6. These determinants include adhesion to epithelial cells, invasion of urinary tract cells, intracellular biofilm formation, antibiotic resistance, and the ability to transfer resistance determinants to bacterial cells inhabiting the same ecological niche, all of which are among the most important features enabling UPEC to cause persistent or recurrent infections61. Consequently, the risk, severity, and clinical outcome of UTIs are likely determined by complex interactions between host susceptibility and the urovirulence potential of genetically diverse E. coli strains62–65 .

The use of bacteriophages and phage cocktails to combat infections caused by uropathogenic bacteria is one of the promising and constantly developed methods of biocontrol of pathogens66–68. This study aimed to provide preliminary insights into the relationship between the genomic features of a tested UPEC strains and their potential relevance to bacteriophage therapy. By elucidating the genetic underpinnings of UPEC strains, this research contributes to the development of targeted phage therapies that could offer a viable alternative to antibiotics in the treatment of UTIs. The findings from this study have the potential to inform future phage therapy protocols, not only for UTIs but also for other bacterial infections where antibiotic resistance is a growing concern. This study focused on the comparative analysis of five UPEC genomes with the aim of identifying potential targets for bacteriophage therapy. By examining the genomic structure, virulence factors, and resistance mechanisms present in these UPEC strains, this research sought to uncover the vulnerabilities that could be exploited by phages. A detailed understanding of the genomic features of UPEC is crucial for designing effective phage-based treatments, as it allows for the identification of specific bacterial receptors, metabolic pathways, and genetic determinants that are critical for phage infection and replication. The rationale behind selecting multiple UPEC genomes for this study lies in the genetic diversity observed among UPEC strains.

The basis for the large diversity of UPEC is the dynamic structure of their genome69,70. It can be conventionally divided into two parts. The first includes the core genome, which consists of housekeeping genes involved in replication, transcription and translation. The core genome determines the basic metabolic functions of bacteria and is characterized by stability. The second part is much more flexible, consisting of a set of genes that are often unique to individual strains and includes mobile elements, like plasmids, phages, and genomic islands71–73. These genes may be acquired or lost depending on adaptive needs and influences the colonization and pathogenicity of bacteria. The structure and size of the genome reflect the functioning and habitat of bacteria. Mobile elements increase the cell’s adaptability to a changing environment, which therefore increases the genome size.

In our study, isolate EC3 had the smallest genome size. It also had the lowest number of subsystems, coding sequences and the number of RNAs. Isolate EC256 had the largest genome and the largest number of subsystems, isolate EC164 had the largest number of coding sequences and RNAs. The GC content was relatively consistent. Despite differences resulting from the need to adapt to the environment, comparisons of the genomes of all isolates confirmed that they belong to E. coli species and are relatively closely related to each other. The genome isolates and EC3 was closest to the UPEC_CFT073 reference genome. The most distinctive strain was EC256, but it should be remembered that it was the only multi-drug-resistant strain and seems to be the most virulent.

The most frequently identified plasmids were those from the IncF group. This is a family of plasmids commonly found in Gram-negative bacteria, including commensal ones. However, they may carry genes associated with virulence and antibiotic resistance, especially resistance to extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), but also to aminoglycosides, tetracyclines and carbapenems especially resistance to extended-spectrum β-lactamases (ESBLs), but also to aminoglycosides, tetracyclines and carbapenems74,75. What is more, their remarkable intracellular versatility and the rapid evolution of the regulatory sequences of their replicons enable them to effectively spread among Enterobacteriaceae. In isolate EC149, we identified a pO111 plasmid that harbored the colistin resistance gene mcr-1.1, suggesting a potential role of this plasmid in the transfer of colistin resistance76,77. This is an important observation because colistin is often a “last resort” drug and the increase in resistance has serious consequences. We also detected the IncM2 plasmid in isolate EC256. In the literature, this plasmid is associated with the presence of the gene encoding NDM-1 carbapenemase. Moreover, plasmids from the IncM2 group encoding blaNDM-1 are considered emerging threats and require constant monitoring78. In addition to their role in the dissemination of antimicrobial resistance genes, plasmids may also influence bacteriophage susceptibility by carrying phage defense systems, such as restriction–modification systems, abortive infection systems, and other mobile genetic elements that can interfere with phage infection, thereby potentially affecting the efficacy of phage therapy79.

The studied bacterial strains exhibit varied resistance profiles, with a concerning level of resistance to commonly used antibiotics like ampicillin. The presence of resistance in strains from different phylogenetic groups, especially to broad-spectrum antibiotics, suggests the potential complexity in treating infections caused by these strains, this is in line with our previous observations6. Globally, the high occurrence and increasing antibiotic resistance among uropathogenic E. coli constitute one of the most important health problems, which consequently generates high financial costs of treatment80,81. The consistent susceptibility to carbapenems and nitrofurantoin highlights these as potential treatment options for infections caused by these strains. However, the resistance observed in some strains to other commonly used antibiotics emphasizes the importance of accurate susceptibility testing before treatment. The alarming rate of drug resistance among E. coli strains, accompanied by the capacity for intracellular pathogenesis, has elevated to the ranks of the monitored species by European Centre for Disease Prevention and Control82, . More than half of E. coli isolates were resistant to at least one antimicrobial group. Our previous study of 784 E. coli isolated from various clinical patients indicated similar phenomenon6, especially in the case of penicillins and blood-derived isolates. Moreover, only 26% E. coli isolate were sensitive for all antibiotics. The Global Antibiotic Resistance Surveillance Report in 2025 confirmed that E. coli and K. pneumoniae are the leading drug-resistant Gram-negative bacteria found in bloodstream infections83. Among them, the E. coli uropathogens exhibit considerable variability in susceptibility to trimethoprim–sulfamethoxazole, with reported resistance levels spanning approximately 14.6% to 60% in Europe84–88.Besides the extensive use of fluoroquinolones, particularly ciprofloxacin, has also contributed to a steady rise in resistance of UPEC bacteria to this class of drugs. Fluoroquinolone resistance in UPEC was markedly more prevalent in low- and middle-income regions (55.5–85.5%) compared with high-incomecountries, where reported values range from 5.1% to 32.0%89. In Poland, resstance toFQs was observed in about 30% of UPEC isolates90. It is worthadding that the presence of genetic determinants associated with fluoroquinolone resistance is accompanied by reduced susceptibility to these antibiotics, as observed in our studies. Although amoxicillin–clavulanate remains a recommended first-line option for the treatment of pyelonephritis and complicated urinary tract infections, its effectiveness varies geographically. Resistance rates among UPEC isolates to this medicament ranged from 5.3% in Germany87 to 37.6% in France91. he continuing upward tren in antimicrobial resistance underscores the importance of prudent antibiotic use in the management of UTIs89. The aim of this work was therefore to thoroughly screen E. coli for its hidden potential for drug resistance and virulence, which could become a target for new tools to combat this pathogen. The studied strains were susceptible to most antibiotics, with the exception of one strain (EC256). Despite this, a large number of genes associated with drug resistance were detected. This observation further underscores how dangerous E. coli strains are. Here, most of the detected ARGs related to efflux system and the antibiotic inactivation. These data are in good agreement with literature data, where β-lactamases89 and efflux pumps92 are pointed as a major source of resistance.

Taken together, genomic analysis of several UPEC clinical isolates revealed substantial diversity among them, attributable to the dynamic nature of their genomes. All tested isolates carried stable core genes responsible for essential metabolic processes, virulence factors, and variable genome fragments in the form of mobile genetic elements, including prophages, plasmids, and determinants linked to multidrug resistance. The genomic variability observed among the analyzed isolates likely reflects adaptation to diverse ecological niches and poses challenges for treatment. It should be noted that each phylogenetic group was represented by a single isolate in the present study. Therefore, the observed genomic and phenotypic characteristics should be interpreted as isolate-specific observations rather than representative features of entire phylogenetic groups. Nevertheless, the findings illustrate the remarkable diversity that exists among UPEC strains belonging to different evolutionary lineages.

Because the genomic heterogeneity and multidrug resistance of UPEC may limit the effectiveness of conventional antimicrobial therapies, we next sought to isolate and characterize lytic bacteriophages using these same UPEC isolates as hosts and to determine their potential utility as precision antimicrobial agents against multidrug-resistant UPEC populations.

Five new lytic bacteriophages targeting UPEC were isolated from municipal sewage. Host range testing and EOP analyses revealed substantial variability in infectivity among these phages, with phage vB_EcoS-149_4M standing out as the most promising therapeutic candidate. This phage demonstrated the broadest and strongest activity, infecting 27% of all tested UPEC isolates with high or medium efficiency. Interestingly, several isolates allowed for phage propagation even better than the primary host, the EC149 strain. Furthermore, vB_EcoS-149_4M displayed satisfactory stability under various physical and chemical conditions, retained high infectivity during prolonged incubation with mammalian cell cultures, and exhibited no cytotoxic effects toward human bladder (T24) or kidney (HEK293) cells. An additional advantage was the lack of toxin and antibiotic resistance genes in its genome, as well as the presence of phage genes encoding lytic proteins. On the other hand, two other tested phages, vB_EcoS-179_3 and vB_EcoP-179_5, are highly specific and able to infect only the primary host, strain EC179.

Taxonomic and phylogenetic findings confirm that the investigated bacteriophages represent new species, thereby expanding current knowledge of anti-UPEC phage diversity. In total, five novel bacteriophage species were proposed in this study: Hanrivervirus vB_EcoS-149_4M, Warwickvirus vB_EcoS-149_6D, Nonavirus vB_EcoS-164_2, Kagunavirus vB_EcoS-179_3, and Vectrevirus vB_EcoP-179_5. All of them were clearly separated into distinct clusters, reflecting their pronounced genomic diversity and assignment to different phylogenetic lineages. Their identification enriches the pool of 98 anti-UPEC phages that we reviewed in our recent study93 however, the total number of known lytic phages capable of infecting uropathogenic bacteria is still insufficient with respect to the number and diversity of identified UPEC strains. A good example is the highly virulent EC256 strain investigated in this study, for which no bacteriophage could be identified.

The marked differences in susceptibility to the isolated bacteriophages among the analyzed UPEC strains appear to reflect differences in their genomic architecture. Despite belonging to the same bacterial species and, in some cases, forming closely related phylogenetic clusters, the strains exhibited distinct susceptibility patterns, as phages isolated on EC149 failed to infect EC164 and EC179, and vice versa (Figs. 2 and 3). This observation suggests that productive phage infection is primarily governed by strain-specific genomic determinants.

Comparative genomic analyses revealed considerable heterogeneity in genes associated with bacterial surface structures, including O-antigen-associated loci, surface polysaccharide biosynthesis genes, and fimbrial adhesins (Table 5). Since these structures determine the composition and accessibility of phage receptors, their differential distribution likely contributes to the observed variability in phage infectivity and host range. This interpretation is further supported by the broad host range of vB_EcoS-149_4M and the highly restricted activities of vB_EcoS-164_2, vB_EcoS-179_3, and vB_EcoP-179_5, as well as by the inability to isolate phages infecting EC3 and EC256 (Fig. 3).

In addition, differences in prophage content and CRISPR-Cas systems (Tables 6 and 7) may further modulate phage susceptibility through mechanisms such as superinfection exclusion, modifications of cell surface structures, or other defence functions encoded within mobile genetic elements. Collectively, our findings indicate that genomic heterogeneity among UPEC strains is accompanied by substantial variability in susceptibility to lytic bacteriophages, highlighting the importance of considering bacterial genomic diversity when developing precision phage therapy strategies against UPEC infections.

The combined bacterial and phage characterization highlights both the complexity of UPEC as a pathogen and the potential of well-selected bacteriophages as therapeutic tools. This broader study provides an essential groundwork for phage therapy research, as it not only identifies effective anti-UPEC phages but also includes their detailed assessments, highlighting their potential relevance for future applications. The efficacy of vB_EcoS-149_4M, in terms of host range, stability, and safety, positions it as a strong candidate for further preclinical development. In contrast, due to extremely high specificity toward one bacterial strain and sensitivity to most external factors, phages vB_EcoS-179_3 and vB_EcoP-179_5 do not seem to be appropriate for universal therapy, although they may be used in an individual approach. Such preclinical characterization is a critical step before moving toward in vivo testing and eventual clinical application to combat drug-resistant UPEC infections.

Overall, this research lays the groundwork for phage therapy against MDR UPEC infections by providing (i) a genomic insight into strain-specific determinants, (ii) a detailed preclinical assessment of candidates for therapeutic phages, (iii) evidence supporting the feasibility of integrating genomic data with phage selection to enhance therapeutic precision. Such an approach may have an impact not only on future UTI treatment protocols but also on broader phage therapy applications against other antibiotic-resistant bacterial pathogens.

Conclusions

Urinary tract infections caused by uropathogenic Escherichia coli remain a significant clinical challenge, largely due to the high genetic diversity, adaptive potential, and increasing antimicrobial resistance of these bacteria. The comparative genomic analysis performed in this study confirmed that UPEC strains possess a conserved core genome alongside highly dynamic mobile genetic elements, including plasmids and prophages. These elements not only contribute to virulence and environmental adaptability but also facilitate the dissemination of antibiotic resistance determinants, including those conferring resistance to last-resort drugs. The presence of numerous resistance-associated genes, even in phenotypically susceptible isolates, further emphasizes the hidden threat posed by these pathogens and highlights the limitations of conventional antibiotic-based therapies. Importantly, our findings demonstrate that integrating genomic insights with bacteriophage research provides a promising avenue for the development of alternative therapeutic strategies.

The combined genomic and phage-based approach presented here provides a solid foundation for future investigations and supports the potential implementation of precision phage therapy as an effective tool against multidrug-resistant UPEC pathogens.
