Future demand for visceral surgeons in Switzerland: an empirical study
Authors: Hans Grossen, Matthias Baechtold, Pia Antony, Pietro Renzulli, Fabian Hauswirth, Pascal Probst, Markus K. Muller
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
IntroductionSwitzerland’s aging population and evolving healthcare demands are expected to increase the need for specialized surgeons. Meanwhile, ongoing discussions about reduced work hours for residents and a growing trend toward minimally invasive and outpatient procedures may influence training opportunities and inpatient procedural volumes. The aim of this study was to evaluate workforce, training, and procedural volumes in surgery to determine the future demand for visceral surgeons in Switzerland.MethodsThis study analysed national datasets from 2009 to 2023 to assess trends in medical school enrolments, recognised medical diplomas, residency positions, and the availability of general and visceral surgeons. Additionally, surgical procedure volumes were evaluated with a particular focus on pancreatic surgery as a proxy for complex cases and appendectomies for simple procedures. Linear regression models projected developments up to 2040, incorporating Swiss population growth forecasts.ResultsThe number of medical diplomas - both Swiss-issued and foreign-recognized - showed an increase from 2,725 in 2011 to 4,647 in 2023. Projections indicate a further rise, reaching over 6,000 by 2040. Although total surgical residency positions declined slightly, spots dedicated to general surgery candidates grew by 8.7%. The overall pool of general surgeons increased from 1’127 in 2009 to 1’557 in 2023, with the proportion of women rising from 10.6 to 24.9% within the same interval. From 2013 to 2023, annual appendectomies increased from 11,529 to 12,412 (7.7% growth), and forecasts project a further rise of approximately 14% by 2040, although the procedures per surgeon are expected to decrease due to ongoing workforce expansion. Visceral surgeons numbered 303 by 2023 (11% female), and forecasts suggest a 50% increase to approximately 462 by 2040. Pancreatic surgery cases grew by 14.5% between 2016 and 2023 and are projected to increase by an additional 25% by 2040. However, the number of complex surgeries per visceral surgeon is projected to decline steadily over the next years.ConclusionsThe expansion of the surgical workforce is projected to overcompensate for the growth of Switzerland’s population. In combination with a shift towards outpatient care and reduced working hours, procedural exposure could become critical in general and visceral surgery. Ensuring that training pathways and practice models adapt to these evolving demands will be vital to maintaining high standards of surgical care.

Introduction

Switzerland’s healthcare system is internationally recognized for its high standard of care and comprehensive insurance coverage [1]. Nonetheless, shifts in the demographic landscape, primarily the aging population, along with the ensuing financial encumbrance, persist in imposing novel demands on the infrastructure [2, 3]. Gastrointestinal diseases, particularly prevalent in an aging population, are chief among the growing demands that make visceral surgery essential for addressing both acute and chronic conditions [4]. Epidemiological data indicate an increasing prevalence of these conditions, while parallel advances in minimally invasive and robotic techniques [5–7] require a surgical workforce capable of adapting to novel procedures. However, it remains uncertain whether current training programs and workforce planning will sufficiently meet future needs for specialized surgeons.

Concurrently, a discourse is underway in Switzerland concerning the working conditions in the medical profession, with a particular emphasis on the work schedules of resident doctors [8, 9]. The proposed 42 + 4-hour workweek [10, 11] has the potential to reduce workload; however, its feasibility in surgical specialties remains to be determined [12, 13].

A significant factor influencing surgical quality is the “volume–outcome relationship,” whereby higher procedural volumes correlate with better patient outcomes [14, 15]. This dynamic is especially relevant for visceral surgery, where many interventions are technically challenging, and repeated operative experience can refine surgical skills [16].

Against this backdrop, the present study evaluates the status of surgical education, newly conferred medical diplomas, and residency positions in Switzerland, as well as the current pool of general surgeons and surgeons specialized in visceral surgery.

Methods

This study analyzes the development of Switzerland’s medical workforce and surgical capacities, focusing on trends and numbers from education to specialization. Using data from 2009 to 2023, it examines medical diplomas, residency positions, and the supply of general and visceral surgeons, alongside surgical procedure volumes and population growth projections.

Data sources and timeframes

The datasets analysed in this study originate from various official sources, including the Federal Statistical Office, the Swiss Institute for Medical Education, the Swiss Medical Association, MedReg and versorgungsatlas.ch. As each database is managed independently, the availability and granularity of data vary across institutions. Consequently, the respective timeframes differ: data on medical diplomas were available from 2011 onward, appendectomy volumes from 2013, and pancreatic procedures from 2016 due to a change in classification within the HSM framework. Workforce data for general surgeons span from 2009, while data on visceral surgeons are available from 2010. These variations reflect both the historical evolution of data collection practices and regulatory shifts. While this heterogeneity may influence temporal comparability between variables, all available data were included to maximise the robustness and completeness of the trend analyses and projections.

Medical students

Enrollment in Swiss medical schools was analyzed over the period 2019–2024 using data provided by Swiss universities. In a survey conducted by the Swiss Medical Student Association in 2023, the intended specialization of medical students was surveyed.

Medical diplomas

In Switzerland, a federal diploma, or the recognition of a foreign diploma by the Medical Professions Commission (MEBEKO), is required to practice medicine under qualified supervision. The following data were analyzed in the study over the period from 2011 to 2023 using data from the Federal Statistical Office (Bundesamt für Statistik): the number of federal diplomas, the number of recognized foreign diplomas, the gender distribution, and the average age.

Residency

In Switzerland, the requisite qualification for independent practice is a Swiss postgraduate medical specialty or an equivalent foreign qualification recognized by the authority. In the field of surgery, this is equivalent to obtaining the title of general surgeon (Facharzt Chirurgie). The training period is six years and is completed in certified training centers, which are classified according to the maximum possible creditable training time (A = 4 years, B1 = 1 year, B2 = 2 years, B3 = 3 years).

The following data was obtained through the Swiss Institute for Medical Education (SIWF) over the period from 2017 to 2023: the total number of available surgical residency positions, the number of general surgery residency positions, origin of the medical diploma, and the workload of the positions offered.

General surgeons

The following data, provided by the Swiss Medical Association (FMH), were collected for the period 2009–2023: the number of practicing general surgeons, the gender distribution, and the number of newly awarded general surgeon titles of Swiss origin. The number of recognized general surgeon titles from foreign countries over the specified period was obtained from the Federal Statistical Office.

Specialty in visceral surgery

Upon certification as a general surgeon, one may pursue further specialization, leading to the acquisition of a specialization in visceral surgery (Schwerpunkttitel Viszeralchirurgie). In contrast to the title in general surgery, the specialization title in visceral surgery must be completed in Switzerland, and a foreign title cannot be recognized as such. All active surgeons with a specialization in visceral surgery, as of May 2023, were identified using the Medical Professional Register (MedReg). As of January 2018, all university-educated medical professionals in Switzerland are required to be registered in the MedReg.

The dataset includes information on surgeons specialised in visceral surgery, comprising the following variables: number of specialists, gender, year of birth, nationality, canton of professional licensure, country of medical degree acquisition, date of degree conferral, country of general surgery qualification, date of general surgery certification, and date of specialisation in visceral surgery. Furthermore, the number of physicians who have obtained certification in visceral surgery between the years 2010 and 2023 was identified through an analysis of the FMH physician statistics.

Surgical procedures

Data about surgical procedures was obtained from the Federal Statistical Office and versorgungsatlas.ch.

All appendectomies performed in Switzerland were documented for the 11-year period between 2013 and 2023.

Furthermore, all pancreatic operations classified as highly specialized medicine (HSM) were identified using the CHOP code. Due to changes in the methodology employed for data collection, the data were only evaluated between 2016 and 2023.

Population data

The population of Switzerland for the years 2009 to 2023, as of December 31, was obtained from the Federal Statistical Office. Furthermore, a forecast on population development for the years 2024 to 2040 was obtained from the Federal Statistical Office.

Statistical analysis

All data obtained were first presented descriptively. A regression analysis was performed with a 95% confidence interval up to the year 2040 for the following datasets: number of awarded or recognized medical diplomas, number of general surgeons and number of visceral surgeons. The population growth forecast obtained from the Swiss Federal Statistical Office was used to define the capita per medical diplomas, the capita per general surgeon and the capita per visceral surgeon. It was also used to predict future numbers of operations pertaining surgical procedures within the HSM. Statistical analysis was conducted using Microsoft Excel and RStudio.

Results

The results show a steady increase in medical school enrollment, diplomas, and the number of general and visceral surgeons between 2009 and 2023. Projections extending to 2040 suggest the persistence of this growth trajectory, with shifts in gender distribution and specialization trends. Population development and surgical procedures, including appendectomies and highly specialized pancreatic surgery also exhibit consistent, but more moderate growth.

Medical students

Since 2019, the number of students starting medical school in the first year of the bachelor’s degree has increased from 2057 to 2195 (+ 138 or + 6.7%) in 2024. Over the same period, the number of places available in the first year of the master’s degree has also risen from 1163 to 1470 (+ 307 or + 26.4%).

In a 2023 survey of 2,292 Swiss medical students (1,246 enrolled in the bachelor’s program and 1,046 enrolled in the master’s program), 819 students (35.7%) indicated that they aspired to a career in a surgical specialty. The proportion of bachelor’s students (519, or 41.7%) was higher than that of master’s students (300, or 28.7%). It should be noted that the surgical specialties were not subdivided.

Medical diplomas

In the year 2023, a total of 4,647 diplomas in the field of human medicine were either issued or recognized. A total of 1,284 graduates (28%) received Swiss diplomas. The proportion of women in the Swiss cohort was 62%, the average age was 27 years. Additionally, 3,363 (72%) foreign diplomas were recognized, with women accounting for 53% of this cohort and an average age of 35 years.

In 2011, a total of 2,725 diplomas were recognized, of which 795 (29%) were of Swiss origin and 1,930 (71%) were of foreign origin. At that time, the proportion of women was 58% for Swiss diplomas and 48% for foreign diplomas.

Residency

A total of 86 surgical training centers are currently in operation within the Swiss healthcare system. Of these, 22 are designated as category A, 13 as category B1, 32 as category B2, and 19 as category B3.

The total number of positions offered exhibited a slight decline, from 1,148 in 2017 to 1,042 in 2023, representing a 9.2% reduction. However, it should be noted that not all positions are designed for specialization in surgery. In fact, positions for general surgery candidates demonstrated an increase from 2017 (589 positions) to 2023 (640 positions), representing an 8.7% growth.

In 2017, 48.5% (557/1,148) of positions were filled by residents with a Swiss federal diploma. In 2023, this proportion increased to 51% (532/1,042). The workload remained relatively consistent. Nearly all positions were occupied by full time employees. In 2017, the proportion of full-time employees was 98.3% (1,128/1,148), and in 2023 it was 95.3% (999/1,042).

General surgeons

In 2023, a total of 1,557 individuals in Switzerland held the title of general surgeon. Of these, 387 (24.9%) were female and 1,170 (75.1%) were male. In 2009, there were 1,127 general surgeons, of whom 120 (10.6%) were female and 1,007 (89.4%) were male. Over this period, this corresponds to an increase of 430 surgeons, representing a 38.2% growth in the total number of surgeons. The female population increased by 267, which corresponds to a 222.5% growth in the number of female surgeons. The male population increased by 163, which corresponds to a 16.2% growth in the number of male surgeons.

In 2009, individuals who obtained their certification in general surgery in Switzerland made up 62% (54/87) while 38% (33/87) had their foreign certification recognized. By 2013, the percentage of foreign-recognized surgeons had risen to 61% (70/115). In recent years, the distribution has become more balanced, with 52% (55/106) of general surgeons obtaining their license in Switzerland in 2023.

The forecast shown in Fig. 2 projected an increase in the number of general surgeons from the observed data in 2023 to approximately 2,172 surgeons (95%-CI: 2,091 to 2,253) by the year 2040. The model estimates an annual increase of approximately 33.35 surgeons per year, which is consistent with the historical trend observed from 2009 to 2023. The linear regression model demonstrates a robust correlation with the historical data, with an R-squared value of 0.98, indicating that 98% of the observed variation in the number of surgeons over time is explained by the model.Fig. 2Forecast of general surgeons

Specialty in visceral surgery

As of May 2023, there are 303 individuals who have been awarded a specialization in visceral surgery. A total of 270 individuals (89%) are male, while 33 (11%) are female. The mean age is 57.4 years with a standard deviation of 9.6 years. Their age range is between 41 and 80 years. The male group exhibited a higher average age (58.6 years, SD 9.5 years) than the female group (51.6 years, SD 9.0 years).

The majority of these individuals (262/303; 86%) are Swiss citizens, with three individuals holding dual citizenship. The next largest group is from Germany (26/303; 8.5%), followed by Italy (5/303; 1.7%). It is notable that other nationalities, such as Austria or Greece, are only found in isolated cases.

With regard to professional practice permits, 198 individuals (51.5%) were issued permits in German-speaking Switzerland, 90 (23.4%) in French-speaking Switzerland, 20 in Italian-speaking Switzerland, and 76 in bilingual cantons (French- and German-speaking). The overall increase in numbers can be attributed to multiple professional practice permits for individual persons.

A total of 255 (84.1%) individuals received their medical diploma in Switzerland, 29 (9.6%) in Germany, 7 (2.3%) in Italy and 5 (1.7%) in Austria. The remaining countries were represented only in isolated cases. The mean age at the time of licensing was 26.7 years (SD 2.2 years), with a range of 24 to 42 years.

A total of 280 (92.4%) individuals received their certification in general surgery in Switzerland, 16 (5.2%) in Germany, 3 (0.9%) in Austria, followed by Italy (2/303; 0.7%) and Romania and Slovakia (1/303; 0.3%, each). The mean age at the time of acquiring the title of general surgeon was 35.4 years (SD 2.9 years). The mean time span between medical diploma and certification was 8.6 years (SD 2.9 years).

The mean age at which a specialization in visceral surgery was obtained was 44.5 years (SD 5.3 years), with a range of 36 to 61 years. The mean time between becoming a general surgeon and obtaining specialization was 9.2 years (SD 4.9 years). The mean time between receiving a medial diploma and specialization was 17.9 years (SD 5.5 years).

Outpatient procedures

Of particular interest is the sharp increase in outpatient procedures, shown in Fig. 5. They have been recorded in total numbers since 2015, but not disaggregated. In 2015, there were 3,442,408 outpatient procedures; in 2022, this figure rose to 4,746,170, representing a 37.9% growth.Fig. 5Outpatient procedures between 2015 and 2022

Outpatient procedures between 2015 and 2022

This development is not only the result of surgical and technological advances but is also driven by health policy. Since January 1, 2019, the Swiss Federal Office of Public Health has enforced the “ambulant vor stationär” (“outpatient before inpatient”) regulation, mandating that selected procedures be performed on an outpatient basis to qualify for reimbursement under compulsory health insurance, unless strict medical exceptions apply. The original list included six procedure groups and was expanded in January 2023 to cover 18 categories [17]. These legal requirements have strongly incentivized the shift toward outpatient care, significantly contributing to the increase shown in Fig. 5.

Although minor fluctuations were observed on an annual basis, including a slight dip in 2017, the overall trajectory remained upward, with a particularly notable rise between 2019 (12,230 procedures) and 2020 (12,727 procedures). Discussion

This empirical study provides a timely assessment of the Swiss surgical workforce, highlighting significant developments in medical education, residency training, and the availability of specialized visceral surgeons. The findings indicate a sustained increase in the total number of medical diplomas and the number of residency positions dedicated to general surgery, alongside a growing pool of board-certified general and visceral surgeons. However, these trends give rise to significant concerns, including the potential challenges and implications for the future of surgical care. Specifically, despite the expansion of the surgical workforce, there has been an inadequate increase in the volume of surgical procedures, both common (e.g., appendectomies) and complex (e.g., pancreatic surgeries), which underscores the need for further investigation and potential solutions.

Rising supply of surgeons amid changing demographics

In contrast to the United States, where there is a shortage of physicians—particularly general surgeons [18]—Switzerland’s medical workforce has been steadily expanding. The ongoing increase in medical study programs, coupled with a corresponding rise in the number of diplomas awarded, indicates that Switzerland’s healthcare labor pipeline will remain strong in both the near and intermediate future. Linear regression forecasts indicate that, by 2040, Switzerland may see a more than 80% increase in the number of medical diplomas per capita compared to 2011 levels. This growth in the number of newly qualified doctors could mitigate concerns over physician shortages and address rising healthcare demands driven by an aging population. It is noteworthy that while the absolute numbers of Swiss and foreign diplomas have increased substantially, the ratio between Swiss and foreign-trained doctors has remained relatively stable throughout the observed period, hovering around 30% for Swiss diplomas and 70% for foreign-recognized diplomas in 2023. While international graduates contribute to meeting immediate workforce needs, their long-term retention and integration remain vital considerations for ensuring continuity of care.

While this study focuses on absolute surgeon headcounts, it is increasingly important to distinguish between nominal workforce size and actual clinical capacity. Recent surveys indicate that younger generations of physicians, both male and female, are more likely to prefer part-time work arrangements, motivated by work-life balance, family responsibilities, and personal well-being [19]. This shift is particularly relevant in surgery, where the traditional expectation of full-time clinical intensity may no longer align with evolving career preferences.

At the same time, the gender distribution within surgery is undergoing significant transformation. The proportion of female general surgeons has risen from 10.6% in 2009 to nearly 25% in 2023, and women now represent the majority of medical students in Switzerland. Although visceral surgery remains predominantly male, this too is expected to shift over time. Given that part-time employment is more frequently adopted by female physicians, particularly during child-rearing years, this demographic change may further increase the prevalence of reduced clinical workloads.

Together, these generational and gender-driven trends suggest that headcount-based projections may overestimate future workforce capacity.

Outlook for surgical residency and training

Notwithstanding a slight decrease in total surgical residency positions from 2017 to 2023, the number of designated posts for those pursuing a specialization in general surgery increased by 8.7%. This trend is indicative of initiatives aimed at aligning resident opportunities with future demands for surgical care. However, the introduction and potential extension of a 42 + 4-hour workweek for residents raises concerns regarding the preservation of adequate operative exposure [13]. Surgery is a highly technical field, and competency is highly dependent on the volume–outcome relationship, with repeated hands-on practice being essential for skill acquisition [20, 21]. A reduction in work hours, without concomitant educational innovation, may potentially compromise the depth and breadth of training. Additionally, the ratio of surgeons to the population is projected to rise, meaning a finite number of procedures will be distributed among a larger pool of practitioners—further limiting the average number of cases per surgeon and constraining operative experience, particularly for trainees.

Visceral surgery: supply and demand

Projections indicate that the number of visceral surgeons will grow by approximately 50% by 2040. This projection is predicated on the assumption that the aging population and the prevalence of complex gastrointestinal pathologies will result in an increased demand for surgical interventions. However, the analysis also indicates that advances in minimally invasive and robotic technologies are altering the landscape of surgical practice [22, 23]. The relatively modest increase in the demand for inpatient procedures, particularly in light of the growing trend toward outpatient surgeries, gives rise to questions regarding the potential outcomes of the ongoing increase in visceral surgeons. Specifically, there is a concern that this increase may not result in a balanced surgeon-to-patient ratio, or, in certain geographic regions, may lead to a state of overcapacity. In particular, an examination of appendectomy trends underscores this concern. While the total number of procedures increased by only about 7.7% from 2013 (11,529 procedures) to 2023 (12,412 procedures), the total number of surgeons increased by nearly 22.9% over the same period (from 1,267 to 1,557). This discrepancy in growth rates resulted in a decline in the average number of appendectomies per surgeon per year from 9.1 to 7.97 (a 12.4% decrease). Projections suggest that this trend will continue: although the volume of appendectomies is expected to increase from 12,846 in 2024 to 14,690 in 2040 (an increase of 14.3%), the number of surgeons is expected to grow more rapidly (from 1,638 to 2,172). As a result, the number of procedures per surgeon is expected to continue to decline.

Another notable trend emerged from the analysis of pancreatic surgeries within highly specialized medicine: a steady annual growth of approximately 1.4%, driven by demographic aging and broader indications for complex procedures. Despite this increase, the number of pancreatic surgeries per visceral surgeon is projected to decline, reflecting a disproportionately rapid expansion of the specialized surgical workforce. This trend suggests a diminishing operative caseload per surgeon, which may dilute technical experience and challenge the maintenance of procedural proficiency.

High procedural volume has been consistently associated with better surgical outcomes in complex domains such as pancreatic surgery. For instance, German registry data have demonstrated significantly lower morbidity and mortality rates in high-volume centers compared to low-volume hospitals [14]. Considering the forecasted case dilution, further regionalization of complex surgical care may be warranted. Designating high-volume reference centers and introducing minimum case thresholds could help consolidate expertise, preserve quality, and optimize resource allocation.

In addition to demographic and procedural factors, recent regulatory developments may significantly influence future workforce growth. Since the amendment of Article 55a of the Swiss Health Insurance Act in July 2021, cantons have been authorised to implement regional quotas limiting the number of outpatient physicians per specialty [24]. Several cantons—such as both Basel—have already introduced such caps, which apply to various surgical specialties [25]. These measures are intended to control healthcare costs and prevent regional oversupply. However, they may also restrict future growth of the surgical workforce, particularly in urban areas with already high provider density. The long-term impact of these quota systems remains uncertain.

Impact of outpatient shifts

A notable finding is the substantial increase in outpatient procedures, which rose by nearly 38% from 2015 to 2022. This shift is likely attributable to advancements in minimally invasive surgery, the adoption of “fast-track” recovery protocols, and modifications in healthcare reimbursement. While outpatient approaches have been shown to improve patient satisfaction, reduce costs, and optimize hospital resources, they also alter the overall volume of inpatient surgical training cases. This development raises concerns that surgical cases may increasingly be shifted away from teaching hospitals to outpatient clinics, potentially limiting training opportunities.

Limitations

Several limitations merit attention when interpreting these findings. First, although the data included a comprehensive range of sources (e.g., Federal Statistical Office, SIWF, FMH), small discrepancies may exist due to reporting delays, classification changes, or incomplete data capture. Second, the study’s regression models assume linear growth based on current and historical patterns, which may not account for policy changes, economic influences, or unforeseen shifts in surgical demand (e.g., abrupt changes in migration patterns, technological disruptions, or health crises). Appendectomies as a general surgical procedure and pancreatic surgery in the highly specialized field served only as a proxy and may not reflect the real development of all operative procedures in the coming years. Furthermore, the requirements for obtaining the specialty title in visceral surgery have been reduced in recent years, which may cause the projection to overestimate future growth. Finally, variables such as regional disparities, urban–rural divides, and the precise impact of new work-hour regulations were beyond the scope of the present analysis.

Conclusions

This study projects a steady increase in the number of general and visceral surgeons in Switzerland. This increase is driven by two factors: growing medical school enrollments and the ongoing influx of foreign-trained physicians. While this trend may assuage concerns regarding a potential shortage of surgeons, it gives rise to inquiries concerning adequate procedural exposure and the maintenance of high-quality care, particularly in technically demanding domains such as visceral surgery, where the volume-outcome relationship is well-established. Furthermore, the transition toward outpatient settings and the proposal of reduced resident work hours may limit operative opportunities for trainees. Finally, while there has been an improvement in the representation of female surgeons in general surgery, visceral surgery remains heavily male-dominated, underscoring the need for efforts to promote diversity. These findings underscore the necessity of continuous monitoring of workforce development, refinement of training curricula, and ensuring adequate case volume for surgeons to preserve Switzerland’s high standard of surgical care.
