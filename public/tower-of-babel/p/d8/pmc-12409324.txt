PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial homeostasis imbalance aggravated gingival lesions of diabetic periodontitis by disrupting ECM remodeling of human gingival fibroblast
Authors: Junling Huang, Yi Li, Qingyuan Ye, Rui Li, Yazheng Wang, Qintao Wang, Jinjin Wang
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Diabetic periodontitis (DP) usually has more severe clinical symptoms compared with chronic periodontitis (CP), especially the worsened gingival lesions. Mitochondrial homeostasis plays an important role in both diabetes and periodontitis, and some studies have already confirmed its role in DP. However, there are few reports on whether mitochondrial homeostasis is involved in regulating DP gingival lesions. In this study, we focused on the pathological changes of the gingival connective tissues from DP and identified the extracellular matrix (ECM) pathway as the key regulatory pathway. Additionally, we found that the pathological changes in DP gingiva were accompanied by significant alterations in the morphology and structure of cellular mitochondria. We discovered that the regulatory molecule PGC-1α related to mitochondrial quality control (MQC) was significantly downregulated in DP group through RNA sequencing. Furthermore, by using high glucose and LPS co-stimulation to simulate DP environment in vitro, we confirmed the changes in ECM synthesis and remodeling of human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs), accompanied with the abnormal mitochondrial morphology, structure and function, including the presence of internal vacuolation, the increased area and perimeter, the loss of cristae, as well as the increased mtROS, the decreased ATP, mitochondrial membrane potential, and the mtDNA copy number, and even the reduced number of mitochondria in HGFs, which suggesting the changes of mitochondrial homeostasis. Moreover, we verified that upregulating PGC-1α could reverse the above phenomena in HGFs. Finally, periodontal injection of PGC-1α agonists (ZLN005) was found to ameliorate the abnormal ECM synthesis and remodeling by improving mitochondrial homeostasis in DP rats, demonstrating a significant therapeutic effect on DP gingival lesions and moderating the progression of DP. In conclusion, our study proposed the possible therapeutic effect of PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial homeostasis in the aggravated gingival lesions of DP, providing new ideas for the clinical treatment of DP.

Introduction

Periodontitis is a chronic inflammatory disease characterized by progressive destruction of the periodontal supporting tissues, affecting approximately 62 % of dentate adults [1]. Periodontitis is closely associated with various systemic diseases, among which the two-way relationship between periodontitis and diabetes has been widely studied [2]. Clinical studies reveal that diabetes could exacerbate the destruction of periodontal soft tissues, as reflected in the 0.61 mm deeper periodontal pocket and the 0.89 mm higher attachment loss than those without diabetes [3]. Moreover, the reduced number of human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs) and the decreased secretion level of collagens, accompanied by the elevated catabolism of extracellular matrix (ECM) could be found in the gingival connective tissues of diabetic periodontitis (DP) [4].

Together with gingival epithelium, the gingival connective tissues also serve as a crucial barrier for the host to resist microbial infections [5]. The destruction of gingival connective tissues is a key factor in the progression of periodontal diseases, while the resident cells in gingival connective tissues play vital roles in maintaining tissue homeostasis and regulating immune responses [6,7]. HGFs, the dominant cells in the gingival connective tissues, serve not only as sentinel cells for innate immune responses, but also as main regulators of ECM remodeling in gingival connective tissues [8]. Dysfunctional HGFs could exacerbate periodontal inflammation by oversecreting matrix metalloproteinases (MMPs), excessively recruiting leukocytes, and inducing osteoclastogenesis [7], which results in severe gingival lesions, including bleeding, swelling and fragility of gingival connective tissues. Clinically, we found that DP patients often had worse periodontal indicators and more deteriorated gingival lesions, which might be associated with the disordered ECM remodeling. However, the alterations of HGFs functions and its mechanisms in DP still remain unclear.

Mitochondria are essential organelles for regulating cellular functions. Under physiological conditions, the quantity, quality and internal environment of mitochondria are maintained in a relatively stable state, which is known as mitochondrial homeostasis [9]. A large number of studies have confirmed that the disruption in mitochondrial homeostasis was closely associated with diabetes and periodontitis. For example, the imbalanced mitochondrial homeostasis could exacerbate insulin resistance and trigger diabetes [10]; Meanwhile, in periodontitis, most studies focused on the relationship between the imbalanced mitochondrial homeostasis and the abnormal cellular functions of periodontal ligament stem cells (PDLSCs), such as the decreased osteogenesis, the increased apoptosis and son on [11,12]. Furthermore, the abnormalities of mitochondrial structure and function have also been found in HGFs of periodontitis [13]. All the above-mentioned studies have proved that mitochondrial homeostasis has an important influence on the function of periodontal tissue cells in periodontitis environment. However, when diabetes and periodontitis co-occur, what happens to mitochondrial homeostasis in HGFs is unknown, and the role of mitochondrial homeostasis in regulating the abnormal function of HGFs in diabetic periodontitis deserve further study.

Based on the above findings, this study aims to investigate the relationship between mitochondrial homeostasis and physiological functions of HGFs associated with DP. Particularly, we will focus on the potential effects of mitochondrial homeostasis alterations on the collagen metabolism associated with ECM remodeling in HGFs, aiming to provide experimental bases for treating DP.

Materials and methods

Collection of human gingival connective tissue samples

The study was approved by the Ethics Committee of the Third Affiliated Hospital of Air Force Military Medical University (KQ-YJ-2023-181). Gingival connective tissues were collected from patients undergoing periodontal surgery at the Department of Periodontology, School of Stomatology, Air Force Medical University. All subjects were provided written informed consent before being recruited in the study. Gingival connective tissues from healthy control group (HC) were collected undergoing crown lengthening surgery, while tissues from chronic periodontitis group (CP) and DP group were collected during flap surgeries. Inclusion and exclusion criteria, along with clinical characteristics of the subjects are shown in Tables S1 and S2.

HE and Masson staining

Human gingival connective tissues were fixed in 4 % paraformaldehyde for 48 h. Subsequently, the samples undergone tissue dehydration, paraffin embedding, and sectioning (4 μm thickness). The sections were stained following the protocols of the HE Staining Kit (Solarbio, China) and the Masson's Trichrome Staining Kit (Solarbio, China). Images were captured with a slide scanner microscope (Olympus VS200, Japan).

RNA sequencing

After obtaining the human gingiva, the epithelium was carefully removed, then the gingival connective tissues preserved in RNA Stabilization Reagent (Beyotime, China) were sent to Lianchuan Biotechnology (Hangzhou, China) for RNA sequencing. Once outlier samples were excluded based on the Pearson correlation analysis and PCA analysis, DESeq2 tool was used to identify differentially expressed genes (DEGs) with the thresholds of Fold Change >1.5 and P < 0.05.

Transmission electron microscope (TEM) observation

The cell and tissue samples were fixed in 2.5 % glutaraldehyde for 48 h. Then the samples were prepared into ultra-thin sections (approximately 70–100 nm thickness) following the method described by Liu et al. [13]. The morphology and structure of mitochondria were observed by TEM (Tecnai G2, USA). The morphology and quantity of mitochondria were semi-quantitatively analyzed by ImageJ software [14].

Primary culture and stimulation of human gingival fibroblasts (HGFs)

HGFs were extracted from the gingival connective tissues of healthy subjects as previous study reported [15]. HGFs were cultured in Minimum Essential Medium-α (MEM-α, Pricella, China) containing 10 % serum and used for experiments between passages 3 and 8. HGFs were divided into four groups [1]: negative control group (NC), with cells cultured in medium with no treatment [2]. LPS stimulation simulated periodontitis group (LPS), with cells cultured in medium containing 5 μg/mL porphyromonas gingivalis lipopolysaccharide (Pg. LPS, InvivoGen, USA) for 24 h [3]. high glucose group (HG) with cells cultured in medium containing 50 mM glucose (Sigma, USA) for 24 h [4]. dual stimulation group (HG + LPS) with cells cultured in medium with 5 μg/mL Pg. LPS and 50 mM glucose for 24 h. Methods for the characterization of HGFs and the selection of stimulation concentration were described in Supplementary Materials.

Scratch assay

A region approximately 1 mm wide was scratched across the monolayer cell using the tip of a 200 μL pipette. The cell debris was removed using PBS, and the wound healing process was observed under an inverted microscope at each time point. Cell migration rate was analyzed by ImageJ software.

Cell viability

HGFs were incubated with Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK 8, InCellGen, USA) for 2 h, and the supernatant was absorbed and measured for absorbance at 450 nm using a microplate reader.

Sirius red staining

After being fixed with 2.5 % glutaraldehyde overnight, HGFs were stained with Sirius red (LEAGENE, China) for 1.5 h. 0.01 mol/L hydrochloric acid was used to wash the unbound dye. Subsequently, HGFs were observed and photographed under stereo microscope after natural drying. 0.1 mol/L sodium hydroxide was used to elute the bound dye, and the supernatant was aspirated and measured for absorbance at 540 nm using a microplate reader.

Mitochondrial function assays

Mitochondrial ATP levels in HGFs were measured following the protocols of the ATP assay kit (Beyotime, China). Total DNA from HGFs was extracted by TIANamp Genomic DNA Kit (TIANGEN, China) and the relative abundance of the mitochondrial Cytochrome b gene (Ctyb) to the nuclear gene (Beta2-microglobulin) was determined by qRT-PCR. The relative mitochondrial DNA (mtDNA) copy number was calculated as the study reported [16]. HGFs were incubated with 5 μM MitoSOXTMRed (Invitrogen, USA) for 30 min to evaluate the mitochondrial reactive oxygen species (mtROS) level. HGFs were incubated with 200 nM Mito-Tracker (Beyotime, China) for 20 min or 200 nM tetramethylrhodamine methyl ester (TMRM, Invitrogen, USA) for 30 min to evaluate mitochondrial membrane potential. Images were observed and captured by confocal laser scanning microscope (CLSM, Olympus, Japan) and fluorescence intensity were semi-quantitatively analyzed by ImageJ software.

Cell transfection

The PGC-1α overexpression plasmid and its empty vector were transferred into HGFs using Lipofectamine3000 (Invitrogen, USA), respectively. HGFs were divided into NC group (transfected with empty vector) and OV group (transfected with PGC-1α overexpression plasmid). The transfection medium was replaced with fresh medium after transfection for 6 h and HGFs were stimulated with 5 μg/mL Pg. LPS and 50 mM glucose after transfection for 24 h.

Real-time quantitative reverse transcription polymerase chain reaction (RT-qPCR)

RNA from tissues and cells was extracted with TRIzol (Accurate Biology, China), and reverse transcribed with M-MLV reverse transcription kit (Accurate Biology, China). RT-qPCR was carried out using the SYBR Green Pro Taq HS Premix (Accurate Biology, China). Data was analyzed using 2-△△CT. The primer sequences are listed in Table S3.

Western blot

Proteins from tissues and cells were harvested using RIPA (Beyotime, China), and quantified by BCA Protein Assay Kit (Beyotime, China). Proteins were electrophoresed in the SDS-PAGE buffer for 1.5 h, then transferred to polyvinylidenefluoride membranes (Millipore, USA). The membranes were further blocked by QuickBlock buffer (Beyotime, China) for 30 min, then incubated with primary antibody working solution at 4 °C overnight. After being incubated with secondary antibody working solution at room temperature for 2 h, the membranes were visualized by using the ECL substrate (AccuRef, China).

Construction and identification of diabetic rat model

The study was approved by the Animal Ethics Committee of the Air Force Military Medical University (IACUC-20241486). 4-week-old Sprague-Dawley male rats were randomly divided into the following 4 groups (n = 6): (1) healthy control group (HC) with no intervention and normal diet. (2) chronic periodontitis group (CP) with the second molar (M2) of maxilla ligatured by 0.2 mm wire for 2 w and normal diet [17]. (3) diabetes mellitus group (DM) with a high-fat and high-glucose diet for 4 w and then single intraperitoneal injection of streptozotocin (STZ, 35 mg/kg, Solarbio, China). 7 d after STZ administration, rats with fasting blood glucose (FBG) ≥16.7 mmol/L were identified as DM rats [18]. (4) diabetic periodontitis group (DP) with diabetes model and chronic periodontitis model established continuously (the method is the same as above), and the time interval was 2 w [19].

Micro-computed tomography (Micro-CT) scanning

After being fixed with 4 % paraformaldehyde for 48 h, the rat maxillae were scanned by Micro-CT (Siemens, Germany). Mimics software was used to reconstruct and analyze the distance from the cement-enamel junction (CEJ) to the alveolar bone crest (ABC) in M2. Regions of interest (ROI) were defined by the interproximal area between the first and second molars, starting coronally by a line drawn between the adjacent molar furcation and continuing in an apical direction. The bone volume fraction (BV/TV) and the bone mineral density (BMD) of ROI were further determined.

Animal treatment

ZLN005 (MedChemExpress, USA), an agonist of PGC-1α, was diluted to 0.1 mg/mL working solution with corn oil and used for subsequent experiments. DP rats were randomly divided into two groups (n = 6). (1) experimental group (DP + ZLN005), approximately a total of 30 μL ZLN005 working solution was injected into the periodontal pocket of M2 during the process of periodontitis model modeling. (2) control group (DP), equal volume of corn oil was injected at the same sites of M2. The wire was removed after 2 w, and the periodontal pocket depth of the proximal, the middle and the distal sites of palatal side of M2 was determined by a periodontal probe. Subsequently, a vernier caliper was used to accurately read the marker. Subsequent experiments were performed according to the methods described in 2.2, 2.4, 2.11, 2.12 and 2.14.

Statistical analysis

Data are shown as mean ± SD and analyzed with GraphPad Prism 8 software. Student's t tests were used to analyze the differences between two groups, while one-way ANOVA, followed by Tukey's post hoc test, were used for comparisons of more than two groups. The experiments were repeated at least 3 times and p value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Observation of human gingival characteristics from diabetic periodontitis and screening of key molecules involved in the gingival lesions

Intraoral photographs of clinical subjects showed the different characteristics, such as the color, shape and texture of human gingiva in different groups. Compared with HC group, the gingiva in CP and DP were almost abnormal, with dark red color, swelling and soft texture. However, under the same stage and grade periodontitis (Stage III, Grade C), the gingiva in DP group had severe lesions, including softer, more enlarged and frailty, lacking elasticity, with marked congestion and edema at the gingival margin and gingival papillae (Fig. 1A). HE and Masson staining revealed that there were almost no inflammatory cells in HC group, while the collagen fibers were abundant and arranged in a typical wavy pattern. In contrast, a substantial number of inflammatory cells, along with reduced and disordered collagen fibers were found in both CP and DP groups, and especially more pronounced in DP group (Fig. 1B and C). RNA sequencing was used to explore the key molecules affecting the above-mentioned gingival lesions. After removing the outlier samples, a good intra-group correlation was existed in the remaining samples (Fig. S1A), and there was a substantial number of DEGs among HC, CP and DP groups (Fig. S1B). The results of GO and KEGG enrichment showed that there were a large number of DEGs enriched in ECM-related terms and pathways (Fig. 1D, marked with red box). These results confirmed our previous hypothesis that the abnormal ECM remodeling was associated with gingival lesions in DP group. Furthermore, the sequencing data indicated that in the molecules associated with ECM remodeling, MMP1, MMP3, TIMP1 and COL1A1, which involved in collagen metabolism, were significantly different among groups (Fig. 1E). High glucose and LPS stimulation were used to simulate diabetic periodontitis to investigate its effect on the morphology and function of HGFs mitochondria

Semi-quantitative analysis of mitochondrial morphological parameters indicated that the number of mitochondria was significantly reduced, while the area, perimeter, long axis and short axis of the mitochondria were increased in stimulation groups (Fig. 4B); Besides, compared with other groups, mitochondria in HG + LPS group displayed a marked reduction in quantity alongside significant enlargement in morphological parameters, which was consistent with the histological results in 3.2. CLSM and the semi-quantitative analysis of fluorescence intensity demonstrated that the HG + LPS stimulation induced the highest level of mtROS (Fig. 4C and D), coupled with the lowest levels of mitochondrial ATP (Fig. 4E), mtDNA copy number (Fig. 4F) and mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 4G and H). Overexpression of PGC-1α could reverse the dysfunction of ECM remodeling of HGFs by alleviating oxidative stress and improving mitochondrial homeostasis under high glucose and LPS stimulation

Following transfection with PGC-1α overexpression plasmid (OV), HGFs exhibited stable and elevated expression of PGC-1α (Fig. S4A–C). Moreover, a decreased level of mtROS (Fig. 5A and B) and an increased level of mitochondrial ATP (Fig. 5C), mtDNA copy number (Fig. 5D) and mitochondrial membrane potential (Fig. 5E and F), which indicating improved mitochondria function, were observed in HGFs in OV group under HG + LPS stimulation. Besides, the mRNA and protein levels of SOD2, an important scavenger of mitochondrial ROS, was significantly increased in OV group, while the protein level of acetylated SOD2 (ac-SOD2) was significantly decreased (Fig. 5G and H). The ratio of ac-SOD2/SOD2 was lower in OV group, indicating that the oxidative stress was mitigated through overexpression of PGC-1α under HG + LPS stimulation (Fig. 5I). We further observed that, under stimulation with HG + LPS, the mRNA and protein levels of MMP1 and MMP3 were markedly reduced in OV group, yet the expression of TIMP1 and COL1A1 were significantly increased, which suggesting the effective improvement in collagen metabolism disorders (Fig. 5J–L). Sirius red staining provided a visual confirmation of the enhanced collagen secretion capacity of HGFs in OV group (Fig. 5M and N). Vernier caliper was used to measure the periodontal probing depth in both groups, and we found that ZLN005 could largely reduce the periodontal probing depth (0.82 ± 0.08 mm vs 0.54 ± 0.07 mm), and the difference was statistically significant (Fig. 7B). Discussion

Diabetes could significantly increase the risk of periodontitis and accelerate the destruction of periodontal tissues [3,20]. The mechanisms might be that diabetes could increase oral microbiota pathogenicity, osteoclast activity, and inflammatory factor secretion [[21], [22], [23]]. Moreover, researches indicated that diabetes could synergize with periodontal inflammatory stimuli, leading to an imbalance in ECM remodeling, usually with excessive ECM degradation [24,25]. Our sequencing results also verified that the molecules related to ECM remodeling were significantly different in gingival connective tissues from DP, suggesting that the abnormal ECM remodeling may constitute a critical pathogenic driver of aggravated gingival lesions in DP.

ECM serves as a platform for intercellular and host-microbiota communication. It is constantly remodeling in response to external stimuli [26]. ECM remodeling maintains balanced during physiological conditions: ECM synthesis is mediated by growth factors, and enzymes such as adamalysins, meprins, and MMPs are responsible for ECM degradation [27]. Collagens are the most abundant proteins of the ECM, which could be degraded by the overactive MMPs, thus leading to the destruction of gingival connective tissues [28]. Collagen metabolism is a crucial process in ECM remodeling, which is closely related to the pathogenesis of periodontitis [25,29]. In this study, we revealed that when diabetes and periodontitis con-occur, the aggravated gingival lesions such as fragile and lack elasticity gingiva, and disordered collagen fibers were found in DP; Meanwhile, the MMP1 and MMP3 were significantly upregulated, yet the TIMP1 and COL1A1 were significantly downregulated. All these results suggesting that the collagen metabolism associated with ECM remodeling was disrupted in gingival connective tissues from DP. Furthermore, the in vitro experiments also confirmed that HG + LPS stimulation could seriously affect the expression of molecules related to ECM remodeling and disrupt the collagen metabolism, which result in the abnormalities of ECM remodeling in HGFs.

In addition to providing the necessary mechanical and communicational support for cells, ECM was also a reservoir of signaling molecules, and mitochondria was functioning as a signaling hub within the cell, which responded to various signaling events from ECM([30]). The ECM remodeling could induce mitochondrial homeostasis imbalance through the TGF-β signaling pathway [26]. Unquestionably, maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis also assumes paramount significance in preserving normal cellular functions, including ECM synthesis and remodeling [31]. It is worth noting that the imbalanced mitochondrial homeostasis has been considered to be closely related with many diseases, including diabetes and periodontitis [32,33]. Moreover, when diabetes and periodontitis coexist, the imbalance of mitochondrial homeostasis has also been strongly confirmed in our study, which was consistent with the findings of Sun et al. [34].

Mitochondrial homeostasis is maintained by mitochondrial quality control (MQC), including mitochondrial biogenesis, mitochondrial dynamics, mitophagy, etc., and a variety of molecules are involved [35]. Abnormalities in MQC can lead to an imbalance in mitochondrial homeostasis. However, recent studies have shown that targeting certain key molecules involved in MQC is expected to restore mitochondrial function, improve the imbalance of mitochondrial homeostasis, and thereby play a therapeutic role in diseases [36]. For example, inhibiting p-Drp1S616-mediated mitochondrial fission could attenuates fibroblast activation in renal fibrosis, thereby slow down the development of chronic kidney disease [37]; Regulating PINK1/Parkin-mediated mitophagy was a novel therapeutic strategy for the treatment of neurodegenerative diseases [38]. In our study, we investigated numerous classic molecules involved in the MQC through RNA sequencing, and also evaluated molecules related to mitochondrial dynamics through Western blot in HGFs. Regrettably, the RNA sequencing results showed no significant differences in mitochondrial dynamics or mitophagy-related molecules among groups. Notably, only PGC-1α exhibited statistically significant among groups, suggesting that PGC-1α was served as the key molecule regulating mitochondrial homeostasis in DP. However, in vitro study, among the molecules related to mitochondrial dynamics, Mfn1/2 exhibited differences among groups, suggesting the expression of Mfn1/2 might be subject to post-transcriptional regulation in the context of DP. Mfn1/2 were reported to regulate mitochondrial morphology, for example, knocking down Mfn1 induced mitochondrial fragmentation, while knocking down Mfn2 resulted in swollen and spherical mitochondria [39]. In our study, the expression of Mfn1/2 was significantly reduced in DP environment; Meanwhile, the mitochondrial morphology showed swelling, vacuolar changes, and even fragmentation, which was consistent with previous studies. However, PGC-1α was the central regulatory molecule in MQC system, and PGC-1α could bind to the promoter region of Mfn2, thus regulating the transcriptional level of it [40]. Moreover, studies indicated that the decreased PGC-1α could also lead to a reduction of Mfn1/2, thereby regulating the mitochondrial fusion process [41]. Therefore, in this study, based on the RNA sequencing and Western blot results, the focus was placed on the significant role of PGC-1α in regulating mitochondrial homeostasis in DP.

PGC-1α is a pivotal molecule involved in the mitochondrial biogenesis [42], and the level of PGC-1α is found decreased in various inflammatory diseases, including inflammatory bowel disease and ischemia reperfusion injury [43,44]. Our research has also revealed that PGC-1α was significantly reduced in gingival connective tissues from DP and HGFs stimulated by HG + LPS. Moreover, an increase of mtROS and a decrease of both ATP and mitochondrial membrane potential were observed, indicating the disruption of mitochondrial homeostasis. The possible mechanisms of PGC-1α regulating mitochondrial homeostasis were numerous and multifaceted. PGC-1α could potentiate the transcription of tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle genes through the coactivation of coupled transcription factors, such as estrogen-related receptor (ERR) and GA-binding protein-a (Gabpa), leading to an increase of ATP production [45]. Moreover, as the master regulator of oxidative stress response, diminished PGC-1α levels impaired the expression of antioxidant genes, leading to accumulated ROS and exacerbation of oxidative damage [46]. Besides, compromised mitochondrial electron transport chain (ETC) activity and diminished mitochondrial membrane potential were all strongly correlated with downregulated PGC-1α expression in diabetic conditions [47,48], and suppressed PGC-1α could further induce quantitative reduction of mitochondrial DNA in DP(49). All the above interconnected mechanisms likely driven mitochondrial homeostasis disruption in DP pathogenesis, which warranted further investigation in our subsequent studies.

Given the critical role of PGC-1α in regulating mitochondrial homeostasis, numerous studies have targeted PGC-1α as a promising therapeutic strategy for various diseases [33,50,51]. Sun et al. [49] discovered that using silibinin to preserve PGC-1α expression can effectively enhance the osteogenic ability of periodontal ligament cells, thus attenuating DP. Similarly, Fu et al. [52] found that the overexpression of PGC-1α could effectively suppress the inflammatory response of cementoblasts, which was positive for the prevention and treatment of periodontitis. In our study, we initially revealed that the mitochondrial homeostasis imbalance in DP is closely related to the abnormal gingival lesions and the disordered ECM remodeling in gingival connective tissues. To address the aforementioned abnormal pathological conditions, PGC-1α overexpression plasmid were transfected into HGFs to further verify the therapeutic effect of PGC-1α on regulating mitochondria homeostasis and the ECM remodeling. Our results finally demonstrated that up-regulated PGC-1α could reverse the abnormal mitochondrial function in HGFs under DP through increasing the levels of SOD2 and alleviating the oxidative stress. SOD2 was the main antioxidant enzyme in mitochondria and the activity of SOD2 could be reduced due to acetylation [53]. Therefore, the increased SOD2, the decreased ac-SOD2 and the decreased ratio of ac-SOD2/SOD2 indicated that overexpression of PGC-1α could significantly increase the activity of SOD2, resulting in a reduction in mtROS, thereby alleviating the oxidative stress of the cells, restoring the damaged mitochondrial function, which manifested as an increase in mtDNA copy number, an increase in the levels of mitochondrial ATP and mitochondrial membrane potential [54,55]. Besides, up-regulated PGC-1α finally improved the process of collagen metabolism in HGFs under DP. All these results confirmed the positive effects of PGC-1α in maintaining mitochondrial homeostasis and the ECM remodeling of gingival connective tissues, thereby alleviating gingival lesions and slowing the pathological progression in DP.

The regulatory impact of PGC-1α on ECM remodeling has been reported in osteoarthritis [[56], [57], [58]], kidney fibrosis [59,60] and systemic sclerosis [61]. Although our study similarly elucidated the reciprocal regulation between PGC-1α-mediated mitochondrial homeostasis and the ECM remodeling in DP, the underlying molecular mechanisms remained to be fully clarified and warranted further investigation. In addition, we were also surprised to find that overexpression of PGC-1α could significantly alleviate alveolar bone absorption in DP rats, which may be associated with the reduced secretion of inflammatory cytokines of HGFs.

In conclusion, mitochondrial homeostasis and ECM remodeling are essential for maintaining overall health, yet both of them undergone pathological changes in DP. Our study proved that PGC-1α mediated mitochondrial homeostasis plays an important role in ECM remodeling in HGFs. That is, to regulate the expression of PGC-1α could improve the abnormal mitochondrial structure, morphology and function under DP condition, thus ameliorating the disorders of HGFs-mediated collagen metabolism, modifying ECM remodeling, enhancing the barrier function of gingival connective tissues and alleviating inflammatory response, ultimately decelerating DP progression. It provides a novel therapeutic strategy for clinical prevention and treatment of DP from the perspective of organelle intervention.
