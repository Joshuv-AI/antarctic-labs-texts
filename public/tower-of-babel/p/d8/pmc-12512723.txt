Automated grading of embroidery assignments: a multi-region deep learning framework with ResNet-50
Authors: Nanzhen Lin, Yuyang Wang, Gaobo Jin, Jingyi Huang, Kedie Zhang, Chenyan Xie, Qing Han, Yueyuan Yu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundManual grading of dental students’ embroidery assignments is not only labor-intensive but also subjective. To address these limitations, our study proposes an automated grading model based on ResNet-50 architecture enhanced with a multi-region aggregation mechanism. This approach aims to standardize the grading process, improve fairness and efficiency in assessment.MethodsA total of 381 embroidery assignment images were collected from the 2020–2023 student cohorts. The 2022 cohort was designated as an external test set to assess model generalization with different data distributions. We proposed a multi-region aggregation mechanism based on ResNet-50 and compared two aggregation strategies: multi-head attention (MHA) aggregation and average weighting (AW) aggregation. VGG-16, DenseNet-121, ViT, and ResNet-50 were considered as baseline models. All models were trained using 5-fold cross-validation, employing a weighted CrossEntropyLoss to address class imbalance, with evaluation metrics including accuracy, precision, recall, and F1 score.ResultsThe ResNet-50 AW model achieved the highest test accuracy of 80% on the test set, while the ViT and the VGG-16 models achieved 75%, second to ResNet-50 AW. Although models’ performance degraded on the external test set, ResNet-50 AW maintained the highest accuracy of 64% and reduced misclassifications of grade B and C samples. Despite excelling on the validation set, ResNet-50 MHA showed similar performance to ResNet-50 on the test set. ViT and VGG-16 achieved higher accuracy for grade A on both the test set and the external test set.ConclusionThe ResNet-50 AW model highlights the potential of deep learning methods to automate the grading of artistic assignments via a multi-region aggregation mechanism. Further validation of the model’s generalization is needed. Future work should improve dataset quality and diversity and enhance system interpretability to refine the grading process for greater accuracy and transparency.Keywords: Deep learning, ResNet-50, Embroidery, Automated grading, Multi-region aggregation

Background

Manual dexterity is essential for dentists, as they must work in very confined spaces during diagnosis and treatment [1, 2]. Students’ early exposure to art activities has been associated with better academic performance and greater interest in anatomy courses [3, 4]. The School of Stomatology at Zhejiang Chinese Medical University has introduced a practical skills course for first-year dental students, where students will embroider on fabric printed with anatomical patterns of head and neck blood vessels and nerves. However, the current grading process depends on subjective teacher assessments and the lack of standardized evaluation criteria [5], which introduces biases and affects students’ GPAs.

With the rapid development of deep learning, models such as Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) and Vision Transformer (ViT) have demonstrated their remarkable performance in tasks like classification and object detection [6, 7]. The medical image analysis has witnessed notable successes based on these models and their hierarchy variants [8–10]. Their extensive application in aesthetic analysis of artworks [11–13] highlights their potential for grading artistic assignments. Recent studies have explored the automated assessment of students’ artworks for creativity [14].

However, no research has yet addressed automatic grading for embroidery tasks, which are characterized by highly similar visual appearance, specific stitch quality and pattern accuracy requirements, anatomical fidelity, and other subjective assessment factors. Existing models often focus on global assessments of students’ assignments, neglecting local details and lacking interpretability. To overcome these challenges, we propose an automated grading model based on ResNet-50 architecture, enhanced with a multi-region aggregation mechanism. ResNet-50, a well-established architecture known for its deep feature extraction capabilities [15], is suitable for capturing intricate embroidery textures and pattern structures. To enable the model to focus precisely on head and neck neurovascular structures where students practice embroidery, ResNet-50 extracts features from manually annotated regions rather than from entire images. We designed two feature aggregation approaches to combine features from multiple regions: (1) a multi-head attention (MHA) mechanism, which emphasizes the most relevant regions for grading; and (2) an average weighting (AW) mechanism, which treats all regions equally. Through this model, our study aims to establish an objective and fair grading process, thereby reducing teachers’ workload.

Methods

Dataset preparation

A total of 381 embroidery assignments’ images were collected from students in the 2020–2023 cohorts at Zhejiang Chinese Medical University. Each student submitted one image (Fig. 1), and all the images were subsequently converted to JPG format. To assess the model’s generalization performance on a new cohort with potentially distinct score distributions, we randomly selected the 2022 cohort (105 images) as an external test set. This external test set simulates the application of the model to real-world data from a future cohort, such as the 2024 cohort, ensuring a robust assessment of performance across different score distributions.

One student’s assignment

Based on the Embroiderers’ Guild of America (EGA) guidelines [16] and other evaluation standards [17], and considering the overall quality of the students’ embroidery work, we developed the following grading rubric (Table 1), in which the definitions of Area 1 and Area 2 are illustrated in Fig. 2. Every student managed to embroider at least 50% of the primary blood vessels and nerves within either Area 1 or Area 2 during the class. Despite minor aesthetic flaws that incurred deductions, students would still meet the passing threshold.

The reference focus area that the researchers scored

Three researchers evaluated students’ assignments independently according to the rubrics in Table 1, and the final score was calculated as the mean of the three evaluators’ scores. If the gap between the maximum and minimum scores exceeded 10 points, a senior evaluator would reassess the current assignment. Based on the distribution of student scores, the assignments were classified into four grades and labeled accordingly: D (60–70), C (71–80), B (81–90), and A (91–100). These labels were used to train and test models.

ResNet-50 with multi-region aggregation mechanism

We were inspired by the region-of-interest (ROI) techniques from Faster R-CNN [18]. Recognizing the need to focus on critical regions (as outlined in Table 1), we manually annotated these regions (Fig. 3). These regions highlight the primary blood vessels and nerves where students need to embroider. Through this method, we intended to minimize potential interference from irrelevant or distracting elements in the image, such as students’ names or stains.

Manually annotated regions

Specific regions of the images were cropped and independently processed by applying random rotations within ± 30°, along with ColorJitter with parameters set to brightness = 0.2, contrast = 0.2, saturation = 0.2, and hue = 0.1. Subsequently, all the cropped images were resized to dimensions of 224× 224 pixels, converted into tensors, and normalized using standard ImageNet normalization parameters: mean=[0.485, 0.456, 0.406] and standard deviation=[0.229, 0.224, 0.225]. A ResNet-50 model pre-trained on ImageNet, with its fully connected layer replaced by an identity mapping, was utilized to extract features from each region. These features were then passed to an aggregation module, where we tested two approaches:

ResNet-50 with Multi-Head Attention (ResNet-50 MHA) Model: The extracted features serve as the query, key, and value inputs to a multi-head attention layer. Eight attention heads compute attention weights independently, which are then averaged and normalized by a softmax function. This process can be analogous to having eight independent experts grade the assignment—each highlights the regions they consider most critical. Finally, their perspectives are aggregated into a final decision. Figure 4 illustrates this process.

ResNet-50 with Average Weighting (ResNet-50 AW) Model: This model uses a simpler strategy for feature aggregation. It aggregates the extracted features from each region by equal weighting (assigning 1/3 weight to each region). This can be compared to all experts agreeing that each region contributes equally to the final grade and no single region can influence the score disproportionately.

It aggregates the extracted features from each region by equal weighting (assigning 1/3 weight to each region). This can be compared to all experts agreeing that each region contributes equally to the final grade and no single region can influence the score disproportionately.

Baseline model

To evaluate the effectiveness of our multi-region aggregation mechanism, VGG-16, ResNet-50, DenseNet-121, and ViT were selected as baseline models for comparison. The final fully connected layers of VGG-16, ResNet-50, and DenseNet-121 were replaced to accommodate our four-grade classification task, while the classification head of ViT was also modified to align with the four-grade output requirements. All these models were pre-trained on ImageNet.

VGG-16

VGG-16, a prominent deep convolutional neural network, is frequently employed as a research benchmark [19]. By stacking small convolutional filters, a comprehensive hierarchy of visual features can be constructed, capturing complicated patterns from basic edges to intricate textures and shapes in embroidery assignments. Numerous academics have built upon its foundation, tailoring it for specific tasks and attaining promising results [20].

ResNet-50

ResNet-50 was chosen to evaluate the improvement of our multi-region aggregation mechanism. Residual blocks and skip connections not only allow the ResNet-50 to learn deeper features of embroidery assignments [15] but also reinforce its capacity to distinguish intricate or disordered embroidery texture.

DenseNet-121

DenseNet-121 was chosen for its densely connected architecture, which offers better feature-reuse capabilities compared with ResNet-50 [21], allowing it to capture a broader range of information, from intricate stitches to the overall patterns of blood vessels and neurons.

Vision transformer (ViT)

Vision Transformer (ViT) model was included owing to its powerful visual feature extraction capabilities [22]. Unlike the multi-head attention mechanism in the ResNet-50 MHA model, the core of ViT is the multi-head self-attention mechanism. This specific mechanism enables ViT to capture subtle differences in embroidery stitching patterns more effectively. Figure 5 illustrates how ViT segments the image into fixed-size patches and adaptively weighs each patch’s importance. By this patch-based approach, ViT can extract finer-grained features than our multi-region mechanism.

Image segmentation into fixed-size patches for vision transformer processing

Data augmentation for baseline models: We directly applied random rotations in the range of [−30°, 30°]. The ColorJitter function was introduced with the following parameters: brightness = 0.2, contrast = 0.2, saturation = 0.2, and hue = 0.1. Next, all the images were resized to 224 × 224 pixels and normalized using ImageNet parameters: mean = [0.485, 0.456, 0.406] and std = [0.229, 0.224, 0.225].

Evaluation metrics

Some traditional evaluation metrics were selected to measure the performance of deep learning classification models [23]. For all these metrics, the closer they are to 1, the better the model performs.

Accuracy

Accuracy is the indicator that represents the proportion of correctly classified instances (both positive and negative) out of the total number of instances.

Where TP represents true positives, TN represents true negatives, FP represents false positives, and FN represents false negatives.

Recall

Recall explains how many of the TP samples were correctly predicted, which is particularly meaningful in fields like medicine, where identifying true positives is crucial.

Precision

Unlike accuracy, precision attaches more emphasis on the proportion of TP out of all positive predictions, including FP.

F1 score

F1 score is an indicator that combines both Precision and Recall metrics, providing a balanced approach to evaluating a model’s performance.

Experiment

To ensure consistency across training, validation, and testing datasets for all models, we established a global random seed of 42. A total of 276 embroidery images from the 2020, 2021, and 2023 cohorts were used for model training, validation, and testing. Stratified shuffle-split was performed to allocate 20% of the images (56 images) as a test set, reserved for the final assessment. The remaining 220 images were used in a stratified 5-fold cross-validation scheme, with 176 images used for training and 44 images for validation in each fold. For each fold, the model with the lowest validation loss was saved for that fold. Validation performance across the five folds was summarized as mean ± standard deviation (SD), with 95% confidence intervals (CI), for accuracy, precision, recall, F1 score, and loss. A batch size of 16 was used for all training phases across models. We used the AdamW optimizer (initial LR = 1 × 10⁻⁴, weight decay = 1 × 10⁻⁵) with a StepLR scheduler (decay factor 0.1 every 10 epochs). Each model was trained for up to 100 epochs, with an early stopping criterion of no validation loss improvement for 10 consecutive epochs. After cross-validation, we selected the model configuration with the lowest validation loss and tested it on the test set.

Results

Inter-rater reliability among the three evaluators was assessed using Fleiss’ Kappa (κ = 0.52), indicating moderate agreement. To ensure rigorous statistical analysis, a Mann-Whitney U Test was conducted to compare the grade data distribution between the 2022 cohort (external test set) and the combined 2020, 2021, and 2023 cohorts, which showed a significant difference (p = 0.0002). As illustrated in Fig. 6, the 2022 cohort showed a high proportion of grades B and C. The training, validation, and testing sets primarily consisted of assignments graded A, B, and C.

Discussion

In this fine-grained classification task, we enhanced conventional models with a multi-region aggregation mechanism to automate student assignment grading. Experimental results demonstrated that the ResNet-50 AW model significantly outperformed baseline models on both the test set and the external test set. The multi-region aggregation mechanism improved classification performance; ResNet-50 AW achieved the highest accuracy (0.80), surpassing all baseline models. Although all models’ performance degraded on the external 2022 test set, ResNet-50 AW still maintained an accuracy 4% higher than baseline ResNet-50, while reducing misclassifications in grades B and C compared with ViT and VGG-16. Moreover, we observed that both the ResNet-50 MHA and ResNet-50 AW models converged faster than any of the other baseline models during training. Despite the added complexity of the multi-region mechanism, it reduced the feature computation requirements, thereby lowering training time.

However, ResNet-50 MHA’s performance on the test set remained comparable to the baseline ResNet-50, which implies that the multi-head attention mechanism did not work as expected. One potential explanation is that ResNet-50 MHA’s strong learning capacity led to overfitting on the validation set, as evidenced by its lower average loss during validation. Surprisingly, DenseNet-121, despite being a more advanced model than ResNet-50, performed worse than ResNet-50, even underperforming VGG-16. This finding suggests that increasing model depth is not a critical factor for this grading task.

ViT and VGG-16 exhibited comparable accuracy on the test set, although ViT achieved a 4% higher precision than VGG-16. Both ViT and VGG-16 achieved the highest recall for grade A assignments across both test sets, although they showed a tendency to misclassify grade B samples as grade A on the external 2022 test set. This specific grade bias indicates that developing an ensemble model (combining multiple models rather than relying on a single one) could potentially leverage the strengths of each for specific grade levels. Furthermore, it may be worthwhile to determine which model is optimal for predicting a given grade, depending on whether precision, recall, or F1-score is prioritized in an educational setting.

In developing a small-scale project like this, dataset quality and quantity are crucial. The dataset should adequately represent the original score distribution [24], and class imbalance must also be addressed [25]. As illustrated in Fig. 6, the original dataset exhibits a negatively skewed distribution, with grade B samples being more frequent than grade C samples, while the 2022 external test set exhibits a distribution that is closer to a normal (bell-curve) distribution. To mitigate the issue of class imbalance, we implemented class weighting in CrossEntropyLoss. However, this re-weighting may shift the model’s focus away from the actual data distribution, posing a problem in reconciling per-class performance with overall accuracy. Figure 9 illustrates that all models exhibited inferior performance on the 2022 cohort, signifying restricted generalization ability. All models except ResNet-50 AW showed a consistent pattern of misclassifying grade B and C samples as higher grades. Recent findings have achieved promising results by mixed weighting strategies [26], and the MLP-based weight function with a single hidden layer proposed by Shu et al. also offers a potential solution [27]. Thus, future work should consider integrating adaptive weighting techniques to better handle class imbalance.

Since student assignment images were captured using different devices under varying environmental conditions, inconsistencies in viewing angles, lighting conditions, and image formats may introduce distortions, thereby increasing the complexity of region annotation and model training. Moreover, all regions in this study were manually annotated, which potentially introduces subjectivity and increases the workload for instructors. To better address these issues, a feasible solution is to collect all students’ assignments and photograph them under a standardized environment to ensure consistent image quality. Subsequently, automatic object detection models such as YOLO [28] or Mask-RCNN [29] can be introduced to focus on neurovascular regions rather than irrelevant muscular background texture. Integrating this automated region detection system with our ResNet-50 AW model would enable fully automated assignment grading, significantly reduce manual workload, and enhance practical feasibility.

It is essential to consider the interpretability of AI when using it to grade students’ assignments. An interpretable AI can be defined from two perspectives: (1) helping humans understand the decision-making process, and (2) explaining the reasons behind decisions [30]. In our study, Grad-CAM was used to enhance the interpretability of different models and visualize their activation patterns. ResNet-50 AW allocates its focus more effectively across various regions than ResNet-50, resulting in grading decisions that are more interpretable and better aligned with our grading criteria. ViT also captures finer-grained regional details, demonstrating potential for localized grading through pixel-level operations. In traditional education, teachers’ timely feedback (both positive and negative) is crucial for student learning and achievement [31]. Therefore, to provide more effective feedback in an automated grading system, one could design a multi-stage decision model, such as a decision tree [32], where each stage corresponds to a criterion from the rubric and produces an interpretable evaluation. Such an approach would foster a feedback-driven automated grading system in educational settings.

Given the highly subjective nature of artistic work evaluation, continuous numerical scoring is not an appropriate approach. Therefore, we designed the scoring rubrics in Table 1 to make the evaluation process more objective and enable different evaluators to achieve relatively stable results. According to the regulations of the Academic Affairs Office of Zhejiang Chinese Medical University, grade assessment can adopt a hundred-point system, a five-level system, or a two-level system. After comprehensive consideration, the five-level system is more suitable for scoring embroidery assignments. Since such assignments are typically not graded as failing, the actual scoring is divided into four levels. The models developed in this research do not directly apply the standards in Table 1 when assessing images, but produce suggestions based on the scores given by the observers in accordance with the table and the general similarity with the relevant image sample. Although no direct rules are applied in this context, the aim was to produce a more standardized assessment tool for a topic such as hand dexterity, which is prone to subjective evaluation. In addition, our research is consistent with [33]. These analyses emphasize the importance of methodological innovation and trend-aware approaches in improving the fairness, efficiency, and transparency of assessment practices.

A multitude of researchers evaluated each assignment according to standardized criteria during the manual grading procedure. However, the Fleiss’ kappa of 0.52 indicates that achieving fully objective and consistent assessments remains challenging. As pointed out by Jeong et al. [34], consistency can be improved with standardized grading criteria; however, subjective bias among evaluators remains inevitable. Currently, our model functions as an objective assistant in the grading process rather than a replacement for human judgment. Teachers’ expertise remains crucial for delivering and interpreting meaningful pedagogical feedback.

Finally, our study used a fixed random seed for dataset splitting and model initialization to guarantee reproducibility. We did not explore the impact of varying data splitting strategies or test the statistical significance of performance differences between models. Future experiments should employ multiple random seeds to compute averaged performance metrics with confidence intervals, thereby increasing the statistical robustness and reliability of the results.

Conclusion

This study uncovered the potential of deep learning techniques in automating the grading of embroidery assignments in dental education. By integrating a multi-region aggregation mechanism, the ResNet-50 AW model outperformed the standard ResNet-50 and other baseline models on both the test set and the external test set. However, the model’s generalization capabilities still require further exploration, and future work should focus on enhancing the quality and diversity of the training data, increasing the size of the dataset (and including more cohorts), and improving model interpretability. These steps are important for developing a fair and transparent automated grading system.
