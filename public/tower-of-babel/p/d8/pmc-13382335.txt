Differential responses to soybean meal and canola meal based diet protein levels in mid-lactation dairy cows with different milk yield and parity
Authors: Z.Q. Yang, Y.J. Su, B.B. Cao, X.K. Zhang, T.Y. Wu, F.F. Gu, Y. Gong, W.D. Wang, F.X. Wang, R.H. Zhang, X.Y. Zhang, Q. Zhou, Z.H. Wei
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Highlights•Lactation performance of dairy cows fed diets containing 170 g crude protein(CP) /kg dry matter (DM) or 162 g CP/kg DM was compared.•No differences in lactation performance were observed in primiparous cows across the treatment groups, even for the highest-yielding 10% primiparous cows (averaging 45.3 kg/d).•Lactation performance was unaffected by the lower-protein diet (162 g CP/kg DM) in multiparous cows producing <50 kg/d.•The lower-protein diet reduced 1.37kg/d milk yield in high-yielding multiparous cows producing >50 kg/d.•The lower-protein diet decreased milk and blood urea nitrogen concentration, and decreased the molar proportion of rumen branched volatile fatty acids in high yielding cows.

Introduction

Improving our ability to optimize milk yield while enhancing nitrogen efficiency is critical for profitability, human food supply security and dairy industry sustainability (Yoder et al., 2020). Protein is a cornerstone of dairy cow nutrition, playing a pivotal roles in milk synthesis, growth, reproduction, and overall metabolic health by providing essential amino acids for milk proteins, enzymes, hormones, and immune molecules (NASEM, 2021; NRC, 2001). Precision nutrition, emphasizes tailoring nutrient supply to the cow's nutritional requirements at different physiological stages, such as lactation, dry periods, and growth phases is one of the key dimensions for precision nutrition in dairy farming (Huhtanen et al., 2015), thereby enhancing nutrient utilization efficiency, minimizing waste, and supporting sustainable dairy production (Chase & Fortina, 2023; Ma et al., 2024). Protein nutrition is particularly crucial, as imbalances may reduce milk yield, impair health, and increase environmental pollution (Lapierre et al., 2007). And by optimizing the dietary protein supply can lower nitrogen footprints by 35–42% of dairy production under some commercial dairy farm feeding system (Ma et al., 2024).

High-protein diets are common in dairy farms practices support higher lactation performance (Broderick, 2003). In a survey carried out on 103 large scale dairies across the US (613 ± 46 cows; 34.5 ± 0.3 kg of milk per cow per day), nutritionists reported feeding diets with 178 ± 1 g crude protein (CP)/ kg dry matter (DM) (Caraviello et al., 2006). And based on a survey of China Agricultural (Dairy) Research System in 2023 across the country, the dietary CP content for high production cows was mainly formulated between 170–180 g/kg DM, with an average about 175 g/kg DM. Although high-protein diets enhance milk production, they also pose challenges related to nitrogen efficiency and environmental sustainability, as excess nitrogen (N) is excreted in urine and manure (Huhtanen et al., 2015), with approximately 70% of ingested N being excreted through feces and urine, and <30% is incorporated into milk products (Castillo et al., 2000; Groenestein et al., 2019). The excessive N emissions contributing to both eutrophication and acidification processes in terrestrial and aquatic ecosystems (Rufí-Salís et al., 2020; Sutton et al., 2013; Yu et al., 2019), with pronounced effects on local N pollution levels (Finzi et al., 2020; Theobald et al., 2015). Moreover, dietary protein may contribute over 40% of a diet’s total cost (Hanigan et al., 2004; Hanigan et al., 2013).

Low-protein diets have therefore attracted attention as a strategy to improve N efficiency and profitability, as well as mitigate environmental impact while maintaining cow performance (Chase & Fortina, 2023; Hanigan et al., 2013; Lee et al., 2012). This approach represents a sustainable solution for dairy production in the context of increasing resource constraints and environmental regulations (Powell et al., 2013). Broderick (2003) observed that, reducing diet CP from 184 g/kg DM to 167 g/kg/DM did not affect lactation performance of the cows, but a reduction to 151 g/kg DM significantly decreased milk yield of the cows (Broderick, 2003). Ipharraguerre et al. (2005a) observed no impact of diet CP on milk yield when CP reduced from 190 g/kg DM to 166 g/kg DM, but a significant reduction occurred at diet CP around 150 g/kg DM. And Letelier et al. (2022) documented a linear decline in milk yield with decreasing CP in high-producing cows, yield remained stable when CP decreased from 183 g/kg DM to 167 g/kg DM, but dropped by approximately 5 kg/d when CP was reduced to 152 g/kg DM. These findings suggest that reducing dietary CP content within a certain range has no adverse effects on dairy cow lactation performance. However, the protein gradients between treatment groups in these studies are often too wide, whereas practical diet formulation requires more precise adjustments within a narrower range, and producer adoption remains limited due to concerns about potential production losses, with 52% of respondents ranking production losses in their top three challenges when lowering dietary CP (Prestegaard-Wilson et al., 2021).

Soybean meal and double-low rapeseed meal (canola meal) are widely used protein sources in dairy cow due to their high protein content, balanced amino acid profiles, and cost-effectiveness. Soybean meal is rich in lysine and highly digestible, whereas canola meal contains slightly lower lysine but remains an important protein source for high-producing cows (Broderick et al., 2015; Giallongo et al., 2015; Martineau et al., 2013). Although SBM has higher crude protein and most amino acids than CM, ruminal CP degradability and intestinal digestibility of rumen undegradable protein are comparable between them, and CM contains more methionine and cysteine, despite differences in protein secondary structure, both SBM and CM can provide similar benefits as protein source for dairy cattle (Tian et al., 2019). However, their extensive use has raised concerns about cost volatility, environmental sustainability, and competition with human food supply chains (Gerber et al., 2013). These challenges have spurred interest in strategies to optimize protein utilization in dairy diets (Powell et al., 2013). Precise nutrients requirement are the basis of precision nutrition, and cows with different milk yield or different parity (primiparous or multiparous) might has different requirement of some nutrients. Our previous work showed that reducing dietary CP content from 174 g/kg DM to 170 g/kg DM by changing dietary content of soybean meal and canola meal did not compromise lactation performance in primiparous cows (mean ± SD, 35.6 ± 4.70 kg/d) and multiparous cows (mean ± SD, 46.4 ± 6.82 kg/d), even for high yield multiparous cows with milk yield between 50–60kg/d (unpublished data). This suggest moderate dietary CP reduction may be feasible without negative effects on performance. It is unknown, however, whether a further reduction of dietary CP via decreased inclusion of soybean meal and canola meal would impair lactation performance in either primiparous or multiparous cows, or in cows with varying milk yield levels. Thus, the objective of current study was to investigate the effects of further reducing dietary CP by partially decreasing soybean meal and canola meal on lactation performance, blood metabolites and rumen fermentation in mid-lactation dairy cows with different parity and milk yield level.

Materials and methods

Animals, feeds, and experimental design

Five hundred and forty Holstein dairy cows including 274 primiparous cows (milk yield: 37.8 ± 3.98 kg, DIM: 141 ± 24.1 d, mean ± SD) and 260 multiparous cows (milk yield: 44.6 ± 7.13 kg, DIM: 134 ± 21.0 d, Parity: 3.21±1.26, mean ± SD) were used with a randomized complete block design in current study. Cows were blocked by parity, milk yield, DIM and randomly assigned to one of the 2 diets, containing 170 g CP /kg DM (control group, CON) or 162 g CP/kg DM (low CP group, LCP). Dietary CP was varied mainly by changing dietary content of soybean meal and canola meal and beet pulp, and the ingredients and composition of the experimental diets are presented in Table 1. The experiment lasted for 10 weeks, with the first two weeks as adaption and the later 8 weeks used for data and sample collection (formal period). If any cow was withdrawn from the study for reasons such as illness, all the data from the removed cow and the cow of the other group within its balanced block were omitted from the dataset prior to analysis. The experiment was conducted at Jinshan Dairy Farm of Bright Dairy & Food Co., Ltd. All the cows with different diet treatment were housed individually in 4 free-stall barns under same management procedure throughout the study, with multiparous cows feeding the CON diet or LCP diet allocated to two separate barns, while primiparous cows fed the CON and LCP diets housed individually in the remaining two barns. The cows were milked three times daily at 0700, 1500 and 2300 h, and were fed TMR ad libitum (3–5% refusal) three times daily at 0800, 1600 and 2400 h and have free access to drink water.

Sampling, measurement, and analyses

TMR samples were collected from 10 equidistant locations along the feeding channel prior to morning feeding at 2-week intervals throughout the experimental period. Samples were homogenized by quartering technique, and 500 g subsamples were retained and air-dried in a forced-air oven at 55 °C for 48 h, followed by grinding through a 1-mm screen using a Cyclone Sample Mill (UDY Corp., Fort Collins, CO) in preparation for later analysis, and were analyzed for the dry matter (DM, 105 °C for 6 h), ADF and NDF (Van Soest et al., 1991), CP and calcium(Ca) and phosphorus(P) and Ether extract and ash (AOAC, 1990). Since the individual dry matter intake (DMI) is not able to measure because of cows were fed in free-stall barns, the average DMI of each barn was measured and determined by daily measurement of total mixed ration provision and orts for each barn, beginning 21 d pre-trial and continuing through the experimental period.

The milk yield of each cow was recorded daily from three weeks prior to the onset of the trial to the end of the experiment, and then were averaged weekly per cow for later statistical analysis. Milk persistency of each cow during the formal experiment period (last 8 weeks) was calculated based on weekly milk yields, expressed as the percentage of the average milk yield of the final week to that of the first week. And a composite milk sample (composited at a ratio of 4:3:3 following the milking time points) of each cow were collected at the last day of −2 week prior to the start of the trial, and weeks 4 and 8 after the onset of the experiment. The samples were stored with addition of bronopol preservative tablets (Broad Spectrum MicrotabsⅡD and F Control System Inc., Dublin, CA) and were sent to the Shanghai Dairy Herd Improvement testing center for the analysis of milk protein, fat, lactose, total solids, milk urea nitrogen (MUN) and somatic cell count (SCC) using Combi Foss FT+ instrument (Foss Electric, Hillerød, Denmark). The averaged pre-trial 3 weeks of milk yield and pre-trial DHI sample results were used for the milk components yield and pre-trial fat-corrected milk (FCM) yield calculation, as for the experimental period, the average milk yield of 2 weeks before and 2 weeks after each DHI sampling time and the contemporaneous DHI sample results were used for the milk components yield and FCM yield calculation.

Blood samples were collected from 12 high yield multiparous cows per treatment (mean ± SD, CON: 50.6 ± 4.19 kg/d pre-trial milk yield, LCP: 50.5 ± 5.04 kg/d pre-trial milk yield) on d 59 and rumen fluid samples of these cows were collected on 61 post-trial initiation (week 9) prior to morning feeding. The 24 cows were from 12 balanced blocks which blocked by parity, milk yield and DIM before onset of the experiment, with one cow per block randomly assigned to each treatment group. The blood samples were obtained from coccygeal vein into heparinized vacuum tubes, and then centrifuged at 2100 g for 15 min, to obtain plasma and stored at −20 °C for later analysis. Plasma samples were analyzed using an automated Chemistry Analyser BX-4000 (Sysmex Corporation, Kobe, Japan) equipped with colorimetric commercial kits (Medicalsystem Biotechnology Co.,Ltd., Ningbo, China) for albumin (ALB), total protein (TP), β-hydroxybutyric acid (BHBA), non-esterified fatty acids (NEFA), triglycerides (TG), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), glucose (GLU), and blood Urea Nitrogen (BUN). Rumen fluid samples (approximately 150 mL) were collected using an oral stomach tube (Shen et al., 2012), the rumen fluid pH was measured immediately using a pH meter (FE20-FiveEasy Plus™; Mettler Toledo Instruments Co. Ltd., Shanghai, China), triplicate 1-ml samples were dispensed and stored at −20 °C for later analysis of volatile fatty acids (VFA) and ammonia N (NH3-N) as described by Wang et al. (2014).

Statistical analysis

All data were analyzed using SAS software (version 9.0, SAS Institute Inc., Cary, NC). The weekly milk yield and 3.5% FCM yield, content and yield of milk components were analyzed using the PROC MIXED procedure with repeated measurement of the SAS software. The model included diet, week, interaction of diet by week as fixed effects, block and cow within diet as random effects, and the milk yield and 3.5% FCM yield were covariate-adjusted using the 3 weeks averaged milk yield and 3.5% FCM yield pre-trial, milk components content and yield were covariate-adjusted using the test results of milk samples collected pre-trial. For the repeated measurement analysis, the covariance structure was selected according to best fit with Schwarz’s Bayesian information criterion. Plasma and rumen fermentation variables and milk persistency were also analyzed using PROC MIXED of SAS did not contain a time or repeated component, with diet as fixed effect, block as random effect. Results were listed as least squares means and separated using the pdiff option. A statistically significant difference was defined at P ≤ 0.05 and statistical trends were declared at 0.05 < P < 0.10.

Results

Lactation performance

In primiparous cows, milk yield maintained stable throughout the experiment (Fig. 1A). As presented in Table 2, the averaged barn DMI were higher in LCP compared with CON both in pre-trial period (22.82 vs 21.50 kg/d) and during the experimental period (23.38 vs 22.35 kg/d). Reducing dietary CP had no effects on yields of milk, 3.5% FCM, protein and lactose and total solids (P ≥ 0.10), and milk persistency and content of milk fat, protein, lactose, total solids, and SCC (P > 0.10). Discussion

Lactation performance

Protein plays a pivotal role in milk synthesis especially for milk yield and milk protein yield, and is critical for sustaining high productivity (NASEM, 2021; NRC, 2001). Even though, when dietary CP content changes, the relative contribution of different protein sources, as well as the concentrations of RDP and RUP, also shift. Overall, milk yield increased quadratically with dietary CP concentrations increase (Colmenero & Broderick, 2006; NRC, 2001). Once dietary protein sufficiently meet the metabolic and lactation requirements of dairy cows, further increase in CP content has no effect on yield of milk and milk protein (Broderick, 2003; Wang et al., 2007; Wei et al., 2023). However, when dietary CP is inadequate, increases in CP concentration improve milk yield and milk protein yield (Broderick, 2003; Law et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2011, 2012). Primiparous cows may have different protein requirements compared to multiparous cows, as they have additional growth needs besides of maintenance and production requirements, also cows with different milk yield would have different protein requirement. However, research on dietary protein levels for cows of different parities and production levels remains insufficient. Many studies focus solely on the impact of varying protein levels on overall performance, without differentiating between primiparous and multiparous cows or accounting for yield variations (Broderick, 2003; Ipharraguerre et al., 2005a; Law et al., 2009; Lee et al., 2011). Furthermore, the protein gradients between treatment groups in these studies are often too wide, whereas practical diet formulation requires more precise adjustments within a narrower range. In current study, we find that reducing diet protein from 170 g/kg DM to 162 g/kg DM has no effects on lactation performance in primiparous cows or multiparous cows with milk yield under 50 kg/d, but significantly decreased milk yield in multiparous cows with over 50kg/d milk yield. However, the diet protein difference between 170 g/kg DM to 162 g/kg DM is small, given this minimal difference, the underlying biological effect size on our dependent variables is expected to be inherently small in some cows under certain production level, making some statistically significant finding susceptible to random noise, and all significant findings, especially those derived from smaller subsamples in current study, must be interpreted with extreme caution.

For cows with approximately 43 kg/d of milk yield, Ipharraguerre et al. (2005a) reported no effect on milk yield when dietary CP reduced from 190 g/kg DM to 166 g/kg DM, but observed a significant reduction when CP decreased to around 150 g/kg DM. Similarly, a latin square research with 45 multiparous cows (44 kg/d milk yield pre-trial) and 18 primiparous cows (34 kg/d milk yield pre-trial) show that reducing dietary CP from 184 g/kg DM to 167 g/kg DM did not affect lactation performance of the cows, while further reduction to 151 g/kg DM significantly decreased milk yield (Broderick, 2003). But these researches decreased diet CP from about 167 g/kg DM dramatically to around 150 g/kg DM, the effect of diet CP content around 160 g/kg DM while no more than about 170 g/kg DM in cows with different parity and milk yield stay unknown. In the present study with soybean and canola meal as main protein source, reducing dietary CP from 170 g/kg DM to 162 g/kg DM did not affect milk yield in primiparous cows even for the top 10% high yield primiparous cows (mean ± SD, 45.3 ± 2.45 kg of milk/d, Table 3), indicating that 162 g CP/kg DM was sufficient to meet the lactation requirements of primiparous cows under current study, but whether reducing diet CP to 162 g/kg DM will produce adverse effect on lactation performance of primiparous cows with higher milk yield like with yield over 50kg/d still stay unknown. As for multiparous cows with averaged approximately 44 kg/d pre-trial in current study, a reduction in milk yield decreased with dietary CP reducing was observed. The discrepancy is likely attributable to their high nutrient requirements due to the substantially greater milk yield, suggesting that simply reducing diet CP content from 170 g/kg DM to 162 g/kg DM by changing dosage of diet soybean and canola meal is inadequate for high yield cows, dietary protein must be maintained above a certain threshold for diet to prevent milk yield depression.

Further stratified analysis based on averaged milk yield pre-trial in multiparous cows demonstrated milk yield level dependent responses to dietary CP reduction in current study, milk yield decreased significantly only in cow with milk yield over 50 kg/d. Several studies demonstrated no adverse effects on performance when dietary CP reduced from about 180 g/kg DM to 160 g/kg DM in herds averaging approximately 30 kg/d (Ipharraguerre & Clark, 2005b) or 35 kg/d (Wei et al., 2023). Colmenero et al. (2006) found no effect on milk yield across CP levels of 179 g/kg DM, 165 g/kg DM, and 150 g/kg DM in cows averaging 41 kg/d. For cows yielding approximately 43 kg/d, Ipharraguerre et al. (2005a) observed no impact when CP reduced from 190 g/kg DM to 166 g/kg DM, but a significant reduction occurred at CP around 150 g/kg DM. Letelier et al. (2022) documented a linear decline in milk yield with decreasing CP in high-producing cows averaging 49 kg/d pre-trial; yield remained stable when CP decreased from 183 g/kg DM to 167 g/kg DM, but dropped by approximately 5 kg/d when CP was reduced to 152 g/kg DM. These collective findings strongly suggest that high-yielding cows necessitate higher dietary CP levels to sustain lactation performance, integrating the results of the current study, reducing dietary CP from 170 g/kg DM to 162 g/kg DM is likely to decrease milk yield in multiparous cows producing over 50 kg/d, with no effects on milk yield in cows producing under 50 kg/d.

Feed intake

Regarding feed intake, primiparous cows in the present study exhibited higher DMI in the LCP group both pre-trial and during the experiment, indicating no detrimental effects of CP reduction on intake. In multiparous cows, pre-trial DMI was similar between groups, but during the trial, cows in the LCP group consumed ∼0.68 kg/d less than those in CON. Even though lack of the individual DMI in current study resulted in impossible statistical difference analysis between LCP and CON, similar observations used multiparous cows in which DMI significantly decreased with dietary protein content reduction were reported in some researches (Barros et al., 2017; Lee et al., 2011; Liu & VandeHaar, 2020; M'hamed et al., 2001;). Letelier et al. (2022) predicted a ∼0.8kg/d reduction in DMI when dietary CP reduction from 174 g/kg DM to 163 g/kg DM in high-yielding multiparous dairy cows around 130 DIM, which is similar to the averaged 0.68 kg/d herd DMI decrease in current study. The averaged DMI might decreased in high yield multiparous cows when reducing dietary CP from 170 g/kg DM to 162 g/kg DM. As the strong positive relationship between DMI and milk yield (Allen et al., 2019; Hristov et al., 2004), the decline in DMI likely contributed to the reduced milk yield in multiparous cows fed LCP diets.

Blood metabolism

Reducing diet CP will decrease MUN and BUN and ALB (Cantalapiedra-Hijar et al., 2014; Law et al., 2009), the lower MUN and BUN are mainly due to LCP offered lower RDP, thus decreases the diffusion of ammonia from the rumen to the portal blood supply and subsequently decrease urea production (detoxification by-product) in the liver. As for ALB, which mainly synthesized by the liver, the plasma ALB concentration decreasing in LCP was likely a result of lower diet protein supply decreased protein supply for hepatic albumin synthesis (Raggio et al., 2007). Blood NEFA concentration is often a clinical measurement of energy status in dairy cows, NEFA is partially a result of adipose tissue lipolysis (Erickson et al., 1990; Grummer, 2008), the energy requirements of the cow are compensated by lipolysis, this leads to release of fatty acids in the blood, which are reversibly bound to albumin (Adewuyi et al., 2005). The higher NEFA concentration in CON than LCP in current study might due to higher milk yield result in greater energy requirements in CON, and therefore resulted in intensive lipolysis.

Rumen fermentation

Align with previous studies, the molar proportion of isobutyrate and total BCVFA were significantly decreased in LCP in current study (Belanche et al., 2012; Haro et al., 2018; Ipharraguerre & Clark, 2005b; White, 2025). The BCVFA are derived from the degradation of diet branched-chain amino acids (Andries et al., 1987; Firkins, 2023; Mitchell et al., 2023;), the LCP diet offered lower RDP resulted in lower branched-chain amino acids produced in the rumen and the consequent lower BCVFA. Studies have reported that BCVFA are often used by cellulolytic bacteria in the rumen (Andries et al., 1987; Firkins, 2023; Mitchell et al., 2023), BCVFA supplementation increases NDF degradability and the activity of some fiber-degrading microbes (Andries et al., 1987; Wang et al., 2015, 2018). For cows with high milk yield especially, DMI is positively related to NDF digestibility, rapid and extensive ruminal fiber degradation improves particle passage rate from the rumen and alleviates the bulk-fill limitation of feed intake (Allen et al., 2019), increasing NDF digestibility should not only elevate feed intake but also increase milk production. The lower diet CP might decrease rumen BCVFA producing, therefore limit the proliferation and activity of some fiber-degrading microbes, and finally decreased DMI and milk yield of cows in LCP group.

Conclusions

Although many studies have evaluated the independent effects of CP on lactation performance and metabolism of dairy cows, in this study, we observed parity and milk yield dependent responses of mid-lactation dairy cows to dietary CP reduction from 170 g/kg DM to 162 g/kg DM. For soybean and canola based diet with no special adjustment like amino acids supplementation, strategic diet CP reduction to 162 g/kg DM has no impact on lactation performance in primiparous cows even for relative high yield primiparous cows (mean ± SD, 45.3 ± 2.45 kg of milk/d), while decreased the overall milk yield by 0.58 kg/d in multiparous cows. Stratified analysis indicated that dietary CP reduction to 162 g/kg DM has no effects on multiparous cows with milk yield under 50 kg/d, but significantly decreased milk yield in multiparous cows over 50kg/d milk yield. To mitigate the yield-dependent trade-offs between diet protein supply and dairy cows metabolic requirement, caution is recommended when conducting diet CP adjustment under conventional feed ingredient across different parity and different milk yield level.
