Physical Indicators and Cutoff Values Associated with Improvement in Sarcopenia in Older Adults Undergoing Rehabilitation
Authors: Takuro Ohtsubo, Masafumi Nozoe, Katsuhiro Ueno, Takako Matsubara, Yosuke Morimoto
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectives:This study aimed to determine baseline physical indicators and their cutoff values associated with improvement in sarcopenia (IS) in older individuals undergoing rehabilitation.Methods:We retrospectively reviewed 343 older individuals with sarcopenia (median age = 81 years) admitted to a rehabilitation hospital between January 2020 and September 2023 after discharge from acute care hospitals. Sarcopenia was diagnosed using the 2019 Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia criteria; IS was defined as no longer meeting the diagnostic criteria at discharge. Baseline assessments included handgrip strength (HGS), gait speed (GS), skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), and the Short Physical Performance Battery. We used sex-wise multivariate logistic regression analyses to identify factors associated with IS and receiver operating characteristic curve analyses to determine cutoff values.Results:IS was seen in 16 males (17%) and 46 females (18%). Associated indicators were GS (p = 0.019; cutoff: 0.78 m/s) and SMI (p = 0.004; cutoff: 6.7 kg/m2) in males, and HGS (p < 0.001; cutoff: 15.0 kg) and SMI (p < 0.001; cutoff: 5.1 kg/m2) in females.Conclusions:Baseline SMI, plus GS in males and HGS in females, was associated with IS. These cutoff values may help to characterize individuals with different likelihoods of diagnostic improvement.

Introduction

Sarcopenia is a skeletal muscle disorder characterized by a decline in both skeletal muscle mass and function[1,2]. Consequently, it is associated with adverse health outcomes, such as falls and fractures[3,4], functional decline[4,5], long-term care dependency[6], and mortality[4,7–10]. Recent studies have shown that the prevalence of sarcopenia is particularly high in rehabilitation settings[11], which is linked to delayed recovery in independence in activities of daily living (ADLs) and poorer prognosis[12–14]. Yet many of these studies focus on specific disease populations, such as stroke patients, or relatively homogeneous clinical cohorts. Conversely, improvement in sarcopenia (IS) during hospitalization has been associated with better ADL outcomes and increased rates of discharge to home[15], underscoring the clinical importance of addressing sarcopenia in the rehabilitation settings. Despite its relevance, although previous studies have examined sarcopenia-related physical indicators and functional outcomes in rehabilitation settings, the physical indicators associated with IS-defined as a change in sarcopenia diagnostic status among diverse older patients in rehabilitation setting-have not been sufficiently investigated.

The following key physical indicators are commonly used to diagnose sarcopenia: skeletal muscle mass index (SMI), muscle strength indicated by handgrip strength (HGS), lower limb function measured using gait speed (GS), and the Short Physical Performance Battery (SPPB)[2]. We hypothesized that individuals whose admission values are close to, but below, the diagnostic criteria will be highly likely to be classified as IS. Furthermore, exploratory cutoff value may provide cohort-specific reference points for stratifying individuals based on IS potential.

Therefore, the aim of this study was to explore admission-time physical indicators that predict IS during rehabilitation among older individuals with sarcopenia. Additionally, we aimed to explore the optimal cutoff values for each indicator to facilitate early clinical decision-making.

Materials and Methods

Study design and patient selection criteria

In this retrospective cohort study, we included medically stable individuals with musculoskeletal (e.g., hip fracture, compression fracture, and artificial joint replacement), neuromuscular (e.g., stroke, traumatic brain injury, and spinal cord disorders), or other conditions (e.g., cardiovascular and respiratory diseases) who were transferred from acute care hospitals to a 100-bedded rehabilitation hospital in Japan between January 2020 and September 2023. Individuals were excluded if they met any of the following criteria: age under 65 years, having premorbid dependent gait, pacemaker implantation, limb loss, excessive fluid retention that may impair accurate bioelectrical impedance analysis (BIA) assessment, a consciousness disorder or cognitive dysfunction, or refusing consent to undergo the required assessments. Excessive fluid retention was determined based on the clinical judgment of a physical therapist or occupational therapist and documented in the medical record (e.g., clinically evident edema). No individuals were receiving intravenous fluid therapy at the time of BIA assessment. In addition, patients who did not have sarcopenia at admission were excluded from the analysis. This study is reported in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines (Table S1). Consent for participation was obtained using the opt-out method; opt-out information was prominently posted and announced throughout the hospital and its website, allowing participants to choose not to participate.

Rehabilitation

During their stay at the rehabilitation facility, all patients underwent tailored rehabilitation programs to improve independence in ADLs based on their individual functional and performance limitations. The rehabilitation sessions were provided daily for two to three hours per day and included range of motion exercises, muscle strength training, standing and walking exercises, swallowing therapy, cognitive training, and ADL practice. Nutritional status was managed by a registered dietitian and the nutrition support team according to individual condition.

Data collection

Baseline data, including age, sex, and body mass index (BMI), were extracted for all patients from the hospital’s electronic medical records; additionally, information on the primary reason for admission, the Charlson Comorbidity Index (CCI), and length of stay (LOS) in acute care hospitals was collected.

For all patients, the following assessments were conducted within three days of admission and within three days prior to discharge: HGS, GS using a 10-meter walk test, SPPB, SMI using BIA, and the Functional Independence Measure (FIM)[16], including its motor domain (FIM-M), cognitive domain (FIM-C), and total score (FIM-T), and the Mini Nutritional Assessment-Short Form (MNA-SF)[17]. All assessments were performed by trained physical therapists or occupational therapists as part of routine clinical care.

Diagnosis of sarcopenia

Using the criteria proposed by the Asian Working Group for Sarcopenia (AWGS) 2019, sarcopenia was defined as the presence of low muscle mass plus either low muscle strength or low physical function, or the presence of all three conditions[2]. All assessments were conducted within three days of admission to the rehabilitation facility and within three days prior to discharge.

Skeletal muscle mass of the limbs was estimated from the fat-free mass obtained via BIA using the InBody S10 device (InBody, Tokyo, Japan)[13,18]; all measurements were performed in the supine position three hours after lunch. Accordingly, SMI was calculated by dividing skeletal muscle mass (kg) by height-squared (m2). The cutoff value for determining sarcopenia was < 7.0 kg/m2 for males and < 5.7 kg/m2 for females[2].

Muscle strength in terms of HGS was measured using a digital dynamometer (Grip-D T.K.K.5401, Takei Kiki Kogyo, Japan). HGS was assessed with both arms extended at the sides in a standing or seated position; the highest value of three trials was recorded. In individuals with hemiplegia, measurements were taken from the non-paralyzed hand. Low muscle strength was defined as < 28 kg for males and < 18 kg for females[2].

Physical function was evaluated using the SPPB or GS. Low physical function was defined as an SPPB score ≤ 9 and/or GS < 1.0 m/s[2]. When GS was measurable, low physical function was defined as meeting the AWGS 2019 cut-off for either GS or the SPPB score. When GS was not measurable, low physical function was determined solely based on the SPPB score.

Outcomes

The primary outcome of this study was IS, operationally defined as not meeting the AWGS 2019 diagnostic criteria for sarcopenia at the time of discharge. Accordingly, patients were classified into an IS group or an NIS group based on the presence or absence of sarcopenia at discharge.

Statistical Analysis

Data were presented as medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) for nonparametric data and as percentages for categorical variables. Sex-based comparisons were performed using the Mann-Whitney U test, t-test, or Chi-square test, as appropriate.

Multivariable logistic regression analysis was performed separately for males and females using forced-entry methods. Age and primary reason for admission (neurological, musculoskeletal, or other) were included a priori as covariates in all models. Physical indicators at admission (HGS, GS, SPPB, and SMI) were evaluated as explanatory variables; to avoid multicollinearity and to clarify the independent association of each indicator with IS, separate regression models were constructed for each physical indicator, with one indicator entered per model together with the forced covariates. All variables included in the regression models were assessed at admission. For analyses involving GS, only participants with measurable GS values at admission were included. Participants classified as unable to walk were treated as having missing GS values and excluded from regression analyses and ROC analyses related to GS. In addition, a sensitivity analysis was performed by including baseline nutritional status assessed using the MNA-SF score as an additional covariate in the multivariate logistic regression model to examine the robustness of the association. Furthermore, given the limited number of IS in males, an additional sensitivity analysis using Firth penalized logistic regression was conducted to address potential small-sample bias and overfitting. Firth models were fitted separately for each physical indicator (HGS, GS, SPPB, and SMI), with age and primary reason for admission included as covariates. The results of the Firth models were compared with those obtained from the standard multivariable logistic regression analyses.

For each significant variable, the optimal cutoff value for predicting IS was identified using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve analysis conducted separately for both sexes. The optimal cutoff value for each indicator was determined using the Youden index, defined as the point maximizing the sum of sensitivity and specificity. As an additional exploratory analysis, internal validation using a bootstrap resampling procedure was performed for male participants to assess the internal validity of the discriminative performance of selected admission physical indicators. Apparent area under the ROC curve (AUC) was calculated in the original dataset, and internal validation was performed using 2,000 bootstrap resamples. Optimism was estimated as the difference between the bootstrap AUC and the apparent AUC, and optimism-corrected AUC were obtained by subtracting the mean optimism from the apparent AUC. Predictive performance was evaluated using sensitivity, specificity, and the AUC. A p-value of < 0.05 was considered statistically significant for all analyses. All statistical analyses were performed using EZR version 1.61 (Jichi Medical University, Tochigi, Japan), which is a graphical user interface for R (The R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria).

Results

A total of 1302 patients were admitted to our rehabilitation facility during the study period. Among them, 456 patients were excluded for the following reasons: age under 65 years (n = 273), premorbid dependent gait (n = 37), pacemaker implantation, limb loss, or excessive fluid retention that may impair accurate BIA assessment (n = 27), consciousness disorder or cognitive dysfunction (n = 101), and lack of consent to undergo the required assessments (n = 18). Additionally, 379 patients were excluded because they did not have sarcopenia. Thus, a total of 467 patients met the inclusion criteria; among these, 124 were excluded from the analysis due to transfer to another hospital (n = 57) and a lack of adequate data for outcome measures (n = 67). Finally, a total of 343 individuals were included in the final analysis. Figure 1 presents a flowchart for the participant selection procedure. Baseline characteristics of patients excluded due to missing outcome data were compared in Table S2. Overall, the two groups were largely comparable; however, patients with missing outcome data tended to have longer acute hospital stays and higher rates of unable to walk.

Identification of factors associated with IS

Table 3 presents the results of the multivariable logistic regression analysis for IS, by sex. In males, GS models included 65 participants with measurable GS at admission (IS = 9), whereas SMI models included 94 participants (IS = 16). In females, both HGS and SMI models included 249 participants (IS = 46). After adjusting for potential confounders, GS (odds ratio [OR]: 1.47, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.07–2.04; p = 0.019), and SMI (OR: 10.70, 95% CI: 2.15–53.00; p = 0.004) were found to be significantly associated with IS in males. In females, HGS (OR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.11–1.39; p < 0.001) and SMI (OR: 4.55, 95% CI: 1.95–10.60; p < 0.001) were significantly associated with IS. In sensitivity analyses adjusting for baseline nutritional status using MNA-SF score, the direction and magnitude of the association between baseline physical indicators and improvement in sarcopenia remained largely unchanged (Table S9). In an additional sensitivity analysis using Firth penalized logistic regression to address small-sample bias for males, GS and SMI remained significantly associated with IS, with effect estimates broadly consistent with those obtained from the standard multivariable logistic regression models (Table S10).

Discussion

In this study, we examined the association between baseline physical indicators and IS in older patients with sarcopenia undergoing rehabilitation. About 18% of the patients demonstrated IS during routine rehabilitation programs at the time of discharge from the rehabilitation facility. Regarding factors associated with IS status, GS and SMI in males, and HGS and SMI in females, showed significant associations.

The factors, GS, SMI, and HGS, were identified as being associated with IS, which is hardly surprising given that they are indicators used for diagnosing sarcopenia. Our results suggest that apart from diagnosis, these indicators may also serve as candidate markers associated with IS, as they represent reliable measures of muscle strength[2,19,20], physical performance[2,21,22], and mass[2]. Their clinical relevance suggests potential utility for rehabilitation planning for older individuals with sarcopenia.

It is important to acknowledge that the findings of this study should be interpreted considering the potential circularity between the outcome definition and the predictor variables. IS was defined as no longer meeting the AWGS 2019 diagnostic criteria at discharge, while the predictors assessed at admission comprised the same components used in that diagnostic algorithm. Consequently, the present analyses primarily describe associations between baseline severity, or proximity to diagnostic cutoff values, and the likelihood of crossing those thresholds during the rehabilitation period, rather than identifying independent biological or causal prognostic factors for recovery. Accordingly, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating, reflecting the relationship between baseline values of AWGS 2019–related indicators and subsequent diagnostic status.

Low rates of IS in the study cohort

Only 18% of the study patients (17% in males and 18% in females) achieved IS in this study, indicating low improvement rates. Previous studies from rehabilitation settings have also reported that sarcopenia improvement rates are not particularly high during hospitalization. A few studies of patients in the subacute phase after stroke have reported approximately 22%–30% prevalence of IS[15,23], suggesting that recovery from sarcopenia is generally limited.

However, the prevalence rates for IS reported in previous studies[15,23] were slightly higher than those observed in the present study. One possible explanation is that this study included older adults chronically prone to sarcopenia, such as those with fragility fractures unrelated to stroke and those with internal disorders[24–27]. In addition, malnutrition frequently coexists with sarcopenia in individuals undergoing rehabilitation[28] and may influence the likelihood of diagnostic improvement[29,30]. In sensitivity analyses adjusting for baseline nutritional status using the MNA-SF, the associations between baseline physical indicators and IS remained largely unchanged, suggesting that these associations were not solely explained by nutritional status. This cohort consists of individuals undergoing rehabilitation with diverse primary reasons for admission; therefore, these findings should be interpreted as reflecting average patterns across the heterogeneous rehabilitation population rather than disease-specific effects.

Taken together, these findings indicate that low IS rates in the present cohort may reflect the combined effects of baseline disease characteristics, physical impairment, and nutritional vulnerability, rather than any single determinant. In an exploratory sensitivity analysis applying the AWGS 2025 criteria, the prevalence of IS was lower than that observed using the AWGS 2019 criteria. Because no association or cut-off analyses were conducted under the AWGS 2025 framework, the applicability of the present findings and proposed cut-off values to the updated diagnostic criteria cannot be directly inferred.

Sex-based differences

Previous longitudinal studies have suggested that males experience a greater absolute decline in muscle strength with aging compared to females[31,32]. However, because males generally maintain larger reserves of muscle mass and strength[31] throughout life, functional performance indicators, such as GS, which reflects lower-limb strength, balance, and neuromuscular coordination[33–35], may be more strongly associated with recovery potential in this group. In contrast, females tend to have lower absolute levels of muscle mass and strength[31,32] compared to males, and even modest reductions in muscle strength may adversely affect their functional prognosis. Consistent with our findings, HGS has previously been reported to be strongly associated with functional outcomes in females[36,37]. Moreover, sex-related differences in anabolic capacity, such as the reduced albumin fractional synthesis rate observed in females[38], may partly contribute to diminished muscle protein synthesis and recovery potential in this population.

Additionally, baseline characteristics, including higher rates of neurological disorders and poorer nutritional status in males and higher rates of musculoskeletal disorders in females, may also have amplified these sex-based differences. These sex-specific associations are shown in the supplementary analyses (Table S5), where males with IS exhibited higher levels of SMI and GS, while females with IS showed not only greater SMI and HGS, but also better nutritional status and ADL function. Furthermore, descriptive analysis of physical indicators at discharge revealed that while normalization of SMI occurred at equivalent rates between males and females, males who achieved IS tended to do so primarily through recovery of physical function rather than normalization of muscle strength. In contrast, females showed more balanced improvements in both muscle strength and physical function (Table S7, S8). These findings support the idea that nutritional and functional background factors may influence the predictive power of different indicators in males and females; however, these factors cannot be fully elucidated. Given the limited number of IS cases, particularly the low number of male cases, analyses by sex should be interpreted as exploratory and hypothesis-generating. Any observed sex-specific associations should be considered preliminary rather than definitive. The stability of sex-specific estimates requires careful consideration, given the limited number of IS cases in males. The results of sensitivity analysis using Firth penalized logistic regression, while consistent with the standard logistic regression model, support the robustness of the association with GS and SMI despite the small event numbers (Table S10). Nevertheless, these findings should be interpreted as exploratory and require confirmation in larger cohorts.

SMI as a baseline indicator associated with IS for both sexes

SMI was a significant factor for both males and females, indicating that muscle mass forms the foundation of sarcopenia[2] and that maintaining an adequate level of muscle mass is a prerequisite for IS. Previous studies have shown that low muscle mass is strongly associated with reduced muscle strength[39,40] and physical function and predicts mobility limitations[41,42]. The SMI cutoff values identified in this study, i.e., ≥ 6.7 kg/m2 for males and ≥ 5.1 kg/m2 for females, were slightly lower than the diagnostic criteria (male: < 7.0 kg/m2, female: < 5.7 kg/m2)[2]. Especially in males, it exhibited high specificity (0.936), and a value < 6.7 kg/m2 was strongly associated with NIS, making it a practical indicator for identifying male patients in whom IS is difficult.

Considering that previous exercise programs in older adults with sarcopenia have reported improvements in appendicular skeletal muscle mass (ASM) / height[2] of approximately 0–0.38 kg/m2 [43], the cutoff value for males appears to lie within a range that is realistically achievable through intervention. In contrast, the cutoff value for females (5.1 kg/m2) is 0.6 kg/m2 below the diagnostic threshold (< 5.7 kg/m2), which may exceed the degree of improvement typically attainable through exercise alone. Therefore, while both the sensitivity and specificity of SMI for females were moderate, the cutoff value highlights that, in addition to exercise therapy, females require supplemental strategies like nutritional interventions to achieve IS.

Previous studies on stroke patients undergoing rehabilitation reported cutoff values for severe reductions in skeletal muscle mass as 5.6 kg/m2 for males and 4.6 kg/m2 for females[44]. Another study reported these values as 5.2 kg/m2 for males and 4.7 kg/m2 for females[45]. Although these values are lower than those identified in the present study, they were derived from other outcomes, such as improvements in ADL[44] and the occurrence of dysphagia[45]. In the present study, higher cutoff values were expected when predicting IS. Our cutoff values suggest a cohort-specific reference level of muscle mass associated with diagnostic improvement during rehabilitation.

GS as a baseline indicator associated with IS for males

GS was identified as a significant factor of IS in male participants. It is an indicator that comprehensively reflects lower limb muscle strength, balance, and neuromuscular coordination[33–35], and is widely used for predicting functional prognosis[22,46–48].

In the present study, the cutoff value for GS was obtained as ≥ 0.78 m/s. While it has high sensitivity (0.889) and is useful for screening for IS, its low specificity (0.554) limits its ability to distinguish IS cases from NIS cases. This indicates that GS may be used as an early screening indicator rather than as a definitive stratification tool and possesses practical value in identifying male patients who may warrant closer monitoring during rehabilitation.

Previous studies have reported similar threshold values of approximately 0.74–0.80 m/s[22,49,50] as necessary for maintaining independent walking and performing ADLs, supporting the clinical plausibility of our findings. In comparison with the diagnostic threshold for sarcopenia (<1.0 m/s)[2], this stricter threshold may more appropriately reflect the mobility levels required for recovery rather than merely serving as a diagnostic criterion, thereby corroborating its clinical validity.

HGS as a baseline indicator associated with IS in females

Unlike males, HGS was determined as a significant factor of IS in female participants. Compared with males, females generally have less muscle mass[51–53], and decline in muscle strength have been reported to exerting a stronger impact on functional prognosis than muscle mass alone[36,37,54–56]. Several studies have shown that HGS can independently predict ADL improvement and functional outcomes at discharge[14,37,57–60], which is consistent with our results.

The cutoff value for HGS determined in the present study was ≥ 15.0 kg. This value showed high sensitivity (0.804) and may be useful for its low likelihood of missing potential IS; however, its specificity was only 0.640, indicating limited accuracy in identifying NIS cases. Furthermore, HGS may not be suitable as a direct intervention target, but rather serves the role of a primarily predictive indicator, especially useful for screening cases with potential to show IS.

Previous studies involving stroke patients undergoing rehabilitation have reported a cutoff value of 9.0 kg for severe HGS weakness[44,45]. Although this value is lower than that identified in the present study, it was based on other functional outcomes, such as improvement in ADL and the occurrence of dysphagia, similar to those used for SMI. In the context of predicting IS, a higher cutoff value was anticipated. Previous studies including community-dwelling older individuals have reported a cutoff value of 16.0 kg for predicting mobility and ADL impairment[57,61]. Thus, the cutoff value for improvement clearly varies depending on the outcome; however, the cutoff value identified in this study supports the role of HGS as a practical representation of muscle strength required to achieve IS.

Clinical implications

Based on our study results, GS showed a stronger association with IS in males, whereas HGS showed a stronger association in females in this cohort. SMI may also help identify characteristics of individuals, particularly males, for whom IS difficult to achieve through standard rehabilitation care. Such individuals may warrant clinical monitoring with respect to nutritional status[62,63] and muscle-related factors[62,64] during rehabilitation. Evaluating these physical indicators at admission suggests they may serve as potential early triage candidate markers when considering rehabilitation strategies. The proposed cut-off values derived from ROC analyses should be interpreted with caution, as they were derived and evaluated within the same cohort and may therefore be optimistically biased. An exploratory bootstrap validation in males showed that optimism-corrected AUC were similar to the apparent AUC (Table S11), although confidence intervals remained wide, underscoring the uncertainty of discriminative performance in small samples. Internal validation using bootstrap resampling was performed only in males because of the limited number of IS events, and the discriminative performance in females was not internally validated. Accordingly, these thresholds should be regarded as exploratory and cohort-specific reference points rather than definitive clinical decision limits and require external validation.

Study limitations

First, it was conducted as a retrospective single-center study with a relatively small sample size; therefore, causal relationships could not be established, and the generalizability of the findings remains limited. Second, exercise and nutritional interventions were not standardized; also, adherence to these interventions was not assessed, which may have influenced the outcomes. Third, the primary reasons for admission and nutritional status at baseline differed between the sexes. Although the primary reason for admission was included as a covariate in the multiple logistic regression model, these baseline differences may have influenced the results. Fourth, nutritional status was assessed using the MNA-SF; however, a formal diagnosis of malnutrition based on the Global Leadership Initiative on Malnutrition criteria could not be reliably reconstructed, and residual confounding by nutritional status cannot be excluded. Fifth, the study population comprised diverse primary reasons for admission including neurological, musculoskeletal, and other medical conditions. Although primary reasons for admission were included as covariates, subgroup analyses based on diagnostic categories and interaction analyses were not performed due to limited sample sizes and event counts within each subgroup. Therefore, the results of this study, including the proposed cutoff values, should be interpreted as average associations within the mixed rehabilitation cohort rather than disease-specific prognostic markers. Sixth, because of the limited number of IS cases, key factors such as functional status, comorbidities, and length of hospital stay were not fully adjusted for in the multivariable models, and residual confounding may have biased the estimated associations. In addition, the events-per-variable ratio remained suboptimal, particularly in males, raising concerns about overfitting and the stability of the sex-stratified estimates. In addition, GS could not be measured in a subset of individuals with severe mobility limitations; although physical function was assessed using the SPPB for diagnostic purposes, these individuals were excluded from GS-specific regression and ROC analyses, which may have introduced measurement-related selection bias. Furthermore, although individuals with clinically apparent excessive fluid retention were excluded based on clinical judgment, residual measurement bias related to hydration status in BIA assessments cannot be completely excluded. Finally, this study focused on a dichotomous diagnostic outcome based on the AWGS 2019 criteria and did not evaluate continuous changes in individual components; therefore, clinically meaningful improvements that did not lead to reclassification out of the sarcopenia category may have been missed. In addition, although clinically meaningful cutoff values were identified, their external validity remains uncertain and requires confirmation in independent cohorts.

Conclusion

Baseline SMI, along with GS in males and HGS in females, is associated with IS in older patients with sarcopenia undergoing rehabilitation. These sex-specific physical indicators may serve as candidate markers for early stratification of individuals at risk of NIS and might be useful for informing the consideration of tailored interventions. The identified cut-off values should be considered hypothesis-generating and require validation in independent cohorts before clinical implementation.
