HLA Class II Protein Expression Regulation Is Strongly Linked to Cis‐Acting SNPs
Authors: Nicolas Vince, Veron Ramsuran, Mathias Viard, Jeremy Martinson, Steven Wolinsky, Shehnaz Hussain, Eric C. Seaberg, Todd T. Brown, Niamh Spence, Chloe L. Thio, James J. Goedert, W. Keith Hoots, Gregory D. Kirk, Mary Carrington
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTAllele‐specific variability in HLA class I gene expression levels has been associated with many diseases. Although expression variation at the allelic level has not been well‐characterised for the HLA class II genes, differential expression of these genes, as marked by single nucleotide polymorphisms (SNPs), has been implicated in the outcome of several diseases. Here, we measured cell surface expression levels of distinct HLA‐DR, HLA‐DQ and HLA‐DP allotypes in 175 healthy European American donors using locus‐specific antibodies. We identified allotype‐specific variation in the intrinsic cell surface expression levels. To further characterise genetic associations with differential protein expression levels of the HLA class II molecules, we performed a genome‐wide association study (GWAS). This showed that surface expression levels of HLA‐DR, HLA‐DQ and HLA‐DP associated most significantly with rs28383323 (p = 3.9 × 10−10), rs281860696 (p = 2.2 × 10−5) and rs3128928 (p = 1.3 × 10−8), respectively. Two SNPs, rs3128927 and rs9277534, were previously reported to associate with HLA‐DP protein expression and hepatitis B virus (HBV) recovery/persistence. We show that rs3128928, which resides in the COL11A2P1 pseudogene, associates most significantly with HLA‐DP protein expression, whereas the neighbouring rs3128927 associates most significantly with HBV recovery/persistence. Thus, protein expression levels of HLA‐DR, DQ and DP appear to be controlled by cis‐elements that do not mark specific alleles. This finding may help to distinguish disease associations caused by specific HLA allelic effects vs. protein expression levels.

Introduction

The HLA class I and II genes are central to the acquired and innate immune responses through presentation of antigenic epitopes to T cells. The extreme polymorphism of these genes affects the function of the molecules they encode, and accounts for the over‐representation of disease associations with these loci relative to the rest of the genome [1, 2, 3]. Recent studies have focused on the potential effects of HLA expression on disease outcomes [4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10], demonstrating that specific HLA class I and class II expression levels associate with many types of disorders, including viral, autoimmune, neurological and the success of haematopoietic cell transplantation.

The HLA‐A and HLA‐C class I genes have been shown to vary in an allele dependent manner based on mRNA and cell surface levels [4, 11], respectively. In contrast, HLA‐B shows less allele‐specific variability in expression at the mRNA level [12, 13]. Within the HLA class I region, a microRNA (miR148a), a transcription factor (Oct‐1), DNA methylation, coding region variants and alternative polyadenylation sites were identified as regulators of allotype‐specific expression levels of HLA‐C and HLA‐A [11, 14, 15, 16, 17]. Identifying factors responsible for HLA gene regulation could potentially serve as target sites for therapeutic intervention against HLA associated diseases.

Classical HLA class II genes (HLA‐DR, HLA‐DQ and HLA‐DP) are expressed on the surface of antigen‐presenting cells, including B cells, monocytes, macrophages, dendritic cells and thymic epithelial cells involved in T cell selection. HLA class II genes are interferon gamma inducible, leading to their expression on additional cell subtypes, such as activated T cells [18, 19], fibroblasts, astrocytes, endothelial cells, epithelial cells and many tumour cell types [20]. HLA class II gene expression is finely controlled by the MHC class II transactivator (CIITA) protein, encoded by the CIITA gene located on chromosome 16 [21]. Protein–protein interactions between CIITA and other transcription factors allow binding of the complex to the proximal core promoter region of the HLA class II genes, inducing their transcriptional activation [19]. Several studies have demonstrated that a mutation within the CIITA promoter region that results in diminished CIITA expression leads to the development of Bare Lymphocyte Syndrome (BLS), a condition characterised by the absence of HLA class II proteins on the cell surface [21, 22, 23, 24, 25]. While CIITA is essential for HLA class II gene expression [21], fine regulation of HLA class II expression probably occurs at the gene or even allele level through mechanisms involving promoter polymorphisms, epigenetic modifications, response to activation factors, and so forth.

The demonstration of differential allotype‐specific expression levels encoded by the HLA class II loci is incomplete at best [6, 26, 27, 28, 29], in spite of multiple reports implicating an impact of HLA class II expression levels on human disease based on SNP (single nucleotide polymorphisms) genotypes [5, 6, 7, 9, 10]. The rs9277534 (496A/G) variant located within the 3′ untranslated region (UTR) of HLA‐DPB1 was observed to associate both with recovery from hepatitis B virus (HBV) infection [7, 30] and expression of this gene [7], where the GG genotype associated with higher levels of HLA‐DP surface expression and increased risk of HBV persistence. Subsequently, it was shown that recipients of haematopoietic cell transplantation who carried low expression HLA‐DPB1 alleles based on rs9277534 genotype were at increased risk of developing GvHD if the donor carried a high expression genotype [5]. Another study of 16 individuals identified several loci that associated with higher HLA class II gene expression on a haplotype that associates with risk for systemic lupus erythematosus (SLE) [6]. Thus, natural variation in HLA class II expression level may affect disease risk or progression, calling for a systematic measurement of baseline variation in levels of HLA class II molecules.

In this study, we measured the cell surface expression levels of the three classical HLA class II molecules, HLA‐DR, ‐DQ and ‐DP, in healthy European American (EA) donors. Using genome‐wide genotyping, we identified SNPs significantly associated with HLA‐DR, HLA‐DQ and HLA‐DP surface expression levels. The top SNP marking HLA‐DP protein expression associated with HBV persistence along with others previously identified in this same cohort. These data serve as a foundation for studies relating to differential HLA class II protein expression and the impact of this characteristic on human health and disease.

Methods

HLA Class II Surface Expression Levels Using Flow Cytometry

Cell surface expression levels of HLA‐DR, HLA‐DP and HLA‐DQ were measured using flow cytometry. A total of 175 healthy EA donors were recruited from the Frederick National Laboratory for Cancer Research Blood Donor Program. An informed consent was obtained from these donors to participate in the research. Peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMCs) were isolated from fresh blood, and counted using a cell suspension aliquot mixed with trypan blue and an automated cell counter. Immediately after processing, one million PBMCs were stained with the following cocktail of antibodies: anti‐CD3 BV605 (clone UCHT1, BioLegend #300460), anti‐CD19 PE‐Cy5 (clone HIB19, BD Biosciences #555414), anti‐HLA‐DR Pacific Blue (clone L243, BioLegend #307633), anti‐HLA‐DP PE (clone B7/21, BD Biosciences #566825), anti‐HLA‐DQ Alexa Fluor 700 (clone SPV‐L3, Abcam #ab23632). Specificities for HLA class II allotypes were tested using commercially available beads (One Lambda), each coated with 86 individual HLA class II allotypes as previously described [4]. Cells were stained in the dark at 4°C for 20 min, washed and fixed with 300 μl paraformaldehyde (PFA). Samples were run on the BD LSRFortessa and analysed using FlowJo software. Calibration and instrument settings were maintained throughout the duration of the study (Table S1). To assess the cell surface expression of HLA‐DR, HLA‐DP and HLA‐DQ, we employed the following gating strategy: first, singlet cells were selected based on forward scatter area (FSC‐A) versus forward scatter height (FSC‐H) to exclude cell doublets and aggregates. Next, we gated on CD3− CD19+ cells to identify the B cell population specifically. Within this gated population, we analysed the surface expression levels of HLA‐DR, HLA‐DP and HLA‐DQ.

SNP Genotyping and Quality Control

DNA extraction (QIAamp DNA Blood Maxi Kit, Qiagen) was performed on total blood according to the manufacturer's instructions, from a total of 231 donor samples obtained through the Frederick National Laboratory Blood Donor Program. An informed consent was obtained from these donors to participate in the research. Demographic information from these donors, including age, sex, height and weight, was provided. The samples were genotyped for whole genome SNPs using the Illumina OmniXpress 700K. Quality controls on individuals and SNPs were performed using Plink [31]. Nine individuals were excluded based on low call rate (N = 1, ‐‐mind 0.05), wrong sex attribution (N = 3, ‐‐check‐sex), or relatedness (parent–child or full siblings; N = 5, ‐‐genome, PI_HAT > 0.125). For each of the 5 pairs of individuals with relatedness, samples with the highest call rate were included in the study; and 562,826 SNPs from a total of 716,503 passed quality control. SNPs were excluded based on the following criteria: non‐autosomal (N = 19,533), deviation from Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium (N = 1,236, ‐‐hwe 0.001), low genotyping (N = 72,044, ‐‐geno 0.02), or low frequency (minor allele frequency [MAF] < 0.01, N = 72,924, ‐‐maf 0.01). Some SNPs displayed both low genotyping and low frequency thresholds. The study population was stratified using Eigensoft [32], which excluded another 11 individuals that were outliers from European ancestry. We used smartpca default parameters to exclude outliers (‐m default is 5, which corresponds to the maximum number of outlier removal iterations; ‐s default is 6.0, corresponding to number of standard deviations that an individual must exceed to be removed as an outlier). All outliers were removed after 3 iterations. Overall, a total of 211 individuals were included in the analysis. SNP imputation was performed using SHAPEIT [33] and IMPUTE2 [34] with data from the 1000 genomes phase III [35]. SHAPEIT prephasing used default parameters with 1000GP_Phase3.tgz file downloaded from IMPUTE2 website as input map. From these prephased files, IMPUTE2 imputed SNPs using default parameters: chunks of 5 Mb, 1000GP_Phase3 hap and legend files. We obtained 11,411,299 SNPs after imputation, including 9,836,578 with high confidence (info > 0.8).

Genome‐Wide Association Analysis (GWAS)

Of the 211 individuals for whom reliable SNP genotyping was generated, HLA‐DR, HLA‐DQ, HLA‐DP protein expression data were available for 149, 142 and 148 individuals, respectively. Genome‐wide complex trait analysis (GCTA) is a software package well suited to perform GWAS [36]. Here we applied a mixed linear model association (mlma) analysis to test HLA protein expression as a continuous trait using a dense genetic relationship matrix (GRM) focusing only on MAF > 0.05. The GRM estimates relationships between individuals and is then used to account for population structure in the GWAS mlma model. Body mass index (BMI) was included in the model as a covariate since it can have an impact on expression level [37]. We did not include age or sex in our statistical model as they did not change our results. We generated quantile quantile (QQ) plots to examine the log10 of observed and expected p values from GWAS results; the lambda value was calculated in R as lambda <‐ lm(pval_obs~pval_exp). The threshold of significance used in this study is the widely accepted p value of < 5 × 10−8. Finally, we performed conditional analysis (using the same mlma GCTA model) with the goal of determining if any other SNPs were independently associated with HLA class II protein expression by including the top hits at the corresponding locus as covariates. We employed this strategy only for HLA‐DR protein expression GWAS as HLA‐DP had only 1 SNP reaching our threshold of significance and HLA‐DQ had none.

HLA Genotyping

The HLA loci were genotyped using the sequence‐based typing method described previously [7, 38]. Exon 2 of each locus, HLA‐DRB1, HLA‐DPB1 and HLA‐DQB1, was selectively amplified with locus‐specific primers. The amplicons were sequenced using the ABI BigDye Terminator cycle sequencing kit version 1.1 and ABI3730xl DNA analyser (Applied Biosystems). HLA sequences were analysed using Assign software (Conexio Genomics), which matches experimental data to known allele sequences from the International Immunogenetics Information System database (http://www.ebi.ac.uk/imgt/hla).

Linkage Disequilibrium (LD)

LD r 2 was calculated with Haploview [39]. Haploview has a distance limit of 500 kb for calculating LD score. To evaluate the relationship between our lead GWAS hits and HLA allotypes, we performed haplotype estimation using Haploview [39].

Allotype Association With HLA Class II Surface Expression

To evaluate the significance of intrinsic allotype‐specific expression levels for HLA‐DP, HLA‐DQ and HLA‐DR, ANOVA tests were performed in R, with the aov() function comparing the level of protein expression at each locus for each individual as the dependent variable, and the presence of the allotype at the locus as categorical independent variables. To further assess the significance of the p value retrieved with the original data, permutation tests were carried out. For each locus, the HLA typing of the individuals was kept unaltered, but the levels of expression were permuted across the individuals. We performed an ANOVA test for each permutation, using 1000 permutations, and collected the p values. We plotted the p value distributions in Figure S1.

Transcription Factor Binding Site Prediction

The AliBaba (2.0) online prediction tool [40] was used to identify the presence of putative transcription factor binding sites (TFBS) for the top GWAS hits associating with HLA class II protein expression levels. We included 20 bp upstream and 20 bp downstream of each SNP for each of the two alleles. We also included every described SNP in this 40 bp sequence and the different haplotypes. We calculated the haplotypes and their frequencies using Haploview and the 1000 Genomes CEU population [35, 39]. Potential disruption of binding sites was noted for each SNP genotype. Default parameters were used with the threshold set as follows: parsim sequence similarity at 50, matrix width at 10 bp, minimum number of sites for the matrix build at 4, similarity of sequence to matrix at 1% and factor class level 4.

SNP Genotyping in HBV Disease Cohorts

A total of 504 European American donors were obtained from several cohorts: (i) Multicenter Aids Cohort Study (MACS) [41], (ii) Haemophilia Growth and Development Study (HGDS) [42], (iii) Multicenter Haemophilia Cohort Study (MHCS) [43] and (iv) the AIDS linked to intravenous experience (ALIVE) [44]. The individuals were derived from a nested case–control study composed of subjects with persistent HBV infection (n = 184) matched with subjects who recovered from HBV infection (n = 320) as previously described [7]. TaqMan genotyping was performed for the top SNPs identified in our protein expression GWAS marking HLA‐DP protein expression (rs3128927 and rs3128928), as well as the previously described HBV associated SNP rs9277534 [5, 7, 30].

Results

Allotype‐Specific HLA Class II Cell Surface Expression

Cell surface expression levels of HLA‐DR, HLA‐DP and HLA‐DQ were examined on B cells from 175 healthy EA donors. The anti‐HLA‐DR, anti‐HLA‐DP and anti‐HLA‐DQ antibodies used in this study had previously been shown to bind with equal affinity to a set of allotypes corresponding to each locus [45]. Plots showing the continuous distribution of allotype expression levels for HLA‐DR, HLA‐DQ and HLA‐DP suggested an allotype‐specific association with expression at each locus (Figure 1). We performed an ANOVA statistical test to evaluate these associations, each of which was significant: HLA‐DR, p = 2.6 × 10−11; HLA‐DQ, p = 3.5 × 10−3; and HLA‐DP, p = 1.4 × 10−7. To validate these associations, we performed 1000 permutations of expression levels across individuals while keeping their genotype unchanged. We then compared the original p values with the permuted p values (Figure S1). Original HLA‐DR and HLA‐DP ANOVA p values were lower than any of the permuted p values and the original HLA‐DQ p value was in the lowest 1% of permuted p values. These data indicate that intrinsic, differential allotype‐specific expression levels occur across each of these HLA class II loci.

Intra‐individual correlations in protein expression levels between pairs of HLA class II loci were determined, showing a positive correlation for all three comparisons: HLA‐DR versus HLA‐DQ (R = 0.45, p = 8.6 × 10−10, Figure 2A), HLA‐DP versus HLA‐DR (R = 0.67, p = 7.1 × 10−24, Figure 2B) and HLA‐DP versus HLA‐DQ (R = 0.43, p = 6.2 × 10−9, Figure 2C). The HLA class II genes are regulated by a common factor, CIITA [21], so the correlations between the class II cell surface expression levels are not unexpected. These correlations are highly significant, although not particularly strong (R = 0.43–0.67), and suggest that CIITA may be regulating the HLA class II loci within a given individual in a somewhat uniform manner, which concurs with the highly conserved CIITA binding site (across both the HLA class II loci and across individuals). As the CIITA binding site is not polymorphic (it is constituted of the highly conserved SXY module [21]), it cannot account for differential class II expression levels across individuals. Taken together, these data indicate that there are additional locus‐specific regulators of differential HLA class II expression levels within or near each locus.

HLA Class II Region SNP Associations With HLA‐DR, HLA‐DP and HLA‐DQ Surface Expression Levels

We next performed GWAS to identify genetic variants that associate with HLA‐DR, HLA‐DQ and HLA‐DP protein expression levels across 149, 142 and 148 individuals, respectively. Using a mixed model analysis, HLA class II protein expression levels were tested as continuous variables for associations with 9,836,578 genome‐wide SNPs. QQ plots were generated to examine the observed and expected p values for associations between genome‐wide SNPs and the continuum of surface expression levels corresponding to the 3 HLA class II genes (Figure S2). The QQ plots do not show inflation of observed p values, based on lambda values, a measure of correlation between the expected and observed p values, for each HLA class II gene being close to 1.

The only SNPs reaching genome‐wide significance for HLA‐DR and HLA‐DP protein expression were located within the MHC on chromosome 6, and no SNP reached genome‐wide significance for HLA‐DQ (Figure S3). Protein expression of HLA‐DR associated significantly with several SNPs located between the HLA‐DRB1 and HLA‐DQA1 genes (Figure 3), including the top hit, rs28383323 (p = 3.9 × 10−10, β = −0.45, SE = 0.07; Figure 3), mapping 36.4 kb upstream of the HLA‐DRB1 transcription start site (TSS) (Figure S4). Conditioning on the rs28383323 variant, no other SNP was significantly associated with HLA‐DR protein expression. The top hit after conditional analysis on the rs28383323 variant was rs9274722, which maps within the HLA‐DQB1 promoter (p = 4.9 × 10−6; Figure S5).

The peak signal of association with HLA‐DQ protein expression was rs281860696 (p = 2.2 × 10−5, β = 0.60, SE = 0.14; Figure 3), but it did not reach the genome significance threshold. This SNP maps to the HLA‐DQB1 gene promoter region, 428 bp upstream of the HLA‐DQB1 TSS (Figure S4). A single genome‐wide significant variant, rs3128928, marked HLA‐DP surface expression level (p = 1.3 × 10−8, β = 0.54, SE = 0.09; Figure 3). This SNP maps approximately 17.1 kb downstream of the HLA‐DPB1 3′ UTR between HLA‐DPB1 and the HLA‐DPB2 pseudogene (Figure S4) and is in strong LD with other SNPs in close proximity (Figure 3), none of which reach genome‐wide significance. Thus, conditional analysis was not performed.

Finally, we investigated 8 SNPs previously reported as significantly associated with HLA class II differential expression [7, 9, 10, 29]. In our study, we could not replicate a significant association genome‐wide between these 8 SNPs and HLA class II differential expression (Figure S6). Moreover, they show only moderate to low LD with the top hit SNPs of our study, which may explain, in part, the lack of replication.

Putative Transcription Factor Binding Sites Regulating HLA Class II Genes

The Alibaba (2.0) online software [40] predicts TFBS, based on direct binding studies, and provides specific locations of the binding sites within the sequence. Alibaba was used to determine whether the top SNPs associating with HLA‐DR, HLA‐DQ and HLA‐DP protein expression levels might occur within TFBS. The SNP marking HLA‐DR protein expression level (rs28383323) is predicted to occur either within a GATA‐1 binding site (Figure 4) or an Sp1 binding site (Figure S7) only when the G variant is present, depending on the neighbour alternative SNP (rs28362368) genotype A or G, respectively. Whereas, the rs28383323A variant results in a disrupted binding site. The rs28383323A allele is present on haplotypes with HLA‐DRB1*01:01, HLA‐DRB1*07:01 and HLA‐DRB1*14:01 alleles (Figure S8), which show the lowest HLA‐DR cell surface expression level (Figure 1). These data suggest that differential binding of GATA‐1 or Sp1 upstream of the HLA‐DRB1 locus may be responsible, at least in part, for variation in HLA‐DR protein expression levels.

The top variant marking HLA‐DQ protein expression level (rs281860696), which did not reach genome‐wide significance, is predicted to occur within a CEBPa or an Antp binding site in the presence of the A allele, but not when the G allele is present (Figures 4 and S7). The rs281860696A allele is included in haplotypes with HLA‐DQB1*03:01 (Figure S8), which shows the second highest HLA‐DQ protein expression level (Figure 1). If the variant in the CEBPa binding site impacts HLA‐DQ expression, it is clearly working in concert with other variants that alter expression of this gene, as it is not present in the same haplotype as HLA‐DQB1*05:03, the highest expressed HLA‐DQ allotype. Further studies are necessary to confirm this model. Finally, the single genome‐wide significant variant marking HLA‐DP protein expression (rs3128928) is predicted to occur within an Sp1 binding site, but both the A and C alleles are predicted to allow Sp1 binding (Figures 4 and S7). If indeed Sp1 is capable of binding both rs3128928 variants within the predicted binding site, then this variant cannot account for the differential expression of HLA‐DP via an Sp1 mechanism, in spite of the observation that this is the only SNP associating with HLA‐DP protein expression with genome‐wide significance (Figure 3).

SNPs Associating With Both HLA‐DP Protein Expression and HBV Persistence

Previous data have shown that the rs9277534 variant marks HLA‐DP RNA and protein expression levels, and also associates with spontaneous recovery/persistence of HBV infection, suggesting that HLA‐DP expression levels may be involved in HBV immune response [7]. The top variant associating with HLA‐DP protein expression in the present study, rs3128928, and another variant strongly associating with HLA‐DP protein expression that did not quite reach genome‐wide significance, rs3128927 (p = 1.8 × 10−7, β = 0.48, SE = 0.09; Figure S9), are both located downstream of the HLA‐DPB1 gene, 324 base pairs from one another. The association of rs3128927 with HLA‐DP protein expression is no longer significant when conditioning on rs3128928 (p = 0.38, β = 0.08, SE = 0.10). The rs9277534 SNP previously associated with HLA‐DP protein expression was confirmed in the present study (p = 3.6 × 10−6, β = 0.41, SE = 0.09; Figure S9). However, its association with HLA‐DP protein expression does not hold when conditioning on rs3128928 (p = 0.68, β = 0.04, SE = 0.09). These three SNPs are in pairwise LD with one another within our cohort as follows: rs3128928 and rs3128927 (r 2 = 0.67), rs3128928 and rs9277534 (r 2 = 0.60), rs3128927 and rs9277534 (r 2 = 0.89) (Figure S6).

Given the previous association between rs9277534 and HBV recovery/persistence and the lack of an association of rs3128927 and rs9277534 with HLA‐DP protein expression after conditioning on rs3128928, we tested the effect of each of the three SNPs on HBV disease in 504 individuals from four cohorts (see Section 2). rs9277534 had already been typed and tested in the same population [7]. The rs3128927 variant showed the strongest effect on HBV persistence versus recovery (p = 2.8 × 10−4, OR 4.8 [2.1–11.7], MAF = 0.26) in a recessive genetic model (genotype TT vs. others; Table 1), and this association is even stronger when conditioning on rs3128928 (p = 8.0 × 10−5, OR 7.5 [2.9–21.4]). The previously reported variant, rs9277534, also showed strong associations (p = 4.1 × 10−4, OR 3.5 [1.8–7.3], MAF = 0.29) with HBV recovery/persistence, and remains significant when conditioning on rs3128927 (p = 1.4 × 10−3, OR 4.0 [1.7–9.7]) (Table 1). The top hit SNP associating with HLA‐DP protein expression, rs3128928, showed a weaker association with HBV recovery/persistence (p = 0.02, OR 3.0 [1.2–7.6], MAF = 0.22; Table 1), which is no longer significant when conditioning on rs3128927 (p = 0.08, OR 2.6 [0.9–7.5]). Moreover, the haplotype composed of rs3128928_A, the SNP allele associating most strongly with high HLA‐DP protein expression, and rs3128927_T, the SNP allele associating most strongly with HBV persistence, did not show a significant association with HBV recovery/persistence (p = 0.39, OR 1.2 [0.8–1.6], frequency = 0.22). These data raise the possibility that HLA‐DP protein expression levels may have some impact on outcome to HBV infection, but that another more prominent mechanism is driving disease outcome.

Discussion

The HLA region has a significant impact on numerous human diseases mainly due to the central role these molecules play on the immune response and to the extensive polymorphic nature of these genes [2, 3]. In recent years, studies have demonstrated a new layer of complexity, which highlights the association between differential HLA expression and disease outcomes [4, 5, 6, 7, 11, 14]. Therefore, determining the expression variation at the allelic level and identifying factors responsible for gene regulation are important for understanding disease associations with HLA class II. This study interrogated the surface expression levels of the three classical class II genes using a GWAS approach, which identified the SNPs most strongly marking protein expression of each locus. Further, a novel SNP (rs3128927) downstream of the HLA‐DPB1 locus was shown to associate more strongly with HBV persistence than any previously reported SNPs.

The positive correlations in protein expression levels between pairs of class II genes (Figure 2) indicate that they are coregulated, likely due to the common co‐regulatory effect of CIITA on each of the classical HLA class II genes, as demonstrated previously [22, 45, 47, 48]. Interestingly, the intra‐individual correlation was strongest between HLA‐DR and HLA‐DP, even though HLA‐DR and HLA‐DQ are located in much closer physical proximity on chromosome 6. This suggests that the quantitative regulation of HLA class II molecules is not strictly governed by genomic distance. Rather, this could support a model of complex 3D chromatin organisation where distal elements coordinate the expression of HLA‐DR and HLA‐DP. Such interactions could allow for synchronised expression levels while allowing the intervening HLA‐DQ locus to maintain a more independent regulatory profile. This finding adds a new layer of complexity to our understanding of how the MHC region manages the simultaneous but distinct expression of its genes. We have also identified novel markers that may be responsible, in part, for the differential intra‐locus expression levels at each class II locus. Variation in HLA class II expression levels could potentially affect their function beyond the quantity of antigen presentation. For example, certain HLA‐DP allotypes serve as ligands for the activating NKp44 receptor [49], and it is possible that higher HLA‐DP protein expression may trigger stronger NKp44‐mediated NK responses.

The top hits for each of the classical class II genes were found to be within predicted TFBS. The variant marking HLA‐DP protein expression occurred in an Sp1 binding site, but both alleles of this SNP were predicted to allow Sp1 binding, so differential binding of Sp1 to its HLA‐DPB1 TFBS cannot explain the variation in expression of HLA‐DP. The observed expression differences are then likely driven by the collective properties of the allotype. This could include post‐translational mechanisms, such as the intrinsic stability of the HLA‐DPα/β heterodimer or the kinetics of its assembly with the invariant chain. Here, the ‘allotype‐specific’ effect and the ‘SNP‐specific’ effect are effectively inseparable due to the intense LD in the region. Alternatively, the respective variants in TFBS corresponding to HLA‐DR and HLA‐DQ are indeed predicted to determine whether or not a specific transcription factor can or cannot bind to the TFBS. Therefore, these SNPs may directly impact expression of HLA‐DR and HLA‐DQ. rs28383323, which is the top hit marking HLA‐DR expression, is located in a predicted GATA‐1 or an Sp1 TFBS (Figures 4 and S7), depending on the neighbour alternative SNP (rs28362368) genotype A or G, respectively. GATA‐1 is a transcriptional activator and repressor responsible for the regulation of genes within erythroid cells [50]. Sp1 is expressed ubiquitously and is involved in many cellular processes, including immune responses [51, 52]. The top SNP marking HLA‐DQ protein expression levels, rs281860696, did not reach genome‐wide significance. This low association suggests this variant could simply be a marker for a high‐expression haplotype. Then, the ‘allotype effect’ may here be driven by a combination of multiple cis‐regulatory variants or even post‐translational factors. However, it was found to lie within a CEBPa binding site that is subsequently lost in the presence of the G variant at this locus (Figure 4). CEBPa coordinates differentiation of myeloid progenitors, adipocytes, hepatocytes and cells of the lung and the placenta. CEBPa mutations were identified in acute myeloid leukaemia patients [53], but it appears that CEBPa mutations are involved in the development of acute myeloid leukaemia development and not in disease progression [54]. The other found TFBS for Antp (HOXA7 gene) is less known and does not seem to be involved in immune signalling. The SNP rs281860696 is located in the extended 5′ promoter region of HLA‐DQB1, only 428 bp upstream of the TSS, suggesting that this variant could very likely be involved in HLA‐DQB1 gene regulation. However, these are predicted TFBS, so further in‐depth analysis of these variants is required for validation. Our findings underscore that while GWAS can identify the most significant genetic tags, the functional ‘unit’ of expression regulation in the MHC may often be the entire allotype‐specific haplotype.

Recessive genetic model analyses revealed strong associations between HLA‐DP GWAS SNPs and risk of chronic HBV infection (Table 1). The top hit for HLA‐DP protein expression, rs3128928, associates with HBV in a similar manner as the previously described HBV‐associated SNP rs9277534 [7], though with only borderline significance. On the other hand, rs3128927 associated most strongly and very significantly with HBV outcome, in spite of not showing an association with HLA‐DP protein expression after conditioning on rs3128928. These results suggest that HLA‐DP protein expression levels may have some effect, but cannot fully account for the risk of HBV persistence/recovery. Other genetic effects, possibly HLA‐DPB1 allelic effects, must also contribute to the risk of HBV outcome. A recent study explored HLA associations with diphtheria toxin and pertussis toxin vaccination in African populations [55]. Several SNPs within the HLA‐DRB1 gene were identified and associated significantly with vaccine response and with levels of RNA expression of HLA‐DRB1, highlighting the potential importance of HLA class II expression in response to infectious disease and/or vaccination. Unfortunately, none of these SNPs were present in our dataset. Further characterisation is needed to delineate a potential effect of HLA class II protein expression since RNA expression and cell surface expression of HLA molecules are not necessarily correlated. For example, Aguiar et al. showed a highly significant but not strong correlation (R = 0.59) between mRNA expression and cell surface protein expression for HLA‐C [56]. They further showed that among the classical HLA class I loci, HLA‐B has the highest mRNA expression, followed by HLA‐C and then HLA‐A. On the other hand, Apps et al. showed that HLA‐A and HLA‐B cell surface expression is similar, and 10 times higher than HLA‐C [57]. These data point to the complexity of regulatory mechanisms determining gene/protein expression levels.

GWAS is an extremely useful tool for mapping genetic loci to heritable traits, but there are a few drawbacks using this technology. GWAS are designed primarily to capture frequent variation across the genome [58], and not rare variants, which require specific typing technologies, such as next generation sequencing. Nor is GWAS designed to identify epigenetic patterns (e.g., CpG) or copy number variants. In this study, we did not detect any genome‐wide significant SNPs for the HLA‐DQ gene, which may be due to lack of statistical power, and an important rare variant could have been overlooked. In our study, we chose gene specific antibodies that bind to all allotypes with an equal affinity. Nevertheless, measuring cell surface expression of class II can be a challenge due to the highly polymorphic nature of these genes, since measurements stem from expression of a genotype and not a single allele. Further, HLA class II expression level is interferon gamma regulated and is subject to change during an active immune response. While we attempted to select only healthy donors for our study, minor common infections, such as a cold, could have added ‘noise’ in our HLA class II protein expression measurements.

Our data demonstrate that HLA class II protein expression varies in a manner that is significantly allotype‐specific. While we identified common cis‐acting SNPs that influence this variation, which may act through the disruption of TFBS, these variants do not offer a comprehensive explanation for the consistent trends observed across all three loci. The association at the allotype level suggests a more complex regulatory landscape and further analysis of the class II region is required to fully understand the mechanisms and factors that regulate expression of these genes. This may include rare cis‐acting variants, variations in mRNA processing, or post‐translational modifications such as differential protein stability and transport kinetics. For instance, a specific allotype could assemble more efficiently with the invariant chain (Ii) or show faster trafficking to the cell surface, both due to a specific protein sequence. Our findings provide further weight for HLA class II expression governed by locus‐ and allotype‐specific mechanisms that extend beyond the universal control of CIITA. Previous studies have linked HLA expression to disease outcomes, and this study provides a platform for understanding the variation in class II gene expression, which may be helpful in linking class II expression levels to risk of disease. Overall, our study demonstrates a large influence of cis‐genetic structure in HLA class II protein expression levels, which could impact promoter, enhancer, or epigenomic regulation.
