Efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy versus neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy in locally advanced resectable esophageal squamous cell carcinoma
Authors: Yuanyuan Jiang, Zijie Wu, Wei Wang, Jinming Yu, Aiqin Gao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo compare the efficacy and safety of neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NCRT) versus chemoimmunotherapy (NCIT) in resectable locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (LA-ESCC).MethodsPatients with LA-ESCC who underwent NCIT or NCRT were retrospectively enrolled between November 2017 and April 2024. Propensity score matching (PSM) was applied to balance the baseline characteristics. Treatment response, survival outcomes, and safety were compared between groups.Results501 patients were enrolled, with 204 in the NCRT group and 297 in the NCIT group. After PSM, 148 matched pairs were analyzed. The NCRT group had a superior objective response rate (ORR) (68.2% vs. 54.7%, P = 0.023), pathological complete response (pCR) rate (46.6% vs. 20.9%, P < 0.001), tumor regression grade (TRG) distribution (P < 0.001), 2-year disease-free survival (DFS) (77.4% vs. 66.0%, P = 0.007) and overall survival (OS) rates (93.7% vs. 81.5%, P < 0.001) compared with NCIT group. The survival benefit was confined to patients who did not achieve pCR (both P < 0.05). Deeper tumor regression was strongly associated with improved DFS and OS (P for trend < 0.001). The incidence of postoperative complications and adverse events was comparable between groups. However, grade ≥ 3 hematologic toxicity was significantly higher with NCRT (36.5% vs. 6.8%, P < 0.001).ConclusionNCRT demonstrated superior pCR rates and mid-term survival outcomes to NCIT in resectable LA-ESCC, likely related to deeper tumor regression. TRG scores after neoadjuvant therapy predict graded survival benefit, provide more refined prognostic stratification, and complement pCR. Although postoperative complication rates were comparable, NCRT induced higher grade ≥ 3 hematologic toxicity, necessitating vigilant monitoring.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1007/s00432-026-06443-7.Keywords: Neoadjuvant therapy, Chemoimmunotherapy, Chemoradiotherapy, Esophageal squamous cell carcinoma

Introduction

Esophageal cancer is one of the leading causes of cancer morbidity and mortality worldwide (Bray et al. 2024). China accounted for over half of these new cases, with nearly 90% being squamous cell carcinoma (SCC) (Han et al. 2024). Due to the absence of specific early symptoms in esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (ESCC), most patients are diagnosed as locally advanced disease.

Based on the CROSS and NEOCRTEC 5010 trials, neoadjuvant chemoradiotherapy (NCRT) significantly increased pathologic complete response (pCR) rate and survival outcomes in patients with resectable locally advanced esophageal squamous cell carcinoma (LA-ESCC), and has been established as the standard neoadjuvant strategy (Liu et al. 2020; Eyck et al. 2021). With the advent of the immunotherapy era, neoadjuvant chemoimmunotherapy (NCIT) has shown encouraging results in the neoadjuvant treatment of LA-ESCC (Yang et al. 2025b; Liu et al. 2022a, b; Chen et al. 2023). However, there is a lack of high-quality evidence directly comparing the long-term survival outcomes of NCIT with those of NCRT. The phase III SCIENCE trial directly compared NCIT with NCRT and reported preliminary results: the pCR rate of 13% in the NCIT group versus 47.3% in the NCRT group (Leng et al. 2025). However, the mature survival data are not yet available. Several retrospective studies have reported conflicting results regarding the efficacy of NCIT versus NCRT (Yang et al. 2025b; Wang et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2025). For instance, a recent multicenter retrospective study reported significantly higher 2-year overall survival (OS) rate with NCIT compared to NCRT (81.3% vs. 71.3%), as well as improved disease-free survival (DFS) rate (73.9% vs. 63.4%) (Guo et al. 2025). In contrast, others found no significant differences in DFS or OS (Wang et al. 2024). Therefore, the superiority of NCIT versus NCRT remains an open question.

Herein, we systematically compared NCIT and NCRT in terms of therapeutic efficacy, survival outcomes, and safety using real-world data, aiming to provide real-world support for clinical decision-making.

Methods

Study cohort

The retrospective study enrolled patients who received NCIT or NCRT followed by esophagectomy at Shandong Cancer Hospital from November 2017 to April 2024. The study protocol was approved by the Ethics Committee of Shandong Cancer Hospital, affiliated to Shandong First Medical University (Approval No.: SDTHEC202411010), and conducted according to the principles outlined in the Declaration of Helsinki (as revised in 2013). The requirement for informed consent was waived.

Eligible patients met the following criteria: (1) aged between 18 and 80 years; (2) pathologically diagnosed as thoracic ESCC; (3) clinically staged as T1b-2N1-3M0 or T3-4aN0-3M0 (AJCC 8th edition); (4) successful completion of esophagectomy after neoadjuvant therapy and (5) availability of comprehensive clinical data. Exclusion criteria encompassed: (1) non-squamous cell carcinoma histology; (2) presence of synchronous malignancies; (3) insufficient clinical records for analysis. Treatment protocols

All patients received 2–4 cycles of platinum-based combination chemotherapy, comprising cisplatin, carboplatin, or nedaplatin in combination with taxane agents such as paclitaxel, docetaxel, nab-paclitaxel, or paclitaxel liposomes. Patients in the NCIT group received 2–4 cycles of a programmed death-1 (PD-1) inhibitor (e.g., camrelizumab or sintilimab) in combination with chemotherapy, according to institutional protocols. The detailed distribution of PD-1 inhibitors and chemotherapy regimens in the NCIT group is provided in Supplementary Table 3. The NCRT cohort underwent fractionated radiotherapy with a cumulative dose of 40.0–50.4 Gy delivered in 1.8–2.0 Gy per fraction. Both chemotherapy and immunotherapy regimens were personalized based on comprehensive evaluations of individual patient characteristics, including performance status, organ function, and body surface area calculations. Surgical resection was performed within 4–8 weeks following neoadjuvant therapy, contingent on clinical recovery status. The operative strategy was determined by tumor anatomical localization (proximal, middle, or distal esophagus) and multidisciplinary evaluation.

Treatment response and pathological outcomes

Tumor response was evaluated every two cycles during neoadjuvant therapy using CT, MRI, or PET-CT, and responses were classified per RECIST 1.1 criteria (Eisenhauer et al. 2009). The objective response rate (ORR) was defined as the proportion of patients achieving complete (CR) or partial response (PR). R0 resection required microscopic margin negativity (distal, proximal, and peripheral) (Tang et al. 2021). The pCR (ypT0N0) was defined as the absence of viable tumor cells at the primary site and in the resected lymph nodes, whereas MPR was defined as ≤ 10% residual viable tumor in the primary lesion (Wang et al. 2022). Tumor regression was evaluated using the AJCC Tumor Regression Grading (TRG) system, a histopathological scoring system based on residual viable tumor burden and stromal fibrosis (Amin et al. 2017). TRG 0 indicates a complete response (no viable tumor cells); TRG 1 denotes a moderate response (scattered single cells or small clusters); TRG 2 reflects minimal response (tumor present but with fibrosis predominating over tumor); and TRG 3 signifies poor response (extensive residual tumor without significant regression).

Survival analysis

Patients were followed up through clinic visits, hospitalization, and telephone interviews every 3 months during the first 2 years, and then every 6 months from the third to the fifth year, and annually thereafter until study termination (due to death, disease progression, or reaching the predefined cut-off date). DFS is defined as the time interval from the date of surgery to the first tumor recurrence or metastasis, or death of the patient for any reason (whichever occurred first). OS is defined as the time from the start of treatment to death from any cause.

Treatment-related adverse events and postoperative complications

Adverse events were graded according to the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events version 5.0 (CTCAE v5.0). Treatment-related adverse events (TRAEs) retrospectively extracted from medical records and included hematologic toxicity, hepatobiliary toxicity, gastrointestinal toxicity (nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea), and immune-related adverse events (irAEs) including colitis, hepatitis, pneumonitis, and myocarditis, which were assessed in patients receiving NCIT. Radiation-specific toxicities, such as radiation esophagitis and radiation pneumonitis, were evaluated in the NCRT group. Postoperative complications were prospectively recorded and categorized as pulmonary complications (pneumonia, pulmonary atelectasis, pneumothorax), cardiovascular complications (arrhythmia, myocardial infarction, pericardial effusion), surgical complications (anastomotic leakage, chylothorax, recurrent laryngeal nerve injury), and other disorders (e.g., wound infection). Severe complications were defined as Clavien-Dindo grade ≥ III.

Statistical analysis

Between-group comparisons of continuous variables were assessed by the independent samples t-test or the Mann–Whitney U-test, depending on the distribution. Intergroup comparisons of categorical variables were assessed by the χ2 test or Fisher’s exact test. Propensity score matching (PSM) was conducted at a 1:1 ratio with a caliper of 0.2 to ensure a sufficient number of matched pairs without compromising the post-matching balance. The propensity score model incorporated baseline covariates, including age, sex, smoking history, alcohol use, Karnofsky Performance Status (KPS), tumor location, clinical T stage (cT), clinical N stage (cN) and adjuvant therapy. A standardized mean difference (SMD) of less than 0.1 indicates that adequate balance was achieved; a range of 0.1 to 0.2 is acceptable in practice (Austin 2009; Cohen 2013; Chen et al. 2022). An exploratory subgroup analysis comparing the two most commonly used PD-1 inhibitors (camrelizumab and sintilimab) within the NCIT group was performed; no adjustment for multiple comparisons was applied. Results are summarized in Supplementary Table 4. All statistical analyses were performed using R version 4.4.2, with a P value < 0.05 considered statistically significant.

Results

A total of 501 patients were enrolled in this study, with 204 receiving conventional NCRT and 297 receiving NCIT. Baseline characteristics were summarized in Table 1. Before PSM, significant differences were observed between the two groups in clinical N stages and proportion of adjuvant therapy (both P < 0.05). After matching, baseline characteristics were adequately balanced between groups, with SMDs for all covariates below 0.2.

Objective response rate

As shown in Fig. 2a, the ORR was comparable between the NCRT (68.1%, 95% CI: 61.3–74.5%) and NCIT (63.6%, 95% CI: 57.9–69.1%) groups before PSM (P = 0.344). However, NCRT achieved an ORR of 68.2% (95% CI: 60.1–75.6%), significantly higher than NCIT (54.7%, 95% CI: 46.4–62.9%) after PSM (P = 0.023).

Pathological and surgical outcomes

As illustrated in Table 2, R0 resection was achieved in 100% of patients in the NCRT group and 99.3% in the NCIT group before matching (P = 0.516). After PSM, the rates were 100% and 99.3%, respectively (P = 1.000). The NCRT group showed significantly higher rates of pCR (48.0% vs. 25.9%, P < 0.001 before PSM; 46.6% vs. 20.9%, P < 0.001 after PSM) and MPR (74.0% vs. 47.1%, P < 0.001 before PSM; 74.3% vs. 38.5%, P < 0.001 after PSM) compared with the NCIT group (Fig. 2b, Table 2). In addition, the NCRT group showed a more favorable TRG distribution. After PSM, 74.4% of patients in NCRT group achieved deep tumor regression (TRG 0–1) compared with only 38.5% in the NCIT group, while poor response (TRG 3) was observed as 5.4% in NCRT group versus 29.7% in the NCIT group (Table 2). The full distribution across all TRG categories is illustrated in Supplementary Fig. 1.

Survival analysis

The median follow-up was 22.9 months (95% CI: 22.0–25.7 months). After PSM, Kaplan–Meier analysis showed a significantly higher 2-year DFS rate in the NCRT group (77.4%, 95% CI: 70.4–85.0%) than that in the NCIT group (66.0%, 95% CI: 57.6–75.7%) (HR = 0.54, 95% CI: 0.35–0.85; log-rank P = 0.007; Fig. 2c). The NCRT group also demonstrated a markedly improved 2-year OS, with rates of 93.7% (95% CI: 89.5–98.1%) versus 81.5% (95% CI: 74.8–88.8%) in the NCIT group (HR = 0.31, 95% CI: 0.16–0.58; log-rank P < 0.001; Fig. 2d).

To determine whether different levels of tumor regression lead to different patient survival, we analyzed the DFS and OS based on pathological response. As shown in Fig. 3a and b, among patients achieving pCR, DFS and OS showed no difference between the NCRT and NCIT groups. However, in patients who did not achieve pCR, NCRT was associated with significantly higher 2-year OS (94.2% vs. 79.0%) and DFS (71.9% vs. 62.0%) rates compared with NCIT.

To further explore the reason for the above results, we analyzed in depth the association between different pathological responses and patient survival. As shown in Fig. 3c and d, there was an obvious gradient in survival across TRG categories. Patients with TRG 0–1 exhibited the most favorable DFS and OS, followed by those with TRG 2, while those with TRG 3 had the poorest prognosis (P for trend < 0.001 for both). When stratified by treatment arm, a strong survival gradient was evident in the NCIT group, where lower TRG grades were significantly associated with better DFS (P for trend < 0.001) and OS (P for trend < 0.001) (Fig. 3e, f). In the NCRT group, lower TRG grades showed a consistent direction of association with better DFS, although this trend did not reach statistical significance; in contrast, no such trend was observed for OS (Fig. 3g, h).

Perioperative adverse events

To comprehensively assess the treatment safety of different neoadjuvant treatment strategies, we compared the incidence and severity of TRAEs and post-surgical complications in patients receiving NCRT versus NCIT.

We first compared the safety between NCRT and NCIT. As summarized in Table 3, the overall incidence of preoperative adverse events was comparable between groups (before PSM: 87.3% vs. 81.8%, P = 0.103; after PSM: 88.5% vs. 81.8%, P = 0.102). However, distinct toxicity profiles were observed between the two groups. The incidence of Grade 1–2 gastrointestinal toxicity was significantly higher in the NCIT group than in the NCRT group both before (76.1% vs. 44.6%) and after PSM (77.0% vs. 50.7%), with both P < 0.001. In contrast, hematologic toxicities were markedly more frequent with NCRT (before PSM: 80.9% vs. 41.8%; after PSM: 81.1% vs. 41.9%; both P < 0.001), a trend that persisted for grade ≥ 3 events (before PSM: 35.5% vs. 7.1%; after PSM: 36.5% vs. 6.8%; both P < 0.001). No significant differences were observed in hepatotoxicity between groups. In the NCRT group, 45 cases of radiation esophagitis occurred. In the NCIT group, 2 cases of immune-mediated myocarditis, 1 case of immune-mediated colitis, and 1 case of immune-mediated dermatitis were observed in the original cohort (Supplementary Table 1).

Then we evaluated the impact of different neoadjuvant treatments on postoperative complications. As shown in Table 3, the overall postoperative complication rate was similar between the NCRT and NCIT groups, both before (73.0% vs. 74.4%, P = 0.731) and after PSM (70.3% vs. 76.4%, P = 0.237). The incidence of severe complications was also comparable (2.5% vs. 2.4%, P = 1.000 before PSM; 2.0% vs. 0.7%, P = 0.615 after PSM). Notably, anastomotic leakage was significantly more common in the NCRT group before PSM (10.3% vs. 4.7%, P = 0.016), but this difference was no longer statistically significant after PSM (9.5% vs. 4.1%, P = 0.064). The rates of other specific complications, such as pneumonia, pleural effusion, atelectasis, pneumothorax, and pericardial effusion, were comparable between groups both before and after PSM (Supplementary Table 2).

Discussion

While NCRT remains the standard of care for resectable LA-ESCC, NCIT has shown promising results in several phase II clinical trials (Zhang et al. 2021; Yang et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2023). However, the relative efficacy of NCRT versus NCIT has not yet been definitively established. We found that NCRT obtained significantly higher pCR rates and improved 2-year DFS and OS compared to NCIT. These findings suggest that, despite the growing use of immunotherapy, NCRT remains a highly effective standard neoadjuvant regimen.

NCIT has garnered increasing interest in the treatment of LA-ESCC in the past 5 years. Recent prospective phase II trials of NCIT in ESCC reported pCR rates ranging from 18.8 to 25% (Zhang et al. 2021, 2022; He et al. 2022; Yang et al. 2022; Chen et al. 2023), with a meta-analysis estimating a pooled pCR rate of 26.9% (Xu et al. 2023). Preliminary survival data in the NICE-1 trial are also encouraging with 2-year RFS and OS rates of 67.9% and 78.1%, respectively (Yang et al. 2024). These data highlight the potential of NCIT in resectable LA-ESCC. A recent retrospective study reported comparable pCR rates between NCIT and NCRT, but improved DFS and OS with NCIT (Guo et al. 2025). However, the data are highly controversial due to much lower pCR rate in the NCRT cohort (25.9%) than those in CROSS (49.0% in ESCC subgroup) and NEOCRTEC5010 (43.2%) trials.

In our current study, the pCR rate in the NCRT group was 46.6%, while the pCR rate in the NCIT group was 20.9%. These results are consistent with historical data (Liu et al. 2020; Eyck et al. 2021; Tang et al. 2023; Kato et al. 2024). The high pCR rate in the NCRT group highlights the pivotal role of radiotherapy in achieving deep tumor regression.

Notably, NCRT also showed superior 2-year DFS and OS in the current study, which remains heterogeneous in the existing evidence. Some studies showed that NCIT improves DFS and OS compared to NCRT (Yu et al. 2024; Guo et al. 2025), but others observed no significant difference in DFS or OS (Xiao et al. 2022; Wang et al. 2024). We further analyzed the reason for survival benefit, and found that deeper tumor regression upon NCRT might contribute to improved DFS and OS. This finding underscores the critical importance of pursuing tumor shrinkage when selecting a neoadjuvant therapy regimen. In this setting, the approach being tested in the SCIENCE trial—combining radiotherapy, chemotherapy, and immunotherapy—may offer a way to further improve outcomes by targeting both the primary tumor and potential micrometastases (Leng et al. 2025). However, this finding should be further validated in prospective, large-sample clinical trials.

Although pCR is widely used as a key surrogate endpoint in neoadjuvant therapy of esophageal cancer, its ability to accurately predict long-term survival remains controversial (Su et al. 2023). Our data show that patients receiving NCRT achieved better 2-year DFS and OS than those treated with NCIT, especially in those with non-pCR. This implies that using pCR alone may underestimate the clinical benefit of NCRT and underscores its limitations as a universal surrogate for survival. In contrast, TRG captures the continuum of pathological response and has been shown to provide finer prognostic stratification in ESCC (Yang et al. 2025a). In our study, TRG was associated with a graded survival benefit in the overall cohort. This association was statistically significant in the NCIT group, whereas in the NCRT group, although a consistent but non-significant trend toward improved DFS was observed, no such trend was evident for OS, likely due to limited statistical power from small sample sizes within individual TRG categories. These findings collectively suggest that TRG may complement the binary pCR metric by enabling more refined prognostic stratification. Nevertheless, given the retrospective nature of this study and limited statistical power in certain subgroup analyses, these results warrant validation in prospective trials.

This study has some limitations. The risk of selection bias inherent to the retrospective design, although mitigated by PSM, may not have been completely eliminated. In addition, although the current survival data show encouraging differences, longer follow-up is required to confirm the durability of these observed survival benefits, given the relatively short follow-up period. Additionally, different chemoimmunotherapy regimens, radiotherapy techniques and dose selection, and differences in surgical approaches may affect the consistency of results. Notably, patients in the NCIT group received different PD-1 inhibitors, primarily camrelizumab or sintilimab. Although an exploratory analysis suggested no major differences between these two agents, the non-randomized assignment and limited sample size in the sintilimab subgroup preclude definitive conclusions regarding drug-specific effects. Moreover, reliance on traditional pathological endpoints to assess efficacy is limited, and dynamic or molecular characterization could be introduced in the future to enable more accurate stratification and help achieve precision therapy.

Conclusion

NCRT demonstrated superior pCR rates and mid-term survival outcomes to NCIT in resectable LA-ESCC, likely related to deeper tumor regression. TRG scores after neoadjuvant therapy predict graded survival benefit, provide more refined prognostic stratification, and complement pCR. Although postoperative complication rates were comparable, NCRT induced higher grade ≥ 3 hematologic toxicity, necessitating vigilant monitoring.
