Metabolic orchestration of NOD1 signaling by AMPK-mediated phosphorylation of ZDHHC5
Authors: Shaojie Mi, Yue Zhu, Qian Li, Wanjun Zhang, Huadong Pei, Feng Yao, Xuewu Guo, Yali Chen
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
SummaryInnate immune responses must be coordinated with the cellular metabolic state. The intracellular pattern recognition receptor NOD1 detects bacterial peptidoglycans and initiates signaling from cellular membranes, a process regulated by ZDHHC5-mediated palmitoylation. Using biochemical analyses and immune-competent cell models, we show that AMP-activated protein kinase (AMPK) integrates metabolic cues into NOD1 signaling by regulating ZDHHC5. AMPK phosphorylates ZDHHC5 at Ser296 and Ser380, weakens its association with Golga7B, and promotes its displacement from the plasma membrane, thereby reducing NOD1 palmitoylation, membrane localization, and downstream signaling. Conversely, stimulation with the NOD1 agonist C12-iE-DAP suppresses AMPK activity, stabilizing ZDHHC5 at the membrane and sustaining NOD1 activation. These findings define a reciprocal regulatory circuit linking metabolic stress sensing and innate immune signaling, and illustrate how metabolic pathways govern the spatial control of pattern recognition receptors in innate immunity.Subject areas: Biochemistry, Cell biology, Immunology

Introduction

The innate immune system relies on pattern recognition receptors (PRRs) to detect pathogen-associated molecular patterns (PAMPs) and coordinate host defense responses.1,2 Among these, nucleotide-binding oligomerization domain-containing protein 1 (NOD1) is a cytosolic PRR that recognizes γ-D-glutamyl-meso-diaminopimelic acid (iE-DAP), a peptidoglycan component specific to Gram-negative bacteria.3,4 Structurally, NOD1 contains a C-terminal leucine-rich repeat (LRR) domain for ligand sensing, a central NACHT domain for ATP-dependent oligomerization, and an N-terminal caspase activation and recruitment domain (CARD) that mediates downstream signaling.5,6,7

Upon ligand engagement, NOD1 oligomerizes and recruits the adaptor kinase RIPK2 through CARD-CARD interactions, initiating signaling complexes that activate the IκB kinase (IKK) and TAK1 pathways. This cascade culminates in NF-κB and MAPK activation and the transcription of pro-inflammatory genes such as interleukin-6 (IL-6) and C-X-C motif chemokine ligand ½ (CXCL1/2).8 Dysregulated NOD1 signaling has been implicated in a range of inflammatory disorders, including inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), uveitis, rheumatoid arthritis, and certain cancers. Notably, both loss- and gain-of-function variants in NOD1 have been associated with altered inflammatory responses, underscoring the importance of tight regulatory control over this pathway.9,10

One critical layer of NOD1 regulation involves its subcellular localization. Effective NOD1 activation depends on its recruitment to the plasma and endosomal membranes, where it encounters bacterial ligands.4,11 This process is driven by S-palmitoylation, a reversible lipid modification catalyzed by the membrane-localized palmitoyl-transferase ZDHHC5.12 Mutations in NOD1 associated with inflammatory diseases frequently exhibit altered palmitoylation, linking this post-translational modification to immune dysregulation. However, the upstream mechanisms that modulate ZDHHC5 activity and, by extension, NOD1 palmitoylation remain unclear.

In this study, we reveal that cellular energy stress negatively regulates NOD1 signaling through the AMPK-ZDHHC5 axis. We show that AMPK phosphorylates ZDHHC5 at Ser296 and Ser380, disrupting its membrane localization and impairing NOD1 palmitoylation, thereby reducing NOD1 membrane recruitment and downstream activation. Moreover, NOD1 agonist N-dodecanoyl-γ-D-glutamyl-meso-diaminopimelic acid (C12-iE-DAP) suppresses this inhibitory axis, promoting robust inflammatory signaling. Our findings establish a previously unrecognized link between metabolic signaling and innate immunity and suggest that modulating the AMPK-ZDHHC5 pathway could offer therapeutic strategies for NOD1-driven inflammatory diseases, such as Crohn’s disease.

Results

Cellular energy stress suppresses PGN-induced NOD1 signaling

The innate immune response is an energy-intensive process.13,14,15 In the NOD1 signaling pathway, ATP binding is crucial for driving conformational changes in NOD1, facilitating its oligomerization and recruitment of downstream signaling molecules. However, how NOD1 signaling is modulated in response to fluctuations in cellular energy availability remains poorly understood. To address this, we subjected bone marrow-derived macrophages (BMDMs) and immortalized BMDMs (iBMDMs) to glucose starvation (Figures 1A and S1A). Treatment with the NOD1 agonist C12-iE-DAP induced phosphorylation of NF-κB p65 (RELA) and p38 MAPK (MAPK14), indicating activation of the NF-κB and MAPK pathways. However, glucose starvation abolished this response, suggesting that energy stress suppresses NOD1-mediated signaling. Similarly, the glycolysis inhibitor 2-DG (2-deoxyglucose) and the antidiabetic drug metformin elicited comparable effects (Figures 1B, 1C, S1B, and S1C). Consistent with these findings, IL-6 production was significantly reduced under energy stress conditions (Figures 1D and 1E), further supporting the notion that NOD1 activation is critically dependent on cellular energy availability.

AMPK suppresses NOD1 signaling under energy stress

AMPK is a key regulator of cellular energy homeostasis.16,17 To test whether AMPK mediates the observed inhibition of NOD1 signaling by energy stress, we first assessed its activation under metabolic stress. Glucose starvation, 2-DG, and metformin treatment robustly increased AMPK phosphorylation at Thr172 (Figures 1A–1C and S1A–S1C). Inhibition of AMPK using dorsomorphin (compound C) during energy stress restored NOD1-induced phosphorylation of p65 and p38 (Figures 1F and S1D) and reversed the suppression of IL-6 production (Figure 1G), indicating that AMPK activity contributes to the energy stress-induced inhibition of NOD1 signaling. Furthermore, direct activation of AMPK with MK-8722 suppressed NOD1 activation (Figure 1H), supporting a functional link between AMPK activation and inhibition of NOD1 signaling.

Interestingly, stimulation with C12-iE-DAP attenuated AMPK activity in both BMDMs and iBMDMs, as indicated by reduced phosphorylation of AMPK and its downstream target ACC1(acetyl-CoA carboxylase 1)18 (Figures 1A–1C and S1A–S1C). These observations suggest that NOD1 activation negatively regulates AMPK signaling, highlighting a potential reciprocal crosstalk between the NOD1 and AMPK pathways.

Membrane localization of NOD1 is essential for its activation. In GFP-NOD1-expressing HEK293T cells, glucose starvation, 2-DG, and metformin disrupted NOD1 localization to the plasma membrane (Figures 1I, S1E, and S1G). These results were further confirmed by subcellular fractionation experiments (Figures 1J, S1F, and S1H). Notably, Compound C treatment restored membrane localization of NOD1 under energy stress conditions (Figures 1K, 1L, S1I, and S1J), highlighting the specific role of AMPK in regulating NOD1 membrane association.

AMPK inhibits NOD1 palmitoylation

Since NOD1 palmitoylation is essential for its membrane localization,12 we examined whether energy stress affects this modification. In both HEK293T and iBMDM cells, glucose starvation, 2-DG, or metformin treatment significantly reduced NOD1 palmitoylation, as detected by click chemistry reaction and acyl-biotin exchange (ABE) assays (Figures 2A–2D).

To determine whether AMPK mediates this effect, we inhibited AMPK activity with Compound C and observed that this reversed the reduction in NOD1 palmitoylation caused by metformin (Figures 2E and S1K). In mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), metformin reduced NOD1 palmitoylation in AMPKα1/2 (encoded by Prkaa1 and Prkaa2) wild-type (WT) cells but not in Prkaa1/Prkaa2 double-knockout (DKO) cells (Figure 2F), indicating that AMPK plays a direct role in regulating NOD1 palmitoylation in response to energy stress.

AMPK interacts with and phosphorylates ZDHHC5

Although a weak interaction between AMPK and NOD1 was observed, blotting with a specific phospho-AMPK substrate motif antibody confirmed that NOD1 was not a direct AMPK substrate (Figures S2A and S2B).

Given that ZDHHC5 is the palmitoyl acyl transferase (PAT) responsible for NOD1 palmitoylation,12 we next investigated whether AMPK affects this enzyme. Endogenous AMPK was found to interact robustly with ZDHHC5, which binds both AMPKα1 and AMPKα2 isoforms (Figures 3A and S2C). The interaction was further validated by GST pull-down assays (Figure 3B). Using a phospho-AMPK substrate motif antibody, we detected AMPK-dependent phosphorylation of exogenously expressed Flag-ZDHHC5, which was abolished by λ-phosphatase (PPase) treatment (Figures 3C and 3D). This phosphorylation increased following metformin, glucose starvation, or 2-DG treatment (Figures 3E, S2D, and S2E) and was reversed by Compound C (Figure 3F). AMPKα DKO MEFs lacked ZDHHC5 phosphorylation even after metformin treatment (Figure 3G). These results confirm that AMPK directly interacts with and phosphorylates ZDHHC5.

We next sought to identify the specific residues phosphorylated by AMPK. In HEK293T cells, we overexpressed Flag-ZDHHC5, treated the cells with metformin, and then affinity-purified Flag-ZDHHC5 for mass spectrometry (MS) analysis. MS identified Ser296 and Ser380 as candidate AMPK phosphorylation sites (Figure 3H). These residues conform to the consensus motif of AMPK (ϕββXX(S/T) XXXϕ), where ϕ represents a hydrophobic residue and β represents a basic residue,19 and are conserved across species (Figure S2F). Alanine substitution of either residue reduced ZDHHC5 phosphorylation, whereas the double mutant (2A) abolished it completely (Figure 3I). In vitro kinase assays confirmed that the 2A mutant was not phosphorylated by AMPK (Figure 3J), and displayed impaired binding to AMPK (Figure S2G).

AMPK phosphorylation reduces ZDHHC5 plasma membrane association

ZDHHC5 activation is closely tied to its own palmitoylation, which facilitates its membrane localization.20,21 We observed that metformin, glucose starvation, and 2-DG treatment reduced ZDHHC5 palmitoylation in both HEK293T and iBMDM cells (Figures 4A, 4B, S3A, and S3B). Direct activation of AMPK with MK-8722 also suppressed ZDHHC5 palmitoylation (Figure 4C). The metformin-induced reduction of ZDHHC5 palmitoylation was reversed by Compound C (Figure 4D), and was not observed in AMPKα DKO MEFs (Figure 4E), suggesting that AMPK activity contributes to the regulation of ZDHHC5 palmitoylation.

ZDHHC5 predominantly localizes to the plasma membrane. The palmitoylation-deficient mutant of ZDHHC5 (C134S)22,23 shows significantly reduced plasma membrane localization (Figure S3C). Similarly, treatment with 2-BP (2-Bromohexadecanoic acid), an inhibitor of ZDHHC5 palmitoylation (Figure S3D), markedly decreased its association with the plasma membrane (Figures S3E and S3F). AMPK activation, induced by glucose starvation, metformin, or 2-DG treatment, did not affect ZDHHC5 protein stability or degradation (Figure S3G and additional data), but was associated with a redistribution of ZDHHC5 away from the plasma membrane (Figures 4F, 4G, and S3H–S3K). This redistribution was accompanied by an increased nuclear signal of ZDHHC5. The metformin-induced redistributionof ZDHHC5 was reversed by Compound C (Figures 4H and S3L), supporting an AMPK-dependent effect on ZDHHC5 subcellular distribution. Notably, AMPK activation did not result in the obvious enrichment of ZDHHC5 in endosomes or Golgi compartments, and caused a modest reduction in its lysosomal colocalization (Data S1A–S1C). These observations suggest that the redistribution of ZDHHC5 under energy stress is unlikely to be driven predominantly by classical endosomal or Golgi-mediated trafficking routes.

Prior research has shown that Golga7B (Golgi reassembly stacking protein 7B) interacts with ZDHHC5 and stabilizes its plasma membrane localization.24 We found that glucose starvation or metformin treatment significantly weakened the interaction between ZDHHC5 and Golga7B, thereby facilitating ZDHHC5 dissociation from the plasma membrane (Figure 4I). When exogenous HA-tagged ZDHHC5 co-expressed with FLAG-Golga7B in endogenous ZDHHC5-depleted HEK293T cells, HA-ZDHHC5 predominantly localizes to the plasma membrane. Upon glucose starvation, HA-ZDHHC5 dissociated from the plasma membrane and accumulated in the nucleus, similar to the localization pattern of endogenous ZDHHC5 (Data S1D–S1E).

Moreover, the ZDHHC5 2A mutant exhibited increased palmitoylation compared to the wild type and was resistant to suppression by metformin (Figures 4J and 4K). Additionally, the 2A mutant maintained its plasma membrane localization under energy stress conditions (Figures 4L and 4M), indicating that AMPK-mediated phosphorylation negatively regulates both the palmitoylation and plasma membrane association of ZDHHC5. We attempted to explore the functions of ZDHHC5 phosphorylation with its phosphomimicking mutants S2D and S2E. However, these mutants did not exhibit detectable changes in self-palmitoylation or subcellular localization compared to WT ZDHHC5 (Data S1G and S1H). The S2D mutant bound Golga7B to a similar extent as WT ZDHHC5, and this interaction was minimally affected by energy stress, in contrast to the WT ZDHHC5 (Data S1I). These findings suggest that phosphomimicking mutations may not fully recapitulate the effects of phosphorylation on the protein structure and function.

AMPK-mediated ZDHHC5 phosphorylation suppresses NOD1 signaling

Knockdown of ZDHHC5 abrogated NOD1 palmitoylation (Figures S4A and S4B), membrane localization (Figures S4C and S4D), and downstream signaling (Figures S4E–S4G), confirming its essential role in NOD1 activation. To investigate whether AMPK-mediated ZDHHC5 phosphorylation regulates NOD1 signaling, we reconstituted ZDHHC5-deficient (Zdhhc5−/−) BMDM and iBMDM cells with either wild-type (WT) or phosphorylation-deficient (2A) ZDHHC5, and assessed NOD1 activation.

Cells expressing the 2A mutant displayed increased basal levels of NOD1 palmitoylation compared to WT-expressing cells and were resistant to the metformin-induced suppression of NOD1 palmitoylation (Figure 5A). Additionally, energy stress disrupted NOD1 membrane localization in WT-expressing cells, but not in those expressing the 2A mutant (Figures 5B–5D). Consistent with these observations, metformin treatment or glucose deprivation inhibited phosphorylation of p65 and p38 (Figures 5E, 5F, S4H, and S4I), as well as IL-6 and TNF-α production (Figures 5G and S4J), in WT-expressing cells but had no effect in 2A-expressing cells. These findings indicate that AMPK suppresses NOD1 signaling via phosphorylation of ZDHHC5.

The AMPK-ZDHHC5 axis is inhibited by C12-iE-DAP

We next examined how NOD1 activation feeds back on the AMPK-ZDHHC5 axis. As we discovered previously, stimulation with C12-iE-DAP attenuated AMPK activity (Figures 1A–1C and S1A–S1C). Consistently, this stimulation reduced the phosphorylation and increased the palmitoylation and membrane localization of ZDHHC5 (Figures 6A–6D). This corresponded with enhanced NOD1 palmitoylation and membrane localization (Figures 6F and 6G). Activation of AMPK using metformin reversed these effects (Figures 6B, 6E, and 6H), indicating that AMPK inhibits NOD1 signaling pathway.

Importantly, cells expressing the 2A mutant exhibited elevated basal palmitoylation of both ZDHHC5 and NOD1 compared to WT-expressing cells. Moreover, in the 2A mutant cells, C12-iE-DAP stimulation failed to enhance the palmitoylation of ZDHHC5 and NOD1 (Figures 6I and 6J), suggesting that phosphorylation-deficient ZDHHC5 bypasses AMPK regulation. Additionally, we investigated the disease relevance of this metabolic regulatory mechanism by examining the Crohn’s disease-associated NOD1 E266K variant. This mutant showed heightened palmitoylation, which remained sensitive to glucose starvation (Figure 6K), indicating that dysregulation of this AMPK-ZDHHC5-NOD1 axis may contribute to the pathophysiology of disease-associated NOD1 variants.

Discussion

Extensive studies have demonstrated that NOD1/2 signaling is regulated not only by microbial ligands but also by intracellular cues such as endoplasmic reticulum (ER) stress.25,26 Here, we provide evidence that cellular metabolic status also critically regulates NOD1 signaling. We show that the energy sensor AMPK directly phosphorylates ZDHHC5 at Ser296 and Ser380, weakening its interaction with Golga7B. This reduces ZDHHC5 association with the plasma membrane and is accompanied by enhanced nuclear accumulation, attenuating its activity to palmitoylate NOD1, thereby impairing NOD1 membrane localization and signaling. Our findings identify a previously unrecognized AMPK-ZDHHC5-NOD1 axis that links cellular energy availability to innate immune activation (Figure 6L).

In the canonical NOD1 pathway, ATP binding facilitates NOD1 oligomerization and RIPK2 recruitment, both essential for downstream signaling.27 We demonstrate that under energy stress conditions, AMPK activation attenuates NOD1 signaling, consistent with its role in conserving cellular energy by limiting anabolic processes.28 AMPK has been shown to modulate innate immunity, mainly by regulating NF-κB signaling via modulating SIRT1, p300 (EP300), p53 (TP53), and FoxO transcription factors,29 or by regulating macrophage polarization and metabolic programming.30,31 Our findings expand the repertoire of AMPK targets in innate immunity, showing that AMPK acts upstream of NOD1 by regulating its palmitoylation via ZDHHC5.

We also observed that AMPK activity was downregulated following NOD1 activation, as evidenced by reduced phosphorylation of AMPK and ACC1. This suppression likely reflects a metabolic adaptation to supports immune activation. Similar to the response to LPS (lipopolysaccharide) in macrophages and dendritic cells, NOD1 signaling may promote a shift toward aerobic glycolysis (“Warburg-like metabolism”), favoring rapid ATP production and biosynthesis over mitochondrial oxidative phosphorylation. In such contexts, AMPK activity is typically inhibited to relieve its restraint on glycolysis.32 Our findings suggest that NOD1 not only promotes pro-inflammatory signaling but also actively rewire cellular metabolism. This metabolic rewiring likely helps meet the energetic and biosynthetic demands of immune activation, in part through suppressing AMPK.

Moreover, our data suggest that nutrient-derived signals and energy stress converge to modulate NOD1 activity. Palmitic acid, a known activator of NLRP3 (NACHT, LRR, and PYD domains-containing protein 3) via palmitoylation and reactive oxygen species (ROS) generation,33,34 has also been implicated in promoting NOD1 palmitoylation.12 We identify the AMP/ATP ratio as a metabolic cue that negatively regulates NOD1 palmitoylation through AMPK. These findings reveal a nuanced regulatory mechanism whereby energy stress and nutrient signals together influence NOD1 activation through modulation of post-translational modifications.

In neurons, ZDHHC5 trafficking is modulated by synaptic activity and tyrosine phosphorylation by Fyn kinase.35 Our study delineates a distinct regulatory mechanism in immune cells, in which AMPK-dependent phosphorylation of ZDHHC5 at Ser296 and Ser380 within its cytoplasmic domain impairs its localization to the plasma membrane. ZDHHC5 normally associates with Golga7B. Previous work has shown that the ZDHHC5-Golga7B interaction requires palmitoylation of three cysteine residues (Cys236, Cys237, and Cys245) in the C-terminal region of ZDHHC5.24 Phosphorylation at Ser296 and Ser380 may interfere with this palmitoylation, thereby weakening Golga7B binding (Figure 4I). However, AMPK-mediated phosphorylation may also affect the ZDHHC5-Golga7B interaction through a palmitoylation-independent mechanism.

Previous studies have predicted the presence of a putative nuclear localization signal (NLS) within ZDHHC5, indicating that ZDHHC5 may possess an intrinsic capacity for nuclear localization.36 We also observed that energy stress is associated with enhanced nuclear accumulation of ZDHHC5. This coincides with a weakened interaction with the nuclear export factor XPO1 (Exportin-1)37 (Data S1F). The molecular basis and functional consequences of this relocalization remain incompletely understood. Although the interaction with XPO1 suggests that nucleocytoplasmic transport mechanisms may contribute, this observation alone is insufficient to establish a defined trafficking mechanism. While immunofluorescence analyses revealed partial colocalization of ZDHHC5 with intracellular membrane-bound organelles, energy stress did not induce obvious changes in its association with endosomes or the Golgi apparatus, but slightly influenced its association with lysosomes. Given the limited scope of organelle localization examined, more subtle or dynamic trafficking events of ZDHHC5 within cytoplasmic compartments cannot be excluded. In addition, stimulus-dependent nuclear localization of other four-transmembrane proteins, such as Tetraspanin8 (TSPAN8), has been reported to occur via a 14-3-3θ (YWHAQ)- and importin-β (KPNB1)-mediated pathway. Further studies are needed to determine whether analogous or distinct mechanisms govern the intracellular trafficking and nuclear accumulation of ZDHHC5, and to assess the broader implications of this process for cellular signaling and immune regulation.

In summary, our study uncovers a key regulatory mechanism linking cellular energy status to NOD1 signaling via the AMPK-ZDHHC5 axis. Given the established association between aberrant NOD1/2 palmitoylation and inflammatory diseases such as inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), this pathway may represent a therapeutic target. Pharmacological modulation of AMPK activity—using agents such as metformin or 2-DG—could provide a means to fine-tune NOD1 signaling and restore immune homeostasis under pathological conditions.

Limitations of the study

This study defines a regulatory mechanism linking cellular energy status to NOD1 signaling through the AMPK-ZDHHC5 axis using reconstituted iBMDM and primary BMDM models. While these systems support a role of AMPK-mediated phosphorylation in modulating ZDHHC5 function and NOD1 signaling, additional in vivo studies will be required to establish the physiological relevance of this pathway under complex metabolic and inflammatory conditions. In addition, energy stress promotes nuclear accumulation of ZDHHC5, but the molecular mechanisms governing this relocalization and its functional consequences remain to be determined. While ZDHHC5 partially associated with intracellular membrane compartments, energy stress did not produce pronounced changes in its localization to endosomes or lysosomes under the conditions tested, indicating that further work is needed to fully define the intracellular trafficking of ZDHHC5.

Experimental model and study participant details

Mice

All experiments with mice followed the ethical policies and protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee at the National Center for Protein Sciences-Beijing (NCPSB) (Approval no. NCPSB-20240318-45MO). Conditional Zdhhc5 knockout mice (Zdhhc5fl/fl) were generated on a C57BL/6 background by flanking exon 3 with loxP sites. CAGG-Cre-ERTM mice on a C57BL/6N background were purchased from Guangzhou Cyagen Biosciences, and crossed with Zdhhc5fl/fl mice to achieve inducible deletion. Mice were housed in a temperature-controlled facility under a 12 h light/dark cycle with ad libitum access to food and water. Male mice aged 6-8 weeks were used to isolate bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs). Sex as a biological variable was not examined in this study.

Cell culture

Bone marrow derived macrophages (BMDMs) were generated as previously described.39 Briefly, BMDMs were generated by differentiating bone marrow progenitors from the tibia and femur, which were then cultured in m-CSF-containing DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. Immortalized BMDMs (iBMDMs) were kindly provided by Dr. Li Tao.38 AMPKα wild-type (WT) and double knockout (DKO) mouse embryonic fibroblasts (MEFs), HEK293T cells, U2OS cells and iBMDM cells were cultured in DMEM supplemented with 10% FBS and 1% penicillin/streptomycin at 37 °C with 5% CO2. All cells were monitored by mycoplasma PCR testing and maintained in mycoplasma-free conditions.

Plasmids

Full-length cDNA or truncated mutants of ZDHHC5 were cloned into the FLAG-tagged vector (pCMV-3×FLAG), HA-tagged vector (pCMV-HA), GST-tagged vector (pGEX-4T-2), or pHAGE to generate Lenti-virus expression plasmids. Full-length cDNA or truncated mutants of NOD1 were cloned into the FLAG-tagged vector (pCMV-3×FLAG). Full-length cDNA of AMPKα1/2 were cloned into the S-SBP-FLAG-tagged vector (pIRES2-EGFP) or GST-tagged vector (pGEX-4T-2). All mutants were generated by site-directed mutagenesis and confirmed by sequencing. Full length FLAG-NOD1, GFP-NOD1 and mCherry-ZDHHC5 were kind gifts from Dr. Dante Neculai.

The gRNA interference plasmids were constructed with Lenti-CRISPR-V2. RNA interference plasmids were constructed with pLKO.1. All siRNA oligonucleotides were purchased from GenePharma. All lentiviral supernatants were generated by transient transfection of 293T cells with helper plasmids pSPAX2 and pMD2G and harvested 48 h after transfection. Supernatants were passed through the 0.45 μm filter and used to infect cells in the presence of Polybrene (8 μg/ml).

Method details

Immunoprecipitation

Cells transfected with indicated plasmids were collected and lysed in NETN buffer (10 mM Tris-HCl [pH 8.0], 100 mM NaCl, 1 mM EDTA, and 0.5% NP-40) with protease inhibitors on ice for 30 min, then sonicated for 15 sec. Lysates were cleared by centrifugation at 12,000 rpm for 10 min at 4°C. Then, cell lysates were subject to FLAG M2, or HA beads. After rotation for 8 h at 4 °C, beads were washed with NETN buffer three times, and samples were boiled with 2× sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS) loading buffer and were subject to immunoblot with indicated antibodies.

For endogenous immunoprecipitation (IP), cell lysates were incubated with indicated antibody at 4 °C for 6 h, and then were subject to Protein A/G beads for 4 h at 4 °C. Beads were then washed with NETN buffer three times, and samples were boiled with 2× SDS loading buffer, and were analyzed by immunoblot with indicated antibodies.

GST-pull down assay

For the AMPKα1/2 GST-pull down assay, GST fusion proteins were purified from E. coli BL21 bacteria, and immobilized onto glutathione sepharose 4B at 4 °C overnight. HEK293T cells transfected with indicated constructs were treated as indicated. Then, cells were lysed in the NETN buffer with the protease inhibitor and incubated with the Sepharose immobilized with indicated proteins at 4 °C for 8 h. Sepharose were then washed three times with the NETN buffer and boiled in 2× SDS loading buffer. Samples were subjected to immunoblot with indicated antibodies.

In vitro kinase assay

GST fusion-WT/S296A/S380A/2A ZDHHC5 proteins (ZDHHC5-150AA, amino acids 254-404) were expressed and purified from E. coli BL21 bacteria. Purified AMPK was incubated with 0.1 μg various substrates in kinase assay buffer (15 mM HEPES, pH 7.0, 450 μM dithiothreitol, 18.75 mM MgCl2, 6.25 mM β-glycerophosphate, 1.25mM EGTA and 125 μM ATP), with 150 μM AMP, at 30 °C for 30 min. Samples were analyzed by Western blot with indicated antibodies.

Immunofluorescent staining

Cells cultured on coverslips were fixed by 4% paraformaldehyde for 15 min at room temperature and then fixed cells were rinsed 3 times with PBS, permeabilized and blocked with 0.1% saponin, 5% goat serum in PBS for 1 h at room temperature (RT), followed by incubation with primary FLAG antibody diluted in 0.05% saponin, 1% goat serum overnight at 4 °C. Then, cells were rinsed 3 times with PBS and then incubated with secondary antibody diluted in 0.05% saponin, 1% goat serum for 1 h at room temperature. Cells were counterstained with 100 ng/ml 4',6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI) for 2 min to visualize nuclear DNA. All the samples were visualized by a Nikon A1R fluorescence confocal microscope and the Nikon NIS software.

Click chemistry reaction for palmitoylation

Click chemistry reaction for protein palmitoylation was detected as described.40 Briefly, cells were incubated with 100 μM palmitate analogue alk-C16 for 8 h, followed by lysis in 200 μL of 1% SDS TEA buffer (150 mM NaCl, 50 mM triethanolamine, pH 7.4) containing a protease inhibitor cocktail and 50 U/mL nuclease. The lysates were diluted with TEA buffer to achieve a final SDS concentration of 0.2%, and the target protein was immunopurified using anti-FLAG agarose beads. Proteins were eluted in 50 μL TEA buffer containing 4% SDS. For click chemistry labeling, reagents were added sequentially as follows: 0.5 μL of 10 mM biotin picolyl azide, 1 μL of 50 mM TCEP, 1 μL of 5 mM TBTA, and 1 μL of 50 mM CuSO4. The reaction mixture was thoroughly mixed and incubated at room temperature in the dark for 1 h. Subsequently, 5× SDS loading buffer was added, and the samples were heated at 95 °C for 10 min. Samples were analyzed by Western blot with indicated antibodies.

Acyl-biotin exchange

Acyl-biotin exchange (ABE) assays were performed essentially as previously described.41 Samples were suspended in 150 μL lysis buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl,2% SDS, with protease inhibitor cocktail) with 50 mM N-ethylmaleimide (NEM) and 50 U/ml nuclease. Lysates were centrifuged and supernatants were treated with 8 μL of 200 mM neutralized TCEP for 30 min at room temperature in the dark with rotation. Reduced cysteines were blocked by NEM, and the capping reaction was terminated via methanol-chloroform-water precipitation. The samples were split into two equal portions and incubated for 2 h at room temperature in 150 μL of lysis buffer with either 0.75 M hydroxylamine or a negative control. Following another round of methanol-chloroform-water precipitation and brief air-drying, the samples were dissolved in 150 μL of lysis buffer containing 200 μM biotin-BMCC and gently mixed at room temperature for 2 h. The samples were precipitated again, dissolved in 120 μL of lysis buffer, and aliquoted 20 μL was reserved as a loading control and 100 μL was diluted 1:10 in Tris buffer and incubated with 10 μL of streptavidin beads overnight at 4 °C with rotation. The beads were washed three times with PBS containing 1% SDS. Both the beads and loading control samples were mixed with SDS loading buffer and heated at 95 °C for 10 min. Proteins were resolved by SDS-PAGE and analyzed via Western blotting.

Subcellular fractionations assays

After the indicated stimulation, total cell lysates were prepared by lysing cells from a 10 cm dish in 1 mL of RIPA buffer on ice. The cytosolic fraction and membrane fraction were separated by sucrose gradient fractionation, as previously described.42 Briefly, cells were cultured in 10 cm dishes until reaching 90% confluency in a 37 °C incubator with 5% CO2. The cells were rinsed twice with ice-cold PBS and immediately resuspended in 1 mL of sucrose buffer (250 mM sucrose, 20 mM HEPES, 10 mM KCl, 1.5 mM MgCl2, 1 mM EDTA, 1 mM EGTA, 1 mM DTT, 1× protease inhibitor, and phosphatase inhibitor, pH 7.4) per dish on ice, followed by harvesting with a cell scraper. The lysates were incubated on ice for 30 min and then sequentially centrifuged at 720 g for 5 min at 4 °C, with the pellet collected as the nuclear fraction. The supernatant was transferred to a fresh 1.5 mL tube, then centrifuged at 10,000 g for 10 min at 4 °C. To isolate the cytosolic fraction, the resulting supernatant was subjected to ultracentrifugation at 100,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C. The cytosolic fraction was concentrated by methanol precipitation and resuspended in 2% SDS buffer (100 mM Tris-HCl, pH 7.2, 150 mM NaCl, 2% SDS, 1× protease inhibitor, and phosphatase inhibitor, pH 8.0). The pellet obtained after the initial ultracentrifugation was resuspended in 1 mL of sucrose buffer, followed by an additional ultracentrifugation step at 100,000 g for 1 h at 4 °C. The resulting pellet, representing the membrane fraction, was resuspended in 2% SDS buffer. Both fractions were resolved by SDS-PAGE and analyzed via Western blotting.

ELISA

Cells were seeded into 6-well plates and treated as indicated. Samples from the culture medium were then analyzed with mouse IL-6 and TNF-α with ELISA kit according to manufacturer’s instruction.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Quantitative analysis of chemiluminescence signals in Western blots was performed using ImageJ. ZDHHC5 co-localization with organelle markers under different treatment conditions was performed at the single-cell level by calculating Manders’ overlap coefficients with ImageJ. Statistical analyses were conducted in GraphPad Prism, based on at least three independent biological replicates. Depending on the experimental design, two-tailed Mann-Whitney U test, unpaired t-tests, one-way ANOVA, and two-way ANOVA were used as appropriate. Data were presented as mean ± standard deviation (s.d.), and p < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.
