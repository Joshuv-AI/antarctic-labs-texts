Component Clinical and Cost Outcomes Complementing Value of Information: Case Study of a Tuberculosis Diagnostics Clinical Trial
Authors: Pamela Pei, Livia Qoshe, Grady Florance, Ankur Pandya, Milton C. Weinstein, Kenneth A. Freedberg, A. David Paltiel, Krishna P. Reddy
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeConventional value-of-information (VOI) analysis bases decisions on a composite cost-effectiveness measure that aggregates clinical and cost outcomes. We sought to highlight potentially informative tradeoffs by estimating and reporting the “disaggregated” components, allowing stakeholders—such as clinicians, patients, trial funders, policymakers, and trial networks—to identify what drives the value of additional research.MethodsWe used a microsimulation to project the value of a hypothetical trial comparing mortality associated with different tuberculosis screening strategies among hospitalized patients with human immunodeficiency virus (HIV) in South Africa. Apart from expressing VOI as conventional, “aggregated” net monetary benefit (NMB), we assessed “disaggregated” outcomes that contribute to NMB calculations: tuberculosis cases detected, deaths, life-years, and healthcare costs associated with trial-informed decisions. We varied key parameters in probabilistic sensitivity analysis. We considered two willingness-to-pay thresholds: US $3000/year of life saved (YLS) (~50% of South Africa’s per-capita gross domestic product [GDP]); and $780/YLS, the willingness-to-pay value where decision uncertainty is highest, per the baseline analysis.ResultsAt willingness to pay = $3000/YLS, implementing the post-trial optimal strategy would produce favorable incremental NMB ($654.3 million) and better clinical outcomes (57,000 additional tuberculosis cases detected, 16,000 fewer deaths, and 294,000 more life-years), but it would incur an additional $37.4 million in healthcare costs over 5 years. At willingness to pay = $780/YLS, implementing the post-trial optimal strategy would yield positive incremental NMB of $2.5 million while lowering healthcare costs by $11 million, but it would produce worse clinical outcomes: 18,000 fewer tuberculosis cases detected, 6,000 additional deaths, and 80,000 fewer life-years.ConclusionsThough a trial may yield positive NMB in conventional VOI analysis owing to expected cost savings, some clinical outcomes might be unfavorable. To better inform priorities, VOI studies should include disaggregated outcomes alongside aggregated NMB.

Introduction

Clinical trials are considered the gold standard for informing clinical practice [1]. However, the resources required to complete trials are substantial [2]. Decision-makers, including funders and clinical trials networks, must decide on which trials to allocate limited resources. Value-of-information (VOI) analysis provides a formal framework for assessing the relative value of different trials, enabling decision-makers to maximize the information gained within fixed research budgets [3, 4].

VOI analysis typically uses an aggregated outcome, such as net monetary benefit (NMB), as its metric. NMB combines a measure of overall clinical benefit (e.g., life-years) and a measure of cost. However, NMB obscures the relative contributions of changes in health outcomes and costs, as well as components of health outcomes and costs that might be important to policymakers. For example, policymakers might consider specific reference points, such as additional deaths compared with current practice. Disaggregated outcomes address this limitation by reporting the individual components of value—such as life-years, clinical events, and costs—allowing decision-makers to interpret each element independently and understand what drives the overall results. Disaggregated outcomes provide transparency about how an intervention affects specific dimensions of value, separate from the combined summary measure. Including these disaggregated outcomes in VOI calculations and decision rules would allow VOI to be interpreted from various perspectives, such as trial network members, funders, and policymakers. Indeed, both the Professional Society for Health Economics and Outcomes Research (ISPOR) and the US 2nd Panel on Cost-Effectiveness Analysis in Health and Medicine recommend reporting both aggregated and disaggregated outcomes in cost-effectiveness analysis [5, 6].

We previously conducted a VOI (expected value of sample information) analysis around a proposed 2600-participant trial of tuberculosis (TB) screening strategies among hospitalized people with human immunodeficiency virus (PWH) in South Africa, reporting outcomes in terms of NMB [7]. Screening tests included sputum Xpert, a molecular assay, and two urine lipoarabinamonnan (LAM) tests: AlereLAM and FujiLAM. Here, we expand on that analysis to include disaggregated outcomes to assess how decisions based on the trial results could affect different component clinical and cost outcomes. Our objective was to illustrate how reporting and exploring disaggregated outcomes can shed new and useful light on important tradeoffs and thereby promote more informed decision-making.

Methods

Analytic Overview

We used the Cost-Effectiveness of Preventing AIDS Complications (CEPAC)-International microsimulation model to project clinical and cost outcomes. The CEPAC model is programmed in C++. While assuming decisions are made on the basis of maximizing NMB, i.e., willingness to pay (WTP) × life-years (LY) − lifetime healthcare costs, we assessed the value gained from the trial in the form of various component clinical outcomes, including TB cases detected, deaths averted, life-years gained, and healthcare costs incurred, to complement NMB-based estimates. Life-years are not adjusted for quality-of-life; most of the disability-adjusted life-year decrements due to tuberculosis are from mortality [8–11].

Prior TB Screening Value-of-a-Trial Analysis

In our previous analysis, we evaluated the total value of a trial (VOT) as an aggregated composite outcome, measured by the gain in conventional NMB [12, 13]. VOT comprises: (1) VOI, representing the NMB benefit of reducing uncertainty through the trial (traditional VOI) and (2) value of implementation (VOM), representing the gain in NMB from improving the uptake of the optimal screening strategy that would be prompted by the trial results [13, 14].

The proposed trial would compare the 2-month mortality associated with three different TB screening strategies among hospitalized PWH in South Africa: (1) sputum Xpert alone (Xpert); (2) a combination of sputum Xpert and urine AlereLAM (Xpert + AlereLAM); and (3) a combination of sputum Xpert and the more sensitive and costlier urine FujiLAM (Xpert + FujiLAM). The trial would enroll 2600 participants, a sample size comparable to the STAMP trial of TB screening strategies for hospitalized PWH in South Africa and Malawi [15]. We compared these three strategies to current practice, assumed to be 50% Xpert and 50% Xpert + AlereLAM, reflecting suboptimal implementation of guideline-recommended AlereLAM [16, 17].

Inputs for Probabilistic Sensitivity Analysis

In probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA), we varied the following key parameters simultaneously using joint probability distributions to account for parameter uncertainty: diagnostic yield, specificity, and cost of the three screening strategies; TB prevalence; sputum provision probability; empiric TB treatment probability (treatment in the absence of a positive test result); the probability of disengagement from TB care post-hospitalization; and the monthly probability of death among people with untreated TB. Diagnostic yields by strategy for patients in the low-CD4-count (< 200/µL) and high-CD4-count (≥ 200/µL) groups were 33% and 33% for Xpert, 62% and 35% for Xpert + AlereLAM, and 69% and 47% for Xpert + FujiLAM [18]. Distributions of other parameters are presented in Table 1. We performed 10,000 iterations per strategy in the PSA, with a cohort of 2,000,000 individuals per iteration.

Population-Level Inputs

To estimate the overall population-level VOT, we considered an effective time horizon of 5 years until new diagnostics replace the existing ones, and an annual target population of 500,000 PWH hospitalized in medical units in South Africa, i.e., total of 2.5 million over 5 years [38, 39]. For all trial outcomes and PSA iterations, we modeled linear uptake of the post-trial optimal screening strategies over 5 years, starting at 0% in year 1 and ending at 80% in year 5. We discounted life-years and costs at 3% per year (Table 1) [6].

Model Outcomes

Pre-Trial and Post-Trial Decisions

The pre-trial optimal strategy is the one with the highest average NMB, given current information. The post-trial optimal strategy is determined, for each combination of parameter values (“PSA iterations”), as the strategy with the highest NMB. We expressed the post-trial optimal decision as a probabilistic distribution by calculating the proportions of PSA iterations in which each strategy has the highest NMB.

Value of a Trial

To complement conventional NMB-based VOT estimates reported in the previously published analysis, we assessed VOT in the form of various component outcomes: additional TB cases detected, all-cause deaths averted, life-years gained, and additional healthcare costs incurred [12]. These outcomes represent the cumulative change in 5-year model projections from current practice to the post-trial optimal strategy. We determined each component outcome over a range of WTP thresholds, US $0–3000 per year of life saved (YLS), with particular focus on the outcomes at two key thresholds: base case of $3000 per year of life (YLS) saved (approximately 50% of South Africa’s annual per-capita gross domestic product and approximating a published opportunity cost-based threshold); and $780/YLS (the pre-trial incremental cost-effectiveness ratio between the two undominated strategies determined in our previously published baseline analysis, where the decision is most impacted by parameter uncertainty) [12, 40]. The WTP threshold can also be interpreted as the health-system opportunity cost in settings with fixed budgets [41–43].

We also evaluated the uncertainty around component outcome projections. Within each PSA iteration, we compared the post-trial decision component outcome with current practice, where the post-trial decision is based on maximizing NMB. We then estimated the proportion of PSA iterations in which the outcome would become worse post-trial (e.g., fewer TB cases detected, more deaths, fewer life-years, higher healthcare costs). We plotted the PSA results on the four quadrants of the cost-effectiveness plane to illustrate the tradeoff between the incremental health benefit (life-years) and incremental healthcare costs in the post-trial iterations compared with current practice [44].

Results for Other Willingness-to-Pay Thresholds

Apart from reporting detailed estimates for two WTP thresholds, we also examined the results across a range of WTP thresholds. We determined the optimal pre-trial and post-trial decisions (on the basis of NMB, which in turn depends on WTP), and whether each component outcome would improve or worsen post-trial from the NMB-based decision.

At a WTP threshold of $780/YLS, VOT would be $2.5 million (VOI would be $100,000; VOM would be $2.4 million). The gain in NMB post-trial would be driven by lower healthcare costs, which would decrease by a mean of $11 million over 5 years. However, in 82.4% of iterations, the optimal post-trial strategy (Xpert) would lead to worse clinical outcomes compared with current practice, detecting a mean of 18,000 fewer TB cases, producing 6000 additional deaths, and yielding 80,000 fewer life-years (Table 2). In contrast, in the remaining 17.6% of iterations where Xpert + FujiLAM would be the optimal strategy, more TB cases would be detected (Fig. 2b) and more deaths would be averted (Fig. 3b) compared with current practice.

The post-trial strategies would have different tradeoffs between health outcomes and costs. Iterations where Xpert would be optimal would fall into the south–west quadrant of the cost-effectiveness plane, indicating that Xpert would be cost-saving while producing fewer life-years lived in the population compared with current practice. In contrast, Xpert + FujiLAM would increase costs and life-years, placing these iterations in the north–east quadrant (Fig. 4b). Even though Xpert is optimal in 82.4% of PSA iterations, the distribution of possible outcomes from Xpert is much smaller than the distribution of outcomes from Xpert + FujiLAM. This makes the point cloud for Xpert appear smaller than that of Xpert + FujiLAM in Fig. 4b.

Results for Other Willingness-to-Pay Thresholds

Outside the narrow range of WTP thresholds between $770 and $800/YLS, there would be no uncertainty about the post-trial optimal strategy. Below a WTP threshold of $770/YLS, the optimal strategy would be Xpert in all iterations, while above a WTP threshold of $800/YLS, the optimal strategy would be Xpert + FujiLAM in all iterations (Fig. 1). Below a threshold of $770/YLS, the optimal strategy in terms of NMB (Xpert) would lead to fewer TB cases detected, more all-cause deaths, and fewer life-years, while also producing lower healthcare costs compared with current practice. Conversely, above a threshold of $800/YLS, the optimal strategy in terms of NMB (Xpert + FujiLAM) would lead to more TB cases detected, fewer all-cause deaths, and more life-years, while producing higher healthcare costs compared with current practice. Discussion

VOI and VOM analyses project the incremental changes associated with decision-making before versus after a trial is conducted. Traditionally, these changes are expressed as NMB. In this analysis, we evaluated the same changes through various component outcomes, demonstrating that a trial that yields positive incremental NMB in a conventional VOI analysis could still produce unfavorable component clinical and/or cost outcomes in some iterations [45–50].

The number of potential clinical trials far exceeds the resources available to support them, as each trial demands substantial investments of time, money, and personnel. The VOI and VOM frameworks offer a formal structured approach to estimating the potential value of individual trials in improved decision-making and health outcomes, which can play a crucial role in prioritizing which trials to pursue. VOI and VOM analyses are now increasingly applied in various fields, such as HIV, cardiovascular disease, cancer, substance use disorder, and coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) [45–50]. As such, there is a growing need to deepen our understanding of the mechanics and implications of these methodologies.

Net monetary benefit is an aggregated composite outcome that encompasses various component clinical and economic outcomes. However, changes in the component outcomes, such as incident disease, deaths, and costs, may be concealed by the presentation of a single composite metric. In our example with a WTP threshold of $780/YLS, the NMB of the trial would be positive, not because it would lead to expected health gains, but because the ratio of expected incremental cost savings to the incremental loss of life-years exceeds the WTP. In terms of the four quadrants of cost-effectiveness space, this result lies in south–west quadrant III (cost-savings, but loss of life-years) [44].

Different people might base their decisions on different clinical and economic reference points. Although NMB accounts for both costs and health benefits, we report costs separately for stakeholders who may wish to consider economic outcomes in isolation from health effects. There could be cost-saving interventions that decision-makers deem worth pursuing despite a lack of health gains; whether or not to consider such interventions is a normative choice [51, 52]. Therefore, transparency in the component outcomes of VOI and VOM metrics could better inform decisions. Including component outcomes alongside conventional aggregated NMB-based estimates in VOI and VOM analyses would provide decision-makers with a more nuanced understanding of the results, helping to better align priorities and preferences.

Furthermore, the VOI and VOM frameworks can be readily adapted to accommodate other decision rules on the basis of component outcomes. Our analysis maximizes NMB, but disaggregated outcomes reveal which components drive value. VOI and VOM could be recalculated using specific outcomes or weighted combinations to reflect stakeholder priorities, supporting multicriteria decision-making if desired [53]. Furthermore, constraints such as the exclusion of strategies that increase deaths, could be integrated into the decision-making process. Otherwise, given the uncertainty propagated through probabilistic analyses, it is possible for a high-risk intervention that results in early mortality to appear favorable in a VOI analysis, if it sufficiently decreases costs [50]. Such an intervention would not be evaluated in a conventional cost-effectiveness analysis, but a VOI analysis could unintentionally favor a trial that values such an intervention on expectation.

Lastly, if stakeholders prioritize specific disaggregated outcomes, either as objectives or constraints, this could alter the expected net benefit of sampling and, consequently, the optimal trial sample size. In this analysis, we considered a fixed sample size equivalent to that of the STAMP trial [15].

To enhance decision-making among clinical trialists and policymakers, projections of component outcomes, including the expected changes in life-years and costs separately, as well as the different clinical outcomes that contribute to life-years, should be reported alongside conventional aggregated NMB-based VOI and VOM estimates. This addition would provide valuable insights for more informed decisions in planning clinical trials.
