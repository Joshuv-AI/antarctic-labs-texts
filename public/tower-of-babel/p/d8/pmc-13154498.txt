Association between cumulative TyG/AIP-derived indices and terminal survival time in older patients with circulatory system diseases
Authors: Peng Li, Yanjun Shi, Yan Feng, Jianlan Xue, Jianhua Ma, Xueyan Gu, Li Su, Xiaochun Li, Junling Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAs novel alternative indicators of insulin resistance (IR), the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) and atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) are closely associated with the occurrence and progression of circulatory system diseases (CSD). However, it remains unclear whether TyG-derived indices (TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR) and AIP-derived indices (AIP, AIP-BMI, AIP-WC, AIP-WHtR) are associated with the terminal survival time of older patients with CSD.MethodsA dynamic cohort study was conducted involving older adults aged ≥ 60 years with the underlying cause of death attributed to CSD and who had completed at least three follow-ups. Cumulative TyG- and AIP-derived indices were calculated from longitudinal data. Logistic regression (LR), generalized linear models (GLM), generalized estimating equations (GEE), and restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to analyze the association between each cumulative index and terminal survival time. Subgroup analyses, receiver operating characteristic (ROC), net reclassification improvement (NRI), and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were conducted to assess the robustness and predictive performance of each index.ResultsA total of 2302 participants were included, with a median follow-up time of 34.10 months and a median terminal survival time of 12 months. After adjusting for covariates, both LR, GLM, GEE, and RCS showed that higher cumulative TyG- and AIP-derived indices were associated with shorter terminal survival time, with nonlinear relationships observed. Subgroup analysis revealed that the cumulative TyG- and AIP-derived indices were generally significantly associated with terminal survival time across age and gender strata (except for the cumulative AIP index in females), and significant age interaction was observed for cumulative TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR. The results of ROC, NRI, and IDI indicated that the cumulative indices of TyG-BMI and TyG-WC significantly outperform other indicators in predictive efficacy.ConclusionReducing TyG- and AIP-derived indices may help prolong terminal survival time in older patients with CSD. Furthermore, monitoring TyG-BMI and TyG-WC holds potential value for terminal survival time and may be considered for future integration into comprehensive assessment systems.Keywords: TyG, AIP, Terminal survival time, Circulatory system diseases, Insulin resistance

Introduction

Circulatory System Diseases (CSD) represent a collective category of disorders centered on cardiovascular and cerebrovascular pathologies, which have long constituted the predominant health concerns worldwide [1, 2]. The Global Health Estimates 2021 ranked CSD as the leading cause of death, accounting for 28.11% of all fatalities globally [3]. Older adults, due to physiological changes including diminished vascular endothelial function and reduced metabolic regulatory capacity, constitute a high-risk group for CSD and exhibit increased susceptibility to its adverse effects [4]. Epidemiological data demonstrate that the risk of CSD morbidity and mortality increases rapidly with advancing age, and older individuals often face poorer prognoses and greater healthcare burdens [5]. Against the backdrop of accelerating global population aging, thoroughly investigating the critical factors influencing prognosis in older CSD patients is essential for optimizing clinical decision-making and reducing mortality rates.

Insulin resistance (IR) is a major risk factor for CSD, with pathogenic mechanisms involving multiple pathways, including atherosclerotic plaque formation, ventricular hypertrophy, and diastolic dysfunction [6]. Compared to the hyperinsulin-positive glucose clamp test (the gold standard for IR assessment), which is costly and difficult to implement in large populations, novel IR surrogate markers have emerged as important tools for risk stratification and prognosis due to their simplicity and cost-effectiveness. These include the triglyceride-glucose (TyG) and its derivatives combined with body mass index (BMI), waist circumference (WC), and waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), namely TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR, as well as the atherogenic index of plasma (AIP) and its derivatives AIP-BMI, AIP-WC, and AIP-WHtR [7]. Previous studies have demonstrated that TyG and AIP indices are closely associated with the incidence and mortality risk of various CSD, including cardiovascular disease (CVD) [7–9], IHD [10], cerebrovascular disease [11, 12], and hypertension disease (HD) [13]. However, existing studies have primarily examined the associations between IR indices and specific point status (such as diseased/non-diseased, deceased/alive), with limited exploration of patient survival time. Particularly, systematic research on terminal survival time, defined as the interval from the last clinical healthcare-seeking event attributable to the underlying cause to death, remains virtually unexplored. Clinically, the last etiologically related healthcare-seeking behavior often marks a turning point at which the disease state shifts toward greater instability or deterioration; physiologically, this interval may partly reflect an individual’s overall capacity to maintain vital functions in late life. From a practical perspective, compared with traditional survival times such as diagnosis-to-death or hospitalization-to-death, the terminal survival time excludes the prolonged pre-terminal course, enabling a precise focus on the lethality of the underlying cause event itself, while delineating an actionable window for late-stage intervention and end-of-life care planning. By quantifying the high-risk period from the last pathogenic medical encounter to death, this metric may directly inform strategic optimization of terminal management for CSD.

Based on the above research gap, this study relied on a dynamic cohort to explore the association between TyG- and AIP-derived indices and the terminal survival time of older patients with CSD, and to assess the independent predictive value of different indicators. This is expected to reveal the impact of IR on the terminal survival time of older patients with CSD, thereby providing a basis for optimizing clinical intervention strategies, improving prognosis, and quality of life.

Method

Study population and data sources

This dynamic cohort study was conducted in the city of Gansu Province, China, targeting older adults aged 60 years and above whose underlying cause of death was CSD. The study data were derived from two sources. The first was the electronic health records of older adults from 2012 to 2023, established under the national health management program for older adults. This system provides annual health examinations for all residents aged ≥ 60, aiming to monitor chronic diseases and assess overall health status. The second source was the mortality surveillance system, which records all registered deaths. By linking these two datasets using a unique personal identification code, we identified individuals who had undergone at least 3 health examinations and whose underlying cause of death was classified as CSD. Inclusion criteria: participation in at least 3 health examinations; the underlying cause of death was CSD (ICD-10 code: I00 - I99). Exclusion criteria: cases with more than 20% missing data in key variables, logical inconsistencies identified by data validation, or extreme outliers in key biochemical markers.

Data measurements

During the health examination, information on age (years), gender (male/female), marital status (married/other), smoking status (never smoking/former smoking /current smoking), alcohol use (never drinking/occasional drinking/regular drinking), and examination date was recorded. Specifically, for smoking status, never smoking is defined as never smoking or smoking fewer than 20 packs in a lifetime; former smoking means having smoked at least 20 packs but quit within the past 12 months; current smoking refers to having smoked at least 20 packs in a lifetime and still smoking within the past 12 months [14]. For alcohol use, never drinking is described as no drinking in the past or on most weeks; occasional drinking refers to having drunk in the past 12 months but less than once a week, and not drinking on most weeks; regular drinking refers to regular weekly drinking in the past year [15]. Standardized protocols were used to measure participants’ height, weight, WC, and blood pressure. Additionally, fasting venous blood samples were collected to perform laboratory tests, including measurements of fasting blood glucose (FBG), alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), total bilirubin (TBIL), serum creatinine (SCr), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), total cholesterol (TC), triglycerides (TG), low-density lipoprotein cholesterol (LDL-C), and high-density lipoprotein cholesterol (HDL-C). Furthermore, mortality surveillance data were used to collect information on the underlying cause of death for participants.

Calculation of indicators

The primary outcome variable was terminal survival time, defined as the interval between the last CSD-attributable clinical healthcare-seeking event (outpatient, emergency, or inpatient) and death. This interval was obtained from the Chinese Medical Certificate of Death and determined by matching the ICD-10 code of the underlying cause with the direct causes of death. In this study, recorded intervals shorter than one month were uniformly recoded to 0.5 months, and participants were divided into long-term and short-term survival time groups based on the median terminal survival time.

The primary exposure variables were cumulative indices derived from multiple measurements of IR, including the cumulative indices of TyG, TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR, AIP, AIP-BMI, AIP-WC, and AIP-WHtR. Cumulative indices provide a more comprehensive assessment of an individual’s metabolic status compared to single-time measurements. It is worth noting that in addition to the IR index, we similarly calculated cumulative measurements of age, BMI, WC, waist-to-height ratio (WHtR), blood pressure, and laboratory indices.

Statistics analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 and R 4.3.1. Firstly, the dataset in this study contains 3.63% missing values, which are assumed to be randomly missing. Therefore, the multiple imputation of chained equations method was employed to impute the missing values [16]. Secondly, the histograms showed that all continuous variables approximately followed a normal distribution, so they were expressed as mean and standard deviation, and comparisons between groups were made by ANOVA. Categorical variables were expressed as frequency and percentage, and comparisons between groups were made by chi-square test. Thirdly, Spearman’s correlation coefficients were calculated to estimate the associations between exposure factors and outcomes. Variance inflation factor (VIF) was used to assess multicollinearity among variables. Variables with VIF > 5 were considered to have severe multicollinearity and were excluded from subsequent multivariate analyses.

In multivariate analysis, based on four variable types of terminal survival time—binary, continuous, cumulative, and non-cumulative longitudinal repeated measures variables—we constructed three models: logistic regression (LR), generalized linear model (GLM), and generalized estimating equations (GEE), respectively. Each model was analyzed in three steps: unadjusted, adjusted for gender, age, marital status, smoking status, alcohol use, and disease type, and fully adjusted for all covariates. Specifically, for GLM, the gamma distribution with a log link function was selected as the link function [17]. For GEE, this study selected the independent structure as the optimal working correlation matrix for the GEE model based on the minimum quasi-likelihood under independence model criterion (QIC) and corrected QIC (QICC). Further, restricted cubic splines (RCS) were used to explore the potential linear and nonlinear associations between cumulative TyG- and AIP-derived indices and terminal survival time, and the optimal number of knots was determined by minimizing the Akaike information criterion (AIC) and Bayesian information criterion (BIC). Subgroup analyses were conducted to evaluate the consistency of associations across different populations. Additionally, the predictive utility of different indices for terminal survival time was assessed using receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curves in both the overall population and subgroups. Finally, the net reclassification improvement (NRI) and integrated discrimination improvement (IDI) were employed to further evaluate the good-performing indicators identified by ROC analysis.

Results

General information

A total of 2302 older patients with CSD were included in the study, with a median follow-up duration of 34.10 months and a median terminal survival time of 12 months. As shown in Table 1, the mean baseline age of the participants was 72.72 ± 5.52 years, with 1156 males (50.22%) and 1146 females (49.78%). Compared to the short survival time group, the long survival time group had a significantly higher baseline age and cumulative age, as well as lower cumulative values of BMI, WHtR, RSSBP, RSDBP, ALT, TG, and LDL-C (P < 0.05). Additionally, there were statistically significant differences between the two groups in terms of smoking status and disease type distribution (P < 0.05).

RCS

Multivariable-adjusted RCS analysis was performed to assess the dose-response relationship between various indices and terminal survival time, as shown in Fig. 5. All cumulative indices demonstrated a non-linear negative association with terminal survival time in older patients with CSD (P for overall < 0.05, P for non-linear < 0.05). The thresholds for each index indicator at OR = 1 were provided (TyG: 8.63; TyG-BMI: 201.75; TyG-WC: 703.33; TyG-WHtR: 4.46; AIP: 0.35; AIP-BMI: 7.97; AIP-WC: 28.04; AIP-WHtR: 0.18). These results indicate that low levels of cumulative indices (below their respective thresholds) were associated with reduced probability of short terminal survival.

Subgroup analysis

Given the small sample sizes in the smoking status and disease type subgroups, the final subgroup analysis was limited to age, which was significantly associated with terminal survival time in group comparisons, and gender, which was clinically relevant. As shown in Fig. 6, after adjustment for covariates, higher cumulative levels of the TyG- and AIP-derived indices were generally associated with an increased risk of short terminal survival time across both age strata and in both genders (all P < 0.05), except that the association for cumulative AIP was not statistically significant among females. Additionally, the associations between cumulative TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, TyG-WHtR indices and terminal survival time were affected by the age interaction (P for interaction < 0.001).

Prediction performance of various indices

The results of the ROC curve analysis are shown in Fig. 7. Among the eight IR indices, the cumulative TyG-BMI and TyG-WC indices demonstrated superior predictive performance to the other indices for terminal survival time in older CSD patients, with AUC values of 0.60 (95% CI: 0.57, 0.62) and 0.60 (95% CI: 0.58, 0.62), respectively. Age-stratified analyses revealed consistently superior discriminatory power in patients ≥ 75 years compared to those < 75 years in all indices. Inter-gender comparisons demonstrated marginally higher AUC values for the TyG-BMI index and TyG-WHtR index in male patients than in females, while other indices exhibited comparable performance between genders. Notably, the TyG-derived indices, specifically TyG-BMI and TyG-WC, demonstrated better predictive performance than AIP-derived indices in the total population, patients aged ≥ 75 years, and both gender subgroups. NRI and IDI analyses further validated this trend. As shown in Figs. 8 and 9, the NRI and IDI values for TyG-BMI and TyG-WC were notably greater than 0 compared to other indices (P < 0.05).

Discussion

Based on longitudinal dynamic cohort data, this study constructed cumulative indices by integrating annual measurement values to initially assess the relationship between cumulative TyG- and AIP-derived indices and terminal survival time in older patients with CSD. Cumulative indices capture an individual’s long-term metabolic exposure more comprehensively, thereby offering superior representativeness and accuracy. The analyses showed that the median terminal survival time for patients with CSD was 12 months, and the cumulative TyG- and AIP-derived indices were significantly associated with terminal survival time. Notably, the age-related effect modification was observed for the associations of terminal survival time with cumulative TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR indices. ROC, NRI, and IDI analyses further indicated that the cumulative TyG-BMI and TyG-WC indices showed an advantage over other indicators in predicting terminal survival time. These findings highlight the importance of dynamically monitoring IR levels in the prognostic assessment of older CSD patients.

This study found that the median terminal survival time for older CSD patients was 12 months, which was significantly shorter than the median overall survival time previously reported for CSD-related diseases [18–21]. On one hand, compared to the interval from diagnosis or hospitalization to death, this study primarily focused on the interval from the last CSD-attributed clinical healthcare-seeking event to death. On the other hand, as a resource-limited region, factors such as healthcare accessibility patterns, health literacy, and delays in care-seeking in Gansu Province may also influence terminal survival time. However, it should be noted that this cohort study required participants to complete at least three annual free health examinations. Consequently, the sample in this study is primarily restricted to individuals who maintained relatively stable contact with the public health service system and demonstrated better adherence to examinations. Importantly, the TyG- and AIP-derived indices were obtained from standardized laboratory tests conducted as part of the routine National Public Health Service Program, rather than being triggered by symptom-driven hospital visits. Therefore, the risk of bias in exposure assessment stemming from variations in healthcare access, health literacy, or care-seeking delays is likely reduced. Conversely, these contextual factors are more likely to influence the timing of the “last documented CSD-related healthcare-seeking event”, thereby affecting the measurement of terminal survival time. It is worth noting, nevertheless, that the directionality of such impacts is not absolute. For instance, limited healthcare accessibility may lead to earlier treatment discontinuation before death (resulting in longer observation intervals) or delayed treatment until critical illness (resulting in shorter observation intervals). Consequently, these mechanisms are more likely to introduce heterogeneity and measurement variability, thereby weakening the true exposure-outcome association and reducing statistical power, rather than consistently biasing association estimates in a specific direction [22]. Moreover, the annual trajectories in this cohort were relatively continuous (Fig. 2), and the consistent association between TyG/AIP-derived indicators and terminal survival time was observed across multiple modeling strategies, which further supports the robustness of our results. Nevertheless, due to the lack of direct measurements for healthcare access, health literacy, and care-seeking delays, the possibility of residual confounding cannot be entirely ruled out. Future studies incorporating more comprehensive socioeconomic and healthcare utilization indicators are warranted to further validate these findings.

Terminal survival time of older CSD patients was reduced with increasing cumulative TyG- and AIP-derived indices, exhibiting a significant non-linear association between them. TyG- and AIP-derived indices reflect the severity of IR, several mechanistic pathways may underlie their impact on CSD progression: (1) Endothelial dysfunction: In IR conditions, impairment of insulin signaling pathways such as PI3K/Akt attenuates endothelial nitric oxide synthase (eNOS) activity, resulting in diminished nitric oxide (NO) synthesis that exacerbates endothelial dysfunction [6, 23]. (2) Inflammatory response: Obesity, as a chronic low-grade inflammation, can cause adipose tissue to release excessive free fatty acids (FFAs) and adipokines such as tumor necrosis factor-alpha (TNF-α), interleukin-6 (IL-6), monocyte chemotactic protein-1 (MCP-1), tissue inhibitor of metalloproteinases-1 (TIMP-1), retinol-binding protein-4 (RBP-4), and leptin, which collectively induce the development of IR [24, 25]; IR further suppresses the activity of mammalian target of rapamycin complex 2 (mTORC2), resulting in upregulated MCP-1 expression, enhanced monocyte migration into adipose tissue, and subsequent differentiation into pro-inflammatory M1 macrophages. These macrophages secrete large amounts of TNF-α, IL-6, and interleukin-1β (IL-1β), inducing more extensive and severe inflammatory responses, thereby forming a vicious cycle of “inflammation-IR” that exacerbates the progression and instability of atherosclerotic plaques [26, 27]. Consistent with this metabolic-vascular crosstalk, emerging evidence links novel adipokines (e.g., CTRP1/CTRP5) directly to visceral adiposity and subclinical atherosclerosis markers like carotid intima–media thickness [28]. Moreover, the causality of these pathways is reinforced by studies showing that insulin-sensitizing agents (rosiglitazone) significantly reduce oxidative stress, while restoring metabolic signaling via AMPK activation mitigates cardiovascular toxicity, highlighting the therapeutic value of targeting these axes [29, 30]. (3) Lipid metabolism disorders: IR raises triglyceride and very-low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) levels, lowers HDL concentrations, and promotes the formation of small dense LDL (sdLDL), thereby forming a pro-atherosclerotic lipid profile that increases the risk of CSD [31]. (4) Myocardial dysfunction: IR activates the renin–angiotensin–aldosterone system (RAAS), triggering excessive secretion of angiotensin II (Ang II) and aldosterone. Ang II directly stimulates the sympathetic nervous system (SNS), promoting the release of norepinephrine and increasing postsynaptic sensitivity. SNS activation subsequently provides feedback stimulation of renin secretion, thereby initiating and sustaining RAAS activity. The mutual reinforcement between RAAS and SNS forms a positive feedback loop that jointly drives myocardial hypertrophy, fibrosis, and impaired contractility, ultimately exacerbating myocardial injury [31–35]. Acting synergistically, these mechanisms precipitate acute cardio- and cerebrovascular events and chronic organ failure, significantly reducing terminal survival time. Conversely, lower cumulative TyG- and AIP-derived indices indicate attenuated IR severity and weakened related pathological processes, thereby prolonging survival. Furthermore, the observed non-linear relationship indicates that the effects of TyG- and AIP-derived indices on terminal survival time dynamically adjust with metabolic status, consistent with previous reports in CVD, cerebrovascular disease, and related metabolic diseases [8, 12, 36]. The adjustability of these indices suggests that targeted interventions, such as regular exercise, weight reduction, limiting intake of fats and carbohydrates, avoiding alcohol consumption, and glucose/lipid-lowering pharmacotherapy, may reduce associated risks, preventing premature death in CSD patients [37]. Notably, the small effect sizes estimated from parametric models (LR, GLM, GEE) may be attributed to the inherent limitations of linear models in capturing nonlinear associations. In contrast, RCS analysis revealed a strong association pattern between indicators and terminal survival time within specific threshold ranges, demonstrating that maintaining lower indicator levels holds significant clinical importance for prolonging terminal survival time.

Cumulative TyG and AIP-derived indices were generally associated with terminal survival time across age and gender strata, and significant age interaction was observed for cumulative TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR. Previous studies indicate that older individuals tend to experience higher baseline oxidative stress levels and shorter telomere lengths, heightening susceptibility to IR [38]. Higher TyG- and AIP-derived indices further exacerbate oxidative stress and telomere loss, forming a feedback loop of aging that accelerates cellular damage and deteriorates survival outcomes [39, 40]. Additionally, the interaction between age and cumulative TyG-BMI, TyG-WC, and TyG-WHtR suggests that the combined effect of TyG, characterized by abnormal blood glucose, and obesity indicators on terminal survival time is age-dependent, with increasing age amplifying the negative effects of TyG-obesity indicators. Conversely, AIP-derived indices primarily reflect dyslipidemia, whose association with terminal survival time appears predominantly driven by lipid metabolism and obesity dynamics, showing less age dependency. It is worth noting that cumulative AIP was not statistically significant among females, which may reflect a weaker effect or differences in exposure distribution.

Cumulative TyG-BMI and TyG-WC indices demonstrate superior performance compared to other indicators in predicting short-term terminal survival time for older patients with CSD. This aligns with previous studies demonstrating the superior predictive performance of TyG-BMI and TyG-WC over TyG or AIP indices in CSD, such as cardiometabolic multimorbidity (CMM), stroke, and coronary heart disease (CHD) [41–43]. In the current study, the predictive performance of the cumulative TyG-BMI and TyG-WC indices in the general population was 5.26% higher than that of the cumulative TyG index. This improvement is primarily attributable to the integration of obesity indicators, with BMI reflecting subcutaneous fat deposition and WC reflecting visceral fat accumulation [44]. Notably, while the predictive performance of the cumulative TyG-WHtR index is inferior to that of the cumulative TyG-BMI and TyG-WC indices in total populations, it approaches them in certain subgroups and outperforms other indicators. Additionally, despite cumulative AIP-derived indices also incorporating obesity indicators, they display limited predictive performance in terminal survival time of CSD, which may be related to the AIP index’s primary reflection of lipid metabolism disorders. Furthermore, the associations between different indicators and various disease types also vary. For example, in cardiovascular diseases, TyG-WC shows the strongest association with total CVD, congestive heart failure, and angina pectoris, while TyG was most strongly associated with CHD [45]. Thus, future research should investigate whether TyG-BMI and TyG-WC exhibit broader or stronger associations with CSD subtypes, which is crucial for elucidating their predictive utility.

Strengths and limitations

Our study has the following advantages. First, this study utilized longitudinal cohort data in which all subjects underwent at least three follow-up measurements to calculate cumulative TyG- and AIP-derived indices, which provide a comprehensive reflection of metabolic burden levels in CSD patients. Second, multiple statistical methods (LR, GLM, GEE, and RCS) were used and confounding factors were controlled as much as possible to validate the association between different cumulative indices and terminal survival time, strengthening the robustness of the results. Finally, this work provides important evidence that TyG- and AIP-derived indices are risk factors for terminal survival time through systematic investigation and analysis.

Several limitations should be acknowledged. First, this single-center study may have selection bias with limited external generalizability. Second, although this study adjusted for available major clinical and demographic variables, some important potential confounding factors were not included, such as ethnicity, educational level, physical activity, family support, mental status, diet, nutritional status, treatment differences, disease severity, and comorbidities (e.g., diabetes, renal disease, or cancer). Third, our data were primarily derived from routine health examination records. Because individual-level medication information was unavailable, residual confounding due to medication use cannot be excluded. However, several prior studies have shown that TyG/AIP-derived indices remain independently associated with CSD-related diseases even after adjusting for dyslipidemia medications, antidiabetic drugs, statins, and other pharmaceuticals, suggesting that medication use alone may not fully explain the observed associations [46–49]. Nevertheless, future research remains necessary to incorporate more comprehensive factors to validate our findings. Fourth, subgroup analyses in the current study were primarily restricted to age and gender. Further exploration of marital status, smoking, alcohol use, and CSD disease types was not conducted due to sample size constraints and the time-varying nature of behavioral variables. These aspects will be addressed in our future research. Finally, the AUC (0.60) of the cumulative TyG-BMI and TyG-WC indices indicates limited independent predictive capability, necessitating cautious interpretation. Their value should be primarily focused on the clinical application potential in risk stratification, such as serving as a simple and cost-effective component within broader risk assessment tools, rather than as independent diagnostic predictors.

Conclusion

TyG- and AIP-derived indices demonstrated significant associations with terminal survival time in older CSD patients, with cumulative TyG-BMI and TyG-WC indices exhibiting better predictive performance than other indicators. These findings suggest that incorporating TyG-derived indices into routine metabolic assessment systems for older CSD patients could serve as simple, economical, and effective prognostic monitoring tools, thereby delaying disease progression and improving survival outcomes.
