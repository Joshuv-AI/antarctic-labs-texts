Bletilla striata polysaccharide microneedles with astragaloside Ⅳ loaded ZIF-8 nanoparticles for wound healing and anti-scar treatment
Authors: Rezhemu Jimo, Jun Ma, Shixing Edi, Ying Sun, Yujie He, Qian Peng, Rui Zeng, Kaijun Gou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractPathological scars (PS) represent a common complication arising post wound healing, potentially resulting in disfigurement and impaired skin functionality in severe instances. Addressing the clinical challenge wherein conventional single treatment modalities struggle to concurrently expedite wound healing and accurately impede scar formation, this study designed and prepared a pH-responsive Bletilla striata polysaccharide (BSP) microneedle patch (BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs) using a PDMS mold. This MNs with astragaloside Ⅳ (As) encapsulated in zeolite imidazolate framework (ZIF-8) can intelligently respond to pH variations at different stages of wound healing to enable controlled release of As. When further combined with BSP, they achieve precise synergistic therapy for wound healing and scar inhibition. Furthermore, a thorough and systematic characterization and performance assessment of the MNs were conducted, followed by an evaluation of their efficacy in enhancing wound healing and suppressing scarring using a rat model with full-thickness skin defects and a rabbit ear scar model. The experimental results showed that the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs exhibited favorable mechanical properties and skin penetration capabilities, alongside remarkable antibacterial efficacy and satisfactory biosafety profiles. Drug release kinetics indicated that the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs released 76.35% of the payload at pH 5.5 and 25.78% at pH 7.4 after 48 h, highlighting an accelerated release of As with decreasing pH levels. Further in vivo experiments demonstrated that the closure rate of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs reached 96.85 ± 0.9% on day 14 (p < 0.001), with nearly complete wound healing by day 21, suggesting a mechanism involving the activation of VEGF and inhibition of TNF-α, leading to expedited wound closure. Notably, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs prevented scar formation (minimal hypertrophic scarring observed on day 35) by reducing TGF-β1, reducing the type I/III collagen ratio, and regulating collagen deposition within the TGF-β1/Smads pathway. In conclusion, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs can synergistically promote wound healing and mitigating scar formation, providing a promising drug delivery strategy for wound repair and scar prevention.Graphical abstract Keywords: Bletilla striata polysaccharide, ZIF-8, Microneedles, Wound healing, Anti-scarring

Introduction

Pathological scars (PS) are an inevitable consequence of the body’s response to trauma repair. PS formation occurs during the late inflammatory and proliferation stages of wound healing when there is excessive fibroblast proliferation and over-deposition of the extracellular matrix, leading to scar formation in the remodeling stage [1, 2]. Common causative factors for PS include trauma, surgery, burns, acne, and skin infections, with the incidence of pathological scarring ranging from 40% to 70% following skin trauma and up to 80% after burns [3]. PS often cause symptoms such as skin itching, pain, disfigurement, and impaired skin function, significantly impacting patients’ quality of life [4]. Thus, it is crucial to inhibit scar formation during the wound healing process. Current research indicates that certain innovative wound dressings have made significant progress in facilitating wound healing. For example, advanced technologies like smart wearable devices and intelligent monitoring systems based on multifunctional hydrogel-based drug delivery platforms have shown promise in promoting wound healing by enabling precise treatment, real-time monitoring, health diagnosis, and personalized management [5, 6]. Furthermore, innovative wound dressings (such as microneedles, hydrogels), which exhibit dual capabilities of promoting wound healing and inhibiting scarring, can intelligently respond, programmatically release drugs, and synergistically function to facilitate scar-free wound healing [7–10]. Despite these notable advancements, the field continues to grapple with fundamental challenges, including the need to address the dynamic coordination imbalance between pro-healing and anti-scarring processes during the wound repair process. Specifically, the challenge persists in achieving precise intervention within the dynamic regulatory timeframe between wound healing and scar formation, resulting in the inability to effectively synergize clinical efficacy for functional healing and aesthetic restoration for scarless regeneration [11, 12]. Therefore, the innovation of a novel therapy capable of expediting wound healing while precisely inhibiting scar formation holds significant clinical relevance and promising prospects for improving patients’ recovery and quality of life.

Microneedle technology, as an innovative minimally invasive transdermal delivery platform, has paved the way for scarless wound repair [9]. Among them, the intelligent responsive microneedle drug delivery systems leverage the dynamic cues from the wound microenvironment, such as pH variations and ROS levels, to achieve precise temporal and sequential drug release [13, 14]. This approach surpasses the limitations in spatial and temporal control associated with conventional anti-scarring therapies, offering a more effective and accurate integrated solution for wound repair, scar prevention, and treatment. Bletilla striata polysaccharide (BSP) has emerged as a promising candidate for research in wound repair and anti-scarring due to its “medicine and adjuvant integration” property [15]. Featuring excellent biocompatibility, high moisture retention capacity, and high viscosity, BSP proves highly suitable for formulating advanced delivery systems like microneedles and hydrogels to enhance wound healing efficiency [16, 17]. Importantly, BSP demonstrates significant therapeutic efficacy in the initial stage of wound healing owing to its antioxidative and anti-inflammatory properties, along with its ability to activate the TGF-β1/Smads pathway to enhance the expression of EGF and VEGF [18]. Although BSP exhibits pronounced pro-wound-healing effects, its anti-PS effect is relatively limited due to its propensity to excessively stimulate collagen deposition during the proliferative phase. Previous research from our group has indicated that BSP-loaded microneedles incorporating asiaticoside can inhibit scar formation by suppressing the fibrosis-related genes TGF-β1 and COLⅠ in the late stage of wound healing [15]. These findings underscore a critical scientific challenge necessitating immediate attention: the precise modulation of the TGF-β1/Smads pathway during the advanced phases of healing and the dynamic regulation of this pathway in tandem with BSP to synergistically achieve effective scarless wound healing.

Astragaloside Ⅳ (As), the primary bioactive constituent of Astragalus membranaceus, demonstrates notable anti-scarring properties. Chen et al. [19] found that As effectively reduces collagen I/III levels and TGF-β1 secretion by fibroblasts in a dose-dependent manner (25–100 µmol/L), thereby exerting anti-scar effects. Furthermore, As facilitates the normalization of collagen arrangement and skin structure by modulating the TGF-β1/Smads signaling pathway and down-regulating the expression of Smad2/3 and α-SMA [20, 21]. When combined with BSP, As exhibits dynamic regulation of the TGF-β1/Smads pathway, thereby achieving a highly efficient synergistic effect in scar-free wound healing. However, the limited solubility of As hampers its bioavailability, thereby restricting its clinical application. Therefore, the identification of a suitable drug carrier is imperative to decrease dosing frequency and enhance the bioavailability of As for improved clinical application [22]. Zeolite imidazolate framework (ZIF-8) is a class of crystalline porous materials constructed by bridging metal ions with organic linkers, characterized by easy synthesis, high stability, large specific surface area, and high porosity [23–25]. These features endow ZIF-8 with a substantial drug-loading capacity and the ability to improve the bioavailability of poorly soluble drugs. Notably, ZIF-8 displays excellent biocompatibility, with the Zn²⁺ it contains capable of disrupting bacterial membranes and upregulating vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF) expression [26], which confers significant advantages in wound healing compared to other nanoparticle formulations.

Furthermore, ZIF-8 is frequently employed as a construction material for pH-responsive drug delivery systems due to its exceptional pH sensitivity [27]. Specifically, ZIF-8 exhibits stability in neutral to alkaline pH environments (pH > 7) while demonstrating instability in acidic environment (especially in the pH < 6.4). The degradation mechanism of ZIF-8 in acidic settings involves the following process [28, 29]: the coordination bond between Zn²⁺ and the imidazole nitrogen atom is inherently stable, maintaining the integrity of the framework structure. However, the 2-methylimidazole ligands in ZIF-8 undergo protonation in an acidic environment, disrupting the coordination interaction between zinc ions and the imidazole ring, consequently leading to structural degradation. Thus, the excellent pH sensitivity of ZIF-8 allows accelerated degradation under acidic conditions, enabling the controlled release of drugs in response to the pH microenvironment of the wound [27, 30]. Current research has demonstrated that the pH microenvironment of wounds undergoes a transition from neutral/alkaline conditions (pH > 7) to acidic levels (pH 4 ~ 6) during the healing process [31–33]. Specifically, in the initial stages of wound healing encompassing hemostasis, inflammation, and proliferation, the wound pH surpasses 7.4. In the final remodeling phase, the wound environment gradually turns acidic, with a pH ranging from 4 to 6. This characteristic of pH variation aligns precisely with the drug release mechanism of As@ZIF-8, thereby facilitating the targeted inhibition of scar formation with precision.

In this study, an intelligent responsive drug-release microneedles patch (BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs) was successfully fabricated by blending As@ZIF-8 with BSP using a PDMS mold, enabling the programmed regulation of inflammation, proliferation, and remodeling stages. Upon application at the wound site, BSP and As@ZIF-8 collaboratively exhibit antibacterial, anti-inflammatory, and cell proliferation effects to promote wound healing in the early stages. Subsequently, as the wound progresses to the late proliferation and remodeling stages, the controlled release of As from As@ZIF-8 in response to pH variations facilitates precise scar formation inhibition, leading to scarless wound healing by modulating type I/III collagen levels and regulating collagen deposition within the TGF-β1/Smads pathway. Through a comprehensive assessment involving in vivo and in vitro experiments, the feasibility of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs in promoting wound healing and preventing scarring was thoroughly evaluated. In summary, this study proposes a promising drug delivery strategy to achieve rapid wound healing and targeted scar suppression.

Materials and methods

Materials

Bletilla striata (B. striata) was acquired from Yunnan Province (Yunnan, China). Astragaloside Ⅳ (As, content ≥ 90%) was obtained from Chengdu Alfa Biotechnology Co., Ltd (Chengdu, China). Zinc nitrate (content ≥ 99.0%) was purchased from Tianjin Damao Chemical Reagent Factory (Tianjin, China). 2-Methylimidazole (content ≥ 99.0%) was purchased from Shanghai Titan Scientific Co., Ltd (Shanghai, China). Male Sprague Dawley (SD) rats weighing 180–220 g and Male New Zealand Rabbits weighing 1.5–2.0 kg were obtained from Chengdu Dossy Experimental Animals Co., Ltd. (Chengdu, China). Phosphate buffered saline (PBS), high glucose (4.5 g/L), Dulbecco’s modified Eagle’s medium (DMEM), Fetal bovine serum (FBS), Trypsin-EDTA (0.05%), and penicillin-streptomycin (10,000 U/mL) were sourced from Gibco (Grand Island, America). Deionized water was utilized throughout the experiments. All other chemicals employed in this study were of analytical grade.

Preparation of ZIF-8 NPs and As@ZIF-8 NPs

The nanoscale ZIF-8 and As@ZIF-8 were synthesized using a one-pot method with slight modifications based on literature methods [26, 30]. Initially, 2-methylimidazole (330 mg, 4.019 mmoL) was dissolved in 10 mL of anhydrous methanol and stirred at room temperature for 30 min until a homogeneous solution (solution A) was obtained. Subsequently, a solution of Zn (NO3)2·6H2O (150 mg, 0.5 mmoL) was dissolved in 5 mL of deionized water and stirred at room temperature for 30 min until a homogeneous solution (solution B) was achieved. Finally, solution B was slowly added dropwise to solution A under magnetic stirring. Following stirring at room temperature for 3 h, a milky white suspension wasproduced. This suspension underwent centrifugation at 10,000 rpm for 15 min, followed by three washes with methanol and subsequent drying to obtain ZIF-8 powder. The encapsulation of As into ZIF-8 NPs (As@ZIF-8 NPs) was achieved following a similar process as described previously. The key variation involved the introduction of 10 mg As into the 2-Methylimidazole solution (solution A) during the preparation of As@ZIF-8 NPs.

Preparation of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs

Utilizing the research group’s prior expertise [34], BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs were formulated by blending BSP with As@ZIF-8. Briefly speaking, an appropriate amount of ZIF-8 or As@ZIF-8 powder was dispersed in deionized water (3.0 mg/mL), followed by the addition of BSP (15% w/v), and thorough mixing at room temperature. Subsequently, the resulting mixed prepolymer solution was loaded into a PDMS mold, subjected to centrifugation at 4000 rpm for 15 min to ensure complete mold filling and bubble removal. Finally, the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs were manually extracted from the punch mold after complete curing at room temperature.

Characterizations and properties of ZIF-8 NPs and As@ZIF-8 NPs

The ZIF-8 and As@ZIF-8 NPs sizes were measured using a particle size analyzer. The morphologies of ZIF-8 and As@ZIF-8 NPs were characterized using scanning electron microscopy (SEM) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Fourier infrared spectroscopy (FTIR) was employed to confirm the successful synthesis of ZIF-8 and As@ZIF-8 NPs. X-ray diffraction (XRD) analysis was conducted to confirm the crystalline structure of ZIF-8 and assess the loading of As. The encapsulation efficiency and drug loading capacity of As@ZIF-8 NPs were determined by establishing a high-performance liquid chromatography(HPLC)-evaporative luminescence detection (ELSD) method [35, 36]. Specifically, the content of As was detected using acetonitrile-water (32:68) as the mobile phase, and detected by evaporative light scattering detector. Finally, the pH-responsive drug release properties of As@ZIF-8 NPs were evaluated in vitro at pH 5.5 and pH 7.4 using the dialysis bag technique with PBS as the release medium. The cumulative release profile was obtained using the above-mentioned HPLC-ELSD detection method.

Characterizations and properties of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs

To comprehensively characterize the physical structure of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs, detailed morphological images were captured from various perspectives to document and analyze their appearance. Subsequently, SEM was employed to precisely elucidate the microstructural features of BSP-As@ZIF-8 microstructure with an acceleration voltage set of 5 kV. Furthermore, the relevant properties of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs were evaluated. The mechanical properties of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs were objectively examined with a texture analyzer at a rate of 0.1 mm/s. Additionally, the puncture performance of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs was tested by inserting them into aluminum foil with appropriate force applied by the thumb [37]. To the potential skin irritation caused by BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs, the MNs were applied to the shaved backs of depilated SD rats for 1 min before removal. Following removal, the skin condition and other phenomena were observed at 5-minute intervals, documenting the appearance of pinholes and the duration until redness subsided. Subsequently, to further evaluate the in vivo skin-puncturing ability, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs were pressed onto the back of SD rats (after hair removal and anesthesia) for 3 min. The skin tissue at the microneedle puncture site was then excised. Post histological processing with H&E staining and paraffin embedding, the skin tissue sections were examined under a microscope to visualize the microneedle penetration.

In addition, in vivo dissolution experiments were performed. BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs were inserted into the backs of shaved rats and subsequently removed at specified time points to assess the morphological alterations of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs. Finally, the degradation properties of BSP MNs, BSP-ZIF-8 MNs and BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs were investigated. BSP MNs, BSP-ZIF-8 MNs, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs (M0) were weighed and placed in 10 mL PBS (pH = 7.4), subjected to agitation in a shaking incubator (100 rpm, 37 ℃). At predetermined intervals, the microneedles were retrieved for freeze-drying, and the weights (Mt) of BSP MNs, BSP-ZIF-8 MNs, and BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs were weighed. Anti-bacterial performance

The antimicrobial activity of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs was assessed using gram-positive Staphylococcus aureus (S. aureus, ATCC 43300) and gram-negative Escherichia coli (E. coli, ATCC 25922). BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs samples were placed in a sterile environment and sterilized under UV light for 30 min to eliminate surface bacteria. Subsequently, 200 µL of a 1 × 106 CFU/mL bacterial solution and 800 µL of culture solution were placed in centrifuge tubes and incubated for 12 h at 37 ℃ in a CO2 incubator. The bacterial concentration was then diluted to 1 × 102 CFU/mL, and 100 µL of the solution was plated onto solid growth medium. Following an 18-h incubation period with the samples, the bacteria were observed and counted. Moreover, the agar disk diffusion test was performed to evaluate the antibacterial activity of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs. The 1 × 106 CFU/mL bacteria were inoculated on the agar plate. The sample was placed in the center of the hole and incubated at 37 ℃ for 24 h to assess antibacterial activity. Finally, the petri dish was removed from the incubator, photographed, and the inhibition zone of all samples was measured using ImageJ. The experiment was repeated three times.

Cytotoxicity detection by flow cytometry

The co-culture method of L929 fibroblasts with the samples was consistent with the CCK-8 method. After a 24-hour co-culture with hydrogel solutions in 24-well plates, the medium was aspirated, and the cells were rinsed twice with sterile PBS. Subsequently, the cells were stained with the live and dead cell staining kit according to the manufacturer’s instructions, incubated at 37 ℃ for 15 min, and then washed twice with PBS to remove excess dye. The cells were resuspended in PBS, and the fluorescence intensity was measured using a BD FACSCanto equipped with a 488 nm laser in the FL1 channel. Data analysis was performed using FlowJo analysis software.

Cell scratch assay

The cell scratch assay was conducted to simulate an in vitro wound model and evaluate the impact of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs on in vitro wound scratches. L929 fibroblasts cultured in 6-well plates were observed under a microscope until they reached 80–90% confluence. Subsequently, scratches of the same width were made in each well, and the scratched cells were washed with PBS. Following this, 2 mL of 14 mM samples filtrate was added to each well. Finally, the plate was placed in a cell culture incubator for 24 h. Cell migration at different time points (0 and 48 h) was observed and recorded under a microscope.

Pharmacodynamic evaluation of in vivo wound healing

The pharmacodynamic studies of wound healing was performed using male SD rats (weighing 180–220 g). The handling of all animals adhered to animal welfare regulations and standardized guidelines, with approval from the Ethics Committee of Southwest Minzu University (NO. SWMU-202401089) obtained. Full-thickness skin defect wound models were created by inducing circular wounds with a 10 mm diameter on the backs of SD rats (4 wounds per rat). The MNs samples were applied to the wound following disinfection by 30 min of ultraviolet irradiation, with the application repeated once every 3 d for 21 d, while the control group received no treatment. Subsequently, these wounds were photographed at 0, 3, 5, 7, 14 and 21 d. The amount of wound closure was determined using the following equation:

Following 21 d of treatment, male SD rats were sacrificed. Wound samples were randomly selected from each group for histological examination. H&E staining and Masson’s trichrome staining were employed to assess epithelial reformation and collagen deposition. Immunohistochemical analysis was conducted using staining for TGF-β, TNF-α, and VEGF.

Pharmacodynamic evaluation of in vivo anti-scar

The pharmacodynamic studies of anti-scar were performed using male New Zealand white rabbits (3–4 months old). A 10 mm diameter biopsy punch was utilized to create the rabbit ear scar model by completely removing the perichondrium. The wounds were treated with MNs samples (ultraviolet irradiation for 30 min) of 10 mm diameter once daily for 35 d. During the treatment, the wound closure and scar formation were monitored through photographic observations. Subsequently, on the 35 d, the scars were harvested for histological analyses including H&E staining, Masson’s trichrome staining and Sirius Red staining. The anti-scar effect of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs was assessed by examining collagen deposition and the ratio of type I to type III collagen in the scar tissue.

Statistical analysis

All experimental data were independently replicated at least three times, and the outcomes were expressed as mean ± standard deviation (mean ± SD). Group comparisons were evaluated using t-tests or ANOVA. Statistical analysis was performed using OriginPro2021 and GraphPad Prism 8.0 software to determine significant differences, with p < 0.05 considered significant. Non-significant results were denoted as NS, while *p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, ***p < 0.001, and ****p < 0.0001 represented increasing levels of statistical significance.

Results and discussion

Preparation of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs

In this study, ZIF-8 NPs and BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs were successfully synthesized (Fig. 1). ZIF-8 comprises tetrahedral Zn2+ and imidazolic acid ligands through coordination bonds, where the nitrogen atom of 2-methylimidazole forms a stable coordination bond with Zn2+ by donating a lone pair of electrons [38]. Nanoscale ZIF-8 NPs, characterized by a substantial surface area and significant porosity, are increasingly recognized in the realm of antimicrobial agents for their potent antimicrobial properties, acid-responsive degradation capabilities, and favorable biocompatibility [39].

The preparation process of BSP-As @ ZIF-8 MNs

Characterizations and properties of ZIF-8 and As@ZIF-8 NPs

ZIF-8 NPs and As@ZIF-8 NPs with a rhombic dodecahedral structure were successfully synthesized via a one-pot method, maintaining their morphological integrity (Fig. 2A). The particle size of As@ZIF-8 NPs (540 nm) was observed to be larger than that of ZIF-8 NPs (402 nm) due to the encapsulation of As (Fig. 2B). FTIR analysis revealed characteristic N─Zn, C═N and C─N stretching vibration peak at 998, 1395 and 1151 cm−1, respectively, confirming the successful synthesis of ZIF-8 NPs [40]. Similarly, As@ZIF-8 NPs and ZIF-8 NPs exhibited identical characteristic peaks, as the features of As were shielded by ZIF-8 NPs, providing evidence of the encapsulation of As within ZIF-8 NPs (Fig. 2C) [41]. XRD analyses revealed distinct diffraction peaks at 2θ = 7.4°, 10.4°, and 12.7°, corresponding to multiple planes such as (011), (002), and (112), respectively, confirming the successful synthesis of ZIF-8. Moreover, the pattern of As@ZIF-8 NPs was consistent with that of ZIF-8, indicating that the incorporation of As occurred in an amorphous state without altering the crystalline structure of ZIF-8, consistent with the FTIR findings (Fig. 2D). Upon determination, the drug loading capacity and encapsulation efficiency of As@ZIF-8 NPs were 12.3 ± 0.8% and 82.7 ± 1.5%, respectively. Finally, the drug release studies revealed a rapid release of As from As@ZIF-8 within the first few hours under pH 5.5 conditions, with a cumulative release of 76.35% after 48 h. However, the release of As at pH 7.4 was notably slower, with only 25.78% released after 48 h, indicating the acid-responsive controlled release capability of As@ZIF-8 (Fig. 2E). These above pH-responsive drug release results align with the inherent stability of ZIF-8 in neutral to alkaline environments (pH > 7) and its instability in acidic conditions (pH < 6.4).

Characterizations and properties of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs

The BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs patch featured a unique array design comprising a 20 × 20 microneedle layout with 300 μm spacing, exhibiting uniform cone-shaped microneedles with a diameter of approximately 200 μm and a height of 600 μm (Fig. 3A-B). The mechanical strength of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs surpassed the minimum force required for skin penetration (0.9 N), indicating their favorable potential for skin penetration (Fig. 3C). Additionally, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs could successfully puncture aluminum foil, further confirming their powerful piercing ability (Fig. 3D). Masson staining results demonstrated the successful penetration of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs into the skin, providing additional microscopic-level evidence to support the aforementioned findings (Fig. 3E). In addition, skin irritation of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs was evaluated, demonstrating that after successful penetration and subsequent removal of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs, the skin returned to its baseline state within approximately 25 min without notable irritation, underscoring a favorable safety profile (Fig. 3F). Furthermore, the soluble MNs have the characteristics of dissolution and drug release post-insertion into the skin. Prior to insertion, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs displayed intact shapes with sharp tips. After 5 min of insertion, the tip began to dissolve, leading to bluntness. Within 30 min, the MN tips were nearly completely dissolved (Fig. 3G), suggesting that drug dissolution and release occur through tissue fluid absorption post-insertion. In vitro experiments revealed that the degradation rates of BSP MNs, BSP-ZIF-8 MNs, and BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs within 90 min were 93.34%, 89.94% and 88.37%, respectively (Fig. 3H).

The anti-bacterial performance and hemolysis rate of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs

When a wound is exposed to the external environment, it becomes vulnerable to colonization by pathogenic bacteria such as S. aureus and E. coli [42, 43]. Figure 4A-D, the incorporation of ZIF-8 significantly enhanced the antibacterial efficacy, validating the remarkable antibacterial property of ZIF-8. For wound treatment, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs demonstrated notable inhibition of bacterial growth and replication, achieving bacteriostatic rates exceeding 95% against both bacterial strains. This potent antibacterial effect contributes to enhanced wound healing. Additionally, the agar plate diffusion test was employed to provide a more representative assessment of the actual in-situ release characteristics and antibacterial properties of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs on the skin. The observed size of the inhibition zones in each experimental group correlated with the colony counting method, with larger inhibition zones compared to the control group. In particular, the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs group exhibited the most substantial inhibition zone against S. aureus and E. coli, measuring (22.3 ± 1.2 mm) and (28.9 ± 0.96 mm), respectively, underscoring the robust antibacterial properties of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs and their efficacy in promoting wound healing (Fig. 4A-B, E-F). Furthermore, the hemolysis rate of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs was below 5% (Fig. 4G), indicating exceptional compatibility with red blood cells and the absence of a hemolytic effect. These results suggest that BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs are a wound dressing with excellent biocompatibility.

Cytotoxicity and cell scratch assay of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs

The experimental data revealed that the viability of L929 cells co-incubated with As, ZIF-8, As@ZIF-8, BSP MNs, BSP-ZIF-8 MNs and BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs samples was 98.5%, 98.8%, 98.4%, 97.3%, 98.6%, and 99.1%, respectively. There results indicated no cytotoxic effects in any of the groups. Upon the addition of ZIF-8 and As@ZIF-8 to BSP MNs, the cell viability continued to rise, suggesting a synergistic effect of each component material (Fig. 5A). Furthermore, CCK-8 test results demonstrated that various concentrations of all samples exhibited no significant cytotoxicity after 24-hour co-culture with L929 cells, with cell viability exceeded 90% (Fig. 5B). Interestingly, the cell activity values of the BSP MNs, BSP-ZIF-8 MNs, and BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs in the three groups consistently exceeded those of the other groups at all concentrations, with cell viability even surpassing 100%, thereby suggesting that the inclusion of BSP provided a protective and growth-enhancing effect on the cells. In conclusion, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs demonstrated non-toxicity to cells and exhibited favorable biosafety. The effect of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs on L929 fibroblast migration was further evaluated. As shown in Fig. 5C-D, the migration rates followed a hierarchical trend: control group (31.09 ± 1.85%) < As (32.15 ± 2.11%) < ZIF-8 (33.54 ± 1.97%) < As@ZIF-8 (40.69 ± 2.34%) < BSP MNs (42.39 ± 1.76%) ≈ BSP-ZIF-8 MNs (42.55 ± 2.09%). Notably, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs demonstrated a significantly elevated migration rate (52.70 ± 1.92%, p < 0.001) compared with other groups. This enhanced migration can be attributed to the synergistic effects of As, ZIF-8, and BSP in promoting in vitro wound healing. The utilization of ZIF-8 as a carrier enhances the stability and bioavailability of As, enabling pH-responsive controlled release [27, 44]. In addition, the combined actions of As and BSP promote fibroblast proliferation and migration [45, 46]. Collectively, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs exert a synergistic enhancement effect of promoting cell migration, thus expediting the process of wound healing.

In vivo wound healing study

The wound healing efficacy of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs was evaluated using a rat wound model. Analysis of the experimental data from the wound healing process diagrams (Fig. 6A, B) revealed a reduction in wound area size after 5 d of treatment, with significant wound closure in the BSP MNs, BSP-ZIF-8 MNs and BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs group on 14 d compared to the control group. The results of the wound closure assay presented in Fig. 6C demonstrated accelerated closure rates in all groups within the initial 7 days compared to baseline (p < 0.01). Particularly significant was the achievement of a 90.55 ± 1.2% closure rate by the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs, which significantly exceeded that of the Control group (78.48 ± 1.8%, p < 0.001), likely attributable to the ZIF-8-mediated enhancement of drug bioavailability. During the proliferative phase (days 7–14), Control wounds displayed a gradual closure progression from 78.48 ± 1.8% to 87.93 ± 1.5%. Conversely, the treatment group maintained an accelerated closure rate, in which BSP MNs reached 92.04 ± 0.4%, BSP-ZIF-8 MNs reached 91.67 ± 0.7%, and BSP-As @ ZIF-8 MNs reached 96.85 ± 0.9% (p < 0.001). These findings suggest a consistent wound healing-promoting effect in vivo with the in vitro cell migration promotion effect (As@ZIF-8 MNs > BSP MNs ≈ BSP-ZIF-8 MNs > control group). Ultimately, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs achieved near-complete healing during the remodeling phase (days 14–21).

Furthermore, H&E staining analysis demonstrated that the control group had a thickened epidermal layer on the wound surface, showing increased accumulation of inflammatory cells and fibroblasts. In contrast, the groups treated with BSP MNs, BSP-ZIF-8 MNs, and BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs displayed thinner epidermal layers with reduced inflammatory factors on the epidermis. Notably, these treatment groups exhibited vascular and hair follicle structures, with the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs group notably demonstrating abundant subcutaneous hair follicles that closely resembled normal skin tissue (Fig. 6D). Additionally, Masson staining showed that BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs had more pronounced collagen deposition (28.43%) with a more ordered distribution of collagen fibers compared to the control group with disordered collagen deposition (Fig. 6E-F). These observations suggest that the ZIF-8 carrier facilitates phase-specific controlled release of As throughout the wound healing process, leading to a dual therapeutic effect: promoting early-stage tissue regeneration through anti-inflammatory and pro-angiogenic effects, while suppressing late-stage scar formation through orderly collagen deposition [47, 48].

Immunohistological analysis

During the wound healing process, tumor necrosis factor-α (TNF-α), vascular endothelial growth factor (VEGF), and transforming growth factor-β1 (TGF-β) collectively play roles in anti-inflammatory, pro-angiogenic, and regulatory effects on extracellular matrix (ECM) balance and collagen metabolism, which are crucial for wound repair [49–52]. The immunohistochemistry staining and the quantitative analysis were employed to comprehensively investigate the characteristics of the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs. The results revealed that the expression level of TNF-α in BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs (4.33%) was significantly lower than that in the Control group (11.82%) (p < 0.0001), and obviously lower than that in the BSP-ZIF-8 MNs (7.25%) and BSP MNs (9.20%) groups (Fig. 7A-B). Furthermore, VEGF expression level of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs (13.08%) was significantly higher than that in the Control group (6.34%) (p < 0.0001), and obviously higher compared to the BSP-ZIF-8 MNs (10.81%) and BSP MNs (8.62%) groups (Fig. 7A, C). These findings indicate that BSP and As in the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs drug delivery system exerted a strong anti-inflammatory and angiogenic effect in a synergistic manner. In addition, in terms of TGF-β1 expression, the BSP-As-ZIF-8 MNs group demonstrated a reduced expression level (5.77%) compared to the Control group (9.36%) (p < 0.001) (Fig. 7A, D), implying their superior performance in modulating the cellular microenvironment and facilitating organized repair processes.

In vivo anti-scar study

This study further assessed the efficacy of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs to inhibit scar formation in the rabbit ear wound model. During wound healing (Fig. 8A-C), the wound area in each treatment group decreased after 7 d of treatment, leading to significant wound closure and scab formation by 14 d. After 35 d of treatment, all wounds in each treatment group were basically completely healed. However, apart from the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs, the other treatment groups formed red and elevated hypertrophic scars at 35 d. In contrast, the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs treatment group displayed minimal hypertrophic scarring, indicating their potential for scarless wound healing. H&E staining results further showed that BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs group exhibited limited proliferative fibroblast infiltration and organized proliferation in both the epidermis and dermis compared to the Control group (Fig. 8D). Furthermore, the results of Sirius Red and Masson staining showed that the ratios of type I (red) to type III (green) collagen in both groups treated by BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs, with and without laser, were notably lower than those in the Control group, highlighting the scar-reducing effects of BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs (Fig. 8E-H). In summary, the results from rat wound and rabbit ear wound model studies suggested that BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs can enhance early-stage wound healing through anti-inflammatory and angiogenic mechanisms, while also providing anti-scarring effects in later stages by reducing TGF-β1 levels, decreasing the collagen I/III ratio, and regulating the collagen deposition. This beneficial outcome is attributed to the ability of ZIF-8 in the BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs drug delivery system to modulate As release in response to the wound microenvironment changes, thereby synergizing with BSP to facilitate scarless healing.

Conclusion

This study introduced an innovative microneedle delivery approach designed to respond intelligently to the pH microenvironment for scarless wound healing. Initially, the nanoscale ZIF-8 and As@ZIF-8 were synthesized by a one-pot method. Subsequently, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs were fabricated by injecting the co-mixing prepolymer solution of BSP and As@ZIF-8 into a PDMS mold. BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs can controllably release As according to the pH changes in different wound healing stages, thereby promoting scarless wound healing, effectively addressing the clinical challenge of balancing rapid wound closure and precise scar inhibition. Moreover, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs exhibited excellent mechanical properties, skin penetration capabilities, antibacterial ability, cell migration promotion, and biocompatibility. Notably, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs can promote wound healing by promoting epidermal regeneration to accelerate the wound-healing process, downregulating TNF-α to reduce inflammation, and upregulating VEGF to promote angiogenesis. Simultaneously, these MNs can regulate TGF-β expression, inhibit collagen deposition, and reduce the type I/III collagen ratio, thereby exerting anti-scar effects. Collectively, BSP-As@ZIF-8 MNs exhibit promising application potential in the realm of wound healing therapy, offering a viable solution for scarless wound recovery.
