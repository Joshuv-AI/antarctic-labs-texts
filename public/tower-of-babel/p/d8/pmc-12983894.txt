Perspectives of female and under-represented physicians on well-being in medicine: a qualitative study from an academic medical centre in the USA
Authors: Anish K Agarwal, Rachel E Gonzales, Meghana Sharma, Devon Schroeder, David A Asch, Nandita Mitra, Lauren Southwick, Lisa M Bellini, Raina M Merchant
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundStress and burnout are pervasive among physicians. Academic physicians who are female and physicians who are under-represented in medicine (URM) face inequities in the workplace and beyond. Understanding their experiences is crucial for workforce sustainability and diversity, especially given the disproportionate effects on these individuals and overall workforce capacity.ObjectiveTo qualitatively explore the perspectives of academic female and URM physicians and identify key themes affecting their careers and well-being.DesignSemi-structured interviews were conducted with 30 physicians at an urban academic health system. Interviews were audio-recorded, transcribed and thematically analysed using a general inductive approach. Interview guides were informed by prior literature and constructs.InterventionNone.Setting and participantsFemale and URM physicians from a large, academic medical centre were recruited via email. Participants self-reported demographic information, including sex, race, ethnicity and tenure.Outcomes and measuresThe primary outcomes encompassed the main themes identified through the analysis of interviews with female and URM physicians regarding their perspectives on well-being, mental health and academic medicine.Results30 female or URM physicians were interviewed (27 (90%) female; 14 (47%) black, Asian or multi-racial). Thematic analysis revealed four key themes: physician identity (URM, female, family), well-being in the workplace (emotional health, staffing burden, non-clinical responsibilities), barriers to accessing well-being resources (workplace environment, culture, overgeneralisation) and facilitators to well-being (physician camaraderie, leadership support). Physicians discussed how their identities influenced their experiences of well-being. They highlighted emotional health challenges, staffing burdens and administrative tasks contributing to stress. Barriers to accessing resources included workplace culture and broad-based interventions, while supportive leadership and camaraderie were identified as facilitators of access.ConclusionFemale and URM physicians face systemic challenges impacting their well-being and careers. These findings underscore the need to address systemic changes and specifically design programmes focused on promoting the well-being and inclusivity of female and URM physicians. Tailored interventions to these individuals, supportive leadership structures and collaborative working cultures are crucial for addressing these issues and sustaining a diverse physician workforce.

Introduction

Emotional stress and burnout persist in medicine, threatening both individual health and the long-term resilience of the largest sectors of the US workforce.13 The pandemic exacerbated existing acute stressors, burdening an already strained system, and these stressors persist. Specifically, physicians grapple with moral injury (eg, the distress associated with forsaking personal principles in demand-resource mismatch scenarios4), workforce shortages, increasing workloads and administrative tasks.5 Attention to these challenges has been amplified by health systems, academies, medical societies, policymakers and the media.6 7

The culture of medicine and US society places additional challenges on female physicians, including, but not limited to, sexism in the workplace and disproportionately high dependent care responsibilities outside of the workplace—both of which contribute to higher risk for burnout.8 9 Female physicians in academic medicine report slower career progression and development,8 9 particularly in academic achievements, due to historical barriers in work productivity.10

Additionally, physicians from racial or ethnic backgrounds that are traditionally under-represented in medicine (URM) experience heightened and compounded stress and mental health outcomes.11 These physicians encounter microaggressions, overt acts of racism and structural racism within and outside of their clinical roles.1216 URM physicians also experience social isolation within the workplace.1 11 These overlapping social identities, including but not limited to gender, race and caregiving status, interact to produce distinct and compounded forms of disadvantage. For URM female physicians, the convergence of sexism, racism and disproportionate caregiving expectations may uniquely shape experiences of stress, well-being and academic career progression.

The historic structures and culture of academic medicine create barriers and challenges that disproportionately impact female and URM physicians and negatively impact their careers and well-being. A gap persists in identifying, describing and investigating these nuanced areas from the perspective of those physicians experiencing these inequitable stressors. Given the complexity of these lived experiences, qualitative methods provide a crucial tool for capturing a more comprehensive understanding of URM and female physicians’ experiences with workplace well-being. The objective of this study was to deploy qualitative interviews to identify key themes affecting the well-being of URM and female physicians within and outside the workplace.

Methods

Study overview

Between February and March 2023, we conducted semi-structured qualitative interviews with 30 physicians at a large, urban academic medical centre with over 6200 physicians across four sites. This qualitative study was designed to identify and explore a broad range of experiences relating to workplace well-being among female and URM physicians. This study adhered to the Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research reporting guidelines.11

Participants were recruited via email invitations sent to organisational listservs within the health system, focusing on female and URM physician faculty. A convenience sample of 30 individuals who received this email and completed informed consent were selected for participation in this study. The sample size was established before recruitment based on prior experiences of achieving thematic saturation in similar qualitative studies.17 18 This sampling strategy was suitable for capturing experiential depth within a defined institutional context and aligns with qualitative descriptive approaches that focus on contextualised understanding rather than population-level generalisation. Participants’ self-reported demographic characteristics, including age, sex, race, ethnicity and tenure (table 1).

Participant demographics and characteristics

This study was conducted within a constructivist epistemological paradigm, which assumes that experiences of well-being and career sustainability are socially constructed and shaped by institutional, cultural and identity-based contexts. We employed a qualitative descriptive design, using reflexive thematic analysis, to capture participants’ perspectives in a manner closely grounded in their own language and meanings, thereby informing organisational practice rather than generating theory.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and the general public were not involved in the study design. The results of the study will be disseminated to all participants and shared with the broader academic community.

Interviews

Standardised interview guides (online supplemental appendix) were developed based on the Consolidated Framework for Implementation Research (CFIR).19 CFIR was used as a sensitising framework during the development of the interview guide to ensure coverage of the various levels of organisational impact on well-being. CFIR did not guide data coding or analysis. The interview questions (online supplemental appendix) were aligned with the National Academy of Medicine framework on clinician well-being,20 themes from prior research and focus groups with healthcare workers on topics such as burnout and equity.21 22 The guide was designed to explore the well-being of female and URM physicians, as well as the barriers and facilitators to accessing and receiving well-being, mental health and culturally sensitive support resources. Interview domains included: (1) conceptualisations of physician well-being; (2) the impact of workplace environment and workload on well-being; (3) the influence of identity, including gender, race/ethnicity and caregiving roles; (4) experiences with peer and community support; (5) awareness, access and use of institutional well-being and mental health resources; (6) perceived barriers and facilitators to accessing support; and (7) perspectives on leadership, policy change and future career sustainability. The interview guide was created by the research team, which includes experts in qualitative methods (AKA, REG, LS), health equity and leadership (AKA, RMM) and mental health (AKA, RMM). Before the implementation, the guide underwent testing among the study team (MS, LS, REG). Beyond the guide, participants were allowed time and space to add additional thoughts or commentary.

Interviews were conducted using a virtual video communications platform. Interviews were conducted by a female, South Asian research team member with over 7 years of experience in qualitative research methods (MS). The interviewer had no prior relationship with the participants and communicated the research goals at the beginning of each interview and had no clinical medical training. The interviews, which ranged from 20 to 40 min in length, were audio-recorded, transcribed and de-identified. As approved by the IRB, participants provided verbal consent at the beginning of the interview and were compensated with a US$100 gift card.

Thematic analysis

A thematic analysis of interview transcripts was conducted using a general inductive approach as previously described in the literature23 24 to understand workplace well-being among female and URM physicians through their experiences. Research staff (MS, LS, REG) employed an emergent, iterative open-coding process to develop an initial codebook, which was revised iteratively throughout the coding process. To ensure reliability within coding, two authors (MS, REG) double-coded 25% of the transcripts for ongoing inter-rater agreement, measured by Cohen’s kappa (above 0.8). Through iterative team discussions, codes were clustered into higher-order categories reflecting patterns across participants. These categories were further refined into themes by examining how experiences related to perceived career sustainability, institutional value and decisions about remaining in academic medicine. Coding discrepancies were identified, discussed and resolved among team members (MS, REG). Thematic saturation was considered achieved when no additional codes were being added to the codebook. NVivo, V.14 (QRS International), was used for data storage, management and coding.25 Data analysis was performed from April to May 2023.

Results

30 URM or female physicians participated in the study (table 1). In short, the sample consisted of 90% females, 50% white and 46.7% black, Asian or multiracial. The responses were organised into four themes with subthemes: (1) impact of intersectional identities (race; gender; parent or caregiver), (2) well-being in the workplace (emotional health; staffing burden; working at top of licence), (3) barriers to accessing and receiving well-being resources (workplace environment; culture; broad scope) and (4) facilitators to well-being resources (social support; leadership) (online supplemental table). Across themes, participants consistently linked well-being to perceptions of long-term career viability, including decisions to reduce effort, seek alternative roles or question remaining in academic medicine.

Theme 1: impact of intersectional identities

Participants’ narratives demonstrated that gender, race/ethnicity and caregiving responsibilities were rarely experienced in isolation. URM women physicians described how these identities intersect to amplify workplace scrutiny, limit flexibility and intensify guilt related to caregiving. These compounded pressures shaped not only day-to-day well-being but also long-term perceptions of sustainability in academic medicine.

Race

URM participants shared how their race or ethnicity often was perceived as ‘a topic of conversation’ with their patients and believed it negatively affected many of their racially or ethnically diverse patient–doctor relationships. One participant reflected, “they see a young black female provider and … there’s a level of respect that is missed” (Participant 30, non-Hispanic black female). Participants described how their race or ethnicity influenced their personal well-being, particularly due to isolation within their specialty or broader career path. A participant elaborated, “I know most places I enter when it comes to medicine, I’m going to be othered. That multiplies the stress that I perceive” (Participant 29, non-Hispanic black male). The limited racial or ethnic diversity among peers created additional barriers to seeking assistance, mentorship and advice in advancing one’s career.

Gender

Female physician participants shared significant feelings of frustration when discussing gender biases and experiences of sexism in academic medicine from their patients and their colleagues. One participant said, “I definitely get triggered when my patients in the hospital have a hard time understanding that I am the physician taking care of them (and not their nurse), and I think it’s because of gender” (Participant 10, non-Hispanic white female). This mislabelling based on gender was an almost universal theme among female physicians, who persistently had to validate or describe their role as a physician and not a nurse. Female physicians highlighted significant areas of gender inequity, including compensation, access to resources (eg, administrative support, research funding), and professional expectations from leadership. They shared experiences of working in male-dominated medical specialties and settings where they lacked a support system, and to succeed, “you really have to push the boundaries to get anything that men get, so that creates an extra layer of stress” (Participant 2, Hispanic Asian female).

Parent or caregiver

Many physician participants shared how familial responsibilities, in addition to their clinical duties, impacted their ability to prioritise their well-being and career growth. The lack of time available for dependent care, combined with the pressure of meeting academic and clinical expectations, proved to be stress-inducing. Building on the prior subtheme, female physicians described perceptions of higher clinical workload, lack of resources and a disproportionately high burden of dependent care. A participant reflected, “It’s all about time … I just don’t have enough hours in the day to do the work, see and care for my children, keep my household going” (Participant 5, non-Hispanic white female). Another expressed struggling with feeling “guilty working so much that I spend so much time when I’m not working with [my family], but I never have time for myself” (Participant 3, non-Hispanic Asian female). Participants reflected on the expectations they felt, sharing, “the expectation is that you should compromise your family life to be able to develop your career” (Participant 2, Hispanic Asian female). Participants noted stigma associated with being a mother in medicine, sharing, “… being a mother and being a woman, I think there are times when there’s a stigma that says, ‘You know as soon as her kids get sick, she’s not going to show up” (Participant 23, non-Hispanic white female).

Theme 2: well-being in the workplace

Emotional and mental health

Participants identified a growing and burdensome amount of moral injury in their clinical practice. Moral injury, as described by the participants, reflected a feeling of emotional distress when one is unable to help a patient due primarily to inefficiencies or structural issues within the health system. One participant shared, “There’s patients asking for my help that I cannot help. The system does not give me the tools, nor does it support me in helping the patients in front of me” (Participant 1, non-Hispanic white female). Increasing clinical demands negatively affected their health, as physicians require more recovery time from high-acuity patients, increased clinical volume and stressful healthcare environments. One participant felt that the chronic stress and sleep deprivation continued to take an extreme toll on their personal health, stating, “I think this job is going to kill me” (Participant 26, non-Hispanic multiracial female). Another voiced concern related to the long-term effect on the workforce more broadly, saying “physician burnout is too high, suicide levels are too high, alcohol and substance abuse are too high. Something has to give because we (physicians) are really suffering, and if it doesn’t give, I think we’re all going to be in trouble” (Participant 23, non-Hispanic white female).

Staffing burden

Many participants felt that the increased patient volume and lack of additional supporting staff (eg, technicians, aides, social workers) did not allow them enough time or space to incorporate healthy habits that addressed their own well-being. As one stated, “the pressure to see more and more patients without appropriate clinical support has been increasing over time” (Participant 26, non-Hispanic multiracial female). Another shared, “the demands of the job … especially the constant turnover of staff definitely affects well-being” (Participant 9, non-Hispanic white female). Participants reported that a persistent lack of input or decision-making capacity regarding non-physician staffing directly affects the time they spend at work and their overall stress levels. Participants described physicians, especially URM and women physicians, having to take on additional administrative and non-clinical tasks to keep operations going within the context of losing ancillary staff. Participants felt that the system did not focus on retention, which in turn ‘degrades the feeling of being valued’. Even when patient care goals are met, participants often felt that they were being endlessly squeezed for productivity: ‘It’s never enough’.

Non-clinical responsibilities

Participants felt that administrative tasks reduced their ability to deliver high-quality patient care and contributed to feelings of extreme stress. Participants stated that increasing task loads added to their responsibilities substantively and prevented them from practising medicine. “I feel like I can’t keep up. I can’t be responsible, and I feel like I’m letting people down because I don’t have the time in my day to answer emails or patient messages. It is nonstop” (Participant 12, non-Hispanic white female). Participants felt that if these issues were addressed, their well-being would improve as they would be able to “focus on the work that’s really important and satisfying” (Participant 15, non-Hispanic Asian female) and “just focus on my job—seeing patients and doing research” (Participant 9, non-Hispanic white female).

Theme 3: barriers to accessing and receiving well-being resources

Participants also discussed barriers and facilitators to accessing and receiving well-being, mental health and culturally sensitive support resources from their employers and across the health system. Barriers included the workplace environment, broadly defined resources and institutional culture.

Workplace environment

Participants described aspects of the work environment that limited their ability to address their well-being and mitigate the growing workplace stressors. Participants pointed to a lack of personal time as an issue with accessing well-being resources at work, with one participant sharing that “having some place for a physician to go and decompress in the middle of the day is important” (Participant 30, non-Hispanic black female). This lack of ability to access well-being resources was coupled with and exacerbated by a lack of time during the workday to take action to feel well. One participant noted that the clinical environment, “sort of devalues you … [it can] be quite challenging to keep yourself feeling well in this environment” (Participant 23, non-Hispanic white female). Another participant noted that their work schedules do not allow them to attend institutional well-being resources, with one sharing, “I’m not interested in anything that I would have to schedule and get more childcare for—it seems anti-wellness” (Participant 1, non-Hispanic white female). This was a key factor for participants, as they noted that the health system often offered resources for mental health and well-being care. Still, the timing of these sessions did not align with their daily work schedules, requiring them to use additional time outside of work. This tension then builds on prior themes of a general lack of time outside of work, creating a cycle of stress, fighting for time and an inability to access the support provided by their health system.

Culture

Competitiveness in the workplace can be a hindrance to seeking out well-being resources or support. Participants reported feeling an extreme ‘pressure to succeed’ with taking issue in how ‘narrowly that stress is defined’, adding that it can ‘create a lot of pressure and stress’. URM and female participants noted that this was true across academic medicine, but they felt it was especially heightened for URM and female physicians. A participant shared a ‘feeling of guilt’ for setting boundaries to improve work-life balance, stating “not everybody is subscribing into the notion [of well-being]” (Participant 23, non-Hispanic white female).

Participants believed that the culture of medicine is in dire need of change. They supported a substantial need, driven by top-down leadership and also by changes from their peer physician colleagues. One participant shared,

Unfortunately, you know, the way it works is that the more patients you see, the more money that is made, and so I think there are times when leadership or enterprise or management would like you to really see as many patients as possible and that can be incredibly daunting, and it can cause people to be depressed (Participant 23, non-Hispanic white female)

Overgeneralised or broad scope

Participants felt that the current structure for mental health or well-being offerings was too generalised and stated a preference for tailored interventions by specialty, gender, career level and familial status. Participants felt that wellness was ‘like a thumbprint and unique to every person’. One participant shared,

It needs to be really super individualized. I don’t think there’s anything that you can do that’s going to help across the bar. And in some cases, it just makes it worse, because then it’s like why are you wasting resources on something that you think is not a good idea, and I never told you it (the resource) was a good idea. And then that just makes people feel worse, you know. So, I think anything would have to be really individualized (Participant 20, non-Hispanic white female).

Theme 4: facilitators to well-being resources

Participants also highlighted leadership and social support as major facilitators to accessing and receiving well-being, mental health and culturally sensitive support resources.

Physician camaraderie

Participants reported using various tools and resources to address and improve their personal well-being. Participants place high importance on collegial relationships with one another as a contributor to a positive culture that enables the receipt and sharing of support resources. This was elevated and amplified when peers were female or URM physicians. A participant shared that having support from colleagues meant that “people realize that they have allies and people with shared experiences. I think that could make the work environment far more pleasant, it goes back to that sense of belonging” (Participant 28, Hispanic multiracial female).

Many participants reported the impact of strong social support from colleagues on their personal well-being, both within and outside the workplace. Participants shared many ways in which colleagues support one another, from respecting time off to covering shifts, allowing physicians to “feel very secure knowing someone’s willing to do it and that it wouldn’t affect patient safety” (Participant 11, non-Hispanic white female). Through gathering and celebrating milestones as well as sharing tips and techniques for career and family life with one another, participants felt ‘a big source of support’.

Leadership

Leadership played a very important role in facilitating positive well-being for physicians. Specifically, “if [leaders] have empathy and respect the work you’re doing and treat you like a human being, you’re going to be happy in your workspace” (Participant 12, non-Hispanic white female). They appreciated when leadership went above and beyond to address and prioritise well-being in the workplace. One participant shared how their division created a wellness champion position, which provides “small tokens to know that someone’s thinking about us beyond our productivity” (Participant 5, non-Hispanic white female). Participants shared that “everyone’s door is always open, which is phenomenal” (Participant 6, non-Hispanic white female), and that informal mentorship and formal mentors were key support resources. They felt supported and improved when they were able to consult with mentors on clinical questions, as well as share their personal feelings and concerns.

Discussion

Female and URM physicians experience structural racism and sexism within their clinical practice and beyond it. The structures of medicine thus create disproportionate risk and challenges related to mental health, well-being and careers for female and URM physicians. Despite a renewed attention to burnout in healthcare, these issues and structures remain in place. As health systems understand the importance of elevating the diversity of a physician workforce to ensure high-quality patient care, insights are needed to contextualise and investigate the experiences of female and URM academic physicians.

This qualitative study specifically explored these issues and identified key contributors to stress and well-being in medicine, as well as barriers and facilitators to mitigating burnout and supporting URM and female physicians in academic medicine. Our findings highlight how various identities magnify stress exposure. URM women physicians described cumulative burdens, including heightened clinical expectations, reduced access to mentorship and stigma surrounding flexibility, which influenced decisions about productivity and career aspirations. These intersectional dynamics may help explain persistent disparities in promotion, compensation and leadership representation despite ongoing well-being initiatives.

There was a notable need and support for a top-down, leadership approach to motivate and lead a change in the culture of well-being, and to improve the alignment of stakeholders to prioritise well-being. The National Academy of Medicine and the Surgeon General have underscored the need for cultural shifts in medicine, as highlighted by multiple initiatives, including a ‘Workforce Well-Being Day’.26 A culture shift away from competition and productivity—which are heightened threats for female and URM physicians—and toward collaboration was a key component, as many felt the shift to clinical metrics distracted from delivering high-quality patient care. This is a unique perspective voiced from the frontline, specifically to health system leaders, who often attempt to balance morale and productivity.27 Leaders must recognise that the historic culture of academic medicine has created an environment where URM and female physicians begin behind their male or white counterparts. A focus on productivity and competition, as highlighted by these participants, only further exacerbates this problem, contributing to burnout and isolation. Many participants emphasised that a ‘one size fits all’ system approach to well-being will not work, but rather research is needed to develop individualised and dynamic systems to support physicians’ needs, which vary over time.

Leaders play a crucial role in the eyes of female and URM physicians, and these leaders can serve as exemplars and change agents by prioritising well-being for their employees. This can be achieved by creating time for their team and removing tasks from their daily workload. Leaders can advocate for autonomy, improvements and investments in the built environment, thereby building community and facilitating peer interaction. Overall, physicians noted that leaders who assisted in fostering collaborative and supportive environments spoke to them as individuals. The feasibility and effectiveness of future leadership-driven interventions, such as structural actions including resource allocation, workload redistribution and transparent communication, can be evaluated through mixed-methods approaches, including pre-implementation and post implementation assessments, longitudinal surveys of well-being and burnout, and qualitative follow-up interviews. Pilot programmes, such as peer-to-peer coaching, flexible scheduling pathways and guided mentorship for URM or female physicians, within divisions, may allow iterative refinement before system-wide scale-up.

These findings reveal that physicians from an urban academic centre believe health systems should invest in understanding the specific well-being needs of URM and female physicians across physician groups, especially for these groups that often feel ‘othered’ or ‘isolated’ within healthcare. Efforts can be made at an organisational level to promote well-being as a culture, as well as to foster social connection and peer interaction as social support mechanisms for all physicians. Well-being offerings and interventions should be targeted to address the stated well-being concerns in place of a ‘one size fits all approach’. The disproportionate representation of women in the sample shapes how racial and ethnic identity themes emerge in this analysis. While experiences of racism and marginalisation were salient, they were often intertwined with gendered expectations and caregiving roles. It is challenging to generalise these findings to URM physicians in settings with varying gender compositions or institutional cultures.

Strengths and limitations

This qualitative study has limitations. Participants were physicians from an urban, academic health system, and their viewpoints may not be representative of the general physician population. Participants were recruited through targeted faculty listservs using a convenience sampling strategy, which introduced selection bias toward those who were engaged with or affected by well-being concerns. The sample was predominantly female (90%), which likely amplified gender-related themes while limiting the depth of insight into the experiences of URM male physicians. As a result, racial and ethnic identity themes in this study should be mainly interpreted through the lens of female physicians’ experiences. This study focused on understanding URM and female physicians with experience practising during the pandemic or in the initial phases following it. Prior research has shown that the presence of stigma for minority populations can affect reporting of mental health conditions and may have influenced responses. Furthermore, although rooted in published literature and constructs, the interview guide may have introduced confirmation bias or other biases in the themes.

Conclusions

This qualitative study adds to the literature by focusing on female and URM physicians to identify key themes related to their well-being and careers. The themes here shed light on the complex interplay of identity, workplace dynamics and access to resources. These physicians shared experiences of bias and inequities throughout the academic and clinical environments, which continue to grow competitive and hyper-focused on productivity. They advocated for tailored interventions and resources that recognise individual needs rather than a one-size-fits-all approach. Physician camaraderie and supportive leadership were highlighted as crucial facilitators for well-being. Healthcare systems must prioritise efforts to create a culture of support, inclusivity and collaboration, which is essential for sustaining a diverse workforce.
