Why we need medical entomologists more than ever
Authors: Stefanie Meredith
Published: 2026

Towards the end of the nineteenth century and into the twentieth century, the importance of medical entomology for identifying vector-borne diseases (VBDs) and understanding disease transmission was clearly recognised.1 The identification of many of the vectors of diseases of the tropics—filariasis, malaria, yellow fever, trypanosomiasis—and the role of simuliidae in the transmission of onchocerciasis, led to a better understanding of disease transmission and, consequently, means of prevention. More recently, other outbreaks and the changing epidemiology of diseases such as Lymes disease, Zika and chikungunya have highlighted attention on the increasing expansion of vector-borne infectious diseases, not only in tropical countries, but globally.2

Entomological data play a critical role in understanding the transmission dynamics of VBDs and are essential for the design, implementation and evaluation of disease-control programmes. Knowledge of the vector species’ composition, abundance, distribution and seasonal variation is important for understanding disease transmission. Parameters such as vector density, biting rates, longevity and host preference determine the intensity of transmission. Measures like the entomological inoculation rate and vectorial capacity directly link vector biology to human infection risk.3 Thus, in malaria-control programmes, entomological data inform the selection and timing of vector-control interventions such as insecticide-treated nets, indoor residual spraying, larval source management and environmental modification.

Vector behaviour is constantly changing, as, for example, changes in the environment and insecticide use result in modification of resting behaviour (endophilic vs exophilic) and feeding times. Vector species are also changing; for example, Anopheles stephensi, a highly competent urban malaria vector species endemic in South Asia and the Persian Gulf, has now colonised eight countries in sub-Saharan Africa since 2013 and is now a major vector of urban malaria in Africa.4 Aedes aegypti and Aedes albopictus are spreading rapidly throughout Eurasia, Europe and the Americas, increasing the risk of chikungunya, dengue and Zika outbreaks in previously non-endemic regions.5 West Nile virus has also spread across North America and Europe through transmission by Culex species.6

The epidemiology of other vector species is also changing; for example, the epidemiology of leishmaniasis has changed significantly in recent years with climate change, migration, conflict and increased human and animal mobility, and these are therefore intensifying the spread of the different forms of the disease. Consequently, it is clear that entomological surveillance should be a part of routine disease surveillance data collection to monitor interventions for insecticide resistance, changes in vector-biting behaviour and vector species, etc. Experienced medical entomologists are needed now more than ever, and moreover, in our rapidly changing world, it is also important for entomologists to incorporate an integrated vector control and One Health approach.7

Sadly, there is a widespread shortage of trained medical and public health entomologists, particularly in low- and middle-income countries, where VBDs are most prevalent. This lack of entomological capacity is a persistent challenge for the control and elimination of many VBDs. Professionals with a master’s degree or graduate certificate in medical entomology are increasingly rare in the public health field. This gap limits effective vector surveillance, outbreak response and integrated vector-management efforts. There are, however, organisations such as the WHO/the Special Programme for Research and Training in Tropical Diseases (TDR) and the Global Vector Hub that are addressing this gap and have created resources like the Global Atlas of Medical Entomology Schooling (GAMES) to improve visibility and access to available courses.8,9

This supplement is highlighting an innovative initiative by the END Fund to support training a new cadre of African entomologists through a 3-y mentorship programme and is addressing the critical need for Simulium-trained field entomologists to move the onchocerciasis-elimination programmes forward towards success. As described by Heggen et al.,10 the hands-on mentorship programme is designed to equip early- to mid-career entomologists in all aspects of field work, surveillance, data analysis and documentation. A critical component of the training given was for each student to take an area of operational onchocerciasis-related entomology and develop a publishable document on their chosen area: essentially a review and an interpretation/comment of an important area of onchocerciasis entomology. This supplement is the outcome of their work, and the articles cover a range of the importance of entomological data in the onchocerciasis-elimination programmes.

As described above, entomological data are critical for the design, monitoring and surveillance of VBDs; over the 28 y of its operations, the Onchocerciases Control Programme, which was initially based on insecticide interventions and studies on the Simulium damnosum vectors, accumulated an extraordinary amount of data and operational experience on both the vectors, transmission and epidemiology. Likewise, The African Programme to for Onchocerciasis Control, although based on mass drug administration, collected an important set of data from 1995 until its closure in 2015. The information is important historically and a key resource for the current onchocerciasis-elimination programmes. As de Souza et al.11 describe in their paper, these unique data are finally available and accessible from the WHO Institutional Repository for Information Sharing (WHO IRIS).

The other articles in the supplement cover some of the broad issues for elimination programmes such as the impact of seasonal changes (Ayisi et al.12) and specific examples from countries: microplanning for entomological surveys in Senegal (Sy et al.13); using community knowledge for surveys and mapping (Atekem et al.14 and Boakye et al.15); and interrupting transmission in Chad (Mahamat Oumar et al.16) and Niger, the first country in Africa to achieve elimination of transmission (Adamou et al.).

Over the next phases of this initiative, we look forward to the cohort of entomologists contributing to the elimination of onchocerciasis transmission and motivating and training others in operational entomology.
