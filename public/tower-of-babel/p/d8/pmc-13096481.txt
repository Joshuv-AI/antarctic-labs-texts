Post-market surveillance of antiretroviral drug quality in Tanzania in the context of rising HIV drug resistance
Authors: Eulambius M Mlugu, Jacob B Mhagama, Raphael Zozimus Sangeda, Peter P Kunambi, Thomas Samwel, Nellin Shiletiwa, Goodluck Gotora, Sunzy M Chambiri, Damas Matiko, Danstan Hipolite, Yonah H Mwalwisi, Adam Mitangu Fimbo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractHuman Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) remains a major global health burden. Antiretroviral (ARV) drugs play a key role in the control of HIV. However, their effectiveness could be affected by poor-quality drugs, risking treatment failure and HIV drug resistance (HIVDR). Reports on the quality of ARV drugs from sub-Saharan Africa remain low despite the growing threat of HIVDR. This study presents Tanzanian post-market surveillance (PMS) data on ARV quality, offering evidence to strengthen monitoring systems and inform HIV/AIDS control strategies. This was a descriptive cross-sectional study conducted across 15 regions of mainland Tanzania. ARVs were randomly sampled from the port of entry and distribution outlets. All samples underwent Tier I screening, which included visual inspection, disintegration tests, and Thin Layer Chromatography using a Global Pharma Health Fund Minilab. Samples from the distribution outlet were also subjected to product information review (PIR). All samples that failed Tier I screening, irrespective of their source, were subjected to Tier II confirmatory testing using standard methods. In addition, 10% of samples from distribution outlets that passed Tier I screening were randomly selected for Tier II confirmatory testing in accordance with the national PMS guideline. Samples were considered to pass if the test results complied with the standard pharmacopeia acceptance criteria. A total of 1432 ARV samples were collected from July 2019 to December 2023. Of these, 81.8% (1171/1432) were collected from the port of entry. The first line combination (dolutegravir + lamivudine + tenofovir disoproxil fumarate) comprised about two-thirds (68%) of the screened samples. Nearly half (49.4%) of ARVs from the distribution outlets did not comply with the PIR requirements of the approved product information. Failure to comply with the PIR requirement risks the public health, safety and effective use of ARVs. All samples passed Tier I and Tier II tests. This study demonstrates that ARVs circulating in Tanzania meet the required quality standards; however, the observed labeling deviations necessitate urgent corrective action. In light of rising HIVDR, continuous quality surveillance remains essential.Keywords: Antiretroviral drugs, Quality, HIV drug resistance, Post-market surveillanceSubject terms: Diseases, Drug discovery, Health care, Medical research, Microbiology

Introduction

Human Immunodeficiency Virus (HIV) infection and Acquired Immunodeficiency Syndrome (AIDS) remain a public health problem, causing morbidities and mortalities globally. About 40.8 million people were living with HIV by the end of 20241,2. Two-thirds of the estimated people living with HIV are from the African region, with more than half living in Sub-Saharan Africa2. In Tanzania, about 1.7 million people are living with HIV/AIDS, with a prevalence of about 4.7% as of 20223. Antiretroviral therapy (ART) plays a pivotal role in the global battle against HIV/AIDS, both for prevention and treatment.

To drive the fight against HIV/AIDS, the Joint United Nations Program instituted the 95-95-95 AIDS program targeting to achieve 95% of all people living with HIV know their status, 95% of those diagnosed receive ART and 95% of those receiving ART have viral loads suppressed by the end of 20304. Commendable interventions have been implemented to achieve these goals and substantial progress has been made. In 2024, 87% of all people living with HIV globally knew their status, 77% were receiving antiretroviral therapy (ART), and 73% had their viral loads suppressed2. In Tanzania, particularly, 88% were aware of their status, 83% had HIV viral load suppressed and the coverage of ART has increased to 86%5.

The effectiveness of the ART depends largely on the widespread availability and accessibility of quality antiretrovirals (ARVs) dispensed throughout the healthcare system. As access to ARVs continues to improve, routine surveillance and monitoring of ARVs’ quality is of paramount importance so as to inform the public and policy decisions. Addressing the complexities of ARV quality requires a multifaceted approach, including regulatory bodies, healthcare providers, pharmaceutical companies, and international partners6.

The dynamic nature of the pharmaceutical market, especially in low-middle-income countries (LMICs), poses numerous challenges to maintaining the quality of drugs, including ARVs. Some of the challenges include regulatory hurdles and issues in the supply chain, especially the potential infiltration of substandard or falsified medicines into the market due to the porous nature of supply chains7. Several studies from different countries have reported varying prevalence of poor-quality ARVs. One large study that evaluated the quality of ARVs dispensed from developing countries and internet pharmacies reported a few samples that failed dissolution tests8. A recent systematic review reported poor quality ARVs in developing countries, with 1.4% of the samples failing at least one quality test9. A previous study from Tanzania reported some poor-quality ARV drugs in the Tanzanian market10. Similarly, a study done in Kinshasa, Democratic Republic of the Congo, reported 10% of the ARVs tested failed the assay test11. On the other hand, a study from Cameroon reported acceptable quality of ARVs in the market12. No matter how low they might be, poor-quality ARVs will compromise the achievement of HIV/AIDS targets.

Substandard or falsified ARVs not only lead to compromised health outcomes but also pose threats to the growing burden of HIV drug resistance (HIVDR). As patients are exposed to sub-optimal treatment regimens, the virus can mutate and develop HIVDR, posing a significant threat to the treatment success and further transmission of resistant HIV. The increasing burden of HIVDR in eastern African countries, including Tanzania, is reported13,14 and may be attributed to poor quality ARVs among other factors. However, there is limited data regarding the quality of ARVs, especially from developing countries.

The Tanzania Medicines and Medical Devices Authority (TMDA) is legally mandated to safeguard medicines’ safety, efficacy, and quality, including ARVs. Through regular post-market surveillance (PMS), the TMDA monitors the quality of ARVs circulating in the Tanzanian market. A previous similar study using the same approach was done more than ten years ago and reported a few ARVs failing quality tests15, highlighting the need for continuous monitoring of ARV quality in Tanzania. Tanzania changed the first-line ART in 2019 to a combination of Lamivudine/tenofovir/dolutegravir (TLD). Since the change of the first-line ART in 2019, the quality of ARVs circulating in the market has not been reported. This study investigated the quality of ARVs circulating in the Tanzanian market from 2019 to 2023 through annual PMS. The findings of this study add to the body of literature indicating the quality of ARVs in Tanzania and serve as evidence of continued PMS in the African region for effective control of HIV and the elimination of AIDS by 2030.

Methods

Study design and settings

This PMS employed a descriptive cross-sectional design. The study was conducted according to the national PMS schedule16, whereby samples from the port on entry were collected each year (2019 to 2023), but the samples from distribution outlets were collected after each year (skipping one year). Thus, in the five years, samples from the distribution outlets were only collected in two years (2020/2021 and 2023/2024). The study was conducted in 15 out of 26 regions of Tanzania’s mainland, namely Arusha, Dar es Salaam, Dodoma, Coast, Iringa, Kagera, Katavi, Kilimanjaro, Mara, Mbeya, Morogoro, Mwanza, Mtwara, Njombe and Tanga. The regions were selected based on the pre-defined criteria, including highly populated regions, regions bordering other countries, regions with high prevalence of HIV infection and those reported to have medicine quality problems. The criteria used for selected regions reflect the representativeness of the national administrative zones in mainland Tanzania and followed the national PMS guideline for sampling representative regions16. Samples were collected for five years from 2019 to 2023 at the entry points and medicines distribution outlets such as Medical Store Departments (MSD), public and private hospitals, dispensaries and health centers as previously reported17. A purposive sampling technique was used to select medicine collection sites from national, regional and district levels down to the lower distribution channel.

ARVs selection and sampling

ARVs selected in this surveillance were those frequently prescribed for the management of HIV infection, and all that were available in the selected outlets and port of entry as per National Guidelines for Management of HIV/AIDS18. These included the products that contain either a single dose or as a fixed-dose combination (FDC).

ARVs sample collection

A purposeful sampling method was used in the collection of the selected ARV samples. The collection of samples was done in two distribution levels. Level I covered the port of entry, Muhimbili National Hospital (MNH) and the Medical Stores Department (MSD) as previously described17. Level II covered public and private health facilities, including hospitals, health centers and dispensaries. Trained drug inspectors collected samples according to the prepared sampling plan and TMDA standard operating procedures as previously described17. According to the sampling plan, one brand of surveyed medicine type at each collection site was collected. All samples were collected in their original containers and information details were filled in the sample collection tool as reported in the previously publication17. Each collected sample was coded according to the prescribed coding format. Coded samples with their respective sample collection tool were sealed in the labelled sampling envelope. Storage and handling of samples during collection, transportation and before analysis complied with the manufacturer’s instructions and the TMDA sample chain of custody procedures.

Quality evaluation

Product information review (PIR)

Before further laboratory analysis, samples collected from medicine distribution outlets were subjected to PIR as previously reported17. Each sample was evaluated visually on the physical appearance of the primary and secondary packaging labeling, and package information leaflets (PIL). During PIR, the following parameters were reviewed; product’s brand and generic name, the appearance of the dosage form packaging such as color uniformity, dosage form and strength of the medicine, name and physical address of the manufacturer, batch or lot number, date of manufacturing and expiration date, TMDA registration number, packaging and pack size, Patient prescribing Information Leaflet, language and storage condition as previously described17. The sample was considered a failed PIR test if the observed item differed from the information of the approved product information retrieved from the TMDA Regulatory Information Management System.

Tier I laboratory screening

All samples were subjected to screening tests using the Global Pharma Health Fund® (GPHF) Minilab kits manual at the TMDA WHO prequalified laboratory. The performed tests were visual inspection, disintegration for the solid dosage forms, and an identification test using the Thin Layer Chromatography (TLC) method as previously reported17,19.

Visual inspection

All sampled solid dosage forms were examined for shape, size, brittleness, breaking, altered surfaces, odor, embossing and debossing, physical damage, altered surface and color uniformity. A product was considered failed visual tests if observed to have any defects listed above if the color and odor differed from those of the approved product as previously reported17,19.

Simple disintegration test

A simple disintegration test was performed to assess the possibility of instant release of oral solid dosage forms. This was done using a 100mL wide-neck glass bottle filled with water heated to 37 °C as previously reported17,19. The tablets were shaken or stirred occasionally. Disintegration was required to occur within 30 min. The sample failed if it did not disintegrate within 30 min, consecutively, in three tests. Disintegration time was recorded and compared against reference monographs/product specifications.

Product identification by TLC

The TLC method was used for product identification and qualitative determination of active ingredients, related substances, and impurities in the dosage forms. This method compared spot test samples and reference solutions according to the GPHF Minilab as previously reported17,19. The principal spot obtained with the test sample was required to correspond with the chromatographic runs of the standard solution in terms of color, shape, size, intensity and retardation factor (Rf) value. The test sample was considered failed if the Rf value of the test sample was different by more than 10% from that of the standard sample or the intensity of the spot was less than that of a reference containing 80% of the stated amount of the API as reported previously17,19. The sample was considered failed if observed in all three independent experiments.

Tier II laboratory confirmatory testing

All samples that failed or had doubtful results of Tier I, regardless of the collection source, and 10% of samples from medicines distribution outlets that passed the screening test were subjected to confirmatory testing Tier II. The selection of 10% aligns with a risk based post market surveillance framework, which is a statistically meaningful quality check for public protection as guided by TMDA16. Confirmatory tests were done at the TMDA-WHO prequalified laboratory as per USP pharmacopeia monograph requirements9 or in-house product specifications. Typical parameters tested were identification, dissolution, assay, related substances and weight uniformity.

Data management and analysis

The collected data were checked for inconsistencies. They were double-entered into a Microsoft Excel sheet, verified, and exported to SPSS (version 26) software for analysis. Results for PIR, screening, and conformity are expressed in percentages.

Results

Samples collected

A total of 1432 ARV drug samples were collected from July 2019 to December 2023. Of these, 81.8% (1171/1432) were collected from the port of entry (POE), and only 261 samples were collected from medicine distribution outlets.

All the samples from the POE were collected in the Dar es Salaam region. Samples from the distribution outlets were collected from 15 regions in mainland Tanzania in two years, 2020/2021 and 2023/2024. Quality evaluation

Product information review

All collected samples from medicine distribution outlets were subjected to a PIR test. Nearly half (49.4%, 129/261) of ARVs sampled did not comply with the PIR requirements as per approved product information. Out of these samples, 91.5% (118/129) were collected in 2023.

The majority of the samples that failed PIR had incomplete leaflets (39.5%) and artwork deviations (39.5%). Other deficiencies observed were inappropriate language used in labeling and package inserts and shelf-life deviation. On the other hand, all sampled medicines were found to be registered in Tanzania, but some of the labels did not indicate the Tanzania registration number (Fig. 4).

Percent of deficiencies observed among surveyed ARVs.

Laboratory tier I screening test

All ARVs sampled from POEs and medicine distribution outlets conformed to tier I screening test requirements. All the samples had uniform shape, color, size, and brittleness, whereas no breaking, altered surfaces, odor, embossing and debossing, physical damage and altered surface were observed. All solid dosage forms disintegrated within 30 min with a median disintegration time of 8 (1–15) min

For the TLC identification test, the principal spot obtained for test samples was comparable to the chromatographic runs of the standard solution in terms of color, shape, size, intensity and retardation factor (Rf) value. The Rf values of the test samples were within 10% of those of the standard sample, complying with the regulatory requirement.

Discussion

The global market of medical products is characterized by varying standards for medical products, leaving patients in low- and middle-income countries vulnerable to the risk of receiving substandard medicines7,19. The rigorous assessment and monitoring of the quality of medical products are essential for safeguarding public health and ensuring that medical products circulating in the country meet the required quality standards. The present study reports the findings of routine post-market surveillance of ARV drugs. Samples were collected from various levels of the supply chain, including national, regional, and district distribution outlets, as well as ports of entry.

In this study, the majority of samples (80%) were collected at the port of entry. This could be explained by the design of this PMS, as samples were collected annually but only in two years from distribution outlets. This is unlikely to affect the results of this study because ARVs are only procured by the government and distributed across the country; thus, the 20% from distribution outlets reflects the real-world situation. The majority of samples from distribution outlets were collected from Dar es Salaam, Mbeya, and Kilimanjaro, indicating high availability of ARVs in these regions, possibly due to the high corresponding prevalence of HIV5. The majority of samples were mainly sourced from hospitals, possibly because hospitals stock both first- and second-line ARVs, unlike lower-level distribution outlets, which stock only first-line ARVs. Additionally, the lower number of samples from MSD could be explained by the fact that MSD offices were found in only seven of the 15 regions included in this study.

On the other hand, all the sampled ARV medicines were imported, which shows the country’s dependence on imported ARVs from abroad. This is due to the lack of a domestic pharmaceutical industry that manufactures ARVs in Tanzania; thus, all ARVs are currently procured by the government from abroad and distributed to the public and private health supply chain. A lack of domestic manufacturing capacity for ARVs is likely to make the country vulnerable in case of public health emergencies, such as that observed during the first phase of the COVID-19 pandemic20. There is a need to attract investment in ARV domestic pharmaceutical manufacturing.

In this surveillance, about two-thirds of all the collected ARV drug samples were fixed-dose combination (FDC) of tenofovir/lamivudine/dolutegravir (TLD). This can be explained by the fact that TLD is the recommended first-line treatment of HIV infection in Tanzania since 20198, implying that the majority of people living with HIV (PLHIV) are on a first-line regimen. The high prevalence of TLD samples reflects Tanzania’s adherence to global and national HIV treatment. The WHO advocates that FDCs are desirable as they simplify treatment by improving adherence and clinical outcomes and hence improve quality of life for PLHIV. By combining three ingredients into a single pill, FDCs reduce pill burden, minimize the risk of missed doses and enhance long-term adherence.

About half of the samples collected from the distribution outlets failed PIR according to TMDA guidelines21. The majority of the samples (90%) that failed the PIR test were collected in 2023/2024. Such a finding cannot be explained only by the difference in the number of samples collected in 2020/2021 (40%) versus those collected in 2023/2024 (60%), but implies a growing trend of PIR non-compliance by the manufacturers. The majority of observed discrepancies were inappropriate language on the label and leaflet, as well as artwork deviations from approved product labels. The primary discrepancies were incorrect language on labels and leaflets, as well as deviations in packaging artwork, highlighting gaps in adherence to established pharmaceutical regulations. This is similar to the previous studies done in Tanzania16,22. This raises concerns about regulatory compliance and patient safety. Accurate product information is critical for ensuring that medicines are used safely and effectively. Labels and patient leaflets provide essential details, including dosage instructions, warnings, contraindications, and storage conditions. Such information also assists healthcare professionals in prescribing and dispensing medicines and informs patients and consumers about their medicines, safe use and storage of the respective medicines. When these elements are incorrect or unclear, patients and healthcare providers may struggle to use the medication properly, increasing the risk of misuse or adverse events. The dangers associated with the use of improperly labelled medicines may result in medication errors, irrational prescribing and dispensing of the ARVs, as well as use by the patients, which often have serious consequences to the health of the patients.

Labelling failures are not only regulatory breaches but also compromise public health and rational use of medicine. Even if the content of medicines complies with regulatory requirements, labelling failure has serious public health consequences. Illegible labels or wrong instructions may result in underdosing, overdosing, or wrong drug administration. Underdosing may lead to sub-optimal plasma concentration, a cause for therapeutic failure and risk for HIVDR. Overdosing can lead to toxicities or adverse drug reactions, which may cause morbidities, raising treatment costs and sometimes death. Labelling failure can also lead to poor treatment outcomes, morbidities, and poor medicine adherence, resulting in resistance development and further spread of HIVD-resistant strains, which is a current growing public health concern.

All sampled ARVs from points of entry (POEs) and medicine distribution outlets conformed to both screening and confirmatory test requirements. These results are consistent with recent findings from Cameroon12, suggesting a regional trend of improved ARV quality. However, the findings differ from earlier studies conducted in Tanzania10,15,23 and the Democratic Republic of Congo (DRC)11, where substandard ARVs were detected. This difference may reflect progress made over the years through the implementation of post-market surveillance (PMS) programs and strengthened regulatory oversight in Tanzania. Despite that, the findings of this study did not find drugs with poor quality attributes; the reported HIVDR in Tanzania13,14 is of great concern and suggests the need for continuous strengthening of ARV quality monitoring and HIV services, including adherence to ART.

The improvement observed in this study suggests increased adherence to regulatory requirements by market authorization holders and better compliance with storage and distribution standards by health facilities and medicine outlets. It may also be attributed to the procurement practices in Tanzania, where ARVs are primarily procured through government agencies with donor support. Donor-funded procurement often imposes stringent conditions, including requirements that all suppliers be WHO pre-qualified. These procurement safeguards reduce the risk of substandard or falsified products infiltrating the supply chain, thereby ensuring consistent medicine quality.

Moreover, the findings highlight the value of sustained PMS activities in detecting and deterring the circulation of poor-quality medicines. Continued monitoring not only assures the public of the quality of ARVs but also strengthens trust in health systems and supports the achievement of national and global HIV/AIDS targets. Nonetheless, it is important to maintain vigilance, as challenges such as porous borders, supply chain vulnerabilities, and limited domestic manufacturing capacity may still pose risks of infiltration by substandard or falsified medicines.

This study included more than one thousand samples collected from representatives of the supply chain distribution channels in Tanzania. The findings of this study can be generalized to other regions of the country, as this study included samples from 15 regions, which were rationally selected.

Conclusion

This study indicates that ARVs circulating in Tanzania meet the required quality standards. PMS findings need to be integrated into the national HIV program quality assurance to ensure sustainable quality of service across the continuum of HIV care. The observed deviations in labeling orientations have serious public health consequences, and strengthening regulatory enforcement on labeling is recommended. The findings call for continuous monitoring of the quality of drugs, including ARVs.
