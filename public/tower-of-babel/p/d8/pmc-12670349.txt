Generative Artificial Intelligence in Violence Risk Assessment: Emerging Technology and the Ethics of the Inevitable
Authors: Neil R. Hogan, Gabriela Corăbian
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTRecent developments in artificial intelligence (AI) have stimulated considerable excitement and discussion regarding the potential impacts on people's lives and work. In particular, proposed and realized applications of generative AI have appeared across multiple industries and domains, including at the intersection of behavioral science and the law. This manuscript presents an ethical analysis of applications of generative AI to violence risk assessment, guided by the ethical principles of autonomy, beneficence and non‐maleficence, and justice. The authors argue that generative AI, although capable of producing novel content, is nonetheless vulnerable to ethical problems, including through its exposure to biased training data. Issues such as limited transparency in decision making and the potential for the perpetuation and exacerbation of racial disparities are discussed. The authors recommend that professionals approach generative AI with due caution, as they would with any novel or emerging risk assessment approach, and suggest continued evaluation and research.

Introduction

As illustrated by the very existence of this special issue, recent advancements in artificial intelligence (AI) technology have created something of an international cultural phenomenon. Lyu (2024) recently served as the editor of a book summarizing applications of generative AI, a form of AI technology which has recently captivated the attention of the public and professionals alike. The chapters in Lyu's book were authored by an extraordinarily large and diverse sample of experts from various fields. Even brief examination of the chapter titles immediately provides the reader with a sense of the scope of the potential impacts that this rapidly advancing technology could have on society, with entries focused on highly specialized medical applications (e.g., enhancing specific radiological images, drug discovery, and studying seizures), activities in the construction industry, education, image generation, marketing campaigns, fire safety, and more. Recently, Tortora (2024) provided a similar service for professionals with interests in criminal justice and forensic mental health, by authoring a wide‐ranging review of potential generative AI applications in forensic psychiatry, as well as corresponding ethical and practical considerations. This commendable work covered much ground, both in terms of defining technical terms, and drawing professional attention to several noteworthy issues. The purpose of the current article is to expand upon one of the domains briefly flagged by Tortora, by analyzing ethical considerations associated with the application of generative AI to violence risk assessment.

Terms of Reference

Per Kalota (2024) there is no single universally accepted definition of AI. To maintain consistency with prior evaluations of the ethics of AI in forensic/correctional mental health systems (Cockerill 2020; Hogan et al. 2021; Spivak and Shepherd 2021), the current authors will rely upon the definition offered by Cockerill (2020), which indicates that AI comprises “computer algorithm[s] capable of performing some function previously thought to be exclusive to human intelligence” (p. 1). The following analysis will focus in on two subcategories of AI, discriminative AI and generative AI, with the latter category serving as the primary subject. Throughout this paper, the term discriminative AI will be used to refer to algorithms designed for classification or discrimination tasks. In the context of violence risk assessment, predictive discriminative AI models seek to make binary distinctions between two classes, namely future recidivists and non‐recidivists. Discriminative AI applications can distinguish themselves from manual statistical approaches, by virtue of the sheer volume of data they can synthesize, and in some cases, their purported ability to learn from their own mistakes and refine their own predictions without intervention (Kalota 2024). Generative AI on the other hand, comprises models that can go beyond simple classification or prediction tasks, by producing new and often complex content. Applications of generative AI vary, with some models capable of translating information across formats (Kalota 2024), such as converting written prompts into images or vice versa. For the purposes of this article, it is most important to note that generative AI can be used to produce verbal and written narratives in response to prompts, which could include the formulation of a risk estimate or even an entire report, as will be discussed further below.

With respect to risk assessment terminology, an understanding of Bonta and Andrews' (2016) distinctions between generations of tools used to predict criminal reoffending will help readers interpret some of the arguments in this paper. The first generation comprises unstructured or discretionary approaches, relying exclusively upon the personal judgment of individual evaluators. The second comprises atheoretical and statistically derived actuarial tools, which use algorithms to compute scores and link them to data from normative samples. These tools typically rely heavily upon historical variables, such as prior conviction histories. Second generation tools can produce both relative risk indicators, like percentile ranks, or absolute risk indicators, like recidivism probability estimates. The third generation comprises structured approaches with both an empirical and a theoretical underpinning. In other words, they consider risk factors that the developers have reason to believe are conceptually linked, not simply statistically associated, to offending behavior. For instance, they often consider the expression of procriminal attitudes or substance abuse problems that appear to be contributing to disinhibition. The fourth generation comprises approaches that incorporate the characteristics of the third, but which are also capable of directly evaluating changes in risk (e.g., assigning a rating to observed reductions in harmful behaviors achieved through a formal treatment program).

Prior Discussions of Ethics and (Discriminative) AI in the Risk Assessment Literature

Although the current paper will focus on analyzing whether and to what extent generative AI could have positive or negative ethical consequences for the field of risk assessment, the authors will endeavor to do so in part by comparing and contrasting this technology with alternatives. To this end, the paper will review a related series of articles which explored potential opportunities and ethical issues raised by discriminative AI, each of which can provide context for the following discussion.

The first article, authored by Cockerill (2020), introduced areas in which the power of discriminative AI could be used to improve and expand upon existing risk and threat assessment practices, both within forensic mental health and law enforcement contexts. Cockerill noted some risks associated with the expansion of AI in these domains, such as privacy and legal concerns associated with the hypothetical collection of voluminous personal data required to maximize the computing power of AI in predictive policing. Cockerill's conclusory recommendations included the suggestion that AI technology should be included in forensic professionals' educational curricula, given that AI appeared poised to become increasingly integrated directly and indirectly into forensic psychiatric practice.

Subsequently, Hogan et al. (2021) expanded upon Cockerill's analysis, arguing that discriminative AI for the purposes of risk assessment shared many ethical pitfalls with existing methods, and particularly with second generation risk tools. Emphasis was placed on the potential for racial disparities to arise and compound using simple prediction paradigms, in light of empirical data suggesting that recidivism variables—the ostensible ground truth of most risk assessment paradigms—can be biased. They also contrasted discriminative AI with third and fourth generation tools, which have been found not only to be capable of demonstrating comparable (and sometimes superior) predictive validity to pure prediction models, but which can also directly guide interventions. Relatedly, they referenced the classic work of Steadman (2000) to make the point that (at least some) risk assessment practices have long since progressed from binary prediction paradigms to more probabilistic models, which seek to rank order individuals and match them to interventions. Ultimately, Hogan and colleagues cautioned risk assessment professionals against becoming overzealous about the potential benefits of discriminative AI, and argued that all else being equal, theoretically meaningful, explainable, and testable risk assessment practices were preferable from an ethical perspective.

Spivak and Shepherd (2021) provided a relatively brief commentary in reply to Hogan et al. (2021). In large part, these authors opined that many of the concerns raised in the prior article did not apply uniquely, or to a substantively greater degree, in relation to AI than they applied to extant practices, and particularly second generation tools. Although such arguments were presented in a manner that could be perceived by some readers as a rebuttal to the prior article in some respects, it is noteworthy that the two teams' arguments shared much in common, given that one of Hogan et al. (2021) central arguments was that discriminative AI was largely analogous to existing second generation risk tools. That said, Spivak and Shepherd (2021) appeared to disagree more directly with their counterparts on the relative ethical merits of emphasizing psychologically meaningful risk factors, stating that they were “unclear as to why racial biases that result from consideration of this sort of variable should be treated differently to any other” (p. 3). The authors concluded their commentary by encouraging readers to take a balanced view when considering new technology in risk assessment, while noting that unbridled enthusiasm could lead to unrealistic expectations.

Most recently, Ratajczak and Cockerill (2023) authored an article intended both to follow up on Cockerill's (2020) contribution, and to add to the discussion of the potential for AI to contribute to or mitigate bias in risk assessment. They cited examples of AI being used to great advantage in diverse areas of medicine (e.g., radiology, surgery), and also suggested that it has been used to predict recidivism in a manner that has arguably exceeded the predictive validity of alternatives (a suggestion that will be disputed, to some degree, later in this paper). Having touched on some of the means whereby risk prediction models can create adverse impacts among disadvantaged groups, they also cited evidence that work is actively ongoing to study and address such impacts. They also argued that given the premise that AI carries the potential to improve the accuracy of violence risk assessments to such a degree that the absolute occurrence of violence could drop significantly (for all groups), withholding such technology due to differential benefits across groups would be the less ethical choice. Overall, Ratajczak and Cockerill (2023) presented an optimistic view of the future of AI in forensic psychiatry, referencing “the massive potential improvements in accuracy and efficiency that AI promises” (p. 3) in support of the conclusion that “its implementation…is inevitable” (p. 3).

The Current Ethics Analysis

Each of the articles described in the prior section were focused on discriminative AI models, and their utilization in violence or recidivism prediction. Given the relatively novel and recent expansion of generative AI technology into mainstream consciousness, the authors believe that an updated ethical review is in order. This manuscript comprises a theoretical analysis of the ethical implications of introducing generative AI into the field of risk assessment. Following the lead of the aforementioned articles, this analysis will consider Beauchamp and Childress' (2013) ethical principles of autonomy, beneficence and nonmaleficence, and justice. The authors endeavor to consider each principle in turn, while generating a non‐exhaustive, but hopefully illustrative series of examples of generative AI applications that could help or hinder risk assessment professionals' efforts to honor the principles. Emphasis will be placed on how ethical issues arising from generative AI may differ either in kind or in degree, from prior technology.

Autonomy

With respect to the principle of autonomy, Hogan et al. (2021) previous analysis of discriminative AI opened with a discussion of a case example presented by Cockerill (2020). In the hypothetical scenario, an individual in the community was flagged as being at risk for violence by an algorithm, based on factors like his internet browsing history and consumption of other media (e.g., violent films), prompting proactive responses from law enforcement and mental health professionals. Hogan et al. (2021) pointed out that from an ethical perspective, this example did not raise entirely novel privacy or autonomy concerns, because both law enforcement and healthcare professionals already balance the competing interests of individual rights and public safety. Although Cockerill (2020) reasonably pointed out that AI has the theoretical capacity to utilize a greater number of data points than existing technology, it is important to note that technological capability does not guarantee greater access to data, either legally or operationally. Law enforcement agencies can and do rely upon open‐source intelligence gathering techniques to utilize information that is in the public domain (e.g., social media posts). However, other personal or health related information, such as records of a person's movements that may be stored on their smartphone, or biometric data stored on their fitness tracker, would be no easier for law enforcement or healthcare professionals to access lawfully simply because the AI tools capable of incorporating this information now exist.

Hogan et al. (2021) expressed greater concern for informed consent. Third and fourth generation violence risk measures (e.g., Douglas et al. 2013; Wong and Gordon 1999–2016) tend to comprise both well‐established historical risk factors (e.g., prior violent behavior) and criminogenic needs (e.g., potentially changeable risk factors, like substance abuse problems) that can be reasonably easily explained and understood by most parties. Evaluators could easily explain that they will be rating such risk factors, and that generally speaking, the more risk factors that are present, the greater the likelihood of a future offense that the evaluator can infer (e.g., Hogan and Olver 2019; Olver et al. 2022). In contrast, to the extent that an AI algorithm indeed contains data elements and decision‐making parameters that go beyond human comprehension (either due to complexity or a lack of transparency), it would be difficult for evaluees to meaningfully consent or assent to the risk assessment process. It also bears mentioning that incomprehensible models would likely also create challenges for triers‐of‐fact and legal counsel who would otherwise provide a gatekeeper function for risk assessment evidence in the courtroom, with unknown effects upon evaluees.

Generative AI could impact informed consent in ways that differ from discriminative AI (and other extant prediction models) in both nature and extent, but the potential threats would vary with the specific application of the technology. Would the evaluator utilize generative AI to draft a case history, which they could then use as the basis for manually undertaking next steps? For instance, an evaluator could present a generative AI application with large amounts of text, including the transcript from a clinical interview, as well as reams of other records they deem relevant (e.g., patient records, police reports, correctional reports), and prompt the application to produce a brief summary, which could expedite the process of organizing that data into relevant risk or need domains. The evaluator could then rely upon this AI‐generated summary to manually score risk tools and generate a formulation and recommendations. Should professionals so choose, generative AI could be relied upon more heavily, to draft the entirety of a risk assessment report. In theory, there is no reason generative AI could not be fed the same primary data the clinician would have reviewed and prompted to: prepare a case history; identify specific risk/need areas; provide summary ratings of risk or recidivism probability estimates, and provide risk management recommendations. Furthermore, after a period of incarceration or supervision, professionals could upload the prior report and some updated notes, then prompt the AI application to summarize the client's progress or make recommendations for privileges to be granted. Such AI‐generated reports could be supervised to varying degrees, depending on organizational or a professional's needs, faith in the output, and the availability of alternatives.

Depending on how generative AI is utilized, it is also possible that it could increase evaluees' involvement in the risk assessment process. Perhaps generative AI could incorporate audio from a clinical interview into a correctional and social history in a matter of moments, allowing an evaluee an opportunity to comment on its perceived accuracy before the professional proceeds with the rating of risk instruments. Such an approach would support evaluees' autonomy and their involvement in the process.

The threat of generative AI to evaluees' abilities to provide informed consent will vary on a few grounds, with important considerations being the explainability of the model and the available of evaluative evidence. Again, not all generative AI applications are (or will be) created equal. Per Kalota (2024), “[t]he explainability of AI is not a new problem” (p. 10), but it is a problem for which interdisciplinary solutions are being sought. The pursuit of explainable AI involves the development of models that can be understood, monitored, and ultimately trusted by human beings. Although not always present in sweeping discussions of generative AI and its potential applications in society, there is recognition in the literature that the technology is not equally suited to all domains. As examples, Ali et al. (2023) and Kalota (2024) have recognized that more explainable models are required in domains like healthcare than might be required by salespersons. Although the technical details of approaches to explainable AI are beyond the scope of this paper, Sahoh and Choksuriwong’s (2023) systematic review can provide interested readers with some examples with respect to high stakes decision‐making. For the current purposes, as far as explainable or “white‐box” (Ali et al. 2023, 2) applications are achievable for risk assessment practice, respect for informed consent could be increased, acknowledging that a model deemed explainable to a programmer may not be so to a layperson.

Beneficence and Nonmaleficence

Hogan et al. (2021) prior analysis of ethics and discriminative AI in risk assessment raised the potential for harm to result from problematic feedback loops. They argued that when predictions trigger interventions, iterative learning processes can become muddled. On the one hand, positive feedback loops can be created if a prediction precipitates an act of violence. If police respond to an acutely ill person suffering from paranoid delusions, the show of force could itself trigger reactive violence. As an ostensibly correct prediction, such an incident would increase the probability of similar predictions and actions in the future. Clearly, risk assessments that themselves precipitate violence would violate the principle of nonmaleficence. On the other hand, negative feedback loops can also be problematic for algorithmic risk predictions. If an algorithm were to successfully prompt preventive measures among inmates at risk of institutional violence, then the algorithm could incorporate that information as a false prediction and adjust accordingly. This concern could apply to any outcome‐informed risk assessment technique, AI‐driven or not, in contexts in which assessments guide intervention.

Questions of how and to what extent generative AI applications might lead to beneficial outcomes, or mitigate potential harms, are as nuanced as the applications themselves. The concerns about feedback loops above apply to binary classification tasks, like predicting recidivists and non‐recidivists. Generative AI can produce output of a different nature, and of infinitely greater complexity. Take two examples: large language models, and transformer models. Language models create text by relying upon trends observed in other text (Kalota 2024); in other words, they rely upon the probabilities of specific words following other identified words within existing written samples. Transformer models on the other hand can convert data between formats, such as synthesizing written prompts into visual formats (Kalota 2024), and vice versa. Consumer AI applications, for instance, can be prompted to produce visual images of various objects and scenes from text‐based narratives. Thus, language models can answer questions and produce written narratives from text prompts (e.g., “tell me if my client a good candidate for release, and provide a rationale”), while a transformer model could be used to produce a written description of an inmate's appearance from a photograph of the individual. Theoretically, generative AI could produce a coherent narrative about an evaluee's history, mental status, and risk profile, by analyzing interview transcripts, correctional files, demographic information, and even images of the evaluee. The potential for such technology to address unmet needs for services, such as in nations with limited capacity (Ogunwale et al. 2024), is exciting to many. Foundationally, forensic risk assessments can benefit the community by helping prevent the harms that they predict. The process can also directly benefit the evaluee by guiding interventions that reduce the likelihood of further criminal justice involvement, or by steering them away from unnecessary interventions. Thus, technology increasing access to valid risk assessments is desirable.

The prospect of generative AI as a standalone risk assessment approach should not be considered without acknowledgment of potential disadvantages. To put it plainly, development of a generative AI application capable of producing a coherent narrative of an evaluee's social history and corresponding risk assessment ratings is only beneficial if the narrative is accurate. Incorrect answers produced by AI, also called hallucinations, are among “the most well‐known weaknesses” (Stratis 2024) of large language models. Tortora (2024) summarized several domains in which generative AI has already been observed to err or mislead, by providing “seemingly plausible but incorrect answers when asked about scientific topics” (p. 9), providing lawyers with non‐existent cases to support legal arguments, and by suggesting non‐existing scientific articles for researchers to cite. Any of these examples could directly or indirectly lead to significant harmful impacts on an evaluee in a forensic context. In a general sense, professionals informing opinions or guiding practice based on fabricated case law or scientific evidence is problematic. More concerning to the current writers is the following question: if generative AI can fabricate case law and scientific evidence, could it not do the same with someone's social history, risk for recidivism, or apparent need to be detained? To the extent that generative AI is used to ameliorate resource/professional gaps, concerns should correspondingly increase about the adequacy of resources available to oversee/audit the content generated by AI.

Justice

The possibility of generative AI applications producing entirely incorrect output has the potential to impact everyone, regardless of their ancestry, age, sex, or gender identity, or any other identifiable characteristic. Nonetheless, there is reason to be wary of the potential for such risks to be distributed in an inequitable manner. The following discussion will focus largely on the impact of race on risk assessment.

Biased Recidivism Variables and the Ground Truth Problem

To judge the accuracy, and by extension the cross‐cultural validity of any risk assessment approach, one must identify an appropriate metric of success. Neal et al. (2022) have argued that for many types of forensic psychological assessment, there is simply no “known or knowable objective ground truth” (p. 174). For example, if a professional offers an opinion on whether an evaluee should be deemed not guilty by reason of insanity (NGRI), how might one determine if they were correct or not? NGRI findings are based on subjective opinions, and evaluators and members of the judiciary may draw different conclusions from the same evidence. Risk assessments arguably boast firmer forms of ground truth than NGRI assessments, at least in terms of building normative databases of recidivism, acknowledging that one cannot know if a prediction of an individual's future outcomes is correct in any given moment. Unfortunately, as pointed out by authors such as Vincent and Viljoen (2020) recidivism outcome variables do not capture all offending behaviors. Many instances of violent behavior never result in a sanction, and the correspondence between behaviors and sanctions can vary among racial groups. Bias within recidivism variables is a concern for any risk assessment process that is predicated upon statistical relationships between predictions and recidivism outcomes.

If people with different racial identities are differentially likely to be apprehended for the same behavior, then this could lead to injustice via risk assessment, whether one relies upon a relatively brief 10‐item risk measure or a sophisticated AI model. Empirical data has indeed suggested that arrest decisions can vary based on the racial identity of the subject of an investigation (Kochel et al. 2011). Kochel's meta‐analytic review indicated that even after controlling for seemingly relevant considerations such as the amount of evidence, the number of witnesses, the suspect's prior criminal justice involvement, and the severity of the incident in question, members of visible minority groups in the United States were disproportionately likely to be arrested by police. Charging decisions can also demonstrate disparity. Hogan and Corăbian (2024) observed that among a sample of high frequency and high severity intimate partner violence offenders in Canada, the Indigenous subgroup was significantly more likely than their counterparts to have been accused of an act of violence without incurring a formal charge. Even when accused individuals reach the point of formal trials, the likelihood of conviction can vary on racial grounds. Meta‐analytic evidence derived from decisions rendered by juries (Devine and Caughlin 2014) has indicated that the probability of guilty verdicts varies with the racial identities of both the jurors and defendants. Thus, when it comes to dependent variables for artificial intelligence applications to pursue, the field of risk assessment also has ground truth problems with which to grapple.

What Do Recidivism Variables Have to Do With Generative AI?

At this point, astute readers may well have recognized that the preceding commentary about recidivism outcome variables is not specific to generative AI. Hogan et al. (2021) raised such concerns while arguing they were applicable to both traditional actuarial methods and AI technology, albeit with the caveat that AI driven risk predictions could exacerbate and obscure the associated challenges. That said, racial disparities in ground truth can pose significant and wide‐ranging problems for generative AI applications as well.

Having established that there can be difficulties in establishing or operationalizing a ground truth when it comes to recidivism prediction, such difficulties have been recognized as a greater and more complex problem for generative AI. Weinberg (2024) pointed out that discriminative AI can be evaluated using a variety of “relatively straightforward” metrics. As an example, evaluators can use statistical measures like area under the curve values to assess the accuracy of a model's predictions, much in the same way scientific researchers have long used them to assess the predictive validity of traditional risk assessment measures (e.g., Hogan and Olver 2016, 2018, 2019). Per Weinberg (2024), the evaluation, and ultimately the training and refinement of generative AI is more difficult, because the outputs can be of a qualitative nature.

Recall that generative AI applications can produce a wide range of output, and so they could take on a range of supportive and leading roles in a risk assessment process. Generative AI could complete tasks that are reasonably objectively evaluated for accuracy, like transcribing verbal interview data, or tasks that are more subjective, like identifying key themes from collateral documents and drafting a concise history. Generative AI could prepare an entire risk assessment report, from the referral question to the risk management recommendations. To evaluate how generative AI performing such functions could pose a threat to the principle of justice, it is necessary to reflect to some degree upon the inner workings of the technology.

To be clear, a discussion of the statistical and computing processes underlying the various forms of generative AI is beyond the scope of this paper, and would be better obtained from others, but the current authors do wish to encourage readers to reflect broadly upon the data upon which generative AI is based. One cannot make bricks without clay, and generative AI cannot produce narratives without data. When it comes to generative AI, explanations of its foundational and input data are complex. Per Stratis (2024), like the manner in which a commercial search engine might attempt to scour data from the entirety of the internet, developers of language based generative AI models try to grant them access to extraordinarily large amounts of text. In essence, models can gradually learn to predict which words are most likely to follow other words, and in which contexts, to replicate human speech. More data leads to greater success, meaning that some developers elect to use all manner of input data, ranging from internet sites, to popular literature, to scientific journal articles, to social media content, and more. While such approaches maximize the likelihood of producing coherent verbal narratives that could mimic human speech, reliance upon the entirety of the information available on the internet can leave something to be desired with respect to the accuracy and validity of the outputs.

Developers also tend to engage in various forms of training (Tortora 2024) with respect to their models, in attempts to increase the validity of the outputs. This can involve seeking user input into the general cogency of a chatbot's responses, or it could be something more specific, like asking healthcare experts whether technical output meets practice standards. It also bears noting that because developing generative AI models is resource intensive, many AI applications that may appear novel to the casual observer, are in fact third‐party adaptations of large centralized models (Stratis 2024). In other words, generic models which have already undergone extensive training (e.g., learning based on popular media in the public domain) often underpin specialist generative AI applications. Bringing the discussion back to generative AI and risk assessment, it is worth reflecting on the data used to train the AI models that could be used to contribute to violence risk assessments.

Realistically, future applications of generative AI to risk assessment would likely be developed using a variety of both specific and non‐specific information sources. As noted above, many specialized generative AI applications are adaptations of existing, generic models. If the language capabilities of a given generative AI model are informed by human discourse (i.e., internet posts, newspapers, books, and other materials of varying age and quality), then it is not difficult to imagine how harmful biases might enter into an application's lexicon. Examples of stereotyped language abound, whether people recognize them or not. As a brief illustration, consider the language used to describe athletes in popular culture. Over 2 decades ago Eastman and Billings (2001) studied the language used by sports commentators to describe university athletes in the United States. They found that the descriptors applied to basketball players varied based on the athletes' racial identities, with Black athletes more likely to be subject of comments about their physical athletic prowess, for example, and White athletes more likely to be commended for their intellectual aptitude. More recently, an analysis of commentary on televised football (soccer) matches from four major European leagues (McLoughlin 2023) indicated that the frequency of specific adjectives varied with athletes' skin tones. The results also indicated that both praise and criticism varied with skin tone, with the former disproportionately offered to lighter skinned athletes and the latter leveled disproportionately against darker skinned athletes. Without too much effort, most readers can likely imagine other examples of stereotyped media depictions or descriptions, ranging from the subtle to the overt.

How might developers address concerns about biased language creeping into the vernacular of generative AI applications, such as tendencies to disproportionately apply positive or negative attributes (or praise vs. criticism) to different groups? Training models using specialized data is one option. For instance, although developers typically require vast amounts of data, they might choose to emphasize news media, literature, or other information that pertains specifically to criminal justice involvement. There are empirical reasons to be cautious about such a strategy. Barlow (2011) summarized literature indicating that news media portrayals of violent incidents vary widely based on the racial and ethnic identities of perpetrators and victims. As one example, Lundman's (2003) quantitative analysis of news media depictions of homicides indicated that specific combinations of perpetrators and victims, such as a Black perpetrator and a white victim, received significantly more media attention. Thus, certain racial and ethnic groups are likely to be overrepresented within the available written descriptions of offending behavior, to a degree that is not simply explained by objective differences in offending rates.

To mitigate the impacts of stereotyped or prejudiced language that can be observed in everyday language, one could consider a narrower set of inputs. Correctional outcome data, like rates of reported crimes or violent acts, have already been identified as imperfect metrics, but are likely to be more objective than news media portrayals. Another strategy would be to train models on the existing work of professionals who have ethical responsibilities to pursue and uphold the ethical principle of justice. Neither strategy is likely to be a panacea, however.

To feed generative AI models relatively objective criminal justice outcome data, such as the characteristics of individuals and groups that tend to offend, reoffend, or desist, would be to feed the models evidence of disparity. According to statistics from the United States (Carson 2020) the imprisonment rate of Hispanic persons in that nation is roughly double that of white persons, and the imprisonment rate of Black persons is roughly double that of Hispanic persons and quadruple that of white persons. The New Zealand Ministry of Justice (2023) has reported that although Māori persons comprise only about 15% of the nation's population, they comprise about 52% of the prison population. Statistics from Canada (Cotter 2021) indicate the Indigenous Peoples of that nation face disproportionate rates of many negative criminal justice outcomes, including higher rates of justice involvement in general, higher rates of incarceration (vs. community supervision), higher security placements within correctional systems, higher rates of segregation, and higher rates of parole revocation. If a generative AI model has been trained on such data, and tasked with describing a typical recidivistic offender, what might it say? If a generative AI model was asked to convert a narrative of a typical recidivistic offender into an image, what might the individual look like?

The authors wish to note here that although we believe racial disparities and the specter of potentially unfair treatment must be faced, we also recognize that observed group differences are not proof such treatment. Age, for instance, might be flagged by AI because of a correlation with recidivism, and generative AI could disproportionately describe younger people as having more serious risk factors, or as having higher levels of impulsivity than their older counterparts. Such differences might be stark, but supported by external evidence, and relatively uncontroversial. Only by tracking such data, however, can evaluators and other stakeholders hope to meaningfully reflect upon whether using such content to inform decisions is indeed ethical and defensible. Weighing the advantages (e.g., predictive accuracy) and disadvantages (e.g., prejudicial treatment of marginalized groups) of including or excluding any given risk factor from a structured tool, should be a fundamental part of the tool development process. To do this, one must be able to delineate what the risk factors under consideration are. Generative AI could be used to produce complex written narratives about justice‐involved persons that influence high‐stakes decisions, but it is not clear if professionals will be able to implement a similar process of identifying contributing factors, weighing them in terms of their probative versus prejudicial value, and making ethical and pragmatic decisions about their inclusion.

The prospect of relying on professional input to supplement the training of generative AI models is also a strategy with potential pitfalls. While some authors (e.g., Vincent and Viljoen 2020) have recommended that professional judgment be considered as a means of mitigating the impacts of potentially biased criminal justice processes and recidivism variables, there is also recognition within the professional literature that bias on the part of evaluators is a concern whenever risk assessments are conducted by human beings (Hart 2016). As an empirical illustration of this point, Orton et al. (2021) observed that having been given the option to override risk categories produced by an empirical actuarial tool (i.e., to assign a higher or lower risk rating than that assigned by way of an algorithm), evaluators' subjective decisions amplified rather than diminished racial disparities in risk categories. More specifically, among the sample of over 40,000 people serving probation sentences (i.e., community‐based correctional supervision), persons identified as White or Indigenous had greater odds of being subjected to an upward override of their risk levels, while persons identified as Hispanic or Asian had lower odds of being subjected to upward overrides. The overrides, which were disparately applied across racial groups, also degraded the predictive accuracy of the risk categories.

Relatively little data is available to answer the question of whether marginalized groups are more or less likely to be rated as high risk using structured professional judgment (SPJ) guidelines, which encourage evaluators to consider a standardized/predetermined set of risk factors, and then proffer subjective summary risk ratings using professional discretion (Vincent and Viljoen 2020). That said, there are now three known studies focused on this area, all of which were conducted in Canada and focused on differences in risk ratings assigned to Indigenous and non‐Indigenous persons (Helmus and Forrester 2014; Hogan and Corăbian 2023, 2024). In each of the three studies, evaluators assigned higher risk ratings to Indigenous persons using at least one SPJ tool. At the same time, each study revealed other differences between the subgroups which could feasibly account for at least some of the observed disparities, such as differences in the severity of violent histories. Nonetheless, this nascent body of research suggests that racial disparities can be observed among SPJ judgments, acknowledging that the sources of the disparities are not fully understood and so it is unclear if they provide evidence of a lack of fairness, per se, using generally accepted professional standards (e.g., American Educational Research Association et al. 2014).

What implications does the research on professional overrides and SPJ risk ratings have for the ethical use of generative AI? The current writers wish to highlight two main points. First, if a databank of risk assessment reports and written opinions produced by professionals are used to train generative AI models, then the generative AI models will be susceptible to reflecting and perpetuating any disparities found therein. Since it appears that some professionals may rate members of specific communities as higher risk than members of other communities despite observing similar actuarial/risk profiles (Orton et al. 2021), it follows that professionals' written work or oral testimony could contain numerous other types of disparities in terms of verbiage and adjectives applied to different groups. Second, if the same professionals are asked to evaluate the products of generative AI in terms of objectivity, validity, or fairness, it begs a question of why one would expect those subjective judgments to be any less susceptible to racial disparity or potentially biased assumptions about certain groups.

Reading the aforementioned arguments, one could conclude that with respect to justice, they make as strong a case against extant methods as they do against generative AI. However, similar problems can vary by degree, and professionals should reflect on whether any given AI application would make risk assessments more or less difficult to examine, understand, and refine. Finally, those still unconvinced that generative AI poses any unique or incremental ethical threats, should consider recent research indicating that output from AI models can demonstrate bias against particular social groups, in part because the “training or inference procedure itself may amplify bias, beyond what is present in the training data” (p. 1107, Gallegos et al. 2024).

Discussion

Much has been made about the revolutionary potential of AI in various fields, including violence risk assessment. It is evident that generative AI could assist risk assessment professionals in myriad ways, from expediting mundane tasks like transcribing notes, to assisting with literature reviews, to producing novel and potentially precise recidivism risk estimates. Current technology is closer to fulfilling its promise in some of these domains than it is in others.

The current analysis was motivated in part by the observation that the discourse surrounding generative AI is such that “exaggerated and sensationalistic portrayals” (Tortora 2024, 8) of the capacities and benefits abound. That said, some authors, like Ratajczak and Cockerill (2023) have made reasonably sound arguments about the potential benefits of real and hypothetical AI applications, while also arguing that to stand in the way of major technological advancements could eventually prove to be tantamount to unethical practice. In support of the assertion that AI has already improved upon traditional risk assessment tools, Ratajczak and Cockerill (2023) cited meta‐analytic evidence of AI predicting general recidivism with an average area under the curve value of 0.74. Is this truly evidence of superiority relative to all current approaches? The Violence Risk Scale (Wong and Gordon) is a fourth generation tool and as such it is comprised of psychologically meaningful risk factors; its predictive validity has been studied both with respect to total scores and dynamic change scores and its cross‐cultural validity, and Olver et al. (2022) have reported area under the curve values of 0.73 and 0.77 for general recidivism over 3‐year and 5‐year follow‐ups, respectively. These statistics are offered simply to remind readers that both AI and current methods are varied, and that hopes for vast improvements have yet to be fully realized by AI. The current writers also wish to be clear in our view that the argument that the implementation of a new technology is “inevitable” (Ratajczak and Cockerill 2023, 244) should inspire a greater, not a lesser, drive to analyze the ethical impacts of that technology.

Is there genuine cause to be particularly concerned about the ethical implications of using generative AI for violence risk assessments? Both Hogan et al. (2021) and Spivak and Shepherd (2021) have argued that many ethical issues that could be viewed as limitations of AI are also applicable to existing methods, and particularly second‐generation risk tools, which remain in common use. But what conclusions are professionals to draw from this shared premise? Does this argument imply that no particularly elevated degree of caution is required when utilizing new technology? Spivak and Shepherd (2021) also refuted Hogan et al. (2021) argument that theoretically or psychologically meaningful risk factors were preferable to atheoretical statistical markers from an ethical perspective. Specifically, Spivak and Shepherd (2021) opined that risk factors like attitudes supportive of violence were “as susceptible as any other to racial biases … [and so the authors] are unclear as to why racial biases that result from consideration of this sort of variable should be treated differently to any other” (p. 3).

From the first premise above, we propose that readers should draw the conclusion that caution is indeed warranted in applying generative AI to risk assessment. We propose this not despite the fact that issues like biased recidivism variables could also apply to current methods, but largely because of that fact. Even generative AI relies upon data selected or created by humans in one form or another, and given the stakes involved in violence risk assessment, we believe that questions about appropriate inputs, outputs, and definitions of ground truth should be addressed openly, methodically, and scientifically.

With respect to the second argument above, suggesting an equivalency between all forms of risk factors (in terms of bias) that lead to disparities, it is important to note that bias has more than one definition. Per recognized psychological testing standards (American Educational Research Association et al. 2014), disparities in testing outcomes across groups do not constitute evidence of bias or lack of fairness. Disparities in risk scores and risk ratings derived from a given tool could reflect genuine differences in the construct of interest, which is the likelihood of violent behavior. In contrast, if racial disparities in risk scores or risk ratings can be attributed to policing activities that are known to be unfairly skewed against a racialized community, then the differences would be considered a form of construct‐irrelevant variance, or bias, and the scores could be deemed unfair. Unfortunately, it is often unknown whether observed testing “differences are due to real differences between groups in the construct being measured or to some source of bias” (American Educational Research Association et al. 2014, 54), and ambiguous recidivism variables mean this statement often applies in the case of violence risk assessment tools. Given this uncertainty, relying on atheoretical prediction models becomes more difficult to justify. For instance, AI might determine that seemingly innocuous correlates, such as the color of one's mailbox or the name of their preferred restaurant might predict “recidivism”, but these variables might disproportionately lead members of racialized communities to be labeled as high risk. Let us contrast these examples with a psychologically meaningful risk factor (one of many that together comprise a structured measure), entitled Attitudes Supportive of Violence. An evaluator learns that an evaluee has repeatedly assaulted his female partner after observing her speaking with other males; during a clinical interview, he explicitly stated that if his partner continues to speak to other males, he will feel justified in assaulting her again as retribution. Empirical research indicates that this risk factor (and the tool to which it contributes) is associated with “recidivism”, but also indicates the item is more often rated as present among a racialized community. What is an evaluator to think? It is certainly possible that bias in terms of racist or other stereotypes could influence how certain evaluators rate such qualitative items. On the other hand, the rationale for the rating can be examined and tested by observers. We believe that the threat to fairness posed by these two types of risk factors is not equivalent. Conceptually meaningful risk factors are typically easier to explain, challenge, and justify, than black‐box predictors. Readers may draw their own conclusions.

The potentially vast breadth of hypothetical generative AI applications in risk assessment means that, much like the ethical considerations identified above, there is a commensurately wide range of worthwhile future directions and opportunities for further research. For example, it would be prudent to conduct research directly comparing examples of AI produced work against those of human experts, using similar file information. Such research could examine whether experts believe that critical information is more likely to be omitted by the AI application versus the human authors, or vice versa. Ideally, researchers will collect empirical data on rates and types of hallucinations produced by any given application, which could be compared against error rates found within comparable human‐authored examples. It would also be worthwhile to study the extent to which generative AI applications produce content that varies based on known characteristics of evaluees (e.g., age, sex, ethnocultural background), not just with respect to risk classifications, but also with respect to qualitative descriptors and/or recommendations for specific services or management strategies. Finally, over the longer term, researchers should examine whether all risk assessment practices, including those guided by AI, can ultimately guide interventions that reduce rates of violence (Rettenberger and Eher 2024).

Conclusions

Generative AI appears to be an expanding technological force, and this ethics analysis only skims the surface of the possible applications at the intersection of behavioral sciences and the law. We urge readers to exercise caution in applying this technology to risk assessment practice, as we would with respect to any and all current and emerging approaches to violence risk assessment. Perhaps the most critical threat to the ethical application of generative AI to risk assessment is the possibility that overconfidence in the technology's impressive capabilities (Tortora 2024) should lead to complacency and deference. Generative AI, like all technology used to inform high‐stakes decisions about people's lives and liberties, should be the subject of ongoing and rigorous scientific evaluation. Perhaps generative AI could even be applied in support of such evaluation research.
