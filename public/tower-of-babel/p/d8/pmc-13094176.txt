Analysis of prognosis in early-stage cervical cancer patients post-conization treated with laparoscopic versus open radical hysterectomy
Authors: Yu-meng Cui, Yu-mei Shan, Yun-yun Xiao, Yue Gao, Xiao-chuan Yu, Meng Jiang, Hua-li Wang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveThis study aimed to compare oncologic outcomes between laparoscopic radical hysterectomy (LRH) and open radical hysterectomy (ORH) in patients with early-stage cervical cancer (FIGO 2018 IA2–IIA1) who underwent cervical conization, and to evaluate independent risk factors influencing their prognosis.MethodsWe retrospectively analyzed clinical data from 397 cervical cancer patients (FIGO 2009 IA2–IIA1) treated with LRH or ORH following conization at Dalian Women and Children’s Medical Center (Group) and Shengjing Hospital, China Medical University, between January 2013 and December 2021. Clinical indicators, including demographics, HPV status, tumor features, and postoperative pathology, were collected. After restaging according to FIGO 2018, 353 patients remained stage IA2–IIA1 (225 ORH, 128 LRH). A 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) approach balanced clinical variables between groups. Primary endpoints were overall survival (OS) and progression-free survival (PFS). All patients included in the FIGO 2018 IA2–IIA1 cohort were pathologically confirmed to be node-negative, as lymph node metastasis automatically results in classification as stage IIIC1p and such patients were excluded from the primary analysis. Nodal status was assessed through systematic pelvic lymphadenectomy with or without aortic bifurcation lymph node dissection, depending on surgeon preference and guideline recommendations. All lymph nodes were examined by dedicated gynecologic pathologists to ensure accurate staging. Multivariate Cox regression analysis was conducted post-PSM to identify independent prognostic factors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05.ResultsOf the original 397 patients, 353 retained FIGO 2018 stage IA2–IIA1. After PSM, no significant differences were observed in OS (89.9% ORH vs. 90.3% LRH, p = 0.907) or PFS (87.3% ORH vs. 89.5% LRH, p = 0.907). In the ITT sensitivity analysis that included all FIGO 2018 IIIC1p patients, the survival trends remained consistent with the primary analysis, and no significant differences in OS or PFS were observed between the laparoscopic and open surgery groups, supporting the robustness of our findings. Multivariate analysis identified BMI, tumor size, cervical stromal invasion depth, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), and postoperative adjuvant therapy as independent prognostic factors affecting both OS and PFS (all p < 0.05).ConclusionLaparoscopic radical hysterectomy demonstrated comparable oncologic outcomes to open surgery following cervical conization in patients with FIGO 2018 IA2–IIA1 cervical cancer. Prognosis was independently influenced by BMI, tumor size, stromal invasion depth, LVSI, and adjuvant therapy.Keywords: Cervical cancer, Cervical conization, Laparoscopic radical hysterectomy, Open radical hysterectomy, Propensity score matching, Prognostic factors

Cervical cancer remains the fourth most common cancer among women globally, primarily driven by persistent infection with high-risk human papillomavirus (HPV), particularly types 16 and 18. Risk factors include early sexual activity, multiple pregnancies, socioeconomic factors, and sexual behavior. Despite declining incidence rates in certain regions, notably Asia, cervical cancer continues to pose a significant health burden, with an estimated 604,127 new cases and 341,831 deaths worldwide in 2020 [1, 2].

Current guidelines, notably those from the National Comprehensive Cancer Network (NCCN), recommend radical hysterectomy with pelvic lymphadenectomy for cervical cancer FIGO stages IA2-IIA1, employing either open surgery, laparoscopic, or robotic-assisted approaches. However, findings from the landmark Laparoscopic Approach to Cervical Cancer (LACC) trial in 2018 demonstrated significantly higher recurrence and mortality rates associated with laparoscopic radical hysterectomy compared to open surgery, prompting NCCN guidelines to endorse open surgery as the standard treatment. Although this has been supported by further meta-analyses and international guidelines (ESGO, BGCS), some studies report comparable survival outcomes between laparoscopic and open surgery in carefully selected early-stage patients, particularly those with tumors < 2 cm [3, 4].

Recent evidence suggests cervical conization performed prior to radical hysterectomy might mitigate the oncologic risks associated with laparoscopic surgery by minimizing tumor spillage and dissemination [5, 6]. A systematic review indicated improved survival and lower recurrence rates in patients undergoing preoperative conization compared to those without conization, particularly when employing laparoscopic surgery [7].

The revised 2018 FIGO staging system integrates clinical, pathological, and imaging findings, addressing previous staging limitations. Yet, clinical guidelines continue to rely substantially on trials using the older FIGO 2009 staging system [8–13].

Therefore, this retrospective cohort study compares survival and recurrence outcomes of laparoscopic versus open radical hysterectomy following conization in FIGO 2018 stage IA2–IIA1 cervical cancer patients. Propensity score matching (PSM) was used to balance clinical indicators, and independent risk factors influencing prognosis were analyzed, aiming to provide robust clinical guidance in this context [14–16].

Study population

General information

This retrospective study involved 397 patients diagnosed with FIGO 2009 stage IA2-IIA1 cervical cancer who underwent cervical conization followed by either laparoscopic or open radical hysterectomy at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Departments of Dalian Women and Children’s Medical Center (Group) and Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University between January 1, 2013, and December 31, 2021.

Surgical technique

All laparoscopic radical hysterectomies in both centers were performed according to standardized oncologic principles. Importantly, a uterine manipulator was not used in any laparoscopic case in order to minimize potential tumor disruption. Colpotomy was performed entirely via the vaginal route rather than intracorporeally, thereby avoiding direct exposure of cervical tissue to the pneumoperitoneum. Surgical specimens were removed en bloc through the vagina without morcellation or fragmentation. Because cervical conization had removed the visible exocervical tumor before radical hysterectomy in all patients, no specimen containment bag was required. Pneumoperitoneum pressure was maintained at 10–12 mmHg throughout the procedure. All operations were performed by senior gynecologic oncologists with experience exceeding 50 prior laparoscopic radical hysterectomies, and each center annually performs approximately 40–60 radical hysterectomies, ensuring a high-volume environment. In addition to the protective effect of prior conization, several technical features of the laparoscopic procedures in our study may have further reduced the likelihood of tumor spillage. First, no uterine manipulator was used, eliminating one of the mechanisms hypothesized to contribute to tumor extrusion in minimally invasive surgery. Second, the exclusive use of a vaginal approach for colpotomy, rather than intracorporeal colpotomy under pneumoperitoneum, minimized direct peritoneal exposure to cervical tissue. Third, en bloc removal of the specimen through the vagina avoided any form of morcellation or fragmentation. Fourth, low-pressure pneumoperitoneum (10–12 mmHg) was used consistently, which may reduce aerosolization or dispersion of tumor cells. Lastly, all procedures were performed by high-volume gynecologic oncologists, a factor associated with lower recurrence risk in several surgical oncology domains. These standardized surgical techniques likely worked synergistically with prior conization to reduce intraoperative tumor exposure, providing a potential mechanistic explanation for why laparoscopic radical hysterectomy did not exhibit inferior outcomes in our cohort.

Staging of the selected cases was initially based on the FIGO 2009 criteria, later re-evaluated according to the FIGO 2018 criteria. Although all patients were initially diagnosed and staged according to FIGO 2009, the primary oncologic outcome analyses (OS and PFS) in this study were conducted using the FIGO 2018 staging system. This approach was adopted because FIGO 2018 provides more precise risk classification, particularly regarding lymph node status, and allows for the consistent exclusion of patients upstaged to FIGO 2018 stage IIIC1p from the primary survival analysis to prevent stage-migration bias. Analyses based on FIGO 2009 were performed only as secondary/exploratory analyses to facilitate comparison with historical cohorts and prior literature. All propensity score matching and multivariable Cox analyses were therefore primarily conducted within the FIGO 2018 IA2–IIA1 population. Ethical approval was obtained from the Ethics Committees of Dalian Women and Children’s Medical Center (Group) and Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University. Patient Staging and Statistical Analysis Details

Observation indicators

Collected clinical variables included demographics, body mass index (BMI), smoking status, HPV infection status, surgery year, conization year, margin status after conization, interval between conization and radical hysterectomy, clinical staging (FIGO 2009), tumor size, tumor grade, parametrial infiltration, vaginal wall invasion, depth of cervical stromal invasion, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), lymph node metastasis, postoperative pathology, and postoperative adjuvant therapy. All patients were restaged according to FIGO 2018 criteria.

Follow-up

Patients underwent postoperative follow-up every 3–6 months for the first two years, every 6–12 months for years three to five, and annually thereafter. Follow-up was conducted via outpatient visits or phone calls by specialist gynecologists. Data collected included postoperative adjuvant therapy, survival status, date and cause of death, recurrence date, and site of recurrence. The last follow-up occurred on September 30, 2023. The primary endpoint was overall survival (OS), defined from the date of initial diagnosis to death from any cause. The secondary endpoint was progression-free survival (PFS), defined from the date of initial diagnosis to the first documented disease recurrence. Because the interval between diagnosis, conization, and radical hysterectomy varied among patients, we performed an additional sensitivity analysis in which both OS and PFS were re-calculated using the date of radical hysterectomy as time zero, in order to assess and minimize the potential impact of immortal time bias.

Statistical methods

Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 26.0 software. Continuous variables not normally distributed were reported as medians and analyzed using Mann-Whitney U tests, while categorical variables were presented as frequencies and percentages and compared using Chi-square or Fisher’s exact tests. In the primary analysis, time-to-event was measured from the date of initial diagnosis. To evaluate the potential influence of immortal time introduced by the interval between diagnosis, conization, and radical hysterectomy, we conducted a sensitivity analysis in which the time origin for OS and PFS was redefined as the date of radical hysterectomy. Kaplan–Meier and Cox regression analyses were repeated using this alternative time scale, and the results were compared with those of the primary analysis.

To balance potential confounders, a 1:1 propensity score matching (PSM) analysis was performed between the laparoscopic and open surgery groups. Propensity scores were estimated using a logistic regression model in which the dependent variable was the type of radical hysterectomy (laparoscopic vs. open). The following baseline covariates were included in the propensity score model: age, BMI, smoking status, HPV infection status, surgery year, conization year, margin status after conization, interval between conization and radical surgery, clinical stage (FIGO 2009), tumor size, tumor grade, parametrial infiltration, vaginal wall invasion, depth of cervical stromal invasion, lymphovascular space invasion (LVSI), lymph node metastasis, postoperative pathology, and postoperative adjuvant therapy. Covariate balance before and after matching was assessed using standardized mean differences (SMDs) for all variables included in the propensity score model. An absolute SMD < 0.1 was considered indicative of adequate balance. SMDs are presented in Supplementary Table S1, and Love plots illustrating covariate balance before and after matching are provided in Supplementary Figures S1 (FIGO 2009–based matching) and S2 (FIGO 2018–based matching).

Matching was conducted using 1:1 nearest-neighbour matching without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. For all variables entered into the propensity score model, there were no missing values; “HPV not tested” was treated as a separate category within the HPV variable. Therefore, no imputation was performed and no patients were excluded from PSM due to missing covariate data.Survival analyses were conducted using Kaplan-Meier curves, and comparisons were performed with the log-rank test. Multivariate Cox proportional hazards models were employed post-PSM to calculate hazard ratios (HR) and 95% confidence intervals (CI) for independent prognostic factors. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. For the multivariable Cox models, tumor size was entered as a categorical variable with three levels (< 2 cm, 2–4 cm, ≥ 4 cm), coded using dummy variables and with tumors < 2 cm serving as the reference category. Depth of cervical stromal invasion was modeled as a binary variable (< 1/2 vs. ≥ 1/2 stromal thickness), and LVSI was entered as a binary variable (no vs. yes), both with the absence or shallower invasion category as the reference group.

To assess the impact of excluding FIGO 2018 IIIC1p patients, an intention-to-treat (ITT) sensitivity analysis was performed. All node-positive patients upstaged to IIIC1p were included in the analysis and retained in their original groups based on FIGO 2009 staging and initial surgical assignment. Kaplan–Meier and Cox proportional hazards models were repeated to test whether the inclusion of IIIC1p patients altered survival estimates.

Study participants

A total of 397 patients diagnosed with FIGO 2009 stage IA2–IIA1 cervical cancer who underwent conization followed by either laparoscopic or open radical hysterectomy from January 1, 2013, to December 31, 2021, at the Obstetrics and Gynecology Departments of Dalian Women and Children’s Medical Center (Group) and Shengjing Hospital of China Medical University, were included according to established inclusion and exclusion criteria. Among them, 255 underwent open radical hysterectomy (ORH group: IA2 = 21, IB1 = 206, IB2 = 13, IIA1 = 15) and 142 underwent laparoscopic radical hysterectomy (LRH group: IA2 = 17, IB1 = 116, IB2 = 3, IIA1 = 6). For the FIGO 2009–based analysis (n = 397; 255 ORH and 142 LRH), 1:1 propensity score matching yielded 133 matched pairs (266 patients in total), resulting in 131 patients who remained unmatched and were excluded from the matched analysis. For the FIGO 2018 IA2–IIA1 cohort (n = 353; 225 ORH and 128 LRH), 113 matched pairs (226 patients) were retained after PSM, and 127 patients were excluded from the matched set.

After restaging based on FIGO 2018, 353 patients remained classified within stages IA2–IIA1, comprising 225 patients in the ORH group (IA2 = 30, IB1 = 150, IB2 = 30, IB3 = 5, IIA1 = 10) and 128 patients in the LRH group (IA2 = 14, IB1 = 104, IB2 = 4, IB3 = 2, IIA1 = 4). Thirty-three patients were reclassified to FIGO 2018 stage IIIC1p (ORH = 22, LRH = 11), and 11 patients were downgraded to IA1 (ORH = 8, LRH = 3). In the primary analysis, patients upstaged to FIGO 2018 IIIC1p due to pathologically confirmed lymph node metastasis were excluded to prevent stage-migration bias and maintain a homogeneous early-stage cohort. However, acknowledging that exclusion of node-positive patients may deviate from the intention-to-treat (ITT) principle, we additionally performed a sensitivity analysis using an ITT approach, in which all FIGO 2018 IIIC1p patients (n = 33) were reintegrated into the survival analysis according to their initial FIGO 2009 stage and surgical approach. The results of the sensitivity analysis were compared with the primary per-protocol analysis to evaluate the robustness of our findings. Survival and recurrence outcomes

The median follow-up period was 76 months (range: 22–128 months). According to FIGO 2009, recurrence occurred in 38 ORH patients (38/255) and 23 LRH patients (23/142); deaths occurred in 30 ORH patients and 16 LRH patients. Progression-free survival (PFS) rates were 86.7% (ORH) vs. 84% (LRH, P = 0.543, Fig. 2A); overall survival (OS) rates were 92.5% (ORH) vs. 91.1% (LRH, P = 0.359, Fig. 2C). After PSM, PFS rates were 82% (ORH) vs. 84.7% (LRH, P = 0.779, Fig. 2B), and OS rates were 88% (ORH) vs. 92.7% (LRH, P = 0.912, Fig. 2D), showing no significant differences between groups.

Discussion

The revised FIGO 2018 staging system integrates clinical, pathological, and imaging findings to address limitations of earlier classification, particularly the omission of lymph node involvement under FIGO 2009. However, current clinical guidelines and much of the existing evidence base, including several influential trials, continue to rely heavily on studies designed under the older FIGO 2009 framework [8–13]. This discrepancy creates challenges when interpreting contemporary clinical outcomes using updated staging criteria and highlights the need for studies that evaluate surgical modalities within a FIGO 2018–defined population.

In this context, our study provides new evidence focusing on a cohort of FIGO 2018 IA2–IIA1 cervical cancer patients who uniformly underwent cervical conization prior to radical hysterectomy. We found that laparoscopic radical hysterectomy demonstrated no significant difference in progression-free survival or overall survival compared with open radical hysterectomy, both before and after propensity score matching. These results remained stable in sensitivity analyses that reintegrated patients upstaged to IIIC1p, supporting the robustness of our findings. Several tumor-related factors—including tumor size, depth of cervical stromal invasion, LVSI, and BMI—were identified as independent predictors of prognosis, consistent with well-established pathological determinants of cervical cancer outcomes.

The recurrence site distribution showed that local recurrence (mainly vaginal cuff) was the most common pattern in both groups, consistent with the biological characteristics of early-stage cervical cancer. Notably, the proportion of pelvic and distant recurrence was relatively low (27.5% and 21.6%, respectively), and there was no significant difference between surgical approaches. This finding aligns with our hypothesis that preoperative conization reduces tumor bulk and removes exocervical lesions, minimizing intraoperative tumor spillage and dissemination—especially for minimally invasive surgery, which was previously associated with higher spillage risks. The similar recurrence site profile further validates the oncologic equivalence of LRH and ORH in this selected cohort.

These findings differ from the landmark LACC trial, which reported significantly worse oncologic outcomes with minimally invasive radical hysterectomy. Several factors may account for this divergence. First, unlike LACC, which relied on FIGO 2009 staging, our primary analysis was conducted exclusively within a strictly node-negative FIGO 2018 cohort, reducing the biological heterogeneity introduced by occult lymph node metastasis. Second, surgical practices and technical precautions have evolved considerably since LACC, and evidence from multiple observational studies has demonstrated comparable outcomes for minimally invasive surgery in appropriately selected early-stage patients [3, 4]. These differences underscore the importance of patient selection, staging accuracy, and procedural standardization when interpreting cross-study variations in oncologic outcomes.

A distinctive feature of our cohort is that all patients underwent cervical conization prior to radical hysterectomy, which may have played a significant role in the observed oncologic equivalence between surgical approaches. Increasing evidence suggests that conization may reduce the risk of intraoperative tumor exposure, thereby mitigating concerns about tumor spillage during minimally invasive procedures [5, 6, 17]. A recent systematic review has also demonstrated that preoperative conization improves survival outcomes in patients undergoing minimally invasive radical hysterectomy [7]. Conization reduces tumor bulk, removes exocervical lesions most vulnerable to CO₂ pneumoperitoneum or instrument manipulation, and decreases the likelihood of positive surgical margins. These mechanisms collectively may help explain why our findings differ from prior reports in which conization was not universally performed.

The prognostic factors identified in our multivariate analysis—particularly tumor size, stromal invasion depth, and LVSI—are aligned with previous research and reinforce the biological relevance of these parameters [14–16]. Importantly, the beneficial effects of postoperative radiotherapy and chemotherapy observed in our study further emphasize the need for individualized adjuvant treatment strategies in early-stage cervical cancer, even among patients who undergo complete tumor resection.

Despite the strengths of a multicenter cohort and modern staging reclassification, certain limitations must be acknowledged. The retrospective nature of the study may introduce inherent selection bias, and differences in surgical expertise or perioperative practices across institutions could influence outcomes. Moreover, although conization improves tumor containment, the degree to which surgical technique, surgeon experience, and protective intraoperative measures contribute to oncologic safety remains an important area for future clarification.

Future research should focus on prospective multicenter studies incorporating modern staging systems and standardized conization protocols, as well as mechanistic studies investigating how preoperative tumor removal influences local recurrence patterns. Additionally, the integration of molecular markers may offer refined prognostic stratification and help determine which patient subsets may benefit most from minimally invasive approaches.

In the context of early-stage cervical cancer, fertility preservation has become an increasingly important component of modern surgical management, particularly for young patients with reproductive goals. Although the present study focused exclusively on radical hysterectomy after conization, contemporary practices now incorporate a range of fertility-sparing options—including radical trachelectomy, simple trachelectomy for highly selected microinvasive disease, and even conization alone in select IA1–IA2 cases with negative margins—reflecting a broader shift toward individualized treatment. The growing implementation of sentinel lymph node (SLN) mapping has further improved the precision of surgical staging, reducing the morbidity associated with full pelvic lymphadenectomy while maintaining high diagnostic accuracy. Recent prospective initiatives [18–21], have demonstrated the safety and feasibility of combining fertility-sparing surgery with SLN mapping in carefully selected patients, underscoring the importance of accurate nodal assessment in tailoring the extent of surgical intervention. While our cohort was limited to patients undergoing radical hysterectomy, the techniques evaluated in studies like ETERNITY highlight meaningful opportunities to integrate tumor biology, fertility considerations, and minimally invasive approaches into future clinical algorithms. Taken together, these developments emphasize the need for continued refinement of patient selection and staging strategies in the management of early-stage cervical cancer.

In summary, our findings suggest that in a FIGO 2018 IA2–IIA1, node-negative cervical cancer population treated with preoperative conization, laparoscopic radical hysterectomy may provide oncologic outcomes comparable to open surgery. These results highlight the importance of updated staging, careful patient selection, and tumor-protective surgical strategies when evaluating the role of minimally invasive surgery in early-stage cervical cancer.

Conclusion

For patients with FIGO 2009 and FIGO 2018 stage IA2-IIA1 cervical cancer after conization, laparoscopic radical hysterectomy demonstrates oncological outcomes comparable to those of open radical hysterectomy.
