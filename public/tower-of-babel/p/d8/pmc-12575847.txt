NT-I7, a novel long-acting interleukin-7, promotes anti-PD-1 efficacy in an autologous humanized melanoma model
Authors: Yee Peng Phoon, Alexandra A. Wolfarth, Pauline Funchain, Jennifer S. Ko, Donghoon Choi, Eliska Dedkova, Daniela Duarte Bateman, Jessica Corvin, Jan J. Melenhorst, Brian R. Gastman
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Despite recent advancements in immunotherapy, most cancer patients still struggle to achieve sustained benefits, highlighting the need for new treatment strategies. In the past, lack of available models to assess immunotherapeutic combinations hampered development. In this new study, we utilized a novel all-autologous humanized melanoma mouse model to assess the efficacy of NT-I7 (human-reagent grade, efineptakin alfa), a long-acting human IL-7. Given that NT-I7 has been shown to enhance T cell proliferation and survival in both humans and mice, we hypothesized that NT-I7 would improve the engraftment of patient immune cells and the effectiveness of anti-PD-1 therapy in our humanized melanoma model, which was reported to accurately mimic actual clinical outcome, providing more precise assessment of clinical efficacy and relevance. Our findings indicate that NT-I7 significantly enhances T cell engraftment. We discovered a synergistic effect between NT-I7 and anti-PD-1 (Pembrolizumab) that notably augments immunotherapeutic efficacy through the expansion of TCM cells and sustained cytotoxicity. In sum, our humanized model reveals that NT-I7 holds great promise as a next-generation therapy to enhance clinical responses and patient care.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-025-20253-w.

Introduction

Over the last decade, cancer treatment has shifted towards targeting the host instead of the tumor1–3. The discovery of immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICIs), particularly PD-1 blockade has revolutionized melanoma patient care4–8 and significantly improved survival in advanced cases9–11. Despite recent advancements in ICIs combination, many patients remain resistant to therapy6,8,12. In fact, several clinical trials have reported disappointing outcomes in recent years13,14. This highlights a key gap in current immunotherapy: the lack of effective strategies to overcome resistance and achieve durable responses. Consequently, substantial efforts are being made to discover new targets and innovative strategies to improve anti-tumor immune responses, particularly stimulating immune milieu in the tumor microenvironment (TME) to enhance durable responses.

Interleukin-7 (IL-7) plays a key role in inducing and maintaining memory CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, by enhancing their proliferative capability, longevity, and functionality15–17. Both CD4+ and CD8+ memory T cells can acquire effector functions to eradicate tumor cells18,19. IL-7 promotes central memory T cells (TCM) and stem cell memory-like (TSCM) phenotypes, which exhibit superior persistence and anti-tumor immunity19–24. TCM cells, with high proliferative capacity, are more effective than terminally differentiated effector memory T cells (TEM) in modulating anti-tumor responses19,23,25,26. Therefore, IL-7 plays a vital role in boosting immunotherapy efficacy. However, its clinical potential is limited by a very short half-life27, reducing sustained therapeutic impact.

Engineered cytokines like IL-7, with improved half-life and stability, show promise in enhancing immune activation and overcoming resistance when combined with ICIs by modulating immune responses and reshaping the TME28,29. NT-I7, a long-acting engineered recombinant human IL-7, further improves half-life, structural stability, bioavailability and potency30,31. It expands naïve and memory T cells, restores lymphocyte counts31, and may synergize with anti-PD-1 by enhancing T cell quantity and function, potentially improving outcomes in ICI-resistant patients. We posit that NT-I7 enhances anti-tumor efficacy by boosting memory T cell responses.

A crucial step in evaluating this new therapeutic target is the use of a suitable preclinical models that accurately mimic clinical outcomes; however, such models remain limited32–34. To address this challenge, we developed a novel all-autologous melanoma humanized mouse model that recapitulates actual clinical outcomes using matched melanoma patient tumors and immune cells35. Using this model, we reveal that NT-I7 enhances memory T cell responses and synergizes with anti-PD-1 to improve anti-tumor efficacy. Collectively, our study substantiates the potential of NT-I7 as a promising next immunotherapeutic agent to overcome immunotherapy resistance and enhance clinical efficacy.

Results

NT-I7 improves anti-tumor efficacy in an advanced patient-derived xenograft melanoma model

The suitability of a preclinical model for evaluating immunotherapeutic agents depends on treatment tolerance and optimal dosage for engraftment in efficacy studies. To investigate these factors, we examined drug tolerance, tumor engraftment, and anti-tumor efficacy by administrating two doses of NT-I7 (10 mg/kg) by intraperitoneal injection (IP) for rapid absorption at different intervals: days 1 and 8 (group A) and days 1 and 15 (group B) post-adoptive cell therapy (ACT) with 1x106 matched peripheral blood mononuclear cells (PBMC), as illustrated in the treatment schematic (Fig. 1A). All mice tolerated the treatment well, showing no significant weight loss (data not shown). In two independent experiments, both the 1-week (Group A, days 1 and 8) and 2-week (Group B, days 1 and 15) NT-I7 dosing interval significantly reduced tumor burden compared to control (Fig. 1B-C). On day 27 post-ACT, Group A exhibited faster tumor control than Group B (Fig. 1B), although both groups showed comparable anti-tumor effect by day 33 (Fig. 1C). Donor clinical characteristics are summarized in Table 1. PBMC engraftment, assessed by CD45 expression, was limited in the spleens of both groups and in Group B tumors (Fig. 1D). Although NT-I7 effectively perturbed tumor progression after PBMC infusion, we observed limited PBMC engraftment. This raised the question of whether the low engraftment resulted from insufficient PBMC transplantation, prompting us to further expand the autologous PBMC with high-dose recombinant human interleukin-1 (rhIL-2; 6000IU/ml) for 2 weeks to increase the cell number for ACT. The treatment schematic is as shown (Fig. 1E). Using expanded autologous PBMC, NT-I7-treated mice showed improved efficacy compared to the control group; however, tumor growth reduction was not significant, regardless of the cell numbers for ACT (2x106 and 5x106) (Fig. 1F). Similarly, CD45 engraftment was minimal in both spleens and tumors, regardless of cell number (Fig. 1G). Cumulatively, NT-I7 enhanced anti-tumor efficacy independent of ACT cell number.

NT-I7 therapy combined with autologous PBMC transplantation improves efficacy and T cell engraftment

To further optimize the model, we next compared engraftment and anti-tumor efficacy of unexpanded and expanded autologous PBMC using subcutaneous injection, which better mimics clinical practice through gradual systemic absorption. Mice were treated twice with NT-I7 subcutaneously at 1-week intervals, followed by 1x106 ACT of either unexpanded or expanded autologous PBMC, as shown in the treatment schematic (Fig. 2A). Interestingly, unexpanded PBMC ACT significantly enhanced anti-tumor efficacy compared to expanded PBMC ACT (Fig. 2B). Besides improving efficacy, unexpanded PBMC also promoted CD45 T cell engraftment in both spleen and tumor compared to expanded PBMC (Fig. 2C). Further analysis via flow cytometry revealed that expanding PBMC led to a loss of CD127, the IL-7 receptor-alpha chain. This loss of CD127 expression correlated with reduced efficacy and CD45 engraftment (Fig. 2D). These findings were corroborated by an additional independent experiment, which confirmed that NT-I7 treatment paired with unexpanded PBMC ACT led to improved tumor growth reduction compared to that of expanded PBMC ACT (data not shown). NT-I7 also enhanced spleen enlargement (Fig. 2E) and T cell engraftment (Fig. 2F) in unexpanded PBMC ACT but not in expanded PBMC ACT. While NT-I7 nominally increased CD8+ T cells (average range of 4.4-6.7%), it preferentially expanded CD4+ T cells (average range of 82-91%) in both spleen and tumor (Fig. 2G). The median fold ratio of CD4/CD8 was notably higher in both spleen (10.6-fold) and tumor (21.8-fold) after PBMC ACT compared to that of the unexpanded patient PBMC (6.4-fold) (Fig. 2G). Synergistic anti-tumor efficacy of NT-I7 and PD-1 blockade

After establishing the humanized model, we next evaluated whether NT-I7 could enhance the efficacy of anti-PD-1 therapy, which is the current standard of care for metastatic melanoma. In this study, we used tumor resections from immunotherapy-naïve advanced melanoma patients who had not received any prior immunotherapy before resection but later underwent adjuvant immunotherapy to model advanced (stage 3-4) melanoma. These three patients exhibited different clinical outcomes: two did not respond to treatment (non-responders, referred to naïve-to-non-responder), while one showed no evidence of recurrence (NED, referred to naïve-to-ned) (Table 1, patient #4-6).

We began by characterizing and evaluating the phenotype of these three resected melanoma patient tumor samples used to develop the autologous humanized model. Using the Akoya Opal multiplex immunofluorescence system, we found that CD8+ T cells had infiltrated the TME alongside SOX10+ melanoma tumor cells in the resected tumors (Fig. S1B). These infiltrating CD8+ T cells expressed the KI-67 proliferating marker (Fig. S1B), suggesting active proliferation of T cells in the TME.

Further, we performed T cell phenotyping in the peripheral blood of these melanoma patients using flow cytometry. Regardless of clinical outcomes, we found no significant differences in the average percentages of CD45+ (97.4±0.5% of live cells), CD3+ (51.8±1.3% of CD45+ cells), CD4+ (51.0±8.8% of CD3+ cells), and CD8+ (40.0±5.5% of CD3+ cells) T cells (Fig. S1C). Both naïve-to-non-responders and naïve-to-ned patients had similar distributions of CD4 and CD8 T cell subsets (Fig. S1D-G). Notably, CD45RA+CCR7+CD28+CD95- naïve T cells (Tnaïve) constituted the highest mean percentages (CD8, 86.5±2.6%; CD4, 94.7±0.4%), followed by CD45RA-CCR7+CD28+CD95+ TCM: (CD8, 19.9±1.5%; CD4, 25.1±12.6%), CD45RA+CCR7+CD28+CD95+ TSCM (CD8, 2.2±0.6%; CD4, 5.3±0.4%), and CD45RA-CCR7-CD28-CD95+ TEM (CD8, 1.1±0.5%; CD4, 0.021±0.02%). Additionally, all three samples expressed CD127 in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, with naïve-to-non-responders showing higher levels than naïve-to-ned, although the difference was not significant (Fig. S1H). We also assessed the expression of inhibitory checkpoint proteins, including TIM3 and PD-1, on CD8+ T cells. On average, both naïve-to-non-responders and the naïve-to-ned sample displayed similar levels of PD-1 and TIM3, except for one naïve-to-non-responder who exhibited lower TIM3 expression (Fig. S1I).

After adoptive transfer of matched unexpanded PBMC from a melanoma patient, tumor-bearing mice received four different treatment modalities: (i) anti-PD-1 monotherapy, (ii) NT-I7 monotherapy, (iii) combination therapy of anti-PD-1 and NT-I7, and (iv) control vehicle. The mice received four cycles of anti-PD1 (10 mg/kg) and two cycles of NT-I7 (10 mg/kg) treatments. Treatment efficacy and flow cytometry analysis were assessed on days 32 and 39. In this study, successful PBMC engraftment was defined as having more than 500 CD45+ viable cells, as quantified by flow cytometry. In samples from the naïve-to-non-responder melanoma patients, mice treated with NT-I7 (100%) and a combination of NT-I7 and anti-PD-1 (93%) treatments presented with the highest engraftment rates compared to the anti-PD-1 and control groups (Fig. 3B). Similarly, in the sample from naïve-to-ned melanoma patient, both NT-I7 monotherapy (73%) and a combination of NT-I7 and anti-PD-1 (40%) exhibited the highest engraftment rates (Fig. 3B), albeit at lower percentage compared to that of naïve-to-non-responder. Interestingly, only 13% of anti-PD-1-treated mice displayed CD45 engraftment in both naïve-to-non-responder and naïve-to-ned (Fig. 3B). It is important to note that NT-I7, whether administered alone or in combination with anti-PD-1, greatly enhanced CD45 engraftment rate.

Overall, we observed a significant enlargement of spleen size in mice treated with NT-I7 and the combination therapy of NT-I7 and anti-PD-1, while no enlargement was noted in the anti-PD-1 and control groups (Fig. 3C). Corroborating with engraftment rates and spleen enlargement, both NT-I7 and combination therapy NT-I7 and anti-PD- groups had a significantly higher number of CD45+ cells in the spleen (Fig. 3D).

Remarkably, combination therapy of NT-I7 and anti-PD-1 significantly promoted anti-tumor efficacy in naïve-to-non-responders, surpassing the efficacy previously demonstrated by NT-I7 monotherapy (Fig. 3E, left panel). When examined on an individual patient basis, each naïve-to-non-responder patient displayed a distinct efficacy profile, indicating interpatient heterogeneity (Fig. 3E, middle and right panels). In patient 4, the combination therapy showed the greatest anti-tumor efficacy compared to that of all other treatment groups. Although both anti-PD-1 and NT-I7 monotherapies projected significant tumor inhibition compared to the control group, the difference was relatively narrow in comparison to the combination therapy of NT-I7 and anti-PD-1 (Fig. 3E, middle panel). In patient 5, NT-I7 monotherapy and combination therapy of NT-I7 and anti-PD-1 exhibited similar anti-tumor efficacy, both significantly higher than that of anti-PD-1 monotherapy and the control group (Fig. 3E, right panel).

In contrast, the naïve-to-ned melanoma patient displayed a delayed response beginning on day 37. While the effect was not as pronounced as in the naïve-to-non-responders, NT-I7 monotherapy and the combination therapy of NT-I7 and anti-PD-1 appeared to yield the best efficacy (Fig. 3F). Of note, this is the only preclinical model to date that potentially recapitulates the actual clinical outcomes of these patients to ICI immunotherapy.

In sum, the combination treatment of NT-I7 and anti-PD-1 was significantly more efficacious in controlling tumor progression (Fig. 3E-F). These findings suggest a synergistic effect between NT-I7 and PD-1 blockade in suppressing tumor progression. Additionally, our humanized model also exhibited interpatient heterogeneity, which is a crucial factor to consider in the preclinical model development for testing novel immunotherapeutic agents.

We further characterized the CD45-engrafted cells in spleen by flow cytometry. Independent of treatment and clinical outcome, CD4+ T cells were more prevalent than CD8+ T cells (Fig. 3G-H), especially in naïve-to-ned patients (Fig. 3H). Fascinatingly, we discovered a major shift towards CD8+ T cell enrichment following NT-I7 treatment in naïve-to-non-responders (Fig. 3G). Moreover, NT-I7 worked synergistically with anti-PD-1 to nearly double the proportion of CD8+ T cells (40.6%) compared to anti-PD-1 monotherapy (18.7%). In the naïve-to-ned sample, the combination of NT-I7 and anti-PD-1 also increased CD8+ T cell expansion relative to anti-PD-1 monotherapy, although to a lesser extent than observed in the naïve-to-non-responders (Fig. 3H). These findings suggest that NT-I7 may play a role in promoting T cell expansion, elevating cytotoxic activity, and impeding cancer progression.

Immune cell phenotyping in an autologous tumor-reactive ex vivo co-culture

Alongside the characterization of melanoma patient samples, we further explored how NT-I7 promotes improved anti-tumor efficacy. Numerous studies have indicated that T cell states influence anti-tumor immunity36–38, leading us to posit that NT-I7 modulates T cell phenotypes to ameliorate treatment efficacy.

To elucidate the impact of NT-I7 on tumor-reactive T cells, we conducted autologous ex vivo co-cultures of melanoma tumor cells with autologous PBMC treated with either NT-I7 or recombinant human IL-7 (rhIL-7). After seven days of co-culture, these PBMC were subjected to multiplex flow cytometry and UMAP analyses for immune cell phenotyping. The UMAP analysis revealed a total of 20 clusters when all samples were analyzed collectively (Fig. 4A, Fig. S2A-B). Violin plots illustrated the protein expression of selected immune markers across all clusters (Fig. 4C), while the heatmap plot demonstrated the immune cell protein expressions for each cluster (Fig.S2C). When pooling all samples, a large proportion of the clusters consisted of CD45+ cells (Fig. 4B, Fig.S2C). Within these, just over half of the CD45+ clusters expressed CD3 T cell marker, with a higher number of CD4+ clusters compared to CD8+ clusters (Fig. 4B, Fig. S2C). Clusters 0, 1, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, and 15 had high levels of KI-67, indicating the presence of actively proliferating cells in these clusters (Fig.6D).

Novel agents that promote T cell proliferation are a promising strategy to boost the effectiveness of immunotherapy. Compared to the rhIL-7 and control groups, as well as other clusters, there was obvious enrichment of proliferation marker KI-67 in the NT-I7 treatment group, with all clusters (clusters 0, 6, 8, 9, 10) exhibiting high KI-67 levels (Fig. 6D, Fig. S3C). This evidence underscores the role of NT-I7 in promoting T cell proliferation.

Of note, CD4+ T cells were preferentially expanded in NT-I7 treatment group (clusters 0, 6, 8 and 10), with only cluster 9 enriched with CD8+ T cells. In contrast, both rhIL-7 and control groups exhibited equal enrichment of CD4+ T cells (rhIL-7: clusters 3, 17; control: cluster 14) and CD8+ T cells (rhIL-7: clusters 12, 13; control: cluster 15) (Fig. 7B). Therefore, all treatment groups expand both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. However, our findings indicate that NT-I7 treatment favored expansion of unique CD4+ T cell clusters compared to the rhIL-7 and control groups. The other non-treatment-specific clusters also showed increased CD4+ T cell expansion (clusters 1, 2, 4, 5, 7, 11), while clusters 16 and 18 displayed enrichment of CD8+ T cells (Fig. S3D).

Both NT-I7 and rhIL-7 were effective in inducing TCM in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells; however, NT-I7 demonstrated a stronger preference for promoting TCM compared to rhIL-7 treatment (Fig. 7B). Notably, all clusters are associated with NT-I7 treatment (clusters 0. 6, 9, and 10) exhibited a predominance of TCM states, particularly in CD4+ TCM (clusters 0,6, 8, and 10), with cluster 8 containing a mix of Tnaïve and TCM cell markers (Fig. 7B). On the contrary, rhIL-7 only had one TCM cluster for both CD4+ (cluster 3) and CD8+ (cluster 12) T cells, each mixed with Tnaïve, while other clusters were primarily Tnaïve (cluster 17) or combinations of TEM and Teff cells (cluster 13) (Fig. 7B). In the control group, one cluster was CD4+ TCM (cluster 14) and the other was Texh (cluster 15) (Fig. 7B). Other non-treatment-specific clusters also displayed TCM cell states, but to a lesser extent: 66.7% of CD4+ T cell clusters (4 out of 6) and 50% of CD8+ T cell clusters (1 out of 2). The remaining CD4+ T cell clusters induced TEM (cluster 5) and a mix of TCM and Treg cells (cluster 1), while the CD8+ T cell cluster was primarily Teff (cluster 18) (Fig. S3D). In sum, IL-7-based treatment enhanced the TCM state, with NT-I7 demonstrating a greater capacity to enhance CD4+ TCM prevalence.

Collectively, our findings indicate that NT-I7 promotes proliferative and TCM state in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells. These properties of proliferation and central memory are recognized as vital factors associated with the efficacy of immunotherapy39–44.

NT-I7 induces high durable and sustainable cytotoxic property

The effectiveness of immunotherapy and anti-tumor immunity largely relies on the cytotoxic activity and characteristics of T cells. To explore this further, we conducted a multiplex cytokine secretome analysis using Isoplexis, a single cell proteomics platform, to examine the cytokine profiles. Through this multiplex cytokine proteomics analysis, we aimed to unravel how NT-I7 and rhIL-7 modulate extracellular cytokine profiles during both an initial phase (6 hours) and the prolonged phase (7 days), using supernatant collected from the co-culture model described above.

High cytotoxicity is known to drive positive outcomes in cancer immunotherapy. Thus, we focused on measuring the secretion of cytotoxic molecules, including perforin and granzyme B (GZMB). At the 6-hour time point, none of the treatment groups showed an increase in perforin levels. However, by day 7, all treatment groups displayed elevated perforin levels, with the NT-I7-treated group exhibiting the most significant increase (Fig. 7C). Comparably, GZMB secretion was minimal at 6-hour across all treatment groups but rose by day 7. Although the increase did not reach statistical significance, NT-I7 treatment tended to induce a higher GZMB level compared to the rhIL-7 and control groups (Fig. 7D). The notable elevation in cytotoxic molecules following NT-I7 treatment suggests that NT-I7 enhances cytotoxic function. In summary, NT-I7 promotes durable and sustainable cytotoxic functionality, which is crucial for improving anti-tumor immunity and immunotherapy efficacy.

Discussion

The field of immunotherapy has been actively pursuing the development of a mouse avatar that accurately models the clinical setting to enhance the precision of drug testing32–34. We successfully developed an all-autologous melanoma humanized mouse model that captures patient heterogeneity, and importantly, closely mimics actual clinical response. Leveraging on this model, we showed that NT-I7 works synergistically with anti-PD-1 to boost anti-tumor efficacy. Consistent with clinical outcome, anti-PD-1 alone did not prevent tumor progression. This autologous humanized model serves as an effective and suitable model for evaluating new immunotherapy agents.

Memory T cells have superior proliferation, function, and longevity than effector cells18–22, with TCM cells providing more durable anti-tumor immunity than effector memory or terminally differentiated effector cells25,45,46. Our comprehensive multiplex flow cytometry assay revealed that IL-7-based treatments (NT-I7 and rhIL-7) selectively expand T cells and enhance TCM state, aligning with the known role of IL-7 in maintaining memory-like T cells15–17. While NK cells may contribute to anti-tumor effects, their activity is not notably enhanced by NT-I7, suggesting MHC I–independent killing is not its primary mechanism. Notably, NT-I7 preferentially promotes central memory differentiation in both CD4+ and CD8+ T cells, particularly CD4+ TCM cells, which are vital for sustained anti-tumor immunity. CD4+ and CD8+ T cells play complementary roles in anti-tumor immunity, especially under immune checkpoint blockade and cytokine-based therapies. While CD8+ T cells serve as key cytotoxic effectors, CD4+ T cells support their function and persistence41. NT-I7, a long-acting IL-7 cytokine, enhances the survival, expansion, and tumor infiltration of both subsets, boosting anti-tumor activity. In therapy-resistant patients, NT-I7 combined with anti-PD-1 equally increased CD8+ and CD4+ T cells, suggesting a synergistic enhancement of anti-tumor immunity through coordinated activation of both subsets, crucial for optimal therapeutic outcomes. Given NT-I7’s strong tendency to promote central memory differentiation in both CD4 and CD8 T cells, NT-I7 could confer superior anti-tumor activity of PD-1 blockade by generating TCM cells that support sustained antigen-specific responses, corroborating findings from published studies20–22,25,41,45.

Although cytotoxic CD8+ T cells have traditionally been associated with favorable immunotherapy outcomes, emerging evidence highlights the pivotal role of CD4+ T cells anti-tumor immunity20,22,25,40–44,47. CD4+ T cells contribute to tumor control through multiple mechanisms, including supporting CD8+ effector cell function, and modulating the TME via cytokine secretion40,44. This synergy between CD4+ and CD8+ T cells proven to be more efficient in rendering anti-tumor response than CD8+ T cells alone40,44. In glioblastoma, for example, anti-PD-1 efficacy partly depends on CD4+ T cells in modulating CD8+ T cell exhaustion and sustaining their effector function43. These findings underscore the need to consider both CD4+ and CD8+ T cell dynamics when evaluating immunotherapeutic strategies.

Our data reveal that NT-I7 preferentially expands TCM cells, especially multifaceted CD4+ TCM. Given the crucial role of CD4+ T cells in enhancing anti-tumor response and mitigating CD8+ T cell exhaustion, NT-I7 may incite better immunotherapy outcomes by enriching both CD8+ and CD4+ TCM. In line with Ding et al48, memory CD4+ T cells can activate CD8+ T cells to inhibit tumor progression in vivo. Furthermore, our observation of CD4+ T cell enrichment aligns with recent studies demonstrating that NT-I7 significantly increases CD4+ T cells in a Phase I clinical trial of NT-I7 and in CD4+ chimeric antigen receptor (CAR)-T xenograft models31,49,50. Collectively, NT-I7 holds promise for enhancing treatment responses by sustaining CD4+ T cell expansion.

TCM cells can rapidly produce various effector molecules, including cytotoxic molecules like GZMB and perforin, upon antigen stimulation, enabling swift immune responses51. Besides substantiating that TCM cells preferentially differentiate into potent effector T cells with elevated cytotoxic molecules like perforin and GZMB, our findings also highlight NT-I7’s contribution in sustaining durable cytotoxic activities. This indicates that NT-I7 enhances anti-tumor immunity by fostering cytotoxic cytokine production and maintaining long-lasting durability. Consistent with our prior CAR-T study in leukemia, activated CD4+ T cells exhibited persistent cytotoxicity following treatment42. Therefore, our data infer that NT-I7 enhances immune responses by promoting CD4+ T cell expansion, which capable of predominantly differentiating into long-lasting, cytotoxic effector cells upon antigen re-encounter.

In conclusion, our findings indicate that NT-I7 preferentially enhances the expansion and survival of early differentiated CD4+ and CD8+ TCM, potentially boosting cytotoxic activities and augmenting greater immunotherapeutic response in refractory melanoma patients. Beyond promoting TCM expansion, NT-I7 also elevates cytotoxic function, resulting in improved immunotherapeutic efficacy. Further studies involving spatialomics and mechanistic functions are warranted to better decipher the underlying mechanism and signaling networks involved, paving the way for more versatile and optimized treatment modalities. To better understand CD4+ and CD8+ T cell responses, TCR sequencing is essential for uncovering clonal expansion and antigen specificity, offering mechanistic insight into the immune subsets driving anti-tumor efficacy. Further preclinical studies are needed to clarify the roles of NK cells and CD4 and CD8 T cell subsets in NT-I7-mediated efficacy. Although there is no direct evidence, NT-I7 may promote tertiary lymphoid structure (TLS) formation through memory T cell expansion, increased CCL19, and enhanced T cell infiltration, features associated with TLS development52,53. Since TLS presence is associated with improved checkpoint inhibitor responses in melanoma54,55, further investigation of TLS in NT-I7 therapy is warranted.

Materials and methods

Mice. NOD-Scid IL-2 receptor-γ null (NOD.cg-PrkdcscidIl2rgtm1Wjl/SzJ), male NSG mice (Stock No. 05557), 6-8 weeks, were purchased from The Jackson Laboratory (Bar Harbor, ME). NSG mice were also obtained from an in-house breeding colony. Animals were housed in a pathogen-free barrier facility in the Biological Resources Unit of the Cleveland Clinic Lerner Research Institute under a protocol approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC) in accordance with NIH Office of Laboratory Animal Welfare guidelines.

Patient specimen processing. Matched blood and tumor specimens were obtained from melanoma patients at time of surgery. Blood was obtained by venipuncture and PBMC were isolated by density gradient centrifugation using LymphoprepTM according to manufacturer’s protocol (Stemcell Technologies, Canada) and cryopreserved until further analysis or use. Surgical resected tumors were rinsed with antibiotic-containing media and minced with scalpels under sterile conditions. Minced tumors were dissociated using 1500 U/ml collagenase IV (Gibco/Life Technologies), 1000 U/ml hyaluronidase (Sigma), and 0.05 mu/ml DNAse IV (Gibco) in RPMI for 30–60 min at 37°C. The resulting single-cell suspensions of tumor cells were cryopreserved until further analysis.

Patient-derived xenograft. Tumor cells (5–10x106 cells) were used to establish melanoma patient-derived xenografts (PDXs) by subcutaneous injection into flank of NSG mice in 1:1 Matrigel (Corning, Inc.). After outgrowth of the primary tumor, the tumor was harvested, digested in the same collagenase/hyaluronidase solution described hereafter, and injected subcutaneously into flank of NSG mice as described above for not more than three generations.

Autologous humanized mouse model. For the autologous humanized model generation, 1x106 digested PDX tumor cells were implanted subcutaneously in 100 µl Matrigel at a 1:1 ratio into the flank of NSG mice. On day seven, 1-2x106 autologous PBMC in 100 µl sterile saline were injected intravenously into mice. The next day, drugs were administered according to stipulated dosage regimen for each experiment either intraperitoneally (IP) or subcutaneously (SC) based on mouse body weight. Tumor diameter was measured using caliper every third day for the duration of the experiment. Each treatment group consisted of five mice unless stated otherwise. All experiments were concluded at study endpoints or when vehicle-group tumor size or dimension exceeded humane endpoints (20 mm). All interventions were performed without the use of anesthesia, and all mice were euthanized using a 30-70% per minute displacement of chamber air with compressed carbon dioxide followed by cervical dislocation, in compliance with IACUC guidelines. In addition, all experiments were conducted in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines (https://arriveguidelines.org) to ensure comprehensive and transparent reporting of both methods and results.

For Fig. 1(a), NT-I7 (10 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally injected on days 1 and 8 (Group A), and days 1 and 15 (Group B). For Fig. 1(b), NT-I7 (10 mg/kg) was intraperitoneally injected on days 1 and 8.

For Fig. 2, NT-I7 (10 mg/kg) was subcutaneously injected on days 1 and 8.

For Fig. 3, a total of four cycles of treatment (1 week/cycle) were administered for each group subcutaneously. NT-I7 (10 mg/kg) was injected on day 1 (cycle 1) and day 15 (cycle 3). Anti-PD-1 (10 mg/kg) was injected twice weekly for every cycle.

Mouse tissue processing. At study endpoint, mouse tumors, spleens, and blood were extracted from each mouse. Mouse spleens were dissociated by pressing through a 40 μM cell strainer followed by red blood cell (RBC) lysis (Biolegend, #420302), and splenocytes were used for analyses or cryopreserved. Blood was extracted via cardiac puncture, followed by RBC lysis, centrifugation, and resuspension with appropriate buffers for further procedures. Tumor-infiltrating lymphocytes (TILs) were isolated by disruption of tumor sample in the same manner as human patient tumor described above. After digestion, single cell suspension was subjected to density gradient centrifugation to obtain TILs for downstream assays or cryopreserved.

Multiplex flow cytometry analysis. Fluorescence-conjugated antibodies for flow cytometry analysis were stipulated in Supplementary Table S1. Dead cells were excluded using viability dye (Supp. Table S1). All antibody staining was performed in phosphate-buffered saline with 0.2% bovine serum albumin. Flow cytometry analysis was performed, and data were collected on Fortessa LSR II (BD Biosciences) and Cytek Aurora (Cytek Biosciences) instruments. Data were analyzed using FlowJo software (FlowJo Inc, Salem OR). Quality checked was performed using FlowAI prior to analysis. High-dimensional multiplex immunophenotyping and flow cytometric characterization of autologous PBMC/tumor co-culture were performed using Flowjo plugins, including Uniform Manifold Approximation and Projection (UMAP, v3.1), clustering and heatmap (FlowSOM, v3.0.18), and violin plots (ViolinBox, v5.1.11).

Autologous co-culture assay. Tumor cells were seeded into a 96-well plate and followed by 50 Gy irradiation. Next, autologous PBMC were co-cultured with irradiated-tumor cells at 1:1 ratio. The cells were treated with NT-I7 (100 ng/ml) or rhIL-7 (100 ng/ml). Non-treated cells served as controls. Supernatant were collected at six hours and after seven days for multiplex cytokine secretome assay. After seven days of co-culture, treated cells were collected and subjected to multiplex flow cytometry analysis.

Highly-multiplexed cytokine secretome analysis. Supernatant collected at six hours and after seven days from co-culture assay were subjected to multiplex cytokine secretome immunoassay. Cytokine secretome was determined using Codeplex chip and Isolight instrument (Isoplexis). The assay was conducted according to manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, an aliquot of 5.5 µl of each sample (in duplicate) was loaded into each macrochamber of Codeplex chip. Next, the chip was loaded into an Isolight automation system. Secreted cytokine expression was recorded, and data analysis of IsoPlexis Human Adaptive Immune panel was performed by Isospeak software.

Clinical data. A total of seven advanced melanoma patient specimens were used in this study: three male and four female with an overall median age of 60 years old. All melanoma patient specimens were obtained from treatment-naïve patients who had not received any immunotherapy (patient #2-6), except for patient #1, who had previously undergone immunotherapy (refer Table 1). Specimens from non-responding melanoma patients were used in Fig. 1(a). We progressively follow up on the clinical response of naïve patients. The current follow-up response of all patients, as of April 2023, was tabulated in Table 1.

Statistics. Means of all groups were compared for statistical differences by a One-Way Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) unless stated otherwise. A Bonferroni t test was then used to understand the statistical difference between two groups when more than two groups were compared. Data was presented as means±SEM. Significance levels were set to p<0.05.

Study and ethics approvals. All human specimen and tissue were obtained at the Cleveland Clinic under a protocol approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB #3164) with written informed consent obtained from each patient. All animal studies were performed in accordance with the guidelines of and approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee (IACUC #2270) at the Cleveland Clinic.
