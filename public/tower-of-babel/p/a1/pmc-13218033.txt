Application of the PDCA cycle management in quality improvement of endotracheal intubation in emergency department among standardised training residents
Authors: Zhanpeng Tan, Fang Lai, Xiaoya Li, Deyang Li, Hui Li, Shiyi Liu, Suyi Yang, Chunjiang Yan, Guowei Li, Fengxian He, Ruifeng Zeng, Huanmei Li
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEndotracheal intubation (ETI) stands as a cornerstone intervention within the emergency department, yet its efficacy can be undermined by a myriad of variables. For patients teetering on the brink of critical illness, securing the endotracheal tube during the initial attempt is not merely preferable but imperative. Consequently, elevating the first-pass success (FPS) rate achieved by emergency physicians becomes a vital strategy to curtail adverse outcomes and safeguard patient stability. The plan-do-check-act (PDCA) cycle, also known as the “quality loop”, is a management approach for continuous improvement. This study aimed to improve the FPS rate of ETI performed by standardised training residents(STRs) in the emergency department using the PDCA cycle management approach. It also sought to increase the difficult airway identification success rate while reducing the multiple-attempts rate and overall complications.MethodsThis is a single-center, retrospective before-and-after study. We collected and compared data from STRs rotating through the emergency department before (January 2021 - December 2022, control group, n = 36) and after (January 2023 - December 2024, experimental group, n = 44) implementing the PDCA cycle. The PDCA plan included:Plan: Set goals for standardized operations, skill advancement, and refined complication prevention; establish key indicators including FPS rate, multiple-attempts rate, difficult airway identification success rate, and overall complication rate.Do: Implement a tiered training system (admission system + graded practice), standardized operating procedures (SOP), and comprehensive quality control (use of video laryngoscopy, difficult airway protocols).Check: Monitor how well the procedures were followed in real-time (completeness of steps, correctness of equipment use).Act: Keep improving training based on feedback from the data.ResultsCompared to before PDCA implementation, the FPS rate of the experimental group increased significantly from 55.6% (control group, 20/36) to 86.4% (38/44) (P < 0.01). The rate of complications included esophageal intubation (6.8% vs. 27.8%) and laryngeal edema/vocal cord injury (4.5% vs. 30.5%), both of which decreased significantly compared to the control group, which was statistically significant (P < 0.01). The difficult airway identification success rate between these two groups were 30.8% and 92.9%, respectively, with statistical significance (P < 0.01). Results by experience level showed that the FPS rate for third-year STRs increased from 50.0% to 85.2%, while the FPS rate for second-year STRs increased from 62.5% to 88.2%.ConclusionThe PDCA cycle management significantly improves the FPS rate of ETI and contributed to operational safety through a tiered training system, standardized procedures, and dynamic quality control. This approach provides a reference model for developing technical talent in emergency medicine and holds potential value in managing airways of critically ill patients.Keywords: Plan-do-check-act cycle, Standardized training residents, Endotracheal intubation, First-pass success rate, Emergency department

Endotracheal intubation (ETI) is an essential and life-saving procedure performed in the emergency department, as it plays a crucial role in securing and maintaining a patient’s airway, ensuring that it remains unobstructed and clear, thereby facilitating effective ventilation and oxygenation during critical medical situations. The success or failure of this procedure has a direct impact on patient safety, especially for critically ill patients. Several factors, such as the patient’s anatomy and physiology, can increase the difficulty of intubation and reduce its success rate [1]. For critically ill patients, achieving successful placement of the endotracheal tube on the first intubation attempt is crucial. Research has shown that a successful initial attempt can significantly reduce the likelihood of adverse events; conversely, as the number of attempts increases, the risk of complications rises considerably [2]. Therefore, improving the first-pass success (FPS) rate of ETI by emergency physicians is essential for minimising complications.

Technology for ETI is continually evolving. Advances like video laryngoscopes, bougie-assisted intubation, and ultrasound guidance have improved the safety and accuracy of intubation in specific scenarios [3–5]. Consequently, emergency physicians must be proficient in various techniques and select the most suitable tool or method based on each patient’s unique situation to optimise success rates and minimise risks.

To enhance the success rate and safety of ETI, many emergency departments have implemented quality improvement projects. These initiatives include standardised operating procedures, enhanced airway management training, and improved preparation of equipment. As a result, the FPS rate for ETI has significantly increased, while the frequency of multiple attempts and overall complications has decreased [6]. ETI is particularly challenging for standardised training residents(STRs), as it requires a solid theoretical foundation and ample practical experience. Studies have indicated that the overall success rate for ETI performed by STRs using video laryngoscopes is higher compared to that using traditional direct laryngoscopes, with fewer complications reported [1]. Therefore, it is crucial for STRs to enhance their skills through systematic training and repeated practice to ensure that the FPS rate continues to improve.

The PDCA cycle (Plan-Do-Check-Act) is a widely used quality management tool that promotes continuous improvement of work processes and their results. This cycle proves effective in various fields, including healthcare, education, and manufacturing.

In healthcare, the PDCA cycle has been successfully implemented in several ways. It has standardised nursing management in intensive care units, improving both nursing quality and patient survival rates [7]. It has also optimised the management of cancer pain medications to enhance medication adherence and pain control [8]. Additionally, pharmacist-led PDCA initiatives have reduced prescription abandonment rates [9], and integrating risk factor management has lowered operating room infection rates while improving nursing quality [10]. In education, the PDCA cycle has been applied to manage postgraduate medical education, leading to improved quality by optimising online teaching methods and strengthening public health knowledge training [11]. Overall, the PDCA cycle is a flexible and effective management tool that can enhance efficiency and quality through iterative optimisation across different fields.

Building on these applications, this study utilised the PDCA cycle to enhance the training and quality management of ETI for STRs in the emergency department. It systematically analysed the causes of intubation failure and developed improvement measures for each step of the process. Through continuous monitoring and reinforcement of these measures, the study achieved a significant increase in the FPS rate among STRs and also lowered the incidence of complications. The key findings of the research are summarised as follows.

Method

This single-center, retrospective before-and-after investigation leveraged the PDCA cycle to engineer a comprehensive training and quality assurance framework for ETI (Fig. 1). We compared the outcomes of STRs who rotated through the emergency department after PDCA implementation, from January 2023 to December 2024, in the experimental group (n = 44), with those of STRs who underwent traditional training before PDCA implementation, from January 2021 to December 2022, in the control group (n = 36). We analysed differences between the two groups in several outcomes: FPS rate of ETI, multiple-attempts rate, overall ETI success rate, difficult airway identification success rate, and overall complication rate. All data and results were recorded based on the attending physician’s real-time documentation and subsequent postoperative medical record reviews to ensure accuracy and objectivity. The unit of analysis is the ETI attempt. Each case represents a distinct ETI procedure. Multiple procedures could be performed by the same resident during their rotation.

Airway Assessment and Decision-Making Ability [15]

Difficult Airway Identification: The process of recognising challenging airway situations through physical examinations or ultrasound assessments of glottic position and laryngeal anatomical variations.

A difficult airway is characterized by an intubation that is not achieved through standard means, that is defined by the following characteristics: presence of facial trauma and a small mandible; lung stiffness, which may include pulmonary oedema and limited mouth opening (less than 3 cm); obesity; a Mallampati classification greater than 3; airway obstruction, such as airway oedema or a history of tracheal stenosis, short neck and cervical immobilisation.

Literature review:

Literature review:

The FPS of ETI in the emergency department ranges from 80.1% to 86.8% [16–18].

For difficult airways, the FPS rate of video laryngoscopes, such as the GlideScope, is between 78% and 91% [19, 20]. In contrast, the success rate for using a Frova intubation tube in difficult airway cases is 88% [21].

The overall ETI success rate in the emergency department is 96.8% [22].

Complications associated with ETI include immediate injuries such as tooth damage, which is more common with direct laryngoscopy [23] and mucosal bleeding, as well as a high risk of aspiration in the emergency setting [24]. Delayed complications can involve laryngeal oedema (which can lead to dyspnea) [25], and vocal cord injuries (which can cause dysphonia) [26, 27].

D——Do

Tiered Training:

Tiered Training:

Curriculum Structure: The training program comprises two main components: theoretical training and simulation training, with both delivered monthly to facilitate continuous learning and skill refinement.

Theoretical Training: One theoretical course is delivered monthly. The course covers airway anatomy, principles of equipment operation, and strategies to prevent and treat complications, thereby equipping trainees with foundational knowledge essential for clinical practice.

Simulation Training: High-fidelity simulators (Laerdal SimMan) are utilized for monthly scenario drills to enhance skills in managing difficult airways, particularly in scenarios characterized by obesity and cervical spine fixation, thereby improving practical proficiency and decision-making under pressure.

Faculty Supervision: Each training session is overseen by experienced faculty members who provide real-time feedback and guidance, ensuring adherence to best practices and safety protocols.

(2)Access System and Clinical Monitoring:

Access System and Clinical Monitoring:

Curriculum Structure: The access system and clinical monitoring phase are structured around progressive skill acquisition and assessment, ensuring that STRs are adequately prepared before independent clinical practice.

Learning Objectives: The primary objectives are to develop proficiency in ETI techniques, enhance decision-making skills under pressure, and ensure patient safety through supervised practice.

Simulation Assessment for STRs: STRs must pass three separate assessments, each requiring a success rate of 90% or higher, completion within 5 min, and the occurrence of no more than one complication per assessment before engaging in clinical operations. Assessment methods include standardized scenarios, timed tasks, and complication tracking, all designed to evaluate both technical skill and clinical judgment.

Graded Practice:

First Assistant: Under the direct supervision of a faculty member, trainees participate in 5 intubations, where they are responsible for pre-oxygenation and equipment preparation. This role aims to familiarize trainees with the procedural workflow and the importance of pre-procedure readiness, aligning with the competency-based training framework’s emphasis on foundational skill development.

Main Operator: After being evaluated by a senior physician and achieving a total score of at least 80 points on the “Operation Score Sheet”, STRs may operate independently. However, the first three cases must be completed under the supervision of a senior physician. Supervision involves real-time feedback, observation of technique, and the assurance of adherence to safety protocols, reflecting an adult learning theory approach that emphasizes experiential learning and mentorship. This graded practice model, progressing from assisted to independent practice, is grounded in the competency-based training framework, ensuring that trainees achieve and demonstrate the necessary skills before advancing.

(3)Resource Optimisation:

Resource Optimisation:

Equipment Maintenance and Upgrades: Calibrate laryngoscope blades weekly and incorporate video laryngoscopes.

Teaching Tools: Develop an “Emergency Intubation Process Guidance Card” and create a video library featuring examples of typical errors encountered during both simulated and real operations.

C——Check

Process Execution Monitoring:

Process Execution Monitoring:

Curriculum Structure and Assessment Methods: The instructor uses the “Endotracheal Intubation Operation Monitoring Form” to record each operation immediately as it occurs. The monitoring indicators include operator and assistant qualifications, the completeness of each step, and equipment usage standardisation. Additional metrics assessed are the FPS rate, the overall ETI success rate, the multiple attempts rate, the difficult airway identification success rate, and the overall rate of complications.

These metrics are aligned with competency-based training frameworks, focusing on measurable outcomes that reflect skill proficiency and clinical decision-making.

(2)Data Feedback Mechanism:

Data Feedback Mechanism:

Faculty Supervision and Link to Educational Theory: During weekly meetings, monitoring data is summarised, and common issues are reviewed and discussed. This faculty supervision ensures that feedback is constructive, evidence-based, and tailored to individual trainee needs, adhering to adult learning theory principles that emphasize active engagement and self-directed learning. For example, one high-frequency problem identified is that “laryngoscope insertion too deep, causing bleeding,” which accounts for 20% of complications. Six months after implementation, a mid-term evaluation revealed that the FPS rate had increased to 80%. It was also found that 80% of cases that did not meet the clinical standard had failed to perform an airway assessment. Based on these findings, in line with the competency-based training framework’s iterative improvement approach, the training focus will be adjusted, emphasising the airway assessment module.

Statistical analysis

SPSS 17.0 software was utilised for statistical analysis. Counting data are reported as frequency and percentage (n, %). For measures that can be described with a confidence interval, 95% confidence intervals (95% CI) were calculated to provide an estimate of the range within which the true population parameter is likely to fall. For comparisons, the Chi-square test was utilised; however, when actual frequencies were low, Fisher’s exact probability method was applied, with an inspection level of α = 0.05.

Discussion

Practical effects of the PDCA cycle on ETI training for emergency STRs

This study applied the PDCA cycle management method to establish quality improvement goals. These goals focused on standardised operations, skill advancement, and effective management of complications. A structured training system was developed through a systematic literature review and analysis of clinical practices, incorporating an admission system and graded practice alongside standardised operating procedures (SOPs) and quality monitoring indicators to create a dynamic quality control loop. The results show that the FPS rate of ETI among STRs increased from a baseline of 55.6% to 86.4% (P < 0.05); the difficult airway identification success rate improved by more than 60%; and the complication occurrence rate decreased to 15.9%.

The FPS rate (86.4%) in this study is higher than that reported in similar quality improvement studies [6]. This improvement correlates with several advantages, including: (1) a step-by-step training system—a three-level model comprising admission assessment, graded practice, and supervised operation—that ensures skills meet standards before clinical practice; and (2) a Dynamic quality control mechanism involving real-time monitoring of operational compliance and ongoing process improvements during weekly meetings.

Standardized training and comprehensive quality control enable STRs to develop independent airway management capabilities while optimising the allocation of teaching resources. Moreover, the SOP and quality control system cultivated STRs’ clinical thinking process of “assessment - contingency plan - execution - review,” providing a model that can serve as a reference for building specialized technical teams.

Key factors for improving ETI skills of STRs

ETI is a critical emergency procedure, and mastering this skill is vital for ensuring patient safety. Early in their training, STRs often experience a low success rate with intubation due to limited clinical practice [38, 39]. However, simulation training allows repeated practice in a risk-free environment, which significantly enhances technical skills and reduces complication risks [40, 41].

A teaching model that focuses on tiered training and job competency, and emphasises repeated practice and immediate feedback, is more effective than traditional methods [42]. Despite this, the overall success rate of STRs when managing difficult airways remains suboptimal [43]. This indicates that current training programs must better enforce standard procedures and enhance trainees’ ability to manage complex scenarios during basic skills training.

Clinical value of standardised procedures and equipment specifications

Necessity of process standardisation

Lack of standardised procedures significantly contributes to emergency intubation failures. Standardised procedures clarify essential steps and protocols, reducing operational uncertainty. Studies demonstrated that implementing difficult airway management guidelines notably decreases the rate of intubation failures [34, 35]. Additionally, intubation in non-standard positions, such as lateral or semi-recumbent positions, requires specialised training to ensure effectiveness in emergencies [44]. Maintaining the integrity of each step is crucial. For example, adjusting positions and controlling the angle of the laryngoscope are important; these actions directly influence the success rate of intubations [45].

Standardised equipment operation as a key to success

Research indicates that video laryngoscopes significantly reduce the time from laryngoscope insertion to cuff inflation, a crucial operational efficiency measure [46, 47]. They also enhance both the FPS rate and the glottis exposure rate in patients with cervical spine fixation [48]. Additionally, the adaptability of catheter types, combined with standardised operation of suction equipment, helps minimise the risk of oesophageal intubation [49, 50]. The Airtraq laryngoscope is known for optimising glottis exposure [51], while video laryngoscopes such as the C-MAC offer improved visibility and higher success rates for intubation in ICU settings [52].

Mandatory implementation of difficult airway plans

Failure to implement difficult airway plans is a major contributor to human error in emergency intubation. Conducting a comprehensive airway assessment, including identifying anatomical variations and activating a plan that provides additional resources like spare flexible bronchoscopic, significantly enhances the ability to manage complex cases [36, 37].

Collaborative strategies for complication prevention and control

The overall incidence of complications in this study exhibited a downward trend (P = 0.068). Increasing the sample size may help confirm its statistical significance. This downward trend highlights the potential benefits of technical improvements and training enhancements. However, complications such as lip or dental injury did not demonstrate significant improvement, indicating the need to enhance precise techniques, such as controlling the angle of laryngoscope insertion, and equipment selection, such as using flexible catheters to minimize mucosal friction, in future training sessions. Therefore, reducing complications requires a multi-faceted approach that includes standardising operating techniques—for example, using video laryngoscopes to minimise mucosal damage [53]. Other strategies involve optimising equipment parameters, such as adapting catheter diameters and controlling cuff pressure, as well as strengthening postoperative monitoring and emergency response mechanisms [54].

Two key aspects of training quality: cognitive framework and simulation evaluation

The importance of standardised assessment of theoretical knowledge

The standardised theoretical assessment system is essential for developing STRs’ cognitive framework. This framework includes key knowledge areas such as the three-dimensional anatomical structure of the larynx, the pathological and physiological processes involved in oxygen consumption, and the principles for addressing complications, including preventive measures and emergency treatments for aspiration [55].

The core value of the theoretical assessment can be summarised in the following aspects [56]. These aspects highlight its role in strengthening the scientific basis and facilitating practical application:

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, as a single-center retrospective study, it is subject to temporal confounding. Although the baseline characteristics of the STRs were comparable between the two groups, and the use of video laryngoscopy was standardised as part of the intervention, unmeasured changes in the clinical environment could still have influenced the results. Second, differences exist between simulation and actual clinical practice. Well-designed simulation training might not fully replicate complex emergency scenarios, such as positioning restrictions for patients with cervical spine stabilization, which could lead to limitations in skill transfer. Third, the scope of complications reported is limited, as delayed complications were not included in this study. Additionally, the small sample size and low event rates for specific complication subtypes may have underpowered the study to detect significant differences in these outcomes.

Future studies should adopt a prospective randomised controlled design and conduct multi-center validation to improve generalizability. Moreover, integrating virtual reality technology may increase the clinical relevance of simulation training.

Conclusion

This study developed a standardised training system for ETI using the PDCA cycle management method. The main objectives of this system are to establish standardised procedures, enhance skills, and improve the prevention and control of complications. By implementing a step-by-step training approach, applying standardised operational procedures, and dynamically managing various quality control indicators through a closed-loop system, the airway management skills of STRs rotating through the emergency department showed improvement. This model offers a quantifiable and sustainable reference framework for developing emergency specialists and holds potential value in improving the airway safety management of critically ill patients.
