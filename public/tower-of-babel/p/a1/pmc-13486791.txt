Genetic or pharmacological inhibition of hepatic TMEM141 attenuates MASH and fibrosis via the ROS-HNF4α signaling pathway
Authors: Jiayou Wang, Chwen-Lih Chen, Raja Gopoju, Shuwei Hu, Yang Wang, Hui Wang, Zaid Batayneh, Xiaoli Pan, Yeong-Renn Chen, Jeffrey Mellott, Patrick Kang, Lakshitha Gunawardana, Liya Yin, Yanqiao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractMetabolic dysfunction-associated steatoic liver disease (MASLD) and its advanced form, metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), have become a global epidemic due to obesity and genetic susceptibility. Here, we report transmembrane protein 141 (TMEM141), a protein with a largely unknown function, as a key regulator of MASH and MASH-associated fibrosis. Hepatic TMEM141 is reduced in MASLD/MASH patients and mouse models of MASLD/MASH due to increased microRNA-149. Unexpectedly, genetic inactivation of hepatocyte TMEM141 protects against MASLD/MASH induced by a high fat/cholesterol/fructose (HFCF) diet, whereas hepatic overexpression of human TMEM141 exacerbates MASLD/MASH. We further show that TMEM141 localizes within mitochondria and interacts with mitochondrial complex I to regulate ROS production. Inactivation of TMEM141 reduces ROS production, induces hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α (HNF4α), and fails to protect against HFCF diet-induced MASH or fibrosis in the absence of hepatocyte HNF4α. Finally, pharmacological inhibition of hepatic TMEM141 by N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc)-siTmem141 significantly promotes the reversal of HFCF diet-induced MASH and fibrosis. These findings underscore a critical role of TMEM141 in MASH development and suggest that targeting hepatic TMEM141 may represent a promising strategy for treating MASH and MASH-associated fibrosis.Subject terms: Liver fibrosis, Stress signalling

Introduction

Metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD) is the most prevalent liver disease worldwide, affecting over 30% of the global population1–3. MASLD encompasses metabolic dysfunction-associated steatotic liver (MASL) and metabolic dysfunction-associated steatohepatitis (MASH), a severe form of MASLD with inflammation. MASH can lead to cirrhosis and hepatocellular carcinoma. The progression and severity of MASLD involve multiple pathological processes and mediators, such as lipotoxicity, impaired mitochondrial function, reactive oxygen species (ROS), defective lipolysis, and inflammation1,4–12. While the recent FDA approvals of resmetirom (Rezdiffra) and Wegovy (semaglutide) represent an important advance in the treatment of MASH with moderate to advanced fibrosis, their modest efficacy (20-30% for MASH resolution and 10–15% for fibrosis resolution)13,14, highlights the ongoing need for more effective therapeutic options.

Transmembrane protein 141 (TMEM141) is a member of the TMEM protein family. TMEM141 has 108 amino acids and contains two transmembrane domains with loosely packed helices, suggesting its potential involvement in signal transduction15. A genetic variant of TMEM141 is associated with neurodevelopmental disorders16. TMEM141 is highly conserved across species, with 79% amino acid identity between humans and mice. So far, the exact biological function of TMEM141, particularly in the liver, is entirely unknown.

Hepatocyte nuclear factor 4α (HNF4α), a member of the nuclear receptor superfamily, is highly expressed in the liver and plays a crucial role in liver function17. HNF4α expression is significantly reduced in MASLD patients and mouse models of MASLD18,19. Overexpression of hepatic HNF4α protects against MASLD, whereas loss of hepatocyte HNF4α has the opposite effect20. Recent studies have also shown that lipid nanoparticles (LNPs)-mediated delivery of HNF4α mRNA attenuates liver fibrosis in preclinical models21. These studies highlight the critical role of hepatic HNF4α in protection against MASH and fibrosis.

In this study, we characterized the expression, regulation, and function of TMEM141 as well as the underlying mechanisms. We also explored the translational potential of our findings by delivery of TMEM141 siRNA via N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc). Our data demonstrate that hepatocyte TMEM141 plays an important role in MASH development and fibrosis. Targeting hepatocyte TMEM141 may represent a promising strategy for preventing and treating MASH and MASH-associated fibrosis.

Results

Hepatic TMEM141 is repressed in MASLD/MASH via inducing miR-149

Farnesoid X receptor (FXR) is a nuclear hormone receptor whose activation is shown to have beneficial effects on lipid and glucose homeostasis22,23 and is a therapeutic target for MASH24,25. To identify FXR-regulated genes, we performed microarray analyses that showed that treatment with GW4064, a specific FXR agonist, significantly induced hepatic Tmem141 mRNA expression in mice (Supplementary Table 1), a finding further confirmed by qRT-PCR (Supplementary Fig. 1). Previous studies have shown that a few TMEM proteins, such as TMEM41B26 and TMEM13527, are involved in lipid metabolism. So far, nothing is known about the tissue distribution, regulation, or function of TMEM141 in the liver. Western blotting showed that TMEM141 was highly expressed in the liver, heart, and kidney, to a lesser extent in the spleen, and low or absent in the intestine or white adipose tissue (WAT) (Fig. 1a, b).

We then examined whether hepatic TMEM141 expression was changed under pathological conditions. In C57BL/6 mice fed a diet enriched in high-fat/high-cholesterol (i.e., Western diet (WD)) or high fat/cholesterol/fructose (HFCF) for 16 weeks, hepatic TMEM141 protein levels were reduced by ~ 50% (Fig. 1c, d). In MASL and MASH patients, hepatic TMEM141 protein levels were reduced by 37% and 59%, respectively (Fig. 1e, f).

To explore the molecular mechanism underlying TMEM141 downregulation, we focused on miR-149, as we have previously reported that hepatic miR-149 is highly induced in WD or HFCF diet-fed mice and MAFL or MASH patients19. Importantly, when mice were fed an HFCF diet for 4, 8, or 16 weeks, which mimicked the progression of MASLD/MASH, hepatic TMEM141 protein levels progressively declined, whereas hepatic miR-149 levels progressively and markedly increased (Supplementary Fig. 2a–c). Therefore, we examined whether miR-149 regulated TMEM141 expression. HFCF diet or AAV8-mediated overexpression of miR-149 did not alter Tmem141 mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 3a–c). However, hepatic miR-149 overexpression reduced hepatic TMEM141 protein expression by 55% (Fig. 1g, h). In contrast, the inhibition of miR-149 by antagomirs increased hepatic TMEM141 protein levels by 3.3 fold (Fig. 1i, j) without affecting Tmem141 mRNA expression (Supplementary Fig. 3d, e). Consistent with the unchanged Tmem141 mRNA levels in vivo, overexpression of miR-149 did not alter Tmem141 mRNA expression in primary hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 3f). These data suggest that miR-149 may be involved in inhibiting TMEM141 expression by a post-transcriptional mechanism during the MASLD progression.

miRNAs regulate gene expression by altering mRNA degradation or translational repression28,29. Since Tmem141 mRNA levels did not change by HFCF diet or miR-149 overexpression or knockdown (Supplementary Fig. 3a–f), we determined ribosome-bound Tmem141 mRNA levels in the liver after immunoprecipitation (IP) with an antibody against ribosomal protein S6 (RPS6), a key component of the 40S ribosomal subunit. The data showed that miR-149 overexpression reduced ribosome-bound Tmem141 mRNA levels by 38% (Supplementary Fig. 3g), suggesting that miR-149 prevents Tmem141 mRNA from being efficiently recruited to ribosomes for translation. Consistent with this finding, cycloheximide (CHX), a potent inhibitor of protein synthesis, time-dependently inhibited TMEM141 protein expression, and this effect was largely blunted by miR-149 inhibition in primary hepatocytes (Supplementary Fig. 3h, i). These data indicate that miR-149 reduces TMEM141 protein levels via translational repression.

To determine whether miR-149 interacted with Tmem141 mRNA directly, we performed Argonaute 2 (Ago2) RNA immunoprecipitation (Ago2 RIP) assays. Ago2 is the core component of the RNA-induced silencing complex (RISC). Overexpression of miR-149 increased Ago2-associated Tmem141 mRNA by 3-fold (Fig. 1k), indicating that miR-149 may interact with Tmem141 mRNA physically. We then investigated whether miR-149 regulated the activity of the Tmem141 3’UTR. We identified a candidate miR-149 binding site in the 3’UTR of Tmem141 mRNA (Supplementary Fig. 3j). Transient transfection studies showed that miR-149 mimics reduced Tmem141 3’UTR activity by 57%, and this reduction was abolished when the candidate miR-149 binding site was mutated (Fig. 1l), suggesting that Tmem141 is a direct downstream target of miR-149. Taken together, the data in Fig. 1 demonstrate that TMEM141 is highly expressed in the liver and downregulated in MASLD, which is likely mediated, at least in part, by miR-149 induction.

Overexpression of hepatic TMEM141 exacerbates HFCF diet-induced MASLD and fibrosis in mice

To explore the role of hepatic TMEM141 in MASLD, we generated an AAV overexpressing human TMEM141 protein under the control of an albumin promoter (AAV8-ALB-hTMEM141). We then i.v. injected AAV8-ALB-hTMEM141 and AAV8-ALB-Null (control) into C57BL/6 mice, followed by feeding with an HFCF diet for 16 weeks. There was no change in body weight between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 4a). However, there was an increase in body fat content when mice infected with AAV8-ALB-TMEM141 were fed an HFCF diet for 4 weeks (Supplementary Fig. 4b). AAV8-ALB-TMEM141 significantly increased hepatic TMEM141 protein levels as well as plasma aspartate aminotransferase (AST) and alanine aminotransferase (ALT) levels (Fig. 2a–c). AAV8-ALB-hTMEM141 also significantly increased hepatic triglycerides (TG), free fatty acid (FFA), total cholesterol (TC), and free cholesterol (FC) levels (Fig. 2d–f), and raised hepatic levels of hydroxyproline, a marker of liver fibrosis, by 174% (Fig. 2g).

Histological analysis confirmed that AAV8-ALB-hTMEM141 induced neutral lipid accumulation and collagen deposition (Fig. 2h) as well as macrophage accumulation (Fig. 2i). Quantitative analyses showed that AAV8-ALB-hTMEM141 increased MASLD activity score (Fig. 2j), F4/80-positive cell accumulation (%) (Fig. 2k), and fibrosis (% area) by 34% (Fig. 2l). Consistent with the phenotypic changes, TMEM141 overexpression repressed hepatic genes involved in lipolysis (carboxylesterase 1 g (Ces1g)30 and Ces2c5) but induced genes involved in inflammation or fibrogenesis (Tnfα, Il1β, F4/80, Icam1, Vcam1, iNos, Timp1, Col1a1) (Fig. 2m). Thus, the data of Fig. 2 demonstrate that hepatic overexpression of TMEM141 exacerbates HFCF-induced MASLD and fibrosis in mice.

Genetic inactivation of hepatocyte TMEM141 mitigates HFCF diet-induced MASH and fibrosis

To further characterize the role of TMEM141 in MASLD, we generated Tmem141fl/fl mice and i.v. injected these mice with AAV8-TBG-Cre or AAV8-TBG-Null to generate hepatocyte-specific Tmem141−/− (Tmem141Hep−/−) mice and Tmem141fl/fl (control) mice, respectively. The resultant mice were then fed an HFCF diet for 16 weeks. There was no change in body weight or body fat content between these two groups (Supplementary Fig. 4c, d). Hepatic TMEM141 protein levels as well as plasma AST and ALT levels were reduced by 75% (Fig. 3a), 44%, and 57% (Fig. 3b), respectively, in Tmem141Hep−/− mice.

Tmem141Hep−/− mice had a significantly reduced liver-to-body weight ratio (Fig. 3c). Hepatic levels of TG (Fig. 3d), FFA (Fig. 3e), TC and FC (Fig. 3f), and hydroxyproline were reduced by 39, 44, 36, 35, and 53%, respectively, in Tmem141Hep−/− mice (Fig. 3g). Histological staining showed reduced hepatic lipid accumulation (Fig. 3h), fibrosis, and macrophage accumulation (Fig. 3i). Quantitative data showed that loss of hepatocyte Tmem141 reduced MASLD activity score by 25% (Fig. 3j), hepatic F4/80-postive cells by 41% (Fig. 3k), and fibrosis by 30% (Fig. 3l). At gene expression levels, loss of hepatocyte Tmem141 significantly induced hepatic genes involved in lipolysis (Ces1g, Ces2c, Atgl) but repressed hepatic genes involved in inflammation or fibrogenesis (Tnfα, Il1β, F4/80, Icam1, Vcam1, iNos, Timp1, Col1a1) (Fig. 3m).

We also fed female Tmem141fl/fl mice and Tmem141Hep−/− mice an HFCF diet for 16 weeks. While there was no difference in body weight or body fat content between the two groups (Supplementary Fig. 5a, b), Tmem141Hep−/− mice had reduced plasma AST and ALT levels, and reduced hepatic TG, TC, FC, and hydroxyproline levels (Supplementary Fig. 5c–h). These data indicate that loss of hepatic Tmem141 in female mice also protects against HFCF diet-induced MASLD.

Together, these data demonstrate that genetic inactivation of hepatocyte TMEM141 ameliorates MASH and fibrosis induced by the HFCF diet.

TMEM141 localizes to the mitochondria and endoplasmic reticulum

To investigate the mechanism by which hepatocyte TMEM141 regulates the development of MASLD/MASH, we first examined its subcellular localization. Analysis of liver subcellular fractions revealed that TMEM141 predominantly localized to the mitochondria, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), and plasma membrane, with little or no presence in the lysosome, Golgi apparatus, or nucleus (Fig. 4a).

To identify TMEM141-interacting proteins, we performed co-immunoprecipitation using a TMEM141 antibody. We then performed non-targeted LC-MS/MS, identifying 1,542 proteins interacting with TMEM141 (fold change > 1.5, P < 0.05) (Supplementary Table 2). Mitochondrial proteins were among the top identified proteins (Supplementary Table 2). Western blot assays confirmed that TMEM141 interacted with mitochondrial proteins, including VDAC1 (outer membrane protein), NDUFB8 (Complex I), SDHB (Complex II), UQCRC2 (Complex III), COX5B (Complex IV), and ATP5A (Complex V) (Fig. 4b). GO and KEGG pathway enrichment analyses indicated that TMEM141-interacting proteins were primarily localized to mitochondria, ER, and plasma membrane, and were involved in metabolic pathways, neurodegeneration pathways, reactive oxygen species (ROS) production, oxidative phosphorylation, and MASLD (Fig. 4c). Next, we performed trypsin digestion assays in the presence or absence of digitonin, a cell membrane-permeabilizing agent. In the absence of digitonin, trypsin digested VDAC1 (an outer membrane marker) but not COX IV (an inner membrane marker) (Fig. 4d; second lane). In the presence of digitonin, trypsin digested both VDAC1 and COX IV (Fig. 4d, third lane). These data suggest that TMEM141 is localized to the inner mitochondrial membrane or matrix.

To further explore the mitochondrial localization of TMEM141, we performed blue native PAGE (BN-PAGE) to separate individual complexes as described31, followed by probing with complex-specific antibodies. The data showed that TMEM141 was present in complex I, Complex II, complex V, and the supercomplex III2 + IV1/Vn (Fig. 4e). We then performed an immunolocalization study in HepG2 cells using antibodies against FLAG (for TMEM141) or ATP5A (for inner mitochondria), which confirmed the presence of TMEM141 in mitochondria (Fig. 4f). Lastly, immunogold electron microscopy of hepatocytes showed that TMEM141 was present in the inner mitochondrial membrane (Fig. 4g, h). Together, the data in Fig. 4 indicate that TMEM141 localizes to the inner mitochondrial membrane, the ER, and the plasma membrane.

TMEM141 interacts with mitochondrial complex I to promote ROS production

We performed molecular docking to understand how TMEM141 bound to mitochondria, which showed that TMEM141 interacted with the ND1 domain of Complex I (PDB: 5LNK) (Fig. 5a). Confocal microscopy confirmed that TMEM141 was present in complex I (Fig. 5b, c). Complex I is a major source of reactive oxygen species (ROS) within mitochondria32, including superoxide (O2●−) and hydrogen peroxide (H2O2). Indeed, overexpression of TMEM141 significantly increased mitochondrial O2●− production in the presence or absence of menadione (Supplementary Fig. 6a). Menadione is an inducer of mitochondrial O2●− production via one-electron transfer reactions33–35. In the liver, overexpression of TMEM141 induced hepatic ROS levels by 128% and H2O2 levels by 174% (Supplementary Fig. 6b, c). These data suggest that TMEM141 may interact with complex I to promote ROS production.

In contrast to the overexpression studies, genetic inactivation of Tmem141 in primary hepatocytes reduced basal mitochondrial O2●− levels by 50% and prevented menadione-induced mitochondrial O2●− by 37% (Fig. 5d), indicating that loss of TMEM141 suppresses mitochondrial ROS generation.

To explore how loss of TMEM141 prevented mitochondrial ROS production, we isolated mitochondria from wild-type or Tmem141−/− mice. Genetic inactivation of Tmem141 reduced complex I activity by 45%, as determined by rotenone-sensitive Q1-mediated NADH oxidation (Fig. 5e). Loss of Tmem141 also reduced oxygen consumption rate (OCR) by 33% at state 3 and 20% at state 4 (Fig. 5e–h), and the respiratory control index by 25% (Fig. 5i). We also used electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) to determine the oxidation of CM-H hydroxylamine to its nitroxide form, which was reduced by 68% (Fig. 5j), indicating that loss of Tmem141 markedly reduces mitochondrial redox activity. Consistent with these ex vivo data, loss of Tmem141 reduced hepatic H2O2 levels by 42% in HFCF diet-fed mice (Fig. 5k). Thus, the data of Fig. 5 suggest that TMEM141 interacts with complex I to promote ROS production.

Hepatic TMEM141 regulates HNF4α expression via the ROS-ERK/JNK pathway

In line with the finding from Tmem141−/− mice (Fig. 5k), hepatic ROS levels were reduced by 50% in HFCF diet-fed Tmem141Hep−/− mice (Fig. 6a). ROS are known to function as signaling molecules36, which can activate mitogen-activated protein kinases (MAPKs)37–39, nuclear factor erythroid 2-related factor 2 (NRF2)40,41 (a transcription factor regulating oxidative stress and inflammation42), hypoxia-inducible factor 1α (HIF1α)43, and nuclear factor Kappa B (NF-κB)44. Consistent with the reduced ROS levels, hepatic phosphorylated ERK1 and JNK1 levels were reduced by 39% and 73%, respectively, in Tmem141Hep−/− mice (Fig. 6b, c). In contrast, hepatic levels of NRF2, HIF1α, NF-κB, or phosphorylated p38 MAPK were unchanged (Supplementary Fig. 7a, b). These data suggest that hepatic ERK and JNK are specifically inhibited in Tmem141Hep−/− mice.

Previous studies have shown that overexpression of hepatocyte HNF4α protects against, whereas loss of hepatocyte HNF4α aggravates, HFCF diet-induced MASH20, and that LNP-mediated delivery of HNF4α mRNA ameliorates liver fibrosis in preclinical models21. Thus, we also examined hepatic HNF4α levels. Intrigulingly, hepatic HNF4α protein levels were increased by 2-fold in Tmem141Hep−/− mice (Fig. 6b, c). In contrast, AAV-mediated overexpression of hepatic TMEM141 reduced hepatic HNF4α protein levels by 33% and induced phosphorylated ERK and JNK levels by 3.1 fold and 1.4 fold, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 8a–d).

To investigate whether the change in ROS levels caused the alterations of phosphorylated ERK and JNK levels and/or HNF4α levels, we treated primary hepatocytes with either H2O2 or menadione (to induce mitochondrial O2●− levels). H2O2 treatment reduced HNF4α protein levels by 42–72% while inducing phosphorylated JNK1 protein levels by 2.3-3.5 fold in a time-dependent manner (Fig. 6d, e). Similarly, menadione reduced HNF4α protein levels by 45-79% in a time-dependent manner, while markedly inducing phosphorylation of ERK1 and JNK1 (Fig. 6f, g).

Next, we investigated whether ERK and/or JNK activation regulates HNF4α expression. Treatment of primary hepatocytes with U0126 (ERK inhibitor) or SP600125 (JNK inhibitor) increased HNF4α protein levels by ≥ 2 fold (Fig. 6h, i). Thus, the data of Fig. 6d–i suggest that ROS may activate ERK/JNK to inhibit HNF4α expression.

To investigate whether loss of hepatocyte TMEM141 induces HNF4α via inhibiting the ROS-ERK/JNK pathway, we performed two complementary studies. Since hepatic ROS levels were markedly reduced in Tmem141−/− or Tmem141Hep−/− mice (Figs. 5k, 6a), we isolated primary hepatocytes and treated Tmem141−/− hepatocytes with menadione or H2O2 to restore cellular ROS levels. Tmem141 loss significantly increased HNF4α protein levels, and this increase was blunted by supplementation with ROS (menadione treatment or H2O2) (Fig. 6j, k; compare the vehicle (veh) groups). Interestingly, inhibition of ERK and JNK activation by U0126 and SP600125 (U + SP) fully restored HNF4α expression in menadione or H2O2-treated Tmem141−/− hepatocytes (Fig. 6j, k; compare the “U + SP” groups with the “Veh” groups). These data suggest that loss of TMEM141 induces HNF4α expression by inhibiting the ROS-ERK/JNK pathway. To further validate this pathway, we also overexpressed ERK and JNK in Tmem141+/+ or Tmem141−/− primary hepatocytes using lentiviruses, as hepatic ERK and JNK were markedly repressed in Tmem141−/− mice (Fig. 6b, c). These hepatocytes were then treated with a lipid cocktail containing fatty acids and cholesterol to mimic the HFCF diet feeding. Consistent with the finding of Fig. 6j, k, overexpression of ERK and JNK completely blocked the induction of HNF4α expression in Tmem141−/− hepatocytes (Fig. 6l, m). Thus, the data of Fig. 6j, m indicate that the inhibition of the ROS-ERK/JNK signaling is essential for the induction of HNF4α in the absence of TMEM141.

To investigate whether overexpression of hepatocyte TMEM141 inhibits HNF4α expression via activating the ERK/JNK pathway, we overexpressed TMEM141 in primary hepatocytes in the presence of a lipid cocktail, with or without the addition of U0126 and SP600125. TMEM141 overexpression inhibited HNF4α protein expression by 77%, which was fully restored by U0126 and SP600125 (Supplementary Fig. 9a, b). In addition, TMEM141 overexpression increased intracellular triglyceride (TG) levels by 53%, which was completely abolished by U0126 and SP600125 (Supplementary Fig. 9c). Furthermore, overexpression of HNF4α completely blocked TMEM141 from increasing intracellular TG accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 9d, e). These data suggest that overexpression of TMEM141 inhibits HNF4α expression and induces TG accumulation by activating ERK/JNK.

Taken together, our gain- and loss-of-function studies have demonstrated that hepatic TMEM141 regulates HNF4α expression via the ROS-ERK/JNK pathway.

Genetic inactivation of TMEM141 prevents HFCF diet-induced MASH and fibrosis via induction of hepatic HNF4α

Given that hepatic HNF4α plays a critical role in protection against MASH and fibrosis20,21,45,46 and is highly induced in Tmem141Hep−/− mice (Fig. 6), we investigated whether hepatic HNF4α mediated the protective effect on MASH and fibrosis development in these mice. Tmem141fl/fl mice and Tmem141fl/flHnf4αfl/fl mice were i.v. injected with AAV8-TBG-Cre or AAV8-TBG-Null to generate mice with hepatocyte-specific deletion of Tmem141 alone or in combination with Hnf4α, along with corresponding control mice (Tmem141fl/fl or Tmem141fl/flHnf4αfl/fl), respectively. These mice were then fed an HFCF diet for 16 weeks. Loss of hepatocyte Tmem141 significantly induced hepatic protein levels of HNF4α and cholesterol 7α-hydroxylase (CYP7A1), which was completely blocked by inactivation of hepatocyte Hnf4α (Fig. 7a, b). CYP7A1 converts free cholesterol to bile acids and is a known target of HNF4α46. These data validated the knockdown efficiency by AAV8-TBG-Cre.

Next, we analyzed the MASH phenotype in these mice. Consistent with the findings described in Fig. 3, genetic loss of hepatocyte Tmem141 significantly reduced plasma levels of ALT by 45% (Fig. 7c) and AST by 25% (Fig. 7d) as well as hepatic levels of TG (Fig. 7e), FFA (Fig. 7f), TC (Fig. 7g), FC (Fig. 7h), and hydroxyproline by 58% (Fig. 7i). Interestingly, these beneficial effects were abolished completely by genetic inactivation of Hnf4α (Fig. 7c–i).

Histological staining by H&E (Fig. 7j), F4/80 (Fig. 7k), and picrosirius red (Fig. 7l) revealed reduced neutral lipid and macrophage accumulation as well as fibrogenesis in Tmem141−/− mice but not in Tmem141−/−Hnf4α−/− mice (Fig. 7j–l). In line with the biochemical and histological changes, the MASLD activity score was significantly reduced in Tmem141−/− mice but not in Tmem141−/−Hnf4α−/− mice (Fig. 7m). More strikingly, hepatic F4/80-positive cells and fibrosis were reduced by 36% and 37%, respectively, in Tmem141−/− mice but not in Tmem141−/−Hnf4α−/− mice (Fig. 7n, o).

At the mRNA level, loss of hepatocyte Tmem141 significantly induced hepatic expression of genes involved in bile acid synthesis (Cyp7a1) or lipolysis (Ces1g, Ces2c) and reduced hepatic genes involved in inflammation or fibrogenesis (Tnfα, Il1β, Col1a1) (Fig. 7p). Such changes in hepatic gene expression were abolished entirely in the absence of hepatocyte Hnf4α (Fig. 7p). Taken together, the data of Fig. 7 demonstrate that loss of hepatocyte TMEM141 prevents the development of MASH and fibrosis via the induction of HNF4α.

Loss of hepatic TMEM141 induces lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation and inhibits de novo lipogenesis

Our previous studies have shown that HNF4α induces lipolysis and fatty acid oxidation (FAO) to reduce hepatic fatty acid and TG levels, and lower hepatic cholesterol levels by promoting bile acid synthesis from cholesterol20. The findings that loss of hepatocyte TMEM141 induces genes involved in lipolysis (Ces1g, Ces2c, Atgl) and prevents MASH and liver fibrosis via HNF4α induction (Figs. 3m, 7), led us to investigate the role of Tmem141 deficiency in lipolysis and FAO. Loss of hepatocyte Tmem141 significantly increased TG hydrolase activity by 34% in the liver (Fig. 8a) and FAO by 39% in primary hepatocytes (Fig. 8b) when 3H-triolein or 3H-palmitate was used as substrates, respectively. Consistent with the induction of FAO, plasma β-hydroxybutyrate levels were increased by 165% (Fig. 8c). In addition, loss of hepatocyte Tmem141 reduced hepatic de novo lipogenesis (DNL) by 43% (Fig. 8d), as determined by deuterium-labeled palmitate in the liver. Consistent with the reduced DNL, the secretion rate of very low-density lipoprotein (VLDL) triglycerides (VLDL-TG) was reduced by 43% in Tmem141Hep−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 10a, b). As a result, plasma TG and cholesterol levels were significantly reduced in Tmem141Hep−/− mice (Supplementary Fig. 10c, d).

At gene expression levels, loss of hepatocyte Tmem141 significantly induced hepatic genes involved in mitochondrial or peroxisomal FAO (Pgc1α, Acsl1, Cpt2, Acox1), ω-oxidation (Cyp4a10, Cyp4a12, Cyp4a13, Cyp4a32, Cyp4f13, Cyp4f14, Cyp4f16)47, or ketogenesis (Hmgc1, Hmgcs2), and repressed hepatic genes involved in DNL (Fasn, Acc1, Scd1) or VLDL secretion (Mttp, Apob) (Fig. 8e and Supplementary Fig. 10e). These gene expression data support the functional changes in lipolysis, FAO, and DNL (Fig. 8a–d), which together account for the reduced hepatic FFA and TG levels in Tmem141Hep−/− mice.

Since HNF4α is a master regulator of bile acid metabolism, we also determined if TMEM141 regulates bile acid metabolism. In Tmem141Hep−/− mice, hepatic genes involved in bile acid synthesis were significantly induced in mRNA (Cyp7a1, Cyp8b1, Cy27a1, Cyp7b1) (Supplementary Fig. 10f) or protein levels (CYP7A1, CYP8B1, CYP27B1) (Supplementary Fig. 10g, h). Consistent with such changes in gene expression, plasma bile acid levels were increased by 2-fold (Supplementary Fig. 10i), and bile acid pool size was increased by 20% (Supplementary Fig. 10j). The increased bile acid synthesis may account for the reduced hepatic cholesterol levels in Tmem141Hep−/− mice.

In contrast with the findings from Tmem141Hep−/− mice, AAV-mediated overexpression of hepatic TMEM141 reduced TG hydrolase activity by 31% in the liver (Fig. 8f), FAO by 18% in primary hepatocytes (Fig. 8g), and plasma β-hydroxybutyrate levels by 22% (Fig. 8h), and increased hepatic DNL by 74% (Fig. 8i) and VLDL-TG secretion rate by 19% (Supplementary Fig. 11a, b). Consistent with these findings, hepatic TMEM141 overexpression significantly increased plasma TG and cholesterol levels (Supplementary Fig. 11c, d). At gene expression levels, overexpression of hepatic TMEM141 significantly repressed hepatic genes involved in lipolysis (Ces1g, Ces2c) (Fig. 2m), FAO (Acsl1, Ehhadh, Acox1), ketogenesis (Hmgc1, Hmgcs2), or ω-oxidation (Cyp4a10, Cyp4a12, Cyp4a14, Cyp4a32, Cyp4f13, Cyp4f14, Cyp4f16) while significantly inducing hepatic genes involved in DNL (Fasn, Acc1, Scd1) or VLDL secretion (Mttp, Apob) (Fig. 8j and Supplementary Fig. 11e).

Collectively, our data demonstrate that loss of hepatocyte TMEM141 prevents the development of MASLD/MASH by inducing lipolysis, FAO, and bile acid metabolism, and inhibiting DNL, which further supports the findings of Fig. 7.

Pharmacological inhibition of hepatocyte TMEM141 improves MASH and fibrosis

N-acetylgalactosamine (GalNAc)-siRNA conjugates, which target hepatocytes specifically by binding to the asialoglycoprotein receptor (ASGPR)48,49, have been used in multiple clinical trials50–52. We assessed the therapeutic potential of targeting TMEM141 by using GalNAc-siRNA conjugates (GalNAc-siTmem141) for inhibiting hepatic TMEM141 expression. C57BL/6 mice were fed a Western diet (which did not have fructose) for a total of 16 weeks. Starting at week 12, the mice were i.v. injected weekly with GalNAc-siTmem141 or GalNAc-siScr (control) for 4 weeks (Supplementary Fig. 12a). GalNAc-siTmem141 treatment did not affect body weight (Supplementary Fig. 12a) but reduced hepatic Tmem141 mRNA by 58% and protein levels by 88%, without affecting Tmem141 mRNA or protein levels in the heart or kidney (Supplementary Fig. 12b-d), suggesting that GalNAc-siTmem141 targets the liver specifically. GalNAc-siTmem141 significantly reduced the liver-to-body weight ratio (Supplementary Fig. 12e), plasma ALT levels by 41% (Supplementary Fig. 12f), and significantly reduced hepatic levels of TG, FFA, TC, FC, and hydroxyproline (Supplementary Fig. 12g–j). H&E or picrosirius red staining, as well as F4/80 staining, revealed a reduction in hepatic neutral lipid and macrophage accumulation and fibrosis in GalNAc-siTmem141-treated mice (Supplementary Fig. 12k, l). Quantitative analyses showed that GalNAc-siTmem141 significantly reduced the MASLD activity score (Supplementary Fig. 12m) and decreased F4/80-positive cells and fibrosis by 39% and 29%, respectively (Supplementary Fig. 12n, o). At the gene expression level, GalNAc-siTmem141 significantly induced hepatic HNF4α and CYP7A1 protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 12p, q) and hepatic genes involved in lipolysis (Ces1g, Ces2c), and repressed hepatic genes involved in inflammation or fibrogenesis (Il1β, F4/80, Icam1, α-Sma, Col1a1, Tgfβ) (Supplementary Fig. 12r).

The data of Supplementary Fig. 12 demonstrate that therapeutic inhibition of hepatocyte TMEM141 improves Western diet-induced MASH and fibrosis. Resmetirom has recently been approved to treat MASH. To investigate whether pharmacological inhibition of TMEM141 could achieve a similar effect on MASH reversal to resmetirom, we fed C57BL/6 mice an HFCF diet for 16 weeks. Starting at 12 weeks, the mice were administered with GalNAc-siTmem141 (weekly), resmetirom (daily), or control (GalNAc-siScr plus vehicle). Treatment with GlaNAc-siTMEM141 or resmetirom caused a small decrease in body weight (Fig. 9a) and reduced many MASH-related parameters to comparable levels, including plasma ATL (46% vs. 60%) (Fig. 9b), plasma AST (29.5% vs. 28.7%) (Fig. 9c), liver-to-body weight ratio (15.7% vs. 31.3%) (Fig. 9d), hepatic TC (34.7% vs. 34.5%) (Fig. 9e), hepatic TG (36% vs. 60%) (Fig. 9f), or hepatic hydroxyproline (46.5% vs. 65%) (Fig. 9g). These biochemical changes were also supported by histological staining by H&E, oil red O, or picrosirius red, or immunohistological staining with F4/80 (Fig. 9h, i). GlaNAc-siTMEM141 or resmetirom treatment also achieved comparable levels of reductions in MASLD activity score (52% vs. 64%) (Fig. 9i), hepatic F4/80+ macrophages (44.9% vs. 52.9%) (Fig. 9k), or fibrosis area (46.1% vs. 59.2%) (Fig. 9i). Gene expression data show that GalNAc-siTmem141, but not resmetirom, markedly reduced hepatic Tmem141 mRNA and protein levels (Fig. 9m–o). GalNAc-siTmem141, but not resmetirom, also significantly induced Cyp8b1, Ces1g, and Ces2c expression (Fig. 9o). In addition, GalNAc-siTmem141 and resmetirom induced Cyp7a1 mRNA by 4.5-fold and 2.9-fold, respectively, and reduced hepatic mRNA levels of Tnfα, Il1β, Icam1, F4/80, Tgfβ, and α-Sma to similar extents (Fig. 9o).

Since human and mouse TMEM141 share high amino acid homology, we evaluated the translational potential of our findings in mice by treating human primary hepatocytes with TMEM141 siRNA. The data showed that treatment with TMEM141 siRNA resulted in a more than 40% reduction in TMEM141 mRNA and protein levels (Supplementary Fig. 13a–c). Treatment with TMEM141 siRNA also induced HNF4α, CYP7A1, CYP8B1, CES1, and CES2 mRNA levels, and reduced TNFα, IL1β, and IL6 mRNA levels (Supplementary Fig. 13a) and cellular TG accumulation (Supplementary Fig. 13d, e). Thus, inhibition of TMEM141 in human hepatocytes produces effects similar to those observed in mice.

Discussion

So far, little is known about the biology of TMEM141. The present study reveals an unexpected role of hepatic TMEM141 in the pathogenesis of MASLD/MASH. Hepatic TMEM141 is downregulated in MASLD/MASH, likely due to the induction of miR-149. Interestingly, genetic or pharmacological inhibition of hepatic TMEM141 ameliorates HFCF diet-induced MASH and fibrosis, suggesting that reduced hepatic TMEM141 in MASLD/MASH serves as a protective mechanism against disease progression.

We have employed both gain- and loss-of-function approaches to investigate the role of hepatic TMEM141 in MASH development and fibrosis, with a focus on loss-of-function studies, as inhibition of hepatic TMEM141 shows beneficial effects. Understanding how TMEM141 initiates its signaling pathway is of great interest, but also a significant challenge given its unknown biological functions. Our data suggest that TMEM141 is localized to the inner mitochondrial membrane, which is supported by trypsin digestion, confocal microscopy, and transmission electron microscopy. Molecular modeling suggests that TMEM141 interacts with the ND1 subunit of complex I. Complex I is the primary source of cellular ROS production53. The interaction between TMEM141 and complex I promotes ROS production, which is supported by our overexpression studies. In the absence of TMEM141, mitochondria are predisposed to a more reductive physiological environment, with diminished redox activity and reduced cellular ROS levels. Thus, TMEM141 in mitochondria may be required for cellular ROS production.

ROS are important signaling molecules that have been previously shown to activate ERK and JNK37. However, whether and how ROS regulates the expression of HNF4α, a liver-enriched transcription factor, has not been examined previously. Hepatic HNF4α is markedly repressed in MASH and liver fibrosis18,19,21,45,54, and plays a crucial role in protecting against MASH development and fibrosis20,21. AAV-mediated overexpression of hepatic HNF4α significantly reduced, whereas loss of hepatic HNF4α aggravates the development of MASLD/MASH20. Adenoviral45 or lipid nanoparticles21-mediated delivery of HNF4α mRNA significantly reduces hepatoxin or cholestasis-induced liver fibrosis. HNF4α protects against MASLD/MASH by inducing genes involved in cholesterol catabolism (CYP7A1, CYP8B1), fatty acid oxidation (CPT1, CPT2), and lipolysis (CES1 and CES25,30), and inhibiting genes involved in inflammation and fibrogenesis20. Our current studies show that TMEM141 regulates hepatic HNF4α expression via the ROS-ERK/JNK pathway (Supplementary Fig. 14), findings supported by in vivo and in vitro studies. In line with our findings, previous studies have shown that ERK/JNK inhibits HNF4α expression by activating AP-155,56, a transcription factor complex of c-Fos and c-Jun. Thus, in the absence of TMEM141, reduced hepatic ROS levels lead to the inhibition of ERK/JNK and a subsequent induction of HNF4α.

Consistent with the finding that loss of hepatocyte TMEM141 protects against MASH and fibrosis via hepatic HNF4α induction, TMEM141 loss induces lipolysis, FAO, and bile acid production and inhibits DNL. These functional changes, together with reduced ROS production, can lead to reduced lipotoxicity and oxidative stress, thereby protecting against MASH development. In contrast, overexpression of human TMEM141 in the liver increases ROS production and reduces HNF4α expression by activating the ERK/JNK signaling, resulting in reduced lipolysis and FAO and increased DNL. As a result, overexpression of human TMEM141 in the liver promotes the development of MASH and fibrosis. This latter finding is supported by the observation that inhibition of TMEM141 in human primary hepatocytes lowers cellular triglyceride levels and regulates the expression of many genes in a pattern similar to that observed in Tmem141Hep−/− mice, suggesting that human and mouse TMEM141 share similar functionality in the development of MASLD/MASH.

During overnutrition, excessive mitochondrial activity overproduces ROS, leading to oxidative stress and damage. Interestingly, loss of hepatocyte TMEM141 inhibits mitochondrial activity and ROS production, yet exhibits beneficial effects on lipid homeostasis, MASH development, and fibrosis. It is likely that the loss of TMEM141 prevents the HFCF diet from inducing excessive mitochondrial activity and ROS production. Consistent with our findings, many genes involved in ω-oxidation, mitochondrial FAO, or peroxisomal FAO are induced to prevent fatty acid accumulation in the absence of hepatic TMEM141. These compensatory changes are important for protection against liver steatosis and damage in the absence of hepatic TMEM141. However, we acknowledge that chronic activation of these pathways may lead to unintended metabolic consequences, such as the accumulation of intermediate metabolites (e.g., dicarboxylic acid). Therefore, long-term studies are needed to evaluate the safety of TMEM141 inhibition.

Inhibition of ROS production or reduction of ROS levels has shown beneficial effects on MASLD/MASH or fibrosis. Vitamin E, known for its antioxidant effect, improves MASLD activity score, MASH, and MASH resolution in clinical trials57,58, but causes hemorrhagic stroke or prostate cancer58. A dual NADPH oxidase 1/4 inhibitor (setanaxib) is shown to inhibit ROS production and liver fibrosis in a preclinical setting59. N-acetylcystein (NAC), a precursor of glutathione, improves liver pathology in rats with steatohepatitis60 and in individuals with obesity and MASLD in a pilot study61. In line with these clinical or preclinical studies, our studies show that genetic or pharmacological inhibition of TMEM141 reduces the development of MASLD/MASH and fibrosis by inhibiting the ROS signaling. Since ROS also plays an important role in cellular signaling and function62,63, broadly targeting ROS signaling may cause unwanted side effects. Thus, targeting ROS production or levels in a cell type or organelle-specific manner may be necessary.

The growing epidemic proportions of obesity have significantly increased the incidence of MASLD/MASH. MASLD is found in 75% of people with obesity and 69% of people with type 2 diabetes64. Rezdiffra (resmetirom) and Wegovy (semaglutide) have recently been approved by the FDA for the treatment of MASH in adults. However, Rezdiffra achieves MASH resolution without worsening fibrosis in only 25.9–29.9% of patients, compared with 9.7% in the placebo group. In other words, Rezdiffra is effective in only 16.2–20.2% of MASH patients65. Moreover, Rezdiffra improves fibrosis in only 10-13.7% of patients after subtracting the regression rate in the placebo group65,66. Wegovy has similar modest efficacy in MASH or fibrosis resolution13,14. Thus, there is an urgent need to identify new targets and develop new therapies to treat MASH and MASH-associated fibrosis. Our studies demonstrate that genetic or pharmacological inhibition of hepatic TMEM141 markedly inhibits MASH development and fibrosis. Interestingly, pharmacological inhibition of hepatic TMEM141 ameliorates MASH and fibrosis to an extent comparable to that of resmetirom. Resmetirom, a select thyroid hormone receptor β (THβ) agonist that is expressed mainly in the liver, induces FAO and bile acid synthesis, improves mitochondrial function, inhibits DNL, and reduces lipotoxicity67. Wegovy, a GLP-1 receptor agonist that is not expressed in hepatocytes, reduces appetite, energy intake, and body weight, indirectly inhibits DNL, and improves insulin sensitivity68. In our studies, hepatocyte-specific TMEM141 inhibition targets mitochondria in hepatocytes, leading to reduced ROS production, lipotoxicity, and DNL, and to increased FAO and bile acid production. Thus, TMEM141 inhibition exhibits both overlapping and distinct mechanisms of activation relative to resmetirom and Wegovy. It would be interesting to investigate whether TMEM141 inhibition has additive or synergistic effects with resmetirom or Wegovy in treating MASH and MASH-associated fibrosis.

In conclusion, this study has identified an unexpected function of hepatocyte TMEM141 in regulating the pathogenesis of MASLD/MASH and fibrosis. We have also elucidated a mechanism underlying this regulation. Our studies suggest that targeting hepatocyte TMEM141 may be a promising strategy for treating MASLD/MASH and fibrosis.

Limitations of the study

Although our molecular model has suggested that TMEM141 interacts with the ND1 domain of mitochondrial complex I, the functional interaction between them remains to be determined. We will explore whether the ND1 mutation abrogates TMEM141-mediated ROS regulation. In addition, GalNAc-siTmem141 administration inhibits mitochondrial activity and induces compensatory metabolic adaptation; its long-term safety warrants further investigation. Finally, the sample size from the human primary hepatocyte study is small, and there is variability in human samples. These limitations warrant validation in humanized models or clinical samples.

Co-Immunoprecipitation (Co-IP) and LC-MS/MS

Co-IP was performed using a Pierce MS-Compatible Magnetic IP Kit (Protein A/G) (cat # 90409, ThermoFisher) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Liver tissue ( ~ 100 mg) was homogenized in ice-cold IP lysis buffer with protease inhibitors. After centrifugation, supernatants containing 1000 µg of protein were incubated with 5 µg of specific antibodies and 0.25 mg of protein A/G Magnetic Beads (ThermoFisher; cat # 80105 G) at 4 oC overnight. Beads were washed with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (50 μL each time) three times. Each time, the supernatant was kept and pooled. After the third wash, 5 μL of DTT (5 μg/μL in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate) was added, and the sample was incubated at 56 °C for 15 min. After the incubation, 5 μL of iodoacetamide (15 mg/ml in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate) was added, and the sample was kept in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Sequencing grade-modified trypsin (Promega, Madison, WI) prepared in 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate was added to the sample at an estimated enzyme-to-substrate ratio of 1:20 or 1:100, and the reaction was carried out at 37 °C overnight. The reaction was quenched the next morning by adding acetic acid for acidification. Supernatant was collected and concentrated for LC/MS/MS analysis.

Liquid chromatography-nanospray tandem mass spectrometry (LC/MS/MS) of protein identification was performed on a Thermo Scientific Orbitrap Fusion mass spectrometer equipped with an EASY-Spray™ Source operated in positive ion mode, as described75. Samples (6.4 µL) were separated on an easy spray nano column (PepmapTM RSLC, C18 3 µ 100 A, 75 µm X150 mm Thermo Scientific) using a 2D RSLC HPLC system from Thermo Scientific. Each sample was injected into the µ-Precolumn Cartridge (Thermo Scientific) and desalted with 0.1% Formic Acid in water for 5 min. The injector port was then switched to inject, and the peptides were eluted from the trap onto the column. Mobile phase A was 0.1% Formic Acid in water, and acetonitrile (with 0.1% formic acid) was used as mobile phase B. Flow rate was set at 300 nL/min. Typically, for IP samples, a 2 hr gradient is used: mobile phase B was increased from 2% to 20% in 105 min, and then increased from 20–32% in 10 min, and again from 32–95% in 1 min, and then kept at 95% for another 4 min before being brought back quickly to 2% in 1 min. The column was equilibrated with 2% mobile phase B (98% A) for 15 min before the next sample injection.

MS/MS data was acquired with a spray voltage of 1.6 KV and a capillary temperature of 305 °C. The scan sequence of the mass spectrometer was based on the preview mode data-dependent TopSpeed™ method: the analysis was programmed for a full scan recorded between m/z 375–1500 and an MS/MS scan to generate product ion spectra to determine amino acid sequence in consecutive scans starting from the most abundant peaks in the spectrum in the next 3 s. To achieve high mass accuracy MS determination, the full scan was performed at FT mode, and the resolution was set at 120,000 with internal mass calibration. The AGC Target ion number for FT full scan was set at 4 × 105 ions, maximum ion injection time was set at 50 ms, and micro scan number was set at 1. MSn was performed using HCD in ion trap mode to ensure the highest signal intensity of MSn spectra. The HCD collision energy was set at 32%. The AGC Target ion number for ion trap MSn scan was set at 3.0E4 ions, maximum ion injection time was set at 35 ms and micro scan number was set at 1. Dynamic exclusion is enabled with a repeat count of 1 within 60 s, and low- and high-mass widths of 10 ppm. Sequence information from the MS/MS data was processed by converting the.raw files into a merged file (.mgf) using MSConvert (ProteoWizard). Isotope distributions for the precursor ions of the MS/MS spectra were deconvoluted to obtain the charge states and monoisotopic m/z values of the precursor ions during the data conversion. The resulting mgf files were searched using Mascot Daemon (Matrix Science, version 2.5.1, Boston, MA) against the most recent UniProt databases. The mass accuracy of the precursor ions was set to 10ppm, accidental pick of 1 13C peaks was also included in the search. The fragment mass tolerance was set to 0.5 Da. Carbamidomethylation (Cys) was used as a fixed modification, and variable modifications were oxidation (Met) and deamidation (N and Q). Four missed cleavages for the enzyme were permitted. A decoy database was also searched to determine the false discovery rate (FDR), and peptides were filtered according at 1% FDR. Proteins identified with at least two unique peptides were considered for reliable identification. Any modified peptides were manually checked for validation. Label-free quantitation was performed using the spectral count approach, in which the relative protein quantitation was measured by comparing the number of MS/MS spectra identified from the same protein in each of the multiple LC/MS-MS datasets. Scaffold was used for data analysis. A Student’s t test was performed using Scaffold 4 (Proteome Software, Portland, OR) to evaluate whether the folder change for certain proteins is significant (p < 0.05). The proteomics data are deposited into the MassIVE database (accession # MSV000099157) and the ProteomeXchange database (Proteome Central # PXD068361).

Ago2 RNA Immunoprecipitation (RIP)

Mouse Ago2 RIP was performed using the MagCapture microRNA Isolation Kit (cat # 297-74201, lot#CKE3647; FUJIFILM) according to the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, liver lysate from 100 mg liver was centrifuged at 20,000 x g at 4 oC and 50 μl of the resulting supernatant was used for RNA extraction by TRIzol as input. For immunoprecipitation, 950 μl of liver lysate was incubated with 60 µl of anti-Ago2 magnetic beads overnight at 4 °C with continuous rotation. The immunocomplexes were washed 3 times, then eluted with 50 µl of elution solution and incubated at 90 °C for 90 s to release protein-bound RNA. Samples were then placed on ice for 1 min, briefly centrifuged, and positioned on a magnetic separation stand for 1 min. After complete separation of the beads, the supernatant containing purified RNA was transferred to a new 1.5 ml microcentrifuge tube and subsequently used for RT-qPCR analysis.

Ribosome-associated mRNA

Ribosome-associated mRNA was isolated as described previously76. Briefly, about 100 mg of liver tissue was homogenized in 1 ml of RIP lysis buffer containing 50 mM Tris (pH 7.4), 100 mM KCl, 12 mM MgCl2, 5 mM EDTA, 1% NP-40, 1 mM DTT, 100 μg/ml cycloheximide, 100 μ/ml RNase inhibitor, and protease inhibitors. Lysates were centrifuged at 10,000 x g for 10 min at 4 oC and 50 μl of the resulting supernatant was collected for RNA extraction by TRIzol as input. For immunoprecipitation, 5 µg of anti-RPS6 antibody (cat # ab70227, lot#1027979-18; Abcam) or control IgG was added to 450 µL of the supernatant overnight at 4 °C, followed by incubation with 40 μl of protein A/G beads (Thermo Scientific, cat # 26149) for 2 h at room temperature. The immunocomplexes were washed three times with the RIP buffer and once with PBS. Co-precipitated RNA was isolated by resuspending the beads in 1 ml of TRIzol reagent and then used for RT-qPCR analysis.

Immunofluorescence staining

HepG2 cells were fixed with 4% formaldehyde, permeabilized with 0.1% Triton X-100, and blocked with 5% BSA. Cells were incubated with primary antibodies (1:300 dilution) overnight at 4 °C and secondary antibodies [mouse IgG, Alexa Fluor 488 (cat # A-21202); rabbit IgG, Alex Fluor 594 (cat # A-21207); rat IgG, Alexa Fluor 488 (cat # A-11006), ThermoFisher) (1:500 dilution) for 1 h. Coverslips were mounted with ProLong Diamond Antifade Mountant (ThermoFisher; cat # P36961), and images were captured using a confocal microscope (Olympus Microsystems, CA, USA).

Liver immunofluorescence staining was performed as previously described77,78. Briefly, liver sections (4 μm thick) were sequentially rehydrated using xylene and graded ethanol, followed by heat-induced antigen retrieval in 10 mM sodium citrate buffer (pH 6.0). For microwave-based antigen retrieval, slides were brought to a boil in the sodium citrate buffer and then maintained at a sub-boiling temperature for 4 min. To permeabilize the tissue, sections were permeabilized for 20 min with 0.3% Triton X-100 in PBST. After blocking with 3% BSA in PBST for 1 h at room temperature, the sections were incubated with the primary antibody (1:300 dilution) overnight at 4 °C. The next day, slides were washed and incubated with an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody (1:500 dilution) for 1 h at room temperature. Finally, the sections were counterstained with DAPI for 15 min.

Immunogold labeling for electron microscopy

Immunogold labeling for electron microscopy was performed as previously described79. Briefly, mice were deeply anesthetized with isoflurane and perfused transcardially with 0.9% saline, followed by 250 mL of a fixative solution containing 2% glutaraldehyde and 2% paraformaldehyde. Tissues were sectioned into 50 µm-thick transverse slices using a Vibratome. The sections were post-fixed in 1% osmium tetroxide for 30 min, dehydrated through a graded series of alcohols, and embedded in Durcupan resin (Sigma-Aldrich; Millipore Sigma, Burlington, MA, USA). Ultrathin sections (50–70 nm) were prepared using a UC6 Ultramicrotome (Leica Microsystems, Buffalo Grove, IL, USA). After drying for 3 h, sections were incubated overnight with anti-TMEM141 antibody (1:500) diluted in 0.05 M Tris-buffered saline with 0.1% Triton X-100 (pH 7.6). Secondary antibody conjugated to 15 nm gold particles (goat anti-rabbit, 1:25 dilution, Ted Pella Inc., Redding, CA) was then applied. Imaging was performed using a JEM-1400Plus transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Peabody, MA, USA) at an accelerating voltage of 80 kV and a magnification of 100,000x.

Molecular modeling of mammalian complex I and TMEM141

The Complex I-TMEM141 docking model was generated based on the Complex I structure (PDB ID: 5LNK80) and the TMEM141 structure (PDB ID: 2LOR15). The membrane proteins (ND1 to ND5 in Complex I, and TMEM141) were used to calculate docking models by web-based protein-protein docking servers HDOCK81 and ZDOCK82. The Complex I/ND1 was assigned to the rigid/receptor molecule, and the TMEM141 was assigned to the flexible/ligand molecule. 20 conformations were generated by each server, and the top 3 possible models were manually incorporated in the PyMOL Molecular Graphics System (Version 3.0, Schrödinger, LLC) for graphical presentation.

Measurement of mitochondrial oxygen consumption rate (OCR)

Mitochondria were isolated from mouse liver by differential centrifugation as previously described83,84. Protein concentrations were determined using the Lowry method with BSA as a standard. NADH-linked OCR was measured at 30 °C using a Clark-type oxygen electrode (Oxytherm, Hansatech Instruments, Norfolk, England) following protocols from earlier publications75,84.

Measurement of mitochondrial redox activities

Mitochondria isolated as described above were subjected to electron paramagnetic resonance (EPR) spin-trapping with CM-H redox probes. The mitochondrial suspension in M-buffer was energized with glutamate and malate in the presence of CM-H, and EPR measurements were conducted at 298 °K as described83,84.

Blue native polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (BN-PAGE)

BN-PAGE was performed following established protocols31. Briefly, mitochondrial samples (50 μg protein) were mixed with a sample buffer cocktail (5 µl 4x NativePAGE sample buffer, 8 µl of 5% digitonin, and 7 µl water) and subjected to electrophoresis under the following conditions: 150 V for 30 min in dark blue buffer, followed by 250 V for 60 min in light blue buffer. Proteins were transferred to PVDF membranes (350 mA, 5 min) or visualized with Coomassie blue R-250 staining.

Isolation of subcellular organelles

Plasma membrane, mitochondrial, endoplasmic reticulum (ER), lysosomal, Golgi, microsomal, and nuclear proteins were extracted from liver tissue using the following kits: Plasma Membrane Protein Extraction Kit (cat # ab284937, Abcam), Mitochondria Isolation Kit (Cat # 890801, ThermoFisher), Endoplasmic Reticulum Isolation Kit (cat # ER0100, Sigma-Aldrich), Lysosome Enrichment Kit (cat # 89839, Thermo Fisher), Golgi Isolation Kit (Cat # GL0010, Sigma-Aldrich), Microsome Isolation Kit (cat # ab206995, Abcam), and Nuclear and Cytoplasmic Extraction Reagents (cat # 78835, ThermoFisher). The purity of isolated organelles was tested using specific organelle markers.

Trypsin digestion assays

Mitochondria were isolated from mouse liver by differential centrifugation as previously described83,84. Protein concentrations were determined using the Lowry method with BSA as a standard. Trypsin digestion assays were performed as previously described85. Briefly, mitochondria (200 μg) were resuspended in 20 mM HEPES (pH 7.6) with 5 μg/mL trypsin, in the presence or absence of 10 µg/mL digitonin, and incubated for 30 min at room temperature. Protein sensitivity to trypsin digestion was assessed by SDS-PAGE followed by immunoblotting. Mitochondria were denatured with 1% SDS at 95 °C for 5 min and stored at − 20 °C for subsequent Western blot analysis.

Body composition

Mouse body fat content was measured by EchoMRI-700 (EchoMRI LLC, Houston, TX) following the manufacturer’s instructions. Briefly, after calibration, a mouse was placed in an appropriately sized tube holder, and the adjustable barrier was slid to minimize the mouse’s movement area. The tube holder was then inserted into the machine for scanning body composition.

VLDL-TG secretion

VLDL-triglyceride (VLDL-TG) secretion was determined as described20. In brief, mice were fasted for 5 hours, and then received an intraperitoneal injection of Poloxamer 407 (1 g/kg body weight) to block lipoprotein lipase activity. Subsequently, blood samples were collected from the tail vein at regular intervals (0, 60, 120, and 180 mins). The triglyceride levels in the collected plasma were then measured using an Infinity reagent. The VLDL-TG secretion rate was calculated based on the slopes of the resulting concentration curves, as described previously86.

TGH activity assay

Hepatic triglyceride hydrolase (TGH) activity was quantified utilizing [3H]-triolein as substrate following an established protocol5. In brief, liver tissues were homogenized in a lysis buffer (50 mM Tris, pH 7.4, 0.1 M Sucrose, and 1 mM EDTA). 100 μg of liver extracts were incubated for 1 h at 37 °C in the presence of a substrate solution containing 0.375 µmol unlabeled triolein, 0.8 µM [3H]triolein, 25 μM egg yolk lecithin, 100 µM NaTaurocholate, 0.1 M DTT, 50 mM potassium phosphate buffer (pH 7.4), and 2% fatty acid-free bovine serum albumin (BSA). The enzymatic reaction was stopped by adding 1 ml of methanol/chloroform/heptane (10:9:7) mixture, followed by 0.8 ml of 0.1 M potassium carbonate and 0.1 M boric acid mixture. Following centrifugation at 800 × g, 0.5 ml of the top phase was measured for radioactivity using a liquid scintillation counter.

Hepatic de novo lipogenesis

Five days before sacrifice (Day 0), a 50 µl baseline blood sample was collected from the mice via the facial vein. On day 5, the mice received an intraperitoneal (IP) bolus of 99.99% 2H2O at a dose of 30 µl per gram of body weight. After four hours, approximately 60 µl of blood was collected from the retro-orbital sinus. Newly synthesized palmitate in the liver was then analyzed using gas chromatography-mass spectrometry (GC-MS), as previously described19. For GC-MS sample preparation, approximately 100 mg of frozen liver tissue was weighed into a 15 mL tube, followed by the addition of 4 mL methanol and homogenization. Subsequently, 8 mL of chloroform was added, and the samples were vortexed vigorously for at least 30 seconds. The homogenates were incubated overnight on an orbital shaker at 4 °C. The following day, samples were centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 15 min, and the supernatants were transferred to glass tubes with screw caps. Then, 6 mL of 0.043% MgCl2 solution was added to each tube, vortexed for 30 s, and incubated overnight at 4 °C to allow phase separation. The next day, the upper aqueous phase and middle protein layer were carefully removed. Approximately 0.5 mL of the lower organic phase was transferred to small glass tubes containing 50 μL of 1 mM C17 chloroform solution as an internal standard. Samples were dried under nitrogen and resuspended in 2 mL of methanol:benzene (4:1, v/v). Next, 200 μL acetyl chloride was added slowly over 1 min. Tubes were tightly capped, sealed with parafilm, and subjected to methanolysis at 100 °C for 1 h. After cooling, 2.5 mL of 12% K2CO3 solution was slowly added to neutralize the reaction. Samples were centrifuged at 800 × g for 5 min, and ~ 400 μL of the upper benzene layer was transferred to short glass tubes and evaporated to dryness. The dried extracts were reconstituted in 100 μL ethyl acetate, vortexed, sealed, and heated at 60 °C for 10 min before transfer into GC-MS vials. Samples (0.5 μL injection volume) were analyzed using an Agilent Technologies 7890B GC system equipped with MassHunter GC/MS Acquisition software (version B.07.06.2704). The front injector was operated in split mode (100:1) with the following settings: injector temperature, 250 °C; pressure, 11.681 psi; total flow, 124.2 mL/min; and septum purge flow, 3 mL/min. Data were analyzed using Enhanced MSD ChemStation software (version F.01.03.2357). Extracted ion chromatograms were monitored at retention times of 15.7–15.9 min (m/z 270.3) for C16 palmitate and 16.3–16.5 min (m/z 284.3) for the C17 internal standard. Peak abundances were integrated, the sum of corrected areas was calculated, and percentage enrichment was determined. Eight biological samples were analyzed per group.

Fatty acid oxidation

Fatty acid oxidation (FAO) was performed using [3H]palmitate as a substrate, as previously described20. In brief, primary hepatocytes were isolated and cultured in 24-well dishes using DMEM media. The media was removed, and the cells were washed with PBS. Serum-free media containing 200 µM palmitate and 0.5% fatty acid-free BSA was added to each well. 0.5 μCi [3H]palmitate was added to each well. The plates were incubated for 2 h. A 100 µl of the supernatant was removed from each well and added to 0.9 ml of charcoal slurry. The mixture was incubated at room temperature for 30 min. The samples were centrifuged at 13,000 rpm for 15 min. A 200 µl of the supernatant was collected to measure radioactivity using a liquid scintillation counter.

Statistical analysis

All results are presented as means ± SD. All studies were independently repeated at least once, yielding similar results, and measurements were taken from distinct biological samples. Data analyses were conducted using GraphPad Prism 10 software, with statistical significance set at P < 0.05. P-values were calculated using the two-tailed, unpaired Student’s t test for normally distributed data, and the Mann-Whitney rank sum test for non-normally distributed data. One- or two-way ANOVA with a Tukey correction for multiple comparisons was performed to compare more than two groups. Sample size was based on statistical analysis of variance and prior experience with similar studies.
