Dose-response relationship between early pregnancy blood pressure and gestational diabetes mellitus based on propensity score matching: a retrospective study
Authors: Rongrong Han, Yanqiang Guo, Minqiang Zhang, Jinhua Pan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is a common pregnancy complication with substantial short- and long-term consequences for mothers and offspring. Whether modest elevations in blood pressure (BP) during early pregnancy are associated with GDM risk, particularly in a dose-response fashion, remains unclear. We examined the association between early pregnancy BP and GDM in a Chinese community-based cohort using propensity score matching (PSM) and restricted cubic spline (RCS) analyses.MethodsThis retrospective study included 11,338 pregnant women who registered for antenatal care in 2020 at eight community health service centers in Linping District, Hangzhou. All women underwent a 75 g oral glucose tolerance test at 24–28 gestational weeks and were classified as having GDM (n = 1,212) or not according to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups criteria. Early pregnancy BP, measured at the first antenatal visit (< 13 gestational weeks), was categorized into low, normal and high groups based on the 10th and 90th percentiles of the BP distribution. PSM (1:3 nearest-neighbor matching without replacement, caliper 0.2) was applied to balance gestational age at registration, residence, parity and cesarean section history between GDM and non-GDM groups. Conditional logistic regression and RCS models were then used in the matched cohort to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and characterize dose-response relationships between systolic BP (SBP), diastolic BP (DBP) and GDM risk.ResultsOverall, 1,212 (10.69%) women developed GDM. PSM yielded a matched cohort of 1,212 women with GDM and 3,636 without GDM, with all standardized mean differences for matching covariates below 0.10, indicating excellent baseline balance. In this emulated case-control setting, conditional logistic regression further adjusted for maternal age, early pregnancy body mass index and number of previous abortions showed that women in the high BP group had more than twice the odds of GDM compared with those in the low BP group (adjusted OR = 2.14, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.26–3.63). RCS analyses revealed a linear dose-response relationship between SBP and GDM (P-overall < 0.001, P-nonlinear = 0.232), with the odds of GDM increasing by 10% for every 10 mmHg increase in SBP (OR per 10 mmHg = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.17), and a nonlinear association for DBP (P-overall < 0.001, P-nonlinear = 0.008), with risk rising when DBP exceeded 80 mmHg. Associations were broadly consistent across all subgroups.ConclusionsElevated BP in early pregnancy is independently associated with an increased risk of GDM. By combining PSM with RCS modeling, this study demonstrates that even modest BP elevations, including values near the conventionally normal range, are linked to higher GDM risk. Early pregnancy BP may therefore serve as a simple, routinely available marker for identifying women at high risk and informing early prevention strategies for GDM.

Background

Gestational diabetes mellitus (GDM) is defined as any degree of glucose intolerance with onset or first recognition during pregnancy and is one of the most common metabolic complications of pregnancy [1]. GDM is associated with a wide spectrum of adverse maternal and neonatal outcomes, including hypertensive disorders of pregnancy, cesarean delivery, macrosomia and neonatal hypoglycemia, and it substantially increases the long-term risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiometabolic disease for both mothers and their offspring [2–4]. Consequently, early identification of women at high risk of GDM has become a key priority in maternal and child health care.

Since the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) diagnostic criteria were introduced and widely adopted in 2011, the reported prevalence of GDM has risen markedly worldwide, although considerable regional variation persists [5, 6]. Recent epidemiological studies in China have suggested that the prevalence of GDM may vary substantially across regions, and in some areas it has been reported to be as high as 24.24% [7]. These trends likely reflect both changes in diagnostic standards and shifts in maternal demographic characteristics, underscoring the need to clarify modifiable risk factors that may facilitate the early prevention of GDM.

GDM is typically diagnosed at 24–28 weeks of gestation, when pregnancy-induced insulin resistance peaks and pancreatic β-cell compensatory capacity may become insufficient [8]. Established risk factors for GDM include advanced maternal age (AMA), pre-pregnancy overweight or obesity and excessive gestational weight gain [9, 10]. The International Federation of Gynecology and Obstetrics (FIGO) defines AMA as maternal age ≥ 35 years, and the proportion of pregnancies in women of advanced age has been increasing rapidly worldwide [8]. Numerous studies have demonstrated that AMA is associated with a higher risk of adverse pregnancy outcomes and metabolic complications [9, 10]. Similarly, pre-pregnancy overweight, obesity and excessive gestational weight gain have been consistently identified as major risk factors for GDM [11–14].

In addition to these traditional metabolic risk factors, emerging evidence indicates that maternal blood pressure (BP) may also play an important role in the development of GDM [15, 16]. Several studies have reported that women with chronic hypertension or prehypertension before or early in pregnancy are at increased risk of GDM compared with normotensive women [17]. Physiologically, BP during normal pregnancy follows a characteristic U-shaped pattern, with a decline from early pregnancy to the mid-second trimester followed by a gradual rise towards term [18]. However, most previous studies have relied on conventional BP categories or thresholds, which may fail to capture the potential impact of subtle BP elevations within the clinically “normal” range during early pregnancy [15–18].

Given that BP levels are usually lower in early pregnancy than in later gestation, even modest increases above an individual woman’s expected BP trajectory may reflect underlying vascular or metabolic dysregulation. Whether such early pregnancy BP elevations, including values that still fall within the normal range, are associated with subsequent GDM risk remains unclear, particularly among women of advanced maternal age or with overweight or obesity. Clarifying this relationship could provide important insights for early risk stratification and for the prevention of GDM using a simple, routinely measured clinical indicator.

Therefore, we conducted a retrospective study of pregnant women receiving community-based antenatal care in Linping District, Hangzhou, China. Using propensity score matching and restricted cubic spline analyses, we aimed to quantify the dose-response relationship between early pregnancy BP and the risk of GDM. Our findings are expected to provide evidence for the early identification of women at high risk of GDM and to inform BP management strategies in early pregnancy.

Research Design and Methods

Study population

This retrospective observational study was conducted among pregnant women who registered for antenatal care at eight community health service centers in Linping District, Hangzhou, Zhejiang Province, China, between January 1 and December 31, 2020. In China, pregnant women are routinely encouraged to establish antenatal care records at community health centers in early pregnancy and to receive standardized follow-up throughout gestation. For the present analysis, we included women with a singleton pregnancy who completed their first antenatal visit before 13 gestational weeks and had complete registration and antenatal examination records. We excluded women with pre-existing diabetes, chronic hypertension, cardiovascular disease, anemia, chronic liver or kidney disease, or severe genetic disorders.

Because our institution does not have an independent ethics committee, the study protocol was reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of the First People’s Hospital of Linping District [Approval No. 2021 (039)]. All procedures were conducted in accordance with the Declaration of Helsinki, and the requirement for informed consent was waived owing to the retrospective use of de-identified data.

Data collection

Data were extracted from the Hangzhou Health Information System for Primary Health Care, which prospectively records demographic characteristics, obstetric history and antenatal examination results for all registered pregnant women. Baseline information collected at the first antenatal visit included maternal age, residence, gravidity, parity, history of abortion and cesarean section, and gestational age at registration. Gestational age at registration was recorded in completed weeks based on the last menstrual period and/or early ultrasound examination, as documented in the medical records.

Height and weight in early pregnancy were measured by trained nurses using standardized procedures with calibrated equipment, with participants wearing light indoor clothing and no shoes. Early pregnancy body mass index (BMI) was calculated as weight (kg) divided by height squared (m2). BP was measured in the sitting position after at least 5 min of rest using an automated sphygmomanometer appropriate for adult cuffs. If the initial measurement showed systolic BP (SBP) ≥ 140 mmHg and/or diastolic BP (DBP) ≥ 90 mmHg, a repeat measurement was performed after an additional 30 min of rest, and the confirmed value was recorded in the health information system.

Definition of exposure variables

Early pregnancy BP was defined as the SBP and DBP measured at the first antenatal visit before 13 gestational weeks. Because BP levels in early pregnancy tend to be lower and exhibit a narrower distribution than in later pregnancy, we categorized BP using percentiles of the early pregnancy BP distribution in the overall study population. Specifically, we first calculated the 10th and 90th percentiles of SBP and DBP separately. Women with both SBP < the 10th percentile (95 mmHg) and DBP < the 10th percentile (60 mmHg) were classified as the low BP (l-BP) group. Among the remaining women, those with SBP ≥ the 90th percentile (123 mmHg) or DBP ≥ the 90th percentile (80 mmHg) were classified as the high BP (h-BP) group, and all others were classified as the normal BP (n-BP) group.

Maternal age was treated as a continuous variable and, where applicable, categorized into clinically relevant groups (e.g., < 35 and ≥ 35 years) for subgroup analyses. Early pregnancy BMI was categorized according to the World Health Organization criteria for adults as underweight (< 18.5 kg/m2), normal weight (18.5–24.9 kg/m2), overweight (25.0–29.9 kg/m2) and obese (≥ 30.0 kg/m2) [19]. Obstetric history variables, including gravidity, parity, number of previous abortions and history of cesarean section, were extracted from the registration records.

Outcome definition

GDM was diagnosed between 24 and 28 gestational weeks using a standard 75 g oral glucose tolerance test (OGTT). According to the International Association of Diabetes and Pregnancy Study Groups (IADPSG) criteria [20], GDM was defined as having one or more of the following plasma glucose values at or above the specified thresholds: fasting ≥ 5.1 mmol/L, 1-hour ≥ 10.0 mmol/L or 2-hour ≥ 8.5 mmol/L. All OGTTs were performed and interpreted by trained clinicians in accordance with routine clinical practice, and the results were recorded in the health information system.

Propensity score matching

Given the observational nature of this study and the substantial differences in baseline characteristics between women with and without GDM, we used propensity score matching (PSM) to reduce confounding by measured variables. A multivariable logistic regression model was fitted to estimate each woman’s propensity score, defined as the probability of being diagnosed with GDM given her baseline characteristics. Covariates included in the propensity score model were gestational age at registration, residence, parity, and history of cesarean section.

Women with GDM were then matched to women without GDM at a ratio of 1:3 using a nearest-neighbor matching algorithm [21] without replacement, with a caliper width of 0.2 of the standard deviation of the logit of the propensity score. After matching, we evaluated covariate balance between the GDM and non-GDM groups using standardized mean differences (SMDs), with an absolute SMD < 0.10 considered indicative of adequate balance. The distributions of propensity scores before and after matching were additionally examined using jitter plots and histograms to visually assess overlap between groups. All subsequent regression analyses were conducted in the matched cohort.

Statistical analysis

Categorical variables were summarized as numbers and percentages, and continuous variables as means with standard deviations or medians with interquartile ranges, as appropriate. Baseline characteristics were compared between women with and without GDM using the chi-square test for categorical variables. After PSM, descriptive statistics were repeated in the matched cohort to confirm baseline comparability between groups.

In the matched cohort, we used conditional logistic regression models stratified by matched sets to estimate odds ratios (ORs) and 95% confidence intervals (CIs) for the associations between BP categories (l-BP, n-BP and h-BP) and the risk of GDM. Model 1 was unadjusted, while Model 2 was further adjusted for maternal age, early pregnancy BMI and number of abortions. To explore potential dose-response relationships between continuous BP measures and GDM risk, restricted cubic spline (RCS) terms for SBP and DBP were specified within the conditional logistic regression models, with three knots placed at the 5th, 50th and 95th percentiles of the BP distributions [22].

Subgroup analyses were conducted, and multiplicative interaction terms were introduced to assess potential effect modification. As a sensitivity analysis, multivariable logistic regression models with similar covariate adjustment and RCS specifications were applied to the full cohort without PSM to evaluate the robustness of the findings. All statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics (version 22.0; IBM Corp., Armonk, NY, USA) and R software (version 4.4.1; R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria), and a two-sided P value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study population

A total of 11,338 pregnant women were included in the final analysis (Fig. 1), of whom 1,212 (10.69%) were diagnosed with GDM. The median maternal age was 29.0 years (interquartile range [IQR]: 6.0), with ages ranging from 15 to 49 years. The median early pregnancy BMI was 21.2 kg/m2 (IQR: 3.9); 10.57% of women were underweight, 75.92% had normal weight, 11.44% were overweight and 2.07% were obese. Overall, 48.09% of participants were primiparas and 51.91% were multiparas. Regarding place of residence, 40.55% of women lived in Hangzhou, 8.86% lived in other cities within Zhejiang Province and 50.60% were from other provinces.

Association between early pregnancy BP and GDM after matching

Conditional logistic regression analyses were conducted in the propensity score-matched cohort to examine the association between early pregnancy BP and GDM (Table 2). In unadjusted models, higher SBP, higher DBP and higher overall BP categories were each associated with increased odds of GDM compared with their corresponding low BP groups. After additional adjustment for maternal age, early pregnancy BMI and number of abortions, women in the high BP group had more than twice the odds of developing GDM compared with those in the low BP group (adjusted OR = 2.14, 95% CI: 1.26–3.63). For DBP categories, participants in the high DBP group also showed a significantly elevated risk of GDM compared with those in the low DBP group (adjusted OR = 1.72, 95% CI: 1.26–2.37). In contrast, the association between SBP categories and GDM risk was attenuated after adjustment and did not reach statistical significance.

Dose-response relationship between early pregnancy BP and GDM

Restricted cubic spline analyses based on conditional logistic regression were performed in the matched cohort to explore the dose-response relationships between early pregnancy BP and GDM risk (Fig. 3). After adjustment for maternal age, early pregnancy BMI and number of abortions, SBP showed a linear positive association with GDM risk (P-overall < 0.001, P-nonlinear = 0.232). Using 123 mmHg (the 90th percentile) as the reference value, the odds of GDM increased by 10% for every 10 mmHg increase in SBP (OR per 10 mmHg increase = 1.10, 95% CI: 1.03–1.17). In contrast, DBP exhibited a nonlinear association with GDM risk (P-overall < 0.001, P-nonlinear = 0.008). As shown in Fig. 3B, the odds of GDM remained relatively stable at lower DBP levels and increased gradually when DBP exceeded the reference value of 80 mmHg, whereas no substantial increase in risk was observed at lower DBP levels.

Subgroup analyses

Subgroup analyses were conducted to assess the consistency of the association between early pregnancy BP and GDM across strata (Fig. 4). Across all subgroups, higher BP levels were consistently associated with an increased risk of GDM. No statistically significant interactions were observed between BP categories and subgroups (all P for interaction > 0.05), indicating that the association between early pregnancy BP and GDM risk was generally consistent across subgroups.

Sensitivity analyses

Sensitivity analyses were performed using multivariable logistic regression models with restricted cubic splines in the full cohort of 11,338 women to evaluate the robustness of the findings (Fig. 5). After adjustment for all covariates, SBP remained linearly associated with GDM risk (P-overall < 0.001, P-nonlinear = 0.295), whereas DBP continued to demonstrate a nonlinear association (P-overall < 0.001, P-nonlinear = 0.005). Each 10 mmHg increase in SBP was associated with a 17% increase in the odds of GDM (OR = 1.17, 95% CI: 1.02–1.34). These findings were consistent with the results obtained from the propensity score-matched analyses.

Discussion

In this large retrospective study based on community-level antenatal care data, we found that elevated BP in early pregnancy was significantly associated with an increased risk of GDM. After controlling for major confounders using PSM, women in the n-BP and h-BP groups had approximately 1.7-fold and 2.1-fold higher odds of developing GDM, respectively, compared with those in the l-BP group. Moreover, dose-response analyses showed a linear relationship between SBP and GDM risk, whereas DBP exhibited a nonlinear association, with the risk of GDM increasing notably when SBP and DBP exceeded approximately 123/80 mmHg. These findings indicate that even modest elevations in BP during early pregnancy, including levels within or near the conventionally “normal” range, may have important implications for GDM risk.

The observed pattern that BP values at or above 120/80 mmHg in early pregnancy are associated with higher odds of GDM echoes the general concept proposed in the American Heart Association/American College of Cardiology (AHA/ACC) guidelines [23], in which elevated BP is linked to increased cardiovascular risk in the general population. Although the present study was not designed to evaluate cardiovascular outcomes, our results suggest that BP management in early pregnancy may have broader implications for both maternal and offspring cardiometabolic health, and they highlight the potential importance of incorporating BP monitoring into early risk stratification strategies for GDM.

Our findings are broadly consistent with previous studies reporting a positive association between elevated BP before or during early pregnancy and the subsequent development of GDM. Prior observational and cohort studies have demonstrated that women with hypertension or prehypertension in early pregnancy are more likely to develop GDM in mid-to-late pregnancy [24–28]. In addition, studies examining BP trajectories across gestation have shown that upward BP trends are associated with impaired glucose metabolism and the development of GDM [29, 30]. By extending these observations, our study suggests that even BP levels within the conventionally normal range, when considered in the context of early pregnancy physiology, may carry an increased risk of GDM.

An important methodological strength of this study lies in the combined use of PSM [31] and RCS modeling [32]. Because BP physiologically declines during early pregnancy and the distribution is narrower than in later gestation, conventional clinical BP cutoffs may not adequately capture subtle risk gradients. By categorizing BP according to population-specific percentiles and simultaneously modeling SBP and DBP as continuous variables, we were able to detect dose-response patterns that might have been obscured by dichotomous or categorical classifications alone. This approach provides a more nuanced understanding of how early pregnancy BP relates to GDM risk and helps to identify potential thresholds for closer monitoring.

The biological mechanisms linking elevated BP in early pregnancy to GDM are likely multifactorial. Elevated BP is closely associated with endothelial dysfunction, oxidative stress, chronic low-grade inflammation and insulin resistance, all of which play central roles in the pathophysiology of GDM [33]. Increased vascular resistance and impaired endothelial function may reduce insulin-mediated glucose uptake in skeletal muscle and other tissues, thereby exacerbating pregnancy-induced insulin resistance [34, 35]. These shared pathways provide a plausible biological explanation for the observed associations between higher early pregnancy BP and increased GDM risk.

Notably, the association between early pregnancy BP and GDM risk was consistent across subgroups stratified by maternal age and early pregnancy BMI, and no significant interaction effects were observed. This suggests that elevated BP may act as an independent risk marker for GDM, irrespective of traditional high-risk characteristics such as advanced maternal age or overweight/obesity. From a public health perspective, these results underscore the potential value of routine BP monitoring in early pregnancy as a simple, low-cost and widely available screening tool for identifying women at increased risk of GDM, rather than one limited to selected high-risk groups.

In summary, this study demonstrates a significant association between elevated BP in early pregnancy and increased odds of GDM, and it characterizes distinct dose–response patterns for SBP and DBP. The findings support the potential role of early pregnancy BP as a simple and informative marker for GDM risk stratification. Future prospective studies are warranted to confirm these associations, to explore the impact of BP trajectories across pregnancy, and to determine optimal BP thresholds and management strategies for GDM prevention in pregnant women.

Limitations

Several limitations of this study should be acknowledged. First, BP was measured at a single time point in early pregnancy, which may not fully capture within-person BP variability or transient fluctuations (e.g., white-coat effects), potentially leading to non-differential misclassification of exposure. Second, the study population was derived from pregnant women receiving antenatal care at community health service centers in a single district, and those who registered may differ from women who did not register in terms of socioeconomic status or health behaviors; therefore, the generalizability of our findings to other populations and healthcare settings may be limited. Third, although PSM was applied to control for major measured confounders, residual confounding from unmeasured factors such as family history of diabetes, dietary patterns and physical activity cannot be completely excluded. Consequently, causal inferences should be made with caution, and our findings should be interpreted as evidence of association rather than proof of causation.

Conclusion

In this retrospective study of pregnant women receiving community-based antenatal care, elevated BP in early pregnancy was significantly associated with increased odds of GDM. Using PSM to construct a well-balanced matched cohort and RCS modeling to characterize dose–response patterns, we found that SBP was linearly associated with GDM risk, whereas DBP showed a nonlinear relationship, with risk increasing notably when BP exceeded approximately 123/80 mmHg. These findings suggest that even modest elevations in early pregnancy BP, including values near the conventionally normal range, may serve as a simple and informative marker for identifying women at higher risk of GDM. Incorporating routine BP monitoring in early pregnancy into comprehensive risk assessment frameworks may help to facilitate earlier identification and targeted prevention of GDM. Future prospective and interventional studies are warranted to confirm these associations, to explore BP trajectories across gestation and to determine optimal BP thresholds and management strategies for reducing GDM risk.
