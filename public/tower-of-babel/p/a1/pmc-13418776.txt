CT and MRI features of renal inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor and its differential diagnosis from clear cell renal cell carcinoma and chromophobe renal cell carcinoma: a study of 13 cases from two centers
Authors: Hongyang Du, Jingyun Wu, Qi Shen, Chang Rong, Kexin Wang, Jing Liu, Jianxing Qiu, He Wang, Naishan Qin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundTo compare the CT and MRI features of renal inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) with those of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and chromophobe renal cell carcinoma (ChRCC).MethodsClinical and imaging data of 13 patients with pathologically confirmed renal IMT, along with 52 patients with ccRCC and 52 with ChRCC treated at two centers between January 2013 and February 2025 were retrospectively obtained, and the lesions’ location, shape, margin, size, secondary changes, density or signal characteristics, and enhancement degree with pattern were analyzed. The clear cell likelihood score (ccLS) was used for the evaluation of MRI characteristics.ResultsThe renal IMT cohort comprised 9 males and 4 females (mean age, 54.2 ± 8.61 years). All lesions were solitary, measuring 1.7–14.4 cm. Notably, ill-defined margins were detected in 9/13 (69.2%), perirenal fascia thickening in 8/13 (61.5%), and non-enhancing region in 3/13 (23.1%). There was no hemorrhage, central scar or calcification. Unenhanced CT showed a mean attenuation of 43.7 HU. On contrast-enhanced CT, 77.8% (7/9) exhibited mild-moderate enhancement, with 55.6% (5/9) showing progressive enhancement and 33.3% (3/9) demonstrating a “slow-in and slow-out” pattern. MRI revealed hypointense or isointense signal on T1WI and T2WI, while 87.5% (7/8) showed restricted diffusion on DWI and arterial-to-delayed enhancement ratio < 1.5. No lesions exhibited microscopic fat or segmental enhancement inversion. The ccLS for renal IMTs were predominantly 1–2. Compared to ccRCCs and ChRCCs, renal IMTs more commonly exhibited ill-defined margins (P1 = 0.037, P2 < 0.001), perirenal fascia thickening (P1 = 0.014, P2 < 0.001) and higher unenhanced CT attenuation (P1 = 0.016, P2 = 0.030). Two renal IMT cases showed suspected postoperative recurrence or metastasis.ConclusionsCT and MRI imaging features can facilitate the diagnosis of renal IMT and differentiate it from ccRCC and ChRCC.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12894-026-02136-y.

Background

Inflammatory myofibroblastic tumor (IMT) is a rare soft tissue neoplasm that presents a continuously evolving landscape since its first description. In 1939, Brunn first described an IMT in the lung, designating it as a distinct inflammatory lesion [1]. Later on, terms—such as inflammatory pseudotumor, plasma cell granuloma, and pseudotumorous myofibroblastic proliferation—have been coined, which indicates early disagreements regarding its true nature [2]. Recent advances in pathology have led to the characterization of IMT as a distinct neoplastic condition, and in 2002, the World Health Organization (WHO) formally defined it as “a mesenchymal tumor composed of differentiated myofibroblastic spindle cells with a prominent plasma cell and/or lymphocyte infiltrate,” a definition that has been retained in later WHO updates [3].

The most common site of IMT is the lungs, followed by the abdominal cavity and retroperitoneum. Notably, IMT affecting the urinary system, particularly the kidneys, is exceedingly rare [4]. It has been reported that the clinical presentation and imaging features of renal IMT often mimic those of renal cell carcinoma (RCC) [2, 5, 6], which increases the rate of misdiagnosis. Considering that some IMTs may not require surgical intervention [7], accurate diagnosis is critical to avoid overtreatment. This study aimed to comprehensively analyze and compare the CT and MRI features of renal IMT with those of clear cell renal cell carcinoma (ccRCC) and chromophobe renal cell carcinoma (ChRCC), to improve the accuracy of preoperative diagnosis.

Methods

Patients

We retrospectively collected the clinical and imaging data of patients treated at Peking University First Hospital and the First Affiliated Hospital of Anhui Medical University from January 2013 to December 2024. The following inclusion criteria were applied: (1) those with pathologically confirmed diagnosis of renal IMT; (2) received preoperative CT or MRI with clear image quality; (3) availability complete clinical records, including sex, age, medical history, and treatment details. A total of 13 renal IMT cases from 13 patients were included. To ensure a balanced comparison, 52 ccRCC and 52 ChRCC cases meeting the same inclusion criteria were randomly selected in a blinded manner from these two centers, achieving a 1:4 ratio for renal IMT to each control group, with strict matching of preoperative imaging modalities to align the distribution of CT-only, MRI-only and combined CT-MRI examinations in control groups with that in the renal IMT group.

Imaging protocols

The CT examination was conducted using multi-detector spiral CT systems (GE Discovery 750 HD, Siemens Somatom Definition Flash, Philips Brilliance iCT 256 or GE Revolution 256). Nonionic iodinated contrast-enhanced CT was performed with iopromide (0.5 g iodine/kg or fixed 90 mL dose), administered at 3.0 mL/s followed by a 20 mL saline flush. Triple-phase enhanced images were acquired at 25–30 s (corticomedullary phase), 90–110 s (nephrographic phase), and 3–5 min (delayed phase) post-injection. To ensure data consistency, we normalized the voxel size (pixel spacing) to 1:1:1 for isotropic images across all scans and standardized the slice thickness for all reconstructed images. The MRI examination was conducted using 3.0 T scanners (GE Discovery 750, Siemens MAGNETOM Skyra or Philips Ingenia) coupled with an abdominal coil. Contrast-enhanced imaging was conducted using a gadolinium-based contrast agent, administered at a dose of 0.2 mL/kg body weight and the injection rate of 1.5 mL/s. The dynamic contrast-enhanced phase included corticomedullary phase (30 s after contrast injection), nephrographic phase (70 s after contrast injection), and delayed phase (180 s after contrast injection). The detailed scanning parameters of CT and MRI are provided in Table S1 − 2.

Imaging analysis

The acquired images were examined by two radiologists (Hongyang Du and Jingyun Wu) with 3 years and 13 years of diagnostic experience in urinary system diseases respectively. They were blinded to the final diagnosis. Interobserver agreement for key qualitative features was assessed using Cohen’s kappa coefficient. Discrepancies were resolved by a third senior radiologist (He Wang) to obtain a final decision. The following imaging features of the lesions were evaluated: (1) side, location within the kidney, shape, boundary, and size; (2) presence of hemorrhage, non-enhancing region, perirenal fascia thickening, enlarged lymph nodes, tumor thrombus, and central scar; (3) unenhanced CT value, presence of calcification, enhancement degree, and enhancement pattern; (4) the signal intensity on T1WI, T2WI, and DWI, degree of enhancement, presence of microscopic fat and segmental enhancement inversion (SEI), and determination of the arterial-to-delayed enhancement ratio (ADER). The characteristics of lesions on MRI were evaluated based on the Clear Cell Likelihood Score (ccLS), a newly proposed scoring system in recent years for solid renal masses. It is graded on a Likert scale from 1 to 5, with higher scores indicating a greater likelihood of the lesion being ccRCC [8]. We determined the score for each lesion on MRI, and the diagnostic algorithm for ccLS is illustrated in Fig. S1 [9]. The measurement methods for lesion size and enhancement degree, as well as the drawing methods for the region of interest (ROI) on CT, are detailed in supplementary material.

Surgery, pathology, and follow-up

Among the 13 eligible cases, 11 underwent surgical resection, while 2 received percutaneous biopsy. Other intraoperative observations included tumor location, morphology, size, margin definition, extent of involvement, and secondary changes. The 13 lesions were histopathologically evaluated through hematoxylin and eosin staining, as well as immunohistochemical (IHC) analysis. The IHC panel included vimentin (Vim), smooth muscle actin (SMA), actin, CD34, CD68, CD117, S-100, and Ki-67, among others. Post-treatment imaging follow-up was reviewed to record cases of tumor recurrence or metastasis.

Statistical analysis

The data were statistically analyzed using R version 4.5.1 (https://www.r-project.org/). Variables that showed a normal distribution were expressed as the mean ± standard deviation. The independent samples t-test was employed to compare values between two groups. Non-normally distributed data were presented as medians (upper and lower quartiles), and group comparisons were performed using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as counts and percentages, with group comparisons performed using χ2 tests when all expected cell frequencies ≥ 1 and ≥ 80% of cells had expected frequencies ≥ 5; otherwise, the Fisher’s exact test was applied (particularly when any expected frequency < 1, > 20% of cells had expected frequencies < 5, or for small sample sizes n < 20). A two-sided P-value < 0.05 was considered statistically significant in all tests.

Results

Clinical characteristics of participants

The overall clinical characteristics of the included 13 patients with renal IMT are presented in Table 1. There were 9 males and 4 females, with a mean age of 54.2 ± 8.61 years (range: 36–66 years). The major manifestations were abdominal or flank pain in 6 patients, urinary frequency in 1, hypertension in 1, lower extremity edema in 1, and weight loss in 1. Three patients were asymptomatic and laboratory tests confirmed elevated inflammatory markers in 5 patients. The imaging protocol comprised contrast-enhanced CT in 5 patients, contrast-enhanced MRI in 4, and both modalities in 4.

The preoperative diagnoses were as follows: RCC in 4 cases, urothelial carcinoma in 2, mesenchymal-origin tumor in 2, lymphoma in 1, and nonspecific malignancy in 2. Only 2 cases (15.4%) were correctly diagnosed as IMT before surgery.

CT and MRI findings

The interobserver agreement was good to excellent, with kappa values ranging from 0.72 to 0.84. All 13 renal IMT lesions presented as solitary, with 7 located in the right kidney and 6 in the left. Among them, 6 were perinephric, 4 were within the renal parenchyma, and 3 were centered around the renal pelvis. Moreover, the lesions appeared oval in 3 cases, round in 2, and lobulated in 8. Notably, 9 lesions presented with ill-defined margins (Fig. 1). The median lesion size was 6.1 cm (range: 1.7–14.4 cm), with non-enhancing region detected among 3 lesions, perirenal fascia thickening in 8 (Fig. 1), enlarged perilesional lymph nodes in 1, and tumor thrombus in the ipsilateral renal vein or inferior vena cava in 2. The analysis found no case of hemorrhage or calcification. On CT imaging, the mean unenhanced attenuation of 9 lesions was 43.7 HU (range: 36–53 HU). In the corticomedullary phase of contrast-enhanced scans, 1 lesion showed no enhancement, 3 presented with mild enhancement, 4 exhibited moderate enhancement, and 1 showed intense enhancement. Except for the one lesion with intense corticomedullary-phase enhancement, which exhibited a “wash-in and wash-out” enhancement pattern, the remaining 8 lesions exhibited progressive enhancement in the nephrographic phase. However, the degree of enhancement was lower compared with that of normal renal cortex. During the delayed phase, 5 lesions demonstrated progressive enhancement, while 3 exhibited mild washout (slow-in and slow-out) (Fig. 1). Delayed-phase attenuation values ranged from 64 to 109 HU, with a mean increase of 36.8 HU compared with unenhanced CT scans. On MRI, T1WI revealed isointensity in 6 lesions (Fig. 2) and hypointensity in 2 (Fig. 3), T2WI revealed hypointensity in 6 lesions (Fig. 2) and isointensity in 2 (Fig. 3), DWI revealed hyperintensity in 7 lesions with decreased signal on apparent diffusion coefficient (ADC) map (Figs. 2 and 3) and isointensity in 1. No lesions exhibited microscopic fat or SEI. During the corticomedullary phase of contrast-enhanced scanning, 5 lesions exhibited mild enhancement, 1 showed moderate enhancement, and 2 presented intense enhancement (Fig. 2). The aforementioned lesion with a “wash-in and wash-out” enhancement pattern on CT had an ADER ≥ 1.5, while the remaining 7 lesions had an ADER < 1.5 (Fig. 2). For the ccLS, half of the total (4 lesions) were rated as ccLS1, and no lesion was classified as ccLS5.

Clinical and imaging features of renal IMT compared with ccRCC and ChRCC

The comparison of clinical and imaging features between renal IMT, ccRCC and ChRCC is shown in Table 2. Further analysis revealed that the ill-defined lesion margins tended to be more frequent in renal IMTs relative to ccRCCs (9/13 [69.2%] vs. 17/52 [32.7%]; P = 0.037). Similarly, perirenal fascia thickening was frequently observed in renal IMTs (8/13 [61.5%] vs. 11/52 [21.2%]; P = 0.014). In contrast, hemorrhage (18/52 [34.6%] vs. 0/13 [0%]; P = 0.013) and non-enhancing region (40/52 [76.9%] vs. 3/13 [23.1%]; P < 0.001) were significantly more common in ccRCCs than in renal IMTs. Similar to ccRCCs, ChRCCs also less commonly present ill-defined lesion margins (2/52 [3.8%] vs. 9/13 [69.2%]; P < 0.001) and perirenal fascia thickening (2/52 [3.8%] vs. 8/13 [61.5%]; P < 0.001) compared with renal IMTs. However, ChRCCs are smaller in size (3.00 [2.58, 4.25] cm vs. 6.10 [3.80, 9.20] cm; P = 0.003) and also rarely present tumor thrombus (0/52 [0%] vs. 2/13 [15.4%]; P = 0.038).

The median unenhanced CT value was higher in renal IMTs compared with the levels in ccRCCs (43.7 ± 4.95 HU vs. 36.8 ± 7.64 HU; P = 0.016) and ChRCC (43.7 ± 4.95 HU vs. 38.9 ± 7.24 HU; P = 0.030). On contrast-enhanced CT, renal IMTs predominantly exhibited mild (3/9 [33.3%]) to moderate (4/9 [44.4%]) enhancement, showing a typical progressive enhancement pattern (5/9 [55.6%]) or a “slow-in and slow-out” pattern (3/9 [33.3%]). In contrast, there was intense enhancement (34/36 [94.4%]) in most ccRCCs with a “wash-in and wash-out” enhancement pattern (34/36 [94.4%]), which differed markedly between the groups (P < 0.001).

On MRI, heterogeneous signal intensity on T1WI was more common in ccRCCs than in renal IMTs (16/32 [50.0%] vs. 0/8 [0%]; P = 0.013), while hypointense on T2WI was significantly more common in renal IMTs (6/8 [75.0%] vs. 5/32 [15.6%]; P = 0.003). More than half of ccRCCs contain microscopic fat, whereas this feature was not observed in renal IMTs (19/32 [59.4%] vs. 0/8 [0%]; P = 0.004). On contrast-enhanced MRI, most renal IMTs showed a mild enhancement degree (5/8 [62.5%]), with ADER < 1.5 (7/8 [87.5%]), that was markedly different from that seen in ccRCCs (P < 0.001). For the final ccLS, the difference between renal IMTs and ccRCCs was statistically significant (P < 0.001), with the former having lower scores and the latter obtaining higher ones. When comparing the MRI manifestations of renal IMTs and ChRCCs, none of the aforementioned imaging characteristics demonstrated statistically significant differences (P > 0.05).

Intraoperative findings, pathological features, and follow-up results

Subsequent analyzes showed that renal IMTs generally exhibited irregular morphology with ill-defined borders and no apparent capsule intraoperatively. Some tumors adhered adjacent to the peritoneum or mesentery. Gross pathological analysis revealed that the tumors had a firm or tough consistency, with cut surfaces appearing gray-white or gray-yellow. Microscopic examination indicated that the tumors were predominantly composed of spindle cells arranged in disorganized or fascicular patterns. Some tumor cells had irregular morphology and mild atypia. The nuclei were oval with inconspicuous nucleoli, and mitotic figures were rare. A dense inflammatory infiltrate was observed throughout the tumor tissue (Figs. 2 and 3). IHC staining showed that all 13 cases tested positive for the Vim and the SMA, with some showing strong positivity (Table 1). The Ki-67 proliferation index ranged from 1% to 50%, with 9 cases showing an index of ≤ 5%, 3 cases of 10%-20%, and 1 case of 50%. Eight patients were followed up, with a median follow-up period of 13.5 months (range: 2–130 months). Among them, 6 patients had no signs of recurrence or metastasis. One 56-year-old male (Ki-67 proliferation index 20%) developed a new lesion in the surgical area one year after right nephrectomy (Fig. 2); which progressively enlarged and showed poor response to chemotherapy, leading to discontinuation of treatment. Another 66-year-old male (Ki-67 proliferation index 10%) developed new solid nodules in both lungs five months after right nephrectomy (Fig. 3). CT-guided biopsy of the lung nodules revealed several areas that were morphologically consistent with the original renal IMT (Fig. 3), and the lung nodules partially regressed following chemotherapy.

Discussion

Renal IMT was first reported by Fisch et al. in 1976 [10], and sporadic case reports and small case series have been published subsequently [2, 6, 11–13]. Liang et al. [12] retrospectively analyzed the CT imaging features of 8 cases of urinary system IMT, among which only 2 occurred in the kidney. Chen et al. [13] conducted a study involving 14 patients with renal IMT, representing the largest sample size reported to date, but their work only focused on the CT manifestations of the lesions. We retrospectively analyzed the CT and MRI findings of 13 patients with renal IMT and compared these imaging features with those of other renal malignancies, thereby providing a more comprehensive characterization of the imaging characteristics of this rare disease.

The etiology of renal IMT is not fully understood, with researchers postulating that it may occur secondary to inflammation caused by surgery, trauma, or irritation [2]. There are also some etiological links to Epstein-Barr virus and herpesvirus infections [2] or clonal rearrangements of the anaplastic lymphoma kinase (ALK) gene located on chromosome 2p23 [14]. In our study, two patients developed lesions secondary to perirenal hematoma or ccRCC surgery. Although IMT is common in children and young adults [15], most patients in our cohort were middle-aged or older, with a mean age of 54.2 ± 8.61 years, and the differences in age were not significant between renal IMT and ccRCC as well as ChRCC. Liang et al. [12] reported a male predominance in renal IMT, which is consistent with the observed male-to-female ratio of approximately 2.3:1. Clinical data show that renal IMT frequently involves the renal parenchyma and renal pelvis, but it may also affect the soft tissues adjacent to the perirenal area [16]. Common symptoms and signs in adults include renal mass (34%), pain (24%), hematuria (17%), lower urinary tract symptoms (10%), and incidental findings on imaging (17%) [17]. In the present study, 4 patients (30.8%) with lesions located in the renal pelvis and perirenal region developed secondary hydronephrosis, suggesting that the clinical manifestations of the disease mainly vary depending on the lesion’s location and size. Three patients exhibited rare paraneoplastic manifestations: hypertension (attributed to ipsilateral adrenal involvement), unilateral lower limb edema (secondary to inferior vena cava tumor thrombus), and weight loss (associated with local tumor invasiveness; the tumor had a Ki-67 proliferation index of 50%), each confirmed by correlative imaging and histopathological findings. These clinical features are not specific and are consistent with those of malignant tumors; however, the imaging findings of renal IMT still have unique features and can reflect its pathological characteristics to a certain extent.

On CT, renal IMT typically appears as an irregular soft tissue density mass with ill-defined margins, and less commonly by calcification or cystic changes [13, 18]. Here, we found that ill-defined margins and perirenal fascia thickening were more common in the renal IMT group than in the ccRCC and ChRCC, which might be linked to enhanced inflammatory response from the surrounding tissues. Conversely, ccRCC more commonly exhibits hemorrhage and non-enhancing region (potentially indicative of cystic change or necrosis), which underscores its malignant heterogeneity. The characteristic histologic features of ccRCC including necrosis and abundant intracellular lipid and glycogen deposits, also contribute to its characteristically lower attenuation values on unenhanced CT imaging [19]. Histologically, IMT is primarily composed of loosely arranged spindle cells and mixed inflammatory cells around small blood vessels [20]. Considering its higher fibrous tissue content, tighter cellular arrangement, and lower water content, renal IMT shows significantly higher CT attenuation on unenhanced scans compared to ccRCC and ChRCC. This unique pathological composition also contributes to the characteristic enhancement pattern on contrast-enhanced scans. Renal IMT characteristically exhibits delayed enhancement patterns due to prolonged contrast agent diffusion through dense collagen fibers and impaired clearance from inflammatory microvasculature. This finding is consistent with results from previous studies by Abduljawad et al. [21] and Wu et al. [11], and it contrasts with the “wash-in and wash-out” enhancement pattern seen in the hypervascular ccRCC. However, the aforementioned case of renal IMT secondary to ccRCC surgery exhibited an enhancement pattern similar to that of the primary tumor, underscoring that not all renal IMTs conform to the typical enhancement pattern. In addition, we observed that ChRCC tend to be smaller in size, with no tumor thrombus was detected. This may be attributed to the lower malignant potential and slower growth rate of ChRCC [22]. As a hypovascular tumor, ChRCC exhibits mild to moderate enhancement [22], which is similar to renal IMT and poses challenges in differential diagnosis. The central scar is present in 19%–34% of ChRCC cases [23]; although no statistically significant difference of central scar was identified between renal IMT and ChRCC in our study, this feature was absent in all renal IMT.

Previous studies [12, 13] have mainly focused on the CT features of urinary system IMTs, with limited reports on MRI findings. The ccLS system incorporates the tumor’s signal intensity, degree of enhancement, and various specific features on MRI into a standardized workflow, demonstrating moderate interobserver agreement [24], and has been applied to differentiate ccRCC from renal oncocytoma and renal epithelioid angiomyolipoma [25, 26]. Given the overlap in its core evaluation indicators, the ccLS can also be utilized for the differential diagnosis of renal IMT. In our study, renal IMT appeared isointensity or hypointensity on T1WI and T2WI, and more than half of the lesions showed only mild enhancement on contrast-enhanced scans, reflecting the presence of fibrous or collagenous tissue components within the tumor. Compared with in-phase images, no definitive decrease in signal intensity is detected on opposed-phase images, indicating the absence of microscopic fat in renal IMT. These characteristics can help to distinguish renal IMT from ccRCC, and can also explain why most renal IMTs have a ccLS score of 1–2. In dynamic contrast-enhanced MRI, ccRCC can reach an enhancement degree 1.4 times that of the renal cortex in the corticalmedullary phase, and rapidly washes out in the delayed phase [27]. Consequently, the degree of enhancement in the corticalmedullary phase is higher than that in the delayed phase, resulting in a relatively high ADER value. We propose that ADER can indirectly reflect the lesion’s enhancement pattern. The ADER value of the vast majority of renal IMTs in our study is less than 1.5, which is consistent with their characteristic of delayed enhancement and the findings observed on contrast-enhanced CT [6]. MRI is also a valuable tool for patients with iodine contrast allergies, renal insufficiency, or when assessing tumor thrombus, so routine MRI evaluation should be emphasized in clinical practice. However, the MRI manifestations of renal IMT and ChRCC show no significant statistical difference. This may be related to their similar cellular density and blood supply patterns [23], and may also be affected by the small sample size of the study. Combining CT findings can be helpful in distinguishing between the two.

Although renal IMT is generally considered a low-grade malignant tumor, radical nephrectomy remains the mainstay treatment modality due to frequent misdiagnosis as RCC. However, studies have shown that conservative treatments such as corticosteroids and broad-spectrum antibiotics have demonstrated favorable clinical effects, and there have been reports of cases involving tumor regression or even spontaneous resolution [28, 29]. Therefore, distinguishing renal IMT from other malignant renal tumors based on imaging features is of critical importance. In patients with a solitary kidney, impaired renal function, bilateral tumors, or limited surgical candidacy, preoperative biopsy for pathological confirmation is also of great clinical significance [12, 28]. IMT shows a recurrence rate of approximately 25% and no more than 5% of cases develop distant metastasis [7]. To our knowledge, no postoperative recurrence or metastasis of renal IMT has been reported to date [17, 30]. In our study, two patients developed possible recurrence and metastasis postoperatively. Previous studies have demonstrated that a relatively high Ki-67 proliferation index may indicate a greater proportion of cells in the active phases of the cell cycle, suggesting a more aggressive tumor behavior [31]. However, the association between Ki‑67 expression and recurrence or metastasis of renal IMT warrants further investigation.

This study has several limitations. First, given the relative rarity of renal IMT, even after collecting cases from two centers over an extended period, the relatively small sample size limits statistical power. The diagnostic value of ccLS for renal IMT is exploratory and requires further validation in larger, multicenter studies to confirm its accuracy and avoid misdiagnosis due to potential overlap with ChRCC. Second, as this study was retrospective in nature, complete standardization of scanners and scanning parameters across different centers was challenging. Nevertheless, this design is consistent with a real-world research setting and facilitates an objective depiction of the universal imaging features of renal IMT. Third, fluorescence in situ hybridization was not routinely performed, implying that the status of ALK gene rearrangement in patients was not confirmed, and the relationship between ALK expression and clinical course, imaging findings, and aggressive behavior remained unclarified. Last but not least, primary renal lymphoma and IgG4-related kidney disease also have overlapping imaging manifestations with renal IMT; however, these two diseases are also rare. Next, we will collect relevant cases and conduct further analysis.

Conclusions

In conclusion, imaging modalities such as CT and MRI can reflect the pathological features of renal IMT, characteristics including ill-defined margins, infrequent hemorrhage or non-enhancing region, thickening of the perirenal fascia, higher attenuation on unenhanced CT, delayed enhancement on contrast-enhanced scans, and isointensity or hypointensity on T1WI and T2WI help differentiate renal IMT from ccRCC and ChRCC. The ccLS provides a new approach for differentiating renal IMT from other malignant tumors, and this study represents an exploratory attempt to evaluate its diagnostic utility. Once diagnosed, conservative management may be attempted to avoid unnecessary surgery; additionally, monitoring for recurrence or metastasis postoperatively is also important.
