Robust intelligent modeling for developing an optimal predictive model of metal powder injection molded dental braces using fuzzy-logic-based multi-objective design
Authors: Wei-Tai Huang, Sheng-Chieh Huang, Shi-Heng Guo, Yo-Ting Lin
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveMetal Injection Molding (MIM) is an advanced manufacturing technology suitable for producing complex, small, and miniature components such as dental braces and biomedical devices. However, product warpage, deformation, and non-uniform temperature distribution during molding often compromise dimensional accuracy, assembly precision, and aesthetic quality. This study aims to optimize the MIM process for dental braces and develop accurate predictive models for quality characteristics.MethodsA robust process design integrated with fuzzy theory was employed to determine optimal process parameters for both single-objective and multi-objective quality optimization. Predictive models based on a Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN) and an Adaptive Network-based Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) were developed. Hyperparameter structures were optimized during model development to improve prediction performance and model robustness.ResultsCompared with the manufacturer’s original process parameters, single-objective optimization improved warpage and average temperature by 85.7% and 9.1%, respectively. For multi-objective optimization, the corresponding improvements were 85.7% and 7.0%. After hyperparameter optimization, the BPNN and ANFIS models achieved prediction accuracies of 96.09% and 97.11%, respectively. The ANFIS model demonstrated superior predictive capability for nonlinear process relationships while requiring less complex parameter tuning.ConclusionsThe proposed intelligent modeling framework effectively improves process quality and provides accurate prediction of key quality characteristics, thereby offering a promising approach for intelligent process optimization and potentially reducing experimental effort during MIM process development. The results demonstrate that ANFIS provides a robust and accurate prediction approach for dental-brace MIM applications and offers significant potential for intelligent process optimization in advanced manufacturing.Keywords: intelligent modeling method, Metal Injection Molding (MIM), dental braces, robust design, fuzzy inference systems, Multiple Performance Characteristic Index (MPCI), Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN), Adaptive Network Based Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS)

Introduction

Dental braces, also referred to as orthodontic braces or colloquially as braces, are devices used in orthodontic treatment to align and straighten teeth into proper occlusal alignment. These appliances are typically employed to correct malocclusions such as misaligned teeth, overbite, underbite, or other irregularities in dental alignment. The primary objectives of orthodontic treatment are aesthetic improvement and functional correction. In recent years, the number of individuals undergoing orthodontic treatment has been steadily increasing. Among the various types of braces currently available, stainless steel alloy brackets are the most commonly used. These brackets are fabricated by incorporating stainless steel alloy into an adhesive base, resulting in a product that is durable, pressure-resistant, and mechanically robust. Their advantages include structural precision, miniature size, and high resistance to breakage, all at a relatively lower cost compared to other types of orthodontic appliances. Due to their metallic nature, however, stainless steel brackets are more visibly noticeable when adhered to the teeth, which places higher demands on both the manufacturing process and the aesthetic quality of the final product. Given the extremely small size and intricate geometry of stainless steel orthodontic brackets, conventional manufacturing techniques face significant challenges in terms of feasibility and cost-effectiveness. As a result, Metal Injection Molding (MIM) has emerged as a preferred manufacturing method for producing such components due to its ability to fabricate complex micro-scale structures with high precision and efficiency. 1

MIM is an emerging technology developed to address the limitations of conventional powder metallurgy. By integrating the advantages of both powder metallurgy and plastic injection molding, MIM enables the fabrication of small, complex components with high mechanical strength. One of its key advantages is near-net-shape forming, which allows for the production of highly finished components with minimal secondary processing, thereby reducing manufacturing time and cost.2,3 MIM provides a rapid and cost-effective approach for the mass production of miniature, high-precision components with complex geometries. It overcomes several inherent drawbacks of traditional powder metallurgy, such as material inhomogeneity, low mechanical strength, difficulty in forming thin-walled parts, and challenges in producing intricate structural features. This makes MIM particularly suitable for the fabrication of micro-scale components that require complex shapes, compact size, and superior surface aesthetics—such as orthodontic brackets and other biomedical devices.4,5 Recent studies have also focused on tooling innovation for MIM manufacturing. Kuo et al. developed a novel tooling approach that improved manufacturing efficiency, demonstrating the continued advancement and industrial applicability of MIM technology for precision manufacturing. 6 In addition, Tadi et al. reviewed recent developments in polymer-based binder systems for MIM and emphasized that feedstock composition and binder characteristics play critical roles in molding quality and process stability. 7

However, MIM still faces several critical challenges. One of the most significant issues arises during the cooling phase of the molding process, where volumetric shrinkage and warpage deformation often occur. These phenomena can compromise the dimensional accuracy and geometric integrity of the molded components, making it difficult for orthodontic brackets to achieve their intended positioning on the teeth. This misalignment may lead to suboptimal orthodontic outcomes or malocclusion. Non-uniform temperature distribution during MIM may adversely affect cooling behavior and dimensional stability. Previous studies have suggested that excessive thermal gradients may also be associated with powder–binder separation and surface defects such as black lines. In addition to thermal gradients, the overall temperature level within the mold cavity can also influence cooling uniformity and part quality. Therefore, average temperature was selected as an optimization objective because it provides a quantitative indicator of the thermal condition during molding and can be directly obtained from CAE simulation. These black lines are caused by excessive temperature gradients during the molding process, which generate shear rate disparities between powder particles. This can result in phase separation between the metal powders and the binder system, ultimately manifesting as visible surface defects. Such black lines typically require additional post-processing to be removed, which increases both the production time and cost associated with MIM. Taken together, the issues of warpage and black line formation significantly affect both the functional performance and aesthetic quality of orthodontic brackets manufactured via MIM.8,9

Currently, Computer-Aided Design (CAD) and Computer-Aided Engineering (CAE) technologies are widely used to support product development and assist researchers in analyzing and optimizing manufacturing processes. With continuous advancements and refinements in recent years, the selection of injection molding process parameters—once primarily reliant on the trial-and-error experience of process engineers—has increasingly been replaced by CAE-based simulations. These tools enable accurate prediction and evaluation of potential molding defects prior to mold fabrication, allowing for real-time modifications and design improvements to molds or process conditions. This significantly enhances the efficiency, precision, and cost-effectiveness of process development. 10

Related studies have demonstrated that the integration of injection molding processes with various intelligent optimization methods can effectively enhance both process efficiency and product quality. Among these, robust process design has emerged as a powerful statistical experimental methodology. By employing orthogonal arrays (OAs), it enables the analysis of a large number of design variables with relatively few experimental runs, thereby facilitating efficient and accurate optimization of process parameters and quality performance. Robust design also allows for precise evaluation of the influence of different control factor levels on quality objectives. For example, Huang and Chou used CAE-based analysis in conjunction with robust design to optimize the warpage deformation of automotive components. Their selected control factors included injection time, material temperature, mold temperature, injection pressure, holding pressure, holding time, coolant type, and coolant flow rate, with warpage deformation used as the primary quality performance index. 11 After optimization, the best process parameter combination reduced warpage by 11.2% compared to the original process. In another study, Amin et al. applied robust process design to optimize the strength of stainless steel MIM components. Their experimental results indicated that cooling time was the most significant factor influencing strength performance. 12 Furthermore, Lin et al. focused on improving powder concentration and mitigating phase separation effects in the MIM process of orthodontic brackets. Their results showed that the optimal parameters for minimizing phase separation—thus reducing black line surface defects—were a filling time of 0.15 s, holding pressure of 14 MPa, material temperature of 230°C, mold temperature of 30°C, and gate size of 0.25 mm. 13

In practical manufacturing environments, it is often necessary to optimize multiple quality objectives simultaneously. However, robust process design traditionally focuses on optimizing only a single quality characteristic. In response, recent research has explored the integration of robust design with other intelligent modeling techniques to enable multi-objective optimization in manufacturing processes. A growing number of studies have combined robust design methodologies with Grey Relational Analysis (GRA) and fuzzy logic theory to identify optimal process parameter settings that account for multiple quality characteristics. For instance, Mohd applied robust process design coupled with GRA to enhance the strength and density of 316L stainless steel micro-components produced via MIM. The results indicated that injection time was the most influential control factor affecting both green part strength and density. 14 Similarly, Lin et al. addressed issues related to powder concentration uniformity and volumetric shrinkage in the MIM process for artificial knee joint components. Using robust design and GRA, they identified optimal process parameters that balanced both quality attributes effectively. 15 In another study, Huang et al. applied intelligent modeling techniques to optimize the MIM process for gear manufacturing. By combining robust design with fuzzy logic, they identified process parameters capable of improving both single and multiple quality characteristics. Their results demonstrate the potential for such methods to reduce process uncertainty, lower production costs, and significantly drive process innovation. 16 Recently, Rosli and Khor further investigated multi-factor optimization for orthodontic single brackets manufactured by metal injection molding, demonstrating that systematic process optimization can effectively improve molding quality and dimensional consistency. 17 However, their study primarily focused on process optimization and did not incorporate intelligent predictive models for simultaneous quality prediction and multi-objective decision-making.

Experimental trials play a critical role in the MIM manufacturing process. However, 18 excessive experimentation leads to significant waste of time and materials. To address this issue, this study aims to develop an accurate predictive model capable of forecasting MIM product outcomes prior to physical trials, thereby reducing unnecessary resource consumption. Currently, Back Propagation Neural Networks (BPNN) and Adaptive Network-based Fuzzy Inference Systems (ANFIS) are commonly used for predictive modeling. Nevertheless, experts hold differing views regarding the predictive accuracy of these two models. The use of a BPNN model requires careful tuning of hyperparameters. Hyperparameters are parameters defined during the model design stage and cannot be determined during training. Common hyperparameters include the number of training epochs, the number of neurons in the hidden layer, and the number of hidden layers. Proper hyperparameter configuration enables the development of a more accurate predictive model and improves its stability and predictive performance. Therefore, systematic hyperparameter tuning is essential for constructing an effective BPNN model.19,20 ANFIS integrates the interpretability of fuzzy inference systems with the self-learning capability of artificial neural networks, enabling effective representation of complex and highly nonlinear process behaviors. Compared with other modeling approaches, ANFIS can achieve rapid convergence and construct quality prediction models with high predictive accuracy even when only limited experimental data are available, making it particularly suitable for quality analysis and prediction in manufacturing process environments. Predictive models based on ANFIS also offer a certain level of accuracy.21–23 In this study, various BPNN hyperparameter combinations were compared, and the combination with the highest predictive accuracy was selected for model development. The two optimization objectives—warpage and average temperature—were incorporated into both predictive models to obtain prediction results. These predicted values were then compared with the actual optimized outcomes to evaluate the accuracy of the two predictive models and to determine which model is most suitable for MIM dental braces.

This study focuses on the application of intelligent modeling technology in MIM for the manufacturing of dental braces. A robust process design approach is employed to configure the process parameters. Robust process design is a powerful statistical method for experimental design. It utilizes orthogonal arrays (OAs) to effectively analyze the influence of multiple design variables through a relatively small number of experiments. Since an OA is a fractional factorial matrix, it ensures the balanced comparison of factors and the evaluation of their interactions at different levels24,25.

In optimizing the process parameters of MIM injection molding for the manufacturing of dental braces, the integration of fuzzy logic with MPCI in robust process design allows for the simultaneous consideration of all quality characteristics in the MIM injection molding process. Current industry practices often rely on the engineer’s field experience, which introduces several uncertainties. The fuzzy logic method used in MPCI helps minimize human-induced uncertainties and does not require complex mathematical calculations.26,27 First, robust process design is employed to identify the optimal combination of individual quality characteristics (warpage and average temperature). This method calculates a single objective and then applies grey correlation analysis to determine the grey correlation coefficient. Next, the optimal process parameters for the multi-performance characteristics of MIM injection molding in the production of dental braces are determined using fuzzy theory. Subsequently, two high-accuracy predictive models were developed: a Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN) and an Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS). Prior to constructing the BPNN model, its hyperparameters were systematically optimized to ensure the development of a highly precise predictive framework. The results obtained from the two optimization experiments were then incorporated into both predictive models, enabling a comparative assessment of their respective prediction accuracies.

Recent studies have highlighted the importance of MIM and powder metallurgy in producing high-performance metallic components, focusing mainly on material properties, sintering behavior, binder systems, tooling, microstructure, and mechanical performance. However, few studies have explored intelligent optimization and predictive modeling of MIM process parameters for orthodontic dental braces, especially under multi-quality requirements. This study aims to improve the process stability and quality of MIM dental braces, which demand high dimensional accuracy and consistent manufacturing due to their small size and clinical use. Among the key quality characteristics, warpage affects dimensional precision, while average temperature reflects thermal behavior during molding. Therefore, this study integrates robust design, grey relational analysis, fuzzy logic, BPNN, and ANFIS to develop an intelligent framework for optimizing and predicting key quality characteristics. The main contribution is a combined optimization and prediction framework that simultaneously considers warpage and average temperature, while comparing BPNN and ANFIS performance. Unlike prior studies focused on materials or single-objective optimization, this approach provides a practical solution for intelligent process optimization of MIM dental braces.

Simulation analysis and modeling methods

Overall experimental design

The experimental framework of this study consists of four main parts, as shown in the overall experimental flow chart in Figure 1. The first step involves constructing MIM dental braces and performing simulation analysis of the original injection process. This step ensures that the results from the CAE simulation analysis align with actual values. The second step applies the robust process design method to optimize a single objective, focusing on MIM dental brace warpage and average temperature. The third step utilizes grey correlation and fuzzy theory to analyze warpage and average temperature. In the final step, the results from the first and second steps are combined to identify the optimal parameter combination for the multiple quality characteristics of warpage and average temperature. In the final step, we establish two accurate predictive models, namely BPNN and ANFIS. In terms of BPNN, in addition to obtaining an accurate prediction model, the hyperparameters of BPNN are optimized before establishing the prediction model. The two optimization results (warpage and average temperature) are input into the two prediction models to compare the prediction accuracy of the two prediction models.

Overall experimental flow chart.

The optimization objectives of this study were to simultaneously minimize two critical quality characteristics of MIM dental braces: warpage deformation and average temperature. Warpage directly affects the dimensional accuracy and assembly precision of dental braces, whereas excessive average temperature may lead to non-uniform cooling, powder–binder separation, and surface defects. Since improving one quality characteristic may adversely affect the other, a multi-objective optimization approach was adopted to achieve balanced overall process performance. Rather than targeting predefined output values, the optimization aimed to minimize both responses simultaneously using the Smaller-the-Better signal-to-noise (S/N) criterion within the robust design framework. The six process parameters selected for optimization, namely injection pressure, holding pressure, filling time, material temperature, mold temperature, and gate size, were chosen because they are the primary controllable variables affecting melt flow behavior, cavity filling, heat transfer, cooling characteristics, and dimensional stability during the MIM process. These parameters have been widely reported in previous MIM studies to significantly influence warpage and temperature distribution, and their operating ranges were determined according to both the manufacturer’s production conditions and relevant literature to ensure practical applicability.

Construction of MIM dental braces

The CAE software used in this study is Moldex3D. To simulate the MIM dental braces, the sprue, runner, cooling circuit, and solid mesh were configured using Moldex3D R14.0 Designer. The solid mesh contained approximately 540,000 to 570,000 nodes in this study. The material used for the MIM dental braces is a composite consisting of 316 stainless steel powder and polypropylene (PP) binder, with a volume ratio of 60% stainless steel powder to 40% PP binder. The original process parameters, provided by the manufacturer, were used to validate the simulation results against actual measurements. The injection molding results obtained from Moldex3D were compared with the actual dimensions of the dental braces. Figures 2 and 3 show the actual image of the metal dental braces and the corresponding measurement points. To ensure the accuracy of the simulation, five measurement points (A–E) on the dental braces were measured using a tool microscope, as shown in Figure 4(a,b) and detailed in Table 1. The mean measurement error between the simulation and actual results was 0.47%, indicating good agreement and confirming the reliability of the simulation model for subsequent process analysis. Based on the validated simulation model, the warpage and average temperature predicted by Moldex3D were used as the optimization responses throughout this study under identical material properties and processing conditions. The original process parameters are as follows: injection pressure of 180 MPa, holding pressure of 100 MPa, filling time of 1.5 s, material temperature of 200°C, mold temperature of 80°C, and gate size of 0.25 mm. Robust design process

The L27 (36) orthogonal array was used to perform the experiments and to identify the factors influencing warpage and average temperature in MIM dental braces. The six main control factors are injection pressure, holding pressure, filling time, material temperature, mold temperature, and gate size, with each factor having three levels. The control factors and their levels are shown in Table 2. The injection pressure ranges from 160 to 200 MPa, holding pressure ranges from 50 to 150 MPa, filling time ranges from 1.2 to 1.8s, material temperature ranges from 190 to 210°C, mold temperature ranges from 70 to 90°C, and gate size ranges from 0.2 to 0.3 mm. Based on preliminary experiments and previous MIM studies, the interactions among the selected process parameters (injection pressure, holding pressure, filling time, material temperature, mold temperature, and gate size) were found to be insignificant compared with their main effects. Therefore, only the main effects were considered in the present study, which is consistent with the application of the Taguchi robust design methodology and enables efficient process optimization while maintaining a manageable number of experimental runs. These values are incorporated into the orthogonal array in Table 3. Moldex3D software was used to obtain 27 sets of experimental data for warpage and average temperature, and the Signal-to-Noise Ratio (S/N) for each set of experimental values was calculated. Smaller values of warpage and average temperature are more favorable. Therefore, these characteristics are considered smaller-the-better S/N ratios, as expressed in (1).

Fuzzy theory analysis

This study primarily addresses the optimization of multiple quality characteristics. Since the robust design process can only calculate a single quality characteristic, and in practice, products rarely exhibit a single defect, the robust design process is combined with grey correlation analysis and fuzzy theory to obtain the optimal parameter combination for multiple quality characteristics. The grey correlation coefficient is calculated by determining the Signal-to-Noise (S/N) values for the warpage and average temperature of the 27 experimental groups. The S/N values are normalized to scale the data between 0 and 1, as higher S/N values are more desirable. The larger-the-better characteristic is used for calculation, as expressed in (2), and the gray correlation coefficient is computed after normalization, as shown in (3). The identification coefficient ζ is typically set to 0.5. Next, fuzzy theory is applied to obtain the optimized process parameters for multiple quality characteristics. In this study, a fuzzy rule table with two inputs and one output is designed. The input variables—total warpage and average temperature—are defined with three levels: Small, Medium, and Large. The variables are categorized as Very Small (VS), Small (S), Medium (M), Large (L), and Very Large (VL), as shown in Table 4. A total of five levels are defined. Nine rules are incorporated into the MATLAB Fuzzy Tool and are defined as a fuzzy rule base. Using Mamdani’s fuzzy inference method, the optimal process parameters are determined through the defuzzification method of the center of gravity. The fuzzy rule base was established according to the optimization objective that simultaneously minimizes warpage and average temperature. The rules were formulated based on the normalized grey relational coefficients obtained from robust design and grey relational analysis, together with the monotonic relationship between the input quality characteristics and the desired MPCI output. This rule structure is consistent with the fuzzy inference methodology widely adopted in previous manufacturing optimization studies. 16

Construction of Back Propagation Neural Network (BPNN) prediction model and optimize factors and levels of hyperparameter

Using a BPNN model to accurately predict the injection molding parameters during mold trials is crucial, as it can significantly reduce both time and material waste. BPNNs enhance model architecture through linear or nonlinear transformations across multiple processing layers, thereby improving computational accuracy and pattern recognition capability. This enables the effective resolution of nonlinear and highly complex problems. The structure of the BPNN model is shown in Figure 6.

The equations for the hidden layer neurons and the output layer are provided in (4) and (5), respectively. Such a complex network architecture requires a greater number of hyperparameters, and identifying appropriate hyperparameter settings may demand substantial time. The BPNN predictive model involves many essential hyperparameters, such as the number of neurons, Mu, and epochs. Therefore, the selection of these hyperparameters must be performed through a systematic and scientifically rigorous optimization process. For the two optimization targets—warpage and average temperature—the relevant factor levels were selected based on preliminary experiments and prior research. 28 The control factors used in this study include the number of training iterations, the number of neurons in the first hidden layer, the number of neurons in the second hidden layer, and the initial momentum (Mu). The ranges for these factor levels were defined as follows: the number of training iterations between 300 and 900; the number of neurons in the first hidden layer between 5 and 25; the number of neurons in the second hidden layer between 7 and 25; and the initial momentum between 0.1 and 0.001. A comparison table of the hyperparameter factor levels used for optimizing the BPNN predictive models for the two targets is provided in Table 5. The 27 sets of process parameters from Table 3 were used as input values, while the experimental values of warpage and average temperature served as the outputs. For a fair comparison with the ANFIS model, the same dataset partition was adopted for BPNN model development and evaluation. Because the available dataset consisted of only 27 samples obtained from the L27 orthogonal array, a fixed training–testing partition was used to provide a consistent basis for comparing the predictive performance of the two models under identical conditions. The Levenberg–Marquardt (Trainlm) algorithm was adopted for model training. The experimental values of warpage and average temperature were treated as prediction targets, and the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE) for each predicted value was calculated, as expressed in (6). After training, validation, and testing, the performance of the BPNN model was evaluated by computing the correlation coefficient (R value) for each subset as well as the overall dataset. In neural network modeling, the R value represents the correlation between the actual outputs and the predicted outputs. The overall R value is the average R value across all input–output pairs in the test dataset and serves as an indicator of the model’s predictive performance for warpage and average temperature. The predicted results were then compared with the actual experimental values to verify the prediction accuracy. The predictive performance of the developed models was evaluated using the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE). For ease of interpretation, the prediction accuracy was calculated as 100% − MAPE (%). Thus, a lower MAPE corresponds to a higher prediction accuracy.

Construction of Adaptive Network based fuzzy inference system (ANFIS) prediction model

The Adaptive Neuro-Fuzzy Inference System (ANFIS) integrates fuzzy theory, fuzzy logic, and fuzzy inference. ANFIS is a fuzzy inference system constructed on the foundation of an artificial neural network. It incorporates the advantages of both artificial neural networks and fuzzy logic—combining the strong learning capability of neural networks with the knowledge‐based characteristics of fuzzy systems. The learning algorithm of artificial neural networks assists in adjusting the antecedent and consequent parameters of fuzzy logic and generates two If–Then rules based on the Takagi–Sugeno fuzzy model. 29 These two rules are presented in (7) and (8). When the input variables X and Y enter the model, each layer sequentially performs the computation of the degree of fit, normalization, inference operations, and summation of output values, ultimately yielding the neural network output value f. The ANFIS architecture replaces conventional fuzzy logic mechanisms. Its primary advantage lies in its ability to obtain inference parameters for fuzzy rules through neural network learning algorithms, thereby endowing the fuzzy inference system with self‐learning capabilities that enable the adjustment of optimal fuzzy rules. Regarding the selection method of ANFIS membership functions, references 14 and 27 compared several types of membership functions and concluded that the Gaussian (Gaussmf) membership function achieves higher prediction accuracy in modeling tasks. Therefore, we adopt the Gaussmf membership function and a membership function layer structure of [2 2 2 2 2 2] to construct the ANFIS model. The ANFIS model architecture is illustrated in Figure 7. 30 ANFIS consists of five layers: the input layer, rule layer, normalization layer, inference layer, and output layer. The corresponding operations for each layer are provided in (9)–(14). For ANFIS model development, the dataset obtained from the L27 orthogonal array (27 samples) was divided into a training set (23 samples, approximately 85%) and a testing set (4 samples, approximately 15%). The training set was used to construct the fuzzy inference system and optimize the model parameters, whereas the testing set was reserved exclusively for evaluating the predictive performance and generalization capability of the developed model. Because the available dataset consisted of only 27 samples obtained from the L27 orthogonal array, a fixed training–testing partition was adopted to provide a consistent and fair basis for comparing the predictive performance of the BPNN and ANFIS models under identical conditions.

Comparison and analysis of accuracy between BPNN and ANFIS prediction models

The numerical results of warpage optimization and average temperature optimization were individually substituted into the two prediction models to obtain the corresponding predicted values. These predicted values were then compared with the two optimized results to calculate the error values, and the mean of the two errors was used to represent the prediction accuracy. The accuracies of the two models were compared to determine their differences and to facilitate the selection of an appropriate prediction model for subsequent experiments. The comparison criterion adopted was the Mean Absolute Percentage Error (MAPE), as expressed in (6). A smaller MAPE value indicates that the prediction model exhibits higher accuracy.

Results and discussion

First, the content of this experiment involves setting the experimental range parameters and measuring the two single objectives. The results obtained using the robust design method are then converted into grey correlation coefficients through gray relational analysis. Since the experiment involves two objectives, they need to be transformed into the Multi-Performance Characteristic Index (MPCI) using a fuzzy inference system with fuzzy rules. Subsequently, the fuzzy rules were processed using the MATLAB toolbox. By comparing the MPCI values, the best combination of parameters for multiple quality characteristics was determined.

Optimal parameters for minimizing warpage

The robust design analysis method was used to obtain the warpage experimental simulation and S/N values for the 27 groups. The results are shown in Table 6. The 6th group exhibited the smallest displacement among the 27 groups, with a displacement of 0.0002 mm. To obtain the optimized parameters, Equation (1) was used to calculate the factors and levels, and the S/N factor response is presented in Table 7. The S/N factor response plot is shown in Figure 8. Table 8 shows the experimental calculation results. The optimized process parameters are A2B2C3D2E3F2, with the following values: injection pressure 180 MPa, holding pressure 100 MPa, filling time 1.8 s, material temperature 200°C, mold temperature 90°C, and gate size 0.25 mm. Analysis of variance (ANOVA) results for warpage are listed in Table 9. It can be inferred that Factor C (filling time) contributed the most (26.73%) to warpage among the six factors. In addition to its statistical significance (p-value), the percentage contribution (26.73%) indicates that filling time has the largest practical effect on warpage among the investigated process parameters. From the analysis, it can be concluded that a longer filling time improves warpage. Comparison and analysis of accuracy between BPNN and ANFIS prediction models

The results indicate that the ANFIS model exhibits superior predictive performance compared with the BPNN model, achieving high accuracy in nonlinear prediction tasks with relatively simple model configuration. Although ANFIS still requires appropriate selection of membership functions and rule-base design, it generally involves less extensive hyperparameter tuning than BPNN, making it well suited for applications with limited data availability. After model construction, training, and testing using the original experimental datasets, the BPNN model yielded an average prediction accuracy of 94.25%, whereas the ANFIS model achieved an average accuracy of 96.79%. Furthermore, validation using the optimized experimental data showed that the average prediction accuracy increased to 96.09% for the BPNN model and 97.11% for the ANFIS model. Previous studies have demonstrated that in systems characterized by highly nonlinear and complex interactions, ANFIS—by integrating fuzzy inference mechanisms with neural network architectures—can simultaneously capture nonlinear relationships among process parameters and interval uncertainty, thereby maintaining strong generalization capability under limited data conditions. In contrast to the BPNN, which relies solely on numerical learning, ANFIS effectively mitigates the influence of data noise and process variability through fuzzy rules, resulting in superior predictive accuracy in the comparative analysis conducted in this study.16,32 Although the ANFIS model achieved high overall prediction accuracy, the test MAPE for warpage (19.8%) was higher than that for the training dataset. This difference is likely attributable to the limited sample size (n = 27), with only four samples used for testing, making the prediction error more sensitive to individual observations. Nevertheless, the model maintained satisfactory predictive performance and accurately captured the nonlinear relationship between the process parameters and warpage. Future studies will incorporate larger experimental datasets and additional validation cases to further improve model robustness and generalization capability.

Conclusions

Concluding remarks

This study uses the robust design process to optimize the MIM process parameters for dental braces, focusing on warpage and average temperature. The robust design, combined with fuzzy theory, is applied to optimize multiple quality characteristics of the process. The following conclusions can be drawn from the experimental results. Using the L27 (36) orthogonal array (OA) for robust design to optimize warpage as a single quality characteristic, the warpage of the original process group is 0.0007 mm. The best warpage in the original orthogonal table is 0.0002 mm (group 6), and the optimal warpage after the confirmation experiment is 0.0001 mm. The optimal combination of parameters is A2B2C3D2E3F2, with injection pressure at 180 MPa, holding pressure at 100 MPa, filling time at 1.8 s, material temperature at 200°C, mold temperature at 90°C, and gate size at 0.25 mm. Compared to the original process group and the best group from the original orthogonal table, the optimized warpage decreased by 85.7% and 50.0%, respectively. To optimize the average temperature as a single quality characteristic, the average temperature of the original process group is 91.4°C. The best average temperature in the original orthogonal table is 84.3°C (group 8), and the optimal average temperature after confirmation experimentation is 83.1°C. The optimal parameter combination is A1B1C3D1E2F2, with injection pressure at 160 MPa, holding pressure at 50 MPa, filling time at 1.8 s, material temperature at 190°C, mold temperature at 80°C, and gate size at 0.25 mm. Compared to the original process group and the best group from the original orthogonal table, the optimized average temperature decreased by 9.1% and1.4%, respectively.

Based on the robust design experiment and gray relational analysis with fuzzy inference, the optimal combination of parameters for multiple quality characteristics—considering both warpage and average temperature—is A1B1C3D2E3F2, with injection pressure at 180 MPa, holding pressure at 50 MPa, filling time at 1.8 s, material temperature at 200°C, mold temperature at 90°C, and gate size at 0.25 mm. The multi-objective optimization results for warpage and average temperature are 0.0001 mm and 85.0°C, respectively. Compared to the manufacturer’s original process, optimizing multiple quality characteristics has improved the results by 85.7% for warpage and 7.0% for average temperature.

The BPNN prediction model, after optimization through comparison of different hyperparameter settings, achieved prediction errors of 8.52% for warpage and 1.51% for average temperature, indicating that the accuracy of this prediction model is within an acceptable range. The optimized warpage and optimized average temperature results were then substituted into the established BPNN prediction model to obtain predicted values. By comparing these predicted values with the actual optimized values of the two objectives, the average error was 3.91%, resulting in a BPNN model accuracy of 96.09%. According to the ANFIS model results, the accuracy of the warpage prediction model is 95.60%, and the accuracy of the average temperature prediction model is 97.99%. When the two optimized prediction values are compared with their corresponding optimized experimental results, the average error is 2.89%, and the ANFIS model accuracy is 97.11%. Each optimization target verifies the accuracy of the BPNN and ANFIS models, and the accuracies of the two are 96.09% and 97.11%, respectively. These results show that the prediction capability of the ANFIS model is superior to that of the BPNN model in this study.

Compared with previous studies on MIM process optimization, which mainly focused on improving a single quality characteristic or optimizing process parameters using conventional robust design or grey relational analysis, the proposed approach integrates robust design, fuzzy-logic-based multi-objective optimization, and intelligent predictive models (BPNN and ANFIS) to simultaneously optimize warpage and average temperature. These results demonstrate that the proposed approach not only enhances the overall quality of MIM dental braces but also provides a reliable predictive framework for reducing process uncertainty and experimental effort. From an industrial perspective, the optimized process parameters can be readily implemented using the existing parameter adjustment functions available in commercial MIM injection molding machines. Parameters such as injection pressure, holding pressure, filling time, material temperature, and mold temperature can be directly programmed through the machine controller without requiring additional hardware modifications. The optimized gate size should be considered during mold design or when manufacturing new molds. Therefore, implementation of the proposed parameter settings mainly involves process optimization rather than equipment replacement, making the proposed approach cost-effective and practical for industrial MIM production. Furthermore, the developed predictive models can assist process engineers in reducing trial-and-error during process setup, thereby shortening process development time and improving production consistency.

Future works

This study has several limitations. The predictive models were developed using an L27 orthogonal experimental design (n = 27), which may limit their generalizability to other MIM products or broader processing conditions. Future work will focus on expanding the experimental dataset and validating the optimized process parameters through physical manufacturing experiments to further verify the robustness and practical applicability of the proposed intelligent modeling framework.

Although the simulation model was validated using dimensional measurements with an average error of only 0.47%, direct experimental validation of warpage and temperature distributions was not conducted in this study. Since all optimization analyses were performed using the same validated CAE model and identical material properties and boundary conditions, the simulation results are considered suitable for comparative process optimization. Experimental validation of warpage and thermal behavior will be conducted in future work. In addition, the predictive models were evaluated using a single training–testing partition because of the limited dataset size. Future studies will employ larger datasets together with repeated random sampling or k-fold cross-validation to further evaluate the robustness and generalization capability of the developed predictive models. In addition, the present study focused on the injection molding stage and did not consider the subsequent debinding and sintering processes, which may also influence the final dimensional accuracy and quality of MIM dental braces. Future work will incorporate these post-molding processes into the optimization framework to establish a more comprehensive intelligent process optimization methodology.
