Iron nutrition and host microbiota regulation in turbot (Scophthalmus maximus L.): The health risk of excess surpasses that of deficiency
Authors: Jinzhu Yang, Xiaoyang Yao, Xiao Li, Weijian Huang, Gang Lin, Mingzhu Li, Kangsen Mai, Yanjiao Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThis research studied the effects of iron (Fe) on physiology and microbiota (intestine and surrounding water) of turbot (Scophthalmus maximus L.). Three purified diets were designed: Fe0, Fe350, and Fe1750, with 0, 350, and 1750 mg/kg Fe supplementation from FeSO4, respectively. A total of 225 fish (initial body weight 8.19 ± 0.08 g) at 84 d of age were evenly distributed into nine tanks (25 fish per tank, 150 L seawater) for a 60-d feeding trial. The results showed that Fe deficiency (Fe0) or excess (Fe1750) had no obvious effects on growth performance (P > 0.05), but significantly affected overall mineral composition as revealed by principal component analysis (P < 0.001) and the expression of Fe transport related genes (P < 0.05). Turbot showed high Fe tolerance through ion transport regulation: under Fe deficiency, gene expression of transferrin/transferrin receptor, divalent metal-ion transporter 2b, and ferroportin were significantly up-regulated, whereas under excess, they were significantly down-regulated (P < 0.05). Iron excess also increased the risk of oxidative stress and liver damage, as indicated by the elevated activities of antioxidant enzymes (P < 0.05) and the increased levels of alanine transaminase (P = 0.002), aspartate transaminase (P = 0.022), and the reduced gene expression of liver forkhead box protein P3b and perilipin 2 (P < 0.05). Compared with Fe excess, Fe deficiency exerted a milder effect on hematological parameters, with moderate changes in plasma biochemical indices. Furthermore, the retention was negatively correlated with Fe addition level (r = −0.787, P < 0.001). Dietary Fe supplementation showed strong negative correlations with the contents of Zn (in intestine, liver, and gill), Mn (in muscle, intestine, and liver), Cu (in blood and intestine), Ca (in liver and gill), and Mg and P (in gill) (r < −0.5, P < 0.05). The analysis of microbiota revealed that the microbial co-occurrence network was most stable in the Fe350 group, with higher abundances or key microecological niches by microbes related to Fe metabolism and host health (e.g., Delftia acidovorans, Pseudomonas fluorescens, and Lactobacillus helsingborgensis). Excessive Fe also enriched the pathogens Photobacterium damselae and Vibrio campbellii in turbot. These results indicate that both Fe deficiency and excess can cause imbalances in physiological health, Fe and microbiota homeostasis, with excess Fe showing greater risks.Keywords: Iron homeostasis, Physiological health, Intestine microbiota, Water microbiota, Scophthalmus maximus L.

Introduction

Iron (Fe) is an essential trace metal element in vertebrates that plays a critical role in mediating a variety of key physiological functions. For instance, it serves as a porphyrin cofactor in hemoglobin and myoglobin for oxygen transport and storage. Additionally, Fe participates in redox processes as an electron carrier through Fe2+ and Fe3+ conversion (Lall, 2022). The process of regulating Fe transport and tissue distribution is primarily facilitated by TF and TFR (the full names of the abbreviations of all proteins can be found in supplementary file abbreviations and full names of genes and proteins). Transferrin binds to Fe to form a complex that transports Fe to the surface of tissue cells through the bloodstream and into cells via the process of cytosis of the transferrin receptor (Lall, 2022; Seyoum et al., 2021). In addition, several proteins are also associated with Fe transport or homeostatic regulation, such as SLC40A1, which facilitates Fe transport from the intracellular to the extracellular; DMT, which is responsible for intestinal Fe absorption; and MTF and MT also contribute to the regulation of Fe homeostasis (Lall, 2022; Seyoum et al., 2021).

The Fe requirements of fish have been comprehensively reviewed (Lall, 2022; Lall and Kaushik, 2021). In general, Fe requirements vary considerably among fishes. For instance, the Fe requirements of Atlantic salmon (Salmo salar) (Andersen et al., 1996), channel catfish (Ictalurus punctatus) (Gatlin and Wilson, 1986), and yellow catfish (Pelteobagrus fulvidraco) (Luo et al., 2017) range from 30 to 60 mg/kg feed, whereas those of gibel carp (Carassius auratus gibelio) (Pan et al., 2009), hybrid tilapia (Oreochromis. niloticus × O. aureus) (Shiau and Su, 2003), and grouper (Epinephelus coioides) (YE et al., 2007) are more than 180 mg/kg feed. Deficiency of dietary Fe leads to anemia and changes in hematological parameters in fish, while excess Fe leads to Fe toxicity such as induced oxidative stress, liver damage, and tissue Fe overload (Lall and Kaushik, 2021). Furthermore, there are complex interaction relationships among minerals (synergism, antagonism, and no significant correlations) (O'Dell, 1989). Therefore, fluctuations of Fe supplementation in the feed may exert a cascading effect on the overall mineral homeostasis, especially considering the mutual Fe–Zn antagonism (Tomar et al., 2022). Notably, apparent digestibility coefficient (ADC) and retention are core parameters for quantifying the Fe utilization capacity of fish. However, most of the Fe nutritional assessment experiments have focused on the physiological responses triggered by Fe itself, and few studies simultaneously investigated Fe interactions with other minerals. Additionally, there is a lack of research evaluating the physiological responses of turbot at different Fe doses in the diet (deficiency, optimization, and excess).

Intestinal microbiota is tightly associated with host nutrient absorption and immune response (Li et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2020). Minerals act through a dose-dependent dual mechanism. On one hand, they drive the construction and functional expression of microbiota communities that are synergistic with host health (e.g., short chain fatty acids synthesis and antimicrobial peptides secretion). On the other hand, they inhibit the colonization and proliferation of pathogens through strategies of nutrient deprivation or metal toxicity (Ma et al., 2023). However, systematic study on the effects of Fe on intestine microbiota in aquaculture is limited. Research on piglets has demonstrated a close correlation between Fe homeostasis and intestine health. Both Fe deficiency and excess led to intestinal microbiota dysbiosis, metabolic disorders, and a decline in immunity (Ding et al., 2020). Additionally, researchers have noted the interactions between water microbiota and host intestine microbiota. However, most of these studies focus on wild samples and ecological studies, and there is still a paucity of relevant research in aquaculture (Chen et al., 2022; Deng et al., 2023; Douglas, 2019; Minich et al., 2022). Moreover, in microbiota studies, partial regions 16S rRNA gene sequencing (e.g., V1, V4, and V3–V4) only provides genus level taxonomic accuracy (Johnson et al., 2019). The present study will utilize full-length 16S rRNA gene sequencing, to provide taxonomic resolution at the species level for biomarker identification.

Turbot (Scophthalmus maximus L.) is a prominent aquaculture species owing to its substantial economic and nutritional value. To date, a comprehensive study on Fe nutrition in turbot remains absent. Therefore, the present study aimed to investigate the effects of Fe on physiological health, Fe homeostasis, and mineral interactions of turbot. To comprehensively assess the role of Fe in the nutritional regulation of turbot, the intestine microbiota and the microbiota of the surrounding water were also analyzed using full length 16S rRNA gene sequencing. This study aimed to provide detailed data references for the mineral nutrition of turbot.

Materials and methods

Animal ethics statement

The Animal Care Committee of Ocean University of China approved the animal care and treatment procedures (ID: OUC-SCXY-2022070101).

Experiment design and feeds

Three experimental purified diets were designed (isonitrogenous and isolipidic, crude protein: 55%, crude lipid: 10%): Fe0, no Fe supplementation; Fe350, 350 mg/kg Fe in the form of FeSO4 as appropriate supplementation; Fe1750, 1750 mg/kg Fe as excessive supplementation, respectively. The dosage setting for appropriate Fe supplementation (Fe350) was referenced from relevant nutritional studies on turbot (Gao et al., 2019; Ma et al., 2015; Wang et al., 2021; Yang et al., 2020; Yu et al., 2024; Zhang et al., 2023) and the content in NRC (2011). The ingredients and proximate compositions of experimental diets is shown in Table 1, referring to that of Gao et al. (2019). The feed preparation followed a previous protocol (Yang et al., 2020). During the entire experimental period, the experimental feeds were stored at −20 °C without light. Every day, a small portion of the feed was taken out and allowed to return to room temperature for feeding.

Feeding trial and sample collection

The feeding trial was conducted in Rizhao Tengyun Aquaculture Co., Ltd., Rizhao, Shandong, China. A total of 225 healthy juvenile turbot (8.19 ± 0.08 g) at 84 d of age were evenly distributed into nine tanks, each filled with 150 L of seawater, with 25 fish placed in each tank. Feed groups were randomly assigned (three tanks/group). Ten additional fish were sampled at d 0 for whole fish mineral analysis. During the feeding trial, the fish were fed to apparent satiation twice a day (06:00 and 18:00). The feeding trial lasted for 60 d. The water quality conditions were as follows: water temperature 16−19 °C; salinity 18‰−19‰; pH 8.0−8.2; dissolved oxygen > 7 mg/L; Fe 0.390 mg/L; Zn 0.004 mg/L; Mn 0.073 mg/L; Cu < 0.001 mg/L; Ca 335 mg/L; Mg 1215 mg/L; P 0.611 mg/L.

Feces collection started on d 50 following a previous protocol (Yang et al., 2024). Briefly, complete and formed feces from each tank were collected gently using a small dip net (each tank was equipped with an individual net). The fecal samples from each tank were separately collected into 1.5 mL centrifuge tubes daily. Excess water at the bottom of the centrifuge tubes was carefully drawn out with a 1 mL syringe. The samples were then stored frozen at −20 °C. Before for ADC analysis, all feces from the same tank were freeze-dried and composited. After the feeding trial, fish were fasted for 24 h, anesthetized with MS-222 (E10521, Sigma Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China), then counted and weighed. Water microbiota samples (1200 mL) were collected from the bottom of each tank and filtered through a 0.22-μm filter membrane (GPWP04700, Sigma Aldrich (Shanghai) Trading Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China). An unfiltered membrane served as a negative control. A total of 12 fish from each tank were collected. Organs were collected and analyzed individually for each fish. Blood was drawn from the caudal vein using dipotassium ethylenediaminetetraacetic acid (EDTA-K2) tubes (QX0002, Wuhan Servicebio Technology Co., Ltd., Wuhan, Hubei, China). Plasma was separated by centrifugation (500 × g, 4 °C, 10 min) for hematological analysis. Then, the intestine, liver, and gills were taken for gene expression analysis. Four fish were selected, and blood, intestine, liver, gills, and muscle were collected for mineral analysis. Four fish were selected, and the whole intestine was collected for intestinal microbiota analysis. The remaining fish were collected for whole fish proximate nutrient composition and mineral analysis.

Proximate compositions of feeds and whole fish

The proximate nutrient composition of feeds and whole fish were analyzed according to AOAC (2023). Moisture was determined using the method 934.01. Samples were dried at 105 °C to a constant weight, which typically took at least 48 h. Crude protein was analyzed using the method 954.01 with a fully automated Kjeldahl analyzer (Kjeltec 8400, FOSS Analytical A/S, Hillerød, Hovedstaden, Denmark). In this process, 10 mL of H2SO4 (concentrated) was added; 0.5 g of CuSO4 was used as a catalyst; and 5.0 g of K2SO4 was added to increase the boiling point of H2SO4. The mixture was digested at 420 °C for 90 min. Crude lipid was determined using the method 2003.06 with a fully automated extraction system (Soxtec 8000, FOSS Analytical A/S, Hillerød, Hovedstaden, Denmark). Petroleum ether with a boiling point of 30−60 °C was used as the extraction solvent. Glass beads with a diameter of 4 mm were added to prevent bumping. The extraction procedure consisted of boiling at 82 °C for 15 min, rinsing at 82 °C for 30 min, and solvent recovery at 82 °C for 20 min. This cycle was repeated 3−4 times until the extracted glass beads became colorless. Ash was determined using the method 942.05. First, samples were heated to complete carbonization in an electric resistance furnace (H22-X3, Hangzhou Joyoung Household Appllances Co., Ltd., Hangzhou, Zhejiang, China), and then they were calcined at 550 °C for 8 h in a refractory brick muffle furnace (SX-4-10, Beijing ever briGht medical treatment instrument Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). Organic matter (OM) content was calculated as the difference between 100% and the ash content.

Hematological parameter analysis

Commercial kits were used to analyze hematological parameters. Kits for analyzing the content of plasma total protein (TP; P0006), the activities of total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD; S0101M) and copper/zinc-superoxide dismutase (Cu/Zn-SOD; S0103) were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology Inc., Shanghai, China. Kits for analyzing the contents of plasma albumin (ALB; BC379), glucose (BC156-2), and malondialdehyde (MDA; BC003-2), the activity of alkaline phosphatase (AKP; BC077-2), and total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC; BC014-2) were purchased from Shanghai Elisa Biotech Co., Ltd., Shanghai, China. Kits for analyzing the contents of plasma total cholesterol (T-CHO; A111-2-1) and triglyceride (TG; A110-2-1), the activities of alanine transaminase (ALT; C009-2-1), aspartate transaminase (AST; C010-2-1), and glutathione peroxidase (GPx; A005-1) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, Jiangsu, China.

RNA extraction and real-time quantitative PCR (RT-qPCR) assay

Total RNA was extracted from intestinal, liver, and gill tissue samples using Direct-zol RNA Miniprep Kits (TR205, Genstone Biotech (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Beijing, China). The reverse transcription reaction was carried out using 5 × All-In-One RT MasterMix (including the AccuRT Genomic DNA Removal Kit for gDNA elimination) (G492, Applied Biological Materials Inc., Richmond, British Columbia, Canada). The RT-qPCR experiments were performed with the BlasTaq 2 × qPCR MasterMix Kit (G891, Applied Biological Materials Inc., Richmond, British Columbia, Canada) on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (96-well, Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The primer information is shown in Table S1. The full names of the abbreviations of all genes can be found in supplementary file abbreviations and full names of genes and proteins. Reference gene screening was carried out following a methodology in a prior study (Yang et al., 2024, 2022). The results of the stability of the candidate reference genes were presented in Table S2. rpl17 was chosen as the reference gene. A no cDNA template control (NTC) was included in each plate. The relative gene expression levels were calculated using the 2−ΔΔCt method (Livak and Schmittgen, 2001).

Minerals analysis

Minerals in feed, whole fish on d 0, surrounding water, final whole fish, muscle, blood, intestine, liver, gill, and feces samples were detected following a previous protocol (Yang et al., 2024). Briefly, 200 mg freeze-dried matter or 200 μL liquid samples were digested using Multiwave 5000 Rotor 41HVT (Anton Paar GmbH, Graz, Styria, Austria) with 10 mL of HNO3 (GR, 65%–70%). After digestion, all samples were made up to a volume of 25 mL. Then, minerals were analyzed using an ICP-OES machine (Avio 200, PerkinElmer Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The wavelengths were set as follows: Zn 206.200 nm, P 213.617 nm, Fe 238.204 nm, Mn 257.610 nm, Mg 285.213 nm, Ca 317.933 nm, Cu 327.393 nm and Y 371.029 nm. The mineral composition (wet weight) of d 0 samples were as follows: Fe 16.26 mg/kg; Zn 13.16 mg/kg; Mn 14.64 mg/kg; Cu 0.599 mg/kg; Ca 9.38 g/kg; Mg 0.400 g/kg; P 4.28 g/kg; moisture 78.2%.

DNA extraction and sequencing

Intestine sample genomic DNA was extracted using DNeasy PowerSoil Pro Kit (47016, QIAGEN GmbH, Hilden, Nordrhein-Westfalen, Germany). Water and water source sample genomic DNA was extracted using DNeasy PowerWater Kit (14900-100-NF, QIAGEN GmbH, Hilden, Nordrhein-Westfalen, Germany). No sample negative controls and positive mock controls (D6300, Zymo Research Corp., Irvine, CA, USA) were set up for quality control during the DNA extraction process, referring to previous studies (Li et al., 2022, 2021). The composition of the mock standard is shown in Table S3. The quality control, library, and sequencing were conducted by Novogene Genomics Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China. The full length 16S rRNA gene was amplified using primers 27F (5′ - AGAGTTTGATCCTGGCTCAG -3′) and 1492R (5′ - GGTTACCTTGTTACGACTT - 3′). After sequencing, the low-quality sequences were initially filtered, and then the mixed samples were split based on the Barcode tags. Subsequently, on the QIIME2, the DADA2 algorithm was used to remove the primer sequences and chimeras. Finally, an amplicon sequence variant (ASV) table and a feature sequence file were generated (Bolyen et al., 2019).

Data visualization

The annotation and visualization were completed using R (v.4.3.2) (R Core Team, 2023). With the R package dplyr (Wickham et al., 2023), ASVs with a single frequency or count were filtered. Based on the Silva 138.2 database (Yilmaz et al., 2014), annotation was carried out using the R package dada2 (Callahan et al., 2016), and the data were cleaned using dplyr. ASVs annotated as Archaea, Unassigned, Chloroplast, and Mitochondria were filtered. Amplicon sequence variant that could not be annotated to the species level inherited the nearest annotatable higher-level taxonomic information with a prefix letter representing the highest annotation level. For example, “c_Gammaproteobacteria” indicated annotation to the Class level, and “f_Saprospiraceae” indicated annotation to the family level.

Identification and removal of contaminant sequences were completed using the R package decontam (Davis et al., 2018). The basic logic is as follows: the abundance of contaminant sequences is often inversely proportional to the concentration of the 16S rRNA gene in experimental samples and positive control samples, and they mostly appear in negative controls (Davis et al., 2018). The quantification of bacterial DNA in the total DNA was performed using the RT-qPCR method on a QuantStudio 3 Real-Time PCR System (96-well, Applied Biosystems, Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc., Waltham, MA, USA). The total volume of the reaction system was 20 μL, including 2 μL of total DNA (intestinal DNA was diluted to 12.5 ng/μL; water DNA was diluted to 2 ng/μL), 0.6 μL of 10 μmol/L forward primer (5′-CCATGAAGTCGGAATCGCTAG-3′), 0.6 μL of 10 μmol/L reverse primer (5′-GCTTGACGGGCGGTGT-3′), 1 μL of SYBR Green I (20×, SL2190, Coolaber Science & Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), 10 μL of TaKaRa Taq HS Low DNA (2×, R090A, Takara Biomedical Technology (Beijing) Co., Ltd., Beijing, China), and 5.8 μL of ddH2O for Low DNA. All operations were carried out on a clean bench (with two alcohol lamps lit to create a sterile environment). The RT-qPCR reaction program was as follows: denaturation at 94 °C for 5 s; amplification and extension at 60 °C for 20 s, with 40 cycles in total. Each plate of the experiment included two NTC wells. When the Ct value of the NTC wells exceeded 37, it indicated that there was no exogenous DNA contamination during the RT-qPCR process. The relative concentration of the 16S rRNA gene was positively correlated with 10−Ct.

The operation of phylogenetic tree construction and the visualization of data assessment, dominant features, alpha diversity indices, and the unweighted pair-group method with arithmetic means (UPGMA) clustering tree referred to our previous article (Yang et al., 2024). The partial least squares discriminant analysis (PLS-DA) was performed on the feature abundance table using the R package mixOmics (Rohart et al., 2017), and the results were visualized using the R package ggplot2 (Wickham, 2016). The R packages ggplot2 and ggClusterNet were used to visualize the co-occurrence network at the Species level (filtering out the features with an occurrence prevalence of less than 50% and an average relative abundance [Ave] of less than 0.01%). Only the edges with a correlation coefficient |r| > 0.8 and P < 0.05 were displayed (Wen et al., 2022). The nodes were colored based on the phylum labels of the features and the niche breadth of microbial community (NBMC) labels (Yan et al., 2022). The natural.con.microp function was used to evaluate the capacity to resist disturbance of the co-occurrence network, and the ZiPiPlot and Hub_score functions were used to identify the keystone taxa (key features). The keystone features were visualized as boxplots using ggplot2, and the groups to which the keystone features belonged were marked with ‘▲‘.

Statistical analysis

All physiological relevant data were subjected to the Shapiro-Wilk test using the R package goftest (Faraway et al., 2021), and QQ plots were drawn using the R package car (Fox and Weisberg, 2019) for comprehensive evaluation. For the data that did not pass the normality test, the Box-Cox transformation was carried out using the R package AID (Yilmaz and Dağ, 2022).

For the growth performance and proximate nutrients compositions data (n = 3), the R package agricolae was used (de Mendiburu, 2023): the aov function was used to perform ANOVA to evaluate the main effects of the feeds (Fe0, Fe350, and Fe1750). The model was specified as:

When the effect was significant (P < 0.05), Tukey's multiple comparisons were conducted through the HSD.test function. All data in the tables were presented as mean and standard error of the mean (SEM).

There were six biological replications of each group of microbiota samples. Specifically, each group included the intestinal samples from six fish, six filtered membranes after water filtration, and for the water source, there were also six filtered membranes. The R package Maaslin2 was used for the screening of differential features (at the species level), with the feed (Fe0, Fe350, and Fe1750) as the fixed factor (Mallick et al., 2021).

Physiological health

Hematological parameters

In Table 3, in turbot plasma, the TP content in the Fe1750 group was significantly higher than that in the Fe0 and Fe350 groups (P < 0.001). The ALB content was the highest in the Fe350 group, followed by the Fe0 group, and the lowest in the Fe1750 group (P < 0.001). The TG content and ALT level were the highest in the Fe1750 group, followed by the Fe0 group, and the lowest in the Fe350 group (P < 0.05). The T-CHO content in the Fe350 and Fe1750 groups was significantly higher than that in the Fe0 group (P < 0.001). The glucose content in the Fe1750 group was significantly higher than that in the Fe0 group (P = 0.037). The level of AST in the Fe0 group was significantly increased compared with the Fe350 and Fe1750 groups (P = 0.022). There was no significant change in the level of AKP among all groups (P = 0.272). Host microbiota

Quality control of 16S rRNA genes sequencing data

A total of 193 ASVs (4167 reads) were identified as contaminant sequences in intestinal samples. These contaminant sequences were mainly annotated as Photobacterium damselae, g_Nitrosomonas, f_Saprospiraceae, c_Gracilibacteria, g_Phycisphaera, etc. (Table S4). A total of 529 ASVs (275,853 reads) were identified as contaminant sequences in water and water source samples. These contaminant sequences were mainly annotated as o_Absconditabacteriales (SR1), g_Rubritalea, f_Saprospiraceae, g_Rubidimonas, Vibrio scophthalmi, c_Gracilibacteria, etc. (Table S5). After contaminant removal, the final dataset comprised 5638 ASVs with 463,273 reads, representing 50 phyla, 95 classes, 211 orders, 248 families, 493 genera, and 390 species (Table S6). The species accumulation boxplot, rank abundance plot, and rarefaction curves confirmed that the final clean ASV table provided a sufficient data volume for subsequent analysis (Fig. S3).

The negative control samples showed a predominance of Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Verrucomicrobiota, and Patescibacteria at the phylum level (Fig. S4A), while Enterovibrio, Rubritalea, Vibrio, f_Saprospiraceae, o_Absconditabacteriales (SR1), Aureispira, Leucothrix, Chryseobacterium, c_Gammaproteobacteria, and c_Gracilibacteria at the genus level (taxa not assigned to genus level are preceded by a letter (c_, class; o_, order; f_, family) indicating their assigned level; Fig. S4B).

Based on the mock control (ZymoBIOMICS Microbial Community Standard; D6300, Zymo Research Corp., Irvine, CA, USA) manual, in the 16S rRNA gene sequencing experiment, it is sufficient to identify taxonomic units of 8 species in the Mock at the genus level (Table S3). In this experiment, further dilution of the Mock was carried out. Through dilution, the sensitivity of identifying contaminant sequences in the positive control was improved. Generally, the higher the dilution factor, the easier it is to detect contaminant sequences in the positive control. The results showed that, at both the phylum and genus levels, the Mock in its undiluted and diluted states yielded results close to the theoretical composition. Specifically, at the phylum level: Pseudomonadota and Bacillota (Fig. S5A); at the genus level: Bacillus, Limosilactobacillus, Staphylococcus, Listeria, Salmonella, Escherichia−Shigella, Enterococcus, and Pseudomonas (Fig. S5B). This indicated that, based on the experimental procedures in this study, contamination was scarcely introduced during the DNA extraction process.

Beta diversity

Both the PLS-DA (Fig. 5A) and UPGMA plots (Fig. 5B) indicated that the microbiota communities differed among the intestine, water, and water source. The PLS-DA subplots for the intestine and water demonstrated that either no supplementation of Fe (Fe0 group) or excessive supplementation of Fe (Fe1750 group) caused a shift in the microbiota community of the turbot intestine and surrounding water (Fig. 5C and D), yet the dose-effect was not prominent. In the intestine, the microbiota community of the Fe0 group shifted to the left along the P1 axis, while that of the Fe1750 group shifted downward along the P2 axis (Fig. 5C). Co-occurrence networks

As shown in Fig. 5, a total of 434 features, belonging to 30 phyla, mainly Pseudomonadota, Bacteroidota, Patescibacteria, and Bacillota, with their NBMC composition dominated by generalist and specialist, were screened for constructing the microbial co-occurrence network. The differences between the random network (E-R network) and the constructed co-occurrence networks indicated the effectiveness of the network structures in each group (Fig. S6A). Although positive correlations were dominant (> 95%) within each group, the distributions of network components (colored by the respective phyla and NBMC) showed notable topological differences among the groups (Fig. 5E and F). As nodes were removed, the natural connectivity of the networks in each group underwent a gradual decrease. However, when compared with the Fe0 and Fe1750 groups, the Fe350 group exhibited a consistent pattern of higher natural connectivity and a lower rate of decline, with the rate following the order Fe350 (48.4%) < Fe0 (49.4%) < Fe1750 (51.5%) (Fig. 5G).

The average clustering coefficient (0.697 < 0.699 < 0.747) and relative modularity (0.88 < 1.97 < 2.10) increased gradually in the order of the Fe0, Fe1750, and Fe350 groups. Key features

As shown in Fig. 6, a total of 42 key features (at the species level) were identified, mainly belonging to Pseudomonadota and Bacteroidota. Among them, the Fe350 group contained five key features, all of which were specialist. Except for f_Paracoccaceae, which was a key feature shared with the Fe0 group, the other four key features (Delftia acidovorans, Listeria monocytogenes, Snodgrassella alvi, and Stenotrophomonas maltophilia) were unique to the Fe350 group. Moreover, the occurrence frequency and relative abundance of these features in the intestine were generally higher than those in the water (Fig. 6). The Fe0 group and the Fe1750 group were identified with 33 (10 generalist, 10 neutral, and 13 specialist) and 17 (two generalist, seven neutral, and eight specialist) key features respectively. Except for Chryseobacterium indologenes, a key feature of the Fe0 group, the occurrence frequency and relative abundance of these key features in the water were generally higher than those in the intestine (Fig. 6). The Fe0 group and the Fe1750 group shared 12 key features (such as f_Saprospiraceae, g_Muricauda, g_Phycisphaera, g_Rubritalea, o_Ardenticatenales, and o_Candidatus Pacebacteria). In addition, the Fe0 group had 20 unique features (such as Chryseobacterium indologenes, g_Aquibacter, g_Maribacter, g_Pikeienuella, and Phaeobacter italicus), and the Fe1750 group had five unique key features (f_Cryomorphaceae, f_Leucotrichaceae, g_Lutimonas, g_Persicirhabdus, and Leucothrix mucor) (Fig. 6).

Discussion

Physiological processes

While the impact of Fe deficiency in fish feed is not typically associated with impaired growth in fish, it can exert an influence on liver health and result in a reduction of blood Fe levels. Excessive Fe in the feed can lead to a decline in fish feeding enthusiasm and even death. However, the dose at which Fe toxic reactions occur in common fish feeds is relatively high. In rainbow trout (Oncorhynchus mykiss), the occurrence of Fe poisoning was observed to be contingent upon dietary Fe levels that exceeded 1380 mg/kg (Lall, 2022). A study of Chinese sea bass (Lateolabrax maculatus) revealed that growth and physiological status remained within normal limits even when the Fe content reached 554 mg/kg. However, growth was slightly poorer when the Fe content was below 180 mg/kg (Wang et al., 2023). In this experiment, neither the absence of Fe supplementation (181 mg Fe/kg) nor excessive Fe supplementation (1927 mg Fe/kg) affected the growth performance of juvenile turbot. The absence of observable growth performance effects can be attributed to two primary factors. Firstly, the 60-d experimental period was relatively short in relation to the full production cycle (which typically spans two years). This may have been insufficient for the appearance of limitations in growth performance resulting from low dietary Fe. In addition, further analysis of physiological indicators revealed their flexible regulatory mechanisms. When the basal diet was Fe-deficient, the intestine could enhance Fe absorption and transport through the “absorption (dmt)-efflux (slc40a1)” regulation. The gills compensated for Fe deficiency through the water (Fe in the experimental water: 0.390 mg/L), with high expression of tf and tfr, which synergistically interacted with the high expression of tf and tfr in the liver to improve Fe utilization. However, the whole-body Fe (14.9 mg/kg) and blood Fe (144 mg/L) were both lower than those in the normal Fe-supplemented group (16.7 and 184 mg/L), indicating that this compensatory effect in turbot is limited and may not be sustainable in long-term culture. When the Fe content in the feed was substantial, the expression levels of tf, tfr, and slc40a1 in the intestine were all expressed low to avoid excessive Fe absorption. This regulation maintained the relative stability of muscle and blood Fe. However, the enrichment of Fe in the whole fish and intestine indicated that the risk of long-term excessive Fe intake still exists. PCA analysis of relevant gene expression and mineral composition also indicated that Fe deficiency or excess affects the overall mineral homeostasis of turbot.

In the Fe0 group, the relatively low expression level of plin2 in the liver may interfere with hepatic lipid droplet metabolism and weaken the lipid storage capacity. Consequently, the levels of T-CHO and T-AOC in the plasma were lower than those in the Fe350 group, which also indicated that the lipid metabolism capacity was affected. The low ALB content and increased levels of ALT and AST in the plasma of the Fe0 group suggested that hepatocytes might have suffered mild damage. Nevertheless, within the 60-d culture period, these changes did not have a substantial impact on the overall antioxidant performance. This finding also indicated that a Fe content of more than 181 mg/kg in the feed was necessary to maintain the normal physiological processes of turbot. In rainbow trout, it was also found that without Fe supplementation, lipid accumulation was significantly lower, and the apparent digestibility of crude lipid was reduced (Evliyaoğlu et al., 2022). In the Fe1750 group, an excess of Fe results in substantial Fe accumulation in the liver and intestine of turbot. This may induce oxidative stress by promoting the generation of reactive oxygen species (Mayneris-Perxachs et al., 2022), which is consistent with the research findings in grass carp (Ctenopharyngodon idella) (Huang et al., 2025). The organism maintains redox balance by upregulating the expression of antioxidant enzymes (such as SOD and GPx) and chelating excessive Fe2+ through the high expression of mt2. However, the low expression of plin2 in the liver, the decrease ALB content and the increase in ALT level in the plasma of the Fe1750 group suggest that signs of liver damage have emerged. The results indicated that although the antioxidant system was activated, long-term Fe overload might exceed compensatory capacity. Although it did not significantly affect growth performance on short-term culture, it had already resulted in abnormal liver function. A dietary Fe level of 1927 mg/kg posed a risk of chronic toxicity to turbot.

In this study, the ADC of Fe and Mn exhibited high variability and were generally negative, which is similar to the findings in salmonid fish (Antony et al., 2019; Silva et al., 2019; Sugiura et al., 1998a,b) and white shrimp (Penaeus vannamei) (Yang et al., 2024). The factors contributing to negative ADC remain unclear. One potential explanation is the presence of endogenous secretion: after Fe and Mn are secreted into the intestine along with bile, some of them are not re-absorbed and are excreted with feces, resulting in a relatively high mineral concentration in feces and a negative ADC. The positive retention of Fe and Mn indicate that turbot can achieve net mineral accumulation through endogenous secretion and reuse (such as the re-absorption of bile-derived Fe/Mn in the intestine) and compensatory absorption from water. It is noteworthy that the ADCs of Zn and Cu were stable and showed little variability in both this study and experiments on salmon, indicating differences in the metabolic patterns of different minerals. In this study, Fe exhibited a strong antagonistic relationship with Zn, Mn, and Cu, which is consistent with the generally recognized mineral interaction relationships (Tomar et al., 2022). Furthermore, the present study demonstrated that Fe had antagonistic effects on the accumulation of Ca, Mg, and P. These data indicate that when the Fe content in the diet is too high, the Fe retention decreases. Moreover, due to the antagonistic effects, the absorption of other minerals is further restricted, which is detrimental to the overall mineral homeostasis of turbot.

Microbiota

In the study of the impact of nutrients on the host microbiota, most of the existing findings focus on the intestine microbiota. However, fish have the habit of ingesting seawater. This dynamic interaction enables the exchange of influence between the microbiota of the water and the intestine (Chen et al., 2022; Minich et al., 2022). Nevertheless, in the study of aquatic animal nutrition, it is rare to simultaneously evaluate both the water and intestine microbiota. This study described the microbiota community structures of the intestine and the surrounding water. The composition of dominant features exhibited a gradually aggregating pattern from the water source to the surrounding water and subsequently to the intestine. This finding suggests that turbot possesses the capacity to construct a microecosystem suitable for its own growth through a dual mechanism of intestine screening and environmental shaping. This host selectivity that is coordinated with the environment has also been observed in wild fish (Kim et al., 2021; Minich et al., 2022).

The present study demonstrates that changes in microbial co-occurrence network parameters such as the links, average clustering coefficient, centralization degree, relative modularity, and natural connectivity all indicate that both Fe deficiency and excess lead to a decrease in the complexity and stability of the microbial network in turbot. Key features (keystone taxa) are the foundation of the stability of the microbial network. They dominate the functional interactions between the microbiota and the host (Kern et al., 2021). Coincidentally, the key features and enriched differential features in the Fe350 group demonstrated microbial functional synergy with the Fe homeostasis. Among these features, Delftia acidovorans can secrete metallic ion binding proteins to reduce the toxicity of free Fe ion in the intestine (Braña et al., 2016). Stenotrophomonas maltophilia possesses a competitive advantage in the uptake of Fe within the intestine microbiota through the specific Fe-siderophore complex. This mechanism serves to avert the potential toxicity of Fe while ensuring the maintenance of sufficient Fe supplies. Furthermore, S. maltophilia has a capacity for nitrogen fixation (Ghosh et al., 2020). Pseudomonas fluorescens has the capacity to suppress the proliferation of the fish pathogen Aeromonas salmonicida through the competitive utilization of Fe (Chauhan and Singh, 2019). Additionally, the presence of Snodgrassella alvi, Lactobacillus helsingborgensis, and Bacteroidetes bacterium promotes a synergistic enhancement of the intestinal defense barrier. S. alvi can induce the expression of antimicrobial peptides and maintain an anaerobic environment in the intestine, thereby inhibiting opportunistic pathogens (Callegari et al., 2021). L. helsingborgensis and B. bacterium are known to produce organic acids, which contribute to immune protection (Olofsson et al., 2014; Tungland, 2018). Excessive Fe also led to the enrichment of some pathogens, such as Photobacterium damselae in the water, which poses a risk of inducing the death of cultured fish (Chauhan and Singh, 2019), and Vibrio campbellii in the intestine, which is a pathogen for fish and crustaceans (Ringø, 2020). Furthermore, the abundance of S. maltophilia was also higher in the Fe1750 group, possibly as a microbiota-mediated regulation in response to Fe toxicity. The microbiota results also indicate that the negative effects of Fe deficiency and excess are more pronounced in the host microbiota with Fe excess.

Finally, it should be noted that the purified diets used in this study have relatively higher bioavailability compared to the complex ingredients in practical commercial diets formulations, especially plant ingredients containing phytic acid. Therefore, in commercial feeds, culturing with the same dietary Fe levels as in this study may yield different results, particularly for the treatments with no addition (Fe0 group) and appropriate addition doses (Fe350 group).

Conclusion

The present study reveals that turbot can tolerate a wide range of Fe content (181−1927 mg/kg) in the feed by regulating physiological processes and microbiota such as Delftia acidovorans, Pseudomonas fluorescens, and Lactobacillus helsingborgensis. Both deficiency and excess Fe induce abnormal liver function, imbalance in antioxidant and mineral homeostasis, and a decline in the stability of the host microbial co-occurrence network. Excessive Fe also enriches the pathogens P. damselae and V. campbellii. Moreover, the health risks associated with Fe excess are more pronounced than those associated with Fe deficiency.
