Surface hydrophobicity and rigidity determines protein corona on orally delivered nanoparticles treating colitis
Authors: Jiawei Wu, Mingjie Ni, Liyun Xing, Yating Wang, Xi Liu, Yaxian Zheng, Lian Li, Yuan Huang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractDisease-specific protein corona adsorbed on nanocarriers determines in vivo delivery efficiency. Macrophages can orchestrate inflammation resolution and mucosa repair, making them a promising therapeutic target in colitis. Here, we show that surface hydrophobicity and rigidity determine colitis-specific intestinal protein corona (C-IPC) on orally delivered nanoparticles for treating colitis, which improves therapeutic efficiency. We show that high surface hydrophobicity boosts overall protein adsorption, leading to improved colon macrophage delivery and therapeutic effect when loaded with budesonide. Moreover, hydrophobicity with high rigidity results in a corona enriched with macrophage-targeting proteins, notably S100A8, generating an optimal C-IPC characterized by both high protein amount and high proportion of targeting proteins. Consequently, high rigidity nanoparticles more effectively attenuates the inflammatory state and restores the immune homeostasis in male rats with colitis. Our work develops a rational strategy of manipulating protein corona formation through physicochemical properties for efficient oral drug delivery, holding broad promise for diverse nanocarriers and pathologies.Subject terms: Drug delivery, Nanobiotechnology, Ulcerative colitis

Introduction

Protein corona, a dynamic layer of biomolecules inevitably adsorbed onto nanomedicines in biological environments, critically governs the drug delivery and therapeutic effect in vivo1–3. Emerging evidence highlights that protein corona formed under pathological conditions contain varied functional proteins, displaying distinct bioactivities to determine the transport and targeting behaviors of nanocarriers4–6. For example, neurodegenerative disease-associated protein corona facilitated blood-brain barrier penetration of nanoparticles by adsorbing the apolipoproteins7,8. The tumor microenvironment-derived protein corona altered nanoparticles biodistribution to enhance their tumor accumulation and targeting9,10. Our pre-study also revealed that the colitis-specific intestinal protein corona (C-IPC) possessed varied functional proteins11, which could moderately enhance the macrophage targeting and internalization of orally administrated nanocarriers. Hence, these disease-specific protein corona possessing unique tissue- or cell-targeting related proteins show great potential in improving the delivery efficiency of nanocarriers. Furthermore, seeking suitable strategy to selectively facilitate the functional protein adsorption may further enhance the targeted drug delivery.

Recent studies have shown that altering the physicochemical properties is promising for governing the protein corona formation on nanocarriers12–15. The surface hydrophobicity and charge of nanocarriers might influence the protein adsorption amount. For instance, hydrophilic polyethylene glycol (PEG) modified nanoparticles exhibited lower protein adsorption due to weakened hydrophobic interaction16,17. In addition, cationic nanoparticles could promote negatively charged serum protein adsorption through electrostatic interaction18. In another aspect beyond overall corona absorption, rigidity and surface chemistry of nanocarriers affect their interaction with specific proteins, resulting in varied protein corona composition13. High rigidity nanoparticles adsorbed more apolipoproteins but fewer complement proteins, thus prolonging the blood circulation19. Besides, through adjusting the amino/hydroxyl ratios of nanovesicles, immunoglobulin adsorption declined and tumor distinctive proteins increased which prolonged circulation and tumor cell internalization10. Thus far, most studies have focused on singular physicochemical property modulation of nanocarriers10,19–21, which cannot sufficiently promote the functional protein adsorption to obtain the desired protein corona. Therefore, developing multiple-properties modulation strategy to synchronously increase protein adsorption amount and selectively upregulate functional protein proportion may effectively enhance the delivery efficiency of nanocarriers.

Macrophages are key effector cells of the innate immune system for intestinal mucosal homeostasis, yet dysregulated macrophages are a central feature of inflammatory bowel disease (IBD), including colitis22. They accumulate in vast numbers in active colitis, being potent producers of pro-inflammatory mediators, such as reactive oxygen (ROS), TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6. Concurrently, intestinal macrophages prevent excessive inflammation by facilitating the expansion of regulatory T (Treg) cells, mainly via the production of IL-1023. Given that macrophages can orchestrate inflammation resolution and mucosa repair, they are regarded as attractive therapeutic targets in colitis24. Several macrophage targeting drug delivery systems have been developed to improve the colitis therapy via suppressing inflammation and restoring intestinal homeostasis25–28. Therefore, targeted delivery of therapeutics to colon macrophages represents a promising strategy for colitis treatment.

In this study, a sequential hydrophobicity-rigidity co-modulation strategy was performed to optimize the C-IPC formation on orally administrated nanoparticles (NPs) for efficient oral macrophage targeted delivery and colitis treatment (Fig. 1). Increasing surface hydrophobicity of NPs tended to enhance the protein adsorption amount during C-IPC formation, thus high hydrophobic NPs (Phobic) loaded with anti-inflammatory drug budesonide showed improved colon macrophage targeting and better colitis treatment. Next, the rigidity of NPs was modulated by varying the amount of interior water layer. Increasing the rigidity of Phobic was found to further promote the macrophage targeted drug delivery, and high rigidity NPs (Hard) could effectively attenuate the inflammation and restore the immune homeostasis in colitis rats. Interestingly, possessing high rigidity selectively adsorbed more macrophage-targeting related proteins on NPs including S100A8 protein, contributing to the superior macrophage targeting and internalization. Our work proposed and verified the concept of rationally tailoring the disease-specific protein corona on nanocarriers through multiple-physicochemical properties co-modulation for efficient targeted drug delivery. More importantly, this strategy may be applicable for various types of nanocarriers and diseases.

Two steps to tailor the C-IPC by hydrophobicity-rigidity co-modulation of NPs.

Firstly, the NPs hydrophobicity is increased to enhance the protein adsorption amount, leading to the enhanced colon macrophage uptake by high hydrophobicity NPs (Phobic@C-IPC). Secondly, the rigidity of Phobic@C-IPC is further modulated to optimize the protein composition, and the high hydrophobicity-high rigidity NPs (Hard@C-IPC) show the best colon macrophage targeting by selectively adsorbing more macrophage-targeting proteins. Finally, the budesonide-loaded Hard@C-IPC achieves superior targeted drug delivery and colitis therapy.

Results

Preparation and characterization of NPs with varied hydrophobicity

In this study, the soyabean lecithin and cholesterol were lipid component to form the lipid-based NPs. To modulate the surface hydrophobicity, different ratios of DSPE-PEG2000 were added to prepare NPs with varied polyethylene glycol (PEG) modification degree29,30. As the PEGylation increased gradually from 10%, 40%, to 80% (mass ratio of DSPE-PEG2000: soybean lecithin) (Fig. 2a), we denoted the NPs as Phobic, Medium and Philic, respectively. All NPs were similar in hydrodynamic diameter ~165 nm measured by dynamic light scattering (DLS) (Fig. 2b, and Supplementary Table 1). The cryo-TEM images (Fig. 2b) showed that NPs possessed the morphologies of spherical shape and particle size ~115 nm, which is lower than the hydrodynamic diameters. For characterization of the hydrophobicity, the hydrophobic domains in the NPs were detected with Coomassie Brilliant Blue (CBB) G-250 dye that participated in strong hydrophobic interactions31,32. We found that the wavelength of the maximum absorption of CBB, a parameter dependent on the hydrophobicity of nanoparticles, gradually increased from Philic, Medium to Phobic (Fig. 2c). In addition, the Rose-Bengal (RB) binding constant33,34 of three NPs gradually increased from 0.005, 0.026 to 0.033 mL/μg (Fig. 2d). Further, to directly measure the hydrophobicity, the contact angles of NPs were detected35. As shown in Fig. 2e, the contact angles increased progressively from average 37.1° for Philic to 45.6° for Medium and 60.5° for Phobic. Taken together, these results suggested that NPs with increased hydrophobicity were successfully prepared (Fig. 2f). All NPs could remain stable in the simulated gastrointestinal (GI) condition without significant change of particle size and PDI (Supplementary Fig. 1). Besides, the Förster resonance energy transfer (FRET) assay using DSPE-PEG2000-FITC and DiI as fluorescent molecule pairs36,37, further verified the structural integrity and stable hydrophilic/hydrophobic modification of NPs after gastric processing in vivo (Supplementary Fig. 2).

Drug-loaded NPs with varied hydrophobicity for colitis therapy

The potency of NPs with increased hydrophobicity as carriers for colitis treatment was investigated. Anti-inflammatory drug budesonide (Bud) was used as the therapeutic cargo38, and the drug-loaded NPs (NPs-Bud) with increased hydrophobicity were prepared as Philic-Bud, Medium-Bud and Phobic-Bud. The drug loading process showed no influence on the particle size, PDI and zeta potential of nanoparticles, and three NPs-Bud all owned a high encapsulation efficiency to ~80% (Supplementary Table 2). The Phobic-Bud had the highest drug loading (2.80 μg/mg), mainly because the Philic-Bud and Medium-Bud contained higher amount of DSPE-PEG2000 therefore increasing the total weight of formulation. Besides, all formulations exhibited similar sustained drug release behavior in the simulated GI condition with ~40% of drug released from NPs-Bud within 10 h (Supplementary Fig. 3).

The treatment regimen was shown in Fig. 3a. The colitis rats were orally administrated with saline, free Bud, Philic-Bud, Medium-Bud and Phobic-Bud, and their body weights were recorded daily. Compared with Saline group, recovery of colitis-induced body weight loss occurred after Phobic-Bud treatment, better than that of Philic-Bud and Medium-Bud groups, while no recovery was observed for Free Bud and Saline groups (Fig. 3b). In Day 8, Phobic-Bud treatment relived the shortening of colon (Fig. 3c, d), reduced the spleen index (Fig. 3e) as well as restored the damaged colon mucosa (Supplementary Fig. 4), which were better than those of Philic-Bud and Medium-Bud groups. Enhanced reactive oxygen species (ROS) generated from infiltrated macrophages leads to pathological inflammatory in colitis39,40. By contrast, Phobic-Bud treatment could decrease the dysregulated ROS level in colon of colitis rats and showed lower ROS level than Philic-Bud and Medium-Bud groups (Fig. 3f). Colitis progression would induce the up-regulation of inflammatory cytokines in colon38–41. After treatment, Phobic-Bud group significantly decreased the abnormal up-regulation of colon TNF-α, IL-1β and IL-6 level in colitis rats, exerting better effect than Philic-Bud and Medium-Bud groups (Fig. 3g–i, Supplementary Fig. 5a–c). Notably, oral administration of free Bud almost showed no therapeutic effect for relieving the colitis progression. Therefore, the Phobic-Bud showed better colitis therapy in alleviating colonic impairment and down-regulating the inflammatory cytokines.

The colitis could induce the disordered immune cells in body including the abnormal regulatory T (Treg) cells and macrophages42,43. For rats suffered with colitis, the Treg cells (CD3+ CD4+ FOXP3+) in colon and spleen both declined significantly compared with healthy rats (Fig. 3j, and Supplementary Fig. 5d). After oral treatment, Phobic-Bud group could upregulate the proportion of Treg cells to about 10.0% in colon and 18.4% in spleen, which were both higher than that of Philic-Bud and Medium-Bud group. IL-10 is an important anti-inflammatory cytokine secreted by various immune effector cells including intestine-resident macrophages to promote expansion and mediate the immunosuppressive function of Treg cells44. Notably, Phobic-Bud group could obviously upregulate the IL-10 level as well compared with Saline group (Supplementary Fig. 5e), which was consistent with the increase of Treg cells. Moreover, Phobic-Bud treatment was able to influence the macrophage polarization and markedly decreased the ratio of M1 type (inflammatory) macrophages (F4/80+ CD11b+ CD86+) to M2 type (anti-inflammatory) macrophages (F4/80+ CD11b+ CD206+) in colon and spleen at the same time (Fig. 3k, and Supplementary Fig. 5f). The free Bud also exhibited very limited effect in regulating the disordered immune cells, demonstrating that the nano-formulations offered enhanced therapeutic benefit for budesonide. Overall, these results demonstrated oral treatment of Phobic-Bud could restore the dysregulated immune cells in colitis. Meanwhile, the result also implied the increased surface hydrophobicity of nanocarriers played an important role on oral drug delivery to colon.

Impact of hydrophobicity on colon macrophage targeting and protein adsorption

Macrophages played a crucial role in the progression of colitis, and targeted delivering therapeutic cargos to colon macrophages was beneficial to suppress inflammation and restore intestinal homeostasis23,27,45. Therefore, the colon macrophage targeting of NPs with increased hydrophobicity in colitis or healthy rats were investigated (Fig. 4a). When oral administration to colitis rat, the overlap of NPs (green) with colon macrophages (red) gradually enhanced with the increased hydrophobicity, and the high hydrophobic NPs (Colitis-Phobic) showed the best distribution in macrophages (Fig. 4b). In comparison, the NPs all barely coincided with the macrophages regardless of their hydrophobicity in the healthy rats (Fig. 4c). Besides, quantitative assay was performed by flow cytometer and similar trend was observed (Fig. 4d). The proportion of macrophages internalizing with NPs (F4/80+ CD11b+ Coumarin 6+) (Fig. 4e) and the fluorescence intensity of the internalized NPs (Fig. 4f) in colitis rats (Colitis-NPs groups) were all increased compared with healthy rats (Healthy-NPs groups). Besides, increasing hydrophobicity could promote the macrophage uptake of NPs in colitis rats, and the Colitis-Phobic group showed superior in vivo macrophage uptake among all (Fig. 4e, f). However, this phenomenon was not observed in healthy rats, and the internalized NPs with increased hydrophobicity by macrophages (Healthy-Philic, Healthy-Medium, Healthy-Phobic) were all limited. Interestingly, these results indicated that increasing hydrophobicity could promote the macrophage targeting of NPs only for the colitis rats. We deemed that hydrophobicity modulation altered the colitis-intestinal protein corona (C-IPC) formation, leading to this phenomenon instead of the hydrophobicity change itself.

To explore whether the hydrophobicity of NPs could influence the NPs-protein corona bio-interface, the in situ intestine study was conducted (Fig. 4g). Fluorescence-labeled NPs were injected into the intestinal lumen of colitis rat to form the C-IPC adsorbed NPs (NPs@C-IPC) in vivo, then the intestinal biofluids containing NPs@C-IPC were withdrawn from the lumen and the NPs@C-IPC were collected using Sephadex G50 gel size-exclusion column (No pre-removal group), and three different NPs@C-IPC were abbreviated as Philic@C-IPC, Medium@C-IPC and Phobic@C-IPC. By contrast, the intestinal segments were rinsed with acetylcysteine (NAC) solution several times to pre-remove the intestinal biofluids before tied at both sides, setting as control (Intestinal biofluids pre-removal group). When pre-removal of intestinal biofluids, the protein amount declined sharply compared with No pre-removal group (Fig. 4h), indicating the formation of in vivo C-IPC on NPs. Moreover, the protein adsorption enhanced obviously with the increased hydrophobicity of NPs, and the protein content of C-IPC on Phobic was 4.0-fold and 1.9-fold higher than that of Philic and Medium (Fig. 4i). Hence, increasing hydrophobicity could enhance the protein content of C-IPC which might amplify the macrophage targeting effect of C-IPC.

Impact of C-IPC with enhanced protein amount on macrophage recognition and uptake

To obtain the NPs@C-IPC formed in vitro, the fluorescence-labeled NPs were incubated with the intestinal biofluids extracted from colitis rats (colitis-intestinal biofluids)11. NPs@C-IPC were then separated from free proteins in the colitis-intestinal biofluids, and collected using Sephadex G50 gel size-exclusion column (Fig. 5a). Slight changes in particle size (Fig. 5b) and surface charge (Fig. 5c) were observed in NPs@C-IPC after incubation with intestinal biofluids, indicating the formation of intestinal protein corona. This was further confirmed by the TEM imaging showing that the NPs@C-IPC basically remained the spherical structure though covered with proteins (Fig. 5d). Quantification of the adsorbed C-IPC in vitro showed that the protein amount of Phobic@C-IPC was ~2.8-fold and 1.5-fold of Philic@C-IPC and Medium@C-IPC (Fig. 5e), which was consistent with the findings for C-IPC formed in vivo (Fig. 4i). Besides, NPs with (+) or without (-) fluorescent labeling owned similar contact angle and protein adsorption (Supplementary Fig. 6), indicating the fluorescent labeling will not interfere with the hydrophobicity modulation and C-IPC formation. Collectively, these results demonstrated NPs with increased hydrophobicity tended to adsorb more C-IPC on the surface.

Next, the influence of C-IPC on the macrophage uptake was investigated. The RAW264.7 cells were used as in vitro macrophage model, and all NPs or NPs@C-IPC showed no cytotoxicity (Supplementary Fig. 7). Notably, after C-IPC adsorption, the macrophage uptake of NPs@C-IPC significantly increased as compared with NPs (Fig. 5f). Among them, the Phobic@C-IPC owned the best macrophage uptake that was 1.8-fold and 5.5-fold higher than Medium@C-IPC and Philic@C-IPC. To verify whether the enhanced effect on macrophage uptake was specific for C-IPC, the healthy-intestinal protein corona (H-IPC) adsorbed NPs (NPs@H-IPC) were also prepared. Though increased hydrophobicity could also enhance the protein content of H-IPC (Supplementary Fig. 8), the improvement in macrophage uptake of NPs@H-IPC were not as profound as NPs@C-IPC (Supplementary Fig. 9). Besides, the epithelial cells (Caco-2 cells) and macrophages (RAW264.7 cells) co-cultured model was established to simulate the colon mucosa in vitro, for investigating the specific enhanced effect of C-IPC on macrophages (Fig. 5g). Caco-2 cells and RAW264.7 cells were co-cultured with different ratios and labeled by fluorescent dye DiI and DiD separately, then the internalized nanoparticles by different cells were detected by flow cytometer (Fig. 5h). In comparison with NPs without protein corona absorption, the relative cell uptake of RAW264.7 cells by NPs@C-IPC were all improved even though the ratio of epithelial cells to macrophages increased to 10:1, and the Phobic@C-IPC could always remain the highest internalization by macrophages (Fig. 5i–k). These results showed that NPs with higher hydrophobicity were preferentially taken up by macrophages, due to its ability to readily form macrophage interacting C-IPC on the surface.

Taken together, increasing hydrophobicity of NPs enhanced protein amount of C-IPC on the surface, endowing the drug-loaded NPs with better delivery efficiency to colon macrophages. However, it should be noted that although the colitis therapy was improved by Phobic-Bud, it still could not be restored to the healthy level in terms of the abnormal inflammatory cytokine level and disordered immune cells (Fig. 3).

Preparation and characterization of high hydrophobic NPs with varied rigidity

The PEGylation degree of NPs was 10% (Phobic) to maintain a high protein adsorption amount, then the rigidity was tuned by varying the amount of interfacial water layer between the polystyrene nanoparticles (PS) core and the lipid shell46,47. As illustrated in Fig. 6a, the PS with hydrodynamic diameter of 90.3 nm (PS-90) or 153.4 nm (PS-150) (Supplementary Table 3) were encapsulated into the NPs by extruding through a mini-extruder device. We named the high hydrophobic NPs encapsulated without PS or with PS-90 and PS-150 as Soft, Semi and Hard, respectively.

The hydrodynamic diameters of Soft, Semi and Hard were uniformly distributed, approximately ranging from 150 ~ 160 nm, with similar zeta potential (Fig. 6b, and Supplementary Table 3). The cryo-TEM images also demonstrated the NPs all owned spherical shape with size about 115 nm, and the empty space inside NPs full of water layer was obviously observed in Soft (Fig. 6b). With the encapsulation of PS-90 and PS-150 (Semi and Hard), the interfacial water layer gradually decreased until completely occupied (Fig. 6b). For characterization of the rigidity, internal water layer of NPs was labeled with rhodamine B46. In comparison, the fluorescence intensity of the water layer gradually reduced from Soft, Semi to Hard (Fig. 6c), indicating the gradual decrease of the amount of internal water as well. Subsequently, Young’s modulus was measured using the atomic force microscope (AFM), which was the crucial parameter for rigidity characterization of nanoparticles46–48. The result showed that the Young’s modulus of Soft, Semi and Hard increased from 10.1, 20.9–64.5 Mpa (Fig. 6d). Therefore, these results suggested the successful preparation of NPs owned similar physicochemical properties but with increased rigidity (Fig. 6e). They also had good stability in the simulated gastrointestinal conditions (Supplementary Fig. 10).

Hydrophobicity-rigidity co-modulation for effective colitis therapy

The Phobic-Bud with increased rigidity (Soft-Bud, Semi-Bud and Hard-Bud) was prepared. Three formulations also possessed similar encapsulation efficiency, drug loading (Supplementary Table 4) and sustained drug release behavior (Supplementary Fig. 11). Following the procedure illustrated in Fig. 3a, the drug-loaded high hydrophobic NPs with increased rigidity (Soft-Bud, Semi-Bud and Hard-Bud) were oral administered to colitis rats for therapeutic treatment. Compared with Saline group, treatments with three drug-loaded NPs could relieve the body weight loss and shortening of colon, but better efficacy was achieved as the rigidity of NPs increased (Fig. 7a–d). The Hard-Bud group could remarkedly decrease the colon ROS level (Fig. 7e), and recovered the damaged colon mucosa (Supplementary Fig. 12), exerting better efficacy than Soft-Bud and Semi-Bud. For inhibiting the inflammation in colon, Hard-Bud group showed similar TNF-α level as Semi-Bud group (Fig. 7f), but could maximally down-regulate the IL-1β and IL-6 levels among three drug-loaded NPs (Fig. 7g, h). More importantly, the Hard-Bud treatment could normalize all three inflammatory cytokines to the healthy level (Fig. 7f–h, and Supplementary Fig. 13a–c).

The improvement of immune cells in colitis rats was next investigated. Consistent with the anti-inflammation ability, the Hard-Bud treatment could also maximally upregulate the Treg cells in both colon and spleen to the healthy level (Fig. 7i, and Supplementary Fig. 13d), and increased the expression of anti-inflammatory cytokine IL-10 (Supplementary Fig. 13e). Moreover, the ratios of M1/M2 macrophages in colon and spleen largely decreased after oral treatment, and Hard-Bud group still exhibited the best efficacy among all, which could restore the dysregulated macrophages to healthy level (Fig. 7j, and Supplementary Fig. 13f).

Taken together, Hard-Bud had good therapeutic effect in vivo, significantly recovering the impaired colon mucosa, inhibiting the expression of inflammatory cytokines and restoring the abnormal immune cells induced by colitis. It indicated that, on the basis of maintaining high hydrophobicity, increasing the rigidity of NPs could further improve the colitis therapy.

Impact of hydrophobicity-rigidity co-modulation on colon macrophage targeting and C-IPC formation

A similar in vivo macrophage targeting study of high hydrophobic NPs with increased rigidity was conducted in colitis models (Fig. 4a). Upon oral administration of the fluorescence labeled NPs to colitis rats, much higher distribution of NPs (green) within colon macrophages (red) was observed compared to healthy rats, with the Colitis-Hard group displaying a greater proportion of NPs uptake by macrophages than the Colitis-Soft and Colitis-Semi groups (Fig. 8a). Quantitative analysis further revealed that the Colitis-Hard group had the highest proportion and quantity of NPs taken up by colon macrophages (Fig. 8b–d), demonstrating that the colon macrophage targeting of NPs could be promoted by increasing the rigidity. Further, the potential uptake of NPs@C-IPC by epithelium and other immune cells in colon was evaluated. It showed that the fluorescence intensity of internalized Hard@C-IPC by macrophages is significantly higher than that of epithelial cells and also obviously better than that of T cells, DC cells and neutrophils (Supplementary Fig. 14), indicating the C-IPC adsorption specifically promoted NPs target towards macrophages in colon. In addition, the in vivo formation of C-IPC on high hydrophobic NPs with increased rigidity was also confirmed (Supplementary Fig. 15). Therefore, the distinctive C-IPC anchored on high rigidity NPs could induce the improved macrophage targeting in vivo.

The NPs@C-IPC with increased rigidity were prepared as illustrated in Fig. 5a. Following C-IPC adsorption, an increase in particle size was noted, but minimal changes were observed in the zeta potential and morphological characteristics (Supplementary Fig. 16). Quantitative analysis of the C-IPC protein adsorbed onto NPs of varying rigidity revealed that Soft, Semi, and Hard all retained a substantial protein content, with no statistically significant differences detected between them (Fig. 8e). This observation indicated that modulating the rigidity of NPs does not significantly affect the protein content of the adsorbed C-IPC. Subsequently, we explored the impact of rigidity modulation on macrophage uptake. The NPs@C-IPC demonstrated no cytotoxic effects on RAW264.7 cells (Supplementary Fig. 17). Notably, while the cellular uptake of Soft@C-IPC, Semi@C-IPC and Hard@C-IPC was significantly enhanced compared to NPs without C-IPC adsorption, Hard@C-IPC exhibited the most pronounced enhancement (4.1-fold) and the highest macrophage uptake overall (Fig. 8f). This trend was not mirrored in H-IPC, where only a marginal improvement in macrophage uptake was observed across all three NPs@H-IPC variants (Supplementary Fig. 18).

Together, our findings indicate that the rigidity modulation strategy can further enhance the beneficial effects of C-IPC on macrophage targeting and uptake, building upon the foundation of hydrophobicity modulation. We supposed that the increased rigidity of NPs optimized the protein composition of C-IPC that possessed high protein amount, endowing the nanocarriers with superior colon macrophage targeting and colitis treatment.

According to the proteomic analysis (Proteomics data file 1, 2), there were 278, 315 and 330 proteins identified separately, and the C-IPC on Hard owned the largest variety of proteins (Fig. 9b). Next, the Gene Oncology (GO) analysis was performed to elucidate the biological function of identified proteins11,51. Interestingly, three C-IPC all contained dozens of proteins involved in the “Receptor-mediated endocytosis” (GO: 0006898), and they also all owned several proteins involved in the receptor binding annotations (Fig. 9c). It’s worth noting that the immunoglobulin receptor, G protein coupled receptor, Toll-like receptor, low-density lipoprotein receptor, EGFR receptor and RAGE receptor were all expressed on the macrophages as reported52–58. Therefore, we deemed that the adsorbed C-IPC could improve the macrophage targeting and uptake in a direct pattern through directly binding to the receptors/transporters on macrophage (Fig. 9d). In addition, three C-IPC all possessed proteins involved in the substance (including carbohydrate, monocarboxylic acid and fatty acid) binding annotations as well (Fig. 9e). These substances could be recognized and transported into cells through specific receptors/transporters, and some of them were already reported to work as ligands for improving the cell uptake of nanoparticles. For instance, the fructose (carbohydrate) and butyrate (monocarboxylic acid) modified nanoparticles were able to improve the Caco-2 cell uptake through glucose transporter (GLUT)59 and monocarboxylic acid transporter (MCT-1)60. Linolenic acid (fatty acid) modification was also reported to improve the cell uptake of micelles through fatty acid transporter (FATP4)61. Based on this, we speculated that the exterior C-IPC could also improve the macrophage targeting and uptake in a indirect pattern, by firstly binding with these substances to form specific ligands and then indirectly binding to the expressed receptors/transporters (Fig. 9f). Consequently, the two receptors/transporters-binding patterns by C-IPC worked together to exert its macrophage targeting effect, and these macrophage receptor binding-related functional proteins within C-IPC were called as macrophage-targeting proteins.

Impact of rigidity on the macrophage-targeting protein selective adsorption

The distinction between the protein composition and protein proportion of C-IPC on NPs with increased rigidity was analyzed. Venn plot analysis showed that unique proteins were observed for each C-IPC (Supplementary Figs. 19a, b). By pairwise comparison, the cluster heatmap and volcano plot analysis both showed the protein composition significantly changed after rigidity modulation of NPs, with multiple of proteins obviously upregulated and down-regulated (Supplementary Figs. 19c, d). Consequently, these altered proteins might generate impact on the macrophage targeting effect of different C-IPC.

In particular, we found several annotations about the receptor binding and substance binding were significantly changed between the C-IPC adsorbed by Semi and Hard (Fig. 10a), including the “Toll-like receptor 4 binding” (GO: 0035662), “Toll-like receptor binding” (GO: 0035325), “RAGE receptor binding” (GO: 0050786) as well as “Mannose binding” (GO: 0005537), “Fatty acid derivative binding” (GO: 1901567), “Long-chain fatty acid binding” (GO: 0036041). Our findings revealed that the C-IPC might mediate NPs binding to receptors/transporters in both direct and indirect patterns to improve the macrophage targeting and uptake (Fig. 9). We deemed that the C-IPC adsorbed by Hard could amplify this mechanism to a greater extent, thus making Hard@C-IPC owned better macrophage targeting than Semi@C-IPC. It’s worth noting that α-mannosidase (MAN2B1), β-mannosidase (MANBA) and protein S100-A8 (S100A8) were the identified functional proteins that involved in almost all of the significantly changed GO annotations (Fig. 10a), and the proportion of them were all upregulated in Hard compared with Semi especially for S100A8 protein (Fig. 10b). Notably, the C-IPC on Hard possessed the highest protein proportion of S100A8 protein among all (Fig. 10c). Then the S100A8 antibody was used to specifically block the S100A8 protein in the C-IPC for evaluating its involvement in macrophage uptake of NPs (Fig. 10d). After pre-blocking S100A8, only the macrophage uptake of Hard@C-IPC was significantly inhibited, while Soft@C-IPC and Semi@C-IPC did not change significantly (Fig. 10e). It suggested that S100A8 was a vital protein in C-IPC adsorbed by Hard to mediate the receptor binding and macrophage uptake (Fig. 10f). In consequence, increased rigidity of NPs could optimize the protein composition of C-IPC by up-regulating the proportion of macrophage-targeting proteins (including S100A8 protein), achieving better macrophage targeting and superior colitis treatment.

In addition, the colitis-intestinal biofluids (C-IF) from rats with mild colitis (Mild) or severe colitis (Severe) (Supplementary Fig. 20) were also detected using the label-free proteomic analysis via LC-MS/MS (Proteomics data file 3). There were 600 and 653 kinds of proteins identified in the Mild and Severe group (Supplementary Fig. 21a), and 239 and 76 kinds of proteins significantly upregulated or downregulated (≥2-fold) in Severe group compared with Mild group (Supplementary Fig. 21b, c), indicating the variable protein composition and level in the intestinal biofluids with the progression of colitis. Notably, the protein content of S100A8 within C-IF also significantly increased in Severe group which was about 3.2-fold higher than that of Mild group (Supplementary Fig. 21d). It was beneficial for C-IPC formation because S100A8 was the desired macrophage-targeting protein.

Discussions

After demonstrating the significant impact of protein corona on the in vivo fate of nanocarriers, researchers have made considerable efforts to utilize them for improving the delivery efficiency. Some progress has been achieved in the parenteral administrated nanocarriers, such as hydrophilic PEGylation to reduce the adsorption of opsonin for prolonging blood circulation62,63, or specific ligand modification to enrich the transferrin corona adsorption for improving transport across the blood-brain barrier64. Encouraged by these advancements, this study is devoted to taking advantage of the colitis-specific intestinal protein corona (C-IPC) with moderate macrophage targeting ability for efficient oral drug delivery. Moreover, to fully explore the macrophage-targeting potential of C-IPC for precise oral drug delivery, a sequential hydrophobicity-rigidity co-modulation strategy was developed to subtly manipulate the C-IPC formation on nanocarriers.

The lipid-based NPs were chosen as the model in this study owing to their drug-loading capacity and controllable physicochemical property65. The hydrophobicity of NPs was modulated by altering the surface PEGylation degree, and high hydrophobic NPs (Phobic) loaded with budesonide fulfilled better colitis therapy after oral administration. Correspondingly, the in vivo colon macrophage targeting and internalization in colitis rats were also enhanced through increasing the hydrophobicity of NPs. In contrast, this phenomenon was not observed in healthy rats, and increasing hydrophobicity could not promote the macrophage targeting. Hence, the improved oral macrophage targeting delivery of NPs was not induced by the increased surface hydrophobicity, but depended on the governed C-IPC on the surface. Higher surface hydrophobicity enriched more proteins on NPs during the gastrointestinal tract transport, resulting in better macrophage delivery and internalization. If C-IPC is viewed as a targeting ligand decorating the surface of nanocarriers, then increasing the surface hydrophobicity of the nanocarriers is equivalent to increasing the density of this targeting ligand, thereby enhancing macrophage targeting and improving therapeutic efficacy in colitis. In this study, the NPs made of soyabean lecithin and cholesterol own lipid bilayer and inner water layer, belonging to liposomes. Additionally, we prepared NPs made of different materials including poly (lactic-co-glycolic acid) nanoparticles (PLGA-NPs)66 composed of polymers and gold nanoparticles (Au-NPs)67 composed of metal material (Supplementary Table 6). It showed that by altering the PEGylation, the increased surface hydrophobicity can also enhance the protein adsorption amount on the PLGA-NPs and Au-NPs (Supplementary Fig. 22). Therefore, we believe the hydrophobicity modulation strategy in this study was feasible for different types of nanocarriers to manipulate the protein corona adsorption.

NPs mainly composed of lipid materials were regarded as soft nanocarriers with low rigidity due to their interior water layer. Hence, the polystyrene nanoparticles with different hydrodynamic diameters were encapsulated into NPs to further regulate the water layer amount for altering the holistic rigidity of Phobic. Notably, Phobic with high rigidity (Hard) showed superior macrophage targeted drug delivery, which effectively alleviated the colitis by restoring the upregulated inflammatory cytokines and dysregulated immune cells back to healthy level. It’s worth noting that rigidity modulation did not impede the protein adsorption induced by high surface hydrophobicity, suggesting that the protein composition especially the functional protein abundance within C-IPC might change along with the rigidity of NPs. There were studies reporting that the rigidity may influence the endocytic pathways of nanoparticles to increase their macrophage uptake68,69. However, in our study NPs with different rigidity could be internalized by macrophages through clathrin-mediated endocytosis, caveolin-mediated endocytosis and micropinocytosis (Supplementary Fig. 23a). Besides, after C-IPC adsorption, the macrophage uptake of the NPs@C-IPC was also involved with these three pathways (Supplementary Fig. 23b). It indicated the increased rigidity and C-IPC adsorption will not change the endocytic pathways, thus the improved macrophage uptake of Hard@C-IPC relied on the involvement of adsorbed functional proteins.

Through proteomics analysis, NPs possessing high rigidity were able to selectively adsorb more macrophage-targeting related proteins such as S100A8 protein in the colitis intestinal biofluids. The S100A8 belonged to the Ca2+ binding S100 protein family and it could interact with the pattern recognition receptors Toll-like receptor 4 (TLR4) and Receptor for Advanced Glycation Endproducts (RAGE), both expressed on macrophages70. Moreover, through comparing the intestinal biofluids from rats with mild and severe colitis, we found the colitis severity could influence the protein composition and level in the intestinal biofluids. These significantly altered proteins could exert a stronger biological effect on immune response, receptor binding and substance binding (Supplementary Fig. 21e, f). Notably, the protein level of S100A8 obviously increased with the progression of colitis, which was conducive to the protein corona formation because S100A8 was the desired macrophage-targeting protein. Therefore, we believe the increased severity of colitis might influence the composition of protein corona on nanocarriers. These significantly altered proteins in C-IPC could exert a stronger biological effect on immune response, receptor binding, and substance binding, which was beneficial for macrophage-targeted drug delivery. In conclusion, benefiting from the protein corona manipulation mediated by the hydrophobicity-rigidity co-modulation, the tailored C-IPC owning both high protein abundance and high macrophage-targeting protein proportion enabled Hard with efficient oral targeted drug delivery for desired colitis therapy.

Through subtly exploiting the natural targeting ability of disease-specific intestinal protein corona formed in the inflammatory condition, the protein corona-adaptive oral drug delivery systems were successfully constructed in this work, which provided a different view for targeted drug delivery and colitis treatment. Different from the conventional tactics for improving the delivery efficiency such as the ligand modification and novel material application, our work proposed and validated the concept of rationally tailoring the disease-specific protein corona through multiple-physicochemical properties co-modulation of nanocarriers for efficient drug delivery. More importantly, this strategy may be applicable for various types of nanocarriers and diseases.

Cells and animals

The Caco-2 cells and RAW264.7 macrophages were obtained from the Institute of Biochemistry and Cell Biology (Shanghai, China). RAW264.7 macrophages were cultured in Dulbecco’s modified eagle medium (DMEM, BasalMedia, China) containing 10% (v/v) fetal bovine serum (FBS, Excell, China), 1% (v/v) penicillin and streptomycin solution (100 IU/mL, Solarbio, China) at 37 °C in an incubator with 5% CO2. Sprague-Dawley (SD) rats (male, 200–220 g, 7 weeks old) were obtained from Chengdu Dossy experimental animals Co.,Ltd (China). The rats were raised in the qualified animal room under pathogen free conditions at constant temperature (25 °C). All animal experiments were performed in compliance with the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Sichuan University (accreditation number, SYXK (Chuan) 2018-113).

The colitis rats were established as in our previous report11. The SD rats were fasted overnight but free access to water. TNBS aqueous solution (5%, w/v) was mixed with equal volume of ethanol to form the TNBS-ethanol solution at a final concentration of 2.5%. Rats were anesthetized by isoflurane (Small Animal Anesthesia Machine, RWD Life Science, China), then medical-grade rubber cannula was inserted about 8 cm through the anus. The TNBS-ethanol solution was infused into the lumen of colon at TNBS dose of 80 mg/kg. Then the rats were kept for a supine position for about 5 min after administration to ensure the sufficient infiltration of TNBS-ethanol in colon. After the treatment, the rats were fed for a week to induce the colitis. According to the “Guideline of assessment for humane endpoints in animal experiment (RB/T 173-2018, China)”, the humane endpoints in this study were set as body weight of rats reduced to more than 20% of initial. These rats would be removed from the experiments and euthanized via intraperitoneal injection of pentobarbital.

Preparation and characterization of NPs with varied hydrophobicity

Soybean lecithin and cholesterol as the lipid component of NPs were mixed with DSPE-PEG2000 at different mass ratios (soybean lecithin: cholesterol: DSPE-PEG2000 = 10:2.5:8, 10:2.5:4 or 10:2.5:1, equal to PEGylation of 80%, 40% and 10%). The mixture was dissolved in 4 mL chloroform, and then the chloroform was removed by a rotary evaporator at 37 °C in a vacuum to form the thin lipid film. Then the lipid film was hydrated with 4 mL distilled water for 5 min to form the crude lipid suspensions, followed by sonication using the ultrasonic-processor (Ningbo Scientz Biotechnology Co., Ltd, China) on ice bath (120 W, 3 min) to finally obtain the NPs with different PEG modification. The fluorescence Coumarin 6-labeled NPs were prepared by extra adding the Coumarin 6 dye to the initial lipid and DSPE-PEG2000 mixture before the evaporation process.

The hydrodynamic diameter, polydispersity index (PDI) and zeta potential of NPs were measured by Malvern Zetasizer (Malvern Instruments Ltd., UK). The morphological characteristics of the NPs were examined by cryogenic transmission electron microscopy (cryo-TEM) using a Titan Krios G4 instrument (Thermo Fisher Scientific). For sample preparation, a droplet of the NPs suspension was applied to a glow-discharged, carbon-coated copper grid (300 mesh, R2/1, Quantifoil Micro Tools). Blotting was conducted for 5 seconds under conditions of 4 °C and 100% humidity employing a Vitrobot Mark IV (Thermo Fisher Scientific). Immediate vitrification was achieved by rapidly plunging the grid into liquid ethane, after which the vitrified sample was stored in liquid nitrogen at 90 K. Subsequently, the grid was transferred into the microscope, which was operated at an accelerating voltage of 300 kV. Image acquisition was performed with a Falcon 4i detector at a nominal magnification of 81,000×.

Finally, the UV absorbance spectrum of the mixture was measured at 550-650 nm using a microplate reader and the CBB solution without NPs were set as control. Preparation and characterization of NPs with varied rigidity

Soybean lecithin, cholesterol as lipid component and DSPE-PEG2000 at mass ratio of 10:2.5:1 (maintain PEGylation of 10%) were dissolved in 4 mL chloroform, and then evaporated to form the thin lipid film. The dry lipid film was then hydrated with 4 mL distilled water without polystyrene nanoparticles (PS), or 4 mL distilled water contained 1 mg of PS with theoretical diameter of 90 nm (PS-90), or 4 mL distilled water contained 1 mg of PS with theoretical diameter of 150 nm (PS-150) for 5 min at 37 °C to form the crude lipid suspensions. The hydrodynamic diameter, PDI, zeta potential and cryo-TEM images of NPs with varied rigidity were also measured.

Water content study

To label the water layer inside NPs, 200 μM rhodamine B in distilled water were applied as the hydration solution. After obtained the labeled NPs, the free rhodamine B was removed from the NPs using a Sephadex G50 gel size-exclusion column, and the collected NPs were diluted to the same concentration. The fluorescence emission spectra of the collected NPs were measured at an excitation wavelength of 510 to 700 nm using a microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA).

Young’s modulus measurement

The NPs suspensions were dropped onto the surface of freshly cleaved mica and air-dried at room temperature. The NPs samples were measured using the atomic force microscopy (AFM, Cypher VRS, Oxford instruments, UK). The Young’s modulus of NPs was then analyzed and calculated using the JKR model in the Igor Pro software.

Colitis therapy in vivo

In Day 0, SD rats (male, 200–220 g, 7 weeks old) were treated with TNBS solution to induce the colitis and the rats without treatment were set as healthy control group (Healthy). In Day 1, the colitis rats were orally administrated with free Bud, Philic-Bud, Medium-Bud and Phobic-Bud at budesonide dose of 0.6 mg/kg/day once a day, and the colitis rats without treatment of budesonide were set as another control group (Saline). The body weight of rats in each group (n = 5) was measured and recorded daily. In Day 8, the rats were sacrificed and the colons and spleens were collected. The collected colons and spleens were gently washed with PBS solution and imaged, then the colon length and spleen weight were measured. The colon mucosa was evaluated by the Hematoxylin-eosin staining (H&E) analysis and Masson analysis. To investigate the ROS level, the colons were grinded on an ice bath and the colon cell suspensions were collected. The ROS level of colon cells was measured using Reactive oxygen species (ROS) assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Briefly, the colon cells were incubated with DCFH-DA (10 μM, ROS probe) at 37 °C for 20 min. After incubation, the cells were washed with PBS solution twice, and the fluorescence intensity of DCF in the cells was detected by flow cytometry.

To investigate the inflammation level, the colons were evaluated by immunohistochemical analysis. Briefly, the colons were fixed with paraformaldehyde (4%, w/v) and embedded in paraffin. Afterwards, the colons were cut into slices, the sections were washed and incubated with goat serum (10%, v/v) for at 37 °C 30 min. Then the colon samples were incubated with anti-TNF-α, anti-IL-1β, anti-IL-6 and anti-IL-10 antibody at 4 °C overnight, and second antibody at 37 °C for 45 min. At last, the cell nucleus was stained with hematoxylin and the samples were recorded by microscope (BX53, Olympus Corporation, Japan). In addition, the inflammatory cytokine levels of different groups were quantitatively measured according to the protocol of the ELISA kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). The inflammatory cytokine concentrations of the colon suspensions were calculated according to the standard curves, and then the protein concentrations of colon suspensions were measured using the BCA protein assay kit. Finally, the inflammatory cytokine concentrations measured by the ELISA kit were normalized by calibrating with the protein concentrations of different groups.

To investigate the immune cells, the colon and spleen cells were stained with FITC-conjugated CD3 antibody and PE/Cyanine7-conjugated CD4 antibody at 4 °C for 1 h. These cells were punched and stained with Percp/5.5-conjugated Foxp3 antibody at 4 °C for 1 h, and then washed with PBS solution thrice, followed by flow cytometry analysis (Treg cells). For quantitative analysis of the macrophages, the colon and spleen cells were incubated with anti-CD16/32 antibody for 30 min. Then the cells were washed with PBS solution twice and stained with anti-F4/80, anti-CD11b, anti-CD86 and anti-CD206 antibody at 4 °C for 1 h. Afterwards, cells were washed with PBS solution and the M1 type and M2 type macrophages were detected by flow cytometry.

The colitis therapy experiment by budesonide-loaded NPs with different rigidity (Soft-Bud, Semi-Bud and Hard-Bud) was also proceeded as described above. The power analysis was conducted to verify the sample number was set reasonably.

Colon macrophage targeting in vivo

The fluorescence labeled NPs were orally administrated to colitis SD rats or healthy SD rats (male, 200-220 g, 7 weeks old). After 6 h, the rats were sacrificed and the colon was collected and washed with saline solution. The colon was fixed with paraformaldehyde (4%, w/v), dehydrated in sucrose (30%, w/v) overnight, embedded into optimum cutting temperature compound (OCT), and sectioned at a depth of 10 mm (Lecia, Germany). Then the colon samples were stained with anti-CD68 antibody and DAPI solution. Finally, the colon samples were imaged by confocal laser scanning microscopy (CLSM) (LSM800, Carl Zeiss, Germany).

For quantitative analysis, the collected colon was grinded on an ice bath and the colon cell suspensions were collected. The colon cell suspensions were stained with anti-F4/80 antibody and anti-CD11b antibody at 4 °C for 1 h. Afterwards, cells were washed with PBS solution and detected by flow cytometry.

Preparation and characterization of NPs@C-IPC

Colitis-intestinal biofluids (C-IF) extracted from the colitis rats was applied to simulate the intestinal milieu11. The fluorescence labeled NPs suspensions were mixed with the colitis-intestinal biofluids in the Protein LoBind tube (Eppendorf, Germany). Then the mixture was incubated at 37 °C for 1 h under shaking. After incubation, the C-IPC adsorbed nanoparticles (NPs@C-IPC) and the free proteins were separated using Sephadex G50 gel size-exclusion column. The NPs@C-IPC fractions were collected, concentrated by ultrafiltration centrifugation (3920 g, 30 min) and finally dispersed with PBS or HBSS solution for further use. The particle size, PDI and zeta potential of NPs@C-IPC were measured by Malvern Zetasizer, and their morphology was detected by TEM. Besides, the H-IPC adsorbed nanoparticles (NPs@H-IPC) were prepared as described above and only the colitis-intestinal biofluids were replaced with healthy-intestinal biofluids from healthy rats.

Macrophage uptake study

RAW264.7 cells were cultured in 24-well plates for 1 day before use. The fluorescence labeled NPs and NPs@C-IPC suspensions (100 μg/mL) were incubated with RAW264.7 cells for 3 h. The RAW264.7 cells were rinsed with PBS solution twice, and then the macrophage uptake was detected by a flow cytometer (Beckman Colter, USA).

In addition, the Caco-2 cells and RAW264.7 cells were stained with fluorescent dye DiI and DiD separately. Afterwards, different ratio (1:1, 5:1, 10:1) of the stained Caco-2 cells and RAW264.7 cells were co-cultured in 24-well plates and then incubated with the fluorescence labeled NPs and NPs@C-IPC suspensions for 3 h. Finally, the internalized nanoparticles by Caco-2 cells or RAW264.7 cells were detected by flow cytometer.

Protein amount of C-IPC

The protein samples from NPs@C-IPC were eluted and then the protein content of the C-IPC formed in vitro was measured using Bradford protein assay kit (Beyotime Biotechnology, China). Briefly, the C-IPC samples (5 μL) were added into 96-well plates, followed by addition of 250 μL Coomassie Blue G-250 staining solution. The absorbance at 590 nm of each sample were recorded by microplate reader (Thermo Scientific, USA). The bovine serum albumin (BSA) solution with the same procedure was recorded for the calibration curve and the protein content of C-IPC samples were calculated. The results were expressed as the protein amount (μg) per nanoparticles (μg).

After anesthesia, the intestinal lumen of colitis rat was rinsed with acetylcysteine (NAC) solution several times to pre-remove the intestinal biofluids before tied at both sides, and then the fluorescence labeled NPs solutions were injected into the lumen for 1 h. Then the suspensions were withdrawn from the lumen and the NPs@C-IPC were collected using Sephadex G50 gel size-exclusion column. Finally, protein content of C-IPC formed in vivo was measured using Bradford protein assay kit, and rat without pre-removal of intestinal biofluids was set as control. In addition, the C-IPC was also detected by SDS-PAGE assay.

Proteomic analysis of C-IPC and C-IF

The C-IPC formed on NPs with different rigidity and the C-IF extracted from mild or severe colitis rats were detected using the proteomic analysis via LC-MS/MS. The filter-aided sample preparation (FASP) procedure was performed. Aliquots of 100 ug of C-IPC or C-IF protein samples were diluted in urea buffer UA (8 M urea, 0.1 M Tris-HCl, pH 8.5) to a final concentration of 0.5% sodium dodecyl sulfate (SDS). Protein samples were further processed in Microcon 30 kDa Centrifugal Filter Units (Merck) in a tempered centrifuge at 24 °C. Samples were added to the filter unit, washed with UA buffer, centrifuged for 15 min at 14000 g, and incubated with 50 mM IAA for 20 min at room temperature (in the dark). SDS was exchanged by four consecutive washes with UA buffer (centrifugation for 15 min at 14000 g) and a single wash with 50 mM ammonium bicarbonate (ABC) buffer followed by centrifugation for 5 min at 14000 g. Proteins were digested using trypsin solution (25 μg/mL) in a 1:50 protein-to-enzyme ratio and incubated for 18 h at 37 °C on a thermoshaker at 600 rpm. The resulting peptides were recovered by centrifugation at 14000 g for 5 min, followed by elution with 50 μL of 50 mM ABC buffer and repeated centrifugation. Combined eluates were acidified using TFA at a final concentration of 1%. Finally, the filtrate was collected and the peptide samples were obtained by lyophilization. Then the peptides dissolved in 0.1% formic acid were loaded onto a Thermo Scientific analytical column (75 μm × 25 cm, 5 μm, C18) and separated by an Easy nano LC system (Thermo Scientific, USA). Mobile phase A was 0.1% formic acid, and mobile phase B was 0.1% formic acid with acetonitrile. Peptides were separated with a linear gradient from 0 to 5% phase B within 5 min, followed by an increase to 30% phase B within 55 min and further to 35% phase B within 5 min, followed by a washing step at 95% phase B and re-equilibration. Then the separated samples were analyzed by mass spectrometric (MS, Obitrap Fusion Lumos, Thermo Scientific, USA). The results (Raw files) were analyzed using the MaxQuant software (version 2.0.1.0). MS/MS spectra were searched against UniProt database.

The amount of protein was calculated using spectral counting (SpC), which represented the total number of the MS/MS spectra for all peptides attributed to a detected protein. The relative quantitative concentration of each protein identified in C-IPC or C-IF was assessed by calculating the total spectral counting (SpC) of all peptides matched to a specific protein, and then normalized for the molecular weight (Mw) of each protein19,71,72. The effect of S100A8 protein on macrophage uptake

The effect of S100A8 protein in the C-IPC was investigated by pre-blocking NPs@C-IPC with the S100A8 antibody. Briefly, the NPs@C-IPC suspensions were incubated with S100A8 antibody solution (1 mg/mL in PBS (pH 7.4), HUABIO, China) for 1 h, and then separated using Sephadex G50 gel size-exclusion column. Afterwards, the resulting NPs@C-IPC with S100A8 antibody pre-blocking (S100A8 antibody group) were incubated with RAW264.7 cells for 3 h. Finally, the cells were washed and collected for the cell uptake detection by flow cytometer. In comparison, the NPs@C-IPC without S100A8 antibody pre-blocking were set as control (Control group). The cell uptake result was presented as the percentage of macrophage uptake in S100A8 antibody group compared to Control group.

Statistical analysis

All data were presented as mean ± standard deviations (SD) and they were obtained at least three times via independent data. All statistical analyses were performed using Student’s t-test or one-way analysis of variance (ANOVA) using the IBM SPSS® software (version 23.0), and p < 0.05 were defined to be statistically significant.
