Small-molecule PCSK9 inhibition enhances BBB amyloid-β clearance and suppresses microglial inflammation in Alzheimer’s disease models
Authors: Jie Miao, Jing Wang, Wenhu Zhou, Junhong Guo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Impaired amyloid-β (Aβ) clearance and chronic neuroinflammation are central drivers of Alzheimer’s disease (AD) progression, yet therapeutic strategies targeting these processes remain limited. Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) negatively regulates low-density lipoprotein receptor family members, including low-density lipoprotein receptor -related protein 1 (LRP1), a key mediator of blood-brain barrier (BBB) Aβ efflux, and has emerging roles in inflammatory signaling. In this study, to define the therapeutic relevance of PCSK9 inhibition in AD-like pathology, we examined SBC-115,076 in AlCl₃-challenged zebrafish larvae and mechanistically validated its effects using complementary endothelial–microglial in vitro models. SBC-115,076 significantly improved locomotor behavior and sensorimotor responsiveness, reduced Aβ deposition and neuronal apoptosis, and normalized oxidative stress, cholinergic dysfunction, and neuroinflammatory markers in vivo. Mechanistically, SBC-115,076 downregulated endothelial PCSK9 and upregulated LRP1, thereby enhancing Aβ endocytosis, lysosomal trafficking, and selective brain-to-blood efflux across an in vitro BBB model. In parallel, SBC-115,076 suppressed Aβ-induced CD36/TLR4 signaling in microglia, attenuated M1-like activation, promoted M2-like polarization, and reduced pro-inflammatory cytokine release. Collectively, these findings demonstrate that small-molecule PCSK9 inhibition ameliorates AD-like pathology through coordinated enhancement of BBB-mediated Aβ clearance and suppression of microglial inflammatory amplification, highlighting PCSK9 as a multifaceted therapeutic target and supporting a BBB-oriented strategy for AD intervention.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1038/s41598-026-46671-y.

Introduction

Alzheimer’s disease (AD) is the most common progressive neurodegeneration and a major driver of dementia in older adults1. Despite extensive research efforts, disease-modifying therapies that reliably halt or reverse AD progression remain limited, resulting in a substantial and growing global health burden1. Neuropathologically, AD features extracellular deposition of β-amyloid (Aβ) plaques, intracellular neurofibrillary tangles, sustained neuroinflammation, synaptic impairment, and a gradual loss of neurons2,3. Despite showing benefits in specific patient subgroups, anti-Aβ monoclonal antibodies have limited broad applicability because of safety concerns, most notably amyloid-related imaging abnormalities (ARIA) such as edema and intracerebral hemorrhage4. These concerns highlight the need for better-tolerated approaches that engage additional, complementary disease pathways.

Proprotein convertase subtilisin/kexin type 9 (PCSK9) is a secreted serine protease best known for controlling cholesterol metabolism by directing low-density lipoprotein receptor (LDLR) family proteins toward lysosomal degradation5. Beyond its well-established role in cardiovascular disease, PCSK9 is also expressed in the central nervous system (CNS), where it influences LDLR-family receptor levels in both neurons and glia and has been linked to neurogenesis, synaptic remodeling, and cognition6,7. PCSK9 abundance appears to be increased in AD, as reported in brain tissue and cerebrospinal fluid8. Mechanistically, cell-based studies indicate that PCSK9 can promote the turnover of low-density lipoprotein receptor-related protein 1 (LRP1) and diminish its membrane availability9, suggesting a potential route by which PCSK9 upregulation could further compromise Aβ clearance in AD. Given the physiological roles of PCSK9 in the CNS, the cognitive and neurobiological consequences of PCSK9-targeted interventions warrant careful and context-specific evaluation6,7,10.

Beyond defective Aβ clearance, persistent neuroinflammation and oxidative stress are widely recognized as factors in the progression of AD11. As Aβ accumulates, microglia become persistently activated, resulting in prolonged inflammatory signaling and increased production of neurotoxic cytokines that aggravate synaptic and neuronal injury11. Emerging studies also implicate PCSK9 in shaping inflammatory responses in peripheral pathologies. In atherosclerosis and related metabolic disorders, PCSK9 enhances macrophage activation and upregulates pro-inflammatory mediators, including TNF-α and IL-6, thereby intensifying downstream inflammatory cascades12,13. Given the macrophage-like identity of microglia, PCSK9 may exert analogous pro-inflammatory effects within the AD brain microenvironment. However, direct evidence linking PCSK9 to microglial activation and phenotype regulation in AD-relevant settings remains limited.

PCSK9 monoclonal antibodies have provided proof-of-concept that PCSK9 suppression can enhance Aβ clearance and mitigate neuropathology in experimental models14. Nevertheless, clinical translation of antibody-based strategies is hindered by invasive administration routes, high cost, limited shelf life, and potential immunogenicity15,16. In contrast, the development of small-molecule PCSK9 inhibitors has emerged as a promising alternative. These agents offer significant advantages, including accessibility, cost-effectiveness, oral bioavailability, and enhanced cellular permeability15–17. SBC-115,076 is a small-molecule PCSK9 inhibitor reported to reduce PCSK9 activity, increase LDLR expression, and modulate inflammatory responses in peripheral disease models17,18. However, its effects on CNS Aβ clearance and neuroinflammation have not been systematically investigated.Zebrafish (Danio rerio) provide a tractable and efficient platform for modeling neurodegenerative diseases due to their genetic conservation with mammals, rapid development, optical transparency at larval stages, and suitability for high-throughput pharmacological screening19. Aluminum exposure induces a spectrum of AD-like features, including cholinergic dysfunction, oxidative stress, neuroinflammation, amyloid-like aggregation, and behavioral impairment20. Accordingly, AlCl₃-treated zebrafish larvae reproduce key pathological and functional characteristics of AD and are increasingly employed for early-stage evaluation of candidate therapeutics21,22.

In this work, we evaluated whether SBC-115,076 confers therapeutic benefit in AD-like pathology and delineated the mechanisms that may underlie its effects. Using an AlCl₃-induced zebrafish larval model in combination with endothelial and microglial cell systems, we tested the hypothesis that pharmacological PCSK9 inhibition ameliorates AD-like phenotypes through dual and cooperative mechanisms: (i) restoration of blood-brain barrier (BBB)-mediated Aβ clearance via the PCSK9–LRP1 axis and (ii) suppression of Aβ-driven microglial inflammatory activation through modulation of CD36/TLR4 signaling. Our findings demonstrate that small-molecule PCSK9 inhibition improves behavioral performance, reduces amyloid-like deposition and neuronal apoptosis, normalizes oxidative and cholinergic dysfunction, enhances endothelial Aβ efflux, and promotes anti-inflammatory microglial polarization. Collectively, these results identify PCSK9 as a multifaceted therapeutic target and support small-molecule PCSK9 inhibitors as a promising, BBB-oriented strategy for AD intervention.

Results

SBC-115,076 ameliorates AlCl₃-induced locomotor and sensorimotor deficits in Zebrafish Larvae

To evaluate whether SBC-115,076 alleviates AlCl₃-induced neurobehavioral impairment, spontaneous locomotor activity and light–dark transition responses were assessed in 6 days post-fertilization (dpf) zebrafish larvae. Control larvae exhibited normal exploratory swimming patterns with evident thigmotaxis, whereas larvae exposed to AlCl₃ displayed disorganized movement trajectories, reduced thigmotactic behavior, and frequent episodes of motor freezing (Fig. 1A). Quantitative behavioral metrics showed that AlCl₃ exposure significantly reduced both total locomotor distance and average swimming speed relative to control larvae (Fig. 1B, C).

To determine the effective concentration of SBC-115,076, larvae were treated with three doses (10, 20, and 40 µM). The 10 µM dose failed to produce a significant improvement in locomotor parameters. In contrast, treatment with 20 or 40 µM SBC-115,076 significantly restored total distance traveled and swimming speed relative to the AlCl₃ model group (Fig. 1B, C). As no additional benefit was observed at 40 µM, the 20 µM dose was selected for subsequent experiments.

Sensorimotor responsiveness was further assessed using the light–dark transition paradigm. AlCl₃-treated larvae exhibited markedly reduced locomotor activity during both light and dark phases, as well as a blunted locomotor response following light–dark transitions (Fig. 1D–F). Administration of 20 µM SBC-115,076 significantly improved locomotor performance across the entire 60-min recording period and restored responsiveness to illumination changes, achieving a dyskinesia recovery rate of 74.5% (Fig. 1D–F). Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SBC-115,076 effectively rescues AlCl₃-induced locomotor dysfunction and sensorimotor deficits, indicating its neuroprotective potential in this AD-like zebrafish model.

SBC-115,076 attenuates AlCl₃-induced Aβ deposition and neuronal apoptosis in Zebrafish Larvae

To determine whether SBC-115,076 modulates cerebral Aβ pathology, immunohistochemical staining was performed to assess Aβ deposition in the brains of zebrafish larvae. Compared with control larvae, AlCl₃ exposure induced a marked increase in Aβ-positive staining in the brain (Fig. 2A, B), indicating enhanced Aβ deposition under AD-like conditions. Treatment with SBC-115,076 significantly reduced the Aβ-positive area relative to the AlCl₃ model group (Fig. 2A, B). Consistent with these findings, Thioflavin S staining showed increased amyloid-like deposition in the AlCl₃ group, which was also attenuated by SBC-115,076 treatment (Fig. S1A, B).

Neuronal apoptosis was evaluated by terminal deoxynucleotidyl transferase dUTP nick end labeling (TUNEL) staining. AlCl₃-exposed larvae showed a pronounced increase in TUNEL-positive cells in the brain (Fig. 2C, D), whereas SBC-115,076 treatment significantly reduced apoptotic cell density. Consistently, in vivo acridine orange (AO) labeling revealed enhanced brain fluorescence in the AlCl₃ group, which was attenuated by SBC-115,076 treatment (Fig. S1C).

To assess in vivo target engagement, pcsk9 mRNA expression was quantified in zebrafish larvae. AlCl₃ exposure resulted in a significant upregulation of pcsk9 transcripts, whereas treatment with SBC-115,076 effectively reversed this pathological increase (Fig. 3A), indicating successful target modulation in vivo.

To probe cholinergic involvement in AD-like pathology, we next measured acetylcholinesterase (AChE) activity. Larvae in the AlCl₃ model group exhibited a marked elevation in AChE activity compared with controls. SBC-115,076 treatment significantly reduced AChE activity, restoring it toward baseline levels observed in the control group (Fig. 3B).

Oxidative stress parameters were next assessed to determine whether SBC-115,076 alleviates AlCl₃-induced redox imbalance. Consistent with oxidative injury, AlCl₃ exposure significantly increased hydrogen peroxide (H₂O₂) production and malondialdehyde (MDA) levels, while reducing superoxide dismutase (SOD) activity and the content of reduced glutathione (GSH) (Fig. 3C–F). Treatment with 20 µM SBC-115,076 significantly attenuated H₂O₂ accumulation and lipid peroxidation, while restoring endogenous antioxidant defenses, as evidenced by increased SOD activity and GSH levels (Fig. 3C–F). Together, these results align with prior evidence implicating oxidative stress in amyloid-associated neurotoxicity23.

Neuroinflammatory responses were further evaluated by quantitative real-time PCR (qPCR) quantification of pro-inflammatory mediators. AlCl₃ exposure robustly increased il-1β, il-6, tnf-α, and nos2a expression (Fig. 3G), consistent with an AD-like inflammatory state3. In contrast, SBC-115,076 significantly reduced the induction of these genes (Fig. 3G).

Collectively, these results demonstrate that SBC-115,076 simultaneously ameliorates cholinergic dysfunction, oxidative stress, and neuroinflammation in vivo, highlighting its multi-target neuroprotective potential in an AlCl₃-induced zebrafish model of AD-like pathology. To clarify the molecular basis of these effects, we subsequently performed in vitro experiments in endothelial cells and microglia.

SBC-115,076 upregulates LRP1 and promotes Aβ uptake and efflux in bEnd.3 endothelial cells

A Cell Counting Kit-8 (CCK-8) assay was first conducted to identify non-cytotoxic and pharmacologically effective concentrations of SBC-115,076 for endothelial studies. Aβ₁–₄₂ exposure significantly reduced bEnd.3 cell viability, whereas SBC-115,076 co-treatment (5–20 µM) produced a dose-dependent improvement in cell survival. The strongest cytoprotective effect was observed at 20 µM, which was subsequently used for downstream assays (Fig. S2A).

To investigate whether SBC-115,076 modulates the PCSK9-LRP1 regulatory axis in endothelial cells, PCSK9 expression was first examined in Aβ-treated bEnd.3 cells by immunofluorescence staining. PCSK9 immunoreactivity was detectable under basal conditions and was further increased after Aβ₁–₄₂ exposure; this increase was attenuated by SBC-115,076 treatment (Fig. S3A, B). Consistent with this protein-level trend, quantitative analysis following 24 h of exposure showed that SBC-115,076 significantly downregulated Pcsk9 mRNA while concomitantly upregulating Lrp1 mRNA expression (Fig. 4A, B), in line with previous evidence that PCSK9 negatively regulates LRP1 stability and abundance9.

The functional consequences of LRP1 upregulation on Aβ handling were next evaluated. bEnd.3 cells were exposed to 5-TAMRA-labeled Aβ₁–₄₂, and intracellular Aβ accumulation was quantified by flow cytometry. SBC-115,076 treatment significantly increased the intracellular mean fluorescence intensity (MFI) at 2, 4, and 8 h compared with untreated controls (Fig. 4C, D, Fig. S4), indicating enhanced Aβ uptake. Confocal microscopy further revealed increased colocalization of internalized Aβ with LysoTracker-labeled lysosomes in SBC-115,076-treated cells (Fig. 4E), suggesting facilitated lysosomal trafficking and degradation of Aβ.

To determine whether SBC-115,076 enhances endothelial Aβ transcytosis, a Transwell-based in vitro BBB model was established to quantify bidirectional Aβ transport. SBC-115,076 treatment significantly increased the clearance quotient (CQ) in the brain-to-blood-like direction (B→A), while CQ remained unchanged in the blood-to-brain-like (A→B) direction (Fig. 4F, G). These findings indicate that SBC-115,076 selectively promotes endothelial Aβ efflux without enhancing Aβ influx. This effect is consistent with the established role of endothelial LRP1 as a major mediator of BBB Aβ clearance24 and aligns with recent BBB-targeted strategies demonstrating rapid Aβ removal and therapeutic benefit25.

Collectively, these results demonstrate that SBC-115,076 suppresses PCSK9 expression, upregulates LRP1, and functionally enhances both endothelial Aβ uptake and directional efflux across the BBB, thereby supporting its potential as a modulator of Aβ clearance pathways in AD.

SBC-115,076 suppresses Aβ-induced microglial activation and promotes M2-like polarization via the CD36/TLR4 axis in BV2 cells

The cytocompatibility and protective effects of SBC-115,076 in microglia were first evaluated using a CCK-8 assay. Exposure to Aβ₁–₄₂ significantly reduced BV2 cell viability, whereas co-treatment with SBC-115,076 markedly improved cell survival (Fig. S2B), indicating a protective effect against Aβ-induced cytotoxicity.

To evaluate whether SBC-115,076 alters PCSK9 expression in microglia, endogenous Pcsk9 mRNA levels were quantified. SBC-115,076 treatment significantly downregulated Pcsk9 expression in BV2 cells (Fig. 5A). In the Aβ-induced inflammatory model, the expression of the scavenger receptor Cd36 and the pattern-recognition receptor Tlr4 was markedly upregulated; notably, SBC-115,076 treatment significantly suppressed the expression of both genes (Fig. 5B). These receptors are well established as key mediators of Aβ-driven microglial activation and inflammasome-associated inflammatory signaling26,27.

Microglial polarization was subsequently analyzed by flow cytometry. Aβ stimulation led to a significant increase in the expression of M1-like surface markers CD80 and CD86, indicating a pro-inflammatory phenotype. A significant downregulation of these markers was observed following SBC-115,076 treatment (Fig. 5C, D,F, G). Although Aβ exposure alone did not significantly elevate the M2-associated marker CD206, SBC-115,076 markedly increased CD206 expression (Fig. 5E, H), indicating polarization toward an anti-inflammatory, M2-like state.

In line with the observed phenotypic changes, enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) measurements showed that SBC-115,076 significantly reduced the release of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α into the culture supernatant compared with Aβ stimulation alone (Fig. 5I–K).

Collectively, these findings demonstrate that SBC-115,076 attenuates Aβ-induced microglial activation and promotes M2-like polarization, likely through suppression of CD36/TLR4-mediated inflammatory signaling. This interpretation aligns with reports implicating PCSK9 in CD36- and TLR4-linked inflammatory signaling12,28–30, supporting a PCSK9-centered immunomodulatory effect of SBC-115,076 in microglial cells.

Discussion

This study demonstrates that the small-molecule PCSK9 inhibitor SBC-115,076 alleviates AD-like pathology in an AlCl₃-induced zebrafish larval model. By integrating in vivo behavioral and biochemical analyses with complementary in vitro BBB and microglial systems, we identify two cooperative and mechanistically distinct actions of SBC-115,076: enhancement of endothelial Aβ clearance and suppression of Aβ-driven microglial inflammation. Together, these effects converge to reduce oxidative stress, neuronal apoptosis, and functional deficits.

Defective Aβ clearance is broadly considered a key pathogenic factor in sporadic AD31. Among clearance pathways, LRP1 plays a dominant role in mediating BBB Aβ efflux, and reduced LRP1 availability markedly compromises this process24. PCSK9 promotes lysosomal degradation of LDL receptor family members and negatively regulates LRP1 surface expression9. Although clinical studies report elevated PCSK9 levels in AD-relevant cohorts, results vary across studies8,32. Previous work using PCSK9 monoclonal antibodies or genetic ablation has provided proof-of-concept that PCSK9 repression can attenuate Aβ pathology and neuroinflammation14,33. Our findings extend these observations to a small-molecule PCSK9 inhibitor.

Specifically, SBC-115,076 reversed pathological Pcsk9 upregulation and increased Lrp1 expression in endothelial cells, resulting in enhanced Aβ endocytosis, lysosomal trafficking, and selective brain-to-blood-like (B→A) efflux across an in vitro BBB model. The lack of effect on blood-to-brain-like transport is consistent with the established directionality of LRP1-mediated Aβ clearance24. These findings align with recent evidence that modulation of BBB transport mechanisms can rapidly enhance Aβ removal and improve functional outcomes in AD models25. Collectively, our data support targeting the PCSK9-LRP1 axis as a viable strategy to restore BBB-dependent Aβ clearance14,33.

Neuroinflammation represents another critical driver of AD progression11. Aβ oligomers activate microglial pattern-recognition receptors, including CD36 and TLR4/6, leading to inflammasome activation and amplification of pro-inflammatory cytokine release26,27. In BV2 microglia, Aβ exposure increased Cd36 and Tlr4 expression and induced an M1-like pro-inflammatory phenotype. SBC-115,076 substantially dampened these inflammatory responses, decreasing M1-associated markers while increasing the M2-associated marker CD206, and it also reduced the release of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α.

This immunomodulatory profile is consistent with peripheral studies demonstrating that PCSK9 promotes CD36-dependent lipid uptake and TLR4-associated inflammatory signaling12,28–30. It also aligns with CNS studies reporting reduced inflammatory injury under PCSK9 inhibition in models of ischemia and alcohol-associated neurotoxicity34,35, as well as reports implicating PCSK9 in exacerbating anesthesia-related neuroinflammation36. Our findings add AD-relevant evidence that small-molecule PCSK9 inhibition can disrupt the Aβ-microglia inflammatory amplification loop.

The AlCl₃-induced zebrafish model captures several key AD-like phenotypes, including oxidative stress, cholinergic impairment, neuroinflammation, and amyloid-like deposition21,22,37. SBC-115,076 normalized antioxidant defenses by restoring SOD activity and GSH content while reducing MDA levels, likely reflecting both decreased Aβ burden and attenuation of inflammation-driven reactive oxygen species production23. Correction of AChE hyperactivity is also noteworthy, as AChE contributes to acetylcholine depletion and may facilitate the formation of neurotoxic Aβ-AChE complexes38. These biochemical improvements were accompanied by reduced Thioflavin S-positive signals, diminished neuronal apoptosis, and recovery of locomotor and stimulus-response behaviors, indicating functional relevance of the molecular effects.

The CNS safety of PCSK9 modulation warrants careful consideration. PCSK9 participates in neurodevelopment, synaptic plasticity, and cognitive processes under physiological conditions6,7,39. Early genetic knockdown of pcsk9 in zebrafish leads to severe developmental abnormalities7, and neuronal PCSK9 in mammals influences synaptic receptor localization and memory formation6. However, in Aβ-accumulating environments, PCSK9 may become maladaptive by impairing cholesterol homeostasis and increasing neuronal vulnerability40. Importantly, our pharmacological intervention differed fundamentally from early genetic suppression. SBC-115,076 was administered during 3–6 dpf, a developmental window following major CNS patterning and coinciding with synaptic refinement41. The absence of overt toxicity and the lack of additional benefit at higher doses suggest a therapeutically actionable window, consistent with target-mediated dynamics reported for PCSK9-directed therapies42. Notably, large cardiovascular trials of PCSK9 antibodies such as evolocumab have not demonstrated cognitive harm10,43, although long-term safety in AD-specific contexts remains to be established44.

Small-molecule PCSK9 inhibitors represent a distinct therapeutic strategy compared to anti-Aβ monoclonal antibodies. By enhancing BBB-mediated efflux rather than directly binding parenchymal Aβ, PCSK9 inhibition may theoretically circumvent the risk of ARIA while providing a complementary disease-modifying mechanism14,45. We selected SBC-115,076 as a representative small-molecule PCSK9 inhibitor with reported peripheral bioactivity and anti-inflammatory properties17,46, and demonstrate its capacity to mitigate AD-like phenotypes through coordinated regulation of Aβ trafficking and neuroinflammatory responses. Given the frequent coexistence of vascular risk factors, dyslipidemia, and AD47, such dual-acting compounds may be particularly attractive for integrated disease management.

Current therapeutic strategies for AD are increasingly shifting toward multi-targeted interventions capable of simultaneously addressing multiple pathological features, including Aβ aggregation, tau pathology, and neuroinflammation48. In this context, our findings suggest that PCSK9 inhibition may represent a dual-acting strategy by enhancing Aβ clearance while attenuating neuroinflammatory responses. As illustrated in Fig. 6, SBC-115,076 appears to exert these effects through coordinated regulation of the endothelial PCSK9-LRP1 axis and the microglial CD36/TLR4 pathway, thereby targeting both BBB-mediated Aβ transport and inflammatory activation.

This study has several limitations. We relied on an AlCl₃-induced zebrafish model and immortalized cell lines, which, although suitable for mechanistic screening, do not fully recapitulate the complexity of mammalian AD pathology. Brain pharmacokinetics of SBC-115,076 were not assessed, limiting conclusions regarding CNS exposure. Downstream inflammatory cascades, such as NF-κB or NLRP3 inflammasome activation, were not directly interrogated. In addition, long-term efficacy and safety were not evaluated in mammalian AD models.

Future work should delineate the pharmacokinetic properties and brain exposure of SBC-115,076 and assess its long-term efficacy and safety in transgenic AD mouse models (e.g., APP/PS1 and 5×FAD), with endpoints encompassing synaptic plasticity, neuronal survival, and cognitive performance. Furthermore, integrated transcriptomic and proteomic analyses will be employed to map PCSK9-dependent signaling networks governing microglial activation and phenotype switching. Together, these efforts will help clarify the translational potential of small-molecule PCSK9 inhibition as a disease-modifying strategy for AD.

Methods

Chemicals and reagents

SBC-115,076 (C₃₁H₃₃N₃O₅; MW = 527.61 g/mol; CAS No. 489415-96-5) was purchased from MedChemExpress (MCE, Shanghai, China; Cat. No. HY-12402). Thioflavin S (Cat. No. T1892), aluminum chloride (AlCl₃; Cat. No. 563919), and tricaine methanesulfonate (MS-222) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich (St. Louis, MO, USA). Aβ₁–₄₂ and 5-TAMRA-labeled Aβ₁–₄₂ (5-TAMRA-Aβ₁–₄₂) were purchased from Qiangyao Biotechnology (Shanghai, China).

DMSO, 4% paraformaldehyde (PFA), LysoTracker Green, DAPI solution, proteinase K, acridine orange staining kit, one-step TUNEL apoptosis assay kit (green fluorescence), Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit, a BCA protein assay kit and Cell Counting Kit-8 were purchased from Beyotime Biotechnology (Shanghai, China). 0.5% Triton X-100, 20 × Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) and Bovine serum albumin (BSA) were purchased from Servicebio (Wuhan, China).

Assay kits for acetylcholinesterase (AChE), total superoxide dismutase (T-SOD; WST-1 method), malondialdehyde (MDA; TBA method), and reduced glutathione (GSH; microplate method) were purchased from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute (Nanjing, China). Mouse IL-6, IL-1β, TNF-α, and MCP-1 ELISA kits were obtained from Shanghai Jianglai Industrial Limited By Share Ltd (Shanghai, China). All other chemicals were of analytical grade.

SBC-115,076 used in the cellular experiments was dissolved in DMSO to create stock solutions. For all treatment groups and their corresponding vehicle controls, the final concentration of DMSO in the culture medium was strictly maintained at 0.1% to ensure consistency across different cell-based assays and to minimize potential cytotoxicity.

Zebrafish husbandry and AD-like model establishment

Embryos of wild-type AB zebrafish (Danio rerio) were purchased from Weifang Jinghe Biotechnology Co., Ltd. (Shandong, China). They were maintained in E3 medium at 28.5 ± 0.5 °C with a 14:10 h light–dark cycle, and developmental stages were determined based on standard morphological landmarks49. All zebrafish procedures were reviewed and approved by the Ethics Committee of Xiangya School of Pharmaceutical Sciences, Central South University. All experiments were conducted in accordance with institutional guidelines and relevant national regulations for the care and use of laboratory animals. This study is reported in accordance with the ARRIVE guidelines.

To induce AD-like phenotypes, 3 dpf larvae were exposed to 80 µM AlCl₃ in E3 medium containing 0.1% DMSO for 72 h (until 6 dpf), with minor modifications of published protocols50. Controls were maintained in E3 medium containing 0.1% DMSO only.

For dose screening, SBC-115,076 was co-administered with AlCl₃ at final concentrations of 10, 20, or 40 µM (final DMSO 0.1% in all groups). Based on behavioral outcomes, 20 µM SBC-115,076 was selected for subsequent in vivo studies. The experimental groups were as follows: (1) Control, (2) AlCl₃ model, and (3) AlCl₃ + SBC-115,076 (20 µM). The treatment medium was refreshed daily. At 6 dpf, larvae were collected for behavioral assessment, Aβ deposition analysis, apoptosis assays, oxidative stress and cholinergic biomarker measurements, and qPCR. Following behavioral testing and prior to tissue collection, larvae were euthanized by immersion in an overdose of MS-222 (300 mg/L) until complete cessation of opercular movement, followed by rapid chilling in a 2–4 °C ice-water slurry to ensure death.

For in vitro experiments, a 24-h treatment with 20 µM SBC-115,076 was used as the default condition unless noted otherwise.

Cell culture

bEnd.3 cells and BV2 cells were purchased from Haixing Biotechnology (Beijing, China). Cells were cultured in DMEM (Gibco, Grand Island, NY, USA) supplemented with 10% FBS (Haixing Biotechnology) and 1% penicillin–streptomycin (Gibco) at 37 °C in 5% CO₂.

Preparation of Aβ₁–₄₂ oligomers

To prepare soluble Aβ₁–₄₂ oligomers, 1 mg peptide was first dissolved in HFIP and sonicated, then dried under nitrogen and lyophilized to obtain a peptide film for storage. The film was subsequently reconstituted in DMSO (stock), diluted in complete medium containing 10% FBS, and incubated at 37 °C for 24 h to promote oligomerization. After incubation, samples were clarified by centrifugation (16,000×g, 20 min, 4 °C) to remove large aggregates, and the supernatant fraction was used in downstream assays.

Behavioral assays

All assays were conducted at 6 dpf using the Zebralab tracking system (ViewPoint Life Sciences, Lyon, France) at 28 °C.

Spontaneous locomotion

Each larva (n = 10 per group) was placed into an individual well of a 96-well plate containing 200 µL E3 medium. After 10 min of acclimation under light, spontaneous movement was recorded for 6 min under constant white light at 500 lx. Locomotor performance was quantified as total distance traveled (mm) and mean speed (mm/s), and swimming activities were classified as low (< 1 mm/s), medium (1–10 mm/s), or high (> 10 mm/s) speed.

Light–dark transition test

For assessment of sensorimotor integration, larvae (n = 16 per group) were individually allocated to 48-well plates containing 500 µL E3 medium per well. After 10 min light adaptation, larvae were subjected to three consecutive light–dark cycles (total 60 min), each consisting of 10 min dark and 10 min light (500 lx). Total distance and mean speed were recorded. The dyskinesia recovery rate (DRR) was calculated as follows:1\documentclass[12pt]{minimal} \usepackage{amsmath} \usepackage{wasysym} \usepackage{amsfonts} \usepackage{amssymb} \usepackage{amsbsy} \usepackage{mathrsfs} \usepackage{upgreek} \setlength{\oddsidemargin}{-69pt} \begin{document}$$DRR\%=\frac{AS_{sample}-AS_{model}}{AS_{vehicle}-AS_{model}}\times100\%$$\end{document}

where AS denotes the average speed over the 60-min test.

Biochemical measurements

For each group, three biological replicates were prepared (30 larvae per replicate). Larval tissue was homogenized in ice-cold PBS (1:9, w/v), followed by centrifugation (12,000×g, 10 min, 4 °C). Supernatants were harvested and used for downstream biochemical measurements.

AChE and SOD activities, MDA content, and GSH levels were determined by commercially available assay kits according to the manufacturer’s operating procedures. Total protein was measured by a BCA assay, and all readouts were normalized to protein content.

H₂O₂ assay

H₂O₂ levels in zebrafish larvae were quantified using a commercial Hydrogen Peroxide Assay Kit following the supplier’s protocol. For each replicate, 30 larvae were pooled, homogenized on ice in the kit-provided lysis buffer, and centrifuged (12,000×g, 5 min, 4 °C). The resulting supernatant and detection reagent were incubated for 30 min at room temperature, and the absorbance was read out at 560 nm. H₂O₂ concentrations were calculated by standard curves and normalized to total protein. Data are reported as fold changes relative to the control group.

Immunohistochemistry and quantitative analysis of Aβ

Zebrafish larvae were fixed in 4% PFA, embedded in paraffin, and cut into 4 μm coronal sections. For Aβ immunohistochemical staining, the sections were deparaffinized in xylene and rehydrated through a graded ethanol series. Heat-induced antigen retrieval was performed in Tris-EDTA buffer (pH 8.0) for 30 min, followed by quenching of endogenous peroxidase activity with 3% H2O2 in the dark for 15 min at room temperature. To minimize non-specific binding, sections were blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min and then incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary rabbit anti-Aβ antibody (1:500; GB150027, Servicebio, Wuhan, China). After washing with PBS, sections were incubated with an HRP-conjugated goat anti-rabbit secondary antibody (S-vision) (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 50 min at room temperature. The immune complexes were visualized using a 3,3’-diaminobenzidine substrate kit, yielding a characteristic brown reaction product. Sections were subsequently counterstained with hematoxylin, dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a permanent medium. Finally, the Aβ burden was quantified as the positive area fraction using ImageJ software (National Institutes of Health, USA).

ThioS staining for Aβ-Like deposition

After being fixed with 4% PFA, the larvae were dehydrated and embedded in paraffin. Subsequently, the tissue sections (with a thickness of 4 μm) were dewaxed with xylene and re-hydrated using ethanol of varying concentrations. Sections were incubated with 0.1% ThioS prepared in 50% ethanol for 10 min under dark conditions, then washed three times in 80% ethanol (10 s per wash) to reduce nonspecific fluorescence. Following a rinse in distilled water, slides were mounted in anti-fade medium and visualized using a Zeiss Axio Observer 5 fluorescence microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

Cell viability assay

bEnd.3 and BV2 cells were plated in 96-well plates (1 × 10⁴ cells/well) and treated for 24 h with 20 µM Aβ₁–₄₂, with or without SBC-115,076 (5–20 µM). Cell viability was quantified by CCK-8, with 10 µL reagent added to each well and incubated for 60 min at 37 °C before measuring absorbance at 450 nm.

AO and TUNEL staining

The live AO labeling was performed by incubating the larvae in acridine orange (5 µg/mL, E3) at room temperature and in the dark for 30 min. The larvae were then washed three times with fresh E3, anesthetized with 0.02% MS-222, embedded in 1% low-melting-point agarose, and their brains imaged using a Zeiss LSM 900 confocal microscope (Zeiss, Germany).

For TUNEL detection, paraffin brain sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and treated with proteinase K (20 µg/mL; 37 °C, 30 min) to enable permeabilization. TUNEL reactions were set up following the kit instructions and carried out for 60 min at 37 °C in a humidified, light-protected chamber. Sections were washed with PBS, counterstained with DAPI, mounted, and imaged using a Zeiss Axio Observer 5 fluorescence microscope. TUNEL-positive cells were counted and expressed per unit area (mm²).

qPCR

RNA extraction was performed on zebrafish larvae (three biological replicates per group; 30 larvae per replicate) and on cultured cells using the M5 Universal Plus RNA Mini Kit (Mei5bio, Beijing, China). Reverse transcription was carried out with HiScript II QRT SuperMix for qPCR (Vazyme, Nanjing, China). Amplification was run on a Bio-Rad CFX Connect platform (Hercules, CA, USA) using ChamQ Universal SYBR qPCR Master Mix (Vazyme). Oligonucleotide primers were obtained from Sangon Biotech (Shanghai, China), and primer sequences are listed in Supplementary Table S1. Target transcripts included pcsk9, il-6, il-1β, and nos2a in zebrafish, and Pcsk9, Lrp1, Cd36, and Tlr4 in mouse cells. Gene expression was quantified by the 2^−ΔΔCt approach, normalized to actb1 (zebrafish) or Actb (mouse cells).

Immunofluorescence staining

Following fixation, cells cultured on coverslips were permeabilized with 0.5% Triton X-100 (G1204, Servicebio) for 20 min at room temperature. After three washes with PBS, the coverslips were blocked with 3% BSA for 30 min at room temperature to minimize non-specific binding. Subsequently, the cells were incubated overnight at 4 °C with a primary antibody against PCSK9 (1:200; ab181142, Abcam, Cambridge, UK). On the following day, the cells were washed and incubated with Cy3-conjugated goat anti-rabbit IgG secondary antibody (Servicebio, Wuhan, China) for 50 min at room temperature in the dark. Nuclei were counterstained with DAPI for 10 min. Finally, the coverslips were mounted using an anti-fluorescence quenching mounting medium. Immunofluorescence images were captured and observed using a Zeiss Axio Observer 5 fluorescence microscope.

Aβ uptake

bEnd.3 cells were plated in 6-well plates (5 × 10⁵ cells/well) and pretreated with SBC-115,076 (20 µM) for 24 h. Cells were subsequently exposed to 500 nM 5-TAMRA-Aβ₁–₄₂ for 2, 4, or 8 h. After washing, fluorescence was quantified by flow cytometry (CytoFLEX, Beckman Coulter, Suzhou, China), and Aβ uptake was expressed as MFI.

For qualitative assessment of lysosomal colocalization, cells were incubated with 5-TAMRA-Aβ₁–₄₂ for 4 h and labeled with LysoTracker Green (100 nM) for 60 min after washing. Cells were fixed in 4% PFA and stained with DAPI and imaged with a ZEISS LSM 900 confocal microscope.

Microglial polarization analysis

BV2 cells were plated in 6-well plates (5 × 10⁵ cells/well) and exposed to SBC-115,076 (20 µM) for 24 h. Cells were then collected and stained with PE-conjugated anti-CD80 (BioLegend; Cat# 104707), PE/Cyanine7-conjugated anti-CD86 (BioLegend; Cat# 105014), and PE/Dazzle 594-conjugated anti-CD206 (BioLegend; Cat# 141731). Fluorescence signals were acquired on a CytoFLEX flow cytometer (Beckman Coulter, China). CD80 and CD86 were used as M1-like markers, whereas CD206 served as an M2-like marker, and results were reported as MFI.

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA)

For cytokine measurements, BV2 cells (1 × 10⁵ cells/well) were seeded into 24-well plates and treated for 24 h with 20 µM Aβ₁–₄₂, with or without 20 µM SBC-115,076. Subsequently, the supernatant was collected and centrifuged at a centrifugal rate of 1000×g for 10 min to remove particulate impurities. The concentrations of IL-6, IL-1β, and TNF-α were then detected by ELISA. The optical density value of a sample was determined at a wavelength of 450 nm using an Infinite M200 PRO plate reader (Tecan, Austria).

Statistical analysis

All statistical testing was carried out in GraphPad Prism (v10.5). Results are expressed as mean ± SEM with a minimum of three independent biological replicates per condition (n ≥ 3). Differences between two groups were evaluated by an unpaired two-sided Student’s t-test, whereas comparisons across multiple groups were assessed using one-way ANOVA with Tukey correction for post hoc testing. A threshold of P < 0.05 was used to denote significance.

Conclusions

In summary, this study demonstrates that the small-molecule PCSK9 inhibitor SBC-115,076 effectively mitigates AD-like pathology in an AlCl₃-induced zebrafish larval model and reveals coordinated mechanisms involving endothelial Aβ clearance and microglial immunomodulation. Pharmacological inhibition of PCSK9 restored LRP1 expression in endothelial cells, thereby enhancing Aβ uptake, lysosomal trafficking, and directional efflux across a BBB-like barrier. Concurrently, SBC-115,076 attenuated Aβ-driven microglial activation by suppressing CD36/TLR4 signaling, shifting microglia away from a pro-inflammatory M1-like phenotype toward an M2-like, anti-inflammatory state. These molecular and cellular effects translated in vivo into reduced Aβ deposition, oxidative stress, cholinergic dysfunction, and neuronal apoptosis, ultimately resulting in significant behavioral improvement. By targeting Aβ clearance rather than directly binding parenchymal Aβ, small-molecule PCSK9 inhibition may provide a complementary and potentially safer alternative to antibody-based therapies, with reduced risk of ARIA. Although further studies are required to define brain pharmacokinetics, long-term safety, and efficacy in mammalian AD models, our findings establish proof-of-concept that PCSK9 represents a convergent regulator of vascular and neuroimmune pathways relevant to AD. Collectively, this work supports the development of small-molecule PCSK9 inhibitors as a novel, BBB-oriented, and multi-target therapeutic strategy for AD.
