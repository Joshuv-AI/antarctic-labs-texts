Physical Exercise Intervention Improves Cardiac Function and Reduce Recurrences in Non-permanent Atrial Fibrillation: A Systematic Review and Meta-analysis
Authors: Editya Fukata, Rivaldo Hardani, Nabilah Mukti, Ferrisaga Pranawa, Fahmy Rusnanta, Ardian Rizal
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
ObjectivesEvidences indicate positive effect of physical exercise on exercise capacity and quality of life in patients with AF. However, whether it improves cardiac function and reverse cardiac remodeling in AF patients was unknown. We aim to evaluate the effects of exercise intervention on cardiac function and structure, and the risk of AF recurrence in patients with non-permanent AF.MethodsA systematic review and meta-analysis was conducted following PRISMA guidelines. Searches were performed in PubMed, ProQuest, Google Scholar, ScienceDirect, and Scopus to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing exercise interventions combined with standard AF treatment versus standard treatment alone in patients with non-permanent AF. The outcomes included changes in LVEF, left atrial indexed volume (LAVi), the E/e′ ratio (early mitral inflow velocity/early diastolic mitral annular velocity), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd), and the incidence of AF recurrence.ResultsA total of ten RCTs involving 1013 participants met the inclusion criteria. The intervention group participated in supervised exercise sessions, with or without additional home-based exercise, alongside routine AF treatment. Exercise modalities varied, mostly included continuous or interval aerobic training ± strength training. The analysis revealed a significant increase in LVEF following exercise intervention (MD = 2.16 %, 95 % CI: 0.27 to 4.05, p = 0.03, I2 = 74 %). However, no significant differences were observed in LAVi, LVDd, or the E/e′ ratio. Interestingly, exercise intervention was associated with a reduced risk of AF recurrence compared to the control group (RR = 0.82, 95 % CI: 0.68 to 0.99, p = 0.04, I2 = 33 %). Subgroup analysis revealed that interval training appears to offer greater benefit in reducing AF recurrence, while continous training results in greater improvement of LVEF.ConclusionExercise intervention improves LVEF and reduces AF recurrence, but does not affect cardiac remodeling. This effect is dependent on the type of exercise regimen.

Introduction

A trial fibrillation (AF) is the most common cardiac arrhythmia, affecting at least 59 million people globally in 2019 [1]. Heart failure (HF) is the most frequent long-term complications among individuals with AF, affecting approximately half of AF patients. Individuals with AF exhibit a four-to five-fold higher risk of developing HF compared to the general population, as shown in two meta-analyses [2,3]. Echocardiographic evaluation in AF patients provides valuable information for guiding clinical decisions on anticoagulant, rate-control, and rhythm-control strategies [4,5]. Additionally, it serves as a valuable tool for detecting coexisting or underlying cardiac pathologies and predicting the risk of future HF events [6]. Key echocardiographic parameters to evaluate include the presence of structural heart disease, ventricular dysfunction, and cardiac chamber remodeling [4,7].

Physical exercise, as a part of cardiac rehabilitation, has shown promising results as supporting treatment for various cardiovascular diseases [8]. While current European Society of Cardiology (ESC) and European Heart Rhythm Association (EHRA) guidelines acknowledge the benefits of exercise in non-permanent AF, they do not endorse a specific regimen and recommend individual tailoring to enhance cardiorespiratory fitness and reduce the risk of AF recurrence [4]. Several meta-analyses have summarized the effects of exercise on AF patients, revealing improvements in exercise capacity, cardiopulmonary function (in term of maximum oxygen consumption (VO2Max)), resting heart rate, AF symptom burden, and health-related quality of life (QoL) compared to control [9–11]. A recent Cochrane review including 20 randomized controlled trials (RCTs) on adults with any subtype of AF further reaffirmed these functional benefits [12]. However, to our knowledge, none of them focus on cardiac structural or functional remodeling as primary outcomes.

Despite evidences indicating the benefits of physical exercise on exercise capacity and QoL in AF patients are well studied, limited data exists on whether these benefits are also accompanied by improvement in cardiac function and cardiac remodeling indices. This represents a critical knowledge gap in both clinical practice and guideline development. In addition, many AF patients are hesitant to engage in regular physical activity due to the fear of precipitating AF recurrence and some healthcare providers also face the same apprehension, which may limit the implementation of physical exercise as a treatment modality [13,14].

Therefore, as an attempt to provide additional evidence for clinicians on the benefits and safety of physical exercise for AF patients, this review aims to: (i) explore the impact of physical exercise on the echocardiographic measures of cardiac function and remodeling; (ii) investigate the impact of physical exercise in AF patients on the risk of AF recurrence.

Material and methods

This present meta-analysis was conducted according to the Preferred Reporting Items for Systematic Reviews and Meta-Analyses (PRISMA) 2020 guidelines [15]. The protocol of this study has been registered in PROSPERO (CRD42024602677). The data used in this study are all secondary, thus ethical approval is not required.

2.1. Data sources and search strategy

A systematic literature search was conducted using five electronic databases: PubMed, ProQuest, Scopus, ScienceDirect, and Google Scholar, covering studies published up to 2025. The search aimed to identify randomized controlled trials (RCTs) that examined the effects of physical exercise on echocardiographic parameters of cardiac function and remodeling and AF recurrence in adults with non-permanent atrial fibrillation (AF). Search terms were developed using a combination of Medical Subject Headings (MeSH) and free-text keywords, tailored to each database using Boolean operators. The primary concepts included: atrial fibrillation, physical activity, exercise or cardiac rehabilitation, and echocardiographic or recurrence. For example, the free-text searching strategy used in ProQuest and PubMed included the following terms: (“Atrial Fibrillation”) AND (“Exercise” OR “Cardiac Rehabilitation” OR “Physical Activity”) AND (“Recurrence” OR “Adverse Events” OR “Cardiac function” OR “ejection fraction”). Advanced searching incorporating MeSH and additional synonyms (e.g., “afib”, “training”, “left atrial volume”, “left ventricular function”, “ejection fraction”) was further conducted to improve sensitivity. To minimize the risk of missing relevant trials, articles and reference lists included in relevant systematic reviews were also reviewed (see Supplementary Table 1) (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1459&window=additional_files&co-ntext=jsha).

2.2. Study selection and eligibility assessment

The initial search results were processed for duplicate removal utilizing web-based screening tool Rayyan (https://www.rayyan.ai/). After the removal of duplicates, the titles and abstracts of all obtained studies were screened for eligibility by 4 researchers (EF, RH, NM, FP) according to the inclusion and exclusion criteria described below. Any disagreements were resolved by consensus. Studies were included for further analysis if they met all the inclusion criteria: (i)Population: adults with nonpermanent AF without evident HF; (ii)Intervention: physical exercise intervention in addition to standard medical treatment; (iii)Comparison: standard treatment (medical and/or ablation therapy); (iv) Outcome: Reporting at least one echocardiographic parameter of cardiac function (including LVEF, LA indexed volume, LV end-diastolic diameter, E/e′ ratio) and/or AF recurrences; (v)Study: randomized controlled trial. Studies other than original research articles, animal studies, written in a language other than English, or lacking sufficient data for meta-analysis were excluded. The full-texts of the remaining studies were subsequently reviewed to assess their eligibility for further analysis.

2.3. Outcome measures

The primary outcome of interest was the difference in mean changes of echocardiographic parameters reflecting cardiac structure and function between the treatment and control groups, specifically left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF), left atrial volume index (LAVi), left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd), and E/e′ ratio (early mitral inflow velocity to early diastolic mitral annular velocity). The secondary outcome was the incidence of atrial fibrillation (AF) recurrence, defined as the number of patients who experienced at least one documented episode of AF during the follow-up period, either diagnosed in a clinical setting or detected through ambulatory monitoring such as Holter ECG.

2.5. Assessment of the risk of bias and study quality

The risk of bias for each included study was independently assessed by three investigators (RH, NM, FP) using the Revised Cochrane Risk of Bias Tool for Randomized Trials (RoB 2) Excel Tool [16]. The risk of bias for each study was evaluated based on: bias arising from the randomization process, bias due to deviations from intended interventions, bias resulting from missing outcome data, bias in outcome measurement, and bias in the selection of reported results. Each study was then categorized as having a “low risk,” “high risk,” or “some concerns” of bias. Any disputes in judgments were resolved by consensus among the three reviewers.

2.6. Statistical analyses

Meta-analyses were conducted using the RevMan 5.4.1 software. Standard treatment group was considered as the comparator for this meta-analysis. The risk ratio (RR) is the ratio of the risk of an event in the two groups which was used as the effect size (ES) estimation of AF recurrences in present study. In this study, an event was defined as the number of patients experiencing at least one AF recurrence during study period, either diagnosed in healthcare facilities or recorded during Holter monitoring or loop recorder. On the other hand, the mean difference (MD) was used as the ES estimation for change in echocardiographic parameters from baseline to the end of the study period, . If the sample size, mean change from baseline (CFB) value, and standard deviation of the mean changes from baseline (SDCFB) were available from the study, the analyses were conducted directly. Otherwise, mean CFB values and SDCFB were imputed from the baseline values, final values, and their standard deviations using the following equations in accordance to Cochrane Handbook for Systematic Reviews of Interventions [17]:

SDbaseline represents the SD of the pre-test and SDfinal corresponds to the SD of the post-test. The ″r′ symbolizes the correlation between baseline and final measurements, although this correlation value is typically not reported in studies. In fact, none of the included studies reported the r-value. Consequently, this review used an imputation process to obtain the missing outcome data. When SDbaseline and SDfinal values were available, SDCFB was calculated using the aforementioned formula by assuming an r-value of 0.7, following a conservative approach used in previous systematic reviews [18]. A sensitivity analysis was conducted to assess the robustness of the pooled effect size for different assumed correlation coefficient (Corr) used in the imputation formula from the base value of 0.7 to a more conservative values of 0.5, as recommended in the Cochrane Handbook [17].

Heterogeneity was assessed using the chi-squared (Chi2) test, while the level of heterogeneity was quantified with the I2 statistic. I2 values were interpreted as low (25 %), moderate (50 %), and high (75 %) [19]. If no significant statistical heterogeneity was detected (p > 0.05 in the Chi2 test or >50 % in I2 values), a meta-analysis was conducted using a fixed-effect model with inverse variance for continuous data, along with a 95 % confidence interval (CI). In cases where significant statistical heterogeneity was present, a more conservative random-effects model was applied, also using inverse variance for continuous data and a 95 % CI. Additionally, in order to evaluate the influence of each study on the overall effect size and to identify the potential source of bias, a sensitivity analysis was performed using the leave-one-out method by removing one study at a time and repeating the analysis [20].

The overall effects were then presented using forest plots, and potential publication bias was assessed visually through a funnel plot. An asymmetric funnel plot indicates the likelihood of publication bias. A p-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant. Subsequently, the Grading of Recommendation Assessments, Development, and Evaluation (GRADE) framework was used to determine the quality of pooled evidence about each outcome [21].

Results

3.1. Search results

An initial search strategy identified 1615 potential articles. After removing duplicates, 1489 studies remained and were screened based on the titles and abstracts. This process resulted in the exclusion of 1467 articles. Due to inaccessible full-text, 3 studies were excluded. Subsequently, the authors assessed the full-text of 19 articles for eligibility. Of these, 8 articles were excluded for the following reasons: 2 study was written in non-English literature [22,23], 5 studies included patients with permanent atrial fibrillation [24–28], 1 study was a non-randomized controlled trial [29], and 1 study did not evaluate the outcome of interest [30]. Ultimately, 10 studies met the inclusion criteria and were included in the systematic review and meta-analysis [31–40]. The study selection process is outlined in Fig. 1.

3.2. Risk of bias assessments

The risk of bias assessment is presented in Fig. 2. Five studies were considered to have low overall risk of bias [31,32,36,40], while four studies appeared to have some concerns regarding the overall risk of bias [34,35,38,39]. Two remaining studies were considered to have high overall risk of bias [37].

3.3. Characteristics of the included studies

Table 1 presents a summary of 10 studies examining the impact of physical exercise interventions on patients with AF. The studies’ populations include those with non-permanent AF and persistent AF post-ablation and were conducted across multiple countries, although mainly conducted in European countries such as Norway, Italy, Denmark, the Netherlands, and UK. Other studies were conducted in Canada, Australia, Japan, and South Korea. The sample sizes varied, ranging from 33 to 245 individuals, and included mostly male patients (69.5 ± 10.3 %), with ages typically around 60–70 years. The control group primarily involved guideline-based AF care, with a median follow-up duration was 12 months, ranging from 4 to 24 months. Key cardiac parameters assessed included LAVi, LVEF, left ventricular mass, and various other echocardiographic measures.

Table 2 provides a comprehensive summary of the exercise interventions employed across the included studies, detailing the frequency, intensity, duration, type, volume, and progression of exercise. Intervention durations ranged from 8 weeks to 12 months, with a median duration of 20 weeks and frequencies typically ranging from 1 to 3 sessions per week, and targeted volume of exercise ranged from 60 to 210 min/week. Most studies utilized moderate to high-intensity training (50–95 % of peakHR), while only one study incorporated low-intensity training [37].

In terms of exercise type, aerobic training was the most commonly employed intervention, either as a standalone approach or in combination with strength/resistance training. Most programs were facility-based, providing supervised training sessions and guidance for home exercises. Aerobic interval training (AIT), characterized by high-intensity intervals interspersed with recovery periods, was utilized in five of the ten studies. This approach often involved the use of equipment such as treadmills, ergocycles, or rowing machines. Continuous aerobic training, typically performed at moderate intensity, was implemented in four studies [33,34,38,39]. Strength training, often included alongside aerobic exercise, involved resistance exercises performed at varying intensities, commonly quantified as percentages of one repetition maximum (1RM). Four of ten studies detailed the progression of exercise interventions, which varied but generally involved a gradual increase in either exercise intensity, duration, or both. For instance, some studies progressively extended session durations, such as adding 5 min per week [31], or lengthening interval durations while shortening recovery periods [40]. Additionally, progression in exercise volume was performed in one study by increasing the overall physical activity time by approximately 20 % per week until the target volume was achieved [32]. Other interventions focused on incremental increases in intensity, with target heart rates adjusted according to individual improvements in peak heart rate over time [35].

3.4. Meta-analysis

3.4.1. Physical exercise effect on echocardiographic measures of cardiac function

Seven studies [31,32,34–38] evaluated the effect of exercise on the LVEF among AF patients. Due to considerable heterogeneity between these studies (I2 = 74 %, P = 0.0003), the meta-analysis was performed using the random-effects model. There were significant positive effects of exercise intervention on LVEF changes compared to the control group (MD = 2.16 %, 95 % CI: 0.27 to 4.05, p = 0.03) (Fig. 3). The slightly asymmetric funnel plots indicate the presence of publication bias for LVEF changes.

To test the robustness of the findings, two sensitivity analyses were conducted. First, we performed leave-one-out method to identify the study which is causing high heterogeneity. The sensitivity analysis showed that the study by Bittman et al. (2022) [31] was the cause of the heterogeneity of the results (I2 = 0 % upon removal), but the analysis results did not change (MD, Random, 2.94 [CI 95 % 1.97, 3.91]), indicating that the results were stable, as shown in Supplementary Table 3 (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1459&window=additional_files&co-ntext=jsha). Additionally, we also excluded Pippa et al. (2007) [37], due to high overall risk of bias, and Bittman et al. (2022) [31], due to the potential source of high heterogeneity and publication bias. Upon removal of those studies, the heterogeneity substantially decreased (I2 = 3 %), and the effect remained statistically significant with narrower confidence interval (MD = 2.91 %, 95 % CI: 1.93 to 3.90, p < 0.00001; see Supplementary Figure 1) (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1459&window=additional_files&co-ntext=jsha), supporting the consistency and precision of the pooled estimates.

Secondly, we used a more conservative correlation coefficient (Corr = 0.5) for imputed standard deviations in change-from-baseline data, which also yielded consistent pooled effects (MD = 2.08 %, 95 % CI: 1.18 to 2.99, p < 0.0001; see Supplementary Figure 2A) (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1459&window=additional_files&co-ntext=jsha), confirming the robustness of the imputation method.

Additionally, subgroup analysis was conducted based on the type of exercise intervention (see Supplementary Figure 3A) (https://www.j-saudiheart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1459&window=additional_files&co-ntext=jsha). Subgroup analysis comparing the effects of continuous training versus interval training on LVEF showed differing results between the two exercise types. Continuous training (3 studies) demonstrated a statistically significant improvement in LVEF compared to control (MD = 3.42 %, 95 % CI: 2.08 to 4.75, p < 0.00001; I2 = 0 %), while interval training (4 studies) showed a smaller and statistically non-significant improvement (MD = 0.97 %, 95 % CI: –0.26 to 2.19, p = 0.12) and substantial heterogeneity (I2 = 79 %). The test for subgroup differences was significant (Chi2 = 6.99, p = 0.008; I2 = 85.7 %), indicating that the type of exercise might be an effect modifier, with continuous training yielding more consistent improvements in LVEF.

Meanwhile, three studies examined the effect of exercise intervention on LAVi and LVDd changes, and four studies on E/e′ ratio [31,32,34,35]. All those studies showed a low level of heterogeneity, thus a fixed-effect model was used. The meta-analysis revealed no significant between-group differences in other echocardiographic findings, including LAVi (Fig. 4) (MD = 1.49 mL/m2; 95 % CI: −1.50 to 4.47 mL/m2, p = 0.33, I2 = 25 %); LVDd (Fig. 5) (MD = 0.02 cm; 95 % CI: −1.17 to 1.21 cm, p = 0.97, I2 = 0 %), and E/e′ ratio changes (Fig. 6) (MD = −0.14; 95 % CI: −0.81 to 0.54, p = 0.69, I2 = 0 %), indicating that exercise intervention did not result in cardiac structure improvement. The absence of publication bias was observed in the LAVi, LVDd, and E/e′ ratio changes based on symmetric funnel plot. Moreover, the quality of evidence for each cardiac function outcome according to the GRADE framework was rated as low to moderate (Supplementary Table 2) (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1459&window=additional_files&co-ntext=jsha).

3.4.2. Physical exercise effect on the risk of AF recurrences

Seven studies [31–34,36,39,40] encompassing 634 participants met the inclusion criteria for evaluating AF recurrences. The pooled analysis showed that participants in the exercise group had a significantly lower risk of AF recurrence compared to the control group (RR 0.84, 95 % CI: 0.73 to 0.97, p = 0.02), with moderate heterogeneity observed (I2 = 33 %) (Fig. 7). This suggests a 16 % relative risk reduction (RRR) associated with exercise interventions.

To better understand the practical implications, we calculated the absolute measures. The control event rate (CER) was 174 out of 316 (55 %) and the experimental event rate (EER) was 150 out of 318 (47 %), yielding an absolute risk reduction (ARR) of 8 %. This translates to a number needed to treat (NNT) of 13, meaning that for every 13 individuals who undergo a structured exercise intervention, one case of AF recurrence would be prevented.

While the relative effect size (RR = 0.84) indicates a modest reduction, the absolute benefit is noteworthy considering the chronic and recurrent nature of AF and its impact on quality of life and healthcare utilization. However, the asymmetry of the funnel plot suggests potential publication bias. Furthermore, based on the GRADE framework, the overall certainty of evidence was rated as moderate (see Supplementary Table 2) (https://www.j-saudiheart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1459&window=additional_files&co-ntext=jsha).

To further explore whether the type of exercise influenced the treatment effect, we performed a subgroup analysis comparing continuous training versus interval training (see supplementary figure 3B) (https://www.j-saudi-heart.com/cgi/editor.cgi?article=1459&window=additional_files&co-ntext=jsha). This analysis revealed that in the continuous training subgroup (4 studies), the reduction in AF recurrence was not statistically significant (RR 0.96, 95 % CI: 0.79 to 1.16; p = 0.68), with no observed heterogeneity (I2 = 0 %). In contrast, in the interval training subgroup (3 studies), exercise led to a significant reduction in AF recurrence (RR 0.68, 95 % CI: 0.55 to 0.85; p = 0.0005, I2 = 0 %). This indicates that interval training was associated with a 32 % reduction in the relative risk of AF recurrence compared to control. Importantly, the test for subgroup differences was statistically significant (p = 0.02, I2 = 81.5 %), suggesting that the effect of physical exercise on AF recurrence may differ depending on the type of training performed. Specifically, interval training appears to offer greater benefit in reducing AF recurrence than continuous training.

Discussion

While the benefits of exercise in improving exercise capacity and QoL in AF patients are well-documented, its association with the changes in echocardiographic parameters of cardiac function remains underexplored. Additionally, concerns about the potential for exercise to trigger AF recurrence have contributed to the under-prescription of physical activity on AF patients. The findings of this study provide additional evidence on the effects of physical exercise in non-permanent AF patients, particularly regarding changes in cardiac function and structure, and the risk of AF recurrence. The present study demonstrated that compared with the control group, the addition of planned exercise intervention significantly improved LVEF and reduced AF recurrence compared to standard treatment alone, but did not affect LAVi, LVDd, or E/e′. The rationale lies in the ability of exercise intervention to enhance vascular endothelial function, exhibit anti-inflammatory properties, mitigate myocardial remodeling, improve myocardial ischemia, and exert antithrombotic effects [41]. These combined benefits effectively reduce the risk factors associated with AF recurrence and complication.

Heart failure (HF) is strongly associated with AF and is the leading cause of mortality in AF patients. [7]. Additionally, AF patients with lower LVEF (40–49 %) are independently associated with a higher incidence of HF and HF-related hospitalizations compared to those with normal LVEF [42]. On the other hand, patients with both left atrial enlargement (LAE) and reduced EF exhibited fivefold higher rates of AF and almost two-fold higher rates of cardioembolic stroke, highlighting LA dilation combined with reduced EF as a reliable predictor of AF and cardioembolic stroke [43]. These findings suggest that assessing left ventricular function can provide additional value for risk stratification in AF patients beyond conventional risk factors. Furthermore, improving LVEF could logically reduce the risk of incident HF in AF patients. The present study demonstrated that physical exercise interventions significantly improved LVEF in AF patients, particularly continuous aerobic training. Supporting these findings, previous meta-analysis reported slight improvements in LVEF (MD = 0.09 %) among AF patients undergoing cardiac rehabilitation after radiofrequency catheter ablation (RFCA) therapy [20]. In contrast, other meta-analysis reported no significant improvement in LVEF following cardiac rehabilitation in AF patients undergoing RFCA [44]. Nevertheless, the reliability of both meta-analysis is low due to the limited number of included studies (4 and 3 studies respectively) and high heterogeneity between studies. Moreover, our subgroup analysis based on exercise type revealed that continuous training significantly improved LVEF, whereas interval training showed a non-significant effect. These results indicate the importance of exercise type in cardiac rehabilitation and suggest that continuous training may be more effective in improving LVEF in AF patients. One possible explanation is that continous aerobic training provides more sustained haemodynamic stimulus which promotes steady-state improvements in myocardial oxygen balance, endothelial function, and cardiac contractility, possibly by reducing afterload and increasing contractility [45,46]. Due to lower heart rate during continuous training, it may also prolong diastolic filling time and myocardial perfusion, which potentially improves left ventricular contraction [47]. Moreover, the lower variability and reduced autonomic stress associated with continuous training may be appropriate for patients with AF, who often have impaired rate control and arrhythmia-related cardiac strain. Interestingly, previous RCT reported that aerobic interval training for 12 weeks increases LVEF by 10 % (in absolute term) in HFrEF patients, while no significant changes in LVEF observed in continuous aerobic training [48]. In contrast, previous meta-analysis in HFrEF patients showed that interval training was not superior to continuous training in improving LVEF (WMD = 3.17 %; 95 % CI −0.87 to 7.22 %) [49].

Left atrial enlargement (LAE) is frequently observed in AF patients and is hypothesized to contribute to cardioembolic stroke by promoting blood stasis, endothelial injury, and thrombus formation [43]. Among the various methods for quantifying left atrial size, the European Society of Cardiology recommends using LAVi due to its accuracy and reliability in predicting all-cause mortality and ischemic stroke [50]. Echocardiographic assessment of the left atrium using LAVi has demonstrated superior predictive ability for AF recurrence compared to other measurement techniques [51]. Although some studies have reported that exercise training can reverse LA dilation in patients with AF [32], our findings showed no significant differences in LAVi between exercise and control group. Therefore, the exercise intervention in this study might not sufficient to reduce LA size.

The evaluation of left ventricular end-diastolic diameter (LVDd) during exercise is critical for assessing cardiac adaptations in patients with AF. An increase in LVDd is associated with worse clinical outcomes. Notably, patients with LVDd >60 mm exhibit a significantly higher incidence of major adverse cardiovascular events (MACE) compared to those with LVDd <60 mm (P < 0.001) [52]. Additionally, a prospective study assessing LVDd in AF patients reported a significant association between larger LVDd and the presence of left atrial appendage thrombus in AF patients [53]. Although several studies have suggested that exercise may reverse LVDd through mechanisms such as reduced cardiac remodeling, increased stroke volume, and improved diastolic function [34,54], our findings showed no significant differences in LVDd changes between exercise and control group.

The early transmitral flow velocity (E) to mitral annular diastolic velocity (e′) ratio offers semi-quantitative information about left ventricular filling pressure (LVFP) and its relationship to systolic function in AF [55]. Elevated LVFP has been independently associated with reduced exercise capacity in AF patients [56]. Furthermore, increased E/e′ ratio has been associated with increased risk of LAA thrombi in AF patients [55]. However, in this study, no significant changes in the E/e′ ratio were observed after exercise intervention. Collectively, our results showed that exercise intervention in included studies failed to cause significant changes in cardiac structure remodeling. However, this may be attributed to the low number of studies (n = 4) reporting LAVi, LVDd, and E/e′ value. Moreover, the intervention duration might be insufficient to cause cardiac reverse remodeling which often take years to attain [57,58].

AF recurrence is a clinically important outcome, as many AF patients and some clinicians are still apprehensive about participating (or recommending) regular physical exercise, due to fears of inciting AF episodes. However, present meta-analysis demonstrates that incorporating exercise intervention in the treatment of non-permanent AF is not only safe, but also effective in reducing the risk of AF recurrence. Specifically, our findings demonstrated that interval training significantly lowered the risk of AF recurrence compared to usual care, while continuous training did not result in a statistically significant reduction, indicating a potential difference in efficacy between exercise modalities. The beneficial effects may be attributed to physiological mechanisms involving the modulation of autonomic balance by reducing sympathetic tone and increasing vagal activity, which is particularly relevant in AF pathophysiology where autonomic dysregulation is common [59]. This finding aligns with prior meta-analysis [60], which reported a reduction in AF recurrence; although, only three studies were included in the meta-analysis that examined AF recurrence in comparison to seven studies in our meta-analysis.

Our study has a few limitations. First, heterogeneity in patients characteristics across included studies were observed. This might be attributed to variability in demographic characteristics (i.e. sex, age) and the presence of comorbidities, including ischaemic heart disease, diabetes mellitus, hypertension, and metabolic syndrome, which could have influenced both baseline risk and responsiveness to exercise interventions. Moreover, the training regimens were heterogenous in terms of intervention duration, exercise modes, and overall volume. To address the observed heterogeneity, we attempted to resolve it by excluding two studies with high risk of bias and outlier characteristics. Additionally, we conducted a subgroup analysis based on exercise type (continuous vs. interval) to minimize overall heterogeneity. Second, our comparison relied on the latest available data on AF recurrence at the end of follow-up periods, which ranged from 4 to 24 months. This variability in the duration of exercise intervention and follow-up duration likely introduced additional heterogeneity. Furthermore, six of the ten included studies demonstrated a mismatch between the duration of the exercise intervention and the follow-up period, potentially leading to underdetection or overdetection of AF recurrence rates. Importantly, most studies did not evaluate the long-term effects of interventions beyond one year, limiting the ability to detect late-occurring events. Third, while echocardiographic measurements of cardiac function were standardized across studies, methodologies for assessing AF recurrence varied. Detection methods ranged from reported emergency room visits for irregular rhythms to 24-hour Holter monitoring and implantable loop recorders. Furthermore, some studies lacked detailed descriptions of their recurrence detection methods, potentially reducing the detection power for AF events.

Conclusions

In conclusion, our meta-analysis suggests that exercise interventions in patients with non-permanent atrial fibrillation may lead to modest improvements in left ventricular ejection fraction (LVEF) and a reduction in AF recurrence, but not in LAVi, LVDd, and E/e′ ratio. Interval training appears to offer greater benefit in reducing AF recurrence, while continous training results in greater improvement of LVEF. However, due to the limited number of studies, high heterogeneity, and low certainty of evidence, further high-quality randomized trials with larger sample sizes are needed to confirm these effects and clarify optimal exercise regiments.
