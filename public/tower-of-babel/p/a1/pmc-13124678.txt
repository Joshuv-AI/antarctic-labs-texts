How to … Coach Problem Representation to Strengthen Diagnostic Reasoning in Trainees
Authors: Cody Clary, Adam Cohen, Satid Thammasitboon
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTProblem representation (PR) is a concise synthesis of clinical information that captures the core diagnostic problem in a single statement, often referred to as a ‘one‐liner’. Although central to diagnostic reasoning, this skill is frequently underdeveloped in medical training. Many trainees struggle to transform clinical data into meaningful representations, which can hinder communication, diagnostic reasoning and patient safety. The Assessment of Reasoning Tool–Reconstructed (ART‐R) provides a structured framework that educators can adapt as a coaching rubric to strengthen PR during clinical teaching. Using the ART‐R, faculty can guide trainees to refine their PR by focusing on three elements: (1) clarity of the synthesized clinical problem; (2) emphasis on diagnostically relevant positive and negative findings; (3) use of precise medical terminology. This micro‐coaching approach can be naturally embedded into clinical rounds through brief prompts that encourage trainees to refine their one‐liner summaries. Through repeated practice, trainees develop increasingly abstracted representations that activate illness scripts and support diagnostic hypothesis generation. Coaching PR in this way provides a practical, low‐burden strategy for strengthening diagnostic reasoning during routine clinical teaching. Integrating this approach into patient presentations can help educators cultivate stronger diagnostic reasoning.

Problem Representation (PR): A Critical Skill for Diagnostic Expertise

Picture this: You are leading rounds for a new admission—a 6‐month‐old infant with respiratory distress. The trainee concludes the presentation with the following assessment:

This is a 6‐month‐old male born at 39 weeks via uncomplicated vaginal delivery who was in his usual state of health until Friday when he started coughing and breathing faster. His parents noticed he wasn't feeding as well and brought him to the emergency department where he was noted to have oxygen saturations in the low 90s.

While detailed, the summary leaves the team uncertain about the core clinical problem. Is this bronchiolitis, pneumonia or viral wheezing? The summary includes multiple details but lacks abstractions, prioritization and diagnostic direction. As a result, the team struggles to determine which diagnoses should be considered first.

Does This Scenario Sound Familiar?

At the heart of this common clinical teaching challenge is a fundamental diagnostic reasoning skill: PR. A PR is a concise ‘one‐liner’ statement, typically located in the assessment portion of a clinical presentation, that synthesizes key patient information into a diagnostically meaningful statement by distilling complex clinical data into an abstracted summary. In practice, PR functions as the bridge between data gathering and diagnostic hypothesis generation [1, 2, 3].

Classic formulation of PR includes three core elements: patient context (e.g., demographics and risk factors), time course of illness and clinical syndrome. When combined with key discriminating features and abstracted clinical language (i.e., semantic qualifiers: paired descriptors that abstract clinical findings, such as acute vs. chronic, focal vs. diffuse and constant vs intermittent), these elements help clinicians generate diagnostic hypotheses and activate relevant illness scripts [3].

Research comparing novice and expert clinicians shows clear differences in how PRs are constructed. Experts tend to produce shorter, more abstracted summaries that emphasize discriminating clinical features, whereas novices often produce longer summaries that rely heavily on raw clinical details. In a recent study, McQuade and colleagues found that expert PRs were more concise and more likely to include abstracted terminology and diagnostically meaningful features than those generated by novice clinicians [3].

Despite its importance, PR remains underemphasized in medical training as reflected by limited literature examining trainee competency on this skill. Competency frameworks worldwide emphasize diagnostic reasoning as a core clinical skill. In the United States, the Accreditation Council for Graduate Medical Education highlights clinical reasoning and diagnostic synthesis within the Patient Care competencies. Similar expectations appear in international frameworks, including those from the Royal College of Physicians and Surgeons of Canada and the United Kingdom's General Medical Council [4, 5, 6]. Importantly, the ability to synthesize patient information to support diagnostic reasoning is also emphasized in the training standards across health professions, including nurse practitioner and physician assistant education [7, 8]. The importance of this shared objective is underscored by the National Academies of Sciences, Engineering and Medicine, which identified improved diagnostic reasoning as a key strategy for enhancing patient safety [6, 9]. Our recent study of 36 pediatric interns found that many struggled to translate clinical data into meaningful PRs [10]. This gap carries potential risks. Misrepresenting the clinical problem can lead to misdiagnosis and patient harm [9]. As educators, we must recognize the critical nature of this skill and work to bridge this gap in our training programs.

Coaching Trainees Toward Stronger PRs

Several frameworks have been proposed to teach and evaluate diagnostic reasoning, including PR [2, 11]. Among available tools, we use the Assessment of Reasoning Tool–Reconstructed (ART‐R) to guide coaching on PR [12, 13]. The ART‐R is a theory‐informed framework designed to assess five domains of diagnostic reasoning, one of which is PR. Though originally developed as an assessment instrument, we adapt the PR domain of the ART‐R as a coaching rubric that faculty can use during real‐time clinical teaching (Table 1).

We use these elements to structure real‐time coaching during rounds with a focus on helping trainees build the ‘mental scaffolding’ that experts use to retrieve knowledge and generate diagnostic hypotheses. This approach differs from traditional feedback such as ‘read more’ or ‘slow down your thinking’, which has limited empirical support for improving diagnostic reasoning [14]. Through this coaching process, the trainee might revise the PR to:

Importantly, the trainee arrives at this PR through active reasoning and reflection rather than passive correction and is now ready to generate diagnostic hypotheses. Repetition of this coaching across clinical encounters fosters diagnostic habits that support both communication and diagnostic reasoning.

Why This Works: Strengthening Knowledge Access Through PRs

This teaching approach is grounded in evidence from cognitive psychology and clinical expertise literature. Norman and colleagues suggest that the diagnostic advantage of experts lies not in superior reasoning but in the way their clinical knowledge is organized and retrieved. Expert clinicians have robust, efficiently accessed illness scripts, which are structured knowledge networks that allow for rapid pattern recognition and hypothesis testing [15].

When deliberately taught, PR becomes more than a summary or communication tool; it provides a visible representation of internal knowledge structures. It cues memory, reveals diagnostic priorities and fosters the integration of new information into more coherent illness scripts. In this way, PR functions as both a diagnostic mirror and a learning lever. By coaching trainees to refine their one‐liners using the ART‐R framework, we are not just improving how they present cases. We are guiding them to reorganize how they understand those cases. This practice helps strengthen the very knowledge networks that underlie diagnostic accuracy.

Rather than relying solely on cognitive bias training or abstract reasoning strategies, we focus on what trainees can do in the moment: reframe the clinical problem with clarity, precision and diagnostic intent. This makes PR a powerful, practical tool to scaffold expertise at the bedside.

Impact and Lessons Learned

Since integrating PR coaching into our clinical teaching, we have observed notable shifts in how trainees engage with clinical cases. Case summaries are clearer. Differentials are more focused and prioritized. Case discussions become collaborative reasoning exercises rather than passive report‐outs. Trainees who once listed findings without synthesis now offer representations that anchor diagnostic thinking and spark productive dialogue.

Importantly, this approach requires no curricular overhaul, and it integrates naturally into daily rounds. Faculty can simply ask:

Can you refine your one‐liner using clarity, key findings, and medical precision?

Offer feedback aligned with these elements and revisit the skill at each encounter. These brief, repeated micro‐coaching moments promote progressive diagnostic sophistication without disrupting clinical workflow.

Because PR is fundamental to how clinicians synthesize and communicate clinical information, this coaching strategy can be applied across health professions education programmes where learners engage in diagnostic reasoning.

Call to Action

PR is an essential step in diagnostic reasoning and a skill that can be deliberately strengthened through coaching. When educators prompt learners to refine their PR statements, we are not just polishing presentations; we are shaping how knowledge is organized and applied.

We urge clinical educators to embrace PR coaching as a high‐yield, low‐burden strategy to accelerate diagnostic expertise. With the ART‐R framework as a guide, each patient encounter becomes an opportunity to build stronger diagnostic minds.
