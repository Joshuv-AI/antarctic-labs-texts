Trends in Cardiac Rehabilitation Participation Among Veterans and Medicare Beneficiaries With Stable Heart Failure, 2012 to 2022
Authors: Saron Hunegnaw, Alexis Beatty, Linda G. Park, Ning Zhang, Thomas J. Hoffmann, Mary A. Whooley
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundHeart failure (HF) affects 6.7 million American adults and is associated with significant morbidity and mortality. Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) is a behavior modification program recommended to improve health outcomes in patients with HF. In 2014, Medicare began covering facility‐based CR for patients with stable HF, and some Veterans Affairs (VA) facilities began offering home‐based CR.MethodsWe conducted an observational study to assess CR participation among patients with stable HF and patient characteristics associated with participation. National electronic health record data from the VA and claims data from a 5% Medicare sample were used to identify patients using International Classification of Diseases, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) or Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes. CR participation was determined using Current Procedural Terminology codes and VA stop codes during the 12 months following HF diagnosis. A multivariable logistic regression was constructed to identify patient characteristics associated with participation.ResultsFrom 2012 to 2022, VA participation increased from 7.4% to 10.4% and Medicare decreased from 7.4% to 5.1%. From 2016 to 2022, 13.2% of veterans and 6.7% of Medicare beneficiaries with stable HF participated in CR. Older age, female sex, and non‐White race were associated with lower participation, and comorbid ischemic heart disease was associated with greater participation (odds ratio [OR], 1.48 [95% CI, 1.45–1.52] in VA; OR, 2.69 [95% CI, (2.62–2.78] in Medicare).ConclusionsFollowing the VA's implementation of home‐based CR and Medicare's facility‐based CR coverage for stable HF, participation increased among veterans but not among Medicare beneficiaries. Lower participation was observed among women and non‐White patients. These findings suggest that covering home‐based CR could improve participation among Medicare beneficiaries.

Heart failure (HF) remains a significant public health concern, affecting millions of American adults, and its prevalence is expected to increase in the coming decades. 1 The onset of HF occurs when the heart is unable to pump blood efficiently due to weakening or stiffening of the heart muscle, resulting in reduced blood flow being sent from the heart to the body. 2 , 3 Younger patients are developing HF at accelerating rates, and it is estimated that 1 in 4 adults will develop HF in their lifetime. 1 , 4 Veterans, in particular, may have a higher predisposition to developing HF compared with the general population due to their unique experiences and their increased risk for posttraumatic stress disorder. 5 In recent years, there have been improvements in cardiovascular disease treatments and HF management, but heart disease continues to be a significant contributor to mortality and morbidity, especially among older US adults. 4 , 6

Cardiac rehabilitation (CR) has emerged as a viable behavior‐based intervention for patients with HF that improves outcomes through exercise‐based rehabilitation, counseling, education on the importance of medication adherence, and dietary modifications, etc. 7 , 8 , 9 Despite the clear advantages of CR in HF management, participation rates remain consistently low. 7 , 10 , 11 The Veterans Health Administration (VA) implemented home‐based cardiac rehabilitation (HBCR) to improve CR participation rates, but the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services (Medicare) has not yet widely implemented alternative options to facility‐based cardiac rehabilitation (FBCR) such as HBCR due to lack of reimbursement options. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15 An analysis done using data from Medicare and the VA found CR participation rates of only 2.6% and 2.3% from 2007 to 2011, respectively. 10 In February 2014, Medicare expanded coverage of CR to include patients with chronic systolic HF with an ejection fraction of ≤35%. 10 , 16 However, trends in CR participation after Medicare expanded coverage remain unknown. This analysis sought to (1) examine trends in CR participation among patients with stable HF from 2012 to 2022 and (2) assess if CR participation differs by patient demographics.

Methods

The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board at the University of California, San Francisco, and by the Research & Development Committee at the San Francisco VA (IRB approval number=12‐08381) with a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act waiver for informed consent. The authors do not have ownership of the data or the authority to execute a data use agreement, but readers may contact the corresponding author for guidance on how to request a VA data use agreement. Information about access to VA data can be found at https://www.data.va.gov/ and https://www.virec.research.va.gov/.

A retrospective cohort study was carried out using national data from 2 distinct sources: the VA health care system's electronic health records and Medicare claims data. Patients with stable HF who had International Classification of Diseases (ICD) codes for HF associated with ≥2 outpatient visits were identified from January 1, 2012, to December 31, 2022, from a 5% sample of Medicare beneficiaries and all veterans enrolled in VA health care.

Cohort Construction

Primary ICD, Ninth Revision (ICD‐9) and Tenth Revision (ICD‐10) codes were used to identify patients with stable HF (ICD‐9: 402.11, 402.91, 404.11, 404.13, 404.91, 404.93, 428.0, 428.1, 428.20, 428.22, 428.30, 428.32, 428.40, 428.42, 428.9 and ICD‐10: I50.1, I50.20, I50.22, I50.30, I50.32, I50.40, I50.42, I50.82, I50.9, I11.0, I13.0, I13.2). If patients participated in CR, the last ICD diagnosis date before starting CR was selected as the index date to make sure that patients were credited for having participated in CR and to avoid underestimating participation rates. For patients who did not participate in CR, we randomly selected 1 of their ICD diagnosis dates within the study time frame. We excluded patients who died within 30 days following hospitalization. Patients with any skilled nursing facility claim or cardiovascular disease‐related inpatient hospitalization within 6 weeks (42 days) before outpatient visits were excluded, defined by a primary or secondary discharge diagnosis code of ICD‐9 390* to 445* or ICD‐10 I00* to I77*. We also excluded Medicare patients <68 years of age to provide a lookback period of at least 3 years for CR and comorbid conditions.

Outcome Definition

CR participation was defined as attending ≥1 CR sessions (VA, non‐VA, Medicare Part B, or Medicare Part C) during the 3 years before or within a year following HF diagnosis. The total number of CR participants was gathered from VA outpatient files, non‐VA care claims, and Medicare claims using VA stop code 231, CR Current Procedure Terminology codes: 93797, 93798, or Healthcare Common Procedure Coding System codes S9472, S9473, G0422, G0423, and G0424. We included pulmonary rehabilitation codes S9473 and G0424 because they are sometimes entered inadvertently instead of CR codes S9472 and G0423. Given the low overall participation rates and the overlap between HF and pulmonary symptoms, we wanted to ensure we captured all possible CR. Comorbid conditions were defined based on the presence of 1 inpatient or 2 outpatient ICD codes in the 3 years before HF diagnosis (see Table S1).

Statistical Analysis

Patient demographic characteristics and comorbidities (see Table S1) for CR participants and nonparticipants were compared using t tests for continuous variables and chi‐square for categorical variables. We constructed a multivariable logistic regression model to identify characteristics independently associated with CR participation between 2016 and 2022 (after ICD‐10 codes were introduced). Statistical significance was defined a priori as P<0.05. SAS Enterprise Guide 8 were used to conduct all analyses.

RESULTS

Among veterans with stable HF, participation in CR increased from 7.4% in 2012 to 10.4% in 2022, peaking at 16.2% participation in 2018. Among Medicare beneficiaries with stable HF, participation in CR dropped from 7.4% in 2012 to 5.1% in 2022, peaking at 8.7% participation in 2018 (Figure 1). The number of veterans who received CR provided by VA or purchased by VA increased substantially over time, whereas there was little change in the number of veterans who received CR covered by Medicare (Figure 2).

From 2016 to 2022, a total of 346 800 veteran outpatients were seen at the VA with stable HF. From this cohort, 45 848 participated in ≥1 CR sessions, resulting in 13.2% participation. Among Medicare beneficiaries, there were a total of 323 813 outpatients seen for stable HF from 2016 to 2022. Of these, 21 589 (6.7%) participated in ≥1 CR sessions. Among veterans, the average age of CR participants was 72.2±9.3 compared with 74.7±11.1 (P<0.0001) among nonparticipants (Table 1). For Medicare beneficiaries, the average age of CR participants was 77.0±6.1 compared with 79.8±7.8 (P<0.0001) among nonparticipants (Table 2). In both settings, younger patients were more likely to participate in CR.

In multivariable analyses, patient characteristics most strongly associated with participation in both veterans and Medicare beneficiaries were White race, ischemic heart disease, percutaneous coronary intervention, history of coronary artery bypass grafting, and (especially in Medicare beneficiaries) nonsystolic HF (Table 3). Male sex was associated with a 9% (95% CI, 2%–16%) greater odds of participating in CR among veterans and a 51% (95% CI, 46%–55%) greater odds of participating in CR among Medicare beneficiaries. The odds of participating in CR were 20% (95% CI, 18%–23%) lower among Black versus White veterans and 50% (95% CI, 47%–52%) lower among Black versus White Medicare beneficiaries. Hispanic ethnicity was associated with a 16% (95% CI, 11%–20%) lower odds of participating among veterans and a 78% (95% CI, 74%–81%) lower odds of participating among Medicare beneficiaries.

Other patient characteristics associated with greater participation among veterans included higher income (odds ratio [OR], 1.59 [95% CI, 1.52–1.66]; P<0.0001), marital status, and Midwest geographic location (OR, 1.38 [95% CI, 1.34–1.43]; P<0.0001). Additionally, veterans with diabetes (OR, 1.05 [95% CI, 1.03–1.07]; P<0.0001), ischemic heart disease (OR, 1.48 [95% CI, 1.45–1.52]; P<0.0001), percutaneous coronary intervention (OR, 3.43 [95% CI, 3.32–3.54]; P<0.0001), and coronary artery bypass grafting (OR, 6.48 [95% CI, 6.18–6.79]; P<0.0001) were more likely to participate in CR. Similar factors were associated with participation in Medicare beneficiaries, except that depression was associated with lower participation (OR, 0.79 [95% CI, 0.75–0.83]; P<0.0001).

When compared with patients who had systolic HF, Medicare beneficiaries with diastolic HF (OR, 0.79) or unspecified HF (OR, 0.66) were less likely to participate. Midwest location was associated with an 80% greater odds of participation among Medicare beneficiaries (OR, 1.80 [95% CI, 1.72–1.88]; P<0.0001). There was minimal difference in participation rates between veterans with systolic versus diastolic HF. However, veterans with unspecified HF were less likely to participate than those with systolic HF (OR, 0.90 [95% CI, 0.88–0.92]; P<0.0001).

DISCUSSION

Trends in CR participation among veterans with stable HF increased significantly from 2012 to 2022, with participation peaking in 2018 at 16.2% before the COVID‐19 pandemic. We did not observe a meaningful increase in CR participation from 2012 to 2022 among Medicare beneficiaries with stable HF. These data show that coverage for traditional FBCR was associated with only minimally increased participation rates in Medicare patients. The highest participation among Medicare beneficiaries was 8.7% in 2018. In contrast, the VA saw a substantial increase in the number of Veterans with HF who participated in CR provided or purchased by the VA, while the number of Veterans who received CR covered by Medicare remained relatively stable. Participation trends among both veterans and Medicare beneficiaries decreased after 2020 due to COVID‐19 lockdown restrictions, affecting attendance at FBCR programs. 17 , 18 Nonetheless, CR participation rates more than doubled in veterans with stable HF from 2012 to 2018 and remained largely unchanged for Medicare beneficiaries.

There are many potential reasons for the gap in CR participation rates between veterans and Medicare beneficiaries. First, Medicare does not cover CR for patients with diastolic HF, whereas the VA does. Accordingly, Medicare beneficiaries with nonsystolic HF had significantly lower odds of participating than those with systolic HF, but participation rates were similar for veterans with systolic and diastolic HF. Second, Medicare beneficiaries have a 20% copay for CR services (assuming they have already met their Part B deductible for the year), and this may be a deterrent to participation, whereas veterans have no copay for virtual care services. Third, the VA is an integrated health care system where services are coordinated, potentially making referral pathways easier to navigate. For many Medicare beneficiaries, there may be no relationship between the facility where they receive care for HF and the facility that provides CR, so the patient may be responsible for coordinating their own care. Fourth, the VA health care system has an incentive structure that benefits from preventive care, resulting in better long‐term outcomes for patients, whereas fee‐for‐service providers may not benefit from prevention. 19 , 20 Finally, some VA facilities offers HBCR, and offering HBCR has been associated with higher participation rates. 12 , 13 , 14 , 15

We found that CR participation rates differed by sex, race, and ethnicity in both populations, but these disparities were much less pronounced in veterans versus Medicare beneficiaries. Our results provide reassurance that the availability of HBCR has not resulted in the potential inequities observed with other virtual care technologies. 21 A common barrier to CR participation is a lack of awareness about CR and its components. 22 , 23 , 24 Additionally, the lack of transportation to facilities offering CR remains a significant barrier to participation in FCBR. 10 , 11 , 22 , 25 Given that the common barriers to CR participation, such as sex, socioeconomic status, and transportation, are well established in the literature, our analysis adds that expanding coverage for CR in 2014 did not significantly affect CR participation rates among Medicare beneficiaries. Shifting the focus from CR coverage to implementation of HCBR, including virtual options, could improve CR participation rates among Medicare patients. 13 , 26

HBCR includes telehealth options that can reach rural patients with HF who do not have time or access to transportation to attend FBCR which may lessen the regional and socioeconomic status disparity. 27 In addition, prior studies have shown that HBCR, compared with FBCR, has a substantially higher participation rate with better outcomes in time to enrollment and functional status among veterans. 28 , 29 We hypothesize covering HBCR services for Medicare recipients could have similar effects and help increase overall CR participation rates. Offering providers incentives for referring HF patients to CR across VA and Medicare hospitals may also increase CR participation by spreading awareness to patients that rehabilitation programs for HF management are available.

Limitations of our analysis include the use of electronic health records and claims data, which are prone to data entry and coding errors. However, we would have expected this to result in greater (rather than lesser) participation among Medicare beneficiaries where Medicare's fee‐for‐service payment system generates greater (rather than lesser) coding of CR than the VA single‐party payer system, which has less incentive for billing. Data from VA electronic health records may not be entirely accurate due to data inconsistency and interoperability issues across its 139 electronic health record instances. In addition, left ventricular ejection fraction measurements were not available from Medicare claims, so we were not able to differentiate patients with left ventricular ejection fraction ≤35% versus >35% and had to rely on ICD‐10 codes for systolic and diastolic HF.

Conclusions

In conclusion, CR participation remains low among patients with HF, despite the expansion of coverage in 2014 for Medicare beneficiaries. Low provider referrals to CR programs and awareness of CR also contribute to low participation rates. 30 HBCR presents an alternative option for increasing participation and completion rates by offering flexible care options that meet the needs of patients outside a traditional facility setting while producing similar health outcomes. 15 , 28 Medicare expanded virtual CR coverage during the COVID‐19 pandemic, but adoption of virtual CR was limited due to uncertainty about its long‐term financial sustainability after temporary COVID‐19 flexibilities ended. 18 Our analysis shows recent trends in CR participation are on a steady upward trend at the VA; however, significant strides are still needed to reach more patients with HF. Investing in HBCR could have a considerable impact among Medicare beneficiaries.
