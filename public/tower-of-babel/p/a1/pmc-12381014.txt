The individuality of plantar pressure indices enables the reduction of descriptive gait data required for diagnostic purposes
Authors: Ewa Latour, Emilia E Latour, Jarosław Arlet
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe diagnostic utility of gait analysis requires reducing the amount of measurement data while ensuring minimal loss of assessment accuracy, which in turn necessitates selection of gait descriptors that can capture unique gait characteristics with the greatest effectiveness. As changes in movement are more apparent in the spatial distribution of forces, we started from the assumption that kinetic indices can capture gait variation more clearly than kinematic indices. We juxtaposed the individuality of barographic indices across foot regions for force, pressure, plantar area, and contact time. We captured pressure distributions during free gait from 19 individuals in a highly homogeneous group of young healthy women who participated 200 walking trials. Time presented the highest individuality, the lowest regional differentiation, and the lowest variation. Force, pressure, and surface area exhibited comparably high individuality for certain parts of the foot. Intraindividual variation constituted a more individual feature than did the values themselves. Since the temporal domain proved to be more informative for individual gait patterns, it should be prioritised in diagnostic assessment. Regional differentiation was found to be the most informative data for gait variation and can be used to increase the diagnostic utility of gait descriptive data.Subject terms: Biophysics, Physiology

Introduction

Locomotion is described as the effort of the body to move its canter of mass in a desired direction while maintaining stability1. There are various factors that affect walking, and locomotion is shaped by adaptation and optimisation processes2. According to current conditions, gait remains stable in some respects, whereas in others, it changes3, and there is no unified opinion regarding which indices should be employed to assess the resulting gait variability4. Gait changes with maturation, ageing and illness5. The link between changes in gait variability and stability remains unclear6, and assessing the variation in gait descriptors is crucial for diagnostic applications of movement analysis7. Furthermore, “vast evidence suggests that movement should be interpreted as a combination of multiple parameters rather than isolated ones”8. Owing to the resulting complexity of gait description, sophisticated statistical analysis methods must be developed, as the currently employed methods do not enable the establishment of precise diagnostic applications9–11. However, for diagnostic utility, the number of variables should be reduced as much as possible without considerable loss of accuracy in the assessment. This task involves developing a method for selecting variables that provide the most information about individual gait characteristics. This necessitates the establishment of individuality as the ability of an assessed variable to indicate differences among individuals.

Gait variability has been interpreted as an expression of dynamic motor control within the specific biomechanical constraints of individual walking11. Consequently, differences in gait efficiency should manifest in different parameters, both at the population and individual level. The resulting scope of intra- and interindividual variation characterises all complex processes occurring in living organisms and is therefore called biological or natural12. The extent of the intraindividual variation reflects the optimisation of an individual’s gait performance, which occurs by walking at a self-selected speed13. However, it remains unclear how the optimisation develops or why it has only been observed in the kinetic description of propulsion1. Gait optimisation can be reflected in the description of individual idiosyncracies; however, identifying movement control processes by characterising the individuality of gait patterns remains difficult14,15. This is because a variation in movement task completion can be represented differently depending on the means of description, task characteristics, and body region investigated16. Thus, personal uniqueness in reaching a universal gait pattern17 is visible in the differences in segmental variation in body movement descriptors18. Therefore, for accuracy, the diagnostic evaluation should consider that the individuality of functioning varies across body parts. The analysis of the regional individuality of the kinetic descriptor of weight shifting, expressed by the relationship between intra- and interindividual variation, has confirmed the differentiation of individuality of functioning of particular foot parts according to their various roles in propulsion19. This difference reveals the necessity of assessing the measurement data separately for each foot region. Furthermore, the level of individuality in specific regions highlights the necessity of using reference values on the basis of intraindividual variation rather than relying solely on population norms to increase the precision of assessment. The opportunity to use the index of individuality to express biological variation as an inter- and intraindividual variation ratio, which is used mainly in biochemical analytical procedures20, enables diagnostic estimates to be made on the basis of a possibly small number of measurement trials21. Should the individuality of the nonkinetic descriptors prove sufficiently pronounced to provide significant additional information for the gait description, their inclusion in the gait analysis would be a rational next step. Identifying which descriptors for which foot parts represent the highest individuality would facilitate the assessment of their informativeness, thereby providing criteria for reducing the measurement data in diagnostic analysis.

Taken together, the effects of individual adaptation and optimisation processes should be represented in gait assessment by characteristics of inter- and intraindividual variation. The optimisation of force distribution across body parts to maintain balance with minimal energy expenditure during gait manifests itself in the stabilisation of the temporal pattern of movement22. The cause of movement is thus visible at the kinetic rather than the kinematic level1. Therefore, we assumed that individual gait pattern visibility is greatest for force and lowest for time. Consequently, we hypothesised that the individuality of the force values describing gait patterns would exhibit greater differences across regions than over time. To verify this hypothesis, we analysed the regional distributions of individuality indices of pedobarometric descriptors in various domains on the basis of gait patterns in which propulsion-driven regional differences in individuality in kinetic indices were previously confirmed19. We assessed the pressure, time, force and area of interaction between the foot and the ground, employing their commonly used descriptors. To standardise the assessment and allow for reliable interindividual comparisons, we evaluated the normalised values of these descriptors. To ensure that the most accurate evaluation of variation was rooted in fundamental motor control principles rather than the variety of factors influencing gait, we prioritised the homogeneity of the study group. This homogeneity allows the identification of biological variation in gait21. We selected healthy individuals following the ”gold standard for functional movement”, specifically at an age when mature gait patterns are fully developed and when musculoskeletal deformities have not yet emerged; thus, their muscular, skeletal, and neurological systems have achieved optimal gait adaptability23. Additionally, we restricted participation to individuals of a single sex and those with a uniform cultural and social background. The obtained homogeneity facilitated a reduction in the size of the study group while maintaining reliable statistical analysis results21. To determine individual variability profiles, we took pedobarographic measurements during 200 separate walking trials, which was the number deemed suitable for capturing individual gait patterns24.

Methods

We estimated the regional differences in the individuality indices of force, pressure, time, and area descriptors for distributions of plantar pressure (PP) obtained in free gait. To achieve this goal, we employed a method to quantify biological variation in gait by relating intra- to interindividual variation in a possibly homogeneous group. We set the resulting indices of individuality against decision thresholds that determines the way of carrying out analytical procedures to maintain the accuracy of the assessment25.

Participants

The study group consisted of nineteen young women of similar age (19.7, SD = 0.6 years) from the same cultural background who had the following values of mass, height, and BMI: 60.33 (SD = 6.81) kg, 1.67 (SD = 0.04) m, and 21.58 (SD = 2.44) kg/m2, respectively. All had a similar lifestyle, were physically active but not involved in sports, and showed no observable signs of disease that could affect musculoskeletal function. Each participant provided informed consent before participating in the study. The study adhered to the guidelines of the Declaration of Helsinki. The Institutional Review Boards of the Ethics Committee at Poznan University of Medical Sciences (Act 1068/16; Archived Number 10/November 2016) approved the measurement protocol used in this study.

Measurements

All measurements employed an emed-m system comprising a pedobarometric platform (Novel, Munich, Germany; active area: 395 × 240 mm; 3792 capacitance-based force transducers at a resolution of 4 sensors/cm2) embedded in a 5-m walkway and software to perform a preliminary analysis of the obtained temporal‒spatial distribution of the foot PP. We collected measurement data using the second step protocol26, registering the PP distribution during the stance phase of the second step. While familiarising participants with the testing procedure, we instructed them to look straight ahead to avoid targeted movements that might alter their gait. We recorded 200 steps for each participant24, alternately: 100 steps for each foot, totalling 2 × 100 = 200 steps, which amounts to 2 × 19 × 100 = 3800 measurements.

The emed-m software calculates a set of indices describing the process of body weight shifting through the plantar area of the foot for ten parts (masks) separated with artificial intelligence algorithms: 1 – heel, 2 – midfoot, 3–7 – the five metatarsal heads (MTH1-MTH5), 8 – hallux (first toe), 9 – second toe, 10 – third to fifth toes, and 0 – the entire foot surface (Fig. 1).

In general, the individuality for intraindividual variation (IvI) was greater (Fig. 3D, Fig. 4D, Fig. 5D, Fig. 6D) than that for the values (IxI) of the analysed indices (Fig. 3B, Fig. 4B, Fig. 5B, Fig. 6B). None of the IxI and IvI indices fell below − 0.336, the recommended decision threshold to permit the use of population-based reference standards. Hence, no region fell under the criteria of assessment on the basis of population variation. Furthermore, for almost all the masks, the indices of individuality exceeded 0 (Vintra > Vinter). The sole exceptions were mask 3 (MTH1) for ftp and masks 3, 4, and 5 (MTH 3,4,5) for pa, where the IxI values were less than 0, which was still far from the − 0.336 threshold. Overall, the indices of individuality had values closer to the range above 0.512, which is perceived to signal a strong bias towards a diagnostic pattern on the basis of internal rather than population variation. This effect was most prominent for IvI, especially for ctp and pa.

Similarly, we identified apparent differences between masks 2 and 3 for ptp (Fig. 6B) and pa (Fig. 4B) but not for ctp (Fig. 3B). Discussion

This study aimed to ascertain which descriptors of body weight shift through the foot are the most informative of individual gait patterns. As movement stems from force, we assumed that description in a kinetic domain would exhibit differentiation in the individuality of foot parts functioning more than in other domains and, for this reason, would enable distinguishing individual gait patterns most effectively. We analysed the individuality of the barographic indices of force, pressure, area and time across foot regions in a homogeneous group of healthy young women. We found that individuality was the most pronounced for time and exhibited greater regional consistency than the other descriptors.

For all descriptors analysed, the distributions of intra- and interindividual regional variations represented a linear relation of a similar slope, which translated into the relative similarity of their ratios, representing individuality IxI (Fig. 2). This result demonstrates the similarity in the degree of subordination of an individual’s walking pattern to the overarching walking pattern across different description domains, regardless of the level of variation in their descriptors. However, captured in the background of similarity, differences in regional IxI distributions across the descriptors reflect domain-related differentiation in the extent of individuality. Additionally, the differentiation of individuality of internal variation (IvI) clearly prevailed over the differentiation of individuality of values (IxI) (Fig. 7), demonstrating that individual control over variability plays a substantial role in gait realisation. Among the analysed descriptors, the time descriptor ctp demonstrated the highest level of individuality across the foot parts, as evidenced by the highest average values of both IxI and IvI and the highest consistency exhibited by the effect size of Cohen’s d for IxI (2.65 [1.93; 3.36]) and, to a much greater extent, for IvI (3.71 [2.99; 4.42]) (p < 0.01) (Table 1). These results support the hypothesis that the individuality of the force gait descriptor results in greater regional differentiation than does the individuality of the time descriptor. However, the indices of individuality for the kinetic descriptors were generally lower than those for the temporal descriptor and approached those of the latter only for specific foot regions. Thus, we conclude that, in accordance with this hypothesis, contrary to the initial assumption, individual realisations of the gait pattern can be more effectively distinguished on the basis of the temporal domain than on the basis of the force, pressure and area domains of gait description.

The finding of the highest individuality featuring the least variable index, i.e., time, reflects and elucidates the known coexistence of the stability of the temporal gait pattern with the variety of kinetic gait patterns1,28. Considering the motor control concept of adaptive freezing of degrees of freedom (DOFs) in reducing the redundancy of motor patterns29, the captured similarity in individuality levels between the most variable kinetic descriptors and the least variable temporal descriptors coherently represents the individual freezing of DOFs across all parameters analysed. However, the regional differentiation observed within this similarity was weakest for the temporal descriptor, which consistently exhibited a high individuality level in each foot region. This finding implies the subordination of weight shifting to individual timing in each foot region, a property that ensures a high degree of repeatability in the execution of an individual gait pattern. In the past, the consistent replication of temporal gait patterns gave rise to conviction about the ‘invariance’ of the relative timings30, which led to the concept of a central pattern generator31,32. Subsequent observations, however, demonstrated that the variation in relative timing merited attention, and pattern generation revealed the complexity attributable to DOFs in joint movement33. The present study captured the temporal manifestation of the adaptive freezing of DOFs in the individual implementation of the gait pattern with pronounced temporal individuality. This finding provides a clear rationale for employing the time descriptor as a source of information regarding the implementation of a movement task pattern in the context of diagnostics. The high informativeness of the time description of gait has also been recognised in studies, in which it has been successfully employed for gait recognition34 and the creation of the “gait signature”35. Notably, the conditions under which our study was conducted enabled the capture of natural gait patterns, as opposed to studies that employed a treadmill or metronome, which imposed a fixed speed or rhythm36–38. Thus, the temporal consistency observed at self-selected walking speeds and rhythms manifested individual adaptive processes that optimised gait. Consequently, adaptive optimisation should be regarded as the cause of the enhanced observability of the individuality of the temporal descriptor compared with other descriptors. Thus, our findings challenge the prevailing opinion that kinetic manifestations of DOF freezing in gait predominate over temporal manifestations, demonstrating that individuality is indeed more evident in temporal descriptors, as they are the least variable and thus more controlled.

The observed level of individuality of intraindividual variability (IvI) corroborates the literature notion of the high utility of analysis of this variability in gait assessment12. The highest levels of IvI for ctp and pa demonstrate that individual gait features manifest most potently in the control of time and area of foot‒ground interaction. The highest IvI for ctp in the central forefoot, i.e., the most stressed area (Fig. 3), demonstrates that controlling the loading time of the region of the foot bearing the highest load plays a pivotal role in the realisation of an effective yet stable gait39. The contact area in the heel and midfoot (Fig. 4) is highly individual, as expressed in both IxI and IvI for pa. This finding reinforces the established notion that the spatial configuration of these foot regions plays a pivotal role in determining an individual’s pattern of weight transfer through the foot40–42. The most variable weight-shifting descriptors, force (ftp) and pressure (ptp), consistently reproduced the regional differentiation in individuality levels (IxI) for the previously assessed kinetic descriptor of this process19. Unlike the first foot ray, which was the strongest and secured by the other limb in the body weight shift, the second one exhibited greater individuality, comparable to that of the midfoot. Furthermore, the notable degree of IvI for ftp and ptp, observed in the central forefoot and heel regions, aligns with the exceptionally high degree of IvI of nonkinetic descriptors mentioned above. The resulting complex picture of variation, evaluated in terms of individuality and corroborating the current knowledge about the variability and motor control of gait, demonstrates that individual features of gait manifest themselves most prominently in the time descriptor, with particular emphasis on the temporal regime of the central forefoot. This constitutes locomotion-based proof of appropriate forefoot timing of the push-off during propulsion, aiding the metabolic efficiency in the reverted pendulum repetitive movement, as previously mentioned43. Therefore, one can explain the greater observability of the individuality of temporal descriptors by maintaining the time regime of the heaviest loaded part of the foot to achieve rhythmicity in energetic gait optimisation.

The capture of general motor control phenomena became possible due to the homogeneity of the study group, which exhibited a healthy, mature gait. This homogeneity serves to reduce the random components of interindividual variability to an achievable minimum, thereby facilitating the identification of biological variations in gait that reflect the properties of motor control. Consequently, the results obtained chiefly substantiate the general principles of movement control, thus enabling the generalisation of our findings. These results support the findings of the extant literature data44–47 regarding both the values and the variation. They also corroborated earlier research48 by showing that the heel and forefoot varied the least. This observation lends further support to the predominant temporal manifestation of adaptive gait individuality that has been revealed, thereby providing a robust rationale for prioritising the time index as the most informative pedobarometric descriptor for diagnostic analysis.

The revealed differences in informativeness between the gait descriptors justify the inclusion of individuality analysis in the gait assessment. The identification of highly informative descriptors characterising specific foot regions offers the possibility to choose them for diagnostics and to implement procedures that would preserve the precision of the assessment, leading to a reduction in the number of measurement repetitions necessary. In highly informative regions, one can more precisely predict the degree to which the second measurement may deviate from the first measurement just after the first measurement by taking intraindividual variation into account than by basing predictions on the population norm. Detailed procedures, which have already been developed in the field of biochemical diagnostics49, enable the use of biological variation to reduce the number of measurements required to obtain an estimate accurate enough for the purposes of assessment. Our results show that the employment of population norms remains diagnostically reasonable only for the force at the first metatarsal head or the contact area of the paracentral part of the forefoot because of a sufficiently low individuality level (Fig. 5). However, for almost all regions and descriptors, the calculated individuality levels highlight the need to use reference values on the basis of intraindividual variation rather than relying solely on population norms. Identifying the time domain as the most informative region consistently in the whole foot and the foot regions with the highest informativeness in the remaining domains is fundamental to implementing data reduction, as they provide individual characteristics most precisely, even with the smallest possible number of measurement trials21. The introduction of the index of individuality as an additional variable into the data description may increase the number of descriptive indices. However, limiting the number of variables in the description according to the criterion of the value of this index and then limiting the number of measurements according to this value should decrease the size of the dataset without significant loss of precision in the assessment.

The index of individuality offers valuable benefits for gait analysis, as it enhances diagnostic precision by parameterising the difference between the mean of the measurements obtained from an individual and the population average. This allows for a more reliable assessment of the probability with which this difference one can perceive as a deviation from the norm. By knowing the population norm and the index of individuality of the measured quantity, one can approximately determine its intraindividual variation and thus predict the value of the second measurement result on the basis of the first more precisely than on the basis of only the population norm. On the basis of a single measurement, one can also make a more precise preliminary estimation of the magnitude of the deviation from the population norm. In particular, when comparing measurement results from two regions of the foot with similar population averages, for regions with a higher index of individuality, one can achieve similar precision in estimating the individual average with fewer measurements. For this foot region, one can also observe the size of the effect of changes between the results of successive measurement sessions more clearly. In the interpretation of the measurement results, the high value of the index of individuality suggests that the difference between the measured values and the population average may not stem from a pathological condition but rather from an idiosyncratic characteristic of the individual’s gait. Knowledge of the index of individuality thus enables a more precise determination of the extent of gait changes resulting from implemented treatment, physical rehabilitation, or deterioration of the pathological condition. The high value of the index of individuality in the assessment of the difference between values obtained in successive measurements renders this difference more significant for diagnostic judgement, as it occurs against a background of lower intraindividual variability. In the context of pedobarometric measurements, this difference will thus be regarded as more substantial for foot regions featuring a higher index of individuality. The above applications exemplify the contribution of the index of individuality to the enhancement of the informativeness of measurement data.

Our research demonstrates the importance of the index of individuality in shaping diagnostic precision as well as its numerous potential applications in facilitating data analysis and interpretation in gait studies. The regional specification of individuality degree in movement task completion provides a framework for complex gait variability assessment. The enhancement of the quality of analysis of variation by normalisation with the index of individuality enables the revelation of the hierarchy of particular parameters that describe gait. The knowledge about the values of individuality of pedobarometric descriptors of the functioning of particular foot parts facilitates recognition of the role they play in individual optimisation of gait. Comparative analysis of the individuality indices in study groups isolated on the basis of different criteria (for example, comorbidities, environmental factors, etc.) will help assess the influence that individually differentiating factors have on the adaptive abilities of gait. Moreover, the outcome of this study could serve as a normative reference point for mature gait free from pathologies. All factors that may interfere with the process of gait realisation are likely to be captured against the obtained ranges of values and variations of PP descriptors for young and fully fit individuals. The resulting profiles of differences could be used to diagnose pathologies. The identification and classification of these profiles via diagnostic methods have the potential to increase their efficacy, thereby facilitating a better understanding of the mechanisms underlying movement pathologies.

The findings presented herein are based on the conviction derived from previous research that descriptions of biological variation obtained from measurements performed according to the gold standard reflect general properties of motor control and are therefore applicable to all individuals. The effectiveness of this description in the assessment of gait characteristics requires verification by evaluating the accuracy of the diagnostic criteria, especially in the context of older individuals or those whose gait is not yet fully mature. As this verification requires multiple comparative analyses against clinical methods and constitutes a separate research question, this issue is not addressed in this study. Addressing this limitation sets the direction for future work on the application of measures of individuality in the development of diagnostic tools. Undoubtedly, however, our work provides a ready-made individuality norm for the gold standard of gait assessment and a comprehensive concept of assessing the variation in gait descriptors. Selecting variables that provide the most information about individual gait characteristics among a combination of multiple parameters will remove limitations crucial for the development of diagnostic applications of movement analysis.

Conclusions

The most informative among descriptors regarding body weight shift through the plantar area of the foot during walking is the time descriptor. The uniformity of the distribution of this descriptor in the forefoot allows any of the forefoot regions to be selected for diagnostic evaluation, as it enables the drawing of conclusions regarding the values represented by the other forefoot regions. Other descriptors were comparatively informative for certain foot parts due to the regional differences in individuality: contact area in the heel and midfoot, pressure and force in the midfoot and second footray. Intraindividual variation constitutes more individual features than values do, mainly for time and area, and less for force and pressure. The individuality levels calculated for pressure, force, time, and area of ground interaction underscore the need to relate to intrasubject variability rather than only to population norms of values for diagnostic precision. Only the force measurement at the first head of the metatarsal bone and the contact area of the paracentral part of the forefoot characterise the individuality level as low enough to validate the use of population norms. The incorporation of the index of individuality during the diagnostic analysis of reduced measurement data enables the assessment of possible deviations from the population model on a probability scale, as opposed to a decision threshold. This approach also supports maximum sensitivity to changes in the results of subsequent measurement sessions.
