Buffer stabilized sodium butyrate enhances early growth and gut barrier function in broilers by modulating SCFA, antioxidant, and microbiota composition
Authors: Mebratu Melaku, Huaibao Zhao, Ruqing Zhong, Xiaobin Wen, Chengzeng Luo, Jiaheng Li, Awais Ali, Habtamu Ayalew, Bao Yi, Liang Chen, Hongfu Zhang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundEarly intestinal development plays a significant role in determining long-term broiler health and performance; however, information on the effects of new buffer salt-protected sodium butyrate (NBSB) during this stage remains limited. The present study evaluated the early stage effects of NBSB on growth performance, intestinal health, and cecal microbiota, building on our previous 42-day study using ELISA, RT-qPCR, gas chromatography, and 16S rRNA sequencing. In total, 192 day-old male broiler chicks were allocated to three groups using a completely randomized design with eight replicate cages per treatment (eight chicks per cage): (1) a corn soybean basal diet (CTRL), (2) CTRL + 0.01% aureomycin antibiotic (ANBX), and (3) CTRL + 0.08% NBSB.ResultsCompared with the CTRL, NBSB increased the final body weight by 6.1%, ADG by 8.0%, and reduced FCR by 5.0% (P < 0.05), with no significant difference from the ANBX group. NBSB also significantly increased IL-10, IgA, IgG, and decreased TNF-α levels (P < 0.05) compared to ANBX. Additionally, NBSB increased villus height (VH) in the ileum and the VH: CD ratio in both the jejunum and ileum (P < 0.05). It further upregulated the expression of tight junction proteins, SOD, and T-AOC/MDA ratio in both intestinal segments, along with T-AOC levels in the jejunum (P < 0.05). NBSB also elevated acetic (AA) and butyric (BA) acid contents in the small and large intestines (P < 0.05). 16S rRNA analysis revealed that NBSB was associated with changes in microbial composition, including significant enrichment of SCFA-producing taxa, such as Ruminococcaceae, Eubacterium_hallii_group, and Ruminococcus (P < 0.05).ConclusionsThese associations suggest that NBSB may have potential as a supportive alternative to antibiotics in healthy broilers, although further studies under disease challenge conditions are needed.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12917-026-05494-9.Keywords: Buffer salt protected sodium butyrate, Starter phase, Intestinal barrier, Microbiota composition, Broiler performance

Introduction

Early development of intestinal health and the gut microbiota is crucial for efficient nutrient digestion and immune function in broilers [1]. However, the digestive system of newly hatched broilers is functionally immature, increasing their sensitivity to pathogenic bacteria and environmental stressors [2]. This challenge has become even more critical following the global ban on in-feed antibiotics, which has increased interest in safe, gut-modulating alternatives [3, 4].

Consequently, this regulatory shift has prompted greater interest in sustainable alternatives, such as organic acids and their derivatives [5]. Among these, sodium butyrate (SB), particularly when delivered through protected formulations, has shown promise for supporting gut health and performance in poultry [6].

In the intestinal tract, SB dissociates into butyric acid, which serves as a major energy source for epithelial cells [7] and supports intestinal function, immune regulation, and microbial homeostasis [8]. Additionally, SB has been shown to modulate immune responses and promote oxidative balance in young animals, including broilers, piglets, and calves [9–11]. These effects are partially mediated by the upregulation of tight junctions and immune-related genes. For example, as a histone deacetylase inhibitor (HDACi), SB enhances histone acetylation, thereby promoting tight junction gene expression and gut barrier function [12].

The efficacy of SB depends on its site-specific release and stability within the gastrointestinal tract [13]. Inconsistent results across studies may reflect differences in SB release kinetics and delivery systems [14–16]. To address this, novel buffer salt-protected sodium butyrate (NBSB) formulations have been developed to stabilize the local pH and enhance targeted intestinal delivery [17].

Previous short-term studies (< 21 days) have shown that chemically protected sodium butyrate (CSB) or coated SB can improve early growth performance, intestinal morphology, antioxidant capacity, and immune responses in broilers [18–20]. However, these studies did not integrate all critical indicators of gut health, such as tight junction gene expression, short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) profiles, and cecal microbial diversity, within the same early-phase framework.

Although our previous work evaluated NBSB over 42 days [17], that study did not specifically address the starter-phase (1–21 d) responses that govern gut maturation and microbiota establishment. Because early interventions can have lasting effects, an integrated approach is necessary to link early performance with the underlying molecular and microbial mechanisms governing gut development.

Therefore, we hypothesized that NBSB enhances early gut development and associated microbial and immune indicators in broilers. To test this hypothesis, the present study investigated the effects of NBSB on growth, serum immunity, intestinal morphology, antioxidant status, short-chain fatty acid contents, and cecal microbial diversity during the starter phase in broiler chickens.

Materials and methods

Study design and experimental diet

This study analyzed data and samples collected on day 21 from the same experimental trial described in our previous 42-day study [17].

A total of one hundred ninety-two one-day-old healthy male Arbor Acres broilers (purchased from Huadu Broiler Company, Beijing, China) with an initial weight of 45.3 ± 0.18 g were randomly divided into 3 dietary treatments in a completely randomized design (CRD). Each treatment consisted of 8 replicates with 8 chicks per replicate. The treatment diets included: (1) a corn-soybean basal diet (control diet, CTRL), (2) a basal diet + 0.01% aureomycin antibiotic (ANBX), and (3) a basal diet + 0.08% new buffer salt protected sodium butyrate (NBSB). The dosage level for NBSB was determined on the basis of previous test experiments combined with a previous study [21], and the ANBX inclusion criteria were taken from previous studies [20, 22]. The experimental diets were formulated on the basis of the Arbor Acres Broiler Nutrition Specifications [23]. The analyzed chemical composition and nutrient content of the diets are presented in Table (S1). The NBSB product was supplied by Fujian Alliance Biotechnology Co., Ltd., Fujian, China and it’s concentration and purity were provided according to manufacturer’s standards. The product is designed to reduce hydrolysis in low pH conditions and ensure effective delivery to the hind gut.

Prior to chemical analysis, diet samples were finely ground to pass through a 0.5‑mm sieve. The crude protein was analyzed using Kjeldahl method following AOAC (2006) method 990.03 and the gross energy (GE) content of the diets was analyzed using an adiabatic oxygen bomb calorimeter (model 6400, Parr Instrument Co., Moline, IL, USA) as described by Ma et al. [24]. Metabolizable energy (ME) was then calculated from GE and nutrient composition. Calcium and total/available phosphorus were determined according to AOAC (2006) methods 984.01 and 965.17, consistent with recent poultry studies [25]. Lysine and methionine were quantified by ion‑exchange chromatography using an automated amino acid analyzer (Hitachi L‑8900, Tokyo, Japan) after hydrolysis with 6 N HCl for 24 h, following AOAC (2011) Method 994.12, as described by Sun et al. [26].

Chicken housing and management

Before chick placement, the floor, cages, feed trough, and water lines were thoroughly cleaned, fumigated, and disinfected. The experimental chickens were raised in three-layer cages (1.25 × 0.8 × 0.45 m per cage) and provided mash feed and clean water ad libitum [27]. Each cage was equipped with a separate feeding trough and nipple drinkers. The room temperature was controlled at 34–35 °C for the 1st 3 d, then gradually lowered by 2–3 °C per week to reach 22 °C by 21 d of age [28]. Relative humidity was maintained between 50 and 60% and ventilation was adjusted to maintain ammonia concentrations below 10 ppm. Lighting was provided continuously for the first 24 h, followed by a 23 h light: 1 h dark cycle for the reminder of the experiment [26]. The house was cleaned daily and ventilated adequately to keep a healthy environment for the chickens. All chickens received vaccinations for Newcastle disease (d1, d10), infectious bronchitis (d1, d10), highly pathogenic avian influenza (d1), and infectious bursal disease (d1) following standard vaccination practices [29]. Throughout the experimental period, bird health was monitored daily, and any mortality was recorded. All husbandry procedures complied with the Arbor Acres broiler strain management guidelines [30] and the protocols of the State Key Laboratory of Animal Nutrition and Feeding, Institute of Animal Sciences, Chinese Academy of Agricultural Sciences, Beijing, China.

Growth performance measurement and sample collection

The body weight and feed intake of the chicks were recorded weekly for each replicate. The average daily gain (ADG), average daily feed intake (ADFI), and feed conversion ratio (FCR) were subsequently calculated.

On day 21, one broiler per replicate (close to the average weight of the cage) was selected, and blood was collected from the wing vein. After collection, birds were euthanized by cervical dislocation. Blood sample were incubated for 3–4 h, and then centrifuged at 3,000 rpm for 10 min at 4 °C. The serum was separated and stored in 1.5 mL electropolished tubes at -20 °C for the immunity assay. The birds were euthanized, and their internal organs were dissected. Sections of the jejunum (from the pancreatic loop to Meckel’s diverticulum), and ileum (from Meckel’s diverticulum to the ileocecal junction), approximately 3–5 cm in length, were collected from the mid region and immersed in 4% paraformaldehyde solution for histomorphological examination. Digesta and tissues from the jejunum and ileum were stored at − 80 °C for subsequent analysis of short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), mRNA expression, and antioxidant status. Cecal digesta were also collected for SCFA quantification and 16S rRNA microbiota profiling.

Serum immune indicators

The serum levels of TNF-α (catalog code 88-7346), IL-1β (catalog code 88-7261), IL-6 (catalog code 88-7066), and IL-10 (catalog code 88-7106) and the immunoglobulins IgA, IgG, and IgM (catalog code HY-753) were measured via ELISA kits (Thermo Fisher Scientific Inc. Vienna, Austria) using a SpectraMax M2 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA) following the instructions provided by the manufacturer.

Microscopic architecture of the small intestine

Histological analysis was conducted via methods previously described in our laboratory [18]. Briefly, jejunum and ileum tissue samples were dehydrated in graded ethanol, cleared with xylene, embedded in paraffin, sectioned at 5 μm, and stained via the hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) method under a light microscope (Leica Microsystems, Pte Ltd., SG-608924, Singapore) by examining the histological sections at 5–10X magnification [31]. Villus height (VH) and crypt depth (CD) were measured via the Leica Application Suite (LAS X v4.9, Leica Microsystems CMS, GmbH, Heerbrugg, Switzerland) and the VH: CD ratio was subsequently calculated [20]. For each bird, ten well oriented villi and crypts were measured per intestinal segment, and the mean values were used for statistical analysis, with the individual bird serving as the experimental unit, as described by Belote et al. [32].

Gene expression profiling by RT‒qPCR

Total RNA was extracted from 100 mg of jejunal and ileal tissue via TRIzol® reagent (Life Technologies, 15596018, Carlsbad, California, USA), and the purity was confirmed via a NanoDrop 2000c (Thermo Fisher). The RNA was reverse transcribed into cDNA via PrimeScript™ RT reagent kits with gDNA eraser (Takara, Dalian, China). Quantitative real-time PCR (RT‒qPCR) was performed via TB Green® Premix Ex Taq™ reagent kits (Tli RNaseH Plus, Takara Biomedical Technology Co., Ltd., Beijing, China) on an ABI Q7 Flex system (Applied Biosystems, USA). RT‒qPCR amplification occurred in 3 stages: first, a predenaturation stage with one cycle at 95 °C for 30 s; second, a denaturation stage with 40 cycles at 95 °C for 5 s; and finally, a dissociation curve stage with one cycle at 60 °C for 30 s. The primer sequences and housekeeping gene (β-actin) used are listed in Table S2. The relative gene expression was calculated via the comparative (2−ΔΔCT) method.

Evaluation of antioxidant enzyme activity

Approximately 0.1 g of jejunum and ileum tissue was homogenized with 0.9 mL of NaCl, incubated at room temperature for 5 min, and centrifuged at 3000 rpm for 10 min. The resulting supernatants were assayed for superoxide dismutase (SOD, cat#A001-3), total antioxidant capacity (T-AOC, cat#A015-1), and malondialdehyde (MDA, cat#A003-1) levels via kits from Nanjing Jiancheng Bioengineering Institute, Nanjing, China. The total protein concentration was measured via a Bicinchoninic Acid assay kit (Thermo Scientific, USA). Absorbance was measured using SpectraMax M2 microplate reader (Molecular Devices, San Jose, CA, USA).

Short-Chain Fatty Acid (SCFA) quantification

Short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs: acetic, propionic, and butyric acids) in digesta from the jejunum, ileum, and cecum were quantified by gas chromatography. Approximately 0.5 g of digesta was mixed with 25% metaphosphoric acid, incubated at 4 °C for > 2 h, centrifuged at 1,000 rpm for 10 min, and filtered through a 0.45 μm Millipore filter. SCFA analysis was performed using an Agilent 7890 N7890N gas chromatograph (Agilent Technologies, USA).

16S rRNA-based cecal microbiota profiling

Genomic DNA was extracted from a total of 24 cecal samples using AxyPrep DNA gel recovery kit (AXYGEN Biosciences, USA), with three technical replicates per sample. The V3–V4 region of the 16S rRNA gene was amplified via PCR via the primers 338 F (5’ACTCCTACGGGAGGCAGCAG-3’) and 806R (3’GGACTACHVGGGTWTCTAAT-5’). Amplicons were purified, pooled in equimolar concentrations, and sequenced on an Illumina MiSeq PE300 platform (Illumina, USA) by Majorbio Bio-Pharm Technology Co., Ltd. (Shanghai, China) [17]. The sequencing data were processed via UPARSE (v11, http://www.drive5.com/uparse/), Mothur (v2.13, https://www.mothur.org/wiki/Download_mothur) for α diversity, and R version 3.3.1 with the vegan package (version 2.5-3) for β diversity analysis (Bray–Curtis distance, PCoA, and ANOSIM) on the Majorbio cloud platform. The Kruskal‒Wallis test was used to assess differences in taxon abundance among treatment groups, followed by Dunn’s post hoc test for multiple comparisons.

Statistical analysis

Data on growth performance and gut health parameters were assessed for normality via the Shapiro‒Wilk test and for homogeneity of variances via Levene’s test; both assumptions were met (P > 0.05). Data were then analyzed via one-way ANOVA followed by Tukey’s post hoc test, using SPSS v27.0 (IBM, Armonk, NY, USA). Results are presented as mean ± SEM. Statistical significance was set at P < 0.05 or P < 0.01. Graphs were created in GraphPad Prism v10.2.0, and Spearman correlations with heatmaps were generated using OriginPro® 2024 (OriginLab, Northampton, MA, USA). The strength of correlations was interpreted based on Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient (r) as follows: very weak (|r| < 0.20), weak (0.20 ≤ |r| < 0.40), moderate (0.40 ≤ |r| < 0.60), strong (0.60 ≤ |r| < 0.80), and very strong (|r| ≥ 0.80).

Small intestinal histomorphological structure

The jejunum and ileum are the main intestinal segments responsible for nutrient digestion and absorption, which are essential for rapid growth and feed efficiency in broilers. As shown in Fig. 1, compared with ANBX, NBSB significantly decreased (P < 0.05) the crypt depth (CD) and increased (P < 0.05) the VH: CD ratio in the jejunum (Fig. 1C). Compared with ANBX, NBSB significantly increased the VH (P < 0.05) and the VH: CD ratio (P < 0.01) in the ileum (Fig. 1D). However, there were no notable differences in VH in the jejunum or CD in the ileum segments among the three groups.

Intestinal barrier and immune gene expression

As shown in Fig. 2A-B, dietary NBSB supplementation significantly upregulated the mRNA expression of tight junction proteins ZO-1, Claudin-1, and occludin in both the jejunum and ileum (P < 0.05). In addition, Mucin-2 expression was significantly upregulated in the jejunum (P < 0.01) and tended to increase in the ileum (P = 0.063) compared with both the CTRL and ANBX groups.

Small intestine antioxidant capacity

Analysis of antioxidant indicators showed that SOD activity and the T-AOC/MDA ratio significantly increased (P < 0.05) in both the jejunum and ileum of the NBSB group (Fig. 3A-B). Additionally, T-AOC levels in the jejunum were significantly greater (P < 0.01) with NBSB supplementation. MDA levels tended to decrease both in the jejunum (P = 0.077) and ileum (P = 0.097) compared to the CTRL and ANBX groups (Fig. 3A-B).

SCFAs contents in the intestinal segments

As shown in Table 3, the NBSB group showed significantly higher levels of acetic acid (AA) in the jejunum (P < 0.05) compared to the ANBX group, and in the ileum and cecum (P < 0.001) compared with the CTRL group. The NBSB group also significantly elevated (P < 0.001) butyric acid (BA) content in the jejunum, ileum, and cecum compared with the CTRL and ANBX groups. Furthermore, propionic acid (PA) levels in the ileum significantly greater (P < 0.001) in the NBSB group than in the other groups. Overall, NBSB significantly increased (P < 0.001) AA and BA in the jejunum and cecum, as well as PA and BA levels in the ileum.

Cecal microbial diversity and composition

In total, 1,143,552 quality-filtered sequences were obtained from cecal samples using 338F_806R primers, corresponding to 676,314,455 bases with an average read length of 409.32 bp. The estimated Good’s coverage exceeded 99.99% (~ 1.0) across all 24 samples, indicating sufficient sequencing depth (Table 4 and Table S3). At a 97% sequence similarity threshold and a minimum taxonomic classification confidence of 0.7, a total of 6 phyla, 11 classes, 34 orders, 58 families, 133 genera, 245 species, and 590 OTUs were identified.

At the phylum level, the dominant bacterial taxa across all groups were Firmicutes (85.93%), Bacteroidota (12.31%), Proteobacteria (1.35%), Actinobacteriota (0.37%), and Cyanobacteria (0.02%) (Fig. 4C). No significant differences were observed among treatment groups at the phylum level (P > 0.05). At the genus level, the most abundant genera included Faecalibacterium, Bacteroides, unclassified_f__Lachnospiraceae, norank_f__norank_o__Clostridia_UCG-014, Ruminococcus_torques_group, Lactobacillus, Butyricicoccus, and Lachnoclostridium, and Alistipes. The relative abundance of norank_f__Ruminococcaceae was higher in the NBSB than in the ANBX group (2.15% vs. 0.99%, P < 0.05, Kruskal–Wallis test followed by Dunn’s post hoc test) (Fig. 4D). Further taxonomic comparison showed that, compared with the CTRL and ANBX groups, the NBSB group had significantly higher relative abundances of norank_f__Ruminococcaceae, Eubacterium_hallii_group, GCA-900,066,575, Ruminococcus, and Family_XIII_AD3011_group (P < 0.05), indicating selective enrichment of several SCFA-associated taxa. Conversely, the ANBX group had significantly higher relative abundances of Marvinbryantia and Eubacterium ventriosum_group (P < 0.05) compared with the NBSB group, while Romboutsia and Oscillospira (P < 0.05) were more abundant in the CTRL than in the NBSB group (Fig. 4E). These results indicate that NBSB supplementation induced distinct shifts in cecal microbial composition compared with both CTRL and ANBX groups. LEfSe analysis further supported these differences (Fig. S1).

Spearman correlation between gut metabolites and the cecal microbiota

Spearman correlation analysis was performed to explore the statistical associations between the cecal microbiota at the genus level and host intestinal barrier gene expression and SCFA content. These correlations suggest potential relationships between microbial composition and host intestinal defense and inflammation regulation but do not imply causation (Fig. 5). The analysis revealed that norank_f__Ruminococcaceae was moderately to strongly positively correlated with ZO-1 expression in the jejunum; claudin-1, occludin, and mucin-2 in both the jejunum and ileum; and with AA and BA contents (P < 0.05). Romboutsia was moderately positively correlated with claudin-1 and mucin-2 in the ileum, as well as with PA and BA (P < 0.05). Similarly, Eubacterium_hallii_group was moderately positively correlated with ZO-1 and occludin in the jejunum, mucin-2 in the ileum, and BA contents (P < 0.05).

GCA-900,066,575 showed moderately positive correlations with ZO-1 in the jejunum, claudin-1 in the ileum, and both AA and BA (P < 0.05). Ruminococcus was moderately positively correlated with ZO-1 in the jejunum and BA (P < 0.05). Additionally, the Family_XIII_AD3011_group was moderately to strongly positively correlated with ZO-1, claudin-1 and occludin in the jejunum, mucin-2 in the ileum, and PA and BA (P < 0.05).

In contrast, Oscillospira was moderately negatively correlated with occludin in the ileum and PA (P < 0.05). Marvinbryantia showed moderate to strong negative correlations with mucin-2 in the jejunum, ZO-1, claudin-1 and mucin-2 in the ileum, and with AA and BA (P < 0.05). Finally, Eubacterium_ventriosum_group was moderately negatively correlated with ZO-1 in both the jejunum and ileum, claudin-1 and occludin in the ileum, and AA (P < 0.05).

Discussion

The newly developed buffer salt-protected sodium butyrate (NBSB) is an advanced form of SB designed to enhance gut health and growth performance in poultry. The present study was conducted under controlled conditions without a pathogenic challenge; therefore, the growth promoting effects of antibiotics may have been limited [33], which may explain the comparable or improved performance observed in the NBSB group. Consistent with previous reports in which protected SB formulations were used, NBSB supplementation improved ADG and reduced FCR in 21-day-old broilers. Similar early-phase improvements in body weight gain and feed efficiency have been reported with CSB and CSB combined with xylo-oligosaccharides (XOS) in broilers [18, 20]. Supporting this, Zhang et al. [34] reported increased feed efficiency, although no change in the ADFI was detected in laying hens fed 800 mg/kg CSB. In contrast, Wu et al. [21] reported no significant growth response to protected SB in 21-day-old broilers, indicating that performance outcomes may depend on formulation, release profile, dietary composition, inclusion level, and intestinal maturity. These growth-promoting effects are therefore most likely mediated through improvements in intestinal function and nutrient utilization rather than a uniform response to all SB products.

Early age broilers are immunologically immature and highly susceptible to pathogens. Previous studies have shown that dietary SB improves immunity by stimulating the synthesis of immunoglobulin [35]. In our study, NBSB increased IL-10, IgA, and IgM levels while reducing TNF-α expression. These results align with Zou et al. [36], who reported IL-10 upregulation in broilers under DSS-induced stress. Wan et al. [37] similarly reported elevated anti-inflammatory cytokines and immunoglobulins in piglets supplemented with CSB, suggesting a conserved SCFA-mediated mechanism across species. Sodium butyrate may modulate immune responses through NF-κB signaling, enhancing anti-inflammatory effects and suppressing pro-inflammatory cytokine expression [38].

The small intestine plays a vital role in digestion and nutrient uptake. VH and CD reflect intestinal health, with higher VH: CD ratios suggesting better nutrient absorption [39]. In this study, NBSB decreased jejunal CD and increased ileal VH and VH: CD ratio. These improvements align with those of Zhao et al. [20], who reported that CSB enhanced VH and VH: CD in 21-day-old broilers. Similarly, Deng et al. [18] reported similar ileal but not jejunal effects with early CSB supplementation, suggesting that segmental response differences may depend on the dose and intestinal segment. Such improvements are biologically plausible, as butyrate promotes enterocyte proliferation and differentiation, inhibits apoptosis [40], and stimulates digestive enzyme secretion and glucagon-like peptide-2 (GLP-2) production, thereby supporting mucosal development and function [41, 42].

The intestinal barrier is maintained by tight junction proteins (ZO-1, occludin, and claudin-1) and mucin-2, which limit the translocation of luminal contents and support gut homeostasis [43, 44]. In the present study, NBSB upregulated the expression of these genes in the jejunum and ileum segments, which is consistent with findings in piglets and heat-stressed broilers supplemented with protected SB [19, 37]. Butyrate enhances tight junction integrity by activating AMPK [45] and improving cellular ATP levels [46]. As an HDAC inhibitor, it promotes the transcription of intestinal barrier-associated genes [47, 48]. These results support the association between NBSB supplementation and improved intestinal barrier function.

Oxidative stress impairs epithelial function and increases inflammatory responses in broilers [49]. Antioxidant enzymes such as SOD and T-AOC neutralize reactive oxygen species (ROS), while MDA indicates lipid peroxidation [50]. NBSB increased T-AOC and the T-AOC/MD ratio in the jejunum and the SOD and T-AOC/MDA ratios in the ileum, with a trend toward lower MDA levels. These results align with those of Deng et al. [18], who reported similar antioxidant improvements in 21-day-old broilers. Collectively, these findings are consistent with the known roles of butyrate in supporting epithelial metabolism, barrier integrity, immune regulation, and antioxidant defense through metabolic and epigenetic pathways.

SCFAs are key mediators of intestinal health in poultry [51]. In this study, NBSB increased AA and BA in the jejunum and cecum and PA and BA in the ileum, consistent with reports in piglets and 21-day-old broilers receiving protected SB [18]. These increases were associated with improved intestinal barrier and immune-related indicators, supporting a link between SCFA enrichment and gut health, although causality cannot be inferred from this study.

Microbial diversity supports gut homeostasis and immune competence in broilers [52]. In this study, NBSB supplementation increased the Shannon index, indicating higher microbial diversity, consistent with findings in coated SB supplemented ducks [53]. Although phylum-level differences were not significant, Firmicutes predominated in the NBSB group, reflecting its role in fiber fermentation and SCFA production [54]. At the genus level, NBSB increased the abundance of norank_f__Ruminococcaceae and Eubacterium_hallii_group, which are well-known SCFA-producing taxa associated with gut health in poultry and other species. These major butyrate producers enhance intestinal barrier integrity by upregulating tight junction proteins and stimulating mucin secretion in poultry models [55], suggesting a role in the improved barrier function observed in NBSB-supplemented broilers without implying direct causality.

Cecal microbiota play a vital role in broiler growth and gut health via SCFA production and modulation of intestinal immunity [56]. Spearman correlation analysis revealed associations between norank_f__Ruminococcaceae and higher expression of several tight junction genes and greater AA and BA contents. Romboutsia, Eubacterium_hallii_group, and Ruminococcus were also moderately positively correlated with tight junction genes and SCFAs content (Fig. 5). These genera are consistent with established SCFA producing taxa known to support intestinal integrity in chickens, pigs, and humans [57, 58]. The observed network of correlations is consistent with the hypothesis that NBSB enhances early growth, barrier function, antioxidant defense, and SCFA production in broilers in part through modulating the cecal microbiota. However, these relationships are associative, and causality cannot be inferred from correlation analysis alone. While NBSB appears to promote a microbiota profile compatible with improved gut function, future studies using fecal microbiota transplantation or germ-free models are required to confirm microbial mediation. In addition, the present trial was conducted under non-infectious conditions, which may limit extrapolation to disease-challenge settings. Furthermore, functional prediction of microbial communities (e.g., using PICRUSt2) should be considered in future research.

Conclusion

Our study showed that NBSB improved growth performance and intestinal health in young broilers under noninfectious conditions. These effects were reflected in enhanced intestinal morphology (VH and VH: CD ratio), improved barrier function (IL-10, ZO-1, claudin-1, and occludin expression), increased antioxidant capacity, and elevated SCFA (AA and BA) contents. In addition, NBSB supplementation modulated cecal microbiota by enriching SCFA-producing taxa, including Ruminococcus and Ruminococcaceae, which were positively correlated with SCFA contents and gut barrier gene expression. Overall, NBSB showed comparable effects to aureomycin on growth performance and intestinal function. These findings suggest that NBSB may serve as a potential alternative to antibiotics in healthy broilers; however, further studies under disease-challenge conditions are required to confirm its efficacy.
