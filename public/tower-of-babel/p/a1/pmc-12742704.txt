Endovascular treatment of patients concurrent with type 3A aortic syndrome and degeneration (infrarenal) abdominal aortic aneurysm
Authors: Shih-Ying Mei, Chun-Yang Huang, Chun-Che Shih, Chiao-Po Hsu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground:Advances in endovascular treatments have improved surgical outcomes for patients with aortic pathologies. However, for patients with comorbid aortic dissection and degenerative abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA), effective surgical treatment is often a challenge. Here, we analyzed the outcomes of endovascular treatment in patients who concurrently had acute DeBakey type 3A aortic syndrome and degenerative infrarenal AAA.Methods:From 2012 to 2019, 11 patients were diagnosed concurrently with acute type 3A aortic syndrome and degenerative infrarenal AAA that met intervention criteria (typical acute descending aortic dissection, or thickness of hematoma or ulceration greater than 10 mm in intramural hematomas [IMH] or penetrating aortic ulcers [PAU], in addition to AAA diameter >5 cm). Three patients had a typical dissection, three had IMH, and five had PAU.Results:Four men underwent a one-stage operation, and preoperative cerebrospinal fluid lumbar drainage was instituted in three patients. The other seven patients underwent a two-stage operation consisting of endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) followed by thoracic endovascular aortic repair (TEVAR) over a period ranging from 3 to 52 months. Follow-up continued until the end of 2022. No spinal cord ischemia (SCI) was present in either group. In the one-stage group, one patient died of intracranial hemorrhage 1.5 months after the operation. The other three patients did not experience any aortic event requiring reintervention. In the two-stage group, four patients required reintervention, including one type I endoleak, 2 type II endoleaks, and one visceral artery stent compromise. In this group, four patients died during follow-up.Conclusion:Mid-term outcomes were acceptable in patients with concurrent acute type 3A aortic syndrome and degenerative infra-renal AAA, managed with EVAR and TEVAR, both simultaneously and sequentially. The incidence of SCI was low, and aortic coverage spared the segment from T8 to L1.Keywords: Degeneration (infrarenal) aortic aneurysm, Endovascular treatment, Type 3A aortic syndrome

1. INTRODUCTION

Aortic dissection and aneurysm are major pathological conditions that share risk factors such as systemic hypertension and atherosclerosis.1 Because aortic dissection and aneurysm are fairly prevalent conditions, one can reasonably assume that they can occur concurrently in the same patient. Hence, patients may frequently be diagnosed with a mild abdominal aortic dilation (aneurysmal change) or ascending aortic dilation in addition to an acute descending aortic dissection. However, cases of concurrent aortic dissection and aneurysm that both warrant surgical intervention are infrequently observed in clinical practice.

The prevalence of degenerative abdominal aortic aneurysm (AAA; aneurysms >5 cm in diameter) among patients with acute aortic dissection has been estimated to be approximately 5% to 7%.2,3 However, a recent report revealed the incidence to be as low as 0.3% (15 cases of 4292 descending thoracic and abdominal aortic interventions).4 This thus raises the question regarding which of these estimates is more reliable or acceptable. Furthermore, many reports have not clearly distinguished between cases of aortic dissection coexisting with degenerative AAA and cases of aortic dissection extending to the infrarenal aorta and subsequently leading to aneurysm formation. This lack of distinction might create uncertainty regarding the incidence and treatment of the condition.5,6 Nevertheless, comorbid aortic dissection with degenerative aneurysm or aortic dissection with chronic aneurysm formation appears to increase the risk of aortic rupture.2,3

Currently, clinical practice guidelines and the literature lack a standard treatment for cases of comorbid aortic dissection and aneurysm that are both indicated for surgery. Before the prominence of stent grafts, single-stage open repair (thoracoabdominal incision) was an adequate but challenging approach for both surgeons and patients. Clinical data regarding the risks of infrarenal AAA graft repair in patients with aortic dissection or aortic dissection repair in patients with infrarenal AAA remain scant. When AAA is repaired before thoracic aortic dissection, the dissection may extend to the abdominal aorta and terminate at the suture line of a previously repaired AAA graft, although the outcomes of this approach are unpredictable. Previous studies have indicated that this approach decreases the likelihood of rupture but increases that of malperfusion due to false lumen expansion above the suture line.3,7–9

With the popularization of stent grafts, both open repair and endovascular aneurysm repair (EVAR) have become class I recommendations for patients with large nonruptured infrarenal AAA, given acceptable surgical risk levels and anatomy. EVAR is preferred to open repair because it is simpler and less invasive, as is thoracic EVAR (TEVAR) in thoracic pathologies. TEVAR is a class I recommendation for complicated acute type B aortic dissection and class IIa or IIb recommendation for uncomplicated acute type B aortic dissection.1,10

However, treatment for comorbid aortic dissection and degenerative infrarenal AAA remains challenging for surgeons given the number of available options, including one-stage, two-stage, and hybrid procedures (ie, stent grafts combined with open repair). Similar technical considerations of multi-segment endovascular coverage have been reported in other vascular territories.11

The risk of spinal cord ischemia (SCI) is a critical concern. SCI risk after longer aortic coverage has been reported to be as high as 7% to 14% for patients with previous or simultaneous abdominal aortic repair. However, few studies have compared this risk between simultaneous and sequential treatments.5,12 Another concern is aortic transformation between stages in two-stage procedures. When EVAR is performed first, the upper landing zone of the stent graft may be compromised and cause aortic rupture (without suture line), or the stent graft may collapse in cases of dissection progression.13–15 Proximal stent grafts may also cause new dissections to merge with suprarenal dissections.6

To address the aforementioned uncertainties and gaps in research and practice, this study reviewed the endovascular outcomes of patients who presented with comorbid acute DeBakey type 3A aortic syndrome (dissection limited to the descending thoracic aorta) and enlarged AAA (>5 cm in diameter). Patients who underwent DeBakey type 1 or 2 aortic dissection were excluded because current guidelines recommend open surgery in such cases.1 Patients with descending aortic dissection extending to the infrarenal aorta with aneurysm formation (usually classified as chronic DeBakey type 3B) were also excluded because such comorbid conditions fall outside the scope of our research objectives and because open surgery remains the first-choice treatment for post dissection aortic aneurysm.16

2. METHODS

2.1. Population

This retrospective observational study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of Taipei Veterans General Hospital (approval number: 2022-02-011CC).

Patients were included if they had comorbid acute DeBakey type 3A aortic syndrome, including typical dissection, intramural hematoma (IMH), or penetrating aortic ulcer (PAU) along with degenerative infrarenal AAA (AAA diameter >5 cm). Acute aortic syndrome was defined as a symptomatic presentation with imaging evidence of either typical dissection or a hematoma or ulceration thickness of >10 mm in IMH or PAU cases. Patients whose acute aortic syndrome developed after EVAR were excluded, allowing us to focus on the postintervention behavior of DeBakey type 3A aortic syndrome.

Beta blockers were prescribed for all patients as the first-line medication. Other antihypertensive medications were prescribed by the surgeon in charge to reduce the risk of disease progression.

The estimated glomerular filtration rate (eGFR, in mL/min) was calculated using the Cockcroft-Gault equation: eGFR = (140 − age) × (weight, kg) × (0.85 [if female])/(72 × Cr, mg/dL).17

Body mass index was calculated as weight in kilograms divided by height in meters squared (kg/m2).18 Coronary artery disease was defined as a history of percutaneous coronary intervention or coronary artery bypass graft, and cerebral vascular accident was diagnosed on the basis of neurologist assessments or imaging findings documented in medical records.

2.2. Surgical techniques

Because of the lack of established clinical guidelines, the surgeon in charge decided whether to apply a one-stage or two-stage approach on the basis of the specificities of the patient’s condition. Preemptive lumbar cerebrospinal fluid drainage was performed before TEVAR when SCI was a concern.

During TEVAR procedures, after bilateral femoral arteries were sheathed and heparin was administered intravenously, a thoracic aortogram was performed using angiocatheters to confirm true lumen. A stiff wire was advanced to the ascending aorta, and the thoracic stent graft (Zenith TX2, Thoracic Endoprosthesis, Cook, Bloomington, IN; Gore TAG, Thoracic Endoprosthesis, W.L. Gore & Associates, Flagstaff, AZ; or Valiant Captivia, Medtronic Vascular, Santa Rosa, CA) was delivered retrogradely through the femoral artery. The size of the proximal stent graft was selected on the basis of the maximal diameter of the proximal healthy aorta with 10% to 15% oversizing. When more than one piece of stent graft was required, a “distal-first” technique was applied to prevent new entry induced by the distal stent graft.19 The stent graft covered the proximal aortic entry tear, blocking flow into the false lumen and ulceration. Coda balloon angioplasty of the proximal and/or distal landing zones was performed to ensure stent graft stability after deployment. Final aortography was performed to identify possible endoleaks or false lumen flow.

For EVAR procedures, abdominal aortography was first performed. EVAR (Zenith Endograft, Cook Medical, Bloomington, IN; Gore Excluder, W.L. Gore & Associates; or Medtronic Endurant, Santa Rosa, CA) was then performed before TEVAR through a one-stage approach. The main body of the stent graft was delivered retrogradely through the femoral artery. The proximal part of the stent graft and ipsilateral limb were deployed partially. The contralateral limb was cannulated from the contralateral femoral sheath. The limb stent graft was delivered and deployed at the contralateral limb. The main body and ipsilateral limb were deployed fully with or without extension with a limb stent graft. Coda balloon angioplasty was applied at the proximal and bilateral distal segments and those overlapping the stent graft to reinforce the landing zone and prevent endoleak. Final aortography was performed to identify possible endoleaks and confirm stent graft position.

2.3. Adjunctive procedures

A vascular bypass (subclavian-subclavian or carotid-subclavian) or “off-the-shelf” technique (eg, chimney, periscope, in situ fenestration, or sandwich) was performed before TEVAR or EVAR when the TEVAR stent graft landed in zone 1 or 2 or when EVAR lacked an adequate landing zone.20 For one patient, a sandwich technique was performed by using parallel grafts to the superior mesenteric artery and celiac trunk during TEVAR. To apply these off-the-shelf techniques, a covered stent was used with a Viabahn (W.L. Gore & Associates) or an Advanta V12 graft (Atrium Medical, Hudson, NH). Endoanchor (Medtronic Vascular) was used to fix the proximal end of the stent graft in zone 3 in one patient. In another patient, an aortic bare metal stent (Zenith Dissection Endovascular Stent, Cook Medical) was extended from the distal end of the TEVAR stent graft to the EVAR main body.

To prevent type II endoleak, coil embolization was performed after TEVAR, following bypass of the left subclavian artery (or of the internal iliac artery if the common iliac artery length was inadequate as a landing zone for EVAR stent graft deployment).

2.4. Aortic assessment and follow-up

Major vascular complications were recorded. Patients were followed regularly and subjected to computed tomography angiography (CTA) surveillance after discharge when possible. CTA was performed using a Hi-Speed Advantage scanner (GE Healthcare, Waukesha, WI) with a collimation of 5 mm, table speed of 10 mm/s, pitch of 2.0, and tube voltage of 120 kV (tube current: 230-250 mA). CTA surveillance was scheduled at 1 month, 6 months, and 1 year after surgery to monitor disease progression.

2.5. Statistical analysis

Continuous aortic remodeling variables are expressed as mean ± SD; these variables were compared between groups by using a Mann-Whitney U test. A p value of <0.05 was considered significant. All analyses were performed using SPSS statistical software (version 29.0; IBM-SPSS, Armonk, NY).

3. RESULTS

During the study period, 239 TEVAR interventions were performed for DeBakey type 3 aortic syndrome, 192 TEVAR interventions for degenerative thoracic aneurysm, and 510 EVAR interventions for infrarenal AAA. Among patients who were treated with these procedures, 11 were identified as having comorbid acute DeBakey type 3A aortic syndrome and degenerative infrarenal AAA. These patients received treatment between January 2012 and January 2019. The end date of follow-up was December 31, 2022.

Among the patients with type 3A aortic syndrome, three had typical dissection, three had IMH, and five had PAU. Four patients had significant coronary artery disease following either coronary bypass (one patient) or stent implantation (three patients), and two patients had a history of cerebral vascular accident. Seven patients had an eGFR value of <60 mL/min (chronic kidney disease more advanced than stage 3). In the one-stage group, all patients received preemptive lumbar drainage except patient 2. The coverage of the aorta ranged from 25.7 to 33 cm. No patient had perioperative SCI. Postoperative acute kidney injury was noted in patient 1 and improved after adequate hydration. However, this patient died of intracranial hemorrhage 1.5 months after surgery. The other three patients remained well without further reintervention for vascular events until the end of follow-up. Type II endoleak without AAA sac enlargement was noted in patient 4.

In the two-stage group, no complications developed within 1 month after initial EVAR. The coverage of the aorta was 11.2 to 20.2 cm. Aortic events were noted in three patients before subsequent TEVAR. These events were an endoleak with an enlarged sac after EVAR in patients 5 and 10 and stent graft migration due to dissection progression from 3A to 3B in patient 6. In patient 5, embolization of the inferior mesenteric artery was performed for a type II endoleak 25 months after EVAR; nevertheless, the aneurysm sac continued to enlarge. The diameter of the thoracic dissection aneurysm increased from an initial 49 to 54 mm in patient 5. This patient received TEVAR 51 months after the initial EVAR procedure. However, due to an unexpected intraoperative complication (an iliac artery rupture), the patient’s enlarged AAA sac was not managed continually after the TEVAR procedure, and the patient died of pneumonia 1 month after surgery. Patient 10 was lost to follow-up for 4 years after the initial EVAR. She returned to the hospital with intermittent back pain; an imaging study revealed an endoleak of undetermined type along with an enlarged AAA sac and an increase in PAU thickness from 8 to 16 mm. TEVAR and an extension cuff for EVAR were applied; however, persistent enlargement of the AAA sac to 10.5 cm was noted 1 year later. Finally, open repair was performed, and the patient was discharged uneventfully 2 weeks after the operation. The type II endoleak was confirmed intraoperatively. The patient died of pneumonia 4 months after surgery. In patient 6, endovascular therapy was performed 51 months after the initial EVAR. Preemptive lumbar drainage was performed, followed by TEVAR. Additionally, an extension cuff for EVAR and a parallel stent graft (periscope) were applied to the right kidney for a type Ia endoleak complicated by EVAR migration. However, the type Ia endoleak persisted after extension cuff placement. Because of the patient’s advanced age, his family refused open repair or further treatment, and the patient died 4 months later. In patient 9, the stent graft to the superior mesenteric artery collapsed 2 months after TEVAR, and the patient received reintervention. The patient died of ischemic heart failure 2 months later.

After TEVAR, the cumulative aortic coverage in the two-stage group was 23.4 to 50.4 cm. No patient experienced SCI after TEVAR. Patient 6 experienced postoperative acute kidney injury and demonstrated improved renal function after 1 week. Bilateral internal iliac arteries were patent after both EVAR and TEVAR procedures in all patients, except for patient 2, in whom embolization of the right internal iliac artery was performed during EVAR. The abdominal aneurysm sac exhibited acceptable remodeling (without increased aneurysm diameter) in three patients in the one-stage group and four patients in the two-stage group after EVAR. No significant difference in abdominal aortic remodeling was observed between the groups. Aggravated thoracic aortic dissection was noted only in patient 6; all other patients exhibited improved or unchanged IMH resolution or thrombosis of aortic ulceration. No significant difference in thoracic aortic remodeling was observed between the one-stage and two-stage groups.

4. DISCUSSION

Although comorbid aortic dissection with atherosclerosis or degenerative aneurysm has been reported, its exact prevalence remains unknown. One study estimated that 7% of aortic dissection cases are comorbid with degenerative AAA,2 whereas 5% of degenerative AAA cases are comorbid with aortic dissection3; both proportions are higher than those observed in our sample. Given that both aneurysm and aortic dissection were diagnosed through surgery or autopsy in the aforementioned study, both diagnoses met the criteria for operation, potentially producing selection bias causing the researchers to overestimate the prevalence of comorbid conditions. Patients without operative indications were excluded from these statistics. By comparison, Gomes and Wallace21 observed aortic dissection in only 1 of their 200 patients with AAA when analyzing computed tomography findings obtained over 7 years. However, the prevalence of this comorbidity is likely >0.5% because of population aging and the popularization and improved diagnostic ability of computed tomography and magnetic resonance imaging since their study. Moreover, IMH and PAU are often overlooked but are frequently comorbid with aortic dissection. In our clinical practice, patients with these pathologies who meet operative indications are rare. In our study, only 11 patients with comorbid acute DeBakey type 3A aortic syndrome and degenerative infrarenal AAA met intervention criteria during an 8-year follow-up. These patients accounted for approximately 4.6% (11/239) of patients who received operations for DeBakey type 3 aortic syndrome, 2.2% (11/510) of those who received operations for AAA, and 0.2% ([11 × 2] − 4]/[239 + 192 + 510]) of those who received aortic interventions performed for descending thoracic and abdominal aortic diseases. Bellosta et al4 reported an incidence of 0.3% within the total number of aortic interventions in their study, reflecting our data and suggesting that intervention with both conditions is rare. The inclusion of patients not meeting operative indications would lower the prevalence.

In the study by Bellosta et al,4 the pathologies of the 15 cases of concurrent type B aortic dissection and AAA, treated with either EVAR and TEVAR or abdominal aorta open repair, were largely similar to those of the patients in our study. However, the majority of their patients (12 of 15) presented with DeBakey type 3B extended aortic dissection, in which the abdominal aneurysm results from dissection rather than atherosclerosis or degeneration. This condition was not considered in our study. Because endoleaks often develop through fenestration near the visceral arteries and may require further intervention after endovascular therapy, conventional open repair of the portion dissecting the abdominal aneurysm is preferable to other endovascular methods.4,22 According to our review of the literature, no relevant report has clearly distinguished between these two conditions (limited 3A aortic dissection with degenerative AAA vs extended 3B aortic dissection with aneurysm formation). Notably, our one- and two-stage approach yielded acceptable and similar results for these comorbidities, although reintervention was often necessary.23

A key treatment challenge is the decision to use a one- or a two-stage approach. In our study, this decision was based on the judgment and experience of the surgeon. Given the concern that longer aortic coverage or replacement in one-stage repair may cause SCI and devastating paraplegia, more patients in our sample received a two-stage approach. In one meta-analysis, the estimated incidence of SCI was 4% to 5% after endovascular repair of the descending thoracic or thoracoabdominal aorta.24 The length of aortic coverage is often used as a predictor of SCI. However, Feezor et al25 and Piffaretti et al5 have reported that the distance of the uncovered aorta proximal to the celiac artery is a stronger predictor of SCI. The origin of the celiac trunk (T12 and L1) is close to that of the Adamkiewicz artery (AKA). The AKA is the primary supplier of blood to the lower two-thirds of the spinal cord and provides collateral circulation to the intercostal and lumbar arteries. In a meta-analysis, Taterra et al26 investigated the anatomical characteristics of AKA, observing that the AKA was present in a majority (84.6%) of the population and usually originated between T8 and L1 (89%). In our study, the segment between T8 and L1 was covered only in patient 9. In all other patients, a native aortic segment was present between TEVAR and EVAR. This may explain why these patients did not experience SCI. In our unpublished results, SCI developed in 33% (4/12) of patients who received T-branch implantation for thoracoabdominal aneurysm; the mean aortic coverage was 33.45 ± 6.78 cm, and 19 of 24 internal iliac arteries and all left subclavian arteries remained patent.

Feezor et al25 also investigated the roles of the internal iliac and subclavian arteries in SCI risk. The spinal cord is thought to be perfused by an extensive collateral network. The “collateral network concept” is composed of an interconnecting complex of vessels in the intraspinal, paraspinous, and epidural spaces and in the paravertebral muscles, involving the intercostal and lumbar segmental arteries as well as the subclavian and internal iliac arteries.27,28 The collateral network concept is supported by animal models.29,30 This concept suggests that preoperative lumbar drainage may not effectively prevent SCI. Given these findings, stent grafting of the descending thoracic aorta and infrarenal aorta that spares the T8 to L1 segments during one-stage procedures in cases of patent bilateral subclavian and internal iliac arteries may correspond to a lower incidence of SCI. However, additional randomized controlled trials are warranted to confirm this association.

Compared with selective lumbar drainage, routine prophylactic lumbar drainage has been associated with a lower rate of postoperative SCI after TEVAR for thoracoabdominal aortic aneurysms but not for isolated thoracic aortic aneurysms or aortic dissection.23 We considered selective lumbar drainage for patients at high risk of developing SCI, including those with left subclavian or internal iliac artery coverage, thoracic aortic coverage of >20 cm, or a history of abdominal aortic repair. According to the criteria of a history of abdominal aortic repair (for second-stage TEVAR) and aortic coverage >20 cm, preoperative lumbar drainage was conducted for all patients in our sample. No patient in our sample experienced SCI, highlighting the key role of patent subclavian and internal iliac arteries and stent graft coverage involving T8 to L1.

Midterm aortic remodeling also yielded acceptable outcomes in both the thoracic and abdominal segments in our high-risk patients. Our results suggest that patients without aneurysmal sac shrinking following EVAR should be closely monitored for endoleaks.31 Type II endoleaks constituted the most common indication for reintervention following EVAR. Persistent type II endoleaks may be associated with inferior sac regression, and early open repair should be considered if reintervention with endovascular repair is unsuccessful.32 Long-term follow-up is necessary for these high-risk patients.33,34

This study has two major limitations. First, the small sample size may limit the generalizability of our findings and robustness of our analyses. Second, the decision to perform a one- or two-stage approach and the decision regarding the duration between both stages and between adjunctive procedures were based on the surgeon’s judgment, the specificities of the patient’s condition, and patient preference, information that is unobtainable retrospectively.

In conclusion, comorbid acute dissection and atherosclerotic aneurysm may increase the risk of aortic rupture. This complex condition warrants aggressive surveillance and treatment. For patients with DeBakey type 3A aortic syndrome and degenerative infrarenal AAA, acceptable management can include either simultaneous or sequential EVAR and TEVAR, with a lower incidence of SCI achievable when the T8 to L1 segments are spared in patients with patent bilateral subclavian and internal iliac arteries. We observed acceptable midterm outcomes in our sample. However, subsequent studies with larger sample sizes and longer follow-up are necessary to determine which approach is best suited for patients with comorbid acute dissection and atherosclerotic aneurysm.
