Diabetes‐Related Distress in Spanish Adults: Clinical, Sociodemographic and Psychosocial Correlates
Authors: Miguel Garrido‐Bueno, Rocío Romero‐Castillo, Ana Cortés‐Lerena, Manuel Pabón‐Carrasco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ABSTRACTIntroductionDiabetes‐related distress refers to the emotional strain experienced by individuals who live with diabetes and face continuous demands of treatment and self‐management. Elevated distress is associated with poor adherence, impaired metabolic control, and diminished quality of life. This study aimed to describe diabetes‐related distress among Spanish adults, analyse its associations with sociodemographic and clinical variables, identify predictors of higher distress, and define patient profiles with distinct psychosocial patterns.MethodsA cross‐sectional analytical study was conducted with 201 adults (n = 135 type 1, n = 66 type 2 diabetes) from two public healthcare centers in Seville, Spain. Participants completed the Spanish version of the 17‐item Diabetes Distress Scale and a questionnaire covering demographic and clinical variables. Statistical analyses included non‐parametric tests, logistic regression, cluster analysis, and discriminant analysis to identify predictive factors and patient groupings.ResultsAmong participants, 39.3% presented low or no distress, 22.4% moderate distress, and 38.3% high distress, with an average score of 2.72. Higher distress was significantly associated with younger age, type 2 diabetes, and combined pharmacological treatment. Diabetes type was the strongest predictor of distress. Cluster analysis revealed three profiles: older adults with type 2 diabetes and low distress, middle‐aged adults with type 1 diabetes and moderate distress, and younger adults with type 1 diabetes and high distress.ConclusionsDiabetes‐related distress is a heterogeneous phenomenon influenced by age, diabetes type, and treatment regimen. Regular assessment and individualised psychoeducational and emotional interventions may improve psychological well‐being and adherence, particularly among younger adults and those under complex therapies.

Introduction

Diabetes mellitus is one of the most relevant public health challenges worldwide due to its high prevalence and the considerable impact it has on both the physical health and psychological well‐being of those affected [1]. The daily management of diabetes requires continuous effort to maintain glycaemic metabolic control, adhere to pharmacological treatment, and adopt healthy lifestyle behaviours [2]. These demands, together with the potential development of chronic complications, may generate a substantial emotional burden known as diabetes‐related distress. This construct refers to the specific emotional strain resulting from living with diabetes and from the perceived difficulties associated with its treatment, interactions with healthcare professionals, or the level of social and family support available [3, 4].

Evidence indicates that high levels of diabetes‐related distress are linked to poorer glycaemic control, lower treatment adherence, and diminished quality of life [5]. Unlike general affective disorders, diabetes distress represents a specific and dynamic phenomenon that may vary depending on diabetes type, duration of the disease, treatment regimen, and sociocultural context [6]. Consequently, its systematic assessment has become an essential component of comprehensive diabetes care.

In recent years, several instruments have been developed and validated to measure diabetes‐related distress and psychological burden [4, 7, 8]. Among them, the Diabetes Distress Scale (DDS) has gained prominence due to its broad use and robust psychometric properties [4]. Most existing research has focused on the psychometric validation of the instrument [9, 10], paying limited attention to the characterisation of clinical and psychosocial profiles across different levels of distress.

Identifying patient profiles that differ in their patterns and intensity of diabetes distress can provide valuable insights for clinical practice. Such knowledge allows for the development of intervention strategies that are more closely aligned with the actual needs of each group [11]. Understanding which combinations of clinical, sociodemographic, or emotional factors are associated with greater distress facilitates more personalised care, more effective follow‐up, and the integration of psychoeducational and emotional support components within diabetes management programs [12].

In this context, the present study aimed to describe diabetes‐related distress in a sample of Spanish adults with diabetes, to examine its associations with sociodemographic and clinical variables, to identify predictors of higher distress levels, and to establish differentiated patient profiles based on shared clinical and psychosocial characteristics. This approach seeks to provide a more precise understanding of diabetes‐related distress in the Spanish context and to inform future interventions directed towards the early detection and management of emotional distress among individuals living with diabetes.

Materials and Methods

Study Design

This was an observational, cross‐sectional, and analytical study that aimed to assess diabetes‐related distress in adults with diabetes in Spain. It followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines [13].

The study was conducted in Seville, a capital city in Andalusia, a region in the south of Spain. It was carried out in two facilities within the Public Andalusian Health System: the Endocrinology and Nutrition Unit of the Virgen del Rocío University Hospital Diagnostic and Treatment Center, and the San Luis Community Health Center.

The Endocrinology and Nutrition Unit is a specialised hospital service dedicated to the prevention, diagnosis, and treatment of endocrine and nutritional diseases, with a particular focus on people with type 1 and type 2 diabetes referred from primary care. It provides patient care based on evidence‐based clinical protocols, educational interventions, and continuous quality improvement. The Unit is accredited at an excellent level of quality and serves as a national and European reference centre for complex endocrine disorders [14].

The San Luis Community Health Center is a primary care facility that holds quality accreditation from the Andalusian Healthcare Quality Agency. Its healthcare model is oriented towards comprehensive and equitable care, with a specific emphasis on chronic disease management, including diabetes. Clinical practice is based on integrated care pathways, preventive strategies, and programs that promote educational interventions [15].

This study followed the ethical standards in the Declaration of Helsinki, the European General Data Protection Regulation, and the Spanish Organic Law 3/2018 on the Protection of Personal Data and Guarantee of Digital Rights [16, 17, 18]. It was approved by the Virgen del Rocio/Virgen Macarena Research Ethics Committee (ID: MGB‐2025‐1), and was registered in the Open Science Framework (https://doi.org/10.17605/OSF.IO/8KUJW).

Participants

The sample was selected with a theoretical sampling guided by eligibility criteria. Inclusion criteria required individuals to have a confirmed type 1 or type 2 diabetes mellitus diagnosis, to be over 18 years old, and to have geographic and temporal availability for the study. Exclusion criteria consisted of having disabling acute or chronic diseases, a severe mental illness, or difficulty speaking and/or comprehending Spanish. In‐person recruitment occurred between July and September 2025. The recruitment sites were the Diabetes Day Hospital, the Patient Education Classroom, and/or the consultation rooms during scheduled appointments with medical and nursing staff at the previously mentioned facilities.

The required sample size was based on the reported prevalence of diabetes mellitus in the adult Spanish population, which is estimated to be 13.8% [19]. The statistical parameters used for this calculation included a 95% confidence level (α = 0.05), an acceptable estimation error of 5%, and a statistical power of 80% (1‐β = 0.80). These parameters set a minimum required sample size of 183 subjects, which was increased to 200 to accommodate potential subject loss.

Data Collection

The Spanish version of the Diabetes Distress Scale (DDS) was administered to person from July to September 2025 at the same places as the recruitment.

The original version of the DDS consists of 17 items rated on a six‐point Likert scale, structured in four subscales. It showed high internal consistency (Cronbach's α ranging 0.87–0.94) and three cut‐off points [4]. The Spanish version of the DDS consists of 17 items rated on a six‐point Likert scale (1 = no problem to 6 = a very serious problem) designed to assess the emotional burden associated with living with diabetes in Spain. The instrument preserves the original four‐dimensional structure of the DDS subscales (DDS SS), comprising Emotional Burden (DDS SS1), Physician‐related Distress (DDS SS2), Regimen‐related Distress (DDS SS3), and Interpersonal Distress (DDS SS4). It demonstrated high internal consistency (Cronbach's α values ranging from 0.881 to 0.952), and retained the original cutoff points: < 2.0 indicating little or no distress, 2.0–2.9 indicating moderate distress, and ≥ 3.0 indicating high distress [20].

Sociodemographic and clinical data were also collected. These included: age, gender (male, female), type of diabetes (type 1 (T1DM), type 2 diabetes mellitus (T2DM)), age at diagnosis, treatment regimen (insulin, oral medication, or both), comorbidities, and treatment centre (Diagnostic and Treatment Centre, or Community Health Centre).

The Spanish version of the DDS, and the sociodemographic and clinical data‐related questions required up to 5 minutes for completion. Data was collected by the authors who then exported the complete dataset into Microsoft Excel version 2508 for subsequent analysis.

Data Analysis

Descriptive statistics were used to summarise participant characteristics. Means, standard deviations, minima, maxima, and percentiles were calculated for continuous variables, while categorical variables were summarised with frequencies and percentages.

Differences in age, years since diagnosis, and age at diagnosis across distress levels were examined with the Kruskal–Wallis test, and effect sizes were estimated with η2 coefficients [21]. The magnitude of the effects was interpreted according to conventional benchmarks: values below 0.01 were considered negligible or null, values around 0.01–0.06 small, 0.06–0.14 medium, 0.14–0.30 large, and above 0.30 very large [22]. Post hoc comparisons were carried out using Dunn's test with Bonferroni correction [23]. Associations between distress level and categorical variables such as gender, diabetes type, treatment, comorbidities, and healthcare centre were assessed using Pearson's chi‐square test, together with Phi, Cramer's V, and Cohen's Kappa coefficients as measures of association [24].

A multivariate ordinal logistic regression with a cumulative logit link function was then performed to identify predictors of distress level. Model adequacy was evaluated using Pearson and deviance chi‐square tests, and model performance was summarised through Cox and Snell, Nagelkerke, and McFadden pseudo‐R2 values [25]. Multicollinearity among independent variables was assessed using the Variance Inflation Factor. All values ranged between 1.01 and 1.37, indicating no relevant multicollinearity among predictors [26].

To identify homogeneous patient subgroups, a hierarchical cluster analysis based on Ward's method and squared Euclidean distance was followed by a k‐means cluster analysis specifying three clusters according to the agglomeration coefficient change [27]. Differences between clusters were tested through one‐way ANOVA and Welch tests with Bonferroni adjustments. Homogeneity of variances was examined with Levene's test, and the robustness of mean comparisons was verified with Welch's test. Effect sizes were calculated using η2, ε2, and ω2 coefficients [28]. A canonical discriminant analysis was subsequently used to validate the cluster solution and determine the variables that best discriminated among groups, reporting eigenvalues, canonical correlations, Wilks' lambda, and Fisher's discriminant functions [29].

All analyses were conducted using Microsoft Excel version 2508 and IBM SPSS Statistics version 29.0 with a significance level of p < 0.05.

Results

Sample Characteristics

The study invited n = 211 people with diabetes. Of these, a total of n = 201 provided completed responses; the remaining n = 10 individuals declined to participate.

Gender was evenly distributed across participants, with 50.2% (n = 101) men and 49.8% (n = 100) women. Their mean age was 51.4 years (SD = 15.0, range = 17–86). Most participants had type 1 diabetes (n = 135; 67.2%) and were treated with insulin alone (n = 148; 73.6%). The mean duration of diabetes was 18.05 years (SD = 13.22, range = 0–65) (Table 1).

The clusters comprised 58, 86, and 57 participants, respectively (Supporting Information S1 and S2) (Figure 1). Cluster 1 included older participants (M = 67.05, SD = 6.25) mainly with T2DM and oral treatment. Cluster 2 included middle‐aged participants (M = 46.94, SD = 5.49) with T1DM and insulin treatment. Levene's test confirmed homogeneity of variances for age (p = 0.737) but not for DDS total scores (p = 0.026) (Supporting Information S3). ANOVA indicated significant differences in age (F(2,198) = 729.97, p < 0.001, η 2 = 0.881) and DDS total score (F(2,198) = 5.919, p = 0.003, η 2 = 0.056). Bonferroni‐corrected comparisons showed that Cluster 1 had significantly lower distress than Clusters 2 and 3 (p = 0.005 and p = 0.018, respectively) (Supporting Information S4). Chi‐square analyses of cluster membership confirmed significant differences by diabetes type (χ 2 = 68.091, p < 0.001, Φ = 0.582) and treatment (χ 2 = 30.406, p < 0.001, Φ = 0.389), but not by gender (χ 2 = 0.819, p = 0.664, Φ = 0.064) (Supporting Information S5).

Canonical discriminant analysis validated the three‐cluster solution (Wilks' λ = 0.111, χ 2 = 430.608, p < 0.001). Two discriminant functions were extracted, explaining 99.10% and 0.90% of between‐group variance, with canonical correlations of 0.939 and 0.250 respectively. The first function was primarily associated with age (standardized coefficient = 1.002, structure loading = 0.996), while the second was defined mainly by type of diabetes (−0.883) and DDS total score (0.555). Cluster centroids were 3.552, −0.007, and −3.604 for the first function, which indicated a separation of the three profiles (Supporting Information S6).

Discussion

Overall Patterns of Diabetes Distress

This study examined diabetes‐related distress in a sample of Spanish adults with the aim of describing its magnitude, associated factors, and clinical and sociodemographic profiles linked to different levels of distress. The results showed that 39.3% of participants reported low or no distress, 22.4% reported moderate distress, and 38.3% reported high distress, with a total mean score of 2.72 (SD = 1.34). These findings indicate that more than half of the participants (60.7%) experienced a meaningful degree of emotional burden, which places this sample in the same levels observed in other studies, where 66.7% of people with major depressive disorder also had moderate or high diabetes distress [30].

Differences by age were significant (p = 0.036). Participants with higher distress were younger, with an average age close to 40 years, whereas those with lower distress were generally over 60. In contrast, neither diabetes duration (mean = 18.05 years; p = 0.672) nor age at diagnosis (mean = 28.85 years; p = 0.068) showed a relationship with distress level. This pattern suggests that age exerts a greater influence than disease duration, possibly due to the challenges of self‐management during life stages that involve heavier work and social demands [31].

Clinical and Sociodemographic Correlates of Diabetes Distress

At the clinical level, significant differences were observed according to diabetes type (p = 0.017). Although most participants had type 1 diabetes (n = 135; 67.2%), those with T2DM (n = 66; 32.8%) exhibited a higher proportion of elevated distress. This finding is consistent with previous research indicating that T2DM tends to experience higher levels of certain domains of diabetes‐related distress, such as emotional burden and regimen‐related distress, compared with those with T1DM [32]. However, Fisher et al. documented a high prevalence of elevated diabetes‐related distress among adults with T1DM. The discrepancy between these findings and those of the present study, as well as the higher prevalence reported in the Behavioural Diabetes Institute infographic (74% in T1DM vs. 62% in T2DM), may be attributable to differences in study populations, age distribution, measurement instruments, healthcare organisation, and sociocultural context [33, 34].

Differences also emerged according to pharmacological treatment (p = 0.035). Participants undergoing combination therapy (n = 40; 19.9%) reported the highest distress scores, followed by those using oral medication (n = 13; 6.5%), whereas those treated exclusively with insulin (n = 148; 73.6%) showed the lowest levels of distress. These differences should be interpreted considering that treatment categories reflect different clinical profiles across diabetes types, as insulin therapy represents standard management in T1DM, while combination therapy is more frequently used in T2DM and is commonly associated with greater clinical complexity [35]. Gender (p = 0.083), the presence of comorbidities (p = 0.214), and healthcare centre (p = 0.935) were not associated with distress, unlike another study in which gender had a significant association with diabetes distress [36].

However, although insulin‐treated participants showed lower overall distress levels, the cluster analysis revealed that younger participants with T1DM receiving insulin therapy presented high distress levels. This suggests that age and clinical profile modulate the relationship between pharmacological treatment and diabetes‐related distress, in line with previous studies showing that diabetes‐related distress is influenced by clinical and sociodemographic characteristics rather than by treatment modality alone [37].

Predictive and Profile‐Based Interpretation of Diabetes Distress

Type of diabetes showed the strongest capacity to predict distress levels. Individuals with type 2 diabetes were more likely to experience high distress (B = 0.703; p = 0.044), whereas those receiving oral treatment were less likely to do so compared with individuals on combination therapy (B = −1.667; p = 0.022). This pattern may be explained by the progressive nature of T2DM and the psychological burden inherent to diabetes management, where greater treatment complexity and the threat of complications contribute to emotional distress and feelings of reduced control [38]. Although the overall explanatory power of the model was moderate, its fit was adequate, supporting the consistency of the findings.

Three distinct patient profiles were identified. The first group (n = 58; 28.9%) comprised older participants (mean = 67.05 years), predominantly with T2DM (74.1%) and oral or combined treatment (86.2%), who reported the lowest distress (mean = 2.22). The second group (n = 86; 42.8%) included middle‐aged adults (mean = 46.94 years), mainly with T1DM (76.7%) and insulin treatment (75.6%) and intermediate distress levels (mean = 2.94). The third group (n = 57; 28.4%) consisted of younger participants (mean = 26.33 years), mostly with T1DM (94.7%) and insulin therapy (94.7%), who exhibited similarly high distress (mean = 2.90). Differences among the three groups were highly significant for age (p < 0.001) and moderate for distress intensity (p = 0.003).

The internal consistency of these profiles was supported by the clear separation among groups, with an overall classification accuracy exceeding 90%. Age was the most influential variable, followed by the type of diabetes and distress level. The gradient observed indicates that distress decreases with increasing age and is more intense among people with T1DM who require insulin therapy, which is consistent with previous findings [39, 40].

These findings can confirm that diabetes‐related distress is a heterogeneous phenomenon shaped by the interaction between personal and clinical factors [3, 4]. Younger people with T1DM represent the most emotionally vulnerable group, likely due to the demands of continuous self‐management and the perceived uncertainty about long‐term complications [41]. In contrast, older adults with T2DM and stable treatment regimens tended to exhibit better adjustment and less emotional impact.

Implications, Limitations, Strengths and Future Research

These results highlight the importance of routinely including distress assessment within diabetes care and education programs. The systematic use of validated instruments allows early identification of individuals at higher risk and facilitates the design of psychoeducational and emotional support interventions tailored to patient needs [42]. Personalised strategies focused on younger individuals and those undergoing more intensive treatments may help improve therapeutic adherence and quality of life [43].

This study had some limitations which should be considered. The cross‐sectional design limits causal inference [44], and the sample was restricted to two healthcare centres located in Seville, which may limit the generalisability of the findings, given potential regional differences in healthcare organisation and sociocultural factors across Spain [45]. Furthermore, relevant psychological and sociodemographic factors, including anxiety, social support, socioeconomic status, and educational level, which may affect diabetes‐related distress, were not included in the present study and could have enriched the analysis [46]. In addition, clinical variables such as glycated haemoglobin levels and diabetes‐related complications were not included and could also influence emotional well‐being [47]. Moreover, participant recruitment occurred in a period that includes summer and holiday months in Spain; therefore, potential seasonal effects on perceived stress and emotional burden should be considered as a possible source of bias [48].

Despite these limitations, the main strengths of this study include the size and representativeness of the sample [19], the use of a validated Spanish version of the instrument [20], and the integration of sociodemographic and clinical data, providing a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon.

Future studies should include longitudinal designs [49] to explore changes in distress over time and its relationship with glycaemic control, as well as assess the effectiveness of brief, individualised psychological interventions among the most vulnerable groups. Replicating this research in other Spanish regions would help confirm the stability of these findings and their applicability to the broader Spanish population [50].

Conclusions

This study provided evidence on the prevalence and characteristics of diabetes‐related distress among Spanish adults with diabetes. More than half of the participants experienced some level of distress, with higher values observed in younger individuals, those with T2DM, and those receiving combined pharmacological treatment. Age was the most influential factor, indicating that emotional adjustment tended to improve with time and disease stability.

Three differentiated profiles were identified, confirming that diabetes‐related distress was a heterogeneous phenomenon influenced by personal and clinical factors. The findings underscored the need to include systematic assessment of distress in diabetes care and to offer individualised psychoeducational and emotional support, especially for younger patients and those under complex treatment regimens.

Future studies should examine the evolution of distress over time and evaluate interventions aimed at reducing its impact and improving adherence and quality of life.
