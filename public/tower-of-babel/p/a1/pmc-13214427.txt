Therapeutic profiling of dihydroartemisinin using in vitro and in vivo models reveals anti-glioma potential
Authors: Sushree Sulava, Dibya Ranjan Jalli, Saurabh Chawla, Shantibhusan Senapati, Debasmita Pankaj Alone
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGlioma constitutes a primary brain tumor with a poor prognosis and survival. The accruing concerns of side effects accompanying the conventional treatment options and chances of tumor relapse have impelled a paradigm shift towards green chemistry for discovering and implementing effective, non-toxic attributes of plant-based products in the medicinal field. We have previously achieved partial rescue of the brain tumor in a Drosophila brain tumor mutant, lethal (2) giant larvae [l(2)gl], using artemisinin and curcumin. In this study, we investigate a key artemisinin derivative, dihydroartemisinin (DHA), using in vitro and in vivo glioma models.MethodsIn vitro drug screening was performed using cell viability, cell cycle analysis, flow cytometry-based apoptosis assay, and immunoblotting to examine the key pathways involved. Subcutaneous and orthotopic murine glioma models were developed, and DHA dosage for administration was standardized. The physiological and behavioral parameters of the animals were monitored, and an in vivo serum biochemical assay was conducted. The extracted samples were subjected to hematoxylin and eosin staining, western blotting, and immunostaining. The findings were compared between the control and the treatment groups.ResultsDHA lowered the proliferation of glioma cells, induced cell cycle arrest at the G0/G1 and G2/M checkpoints in a dose-dependent manner, and promoted apoptosis in vitro. It targeted the Ak strain transforming (AKT) signaling axis by eliciting phospho-AKT (pAKT) inhibition and also induced caspase-3 dependent apoptosis, leading to an effective tumor regression in the subcutaneous flank rat glioma model. The drug delayed tumor invasiveness in the orthotopic glioma models of rat and athymic mice, thus mitigating the disease severity and the associated symptoms. Immunohistochemical analysis in tumors using angiogenesis and proliferation markers showed reduced angiogenesis and proliferative rate post-treatment.ConclusionOur work unveils the therapeutic potential of DHA in modulating glioma progression both in vitro and in vivo. By reprogramming the tumor growth dynamics and targeting key signaling networks involving AKT and caspase-3, DHA may pose as a promising therapeutic candidate for glioma treatment.Graphical abstract Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at 10.1186/s12935-025-04148-4.

Introduction

Gliomas, the most prevalent form of primary brain tumours, represent about 30% of all primary brain neoplasms, accounting for nearly 80% of malignant primary brain tumours. The mortality burden remains substantial, with an estimated mortality rate of 4.42 per 100,000 population [1].

Despite advancements in treatment modalities, gliomas, particularly high-grade variants such as glioblastoma, continue to pose significant therapeutic challenges due to their intrinsic infiltrative growth, localization within the central nervous system, and marked resistance to conventional therapies. These tumors demonstrate a unique capacity for local invasion, disruption of the blood-brain barrier, and adaptation to hypoxic microenvironments, collectively limiting the efficacy of surgical resection and pharmacological interventions [2].

This evolving therapeutic landscape is driving the search for advanced anti-glioma compounds based on natural products that deliver targeted efficacy without eliciting any toxicity. One such candidate is dihydroartemisinin, the active metabolite of artemisinin. Beyond its well-established antiplasmodial efficacy, accumulating evidence highlights the broad-spectrum anti-tumor potential of DHA across various malignancies in gastric cancer, lung cancer [3], colorectal cancer [4], etc. When combined with capecitabine, DHA sensitizes colorectal cancer cells to the chemotherapeutic drug by inhibiting the Glycogen Synthase Kinase-3β/Transcription factor 7/Matrix Metalloproteinase 9 (GSK-3β/TCF7/MMP9) axis, suppressing invasion and survival pathways, thereby escalating apoptosis and tumor suppression [4].

In vitro studies report DHA selectively targeting cancer cells by exploiting their elevated iron levels and transferrin receptor overexpression. Its endoperoxide bridge is activated by intracellular ferrous ions, generating reactive oxygen species (ROS) that induce oxidative stress, DNA damage, and apoptosis. This iron-dependent mechanism spares normal cells with lower iron metabolism, contributing to its favorable therapeutic index [5]. However, its selective cytotoxic efficacy in an in vivo system remains to be tested. In our previous study, we have explored the therapeutic effects of DHA on the lethal (2) giant larvae [l(2)gl] mutant, which is a Drosophila brain tumor model characterized by a loss-of-function mutation in the tumor suppressor gene, leading to neoplastic overgrowth in the brain and imaginal discs, resulting in developmental arrest and lethality [6–9]. We found that treating the l(2)gl homozygous tumor-bearing mutants with DHA not only suppressed tumor growth and restored normal brain and wing architecture but also rescued lethality and improved behavioral outcomes. The robust and selective antitumor efficacy and favorable safety profile observed in the Drosophila model suggest that DHA merits further investigation in mammalian glioma models to substantiate its therapeutic potential [10].

This study systematically explored the anti-neoplastic effects of DHA on glioma progression through an in vitro system and two glioma models in vivo, including Sprague Dawley (SD) rats and nude mice. Due to previously reported cumulative toxicity in several anti-cancer candidates, we explored the safety profile by looking at the biochemical parameters, supported by histological studies supplemented with behavioral observation. Above all, the study highlighted the oncotherapeutic potential of DHA and the associated mechanism.

Materials and methods

Cell lines and animal experimentation

C6 glioma cells (RRID: CVCL_0194) and rat astrocytes (RRID: CVCL_E150) were procured from NCCS Pune Cell Repository (Pune, Maharashtra, India) and Cell Applications (San Diego, California, USA), respectively, and maintained at 37 °C with 5% CO2. The C6 glioma cells were cultured in Dulbecco’s Modified Eagle Medium (DMEM, Himedia) supplemented with 10% Fetal Bovine Serum (FBS, GIBCO, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA), 100U/ml penicillin (Himedia), and 0.1 mg/ml streptomycin (Himedia). The rat astrocytes were maintained in rat astrocyte growth medium (Cell Applications), a ready-to-use medium containing all necessary growth supplements as provided by the manufacturer. Sprague Dawley (SD) rats (6–8 weeks old, male, 200–250 g in weight) and CD1 nude mice (4–6 weeks old, male, 20–25 g in weight) used in the experiment were sourced from NISER Animal House Facility and were maintained under specific pathogen-free conditions. The study was conducted in accordance with the guidelines of the Committee for the Purpose of Control and Supervision of Experiments (CPCSEA) and the Institutional Animal Ethics Committee (IAEC) for laboratory animals (Ethical Approval Nos.: NISER/SBS/AH-251 and NISER/SBS/AH-141). Housing and care of animals were consistent at the institute’s animal facility with a constant temperature of 22–24 °C and relative humidity of 40–70% under a controlled light/dark schedule (12:12 h) and ad libitum access to food and water.

C6-FLuc cells were generated by transfection with a Cytomegalovirus (CMV) promoter-driven Firefly luciferase reporter gene expression plasmid vector pGL4.50 (Promega, Madison, Wisconsin, USA, RRID: Addgene_101787). C6-FLuc cells were cultured in the DMEM medium supplemented with 10% FBS, penicillin and streptomycin antibiotics, and a selective antibiotic, hygromycin (Sigma).

Cell proliferation assay

C6 glioma cells and normal rat astrocytes (culture conditions as described above) were seeded separately in the 96-well plates at a density of 5 × 103 and incubated overnight (37 °C and 5.0% CO2) before respective treatment with different concentrations of DHA (0–200 µM) for 24, 48, and 72 h. After treatment, 20 µl of 3-(4,5-dimethylthiazol-2-yl)−5-(3-carboxymethoxyphenyl)−2-(4-sulfophenyl)−2 H-tetrazolium (MTS) reagent (Promega) was added to each well, and the plates were incubated for 2 h. The optical density at 490 nm was measured using a microplate reader (Varioskan Flash Multimode reader, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA).

Cell cycle assay

C6 glioma cells were collected after a 24-hour treatment period with different concentrations of DHA (0, 25, 75, 150 µM) and incubated with 70% ethanol at −20 °C for 24 h. The fixed cells were centrifuged (1000xg, 5 min, 4 °C), washed with phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), and then incubated with RNase A (30 µg/ml, Sigma) and propidium iodide (1.0 mg/ml, Sigma) in the dark at room temperature for 30 min. Cell cycle distribution was analyzed by flow cytometer (BD LSRFortessa™ Cell Analyzer, BD Biosciences, Franklin Lakes, New Jersey, USA) using the FlowJo™ software.

Apoptosis assay

Approximately 2.5 × 105 glioma cells were seeded in 6-well plates and incubated overnight at 37 °C and 5.0% CO2. The cells were exposed to the drug (0–75µM) for 24 h. The cells were then collected and incubated with Annexin V binding buffer (556554, BD PharMingen) and subsequently stained with Annexin V (550474; BD Bioscience) and 7-AAD (559925, BD PharMingen). After a 15-minute incubation period in the dark, the samples were subjected to flow cytometric analysis using BD LSRFortessa™ Cell Analyzer (BD Biosciences). The acquired data was analyzed using the FlowJo™ software.

Subcutaneous and orthotopic injection of C6 glioma cells and subsequent drug formulation

C6 glioma cells (3 × 106) suspended in 0.9% sterile normal saline were subcutaneously inoculated in the right flank of the SD (Sprague Dawley) rats (RRID: RGD_70508). The tumor volume was measured on alternate days using vernier calipers [Vol (in mm3) = a2bπ/6, where a and b are the mean long and mean short path (mm), respectively] [11]. The animals were orally fed DHA for 14 days. Once the tumor volume reached 100 mm3, the rats were divided into four groups (3 per group): vehicle control (0 mg/kg/d) (peanut oil, Patanjali), 15 mg/kg/d, 30 mg/kg/d, and 45 mg/kg/d DHA. The determined effective in vivo dose was used for treatment in further experiments when the tumor volume reached 250 and 500 mm3. The rats were divided into untreated control and DHA-treated batches (3 per group x 3 independent experiments). As per the ethical guidelines, the tumor-bearing animals were sacrificed at the first signs of distress or before the tumor diameter approached the threshold of 40 mm in any direction.

Subsequent orthotopic injections in SD rats were performed to implant the C6 glioma cells in the brain. The animals were anesthetized using an isoflurane anesthesia system, and the fur on the dorsal side of the head was shaved & the surgical site was prepared aseptically. A midline scalp incision was made, and bregma was marked, followed by the drilling of a burr hole 1.6 mm posterior and 2.0 mm lateral to the bregma on the right. Using a Hamilton neurosyringe (Hamilton 1700 series, Reno, Nevada, USA), 5 × 105 glioma cells suspended in 10 µl of 0.9% sterile normal saline were slowly injected to a depth of 5.0 mm into the brain. The bone hole was covered with clean bone wax. The skin was closed with an absorbable suture, and a 40 mg/kg dose of tramadol hydrochloride injection (50 mg/ml, Domadol) was administered intraperitoneally to counter surgical pain. The wound was dressed topically with Betadine and Neosporin ointments at regular intervals. The behavior of the animals was monitored regularly. After surgery, the animals were randomly divided into two groups (3 per group) and they were orally administered with peanut oil (control) or DHA at 45 mg/kg/d in peanut oil from day 3 onwards via oral gavage. The experimental end point was day 22.

For further validation, a similar procedure was carried out to induce glioma in nude mice by injecting 5 × 105 C6-Fluc cells stereotactically 1.0 mm anterior and 2.0 mm right lateral to the bregma and 3.0 mm deep, with the same post-operative care protocol. After surgery, the animals were randomly divided into two groups (7 per group) and injected intraperitoneally with DMSO (Himedia) (control) or 45 mg/kg/d DHA (treated group) from day 3 onwards. The experimental endpoint was day 18. However, in both the orthotopic models, the animals were euthanized earlier if they exhibited signs of distress, neurological impairment, body weight loss exceeding 20%, or met predefined humane endpoint criteria as described above. The tumor burden signal in nude mice was detected through bioluminescence imaging with the IVIS Spectrum Imaging System (PerkinElmer, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA). Each mouse received 150 mg luciferin/kg body weight (Promega) intraperitoneally 15 min before imaging. Data was analyzed using Living Imaging Software. The peak bioluminescent signals (in p/sec/cm2/sr) recorded at the primary site of injection (brain) or secondary site (spinal axis) were plotted as ROI signals. A schematic timeline demonstrating surgery, treatment following attainment of requisite tumor burden, imaging, and sacrifice schedules is shown in supplementary Fig. 1. Experiments were conducted in triplicate independently, and representative results from the most consistent replicates are presented to best reflect the overall experimental outcomes.

Survival rate and clinical score

The animals were examined daily and scored for clinical symptoms based on weight loss, posture, and activity (coat condition included in rats). The clinical symptoms of each parameter per score have been mentioned below.

Score 0 = No weight loss and normal activity, posture, and coat.

Score 1 = 1–5% weight loss; alert but decreased activity (delay in response to external stimuli);

underconditioned with a slanted back posture; piloerection in coat.

Score 2 = 5–10% weight loss; weak and lethargic activity with shivering and decreased.

movement; underconditioned, weak, and a hunched back posture; rough skin coat.

Score 3 = 10–15% weight loss; inactive unless prodded, responds very poorly to external stimuli;

emaciated and weak, underconditioned with scanty movement and a hunched back

posture with the nose down and stretched towards the posterior; ruffled/ungroomed coat.

Score 4 = More than 15% weight loss.

In vivo toxicity evaluation

Different biochemical parameters, such as alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CRE), were examined in animal serum samples to gauge the overall toxicity of DHA on major organs (liver and kidney). Analysis was done using commercially available CliniQuant kits in a semi-automated biochemical analyzer (Merilyzer, Cliniquant, Bhavnagar, Gujarat, India) as per the manufacturer’s instructions (MerilDiagnostics).

Histology and immuno-histochemical (IHC) assay

Collected tissues fixed in 4.0% paraformaldehyde (PFA) solution for 6 h were stored in a sucrose gradient (15%, 25%, and 30% (w/v)) at 4 °C for cryosectioning. The sections were treated with 0.5% PBST and subjected to blocking in 3.0% normal horse serum (Invitrogen), incubation in anti-CD31 [1:500, Proteintech (28083-1-AP, RRID: AB_2881055), Chicago, Illinois, USA], anti-Ki67 (1:50, Invitrogen (PA1-21520, RRID: AB_559806) Waltham, Massachusetts, USA], anti-Akt (1:50, Affinity biosciences (AF6261, RRID: AB_2835121), Cincinnati, Ohio, USA], anti-phospho-Akt (1:50, Affinity biosciences, AF3262, RRID: AB_2834688), anti-cleaved caspase-3 (1:50, Affinity Biosciences, AF7022, RRID: AB_2835326), anti-mTOR (1:150, Cell Signaling Technology, 2893, RRID: AB_2105622), anti-phospho-mTOR (1:100, Cell Signaling Technology, 5536, RRID: AB_10691552) primary antibodies, respectively at 4 °C overnight and AlexaFlour-488 secondary antibody [1:500, Invitrogen, A32723 (mouse), [RRID: AB_2633275]; A32731(rabbit) [RRID: AB_2633280]] for 2 h in the dark. The 4’,6-diamidino-2-phenylindole (DAPI, Sigma) nuclear-stained (for 20 min) sections were then mounted using Prolong Gold Antifade agent (Invitrogen). Images were acquired by an inverted Leica SP8 laser-scanning confocal microscope.

The organs for gross and histological analysis were collected after transcardiac perfusion of the orthotopic animals under deep anesthesia [12]. The specimen was fixed in 4.0% PFA and subjected to a conventional histological procedure for paraffin-embedding, followed by tissue sectioning (5 μm thickness). Hematoxylin and eosin (H&E) staining was conducted to assess the structural and cellular features of the tissue [13]. Images were captured using an MT5300L LED Trinocular Brightfield Biological microscope (Meiji Techno, Bangalore, Karnataka, India).

Western blotting

Whole protein was extracted from cells or tissues using RIPA lysis buffer (0.15 M NaCl, 1% NP40, 0.01 M sodium deoxycholate, 0.1% sodium dodecyl sulfate [SDS], 0.05 M Tris-HCl [pH 8.0], 1.0 mM sodium orthovanadate, 1.0 mM phenylmethylsulfonyl fluoride) (Sigma). About 40 µg of denatured protein samples were separated by 10% or 12% SDS-polyacrylamide gel electrophoresis (PAGE), transferred onto a Polyvinylidene Fluoride (PVDF) membrane at 15 V for 35 min (Immobilion-P PVDF from Merck Millipore, Burlington, Massachusetts, USA), blocked in 5% Bovine Serum Albumin/PBST (1× PBS containing 0.1% Tween 20) (Sigma), and probed with primary antibody overnight at 4 °C. Horseradish Peroxidase (HRP)-conjugated goat anti-mouse (31444, RRID: AB_228321, Invitrogen) and goat anti-rabbit IgG (31460, RRID: AB_228341, Invitrogen) were used as secondary antibodies (1:5000). The primary antibodies used included AKT (1:2000, AF6261, RRID: AB_2835121) and pAKT (1:2000, AF3262, RRID: AB_2834688) (Affinity Biosciences), procaspase-3 (1:500) and cleaved caspase-3 (1:500) [Novus Biologicals (31A1067, RRID: AB_837846), Centennial, Colorado, USA], respectively and TfR (1:1000, NB100-92243, RRID: AB_1216384). β-actin antibody (1:1000) [Cell Signaling Technology (3700, RRID: AB_2242334), Danvers, Massachusetts, USA] was used as an endogenous control. A chemiluminescence kit (SuperSignal™ West Femto Maximum Sensitivity Substrate, Thermo Scientific, Waltham, Massachusetts, USA) was used for the detection of bands, and signals were detected in a Fusion Solo S Chemi-Doc (VilberLourmat, Eppendorf, Hamburg, Germany). Band intensity was quantified using Evolution Capt software.

Statistical analysis

The data are represented as mean ± SEM. The two groups were compared using the students’ two-tailed t-test. The experiments were conducted at least three times. Statistical analysis was performed using GraphPad Prism software (RRID: SCR_002798). A p-value < 0.05 was considered significant for all data sets (*p < 0.05, **p < 0.01, and ***p < 0.001).

Results

Treatment with DHA targets the AKT/pAKT axis and induces cell cycle phase arrest and caspase-3-dependent apoptosis

An in vitro cell proliferation assay was conducted to determine the effect of DHA on the cellular viability of C6 glioma cells and normal rat astrocytes. After 24 h of 25 µM DHA treatment, the percent viability of glioma cells dropped to about 59.0%. Subsequently, it demonstrated a downward trend with increasing drug dosage. In contrast, the normal rat astrocyte proliferation was inhibited at 75 µM in the same period (Fig. 1A). To explore the drug-induced viability-drop observation, we inspected its effect on the cell subpopulation in different cell cycle stages. A high percentage of cells [(83.1 ± 0.60)%] were found to be in the G1 phase in the 25 µM treatment group, while the 0 µM group showed about (65.6 ± 3.96)% cells in the G1 phase, indicating an arrest at the G1 phase. Meanwhile, with 150 µM treatment, the cell population increased in the G2/M phase [(36.2 ± 0.34)%] from 24.1% in 0 µM DHA untreated cells. The results showed a G0/G1 phase arrest at a low concentration of DHA, while a higher dose treatment yielded a G2/M stage arrest (Fig. 1B). Thus, the antiproliferative effect of tumor cells may be linked with a cell cycle phase arrest.

To examine the role of DHA in the major pro-tumorigenic pathway of AKT/pAKT and induction of the standard cellular death pathway of apoptosis, the cells treated with the drug (0–75µM) were subjected to Annexin V-based flowcytometric analysis and western blotting against the AKT, pAKT, and caspase-3. The results showed an increase in the percentage of apoptotic cells from (56.5 ± 2.42)% in 25µM population to (79.6 ± 4.14)% in 75µM population against (20.1 ± 1.50)% in 0µM, suggesting apoptosis as one of the key contributors in the cytotoxic response elicited by the drug (Fig. 1C). Moreover, a dose-dependent reduction in the phosphorylation of AKT was observed in treated samples, indicating an inhibitory effect of DHA on the signaling (Fig. 1D). In alignment with our flowcytometric findings, an elevated expression of cleaved caspase-3 in blotting studies with increasing dosage of DHA was observed, substantiating the pro-apoptotic activity of DHA (Fig. 1E).

Treatment with DHA arrests cellular proliferation, and reverts ectopic tumor phenotype in the rat glioma model

The anti-tumor effect of DHA was evaluated in a C6 glioma-injected ectopic tumor model, with 14-day oral dosing initiated at 100 mm³ tumor volume and subsequent monitoring of tumor regression. Though the predetermined experimental endpoint was set on day 18, both experimental and humane endpoints (as per the recommendations of the Ethics Committee and Animal Welfare guidelines) were considered throughout the experiment. Animals were sacrificed once they approached or reached the humane endpoint. While, the control batch (0 mg/kg/d) displayed an average tumor volume of (4247.6 ± 526.70) mm3 on day 18. Meanwhile, the 15 and 30 mg/kg/d group showed a slight delay in tumor progression with an average value of (2452.4 ± 267.58) and (1262.3 ± 288.75) mm3, respectively, on day 18 (Fig. 2A).

Interestingly, the 45 mg/kg/d group of animals showed a promising reduction in the tumorigenic pace with the average tumor burden getting reduced to (79.0 ± 10.91) mm3 by day 18 (Fig. 2A). Thus, a drug dose-dependent decrease in tumor volume was observed. No significant difference in body weight was observed among the treatment groups during the course of the experiment (Fig. 2A). The weights steadily increased over the course of the experiment. Apart from the body weight, no changes were seen in food and water consumption in the 45 mg/kg/d drug-treated group. The reduction in the size of the tumors with DHA treatment was very prominent at 45 mg/kg/d dosage, which highlighted the effectiveness of the said dosage in the rat ectopic glioma model. To give a comparative tumor burden at a particular time, representative images from each dose group are presented at the latest ethically permissible time point. While the 15 and 30 mg/kg/d group animals displayed delayed tumor progression till day 18, the 45 mg/kg/d group showed effective tumor regression by day 18 (Fig. 2B).

An angiogenesis study using a Cluster of differentiation 31 (CD31) marker in the resected tumor samples (day 18) revealed an apparent reduction in the microvessel density (Fig. 2C) to about 0.31-fold in the tumor microenvironment in the DHA-treated group (45 mg/kg/d) relative to the control group (0 mg/kg/d), corresponding to about 69% reduction in the levels of vascular endothelial marker post-treatment (Fig. 2D).

Even with a higher tumor burden of 250 mm3, the animals exposed to DHA treatment showed an initial delay in tumor growth, eventually leading to effective tumor regression by day 26 (Fig. 2E). Meanwhile, in the higher tumor-burden cohort (500 mm3), the control animals (0 mg/kg/d) reached the humane endpoint by day 8 and were hence sacrificed. In the corresponding treatment group (45 mg/kg/d), tumor regression was observed from day 8 which continued till day 22 (experimental end point for the treated group) (Fig. 2F). No evident signs of toxicity affecting the body weight and the behavioral parameters were visible in both the studies with varying tumor burden, thus pointing out the safe yet noteworthy anti-tumorigenic effect of DHA at 45 mg/kg/d in the SD rat ectopic model.

The subcutaneous tumor tissue collected from the control and treatment batch confirmed our in vitro findings, wherein the phosphorylation of AKT was reduced in 45 mg/kg/d samples, indicating the abrogatory effect of DHA on cancer cell proliferation (Fig. 2G). Our studies showed a higher apoptotic index (cleaved caspase-3/procaspase-3 expression) in the samples of 45 mg/kg/d dose receiving group (Fig. 2H). This showed that DHA escalated cleaved caspase-3 levels and induced apoptosis in vivo at 45 mg/kg/d dosage, suggesting it to be a contributory mechanism to the anti-tumor activity of the drug.

DHA impedes glioma progression in vivo and slows down the severity of the disease

The anti-oncogenic action of the drug was next assessed in the orthotopic SD rat glioma model. In the treated group, three out of nine animals (33.3%) reached the humane endpoint before study completion, while the untreated group displayed five out of nine (55.6%) animals reaching the endpoint early. The earliest death in the untreated group occurred on day 11 with the earliest death in the treated group recorded on day 19, indicating decreased resilience in the control group. (Fig. 3A). In addition, the brain extracted from the control batch showing mortality on day 18, displayed visible signs of multiple lesions as opposed to the sample obtained from the treated animal from day 20 (data not shown).

Histopathological analysis of the resected brain samples showed a higher tumorous region in the control batch [(170.2 ± 24.06)x105µm2] in contrast to the treatment group samples [(77.3 ± 26.20)x105µm2] (Fig. 3B). Inspection of fine cellular details showed the accumulation of inflammatory cells and neutrophils in the tumor-bearing region in the samples (Fig. 3C). However, weight loss was observed in both groups indicating animals were under stress; thus, weight loss was an obvious finding. While most of the untreated group animals exhibited a hunched back posture and reduced conditioning, a greater proportion of the drug-treated animals remained within scores 1–2, indicating relatively milder impairment. Similarly, lethargic movement with delayed response to external stimuli and ungroomed coat condition were more common in the untreated animals (scores 2–3), while treated animals predominantly showed a lower clinical score, suggestive of the therapeutic benefits of the drug in alleviating the disease severity. (Fig. 3D). Thus, while the mortality rate and survival curves (log-rank p = 0.18) did not show a statistically significant difference between groups, it should be noted that animals in the treated group were sacrificed at a predetermined experimental endpoint of day 22 before natural mortality could occur. As such, true long-term survival could not be assessed for this group. However, clinical condition scoring indicated reduced severity and symptoms of the disease in the treated group at the endpoint compared to controls. Hence, in our study the drug at 45 mg/kg/d dosage was found to be effective in delaying the progression of the tumor in the orthotopic rat model with no significant observable signs of toxicity.

No visual alterations in tissue architecture were observed in major organs such as the liver, kidney, and spleen after drug treatment. Microscopic investigation showed a radial arrangement of the normal hepatocytes around the central vein. In the kidney, normal tubular histology was observed. Meanwhile, no visible abnormalities in typical splenic architecture were observed across the samples. (Fig. 3E). This was in agreement with our blood biochemistry assay, wherein the liver and kidney biofunction markers- alanine aminotransferase (ALT), aspartate aminotransferase (AST), blood urea nitrogen (BUN), and creatinine (CRE) were tested in the animal serum samples to validate our in vivo toxicity findings. The average values indicated no significant hepatotoxicity or nephrotoxicity at a 45 mg/kg/d dose in this model (Fig. 3F).

DHA inhibits tumor cell proliferation and regulates the AKT/pAKT pathway in the orthotopic rat glioma model

To investigate the effect of DHA on glioma cell proliferation, the number of Ki-67 positive cells was counted. A marked reduction in the Ki-67 immunoreactivity was observed in the treated group relative to the control (Fig. 4A). The proliferation index was 2.71-fold higher in the 0 mg/kg/d group compared to the 45 mg/kg/d group (Fig. 4B), indicating the drug’s anti-proliferative action in the treated group samples. The AKT/pAKT axis was studied to find the role of DHA in its regulation. While the total AKT expression was more or less the same in both groups, a sharp decline in the pAKT expression was evident in the DHA treatment group of tumor samples, indicating inhibition of AKT activation by DHA treatment (Fig. 4C). Quantitative assessment showed a significant reduction of pAKT levels by about 2.40-fold in the treatment batch (Fig. 4D). To confirm downstream pathway activation, mTOR activation was assessed in the tissue samples. A pronounced decrease in mTOR and p-mTOR immunoreactivity was observed in the samples from the treatment group (Fig. 4C). The DHA-treated glioma samples exhibited a significant 1.86-fold reduction in total mTOR levels, indicating a substantial suppression of total mTOR expression. Additionally, these samples demonstrated a pronounced 7.22-fold decrease in phosphorylated mTOR (p-mTOR), reflecting a marked inhibition of mTOR pathway activation (Fig. 4D). Consistent with our earlier findings that the treatment markedly decreased AKT phosphorylation, these observations indicate that DHA suppresses mTOR activation downstream of AKT signaling. The coordinated decrease in both pAKT and p-mTOR strongly suggests attenuation of the AKT-mTOR pathway, implying that DHA disrupts a critical pro-survival and growth-related pathway in glioma cells. Apoptosis was studied to clarify the anti-proliferative mechanism of the drug. Whole protein analysis corroborated the caspase-dependent cell death induction by DHA seen in vitro, as the treated samples showed increased apoptotic index (Fig. 4C), with the 45 mg/kg/d group exhibiting almost four times enhanced expression of active caspase-3 relative to the 0 mg/kg/d group (Fig. 4D). Thus, DHA targeted the AKT-mTOR signaling and elevated the levels of active caspase-3 and repressed glioma progression in vivo at 45 mg/kg/d.

DHA delays tumor progression in nude mice xenograft models

To confirm our previously mentioned findings, the study was extended to an immunocompromised athymic nude mouse model induced with a brain tumor. In vivo imaging analysis of the subjects presented a hindered tumor progression in the treatment group of animals (Fig. 5A, B). The fold increase in the mean signal in the Region of Interest (ROI) signal at the second time point (day 15) was delayed in the treatment batch (~ 68.2-fold). In contrast, the control batch displayed a spike in the values on day 15 (~ 109.3-fold) (Fig. 5B). Interestingly, a secondary signal was observed in a few animals, indicating the probability of the metastatic lodging of the tumor cells in the spinal cord region via the cerebrospinal fluid (CSF) (Fig. 5A, C). While three out of five of the control group of animals showed metastasis, with elevated metastatic signals on day 15, and reached the humane endpoint earlier on days 11, 12, and 17, only two out of five of the treatment batch exhibited metastasis; and one reached the endpoint relatively later at day 15 while the rest of the treatment group was sacrificed at the experimental endpoint of day 18 (Fig. 5C, D). The metastatic load in one out of five treated animals on day 15 was lower than in the control group animals on day 15. Since one of the control group animals was found dead on day 17 with the brain in a state of autolysis, the brain was not processed further for histological studies. The brain histopathological analysis confirmed that the mean tumorous area in the 0 mg/kg/d (vehicle control; N = 6) versus 45 mg/kg/d (N = 7) groups were 47.8 × 105 and 15.1 × 105µm2, respectively (Fig. 5E). The drug-treated tissues displayed reduced tumor cellularity and improved tissue organization compared to the densely proliferative untreated controls (Fig. 5F). Though the multifocal distribution of tumor cells was seen in a few of the treatment batch samples, no signs of severe inflammation or hyperemia (an indicator of increased vascularity) were found, suggesting an inhibitory effect of the drug of tumor cell sustenance. In contrast, the tumor-bearing batch of brain tissue samples from the vehicle control group showed viable tumor zones and necrotic patches, and angiogenesis with inflammatory cell accumulation, indicating aggravated tumor growth (Fig. 5G).

The treatment outcome was confirmed in the athymic mice model, wherein day 18 was considered the experimental endpoint based on the clinical condition of the animals. Symptoms evaluated included weight loss, normal versus hunched posture, and activity. The carrier-treated control animals were dull, emaciated, and had poor body condition scores. Being a commonly overactivated pathway in most cancers, the AKT/pAKT axis remains a basic therapeutic development strategy. A decrease in pAKT levels was observed in the DHA-treated samples compared with the control group (Fig. 6C), with AKT phosphorylation getting reduced to about 0.44-fold of the control values (Fig. 6D), which supported our earlier in vitro findings. Upon further conducting IHC analysis on the mTOR, a downstream effector of AKT, we found that DHA administration resulted in a pronounced reduction in both mTOR and p-mTOR levels (Fig. 6C). Quantification revealed a 1.75-fold decrease in total mTOR expression in the treated cohort. A similar trend was observed for p-mTOR, which declined by 4.84-fold in the drug treated samples as compared to the untreated samples (Fig. 6D). This suppression further supports the attenuation of the AKT-mTOR signaling cascade in DHA-treated samples. An investigation into the role of DHA in the induction of apoptosis supported our previous findings in terms of a surge in the levels of cleaved caspase-3 in the 45 mg/kg/d group samples (Fig. 6C), with the graphical analysis showing an elevation of the apoptotic index by about 2.57-fold in the treatment group (Fig. 6D).

Collectively, the results suggested that DHA played a negative role in regulating the AKT-mTOR signaling activity, promoted high expression of cleaved caspase-3 to induce apoptosis in vivo, and brought about delayed glioma progression.

Discussion

Despite different multimodal therapies, the clinical management of gliomas remains limited by tumor heterogeneity, therapeutic resistance, and the challenges of traversing the blood-brain barrier. Natural products, owing to their structural diversity and ability to modulate multiple cellular pathways, have emerged as promising candidates for novel glioma therapies [14]. Artemisinin and its derivatives have been reported to deliver cytotoxicity across leukemia, breast cancer, liver cancer, etc [19]. by targeting key pathways including mechanistic target of rapamycin (mTOR), Myelocytomatosis (MYC), Wingless-related integration site/beta-catenin (Wnt/β-catenin) signaling [20]. Its active metabolite, dihydroartemisinin, with better solubility and bioavailability than its parent compound, has demonstrated anti-cancer effects in multiple malignancies. DHA kills the ovarian cancer cells by inhibiting the hedgehog pathway, potentially through suppression of Smoothened, Frizzled Class Receptor (Smo) expression, triggering cellular death networks [21]. DHA abrogates the invasiveness of gastric cancer cells through Cyclin D1/Cyclin-Dependent Kinase 4 (CDK4)/Retinoblastoma (Rb) signaling pathway [22]. In this study, we evaluated the anti-tumorigenic efficacy of DHA in glioma using complementary in vitro and in vivo models. The C6 glioma cell line, derived from rat glioma, is widely used in glioma research as it closely mimics human glioblastoma multiforme in terms of high proliferative capacity, angiogenic potential, and invasive behavior, as well as key molecular and genetic markers such as upregulation of Platelet-Derived Growth Factor Beta (PDGFβ), Epidermal Growth Factor Receptor (EGFR), and Rat sarcoma (Ras) pathway genes, thus providing a relevant cellular platform for evaluating tumor biology and its therapeutic efficacy [23]. Meanwhile, SD rats offer a syngeneic immunocompetent host enabling orthotopic implantation of C6 cells, preserving tumor-host immune interactions and allowing assessment of tumor growth, invasion, and microenvironment dynamics in a physiologically relevant setting [23, 24]. Nude mice, characterized by an impaired T-cell immune response, permit engraftment of human tumor xenografts without rejection [25, 26]. Thus, using these models in combination enables a thorough comprehensive investigation of anti-glioma candidate efficacy across tumor cell biology and provides therapeutic insights into maximizing the predictive value for clinical outcomes.

In oncotherapeutics, the key highlighted pathways include crucial genes involved in the cell cycle, cell polarity, cell differentiation, and apoptosis [27]. For instance, higher doses of paclitaxel initiate mitotic arrest at the G2/M phase, while lower dosages activate apoptosis at the resting G0 stage [28]. Artemisinin blocks Specificity Protein 1 (Sp1) interaction with the CDK4 promoter, thereby inhibiting CDK4 gene expression. Autophagy also plays a role in cell cycle regulation by activating p21, a CDK inhibitor, which is the case for artesunate’s mechanism that activates Beclin1 [29, 30]. Our study showed a G0/G1 phase arrest with 25 µM DHA treatment and G2/M stage arrest at 150 µM, which may manifest through the downregulation of cyclins and their respective cyclin-dependent kinases, eventually leading to a cascade of reactions for triggering cellular death.

Consistent with the in vitro findings, when orally administered in a subcutaneous rat model bearing a 100 mm3 tumor burden, DHA demonstrated excellent anti-tumor activity by delaying tumorigenesis till a 30 mg/kg/d dose but pronouncedly halted the tumor growth at a 45 mg/kg/d dose. Since many anti-cancer drugs are associated with significant weight loss and reduced appetite as prevalent side effects, we monitored the physiological variables of water intake and body weight. The consistency in the increasing body weight pattern, along with normal physiological and behavioral aspects in the drug-treated ectopic rat model, implied the non-toxic effect of the drug at 45 mg/kg/d. It was interesting to observe that DHA potentially inhibited tumor growth even with an increased tumor burden of 500 mm3. The role of angiogenic signaling is of paramount importance during tumorigenesis, and many anti-cancer drugs and even cancer-savior microRNAs target the molecules associated with pro-angiogenic signaling cascades [31–33]. Platelet Endothelial Cell Adhesion Molecule 1 (PECAM-1)/CD31 was used as a marker for angiogenesis in the ectopic rat glioma model tumor samples, and immunohistochemical studies revealed that DHA abrogated tumor-induced angiogenesis.

Athymic nude mice, lacking functional T-cells, are commonly employed in glioma biology owing to their high susceptibility to tumor formation upon direct injection of glioma cells, leading to rapid and uncontrolled cellular multiplication. The accelerated tumor formation due to a lack of T-cell-based immune surveillance can be fatal to the animal, based on the specific location of implantation. The current study observed that both the rat and nude mouse models developed detectable tumors from day three onwards. The severity and volume of space-occupying tumor lesions were quantified based on the ROI bioluminescent signal in the nude mice model. In our experiment, mortality was observed on days 11–15 in nude mice and 11–21 in rat models. Further, the drug treatment didn’t have significant observable adverse effects throughout the duration of the study. Weight loss was seen in both the groups receiving treatment/vehicle, which was a prominent finding as a brain tumor is a space-occupying lesion whose severity affects vital body functions, such as respiration, blood circulation, thermoregulation, etc.

Tumor progression and severity were relatively high in the control batch. Our study confirmed that at a safe dose of 45 mg/kg/d, DHA retarded the invasive capacity of the glioma cells. An increase in the incidence of spinal metastasis from intracranial glioma in patients from 0.4% to 2% has been correlated with poor patient survival. Shibahara et al., 2019 report the dissemination of invasive glioma cells along the cerebrospinal axis, resulting in spinal metastasis in about 13% of cases of intracranial glioblastomas assessed using spinal Gadolinium Magnetic Resonance Imaging (Gd-MRI) [34]. Ma et al., 2019 reported rerouting of CSF in the murine glioma model, which may lead to cases of spinal metastases [35]. Several other studies report cases discussing how spinal metastases may be symptomatic or asymptomatic as observed in patient cohorts and in autopsy reports of glioblastoma patients, emphasizing the importance of an MRI screening for proper clinical diagnosis of spinal metastasis for effective treatment strategies against glioma [36–39]. In our C6 luciferase cells-based orthotopic glioma model, we observed that the incidence of spinal cord metastasis mostly matched with a higher tumor burden. This indicates that the spreading of cancer cells to the spinal cord might be a consequence of both the proliferative and metastatic nature of these cells. Together, we believe that this experimental tumor model recapitulates several features of spontaneous glioma observed in most clinical contexts, though not all, and has helped us to address our question related to the effectiveness of this drug in glioma cell proliferation and metastasis.

We also found progressive and diffused tumor growth in a few animals in the treatment batch. Still, in comparison to the vehicle control group, the number of animals exhibiting those symptoms and the extent of severity were lower. Also, the bio-functional parameters in serum samples lay in the normal range, which added to our histopathological findings, in which no significant visible structural alterations were observed in liver, kidney, and spleen tissues. Thus, the treatment at 45 mg/kg/d seems promising in delaying the disease progression in the current glioma models used in this study. A higher dosage of 60 mg/kg/d was investigated in a pilot study. However, the models displayed a poor tolerance of the high dose, leading to increasing weakness and eventual mortality (data not shown). Our study highlights the efficiency of the drug at 45 mg/kg/d, though partial, but well tolerated by both the orthotopic murine models.

Various studies have highlighted the cellular intricacies that are being targeted by this drug to halt tumor progression from targeting AKT and Mitogen-Activated Protein Kinase (MEK) pathways [40] to activating cell death pathways [41]. The PI3K/AKT signaling axis is a central driver of glioma progression, particularly in maintaining glioma stem cells (GSCs), and is often crucial in developing therapeutic resistance [42]. Golding et al. demonstrated that AKT activation enhances GSC self-renewal and resistance to apoptosis, reinforcing its role in tumor sustenance and therapy resistance [43]. Aberrations such as Phosphatidylinositol-4,5-bisphosphate 3-kinase catalytic subunit alpha (PIK3CA) mutations and Phosphatase and Tensin Homolog (PTEN) loss [44] lead to persistent AKT signaling, contributing to uncontrolled proliferation, survival, and metabolic adaptation, amounting to an aggressive tumor phenotype. Several anti-cancer drugs exert their therapeutic potential by inhibiting the pro-tumorigenic pathways and activating different cellular death mechanisms. DHA inhibits AKT phosphorylation in various malignancies by targeting upstream and downstream components of the PI3K/AKT pathway. For example, in colorectal cancer (CRC), DHA inhibits the AKT/mTOR signaling pathway, and activation of this pathway reverses its suppressive effects on CRC cell viability, clonogenicity and tumor sphere formation [45, 46]. This inhibitory effect of DHA on PI3K/AKT activation and subsequent downstream mTOR signaling is also seen in the case of melanoma, lung cancer and esophageal squamous cell carcinoma, which leads to reduced proliferation and enhanced cellular death [47–49]. It also decreases anti-apoptotic proteins like Mcl-1 and Bcl-2, with activation of pro-apoptotic pathways, including GSK-3β, which is normally inactivated by AKT. DHA has been reported to suppress the proliferation of lung cancer cells through downregulation of the AKT/GSK3β/Cyclin D1 signaling axis, resulting in G1 phase cell cycle arrest [50]. The dual mTORC1/2 inhibitor TAK-228 significantly reduced tumor growth in patients with hyperactivated PI3K/AKT/mTOR signaling harboring advanced solid malignancies, highlighting both the clinical relevance of this pathway and the need for molecularly guided treatment strategies [51].

Administration of DHA resulted in a pronounced suppression of phosphorylated AKT levels, thereby interfering with the PI3K/AKT signaling cascade. This inhibition was accompanied by a substantial reduction in tumor growth, indicating the compound’s potential as an effective modulator of oncogenic signaling pathways with aberrant AKT activation. These findings were also recaptured in the orthotopic glioma models wherein DHA suppressed both AKT and mTOR activation, which may contribute to the attenuation of downstream processes that promote glioma cell growth and survival.

Another key pathway involves apoptosis, which includes the activation of caspase-3. This cleaved caspase-3 is responsible for activating several downstream targets by proteolytic degradation, which, in turn, induces the breakage of chromosomal DNA and switches on the death network. Our study confirmed the augmented levels of active caspase-3 in cells treated with DHA in a dose-dependent manner and tumor samples obtained from a treatment batch of animals.

DHA exerts its anticancer effects by interacting with intracellular iron to generate reactive oxygen species (ROS), thereby triggering iron-dependent cell death pathways such as ferroptosis. This process involves upregulated autophagy and enhanced ferritin degradation (ferritinophagy), which increases the labile iron pool, leading to mitochondrial dysfunction and lipid peroxide accumulation. Consequently, cellular glutathione (GSH) levels decrease, and the antioxidant enzyme glutathione peroxidase 4 (GPX4) is degraded, facilitating ferroptotic cell death. Notably, overexpression of iron-regulating proteins like ferritin heavy chain (FTH) or iron-sulfur cluster assembly enzyme (ISCU) can mitigate DHA’s cytotoxicity, highlighting the critical role of disrupted iron and redox homeostasis [52–54]. Supporting these mechanisms, preliminary data from a subcutaneous rat glioma model demonstrated decreased transferrin receptor (TfR) levels following DHA treatment, indicating impaired iron uptake and homeostasis within tumor cells, which can sensitize glioma cells to cell death (Supplementary Fig. 2). In addition, Yi et al., 2020 reported that oncogenic activation of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR pathway suppresses ferroptosis in cancer cells through upregulation of lipid metabolism driven by sterol regulatory element-binding protein 1 (SREBP1) and its target stearoyl-CoA desaturase-1 (SCD1). Inhibition of the PI3K-AKT-mTOR axis sensitizes cancer cells to ferroptosis by decreasing SREBP1/SCD1-mediated production of monounsaturated fatty acids, which normally protect against lipid peroxidation. Targeting this pathway, especially mTORC1, thus enhances ferroptotic cell death and synergizes with ferroptosis inducers to regress tumors bearing PI3K or PTEN mutations [55]. This establishes PI3K/AKT/mTOR as a key regulator of ferroptosis susceptibility in cancer, which provides an insight into the possible link between proliferation/cellular death pathways and iron metabolism, warranting future investigation.

Importantly, iron-driven oxidative stress and ROS generation intersect with canonical survival and apoptotic signaling networks. Oxidative stress can inhibit PI3K/AKT signaling as reported Lu et al., 2010 [54]. In glioma, the AKT/mTORC1 signaling pathway plays a crucial role in regulating cell survival and proliferation by promoting lipid synthesis through sterol-responsive element-binding proteins (SREBP-1/2), which activate lipid biosynthesis genes. Inhibition of this pathway, disrupts SREBP function leading to reduced GPX4 levels and enhanced lipid peroxidation, thus triggering ferroptosis and suppressing glioma cell proliferation. This highlights the critical link between AKT/mTOR signaling and ferroptotic cell death, offering a promising target for therapeutic intervention via modulation of cellular lipid metabolism and redox homeostasis [56–58]. Studies report DHA treatment reduces pAKT and downstream mTOR signaling in cancer cells, and that this inhibition is associated with increased apoptosis markers including cleaved caspase-3. In separate studies, DHA-induced ROS and iron accumulation have been linked to mitochondrial outer membrane permeabilization and activation of the caspase-3-dependent cell death, consistent with reports showing DHA activates caspase-3-dependent apoptosis. Thus, the above literature support that iron-mediated ROS production is mechanistically upstream of both ferroptotic lipid peroxidation and mitochondrial apoptotic cascades and can intersect with PI3K/AKT signaling to promote caspase-3 activation, thereby providing a plausible unifying route by which DHA simultaneously elevates lipid peroxidation and activates caspase-3 while inhibiting survival kinase signaling. Integrating the above insights from published literature with the current findings, we present a proposed mechanistic model outlining how DHA might influence the AKT-mTOR signaling cascade with modulation of iron/ROS perturbation in glioma, as depicted in Fig. 7.

DHA demonstrates the ability to penetrate the blood-brain barrier (BBB), as evidenced by detectable levels in the CSF of adults treated for cerebral malaria and significant brain accumulation in rodent models [59, 60]. Pharmacokinetic studies report a plasma half-life for DHA of less than three hours in humans and less than one hour in murine models, presenting a challenge for sustaining therapeutic exposure [61–64]. These pharmacokinetic limitations, combined with DHA’s low solubility and poor bioavailability, hinder its clinical efficacy as a standalone drug in cancer therapy, particularly for glioma. DHA’s clinical efficacy may be further augmented with nano formulations that improve its solubility, stability, and tumor targeting. For example, polymeric and lipid-based nanoparticles of DHA have been developed to enhance drug delivery, prolong circulation, and boost anticancer activity in cervical cancer [65].

DHA demonstrates potent synergy with several chemotherapeutic agents in cancer treatment, significantly improving anti-tumor responses in glioma by enhancing temozolomide’s efficacy via autophagy induction and apoptosis, reducing the required drug dose [66]. It also synergizes with 5-aminolevulinic acid to increase reactive oxygen species and DNA damage, resulting in decreased glioma cell survival [67]. Remarkably, DHA enhances the anti-tumor effects of doxorubicin in triple-negative breast cancer cells by promoting apoptosis and mitochondrial disruption. It similarly potentiates trastuzumab-induced apoptosis in HER2/neu-positive breast cancers [68]. Furthermore, DHA combined with carboplatin inhibits ovarian cancer growth through caspase-dependent pathways [69], and it improves gefitinib’s inhibitory effects on lung cancer cell migration and invasion via modulation of AKT/mTOR/STAT3 signaling [48]. These findings elucidate DHA’s potential as a versatile adjuvant in combinatorial cancer therapies across multiple tumor types. Despite promising preclinical data of DHA across various tumor types, clinical evidence remains limited. DHA is currently in a phase II trial combining DHA with icotinib in advanced non-small cell lung cancer patients, where escalating oral DHA doses are administered until progression or toxicity occurs [70]. Further research is necessary to fully elucidate DHA’s anticancer mechanisms, characterize its metabolites and metabolic pathways in vivo, and establish its clinical efficacy, safety, and resistance profile.

Monotherapy in cancer has proved futile owing to the redundant cellular networks actively working in the tumor microenvironment [71]. Stage IV brain cancer or glioblastoma has been termed a cold tumor due to its unresponsiveness to immunotherapy [72]. Various limitations that block the way in remediating the oncotherapeutic dilemma include poor pharmacokinetic features, the inability to cross the blood-brain barrier, non-specific toxicity, etc. Employing green chemistry or naturally derived products alleviates this scientific burden [14]. Our study showed DHA as a promising therapeutic candidate in multiple brain tumor models. Further studies need to be done to dissect the essential signaling pathways and the associated markers to optimize their action to target cancer cell-specific multiple redundant pathways through various innovative approaches. This study expressed the anti-tumorigenic efficiency of DHA, accompanied by unaffected behavioral activities and a reduced mortality rate in the glioma model organisms receiving the drug treatment. Our research underscores the necessity for exploring novel therapeutic avenues in glioma biology while highlighting the importance of further investigation into DHA for developing precision medicine strategies to improve patient outcomes in glioma.
