Assessment of the concentration of rare earth elements (REEs) in industrially impacted topsoil: a case study from the Jizera Mountains and Upper Silesian Industrial Area (USIR), Poland
Authors: Piotr Fabijańczyk, Jarosław Zawadzki, Adam Łukasik
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
This study investigates heavy rare earth elements (Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, Lu) in soils from two industrially influenced regions in southern Poland: the Jizera Mountains and the Upper Silesian Industrial Region (USIR). A total of 166 soil samples were collected from different land use types, including arable, forested and mountain areas. Sampling involved collecting soil cores up to 30 cm deep using a Humax sampler, with GPS-based georeferencing. Magnetic susceptibility was measured to identify horizons enriched with technogenic magnetic particles linked to industrial pollution. Selected subsamples were analyzed for REEs concentrations using Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry. Elevated concentrations of heavy REEs were observed in the Jizera Mountains, an area naturally enriched in REEs due to the presence of minerals such as monazite and xenotime. Agricultural soils showed significantly higher REEs concentrations than adjacent forests, likely due to fertilizer use and soil disturbance from cultivation. The highest enrichment factor (about 3) and geoaccumulation index (about 1.5) values were observed in mountainous areas, over twice as high as in arable and forest soils. ANOVA revealed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05), with the strongest effect observed for IG Lu. Strong positive correlations (r > 0.7) among REEs suggest shared natural or anthropogenic sources. These findings highlight the combined influence of geological background and human activity on REEs accumulation in soils and demonstrate the utility of EF and IG indices for assessing soil contamination in industrially impacted regions.

Introduction

The growing industrial use of rare earth elements (REEs) raises concerns about their environmental presence and potential impacts. REEs can accumulate in soils and persistently alter microbial communities, enzymatic activities, nutrient cycling, and plant physiology (Beckwith & Butler, 1993; Sager & Wiche, 2024). Heavy REEs tend to bind strongly to soil particles and organic matter, leading to prolonged persistence and higher bioaccumulation potential. Their effects on soil functions, such as carbon and nitrogen turnover, may cascade through ecosystems. Although research on heavy REEs in soils is still limited, increasing industrial demand has driven growing scientific interest. Understanding their distribution is crucial for assessing environmental risks and long-term impacts on soil health and agriculture. Due to their unique properties, strong magnetism, high conductivity, and catalytic activity, REEs are crucial in electronics, renewable energy (wind turbines, EV batteries), and advanced manufacturing (Aychitte, 2025). This widespread use highlights the need to monitor their environmental accumulation, especially in soils.

Rare earth elements comprise the lanthanides, along with scandium (Sc) and yttrium (Y). These elements are often divided into light REEs, medium REEs, and heavy REEs, although the classification of medium REEs varies across the scientific literature. In some studies, elements such as Eu, Gd, Tb, and Dy are grouped as medium REEs, while others classify them differently depending on atomic number or geochemical behavior. In the present study, the focus is on five heavy REEs: holmium (Ho), erbium (Er), thulium (Tm), ytterbium (Yb), and lutetium (Lu), due to their low natural abundance, increasing industrial use, and environmental persistence.

Although global research on REEs contamination has expanded, many studies concentrate on total REEs content or light REEs, with limited focus on heavy REEs. This is particularly evident in Central Europe, where systematic assessments of heavy REEs in soils affected by industrial emissions are still scarce (Fabijańczyk & Zawadzki, 2021; Fabijańczyk et al., 2019).

As indicated by Salminen et al. (2005) and other researchers, REEs mobility and accumulation in soils depend on various factors, including pH, organic matter content, and granulometric composition (Beckwith & Butler, 1993; Cao 2001; Ran & Liu, 1993). These factors influence both natural background levels and anthropogenic inputs. While the present study does not include detailed physicochemical characterization of the soils (a limitation addressed in the discussion), the focus remains on assessing enrichment related to land use type and industrial emissions.

A recent study by Adamczyk et al. (2020) found that REEs content in fly ash emitted by coal-fired power plants may exceed 300 ppm, which is considered a relatively high value (Thomas et al., 2024; Wang et al., 2019). Importantly, such concentration levels of REEs in fly ash are also recognized as a potential economic cutoff for REEs recovery and recycling efforts (National Energy Technology Laboratory 2018). The U.S. Department of Energy has identified such coal fly ash as promising source for REEs extraction technologies. Therefore, particulate fallout from such emissions can significantly contribute to REEs contamination in soil (Baron et al., 2022). Cao et al. (2015) suggested that REEs present in fly ash predominantly accumulate in the organic and humus layers of soil, consistent with the profile distribution of other pollutants. For that reason, this study focused on collecting soil samples from topsoil layers.

In Poland, most existing studies address REEs without detailed subgroup differentiation or comprehensive spatial analysis across varied land use and pollution conditions. The current study aims to help fill this gap by examining heavy REEs concentrations in soils from two contrasting areas in southern Poland: the Jizera Mountains and the Upper Silesian Industrial Region (USIR). These areas differ in land cover, elevation, and industrial history, allowing for comparative interpretation of REEs enrichment patterns. The spatial variability of heavy REEs concentrations in Polish soils affected by industrial activities was assessed, to advance the understanding of their geochemical behavior in such contaminated environments, and to support this analysis with new empirical data collected across different land-use categories. To evaluate the extent of REEs concentrations in soils, two widely used geochemical indicators were applied: the geoaccumulation index (IG) and the enrichment factor (EF). These indices help to assess anthropogenic contributions relative to natural background concentrations. This research is among the first in Poland to address REEs in such detail within a land-use-sensitive and regionally comparative framework.

This study presents important baseline data on the concentrations and distribution of heavy rare earth elements (Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, Lu) in soils from industrially influenced areas. While the focus was on field measurements and concentration assessment, further studies incorporating mechanistic analyses would be valuable to deepen understanding of REEs sources and distribution processes. This work lays the groundwork for such future research by providing essential initial data from this underexplored region.

Sampling areas

To achieve the study’s objectives, seven sampling locations (Table 1, Fig. 1) were selected to represent diverse soil characteristics and land uses, including forests, agricultural lands, and grasslands, all subjected to varying levels of anthropogenic pressure. The sites are located in southern Poland within the Upper Silesian Industrial Region and the Jizera Mountains near the German and Czech borders (Fig. 1). The selection was intentional to include soils affected by different types and intensities of human activity as well as distinct geological settings. Although the Sudetes Mountains (including the Jizera Mountains) and the Upper Silesian region differ significantly in geological substrates, both have experienced current or historical pollution, including transboundary dust deposition influencing the Jizera Mountains from neighboring industrial areas. Additionally, these regions differ in land use (forested vs. arable) and altitude, providing a broad basis for assessing rare earth element distributions across varied environmental and anthropogenic conditions. USIR O1—arable field

The sampling site was located in an arable field (Fig. 2), with its southern boundary adjacent to a public road. The geological substrate consisted of carbonate rocks, limestone, dolomite, and marl, typical of the southern part of the Upper Silesian Coal Basin. The soils were Cambic Leptosol, with a silty-clay texture, alkaline pH, and high calcium and magnesium content (Kabała, 2019; Lis & Pasieczna, 1995). The vegetation consisted mainly of cultivated crops (corn and barley) with ruderal weeds growing along the edges of the field. The area was directly affected by dust carried from a nearby waste heap, resulting from lead and zinc ore processing, located approximately 600 m southwest of the site. USIR F1—forest

The study area was located within a state forest surrounded by agricultural land (Fig. 3). The underlying geology consisted of Quaternary deposits over carbonate rocks. The soils were classified as Brunic arenosol, with a sandy-loam texture, slightly acidic pH, and moderate organic matter content (Kabała, 2019; Lis & Pasieczna, 1995). Vegetation was dominated by mature black alder, accompanied by younger maple, oak, and ash trees. The herbaceous layer included hazel, grasses, and mosses. While The site was exposed to atmospheric deposition, it was not located in the immediate vicinity of major pollution sources.

USIR F2—urban park

This study area was located in a city park with trees up to 200 years old (Fig. 3). The geological substrate consisted of anthropogenically altered Quaternary sands and gravels. The soils were Haplic cambisol, with neutral to slightly alkaline pH and enriched in organic matter (Kabała, 2019; Lis & Pasieczna, 1995). Vegetation consisted of oaks, maples, ash, sycamore, linden, Scots pine, Norway spruce, alder. Some areas of the park were covered mainly by weeds and ruderal shrubs. The area was exposed to urban atmospheric pollution, but no major industrial emission sources were present nearby.

USIR F3—forest

This site encompassed a large forest complex near the Katowice Metallurgical Complex (Fig. 4). The substrate included Quaternary deposits of varying anthropogenic transformation, partly from land reclaimed after sand extraction. The soils were Haplic Podzol, with a sandy-loamy-silty texture, slightly acidic pH, and moderate organic matter content (Kabała, 2019; Lis & Pasieczna, 1995). Vegetation was dominated by pine forests, with birch and poplar in wetter areas where impermeable clay layers were exposed during sand extraction. Jizera F—forest

The site was located on a hill approximately 870 m above sea level. The soils, Haplic cambisol, were developed on Jizera granite, with some genetic horizons yet to form. They exhibit acidic pH with the sandy-loamy-silty texture (Kabała, 2019; Lis & Pasieczna, 1995). Vegetation consisted of mixed mountain forests dominated by Norway spruce, with an understory of blueberries, mosses, and bog plants. The site was exposed to long-range industrial pollution from power plants in Poland, Germany, and the Czech Republic (Zawadzki et al., 2016). It was part of the so-called “Black Triangle,” historically one of the most polluted regions in Central Europe.

Field and laboratory measurements

All soil samples were collected during the summer of 2016, under relatively stable and comparable meteorological conditions. The study design did not include repeated sampling across seasons therefore, potential seasonal variability in REEs concentrations was not assessed, and was not a goal of this study.

Soil samples were collected as 30 cm soil cores using a Humax sampler equipped with 30 cm plastic tubes. Each sample point was georeferenced using GPS in WGS84 coordinate system, and at each sample point two soil cores were collected (except from sampling site USIR F3 where only one soil sample was collected at each sample point). The exact number of collected soil samples was listed in Table 1.

All soil profiles were first subjected to magnetic susceptibility measurements using a Bartington MS2C sensor (Dearing, 1994). The MS2C was used to record the vertical distribution of magnetic susceptibility throughout the 30 cm soil core. This technique is widely employed to detect the presence of technogenic magnetic particles (TMPs), which are commonly associated with industrial pollution (Magiera et al., 2006, 2023). Based on susceptibility profiles, soil subsamples for chemical analysis were taken from layers showing elevated magnetic susceptibility values, typically indicating higher concentrations of industrial pollutants.

Chemical analysis procedure

These subsamples were air dried, homogenized, and sieved through 2 mm mesh to separate the soil skeleton or artifacts. Then, about 250 mg of dried soil sample was digested using 50% (v/v) HNO3, placed in a Teflon vessel, diluted to 108 ml, and transferred to 15 ml vials for Inductively Coupled Plasma Mass Spectrometry (ICP-MS) analysis. The basic procedure applied was BCR sequential extraction, known for its high reproducibility in systematic studies of trace metals in soil components.

To verify the performance of the ICP-MS measurements, the certified reference material Soil GBW-07408 was used for quality control. Although individual concentrations of Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, and Lu in the reference material were not explicitly reported, the instrumental detection limits (IDL) and method detection limits (LOD) were determined following standard procedures. IDL was calculated from the instrumental background signal under low-blank conditions, while LOD was derived as IDL corrected for the baseline concentration (IDL-25%). Measurement uncertainty (MU) was estimated based on the combined contributions of LOD-25% and baseline correction. The use of the reference material and multi-element calibration standards ensured that the method provided adequate sensitivity and accuracy for trace-level determination of REEs in soils.

In Poland, no official permissible limits currently exist for the concentrations of the analyzed REEs (including Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, Lu) in soils. This is due to their very low natural abundance and the lack of specific regulations. Therefore, this study does not provide threshold values for these elements.

The measured concentrations of heavy REEs were utilized to calculate the values of two soil pollution indicators, EF and IG, that have previously been successfully employed in soil contamination studies (Reimann & Caritat, 2000; Krachler et al., 2003, Williams and Antoine, 2020). Following the approach proposed by Krachler et al., 2003, values EF of REEs were calculated relative to concentration of Sc (local Sc background), due to its relatively stable geochemical behavior, low mobility, and minimal anthropogenic influence. Both the EF and IG are semi-quantitative, and their reliability depends on the careful selection of appropriate natural background concentrations and conservative reference elements (e.g., Sc or Fe), which are assumed to be unaffected by pollution. Furthermore, they do not account for soil properties influencing element mobility, nor do they directly reflect bioavailability or ecological risk. Therefore, these indices should be complemented by additional geochemical and environmental data for comprehensive pollution assessment (Loska et al., 1997).

Software used

In this study, data processing and visualization were carried out using QGIS and Statistica software. QGIS was used for creating maps of sampling locations and Land use analysis.

Statistica 13 was used for statistical analyses, including descriptive statistics, correlation analysis, and ANOVA analysis.

Results

Preliminary magnetometric soil cores analysis

Figure 6 presents average magnetic susceptibility profiles for each sampling location, calculated from all collected soil cores in particular sampling area. In forested areas (USIR F1, F2, F3 and Jizera F) magnetic susceptibility typically reached its maximum at shallow depths, around 3 cm to 6 cm below the soil surface. This observation suggested that magnetic particles, likely of anthropogenic origin, were primarily retained in the topsoil layers. In contrast, profiles of magnetic susceptibility from arable areas exhibited distributions of magnetic susceptibility with wider peak, with relatively constant magnetic susceptibility values starting from a depth of approximately 5 cm. This reflected the soil mixing effect of agricultural practices such as plowing, which redistributed magnetic particles more evenly throughout the upper soil horizon. A different pattern was observed in the mountainous areas like Jizera O, where several distinct peaks of magnetic susceptibility were identified at various depths. These irregularities may indicate a more complex waste deposition history, possibly related to historical industrial activities in the area, such as glassmaking.Fig. 6Average magnetic susceptibility in soil cores collected in sampling sites USIR O1, USIR O2, USIR F1, USIR F2, USIR F3, Jizera O, and Jizera F

The highest REEs concentrations in soils were observed in the Jizera Mountains, which is consistent with naturally elevated REEs levels in this region, including heavy REEs (Kowalczyk & Mazanek, 1989). This was particularly evident in the Jizera F area, located on a hill, where individual soil horizons, especially the organic and humus layers, were notably thin. Exceptionally high concentrations of Ho and Yb (4.3 mg/kg and 10.1 mg/kg, respectively) were recorded In this area,, exceeding those in other areas by up to an order of magnitude compared to the USIR sites (see Table 4 for maximum values). Significant differences were also observed regarding the remaining heavy REEs.

A particularly noteworthy finding is the strong association between the Jizera F area, where the highest REEs concentrations were recorded, and the highest values of both the coefficient of variation and positive skewness. This indicates that although peak REEs concentrations were observed here, they may be largely driven by a few extreme values. This interpretation is supported by the comparison of minimum values (see Table 4), which did not differ substantially from those recorded in other study areas. In contrast, the second site in the Jizera Mountains, Jizera O, did not display similarly elevated REEs concentrations when compared to the other sites, including Jizera F.

Several conclusions can be drawn. First, since the Jizera F and Jizera O areas are adjacent, it is unlikely that long-range atmospheric deposition alone is responsible for the elevated REEs concentrations observed in Jizera F soils. Second, the historical operation of the glassworks despite associated poor waste management practices in the Jizera O area also do not appear to have significantly increased REEs concentrations in local soils.

When comparing the measured REEs concentration in USIR soils (Fig. 7) across the areas USIR O1, USIR O2, USIR F1, USIR F2, and USIR F3, several noteworthy observations emerge. First, significantly higher REEs concentrations were recorded in agricultural areas compared to forested ones. This trend was consistently observed across all analyzed heavy REEs: Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, and Lu. These findings suggest that elevated REEs levels in agricultural soils may be linked to cultivation practices, particularly the use of fertilizers. Furthermore, since the areas USIR O2, USIR F1, and USIR F2 are located in close proximity to one another, it is likely that the level of anthropogenic pressure from industrial and energy-related dust emissions was comparable across these sites.

Another interesting observation pertains to the USIR O1 area (Fig. 7), where slightly higher REEs concentrations were recorded compared to other agricultural areas. This suggests that emissions from waste heaps associated with zinc and lead ore processing may contribute to elevated levels of heavy REEs in the soil.

In forested areas, REEs concentrations were generally similar across the USIR F1, USIR F2, and USIR F3 sites. However, the largest site USIR F3 (Fig. 7) exhibited significantly higher values of both the coefficient of variation and skewness. This is likely attributable to the much larger size of this site (5.5 km2) compared to the other forested areas (0.1 km2), which introduces greater heterogeneity in terms of soil conditions, vegetation cover, and other environmental factors. Additionally, reclamation activities following past sand extraction at USIR F3 may have further contributed to the elevated variation and skewness in REEs concentrations.

When these findings are compared with the average values of the EF and IG indices shown in Fig. 8, several noteworthy conclusions emerge. Unlike the average REEs concentrations, EF and IG values did not exhibit so significant differences between individual REEs (Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, and Lu).

As shown in Fig. 8, the highest EF values were observed in the mountain areas, with levels nearly twice as high as those recorded in arable areas and forests, averaging around 3, which suggests moderate enrichment. Values of EF and IG for forested and arable areas were comparable and were equal to about to about 1 (EF) and 1.5 (IG). According to the classification from Table 2, these values indicate low REEs enrichment in soils. These results align with the results of previous studies suggesting naturally elevated REEs concentrations in the Jizera region (Kowalczyk & Mazanek, 1989).

The difference between the highest IG in arable land and the highest EF in the mountains was due to how these indices were calculated and the background values used. IG was calculated based on the natural background concentration of each REEs, while EF was calculated relative to the background concentration of Sc (see Table 3). Since background REEs levels in the mountainous areas were higher than in arable land, IG values appeared lower in the mountains despite possibly higher concentrations of REEs.

A one-way ANOVA was performed to evaluate the differences in mean concentrations of rare earth elements and enrichment factors among three land-use types: arable field, forest, and mountainous area. The results revealed statistically significant differences (p < 0.05) for 14 out of 15 variables. The strongest effect was observed for IG Lu (F = 158.44, p < 0.001), followed by IG Tm, IG Er, and EF Ho, indicating substantial variation in REEs accumulation across the studied environments. Only EF Lu did not show a significant difference between groups (F = 1.91, p = 0.151), suggesting a more uniform distribution of this element regardless of land use. These findings highlight the influence of land use and environmental conditions on REEs behavior and potential anthropogenic enrichment, particularly in mountainous areas.

Despite generally high correlations across all area types, the weakest relationships were consistently observed in the mountainous sites Jizera F and Jizera O. In these locations, REEs concentration distributions exhibited high positive skewness (see Table 4), especially in the Jizera F area. This pattern is likely influenced by the shallow upper soil horizons typical of the region, which may limit the accumulation of pollutants. Moreover, the underlying geology of this region is naturally enriched in REEs, contributing to variability in soil concentrations.

Discussion

So far, the number of studies addressing REEs concentrations in soils, particularly in the areas covered by this study, has been relatively limited. However, the REEs concentrations measured in this study are consistent with those reported in previous works (Kaczor-Kurzawa et al., 2022; Kokowska-Pawłowska et al.; Kwecko, 2016; Lewińska-Preis et al., 2021; Nowak & Kokowska-Pawłowska, 2017; Sager et al., 2024; Lisiak-Zielińska et al., 2024). As indicated by the EF and IG index analysis, REEs concentrations generally corresponded to low enrichment levels. Clearly elevated values were recorded only in the Jizera Mountains, which aligns with earlier findings (Ramos et al., 2016).

It is important to note that while enrichment indices help distinguish natural REEs input from the anthropogenic one, absolute concentration values may still reflect lithological differences. The contrasting geology of the Jizera Mountains and the Upper Silesian Industrial Region likely influences REEs levels, suggesting that spatial variations should be interpreted with caution. This underscores the need to incorporate detailed geological data and site-specific background values in future research (Knut et al., 2025).

The correlation analysis demonstrated strong linear relationships between the concentrations of individual REEs and distinct clusters corresponding to forest, agricultural, and mountainous areas were observed. REEs concentrations were strongly correlated overall, suggesting similar geochemical behavior or a common geogenic source across all areas. Correlation analysis aimed to explore the mutual dependencies between REEs concentrations rather than to identify specific pollution sources, and observed patterns imply the dominance of a single, likely natural, source influencing the distribution of REEs in the soils, despite differences in land use, rather than diverse anthropogenic inputs.

Previous studies on the relationship between REEs concentrations and landscape patterns (Lisiak-Zielińska et al., 2024) have shown significant correlations between REEs contents in plant leaves and, to a lesser extent, in soils. A similar tendency was observed in this study. Statistically significant differences in REEs concentrations were found between soils from arable areas, forests, and mountain areas. However, the most substantial contrasts appeared to result primarily from geological factors. Specifically, differences were evident between mountain areas with crystalline bedrock composed of monzogranites and granodiorites, and the USIR areas characterized mainly by Quaternary deposits such as sands and gravels.

It should also be noted that the interpretation of REEs in this study is limited by the absence of site-specific data on soil properties such as granulometric composition, organic matter content, and pH, all of which are known to affect REEs mobility and retention. Nonetheless, as noted by Lisiak-Zielińska et al. (2024) and Gwenzi et al. (2018), recent years have seen an increase in REEs inputs into the soil environment, which may complicate the interpretation of underlying geochemical processes. Future studies should incorporate these parameters to better disentangle the effects of natural soil conditions from anthropogenic influences.

Conclusions

The results provide valuable insights into the distribution and assessment of REEs in soils from geochemically diverse and industrially influenced regions. Although the study areas were considered industrially influenced or polluted, REEs concentrations were generally low, with averages up to 1.5 mg/kg. The highest concentrations were observed in the Jizera Mountains, reflecting geology rather than anthropogenic influence. Soils on crystalline rocks in mountainous areas had much higher REEs levels than lowland soils on Quaternary deposits, highlighting the need to consider geological background and site-specific references in assessments.

Comparison of REEs concentrations between agricultural and adjacent forest soils, excluding mountainous sites, showed some clear differences possibly linked to fertilizer use or soil disturbance. It is worth noting that the results regarding the impact of land cover on heavy REEs concentrations may not be fully conclusive. However, they suggest a limited influence of land use on REEs accumulation in rural environments and also imply that vegetation and ecosystem traits may affect REEs retention, especially under industrial pressure. This points to the need for further ecological studies on REEs cycling.

Strong internal correlations among heavy REEs (Ho, Er, Tm, Yb, Lu) across all sites indicate a common origin or consistent geochemical behavior, supporting the combined use of geoaccumulation index (IG) and enrichment factor (EF) as reliable tools for pollution assessment.

Although this study did not include detailed data on specific soil properties, its findings contribute to a broader understanding of REEs behavior in soils. Future research could benefit from incorporating additional parameters such as texture, organic matter, and pH to further clarify the interplay between natural variability and anthropogenic influence.
