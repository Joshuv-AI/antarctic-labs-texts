Spontaneous resolution of neonatal umbilical vein thrombosis in the absence of anticoagulant therapy: a case report
Authors: Yu-Jing Long, Yong Luo
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundUmbilical vein thrombosis (UVT) is an uncommon but potentially serious neonatal condition. While most cases are associated with risk factors such as catheterization or congenital anomalies, spontaneous resolution without anticoagulation remains rare.Case presentationA full-term Chinese female neonate, small for gestational age (birth weight 2670 g, < 10th percentile), was delivered via emergency cesarean section at 39 weeks and 1 day because of placental abruption and chorioamnionitis. The newborn presented with poor respiratory effort and cyanosis, with Apgar scores of 6, 10, and 10 at 1, 5, and 10 min, respectively. Initial postnatal ultrasonography revealed a moderately echogenic lesion in the intrahepatic umbilical vein, which was consistent with the results of UVT. The infant required initial respiratory support, empirical antibiotics, and myocardial protection therapy. Laboratory tests revealed mild metabolic acidosis, electrolyte imbalance, and elevated white blood cell count and elevated myocardial enzymes, with no evidence of coagulopathy or hereditary thrombophilia. During the first week, the patient’s clinical status improved steadily with supportive care. Follow-up ultrasonography on day 5 confirmed a stable thrombus with no progression. By the 1-month follow-up, ultrasonography demonstrated complete thrombus resolution and obliteration of the umbilical vein lumen without residual blood flow. No anticoagulant therapy or surgical intervention was administered. The patient was discharged in good condition without complications.ConclusionThis case highlights the potential for spontaneous resolution of neonatal UVT under conservative treatment without the need for anticoagulation. Although UVT is rare and lacks standardized treatment guidelines, early detection via ultrasonography enables timely monitoring and intervention, reducing the risk of complications. In this case, perinatal risk factors such as placental abruption, chorioamnionitis, and neonatal asphyxia may have contributed to thrombus formation. Further studies are needed to explore the role of perinatal inflammatory conditions in neonatal thrombosis and to establish evidence-based management strategies.

Introduction

UVT is a rare neonatal condition commonly associated with preterm birth or invasive procedures [1, 2]. Although infrequent, UVT can lead to serious clinical consequences if not diagnosed and managed promptly. With advances in imaging techniques, particularly ultrasonography, early detection of UVT is now possible during both the fetal and neonatal periods. This report presents a case of isolated UVT in a full-term neonate without apparent clinical symptoms. Early diagnosis through ultrasonography enabled successful monitoring and management of the thrombus, avoiding invasive interventions. This case highlights the importance of early recognition and raising clinical awareness of UVT.

Case presentation

A full-term Chinese female neonate who is small for gestational age, weighing 2.67 kg, was delivered via emergency cesarean section at 39 weeks and 1 day of gestation because of placental abruption and chorioamnionitis. The mother, a primigravida, was diagnosed with placental abruption and chorioamnionitis following episodes of paroxysmal abdominal pain and vaginal bleeding. An abnormal fetal heart rate was detected by nonstress testing (NST), prompting urgent surgical delivery.

Immediately after birth, the neonate presented with weak respiration, cyanosis, and poor responsiveness. The Apgar score was 6 at 1 min owing to compromised respiration, muscle tone, reflexes, and color; the score increased to 10 at both 5 and 10 min through emergency treatment. The maternal prenatal course was otherwise unremarkable, with negative prenatal screening and no evidence of gestational diabetes or preeclampsia. Initial postnatal resuscitation included positive pressure ventilation, airway clearance, oxygen administration, and thermal regulation. The neonate was then transferred to the neonatal ward for further evaluation and management.

On physical examination, mild perioral cyanosis was noted, along with a 30 × 30 mm palpable cephalohematoma on the scalp. Pulmonary auscultation revealed coarse breath sounds with crackles. Meconium-stained amniotic fluid was observed in the upper airway, raising concern for meconium aspiration syndrome (MAS). The neonate’s vital signs included a body temperature of 36.8 °C, a blood pressure of 68/30 mmHg, a heart rate of 179 bpm, a respiratory rate of 68 breaths per minute, and an oxygen saturation of 90%. Abdominal ultrasonography revealed a moderately echogenic lesion measuring 4.8 × 4.3 mm in the intrahepatic segment of the umbilical vein (Fig. 1A). Color Doppler imaging revealed peripheral blood flow surrounding the lesion with absent flow centrally, which was consistent with the results of UVT (Fig. 1B). The umbilical artery appeared atretic. Subsequent laboratory evaluation revealed mild metabolic acidosis on blood gas analysis (pH 7.332, base excess − 8.8 mmol/L, lactate 2.18 mmol/L) with adequate oxygenation (PaO₂ 159.0 mmHg, PaCO₂ 33.0 mmHg, and SpO₂ 99.6%). Electrolyte analysis indicated hypernatremia (Na⁺ 164.8 mmol/L), hypokalemia (K⁺ 2.42 mmol/L), hyperchloremia (Cl⁻ 116 mmol/L), and hypocalcemia (ionized calcium 0.37 mmol/L). A complete blood test revealed leukocytosis (WBC 16.46 × 10⁹/L), with neutrophils and lymphocytes each accounting for 15.2%. The infant’s myocardial enzymes were abnormal. Creatine kinase-MB (CK-MB) was concentration 84 U/L, creatine kinase (CK) concentration was 679 U/L, lactate dehydrogenase (LDH) concentration was 484 U/L, α-hydroxybutyrate dehydrogenase (α-HBDH) concentration was 358 U/L, lactate dehydrogenase isoenzyme concentration was 174 U/L, and ischemia-modified albumin (IMA) concentration was 89.3 U/mL. The hemoglobin concentration was 157 g/L. Inflammatory markers were not significantly elevated: procalcitonin (PCT) was mildly elevated at 0.28 ng/mL, and C-reactive protein was normal. Coagulation tests were unremarkable. The antithrombin III (ATIII) concentration was 53%, the fibrin degradation products (FDP) were concentration were 2.72 μg/mL, the prothrombin time (PT) was 14.2 s, the international normalized ratio (INR) was 1.11, the prothrombin activity (PTA) was 84.9%, the fibrinogen (FIB) was 2.23 g/L, the activated partial thromboplastin time (aPTT) was 41.6 s, the thrombin time (TT) was 17.2 s, and the D-dimer was 0.442 mg/L. The patient’s platelet count was normal at 234 × 10⁹/L. Given that the patient showed no signs of systemic bleeding or multiple thrombotic events and had normal coagulation parameters, thrombophilia-related laboratory tests were not performed. Additionally, owing to the limited diagnostic specificity of these tests during the neonatal period, their absence did not impact the clinical decision-making process.

On the basis of on the clinical presentation, laboratory data, and ultrasonographic findings, the final diagnosis was intrahepatic UVT complicated by MAS.

The patient was treated with supportive care, including oxygen therapy in an incubator, empirical antibiotic therapy with penicillin and ceftizoxime, myocardial protection with adenosine cyclophosphate, and serial ultrasound monitoring of the umbilical vein thrombus. By day 3 of treatment, the patient’s condition had improved, with the WBC count decreasing to 9.31 × 10⁹/L and the PCT concentration decreasing to 0.13 ng/mL. Oxygen therapy was discontinued while antibiotic and cardioprotective treatment were continued. By day 5, the clinical condition remained stable. The WBC count had decreased to 7.39 × 10⁹/L, and the PCT concentration had decreased to < 0.04 ng/mL. Repeat ultrasound revealed a persistent thrombus in the intrahepatic umbilical vein measuring 5.1 × 3.3 mm, without evidence of progression, suggesting a stable thrombus and the need for continued observation. The patient remained clinically stable and was discharged on day 7 without complications.

The patient’s blood pressure remained stable during hospitalization, and serial blood pressure measurements remained within the range of 62–72/30–42 mmHg, suggesting hemodynamic stability during the acute phase.

At the 1-month follow-up, abdominal ultrasonography (Fig. 1C) confirmed complete resolution of the thrombus, with no residual thrombus or obstruction in the intrahepatic umbilical vein. Color Doppler imaging (Fig. 1D) revealed the absence of blood flow in the affected segment, indicating obliteration of the umbilical vein. At 18 months of age, the growth and development of the infant were normal, and thrombosis and other sequelae did not recur. Discussion

Venous thromboembolism (VTE) is relatively uncommon in neonates. In the neonatal intensive care unit (NICU), the incidence of clinically evident thrombosis is approximately 2 per 1,000 neonates [3, 4]. Commonly affected sites include the renal vein, portal vein, and deep veins of the extremities, whereas UVT is rare and typically associated with umbilical abnormalities, catheter placement, or infection [4, 5]. Isolated primary UVT without identifiable risk factors is particularly uncommon.

In neonates, the most important risk factors for thrombosis can be divided into three categories: (1) maternal factors, including infection, placental diseases, metabolic syndrome, antiphospholipid syndrome, inherited thrombophilia, and premature rupture of membranes; (2) neonatal factors, including central venous catheter placement, sepsis, and perinatal asphyxia; and (3) risk factors specific to central venous catheter–related VTE, including low birth weight, prolonged catheter duration (> 6 days), umbilical venous catheter (UVC) malplacement, and mechanical ventilation [6–8].

The occurrence of UVT in this case may have been be related to placental abruption, chorioamnionitis, meconium aspiration syndrome (MAS), perinatal asphyxia, and the need for mechanical ventilation. This aligns with findings by Paola Saracco et al. [1], who emphasized the association between neonatal thrombosis and placental pathology, as well as inflammatory conditions such as MAS. In contrast, Gerdina H. Dubbink-Verheij et al. [2] identified invasive procedures as the predominant cause of UVT. These differing perspectives highlight the multifactorial etiology of neonatal thrombosis and the complex interplay of perinatal risk factors.

According to Virchow’s triad, thrombosis arises from three main mechanisms: blood flow stasis, endothelial injury, and hypercoagulability. Inflammatory conditions such as MAS and chorioamnionitis may trigger systemic inflammation, causing endothelial injury and activation of the coagulation cascade, thereby promoting thrombosis [9]. These processes may also induce the expression of procoagulant factors such as von Willebrand factor (vWF). Neonates have an immature hemostatic system with lower levels of coagulation factors and natural anticoagulants, increasing their susceptibility to thrombosis under pathological conditions [10, 11]. Perinatal asphyxia and mechanical ventilation, both of which were present in this case, are additional recognized risk factors [12, 13].

The neonate presented with hypernatremia and hypokalemia. Previous studies have indicated that hypernatremia may be associated with an increased risk of thrombosis. Severe hypernatremic dehydration can lead to elevated serum osmolarity, cellular dehydration, and increased blood viscosity [14]. This impairs microcirculatory perfusion, and promotes platelet aggregation and activation of the coagulation system, resulting in a hypercoagulable state that subsequently induces cerebral venous sinus thrombosis [15]. Additional cases indicate that severe neonatal hypernatremia can simultaneously cause cerebral venous and aortic thrombosis, suggesting that hypernatremic dehydration may trigger thrombotic events across multiple vascular beds [16]. Evidence for a direct causal relationship between hypokalemia and venous thrombosis remains relatively limited. However, a case report indicated that severe hypokalemia may cooccur with arterial thrombotic events such as acute coronary thrombosis [17].

Management of VTE includes supportive care, anticoagulation, thrombolysis, or surgery, depending on the site, extent, and clinical consequences of thrombosis, as well as the bleeding risk associated with treatment [18]. With respect to anticoagulant therapy, unfractionated heparin and are warfarin are traditionally used for the treatment of thrombosis [18, 19], but these treatmens have several limitations and challenges for newborns. In the clinic, low-molecular-weight heparins (LMWHs) are the optimal choice for neonatal anticoagulation, and therapy is mainly based on heparin administration [19].

In this case, the thrombus was limited in size and did not cause complete occlusion. Additionally, the patient's family preferred conservative management. Therefore, anticoagulation was not administered. The thrombus resolved spontaneously with supportive care and imaging surveillance, which is consistent with previous reports showing that some neonatal thrombi can resolve without anticoagulant therapy [18, 20, 21]. Additionally, some scholars have observed thrombus formation during the closure process of venous catheters, suggesting that thrombus may be associated with vascular fibrosis rather than be a complication [22].

Ultrasonography is the first-line imaging modality for diagnosing VTE in neonates, although venography remains the gold standard [23]. Ultrasonography offers multiple advantages: it is noninvasive, radiation-free, and highly reproducible—making it ideal for clinical use in neonatal patients. In neonates with high-risk factors such as placental abruption, MAS, and perinatal asphyxia, ultrasound enables early detection of venous thrombosis and timely clinical intervention. This case highlights the crucial role of ultrasonography in both the early diagnosis and follow-up of UVT.

The limitations of this case include the absence of thrombophilia screening, a relatively short follow-up period, and the lack of assessment of capillary refill time and urine output. Moreover, it relies on a single case. Larger multicenter studies are warranted to clarify the relationships between UVT, placental pathology, refill time, urine output, and perinatal inflammatory states.

Conclusion

UVT is a rare condition that can impact neonatal outcomes. In this case, the thrombus gradually resolved without complications following appropriate management. Early diagnosis and timely intervention are crucial for improving neonatal outcomes. Furthermore, early ultrasonographic detection of UVT combined with prompt treatment can effectively minimize complications.
