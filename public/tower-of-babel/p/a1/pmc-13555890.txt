Group-based interventions for individuals with dual pathology: a preliminary focus group study
Authors: Lídia Susana Mendes Moutinho, Joana Teixeira, José Afonso Paixão, Sónia Maria Monteiro Ferreira, Olga Maria de Sousa Valentim, Paulo Rosário Carvalho Seabra
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundDual Pathology has a major impact on the individual’s life and on society and is associated with worsening severity and persistence of both mental illness and addiction, which is why scientific evidence increasingly points treatment of patients with Dual Pathology.ObjectiveEvaluate how participants in group therapy experience the group and their relationship with the professionals.MethodsA qualitative descriptive study using focus groups was conducted with 12 participants attending a Dual Pathology Group in a psychiatric hospital in Portugal. The data was analyzed using Braun & Clarke’s reflective thematic analysis methodology. Transcripts of interview recordings were imported into the MAXQDA 24 qualitative software program for thematic content analysis.Results: The results point to the positive contribution of Group teaching carried out by nurses in improving literacy about substance use and psychiatric disorder. The four themes obtained were: Meaning of the Dual Pathology Group; Importance of Healthcare Professionals; Learning and Knowledge Acquisition; Relationship between Alcohol Use and Psychiatric Disorder.ConclusionIncreasing literacy about substance use and psychiatric pathology seems to have an impact on adherence to treatment for patients with Dual Pathology.Keywords: Dual pathology, Dual diagnosis, Group, Psychiatric nursing, Mental disorders, Psychoeducation

Background

The term Dual Pathology (DP) refers to the coexistence of Substance Use Disorder (SUD) and other Psychiatric Disorders [1, 2]. Several epidemiological studies have documented the close relationship between them [3, 4], highlighting that this comorbidity exceeds 50% in psychiatric clinical populations, approximately 17% of the general population and 40% of vulnerable populations including homeless individuals, people with HIV infection, and individuals with hepatitis B and C infection [5, 6].

According to the World Health Organization (WHO) (2022), individuals with mental health disorders are more likely to develop SDU, while, conversely, one-third of people who use psychoactive substances (legal and illicit substances) present with at least one psychiatric disorder. SUD encompasses ten distinct categories of substances, including tobacco, caffeine, cannabis, alcohol or opioids, among others. It is characterized by a cluster of cognitive, behavioural, and physiological symptoms indicating that the individual continues to use the substance despite experiencing significant problems caused by its consumption [7].

Specifically, regarding alcohol, evidence indicates that a substantial proportion of individuals with psychiatric disorders may develop alcohol-related problems [8]. Alcohol consumption, driven by various contextual and individual factors, is often used as a means of coping with different mood states and can provide diverse personal experiences associated with its use [9]. However, alcohol use is also associated with loss of control over consumption and the development of dependence [4] a condition referred to as Alcohol Use Disorder (AUD).

Another common factor among psychiatric disorders and AUD, identified by scientific evidence, is a lack of knowledge about the disease process regarding causes, precipitating factors, treatment, and strategies to prevent psychiatric disorders decompensation and substance relapses [7]. This underscores the need for interventions aimed at increasing knowledge about the disease process and insight, as well as treatment adherence in individuals with DP, with the goal of stabilizing psychiatric disorders and interrupting substance use and/or maintaining abstinence.

In this context, psychoeducation stands out as a highly relevant intervention in the care of individuals with DP, as noted by Labbé, et al. 2025 [10] due to its consistent outcomes and ease of implementation. This intervention can be delivered by nurses, as its main objective is to educate individuals and target groups about the illness, treatment, diagnosis and prognosis, as well as strategies for adapting to the condition [11].

Therapeutic approaches may be conducted individually or in group settings, provided they address both the psychiatric disorder and AUD simultaneously [12]. Integrated treatment appears to be more effective in reducing affective episodes and maintaining abstinence [13, 14].

The repercussions of alcohol use and psychiatric disorders manifest across multiple dimensions of an individual’s life, including physical, familial, and socio-professional domains. These impacts necessitate an interdisciplinary approach aimed at enhancing therapeutic adherence and promoting the psychosocial rehabilitation of individuals with DP. Interventions that address the various compromised spheres are essential for mitigating vulnerability factors and supporting the process of reintegration and functional recovery in individuals with DP.

Given the increasing number of individuals with DP seeking care in psychiatric services, a group-based intervention—the “Dual Pathology Group” (DPG)—was implemented in a psychiatric hospital, specifically designed for outpatients. The group could include individuals who had or had not previously undergone hospitalization in a hospital setting. The inclusion criteria are having AUD and another associated psychiatric disorder and being followed up in one of the treatment unit’s services (either outpatient appointments or day care). The medical diagnosis for these disorders (psychiatric disorders and AUD) is defined by medical evaluation according to Diagnostic and Statistical Manual of Mental Disorders (DSM)-5-TR criteria [7].

This is an open group, in which new participants may be admitted on a weekly basis, up to a maximum of 12 individuals with DP. The weekly intervention consists of sessions lasting approximately 60 minutes.

The multidisciplinary team that facilitates the DPG is composed of different professionals. A nurse is present in every session and share the co facilitation with psychologist or a psychiatrist. All team members have experience in group facilitation and in the treatment of individuals with DP.

The DPG comprises 4 stages. The first involves the initial contact with the group, where new members are introduced (when necessary) and health status is monitored (alcohol consumption and psychiatric symptoms) during the period between group sessions. The second stage includes psychoeducational intervention, which begins with the clarification of the theme, objective, and its presentation through simple language, considering the participants’ experiences. The third stage involves questioning about the theme, expression of feelings, and sharing of experiences. In the fourth stage or conclusion, a summary of the session is conducted, and the understanding of the different group members is evaluated.

In the context of clinical practice and integrated within the multidisciplinary team, nurses, when act, respond to people’s needs by mobilizing the Nursing Process. This is the professional clinical method composed of the stages of data collection, nursing diagnosis, planning, implementation, and nursing evaluation [15]. Given the importance of using standardized language among nursing professionals, nurses should utilize the International Classification for Nursing Practice (ICNP) to determine diagnoses. It facilitates the comparative analyses when nursing assistance is focus and this is a way to systematize intervention Nurses should use this standardized language even when they act in a multidisciplinary team [16]. Individuals with DP, when assessed by the nurse who is part of the multidisciplinary team, present needs that can be identified and classified through nursing diagnoses. Based on these diagnoses, specific interventions are planned and implemented, tailored to the particularities of each individual. This allows nursing care to be systematized across all stages of the nursing process, ensuring a humanized approach grounded in scientific evidence [17, 18].

Accordingly, among individuals with DP, the predominant nursing diagnoses—based on ICNP terminology (2019) [19]—include: “non-adherence to Therapeutic Regime (10030365),” “Compromised Knowledge about the disease (10023826),” and “Alcohol Abuse (10002137).”

“Non-adherence to Therapeutic Regime” (10030365) can be defined “Not following or agreeing with the treatment regimen - self-initiated action for promoting well-being; recovery and rehabilitation; following guidelines without deviation; committed to a set of actions or behaviors. Complies with the treatment regimen; takes medications as prescribed; changes behavior for the better, signs of healing, seeks medications on the indicated date, internalizes the value of health behavior, and follows treatment instructions) (p.38). The “Compromised Knowledge about the disease” (10023826), according to ICNP is “knowledge about the compromised or ineffective pathological process “(p.155) and the “Alcohol Abuse” (10002137) is presented by “Substance Abuse: Misuse of alcohol”.

After four years had elapsed since the onset of the group intervention, the need arose to evaluate its effectiveness as well as the significance of inclusion in a therapeutic group. More specifically, in this section of the study, the aim is to evaluate how participants experience the group and their relationship with the professionals. Thus, this research was guided by the research question “What is the significance of group intervention for individuals receiving outpatient treatment for DP?”

Methods

Study Design:

A descriptive qualitative approach was chosen, which involved face-to-face Focus Groups (FG), and was designed using the methodological framework of Krueger and Casey (2015) [20]. The Consolidated Criteria for Reporting Qualitative Research (COREQ) checklist [21] was followed.

The choice of FG is justified by their potential to explore in depth the perceptions, experiences, and shared meanings among participants who possess similar characteristics and life experiences [22]. This technique makes it possible to capture interactional dynamics, identify areas of consensus and divergence, and access content that emerges more readily in collective dialogue than in individual interviews. Moreover, it supports the production of rich and contextualized data, thereby providing a comprehensive understanding of the phenomenon under study [23, 24]. In conducting the focus groups, the moderator followed a semi-structured guide; however, data generation was not limited to participants’ direct responses to the predefined questions. Consistent with the methodological principles of focus group research, interaction among participants played a central role in shaping the discussion [23]. Throughout the sessions, participants responded not only to the moderator but also to one another, expressing agreement, elaborating on shared experiences, or differentiating their own perspectives. These spontaneous exchanges frequently redirected the flow of conversation beyond the linear sequence of the interview guide and contributed to the emergence of collective reflections as well as divergent viewpoints. For this reason, the analysis considered both the content of individual responses and the dynamic interactions within the group, recognizing (these as integral components of the focus group methodology [23].

Context

Sampling and recruitment

Two FGs were conducted, comprising a total of 12 individuals with DP (6 in the first group and 6 in the second focus group) receiving outpatient treatment at a psychiatric hospital in a Portuguese city.

Participants were selected using a convenience sampling procedure. The inclusion criteria were as follows: (1) individuals with DP (AUD and psychiatric disorder) (≥18 years old); (2) able to communicate in Portuguese; (3) willing to participate in this study; (4) individuals who have been attending the DP group for at least 8 sessions in 6 months. Exclusion criteria include a positive alcohol test at the time of the group session, symptoms suggestive of psychotic decompensation, cognitive impairment, or insufficient proficiency in the portuguese language.

Individuals with DP who met this criterion were invited to take part in the FG after the objectives of the study had been explained to them. Those who simultaneously met the inclusion criteria and agreed to participate were selected for the FG. The sessions were scheduled for times that were convenient for both participants and the researchers who facilitated them. Two focus groups were conducted in different weeks.

Data collection

The FG sessions took place during December 2022, in person, in the room where the DPG meets weekly, at a different moment of DPG. Each FG lasted approximately 60 minutes. Before each FG, participants filled out a brief questionnaire with some sociodemographic variables. The FG sessions were recorded with participants’ permission and later transcribed for more efficient analysis of discussion content and responses.

The interaction in the FG was facilitated with a set of questions (Table 1), and participants were encouraged to share their opinions.

Ethics

The study was conducted in accordance with the principles of the Declaration of Helsinki and was approved by the Ethics Committee of the institution where the group took place (protocol code 010–2019, approved on January 15, 2020). Written informed consent was obtained from all participants prior to their participation in the focus groups.

Data analysis

The content of responses was analyzed using the reflexive thematic analysis methodology of Braun and Clarke (2006) [25]. All FG interviews were transcribed and imported into the qualitative software program MAXQDA for data management.

It was decided not to return the focus group transcripts to participants for review, given the collective nature of the group dynamics. In FGs, overlapping and simultaneous interactions frequently occur, which may hinder the individual validation of each participant’s contribution [26]. Moreover, providing participants with the verbatim transcripts of what was said in the group context could compromise anonymity and potentially generate discomfort or inhibition among participants [27].

According to Braun and Clarke [25] thematic analysis involves six stages. The first is becoming familiar with the data through reading all transcriptions, followed by the second stage, which concerns code generation. Reading the transcriptions allowed relevant information to be grouped into intangible codes.

In the third stage, which corresponds to theme searching, codes were categorized into topics likely related to the meaning of the DPG and the significance of healthcare professionals. Reviewing themes (fourth stage) was confirmed by reviewing all codes and the dataset.

Fifthly, topics were defined and named, producing a final thematic tree. Finally (sixth stage), thematic review was used to help write the report. Coding involved two (co)investigators: the principal investigator (L.M.) coded all transcriptions, which were then independently coded by co-researchers with scientific training and competency in data processing (S.F. and A.P.). When differences or disagreements arose during coding, coders reflected until consensus was reached. A fourth investigator (P.S.) was consulted to reach a final decision. Selected quotations from the portuguese transcription were translated, italicized, and inserted into the subsequent report.

Data analysis was not guided by the goal of achieving data saturation, as this exploratory study, conducted with a small, purposive sample, aimed primarily to understand and describe participants’ views [28]. data saturation was not pursued because the study followed an exploratory design and aimed to capture a range of perspectives rather than achieve thematic exhaustiveness.

Study rigor

The research team, consisting of the principal investigator (female) and the co-investigator (male), both with clinical experience and expertise in dual pathology, was responsible for planning and conducting the interviews. This composition aimed to prevent participants of the same gender from identifying too strongly with each other, thereby avoiding the predominance of a single gender in the discussions. In addition, it was intended to minimise potential feelings of vulnerability in participants in the presence of a moderator of a given gender [29]. To ensure the reliability and validity of the process, the interviewers took part in training and reflexive sessions, which included discussion of the focus group interview guide.

The principal investigator demonstrated effectiveness in facilitating discussions and fostering participant engagement. Prior contact with participants, during the process of obtaining informed consent and collecting sociodemographic data, helped establish an empathetic and assertive relationship. During the interviews, the moderator maintained a neutral stance, avoided value judgments, and allowed sufficient time for participants to express their views.

Conclusions were reviewed by co-authors (investigator triangulation) and resulted in theme and subtheme name revisions to legitimately capture the essence of conclusions. We also maintained an audit trail, which included field notes, coded transcriptions, and comments and revisions from group coding meetings, following best practices in qualitative research.

Field notes were recorded throughout the entire data collection period, immediately after each observation session. They were written by the principal investigator and the co-investigator, both of whom facilitated the FG.

These notes were systematically organised and reviewed alongside the interview transcripts. The field notes played a central role in theme generation, as they allowed for the contextualisation of participants’ statements, the identification of behavioural patterns, and the capture of nuances that did not emerge explicitly in the interviews. In addition, the notes helped guide the development of the final interpretations by providing a more comprehensive understanding of the setting, practices, and social dynamics observed.

Research team and reflexivity

The research team consisted of two certified mental health nurses, two psychologists, and one psychiatrist.

The members of the research team were not involved in delivering the group intervention from which the participants were recruited. The separation between clinical duties and research activities helped minimise potential influence on the data collection process [30].

The principal investigator (PhD) and a co-investigator followed the FG interview. Both have clinical experience in supporting individuals with DP in a psychiatric hospital. They have previous research experience. One of the co-investigators, during the sessions, was responsible for taking notes. Participants were informed about the researchers regarding their personal objectives and the reason for the research.

Themes, subthemes, and illustrative participant excerpts

The data regarding the significance attributed to the DPG were analyzed in relation to the psychosocial meaning of group participation, the therapeutic processes that promote engagement, learning and empowerment through group participation and the understanding of dual pathology through group experience.

Psychosocial Meaning of Group Participation

Regarding the significance of the group, individuals attending the DPG referred to it as a safe space for sharing and emotional expression. The sense of belonging, mutual support and solidarity, reduction of isolation and socialization, were also meanings attributed to the group.

Regarding the safe space for sharing and emotional expression, participants stated that “sharing ideas is important” (E11) and reported that “here I can talk about certain things that I couldn’t talk about elsewhere” (E10), adding that “exchanging experiences is very enriching” (E6). They also noted that, within the DPG, they have the opportunity “to vent (…) to vent about everything (…)” (E3).

The group intervention was portrayed by participants as fostering a sense of belonging and peer identification, described as “a kind of companionship with the people who usually attend regularly (…) a companionship among people who have common problems” (E11). Participants emphasised the importance of being with others, highlighting mutual support and solidarity: “we help each other” (E5) and “I feel the need to talk with you (…) that connection” (E3).

The group was also described as a strategy for reducing isolation and promoting socialization, as participants reported that taking part means “coming to the group means socialising a little” (E10).

Attendance at the group was further underscored in terms of mutual support, solidarity, and its role as a source of support for recovery, which was experienced in various forms: “well, it means support” (E12); “support, support for our recovery; psychological support” (E7); “the group is a source of strength” (E3). Feeling supported was described as important in order “to be able to shed much of the guilt I had (…) by being able to express these situations here in the group, it helped me to forgive myself” (E8).

Therapeutic Processes that Promote engagement

Regarding the significance of healthcare professionals, individuals with DP referred to the importance of the therapeutic relationship based on trust that they establish and maintain with healthcare professionals as a central element of their treatment. “the professionals help us. When we are feeling down, they ask what is wrong” (E5).

In some cases, healthcare professionals were described as reference figures: “they are like a family; they try to help” (E4), or as people within participants’ circle of relationships: “they are friendly people who care a lot about us” (E1).

The relationship established with professionals was also considered important particular emphasis on the empathy and closeness toward the group: “They create a great sense of empathy with the group, and that is very important” (E8), “I feel a need for connection. I feel a need to talk with the professionals” (E9).

Learning and empowerment through group participation

DPG participants presented with different psychiatric disorders, which were therefore addressed individually. In this context, participants emphasised the importance of understanding the illness process during the psychoeducational sessions. These sessions focused on alcohol use disorder, psychiatric disorders, treatment adherence, and lifestyle. Regarding psychiatric disorders, examples included depressive disorders: “I had a deep and very destructive depression (…) today that part is already under control” (E1), as well as strategies for coping with the illness: “At the moment I have a problem called loneliness, but I am dealing well with it” (E6). Bipolar disorder was present in other participants: “I have learned to control my psychiatric illness, which is bipolar disorder” (E12). These participants emphasised the importance of the group in managing their psychiatric condition: “here I have learned to manage (…) the other illness” (E9).

With regard to alcohol dependence, participants reported increased self-understanding, such as “I learned to understand the effect that compulsion (to drink) has on me” (E9), as well as greater insight into the reasons underlying their drinking behaviour: “The effects I was seeking from alcohol were very destructive (…) I realised that when I reach that compulsive point, I seek to black out” (E9). As one participant added, “There are things I am driven to forget and want to forget, but you confront me with reality” (E3).

Participants also reported having acquired strategies to cope with craving: “I learned to deal with my urge to drink” (E5). Strategies to maintain abstinence were also mentioned: “I have learned that it is possible not to drink and that it is possible to live without alcohol” (E2). In addition, participants highlighted the repetition of information as a learning strategy: “I have been learning (…) mainly how to combat alcohol (…) it seems that you are always repeating the same warnings” (E7).

Changes in lifestyle habits were also discussed, such as “resuming physical exercise is making me feel better … I feel balanced and very normal” (E3), and modifying dietary habits through “guidance on diet, medication, on stopping alcohol use and also smoking” (E8). The approach to lifestyle change is described as multifactorial and tailored to individual needs: “The advice given here is important to try to correct the difficulties we have” (E11).

Understanding of dual pathology through the group experience

When asked about the relationship between alcohol use and psychiatric disorder, participants underscored the importance of abstinence, recognising alcohol as a factor that aggravates psychiatric symptoms “when I used to drink, it made my illness much worse” (E1). Abstinence was described as a strategy to prevent the exacerbation of psychiatric symptoms: “It is evident that by freeing myself from alcohol use I am also able to look at the illness that accompanies me” (E7).

The impact of alcohol on treatment was also noted: “I learned that alcohol and medication do not go well together” (E5). Participants also reported that alcohol consumption interfered with adherence to the therapeutic regimen. They indicated that psychoeducational sessions were important in supporting adherence, such as “remembering that there is an injection to be given, to reschedule it” (E7), and highlighted the importance of monitoring pharmacological adherence: “The amount of medication I take has to be strictly monitored by the people who are supporting us (…) and this guidance has been effective” (E10).

Discussion

This study explored the significance of group intervention for individuals receiving outpatient treatment for DP. The analysis identified four interrelated themes: “Psychosocial Meaning of Group Participation,” “Therapeutic Processes that Promote Engagement,” “Learning and Empowerment Through Group Participation,” and “Understanding Dual Pathology Through the Group Experience”, which together illuminate how the DPG becomes a meaningful component of participants’ therapeutic trajectories.

The findings show that the group acquires profound psychosocial significance, functioning as a space of belonging, recognition, and mutual validation. Participants described the DPG as a setting where they felt understood and supported by others facing similar challenges, which is particularly relevant in the context of DP, where social isolation and stigma are common. These experiences resonate with the therapeutic factors [31] yet the present study adds nuance by illustrating how these mechanisms operate specifically among individuals with co-occurring psychiatric disorders and alcohol use disorder in an outpatient setting.

The theme “Therapeutic Processes that Promote Engagement” highlights the importance of the relationships established with both the multidisciplinary team and peers. Participants emphasized the predictability of sessions, the welcoming stance of professionals, and the identification with other group members as key elements that sustained their engagement. These findings are consistent with previous research, which has shown that strong therapeutic alliances [32] whether with clinicians or peers are associated with greater clinical stability and reduced relapse risk. They also echo the existing literature [33] which suggests that abstinence is a vulnerable and dynamic process influenced by individual and contextual factors, underscoring the relevance of structured group interventions that reinforce continuity of care.

The theme “Learning and Empowerment Through Group Participation” shows that the DPG holds significance as a space for developing health literacy, acquiring coping strategies, and reorganizing daily routines. These findings are consistent with the literature on psychoeducational interventions [34, 35] which emphasizes the role of psychoeducation in enhancing decision-making capacity and treatment adherence. However, participants in this study stressed that learning occurred not only through formal educational content but also through the experiential dimension of sharing and observing peers’ experiences—an aspect less explored in previous research and one that highlights the unique value of group-based learning in DP.

“Understanding Dual Pathology Through the Group Experience” illustrates how the DPG contributed to a more integrated understanding of the relationship between psychiatric symptoms and alcohol use. Participants reported gaining greater awareness of relapse triggers, illness mechanisms, and strategies for maintaining stability. These findings are consistent with recent evidence highlighting the importance of psychoeducational and recovery-oriented interventions in fostering self-awareness, illness insight, relapse prevention skills, and sustained engagement in treatment among individuals with co-occurring mental health and substance use disorders [34, 36].

Overall, the findings suggest that the DPG functions as a protective and structuring space, where the combination of psychoeducation, emotional support, and peer interaction fosters abstinence maintenance, clinical stability, and lifestyle reorganization. The value attributed to the multidisciplinary approach reinforces the relevance of integrated interventions for DP [32, 37]. In this sense, the group intervention is not merely an adjunct to individual treatment but a central component of participants’ recovery processes.

Limitations

Regarding limitations, the study’s findings must be interpreted with caution. The sample was small and composed of individuals already engaged in outpatient treatment, which may have favored more positive accounts of the DPG. The focus group format may also have influenced participants’ willingness to express divergent or critical perspectives. Although a systematic thematic analysis was conducted, the absence of investigator triangulation may have shaped data interpretation, and the researchers’ proximity to the clinical context may have introduced interpretative bias; while the inclusion of illustrative excerpts enhanced transparency, it does not fully eliminate this risk.

In addiction the absence of a saturation, driven sampling strategy, restricts the breadth of the results and reinforces the need for caution when interpreting their transferability.In addition, all participants presented alcohol use disorder alongside a co-occurring psychiatric condition, meaning that the findings cannot be directly generalized to individuals with other substance use disorders. Finally, because the study was conducted within a single service, the transferability of the results to other clinical settings or healthcare systems is limited.

Implications for practice and research

This study contributes to raising awareness among healthcare professionals regarding the importance of simultaneously addressing psychiatric disorder and AUD in clinical practice. In the education of healthcare professionals, particularly nurses, greater emphasis should be placed on the need for integrated approaches to ensure therapeutic success. Further research is recommended to better characterise individuals with psychiatric disorder and to evaluate targeted interventions for this population.

Conclusions

The findings indicate that participants generally viewed the DPG as a helpful source of support. They reported that participation in the group was associated with a better understanding of their illness, increased engagement with treatment, and perceived improvements in daily life. Participants also described the therapeutic relationship with healthcare professionals as an important element in supporting trust, engagement, and the recovery process.
