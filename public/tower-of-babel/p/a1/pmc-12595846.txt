Prevalence and predictors of asthma among Indian women: a machine learning-based analysis of NFHS-5 data
Authors: Vini Mehta, Anil Pardeshi, Rayhan Rahman, Illias Sheikh, Ankita Mathur
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAsthma is a growing public health concern in India, but research has largely focused on children or general adult populations, often overlooking women of reproductive age. Prior studies typically use linear models that fail to capture the complex interactions among environmental, socio-demographic, and behavioural risk factors. This study addresses this gap by estimating asthma prevalence in Indian women (15–49 years) and applying machine learning techniques to identify non-linear, high-dimensional predictors using NFHS-5 data.MethodsThis study analysed NFHS-5 data (2019–2021) using a nationally representative stratified two-stage sampling design. A total of 550,746 women aged 15–49 was included after excluding non-responses to asthma-related questions. Asthma status was self-reported. Bivariate Chi-square tests examined associations with environmental, socio-economic, behavioral, nutritional, and geographic variables. A one-sample t-test assessed dietary score differences. Three machine learning models like Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and XGBoost were developed on a balanced dataset using up-sampling in R (caret package). Model performance was evaluated using AUC and accuracy; key predictors were identified via feature importance and predicted probabilities.ResultsAsthma prevalence was 15.4 per 1,000 women (95% CI: 14.9–16.0). Significant associations were observed with environmental (housing, fuel type, sanitation), socio-economic (age, education, caste, religion), behavioral (tobacco, alcohol), and nutritional factors (BMI, dietary score). Random Forest outperformed other models (AUC: 0.912; accuracy: 84.3%; p < 0.001), with dietary score, age, and wealth index as top predictors. Predicted risk was notably higher among older, overweight, less-educated, and urban women (p < 0.05 for all comparisons).ConclusionsAsthma among Indian women is shaped by diverse social, environmental, and behavioral factors. Machine learning, particularly Random Forest, offers valuable predictive insights, highlighting high-risk groups and supporting targeted public health interventions.Trial registrationNot applicable.Keywords: Asthma, Epidemiology, Socio-demographic factors, Indian women, NFHS-5, Machine learning, Random forest

Background

Asthma is a significant global health issue, with rising prevalence and a substantial socioeconomic burden, affecting physical and mental health. In 2019, asthma affected 262 million people globally, resulting in at around 400,000 deaths [1]. The Global Asthma Report (GAR) 2022, published by the Global Asthma Network (GAN), highlights that asthma ranks 24th globally in terms of years lived with disability (YLDs) and 34th in disease burden, as measured by disability-adjusted life years (DALYs) [2, 3]. The disease causes symptoms like wheezing, coughing, and chest tightness, which are triggered by airway inflammation and bronchial muscle constriction [4]. Asthma’s impact extends beyond physical health, leading to sleep disturbances, fatigue, reduced physical activity, and lost work or school days [5, 6]. Despite effective treatments, asthma remains underdiagnosed and undertreated, especially in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), where most asthma-related deaths occur [7].

India bears a significant portion of this burden, with over 2% of adults and 6% of children affected by asthma [5, 8]. Asthma among adult women, particularly between the ages of 15 and 49, remains underexplored, often leading to delayed diagnosis [9]. Many individuals avoid treatment due to concerns about side effects or stigmatization Behavioral, environmental, and genetic factors combine to influence asthma risk, with exposure to pollutants, indoor air pollution from cooking, and socioeconomic disadvantages playing a major role [10, 11]. Additionally, comorbidities such as obesity, mental health disorders, and conditions like GERD and chronic sinusitis often complicate asthma management [12]. Nutritional status also plays a role in airway inflammation and immune regulation central to asthma pathophysiology. Diets rich in antioxidants and omega-3s may offer protection, while processed foods and saturated fats increase risk. A composite dietary score was thus included to evaluate nutrition’s contribution to asthma among Indian women, especially considering the dual burden of undernutrition and obesity. Few studies have examined the complex interplay of these factors, especially among Indian women. Traditional research has focused on children or generalized adult populations, often using basic statistical models [7, 8].

Traditional statistical methods like logistic regression assume linearity and independence, limiting their ability to capture complex risk patterns. In contrast, machine learning models can handle non-linear, multivariate interactions and high-dimensional data. This enables more accurate predictions and identification of hidden risk factors. ML thus supports data-driven asthma risk assessment, especially in diverse populations like India.

Machine learning (ML) techniques, which allow for the modelling of complex, non-linear relationships, provide a more accurate and flexible tool for assessing asthma risk. Kocsis et al. tested three ML models-Support Vector Machines (SVM), Random Forests, and AdaBoost finding that Random Forest outperformed the others in predicting asthma control status [13]. Kumar et al. applied five models, including Random Forest, which achieved the highest accuracy of 0.93 for childhood asthma prediction [14]. Gunawardana et al. used a hybrid model combining Logistic Regression and LightGBM, achieving an AUC of 0.9062 and sensitivity of 79.85% [15]. Chen et al. developed an AI-driven system that analyzed voice changes caused by asthma, with models like Decision Tree, CNN, and LSTM achieving accuracies above 0.8, with LSTM performing best for normal-pitch and low-pitch phonemes [16]. To better capture complex, non-linear relationships among diverse risk factors, this study employs machine learning techniques. Unlike traditional models, ML identifies hidden patterns and enhances predictive accuracy. This approach enables more precise asthma risk stratification to support targeted interventions. This study focuses exclusively on women aged 15–49 years due to their unique asthma risk profile, shaped by hormonal influences, greater exposure to indoor air pollution from gendered household roles, and underdiagnosis linked to social and healthcare disparities. Moreover, NFHS-5 offers detailed, nationally representative data on this priority population under Indian health policy.

This study aims to examine the prevalence of self-reported asthma among women aged 15–49 years in India, based on data from the National Family Health Survey (NFHS)−5. It seeks to identify the key risk factors for asthma, including socio-economic variables (age, education, wealth index, caste, and religion), environmental factors (type of house, cooking fuel, kitchen ventilation), and behavioral factors (tobacco use and alcohol consumption). Additionally, the study explores the role of nutritional, geographical, and residential factors in asthma prevalence. The findings aim to provide insights into the risk factors associated with asthma and inform public health interventions for its prevention and management in India.

Methods

Data source

The present study utilized the data from the nationally representative National Family Health Survey (NFHS)−5, an Indian Version of Demographic Health Survey (DHS). The fifth round of this NFHS survey was conducted during 2019-21 by International Institute for Population Sciences (IIPS), Mumbai, as the nodal agency, in association with ICF International and other agencies. The survey adopted stratified two-stage sample. The first stage was the selection of primary sample unit (PSU) and the subsequent stage was for the household selection. In the rural areas, villages were considered as PSU whereas it was Census Enumeration Blocks (CEBs) in urban areas. The NFHS offers several population, health and diseases specific information at entire national, state/union territory (UT) and district-level in the country. The NFHS-5 interviewed among 724,115 women aged 15–49 years in the country. The survey used a standard sampling weights, and the details of the same are found in the report [17].

Outcome measures

Asthma among women between the ages of 15 and 49 is the study’s objective parameter. All of the women who were questioned were asked if they had asthma and, if so, if they received medication. This made asthma a self-reported condition in the study. Out of the 724,115 women who participated in the NFHS-5, but approximately 550,746 responded to the survey’s asthma-specific inquiry, which is corresponding to their selected background characteristics. The investigation comprised 550,746 women, of whom approximately 7505 confirmed having asthma in India.

Risk factors

Several significant asthma variables have been considered for the purpose of the research. Utilizing the available statistics and the content of previous research, variables were chosen. Environmental factors i.e. type of house, type of cooking fuel, separate room for kitchen, ventilation in kitchen, type of toilet was included. Socio-economic factors were age, education level, wealth index, caste and religion. The behavioural factors considered in this study were tobacco use, alcohol consumption. A composite dietary score was constructed using frequency data from NFHS-5 on intake of fruits, green leafy vegetables, fish, eggs, and milk/curd. Each item was scored from 0 (never) to 3 (daily). The total score ranged from 0 to 15, with higher scores indicating better dietary diversity and nutritional intake. This score was used to assess the association between diet quality and asthma risk. In addition to these, geographical factors and type of residence were also included as a potential risk factor for the study.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics and bivariate analysis with Chi-square test were performed in the study. The one sample t-test with 95% CI was conducted to examine the dietary scores and the risk of asthma. To assess the important determinants of Asthma, the machine learning method was utilized. Since asthma prevalence in the sample was relatively low (15.4 per 1000), class imbalance was addressed prior to model development. The variables which show the significant association in the chi-square test were included in the final prediction model. Except the ventilation in kitchen, all other variables were appeared as significant in the test. The model selection was done based on nature of outcome variable and number of positive samples. The detailed methodology is discussed in the following sections.

Handling class imbalance

As mentioned above, the number of positive samples for Asthma is low (i.e. 7505) as compared to the total sample which could create bias in model training. In order to avoid this issue, the data was balanced using up sampling through the up-Sample function of caret package in R-studio. This method systematically increased the number of Asthma cases in the training set, ensuring the representation and avoiding the risk of underfitting the minority class.

Data splitting

The balanced data was randomly split into training (70%) and testing (30%) subsets. The training dataset was utilized to develop models, whereas the test data was used to evaluate the model performance.

Model development

For this study, three predictive models were trained and evaluated: Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Extreme Gradient Boosting (XGBoost). These models were developed using a balanced dataset to address class imbalance. Subsequently, model performance was assessed using a 30% hold-out test dataset.

For model performance evaluation, the key indicators used to evaluate model performance were the Area Under the Curve (AUC) of the ROC curve and overall accuracy. Additionally, ROC curves were plotted to visually compare the performance of the different models.

The final model was selected based on the performance indicators. Further, feature importance and individual-level predicted probabilities of asthma were generated using the test dataset.

All basic data management and preliminary analyses were conducted using STATA. Machine learning tasks, including model training and validation, were performed in RStudio, utilizing the caret package.

Results

The study analyzed 550,746 Indian women aged 15–49, predominantly rural (71.9%) and Hindu (75.8%), with a self-reported asthma prevalence of 1.4%. While over half had completed secondary education (51.1%), a notable portion remained illiterate (22.5%). Socio-economically, the population was diverse, with OBCs constituting the largest group (39.4%) and a balanced wealth distribution. Most participants lived in pucca houses, used clean cooking fuel, and had access to improved sanitation, separate kitchens, and proper ventilation. Tobacco and alcohol use were low (5.8% and 1.8%, respectively), but nutritional challenges persisted, with 16.1% underweight and 22.6% overweight. Overall, the population showed reasonable living standards amid ongoing educational, nutritional, and environmental health disparities.

Environmental factors such as living in a pucca house (16.3 per 1,000; χ²=45.4, p < 0.001), availability of a separate kitchen (15.9 per 1,000; χ²=20.8, p < 0.001), and access to improved toilets (15.8 per 1,000; χ²=28.2, p < 0.001) were associated with higher asthma prevalence. However, ventilation in the kitchen showed no significant association (χ²=0.97, p = 0.51). Socio-economic indicators revealed strong patterns. Asthma prevalence increased with age, peaking at 23.7 per 1,000 in women aged over 34 years (χ²=1684.4, p < 0.001). Women with primary education had the highest prevalence (21.5 per 1,000), while those with higher education had the lowest (12.0 per 1,000) (χ²=291.1, p < 0.001). Middle-income women exhibited the highest asthma prevalence (17.0 per 1,000; χ²=58.0, p < 0.001). Caste and religion were also significant determinants, with Christians (25.9 per 1,000) and women from the “Others” caste group (18.5 per 1,000) showing higher risk (χ²=144.8 and 129.1 respectively; p < 0.001). Among behavioral and nutritional factors, tobacco users (27.0 per 1,000; χ²=179.3, p < 0.001) and alcohol consumers (23.3 per 1,000; χ²=15.63, p < 0.001) had notably higher asthma prevalence. Overweight individuals had a significantly elevated prevalence (23.4 per 1,000; χ²=737.9, p < 0.001), while underweight women had lower prevalence (10.9 per 1,000; χ²=145.8, p < 0.001). A higher dietary score was also associated with increased asthma risk (χ²=96.2, p < 0.001) (Table 2).

Overall, the findings indicate that asthma prevalence and risk among Indian women are significantly influenced by a combination of environmental, socioeconomic, behavioral, and nutritional factors. The application of machine learning, particularly the Random Forest model, has proven effective in identifying and ranking these determinants, offering valuable insights for targeted public health interventions.

Discussion

This study estimated a self-reported asthma prevalence of 1.4% among Indian women, slightly lower than the 1.9% reported by Agrawal et al. [18]. Both studies identified high BMI, tobacco, and alcohol use as major risk factors. However, unlike Agrawal et al., who linked underweight status with asthma, our findings indicated lower asthma prevalence among underweight women. Additionally, improved housing and higher dietary scores were paradoxically associated with increased asthma risk. The unexpected association between pucca housing and higher asthma prevalence may reflect increased exposure to indoor pollutants such as dust mites, mold, and volatile organic compounds, particularly in urban, less-ventilated settings. Allergen accumulation may also result from chemical cleaning agents and proximity to traffic emissions. Interestingly, dietary score emerged as the strongest predictor of asthma. While higher scores indicate diversity, they may also capture increased intake of processed or allergenic foods, especially in urban diets. This underscores a limitation of dietary indices that emphasize variety over nutritional quality. Food preparation, storage, and additives may further contribute to airway inflammation, warranting qualitative dietary assessments. The use of machine learning techniques enabled a more refined identification of critical predictors such as age, caste, and wealth index [18].

Babatunde Bakare et al. applied Random Forest and SVM models to classify asthma severity based on clinical symptoms, with Random Forest demonstrating higher predictive accuracy (R² = 0.66) [19]. While both studies confirm Random Forest’s predictive strength, their work focused on clinical severity, whereas ours addressed socio-environmental risk profiling among Indian women. Gunawardana et al. used a hybrid Logistic Regression-LightGBM model (AUC: 0.9062) to predict asthma in Sri Lanka, based on clinical and behavioral inputs [15]. Our study also applied machine learning but emphasized socio-demographic and nutritional predictors, using Random Forest (AUC: 0.912) to identify age, diet, caste, and education as dominant predictors. Both studies used nationally representative data but differed in emphasis clinical versus population-level risk factors.

The Global Asthma Network (GAN) observed a decline in wheeze prevalence in India but highlighted gaps in diagnosis and treatment, particularly in children [20]. While our study focused on women aged 15–49, it similarly stressed strong associations with lifestyle and socioeconomic factors, showcasing how machine learning can enhance asthma risk stratification. Malamardi et al. reported a lack of evidence linking outdoor air pollution to asthma hospitalization in South Asian children [21]. In contrast, our study focused on adult women and used data-driven models to assess individual-level risk factors such as fuel type and sanitation, offering a more micro-level perspective. Mahammad and Kumar implemented Decision Tree and ensemble methods like Random Forest and Gradient Boost to predict asthma using public datasets [22]. While both studies used tree-based models, ours incorporated a wider range of behavioral and demographic features specific to Indian women, enhancing the public health implications.

Ekezie et al. reported asthma prevalence of 4.2–8.9% in Nepal and highlighted rural residence and household air pollution as major determinants [23]. Our findings echoed the environmental risks but offered greater granularity by focusing exclusively on Indian women and using predictive analytics. Razzaq et al. observed asthma prevalence of 1.8–11.3% in Pakistani adults, linked to smoking, age, and allergy history [24]. While their study and ours share similar environmental and behavioral risk factors, we applied machine learning to personalize risk stratification in a gender-specific population. Takeuchi et al. found elevated anti-Ascaris IgE associated with wheezing in Bangladeshi children, suggesting chronic exposure risks [25]. Though we didn’t assess parasitic infections, both studies emphasize the impact of persistent environmental exposures on respiratory health. Muraleetharan and Anuradha (2019) linked childhood asthma to poor diet and lack of breastfeeding [26]. Although our study targeted adults, dietary score emerged as a key predictor, reaffirming nutrition’s lifelong influence on respiratory outcomes.

Lan et al. used the ICEES (Integrated Clinical and Environmental Exposures Service) platform to identify asthma predictors such as prednisone use, obesity, and particulate matter exposure [27]. Our study also confirmed environmental and demographic influences but focused on socio-behavioral risks, showing machine learning’s adaptability across contexts. Alwarith et al. reported that plant-based diets and weight control mitigate asthma risk [28]. Our findings reinforce this, with dietary diversity and BMI among the strongest predictors. Choi et al. found penalized models effective for genetic asthma prediction but noted Random Forest’s strength with oversampling [29]. We similarly found Random Forest to be most effective, though our data were socio-environmental, not genomic.

Jayamini et al. reviewed ML models for predicting asthma exacerbations and proposed a conceptual framework [30]. While their focus was on exacerbation prediction, our work emphasized asthma prevalence using cost-effective, non-clinical predictors for public health screening. Talaei et al. linked childhood dietary patterns to adult lung function [31]. Our study supports the life-course nutritional hypothesis, as current dietary diversity predicted asthma risk among adult women. Ross et al. explored biological and treatment-response markers for asthma prediction [32]. While their study emphasized clinical inputs, ours used behavioral and environmental variables, both aiming to uncover non-obvious predictors through ML.

Kong et al. reported a U-shaped relationship between BMI and asthma in adults over 45 [33]. Our results aligned but also integrated broader socio-environmental factors influencing this relationship. Grant et al. inked U.S. asthma disparities to structural inequalities in social determinants like housing and stress [34]. Our findings were consistent, identifying caste, education, and housing quality as significant contributors. Rashmi et al. found asthma more prevalent in wealthier, urban Indian populations aged 45–65 [35]. Our study reaffirmed this, while also providing risk stratification across wealth and educational levels using machine learning. Finally, Manisalidis et al. summarized global evidence on air pollution and respiratory health [36]. Our results supported these findings, especially regarding indoor exposures such as fuel use and sanitation. The findings highlight the need to integrate asthma risk assessment into existing women-focused public health programs. Given the disproportionate burden of indoor air pollution, poor nutrition, and limited healthcare access, asthma screening can be incorporated into Reproductive, Maternal, New-born, Child, and Adolescent Health (RMNCH + A) initiatives and Mission Poshan 2.0. The Pradhan Mantri Ujjwala Yojana offers a platform to mitigate both respiratory and environmental risks. Community-level interventions by ASHAs and ANMs may help reduce underdiagnosis and improve early care-seeking. Targeted awareness campaigns are essential in resource-limited settings. Additionally, ML-based risk stratification could support prioritization of high-risk women in national health surveillance systems.

Limitations

A key limitation of the study is the reliance on self-reported asthma, which may be prone to misclassification due to lack of clinical validation such as spirometry or physician assessment. Second, the cross-sectional nature of the NFHS-5 data limits our ability to infer causality between identified correlates and asthma outcomes. Additionally, while genetic predisposition is a known risk factor, the lack of data on family history especially parental asthma may have influenced the observed associations.

Conclusion

This study highlights a 1.4% prevalence of self-reported asthma among Indian women aged 15–49, revealing significant associations with lifestyle, nutritional, and socio-demographic factors. Using machine learning models, particularly Random Forest, we identified key predictors such as age, caste, education, BMI, dietary diversity, and type of cooking fuel. Unlike earlier studies focused on clinical symptoms or children, our approach emphasizes population-level risk stratification tailored for adult women in India. The model’s high accuracy (AUC: 0.912) demonstrates the value of machine learning in identifying vulnerable subgroups and informing targeted public health interventions. Our findings underscore the complex interplay between socioeconomic status, environmental exposures, and behavioral health, pointing to the need for multifaceted, data-driven strategies for asthma prevention and management. Future work should integrate clinical and environmental exposure data to further enhance prediction and intervention capabilities.
