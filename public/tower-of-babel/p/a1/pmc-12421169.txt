Clinically significant bradycardia in children with respiratory syncytial virus bronchiolitis receiving dexmedetomidine: effect modification by mechanical ventilation
Authors: Rasha S Farag, Aditya S Kalluri, Geetha Iyer, Jennifer P Stevens, Carly E Milliren, James Brian McAlvin
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundLimited evidence exists on the additive risk of bradycardia in children with respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) bronchiolitis receiving dexmedetomidine (DMED). We aim to study the association between RSV bronchiolitis and bradycardia during DMED administration.MethodsThis retrospective cohort study included 273 children under 2 years old admitted to the intensive care units at Boston Children’s Hospital with severe bronchiolitis and sedated with DMED from 2009 to 2022. Children were classified as RSV or non-RSV based on confirmed laboratory results. The primary outcome was a composite measure of clinically significant bradycardia, defined as either a heart rate <60 beats per minute or need for medical intervention(s). The secondary outcome was the minimum heart rate after DMED initiation. Subgroup analyses assessed potential effect modification by age, DMED doses, ventilation mode and pre- versus post-COVID-19.ResultsThe median (Q1, Q3) age was 8.0 (4.0, 13.7) months. Of the children studied, 85 (31.1%) had RSV bronchiolitis and 170 (62.3%) underwent invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV) at DMED initiation. Clinically significant bradycardia was observed in 71 (26.0%) patients with no significant difference between the RSV and non-RSV cohorts (OR: 1.80; 95% CI: 0.95 to 3.39; p = 0.07). Subgroup analyses showed effect modification with an increased likelihood of clinically significant bradycardia in the RSV group undergoing IMV (OR: 2.99 vs 0.45; Χ21=3.6, p=0.04) or admitted before the COVID-19 pandemic (OR: 2.94 vs 0.51; Χ21=4.7, p=0.03). The RSV cohort experienced a significantly greater heart rate reduction after DMED initiation (−8.07 bpm; 95% CI: −13.71 to –2.43; p = 0.005).ConclusionsChildren with RSV bronchiolitis experienced greater heart rate reduction after DMED initiation, with a higher likelihood of clinically significant bradycardia if IMV is in use at DMED initiation or if admitted before the COVID-19 pandemic. Caution is warranted when treating RSV bronchiolitis patients with DMED.Keywords: Therapeutics; Virology; Intensive Care Units, Neonatal; Children; COVID-19

WHAT IS ALREADY KNOWN ON THIS TOPIC

Acute viral bronchiolitis, commonly caused by respiratory syncytial virus (RSV), is a leading cause of hospitalisation in children. While many cases resolve on their own, severe infections can lead to Pediatric Intensive Care Unit (PICU) admission, where dexmedetomidine (DMED) is often used. Although both RSV and DMED are individually associated with bradycardia, it remains unclear whether their combination increases this risk in critically ill children, potentially resulting in clinically significant bradycardia requiring medical intervention.

Introduction

Acute viral bronchiolitis is the leading cause of hospitalisation in children under 2 years of age. Respiratory syncytial virus (RSV) is the most common aetiology, although other viruses have been implicated.1 2 Bronchiolitis is usually self-limited, but respiratory distress, respiratory failure, secondary bacterial infections, apnoea and bradycardia can be observed in severe cases. About 2%–3% of patients require hospitalisation3 and approximately 19% of those require intensive care unit (ICU) admission.4

Dexmedetomidine (DMED; a central α−2 adrenergic agonist) is commonly used in the ICU, alone5 or in combination with other sedative medications,6 to facilitate non-invasive ventilation (NIV) and invasive mechanical ventilation (IMV). DMED use for sedation is widespread due to multiple favourable characteristics: minimal effect on respiratory function compared with other drug classes, some anxiolysis/analgesia in addition to sedation, no active or toxic metabolites, and utility in limiting the use of other sedation classes including opiates/benzodiazepines and preventing emergence delirium.7

Bradycardia is a common side effect of DMED and is reported to occur in as many as 42% of patients, depending on the dose.8 Bradycardia also occurs in about 21% of children hospitalised with RSV.9 10 A few case reports document bradycardia, sinus pause or asystole in RSV patients during DMED use with doses up to 1.2 µg/kg/hour.11,13 However, no studies have been done to determine if RSV bronchiolitis and DMED together pose an additional risk for bradycardia.

Here, we evaluate whether RSV bronchiolitis is associated with a higher risk of clinically significant bradycardia compared with non-RSV bronchiolitis in children undergoing sedation with DMED.

Material and methods

Study design and database

We conducted a retrospective cohort study using electronic medical records (PowerChart) from Boston Children’s Hospital (BCH) over 14 years. The study followed the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology guidelines.14

Study population

We included children 2 years of age or younger admitted to the neonatal ICU, medical-surgical ICU and medical ICU of BCH from January 2009 to December 2022, who were both diagnosed with severe bronchiolitis and treated with continuous DMED infusion. Bronchiolitis was identified from the list of admission diagnoses using International Classification of Diseases (ICD) diagnosis codes (ICD-9 codes15—466.11 (RSV-bronchiolitis); 466.19 (non-RSV bronchiolitis) ; ICD-10 codes16—J21.0 (acute bronchiolitis due to RSV); J21.8 (acute bronchiolitis due to other specified organisms) and J21.9 (acute bronchiolitis, unspecified)). We excluded children older than 730 days as they rarely develop bronchiolitis despite RSV infection.17 To prevent misclassification bias, we also excluded those without laboratory-confirmed RSV status. Additionally, those who received intranasal DMED, intravenous DMED for procedural sedation, or only a loading dose of intravenous DMED without maintenance infusion were excluded.

Patient and public involvement

The patients and/or public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting, or dissemination plans of this research.

Exposure and outcomes

We categorised children diagnosed with severe bronchiolitis requiring ICU admission as RSV bronchiolitis versus non-RSV bronchiolitis, as confirmed either before BCH admission or on admission by real-time polymerase chain reaction (RT-PCR), viral direct fluorescence assay respiratory or RSV point-of-care tests.

The primary outcome was the incidence of clinically significant bradycardia, which was defined as a composite variable of the presence of any one of the following: heart rate below 60 beats per minute (bpm), manifesting as sinus bradycardia, junctional bradycardia, atrioventricular block or asystole; administration of epinephrine (racemic epinephrine, continuous infusion or epinephrine for anaphylaxis were not counted); atropine; or need for cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR) during DMED administration or within 48 hours of DMED discontinuation. The 60 bpm cut-off choice is supported by the Paediatric Bradycardia with a Pulse Algorithm 2020 update.18 The secondary outcome was the lowest heart rate after DMED administration.

Children were followed up from the time of DMED administration until 48 hours after its discontinuation. The outcomes were counted in the analysis if they occurred after 5 min of starting DMED until 48 hours after discontinuation. This time window was chosen based on the onset of action of intravenous DMED (5–10 min), the half-life elimination in neonates (1–9.4 hours), and in infants and children under 2 years of age (1.5–3.3 hours) and the fact that 94%–97% of a drug will be eliminated and its plasma concentrations will be below a clinically relevant concentration after 4–5 half-lives of the drug.8 19

Covariates and potential confounders

A causal directed acyclic graph was designed to identify potential confounders for evaluating the association between RSV bronchiolitis and clinically significant bradycardia in children receiving DMED (figure 1). Covariates identified included patient demographics (age, weight, sex and race), comorbidities (prematurity,20 major congenital anomalies,21 congenital heart disease, chronic respiratory diseases, immunodeficiency or malignancy), history of bradycardia during the admission but prior to DMED initiation, ventilation mode, baseline heart rate on admission and during DMED administration, the lowest heart rate before DMED administration, and the starting and maximum dose of DMED. Additionally, concomitantly administered medications that may cause bradycardia22 were assessed as confounders; these included fentanyl, ketamine, lorazepam, midazolam or morphine administered before or with DMED. Race and ethnicity were collapsed and stratified into four categories: white, black or African American, others and unknown/declined to answer. Comorbidities were assessed based on ICD-9, ICD-10 and Systematised Nomenclature of Medicine–Clinical Terms codes. Comorbidities, including prematurity (babies born alive before 37 completed weeks of pregnancy), cardiac anomalies and major congenital anomalies (non-cardiac, non-respiratory anomalies based on the Birth Defects Surveillance Toolkit of Centers for Disease Control and Prevention) were independently assessed as confounders. The history of bradycardia was assessed as bradycardia at the same hospitalisation encounter before DMED administration as a composite variable, similar to the primary composite outcome definition.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics were performed to compare the baseline demographics and clinical characteristics of the study population. Categorical variables were reported as frequency (percent) and were compared using the Pearson’s chi-squared (χ2) test. Continuous variables were presented as mean (standard deviation; SD) if normally distributed and were compared using Student’s t-test, or median (first and third quartiles; (Q1, Q3)) if non-normally distributed and compared using Wilcoxon rank-sum test. A two-sided p<0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Statistical models were built based on the potential confounders for the association between RSV bronchiolitis and bradycardia. We identified both patient and drug-related risk factors through literature and clinical experience. The patient-related risk factors were age of less than 6 months and comorbidities,23 the lowest heart rate and history of bradycardia before DMED administration in our models to account for the bradycardia induced by RSV more accurately. There is limited evidence about the influence of weight, male sex and race on this association. Drug-related risk factors were DMED dose, concomitant medications that cause bradycardia and DMED maximum dose to account for the greater risk of bradycardia with higher doses.24

For our primary outcome, we used univariable logistic regression to estimate the crude odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs), bivariable logistic regression to identify potential risk factors for bradycardia among the study population, and multivariable logistic regression to adjust for demographic and clinical characteristics. Given the small sample size and following the 1 in 10 rule for logistic regression,25 the models’ building proceeded in four steps to adjust for potential confounders and evaluate the robustness of results. Model 1 adjusted for age, comorbidities, history of bradycardia at the same hospitalisation encounter before DMED administration, concomitant medications that may cause bradycardia, the lowest heart rate before DMED administration and DMED maximum dose. Model 2 adjusted for age, weight, sex, race, comorbidities and DMED maximum dose. In models 1 and 2, comorbidities and concomitant medications were included as a composite variable. Model 3 combined all variables of models 1 and 2. Model 4 included variables of model 3 and further adjusted for DMED starting dose, DMED duration and the five individual comorbidities. Through these models, we investigated the effect of the included covariates as potential risk factors for bradycardia in this population and evaluated the robustness of our results. We assessed multicollinearity using a variance inflation factor threshold of scores over 10.26 Our primary presented model was model 1, as it included the major confounders for the association that were described previously and was a parsimonious model with reduced risk of instability.

For the secondary outcome, we used univariable and multivariable linear regression to examine the differences in the lowest heart rate following DMED administration between patients with RSV and non-RSV bronchiolitis. We tested the assumptions of linear regression.27

Several exploratory prespecified subgroup analyses for the primary outcome were performed to evaluate potential effect modification by (1) age, (2) DMED starting dose using 0.3 µg/kg/hour as the cut-off, (3) DMED maximum dose using 1 µg/kg/hour as the cut-off, (4) ventilation mode at DMED administration (intubated vs non-intubated) and (5) periods before and after COVID-19 due to differences in RSV epidemiology.28 We tested the interaction between our exposure (RSV bronchiolitis) and subgroup variables one at a time, through model 1, using the χ2 test to examine the potential effect modification for the association between RSV bronchiolitis and clinically significant bradycardia.

Sensitivity analyses for our primary outcome were conducted to evaluate the robustness of our findings. This was performed both for an alternate bradycardia threshold of 100 bpm (which does not typically require intervention) and for a narrowed selection criteria which excluded patients who had a history of bradycardia, a maximum dose of DMED greater than 2 µg/kg/hour, or a primary outcome occurring >28 days after DMED initiation. Additionally, we used modified Poisson regression with robust error variance, if log binomial regression did not converge, to calculate the risk ratio for clinically significant bradycardia.

All analyses were performed using Stata, V.17.0 (StataCorp),29 while graphical representation was done by R, V.4.1.3 (R Foundation for Statistical Computing)30 with the devtools and bigsnpr packages.

Unadjusted logistic regression showed no significant association between RSV bronchiolitis and the primary composite outcome measure of clinically significant bradycardia in patients receiving DMED (OR 1.29; 95% CI 0.73 to 2.28; p=0.4). Adjusted multivariable logistic regression models (figure 3) demonstrated a trend towards clinically significant bradycardia in RSV patients receiving DMED compared with the non-RSV cohort (primary model 1; OR 1.80; 95% CI: 0.95 to 3.39; p=0.07). Maximum DMED dose was a risk factor associated with clinically significant bradycardia in all four adjusted models, whereas age, comorbidities and concomitant sedative drugs were not (table 2). Univariable analysis of each variable of the composite outcome did not reveal associations with the RSV cohort (online supplemental Table S2). Adjusted analysis of individual outcome variables was not performed due to the small sample size of subjects who required the intervention.

Results of sensitivity analyses, specifically, modifying the definition of bradycardia to a more sensitive measure of 100 bpm and employing additional exclusion criteria were similar to the primary analysis (figure 5). Moreover, for the primary outcome, modified Poisson regression produced similar results (relative risk 1.42; 95% CI: 0.97 to 2.06; p = 0.07) to the logistic regression that was used in the primary analysis.

Discussion

In this retrospective cohort study, we analysed the incidence of clinically significant bradycardia in 273 patients under 2 years of age who were diagnosed with bronchiolitis and sedated with DMED in the PICUs. Despite the higher likelihood of clinically significant bradycardia found in patients with RSV compared with non-RSV bronchiolitis, it did not reach the arbitrary cut-off point of statistical significance (0.05). However, patients with RSV had significantly lower minimum heart rates (8 bpm on average) once DMED was initiated. To our knowledge, this is the first study to report lower minimum heart rates during DMED administration in patients with RSV versus non-RSV bronchiolitis.

The RSV cohort was younger (5.7 months vs 9.0 months) and had fewer underlying conditions, especially major congenital and cardiac anomalies, than the non-RSV cohort, which is consistent with what has been reported.31 Of note, age and comorbidities were not themselves risk factors for clinically significant bradycardia during DMED administration. This aligns with previous studies with respect to age32 but contrasts with findings related to underlying conditions.33

Many patients in the current study were initially managed with NIV and progressed to IMV as their clinical status worsened. The odds of clinically significant bradycardia were 200% greater in patients undergoing IMV if DMED was initiated after they were intubated. The exact reason for this is unexplained, but DMED has previously been shown to be safe for patients managed with NIV.5 The need for IMV suggests more severe disease and endotracheal intubation itself can provoke a vagal response that causes bradycardia.34 There is no reason per se why a vagal response to the presence of an endotracheal tube would occur more frequently in RSV compared with non-RSV patients. However, it is likely that the combination of RSV infection, DMED administration and IMV contributes in an additive fashion to the risk of bradycardia.

The likelihood of clinically significant bradycardia during DMED administration was two times higher in RSV versus non-RSV subjects before the COVID-19 pandemic, with no differences postpandemic. This difference is unexplained; however, it is important for several reasons. First, the inclusion of postpandemic bronchiolitis patients may explain why the association between RSV bronchiolitis and bradycardia was not significant in the overall study. Second, recent studies have reported epidemiologic differences (age, viral aetiologies) in prepandemic versus postpandemic bronchiolitis that were attributed to an immunity debt from COVID-19 mitigation measures.2835,38 Our data did not identify specific viral aetiologies other than RSV versus non-RSV, so we could not determine if differences in non-RSV viral aetiologies were contributory. In our study, patient ages were similar before and after the pandemic. Others have reported that postpandemic bronchiolitis patients were also similar in age, although some observed them to be older.39 Therefore, it may be that the lack of association between RSV and bradycardia in postpandemic patients was due to differences in bronchiolitis epidemiology. However, this study was not designed to identify such differences. It is plausible that the epidemiology of RSV (and hence the increased likelihood of bradycardia with RSV and DMED) will return to what it was before the pandemic as the immunity debt is recovered.

Both the starting and maximum DMED infusion rates were lower for RSV versus non-RSV subjects (0.3 vs 0.5 µg/kg/hour starting; 1.0 vs 1.3 µg/kg/hour maximum). This is noteworthy considering that DMED-induced bradycardia is dose-related.40 In our study, DMED starting doses greater than 0.3 µg/kg/hour and maximum doses greater than 1 µg/kg/hour were risk factors for clinically significant bradycardia. Therefore, if anything, the lower DMED dosing strategy in RSV subjects should have mitigated bradycardia. It is likely that differences in DMED dosing lessened the magnitude of the effect that we observed.

The results of Poisson regression were consistent with the logistic one despite having a prevalent outcome (26% developed clinically significant bradycardia). The small sample size may account for the wide CI and the lower limit being very close to null. Further larger studies are needed to address this issue.

Strengths and limitations

Overall, this study provided insight into both DMED dosing strategies and patient risk factors that are associated with clinically significant bradycardia. Other strengths of the study include using multiple different models and sensitivity analyses that showed similar outcomes in both magnitude and direction of effects, which validated the robustness of our results. Effect modification by ventilation mode highlights the need for timely and effective respiratory support. The inclusion of postpandemic bronchiolitis patients may have influenced overall study outcomes and was consistent with differences in bronchiolitis epidemiology that others have also reported, an area of active and ongoing studies.

However, our study had certain limitations. First, the retrospective cohort study design is inherently prone to residual and unmeasured confounding. Second, we did not distinguish individual viral aetiologies in the non-RSV cohort, which could produce variation in clinical manifestations. Third, we were not able to assess the severity of RSV based on quantitative viral load as a determinant of RSV symptoms and severity. Fourth, we were unable to account for the cumulative dose of DMED. Fifth, our sample size was not large enough to measure the effects of concomitant sedative medications and dosing strategies on bradycardia (individually or in various combinations). However, all patients, regardless of cohort, were managed with a standardised sedation algorithm. Additionally, the use of electronic medical record data can only capture the encoded events; certain clinical manoeuvres, such as suctioning or endotracheal tube reposition, might be causes of bradycardic events but cannot be captured in the current data set. Finally, the current study was from a single centre and larger studies may be needed to externally validate our findings and make them more generalisable.

Conclusions

In conclusion, this retrospective cohort study identified a statistically significant association between RSV bronchiolitis and clinically significant bradycardia among patients undergoing IMV when DMED was initiated, as well as in those who were admitted to the PICUs before the COVID-19 pandemic; but not after. We conclude that caution is warranted when using DMED in patients with RSV bronchiolitis. If DMED use is indicated, then clinically significant bradycardia should be anticipated, especially as the dose is increased and in those who are intubated.

Future multicentre randomised controlled trials may be helpful in guiding DMED dosing strategies and concomitant sedative medications use in children with RSV bronchiolitis.
