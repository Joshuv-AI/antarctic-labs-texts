An Adult Autism Training With Case Studies and Standardized Patient Encounters for Internal Medicine and Family Medicine Residents
Authors: Brittany N. Hand, Alison Deitsch, Daniel Gilmore, J. Madison Hyer, PREPARE for Autistic Adults Advisory Board, Lisa Juckett, Christopher Hanks, Susan M. Havercamp, Daniel Coury
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
IntroductionAutistic adults are significantly more likely than non-autistic peers to have many physical and mental health conditions and unmet health care needs. One major barrier to meeting their needs is the scarcity of adult primary care physicians who are prepared to care for them.MethodsWe partnered with autistic adults to develop “Promoting Residents’ Excellence in Patient-centered cARE (PREPARE) for Autistic Adults,” a 5-hour training. It includes 6 asynchronous lectures, 6 synchronous case studies, and 2 virtual standardized patient experiences. Residents (N = 29) at a large midwestern US academic medical center completed a pre- and postcourse evaluation and qualitative interviews to report their self-efficacy, attitudes, perceived behavioral control, intention to treat in the future, and knowledge about caring for autistic adult patients.ResultsThe training had medium to very large effects on improving residents’ self-efficacy (d = 2.98), perceived behavioral control (d = 1.08), and knowledge (d = 0.44), but did not improve attitudes toward or intention to care for autistic adults, likely due to ceiling effects. Resident feedback indicated an appreciation for the active learning elements of the training, insights from faculty facilitators and other residents, and the growth they experienced as practitioners.DiscussionThis multifaceted training provides active learning opportunities in a low-stakes environment to increase residents’ confidence in providing primary care for autistic adults. Future efforts include integration of this training into residency curricula at our institution and others.

Introduction

Recent estimates indicate that only 50% of physicians welcome patients with disabilities and less than 50% report high self-efficacy in caring for this population.1 Thus, it is critical to better equip physicians to care for the more than 44 million Americans living with disabilities.2 Disability trainings have been shown to be an effective way to improve self-efficacy in caring for, knowledge about, and willingness to deliver care for people with disabilities.3–6 In a 2021 letter, the United States (US) National Council on Disability (NCD) urged residency programs to adopt disability trainings7 that cover the “Core Competencies on Disability for Health Education,”8 which define the essential knowledge and skills for delivering high-quality care to patients with disabilities. As a result of this increasing emphasis on disability-inclusive care, residency programs require disability focused education content that is evidence informed and ready for implementation.

Medical education on autism has historically focused on pediatric care,3,9 emphasizing early diagnosis, interventions, and family-centered care. In contrast, there is a critical dearth of adult-focused autism medical education materials. As the population of autistic adults increases, with 50,000 autistic adolescents in the US transitioning to adult health care each year,10 it is crucial to address this gap in medical education. Despite autistic adults’ high prevalence of mental and physical health conditions that are routinely managed in primary care settings, such as cardiovascular disease, depression, and anxiety,11 autistic adults experience substantial barriers to accessing quality primary care.12,13 This is in part due to the scarcity of adult primary care physicians trained who are willing and able to care for them.9,14–16 Although physicians often encounter autistic adults in clinical practice, many report low confidence, limited knowledge, and insufficient flexibility to accommodate autistic adults’ needs in health care visits.9,12,13

We sought to mitigate this training gap and meet the need for disability-inclusive medical education. We collaborated with autistic adults and their family members to develop curricular content for residents on caring for autistic adults in primary care settings.17,18 This training is called “Promoting Residents’ Excellence in Patient-centered cARE (PREPARE) for Autistic Adults” and provides residents with opportunities to practice new skills in a supportive, low-stakes environment.17 Aligned with the 6 Core Competencies on Disability for Health Education,8 the training includes 6 lectures, 6 case studies, and 2 formative standardized patient (SP) encounters.

Methods

Curricular Context

We hired autistic adults and their caregivers to advise, guide, and directly inform training content.17 Our advisory board consisted of 6 adults and 4 family members with maximally diverse lived experiences and demographic characteristics (ie, age, gender, US region, race).17

We invited all residents from Family Medicine (FM), Internal Medicine (IM), and Internal Medicine and Pediatrics (Med-Peds) programs at The Ohio State University to participate. These 3 programs collectively had approximately 150 residents. Residents completed the training on their own time. Residents earned up to $250 each as Amazon gift card and up to 5 hours of didactic credit for completing the training. This activity was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board at The Ohio State University (no. 2022B0030). The training was led by facilitators with expertise in autism research, medical education, and clinical care. Those wishing to implement this training at their own institutions should have experience caring for this population or be willing to partner with an autistic adult to deliver it.

PREPARE for Autistic Adults Description

Training overview

PREPARE for Autistic Adults takes approximately 5.4 contact hours for residents.17,18 This includes six 15-minute prerecorded lectures, six 30-minute facilitator-led case studies, and two 20-minute formative SP encounters.17,18 Each case study has an accompanying 2-minute “Why is this case important?” video, where advisory board members share why the case was important and relevant to their experiences.18 The prerecorded lectures and “Why is this case important?” videos should be viewed before the case study. The training covers the 6 Core Competencies on Disability for Health Education: contextual and conceptual frameworks, patient-centered care, clinical assessment, care over the lifespan and transitions, legal obligations, and team-based practice.17,18 Key conceptual frameworks covered include the social model of disability and Autistic SPACE framework,19 which was developed by autistic physicians to highlight autistic adults’ health care needs. We delivered the training over 8 weeks (Table 1). Alternative training schedules are available in Appendix A. Residents completed SP encounters at the midpoint and end of the training.

Training Schedule

Preparatory work for facilitators

This training takes 5.5 hours for faculty, plus 40 minutes per trainee for SP encounters. We developed 3 facilitator guides: (1) an overview of the entire training (see Appendix A); (2) a case study preparation guide (Appendix B); and (3) an SP encounter preparation guide (Appendix C). Before SP encounters, facilitators met with SPs for approximately 1 hour to review the case (Appendix D) and expectations for the experience (Appendix E). We paid autistic members of our advisory board ($30/hour) to portray the patients in the SP scenarios.18 We posted training materials for residents in an online learning management system course.18 We sent residents reminder emails or texts weekly to encourage participation.18

Preparatory work for residents

Each week, residents watched the assigned 15-minute prerecorded lecture (Appendices F–K) and the 2-minute “Why is this case important?” video (Appendices L–Q) at their convenience, before attending the case study.18 At the beginning of the training, we provided residents a handout of key points and resources for their future reference (Appendix R).

Case studies

A faculty facilitator led case studies (Appendix S) synchronously using a videoconferencing platform 3 evenings per week.18 Residents attended the evening that worked best for them.18 The facilitator gave an overview of the case, guided residents through the case study, and facilitated discussions that were interspersed throughout the 30-minute case.18

SP encounters

In SP encounters, autistic adults were paid actors portraying patients in a mock clinical case.17,18 We conducted SP encounters synchronously via videoconferencing.18 At the beginning of the 30-minute encounter, a faculty facilitator read a brief script that oriented the residents to the objective of this experience (Appendix T). Residents then had 10 minutes to engage the SP in a problem-focused medical interview and develop a brief care plan.18 Finally, the faculty facilitator and the SPs provided constructive feedback to the residents and answered resident questions.18

Evaluation Strategy

Learner assessment

We used the following measures to evaluate this training (Appendix U): (1) autistic adult knowledge (bespoke 17-item prototype knowledge assessment18 scored as percentage correct), (2) self-efficacy in caring for autistic adults (Academic Autistic Spectrum Partnership in Research and Education (AASPIRE) Provider Self-Efficacy scale20), (3) attitudes about caring for autistic adults, (4) perceived behavioral control in caring for autistic adults, and (5) intention to deliver patient-centered care to autistic adults in the future (adapted versions of the Planned Behavior Measure21 well-validated item stems22,23). SP encounters were evaluated globally, guided by the questions and prompts identified in Appendix C.

Data collection procedures

Residents completed the measures before and after the training. We asked residents to complete a 15- to 30-minute posttraining interview. Interview guides (Appendix V) included primary questions and example follow-up prompts. We designed our questions to elicit additional information from residents about the efficacy of the training (eg, “What is your overall impression of this training?”; “How has the training affected your willingness to provide care for autistic adults in the future?”; “What kinds of changes or alterations do you think you will need to make to the training so it will work effectively?”).

Data analysis

Using SAS version 9.4 (SAS, Cary, NC), we analyzed quantitative data using frequency (relative frequency [%]) and mean (standard deviation) for unadjusted estimates. We characterized unadjusted pre-post comparisons across outcomes using Cohen's d with the following clinical relevance cutoffs: 0.20 for small effects, 0.41 for medium effects, 0.63 for large effects, and 0.87 for very large effects.24 We derived adjusted estimates using linear contrasts from repeated measures, mixed-effects linear regression models with a random intercept for subject, a first-order autoregressive covariance structure, and between-within denominator degrees of freedom. All models controlled for residency program, gender, age, race, and year of residency. We analyzed interview data with rapid qualitative analysis.

The effect sizes for attitudes and intention were small, likely because residents’ pretest scores were already near the maximum score. Adjusted estimates revealed statistically significant improvements in perceived behavioral control, self-efficacy, and knowledge, but not for attitude or intention (see Table 3). We interviewed 18 residents. Our qualitative analysis revealed 3 overarching themes and 3 subthemes (Table 4).

Table 4.Qualitative Themes

Qualitative Themes

Theme: Active Learning Increases Efficacy

Residents felt that active learning enhanced training efficacy. They reported that the SP encounters and case studies improved their understanding and retention of training content. One resident described these learning activities as “solidifying” course content. Another appreciated that active learning opportunities were realistic, saying: “[SP encounters] provided me the actual opportunity to […] implement some of the thought processes on the go, which is the most realistic compared to real life.” Active learning opportunities enabled residents to practice course concepts in a safe, controlled environment before application in real-world practice. Several residents recommended adding an in-person SP encounter for more hands-on skill practice.

Subtheme: videos provide foundational context but are more passive

While residents preferred active learning, they also recognized the value of prerecorded presentations. One resident said: “[The videos] were a perfect introduction to kind of start thinking of questions and thinking of things that you want to bring up within the case that obviously leads to more discussion and more introspection as well.” Some residents recommended adapting the videos into interactive computer-based trainings with embedded questions to make them more active.

Theme: Insights From Others Increase Efficacy

Residents valued interacting with facilitators and fellow residents throughout the training, enriching their own understanding of the material. Referring to their interactions with other residents in case studies 1 resident stated: I really like that I had the chance to […] be in the case study discussion with other residents, so I got to hear other people's thoughts as well as […] learn some things that I didn't even realize from the cases and add extra aspects that I had no knowledge of.

I really like that I had the chance to […] be in the case study discussion with other residents, so I got to hear other people's thoughts as well as […] learn some things that I didn't even realize from the cases and add extra aspects that I had no knowledge of.

Others’ insights provided residents alternative perspectives when making clinical decisions and practical strategies to implement course concepts into clinical practice. Due to the value of others’ insights, residents recommended ensuring facilitators and autistic adults provide at least 1 constructive feedback point after each SP encounter and recommended prioritizing faculty facilitators who have clinical experience with autistic patients.

Subtheme: hearing from autistic adults and supporters enhanced efficacy

Residents recognized the insights of autistic adults and their caregivers as influential. Multiple residents felt that receiving feedback from autistic adults was helpful to understand their needs and experiences. One resident stated, “I thought that that was really like, powerful and good to see [autistic adults] directly affected by these topics, share their experiences.” The inclusion of autistic adults’ perspectives increased the impact of the training by grounding it in lived experiences. Residents recommended more opportunities to “hear from autistic patients about specific issues that they have and ways that we can avoid it,” which could include a panel discussion or lecture led by autistic adults and supporters.

Theme: I Feel Like I've Really Grown as a Practitioner

Residents described greater competence caring for autistic adults due to the knowledge, strategies, and skills they learned in the training. Moreover, residents described how the training prompted them to reflect on prior experiences and expressed readiness for future encounters with autistic adults. One resident stated: I just feel like my confidence has grown so much in terms of taking care of all patients that might not communicate as easily as a neurotypical patient just through this course […] I really feel like I've grown as a practitioner from it, so that's been wonderful.

I just feel like my confidence has grown so much in terms of taking care of all patients that might not communicate as easily as a neurotypical patient just through this course […] I really feel like I've grown as a practitioner from it, so that's been wonderful.

Additionally, residents often noted that, although focused on autistic adults, the skills they learned in the training were widely applicable to all of their patients. One resident said:

A lot of these concepts are not just ones that I can apply to my neurodivergent patients, but kind of all patients in general. To respect them, kind of regardless of their life conditions. […] I find it valuable to take those skills that I learned throughout the training and apply them elsewhere with my patients.

A lot of these concepts are not just ones that I can apply to my neurodivergent patients, but kind of all patients in general. To respect them, kind of regardless of their life conditions. […] I find it valuable to take those skills that I learned throughout the training and apply them elsewhere with my patients.

Subtheme: I was always willing, but I've been scared

Residents’ increased readiness and improved care for autistic adults were, in part, attributable to a shift in self-efficacy. Consistent with the quantitative data, residents in interviews described how before the training, they had poor self-efficacy in caring for autistic adults, despite high willingness to do so. Reflecting on their new preparedness to care for autistic adults, 1 resident said:

I already was, before this training, very willing to accept autistic adults into my future practice. And so I would say it hasn't changed, but it has made me feel more prepared about doing so it when the situation comes up.

I already was, before this training, very willing to accept autistic adults into my future practice. And so I would say it hasn't changed, but it has made me feel more prepared about doing so it when the situation comes up.

To further enhance their growth as practitioners, residents recommended that we (1) send them their pretest and posttest responses for reflection, (2) add an in-depth session on how to use the resources we shared throughout the training, and (3) distribute a newsletter providing them with regular updates and new resources.

Discussion

To our knowledge, PREPARE for Autistic Adults is a first-of-its-kind disability training to enhance resident physicians’ preparedness in caring for autistic adult patients. Residents felt that the active learning strategies, including SP encounters and case studies, improved their self-efficacy and solidified new knowledge. Consistent with prior literature,25 residents felt that guided discussions with peers and course facilitators promoted clinical reasoning skills. Autistic community members involvement increased the value and impact of the training for residents. Overall, this 5-hour training effectively accomplished our desired goals by improving residents’ self-efficacy, perceived behavioral control, and knowledge about caring for autistic adults.

We also identified some changes to enhance the efficacy of the training (see Table 3). Based on resident feedback, we added content to the facilitator guides highlighting (1) the importance of providing at least 1 constructive point in SP encounter feedback sessions and (2) flexible elements of the training that individual residency programs can decide whether or how to implement. Addressing some recommended changes (eg, interactive computer-based trainings with embedded questions, newsletter, in-depth resource session) was unfeasible at this time, but may be important considerations for future educational scholarly work and for residency programs seeking to implement this training.

We acknowledge several limitations of our evaluation approach. We had a relatively small number of residents from 1 site, so our results may not generalize to other residency programs. We only delivered PREPARE for Autistic Adults 1 time. Voluntary participation and incentives could have contributed to selection bias. While qualitative data reveal that residents perceived changes in their approach to clinical care, we did not directly evaluate this. Additionally, participation in the qualitative interview was optional. This resulted in hearing from only a subset (62%) of residents who participated in the training, which may have contributed to selection bias. Additionally, our assessment of residents’ autistic adult knowledge includes some questions that are not consistent with board-style questions (see Appendix U). For example, some questions asked residents to select which multiple choice option was “not true.” Residency programs wishing to simulate board-style questions may consider revising the wording of these items accordingly.

We also acknowledge limitations inherent to implementation of PREPARE for Autistic Adults. First, this training needs facilitators for active learning activities, which may be challenging at institutions with limited faculty availability. One option could be to have the training facilitated by chief residents who completed the training earlier in residency. Second, time constraints may affect residents’ participation. Offering the training multiple times per year or as an elective option during an outpatient rotation may help. We tested PREPARE for Autistic Adults as an 8-week virtual training; however, we developed sample schedules for delivering the training in as few as 2 short half-day sessions to illustrate how residency programs could adapt implementation to fit their needs.17 Third, engaging the autistic community is critical to implementing PREPARE for Autistic Adults. We encourage facilitators to connect with local autism advocacy groups or use online platforms to engage autistic community members. Fourth, we used SPs who were members of our advisory board who helped us develop this training, so they were already familiar with the SP encounters and overall training goals. This likely made our SP encounters more efficient. Facilitators interested in implementing PREPARE for Autistic Adults will need to recruit autistic adults to act as SPs. We provide some examples of how to connect with autistic adult SPs in Appendix A. Finally, although we recognize that this training focuses on autistic adults specifically, residents identified several ways in which skills and strategies discussed in the training generalize to other patient populations. Given initiatives to expand disability-informed medical education more broadly (eg, National Roadmap for Disability-Inclusive Healthcare26), it will be important to develop or adapt disability curricula to focus on other disability populations in the future.

Future directions for PREPARE for Autistic Adults include offering this training as an elective to IM, FM, and Med-Peds residents at our institution during their outpatient clinical rotation. We will explore alternate schedules such as implementing the training in 2 half-day sessions. We will also partner with residency programs outside our institution to explore the feasibility of implementation in other institutional contexts. We will develop a plan for longer-term follow up and measuring the extent to which participating in PREPARE for Autistic Adults has an impact on real-world clinical care. While originally designed for residents, another potential future direction is to deliver PREPARE for Autistic Adults to medical students as a longitudinal training/curriculum.

In conclusion, we developed PREPARE for Autistic Adults to address the national priority of improving residents’ ability to deliver disability-informed care.7 PREPARE for Autistic Adults significantly increased residents’ self-efficacy, perceived behavioral control, and knowledge about autistic patients. Residents attributed these gains to active learning opportunities and feedback from others, which led to growth as practitioners. Ultimately, we expect that adoption of PREPARE for Autistic Adults will yield a workforce of physicians who are ready to and capable of meeting the health needs of autistic adults.
