Liver microbial DNA signatures and extracellular vesicle epitopes as predictors of post-hepatectomy liver failure
Authors: Shmuel Jaffe Cohen, Guy Meyerovich, Esther Ovdat, Nadine Graubardt, Nir Lubezky
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryInteractions between the livers cellular environment and intestinal microbiota are involved in liver disease progression and health. This study investigates the impact of intra-hepatic microbial particles on liver regeneration (LR) following 70% partial hepatectomy (PH). We focus on the gut-liver axis and the relationship between gut and liver bacterial profiles. The study highlights microbial DNA and extracellular vesicles in liver tissue, examines bacterial translocation, microbiota manipulation, dysbiosis, and microbiome diversity, and assesses the impact of bacterial factors such as motility, spore formation, aerobic nature, and short-chain fatty acid (SCFA) production on LR capacity. We found that bacterial DNA and extracellular vesicles translocate to the liver post-surgery. While gut and liver bacterial profiles differ, some consistency remains. A dysbiotic gut microbiome leads to a dysbiotic liver profile, negatively impacting survival, and LR. Positive correlations with LR were observed for non-acetate and butyrate producers, spore-forming, and non-motile bacteria, whereas Gram-positive bacteria correlated negatively.Subject areas: Gastroenterology, Microbiome

Introduction

Surgical resection of the liver is a common procedure performed for various medical indications, including primary liver tumors, liver metastases from colorectal cancer, and living donor liver transplantation. Major liver resection, which involves the removal of three or more liver segments, carries a significant risk of post-hepatectomy liver failure (PHLF),1,2 which is the main cause of morbidity and mortality following major liver surgery. The liver has a remarkable ability to regenerate in response to most injuries, including liver resection.3 This regeneration involves the proliferation and differentiation of hepatocytes to restore liver mass and function. Hepatic regeneration is induced by several factors, including the innate immune system, progression of the cell cycle through various growth factors, and stimulation of cellular pathways by bile acids and metabolic processes.4 In recent years, research into the human microbiome has advanced, examining its influence on metabolic and inflammatory processes in various organs. The gut microbiota produces short-chain fatty acids (SCFAs), which are essential for optimal liver regeneration (LR) and survival following significant liver resection.5 The development of several liver pathologies has been found to correlate with dysbiotic microbiome compositions, such as those associated with obesity.6 To evaluate the risk of PHLF, K.-P. Janssen proposed assessing mRNA expression levels of lipogenic enzymes in the liver parenchyma after resection. These enzymes use SCFAs, secondary metabolites produced by gut bacteria, to build cell membranes in proliferating hepatocytes. Low SCFA synthesis has been associated with decreased Firmicutes and increased Proteobacteria in the gut, as well as a reduced LR rate.5 Here, we propose exploring the liver microbial profile during regeneration and its impact on the LR process.

We based our hypothesis upon the finding that bacterial DNA and EVs infiltrate the liver in significant, detectable amounts following liver surgery.7 The dynamics by which bacterial DNA, in comparison to EVs, migrate into the liver parenchyma through the liver vasculature involve multifactorial mechanisms, including apoptotic bacteria and an influx of intestinal compounds that result from surgical stress. This influx following surgery through the portal vein is well described by R. P. Cornell: “Sham surgery consisting of midline laparotomy and liver manipulation under ether anesthesia increased portal and systemic plasma concentrations of endogenous endotoxin at 2 h by 10 and 18 times, respectively, above the values for un-operated control animals. Circulating gut-derived LPS levels for 67% liver-resected rats also at 2 h were 23 and 41 times higher than the portal and systemic values, respectively, for un-operated control animals.8

These bacterial molecules interact directly and indirectly with LESCs and many other cells in the body.

This study aims to investigate how different gut microbiome compositions influence the liver microbiome and impact post-operative LR. Using a mouse model, we hypothesize that preoperative microbiota transplantation from distinct sources naive mice, obese mice, and a commercial probiotic,7 will modulate intrahepatic microbial content and alter LR outcomes. To test this, we characterized dynamic changes in hepatic bacterial load and composition during LR by quantifying bacterial DNA (16S rRNA PCR and NGS), analyzing microbial functional profiles (metagenomics), and assessing bacterial vesicle markers (LPS/LTA ratio) via flow cytometry. By comparing stool and liver samples, we explore the relationship between gut and liver microbiota and their combined influence on regenerative capacity.

Results

Characterization of gut and liver parenchymal bacterial DNA load and extracellular vesicles following 70% hepatectomy

A major hepatectomy animal model was used to study the involvement of the liver parenchymal microbiome in survival and LR. Hepatic bacterial DNA load was analyzed 48 h after 70% hepatectomy in mice. We measured the hepatic total bacterial load in naive mice and in mice after surgery. By comparing the percentages of bacterial DNA to total DNA, we observed a significant increase in hepatic bacterial DNA two days post-surgery compared to naive liver mice (Figure 1A). This suggests that bacterial particles, including DNA fragments, migrate into the liver following surgery. In addition to bacterial DNA in the liver parenchyma, we investigated the migration of bacterial EVs, beyond just DNA fragments. Extracellular vesicles (EVs) are important non-cellular fractions that exist in the liver and interact with resident and recruited cells. We hypothesized that intestinal microbiota particles infiltrate the liver after partial hepatectomy (PH). To dynamically track daily changes in hepatic bacterial EV composition post-surgery, we characterized the liver EV fraction’s outer membrane rather than focusing solely on bacterial DNA, which may not be present in all bacterial EVs. Furthermore, eukaryotic cell EVs that present bacterial epitopes on their surface, but do not necessarily carry bacterial DNA, would also be detected by this method.

Using an EV isolation protocol previously validated in our prior study,7 we detected and characterized microbial EVs in both fecal and liver parenchyma samples. EVs were identified in the supernatant following bacterial separation and 0.45 μm filtration, and their size distribution (50–450 nm) was confirmed by nanoparticle tracking analysis and transmission electron microscopy (TEM). Flow cytometry using anti-microbial antibodies (anti-LPS, anti-LTA) enabled rapid profiling of EV populations.

We isolated large EVs, focusing on the size range between approximately 200 and 450 nm. Bacterial EVs were isolated from murine fecal samples using a process primarily based on centrifugal separation. Large bacteria were excluded using centrifugation at 4000 g, while 10,000 g centrifugation concentrated the EV fraction. This method enables the manipulation and labeling of the cells. Bacterial epitopes on the EVs’ outer membrane were stained with fluorescent antibodies, enabling tracking by flow cytometry (FACS) analysis (Figure 1, Schema 1). Nanoparticle tracking analysis quantified the number and size distributions of the vesicles, demonstrating a homogeneous population of fecal vesicles with a modal diameter of 209.6 ± 8.8 nm, reduced to 177.5 ± 4.7 nm after 0.45 μm filtration (Figure 1B).

The same bacterial EV characterization methods used for fecal EVs were applied to liver bacterial EVs. Non-parenchymal liver cell fractions were used as the source for liver EVs, with centrifugal separations as illustrated in Schema 1. Livers were removed, digested into single-cell suspensions, and then subjected to centrifugation at various forces: 50 g to remove hepatocytes, 400 g to remove immune cells, 4000 g to remove bacteria and small fractions, and 10,000 g to collect EVs and perform antibody staining as described in Schema 1. Size, concentration, and intensity of liver EVs were characterized by nanoparticle tracking analysis (Figure 1B). The mean diameter of liver EVs was 220.9 ± 8.6 nm. Filtration through 0.45 μm did not significantly affect the mean diameter, which was 226.5 ± 9.1 nm, and the graph shape remained consistent, suggesting that the main EV size range was between 280 and 400 nm (Figure 1B, main graph peak). A schematic overlay illustration compared liver EVs to fecal EVs, showing that both shared size fractions between 220 nm and 400 nm (Figure 1B).

TEM of liver EVs revealed spherical particles with dimensions ranging from 60.9 to 475 nm. The images showed the phospholipid bilayer structure of the vesicle membrane, as well as monolayer structures and cell debris (Figure 1C).

Livers and fecal samples were collected on the day of the operation and on postoperative days (POD) 1–3. The relative abundance of LTA-positive EVs in the liver significantly decreased on POD 1 (Figure 1D, ∗p = 0.015, n = 4–5). A similar significant decrease was observed for LTA on POD 2 (Figure 1D, ∗∗p = 0.005, n = 5), with a low but significant decrease on POD 3 (Figure 1D, p = 0.067, n = 3–5). The LTA fraction on POD 3 increased compared to POD 2, indicating a return to normal levels (Figure 1D, ∗p = 0.016, n = 3–5). LPS fractions in the liver did not show significant changes post-surgery (Figure 1D). Non-significant changes were observed in the accumulative LPS and LTA percent fractions at the days after surgery in the livers (Figure 1F). Fecal EVs collected after surgery were analyzed, revealing an insignificant reduction in LTA fractions on POD 1 compared to the day of operation (Figure 1C, bottom, n = 4–6). There was a significant increase in LTA between POD 1 and POD 2 (Figure 1D, ∗p = 0.038, n = 5–6). LPS fractions in stools showed a significant increase on POD 1 and POD 2 compared to the day of operation (Figure 1D, ∗p = 0.014 and ∗p = 0.012, n = 4–6). The LTA/LPS ratio in the liver was significantly decreased on POD 1, POD 2, and POD 3 compared to the day of operation (Figure 1E, ∗p = 0.012, ∗∗∗p = 0.0001, and ∗p = 0.038, n = 3–5). The LTA/LPS ratio began to increase and return to normal on POD 3 compared to POD 2 (p = 0.061). Similar patterns were observed in stools, with a significant decrease in the LTA/LPS ratio on POD 1 and POD 2 compared to the day of operation (Figure 1E, ∗p = 0.024 and ∗p = 0.031), followed by an increase between POD 1 and POD 2 (∗p = 0.024). Postoperative accumulated abundances of LTA and LPS showed no significant differences between days (Figure 1F). In summary, LTA was the variable that significantly affected the LTA/LPS ratios in the liver, and both LTA and LPS showed significant variations after surgery in feces. We demonstrate that the EV LTA-positive population, which represents the Gram-positive bacteria population, declined dramatically after surgery, similarly in both liver and feces.

Preoperative microbiota transplantations results in predictable change of gut microbiome following 70% PH

Next, we studied the gut microbiome composition, liver microbiome signature, and 70% hepatectomy outcomes.

Microbiota intervention techniques were utilized to modulate the gut microbiota composition in recipient mice. The experimental design is outlined in Figure 2, Schema 2. In brief, the impact of microbiota transplantation on LR was investigated using a model where mice underwent a two-week course of broad-spectrum antibiotics, followed by gastric gavage with solutions containing four distinct microbiota compositions: stools from naive and obese mice (representing healthy and dysbiotic microbiomes with high and low bacterial richness, respectively); probiotics (with low diversity but known beneficial species); and saline, serving as a vehicle control for antibiotic treatment and representing spontaneous gut reconstitution. The gavage was followed by a 70% PH performed on the fifth day post-microbiota transfer. The success of gut microbiome manipulation was first assessed. Using 16S rDNA next-generation sequencing (NGS), we profiled the bacterial composition in the gavage source solutions (Figure 2A, Sources (S)) and in the feces of recipients four days post-surgery (Figure 2A, Fecal (F)). The gut microbiota compositions of the naive and obese groups closely mirrored those of their respective microbiota donors. In probiotic recipient mice, the bacterial class Clostridia successfully colonized 69% of the gut, with an additional 3% colonization by other probiotic bacteria, while the class Erysipelotrichia, which was not included in the gavage, unexpectedly colonized 27% of the gut in these mice (Figure 2A). Hierarchical heatmap of the top 30 most abundant bacterial genera across experimental groups reflects biological pairing (e.g., naive with naive (Gut)). Taxa (rows) are clustered based on similarity in abundance profiles, illustrating apparent but non-statistically tested differences between microbiota sources and the resulting transplanted microbiomes (Figure 2B). Analysis of α-diversity based on grouped feces (n = 1) for microbiota sources and the resulting transplanted microbiomes, using indices (Richness, Shannon Entropy, and Chao 1 index), observed similarity between the microbiota profiles of the source solutions and the gut microbiota of the recipients (Figure 2C), indicating the effectiveness of the microbiota intervention technique. At the species level, the naive group exhibited the highest species richness (293 species), followed by the obese group (230 species), and the probiotics group (123 species). The probiotics group also demonstrated the lowest phylogenetic diversity and species richness, as indicated by the Chao 1 index (Figure 2C, right).

Spontaneous gut microbiome recolonization was observed in the vehicle group, likely attributable to the background microbiota present in the facility’s mice (Figure 2A). The bacterial classes Clostridia (40.36%), Erysipelotrichia (20.71%), Bacilli (27.81%), Actinobacteria (8.15%), and Verrucomicrobia (2.38%) were identified (Figure 2A), exhibiting low species richness and a low Chao 1 index. Background contamination was also detected in low read counts in the saline group, reflecting potential contamination during bacterial DNA extraction, PCR amplification, and NGS processes. Importantly, this non-specific gut recolonization observed in antibiotic-treated mice receiving saline was absent in the stools of mice receiving probiotics, which exclusively exhibited the phylum Firmicutes (99.52%). This controlled study of gut microbiome compositions now allows us to compare the liver microbial signatures following 70% PH.

The liver microbiome was analyzed using 16S rRNA gene amplicon sequencing. Bacterial DNA was isolated from frozen liver tissue using a low-bacterial-biomass DNA isolation method. To estimate the abundance of taxa, operational taxonomic units (OTUs) were used to identify differences in genus profiles between groups (n = 4–5; Figure 3B). Genus-level analysis of each liver sample revealed similar proportions among recipient mice within the same group, with significant differences observed between groups (Figure 3B). Statistical significance was determined by Student’s t test at the genus taxa level (n = 4–5).

LEfSe analysis was performed to identify bacterial genera significantly enriched in liver samples collected on day 4 following 70% PH in mice that received group-specific microbiota transplants (Naive, Probiotics, Obese, or Vehicle). Using the R implementation of the LEfSe algorithm provided by the Huttenhower Lab,9 we observed distinct taxonomic signatures associated with each group. Notably, the obese group demonstrated a markedly different hierarchical pattern of bacterial enrichment compared to the other groups, with several genera significantly overrepresented across multiple taxonomic levels (LDA score >2.0, p < 0.05). The enriched genera in the obese group included Ambiguous_taxa-15, uncultured bacterium-33, uncultured bacterium-19, Flavobacterium, Ambiguous_taxa-11, uncultured bacterium-18, and uncultured bacterium-20. This unique liver-associated microbiome composition in the obese microbiota recipients may serve as a signature marker for impaired LR, as this group consistently showed reduced regeneration rates following PH (Figure 3C).

We used alpha diversity metrics to assess the diversity within sample communities. Shannon entropy and Simpson’s indexes showed significant differences between groups, with the highest scores observed in the probiotic group and the lowest in the obese group. No significant differences were observed using the Chao 1 index (Figure 3D).

Beta diversity analysis was used to assess differences between treatments. Using the unweighted UniFrac calculation, we measured the phylogenetic relationships between groups. Significant clustering of groups with similar dissimilarity was observed among recipient mice (Figure 3E), suggesting the effectiveness of alpha and beta diversity methods in diagnosing differences between recipients, with potential correlations to LR processes.

The composition of transplanted microbiota differentially affects survival after 70% partial hepatectomy

70% PH was performed on gut-manipulated mice recipients as described previously. The survival rates post-surgery are presented in Figure 5A. Mortality rates were 8.3% in the vehicle group (n = 9), 26.6% in mice receiving FMT from stools of naive mice (n = 15), and 73.3% in mice receiving obese gavage solution (n = 15). The probiotics recipient group showed a mortality rate of 11% (n = 9), with significant differences observed between groups (∗∗∗p = 0.0004, Log rank test). Based on these results, we sought to elucidate the cause of the high mortality associated with obese FMT.

Our murine model involved two main interventions: pre-operative antibiotic treatment (microbiome depletion) and microbiota transplantation using different sources. The effect of pre-operative antibiotic treatment on survival rates following 70% PH was studied in both naive and obese mice. Figure 5B presents data from four to six different experiments (n = 17 to 38). Naive and obese mice showed mortality rates of 20% and 25%, respectively, after surgery without additional interventions. After antibiotic treatment, a significantly higher mortality rate was found in naive mice (∗p = 0.018), while a non-significant trend was observed in obese mice. Following antibiotic treatment and 70% PH, the same survival rate was observed in both naive and obese mice (Figure 5B).

To further investigate, we hypothesized that the obese microbiota source and the intra-gastric intervention of microbiota transplantation played a role in the increased mortality. A dose-dependent study was conducted to confirm the biological effect. We diluted the dose of the transplanted microbiota in the gavage solution by 30%. As shown in Figure 5A, dose reduction resulted in no mortality events, compared to the 73.3% mortality rate observed with the initial full dose of transplanted obese microbiota.

We also compared the bacterial load in the solutions. Bacterial DNA was extracted from three different preparations of microbiota solutions, and 16S rDNA concentrations were compared to known calibrated E. coli 16S rDNA concentrations. Significant differences were observed: 16S rDNA concentrations were 25.2 and 7.96 ng/μL for naive and obese (full dose) solutions and 252.3 ng/μL, respectively (Figure 5C, ∗p = 0.022, n = 3). Although the bacterial load in the obese solution was significantly lower compared to the naive solution, it was still harmful to naive mice undergoing 70% PH in a dose-dependent manner.

The composition of transplanted microbiota differentially affects liver regeneration rate following 70% partial hepatectomy

We investigated the process of LR after 70% PH in mice receiving gavage containing different microbial solutions.

Parameters of LR, including liver-to-body weight ratio and proliferation markers (cyclin D expression and Ki67 staining), were evaluated in the treated mice. The liver-to-body weight ratio was significantly increased in naive FMT and probiotics-transplanted recipients compared to the vehicle group, with no significant differences observed between the obese FMT group and the saline vehicle group. Hepatocyte proliferation was evaluated in resected liver tissue using Ki67 immunohistochemical staining, showed an increasing trend in the naive (p = 0.071) and probiotics (p = 0.058) recipient groups compared to the vehicle group. In contrast, a significant reduction was observed in the obese group compared to the vehicle group (p = 0.023). Cyclin D expression was assessed by RT-PCR showed significantly increased only in the probiotics-transplanted group (Figure 5D). Comparisons between groups showed a highly significant advantage for the probiotics group over the obese FMT recipient group across all three parameters (Figure 5D right panel).

Discussion

Recent advancements in intestinal microbiome research have significantly enhanced our understanding of liver disorders, including malignant and non-malignant cirrhosis, nonalcoholic fatty liver disease (NAFLD), and the LR process.10,11,12,13,14 Despite extensive data linking intestinal microbiome profiles, metabolism, immune responses, and inflammation to liver pathology and regeneration, the role of the intrahepatic microbiome remains less well understood.

This study aims to address this gap by identifying intrahepatic microbial DNA and vesicles associated with post-operative LR and investigating their functional implications in the context of clinically relevant microbiota transplantation.

Our findings reveal dynamic changes in both intestinal and intrahepatic microbial DNA and vesicles following surgery. LR was impaired following transplantation with obese microbiota and enhanced with probiotic microbiota. These effects were significantly correlated with intrahepatic bacterial profiles and their functions.

We employed and extended an EV isolation protocol previously developed in our lab,7 which first demonstrated that commercial probiotic preparations unexpectedly contain biologically active microbial EVs. Building on this, we hypothesized that large microbial EVs could be recovered not only from probiotics but also from feces and liver tissue. Using flow cytometry with anti-microbial antibodies targeting epitopes such as LPS and LTA, along with nanoparticle tracking analysis and TEM, we characterized EVs in these compartments. Our data support the presence of microbial EVs in both gut and liver environments and suggest their role in shaping the hepatic microbial landscape post-surgery. This strategy offers a novel and accessible approach for tracking microbial particles and profiling microbiome composition in complex tissues, with potential translational value.

We focused on the migration of gut-derived bacterial vesicles to the liver after major surgery. Tracking the LPS/LTA vesicle ratio in fecal and liver samples showed immediate post-surgical changes, with LTA-positive vesicles driving these alterations. This aligns with previous studies reporting dynamic gut-liver bacterial shifts after surgeries.5,6 To address the limitation in tracking precision, we used the LPS/LTA ratio per sample and time point. LPS-positive EVs ranged from 10% to 16%, while LTA-positive EVs were ∼10-fold lower (0.5%–1.5%). A significant reduction in LTA-positive EVs was seen in the liver on day 1, with a non-significant overall change in cumulative LTA+LPS-positive EVs, likely due to the lower baseline of LTA-positive EVs. By day 2, both showed non-significant increases. Although we observed increased liver microbial DNA following surgery, this estimation was based on genomic DNA as a reference point. In contrast, intrahepatic microbial vesicle concentrations were not compared to a baseline; instead, the relative distribution between vesicle compartments was analyzed. The liver’s ability to clear bacterial DNA versus EVs may affect their detection timing. Future studies should address the clearance kinetics of each fraction individually.

Our data show a significant negative correlation between LR and intrahepatic Gram-positive bacterial DNA, highlighting the role of LTA and LPS in modulating regeneration. Our previous work showed probiotic-derived vesicles promote LR via LPS-induced VCAM-1 expression in liver sinusoidal endothelial cells, suggesting a role for LTA-positive vesicles in liver recovery. In the obese cohort, reduced regeneration was linked to a high abundance of intrahepatic Gram-positive bacteria, potentially serving as a PHLF predictor. Increased intrahepatic levels of aerobic or facultative anaerobic bacteria, spore-forming bacteria, and butyrate producers were also associated with impaired LR. Aggressive microbiome depletion using antibiotics negatively affected survival in naive mice post-surgery but had less impact on obese mice, suggesting different physiological adaptation to antibiotic intervention.

Emerging evidence links the obese gut microbiome to systemic disorders via metabolic alterations and secondary metabolite changes.15 Higher liver bacterial rDNA in obese individuals versus controls suggests a role for bacterial DNA in NAFLD patient.6 The gut microbiome, encompassing live organisms, cellular debris, secreted vesicles, and free molecular structures known as damage-associated molecular pattern molecules (DAMPs), interacts with the immune system to modulate physiological processes.16 The vast heterogeneity of gut DAMPs is simultaneously recognized by the immune system. Bacterial DNA translocation from the gut into the bloodstream and organs, depending on intestinal barrier integrity, is well-documented phenomenon.17 LPS, enriched in obese dysbiotic microbiota, can induce cytokine storms and sepsis in mice, increasing mortality. Intestinal permeability, impacted by various pathologies including stress.18 Our findings suggest that the gut microbiome modulates immune responses and liver cell function via translocated microbiota particles. Despite post-surgical hepatic microbiome changes, gut composition does not always mirror hepatic profiles, indicating additional intrahepatic DNA sources, such as the portal vein, bile duct, or Kupffer cells.19 Aerobic bacteria in the liver, absent in a healthy gut, were associated with reduced regeneration capacity. Specific bacterial orders differed between naive, obese, and probiotic groups. For example, pseudomonadales, enriched in the obese group, are linked to nonealcoholic fatty liver disease (NASH).19

Our 16S rRNA sequencing revealed distinct intrahepatic bacterial profiles post-surgery. The naive group showed dominance of Enterobacteriales, Bacteroidales, and Erysipelotrichales (83% total). Probiotics and vehicle groups were dominated by Clostridiales (34% and 64%, respectively). The obese group had 10 genera above 3% relative abundance, with Bacillales, Lactobacillales, and Sphingomonadales each ∼14%–18%, but no dominant genus.

The optimal microbiome composition for LR remains under investigation. Our data show that the probiotic group, dominated by Firmicutes, had low diversity and richness but fully supported regeneration. This challenges the traditional notion that higher diversity equates to a healthier microbiome. The spontaneous migration of gut-derived bacterial vesicles to the liver post-hepatectomy raises questions about their role in recovery. Absence of dominant commensals in the gut promotes rare species, impacting liver profiles. The probiotic group’s successful regeneration supports our earlier finding that LTA-positive vesicles promote liver recovery via neutrophil and macrophage clearance from resected liver. The post-surgery drop and normalization of LTA-positive vesicles further supports their essential role.

Microbiota preparation standardization is critical for reproducibility and safety in experimental and clinical contexts. FMT variability in microbial content and viability can cause inconsistent results. Our preparation method, based on DNA concentration in the solution, consistently maintained bacterial load and composition across all experiments, supporting standardization in microbiota-based interventions.

Our findings highlight the importance of microbiome recovery after antibiotic depletion prior to surgery. Mice allowed recovery showed significantly lower mortality after hepatectomy than those operated on immediately, suggesting that even partial microbiome restoration, spontaneous or via transplantation, enhances survival. These results align with prior studies and underscore the importance of timing in microbiota modulation, particularly in preoperative antibiotic use.

Absence of beneficial gut species reduces regeneration and permits expansion of typically suppressed bacteria. Obese fecal microbiota donors led to rare genera dominance in the liver, impairing regeneration. We propose that liver tissue bacterial DNA load and profile may serve as valuable tools for predicting PHLF and guiding therapy.

Conclusion and future perspective

This study highlights the critical role of intrahepatic bacterial DNA and microbial EVs in shaping LR outcomes following major hepatic resection. We demonstrate that specific microbial functions and profiles—particularly an enrichment of Gram-positive, aerobic, motile, and spore-forming bacteria, as well as butyrate producers—are negatively associated with regeneration, especially in the context of a dysbiotic or obese gut microbiome. Conversely, the presence of microbial vesicles with defined immune recognition patterns (e.g., LTA-positive EVs) and specific commensal populations (e.g., Bacteroidales, Clostridiales) appear to support regenerative processes.

Importantly, our findings suggest that intrahepatic microbial DNA content and composition do not simply mirror the gut microbiota but are shaped by selective translocation, immune interactions, and possibly hepatic microenvironmental niches. These observations underscore the liver’s role as a distinct microbial compartment with functional consequences for surgical recovery.

Limitations of the study

A key limitation of this study is that it was conducted in a murine model. While it provides conceptual evidence, the findings require validation in human cohorts to determine whether specific bacterial taxa or liver microbial profiles can reliably serve as early prognostic markers for PHLF in clinical practice. Butyrate producers are known to support LR, consistent with our gut-profile data and SCFA roles in lipid synthesis. However, we observed a negative correlation between regeneration and acetate-producing bacterial DNA in both liver and gut. Microbial DNA in liver tissue does not fully reflect gut composition or function. Future studies are needed to define the roles of bacterial translocation and its effects on LR, especially the significance of dominant commensal species in transplant material.

Experimental model

Study design

The objective of this study was to evaluate the impact of liver microbiome manipulation on intrahepatic microbial profiles and its effects on LR and survival following a 70% PH. Four different microbiota treatments were used: feces from naive mice, feces from obese mice, probiotics, and a vehicle control. Microbiota transplantation was administered via gavage following an initial course of antibiotic treatment. Five male age-matched mice per group were treated, with two to three separate experiments conducted. Survival rates, LR parameters, bacterial DNA load, and microbiome profiles of liver and fecal samples were assessed at 2 or 4 days post-surgery. The success of microbiota transplantation was evaluated by comparing the microbiome of the gavage treatment with that of the recipient mice on day 4 after surgery. The bacterial DNA load in the liver parenchyma was measured on day 4 post-PH and compared with that of healthy naive livers. Naive mice underwent surgery without any microbiome manipulations to assess whether changes in bacterial DNA or bacterial vesicles could be detected in the regenerated liver tissue, as well as to determine the timeline of these changes in the days following surgery.

Animal subjects and ethical compliance

Animal studies were conducted using 8- to 12-week-old male C57BL/6 (225 mice) or BALB/C mice (31 mice) (Envigo, Israel). The mice were maintained in a specific pathogen-free facility, with 12-h light/dark cycles and a constant temperature of 22°C.

All animal studies were performed in accordance with protocols approved by the Institutional Animal Care and Use Committee of Tel Aviv Medical Center, adhering strictly to national, local, and institutional guidelines (Approval Nos. 13-4-16, 37-6-17 and 25-08-18).

Surgical procedures

Animals were anesthetized with ketamine (82.5 mg/kg, Clorketam, Vetoquinol, France) and Sedxylan (22 mg/kg, Urovet, Netherlands) administered intraperitoneally at 10 μL/g. The abdomen was shaved using an electronic razor sterilized with 70% ethanol. A midline laparotomy was performed using scissors. The left and median lobes of the liver were resected, comprising 70% of the liver mass. The abdominal wall was closed with 4/0 Vicryl sutures, and the skin was closed with 3/0 silk sutures. In the sham laparotomy procedure, the small intestine and liver lobes were gently manipulated with sterile swabs, followed by a 100 μL subcutaneous injection of metamizole (100 μg per mouse, Dipyrone, TEVA, Israel).

Feces collection and microbiota transplantation

Feces were collected from baive mice after 1 week of accumulation. Obese mice were induced by exposure to a high-fat diet (Adjusted Calories Diet 60/Fat, Envigo) for 8 weeks, followed by fecal sample collection. For probiotics transplantation, Supherb Bio-25 was used, which, according to the manufacturer, contains at least 25 billion active bacteria of the following species: Bifidobacterium bifidum, Lactobacillus rhamnosus, Lactococcus lactis, Lactobacillus casei subsp. casei, Bifidobacterium breve, Streptococcus thermophilus, Bifidobacterium longum subsp. longum, Lactobacillus casei subsp. paracasei, Lactobacillus plantarum, and Bifidobacterium longum subsp. infantis.

After collection, feces were kept on ice, freshly weighed, homogenized in PBS (100 mg/mL), and filtered through a 100-micron stainless steel mesh. The filtered homogenate was vortexed for 3 min, followed by 1 min of rest to allow sedimentation of aggregates. The supernatant was used for immediate oral gavage administration. For probiotic preparation, one Supherb Bio-25 capsule was dissolved in 3–5 mL of PBS, vortexed for 3 min, followed by 1 min of rest, and the supernatant was used immediately.

For microbiota transplantation, mice were first treated with a broad-spectrum antibiotic regimen in drinking water, consisting of 1 g/L Ampicillin (Sigma-Aldrich-Merck, Germany), Neomycin sulfate (Sigma-Aldrich-Merck, Germany), and Metronidazole (Enzo Life Sciences, US), along with 500 mg/L Vancomycin (BioAvenir, Spain) for 2 weeks. The antibiotic solution was changed weekly. After the completion of the 2-week antibiotic course, 200 μL of processed microbiota was administered via oral gavage. Two doses of obese mice feces were given. The microbiota was administered intragastrically using a stainless steel feeding tube. Food was removed for 2 h prior to microbiota administration and reintroduced immediately afterward.

Method details

Bacterial EVs isolation characterization, and analysis

Fecal bacteria were isolated from fresh murine feces, which were collected on ice, weighed, and immediately processed. Five milliliters of PBS was mixed with 250 mg of feces, followed by vigorous vortexing and filtration through a 100 μm strainer.

For liver EV isolation, non-parenchymal cells were separated using a collagenase-DNase digestion protocol. Briefly, the liver, removed in 4 mL of PBS, was cut into ∼1 mm pieces and mixed with 500 μL of 5000 U/ml collagenase type IV (Sigma, Israel) and 500 μL of 2 mg/mL DNase I (Sigma, Israel). After 45 min of incubation at 37°C, the mixture was pushed through a 100 μm strainer into 15 mL tubes. The cells were centrifuged at 50 g for 5 min, and the supernatant was then centrifuged at 400 g. The resulting suspension was used for liver EV isolation, while the pellet was used for the study of non-parenchymal cells. The EVs (from fecal or liver sources) were centrifuged at 4000 g for 10 min, followed by a second centrifugation at 10000g for 10 min.

Bacterial EVs characterization

Bacterial epitopes on EVs were labeled with antibodies against anti-LTA (Novus Bio, US) and anti-LPS (Abcam, UK), followed by secondary antibodies biotin anti-mouse IgG3 or biotin anti-LPS, and then streptavidin APC (BioLegend, US).

Bacterial EVs analysis

For FACS analysis of bacterial EVs, we used a BD FACScanto II Cell Analyzer. FACS settings for granularity and size were calibrated using E. coli expressing RFP (kindly provided by Dr. Clay Davis, Shraga Segal Department of Microbiology, Immunology, and Genetics, Ben-Gurion University, Beer-Sheva, Israel) and labeled EVs with anti-bacterial epitope antibodies. EVs were filtered using a syringe-driven filter unit (Merck Millipore, Ireland) and stained with fluorescent antibodies (as described above) for size fraction characterization of liver and fecal bacterial EVs. Liver EV populations were analyzed by FACS for their relative abundance of LPS and LTA fractions, comparing their intensity to that of a control antibody or streptavidin-APC control. Fluorescent transgenic red fluorescent protein (RFP) E. coli was used to determine size and granularity as described earlier.7 Transmission electron microscopy (TEM) was performed by adsorbing samples onto Formvar/carbon-coated grids and staining with 2% aqueous uranyl acetate for 30 s. The samples were examined with a JEM 1400 Plus transmission electron microscope (JEOL, Japan). Size distribution of vesicles based on Brownian motion was analyzed using Nano-Sight LM20 (Nano-Sight, Amesbury, UK) for nanoparticle tracking analysis (NTA). Briefly, the EVs fraction was diluted 1:1,000 with PBS at 23°C, and size dispersion was measured. A video of 30–60 s at a frame rate of 30 frames/s was recorded, and particle movement was analyzed using NTA software (version 2.3, Nano-Sight).

Bacterial DNA extraction

For the quantification of liver bacterial DNA, 30 mg of each snap-frozen tissue sample was processed using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen, Hilden, Germany). To optimize the recovery of bacterial DNA, a bead-beating step was incorporated to disrupt bacterial cell walls. The tissue was homogenized in Buffer ATL, mixed with Reagent Dx (Qiagen) and silica beads (Biospec), and treated with Proteinase K. The mixture was vortexed using a vortex adapter (Qiagen 13000-V1-24) and incubated at 56°C for 1 h.

Bacterial DNA quantification

Extracted DNA was then quantified and detected using the Femto Bacterial DNA Quantification Kit (ZYMO Research, California, US).

Next generation sequencing (NGS) study

DNA extraction and purification from bacterial and liver parenchyma samples were conducted using the DNeasy Blood and Tissue Kit (Qiagen), with an additional bead-beating step to enhance DNA release. NGS was performed by HyLabs Laboratories, Israel. The sequencing targeted five regions of the 16S rRNA gene, amplified using 16S primer sets as described by Nejman and Straussman. DNA library preparation, quality control, sequencing on the MiSeq platform (25,000 reads), and subsequent analysis were conducted following the Earth Microbiome Project protocols. HyLabs detailed protocol: DNA concentrations were measured by nanodrop, and 20 ng of DNA was used for 16s library preparation. 16s libraries for Illumina sequencing were prepared using a two-step PCR protocol. The first PCR was done using the pool of 5 primers, to each primer we added a tail (CS1 for the forward primers, CS2 for the reverse primers) that is used in the second PCR reaction. The PCR program used was as follows: 95° 3-min (activation of enzyme), 20 cycles of 98° 10 –seconds; 55° 10 –seconds, 72° 20 –seconds. For the second PCR to add the adaptor and index primers, we use the Fluidigm Access Array index primers for Illumina, the PCR program is the same as in the first step, but only 10 cycles are done instead of 20. The primers were mixed together. After the second PCR, samples are cleaned using Kapa beads (identical to AmpureXP beads) and concentrations were measured by Qubit. The samples were then pooled together in equal amounts, and the pooled sample was run on the Tapestation (Agilent) using a DNA 1000 screentape to determine the size of the pooled libraries. The pooled library was then loaded on the Illumina Miseq and sequenced using a Miseq v2 Kit (500 cycles) to generate 2x250 PE reads. The data were de-multiplexed using Basespace, the Illumina cloud software to generate 2 FASTQ files per sample. Analysis was done as the earth microbiome protocol for the specific primers, the replicons size cut off was less than 120bp. The pipeline was adjusted to reflect the different amplicon sizes, the pipeline went like this: Trim for adaptor and quality, Merge reads, Trim reads again for length, discarding reads shorter than 120 bases, OTU picking using the SILVA database. The FASTQ files were further analyzed using CLC-bio (Qiagen) to generate abundance, OTU tables and for bioinformatics alpha and beta diversity studies.

qRT-PCR

Total RNA from snap-frozen livers (−80°C storage) was extracted (∼30 mg tissue) by means of a PureLink RNA Mini kit (Invitrogen, US). Total RNA concentrations were measured with a NanoDropOne spectrophotometer (ThermoFisher Scientific, US). Reverse transcription for cDNA preparation was performed with a qScript cDNA Synthesis Kit (Quanta Bio, US). RT-PCR was performed on the cDNA for each transcript with an SYBR Green PCR Master kit (Applied Biosystems, US) using its primers set (key resources table, HyLabs, Israel) following the manufacturer’s directions. Triplicate reactions were incubated in an Applied Biosystems StepOnePlus Real-Time PCR System in a 96-well plate for 20s at 95°C, followed by 40 cycles for 1s at 95°C and 20s at 60°C. The threshold cycle (Ct) was calculated by the ABI StepOne Sequence Detection System software v2.3 for each sample. Ribosomal protein large PO was used as internal control. Gene expression levels were quantified with the ABI StepOne Software v2.3, and relative fold expression was calculated by the comparative Ct (2−ΔΔCt) method for relative quantitation of gene expression. Statistical analysis was performed to provide standard deviations for comparisons of gene expression between samples as well as the p-values from t-tests for comparison between biological groups.

Immunohistochemistry for Ki67

Paraffin-embedded liver tissue sections were deparaffinized, rehydrated, and subjected to heat-induced antigen retrieval using citrate buffer (pH 6.0). Endogenous peroxidase activity was blocked with 3% hydrogen peroxide. Sections were incubated with a primary anti-Ki67 antibody (e.g., rabbit monoclonal, dilution 1:100) followed by an HRP-conjugated secondary antibody. Signal detection was performed using DAB substrate, and nuclei were counterstained with hematoxylin. Slides were dehydrated, cleared in xylene, and mounted with a permanent medium. Quantification of Ki67-positive Hepatocytes: Ki67-positive hepatocytes were identified as cells showing distinct nuclear DAB staining. Positive cells were manually counted in at least five randomly selected high-power fields (HPFs, 400× magnification) per sample, and the average number of positive cells per HPF was calculated.

Statistical analysis

Statistical differences between two groups were assessed using an unpaired two-tailed Student’s t test or log rank (Mantel Cox) test and log rank test for trend. All analyses were conducted with Prism 8 software (GraphPad). Graphical data are presented as mean values with error bars representing the standard error of the mean (SEM). Results are expressed as mean ± SEM, with asterisks indicating statistical significance as appeared in figure legend. Outlier values, defined as those exceeding two standard deviations above the mean, were excluded prior to significance calculations.
