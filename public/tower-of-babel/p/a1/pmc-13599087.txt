Evaluating departmental performance and reimbursement–cost pricing distortions under DRG-based payment: evidence from a tertiary hospital in China
Authors: Jackie Zhanbiao Li, Yingqian Lao
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundDiagnosis-Related Group (DRG)-based payment reform aims to improve efficiency and restrain unnecessary expenditure, yet routine hospital indicators often report case mix, cost deviation, and profitability separately. This separation may obscure whether an unfavorable departmental profile reflects low cost-adjusted weighted output, reimbursement–cost misalignment, or an adverse position within the complexity–profitability distribution.ObjectiveThis study develops and applies a DRG-Based Profitability–Efficiency Mapping (DRG-PEM) framework to evaluate departmental performance and identify reimbursement–cost pricing distortions within a DRG-based payment system.MethodsA retrospective analysis was conducted using discharge and cost data from 29 clinical departments in a large tertiary general hospital in China between January and June 2022. The analytical workbook contained 25,379 discharges, 1,962 department–DRG group records, and 595 unique DRG codes. DRG-PEM integrates three sequential modules: the Structural Efficiency Score (SES), the Weight–Cost Deviation Index (WCDI), and a DRG profitability–complexity quadrant map. Scenario simulation was performed for high-deviation DRG groups meeting predefined criteria: baseline WCDI > 40%, theoretical reimbursement below observed cost, complete cost data, and stable department-level case volume.ResultsSES values ranged from 23.86 in Pulmonary Medicine to 71.38 in Stomatology, demonstrating substantial interdepartmental heterogeneity. WCDI values ranged from 31.77% in Urology to 84.56% in Stomatology; similarly high values were observed in Pediatrics (83.72%), Pain Medicine (76.51%), and Anorectal Traditional Chinese Medicine (63.75%). Directional financial indicators showed that high WCDI did not uniformly indicate under-reimbursement: Stomatology had high WCDI but positive average profit per case, whereas ICU combined high CMI (1.81), negative average profit per case (−¥11,286.44), and severe WCDI (46.07%). Scenario simulations showed that reimbursement-to-cost ratios improved after hypothetical weight increases but remained below 1.0 for several high-deviation groups.ConclusionsDRG-PEM provides an implementable department-level framework for distinguishing cost-adjusted weighted output, reimbursement deviation, and structural financial risk under DRG payment. The framework supports integrated assessment of departmental performance and can inform targeted DRG weight adjustment, cost control, and internal resource allocation and institutional operational governance.Supplementary InformationThe online version contains supplementary material available at https://doi.org/10.1186/s12913-026-14989-y.

Introduction

The Diagnosis-Related Group (DRG) payment system has become a major instrument of hospital payment reform in many countries because it links reimbursement to standardized case categories rather than to service volume alone [1–9]. In China, DRG reform has accelerated the transition from fee-for-service incentives toward a more value-oriented payment logic [10–12]. However, implementation in tertiary hospitals remains challenging because case complexity, staffing patterns, technology intensity, and local cost structures are not always sufficiently captured by existing DRG pricing rules [13–20].

A recurrent concern is that DRG payment may generate reimbursement–cost tension when the benchmark rate and assigned weight fail to reflect observed resource consumption [5, 6, 10–16, 18, 19]. In the Chinese CHS-DRG environment, this tension is shaped by base-rate setting, local price-bureau calibration, hospital budget constraints, coding capacity, and regional differences in service costs [13, 16, 18, 19]. Therefore, structural under-compensation reflects the combined effects of clinical complexity, pricing design, and local calibration within the DRG payment system.

Existing DRG studies and related hospital management research have examined expenditure control, length of stay, utilization, case-mix funding, hospital efficiency, operational management, productivity, and organizational development [5, 10, 11, 18–28]. These studies provide important evidence on the effects of DRG reform, but many routine hospital dashboards still report case-mix intensity, cost deviation, and profitability as separate indicators [5, 10, 11, 18–25]. Separate reporting can identify that a department appears unfavorable, but it does not clarify whether the problem is low cost-adjusted weighted output, reimbursement–cost mismatch, or a structurally disadvantageous complexity–profit profile.

Frontier methods such as Data Envelopment Analysis (DEA) and Stochastic Frontier Analysis (SFA) can estimate technical efficiency more rigorously when detailed inputs such as beds, staffing, equipment, and longitudinal output measures are available [24, 25, 29]. For routine hospital management, DRG-PEM provides a simpler, less data-intensive, and managerially accessible diagnostic sequence based on routinely available DRG and cost-accounting data. This design allows departments to be assessed through comparable indicators derived from reimbursement, cost, weight, volume, and profitability information.

To address this diagnostic gap, the present study develops a DRG-Based Profitability–Efficiency Mapping (DRG-PEM) framework and applies it to retrospective discharge data from a large tertiary hospital in eastern China. The methodological contribution lies in integrating three complementary components into a transparent decision sequence: a cost-adjusted weighted-output module, a pricing-deviation module, and a structural positioning module. The study aims to evaluate departmental performance under DRG-based payment, provide evidence for local refinement of DRG-based payment management, and support clearer identification of reimbursement–cost pricing distortions in hospital operations.

Methods

Study design, data source and preprocessing

This study used a retrospective observational design. The dataset was drawn from a large tertiary general hospital in China and covered discharge-related DRG records from January to June 2022. The hospital is a high-volume tertiary institution with broad specialty coverage, marked case-mix heterogeneity, and established DRG management practice.

The analytical sample included 29 clinical departments, 25,379 discharges, 1,962 department–DRG group records, and 595 unique DRG codes. Records were included when department, DRG code, discharge count, DRG weight, benchmark payment, and observed cost fields were complete. Data preprocessing included checking the completeness and consistency of department identifiers, DRG codes, discharge counts, DRG weights, benchmark payment values, and observed cost fields. Department-level discharge totals and DRG group counts were then summarized to characterize the distribution of clinical activity across departments. No additional outlier trimming, extreme-cost exclusion, or minimum-discharge exclusion was applied before calculating department-level WCDI; complete and internally consistent department–DRG records were retained, and low-volume or sparse-code structures were flagged using the stability indicators reported in Appendices 5 and 7.

The benchmark base rate used in the theoretical reimbursement calculation was ¥13,919.54. This rate was applied to the relevant DRG weight to calculate theoretical reimbursement.

DRG-PEM framework

The DRG-PEM framework evaluates departmental performance under DRG payment through three integrated analytical modules: the Structural Efficiency Score (SES), the Weight–Cost Deviation Index (WCDI), and a profitability–complexity quadrant map. Analytical procedures and scenario simulation

The empirical analysis proceeded in four steps. First, descriptive statistics summarized departmental differences in CMI, weighted output, cost, discharge volume, and financial outcome. Second, SES and WCDI were computed for each department using the formulas specified in Appendix 1. Third, departments were mapped into the four-quadrant framework to distinguish complexity-related financial positions. Fourth, the three modules were interpreted jointly to generate management-relevant departmental performance profiles. These analytical steps and their objectives are summarized in Table 2.

Scenario simulation was implemented at the DRG-group level and summarized at the department level for reporting. Eligible DRG groups were those with baseline WCDI >40%, theoretical reimbursement below observed cost, complete cost information, and stable department-level case volume from January to June 2022. For each eligible group, the DRG weight was hypothetically increased by 10%, 15%, and 20%, while observed case mix, discharge volume, coding structure, and cost remained unchanged. These increments were used to assess the sensitivity of reimbursement-to-cost relationships to alternative DRG weight levels. Eligibility was evaluated at the department–DRG record level rather than the whole-department level; therefore, department-level WCDI may diverge from the number of eligible simulated records.

For structured reporting, Table 4 presents five departments containing high-deviation groups and heterogeneous clinical profiles. Scenario-simulation outputs for the five selected departments are reported in Appendix 6, while Appendix 7 summarizes eligibility and stability information for all departments and provides the department-level basis for interpreting the scenario simulation results. The five reported departments served as targeted illustrative archetypes rather than an exhaustive eligibility-based selection. Operationally, heterogeneous clinical profiles refer to variation in case complexity, surgical versus non-surgical services, baseline RCR, and the sensitivity of residual WCDI to proportional weight increases.

Results

Departmental heterogeneity in cost-adjusted weighted output

The observed SES pattern showed substantial heterogeneity across departments. Figure 1 visualizes the department-level SES distribution using the observed indicators reported in Appendix 5. SES values ranged from 23.86 in Pulmonary Medicine to 71.38 in Stomatology. Among high-complexity departments, Orthopedics recorded both high CMI (1.46) and relatively strong SES (69.33), whereas Neurosurgery had the highest CMI in the sample (2.33) but a more moderate SES (42.90), and ICU combined high CMI (1.81) with a relatively low SES (33.70).

Fig. 1Department-level structural efficiency score (SES) distribution

Department-level structural efficiency score (SES) distribution

Low- and medium-complexity departments also showed diverse SES profiles. Pediatrics (CMI 0.33) and Stomatology (CMI 0.67) had relatively high SES values of 58.70 and 71.38, respectively. These results describe department-level cost-adjusted weighted-output positions under observed DRG and cost conditions. Stomatology had 38 discharges and six unique DRG codes, indicating a high SES profile associated with low average cost and concentrated case volume.

Pricing misalignment identified by WCDI and signed indicators

The WCDI analysis showed widespread pricing misalignment across departments. Figure 2 ranks departments by WCDI based on the department-level indicators reported in Appendix 5. Departmental WCDI values ranged from 31.77% in Urology to 84.56% in Stomatology. Twenty-two of the 29 departments exceeded the severe-deviation threshold of 40%, and the remaining seven fell in the moderate-deviation range of 20% to 40%. No department fell within the well-aligned or mild-deviation intervals.

Fig. 2Department-level weight–cost deviation index (WCDI) distribution. Note: The dashed reference line marks the severe-deviation threshold of 40%

Department-level weight–cost deviation index (WCDI) distribution. Note: The dashed reference line marks the severe-deviation threshold of 40%

The signed indicators provided directional financial information for high-WCDI departments. Stomatology had the highest WCDI (84.56%) and the highest SES (71.38), while its department-level average profit per case was positive (¥4,189.47), indicating a profile of large reimbursement–cost deviation with positive financial outcome. ICU had high CMI (1.81), negative average profit per case (−¥11,286.44), and severe WCDI (46.07%), indicating a high-complexity and loss-making profile under the observed reimbursement structure.

Pediatrics showed different results at the department and DRG-group levels. At the whole-department level, Pediatrics had high WCDI (83.72%) and a small positive average profit per case (¥95.14). In the simulation subset for eligible pediatric high-deviation groups, the baseline reimbursement-to-cost ratio was much lower (0.163), showing pronounced reimbursement–cost imbalance within selected DRG groups despite the positive department-level average.

The vertical reference line was set at CMI = 1.00, providing an interpretable benchmark for above-average DRG case-mix weight. The horizontal reference line indicates break-even.

Using this rule, Hepatobiliary and Pancreatic Surgery, Orthopedics, General Surgery, Neurosurgery, Cardiology, Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery, and Oncology occupied the high-CMI profit quadrant. Urology and ICU occupied the high-CMI loss quadrant, indicating high-complexity departments with adverse financial outcomes. Lower-CMI loss departments included Obstetrics, Rehabilitation Medicine, Dermatology, Ophthalmology, Geriatric Traditional Chinese Medicine, and Anorectal Traditional Chinese Medicine.

The quadrant map complements SES and WCDI by adding structural positioning information based on complexity and financial outcome. A department may show relatively strong cost-adjusted weighted output while still exhibiting severe absolute deviation, or it may occupy a profit quadrant while selected DRG groups remain under-reimbursed.

Integrated application of the DRG-PEM framework

The integrated application of DRG-PEM yielded three substantive findings (see Tables 3 and 4). First, high-complexity departments were heterogeneous: several high-CMI departments retained positive financial performance, whereas ICU and Urology combined high CMI with losses. Second, high WCDI did not have a single meaning: it identified large reimbursement–cost deviation, while signed indicators distinguished whether the deviation reflected under- or over-compensation. Third, departments with low volume or few DRG codes, such as Stomatology and ICU, showed marked percentage-based deviation profiles under relatively limited case bases.

Table 4 and Appendix 6 present department-level results summarized from DRG-group simulations for selected high-deviation groups. These reimbursement-to-cost ratios correspond to eligible simulated DRG groups within each department and describe the simulated reimbursement–cost relationship of those groups. Under a 20% weight adjustment, the reimbursement-to-cost ratio increased from 0.589 to 0.706 in General Practice / Family Medicine and from 0.513 to 0.616 in Neurosurgery. Improvement was more limited for Pediatrics: the ratio increased from 0.163 to only 0.195, and residual WCDI remained 80.46%. This pattern indicates persistent reimbursement–cost imbalance where baseline payment shortfalls are extreme.

Discussion

Principal findings and methodological contribution

This study shows that department-level DRG assessment can be strengthened when cost-adjusted weighted output, reimbursement deviation, and structural financial position are interpreted jointly. Previous DRG studies established case-mix grouping and prospective payment logic, and examined cost containment, utilization, length of stay, efficiency, and quality outcomes [1–12, 18–25, 29]. Building on this literature, DRG-PEM translates routine DRG and cost-accounting data into an integrated diagnostic sequence for hospital managers. Frontier methods such as DEA and SFA estimate technical efficiency using multi-input hospital production data, whereas DRG-PEM provides a non-frontier managerial screening framework and is therefore best interpreted as a complementary diagnostic tool for routine departmental assessment [24, 25, 29].

The findings show that high-complexity departments are not uniformly disadvantaged under DRG-based payment. Some high-CMI departments, including Orthopedics, Neurosurgery, Cardiology, Thoracic and Cardiovascular Surgery, and Oncology, maintained positive financial performance. In contrast, ICU and Urology combined high CMI with negative average profit per case. This finding extends earlier work on patient complexity and profitability by identifying department-level heterogeneity in structural financial risk [18, 19, 30, 31].

The analysis also shows that absolute WCDI represents the magnitude of reimbursement–cost deviation rather than a single direction of financial imbalance. Stomatology and Pediatrics had very high WCDI values, but their aggregate department-level financial direction was positive. Conversely, ICU had a negative signed deviation and severe WCDI. Compared with prior DRG payment studies focused on expenditure, efficiency, or case-mix performance, this framework adds directional pricing-deviation assessment using signed deviation and RCR [4–8, 10, 11, 18, 19, 23–25, 29].

The absence of well-aligned or mild-deviation departments represents a notable empirical characteristic of the present hospital dataset. This pattern may be associated with the local base rate, hospital-specific cost structure, tertiary-care resource intensity, and the pragmatic threshold design adopted in this study. Consequently, most departments were classified within the moderate or severe deviation ranges. This observation is consistent with recent Chinese DRG studies reporting variation in the effects of DRG payment reform across regions, hospital types, cost structures, and local implementation environments [10, 11, 13, 18, 19]. The WCDI thresholds applied in this study were developed as pragmatic diagnostic categories and may require multicenter calibration before broader inter-hospital or system-level application.

The pediatric simulation finding highlights the persistence of reimbursement–cost imbalance in selected high-deviation groups. Even after a 20% weight increase, the simulated reimbursement-to-cost ratio for selected pediatric high-deviation groups rose only from 0.163 to 0.195. A proportional increase from such a low baseline remained far below cost coverage, indicating that extreme pediatric under-compensation requires more substantial repricing or supplementary compensation mechanisms.

Practical implications

For hospital managers, DRG-PEM provides a structured basis for prioritizing interventions. Departments with weak SES may require workflow redesign, cost-accounting review, or resource reallocation. Departments with high WCDI but positive signed deviation may require coding review, pricing review, and assessment of reimbursement–cost alignment. Departments with high CMI and losses, especially ICU and Urology in this dataset, require joint review of clinical resource needs, payment calibration, and cost allocation.

For policymakers and payers, the framework identifies candidate areas for DRG weight review, base-rate calibration, or supplementary compensation. High-WCDI departments can be screened through signed deviation, case volume, clinical necessity, and coding quality to distinguish pricing distortion from other sources of deviation. Because WCDI is absolute, these accompanying indicators help define the direction, magnitude, and practical relevance of reimbursement–cost mismatch.

DRG coding quality is a central implementation issue. If coding practice varies across departments, WCDI may partly reflect differences in coding completeness or upcoding incentives rather than genuine pricing distortion. DRG-PEM can be implemented together with regular coding audits and clinical validation of high-deviation groups to strengthen the reliability of department-level interpretation.

Limitations

This study has several limitations. First, it is based on retrospective data from a single tertiary hospital in one calendar year, which limits generalizability to other tertiary hospitals and different phases of DRG reform. Second, the analysis depends on the quality of local DRG coding and cost accounting; coding incompleteness, coding optimization, or overhead allocation rules could affect both WCDI and signed deviation. Third, SES is an internally normalized cost-adjusted weighted-output index rather than a production-frontier estimate of technical or managerial inefficiency. DEA or SFA provide formal efficiency estimation when multi-input and longitudinal data are available. Fourth, the WCDI threshold bands are pragmatic diagnostic categories and require calibration across regions, hospital types, and payment environments before broader comparative application. Fifth, no confidence intervals or formal uncertainty estimates were calculated. The appendix reports discharge volumes and DRG group counts to describe the stability of department-level estimates, while departments with low volume or few DRG codes require careful interpretation. Sixth, the scenario simulation held case mix, coding behavior, discharge volume, and observed costs constant. Behavioral responses to weight changes were outside the simulation design, including possible volume changes in newly profitable groups and fiscal sustainability changes in low-volume groups. Seventh, no outlier exclusion or trimming was applied to extreme DRG-group average costs or very-low-discharge DRG groups; therefore, percentage-based deviation estimates for sparse groups should be interpreted as administrative screening signals rather than definitive evidence of pricing error. Eighth, the absence of well-aligned or mild-deviation departments may reflect the local base rate, hospital-specific cost structure, tertiary-care resource intensity, and pragmatic threshold design; therefore, multicenter calibration is required before broader interpretation of system-wide CHS-DRG pricing failure.

Future research

Future research can extend DRG-PEM to multicenter and longitudinal datasets, including hospitals with different ownership structures, specialty portfolios, regional base rates, and coding maturity. External validation can compare SES with DEA or SFA efficiency scores and examine whether signed WCDI and RCR are associated with subsequent departmental financial stress, coding review findings, or clinical access concerns. Future simulation work can further incorporate sensitivity analysis around base-rate assumptions, alternative WCDI thresholds, and behavioral responses to DRG weight adjustment. Pediatric care, ICU care, and other services with persistent negative signed deviation represent important areas for continued analysis, because incremental proportional adjustments remain limited when baseline reimbursement is far below observed cost.

Conclusion

This study developed and applied a DRG-Based Profitability–Efficiency Mapping framework to evaluate departmental performance under DRG payment in a tertiary hospital in China. By linking SES, WCDI, signed financial indicators, and profitability–complexity quadrant mapping, the framework distinguishes cost-adjusted weighted-output performance, reimbursement misalignment, and structural financial risk. The simulation results show that extreme payment deviations, such as those observed in selected pediatric groups, remain substantial after incremental weight adjustment and indicate the need for structural repricing or supplementary payment mechanisms. The framework provides a practical decision-support approach for integrated department-level DRG performance assessment and payment management.
