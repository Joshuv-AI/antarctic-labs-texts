Beyond health: positioning mental health on the political agenda of the Americas at the 55th General Assembly of the Organization of American States
Authors: Amy Tausch, Renato Oliveira e Souza
Published: 2025

The 55th General Assembly of the Organization of American States (OAS), held in Antigua and Barbuda on June 25–27, 2025, marked a historic and unprecedented time for mental health in the Region of the Americas, featuring a high-level side event dedicated to mental health and the adoption of a landmark resolution to address the regional mental health crisis. Until recently, the topic of mental health has rarely featured prominently on the hemispheric agenda of the OAS, whose mandate is guided by the core pillars of democracy, human rights, security, and development; however, the initiative and leadership of Antigua and Barbuda ensured that it was at the forefront of regional policy discussions this year.

The side event, “Uniting the Americas for Mental Health: From Commitment to Action,” co-hosted by the Government of Antigua and Barbuda and the Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), convened delegates from Ministries of Foreign Affairs of OAS Member States, as well as Inter-American organizations and civil society representatives, to identify achievements and challenges on mental health and reinforce regional collaboration.1 The dialogue also aimed to leverage regional mental health leadership ahead of the Fourth High-Level Meeting on the Prevention and Control of Noncommunicable Diseases and the Promotion of Mental Health and Well-Being, which will take place at the United Nations General Assembly in September. Presentations were made by heads of state and government and OAS ambassadors of Antigua and Barbuda, Brazil, Canada, Chile, Costa Rica, the Dominican Republic, Spain and St. Vincent and the Grenadines. They notably included powerful remarks by the Honourable Gaston Browne, Prime Minister of Antigua and Barbuda, who spoke about the stigma his family experienced due to his late mother's mental health condition, and a video address from Gabriel Boric, President of Chile.

Through the approval of the resolution “Addressing the Critical Mental Health Crisis in the Americas,”2 countries in the region committed to increasing investment in mental health using a person-centered, rights-and equity-based approach. As part of the new resolution, and in coordination with PAHO, OAS Member States will establish a regional mental health partnership to strengthen collaboration, scale up access, and promote the implementation of community-based mental health initiatives in the Americas. Additionally, it calls for the formation of an Inter-American working group to develop a regional action plan aligned with PAHO's Strategy for Improving Mental Health and Suicide Prevention in the Region of the Americas. To raise funding, the OAS will explore the establishment of a fund open to collaboration with regional partners such as PAHO, the Development Bank of Latin America and the Caribbean (CAF), the Caribbean Development Bank, and the Inter-American Development Bank (IDB).3 The resolution also proclaims the second week of October of each year as Inter-American Mental Health Week.

In the Americas, approximately one in six people currently lives with a mental disorder.4 Mental health conditions are a leading cause of disability in the region and a risk factor for suicide, which claims more than 100,000 lives annually.5 The vast majority of people with these conditions, however, are unable to access treatment (only an estimated 18% of people experiencing psychosis receive treatment).6 They also frequently experience stigma, discrimination, and neglect, which further impact treatment access and outcomes, as well as quality of life. The COVID-19 pandemic exacerbated the mental health crisis in the Americas, contributing to an estimated increase in rates of depressive and anxiety disorders by 35% and 32% respectively in 2020.7

Despite the high disease burden attributable to mental health conditions in the region, countries have historically not adequately prioritized mental health, allocating a median of only 3% of their total health budgets to it as of 2019.6 Furthermore, the majority of public financing for mental health continues to go to psychiatric hospitals over community-based care, which is considered more accessible and effective and in line with international human rights standards.

Recognition that there can be no health without mental health has grown in recent years, albeit slowly; yet mental health is still largely considered to be an issue solely of the health sector. The inclusion of mental health as a key topic of discussion at this OAS General Assembly and the adoption of a specific resolution to address it, therefore, signals an important shift in this siloing of mental health. Indeed, newly elected OAS Secretary General Albert Ramdin, who delivered remarks at the side event, noted the strong connection between mental health and the Organization's multisectoral work, linking mental health not only to better overall health and well-being, but to improved social outcomes, greater economic productivity and strengthened human rights for the region. In this regard, he and other speakers pointed to the necessary role of all sectors in improving mental health to achieve healthier and more prosperous communities.

While the 55th OAS General Assembly marked a crucial step in placing mental health on the broader political agenda of the Americas, significant political will, investment and cross-sectoral collaboration by countries in the region will be essential to ensuring that the commitments made in Antigua and Barbuda are translated into action.
