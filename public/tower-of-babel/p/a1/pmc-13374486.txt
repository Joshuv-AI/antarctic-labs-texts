Prevalence and factors associated with depression, anxiety and stress symptoms among married women in Southern Nepal: a cross-sectional study
Authors: Urja Regmi, Yunika Acharya, Pratiksha Paudel, Poonam Subedi, Bandana Paneru, Archana Shrestha, Biraj Man Karmacharya, Shrinkhala Shrestha
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractObjectiveTo determine the prevalence and factors associated with stress, anxiety and depression among married women in Chandrapur municipality, Nepal.DesignWe conducted a cross-sectional study.SettingHousehold Survey of Chandrapur municipality, Nepal.ParticipantsWe enrolled 545 married women from June to August 2022.Outcome measuresWe administered the Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21) through face-to-face interviews to determine the prevalence of stress, anxiety and depression. We performed univariable and multivariable linear regression to identify factors associated with stress, anxiety and depression. We reported 95% CIs with p values <0.05. We conducted all the analyses in Stata 14.ResultsThe prevalence of stress, anxiety and depression was 28.8%, 16.7% and 31.1% respectively. Univariable and multivariable linear regression analysis were conducted to identify factors associated with symptoms of stress, anxiety and depression. Symptoms of stress were significantly associated with Janajati and Dalit ethnicity (β=2.64; 95% CI 1.12 to 4.16; p value: 0.001) and Terai ethnicity (β=2.07; 95% CI 0.33 to 3.80; p value: 0.02), non-Hindu religion (β=-5.02; 95% CI −7.53 to −2.52; p value <0.001), residence other than husband’s house (β=4.37; 95% CI 0.57 to 8.17; p value=0.02) and women’s occupational status (β=3.30; 95% CI 0.64 to 5.96; p value: 0.01). Symptoms of anxiety were significantly associated with residence other than husband’s house (β=2.62; 95% CI 0.11 to 5.13; p value: 0.04) and women’s occupational status (β=5.10; 95% CI 3.34 to 6.86; p value <0.001). Symptoms of depression were significantly associated with non-Hindu religion (β=−3.23; 95% CI−5.24 to −1.22; p value=0.002), and living in joint family (β=−1.51; 95% CI −2.53 to −0.49; p value=0.004) after adjusting for age, ethnicity, religion, education, age of marriage, family type, residence, occupation and migrant husband. Other prespecified variables like age, age of marriage and migrant husband were not significantly associated with the symptoms of stress, anxiety and depression in adjusted analysis.ConclusionStress, anxiety and depression symptoms were present in a significant proportion of women in Chandrapur municipality. Policy makers and health practitioners must provide significant attention in reducing the magnitude of these mental health conditions and reducing its impact on women and families.

Introduction

The burden of mental health conditions is substantial, with an estimated one in seven people worldwide living with a mental disorder.1 Globally, anxiety and depression are particularly prevalent, affecting approximately 359 million2 and 332 million3 people, respectively. Women experience a disproportionate burden of about 50% higher rates of anxiety and depression compared with men.3 These conditions significantly contribute to the global disease burden, accounting for 1 in 6 years lived with disability,4 and can lead to a reduced lifespan of 10 to 20 years for individuals with severe mental illnesses compared with the general population.4 Despite recent improvements and effective treatments, over 75% of individuals in low-income and middle-income countries (LMICs) receive no treatment for mental illness.3 Mental health influences personal growth, community resilience and socioeconomic development,5 with its impact extending beyond direct care costs to substantial productivity loss.6

While global data highlight a significant gender gap in mental health, these disparities are often magnified in LMICs like Nepal where prevalence of depression and anxiety in the general population is at 4% and 17.7%, respectively.6 Notably, these rates are higher among women, with a prevalence of 5.4% for depression and 21.9% for anxiety.7 Women in Nepal are estimated to have twice the probability of experiencing anxiety and more than three times the probability of experiencing depression compared with men.7 Depression is projected to be the leading cause of disease burden by 2030, and it already holds the leading cause among women.8 The patriarchal societal structures prevalent in many LMICs, particularly in South Asia,9 contribute to unique gender-specific stressors for women’s mental health. These stressors include heavy workloads, challenges related to childbirth, abortion/miscarriage, motherhood, caregiving responsibilities, domestic violence and sexual abuse, especially after marriage.1012 Limited access to higher education, being confined to housewife roles and lack of employment opportunities10 11 exacerbate the frequency and severity of mental health issues in women. Studies have consistently reported greater psychological distress in married women compared with married men, and among ever-married women (including those who are married, widowed or separated) compared with never-married women.10 11

Several studies have identified sociodemographic factors associated with depression, anxiety and stress among women including older age, low education attainment, larger family size, longer duration of marriage, low socioeconomic status and unemployment.91317 Scientific evidence further suggests that lower education and income levels are consistently linked to psychological distress through reduced social agency and increased exposure to daily stressors.6 Economic vulnerability, including unemployment and limited financial independence, has been shown to increase susceptibility to mental health problems by limiting a woman’s ability to navigate household power dynamics.18 Family structure also plays a critical role; variations in household size and composition directly influence mental well-being by altering the levels of social support available vs the weight of domestic responsibilities and caregiving burden.19 By examining these specific variables, this study seeks to provide the necessary empirical rationale for how socioeconomic and domestic factors shape the mental health landscape for married women in this context.

However, existing studies in Nepal have largely focused on specific vulnerable groups such as widowed or pregnant women, with comparatively limited evidence examining mental health and associated sociodemographic factors among the broader population of married women. 2021 Understanding these factors from a gender perspective is important for developing targeted interventions and contextually appropriate policies. Therefore, this study aimed to determine the prevalence of depression, anxiety and stress and identify their associated sociodemographic factors, including age, ethnicity, religion, education, occupation, family structure and husbands’ migration status, among married women in Chandrapur Municipality, Southern Nepal.

Materials and methods

Study design and setting

We conducted a cross-sectional study in Chandrapur municipality, a semi-urban city in Southern Nepal with an area of 231 square kilometres.22 According to the 2021 census, Chandrapur Municipality has a total population of 81 807, with women comprising 50.7% (41 503 women). Of these, 65% of women are married, with 35% of marriages occurring between the ages of 15 and 17 years. The female literacy rate is 63.73%, and approximately 80% of households are male-headed.23

Participant recruitment

We enrolled 545 married women for the study from June to August 2022.

Considering 90% power, a 95% level of significance, the prevalence of depression in the Nepali population (p1) is 11.7%,24 and the prevalence of depression among wives of migrant workers in Bangladesh (p2) is 27%,25 with a 17% prevalence of labour migration of Chandrapur municipality, that is, ratio of sample sizes (n2/n1) = 0.17.22 The estimated sample size was 494 married women, where 422 women should be those who live together with their husbands and 72 those whose husbands were labour migrant workers. Considering the non-response rate of 10%, the final sample size of the study was 545 married women. The sample size was calculated through an online calculator from the following link: https://epitools.ausvet.com.au/twoproportions.

The inclusion criteria of the study were: (i) married women aged 18 years or more, (ii) women married for at least 1 year, (iii) residing in that community for at least 6 months, whereas exclusion criteria were women with diagnosed severe psychiatric illness or cognitive impairment, and women who visit the household temporarily for a week or a month. Participants were required to have been married for at least 1 year to ensure that the mental health assessments reflected stable marital and household dynamics rather than acute adjustment phase distress associated with early marriage transitions. Severe psychiatric illness or cognitive impairment was identified through self-report, based on a prior diagnosis by a health professional and/or current use of psychiatric medication. Participants reporting these conditions were excluded.

A multistage systematic random sampling technique was employed. To ensure randomisation, the starting household in each neighbourhood was determined by a random start (a coin flip to choose between the first or second household). From this randomly selected household, every alternate household was approached for data collection. If the woman in the household did not meet the eligibility criteria for our study, we skipped that household and selected the next household in sequence.26 We approached married women who were available at the time of data collection. If a household has more than one woman eligible for this study, only one woman was selected randomly by lottery method to ensure independence of observations and avoid clustering of outcomes within the same household. This process was continued until the sample size was met.

Ethical clearance

We obtained ethical clearance from the Institutional Review Committee (IRC) of Kathmandu University School of Medical Sciences (KUSMS) (IRC/ KUSMS; Approved No. 39/22), Nepal. We provided detailed information about the study to the participant before conducting the interview. Eligible participants provided voluntary written consent to participate in the study. We maintained the confidentiality and anonymity of the participants by keeping all the data on password-protected computers.

Data collection tools and techniques

The researcher (UR) approached the women through household visits and conducted face-to-face interviews using pretested structured questionnaires and entered in an electronic form using the Kobo toolbox. Interviewer administered interviews were conducted in a private space in the women’s house, where no one was present except the participant to ensure confidentiality. The questionnaire consisted of two parts: sociodemographic characteristics, which were adopted from Nepal Demographic Health Survey,27 and the 21-item Depression, Anxiety and Stress Scale (DASS-21)28 to assess the symptoms of stress, anxiety and depression. The data collection tool was pretested among a representative sample comprising 10% of the total study population, and revisions were made based on participant feedback to enhance clarity. The questionnaire used in the interviews is provided in online supplemental file 1.

Outcome variables

The outcome variables were depression, anxiety and stress, assessed using the DASS-21 scale. Each domain was scored and categorised according to standard cut-offs.

Independent variables

The independent variables include sociodemographic characteristics included age (in years), ethnicity (Brahmin/Chhetri, Terai caste, Janajati and Dalit), Religion (Hindu, non-Hindu) education status (no formal education, less than high school, more than high school) and annual household income (in USD)

Family structure was categorised as nuclear or joint, where nuclear family means a household consisting of the woman, her husband, and their children29 and the joint family means a household including extended family members such as in-laws and other relatives.30 Residence was categorised as living in the husband’s household (residing with the husband and/or his family) and living outside the husband’s household (residing in the natal home, alone after separating from husband or another arrangement). Occupation was categorised as homemaker (not engaged in income-generating activities), agriculture (involvement in farming or livestock-related work), job (formal employment), business (self-employed or running a business) and others. Migrant husband status was defined as whether the participant’s husband was currently living in abroad countries for employment purposes (yes/no). Age at marriage (in years) referred to the age at which the participant was first married, while duration of marriage (in years) referred to the number of years since marriage at the time of the survey. These variables were selected based on prior evidence suggesting their association with women’s mental health.

Covariates

All sociodemographic variables were included in the multivariable analysis as potential confounders. Covariates were selected based on conceptual relevance and Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG)-informed assumptions, considering variables associated with both the exposure and outcome that were not part of the causal pathway. The DAG is presented in online supplemental file 3.

Assessment of stress, anxiety and depression symptoms

We used the Nepali validated version of DASS-21 translated by Ghimire and Pandey31 to assess the symptoms of stress, anxiety and depression. The tool was previously used in Nepal.32 The internal consistency of the Nepali version DASS-21 was 0.82 for DASS-stress, 0.80 for DASS-anxiety and 0.77 for DASS-depression.33

DASS-21 has 21 items in total, with 7 items in each category of depression, anxiety and stress. Each participant is asked to score every item on a scale that ranges from 0 to 3, where 0 denotes ‘did not apply to me/Never’, 1 denotes ‘Applied to me to some degree, or some of the time/sometimes’, 2 denotes ‘Applied to me to a considerable degree or a good part of time/often’ and 3 denotes ‘Applied to me very much or most of the time/almost always’. The score ranges from 0 to 63. The maximum score denotes the maximum severity. The acquired score was multiplied by 2 to calculate the final score.28 To determine the prevalence, we classified participants into mild, moderate, severe and extremely severe conditions of stress, anxiety and depression based on the total score of each subdomain.28 (a) Stress score was classified as: normal (0–14), mild (15–18), moderate (19–25), severe (26–33) and extremely severe (34+). (b) Anxiety score was classified as: normal (0–7), mild (8–9), moderate (10–14), severe (15–19) and extremely severe (20+). (c) Depression scores were classified as: normal (0–9), mild (10–13), moderate (14–20), severe (21–27) and extremely severe (27+) based on the recommended cut-off scores of DASS-21.34

Statistical analysis

We summarised the participants’ characteristics in frequency and proportion for categorical variables and mean and SD for normally distributed numerical variables, and median (IQR) for non-normally distributed numerical variables. Income data were initially collected in Nepalese Rupees (NRS) and subsequently converted to US Dollars (USD) for analysis, where 1 US$ = 121.69 NPR, the average exchange rate of 2021 and 2022 as of 4 December 2022.35

We conducted univariable and multivariable linear regression analysis to determine the association between symptoms of stress, anxiety and depression with sociodemographic variables. We analysed data using Stata software (Stata 14). We considered p values <0.05 as statistically significant and presented Beta Coefficients with 95% CIs.

Before the analysis, we tested the assumptions of linear regression: linearity, independence, normality and equal variance. The linearity and equal variance of the relationship were determined by a scatter plot of the residuals vs predicted score. Normality was assessed through histograms and normal Q-Q plots. By design, the outcomes were independent as there was no matching, clustering or paired data that met the assumptions of independence. All assumptions were met. Therefore, we conducted separate multivariable linear regression models for stress, anxiety and depression to examine their associations with sociodemographic variables, adjusting for potential confounders. Potential confounders were identified based on prior literature and theoretical relevance, and all selected sociodemographic variables were included in the multivariable models.

Additionally, to detect multicollinearity, we used the variance inflation factors (VIF) method. The mean VIF was around 1.37, indicating multicollinearity is not problematic.

Patient and public involvement

Patients and/or the public were not involved in the design, conduct, reporting or dissemination plans of this research.

Discussion

This study aimed to determine the prevalence of stress, anxiety and depression and to identify associated factors among married women in Chandrapur Municipality, Nepal. The prevalence of stress symptoms was 28.8%, anxiety symptoms was 16.7% and depression symptoms was 31.2% among married women in Chandrapur Municipality, Southern Nepal. Stress was higher among women of Teari and Janajati and Dalit ethnicity, Hindu religion, women living separately from their husband’s household, and women doing jobs. Anxiety was higher among women living separately from their husbands’ households, Hindu religion, women with high school education and women doing jobs. Depression was lower among women of non-Hindu religions, living in joint families.

The prevalence of stress, anxiety and depression was lower in this study compared with the married pregnant women of Afghanistan, where the prevalence of stress was 59.5%, anxiety was 40.0% and depression was 42.8%.36 Similarly, a study conducted among women in Iran showed prevalence of stress was 64.3%, anxiety was 54.5% and depression was 59.09% among women.13 This variation in prevalence may be attributed to broader socio-political and economic contexts. In Afghanistan, prolonged conflict and instability may have contributed to increased psychological distress among women.37 In Iran, recurrent protests and economic crises, often tied to governmental failures, could have increased mental health issues.38 Such chronic instability and repeated exposure to trauma may result in long-term psychological consequences, potentially explaining the higher prevalence rates in these settings.39

The prevalence of depression and anxiety among the general population in Nepal was 3.2% and 3.6%,40 which is lower than the rates observed in our study. This aligns with findings from other studies indicating that women experience higher rates of anxiety and depression than men.41 42 These disparities may be linked to gender-related social factors such as socioeconomic disadvantage, cultural norms and exposure to violence, all of which contribute to the higher prevalence of mental health symptoms among women.13

A novel finding for our context was the higher prevalence of stress and anxiety symptoms among women who do not live in their husband’s household. This aligns with studies showing increased depression and anxiety levels in separated or divorced women.43 In the context of the study area, women typically live away from their husband’s house due to marital or family disputes, or when their husbands are working abroad. Both scenarios represent significant psychosocial stressors such as social isolation, financial insecurity and the increased burden of household management15 that could contribute to heightened stress and anxiety.

Interestingly, employed women were found to have greater stress and anxiety compared with homemakers. This contrasts with a study from Bangladesh that showed homemakers had significantly higher anxiety than employed married women,44 mentioning that familial and social status, limited control at home, low education, and their involvement only at home in monotonous roles could have heightened the anxiety. However, our findings resonate with studies conducted in India45 46 and Pakistan,47 which also showed that employed women have greater anxiety than unemployed women.47 Work-related stress and anxiety pose significant challenges for employed women. In South Asian contexts such as Nepal, India and Pakistan, married women are often still viewed primarily as housewives, regardless of their employment status. They are expected to manage both professional responsibilities and household duties, including caregiving and family obligations. This dual burden may contribute to increased levels of stress and anxiety among women.48

Furthermore, our findings showed a lower prevalence of depression symptoms among women residing in a joint family. This finding is consistent with various studies from the South Asian context,49 50 which report higher depressive symptoms in individuals living in nuclear families. The robust emotional and social support networks inherent in joint family structures, prevalent in countries like Nepal and India, appear to offer a protective factor. The sharing of financial burdens, joys, grief and daily responsibilities among family members within a joint system can significantly reduce stress and depression.49 50 However, given the cross-sectional design of the study, reverse causality cannot be determined. It is also possible that individuals with pre-existing depressive symptoms may be less likely to reside in a joint family.

Our study has several strengths. First, to our best knowledge, this study is one of the first studies reporting the prevalence and factors associated with stress, anxiety and depression symptoms among married women in Southern Nepal. Second, we employed a rigorous approach by using the pretested, validated Nepali-translated DASS-21 scale to assess symptoms of depression, anxiety and stress, with data cross-checked weekly for accuracy and completeness. Third, we showed results with independent association after adjusting for potential confounders. However, there were some limitations. First, we could not establish the temporality of the risk associations, such as living separately from husband and doing jobs, because of the cross-sectional nature of the study. Second, the generalisability of the study is limited to married women of Southern Nepal rather than overall Nepal. Third, we administered questionnaires through face-to-face interviews, which might lead to the chance of social desirability bias, as mental health is considered taboo in Nepali society, which could have under-reported the symptoms of stress, anxiety and depression. Fourth, we did not measure important factors such as number of children, husband’s mental health status and health-related factors such as menopause, which may influence women’s stress, anxiety and depression. Fourth, the sample size was calculated to estimate the prevalence of depression, anxiety and stress, and was not specifically powered for multivariable regression analyses. Due to which, the study may have had limited statistical power to detect some associations between sociodemographic factors and mental health outcomes. Finally, the small number of participants in the category ‘residing other than husband’s house’ (n=16) may have reduced statistical power and affected the precision of regression estimates.

Conclusion

This study found a high prevalence of stress, anxiety and depression among married women in Chandrapur Municipality, Nepal. Key stressors include living separately from the husband’s household and employment, while protective factors were identified as being of a non-Hindu religion and living in a joint family. These findings highlight the need for targeted mental health interventions, gender-sensitive policies supporting work–life balance and education, and community-based programmes that strengthen the social support of joint family structures. Sensitising the healthcare providers, particularly at the primary care level, to screen for these symptoms, integrating mental health assessments into routine check-ups, can be crucial in mitigating the mental health-related problems among married women.
