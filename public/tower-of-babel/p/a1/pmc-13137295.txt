Stability and cooperativity in chimeric de novo TIM barrels via quarter swapping
Authors: Oscar Rodríguez‐Meza, Isabel Velázquez‐López, A. Jessica Díaz‐Salazar, Sergio Romero‐Romero, Miguel Costas, D. Alejandro Fernández‐Velasco
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe use of repetitive units as building blocks in protein design, mimicking evolutionary duplication events, is a valuable strategy that reduces structural complexity. Such modular designs offer an opportunity to dissect how local contributions are integrated in a context‐dependent manner. sTIM11, the first validated de novo TIM barrel, and its derivatives, the DeNovoTIM collection, were designed following a four‐fold repetition strategy. Here, we examine how chimeric constructs, generated by systematically swapping hydrophilic and hydrophobic quarters from two DeNovoTIMs, modify the energetic folding landscape. In‐depth analysis of thermal unfolding transitions revealed a complex process under thermodynamic or kinetic control, as well as evidence of residual structure at high temperatures. Our findings show that swapping quarters stabilized by different types of interactions alters the thermodynamic stability of the barrel, favoring partially unfolded intermediates where stable quarters remain folded while less stable ones unfold, ultimately leading to a loss of cooperativity.

INTRODUCTION

Protein stability is a crucial property that determines the resistance of the native state to changes in environmental conditions, an essential feature for the function and maintenance of all biochemical processes (Braselmann et al., 2013; Goldenzweig & Fleishman, 2018; Kazlauskas, 2018; Rahban et al., 2022; Robertson & Murphy, 1997). A typical method to analyze protein stability involves the quantification of the change in the equilibrium distribution of macrostates promoted by changes in external conditions, such as temperature, pressure, or the presence of chemical denaturants. The main goal is to calculate the free energy change (ΔG); nevertheless, other valuable thermodynamic properties, such as changes in enthalpy, volume, or accessible surface area (ASA), can be assessed depending on the perturbing agent used to promote unfolding (Ibarra‐Molero et al., 2004; Konermann, 2012; Silva et al., 2001). While the amino acid sequence determines protein stability; predicting the effect of a specific substitution on ΔG requires prior structural information. In addition, the estimation of ΔΔG is not straightforward even when structural data are available because stability effects are context‐dependent and are affected by structural rearrangements in the mutation site (Tokuriki & Tawfik, 2009). Repetitive proteins are a suitable system to study the origins of cooperativity (Petersen & Barrick, 2021), the stability contribution of repeats and the interface between them (Geiger‐Schuller et al., 2018), as well as the differential effect of substitution in structurally equivalent regions (Jenkins et al., 2018; Voet et al., 2014).

The TIM barrel, or (β/α)8‐barrel fold, is one of nature's most versatile and prevalent topologies. It consists of a β‐loop‐α motif repeated eight times; the β‐strands form a parallel cylindrical sheet that forms the inner core when the N‐ and C‐terminal strands interact to close the barrel, which is surrounded by eight α‐helices. This fold is the most common in enzymes, as evidenced by its involvement in diverse chemical reactions, its presence across numerous protein families, and its widespread occurrence in nature (Nagano et al., 2002; Romero‐Romero, Kordes, et al., 2021; Sterner & Höcker, 2005).

The main evolutionary hypothesis regarding the origin of the TIM barrel fold centers on potential duplication and fusion events of ancestral (βα)4 half‐barrels (Höcker, Jürgens et al., 2001), or even smaller fragments (Söding et al., 2006). Despite its composition of eight β‐strands and eight α‐helices, this fold is more accurately described as four (βα)2‐subdomains, giving a four‐fold symmetry (Wierenga, 2001). This modular and repetitive structure is a strategic advantage for the design of new proteins using fragments or repetitive units, whether derived from natural or de novo sequences (Boersma & Plückthun, 2011; Carstensen et al., 2012; Ferruz et al., 2020; Fortenberry et al., 2011; Höcker, 2014; Main et al., 2003; Main et al., 2005; Parmeggiani & Huang, 2017; Seitz et al., 2007; Toledo‐Patiño et al., 2024; Vrancken et al., 2020).

Since the onset of de novo protein design, the TIM barrel fold has been a particularly challenging target, due to its intrinsic complexity (Figueroa et al., 2016). In 2016, Huang et al. designed a de novo TIM barrel based on four repetitions of an αβαβ motif, whose experimentally determined structure showed excellent agreement with the intended model. This four‐fold symmetric protein, named sTIM11, exhibited high thermostability (T m = 88°C) and reversible thermal unfolding, but showed significantly lower conformational stability at room temperature (ΔG = 4.2 kcal/mol) than natural TIM barrels (Huang et al., 2016). Building upon this foundation, subsequent modifications increased hydrophobic packing, in three key regions of the barrel, resulting in a collection named DeNovoTIMs (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021). These proteins display a 22 kcal/mol variation in thermodynamic stability at 25°C and melting temperatures spanning from 47 to 108.8°C. Additional redesign efforts have incorporated structural extensions (Beck et al., 2024; Wiese et al., 2021), salt bridges (Kordes et al., 2022), ligand‐binding sites (Caldwell et al., 2020), and recently, the design of the first enzymatically active de novo TIM barrel (Beck et al., 2025).

The repetitive nature of the DeNovoTIM sequences offers a valuable platform for systematically studying stability determinants in the TIM barrel fold. By treating each αβαβ quarter as an interchangeable modular building block, it is possible to create a new collection of chimeras containing different quarters and identify differences in their physicochemical properties. In this context, Koch et al. analyzed how stability changes when hydrophobic modifications are introduced into the lower peripheral core of DeNovoTIM0, the base design in the DeNovoTIM collection, to yield DeNovoTIM6, a protein with a 45°C increase in T m and a ΔΔG > 6 kcal/mol. The authors reported a positive nonadditive effect, that is, the stabilization induced by the modification of several quarters was higher than the sum of the individual modules (Koch et al., 2024).

The active site in natural TIM barrels is located on the “top” of the barrel, close to the upper peripheral core formed by the C‐terminal part of the β strands, the β‐α loops, and the N‐terminal region of the α helices. The four quarters that form this region in DeNovoTIM6 were modified with hydrophobic residues in DeNovoTIM13. In this work, we employed the modular design approach to investigate the transformation of DeNovoTIM6 into DeNovoTIM13. The three‐dimensional structures of both proteins are known; they exhibit a similar T m and comparable ΔG at 25°C. However, their thermal unfolding is different; for DeNovoTIM13 the transition is partially irreversible and under kinetic control, whereas for DeNovoTIM6 temperature‐induced unfolding is reversible, under thermodynamic control and shows a higher ΔH (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021). By analyzing the urea‐ and temperature‐induced unfolding profiles of all possible quarter combinations between DeNovoTIM6 and DeNovoTIM13, we explored the impact of mixing quarters stabilized by non‐polar and polar interactions in the same barrel. In doing so, we reveal a decrease in thermodynamic cooperativity and a drastic alteration in the energetic landscape of de novo TIM barrels.

RESULTS

Design strategy of chimeras by quarter

DeNovoTIM6 and DeNovoTIM13 have four‐fold sequence symmetry; each of their four quarters (αβαβ) is composed of 46 residues. Chimeric proteins were obtained by combining the quarters from both proteins. When compared to DeNovoTIM6, DeNovoTIM13 has 20 mutations (five per quarter) located on the peripheral upper core of the barrel. These five stabilizing mutations: K2V, A5M, W6L, Y22V, and D29I, and equivalent positions, were introduced “by quarters” into DeNovoTIM6 (Figure 1a). Quarters containing the DeNovoTIM13 sequence were named “a,” “b,” “c,” and “d” according to their sequence order from the N‐terminus to the C‐terminus. Chimeras were named according to the number of modified quarters (Figure 1b), that is, four proteins with only one DeNovoTIM13 quarter (1q series), six proteins corresponding to all the possible combinations containing two DeNovoTIM13 quarters per barrel (2q series), and finally, the four proteins possessing three DeNovoTIM13 quarters (3q series). In the case of group 3q the name was slightly different, starting with the prefix “3q” followed by an “x” which means “unmodified”, indicating the only quarter which is not modified; for example, “3qxb” designates the protein with quarters “a,” “c,” and “d” modified, in other words, the protein where only the quarter “b” is not modified (Figure 1c).

The models predicted by AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024) for all these sequences adopt a compact TIM barrel topology with high confidence, without noticeable changes in secondary structure (Figure 1d and Figure S1). The overexpression yield differed among the three groups, increasing with the number of modified quarters. The exceptions were 2qad and 2qbd, whose expression was very low in the soluble fraction (Figure S2). Besides those exceptions, all proteins were purified to homogeneity and further analyzed by biophysical methods. All chimeras were compact and monomeric (The average Stokes radius [R S ] = 24.6 ± 1.1 Å for all the constructions) as determined from their elution volume in the final SEC purification step (Figure S3). The far‐UV CD analysis of all purified chimeras showed the typical α/β spectra, also observed in the parent proteins (Figure 1d). Despite mutations in key aromatic residues (Table S1), the normalized intrinsic fluorescence (IF) spectra of all chimeras were practically identical, with a maximum emission wavelength of approximately 339 nm, indicating the burial of aromatic residues.

Gradual increase in stability and substantial decrease in cooperativity in urea‐induced unfolding

The conformational stability of all the chimeras was analyzed by urea‐induced unfolding followed by CD and IF. The unfolding/refolding transitions were reversible and at equilibrium after incubation for 24 h, as shown by the coincidence of unfolding and refolding data (Figure S4). Changes in secondary and tertiary structure seemed cooperative, transitions were well‐fitted to a two‐state model (Figure S5a–c). The first half of the collection showed C1/2 value lower than DeNovoTIM6, and progressively shifted to higher urea concentration (Figure S5d). Nevertheless, the parameters obtained from the fit seem not reliable, particularly the obtained m values were very low (Figure S5e). The clear decrease in cooperativity observed in the unfolding of the chimeras suggests the presence of folding intermediates (Myers et al., 1995). Raw CD and IF data were well‐fitted to a three‐state model (Figure S6, Table 1). For DeNovoTIM13 the three‐state analysis gives a higher stability (ΔG2State = 9.5 kcal/mol; ΔGTot3State = 12.3 kcal/mol) and an m value closer to the 2.15 kcal/mol/M expected from parametric equations for a protein of 184 residues (m = 1.54 kcal/mol/M; m T = m 1 + m 2 = 2.0 kcal/mol/M) (Table 1). In contrast, DeNovoTIM6 data fitting produced large errors and inconsistent parameter values (data not shown); therefore, for this protein only the results for data fitting to a two‐state model are reported (Table 1). In order to visualize and compare the unfolding behavior of the chimeras, raw data (Figure S6) were normalized to fractional change (Figure 2a–c). As previously stated, a clear shift to higher urea concentrations as the number of modified quarters increased. The first C1/2 value is close to 3 M except for 3qxb where it increased to 5 M. The second C1/2 value shows a gradual increase in going from DeNovoTIM6 to DeNovoTIM13 (Figure 2d). The total stability of the protein also increases in going from DeNovoTIM6 to DeNovoTIM13. In contrast to the very low m values obtained from the two‐state analysis, the m T values for DeNovoTIM13 and the 3q chimeras are similar to the expected 2.15 kcal/mol/M (Myers et al., 1995).

The m T values obtained are consistent with the strategy of the DeNovoTIM collection, where polar interactions in DeNovoTIM6 are replaced by non‐polar ones in DeNovoTIM13, that is, m T and ΔASAnon‐polar values increase in parallel (Figure S7). ΔGT and m T values gradually increase in going from NT6 to NT13 (Table 1 and Figure 2e), and there is a clear correlation between ΔGT and m T (Figure 2f). This suggests that the increase in stability of the chimeric proteins is related to an increase in hydrophobic packing (Figure S6). Overall, Figure 2 data clearly indicate that stability increases gradually with the number and position of the modifications.

Complex non‐cooperative thermal‐unfolding behavior

The thermal unfolding of DeNovoTIM chimeras was then analyzed by differential scanning calorimetry (DSC). As previously reported (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021), DeNovoTIM6 data were adequately fitted to an equilibrium two‐state model, whereas DeNovoTIM13 endotherms were analyzed using an irreversible two‐state model. In both cases, a single and defined transition was observed. In contrast, chimeras exhibited complex endotherms with partial reversibility, lower melting temperatures and a decreased enthalpy (Figure 3 and Table 2). Proteins from the 1q group displayed wide and very small endotherms, whereas those from the 2q and 3q proteins became more defined (compare panels a to c in Figure 3). Due to the complexity and irreversibility of unfolding transitions, ΔH and T m values were obtained from data fitting to a Gaussian function of two components (Figures S8‐S10). The two melting temperatures obtained from data fitting exhibit a similar pattern; both T m values shift to higher temperatures as the number of modified quarters increases. The difference between the T m for the two transitions (ΔT m ~ 12.2°C) is strikingly similar for all the chimeras (Figure 3d). In all cases, the T m for the first transition (T m1) is smaller than the T m of the parental proteins, whereas for 3q proteins, the T m for the second transition (T m2) was observed at higher temperatures (Figure 3d).

Regarding enthalpy changes for the first transition (ΔH1), there is an abrupt decrease when moving from DeNovoTIM6 to 1q proteins; however, ΔH1 increases significantly with the number of modified quarters and becomes higher in 3q proteins than in DeNovoTIM13. In contrast, the ΔH for the second transition (ΔH2) remains constant around 25 kcal/mol (Figure 3e). The fractional contribution of ΔH2 (fΔH2) to the total enthalpy (ΔHT) is less than 0.25 in 2q and 3q proteins, except for 2qab (Figure 3f). ΔHT varies within the collection (Table 2); the four proteins of group 1q and the two proteins of group 2q, where the first quarter is modified (2qab and 2qac), show small ΔHT values (<65 kcal/mol). In contrast, the remaining proteins of group 2q and all proteins of group 3q have ΔHT exceeding 80 kcal/mol. The enthalpy changes observed can be compared to values expected for a protein of 184 residues based on parametric equations (Robertson & Murphy, 1997). The ΔHTo versus T m plot shows that all the chimeras, along with their parental proteins, exhibit an enthalpy change lower than predicted for a protein the size of DeNovoTIMs (Figure 3g). In general, the obtained ΔH values reinforce the idea that context‐dependent interactions, and other factors such as residual structure in the unfolded ensemble, dictate the thermal transitions of de novo TIM barrels.

Residual structure and mixed events of thermodynamic and kinetic control in thermal unfolding

The previously determined 3D structures of both parental proteins indicate that they are well‐folded; furthermore, their spectroscopic properties are similar to those of the chimeras, which suggests that partial unfolding in the native state is not the primary cause for the low ΔH. Regarding the properties of the unfolded state, the highest temperature where CD data could be measured was 86°C; therefore, only the 1qa, 1qd, and 2qab chimeras show T m2 values low enough (73.1, 80.9, and 79.0°C, respectively, see Table 1) to measure the CD spectra in the post‐transition region (Figure 4a inset). The CD spectra of these chimeras at 86°C exhibit significant intensity in the 210–222 nm region (Figure 4a), suggesting the presence of residual structure in their unfolded state. We cannot rule out that part of the residual CD signal arises from transient temperature‐induced intermolecular associations; nevertheless, the magnitude and spectral profile of the CD signal are consistent with genuine residual structure. This contrasts with the results obtained from chemical‐induced unfolding, where the CD signal of these chimeras was almost lost in 9 M urea (Figure 4a). Similar findings were observed in the less thermostable proteins of the DeNovoTIM collection (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021).

To explore thermal unfolding more thoroughly, we performed a DSC scan‐rate dependence analysis of 3qxb, the most stable protein in the collection. As shown in Figure 4b, the endotherms exhibited unusual behavior; the maximum peak, related to T m1, was scan‐rate independent, whereas the shoulder observed at higher temperatures, corresponding to the second transition, shifted to higher temperatures as the scan rate increased. Therefore, the first transition of 3qxb is under thermodynamic control, as observed in DeNovoTIM6, whereas the second one is under kinetic control, as reported for DeNovoTIM13 (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021). The presence of at least two transitions with both reversible and irreversible characteristics, as well as low reversibility, prevented a formal analysis of the endotherms in terms of equilibrium thermodynamics or activation energies. We observed that the unfolding endotherms of 3qxb do not exhibit a flat post‐transition region; therefore, we extended the DSC scans to 130°C, the upper temperature limit of the instrument. The thermal trace of 3qxb displayed an additional well‐defined transition with a T m higher than 120°C (Figure 4c). This third transition probably corresponds to the unfolding of the residual structure present in the “denatured” state suggested in the previous sections. In the presence of 0.5 M urea, the area under the endotherm increased significantly, whereas the shoulder corresponding to the second transition and the third transition merged into a single one (Figure 4d). A similar behavior observed in the thermal unfolding of DeNovoTIM14 in the presence of urea has been attributed to the unfolding of the residual structure in the “denatured” state elicited by the chaotropic agent (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021). Together, these observations indicate that the thermal unfolding of 3qxb is not a simple extension of the behavior seen for DeNovoTIM6 or DeNovoTIM13, but instead reflects a more complex partitioning of stability in the barrel. The coexistence of thermodynamic‐ and kinetic‐controlled transitions, the emergence of a high‐temperature event above 120°C, and the sensitivity of these features to urea all point to the presence of substantial residual structure in the “denatured” ensemble.

Hydrophobic clusters, H‐bonds, and predicted unfolding intermediates

It is well known that hydrophobic cores and hydrogen bonds are crucial for the folding and stability of protein clusters (Gangadhara et al., 2013; Kathuria, 2016; Kazlauskas, 2018). The interplay between the two types of interactions is less clear. The DeNovoTIM collection was designed to evaluate the effect of replacing polar interactions with hydrophobic ones. The interaction of the external face of the barrel and the surrounding helices forms the peripheral core of the TIM barrel. In DeNovoTIM6, this region is stabilized by intraquarter polar interactions, which are replaced by intraquarter non‐polar interactions in DeNovoTIM13 (Figure 5a,b). As intended by the design, ΔASAnon‐polar (Figure S7) and the size of hydrophobic clusters (Table S2) increase as the number of modified quarters increases. As expected, ΔHT, the area of hydrophobic clusters and m T followed a similar trend (Figure 5c). The only marked exception is DeNovoTIM6, which showed the lowest hydrophobic area and the highest enthalpy. The spatial distribution of hydrophobic clusters indicates that the peripheral hydrophobic core is continuous only when hydrophobic interactions are present in all four quarters (Table S2 and Figure S11). In going from DeNovoTIM6 to DeNovoTIM13, there is a slight decrease in the number of estimated hydrogen bonds, the fact that some chimeras have less or more hydrogen bonds than both parental proteins suggests that hydrogen bond networks are context dependent, therefore, the intraquarter hydrogen bonds present in DeNovoTIM6 should be replaced by new hydrogen bonds in other regions (Figure 5d, Table S2, and Figure S12).

It is important to note that all structural calculations were based on AlphaFold models, which may not capture the atomic detail required to evaluate hydrogen bonds. In fact, the experimental 3D structures of DeNovoTIM6 and DeNovoTIM13 show that not all the designed interactions between the N‐ and C‐terminal quarters are formed; the RMSD between the design and the structure is higher in these two quarters (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021). Therefore, the structural interactions analyzed in Figure 5, Figures S11 and S12, and Table S2 should be interpreted as the maximum number. A further point of analysis is that although the modified residues are located in the internal region of each quarter (Figure 1a), the interactions formed are context‐dependent; therefore, the total hydrophobic area as well as the number of hydrogen bonds is not constant in proteins of the same group (Figure 5c,d).

DISCUSSION

The TIM barrel has been one of the most intriguing and extensively examined folds in protein science (Beck & Romero‐Romero, 2026; Sterner & Höcker, 2005; Wierenga, 2001). We have investigated the impact of sequentially increasing hydrophobic packing in the peripheral upper core of DeNovoTIMs, constructing chimeras using quarters as building blocks. Most of the proteins of the DeNovoTIM collection were adequately fitted to a two‐state unfolding model (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021), although the chimeras described in this work can be fitted to such a model, a clear decrease in the cooperativity of the transitions was observed (Figure 2); therefore, a three‐state model with an on‐pathway intermediate was applied. All the chimeras, as well as DeNovoTIM13, were adequately fitted to this model (Table 1 and Figure S6), whereas DeNovoTIM6 data were adequately fitted only to the two‐state model. As a consequence of the designed increase in non‐polar residues in going from DeNovoTIM6 to DeNovoTIM13, we observed a clear correlation between the change in m T and the increase in ΔG (Figure 2f) and also between hydrophobic packing, m T and ΔH (Figure 5c).

The parental proteins exhibit comparable stability values to those obtained for natural and ancestral reconstructed TIM barrels (Pérez‐Niño et al., 2024; Pyrpassopoulos et al., 2006; Romero‐Romero et al., 2015). We expected that the total stability of the chimeras would be between those of the parental proteins; however, all the 1q chimeras show a lower stability than DeNovoTIM6 (Figure 2e). It has been reported that consistency between local and global structure is required to avoid a decrease in stability and backbone strain in de novo designs (Koga et al., 2021). In this regard, we have previously found that in the DeNovoTIM collection, the agreement between the designs and the experimental structures decreases in the first and last quarters, suggesting that backbone strain is generated upon barrel closure (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021). Polar interactions in DeNovoTIM6 are replaced by bulkier non‐polar interactions in DeNovoTIM13, probably introducing some strain in the chimeras. Therefore, upon the addition of modified quarters, there could be a tradeoff between stabilization and strain. It is therefore possible that, in the 1q group, strain overcomes the stabilization introduced by the inconsistency between local and global structure. This effect may be gradually overcome in chimeras from the 2q and 3q groups (Figure 2).

The thermostability of DeNovoTIM6 and DeNovoTIM13 is very similar (ΔTm = 1.3°C), and both parental proteins show cooperative transitions. In contrast, the chimeras present Tm values spanning a 30°C interval (Figure 3), as well as non‐cooperative transitions. The low ΔH values obtained in temperature‐induced unfolding suggest the presence of residual structure in the “unfolded” state In‐depth analysis of thermal unfolding of 3qxb provided unequivocal evidence of the presence of residual structure in this protein, demonstrated by the presence of additional transitions at higher temperatures. The presence of residual structure in proteins from thermophilic organisms has been associated with the origin of thermal tolerance (Karshikoff et al., 2015; Robic et al., 2003), where a higher propensity for hydrophobic cluster formation exists in the unfolded state (Guzman‐Casado et al., 2003). Therefore, it is probable that the increment in the hydrophobic clusters area in the DeNovoTIM chimeras is responsible for the presence of residual structure in the “unfolded” state. One of the main features of designed proteins is their high thermal stability; thus, it would be interesting to further investigate this topic.

The decrease in cooperativity in both the urea‐ and temperature‐induced unfolding of the chimeras is likely the result of the presence, in the same molecule, of quarters stabilized by polar and non/polar interactions that differ in their intrinsic stability as well as in their response to temperature, resulting in the presence of intermediates and the modification of the energetic landscape. To further explore the nature of these intermediates, chimeras were analyzed using Alphafolding. The extent of secondary structure content, as well as the arrangement between them, is very diverse (Figure S13). Although further research is necessary to characterize the experimentally‐detected intermediates, the variety of structures predicted by Alphafolding, including barrel opening, asymmetric loosening, and localized loss of α‐helices, may highlight which regions of the architecture might be energetically weaker and therefore more likely to participate in early unfolding events. Regardless of that uncertainty, some of these intermediates resemble the 4 + 4 and 6 + 2 folding intermediates observed experimentally (Eder & Kirschner, 1992; Forsyth & Matthews, 2002; Höcker, Beismann‐Driemeyer et al., 2001; Jasanoff et al., 1994; Soberón et al., 2004; Sterner & Höcker, 2005).

CONCLUSIONS

The importance of the TIM barrel fold is largely known, and its potential as a scaffold for enzyme design is fascinating. Our findings show a differentiated contribution of each quarter to stability and exhibited a contrasting role depending on their location, increasing or decreasing stability depending on the context. The physicochemical characterization of the DeNovoTIM chimeras provided diverse thermodynamic parameters for each protein. Further analysis of the multistate unfolding of the 3qxb chimera reveals complex transitions presenting both thermodynamic and kinetic control as well as the presence of residual structure in the unfolding state.

The decrease in cooperative unfolding observed in the chimeras is probably related to the reorganization of the energetic landscape due to the loss of the hydrogen bond network of DeNovoTIM6 and/or the hydrophobic core in DeNovoTIM13. Intermediates are likely a consequence of the presence of quarters stabilized by designed polar and non/polar interactions in the same molecule that differ in their intrinsic stability as well as in their response to temperature. For the chimeras studied in this work, the presence of intermediates can be directly related to the differences in the interactions designed to stabilize each quarter. In natural TIM barrels, where polar and non‐polar interactions are distributed across the barrel, thermal unfolding is generally cooperative, in contrast, intermediates have been frequently found in urea‐ and GdnHCl‐induced unfolding. Their presence and structural properties have been interpreted as remnants of ancestral duplication and fusion events that reflect fold evolution. It is interesting that the presence of regions within the same fold with variable stability endure in time.

AI‐based tools can greatly accelerate the generation of new protein scaffolds and sequences, but their real power emerges when combined with a deeper, experimentally grounded understanding of the physicochemical principles that govern protein stability. Studies such as this one highlight that progress in protein design benefits from an iterative interplay between computational innovation and physicochemical knowledge.

MATERIALS AND METHODS

Protein expression purification

The synthetic genes of proteins from the 1q, 2q, and 3q groups were cloned into the pET29b(+) vector incorporating a C‐terminal His‐tag by Gene Universal Inc. (Delaware, USA). Proteins were overexpressed in the E. coli strain BL21(DE3) in 1 L of LB medium (30 μg mL−1 kanamycin), inoculated with 10 mL of preculture, and incubated at 37°C and 180 rpm until the absorbance reached 0.6–0.8 at 600 nm. Immediately, isopropyl‐D‐1‐thiogalactopyranoside (IPTG) was added to induce the overexpression at 1 mM for proteins of groups 3q and 2q, and 0.4 mM for proteins of group 1q. Cell growth continued by incubation at 30°C and 180 rpm for 16–19 h for proteins of groups 3q and 2q, and 6 h for proteins of group 1q. The cells were harvested by centrifugation (Thermo Scientific, 15 min, 8000 rpm, 4°C) followed by pellet resuspension in buffer A (35 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 35 mM imidazole, pH 8). Subsequently, the cells were lysed by sonication (Sonics Materials VC 750, 10 cycles in 45 s intervals on/off, 30% pulse, 4°C) and then centrifuged (Thermo Scientific, 40 min, 16,000 rpm, 4°C). The supernatant was filtered (0.45 μm Whatman Uniflo, Cytiva) and loaded onto a HisTrap HP column (Cytiva Life Science) coupled to an ÄKTA pure protein purification system (Cytiva Life Science). The bound protein was eluted with a linear gradient of 35–500 mM imidazole using buffer B (35 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, 500 mM imidazole, pH 8). The peak fractions were collected, concentrated, and loaded onto a Superdex 75 preparative grade column by the ÄKTA pure system. The proteins were purified by isocratic elution with 1 CV of buffer C (35 mM sodium phosphate, 300 mM NaCl, pH 8), and the peak fractions were analyzed by SDS‐PAGE. The selected fractions were dialyzed into buffer D (10 mM sodium phosphate, pH 8) for analysis. The protein concentration was determined by absorption at 280 nm using a Cary‐50 spectrophotometer (Varian, Australia), with the estimated extinction coefficient (ProtParam, ExPASy, available at https://web.expasy.org/protparam/; Gasteiger et al., 2005) of 42,970; 35,980; and 28,990 M−1 cm−1 for the proteins from 1q, 2q, and 3q groups, respectively. In our first purification assays we observed turbidity after refrigeration, therefore, after purification, all proteins were stored at room temperature to avoid aggregation.

Spectroscopy characterization

All proteins were analyzed by circular dichroism (CD) and fluorescence spectroscopy. Far‐UV CD spectra were collected in a Chirascan Spectropolarimeter in buffer D, at a protein concentration of 0.5 mg/mL using a 0.1 cm cuvette and a 200–260 nm wavelength range. The raw data was converted to mean residue molar ellipticity by: θ=M·θ/c·l and M=MW/n−1, where M is the mean residue weight, θ is the collected ellipticity in mdeg, c is the protein concentration in mg/mL, l is the pathlength in mm, MW is the molecular weight in g/mol and n is the number of amino acids in the protein (Greenfield, 2006).

Intrinsic fluorescence (IF) spectra were measured at a 0.2 mg/mL protein concentration in buffer D, using a PC1 ISS spectrofluorometer (ISS Inc., Champaign, IL, USA) and a 0.5 × 0.5 cm quartz cell at 25°C. Fluorescence emission spectra were recorded from 310 to 450 nm with a bandwidth of 1 nm and an excitation wavelength of 295 nm. The spectral center of mass (SCM) was calculated from the intensity data (Iλ) measured at each wavelength (λ) by SCM=∑λIλ/∑Iλ.

Thermal‐induced unfolding

Thermal‐induced protein unfolding was followed by DSC (Microcal LLC Autosampler with VP‐DSC Capillary nano calorimeter and stack cooler, Microcal, Malvern, UK). Samples were measured in buffer D at a 1.5 mg/mL protein concentration using a scan rate of 1.5°C/min and a temperature range of 15–120°C. Samples were stored at room temperature in the DSC tray before measurements. The physical baseline (buffer–buffer traces) was subtracted for each protein endotherm. A spline function was applied to subtract the chemical baseline, joining the native and unfolded regions of the traces. The DSC from DeNovoTIM chimeras were deconvoluted using a multiple peak fit to Gaussian peak functions in OriginPro 8.5 software. The integration of each endotherm gives the thermodynamic data for each sample, ΔH and T m . The data presented in Table 1 are the average of at least three endotherms. For the thermal traces of the parental proteins, a two‐state model was applied, reversible for DeNovoTIM6 and irreversible for DeNovoTIM13; the corresponding equations are reported in Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021. The T m and ΔH values reported previously for the reversible unfolding of DeNovoTIM6 (92.3 ± 0.1°C and 125 ± 2.0 kcal/mol, respectively) are very similar to those obtained in this work (92.9 ± 0.3°C and 128.5 ± 1.6 kcal/mol, respectively). For the irreversible unfolding of DeNovoTIM13, the reported T m value is closer to that obtained here (92.8 ± 0.4 vs. 94.2 ± 0.2°C, respectively). In contrast, the ΔH value reported previously (47 ± 5.0 kcal/mol) is lower than the one presented here (107.3 ± 6.0 kcal/mol); this difference may be related to variations in the extent of irreversible aggregation between different samples. The abrupt decrease in heat capacity observed at temperatures higher than Tm for DeNovoTIM13 in our previous work (where samples were preincubated at 4°C in the stack cooler), was not observed in the present work, when samples were preincubated at 25°C. This difference is probably related to irreversible cold unfolding taking place during preincubation. The DSC scan‐rate dependence analysis for the 3qxb protein was carried out at six different scan rates (from 0.5 to 3°C/min) in the 10–120°C temperature range. For the calorimetric traces analyzed until 130°C (where several transitions were detected) and for the traces in the presence of urea, only the physical baseline (buffer–buffer or buffer/urea–buffer/urea traces) was subtracted. Due to the automatic removal of the samples from the calorimeter it was not possible to visually observe aggregation after DSC measurements.

In the thermal unfolding followed by CD, each sample was heated from 25 to 86°C, measuring the ellipticity at 222 nm using a heating rate of 1°C/min using a Peltier (Quantum Northwest, Inc., Washington, USA) and recording the real temperature inside the cell. The unfolded protein was normalized to compare the percentage of structure loss due to the incomplete unfolding at the highest temperature. Measurements were carried out with 0.5 mg/mL in Buffer D for all analyzed proteins. No evident aggregation was observed after heating the samples.

Chemical‐induced unfolding

All samples were prepared at a 0.2 mg/mL protein concentration in buffer D with different urea concentrations from 0 to 9 M, and incubated for 24 h at 25°C. The CD (200–260 nm) and IF (310–450 nm) spectra were recorded simultaneously for each sample. For all urea concentrations, the CD signal at 222 nm and the data from IF SCM were fitted to a two‐ and three‐state models. Data were normalized using Equation 1 (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021): (Equation 1) fU=yobs−yNyU−yN

Normalized data were fitted to a two‐state model (Santoro & Bolen, 1988): (Equation 2) fU=yN+mNurea+yU+mUureae−∆G−mureaRT1+e−∆G−mureaRT

Raw data were fitted to the following three‐state unfolding equation (Romero‐Romero, Costas, et al., 2021): (Equation 3) fU=yU+mU[urea]K1K2+yN+mN[urea]+yI+mI[urea]K11+K1+K1K2 where yobs is the measured CD or SCM, and yN+mNurea, yI+mIurea, and yU+mUurea are the baselines of the native, intermediate and unfolded states. K1 and K2 are the equilibrium constants for the N to I and I to U steps.

Additionally, m is the dependence of ΔG on urea concentration (δΔG/δ[urea]), T is the temperature (298.15 K), and R is the universal gas constant (0.001987 kcal/mol/K). For each protein, CD and IF data were globally fitted, ΔG and m values were shared whereas the baselines for the three states were fitted independently.

Estimation of hydrophobic clusters area, hydrogen bonds and ASA

The analysis of the hydrophobic clusters and hydrogen bonds was conducted using the server ProteinTools (Ferruz et al., 2021, available at https://proteintools.uni-bayreuth.de/). For this, models obtained from the structural prediction of AlphaFold3 (Abramson et al., 2024; available at https://alphafoldserver.com/) were used for estimation. Clusters and hydrogen bonds were colored according to their location. ASA was calculated using VADAR (Willard et al., 2003).

Alphafolding and intermediates in the unfolding pathway

Alphafolding was used to predict the potential intermediates in the thermal‐induced unfolding of the DeNovoTIM chimeras (Chang & Perez, 2025; available at https://github.com/ccccclw/ColabDesign/blob/main/af/examples/alphafolding.ipynb). For all iterative protein structure predictions, the model 1_ptm was used, except for the 1qd protein, where the model 2_ptm was used due to a failure with the first model. The number of iterations was set to 50, the mode was dgram, the number of recycles was maintained at 0, and no template was used. The first structure identified for each protein before converging to the native state was considered an intermediate (Chang & Perez, 2025). However, these predicted folding pathway intermediates are not necessarily thermodynamically or kinetically stable.
