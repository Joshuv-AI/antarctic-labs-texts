The dose-volume histogram–based evaluation of predictors for gross hematuria after postprostatectomy radiotherapy☆
Authors: Byeongdo Song, Younsoo Chung, Sang Hun Song, Hakmin Lee, Sung Kyu Hong
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundGross hematuria following postprostatectomy radiotherapy (PPRT) is common and usually self-limited but could require hospitalization with surgical intervention in severe cases. In the present study, we have evaluated the prevalence and predictors associated with gross hematuria after PPRT.Materials and methodsFrom November 2003 to December 2017, 433 patients underwent radiotherapy after radical prostatectomy for prostate cancer. The Kaplan–Meier survival analysis was utilized to estimate the incidence of gross hematuria following PPRT, and the multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed to assess significant risk factors.ResultsIn our cohort, a total of 124 patients (28.6%) experienced gross hematuria after PPRT within a median follow-up time of 104 months. Among them, 20 patients (16.1%) required transurethral fulguration. The estimated 10-year gross hematuria–free survival rate was 67.7%. The multivariable Cox regression analysis demonstrated that treatment history of anticoagulant/antiplatelet agent [hazard ratio (HR): 1.76, P = 0.019), an absolute bladder V40 Gy ≥median (HR: 1.63, P = 0.047), and a relative bladder V65 Gy ≥median (HR: 1.82, P = 0.019) were associated with gross hematuria following PPRT.ConclusionOur results suggest that gross hematuria after PPRT occurs frequently, especially among patients on anticoagulant/antiplatelet therapy and those with an absolute bladder V40 Gy ≥median and relative bladder V65 Gy ≥median during PPRT. Although most cases of gross hematuria were self-resolved, up to 16.1% required invasive surgical intervention. Limiting PPRT dose exposure to the bladder may also reduce the incidence of gross hematuria.

Introduction

Radical prostatectomy (RP) has been recognized as a stand of care of definitive treatment for clinically localized prostate cancer (PCa). However, 20–40% of patients may experience biochemical recurrence within 10 years after RP.1 Therefore, postprostatectomy radiotherapy (PPRT) has been accepted in clinical field to improve disease control in case of adverse clinicopathologic features.2,3

However, radiotherapy (RT) is associated with several genitourinary and gastrointestinal toxicities, including RT cystitis and subsequent gross hematuria. RT cystitis and further gross hematuria is one of bothersome adverse events following RT, often requiring surgical intervention including energy coagulation.4 According to the CHHiP trial, approximately half of patients who underwent RT for PCa suffered from grade 2 or worse acute bladder toxicity.5 Although the prevalence rate of severe gross hematuria after RT, requiring further invasive intervention, is rater limited (5–10%),6 RT cystitis–related gross hematuria results in early visits of outpatient clinic, additional visits of emergency center, or further invasive interventions declining quality of life of patients.7 With regards to PPRT, 12–18% of patients who underwent RP and subsequent RT experienced gross hematuria within 5 years.8, 9, 10

To the best of our knowledge, previous studies regarding the association between dose-volume histogram (DVH) parameters and PPRT-related hematuria in the postoperative setting are relatively scarce.6,9 In the current study, we report the incidence of PPRT-related hematuria in a single-institution cohort of patients, the types of management undertaken, and the risk factors associated with PPRT-related hematuria.

Materials and methods

Ethical considerations

All study protocols comply with the provisions of the Helsinki Declaration. We removed personal identifiers and anonymized all data. This study was approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) from Seoul National University Bundang Hospital (IRB number: B-2302-813-101), which exempted our study from the requirement for informed consent from patients. The requirement of written informed consent was waived due to the retrospective nature of this study.

Patients and treatment

A total of 472 patients who underwent RP followed by RT for PCa at a single tertiary center from November 2003 to December 2017 were retrospectively evaluated. The patients without available records about RT protocol, those undergoing RT at other medical centers, those with previous history of pelvic RT for other malignancies, and those with a history of urothelial carcinoma were excluded, leaving 433 patients in our cohort. The patients were subdivided into two groups according to the presence of gross hematuria following PPRT.

According to discretions of one radiation oncologist, RT for the whole pelvis (44–46 Gy, 22–23 fractions) followed by prostatic fossa (22 Gy, 11 fractions) was performed to patients with a high risk of pelvic lymph node metastasis. For the patients who were less likely to have pelvic lymph node metastasis, prostate fossa alone was irradiated. DVH parameters were measured as mentioned in a previous study,11 determined using the bladder constraints which were defined by its outer layer. All DVH parameters were assessed as absolute and relative bladder volume, stratified by the median value.

Follow-up and definition of hematuria

All patients were assessed at 4 weeks after PPRT, at 3- to 6-month intervals for the next 5 years and annually thereafter. The identification of gross hematuria was based on electronic medical record at each follow-up outpatient center visit or emergency center visit, with the absence of other causes such as urinary stone, acute urinary infection, or urothelial carcinoma.

When patients experience severe gross hematuria not self-resolved or concurrent with acute urinary retention, Foley catheter insertion and continuous bladder irrigation were performed in the emergency room. For patients with gross hematuria not resolved after continuous bladder irrigation, transurethral fulguration of the bladder was performed after hospitalization. All gross hematuria events were graded by the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v5.0 criteria12 (Supplementary Table 1).

Statistical analysis

Statistical analyses were performed using IBM SPSS Statistics ver. 29.0 (IBM Co., Armonk, NY, USA). Continuous variables were described as median [interquartile range (IQR)] and categorical variables as frequencies (percentages) in each category. A normality test for continuous variables was performed using the Kolmogorov–Smirnov test. Categorical variables were compared using Pearson’s Chi-square test, while continuous variables were compared using the Mann–Whitney U-test for nonparametric variables. The Kaplan–Meier survival curve analysis was performed to evaluate gross hematuria–free survival after PPRT. A multivariable Cox regression analysis was performed to evaluate the predictors for development of gross hematuria, including covariates with a P value <0.10 on the univariable analysis. All statistical tests were two sided, with a P value <0.05 set as the threshold for statistical significance.

Among all men with gross hematuria, the median interval to the initial event was 35.5 months (IQR: 22.5–70.0 months). The patients who experienced gross hematuria after PPRT were more likely to undertake anticoagulant/antiplatelet (AC/AP) agent (44.4% vs. 18.1%, P < 0.001), undergo less neoadjuvant androgen deprivation therapy (0.8% vs. 5.8%, P = 0.021), and receive irradiation to the whole pelvis, in addition to prostate bed (63.7% vs. 44.0%, P = 0.001) in comparison with those without gross hematuria. Those patients are also likely to be irradiated at a higher ratio of dose per fraction (P = 0.029), with a higher relative irradiated bladder volume (V55, V65, and V70; all P < 0.05) than their counterparts. In terms of absolute irradiated bladder volume, the patients with PPRT-related gross hematuria showed a higher bladder volume which was irradiated with more than 55, 65, and 70 Gy (all P < 0.05). Both groups did not show significant differences among other clinicopathologic features (Table 1).

The majority of gross hematuria events was self-resolved without any intervention (N = 82, 66.1%), while the remaining 42 patients (33.9%) visited the emergency center due to gross hematuria and/or acute urinary retention, undergoing urinary catheterization and further continuous bladder irrigation. Approximately half of those 42 patients were discharged from the emergency center with improved urine color and output after bladder washout, while the remaining 20 patients were hospitalized and underwent transurethral fulguration under general anesthesia. There were no gross hematuria events with grade 4 or more by the Common Terminology Criteria for Adverse Events v5.0 criteria. There were no RT cystitis events requiring radical cystectomy in our cohort.

Predictors of gross hematuria after PPRT

In the univariable Cox regression analyses, the usage of AC/AP agents (hazard ratie [HR]: 2.55, P < 0.001), the usage of intensity-modulated RT (compared to three-dimensional conformal RT, HR: 1.87, P = 0.005), the ratio of RT dose per fraction ≥2 (HR: 1.83, P = 0.002), the irradiation of whole pelvis (HR: 2.45, P < 0.001), absolute bladder V40 ≥median (HR: 1.62, P = 0.047), absolute bladder V70 ≥median (HR: 1.73, P = 0.024), relative bladder V40 ≥median (HR: 1.73, P = 0.025), relative bladder V45 ≥median (HR 1.73, P = 0.025), relative bladder V55 ≥median (HR: 1.62, P = 0.046), V65 ≥median (HR: 2.00, P = 0.005), and relative bladder V70 ≥median (HR: 1.71, P = 0.027) were associated with gross hematuria after PPRT. After adjusting all covariates with a P value <0.10 on the univariable analysis, the usage of AC/AP agents (HR: 1.76, P = 0.019), an absolute bladder V40 ≥median (HR: 1.63, P = 0.047), and a relative bladder V65 ≥median (HR: 1.82, P = 0.019) remained as independent risk factors for development of gross hematuria after PPRT (Table 2). Discussion

Gross hematuria and RT cystitis are bothersome adverse events following RT. The prevalence of RT cystitis–related gross hematuria varies from 3% to 39%, probably due to differences in follow-up periods, severity of symptoms at presentation, time interval between RT and symptoms, and differences in RT protocols.7 When RT was performed after RP, the incidences of gross hematuria were rather limited, up to 10%.6,8, 9, 10 However, PPRT-related gross hematuria accounts for a significant proportion of early visit to the outpatient clinic or emergency center visit, declining the quality of life of patients.6

In our cohort, during a median follow-up period of 104 months, the cumulative prevalence rate of PPRT-related gross hematuria was 28.6%, while the 10-year freedom from gross hematuria was estimated as 67.7%. The estimated cumulative incidence rate of gross hematuria in our cohort is rather higher than those of previous studies (12–18%).8, 9, 10 This might be explained by the longer follow-up period of our study (median: 104 months) than those of the previous studies (median: 49–50 months).8, 9, 10 Although a randomized, phase 3, noninferiority trial showed that the incidence of chronic RT cystitis (7%) and further gross hematuria (1%) was relatively limited during a 5-year follow-up period,5 Turchan et al.6 reported a higher cumulative prevalence of gross hematuria after PPRT (39.3%) with a median follow-up duration of 72 months. They also reported that the proportion of patients with AC/AP agents was 25.9%, which was similar to that of our cohort (25.6%) but higher than that of previous study (13%).9

A previous retrospective study reported that approximately half of patients presenting radiation cystitis–related hematuria may require invasive treatment including cystectomy.13 Shirai et al.9 and Tomita et al.10 also reported approximately 45.5% and 62.7% of patients who suffered from gross hematuria after PPRT did not require further invasive intervention, respectively. More recently, Turchan et al.6 reported 39% of gross hematuria after PPRT was self-resolved. In our cohort, 66.1% of all gross hematuria events was self-resolved without any further intervention. There were no patients who required radical cystectomy due to PPRT-related gross hematuria.

In the current study, the usage of AC/AP was one of independent risk factors for gross hematuria following PPRT after adjusting the effects of other clinical factors, which was consistent with the results of previous studies.6,13

The DVH analysis to evaluate the irradiated volume of the target organ of RT or the organ adjacent to the target organ has been accepted as a useful tool for treatment-plan evaluation.14,15 Indeed, DVH-based dose constraints are utilized to comprehensively assess the quality of PPRT in the field of radiation oncology.16 However, to the best of our knowledge, few studies have evaluated the association between DVH parameters and gross hematuria after RT. Sanguedolce et al.13 suggested a bladder V70 >29% as an independent predictor for gross hematuria after salvage RT. Shirai et al.9 suggested a relative bladder V50 Gy of 43% and a V40 Gy of 50% as significant cutoff values for prediction of PPRT-related gross hematuria. In our study, a relative bladder V65 ≥median was also associated with PPRT-related gross hematuria on multivariable analysis, which is consistent with previous research.6

Although previous studies reported only the relative proportion of the bladder receiving a high dose of radiation alone as the DVH-based predictor of PPRT-related gross hematuria,6,9,13 our results also suggested that the absolute volume of the bladder irradiated could be associated with gross hematuria following PPRT. Indeed, in terms of gastrointestinal toxicity or bleeding after RT, not the relative irradiated volume but the absolute irradiated volume was reported as a risk factor.17,18

There are several limitations in our study due to its retrospective design. Since the current study is a single–tertiary medical institution–based study, the RT technique or follow-up protocol could be uniform, but there is a risk of underestimating the incidence or severity of several gross hematuria, which were treated at other centers. Moreover, all events of gross hematuria were physician reported, which could be identified based on electronic medical records; there is also a possibility of underestimation of gross hematuria due to an inconsistency between physician- and patient-reported systems.19 Moreover, our study did not require cystoscopic confirmation of RT cystitis. Despite of limitations as mentioned earlier, the current study has its strengths in that it was performed on a substantial scale over a long-term follow-up period, among few studies which evaluated the association between DVH parameters and gross hematuria after PPRT. Furthermore, there are several potential methods to enhance the potential clinical benefits of DVH-based constraints. For instance, Schad et al.20 proposed that hypofractionated external beam RT could be an ideal method to fulfill the preset DVH-based constraint in the patients with PCa. Recently, a deep learning model has been suggested to assess and predict an exact dose distribution during RT for PCa.21,22

RT cystitis and subsequent gross hematuria is a bothersome late complication of RT for PCa. Our result showed that gross hematuria after PPRT is common, up to 28%. Although a majority of PPRT-related gross hematuria cases were self-resolved, approximately one-third of patients had to visit the emergency center and undergo invasive surgical intervention. AC/AP therapy and an absolute bladder V40 Gy ≥median and a relative bladder V65 Gy ≥median during PPRT were independent risk factors of gross hematuria after PPRT. Limiting PPRT dose exposure to the bladder may also reduce the incidence of gross hematuria.
