TrkB modulates parvalbumin interneuron excitability and spatial memory enhancement induced by long-term rhythmic light flicker
Authors: Yunxuan Wang, Jun Ju, Yifan Pan, Lihai Fan, Shiyu Wu, Shuifa Chen, Jinghong Chen, Hai Yan, Wenhao Ouyang, Kun Song, Sheng-Tao Hou
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
SummaryLong-term 40 Hz light flicker stimulation enhances parvalbumin (PV) interneuron activity in the hippocampal dentate gyrus (DG), increasing GABA release, promoting adult neurogenesis, and improving spatial memory in mice. This stimulation upregulates brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) in the DG granule cells, while the expression of its receptor, TrkB, in PV interneurons remains unchanged. Knock-down TrkB expression in PV interneurons abolished the light flicker-evoked enhancement of PV excitability and inhibitory transmission to granule cells, highlighting the critical role of TrkB signaling in the function of PV interneurons. TrkB knockdown also reduced the increase in low gamma oscillations and blocked the improvement in spatial learning induced by light flicker. Mechanistically, TrkB modulates the flicker-evoked increase in sustained (IK) and transient (IA) components of the voltage-gated K+ channels on PV interneurons. These findings demonstrate that mBDNF-TrkB signaling mediates the excitability of PV interneurons evoked by long-term rhythmic light flicker.Subject areas: Natural sciences, Biological sciences, neuroscience, behavioral neuroscience, Systems neuroscience, Cellular neuroscience

Introduction

An increasing number of studies, including ours, have highlighted the significant benefits of rhythmic 40 Hz light flicker in alleviating cognitive deficits in various neurological disease models, including Alzheimer’s disease (AD),1,2,3 cerebral ischemia,4 autism spectrum disorders (ASD),5 and chemobrain.6 Notably, 40 Hz light flicker has received expedited FDA approval and shown promising results in Phase II clinical trials with patients with AD.7 We recently investigated the long-term effects of 40 Hz visual stimulation and found that sustained exposure to 40 Hz light flicker entrains the hippocampus, promoting neurogenesis in the DG and enhancing spatial learning.8 This effect is mediated by the increased excitability of parvalbumin (PV) interneurons, but not vasoactive intestinal peptide (VIP) or cholecystokinin (CCK) interneurons. The increased excitability of PV interneurons enhances GABA release, creating an inhibitory niche that supports adult neurogenesis and the integration of newly formed neurons in the DG. However, the exact mechanisms underlying the enhancement of excitability of PV interneurons during long-term light flicker treatment remain unclear.

PV interneurons are fast-spiking GABAergic neurons that account for approximately 40% of all GABAergic neurons in the brain.9,10 They synapse onto the somata of excitatory neurons, regulating their activity and promoting synchronous firing.11 Tropomyosin receptor kinase B (TrkB) signaling in PV interneurons is essential for gamma-band network synchronization in the hippocampus.12 The deletion of TrkB impairs several aspects of the functions of PV interneurons, including synaptic excitation, intrinsic excitability, and the innervation patterns of principal neurons.13 Reduced TrkB signaling decreases PV inhibition in the medial prefrontal cortex and disrupts prefrontal network dynamics.14

In contrast, partial agonists of the TrkB receptor enhance inhibitory synaptic transmission and reduce the incidence of spontaneous and evoked epileptiform discharges.15 Moreover, in vivo recordings of local field potentials (LFP) in TrkB conditional knockout mice reveal reduced gamma oscillations in the sensory cortex and increased firing rates of putative excitatory neurons.16 Conditional knockout of TrkB in PV interneurons also reduces the frequency of GABAergic inputs to excitatory pyramidal cells in the hippocampal CA1 region, resulting in diminished rhythmic network activity, particularly in the gamma frequency band (30–80 Hz).4 Therefore, TrkB, a crucial regulator of membrane channel proteins, holds significant potential in modulating the 40 Hz light flicker-evoked excitation of PV interneurons.

TrkB is the cognate receptor for brain-derived neurotrophic factor (BDNF) and mediates critical signaling events that regulate various aspects of neuronal development and function. Notably, 40 Hz stimulation increases the protein levels of BDNF, TrkB, PSD95, and synapsin 1 in the prefrontal cortex (PFC) and hippocampus in a posttraumatic stress disorder model.17 In Alzheimer’s disease models, such as APP/PS1 and 3xTg, 40 Hz stimulation also enhances the expression of BDNF and its downstream proteins in the hippocampus.18,19

Pro-BDNF, with a molecular mass of 28 kDa, can be cleaved intracellularly and extracellularly into a 14 kDa mature BDNF (mBDNF) and a 17 kDa pro-peptide.20 While pro-BDNF binds to the p75NTR receptor, mBDNF specifically binds to TrkB.21 mBDNF increases the number of action potentials by reducing the rheobase without altering the firing threshold in rat sensory neurons.22 Chronic exposure to mBDNF enhances the voltage-gated K+ (Kv) channels, which play an important role in the control of neuronal function.23,24 In general, native Kv currents in neurons are expressed as two major types: a sustained K+ current (IK) and an A-type current (IA).24 IK and IA exhibit distinct properties regarding neuronal firing behavior and differences in K+ currents in regulating action potentials of various neuronal populations.25,26 However, whether 40 Hz light flicker evokes TrkB signaling in PV interneurons, or alters the Kv channels remains unknown.

In this study, we found that 40 Hz light flicker upregulates mBDNF levels in the hippocampal DG granule cells, while the expression of its receptor, TrkB, in PV interneurons remains unchanged. TrkB knockdown, specifically in PV interneurons, abolished the flicker-induced enhancement of PV interneuron excitability and inhibitory transmission to granule cells, highlighting the crucial role of TrkB signaling in the function of PV interneurons. Furthermore, 40 Hz light flicker improved spatial learning, an effect that was blocked by TrkB knockdown in PV interneurons. TrkB knockdown also reduced the light flicker-induced increase in sustained (IK) and transient (IA) K+ currents, demonstrating the importance of TrkB in modulating the excitability of PV interneurons through a mechanism involving Kv channels.

Results

Upregulation of mature brain-derived neurotrophic factor expression evoked by long-term 40 Hz light flicker

We employed two methods to assess changes in BDNF expression following long-term 40 Hz light flicker exposure. First, 6-month-old C57BL/6J mice were exposed to 40 Hz light flicker for 30 days, with room light serving as the control. Mouse hippocampi were collected at 0, 1, 2, 3, 7, and 30 days during the 30-day exposure period for Western blot analysis (Figure 1A). The results showed a transient increase in mBDNF expression on days 2, 3, and 7 compared to day 0, with levels returning to baseline by day 30 (Figures 1B and 1C). In contrast, proBDNF levels did not show significant changes at any time point compared to the control (Figure 1D). The expression level of β-actin was used as an internal control to quantify both proBDNF and mBDNF levels.

Second, we designed a BDNF promoter-driven EGFP expression construct to assess 40 Hz light-induced upregulation of BDNF expression (Figure 1E). A recombinant adeno-associated virus (rAAV) expressing EGFP under the control of the BDNF promoter (AAV-BDNF-EGFP) was generated, as described in the STAR Methods section (Figure S1). The BDNF promoter was selected from the mouse genome (GenBank: NM_001316310.1) and spans the region from 867 bp to 179 bp upstream of the first start codon (ATG), at the end of exon I of the BDNF gene.

Mice were injected with the AAV-BDNF-EGFP construct for two weeks, followed by treatment with 40 Hz light flicker for 30 days. Room light-treated mice served as the control condition group (Figure 1F). Brain sections showed a significant increase in green fluorescence intensity in the rAAV-injected region of the DG compared to the room light treatment control, observed on days 3 and 7 post-treatment with 40 Hz light flicker (Figures 1G and 1H). Additionally, hippocampal lysates were collected and analyzed via ELISA to quantify EGFP expression driven by the BDNF promoter. The results confirmed a significant increase in BDNF promoter activity after 7 days of light exposure compared to the room light control group (Figure 1I), indicating the up-regulation of BDNF expression. Hippocampal lysates were collected and analyzed using qPCR to confirm the elevated expression of BDNF mRNA following 40 Hz light flicker exposure. The results revealed a significant increase in BDNF transcript levels, indicating enhanced BDNF expression (Figure S2). Together, these data demonstrated that 40 Hz light flicker enhanced mBDNF expression in the DG during the first week of exposure to 40 Hz light flicker treatment.

40 Hz light flicker promotes brain-derived neurotrophic factor expression in dentate gyrus granule cells

To identify the specific cell types that express BDNF in response to long-term 40 Hz light flicker treatment, we conducted immunofluorescent staining on brain sections from mice injected with AAV-BDNF-EGFP. Staining for NeuN showed that a significant proportion of EGFP-expressing cells were also labeled with NeuN, indicating that hippocampal granule cells were the primary cell type expressing elevated BDNF levels following 40 Hz light flicker treatment (Figures S3A and S3B). No co-localization occurred between EGFP and other cell makers, including GFAP, DCX (a marker for newborn neurons), or PV (Figure S4).

Tropomyosin receptor kinase B mediates the enhanced excitability of parvalbumin interneurons induced by long-term 40 Hz light flicker

To investigate the role of TrkB, we first performed western blotting to evaluate changes in TrkB expression following long-term exposure to 40 Hz light flicker. No significant changes in TrkB expression occurred on days 1, 2, 3, 7, or 30 during the 40 Hz light flicker treatment (Figure S5).

Next, we investigated the role of TrkB in modulating PV interneurons' excitability using a shRNA-mediated TrkB knockdown approach. We constructed an rAAV expressing shRNA targeting TrkB (designated as shRNA-TrkB) for injection into PVCre mice to specifically reduce TrkB expression in PV interneurons (Figure 2A). A mutated, non-functional shRNA in rAAV served as a negative control (labeled as control virus in Figure 2A). Both constructs express EGFP as a marker for easy detection. The rAAV was injected into the hippocampal DG in 6-month-old PVCre mice (Figures 2B and 2C). After allowing two weeks for shRNA expression, the mice were subjected to 40 Hz light flicker treatment for 30 days (1 h per day), with room light serving as the negative control. To confirm the reduction in TrkB expression, we performed fluorescence in situ hybridization (FISH) to measure the quantity of TrkB mRNA transcript Ntrk2, which revealed a significant decrease in Ntrk2 levels in PV interneurons following rAAV shRNA-TrkB treatment (Figure S6) after two weeks of infection.

After 30 days of light flicker treatment, acute brain slices (300 μm thickness) were prepared, and PV+ interneurons expressing EGFP in the DG region were identified under a fluorescence microscope for subsequent patch-clamp recordings (Figure 2D). We found that the expression of TrkB shRNA and long-term 40 Hz light flicker treatment did not affect the resting membrane potential or membrane resistance of PV interneurons (Figures 2E and 2F). However, in the control virus group, the rheobase current of PV interneurons was significantly decreased, and the firing rate was significantly increased following long-term 40 Hz light flicker treatment compared to the room light control group, consistent with our previous findings.8 Notably, TrkB knockdown significantly reduced PV interneurons' excitability in response to 40 Hz light flicker when compared to the control virus group (Figures 2G–2I). Furthermore, treatment with the TrkB receptor inhibitor K252a also reduced the firing rate and increased the rheobase current in PV interneurons after 40 Hz light flicker treatment without affecting membrane resistance or resting membrane potential (Figure S7). These data suggest that TrkB signaling in PV interneurons is critical in mediating the increased PV excitability induced by 40 Hz light flicker treatment.

Given that PV interneurons send substantial inhibitory projections to local granule cells in the DG, we sought to determine whether alterations in PV interneurons' sensitivity could disrupt their inhibitory control on granule cells. We measured spontaneous inhibitory postsynaptic currents (sIPSCs) in brain slices to address this. The artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) used during the recordings contained 50 μM AP5 and 10 μM CNQX to block NMDA and AMPA receptors, respectively, allowing for the isolation of sIPSCs (Figures 3A and 3B). The results revealed that the frequency and amplitude of sIPSCs significantly increased in the 40 Hz-treated control virus group. In contrast, knockdown of TrkB in the shRNA-TrkB group significantly reduced the effects of 40 Hz stimulation on sIPSC (Figures 3C and 3D). These findings suggest that this increase of sIPSC in granule cells is likely driven, at least in part, by the activation of PV interneurons mediated by the TrkB.

Knockdown of tropomyosin receptor kinase B in parvalbumin interneurons reduces gamma entrainment in the DG

Fast-spiking GABAergic PV interneurons are crucial for driving gamma frequency oscillations in the hippocampus.9,27 To assess the impact of TrkB knockdown on PV-mediated changes in local field potentials (LFPs), electrodes were implanted into the DG of mice for 7 days. LFP recordings were then conducted in freely moving mice during a stationary state. We compared the LFPs between mice injected with the control virus and those with shRNA-TrkB rAAV after long-term 40 Hz light flicker treatment (Figures 4A and 4B). The results revealed that 40 Hz light flicker induced significant entrainment in the DG of both groups (Figures 4C and 4D); however, the level of 40 Hz entrainment was notably reduced in the shRNA-TrkB group (Figure 4E). Interestingly, TrkB knockdown also significantly reduced low gamma (30–50 Hz) and mid-gamma (50–80 Hz) LFPs, while other frequencies were unaffected (Figures 4F–4L). These findings demonstrated that reducing TrkB expression in PV interneurons alters PV activity and disrupts gamma oscillations.

Tropomyosin receptor kinase B knockdown in parvalbumin interneurons reduces spatial memory enhancement induced by 40 Hz flicker

Gamma oscillations are key in temporal encoding, sensory feature binding, and memory storage and retrieval.28,29 Disruptions in gamma oscillations occur in various brain disorders, such as Alzheimer’s disease,30 schizophrenia,31,32 epilepsy,33 and stroke.4 Therefore, we set to determine whether TrkB knockdown in PV interneurons impacts behavioral functions. First, we assessed locomotion and anxiety using the open field test (OFT) and elevated plus maze (EPM) test, with no significant changes observed (Figure S8). Next, we tested recognition and working memory using the novel object recognition (NOR) and Y maze tests and again found no significant alterations (Figure S9). However, the active place avoidance (APA) test revealed that 40 Hz light flicker significantly enhanced spatial memory, as shown by comparisons between the control virus groups with or without 40 Hz flicker (Figures 5A–5E). Importantly, TrkB knockdown in PV interneurons markedly reduced this enhancement in spatial memory, as demonstrated by comparisons between the control virus and shRNA-TrkB groups with long-term 40 Hz light flicker treatment (Figures 5A–5E). These findings demonstrated that reducing TrkB expression in PV interneurons impairs the spatial memory enhancement induced by long-term light flicker treatment. Additionally, these data lend further support to our previous observations that 40 Hz light flicker promotes neurogenesis and enhances spatial memory, which is dependent on the increased excitability of PV interneurons.

Tropomyosin receptor kinase B regulates voltage-gated K+ channels to mediate 40 Hz-induced sensitivity in parvalbumin interneurons

Having established the critical role of TrkB in regulating PV interneurons' excitability and spatial memory, we designed experiments to explore the underlying mechanisms of TrkB function further. First, we examined action potential morphology (Figures 6A and 6B) and found that 40 Hz light flicker did not affect rise time, spike threshold, spike amplitude, half-width, afterhyperpolarization (AHP) amplitude, or AHP duration (Figures 6C–6H). However, it significantly reduced the decay time (Figure 6I). In contrast, TrkB knockdown notably increased action potential decay time following long-term 40 Hz flicker treatment (Figure 6I). Since the decay time of the action potential is linked to K+ ion efflux and neuron hyperpolarization,34 we performed outward K+ current recordings to confirm that TrkB regulates K+ channels in PV interneurons, modulating their electrophysiological properties.

Outward K+ currents are generally classified into two types: sustained K+ current (IK) and transient K+ current (IA). Kv1.x, Kv2.x, and Kv3.x channels are primarily responsible for IK due to their slow activation properties, whereas Kv4.x channels mediate IA, which is characterized by rapid inactivation.24 To pharmacologically isolate the K+ channels, during the recordings, we applied 1 μM tetrodotoxin (TTX) and 50 μM CdCl2 in the aCSF to block voltage-gated sodium channels and most voltage-gated calcium channels (Figure 6J). The programs used to induce IK and IA are illustrated in Figure 6K. The recording showed that 40 Hz flicker treatment enhanced both IK and IA compared to the room light treatment in the control virus-infected groups (Figures 6L and 6M). Both IK and IA were reduced in the TrkB-shRNA group under room light control conditions. However, the 40 Hz light flicker treatment rescued the IK amplitude in the TrkB-shRNA group, while IA remained unaffected (Figures 6L and 6M), suggesting a differential regulatory mechanism for these two currents in the context of TrkB inhibition. These data indicate that TrkB modulates the electrophysiological properties of PV interneurons by regulating the Kv channels.

Discussion

This study examined the role of mBDNF-TrkB signaling in the activation of PV interneurons induced by 40 Hz light flicker. The results revealed that 40 Hz light flicker upregulated mBDNF expression in granule cells in the hippocampal DG during the first week of treatment. Although TrkB expression remained unchanged under 40 Hz light flicker, TrkB was essential for mediating the enhanced excitability of PV interneurons. TrkB knockdown in PV interneurons abolished the flicker-induced increase in PV interneurons' excitability and the inhibitory transmission to granule cells, underscoring the critical role of TrkB signaling in the function of PV interneurons. Additionally, 40 Hz light flicker improved spatial learning, an effect that was blocked by TrkB knockdown in PV interneurons. TrkB knockdown also diminished the light flicker-evoked increase in sustained (IK) and transient (IA) Kv currents, highlighting a mechanism of TrkB in modulating the excitability of PV interneurons through Kv channels.

To demonstrate BDNF upregulation in response to 40 Hz light flicker, we designed an AAV reporter vector with a BDNF promoter driving the expression of EGFP. A significant increase in EGFP expression following 40 Hz light flicker was detected, suggesting an upregulation of BDNF expression. To further validate this, we conducted Western blot analysis, which confirmed the increase in mBDNF, but not pro-BDNF, expression. We speculate that the level of pro-BDNF was maintained at a stable level despite the rapid cleavage of pro-BDNF by proteases (e.g., plasmin), which leads to elevated mBDNF levels. Maintaining a stable, homeostatic level of pro-BDNF appears to be crucial for the balanced trophic support for the neural circuitry.35 In a previous study using the 3xTg Alzheimer’s disease animal model, total BDNF levels also increased with 40 Hz light flicker treatment. However, no differentiation between mBDNF and pro-BDNF was made.19 The present study demonstrated that mBDNF is the key effector in response to long-term light flicker treatment.

Our data also provide strong support for the established mBDNF-TrkB paracrine signaling model, aligning well with the foundational understanding of the neurotrophin system. Specifically, we found that BDNF expression was confined to granule cells in the DG, while its receptor, TrkB, was exclusively expressed in PV interneurons. Published studies have shown that both neurons and glial cells produce BDNF,36,37; however, microglia do not express BDNF or TrkB. Only a small subset of astrocytes and oligodendrocytes in the hippocampus express BDNF.38 Notably, earlier studies demonstrated that the activity-dependent expression of BDNF mRNA is localized to principal excitatory neurons,39 while BDNF mRNA is absent in interneurons, which instead express the TrkB receptor.40,41 This finding has been further validated through the modern single-cell sequencing of the mouse brain.42 As a result, the TrkB receptor is known to be expressed in PV interneurons, where it can be activated by mBDNF released from granule cells in a paracrine fashion.40,41,43 Our study confirmed this non-cell-autonomous mechanism of mBDNF-TrkB action in response to long-term light flicker treatment.

As shown in Figure 1, mBDNF expression peaked on day 7 following light treatment, while the behavioral outcomes persisted through day 30 of treatment. This suggests that the early elevation of mBDNF may initiate long-lasting plasticity, possibly through Kv channel remodeling, which sustains the increased PV excitability beyond the initial mBDNF pulse. A similar enduring effect of TrkB-mediated neuronal plasticity has also been reported in previous studies.23,44

In our parallel studies, we found that prolonged flicker exposure does not affect the regional cerebral blood flow in the DG or the activity of excitatory cholinergic neurons, as well as those interneurons expressing VIP or CCK.8 These findings prompted us to investigate the role of TrkB in PV interneurons. Conditional knockdown of TrkB expression in PV interneurons using shRNA abolished the increased excitability of PV interneurons and the enhanced inhibitory transmission to granule cells induced by 40 Hz light flicker. This suggests that TrkB signaling is critical for the modulation of PV interneuron activity in response to light flicker.

Furthermore, TrkB knockdown reduced gamma entrainment in the DG, consistent with the critical role of PV interneurons in driving gamma oscillations in various brain regions, including the hippocampus,12 prefrontal cortex,45 and the sensory cortex.16 Importantly, the enhancement of spatial memory observed in the APA test during long-term 40 Hz light flicker treatment was blocked by TrkB knockdown in PV interneurons. This further supports the hypothesis that TrkB modulates PV interneurons' excitability, leading to improvements in spatial memory.

Previous studies have shown that gamma-frequency flicker robustly entrains fast-spiking interneurons, including both PV and narrow-waveform somatostatin (SST)-expressing interneurons, in the primary visual cortex.46 Moreover, the optogenetic suppression of either PV or SST interneurons has been demonstrated to disrupt slow gamma oscillations in the ventral hippocampus.47 These findings indicate that the observed enhancement of sIPSCs cannot be exclusively attributed to PV interneurons. Instead, it is possible that this effect arises, at least in part, from the concurrent activation of both PV and SST interneurons. To definitively clarify the role of SST interneurons in adult neurogenesis in response to flickering light, further experimental investigation is necessary.

The behavioral effects of TrkB knockdown were observed exclusively in the APA task, while tasks such as OFT, EPM, Y-maze, and NOR showed no such effects. The specific demands of the APA task can explain this discrepancy. Unlike the other tasks, the APA requires continuous hippocampal-dependent spatial mapping under dynamic conditions (rotation + shock-zone avoidance), which particularly engages the DG circuitry.48 Gamma oscillations driven by PV interneurons are crucial for processes such as path integration and allocentric coding, which are central to the APA task. In contrast, these processes are less essential for tasks such as the OFT, EPM, Y-maze, and NOR, which rely more on prefrontal or perirhinal circuits.49,50 This may explain why these other tasks were insensitive to DG-specific PV-TrkB modulation.

We further investigated the mechanism by which TrkB mediates PV interneurons' excitability. Both IK and IA were reduced in the TrkB-shRNA group under room light control conditions. However, the 40 Hz light flicker treatment rescued the IK amplitude in the TrkB-shRNA group, while IA remained unaffected. IA is regulated by the Kv4 channel, which prolongs the neuronal excitation interval and inhibits high-frequency firing, while Ik is regulated by Kv1.x, Kv2.x, and Kv3.x, which accelerate repolarization and support high-frequency discharge.45,51 The data suggest that TrkB modulates the electrophysiological properties of PV interneurons by regulating Kv channels, supporting previous findings that TrkB regulates voltage-gated K+ channels in PV interneurons.14 This data represents the first report on the TrkB modulation of PV interneurons' excitability through Kv channels in response to rhythmic light flicker treatment.

Previous studies have shown that BDNF-TrkB signaling in PV interneurons influences LFPs. For instance, reduced BDNF-TrkB signaling in PV interneurons of the medial prefrontal cortex disrupts PV-mediated inhibition and increases baseline LFP power across a broad frequency range.14 Silencing PV interneurons amplifies stimulus-evoked LFP responses, while reducing the temporal precision necessary to differentiate successive whisker deflections.52 Additionally, the optogenetic activation of PV interneurons in the supragranular layers effectively entrains gamma-frequency LFP oscillations, while the stimulation of infragranular PV neurons primarily drives theta rhythms.53 The present study, employing shRNA-mediated knockdown of TrkB expression, confirmed a similar effect on TrkB-modulated LFPs induced by long-term light flicker treatment.

Overall, this study bridged a gap in our understanding of the mechanism by which 40 Hz light flicker induces the activation of PV interneurons. The findings demonstrate that TrkB modulates K+ currents to enhance PV interneurons' excitability and spatial memory. These results expand therapeutic potential by highlighting a druggable pathway (TrkB agonists/Kv channel modulators) that could potentiate light flicker effects or offer standalone interventions for cognitive decline, particularly for Alzheimer’s disease and stroke, where the dysfunction of PV interneurons and deficits of neurogenesis are central pathologies.

Limitations of the study

We used BDNF promoter-driven expression of EGFP as a surrogate marker for BDNF expression and validated endogenous mBDNF and pro-BDNF expression using western blotting (Figure 1B). However, direct in situ localization of mBDNF and pro-BDNF expression is required. Additionally, since mBDNF can also upregulate evoked GABAergic transmission in the developing hippocampus by potentiating presynaptic N- and P/Q-type calcium channels,54,55 we cannot exclude the possibility that the beneficial effects of 40 Hz light flicker on spatial memory may involve BDNF enhancing the efficiency of excitatory transmission. Indeed, exogenous BDNF selectively increases evoked excitatory neurotransmission by activating voltage-gated calcium channels and metabotropic glutamate receptors.56,57 Further studies in this direction are warranted. Moreover, the present study primarily focused on the mBDNF-TrkB signaling pathway. Other potential signaling pathways and GABAergic interneurons, such as SST interneurons, may be involved in light flicker-induced PV interneuron activation. Future studies are warranted.

Experimental model and study participant details

Experimental animals

C57BL/6J mice were purchased from the Vital River Laboratory Animal Technology Co., Ltd. (Zhejiang, China). PVCre Mice (B6.129P2-Pvalbtm1(cre)Arbr/J) were purchased from Jackson Laboratory (Strain # 017320, USA) and bred locally. Male mice 6 months old were used for the study. Animals were maintained in a condition-controlled room in a pathogen-free SPFII animal facility (23 ± 1 °C, 50 ± 10% humidity). A 12:12 h light/dark cycle (7 a.m.–7 p.m.) was automatically imposed, and the light intensity was maintained at 15–20 lx during the light period. However, the animal facility room light was at 200 lx during cleaning and experimental operation. Mice were housed in groups of six per individually ventilated cages and given access to food and water ad libitum. Experimenters were blinded to the animals' treatments and sample processing throughout the subsequent experimentation and analyses.

Ethical approval and animal experimentation design

The Animal Care Committee of the Southern University of Science and Technology approved animal experiment protocols (SUSTech-JY2018065-A1). The ARRIVE guideline was followed when designing, performing, and reporting animal experimentation.58 Mice used in the current study were randomly assigned to each group to maintain total randomization.

According to the ARRIVE reporting guidelines, efforts were made to minimize the number of animals and animal suffering. The inclusion criterion was based on the pre-established identical age and sex of the mice. The Animal Experimentation Sample Size Calculator (AEEC) was used to determine the minimum sample size required to test the study hypothesis.59 Results indicated the required sample size to achieve 80% power for detecting a 25% difference between two independent means at a significance criterion of α = 0.05 was n = 3. A minimum of six mice per group were used in the behavioral studies to achieve meaningful statistical differences. At least three mice per group were used for brain slice electrophysiology, immunostaining, Western blotting, ELISA assay and local field potential recording.

Method details

Construction of mouse BDNF promoter expression rAAV

A 690 bp BDNF promoter sequence was identified between 867 bp and 177 bp upstream of the first start codon, located at the end of exon I of the BDNF gene (GenBank: NM_001316310.1) (Figure S1A). This sequence has been shown to effectively mediate the transcription of nine mRNA isoforms, from BDNF IIA to VIII.60,61 UCSC Genome Browser screening indicated that the sequence has a high affinity for RNA polymerase II and is enriched with H3K4me1 and H3K4me3 histone marks, confirming its function as a promoter (https://genome.ucsc.edu/index.html). The BDNF promoter sequence and a 513 bp β-globin intron were synthesized by total gene synthesis (WZ Biosciences Inc., China).

A pAAV-CMV-EGFP-WPRE vector was modified by excising the CMV promoter and inserting the synthesized sequence to create pAAV-BDNF-β-globin intron-EGFP-WPRE. Its detailed construction diagram is provided in Figure S1B. The PCR primers for identifying the positive clone were made with sequences as follows:

5′ PCR primer: CGCAAATGGGCGGTAGGCGTG;

3′ PCR primer: TCGCCGGACACGCTGAACT.

Construction of shRNA to TrkB

To construct pAAV-CMV-DIO-Ntrk2 mir30 shRNA-EGFP-WPRE, the Ntrk2 (GenBank: NM_001025074.2) mir30 shRNA sequence was designed as follows: 3′-GCTTAAAGTTTGTGGCTTACA-5'. The ineffective control shRNA sequence was designed as follows: 3′-GAAGTCGTGAGAAGTAGAA-5'. The pAAV-CMV-DIO-EGFP-WPRE vector was cleaved at the AscI site at the 838 bp position of the plasmid. The shRNA sequence was then ligated into the linearized plasmid, and the plasmid was cyclized using ligase to generate the final construct, pAAV-CMV-DIO-Ntrk2 mir30 shRNA-EGFP-WPRE. The PCR primers for identifying the positive clone were made with sequences as follows:

5′ PRC primer: ACAAACUUACUCCGAAGUCA;

3′ PRC primer: UCACUAAAUUAAAUAUGGUA.

Visual stimulation protocol

The visual stimulation paradigm and methods were essentially as we previously described.4,5 The visual stimulation equipment, TangGuang, consisted of seven modules, including a tunable frequency signal generator and six LED lamps (36 V). The six LED lamps were interconnected in parallel circuits and evenly positioned around two transparent cages that simultaneously housed the control and experimental groups. The light intensity used for the 40 Hz flicker was 700 lux. The control group was subjected to the exact duration of exposure to room lighting with an equivalent intensity. Light stimulation was administered consistently once daily from 6 to 7 p.m. throughout the experiment, lasting up to 30 days. The duty cycle of the square pulse used to generate the 40 Hz flicker is 0.5, meaning the flicker is applied during half of the unit time. The pulse width of the square pulse is 12.5 ms, followed by a 12.5 ms interval between pulses. Forty repetitive units occupy 1 s, ensuring the flicker frequency is 40 Hz. The light intensity used for the 40 Hz flicker is 700 lux, while the control group receives light with the same intensity but at a 50 Hz flicker frequency. All experiments involving the 40 Hz flicker followed a consistent daily stimulation time and duration. The stimulation was performed between 6 and 7 p.m. and lasted 1 h per day for the 30-day period. A habituation period was included in all light treatment procedures. During the habituation phase, animals were placed in the transparent boxes for 1 h between 6 and 7 p.m., without any light exposure. The light treatment procedure began the following day.

Active place avoidance (APA) test

The APA test is a practical, versatile, and repeatable tool to test hippocampus-dependent spatial learning in rodents. The testing protocol was exactly as previously described.62,63,64 The mice were placed on a gridded platform with a 1-meter diameter, enclosed by a clear Perspex cylinder (height: 32 cm; diameter: 77 cm) that rotated at 1 revolution per minute (Bio-Signal Group, USA). A tracking computer defined a 60° shock zone, where any animal entering this region would receive a 0.5 mA foot shock after a 0.5-s delay, with an inter-shock delay of 1.5 s. Four visual cues were evenly distributed around the room, and the animal was consistently positioned in the arena opposite the shock zone for each day of testing.

The APA test protocol included one day of habituation without shocks for 5 min, followed by 5 days of testing with shocks administered for 10 min each day. The grid and platform were cleaned using 75% ethanol between testing trials. Parameters such as total distance traveled, number of shocks received, and number of entries into the shock zone were automatically recorded using a ceiling-mounted video camera positioned overhead. The learning ability during the testing period was evaluated by comparing the parameters recorded on the 1st day with those on the 5th day of testing for each animal. The performance improvement was calculated as a percentage, reflecting the progress made during the task.

Open field test

The open-field test was utilized to assess the level of locomotion and anxiety in mice. The method used was as we previously described.4,5 Before the test, the mice were allowed to acclimate to the testing room for 1 h. Each mouse was then placed in the center zone of the open field (40 × 40 × 40 cm). The open field was divided into 16 sections, with the four middle sections (20 cm × 20 cm) designated as the center area. The EthoVision XT software (Noldus Information Technology, Leesburg, USA) was used to record the total distance traveled by the mice and the amount of time spent in the center area for 10 min.

Elevated plus maze (EPM) test

The EPM test was conducted in a white acrylic apparatus. The maze consisted of two open arms (50 × 10 cm) and two enclosed arms (50 × 10 × 40 cm) with open roofs. The arms intersected at a central square (10 × 10 cm). The maze was elevated 50 cm above the floor by a pedestal fixed beneath the central square. Mice were placed in the center of the maze, facing an open arm, at the test’s start and allowed to explore for 5 min. The number of entries into the open arms and the total time spent in these arms were recorded as measures of anxiety. An entry was counted when all four mouse paws were inside the arm.

Novel object recognition (NOR) test

The experiment was carried out in an open field box (40 cm × 40 cm × 40 cm) as previously described.65 Prior to testing, the mice were allowed to acclimate to the test room for 1 h. In the initial stage, two identical plastic toys were placed in the box’s corners, and the mice had 10 min to explore them. Subsequently, the mice were removed from the box and returned to their home cage. Two hours later, the mice were reintroduced to the test box, with one of the plastic toys replaced by a new toy of similar size but different color and shape. The mice were given 10 min to explore these objects. The time spent by the mouse’s nose tip within a square with a side length of 6 cm was measured, with exploration time automatically recorded by the EthoVision XT software.

Y-maze test

The Y-maze test was conducted to assess spontaneous alternations, reflecting working memory capacity. The maze consists of three arms with high walls and a triangular central platform (30 cm × 5 cm × 15 cm), each arm featuring visual cues at the end. Prior to the test, mice were given 1 h to explore the testing room. During the test, each mouse was placed on the central platform and allowed to move freely for 5 min. The sequence and total number of arms entered by each mouse were recorded using EthoVision XT software. Percentage alternations were then calculated using the formula: percentage alternations = number of three consecutive entries into a new arm/(total number of arms entered - 2).

Stereotaxic surgery

The method used was as previously described.66 Briefly, mice were injected subcutaneously with meloxicam (5 mg/kg) 30 min prior to surgery. Anesthesia was induced using 2.5% isoflurane in air (Product #R540, RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). The mice were then placed on a stereotaxic apparatus, with anesthesia maintained using 1.5% isoflurane in air. Body temperature was kept at 36.5 ± 0.5°C using a heating blanket (Product #TCAT-2DF, Harvard Apparatus, USA).

Following hair removal and sterilization of the scalp with povidone-iodine, a hole was drilled in the skull using a dental drill (Product #78001, RWD Life Science), guided by a stereotaxic frame (Product #68861N, RWD Life Science) to target the bilateral or unilateral dentate gyrus (DG) (coordinates from bregma: AP: −2.0 mm, ML: ±1.5 mm, DV: −2.2 mm) based on the mouse brain atlas. After virus injection, the cranial hole was sealed with sterile bone wax, and the incision was closed using sterile surgical sutures. Meloxicam (5 mg/kg) and penicillin (3000 U per mouse, i.p.) were administered for the first three days post-surgery, followed by a recovery period of at least three weeks for virus expression.

Specifically, C57BL/6 mice were anesthetized with isoflurane and bilaterally injected into the DG with either pAAV-BDNF-β-globin intron-EGFP-3xFLAG-tWPA (concentration: 8.51 × 10ˆ13 v.g/ml) or pAAV-CMV-DIO-Ntrk2 mir30 shRNA-EGFP-WPRE (concentration: 3.3 × 10ˆ12 v.g/ml) at a rate of 0.05 μL/min. After the injection, the needle was held in place for 5 min to ensure proper diffusion of the viral solution before being carefully withdrawn. The mice were then returned to their home cages to recover until fully awake. To minimize discomfort during the recovery period, meloxicam (1 mg/kg, s.c.) and penicillin (3000 U per mouse, i.p.) were administered once daily for three consecutive days.

Brain slice electrophysiology

The protocol was modified from our previous studies.66,67 Mice were anesthetized with 1% pentobarbital sodium and decapitated. The brains were quickly dissected and immersed in ice-cold artificial cerebrospinal fluid (aCSF) containing (in mM): 30 NaCl, 26 NaHCO3, 10 D-glucose, 4.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 1 MgCl2, 194 sucrose, and 1.5 mL of 1M HCl per liter of cutting solution. The solution was bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2. Coronal brain slices (350 μm) were prepared using a vibratome (VT1120S, Leica Microsystems). The slices were allowed to recover for 30 min at 34°C in aCSF containing (in mM): 124 NaCl, 26 NaHCO3, 10 D-glucose, 4.5 KCl, 1.2 NaH2PO4, 1 MgCl2, 2 CaCl2, 10 g sucrose, and 1 mL of 1M HCl per liter of aCSF, bubbled with 95% O2/5% CO2. After recovery, slices were transferred to a holding chamber at room temperature and allowed to stabilize for at least 1 h before recording. For electrophysiological recordings, slices were placed in a recording chamber (RC26G, Warner Instruments, USA) on the x-y stage of an upright microscope (BX51W, Olympus, Tokyo, Japan) and perfused with aCSF at a flow rate of 2 mL/min. All recordings were conducted at room temperature.

To measure sIPSCs in granule neurons, patch clamp pipettes were filled with (in mM): 125 CsMeSO3, 5 NaCl, 10 HEPES (Na+ salt), 5 QX314, 1.1 EGTA, 4 MgATP, and 0.3 Na3GTP. sIPSCs were recorded in aCSF containing 50 μM D(−)-2-amino-5-phosphonopentanoic acid (AP5) and 10 μM CNQX at a holding potential of +10 mV. The acquisition frequency was 20 kHz, and the filter was set to 2.9 kHz.

To assess the excitability of PV interneurons, patch pipettes were filled with (in mM): 128 potassium gluconate, 10 NaCl, 10 HEPES, 0.5 EGTA, 2 MgCl2, 4 Na2ATP, and 0.4 Na2GTP. Current steps ranging from 0 pA to 380 pA (with a 20 pA increment and 1-s duration) were applied. The increment was adjusted to 5 pA to determine the rheobase current, which is defined as the minimum current required to elicit an action potential. The resting membrane potential was measured by injecting a 0 pA current. The acquisition frequency was set to 20 kHz, with a 2.9 kHz filter.

K+ currents were recorded with TTX (1 μM) and CdCl2 (50 μM) in aCSF to block voltage-gated Na+ and Ca2+ currents, respectively. At a holding potential of −70 mV, the voltage-dependent outward K+ currents were evoked by incrementing 500-ms voltage steps from −90 mV to 20 mV in 10 mV increments following a 300-ms hyperpolarizing pulse of −110 mV (transient IA and sustained IK). This protocol was repeated with a 100-ms pre-step to −10 mV, at which IA was inactivated (to isolate IK). IA was obtained by subtracting currents recorded after the −10 mV pre-step from those recorded without the pre-step in the same cell.68

To inhibit TrkB signaling in PV interneurons of brain slices, the TrkB inhibitor K252a (Sigma, 05288-F) was applied via a 50 mL aCSF circulation system at a concentration of 150 nM. After incubating the brain slices in aCSF containing 150 nM K252a for 30 min, the firing rate, rheobase current, resting membrane potential, and membrane resistance of the slices were recorded.

Data analysis was performed using Mini-analysis (Synaptosoft Inc.) to evaluate the frequency and amplitude of sIPSCs. Neuronal spiking was analyzed using Fitmaster software (HEKA Elektronik) to quantify the number of action potentials.

Action potential analysis

Rise time is defined as the time from the beginning of the slow rise of the resting potential of the cell membrane to the peak of the action potential. Decay time is defined as the time from the peak of the action potential to the point where the membrane potential drops to the lowest potential of the hyperpolarization. Spike threshold is defined as the membrane potential at which the rapid rise of the membrane potential starts. Spike amplitude is defined as the difference between the maximum value of the membrane potential and the threshold. Half-width is measured as the duration of the action potential at the voltage halfway between the action potential threshold and peak. AHP amplitude is determined as the minimum voltage following the action potential peak, subtracted from the action potential threshold. AHP time was determined as the time between the action potential threshold and the negative peak of AHP.

Quantification of immunofluorescent staining

The immunostaining protocol was exactly as we previously described.64,69 Briefly, mice were administered phenobarbital sodium salt (0.1 g/kg) to induce anesthesia. Subsequently, the mice were subjected to transcardial perfusion with ice-cold 0.01 M PBS, followed by 4% (weight/volume) paraformaldehyde (PFA) in 0.01 M PBS. Then the brains were retrieved and dehydrated in 15% sucrose for 1 day and 30% sucrose for 2 days. Serial coronal brain sections (40 μm thickness) through the hippocampus were cut and retained for immunostaining. Seventy-two consecutive slices from the beginning of the DG (at AP axis = −1.4 mm from bregma, coronal view) were collected and then preserved in cryo-protectant solution at −20°C. One-sixth of each brain was pooled into one series for immunohistochemistry.

Brain slices were retrieved from cryo-protectant solution and washed afloat 3 times in PBST before staining. The slices were transferred onto glass slides and incubated in 0.5% Triton X-100 at 37°C for 30 min. After permeabilization and washing with PBST, these slices were then blocked in 10% goat serum PBS with 0.2% Triton X-100 at room temperature for 1 h. The primary antibody binding was carried out in 3% goat serum, PBST solution with 0.2% Triton X-100 at 4°C overnight. The following primary antibodies were used: anti-Parvalbumin (Abcam ab181086, 1:500), anti-NeuN (Abcam ab177487, 1:1000), anti-GFAP (Abcam ab7260, 1:5000), anti-DCX (Abcam ab18723, 1:500). On the second day, the slices were washed with PBST 3 times and incubated with the secondary antibody at room temperature for 1 h. After washing, the slides were observed using a Zeiss LSM980 confocal microscope.

Western blotting

Mice were anesthetized, and the hippocampus was dissected. The mass of the hippocampus was weighed, and lysate was added at a ratio of 1 mg of tissue per 10 μL of lysis buffer. To each tube, two stainless steel balls (5 mm in diameter) were added for homogenization. The tubes were loaded onto a cryo-mill and cryolysed at 25 Hz for 90 s. Afterward, the samples were placed on ice for 15 min. The samples were centrifuged at 12,000 rpm for 20 min at 4°C. The supernatant was then transferred to a new EP tube. Protein concentration was quantified using the bicinchoninic acid assay.

Sample preparation for SDS-PAGE. An equal volume of loading buffer was added to each protein sample. A 20-well SDS-PAGE gel (8% or 10% concentration) was prepared. Thirty μg of protein per well was loaded, and electrophoresis was carried out at 80 V for 20 min and then at 120 V for 60 min. After electrophoresis, proteins were transferred to a 0.2 μm pore size PVDF membrane for 40 min at 200 mA.

The membrane was blocked with 5% BSA in TBST for 1.5 h at room temperature. After two 5-min washes in TBST, the membrane was incubated overnight at 4°C with primary antibodies: Anti-BDNF (Abcam ab226843, 1:1500 dilution); Anti-TrkB (Abcam ab187041, 1:5000 dilution); Anti-β-actin (Abcam ab6276, 1:5000 dilution).

RNAscope fluorescent multiplex in situ hybridization

Fluorescent in situ hybridization was performed to detect TrkB transcript Ntrk2 using the RNAscope fluorescent multiplex assays protocol we previously described.66 The mouse brain was collected and immediately frozen in liquid nitrogen. Cryosections of 20 μm thickness of fresh frozen brain tissue were cut. Ntrk2 was detected with the hybridization probes from Advanced Cell Diagnostics Inc (Mm-Ntrk2-tv2, #469221, ACDBio, USA). The staining procedure was carried out precisely according to the manufacturer’s instructions for the RNAscope Fluorescent Multiplex Reagent V2 Kit (ACD-Bio, #323100).

To combine RNAscope and immunohistochemistry, the RNAscope procedure was performed between the primary antibody (Chicken anti-GFP; 1:500; ab13970, Abcam) and secondary antibody (Donkey anti-chicken IgY AF488; 1:500; #703-545-155, Jackson ImmunoResearch Laboratories Inc.) incubations. Antibodies were diluted in the co-detection antibody diluent, and tissue sections were blocked with a co-detection blocker provided by the RNAscope Multiplex Fluorescent Detection Kit v2 (ACDBio, #323110). Following this, tissue sections were mounted with ProLong Gold Antifade Mountant (Thermo Scientific, #P36930).

Enzyme-linked immunosorbent assay (ELISA) to detect EGFP

To measure the quantity of EGFP, hippocampus samples were harvested and ground into a lysate at −20°C. The lysates were then used to perform an ELISA using the EGFP ELISA kit (Signalway Antibody, #EK12312), following the manufacturer’s instructions.

Recordings of local field potential (LFP)

The procedures used were as precisely described.4 To implant electrodes, mice were anesthetized with a mixture of 2.5% isoflurane in N2:O2 (70:30; flow rate 400 mL/min) in an induction chamber. Anesthesia was maintained with 1.5% isoflurane in an N2:O2 mixture during surgery using an isoflurane vaporizer (#R540, RWD Life Science, Shenzhen, China). The animal’s body temperature was maintained at 37.0 ± 0.5 °C using a rectal temperature probe and a heating pad (#TCAT-2DF, Harvard Apparatus, USA).

A cranial window at 1.2 mm in diameter with AP: −2.0 mm, ML: ±1.5 mm, DV: −2.2 mm from bregma over the DG was created using a dental drill (#78001, RWD Life Science) guided by stereotaxis (# 68861 N, RWD Life Science). A 4-channel microwire array electrode (35 μm, Stablohm 650, California Fine Wire Co., USA) was inserted into DG and immobilized using Kwik-Sil (Item #KWIK-SIL, Microprobes for Life Science, Gaithersburg, MD, USA). The tetrode was anchored to the skull bone using four skull screws (1.0 mm diameter) and embedded in dental cement. Mice were moved into a warm (37.0 ± 1 °C) recovery chamber (#DW-1, Harvard Apparatus) for 1 h.

After a 4-day recovery period following electrode implantation surgery, the mice were acclimated to the recording setup by being placed in a box measuring 50 cm in depth, 50 cm in width, and 50 cm in length for 10 min per day until the first recording session. Prior to each recording session, the box was sanitized using 75% ethanol. During the recording session, a helium balloon delicately supported the implant, enabling the mouse to move freely within the box.

The headstage was connected to the OmniPlex Neural Recording Data Acquisition System (Plexon Inc., Dallas, TX). A camera was positioned above the box to record the behavioral states of the mouse. An LED lamp, generating flicker lights at specific frequencies, was placed at the front of the box. Local field potentials (LFPs) were recorded for a minimum of 20 min per mouse. The LFP signals were sampled at 1000 Hz with a band-pass filter set between 0.5 and 120 Hz. Raw data were stored for subsequent offline analysis. Statistical analysis was performed using data from five mice per group.

To identify the most relevant epochs of LFPs for analysis, we investigated the relationship between mouse movement behavior and LFPs using simultaneous video recordings of movement and LFPs recording data. LFPs provide valuable insights into neural activity across various behavioral states, with changes in LFP patterns closely linked to specific movements and motor behaviors of the mouse. For example, LFPs typically show an increase in theta rhythm during voluntary movement or exploration, particularly during running or active navigation. Furthermore, hippocampal theta rhythm is strongly associated with movement velocity, with faster movements often correlating with higher-frequency theta oscillations.4 Based on these considerations, we selected several 5-s epochs of LFPs recorded during the mouse resting state (stationary), as determined by the video recordings, for analysis in order to eliminate potential interference from movement-related alterations in the LFPs.

Quantification and statistical analysis

Data were presented as the mean ± standard error of the mean (SEM). Statistical analyses were performed using Prism (V10, GraphPad Software, La Jolla, CA, USA). The normality of data distribution was assessed using the Shapiro-Wilk test before selecting the appropriate parametric or non-parametric test. An anomaly in the ethological test was defined as a value exceeding four times the standard deviation in the corresponding dataset. An unpaired t-test was used for two-group comparisons if the data were normally distributed; otherwise, the Mann-Whitney U test was applied. For comparisons among multiple groups, one-way or two-way ANOVA with Tukey’s post hoc test was used if the data passed the normality test; otherwise, the Kruskal-Wallis test with Dunn’s post hoc test was applied. Firing rate data were analyzed using two-way repeated measures (RM) ANOVA with Tukey’s or Sidak’s post hoc test. Specific statistical details, including sample size (n), precision measures, tests used, and significance definitions, are provided in the figure legends. A P-value of <0.05 was considered statistically significant.
