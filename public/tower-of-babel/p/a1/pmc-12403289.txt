Intraamniotic hemorrhage: challenges in prenatal detection: a case report
Authors: Li Wang, Caiying Pei, Zhanjie Niu
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
BackgroundSubamniotic hemorrhage, a rare condition involving bleeding between the amniotic membrane and fetal chorionic plate, presents diagnostic challenges. Case presentation A 35-year-old woman at 37 weeks of gestation who presented with lower abdominal discomfort and decreased fetal movements came to our emergency department. Ultrasound revealed medium-strong echoes in the amniotic fluid and inhomogeneous echoes around the umbilical cord. A Category II fetal heart rate baseline (FHR) tracing, indicative of fetal compromise, along with an elevated MCA-PSV approaching the diagnostic threshold for fetal anemia, suggested ongoing fetal hypoxia or hemorrhage and warranted immediate delivery to mitigate the risk of adverse outcomes. An emergency cesarean section revealed significant intraamniotic hemorrhage and a subamniotic hematoma near the umbilical cord insertion.ConclusionsThis case highlights the importance of considering rare etiologies for common symptoms and underscores the value of timely ultrasonography and decisive intervention in preventing adverse fetal outcomes.

Background

Subamniotic hemorrhage is defined as a hemorrhage occurring between the amniotic membrane and the fetal chorionic plate and its occurrence is relatively rare but can significantly impact fetal health. Most cases are discovered postnatally, often resulting from excessive traction on the umbilical cord during the third stage of delivery, associated with increased umbilical venous pressure. We report a case presents the rare and critical occurrence of subamniotic hemorrhage, emphasizing the importance of timely diagnosis and intervention to ensure favorable fetal outcomes.

Case presentation

A 35-year-old multiparous woman (G3P2) at 37 weeks of gestation with a singleton pregnancy, devoid of any notable obstetric or familial history, presented to the emergency department with lower abdominal discomfort and diminished fetal movement persisting for over 10 h. Vital signs at admission were within normal limits (T 36.0℃, P 92/min, R 18/min, BP 118/87 mmHg). The uterus exhibited irregular, painless contractions with minimal uterine tone. Cervical dilation was approximately 1.5 cm, though the cervix remained firm and unyielding. The fetal head had not descended into the pelvis and remained floating. The Bishop score was recorded at 3.

Obstetrical ultrasonography revealed that the placenta was located on the posterior wall of the fundus, with a thickness of 38 mm (Fig. 1A), the floating spot echoes within the amniotic fluid (Fig. 1B) and the fetal stomach was filled with medium-strong echoes (Fig. 1C), a dynamic sense of flow corresponding with gastric peristalsis while no intestinal dilatation was observed, the MCA resistance index (RI) was recorded at 0.65, and the peak systolic velocity (MCA-PSV) showed at 1.3 MoM, just below the threshold for characterizing fetal anemia (< 1.5MoM). Inhomogeneous echoes were observed surrounding the umbilical cord (Fig. 1D). These uterine echoes were interpreted as a blood clot, raising suspicion of fetal gastrointestinal hemorrhage based on ultrasound findings, which showed a full fetal gastric bubble filled with medium-to-strong echoes and a dynamic sense of flow corresponding with gastric peristalsis. The baseline fetal heart rate was 156–168 bpm, absence of accelerations, diminished variability, and late decelerations with weak spontaneous contractions.

The patient reported no discomfort after admission from the emergency department. Laboratory investigations revealed a hemoglobin concentration of 87 g/L (normal range 120–160) with hematocrit levels at 0.30, while platelet count, clotting screen, and renal function were within normal limits. A repeat ultrasound scan, performed 3 h later, demonstrated that the fetal stomach remained distended, filled with medium-strong echoes. The amniotic fluid exhibited poor acoustic permeability, with numerous floating foggy light spots and many flocculent medium-strong echoes, some of which adhered to the umbilical cord with a floating appearance at the edges. The FHRremained at 150–170 bpm with still absence of accelerations.

The volume of medium-strong echoes attached to the umbilical cord continued to increase. The fetus possesses remarkable, though limited, abilities to compensate for blood loss through mechanisms such as preferential perfusion of vital organs and redistribution of cardiac output [1], that allowed the fetus to adapt to the blood-contaminated intraamniotic environment. Based on these observations, the source of intraamniotic hemorrhage could not be definitively confirmed as maternal or fetal in origin. Although maternal anemia was present, fetal hemoglobin levels was unknow at that time. Reduced fetal activity can signal impending fetal compromise. The baseline fetal heart rate was initially recorded at 156–168 bpm at admission and remained between 150 and 170 bpm three hours later. However, the FHR findings in our case including accelerations, reduced baseline variability, and late decelerations consistent with acute fetal compromise per ACOG criteria as Category II FHR tracing [2]. These abnormalities suggest a state of hypoxemia and evolving metabolic acidosis, which may reflect the cumulative effects of hemorrhage and intrauterine stress [3]. Thus, while it is plausible that the hemorrhage developed over time, allowing for a brief period of adaptation, the FHR changes observed shortly before delivery clearly signaled fetal compromise and the need for urgent intervention.

A cesarean section was performed due to the high risk of adverse outcomes for both mother and fetus. During the procedure, the amniotic fluid was found to be bloody, and approximately 400 g of blood clots were removed from the intraamniotic cavity. The newborn boy had Apgar scores of 7 and 9 at 1 and 5 min, respectively, and weighed 3150 g. Several minutes later, the placenta was delivered, revealing bleeding from placental blood vessels near the umbilical cord insertion site. The chronic nature of the hemorrhage was likely due to the pressure of amniotic fluid within the amniotic cavity. During surgery, once the pressure on the vessels was relieved, bleeding spurted from the placental blood vessels. Given the signs of hemodilution, she received 2 units of red cell suspension and 400 mL of plasma during the surgery. By Day 5 of admission, her hemoglobin was 84 g/L, and her hematocrit had increased slightly to 0.312.

Postpartum examination revealed a subamniotic hematoma on the fetal surface of the placenta, in close proximity to the umbilical cord insertion site. A 3 cm diameter rupture of the fetal membrane at the base of the umbilical cord was found with active bleeding still evident, confirming the diagnosis of subamniotic hemorrhage during the surgery (Fig. 2).

The patient was discharged home with her infant on postpartum day 6 and received 3 years of follow-up at our clinical center, both with uneventful outcomes. We had the statement confirming that patient consent has been obtained.

Discussion and conclusions

Subamniotic hemorrhage is defined as a hemorrhage occurring between the amniotic membrane and the fetal chorionic plate, its occurrence is relatively rare [4]. Most cases are discovered postnatally, often resulting from excessive traction on the umbilical cord during the third stage of delivery [5], associated with increased umbilical venous pressure. Vessel trauma leading to subamniotic hemorrhage in utero is thought to be caused by sudden traction due to fetal movements, external pressure, or spontaneously by intravascular pressure at a site of lower vascular wall resistance [6, 7] .

Subamniotic hematoma is defined as a collection of blood located between the amnion and the chorionic plate, typically arising from ruptured fetal vessels near the umbilical cord insertion site. In our case, the hematoma was initially localized and adherent to the fetal surface, supporting the diagnosis of subamniotic origin. However, over time, rupture of the hematoma membrane may have occurred, allowing blood to spill into the amniotic cavity and resulting in intraamniotic hemorrhage. This was evidenced by floating echogenic material in the amniotic fluid and echogenic content within the fetal stomach, consistent with swallowed blood. It is essential to differentiate this from subchorionic hematoma, which lies between the chorionic membrane and the uterine wall and is generally of maternal origin, often seen in early pregnancy. Furthermore, intraamniotic hemorrhage is not synonymous with subamniotic hematoma but may represent its complication following rupture. This sequence of events illustrates the diagnostic complexity when hemorrhagic lesions evolve and highlights the need for careful sonographic and clinical correlation.

Abruptio placentae also be considered in this differential diagnosis. However, classical clinical features commonly associated with placental abruption, such as vaginal bleeding, uterine hypertonicity, uterine tenderness, and painful contractions, were notably absent in this case [8, 9]. However, several key clinical findings argued against a classical abruption, including the absence of uterine tenderness, vaginal bleeding, and uterine hypertonicity. Moreover, both the ultrasound and intraoperative findings indicated a localized hematoma at the fetal surface of the placenta, with rupture of the membranes near the umbilical cord insertion site - features more consistent with a subamniotic hemorrhage. Nevertheless, we acknowledge that a minor, concealed marginal abruption cannot be entirely ruled out, and its potential contribution to the clinical presentation remains plausible. This case may reflect a dual pathology, where a focal marginal abruption near the cord insertion led to secondary subamniotic hemorrhage. Such scenarios highlight the diagnostic complexity of atypical antepartum hemorrhage presentations and underscore the importance of comprehensive clinical, ultrasonographic, and intraoperative assessments.

Ultrasound is valuable in diagnosing subamniotic hemorrhage, enabling accurate in utero diagnosis and differentiation from other placental lesions. The most typical ultrasonographic finding is a cystic lesion protruding from the fetal plate. However, some cases diagnosed postnatally report no abnormal prenatal ultrasonographic findings. The subamniotic hematoma may be confused with other placental masses such as chorioangioma. Differential diagnosis of placental masses

Small chorioangiomas are usually symptomatic.

Large tumors, typically those measuring > 4 cm, can create significant arteriovenous shunting within the placenta to cause high-output heart failure, hydrops, and fetal death.

Subamniotic hematomas during pregnancy are often associated with adverse fetal outcomes, such as fetal growth restriction, fetal distress, and perinatal death due to fetal anemia [4, 11]. In this case, the source of intraamniotic hemorrhage could not be definitively confirmed as maternal or fetal in origin. Although maternal anemia was present, fetal anemia was not observed postpartum, arguing against massive fetal blood loss. The hematoma was located between the amnion and the chorionic plate, with rupture near the cord insertion site observed intraoperatively. This pattern may reflect subamniotic hemorrhage of fetal origin, but the intraamniotic bleeding and maternal anemia raise the possibility of a maternal component. This highlights the diagnostic complexity and potential overlap between maternal and fetal sources of intraamniotic hemorrhage. The fetal outcome largely depends on the size of the hemorrhage. If the bleeding occurs gradually, it could allow the fetus time to hemodynamically manage and recover from the blood loss and contaminated amniotic fluid. Identification is paramount to avoid adverse fetal outcomes, as intraamniotic hemorrhage induced by subamniotic hematoma may lack typical ultrasonographic findings but can be confirmed during a cesarean section.

Intraoperative findings revealed an active hemorrhage originating near the umbilical cord insertion, suggestive of fetal vessel rupture through the amniotic membrane. While subamniotic hematomas typically involve a slow accumulation of blood between the amnion and chorionic plate, this case likely reflects a dual process: an initial chronic subamniotic bleed followed by acute membrane rupture and vessel avulsion, potentially due to mechanical stress or increased intraamniotic pressure. This progression accounts for both the chronic sonographic signs and the sudden decompensation requiring emergency delivery.

In conclusion, several important points are raised. Firstly, when evaluating patients presenting with the common ultrasound finding of mild to intense echoes in the amniotic fluid, physicians should remain vigilant for red flags that suggest rare, atypical, and serious causes. Secondly, while the incidence of subamniotic hemorrhage is generally low and nonspecific, extreme values are particularly significant, as subamniotic hematoma is a rare finding typically reported at the time of delivery and is thought to result from traction on the umbilical cord. Finally, clinical decision-making in the interpretation of surgical treatment results must reflect careful consideration of clinical findings. Decisive clinical decisions are crucial, as symptoms may appear benign, but emergency hemorrhage can lead to adverse fetal outcomes.
