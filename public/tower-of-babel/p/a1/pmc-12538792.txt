Mortality risk prediction in liver transplant candidates: a prognostic model using key clinical variables
Authors: Mohammad Hossein Aghazadeh, Mehdi Azizmohammad Looha, Yadmehr khaleghjoo, Fatemeh Ghoorchi Beigi, Amirhassan Rabbani, Tina Parsa, Yekta Parsa
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackgroundAccurate mortality prediction is vital for liver transplant allocation, but models like MELD-Na and Child–Pugh may overlook important individual risk factors. This study aimed to develop and validate a prognostic model using key clinical variables and both statistical and machine learning methods.Materials and methodsThis retrospective study of 193 liver transplant candidates at Taleghani Hospital analyzed clinical data to identify predictors of waiting list mortality. Key variables were selected based on univariate logistic regression performance and clinical relevance, avoiding overlap with composite scores. These predictors were then modeled using logistic regression and imbalance-handled machine learning approaches, and a nomogram was constructed for individualized risk estimation.ResultsAmong the 193 patients included in the study, 9 individuals (4.7%) died while on the liver transplant waiting list. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, both the MELD-Na score (odds ratio [OR] = 1.25, 95% confidence interval [CI]: 1.06–1.47) and hepatic encephalopathy (OR = 36.94, 95% CI: 5.07–269.28) remained statistically significant. MELD-Na (AUC: 0.86), albumin (AUC: 0.77), and hepatic encephalopathy (AUC: 0.62) were retained as the key non-overlapping predictors of mortality. Among the imbalance-handled models, support vector machine (AUC: 0.85; PPV: 0.41) and naïve Bayes (AUC: 0.88; PPV: 0.38) demonstrated the highest overall performance. Additionally, a clinically interpretable nomogram constructed from the key predictors showed promising performance.ConclusionThis study developed internally validated prognostic models and a clinically interpretable nomogram to predict mortality among liver transplant candidates. The findings support individualized risk assessment and represent an important first step toward integrating machine learning into transplant decision-making. However, external and multicenter validation is required before clinical implementation.Keywords: Liver Transplantation, Mortality, Hepatic Encephalopathy, Supervised Machine Learning, Nomograms

Introduction

Liver transplantation (LT) remains the most effective treatment for patients with end-stage liver disease (ESLD), offering both life-saving functional recovery and curative potential, particularly for hepatocellular carcinoma (HCC). With increasing recognition of conditions such as alcoholic liver disease and chronic viral hepatitis as major indications for LT, there is a growing need to revisit and broaden eligibility criteria to make optimal use of limited donor organs [1–3]. However, the ongoing global shortage of suitable donors has resulted in expanding liver transplant waiting lists, which are often marked by relatively high mortality, extended waiting times, and ethically complex decisions about organ allocation [4, 5]. In this context, accurate assessment of mortality risk is critical to prioritize patients fairly and effectively, ultimately improving clinical outcomes and supporting equitable access to transplantation.

To aid in this process, clinical scoring systems such as the Model for End-Stage Liver Disease (MELD) and Child–Pugh scores are widely used to estimate short-term mortality risk and inform organ allocation decisions [6–9]. MELD-Na, an enhanced version incorporating serum sodium along with bilirubin, creatinine, and INR, is now the standard in many transplant centers globally [10, 11]. Despite their utility, these models have limitations, as they may not fully capture the complexity of liver failure or account for other important prognostic factors that influence patient outcomes on the waiting list [12–15].

In recent years, machine learning (ML) methods have shown promise in enhancing risk prediction by detecting complex and nonlinear associations. Several studies have applied ML in liver transplantation, but many have focused on post-transplant outcomes or used limited feature sets without addressing the challenges of rare-event mortality prediction on waiting lists. Most prior models have reported only the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve (AUC) as a measure of discrimination, without providing sensitivity or positive predictive value (PPV), which are critical for rare-event prediction [16–19]. Therefore, the present study aimed to develop and internally validate prognostic models for waiting list mortality in liver transplant candidates by combining conventional statistical methods with machine learning approaches. Particular emphasis was placed on addressing class imbalance, reporting both discrimination and PPV, and constructing a clinically interpretable nomogram to facilitate individualized risk estimation.

Materials and methods

Study design

This was a retrospective cohort study designed to identify predictors of all-cause mortality among adult patients with ESLD who were listed for liver transplantation. The study was conducted in accordance with the Strengthening the Reporting of Observational Studies in Epidemiology (STROBE) guidelines to ensure methodological rigor, transparency, and completeness in the reporting of observational data.

Setting

This single-center study was conducted at a liver transplantation center affiliated with Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences, Tehran, Iran. This tertiary center serves as a referral hub for advanced liver disease management and maintains a comprehensive electronic- and paper-based registry for all patients evaluated for liver transplantation. The study period extended from March 2021 to March 2025. All clinical, functional, and laboratory data were retrospectively extracted from patient medical.

Participants

Patients were eligible for inclusion if they met the following criteria: (1) age between 18 and 70 years, (2) confirmed diagnosis of ESLD based on clinical, laboratory, and imaging criteria, and (3) active listing on the liver transplant waiting list at any of the participating centers during the study period. Patients were excluded if they (1) had a history of prior liver transplantation, (2) had incomplete or missing baseline data (e.g., anthropometric measurements, laboratory values, MELD-Na or Child–Pugh score components), or (3) lacked follow-up information, resulting in an unknown survival status. After applying inclusion and exclusion criteria, a total of 193 patients were included in the final analysis. Vital status (alive or deceased) was determined based on the most recent available follow-up and documentation in hospital records. For patients who died during follow-up, the cause of death was confirmed to be liver-related in all cases, with no deaths attributable to non-hepatic causes.

Variables

The primary outcome of interest was all-cause mortality while on the liver transplant waiting list. Predictor variables included demographic characteristics (age, sex), anthropometric indices (Body Mass Index [BMI], Lean Body Mass [LBM], Skeletal Lean Mass [SLM], Percent Body Fat [PBF], Total Body Water [TBW]) physical performance (hand grip strength, 6-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) distance and percent predicted), laboratory values (Creatinine [Cr], Glomerular Filtration Rate [GFR], bilirubin, International Normalized Ratio [INR], Sodium [Na], albumin), composite liver scores (MELD-Na, Child–Pugh), immune-related biomarkers (Antinuclear Antibody [ANA], Antimitochondrial Antibody [AMA], Liver Kidney Microsomal [LKM] antibody, Immunoglobulin G [IgG], Immunoglobulin M [IgM]), hematologic parameters (lymphocyte %, neutrophil %, platelet count), echocardiographic data (Ejection Fraction [EF], pulmonary artery pressure), and clinical history (hepatic encephalopathy, ascites, comorbidities, past medical history, portal vein diameter and flow, presence of thrombosis or varices, and underlying liver disease etiology).

Measurements

All data were collected through retrospective review of the institutional liver transplant registry and individual patient medical records. Anthropometric measurements and body composition variables (e.g., LBM, TBW, PBF) were assessed via bioelectrical impedance analysis performed during pre-transplant clinical assessments. Hand grip strength was measured using a standardized handheld dynamometer, and the 6-min walk test was administered by trained staff following American Thoracic Society (ATS) guidelines. Laboratory values were obtained from the hospital’s central laboratory using validated methods and quality-controlled equipment. MELD-Na and Child–Pugh scores were computed using standard formulas. The diagnosis of hepatic encephalopathy was based on clinical evaluation by hepatologists and recorded in the patient's file. All measurements were performed before the endpoint (mortality) occurred to preserve temporal precedence and minimize potential reverse causation.

Study size

The final study sample included 193 adult patients with advanced chronic liver disease listed for liver transplantation. This number was derived from all eligible patients during the study period. A total of 9 patients experienced the primary outcome (mortality). We acknowledge that this small number of events limits the statistical power of multivariable modeling and does not meet the conventional “10 events per predictor variable (EPV)” rule of thumb for logistic regression. To minimize overfitting, we restricted the final multivariate analysis to limited number of variables that showed the strongest and most consistent clinical and statistical associations with mortality. Even so, these results should be regarded as exploratory signals rather than definitive estimates. It is also important to note that studies involving liver transplant waiting lists are typically constrained by the limited number of eligible patients, as such populations are highly specific, closely monitored, and relatively small due to the specialized nature of transplant care. Therefore, our sample size reflects both the practical limitations and the clinical reality of research in this setting.

Statistical analysis

Descriptive statistics

All statistical analyses were conducted using R version 4.5.0, with a significance threshold set at p < 0.05. Descriptive statistics were used to summarize baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics. Continuous variables were presented as means ± standard deviations (SD) or medians with interquartile ranges (IQR) depending on distribution, and were compared using independent t-tests or Mann–Whitney U tests. Categorical variables were reported as counts and percentages, and compared using chi-squared or Fisher’s exact tests as appropriate.

Association

To identify potential predictors of mortality among patients on the liver transplant waiting list, univariate logistic regression analyses were first performed for all variables. Odds ratios (ORs) with 95% confidence intervals (CIs) were calculated to assess associations. Variables with a p-value < 0.05 in univariate analysis, along with those deemed clinically relevant, were subsequently entered into a multivariate logistic regression model using forward stepwise selection to determine independent predictors of mortality.

Prediction

Preprocessing and feature scaling

Continuous predictors were standardized to z-scores within each training fold with parameters applied to the held-out fold to prevent leakage. Binary variables (hepatic encephalopathy) were 0/1 and not scaled.

Univariable models

Univariable logistic regression models were first fit for each predictor individually to generate predicted probabilities. From these predictions, performance was quantified in terms of the area under the ROC curve (AUC), sensitivity, specificity, PPV, negative predictive value (NPV), and overall accuracy. In addition, ROC and precision–recall (PR) curves were produced for each predictor. Thresholds for classification were derived exclusively within the training data as described in the cross-validation procedure.

Feature selection

Multivariable models were then trained using the selected predictors. These predictors were chosen based on their superior performance in univariate logistic regression analyses, where they demonstrated the best balance of AUC, sensitivity, and PPV. In constructing the multivariable models, we deliberately avoided including variables that overlap conceptually or mathematically with composite scores (e.g., bilirubin, INR, or creatinine, which are already components of MELD-Na, and laboratory parameters contained within the Child–Pugh score). This strategy was applied to maintain model parsimony, minimize redundancy, and reduce the risk of collinearity and biased effect estimates.

Multivariable models

Four classifiers were compared: logistic regression with binomial link, support vector machine with a radial basis (SVM-RB) function kernel (hyperparameters tuned by inner three-fold cross-validation within each training fold), naive Bayes (NB) assuming Gaussian likelihood for numeric features, and a feed-forward neural network (NN) with one hidden layer. For each classifier, ROC and PR curves were constructed and performance was reported in terms of AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy with corresponding 95% confidence intervals.

Baseline vs. imbalance-aware pipelines

Given the rarity of outcome events, two modeling strategies were evaluated. In the baseline approach, models were trained directly on the original data in each training fold without resampling, weighting, or calibration, and classification thresholds were chosen using Youden’s J statistic on the training predictions before being applied to the test fold. In the imbalance-aware approach, several techniques were implemented within each training fold only: synthetic minority oversampling with the Random Over-Sampling Examples (ROSE) algorithm to increase the outcome prevalence, class weighting to penalize misclassification of the minority class (with appropriate safeguards to prevent over-penalization), probability calibration via Platt scaling, and threshold optimization targeting the harmonic mean of sensitivity and PPV. When the joint optimization procedure failed to meet minimum clinical requirements for sensitivity or PPV, Youden’s J was used as a fallback. Because the number of events was small, we recognize that these imbalance-handling procedures increase the risk of overfitting and may yield optimistic performance estimates. To mitigate this, all resampling, weighting, calibration, and thresholding steps were strictly confined to the training data to prevent information leakage, and extensive repeated stratified cross-validation (fivefold × 200 repeats) was applied.

Cross-validation and performance estimation

Model performance was estimated using repeated, stratified five-fold cross-validation. Each split was repeated 200 times with different random seeds to stabilize estimates given the limited number of events. Folds were stratified to ensure representation of both outcome classes. For every repeat and fold, models were trained on four-fifths of the data, thresholds were determined on the training predictions, and performance was evaluated on the held-out fold. Out-of-sample predictions from all folds and repeats were then pooled to generate a single ROC curve and PR curve per model, providing a micro-averaged summary of performance across replications.

Reporting of results

AUC and its 95% confidence interval were estimated using DeLong’s method applied to the pooled predictions. Sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy were calculated from the pooled confusion matrix at the cross-validation–selected threshold, with binomial exact confidence intervals obtained according to the appropriate denominators. ROC and PR curves were plotted from the pooled predictions, with PR curves emphasized to reflect performance under class imbalance.

For each univariable and multivariable model under both baseline and imbalance-aware pipelines, results are reported as AUC, sensitivity, specificity, PPV, NPV, and accuracy with 95% confidence intervals, alongside ROC and PR plots. In all cases, thresholds were selected within training folds and fixed for the corresponding test folds to ensure unbiased performance assessment.

Nomogram development

Finally, a nomogram was constructed using significant predictors identified in multivariate analysis to provide a graphical tool for estimating individual mortality risk in clinical settings. The contribution of each variable to total risk was visualized, and a total point score was mapped to a corresponding predicted probability of mortality.

Handling of missing data

Patients with completely missing baseline data were excluded according to the predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria. In the final cohort of 193 patients, most variables were complete; however, a few predictors had sporadic missing values, each affecting less than 5% of observations. These were mainly laboratory and body composition measures, including serum sodium, and bioelectrical impedance–derived indices (e.g., percent body fat and total body water). To address this limited missingness without discarding additional cases, we applied multiple imputation by chained equations (MICE) with a random forest algorithm (mice method = "rf"). Ten imputations with 10 iterations each were generated, and results were pooled across the imputed datasets for model training and evaluation.

Ethical considerations

This study was conducted in accordance with the ethical standards of the Declaration of Helsinki and the relevant guidelines and regulations of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences. The research protocol was reviewed and approved by the Institutional Review Board (IRB) of Shahid Beheshti University of Medical Sciences (Approval Code: IR.SBMU.MSP.REC.1404.172). Given the retrospective design of the study and the use of fully de-identified data, the requirement for informed consent to participate was explicitly waived by the approving ethics committee. We also confirm that no organs or tissues were obtained from prisoners. Patient confidentiality was rigorously maintained, and all data were securely stored and accessed only by authorized personnel.

Results

Baseline characteristics of the study sample by mortality status

Table 1 presents the baseline demographic, clinical, and laboratory characteristics of 193 patients with advanced chronic liver disease on the liver transplant waiting list, of whom 184 (95.3%) were alive and 9 (4.7%) were deceased at the time of analysis. The mean age of the cohort was 49.16 ± 12.55 years, with no statistically significant difference between survivors and non-survivors (p = 0.338). Anthropometric and functional parameters—including BMI, LBM, SLM, TBW, and hand grip strength—were generally comparable between groups. Although non-survivors had numerically lower PBF and 6MWT distance, these differences did not reach statistical significance. In contrast, markers of liver dysfunction and decompensation differed significantly: non-survivors exhibited higher bilirubin (p = 0.001), MELD-Na (p < 0.001), and Child–Pugh scores (p = 0.001), alongside lower albumin (p = 0.003). Hepatic encephalopathy (p < 0.001) and ascites (p = 0.029) were also more prevalent in the deceased group. Other variables, including sex, comorbidity burden, portal vein parameters, and autoimmune serologies, did not show statistically significant associations with mortality. Importantly, the median time on the waiting list was similar between groups (Alive: 28 months [IQR: 15–41]; Deceased: 25 months [IQR: 19–38]), with no statistically significant difference (p = 0.924).

Independent Risk Factors for Mortality in Liver Transplant Candidates

Table 2 showed that several variables demonstrated significant associations with mortality. MELD-Na showed a strong positive association with mortality risk (OR: 1.26, 95% CI: 1.11–1.47, p = 0.001), indicating that higher MELD-Na scores increased the odds of death. Similarly, higher Child–Pugh scores were significantly associated with increased mortality (OR: 2.39, 95% CI: 1.61–4.03, p < 0.001). Lower albumin levels were associated with lower odds of survival (OR: 0.20, 95% CI: 0.06–0.56, p = 0.003), while the presence of hepatic encephalopathy was a strong predictor of mortality (OR: 48.27, 95% CI: 8.65–310.79, p < 0.001). Serum sodium (Na) also demonstrated a borderline significant inverse association (OR: 0.87, 95% CI: 0.76–1.00, p = 0.040). Other variables, including age, BMI, LBM, SLM, TBW, functional measures (e.g., 6MWT), and most laboratory indices, were not significantly associated with mortality in univariate analysis. In the multivariate logistic regression analysis, variables with p < 0.05 in univariate testing were considered, while excluding those that overlapped conceptually or mathematically with composite indices such as albumin and Child–Pugh. Based on this approach, MELD-Na (OR: 1.25, 95% CI: 1.06–1.47, p = 0.008) and hepatic encephalopathy (OR: 36.94, 95% CI: 5.07–269.28, p < 0.001) emerged as independent predictors of death. Given the small number of events, these findings should be interpreted with caution.

Diagnostic performance of individual variables and machine learning models (AUC and PPV) for mortality prediction under classic and imbalance-aware settings

To evaluate the predictive performance of individual clinical, functional, and laboratory variables for mortality among patients on the liver transplant waiting list, we first conducted univariate logistic regression analyses and assessed diagnostic performance using ROC-based metrics. As shown in Table 3, MELD-Na emerged as the strongest single predictor (AUC: 0.86; PPV: 0.18), followed by albumin (AUC: 0.77; PPV: 0.09) and the Child–Pugh score (AUC: 0.77; PPV: 0.23). Hepatic encephalopathy, while showing only moderate overall discrimination (AUC: 0.62), achieved the highest PPV among individual variables (0.57). In contrast, other laboratory and functional measures such as bilirubin (AUC: 0.64; PPV: 0.08), INR (AUC: 0.60; PPV: 0.05), neutrophil percentage (AUC: 0.62; PPV: 0.05), and 6MWT distance (AUC: 0.66; PPV: 0.07) provided only modest utility, while BMI, LBM, TBW, and several additional markers had little to no predictive value, with AUCs near 0.50 and PPVs close to 0.05. Based on this evaluation and after addressing overlap among composite scores, MELD-Na, albumin, and hepatic encephalopathy were retained as the final predictors for multivariable analyses, as they offered the best balance of AUC, sensitivity, and PPV while remaining clinically interpretable. These three variables were then used to train multivariable machine learning models (LR, SVM, NB, and NN). In the baseline (non-imbalance-handled) setting, NB achieved the highest performance (AUC: 0.88; PPV: 0.24), followed by LR (AUC: 0.88; PPV: 0.23), NN (AUC: 0.76; PPV: 0.20), and SVM (AUC: 0.60; PPV: 0.18). With imbalance-aware adjustments, further gains were observed, particularly for SVM (AUC: 0.85; PPV: 0.41) and NB (AUC: 0.88; PPV: 0.38), while LR (AUC: 0.86; PPV: 0.33) and NN (AUC: 0.82; PPV: 0.35) also improved. The ROC and PR curves of these models are presented in Fig. 1, showing that under imbalanced data conditions, SVM and NB achieved comparatively superior performance.

Discussion

This study aimed to predict mortality risk among patients with advanced chronic liver disease on the liver transplant waiting list using both conventional statistical methods and machine learning techniques. MELD-Na, albumin, and hepatic encephalopathy emerged as the most robust and clinically relevant predictors of mortality, with no conceptual or mathematical overlap with one another. These predictors consistently demonstrated the best discrimination, sensitivity, and PPV in univariable analyses and were therefore incorporated into multivariable logistic regression and machine learning models. Both classical models and imbalance-aware approaches were implemented, allowing us to evaluate predictive performance under real-world data distributions characterized by a small number of events. A practical nomogram was also constructed to provide clinicians with an accessible tool for estimating individualized mortality risk. This work should be viewed as a first step, offering an initial framework that future research can build upon. External validation in independent cohorts will be essential to confirm the generalizability of our findings and to guide eventual clinical implementation.

Consistent with previous studies highlighting the prognostic performance of MELD-based models, the present study demonstrated that the MELD-Na score exhibited strong discriminative ability, with an AUC of approximately 0.86 and high sensitivity for predicting mortality among patients on the liver transplant waiting list. This high level of predictive accuracy is in agreement with findings by Kim et al. who reported that the inclusion of serum sodium significantly enhances the model’s capacity to reflect the severity of systemic decompensation in end-stage liver disease [20, 21]. By accurately identifying high-risk candidates, MELD-Na facilitates more equitable and clinically justified organ allocation. These results reinforce the continued utility of MELD-Na as a reliable and practical tool in liver transplant candidate evaluation and suggest that its application may contribute to improved clinical outcomes through enhanced patient prioritization on the waiting list. In the context of rare mortality events, particular emphasis should be placed on sensitivity (the proportion of true deaths correctly identified) and PPV (the proportion of predicted deaths that were actual deaths), as these measures most directly reflect clinical utility. The recently cited studies, however, have rarely reported these metrics in full detail, which represents a limitation in the existing literature. In contrast, our study explicitly addressed this gap by systematically reporting both sensitivity and PPV, thereby providing a more clinically meaningful assessment of model performance. At the same time, this study should be regarded as an important first step, and external validation in independent cohorts will be required to confirm generalizability and support its future integration into clinical practice.

Our study demonstrated that serum bilirubin retained prognostic value, showing a relatively high AUC and sensitivity but a low PPV for predicting mortality among patients on the liver transplant waiting list. This pattern illustrates the common challenge of rare-event prediction, where discrimination may appear strong but positive predictive accuracy is limited. Previous work by Barba et al. identified elevated bilirubin as a strong predictor of mortality in both hematopoietic stem cell and solid organ transplant populations [22], and Spetzler et al. emphasized its central role within the MELD scoring system [23]. Similarly, Legáz et al. reported the prognostic significance of bilirubin in patients with liver metastases from neuroendocrine tumors, reinforcing its broader relevance across diverse liver transplant populations [24]. However, as with other reports [25, 26], these studies primarily focused on discrimination metrics without systematically addressing sensitivity and PPV. Our study not only reaffirms bilirubin’s value as an accessible biomarker of hepatic dysfunction but also highlights the need to interpret its predictive performance using clinically meaningful measures beyond discrimination alone.

In our study, serum albumin emerged as an important predictor of mortality, showing a statistically significant association in logistic regression while also demonstrating a relatively high AUC but only modest sensitivity and PPV. This pattern indicates that while albumin contributes to overall discrimination, its ability to correctly identify and predict deaths remains constrained in rare-event settings. The biological plausibility of this finding is supported by earlier work suggesting that low albumin levels reflect both impaired liver synthetic function and poor nutritional status, which are key factors influencing transplant outcomes. For example, Aloia et al. found that serum albumin levels below 2.5 g/dL were significantly associated with higher five-year mortality in older transplant recipients [27]. Other studies have shown that incorporating albumin into composite indices, such as MELD or the albumin–bilirubin (ALBI) score, enhances prognostic accuracy by capturing additional dimensions of patient status [28–30]. Furthermore, Zhu et al. linked lower preoperative albumin levels with a higher risk of early postoperative complications [31], while Xu et al. provided meta-analytic evidence suggesting that albumin administration may reduce short-term complications in cirrhotic patients, although its effect on long-term survival remains uncertain [32]. Accordingly, our findings and the existing body of evidence suggest that serum albumin is a valuable and practical biomarker that can enhance patient risk assessment when used alongside conventional scoring systems in liver transplantation.

In our study, the Child–Pugh score was statistically significant in logistic regression and demonstrated a strong ability to predict mortality among liver transplant candidates on the waiting list, with an AUC of approximately 0.77. Despite its moderate discrimination, its sensitivity and PPV were limited, reflecting the challenges of prediction in rare-event settings. This finding highlights the continued clinical relevance of the Child–Pugh classification, which has been widely used to evaluate liver function and estimate surgical risk. Previous research, including studies by Hoteit [33] and Gilroy et al. [34], has shown that mortality risk varies across Child–Pugh classes. Patients in Class A tend to have relatively low post-surgical mortality, whereas those in Class C face significantly higher risks. However, the Child–Pugh score includes subjective components such as the assessment of ascites and hepatic encephalopathy. These elements may lead to variability between observers and limit the score's precision in predicting outcomes, as noted by Mai et al. [35]. To address these limitations, more objective scoring systems have been developed. The MELD score, for example, was specifically designed to estimate short-term mortality in patients with decompensated liver disease and has become the standard for transplant allocation, as highlighted by Lai et al. [36]. Despite the widespread adoption of MELD, the Child–Pugh score continues to provide valuable prognostic information in certain contexts. Botta et al. found that in European cohorts, the Child–Pugh classification remained a reliable predictor of both short- and medium-term survival and performed comparably to MELD in some clinical scenarios [37]. Our findings support the continued use of the Child–Pugh score as a simple and informative tool in pre-transplant risk assessment, especially when applied alongside more objective models to enhance decision-making.

In addition to laboratory-based prognostic models, functional assessments have emerged as important complementary tools in evaluating liver transplant candidates. In the present study, the Six-Minute Walk Test (6MWT) distance demonstrated moderate predictive accuracy for waiting list mortality, with an AUC of approximately 0.66. This result is consistent with prior studies by Carey et al. [38] and Dolgin et al. [39] which identified reduced exercise capacity as a significant predictor of adverse outcomes in this population. The 6MWT serves as an integrated measure of physiological reserve, including cardiopulmonary function, muscular strength, and endurance, and acts as a surrogate marker of frailty, a growing consideration in transplant evaluation. Although its discriminative ability was lower than that of the MELD-Na score, the 6MWT remains a practical, non-invasive assessment that provides additional clinical insight when used in conjunction with biochemical indices. These findings support the incorporation of functional performance measures into multidimensional risk stratification frameworks to enhance the accuracy of mortality prediction and guide individualized decision-making in liver transplantation.

Hepatic encephalopathy (HE), a manifestation of advanced hepatic dysfunction characterized by a spectrum of neuropsychiatric disturbances, has increasingly been recognized as an independent predictor of mortality among liver transplant candidates. In our study, the presence of HE demonstrated a moderate predictive capacity, with an AUC of approximately 0.62 for distinguishing between survival and waiting list mortality. However, the PPV was the highest among all variables with value of 0.57. This finding is consistent with previous reports by Moreno et al. [40] and Kerbert et al. who identified HE as a significant clinical determinant of poor outcomes in patients with end-stage liver disease. Importantly, HE may not be fully captured by conventional scoring systems such as MELD or MELD-Na, potentially leading to an underestimation of mortality risk in affected individuals. As such, its inclusion in clinical assessments may enhance risk stratification and support more accurate prioritization for transplantation [41]. Our findings highlight the prognostic value of HE and advocate for its systematic evaluation in transplant candidates to improve allocation equity and clinical outcomes.

In our study, data mining techniques identified MELD-Na, serum albumin, and hepatic encephalopathy as the most important predictors of mortality among patients on the liver transplant waiting list. These three variables were retained because they consistently provided the best balance of AUC, sensitivity, and PPV, while also avoiding overlap.. When analyzed together, they demonstrated strong predictive performance in multivariable models. Among the machine learning approaches, SVM and NB achieved the highest discrimination, outperforming logistic regression and neural networks under imbalance-aware settings. While traditional models such as the Cox proportional hazards model have long been applied to assess mortality risk in this population [42, 43], the use of machine learning remains relatively limited. Prior studies employing artificial neural networks have shown that these methods can capture complex, nonlinear relationships among clinical variables and provide more individualized predictions [44]. Our findings add to this evidence by demonstrating that machine learning, particularly SVM and NB can improve mortality prediction and support clinical decision-making in transplant medicine.

In addition to applying traditional statistical and machine learning models, our study introduced a clinically applicable nomogram to support individualized prediction of mortality risk in liver transplant candidates. This tool incorporates three key predictors, MELD-Na, serum albumin, and hepatic encephalopathy, and presents them in a visual and quantitative format that translates complex clinical information into an easy-to-use risk score. Nomograms have previously shown promise in transplant-related risk prediction. For instance, Yang et al. developed a nomogram to predict early allograft dysfunction and graft loss, achieving AUC values of 70.8% and 66.0% in internal and external validation cohorts, respectively [44]. Similarly, He et al. demonstrated that a nomogram combining tumor characteristics and serum biomarkers outperformed standard criteria in predicting overall survival and recurrence-free survival in hepatocellular carcinoma patients undergoing liver transplantation [45]. In another example, Chen et al. evaluated a nomogram in patients with hepatitis B virus-related acute-on-chronic liver failure and found that it achieved a concordance index of 0.707, outperforming conventional scoring systems such as MELD and MELD-Na [46]. Building on this evidence, our nomogram provides a practical and personalized approach to improve pre-transplant assessment by offering reliable mortality risk estimates. However, as one of the first studies to propose such a tool in this setting, its findings should be considered preliminary. Without external and multicenter validation, the model cannot yet be applied in practice, and future studies using independent datasets will be essential to confirm its robustness and clinical utility.

This study has several limitations that should be taken into account. First, the retrospective design carries inherent risks, including selection bias, missing data, and unmeasured confounding. Although we followed the STROBE guidelines to promote transparency in reporting, the lack of a predefined data collection protocol and the absence of a formal patient registry may have affected the consistency and completeness of our dataset. Second, the small number of observed mortality events (n = 9) restricted the number of variables that could be reliably included in multivariate models, raising the possibility of overfitting. To mitigate this risk, we adhered to the commonly accepted rule of including at least 10 events per predictor variable, applied forward stepwise selection in logistic regression, and verified collinearity diagnostics, with variance inflation factors (VIFs) all below 5. Third, the study was conducted at a single tertiary care center, which may limit the generalizability of the results to broader populations. Fourth, while the machine learning models—particularly SVM and NB—showed promising performance metrics under imbalance-aware evaluation, these findings should be interpreted cautiously given the modest sample size, which could inflate accuracy estimates. Importantly, we selected predictors (MELD-Na, albumin, and hepatic encephalopathy) based on a combination of strong univariate performance, clinical relevance, and the absence of conceptual overlap with composite scores. Even with these safeguards, further external validation in larger, prospective, and multicenter cohorts is essential to confirm the reproducibility and clinical applicability of our findings.

Despite these limitations, this study also has several important strengths. First, this study is among the early efforts to combine traditional statistical methods with machine learning approaches, while explicitly addressing class imbalance and incorporating a clinically interpretable nomogram for mortality prediction in liver transplant candidates. Second, unlike many prior works, we explicitly addressed the issue of class imbalance by comparing baseline models with imbalance-aware approaches, demonstrating how such strategies can meaningfully improve predictive performance. Third, we carefully selected predictors (MELD-Na, albumin, and hepatic encephalopathy) based not only on their statistical performance (AUC, sensitivity, and PPV) but also on their clinical interpretability and lack of conceptual overlap with composite scores, thereby reducing redundancy and collinearity. Fourth, the study followed rigorous methodological standards, including repeated stratified cross-validation with extensive resampling to stabilize performance estimates, adherence to STROBE reporting guidelines, and transparent description of model development steps. Fifth, we translated the findings into a clinically applicable nomogram, offering physicians a practical tool to generate individualized risk estimates at the bedside. Accordingly, these strengths position our work as an important first step toward developing more reliable, validated, and clinically actionable prediction tools for liver transplant candidates.

Conclusion

In conclusion, our study developed and internally validated prognostic models to predict mortality among liver transplant candidates by combining conventional statistical methods with machine learning approaches. We identified MELD-Na, serum albumin, and hepatic encephalopathy as the most robust and clinically relevant predictors of waiting list mortality, carefully avoiding overlap with composite scores to ensure model parsimony and interpretability. When incorporated into multivariable logistic regression and machine learning classifiers, these variables achieved strong predictive performance, with imbalance-aware models such as SVM and NB demonstrating particularly favorable accuracy and PPV under conditions of class imbalance. To improve clinical usability, we also constructed a nomogram that translates these predictors into an accessible bedside tool for individualized risk estimation. This represents a preliminary step demonstrating the potential utility of machine-learning methods for liver transplant candidate evaluation. However, given the small number of events and the single-center nature of our study, the findings should be interpreted as exploratory. External validation using larger, multicenter, and prospective datasets is essential before these models can be considered for routine clinical use or for informing organ allocation strategies.
