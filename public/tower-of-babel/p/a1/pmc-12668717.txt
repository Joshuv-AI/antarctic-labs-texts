Cerebral Oxygenation Pattern during Therapeutic Hypothermia after Perinatal Asphyxia: A Single Center Cohort Study
Authors: Thomas Alderliesten, Corline E.J. Parmentier, Manon J.N.L. Benders, Linda S. de Vries, Daniel C. Vijlbrief, Jeroen Dudink, Maria-Luisa Tataranno, Petra M.A. Lemmers, Floris Groenendaal, Frank van Bel
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
AbstractIntroductionInvestigation the association between cerebral oxygenation and short-term adverse outcome in asphyxiated infants with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) during therapeutic hypothermia (TH).MethodsNear infrared spectroscopic-derived cerebral oxygen saturation (rScO2) pattern during first 4 days was compared to early brain MRI (4–10 days) using the Weeke score to define MRI-derived brain injury of infants with GA >35 weeks treated with TH within 6 h between 2010 and 2023. Weeke scores of >9 were considered adverse short-term outcome. Infants with congenital abnormalities, chromosomal disorders, or on ECMO were excluded.Results292 infants (mean ± 1 SD: GA 39.4 ± 1.9 weeks; BW 3,442 ± 621 g; median [IQR] 5-min Apgar score 4 [3]) were eligible. According to the Sarnat staging, HIE was mild, moderate, or severe in 77, 148, and 67 infants. Adverse short-term outcome/death occurred in 0, 39 (26.4%), and 67 (100%) infants in mild, moderate, and severe HIE groups. Severe HIE infants had higher rScO2 values compared to moderate or mild HIE infants. Mixed model analysis showed an association between rScO2 (dependent variable), time after birth, adverse short-term outcome, and inhaled nitric oxide. Logistic regression showed that adverse outcome had a positive association with rScO2 and first lactate levels.DiscussionHigh cerebral oxygen saturation during TH is associated with adverse short-term outcome. This knowledge may contribute to early counseling and decision-making. Combination with early blood lactate levels strengthened this association. Simultaneous NO-ventilation may confound this association.

Introduction

Despite use of therapeutic hypothermia (TH), a substantial number of infants with hypoxic-ischemic encephalopathy (HIE) experience adverse outcomes [1]. Early information about the severity of brain damage after birth asphyxia in term neonates is important to timely decide whether future additional pharmacological interventions during TH or redirection of care are indicated [2, 3]. Brain MRI performed after rewarming is the gold standard for early assessment of brain injury. However, performing MRI within this time frame is not always possible because of the condition of patients, or due to logistical difficulties. Furthermore, the optimal moment to start additional treatment or to decide on redirection of care may be earlier than 4 days after birth [4, 5].

In addition to neuroimaging and aEEG [6], near infrared spectroscopic (NIRS)-derived regional cerebral oxygen saturation (rScO2) monitoring [7], may be helpful to obtain prognostic information at an early stage. Previous studies showed that abnormally high cerebral oxygen saturations in neonates with HIE during TH may be associated with brain injury. Despite a lowering effect of TH on cerebral metabolism, hypoxic-ischemic brain damage further decreases the need for oxygen, resulting in abnormally high cerebral oxygen saturations [4, 5, 8].

However, the predictive value of rScO2 is based on underpowered studies, only including infants with moderate or severe HIE according to the Sarnat staging [9] which showed conflicting results: two initial studies showed significantly higher rScO2 values during and following TH in the infants who died in the neonatal period or had an adverse outcome at 24 months [4, 5], while other studies failed to confirm these findings [10, 11].

The focus of this large single center cohort study was exclusively on cerebral oxygenation. The aim was to show or reject an association between patterns of rScO2 from (before) start of TH up to 24 h after rewarming and adverse short-term outcome. We hypothesize that abnormally high rScO2 values during and after TH are associated with brain injury on early post-TH brain MRI.

Methods

The study was conducted as a retrospective single center study of prospectively collected data on a cohort of consecutively admitted (near-) term neonates who underwent TH for HIE from 2010 up to mid-2023. Since 2010, all infants (>1,800 g) who received TH were monitored for rScO2 as part of routine clinical care. Data on cooling management, relevant physiologic data sampling, blood sampling, rScO2 and cerebral function (aEEG) monitoring, early brain imaging (MRI), and treatment options were recorded per protocol. Written informed consent was obtained according to institutional and national guidelines.

Clinical Data and Cooling Management

Obstetric and neonatal data were collected from hospital records or collected prospectively (NIRS-derived cerebral oxygenation, physiologic data, and serum determinations such as first lactate levels [<6 h postpartum]). Resuscitation was strated with room air, according to the ILCOR guidelines. Cord clamping occurred almost always as soon as possible given the critical condition of the infant. SaO2 was monitored, whereas blood pressure was measured by indwelling arterial catheters. Blood pressure support and respiratory illness, including nitric oxide ventilation in case of persistent pulmonary hypertension, were started in conformity with guidelines used in our NICU. Hemoglobin and arterial blood gases were measured daily or more frequently if indicated. Anti-seizure medication was started if signs of seizure activity, clinically or on aEEG, depending on availability of aEEG in local hospitals. Most infants were born in affiliated hospitals and transported to our NICU if cooling was indicated. Seizure treatment was coordinated with affiliated hospitals and started with phenobarbital as first-line treatment. Midazolam and/or lidocaine were used as second-line drugs in our hospital according to national guidelines. Cranial ultrasound was performed on admission and repeated on day 1, 2, 3, 5, and 7 or on indication; MRI was performed 4–10 days after birth as part of routine care. HIE-severity during the first 24 h was assessed according to Sarnat staging [9], the highest Sarnat stage was always used for analysis. Body temperature was kept at 33.5 ± 0.5°C for 72 h, rewarming was commenced at a rate of 0.4–0.5°C per hour.

In- and Exclusion Criteria

Infants were included according to our whole body TH protocol criteria: (1) gestational age >35.0 weeks; (2) evidence of perinatal asphyxia by one of the following: Apgar score 5 or less after 5 min, resuscitation/ventilation at least 10 min, cord or first pH (within 1 h of age) <7.00 with a base deficit >16 mmol/L or serum lactate >10 mmol/L; (3) signs of encephalopathy demonstrated by seizures, a Thompson score >7 ([12] or/and abnormal brain activity as demonstrated by aEEG (discontinuous normal voltage <5 μV or worse), and (4) initiation of TH within 6 h after birth. Since 2021, infants with Thompson scores of 7 were also eligible for TH. Infants with known congenital abnormalities, central nervous system infections, or chromosomal disorders and those transferred for ECMO were excluded.

Monitoring Cerebral Oxygenation and Perfusion

We monitored rScO2 as estimator of substantial changes in regional cerebral oxygenation and cerebral perfusion using a 2-wavelength (730 and 810 nm) near-infrared spectrometer (INVOS 5100, Medtronic). A transducer (small adult SomaSensor® SAFB-SM) containing a light emitting diode and two distant sensors (30 and 40 mm) was placed at the frontoparietal side of the head and attached with elastic bandage and replaced every 6 h to prevent displacement [4, 7].

MRI Data Acquisition

MRI was performed on a 1.5 T or 3.0 T system (Philips Medical Systems, Best, The Netherlands) after rewarming and preferably within 7 days postnatally because of pseudonormalization of diffusion-weighted imaging. MRI protocols included T1-weighted and T2-weighted images, diffusion-weighted imaging including apparent diffusion coefficient mapping, susceptibility-weighted imaging, magnetic resonance venography, and single voxel proton magnetic resonance spectroscopy measurements of the basal ganglia and thalamus.

MRIs were scored according to our previously published method [13]. Scores were dichotomized, scores of >9 were considered high as reported and indicated moderate-to-severe brain injury.

Before 2017, infants breathing spontaneously were sedated with chloral hydrate or intramuscular injection of a combination of pethidine, chlorpromazine, and promethazine. Since 2017, only chloral hydrate was used. Heart rate, SaO2, and respiratory rate were monitored during MRI acquisition. Hearing protection was used (Minimuffs, Natus Medical Incorporated) [13].

Physiological Data Collection and Analysis

Blood pressure, SaO2, rScO2, and additional physiologic data were collected prospectively during stable conditions in all infants admitted for TH per protocol: monitoring was started shortly after birth or on admission from before start of TH up to 96 h of age or longer if indicated. To reduce data to manageable proportions over time, representative (single) values of blood pressure, SaO2, and rScO2 were extracted from the electronic database and examined longitudinally at fixed time points after birth, starting before initiation of TH and at 6, 12, 18, 24, 36, 48, 60, 72, 84, and 96 h. All rScO2 values used were taken during normotension. In case of bilateral rScO2− monitoring highest values were chosen.

Statistical Analysis

Data were summarized as mean ± 1 SD, counts with percentages or median and range, where appropriate. Student’s t tests, Pearson’s chi-squared tests, or Kruskal-Wallis tests were used to compare patient characteristics and differences in dependent variables between HIE groups.

To explore cerebral oxygenation (rScO2) over time a mixed model analysis was used as described previously [14] using the individual patient into the model as a random factor. Linear, quadratic, and cubic functions of time after birth were explored to obtain the best fit to the data. Both main effects and interactions were examined. The final model was selected based on the best −2 log-likelihood.

To assess which variables were associated with adverse outcome, defined as a high MRI-score or neonatal death, a multivariable logistic regression was used. Infants who died before MRI could be performed were analyzed as abnormal. Statistical significance was set at p < 0.05. Statistical analysis was performed using SPSS Statistics (IBM Statistics SPSS version 29) or R (R Core Team, R Foundation for Statistical Computing, Vienna, Austria) with the lmer package.

Results

Subjects

A total of 325 infants underwent TH, 33 infants were excluded (see Fig. 1). Finally, 292 infants were included in the study (Fig. 1). According to the Sarnat staging 77 infants had mild (26%), 148 moderate (51%), and 67 severe HIE (23%). Clinical characteristics for groups are shown in Table 1. First pH, lactate (<6 h of life), Thompson score, and neonatal mortality were significantly higher in severe HIE infants compared to mild/moderate HIE infants. A vast majority of infants received phenobarbital because of suspected convulsive activity or sedation in the first hours of life. Ninety infants (53 [79%] and 37 [25%] with severe and moderate HIE) received anti-convulsive medication. The use of inotropics in severe HIE infants as compared to mild and moderate HIE infants was not different.

MRI Findings

In 234 infants early MRI was performed. Fifty-eight infants (moderate HIE n = 9, severe HIE n = 49) died before MRI was performed. High MRI scores were present in 0 (0%), 35 (24% of MRI performed), and 35 (100% of MRI performed) cases in mild, moderate and severe HIE infants, respectively. In infants who died before MRI 52 did so following redirection of care because of extensive abnormalities on repeated cranial ultrasound examinations and severely abnormal aEEG tracings.

Cerebral Oxygenation Time Course

Figure 2 shows the patterns of rScO2 vs. postnatal age in the 3 HIE groups. Consecutive mean values of rScO2 and 95% CI of the mean after start of TH increased with postnatal age in all subgroups, but were significantly higher and exceeded normal values for the sensor used (55–85%) [15] in the severe HIE group as compared to mild and moderate groups. rScO2 values of severe HIE infants showed a further increase after rewarming up to 96 h of age. Mean rScO2 values in mild and moderate HIE groups remained in normal ranges. Infants with mild HIE had slightly lower mean rScO2 values than infants with moderate HIE (not statistically significant). Subgroup analysis of infants with moderate HIE demonstrated that higher rScO2 values were seen in infants with adverse outcome (data not shown). Because of passive cooling during transportation before admission in a substantial number of infants only 108 (37%) newborns had representative pre-TH (>35.0°C) rScO2 measurements. At this pre-TH point of time the largest difference in rScO2 between severe HIE vs. mild/moderate HIE groups was detected (Fig. 2). Cerebral Oxygenation and Short-Term Outcome

Adverse short-term outcome was analyzed through logistic regression at several different time points after birth. Logistic regression showed odds ratios of rScO2 of 1.02–1.05 at different time points between 6 and 96 h, as well as odds ratios of first blood lactate levels (in mmol/L <6 h of life) between 1.09 and 1.13. High rScO2 at 12 h after birth in combination with high first blood lactate concentrations (<6 h of age) was most predictive of adverse short-term outcome. Figure 4 presents examples of probabilities of an adverse outcome for different rScO2 values and 2 exemplary values of lactate. As can extracted from the figure, at several time points (examples are presented of 12, 24, and 72 h) the probability of an adverse outcome including abnormal MRI scores was higher when higher rScO2 values were measured, indicating that high rScO2 values were independently associated with an adverse outcome.

The orange solid, striped, and dotted lines indicate the respective probabilities at 12, 24, or 72 h after birth using the combination rScO2 with high lactate concentrations (lactate of 15 mmol/L). The green solid, striped, and dotted lines indicate the respective probabilities at 12, 24, or 72 h of life using the combination of rScO2 with low lactate concentrations (lactate of 5 mmol/L). As can be distracted from the figure, the combination of abnormally high rScO2 at 12 h of life and early blood lactate values of 15 mmol/L carry the highest probability of an adverse outcome (high MRI-score or neonatal death).

Discussion

Our study with a large single-center cohort of newborns treated with TH confirms that abnormally high cerebral oxygen saturation without or with TH is associated with a high probability of brain injury [4, 5, 16, 17]. High serum lactates (<6 h of life) further strengthens this association which confirmed earlier studies [18]. This can help clinical decision-making at an early stage: Highest probability of adverse outcome was detected in infants who combined high lactate levels and high cerebral oxygen saturations at 12 h postpartum (Fig. 4). This probability indicator is especially helpful when brain imaging with MRI is not possible. Use of supplemental oxygen and pulmonary vasodilators (NO-ventilation) in asphyxiated infants who developed persistent pulmonary hypertension can lead to higher cerebral oxygenation and hence rSO2 values. This was also suggested by the small but significant positive effect of NO inhalation on rScO2. However, this confounding effect on the rSO2 short-term outcome association seems clinically less relevant since exclusion of the NO-ventilated infants had no influence on this association. Our finding that TH has a comparable effect on the pattern of cerebral oxygen metabolism in males and females supports a recent study from Sewell et al. [19], which showed no heterogeneity in effect of TH by sex with respect to neurodevelopmental outcome.

How can we explain the heterogeneity of results of various studies investigating a possible association between the rScO2 pattern and brain damage or outcome in asphyxiated neonates undergoing TH? It is striking that studies using several brands of sensors designed for neonatal use mostly failed to show a robust association between brain oxygenation and outcome [10, 11, 20]. This was not the case in studies in which adult sensors were used showing a strong association between brain oxygenation and long-term outcome [4, 5, 17]. An explanation may be that the highest O2 saturation to be measured by sensors of most commercial NIRS devices is truncated at 95%. Since neonatal sensors read rScO2 values 10% higher on average compared to (small) adult sensors [20, 21], the range of rScO2 measured by neonatal sensors may be too compressed to effectively measure rScO2 values which are exceptionally high as can happen after perinatal asphyxia and during subsequent TH [22].

Although not the primary aim of study, an important finding should be mentioned here. Based on our cooling protocol we included a substantial cohort of infants with mild HIE according to the Sarnat staging or because aEEG was only briefly abnormal. Patterns of cerebral oxygenation of mild HIE infants were not significantly different from moderately affected HIE infants. Although term infants with mild HIE are increasingly subjected to TH, it is still no common practice [23, 24]. Although a recent study by Montaldo et al. [25] reported no benefit of TH on early cerebral MR-markers after mild HIE, infants with mild HIE often demonstrate brain injury on MRI and unfavorable long-term neurodevelopmental outcome [26]. Our favorable short-term results in the mild HIE group with no infants with (MRI-detected) moderate-to-severe brain injury may, therefore, support inclusion of newborns with mild HIE in the TH program. A normal MRI in the first week of life has been considered a good predictor of favorable outcome at 30 months of age [27].

We are aware that a limitation of our study was the “single-center” set-up only reflecting results of a specific population. But our clinical experience with NIRS-derived cerebral oxygenation monitoring and the well-defined clinical protocol may have had a positive effect on the reliability of our study results. Could the long study period (13 years) have influenced our results? Given the protocolized approach, not changed over time, we do not think this is the case. The only difference may be that with time going by, more infants with mild HIE received TH. However, including some milder HIE cases in the present study with probably lower rScO2 values and more favorable outcome do not interfere with the findings of our study. We also did not take into account the postnatal age at the start of TH. Although some studies suggest better outcomes when TH was started within 3 h after birth, the vast majority of our infants started with TH between 4 and 6 h after birth because they had to be transferred to our hospital. Finally, we did not investigate the exclusive effects of mechanical ventilation and sedation and anti-seizure medication on cerebral hemodynamics and oxygenation. Since virtually all infants were mechanically ventilated during TH, we expect that its potentially confounding effect does not impact the study results. This is not entirely true for the use of anti-convulsive drugs: an important effect of these drugs can be a decrease of cerebral activity, which might have impacted patterns of cerebral metabolism, brain perfusion and ScO2.

Conclusion

Despite limitations, we conclude that the robust results of our study confirm results of earlier smaller studies that abnormally high cerebral oxygen saturations during TH, measured with a (small) adult NIRS sensor, were strongly associated with a high MRI-score, indicating brain injury, or with neonatal death. Combination of abnormally high brain oxygenation with high serum lactate levels further improved prediction of an adverse outcome with highest probability as early as 12 h after birth. This knowledge may be an additional tool in treatment decisions or future trials of additional neuroprotective interventions but should be confirmed in a prospective RCT.
