Insights on the Adsorption Properties of Functionalized Polyketone for Diclofenac Sodium Removal
Authors: Lin Wang, Gert-Jan Euverink, Francesco Picchioni
Published: 2025

ABSTRACT
Aliphatic polyketones, known for their low cost, simple synthesis, and ease of functionalization, have gained attention as potential wastewater treatment materials. However, their application for pharmaceutical removal remains underexplored. To investigate their feasibility as drug adsorbents, the polyketone was modified with four functional groups: hydroxyl, amine, imidazole, and benzene rings, using a one-step Paal–Knorr reaction. This catalyst- and solvent-free reaction is simple, generating water as the sole byproduct. The modified polyketone adsorbents were evaluated for their ability to remove diclofenac sodium, a pharmaceutical compound frequently detected in water resources. Batch adsorption experiments revealed that polyketone modified with amine groups exhibited the highest adsorption capacity for diclofenac sodium. Further studies demonstrated that the amine-functionalized polyketone showed good selectivity for diclofenac sodium, even in the presence of other pharmaceutical contaminants. Hence, an amine-modified polyketone was selected for the adsorption behavior studies to explore the underlying mechanism. The results indicated that the removal of diclofenac sodium was governed by electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and π-π interactions, with relative importance varying as a function of solution pH. Adsorption isotherm analysis demonstrated that the Freundlich model provided a better fit to the experimental data, suggesting multilayer adsorption on the adsorbent surface. The Langmuir model indicated a maximum adsorption capacity of 61 mg/g for diclofenac sodium. In conclusion, this study highlights the potential of amine-modified polyketones as an efficient and selective adsorbent for removing diclofenac sodium from aqueous solutions. This material shows promise for applications in wastewater treatment, particularly in environments with mixed pharmaceutical pollutants.

Introduction

Pharmaceuticals, as emerging contaminants in surface water, raise significant concerns. One example is diclofenac sodium (DCFS), a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug (NSAID) used as a pain reliever for humans and livestock. Nevertheless, the great medicinal benefits have led to illicit production and uncontrolled use. Most medications are consumed orally or applied topically, with around 65% oral doses excreted in urine and discharged to conventional wastewater treatment plants (WWTPS). The major challenge to effectively eliminate DCFS from WWTPs is its polarity and good water solubility (50 g/L). Consequently, DCFS can retain its original molecular structure even after passing through traditional WWTPs and contaminating surface water bodies. The concentrations of DCFS in surface water vary from μg/L to mg/L. Even at low concentrations, long-term accumulation in the ecosystem could pose a risk of chronic toxicity to aquatic organisms and human health. Therefore, applying efficient treatment techniques is crucial to removing DCFS from conventional WWTPs.

Adsorption is an attractive technology for its well-known advantages: simplicity in design, scalability, adaptability, low initial cost, high performance, and avoidance of hazardous byproducts. As an adsorbent, activated carbon is frequently used to remove complex pollutants, such as heavy metals and dyes, from wastewater. However, the relatively high costs and considerable energy consumption during preparation hinder their extensive usage. To address these issues, researchers are looking for alternative materials that provide comparable efficiency at lower costs and decreased energy consumption. This makes aliphatic polyketones (PK) an intriguing class of polymeric materials with which to work with. PK is a relatively novel class of polymers made from carbon monoxide (CO), ethylene, and propylene. The 1,4-dicarbonyl moieties present along the backbone are highly reactive, allowing for the ease of incorporation of other functional groups. The Paal–Knorr reaction involves the formation of a pyrrole ring between an amino group and two neighboring carbonyl ones. This reaction offers many benefits, including the absence of catalysts and solvents, the mild reaction conditions, and the water as the only byproduct. The Paal–Knorr reaction has been used as an effective strategy for modifying the PK, enabling the selection of a wide range of amine derivatives for functionalization. This allows the pendant functional groups along the PK skeleton to be tailored according to specific adsorption needs.

Structurally, DCFS consists of two benzene rings separated by a spacer (a secondary amino group); one of which has a carboxylate group (phenylacetic acid), while the other has two chlorine atoms. Each DCFS molecule has two hydrogen donors (from oxygen and nitrogen atoms) and three hydrogen acceptors. Based on its structure, it is rationally expected that the adsorption strategies for DCFS removal could include π-π stacking, electrostatic interaction, ion exchange, and hydrogen bonding, depending on the chemical characteristics of the adsorbent used. Studies have shown that adsorbents functionalized with groups such as hydroxyl and amine exhibit enhanced adsorption capacity for DCFS. For example, Wang and the coauthors found that hydroxylated multiwalled carbon nanotubes (H-MWCNT) with the most hydroxyl groups (25%) performed better in pharmaceutical adsorption via hydrogen bonding. Similarly, Mi et al. compared the adsorption performance of COFs with (OH-MCOF) and without hydroxyl group functionalization. The results show that introducing hydroxyl groups on OH-MCOF enhances its removal effect on DCFS. Another promising method to remove DCFS from wastewater is to graft primary amino groups onto adsorbents. This is exemplified in the work undertaken by Hu et al., who prepared the bilayer amino-functionalized cellulose nanocrystals/chitosan composite for DCFS adsorption. With the loading of large amounts of primary amino groups, DCFS can be effectively removed from the solution via electrostatic interactions and/or hydrogen bonding. Besides, it has been reported that adsorbents functionalized with amino groups exhibit selective adsorption for DCFS. Thus, amine-rich materials are expected to improve the adsorption capacity and selectivity for DCFS.

The other two functional groups, benzene and imidazole, are both aromatic compounds that can form π-π bonds with DCFS rich in π-electrons. For example, Deng et al. developed a naphthyl polymer brush-grafted adsorbent for DCFS adsorption. The two conjugated aromatic rings in 2-naphthyl acrylate provide a large π-electron system, improving its capacity to capture DCFS. Another study by Husin and the coauthors explored the adsorption of DCFS onto Fe3O4 particles embedded with imidazole-based deep eutectic solvents. The enhanced adsorption performance upon coating can be attributed to the π-π interaction between aromatic amines in imidazole compounds and aromatic rings in DCFS structures. Additionally, imidazolium can act as both an H-bond acceptor and a positively charged site during DCFS adsorption, depending on the solution’s pH. Therefore, as previously stated, it is critical to understand the role of each functional group during adsorption, as multiple interactions can occur between the adsorbent and adsorbate.

Given its unique physicochemical characteristics, environmental compatibility, and facile functionalizability as a promising economical precursor, PK can be tailored accordingly to the desired application by altering the functional moieties attached to the PK backbone. This adaptability inspired us to explore the feasibility of employing modified polyketone for DCFS capture in preliminary. In this study, four amine derivatives, namely, 3-amino-1-propanol, 1,2-diaminopropane, benzylamine, and 1-(3-aminopropyl) imidazole, were chosen for PK30 modification via the Paal–Knorr reaction. In this reaction, the primary amines react with dicarbonyl groups on the PK. Accordingly, the attached functional groups, including hydroxyl, amino, benzene, and imidazole, can be introduced along the PK backbone. As a result, four types of polyketone-based materials with different pendant groups were prepared and compared as adsorbents. Among them, the material exhibiting the highest potential for DCFS removal was selected for further investigation. The adsorption behavior was comprehensively evaluated, including adsorption capacity, selectivity, and the effects of inorganic ions and the underlying adsorption mechanism.

Experiment

Chemicals

Alternating polyketone PK30 (30% ethylene and 70% propylene, Mw 5632 Da) was supplied by Shell. , 2,5-Hexanedione (≥98.0%), 1,2-diaminopropane (1,2-DAP, ≥99.0%), benzylamine (BA, ≥99.0%), 3-amino-1-propanol (AP, ≥99.0%), 1-(3-aminopropyl) imidazole (AM, ≥97.0%), chloroform (CHCl3, ≥99.5%), chloroform-D1 (CDCl3, ≥99.8%), dimethyl sulfoxide-d6 (DMSO-d 6, 99.9%), diclofenac Sodium (DCFS, ≥98.0%), ibuprofen sodium salt (IBUS, ≥98.0%), naproxen sodium (NAPS, ≥98.0%), hydrochloric acid (HCl, ≥37.0%), and sodium hydroxide (NaOH, ≥97.0%) were obtained from Sigma-Aldrich. Milli-Q water was used in the experiment.

Synthesis of Functionalized Polyketone

The Paal–Knorr reaction was used to introduce functional groups onto the polyketone (Figure ). A sealed 500 mL round-bottom glass reactor with a reflux condenser and a U-shaped anchor impeller was used for the chemical modification. PK30 (20 g) was added into the glass reactor and preheated at 80 °C to produce polyketone in a liquid state. Subsequently, a certain amount of modifiers (i.e., 3-amino-1-propanol, 1,2-diaminopropane, benzylamine, and 1-(3-aminopropyl) imidazole) were added dropwise into the reactor in the first 20 min. The stirring speed was maintained at 500 rpm for each reaction. During the reaction, the mixture transformed from a low-viscosity light brown to a thick dark brown paste. After a 4 h reaction, the mixture was cooled to room temperature and diluted in 50 mL of CHCl3. The dilution was refined using brine solvent extraction at least three times to remove any unreacted modifier. The extracted solution was dried by a vacuum oven at 40 °C. The final products were collected and then ground into powder. The final products, named PAPx, PBAx, and PAMx, were designated based on the selected modifiers, where x presented the theoretical degree of dicarbonyl unit conversion. In the 1,2-DAP modification system, the reaction stopped within 1 h due to the formation of a rigid solid byproduct, making further stirring impossible. This observation aligns with the earlier findings of Zhang. The obtained product was named PDAPx.

Paal–Knorr reaction of aliphatic polyketone (PK30) with four amine derivatives: 3-amino-1-propanol (AP), 1,2-diaminopropane (1,2-DAP), benzylamine (BA), and 1-(3-aminopropyl) imidazole (AM).

Characterization

Since the polymeric nature of the polyketone substrate often results in low-resolution spectra, as Zhang et al. reported, the exact chemical structure of the product was determined with the help of low-molecular-weight model compounds. The model compounds were reacted between 2,5-hexanedione (i.e., representative of the dicarbonyl groups on the PK30 backbone; 2 g, 17.5 mmol) and AP (1.32 g, 17.5 mmol), 1,2-DAP (1.30 g, 17.5 mmol), BA (1.88 g, 17.5 mmol), or AM (2.19 g, 17.5 mmol) reacted in a three-necked round-bottom flask at 80 °C. After the reaction, the solution was collected, dissolved in DMSO-d 6, and then transferred into NMR tubes for 1H NMR analysis.

For the reactions on PK, the conversion of the 1,4-dicarbonyl groups was determined based on elementary analysis results. 1H NMR spectra of modified polyketones were collected on a Varian Mercury Plus 400 MHz spectrometer using CDCl3 as a solvent. The 1H NMR spectra were further analyzed by using MestReNova software. The infrared fingerprint was used to identify surface functional groups on modified polyketones. Attenuated total reflectance of Fourier-transform infrared (ATR-FTIR) spectra were acquired using an IRTracer-100 (SHIMADZU) with a resolution of 4 cm –1 over 32 scans, covering a wavenumber range of 500–4000 cm –1.

Differential scanning calorimetry (DSC) analysis was performed to estimate the glass transition temperature (T g) of the modified polymers. Approximately 8 mg of each sample was weighed and sealed in an aluminum pan. For PAP60, PBA60, and PAM60, the samples were heated from −40 to 70 °C at a heating rate of 5 °C·min–1, followed by cooling to −40 °C at a cooling rate of 5 °C·min–1. The heating temperatures for PDAP60 and PDAP80 ranged from 0 °C to a maximum of 300 °C. The DSC curves were analyzed using Trios software, and T g values were determined based on their inflection points (Figure S1 and Table S1).

The point of zero charge pH (pHpzc) was determined by the immersion method reported by Bourikas et al. In this experiment, 0.06 g of adsorbent was added to 15 mL of a 0.01 mol/L NaCl electrolyte solution with varying pH values. The pH of the solutions was adjusted from 4 to 11 using 0.1 mol/L HCl or NaOH before introducing the adsorbents. The final pH values of each solution were recorded after the equilibrium was reached. The difference between the final and initial pH values (ΔpH) was calculated and plotted against the initial pH values. The point where ΔpH equals 0 indicates that the surface charge of the adsorbent is neutral, corresponding to the point of zero charge value (pHpzc).

Batch Adsorption Experiments

For the batch adsorption experiments, stock solutions of DCFS (1000 mg/L) were prepared and diluted to achieve the desired concentration levels (5–120 mg/L). A portion (0.04 g) of functionalized polyketone was added into a glass bottle in the presence of 20 mL of solution with targeted pollutants. The solution pH was adjusted with 0.1 M HCl and 0.1 M NaOH. The batch adsorption experiments were conducted in an incubator shaker at 25 °C under 150 rpm. Upon reaching the desired adsorption time, the solution was collected and filtered through a 0.45 μm filter membrane. The filtrate was further analyzed by high-performance liquid chromatography (HPLC) equipped with a C18 column (4.6 mm × 150 mm, 5 μm). The mobile phase was the mixture of 70% HPLC-grade acetonitrile and 30% double-distilled water (containing 1% HPLC-grade formic acid in double-distilled water). The removal efficiency (R) and adsorption capacity (q e) were calculated as follows: R=(C0−Ce)/C0×100 3 qe=(C0−Ce)v/m 4 where C 0 and C e (mg/L) are the initial and equilibrium concentrations of DCFS, respectively. v (mL) is the volume of the DCFS solution, and m (g) is the weight of the adsorbent.

Selectivity is a crucial parameter in evaluating a practical performance. In this research, we assessed this capability in the presence of IBUS and NAPS. As we know, DCFS, IBUS, and NAPs belong to the class of NSAID, which shares similar structural features (Figure ): the presence of carboxylate acid moieties and aromatic groups. For this reason, they are selected as coexisting pharmaceuticals in the system for a comparative assessment of the selectivity ability of the adsorbent to distinguish between these substances. The initial concentrations of each drug were maintained at 3.33 mg/L without any pH adjustment. In addition to pharmaceutical compounds, other common constituents of natural water bodies such as inorganic salts (Na+, Ca2+, Cl–, and SO4 2–) and organic matter (e.g., humic acid) can influence adsorption behavior. To evaluate these effects, adsorption experiments were conducted with ion concentrations ranging from 1 to 100 mmol/L under neutral pH conditions.

ATR-FTIR spectroscopy was used to identify the characteristic adsorption peaks of the polyketone before and after modification. In the spectra of pristine PK30 (Figure ), the absorption peaks in the region 2967–2878 cm –1 correspond to the stretching vibration of aliphatic C–H in the backbone. The characteristic peak at around 1700 cm –1 is associated with the stretching vibration of C=O groups. ,

Additionally, PK30 functionalized with BA and AM shares the same pyrrole backbone but carries different moieties: aromatic groups such as benzene and cyclic imidazole rings. For PBA60, the bands around 695 and 728 cm –1 are associated with the out-of-plane C–H aromatic rings of PBA60, while for PAM60, we can see that a band at 3107 cm –1 corresponds to the C=C–H stretching of the imidazole group. , Overall, the presence of a pyrrole ring and functional groups of amine derivates fingerprint bands in the modified polyketone spectrum and demonstrates successful functionalization.

Adsorption Performance of Modified Polyketones on DCFS Removal

A series of preliminary adsorption studies were conducted to identify the most effective adsorbents for DCFS removal. Each material has distinct functional groups that contribute differently to DCFS adsorption. As shown in Figure a, all modified polyketone materials exhibit adsorption capacity for DCFS, though to varying degrees. Among them, PAP60 (hydroxy-functionalized) and PBA60 (benzene-functionalized) show the lowest removal efficiency, while PDAP60 and PAM60 demonstrate better adsorption performance.

(a) Comparative adsorption performances over PAP60, PBA60, PAM60, PDAP60, and PDAP80 for DCFS from the solution (initial concentration of DCFS: 18 mg/L, contact time: 24 h, room temperature, pH 5 ± 0.05); (b) selectivity of PDAP80 in mixed-drug systems.

PAP60, with hydroxyl groups, shows the lowest capacity of 0.79 mg/g (1.24%), likely depending solely on the electrostatic interaction with DCFS at pH 5. PBA60 primarily depends on π-π stacking interactions, which are less effective, likely due to steric hindrance from bulky benzene rings, limiting its adsorption efficiency. In contrast, PAM60, functionalized with imidazole groups, shows a higher adsorption capacity of 3.79 mg/g (42.13%), probably benefiting from both π-π stacking and strong electrostatic interactions with DCFS. PDAP60 serves as both positively charged carriers or H-bonds donors to bind DCFS molecules, , with the highest adsorption performance of 5.52 mg/g (61.4%).

Between PDAP60 and PAM60, the latter demonstrates better adsorption performance despite their similar dicarbonyl conversion degrees. We further compared their physical properties by examining their glass transition temperatures (T g), as shown in Figure S1, PAM60, with a T g of 12 °C, transitions from a glassy to a rubbery state at room temperature, causing its powdery samples to soften in aqueous solution. By contrast, PDAP60 remains in a glassy state under the same conditions (T g = 107 °C). For practical applications, adsorbents with higher T g values offer a greater stability. Hence, PDAP60 was selected for further experiments. In addition, PDAP adsorbents with higher carbonyl conversion exhibit a relatively higher adsorption rate, which correlates with the increased density of amino groups along the polyketone backbone.

The coexistence of multiple drugs in a system may interfere with the targeted pollution removal; therefore, we evaluated the selectivity of PDAP80 in the presence of various pharmaceuticals. As shown in Figure b, in a multiple-pharmaceuticals system, DCFS removal efficacy was 64%, while IBUS and NAPS were significantly lower, less than 20%. The higher removal rate of DCFS than other drugs demonstrates high selectivity toward DCFS. Based on the preliminary adsorption test and the selectivity adsorption experiment, the PDAP80 adsorbent had the best adsorption performance and good selectivity for DCFS capture; therefore, PDAP80 was chosen for the following adsorption experiment to investigate the adsorption behavior and underlying mechanisms.

DCFS Adsorption on 1,2-DAP-Modified Polyketone: Effects of Adsorbent Dose, Solution pH, and Inorganic Ions

We started by studying the effect of the adsorbent dose on the efficiency of the process. Figure a reveals that a greater PDAP80 dosage results in a higher removal efficiency. This can be justified by the greater availability of adsorption sites provided by the increased quantity of the adsorbent. On the other hand, the adsorption capacity (q e) decreases with the PDAP80 dosage, which can be attributed to less availability of active sites per unit mass at higher loading levels. The optimal PDAP80 dosage was determined to be 0.5 g/L, as this achieves a good removal efficiency while minimizing the cost and resource consumption. The adsorption equilibrium time of DCFS on PDAP80 was also studied, revealing that equilibrium was reached within 6 h. Accordingly, a 24 h adsorption time was chosen for subsequent experiments to ensure consistency in adsorption conditions.

Effects of (a) adsorbent dosage (pH 7), (b) solution pH, (c) chemical species distribution of DCFS and the point of zero charge (pHpzc) of PDAP80 at different pH values, and (d) inorganic ions (initial DCFS concentration: 10 mg/L, reaction time: 24 h, neutral pH).

In theory, the solution’s pH can affect DCFS adsorption by regulating the charge of PDAP80 and the species distribution of DCFS. Therefore, the effect of solution pH on the DCFS removal in the pH range of 5–12 was studied (Figure b). It is important to note that when the solution pH value is lower than 4.5, the solubility of DCFS decreases, leading to slight turbidity in the solution; , therefore, the lowest pH value used in this study was 5. It is shown that PDAP80 exhibits the highest adsorption capacity at pH 5 (82%), followed by a gradual decrease as the pH increases from 5 to 9. In the pH range 5 < pH < 9, DCFS molecules are negatively charged due to the ionization of carbonyl groups. Under such conditions, the point of zero charge (pHpzc) of PDAP80 is approximately 9.5 (Figure c), indicating that −NH2 groups on PDAP80 are protonated to −NH3 +. As a result, an electrostatic interaction between adsorbents and DCFS is easy to form. Under alkaline conditions (pH > 9.5), weak hydrogen bonding exists between PDAP80 and DCFS, which still contributes to adsorption. This adsorption, however, can be further inhibited when excess OH– ions in the solution compete for adsorption sites and repel DCFS. The pH effect study revealed that PDAP80 achieved the highest adsorption capacity at pH 5 and showed good pH tolerance within the pH range of 5–9. This demonstrates its potential applicability for wastewater treatment across varying pH conditions.

Inorganic ions such as Na+, Ca2+, Cl–, and SO4 2– are commonly found in natural water bodies, with concentrations ranging from 0.02 to 93.48 mmol/L for Na+, 0.00–2.63 mmol/L for Ca2+, 0.012–124.118 mmol/L for Cl–, and 0.015–1.066 mmol/L for SO4 2–. , As shown in Figure d, Na+ concentrations between 0 and 10 mmol/L had a negligible influence on DCFS removal. This indicates that the adsorption process likely involves hydrogen bonding and/or π-π interactions, which may contribute to the strong resistance of the adsorbent to interference from common cations at these concentration levels. However, when the Na+ concentration exceeds 100 mmol/L, the adsorption capacity decreases significantly. In contrast, the presence of Ca2+ shows a pronounced impact on DCFS removal compared to that of Na+. This suggests that not only ion concentration but also ion size plays a role in affecting adsorption performance. A possible explanation is that divalent cations like Ca2+ exert a stronger electrostatic shielding effect on the negatively charged DCFS, hindering its interaction with the adsorbent. This implies that the carboxylate groups on DCFS are actively involved in the adsorption process. Similarly, Cl– ions slightly reduce the DCFS removal efficiency when their concentration exceeds 1 mmol/L. SO4 2– ions have a more notable impact, leading to a decrease in removal efficiency once their concentration reaches 1 mmol/L. This may be attributed to competitive adsorption, where coexisting anions like SO4 2– compete with DCFS for active sites on PDAP80, thereby inhibiting effective adsorption.

Adsorption Isotherm Studies

In the adsorption isotherm studies, the initial concentrations were ranged between 5 and 120 mg/L and the obtained data were fitted to both the Langmuir and Freundlich isotherm models (Text S1). The nonlinear fitting results are shown in Figure and Table . In general, the Langmuir model assumes monolayer adsorption, where adsorbents have homogeneous surfaces and no additional interactions between adsorbed compounds. In contrast, the Freundlich model describes multilayer adsorption, with adsorbents having heterogeneous surfaces and inequivalent binding sites. The results indicate that the Freundlich model provides a better fit, with a higher correlation coefficient (R 2 = 0.9891) compared to the Langmuir model (R 2 = 0.9267), possibly suggesting that multilayer adsorption occurs on the PDAP80 surface. Furthermore, the favorable adsorption on the heterogeneous system is supported by the Freundlich model, where the fitted value of 1/n is less than 1, indicating a favorable adsorption process.

Adsorption Mechanism

The PDAP80 adsorbent was functionalized via the Paal–Knorr reaction between the dicarbonyl groups on PK30 and the primary −NH2 on 1,2-diaminopropane. As a result, the PDAP80 adsorbent features a backbone structure composed of pyrrole units and a high density of pendant amines, which can facilitate DCFS adsorption through electrostatic interactions, hydrogen bonding, and π-π stacking. ATR-FTIR analysis of PDAP80 after adsorption, as shown in Figure S2, reveals a new absorption peak at 1576 cm–1, corresponding to the C=O stretching vibration of the COO– groups in DCFS. This indicates the participation of carboxylate groups in the adsorption process and suggests that electrostatic interactions between the anionic DCFS and positively charged sites on the adsorbent surface likely contribute to the adsorption mechanism.

From the pH effect experiment, the adsorbent exhibits the highest removal rate at pH 5, followed by a gradual decrease in removal ability from pH 6 to 9. To elucidate this behavior, pHpzc was measured and determined to be approximately 9.5 (Figure c), indicating that the PDAP80 surface remains positively charged within the studied pH range. Considering that the pK a of DCFS was about 4.15, the DCFS molecule exists primarily in its neutral form at a pH value below 4 and in its anionic form at pH values above 5 (Figure c). The gradual decrease in removal efficiency from pH 5 to 9 can be ascribed to the weakening of electrostatic interactions between the anionic DCFS molecules and the progressively less positively charged PDAP80 surface. As for higher pH values from 9 to 10, the adsorption performance decreases but still maintains certain uptake capacities. This is likely due to hydrogen bonding between −NH2 groups on PDAP80 and −COO or −NH groups on DCFS. Besides, π-π stacking between pyrrole units on PDAP80 and benzene rings on DCFS could also contribute to the adsorption.

The selectivity study revealed that the PDAP80 adsorbent exhibits better adsorptive selectivity toward DCFS compared to NAPS and IBUS. This is likely due to DCFS possessing one additional hydrogen acceptor compared to NAPS and IBUS, enabling stronger binding interactions with PDAP80. Additionally, the butterfly-like aromatic structure of DCFS enhances π-π interactions with the adsorbent surface. These findings further confirm the role of hydrogen bonding or π-π interactions in DCFS adsorption. Furthermore, the good isotherm fitting results obtained by the Freundlich model indicate the multiple binding mechanisms involved in the adsorption. Therefore, electrostatic interaction, hydrogen bonding, and π-π stacking are implied to participate in the DCFS adsorption.

Conclusions

In summary, this study has shown that PK30 modified with 1,2-diaminopropane demonstrates a better adsorption performance toward DCFS and has the potential to selectively capture targeted pollutants, even in the presence of other pharmaceuticals. Langmuir fitting revealed that the as-prepared adsorbent exhibited a maximum absorption capacity of 61 mg/g at room temperature. The amine-functionalized adsorbent offers multiple adsorption mechanisms, including electrostatic interaction, hydrogen bonding, and π-π interaction, allowing effective DCFS adsorption across a broad pH range. This highlights its environmental adaptability, making it suitable for applications in complex environments.
