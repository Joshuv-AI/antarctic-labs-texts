A new formula for calculating normal tissue complication probability
Authors: Tingting Cao, Qingqing Yuan, Zhitao Dai
Published: 2024

ABSTRACT
AbstractPurposeTo facilitate the use of quantitative modeling of biological effects in treatment planning by introducing a simpler function equivalent to the Lyman formula for calculating normal tissue complication probability (NTCP).MethodsWe first provide an approximation of the Lyman‐Kutcher‐Burman (LKB) formula using three parameters (n, m, TD50) as a function of equivalent uniform dose (EUD). The parameters for the new formula are defined in terms of the Lyman model's m and TD50. Conversely, m and TD50 are expressed in terms of the parameters of the new equation. The role of the Lyman volume‐effect parameter n remains unchanged from its role in the Lyman model.ResultsThe new formula, which exhibits a sigmoidal shape, demonstrates symmetry about TD50, akin to the LKB model. The difference in NTCP between the two formulas is less than 0.1%. The parameters (n, m, TD50) are preserved through rigorous mathematical deduction and have been recalibrated to the tolerance data of Emani et al. using the proposed formula. This new model provides a better fit to these data than the model by Burman et al., which was fitted “by eye” rather than using statistical methods.ConclusionWe have developed a formula that represents NTCP as a function of EUD, which proves to be potentially useful. The parameters derived in this study are mathematically robust and offer a superior fit to the data compared to previous efforts. Additionally, the new model fits brain data as well as, if not better than, the LKB model.

INTRODUCTION

With the continuous advancement of radiotherapy technology, the precision of positioning, planning, and delivery in clinical practice has garnered increasing attention. In daily operations, organs surrounding the target often receive high‐dose partial irradiation to ensure the tumor area receives the necessary radiation dose, potentially leading to significant treatment‐related complications. To mitigate these risks, planning decisions may sometimes reduce the dose to part of the target, possibly compromising effective palliation or the likelihood of cure. Alternatively, some planners might opt for a balance by minimizing the partial‐volume irradiation of certain organs at risk (OARs) to intermediate and high doses at the expense of dose uniformity in target volumes. Thus, balancing the dose distribution between the target area and OARs is crucial for optimal radiotherapy planning. 1 , 2

To minimize damage to critical organs and improve planning outcomes, it is essential not only to consider the physical dosimetry criteria of OARs but also to incorporate biological effect criteria. Traditionally, a quantitative model has been employed to calculate complication probabilities for various OARs. Currently, the Lyman‐Kutcher‐Burman (LKB) model is widely used in the normal tissue complication probability (NTCP) models, originally proposed by Lyman and subsequently modified by Kutcher and Burman, using an effective volume method to address the dose‐volume effect and uniform irradiation. 3 , 4 , 5 However, the LKB model involves complex and extensive calculations due to its integral function, which is often overlooked in the planning process. Additionally, Schultheiss and Källman have proposed their respective NTCP models. While these models simplify the calculation process to some degree, their application remains limited due to the introduction of new model parameters with uncertain ranges. 6 , 7

Additionally, the clinical data for the LKB model is based on early conventional conformal radiotherapy. As technology has advanced, more sophisticated radiotherapy technologies have been integrated into clinical practice, such as three‐dimensional conformal radiation therapy (3DCRT), intensity modulated radiation therapy (IMRT), high‐dose fractionation, and proton radiotherapy. 8 . 9 The data initially used for model fitting may no longer be applicable to current clinical research, making it crucial to continuously update clinical data and model parameters. 10 , 11 It is noteworthy that researchers abroad have collected and revised some biological parameters of normal tissues. They have also integrated the Lyman model with the Linear Quadratic model and derived a simplified version of the NTCP model using α and β as model parameters. The results indicate that this model's fit is comparable to that of the LKB model, and the updated data have been used to refine the model parameters. 12

In order to facilitate the widespread use of the normal tissue complication probability model in radiotherapy planning and to simplify the calculation process, we propose a new NTCP calculation formula in this paper, based on the LKB model. This new formula simplifies the LKB model by utilizing an analytic function, while retaining the original model parameters, which can be rigorously deduced using strict mathematical methods.

LKB model

The LKB model is widely used in radiobiological modeling. 3 , 4 The Pinnacle planning system's optimization evaluation module employs this model to calculate the probability of complications. Lyman initially proposed a sigmoid dose‐response (SDR) integral model to describe the dose effect on healthy tissues, either as a whole or in parts, following uniform dose irradiation. Parameter TD50(1) represents the physical dose at which there is a 50% probability of complications to an organ when all volumes are irradiated. Parameter n is the volume effect parameter, and m is the slope factor that controls the NTCP effect curve. These three parameters were fitted based on the clinical data from conventional therapy (Burman et al., 1991) with a single dose of 1.8‐2 Gy. 5

Derivation of the new NTCP formula

The LKB model is characterized by: (a) an ‘S’‐shaped curve in its calculation formula; (b) a dose value at which 50% of complications occur, symmetrical around this point. Although these characteristics have not been directly verified, they do not contradict clinical data. The S‐shaped curve plays a crucial role in providing a basis for clinical complication data. The LKB model revolves around an integral function, similar to the Gauss error function, as depicted in equations (1) and (2).

Fitting result of the new formula

The fitting results for the LKB model and the proposed formula are shown in Figure 1, where the curves of the formulas are depicted on a linear scale. The curve characteristics of the new simplified formula presented in this paper are the same as those in the Lyman model: both exhibit S‐shaped curves. This indicates symmetry around the TD50 data. As depicted in Figure 1, the curve of the new formula nearly coincides with the curve of the LKB model (blue solid line) when ‐4 < x < 4. The variable ‘x’ serves the same purpose as ‘t’ in the LKB model.

Fitting results of the new formula with clinical data of the brain

Currently, the clinical data on normal tissue tolerance doses are primarily based on the research conducted by Emami et al., including data fitting for the LKB model. 5 In this study, we fitted the clinical data on brain tolerance (Data from TD50 and TD5) reported by Emami et al. using both the new formula and the LKB model. The results are displayed in Figure 2(A)‐(B). Figure 2(A) illustrates the function curve of NTCP and EUD. We observed that both the new formula (8) and the LKB model effectively fit the brain tolerance data. However, when the EUD value is less than 50 Gy, the fitting effectiveness of the new formula surpasses that of the LKB model, aligning more closely with the clinical data point around TD5 for the brain.

DISCUSSION

Currently, the LKB model is the most commonly used method to calculate the probability of complications in normal tissues, offering more complete data on model parameters than other models. Due to its integral form, the LKB model requires numerical integration and the associated error functions. In response, we propose a new NTCP formula with a simplified expression that can be computed using basic calculators. This simplification makes NTCP calculations more straightforward and less dependent on complex computational tools, thereby facilitating its use in various clinical and research settings. The new formula retains the original model parameters of the LKB model, including TD50, n, and m, making it applicable across multiple institutions.

First, when compared with the LKB model by fitting data, the new NTCP formula proposed in this study retains the basic characteristics of an S‐shaped curve and symmetry. The results also show that the accuracy of our model is very close to that of the LKB model. As shown in Figure 1, when x < ‐2, the curves of the two formulas diverge slightly, but the difference between the new formula and the LKB model is still less than 0.1%. Figure 2 indicates that the proposed formula also achieves better accuracy in fitting brain tolerance data (TD5 and TD50). Moreover, the fitting NTCP value of the new formula was closer to the clinical data when EUD = TD5, whereas the LKB model calculated a slightly lower value than the clinical data.

Second, the LKB model involves three parameters (TD50, m, n), with TD50 typically derived from clinical data. The proposed model simplifies the parameter estimation process by allowing for straightforward derivation of the parameter m from the other parameters. This not only reduces the complexity of fitting the model to data but also streamlines the process of model customization to specific clinical scenarios. Table 1 shows the fitting results of the parameters (TD50, m, n) for both the LKB and the simplified formula. The parameters we derived are very close to those obtained by Burman et al. However, our parameters provide an even better fit to clinical data, which we believe is a significant advancement.

In an ideal condition, normal tissues are irradiated uniformly, but in reality, the irradiation is non‐uniform. To address this, the LKB model has been adapted using equivalent volume and dose methods. 3 , 4 Niemierko 13 introduced the concept of EUD to replace dose D, which means the probability of injury complications caused by this dose can be approximately equal to that caused by the required non‐uniform dose distribution. In this paper, we also use EUD to replace the uniform dose D in the formula. Additionally, the α/β parameter of normal tissues has been incorporated into the EUD formula, reflecting the biological characteristics of the tissues. This is useful for calculating the biological indices of some unconventional radiotherapy plans, such as SBRT. 14 , 18 In calculating EUD, different fractionation schemes need to be converted into doses per fraction of 2 Gy for computation, known as EQD2, which is detailed in Equation (6). Currently, EQD2 calculations are mainly based on the Linear Quadratic (LQ) model. 15 It has been suggested that for single large‐fraction irradiation, the LQ model needs modification, although there is still no consensus on whether the LQ model should be modified for SBRT.

CONCLUSION

Based on the LKB model, a new simplified formula for NTCP has been proposed in our study. The primary aim of this simplified formula was to enhance the practicality and accessibility of NTCP calculations. Unlike the LKB model, which requires numerical integration due to its integral form and resulting error function, our model presents a simplified expression that can be computed with basic calculators, making the computation of NTCP more straightforward. The new formula, with its sigmoidal shape, indicates symmetry about TD50, similar to the LKB model. The difference in NTCP between the two formulas is less than 0.1%. The parameters obtained in this study are more mathematical and provide a better match to the data than previous work.
