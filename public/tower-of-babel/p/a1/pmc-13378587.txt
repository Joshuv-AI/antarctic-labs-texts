When trust building looks like surveillance: Public polarization toward Chicago policing
Authors: Tony Cheng, Shelley Liu
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
Public debate about policing highlights competing views about whether community initiatives build trust or reinforce surveillance. We argue that perceptions depend on prior state orientations, which can polarize opinions across groups. Using a preregistered survey experiment in Chicago, we examined responses to the police department’s “Sentiment Dashboard,” a project designed to enhance legitimacy by collecting community feedback. Results show that the dashboard increased trust in police among non-Black respondents but reduced trust among Black respondents, especially when framed as monitoring. Among non-Black respondents, dashboard exposure also reduced support for the Black Lives Matter movement and racial equity policies unrelated to policing, such as diverse hiring and climate justice. Mechanism testing with qualitative evidence suggests that treated non-Black participants were more likely to regard police reforms as substitutes for racial equity policies, rendering further measures unnecessary. These findings reveal how state legitimacy initiatives may instead widen racial divides and weaken coalitions for structural reform.

INTRODUCTION

Policing in the United States is at the center of some of the most urgent debates about race, democracy, and public safety (1–4). In the wake of the 2020 Black Lives Matter (BLM) protests, among the largest social movements in US history, cities across the country adopted initiatives intended to demonstrate responsiveness to community concerns. A National Academies report specifically recommended the collection of “omnibus surveys that would provide data on the views of resident safety” (5). Cities have responded by investing in the collection and public reporting of resident sentiment data, designed to provide transparency about how different groups perceive policing. These efforts reflect a broader turn toward “data-driven” governance (6), in which surveys and dashboards promise to show how the state is seen by its constituents.

However, what it means for the police to be “legitimate” is not straightforward (7). On the one hand, legitimacy refers to the willingness of residents to view state authority as rightful, motivating voluntary compliance (8). By this definition, initiatives such as public sentiment surveys should enhance legitimacy by signaling accountability and responsiveness. On the other hand, these same initiatives can also represent projects of legibility: state efforts to build governing capacity by collecting data that renders its population visible and classifiable (9, 10). Legibility projects are often necessary for good governance and service provision and are therefore not inherently coercive. However, especially in the context of policing—a coercive institution—legibility can be experienced as surveillance, which we define as the downstream use or lived interpretation of data collection as monitoring, targeting, or social control (11, 12). From this perspective, police department-led surveys and dashboards risk being interpreted not as trust-building efforts but as extensions of state surveillance.

The coexistence of these two interpretations of state projects raises a central question: When confronted with policing initiatives framed as trust building, do residents perceive them as legitimacy-enhancing or as unwanted surveillance? Existing scholarship evaluating legitimacy-based policing reforms focuses on whether interventions such as procedural justice training, community policing, or feedback mechanisms increase trust in the police (8, 13–15). These works have found mixed and short-term effects and have typically focused on average treatment effects or on whether initiatives improve trust among marginalized communities. Less well understood is whether legitimacy initiatives reduce racial divides in perceptions of policing (16) or instead reproduce or widen them especially at scale. Moreover, existing studies rarely situate legitimacy reforms within broader state projects of legibility and surveillance, despite the growing reliance on data-driven tools to demonstrate accountability. By examining how the same initiative is interpreted across racial groups, this study treats legitimacy as a relational and perceptual process, rather than a uniform institutional outcome that is interpreted similarly across the public.

We argue that perceptions and responses to state projects depend not only on their formal design, but also on residents’ prior orientations toward the state. Populations with long histories and ongoing experiences of discrimination and surveillance may see “trust-building” measures as yet another extension of state control, while others interpret them as evidence of progress. These divergent perceptions can polarize attitudes, shaping not only views of policing itself, but also support for broader racial equity policies.

To evaluate our argument, we examine a Chicago Police Department (CPD) initiative launched in 2017, wherein they partnered with a private survey firm, Elucd (now Zencity), to collect monthly data on resident trust and safety perceptions. In 2020, the CPD further began publishing these results in an online “Sentiment Dashboard” with the intention of increasing transparency by allowing residents to see how opinions varied across time, neighborhoods, and demographic groups. These efforts are not distinct to Chicago. Across the United States and increasingly internationally, cities are contracting with private survey firms to engage with residents on issues ranging from vaccine hesitancy to housing development. Police departments have begun enlisting these services too. Every month, the private survey firm Elucd conducts anonymous and representative surveys through digital advertisements across hundreds of cities from Los Angeles to Grand Rapids, gauging shifts in resident trust toward the police and their safety perceptions within their community. While these initiatives are aimed at bolstering legitimacy through responsiveness and openness, they also bear the hallmarks of legibility: data collection, categorization, and monitoring of community sentiment.

We test how residents interpreted this initiative through an original preregistered online survey administered to a Qualtrics panel of 1083 respondents living in the Chicago metropolitan area. We embedded an experimental component within the survey that randomly assigned a portion of respondents to view the Sentiment Dashboard. Within those that were randomly assigned to view the Dashboard, respondents were further randomly assigned to receive either a brief description of the Elucd surveys as part of (i) a broader initiative to enhance police-community relations (legitimation frame) or (ii) a data collection effort to monitor which neighborhoods should receive more police attention (legibility frame).

Findings reveal fundamental asymmetries across racial divides. First, we find that treated non-Black respondents reported greater trust in police upon learning about the Elucd surveys—regardless of whether the treatment they received was framed as a method of eliciting public input to strengthen community relations or to monitor which neighborhoods should receive more police attention. In contrast, treated Black respondents viewed the Elucd dashboards negatively, reporting substantially reduced trust upon seeing either treatment. Further, our results suggest that Black respondents may have been more sensitive to which frame they received. These patterns indicate that non-Black participants interpreted the Elucd dashboards as a positive police initiative that enhanced legitimacy, whereas Black participants interpreted them as a negative initiative that reduced legitimacy.

Second, these divergences by race also spilled over into broader attitudes toward racial equity policies in asymmetric ways. Treatment not only increased non-Black respondents’ trust in the police—it also decreased support for racial equity policies, such as incentivizing diverse hiring and funding climate justice programs, and support for the BLM movement. On the contrary, we observe no similar effects for Black respondents. Exploratory qualitative evidence from an open-ended question inviting respondents to reflect on what they recalled about the protests provide insights into a mechanism that can explain this divergence. We show that treated non-Black respondents were substantially less likely to remember the protests positively and to reflect upon the causes of the protest (i.e., racial justice). Instead, treatment increased the likelihood that participants wrote about negative protester conduct and the economic damages caused by the protests. These qualitative reflections are consistent with our interpretation that non-Black respondents were more likely to view racial equity policies as unnecessary after having learned about the Sentiment Dashboard, which had a legitimacy-enhancing effect on them.

Our study shows that efforts to build police legitimacy can produce sharply divergent outcomes, deepening trust among some while intensifying distrust among others (17–19). These divides extend beyond policing itself, shaping support for racial equity policies and the capacity of social movements such as BLM to build the coalitions necessary to press for systemic change (20–22).

Police-community initiatives as state projects

States with high capacity and effective governance routinely carry out legibility and legitimacy projects to exercise and sustain power across the state (23–26). Legibility projects classify, monitor, and render populations visible, not only enabling governance and the provision of public goods, but also deepening state capacity for surveillance and control (10, 27, 28). Legitimacy projects, in contrast, aim to build trust and encourage compliance by signaling accountability and responsiveness (8). These categories are not only analytic but also perceptual (7). What appears as a legitimacy effort from the perspective of state actors may be perceived as legibility by those with long histories of harm and distrust (29, 30).

This divergence creates unequal outcomes. Scholarship on policy feedback shows how policies shape politics by creating new constituencies, organizational alliances, and broader motivations and preferences (31, 32). New state programs or actions are undertaken in the context of these dynamics, layered atop historical grievances, fears toward the state, and preexisting entitlements to public benefits (21, 33).

Maximizing compliance and cooperation with state initiatives requires aligning public perceptions with the state’s intent (7), but because perceptions vary across groups, the state faces distinct challenges with different constituencies. For populations already inclined to trust the state, new initiatives may simply present a clarity problem, which requires communication to make their positive intent legible (34). For communities with longstanding grievances, however, the issue is one of commitment: New initiatives are interpreted as further evidence of state neglect or betrayal, regardless of messaging (35). These dynamics are particularly evident within the context of race, where different groups have been unequally subjected to social, economic, and legal hurdles both over time and across space, continuing into the present day (36–40). The result is uneven legitimacy—reinforced among those already trusting and diminished among those already skeptical.

These tensions are visible in the case of policing, one of the most consequential sites of state and society interaction. The 2020 BLM protests reflected deep dissatisfaction with racialized policing and sharp divides over its future. In “race-class subjugated communities” (4), where legibility has long taken the form of surveillance and coercion, distrust of the police runs deep (17). This dissatisfaction shapes whether individuals interpret police presence as protection, threat, or both (39–42).

Recognizing these divides, policing stakeholders have invested heavily in projects meant to legitimate the police. Community meetings, advisory councils, and neighborhood foot patrols are explicitly framed as trust building (43–46), while focused deterrence programs and citizen review boards are similarly supposed to signal responsiveness (47, 48). However, experimental evidence analyzing the impact of community-based interventions on trust and legitimacy generally reports modest, uneven, or short-lived effects (13, 15). Recent qualitative scholarship has provided complementary insights into how entrenched power asymmetries, limited civilian oversight, and organizational resistance constrain the ability of these community efforts to produce sustained trust among Black and Brown residents (16, 44). Since the public ultimately decides how these efforts are received, these initiatives are likely to unfold with the same problems of perception as highlighted above. Non-Black residents, more likely to have experienced police primarily as a protective institution and thus have greater trust in it, are more likely to interpret community-oriented initiatives as evidence of state responsiveness. Black residents, who are likely to exhibit lower trust rooted in cynicism (49) and estrangement (50), are more likely to interpret these same initiatives, even ostensibly trust-building initiatives as extensions of coercion. Even when policing strategies are envisioned and designed to be therapeutic, they can nevertheless be perceived as penal, prompting its target population to resist and avoid police interactions (51). The very same initiative can thus simultaneously bolster legitimacy among one group while deepening alienation among another.

We further hypothesize that these racially divergent perceptions have downstream consequences. Specifically, they may extend beyond policing to shape attitudes toward racial equity policies. Because policing is closely tied to broader struggles over race and justice (20, 52), perceptions of police initiatives—and subsequent changes in perceived trust and legitimacy—are likely to spill over into adjacent racialized policy contexts, such as the removal of Confederate statues (53) or the cancelation of student debt (54). For non-Black residents, who are more inclined to view legitimacy projects positively, these police initiatives increase police legitimacy and may act as substitutes for broader policies. If so, then this may correspondingly lower support for movements such as BLM or for policies addressing structural inequality. For Black residents, who remain distrustful, policing and racial equity policies are viewed as complementary: Dissatisfaction with policing sustains support for broader structural change.

Survey experiment and the case of Chicago

Chicago has long been a focal point for understanding the intersection of racial, spatial, and urban inequalities (55). Controversies around policing stretch from the Red Summer of 1919 to the 2020 BLM protests, punctuated by high-profile cases such as the killings of Rekia Boyd and Laquan McDonald, revelations of the Homan Square facility, and a Department of Justice investigation that led to a 2019 consent decree (52, 56, 57). In response, the CPD has introduced various community policing initiatives and models of civilian oversight aimed at rebuilding public trust (46, 58).

As part of these efforts, the CPD partnered with a private firm, Elucd (now Zencity), to conduct monthly cell phone surveys beginning in 2017. These surveys asked residents about trust in police and perceptions of neighborhood safety. In 2020, amid citywide protests following the murder of George Floyd, the CPD launched a public-facing Sentiment Dashboard that aggregated these data by district, area, and demographic categories.

The Sentiment Dashboard was designed to enhance legitimacy. CPD Commander Angel Novalez of the community policing office described the sentiment data as giving “the department an idea about whether those concerns (in the community) align ‘with our efforts’” (59). The CPD’s intent was to improve police legitimacy in two ways: by inviting residents into a feedback loop on policing and by demonstrating openness through data transparency.

However, we argue that projects framed as legitimacy-enhancing may be interpreted differently across groups. For residents already skeptical of the police, the surveys and dashboard can appear as tools of surveillance and control rather than responsiveness. These divergent interpretations have consequences not only for levels of police trust and engagement, but also for attitudes toward broader racial equity policies.

To test this, we administered an online survey to 1083 respondents living in and around the Chicago metropolitan area. We chose this sample because we are interested in perceptions toward the police as a politically salient state institution and the CPD serves as the primary symbolic and informational reference point in regional discourse surrounding policing (see Supplementary Text for sample recruitment details). Survey respondents were recruited through a Qualtrics panel and were screened based on their age (18 or older) and location (see tables S3 and S4 for full summary statistics). To recruit participants for our survey, we sought specifically to balance by race according to the 2020 Chicago census due to the topic of our research; however, to minimize convenience sample concerns, our ideal target population should broadly match Chicago’s general population across different demographic variables. To that end, we yielded a final sample that was reflective of Chicago demographics along age brackets, race and ethnicity categories, education, and marital status (see table S5). We highlight two imbalances, which may be indicative of the survey-taking population in comparison to the general Chicago population: Our sample is more female (63.7% in our sample versus 51.2% in the population) and is somewhat more likely to have a high school diploma or greater (97.9% in our sample versus 86.6% in the population).

We deployed our survey between November 2023 and January 2024, well after the height of the 2020 BLM protests. The survey sought to achieve three goals: (i) to understand underlying views toward the CPD across different socioeconomic backgrounds and demographics; (ii) to identify how a police-community relations initiative meant to be legitimacy-enhancing—as ongoing citizen surveys to better tailor police efforts—may be differentially perceived by the public; and (iii) to explore the relationship between perceptions of the police and views toward racial equity policies.

Within our survey, we embedded an experimental component wherein we randomly assigned a subset of respondents to learn about the Elucd surveys by introducing them to the Sentiment Dashboard. These treated respondents who learned about the Elucd surveys were further randomized to receive one of two different ways of framing the information about the dashboard, which is fully reproduced in fig. S1. Under the legitimation framing, respondents were first informed about the ongoing data collection efforts by the CPD. They were then told that these surveys help the police “to be responsive to the different concerns people have and help inform their initiatives for strengthening police-community relationships.” Then, respondents were presented with a screenshot of the Dashboard along with a link to see the live Dashboard on the CPD website. Under the legibility framing, participants received much of the same information; however, instead of being reminded that the data are used to strengthen police-community relationships, respondents were informed that the surveys “allow the CPD to monitor and track which neighborhoods should receive more police attention” based on different group characteristics. The legitimation and legibility frames were designed so that they could be plausibly interpretable in multiple ways, reflecting the empirical reality that state initiatives often simultaneously signal responsiveness and monitoring to different publics.

To ensure proper comparison given potential accuracy or attention effects, we controlled for the effects of time spent simply viewing any dashboard at all. The pure control group therefore received a dashboard of similar length from the events platform EventBrite, along with information about ongoing events in Chicago that are hosted by companies and nonprofits. Control treatment conditions are available in fig. S2.

Main survey experimental outcomes

We explore the following sets of preregistered outcomes and provide the full wording of the questions in the Supplementary Materials. Table S2 summarizes our prespecified directional hypotheses for the main survey outcomes and indicates whether each is supported in the data. We provide the raw means of all outcomes, grouped according to treatment conditions and by race, in figs. S3 and S4.

First, if the Elucd surveys as a police-community initiative did successfully shift respondents’ views toward the CPD (either positively or negatively), then their primary effect should be on treated respondents’ perceptions of the police (Hypothesis 1). In the survey, we ask respondents about their degree of trust in a series of American institutions, which is defined on a Likert scale from 1 to 5. Within this battery of questions, our main outcome of interest is trust in the police. To minimize potential demand effects, this primary outcome was not presented immediately after the survey experiment’s treatment conditions.

Second, we examine treatment effects on support for calls for racial equity policies (Hypothesis 2). To estimate these effects, we ask respondents about support for the BLM movement’s (i) overall goals and (ii) their protests. We combine these into a BLM support index, calculated as the predicted values from the first factor of a principal factor analysis. Notably, while prior work shows that BLM support increases when respondents are presented with its specific policy components (60, 61), we aim to measure orientations toward BLM’s goals as the movement is encountered in public discourse, rather than respondents’ familiarity with the movement’s formal policy positions. In addition to support for the BLM movement, we also ask respondents whether they support a series of commonly proposed racial equity policies, including (i) reparations to descendants of former slaves; (ii) increased funding for services in minority communities; (iii) incentives for diverse hiring; (iv) student debt forgiveness; (v) climate hazards funding in minority communities; (vi) removal of racist statues and symbols; and (vii) field trips to the DuSable Black History Museum. Of these outcomes, we consider proposals (i) to (v) as economic policies—meaning that they require additional taxpayer funding or otherwise may be perceived to affect economic outcomes for non-recipients—and (vi) and (vii) as cultural history policies that do not monetarily affect the broader Chicago population.

Open-ended response outcomes

To unpack spillover in support for racial equity policies, we sought to better explain the hypothesized negative treatment effects on support for the BLM movement among non-Black participants. While our survey question asked respondents to indicate their level of support for BLM, it told us less about why increased trust in the police (among non-Black participants) linked to reduced support for the BLM protests. Thus, at the end of the survey, we collected qualitative data on sentiments toward the BLM protests using a free-response question: “In a few sentences, tell us how you remember the 2020 BLM protests.” This broad prompt complemented the survey’s closed-ended questions by capturing the “restlessness” (62) of a historical event such as the 2020 BLM protests. We followed best practices in interviewing techniques by asking respondents how they remembered the events, instead of a more direct question about their conclusions or evaluations about them (63), to elicit richer responses with less social desirability bias that we could then (i) code for their underlying sentiments and (ii) compare to their responses to other survey questions. Our survey was also administered 3 years after the 2020 BLM protests, which further decreases the likelihood of social desirability bias that may appear in open-ended responses offered closer to the height of the protests.

We inductively coded respondents’ comments on the BLM protests (64). We first separately read through all participants’ comments for sensitization to their variety in tone and content. We then agreed upon a pilot codebook and independently coded a random sample of 25 comments. We discussed discrepancies and codes that should be added or amended. For instance, we decided to add a code to capture the emotional toll of the protests that some respondents expressed. After agreeing upon a working codebook, one author coded the remaining comments while noting edge cases for discussion. Last, we grouped the codes into broader categories that summarized the aspects of the protests that respondents commented upon.

This procedure yielded codes across three overarching categories. First, we operationalized respondents’ overall sentiments toward the BLM protests by aggregating their positive (+1) and negative (−1) scores. Thus, a respondent who exhibited both positive and negative sentiments would receive a value of 0—i.e., balanced in the aggregate—while respondents expressing only a positive or only a negative sentiment received a value of 1 or −1. Second, we categorized protest characteristics that respondents mentioned as concerning either protest causes or negative protester conduct. Variables falling within the causes category focused on police violence, race, and concerns about justice and equality. Variables falling within the conduct category, in contrast, focused on economic damages, violence, and emotional fear toward the protests. For both causes and conduct, we coded each variable as 1 if any of their subcomponents were positive, and 0 otherwise.

We note that this coding procedure was not preregistered in our preanalysis plan, and, thus, our analyses should be considered exploratory. Because we could not know ex ante which themes would emerge or how respondents would frame their recollections, we treat this section as inductive and intended to unpack potential mechanisms for our main findings above rather than as confirmatory tests.

Estimation

Our treatment assignment (table S1) allows us to estimate effects using a pooled definition of treatment T, combining T1 and T2 to estimate the effect of learning about the Elucd surveys through the Sentiment Dashboard. We also define treatment by estimating the effects of T1 and T2 separately to identify whether framing effects had differential impacts on Black and non-Black participants (65, 66). We estimate a linear regression with robust standard errors, where respondents’ responses are regressed on their randomly assigned treatment status. To estimate differential effects across Black and non-Black populations as preregistered in our PAP, we further interact treatment status with an indicator for whether the participant selected “Black/African American” as at least one of their self-identified racial categories.

Because our experiment is randomized at the individual level, controls are not necessary to identify a causal effect conditional on balance between treatment and control groups. In table S6, we show that our randomization procedure yielded balanced samples across a battery of pretreatment demographic, social, and economic characteristics. Nevertheless, in our main specifications, we control for individual-level covariates, including race/ethnicity, gender, age, whether the individual lives in Chicago or in a suburb, and their employment status. In tables S7 and S8, we show that our results are robust to both the exclusion of these controls and the inclusion of additional controls.

Last, where we preregistered directional hypotheses in our PAP, we specified one-tailed tests. For consistency and transparency across tables, however, we report two-tailed tests in the manuscript, while preserving the preregistered directionality in our interpretation of results (see table S2 for a summary of prespecified hypotheses).

RESULTS

Our argument and hypotheses are built on the expectation that Black survey participants are, at the outset, already the least trusting of the police. This low trust is clearly evidenced in the data published on the CPD’s Sentiment Dashboard, which was collected by Elucd. Between 2017 and 2022, surveyed Black respondents in Chicago averaged a trust score of 51.51 on a scale of 100, in comparison to a score of 66.38 among white respondents and 59.75 among Hispanic respondents (67). The difference between Black and non-Black respondents’ trust scores is statistically significant (P < 0.00).

Within our survey sample, we show additional evidence consistent with this expectation in Fig. 1. Because the trust question was asked as a treatment outcome, we restrict the sample to only respondents who did not see the Sentiment Dashboard treatments. Absent both pre- and posttreatment measures of trust, this “second-best” strategy’s validity relies on balance between treatment and control groups, which we confirm in table S6. The bar plot (Fig. 1) indicates that race was the most significant predictor of trust in the police, and, in particular, Black respondents exhibited substantially lower trust than any other racial or ethnic group. Figure 1 also shows a lesser level of trust among Hispanics in our sample. In tables S9 and S10, we therefore consider an alternative specification that separately estimates effects on Hispanic participants. In tables S7 and S8, we also include a robustness check where we drop Hispanic participants from our sample and re-estimate our main regressions.

Our findings are consistent with Hypothesis 1. First, across the full sample of survey respondents, seeing the Sentiment Dashboard and learning information about the Elucd surveys did not shift the full sample of respondents’ trust in police. The coefficient in column 1 is statistically insignificant, and the magnitude is close to zero, suggesting a precisely estimated null effect. However, results in column 2 show that the full sample results mask important heterogeneity within the population. While among non-Black respondents, there is a positive and significant treatment effect on trust in the police, among treated Black respondents, seeing the Sentiment Dashboard led to a substantial decrease in trust. While Black respondents in the control group were already less trusting of the police, receiving the treatment substantially increased distrust.

Columns 3 and 4 then investigate sensitivity to framing effects. Consistently, we find that treatment effects on the full sample masked important heterogeneity (column 3). Specifically, neither frame showed a meaningful effect on non-Black respondents: Both legitimation and legibility frames increased trust in the police, and the coefficients are substantively similar in magnitude. These results are consistent with the legitimizing nature of legibility projects among populations who are already broadly supportive of a state institution. For Black participants, in contrast, dashboard framing produced effects that were different in magnitude. Both the legitimation and legibility frames clearly reduced trust in the police, although the coefficient for the legitimacy frame is not statistically significant (P < 0.12). The legibility frame notably resulted in a statistically significant and a larger negative coefficient on trust in the police compared to both the control group and those who received the legitimation frame—reflecting the salience of potential surveillance to Black participants in particular. Despite the substantive difference in statistical significance and coefficient magnitude (0.2 units or 0.17 SDs), however, we are cautious in interpreting these differences, as t tests between the legitimation and legibility frame coefficients are not statistically significant (P < 0.31). Thus, while we cannot conclude that Black participants were more sensitive to framing rather than reacting to the Dashboards as a whole, these results provide suggestive evidence that Black participants may have been more responsive to the specific framing of the dashboards rather than to the dashboard treatment per se.

These results together are consistent with our argument that state legitimacy projects targeted toward improving citizen trust are unlikely to be perceived positively by a population with which it has had historically poor relations.

Spillover effects on support for racial equity policies

Next, we examine how our treatments shifted respondents’ attitudes toward racial equity policies (Hypothesis 2). The Elucd surveys, meant as a police-community relations initiative amidst protest against police violence, may affect downstream attitudes toward the BLM movement and racial equity policies. Among non-Black respondents, we expect that the Sentiment Dashboards’ legitimacy-enhancing effects would lead respondents to be mollified by perceived state efforts toward reform and thus subdue support for further policies. We expect no such similar reduction in support for racial equity policies for Black participants, for whom the Elucd surveys failed to increase positive perceptions toward the police.

In Table 2, we present results for the effects of seeing the Sentiment Dashboard, pooling together the two treatment frames, on support for racial equity. In column 1, we find that respondents were significantly less likely to support the BLM movement’s overall goals and the protests. However, as we see in column 2, the negative effects on support for racial equity are driven by non-Black respondents, for whom exposure to the Dashboard reduces support for the BLM movement’s goals and protests. In contrast, the interaction term for Black respondents is nearly equal in magnitude and opposite in sign to the main effect, implying an effect close to zero among Black respondents. Thus, the pattern in Table 2 reflects a reduction in support among non-Black respondents alongside stability in attitudes among Black respondents.

Alternative specifications for estimating survey outcomes

We consider alternative specifications as robustness checks (tables S7 and S8). Across these five specifications, our results remain statistically significant and similar in magnitude. First, while our preferred specification includes a set of demographic controls to increase statistical power, we also show that results remain unchanged if we do not include control variables in the regressions. Second, we consider an additional set of controls, including whether the individual has ties to law enforcement or the government and their political party affinity—as these variables are likely to be strong predictors of ex-ante trust in the state as well as support for the BLM movement and racial equity policies. Third, to account for potential spatial autocorrelation within geographical areas, we estimate results with standard errors clustered by respondents’ zip code. Fourth, as a small portion of our sample is affiliated with law enforcement and may therefore respond differently to the treatments, we drop them from the sample. Fifth, to account for potential confounding effects from Hispanic respondents who make up a substantial portion of the non-Black sample, we drop them from our sample and show that our results remain unchanged.

Beyond these robustness checks, we also re-estimate our heterogeneous effects models by separating out Hispanic respondents from the non-Black respondents. On the one hand, we may expect Hispanic respondents to respond to police-community initiatives with suspicion. Scholarship has documented similar experiences with racial profiling and other forms of marginalization among Latino and Black populations by the police (68), and Fig. 1 indicates that Hispanic respondents were also somewhat less trusting of the police than white respondents at the outset. On the other hand, issues relating to policing and police-community relations may be more tightly linked to the BLM movement and Black racial justice, such that the police as a state institution may be less politically salient for some Hispanic populations. Consistent with this orientation, studies find that Latino populations often engage in behaviors more commonly associated with white populations—for example, Latino youth in Chicago’s Little Village report high willingness to cooperate with police (69)—while also exhibiting substantial internal heterogeneity (70), including variation in views toward the BLM movement (71). In line with the latter, we show in tables S9 and S10 that Hispanic respondents responded similarly to our treatment conditions as the white respondents across both sets of relevant outcomes (trust in the police and support for racial equity policies). Hispanic respondents also were not especially sensitive to whether the Sentiment Dashboard treatments were framed as a valuable police-community relations tool (legitimation) or as an avenue for greater police monitoring (legibility).

Open-ended responses: Explaining shifts in support for racial equity

Our main survey experimental results have thus far shown evidence for our hypotheses: (i) Viewing the Sentiment Dashboard increased trust in the police among non-Black participants, but decreased trust among Black participants; and (ii) it also reduced support for racial equity policies for non-Black participants only. We argue that this reduced support for racial equity policies is because non-Black participants are more likely to view policing reform and racial equity as substitutes rather than as complements.

To unpack this argument further and show more explicit evidence of this mechanism, we undertake exploratory analyses with our final open-ended response question, which asked participants to reflect on their recollection of the BLM protests in 2020 (which occurred 3 years before our survey). As explained in data and methods above, open-ended responses were coded based on three categories: (i) general positive and/or negative sentiment; (ii) whether they discuss protest causes, including policing, race, or broader justice; and (iii) whether they mention a range of negative protest conduct, such as economic damages, violent behavior, or general fear. Mechanism testing: Policing reform and racial reform as substitutes

Our proposed mechanism is that policing reform is seen as a substitute for racial reform for non-Black participants only. Because the open-ended responses allow us to code multiple protest cause categories within a single participant’s response, this gives us the opportunity to examine whether and how respondents were more likely to bundle multiple protest causes together within their reflections. We expect that, in contrast to Black respondents, non-Black participants are more likely to bundle issues of policing with issues of race and justice.

In Table 4, we examine whether and which different types of protest causes were more likely to be mentioned within the same comment. We find that our treatment, viewing the Sentiment Dashboard, substantially shifted non-Black participants’ associations between protest causes. Second, even as non-Black respondents were ex-ante more likely to focus on conduct rather than cause in comparison to Black respondents, treatment further widened this gap as well (Fig. 2B). Estimated treatment effects show that non-Black respondents’ responses reflected a clear shift away from associating the BLM protests with the need for systemic change and racial equity. Participants who viewed the Sentiment Dashboards were overall 8.6% less likely to mention protest causes in their recollections, and this shift was most clearly driven by reduced likelihood of mentioning police violence (−5.5%) and of race (−5.7%); mentions of broader justice and equality also reduced slightly (−3.7%), although this variable was not statistically significant. Last, instead of discussing the causes of protests, Fig. 2C shows that non-Black respondents became 7.0% more likely to complain about protester conduct in their written descriptions of the BLM protests. While the variable’s individual subcomponents—economic consequences, violent behavior, and fear during the protests—were not statistically significant, the overall conduct variable is statistically significant at the 90% confidence level. This suggests that respondents were likely to mention one of the categories in their comments, but not necessarily all three in the same comment, which is expected as respondents wrote only a few sentences.

DISCUSSION

For decades, scholars and policymakers have sought ways to strengthen police legitimacy (15, 72). The central idea is that greater legitimacy encourages compliance, cooperation, and trust between communities and law enforcement (8). To increase legitimacy, local cities and police departments have introduced initiatives such as community meetings, advisory councils, and resident surveys (46). A core assumption has been that, even if imperfect, these efforts will at least improve perceptions among those with lower trust.

Our findings challenge this assumption. We present causal evidence from a survey experiment to explore how differential perceptions of state projects may subsequently affect people’s sentiments about the state institution in question and the policymaking politics surrounding it. We focus on a prominent police-community initiative that cities across America have adopted: a monthly anonymous survey to gauge residents’ trust and safety perceptions, with results subsequently published online for data transparency. While the initiative was meant to legitimize the CPD during a period of community uprisings, we argue that people were unlikely to uniformly perceive it as such. In our experiment, we informed and differentially framed the department’s Sentiment Dashboard to a representative sample of Chicago residents by emphasizing either police-community relations (legitimation) or police monitoring (legibility).

We find that treatment increased non-Black respondents’ trust in the police and decreased support for racial equity policies regardless of the framing, interpreting the Elucd survey initiative positively as the CPD had intended. In contrast, Black respondents expressed greater distrust of the police overall, but especially so when the initiative was framed as police monitoring, and did not shift their views toward racial equity policies. These perceptions also influenced how respondents recalled the BLM protests, with non-Black individuals viewing the protests more negatively after learning about the sentiment surveys. Our study thus provides empirical support for legitimacy as a relational process—one that not only varies across audiences (7) but is also dynamically updated through ongoing experiences and interactions (73). Focusing on the case of policing, we underscore the racial divides in perceptions of police initiatives and their divergent effects on attitudes toward racial equity.

Our findings also speak to the broader literature on the politics of policing, which emphasizes the dual role of the police as providers of protection and agents of state coercion (17, 18, 74). Initiatives such as the Sentiment Dashboard exemplify this tension. While designed as trust-building measures, they can also be interpreted as surveillance, particularly by disadvantaged groups who are typically cited to justify investment into these initiatives (75). This duality means that legitimacy projects carry risks: Rather than simply failing to increase trust, they can actively undermine it among the populations already most affected by policing.

The asymmetric spillover effects beyond policing are especially noteworthy. Our study contributes to understandings of race and policymaking by examining how policing, as a “racialized organization” (38), shapes racial equity beyond its own domain. Building on research showing that police policies and actions affect the agency of racial groups (3, 76, 77), our experimental evidence shows that a police-initiated policy produced divergent effects on broader equity issues. Specifically, for non-Black respondents, the initiative substituted for broader policies. Evidence of responsiveness in policing reduced the perceived need for racial equity in adjacent contexts, such as incentivizing diverse hiring and funding climate justice programs. For Black respondents, no such substitution occurred; policing and racial equity policies remained complementary. This asymmetry highlights how state projects can fracture solidarity across groups, legitimating the status quo for some, while sustaining demands for change among others.

These findings also suggest an important counterfactual question: Would the Sentiment Dashboards have shifted support for racial equity policies had the 2020 protests not occurred? While our survey cannot definitively answer this, we note that policing and racial inequality are deeply intertwined in US politics and major incidents of police violence (in Chicago and beyond) before and after 2020 continually reinforce this association. That non-Black respondents recalled the protests negatively even 3 years later highlights the lasting salience of these events and the durable linkage between policing and broader racial equity attitudes. Thus, even absent the 2020 protests specifically, we anticipate that legitimacy-building initiatives such as the Sentiment Dashboard would continue to operate through similar perceptual mechanisms, albeit potentially with different historical reference points.

The temporal dynamics of legitimacy initiatives also warrant careful consideration. Prior research suggests that the effects of legitimacy-based interventions on perceptions of police may be short-lived and situationally dependent. For example, a prominent field experiment on community policing finds that positive nonenforcement contact increased police legitimacy but that these effects persisted for ∼21 days, underscoring both the potential and the limits of single-shot interventions (15). However, in practice, legitimacy-oriented reforms are rarely singular events. Instead, they are often continuously rolled out, layered, and reiterated over time. Future research should therefore examine not only the persistence of any single legitimacy intervention, but also the compounding effects of repeated or overlapping legitimacy projects, which may cumulatively shape more durable beliefs and perceptions about the state. The initiative studied here differs from those examined in prior work insofar as residents can be repeatedly exposed to it through multiple channels: participating in ongoing surveys, encountering the dashboard while seeking information, or through CPD communications that publicize findings. This repeated exposure may reinforce initial interpretations or repeatedly reactivate them over time. While we cannot directly test these dynamics in the present study, our findings suggest that legitimacy initiatives can shape interpretive baselines and political memory, underscoring the importance of accounting for cumulative exposure in future research.

These findings offer timely policy insights. Transparency efforts and data dashboards are increasingly adopted across policy domains from education (78), public health (79), and especially in criminal justice (5). Our results emphasize that legitimacy is relational and uneven, and initiatives seeking to build legitimacy may reassure already-trusting publics, but simultaneously alienate those most affected by policing. Cities and police departments should recognize that dashboards are conditional tools whose effectiveness is contingent on whether they reflect and are paired with tangible institutional change. At minimum, they can be designed and deployed with additional accountability mechanisms, consistent with evidence that transparency enhances institutional legitimacy when paired with credible commitments to action (80). For instance, these initiatives can be instituted with (i) specific information about how the results will inform police actions and on what timeline and (ii) departmental accountability metrics, such as uses of force or changes in stop practices that can be evaluated alongside shifts in sentiment. More broadly, initiatives that aim to build legitimacy must not be framed—or evaluated—by their intent or visibility alone, but by whether they produce perceptible improvements in the experiences of those most affected by policing. These institutional changes likely include changes to enforcement practices that are directly observable in residents’ lived experiences, such as uses of force and stop practices. By recognizing these trade-offs, policymakers can better avoid overclaiming what data-driven transparency can deliver and instead situate these initiatives within a broader strategy focused on substantive change.

This study’s limitations highlight valuable directions for future research. While the experimental design supports strong internal validity through random assignment, the study relies on a nonprobability survey sample and therefore has limited external validity. Our goal is not population-level estimation, but causal inference about how individuals interpret a policing initiative when exposed to it; nonetheless, future work using probability samples or field-based designs would strengthen generalizability. Future work can extend the causal insights from this study to population-level estimates using probability sampling or weighting strategies to examine whether descriptive patterns in responses to legitimacy initiatives differ, for instance, by gender or educational attainment. These studies can also productively compare department-specific and generalized trust measures to assess how responses to legitimacy initiatives vary with institutional scope and proximity. Last, our open-ended question on respondents’ recollections of the 2020 BLM protests was intentionally broad to reduce social desirability bias and avoid priming respondents toward particular evaluations. This design choice trades precision for interpretive openness, allowing respondents to surface what they viewed as most salient—whether protest causes, conduct, or emotional impact—which is central to our mechanism testing. Because this qualitative component was not preregistered, however, we interpret it as exploratory and mechanism-generating. Future research could build on this approach through more targeted qualitative interviews, more precise survey measurement strategies, or longitudinal designs to further validate and refine the mechanisms we identify. While our findings are consistent with both historically rooted distrust and the influence of contemporary lived experiences, future work can more directly disentangle these mechanisms to examine how changes in policing practices shape perceptions of legitimacy over time.

Our findings overall emphasize that state initiatives are not neutral interventions and point to the need for caution in designing legitimacy initiatives. Projects such as the Sentiment Dashboard are not substitutes for substantive policies that address underlying grievances. Initiatives meant to display accountability may instead appear as surveillance that heightens perceptions of unwanted monitoring and control. Efforts to build legitimacy must therefore contend with the reality that what reassures one community may deepen distrust in another. Recognizing this relational nature of legitimacy is crucial for advancing a more equitable and durable form of governance without deepening historical divides.
