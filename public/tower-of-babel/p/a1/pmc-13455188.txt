Exploring students’ emotional experiences and responses to AI tutors and their limitations in higher education: a Self-Determination Theory (SDT) perspective
Authors: Huimin Li, Ali Derakhshan
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
BackgroundA growing body of research underscores the role of artificial intelligence (AI) technologies in second language (L2) education. However, the emotional side of adopting AI tutors in the English as a foreign language (EFL) context has received insufficient scholarly attention.PurposeTo address this knowledge gap, this qualitative study sought to uncover EFL students’ emotional experiences and responses to AI tutors.MethodTo do so, semi-structured interviews were conducted with 40 Chinese EFL students recruited through a convenience sampling strategy.ResultsThe results of the thematic analysis indicated different positive and negative emotional experiences associated with the adoption of AI tutors. The most common positive affective-motivational states were excitement, motivation, and engagement, while the negative emotions, anxiety, fear, and frustration, appeared most frequently in the learners. Additionally, it was revealed that, in response to the limitations of AI tutors, the participants exhibited four major emotional reactions: resentment, distrust, disengagement, and demotivation.Conclusion/implicationsThe study highlights the importance of considering learner emotions and emotionality when teaching English through AI tutors.

Introduction

The rise of artificial intelligence (AI) and generative AI technologies in second/foreign language (L2) education has received scholarly attention during recent years [9, 24, 29, 34, 48–50, 60]. This growing interest stems from the transformative potential of AI and generative AI to reshape language learning and teaching practices across diverse contexts [11, 22, 23, 37, 51]. The inclusion of AI has brought about numerous changes in language education domains and has offered several implications for teaching and learning practices of L2 education [19, 20, 31, 32, 36, 56, 61, 62]. AI tools can provide immediate and individualized feedback and learning content tailoring to learners' needs and motivational desires [7, 59].

Building on such benefits, a growing body of research has investigated practical applications and impacts of AI systems. For instance, Derakhshan and Taghizadeh [15] examined the effect of AI on L2 students’ higher-order thinking skills through a phenomenological approach. The results indicated that AI can both enhance and hamper the growth and development of higher-order thinking skills in L2 learners. In another study, Liu and Ma [30] explored the extent to which L2 learners incorporate AI tools, namely ChatGPT and Bing Chat, into their digital language learning. The results revealed that perceived use and ease of use affected learners' adoption of AI. Additionally, three themes regarding the actual use of chatbots as tutors, the relationship between chatbot benefits and increased intention to use them, and chatbot ease of use were identified.

While much of the research has focused on behavioral and cognitive aspects of AI use, AI technologies also play a role in influencing the psychological and affective factors of L2 learners [57]. In this sense, recent studies, drawing on positive psychology (PP) and control value theory (CVT), have illustrated that the integration of AI in language education brings about diverse positive and negative emotions in both teachers and learners [12, 13, 57]. Since AI entails novel teaching and learning approaches, it can cause diverse affective states in language learners [17, 40]. Early studies have shown that AI can influence emotions such as enjoyment, anxiety, and stress and affective-motivational states like engagement and motivation [4, 44, 45, 47, 52]. Within this direction, Seyri and Ghiasvand [42] explored EFL teachers’ emotions and their regulatory strategies in AI-enhanced L2 instruction. The findings illustrated that both positive and negative emotions were experienced during AI inclusion. Moreover, teachers were found to use both up-regulating and down-regulating strategies to control their emotions. Focusing on learners, Yang and Zhao [57] investigated the emotions of 498 EFL learners and their regulation strategies through open-ended questionnaires and semi-structured interviews. The results showed that an array of positive and negative emotions was experienced during AI-based learning. Additionally, learners utilized antecedent-focused and response-focused strategies to regulate their AI-induced emotions.

Among the many AI applications in language learning, intelligent tutoring systems stand out as a particularly promising tool. Intelligent tutoring systems, according to Lin et al. [27], are computer-based learning systems that use AI to offer adaptive and personalized education to learners. These systems can offer adaptive capabilities that respond to students’ learning needs and levels and can intervene at any time to offer assistance with a learning task. AI tutors can keep track of students’ progress, offer feedback and hints, and choose appropriate tasks for students' practice [27]. AI tutors can be beneficial for teachers, as they offer differentiated instruction for each learner, switching the mode of teaching from teacher-centered to learner-centered approaches [43]. Furthermore, they decrease teachers’ workload on daily tasks, including feedback provision and grading, thereby freeing teachers’ time to focus more on the teaching process [46]. Xu et al. [55] stated that L2 learners’ engagement increases in English language learning with AI tutors. In another study, Ruan et al. [39] examined the role of AI-powered tutors, specifically conversational chatbots, in enhancing students’ speaking skills. Their results showed that L2 learners were more engaged compared to those in traditional instruction. Furthermore, AI tutors were effective in enhancing students’ vocabulary learning, and their speaking skills were developed. In addition, students’ satisfaction during instruction powered by AI tutors gained momentum. For instance, Yıldız [58] remarked that students’ satisfaction with AI tutors, such as Duolingo and ChatGPT, is associated with AI’s communicative, behavioral, and cognitive dimensions during the language learning process. Likewise, Das et al. [8] maintained that learners’ satisfaction with AI tutors depends on the AI’s ability to respond appropriately and quickly, as well as helping learners feel autonomous in setting their own learning pace. More importantly, these AI tutors can consider students’ psychological states, namely emotions, motivations, or cognitions [27].

Despite these promising developments, research specifically focusing on L2 learners’ psycho-affective experiences (i.e., emotions, motivational states, and engagement-related responses) in interaction with AI tutors remains limited and under-examined, particularly when compared to the broader and rapidly expanding body of work on AI-enhanced and generative AI-supported language learning contexts. This gap is partly due to the conceptual scope of existing studies, which often treat AI tools in a broad sense, whereas AI tutors represent a more specific category of educational technology. In contrast to general-purpose generative AI tools and chatbots that primarily respond to user queries in an open-ended manner, AI tutors are typically designed as structured, pedagogically driven environments that provide adaptive instructional support and scaffolded learning pathways [1]. In addition, existing research has predominantly focused on the functional outcomes of AI tutors (e.g., language skill improvement and satisfaction) [21], while paying comparatively less attention to learners’ affective experiences during interactions with AI systems and how these emotional experiences may shape learning motivation and continuance intention [18].

Besides their benefits and advantages in educational domains, AI tutors are also reported to have certain limitations. For instance, Krupp et al. [25] posited that learners using AI tutors such as ChatGPT will show limited reflection on their learning processes. Moreover, it is asserted that AI interactions do not inspire critical thinking in learners, leading to a decline in their academic performance [16]. Despite excelling in content production and feedback provision, ChatGPT encounters challenges in the coherent and logical organization of content [60]. In this regard, Kohnke [22] associates GenAI with superficial language processing, which results in the generation of L2 content or essays lacking cohesion. Moreover, AI tutors may sometimes provide content that is too difficult to understand, resulting in students’ dissatisfaction [33]. In the same vein, AI systems can often suggest excessive corrections that weaken human creativity, criticality, and deep thinking [7, 25, 27, 38]. Likewise, Zhang et al. [60] argued that receiving only GenAI feedback and content will negatively affect L2 learners' higher-order skills.

To better understand learners’ emotional responses in AI-enhanced environments, Self-determination theory (SDT) offers a valuable theoretical lens. SDT, proposed by Ryan and Deci [41], is a theory of motivation examining the interplay between psychological needs and behaviors in educational environments. SDT posits that individuals have three basic psychological needs, namely autonomy, relatedness, and competence, that inspire students’ growth and learning. Autonomy is defined as having a sense of ownership over the learning process, which can be fostered through having positive experiences and control over diverse factors. Competence addresses the sense of mastery and progress during learning, which can be enhanced through a supportive learning context that provides opportunities for growth and appropriate feedback. Relatedness concerns one’s sense of connectedness, which is enhanced through respect and a sense of belonging to the community. If these psychological needs are met, students will be motivated to perform the activity. It should be noted that the educational setting plays a crucial role in satisfying these needs. With the emergence of AI technologies, recent studies have extended SDT to the exploration of AI-based education by employing AI tools as a replacement for the teaching context [5, 6, 53]. Given its affordances, AI-based instruction can cater to learners’ autonomy, competence, and relatedness. Learners’ autonomy can be supported by having access to diverse learning content, from which they can choose based on their needs,learners’ competence can be fostered through receiving appropriate instruction or feedback,and learners’ relatedness can be enhanced through the user-friendly nature of AI tools [5]. Xia et al. [53] stated that learners’ psychological needs can be better supported in AI-based education as compared to traditional classes. When their needs for autonomy, competence, and relatedness are satisfied, learners can be more engaged in AI-enhanced learning settings and experience a range of emotions [6]. Hence, SDT may contribute to interpreting learners’ emotions by explaining why they feel certain emotions in relation to their psychological needs.

In summary, the existing literature reveals significant gaps in our understanding of the emotional experiences of L2 learners interacting with AI tutors. The review of the abovementioned literature indicates that, despite the prominence of AI tutors in general education, they have received less attention in L2 education [14, 25]. Moreover, few studies have focused specifically on AI tutors as distinct from broader GenAI-supported learning. Additionally, there is a dearth of research exploring the limitations of AI tutors in L2 education. Operationally, AI tutors in this study concern a range of AI-powered software (e.g., Duolingo, GenieTutor) and chatbots (ChatGPT, Gemini), which are used for feedback, practice, scaffolding, or language-learning support. It should be acknowledged that such tools differ in structure, adaptivity, conversational design, and pedagogical purpose/function. To address the research gaps, this study aims to explore the emotional experiences and responses of EFL learners to AI tutors and their limitations. Moreover, notwithstanding the potential of SDT in examining AI-induced emotions, few studies, if any, have been conducted on exploring learners’ emotions through the lens of SDT. Therefore, the present study adopts SDT as a post-hoc interpretive framework to tap into EFL learners’ emotional experiences during AI-tutor instruction. The findings of this study will contribute to both theory and practice in that it extends the scope of SDT in terms of AI-based education by examining AI-induced emotions and the reasons behind those emotions, as well as providing fruitful information for language instructors in terms of L2 learners’ emotional experiences when adopting L2 instructions based on AI tutors. Method

Context and participants

This study was conducted within the rapidly evolving landscape of English language learning in China, where AI tutors are increasingly integrated into language instruction to support learners’ skill development [35]. Given the growing use of such technologies, it is important to understand how Chinese EFL students emotionally experience and respond to AI tutors in this unique educational environment.

As shown in Table 1, a group of 40 Chinese EFL students was selected through convenience sampling based on their availability and willingness to collaborate. The sample consisted of 17 males and 23 females. The age of the students ranged from 22 to 35 years. They were studying English at various private language institutes in different provinces in mainland China. They had familiarity with AI tutors and regularly used them to learn different language skills and sub-skills (at least three times a week). They used similar AI tutors (e.g., Duolingo, GenieTutor, Langua, and ChatGPT). Regarding proficiency level, the participants reported being at upper-intermediate and advanced levels. Instrument

To gain a deeper understanding of emotional experiences and responses to AI tutors and their limitations, an online semi-structured interview was carried out with a sample of 40 Chinese EFL students. The interview was divided into two sections (Appendix). The first part addressed the students’ demographic background, while the second part encompassed four questions pertaining to their previous experiences with using AI tools and AI tutors and their emotional states and responses to such tools. Given the high proficiency level of the students, the interviews were held in English and took 20 min. The participants were allowed to clarify in Chinese and translation support was available. The interviews were conducted individually, and an audio-recording tool was used to record the sessions for later analysis.

Data collection procedure

An interview was conducted with a sample of Chinese EFL students, who agreed to partake in the study. They signed a consent form at the outset of the study. The objectives and process of the data collection were explained to all students. Their privacy and confidentiality were ensured in a clarification note. They were also informed that no personal information would be disclosed and everything would remain anonymous. A list of interview questions was developed and given to two experts for content validity check. They suggested dividing the first question into two items (as they could not be addressed comprehensively in a single item), making the list of interview questions four open-ended items. Some grammatical and spelling errors were amended based on the experts’ comments. Technical terms related to AI and AI tutoring were explained. The students were invited to online individual interviews at their preferred time. The sessions were friendly, and interviewees were prompted to explain their views as fully as possible. Most of the interview time was devoted to the students’ talking and explanation. It took three weeks to gather the entire interview data as the participants were busy with their university studies and exams. The data were in the form of audio recorded from online interview sessions. They were organized per participant and question for later transcription and data analysis.

Data analysis

The collected audio data of the interviews were transcribed word by word. The transcripts were then sorted based on questions for qualitative data analysis. The thematic analysis model of Braun and Clarke [2] was carefully employed in several steps (Fig. 1). It is noteworthy that an inductive approach was taken to analyze the data. Moreover, SDT was used as a post-hoc interpretive lens and the themes were developed inductively in relation to autonomy, competence, and relatedness. So, the extracted codes/themes are neither SDT-driven nor deductive.Fig. 1Braun and Clarke's [2] Model of Thematic Analysis (p. 79)

Braun and Clarke's [2] Model of Thematic Analysis (p. 79)

First, the transcripts were examined multiple times, and important sentences were highlighted. This helped to gain a general understanding of students’ perceptions and experiences with AI tutors. Next, initial codes were created from those salient sentences. Initial codes were then combined and connected to form larger themes, unified by a common emotional thread. As an example, in producing “distrust” as an emotional response to AI tutors’ limitations, initial codes of “I doubt in AI tutors” and “I am skeptical of such tutoring tools” were connected. The back-and-forth process of mixing codes continued, ending in 15 themes. Next, themes were presented in the form of phrases that reflected the research question and the study's goal. Simple labels were used for themes to make them easily understandable. A descriptive report was then provided to summarize the overall findings. At least two excerpts were provided to support each theme.

It should be noted that coding unit in the reported frequencies was a “mention” across the dataset, referring to a meaningful segment of text (e.g., a phrase, sentence, or short excerpt) in which a distinct emotional experience or response to AI tutors was expressed. Accordingly, the reported frequencies represent the number of coded mentions identified across all interviews rather than the number of individual participants, as multiple mentions could be generated within a single participant’s account. To ensure that the reported patterns were not disproportionately influenced by a small number of highly vocal participants, the distribution of codes was cross-checked across all interviewees during the analysis process. This confirmed that the identified emotional patterns were distributed across multiple participants rather than concentrated in a few cases. Briefly, in this study, only mention-level frequencies were calculated and the distribution across participants was checked to avoid overrepresentation by highly verbal participants. Mention-frequency was selected because our aim was not comparing participants’ emotional experiences. Participant-level prevalence was not calculated but distribution across participants was qualitatively checked.

Rigor of the study

Adhering to the qualitative research paradigm, it was important to establish and maintain the maxims of rigor throughout the study [28]. Credibility was affirmed through member checking, whereby half of the participants (n = 20) were invited to scrutinize preliminary thematic summaries and interpretations derived from their interview data. They were provided with a concise summary of each theme and subtheme and asked to evaluate accuracy, clarity, and resonance with their experiences. Participants offered mainly minor clarifications and wording suggestions, which were incorporated to refine the presentation of findings without altering the underlying thematic structure. To certify confirmability, a veteran external researcher with expertise in L2 education and qualitative research critically examined the data analysis process, including raw transcript excerpts, initial coding outputs, codebook structure, and the final thematic organization. The external audit involved an independent review of code–data consistency, logical coherence between codes and themes, and the transparency of analytic decisions. The auditor provided written feedback and engaged in discussion with the primary researcher,however, no substantive disagreements emerged regarding the overall thematic architecture, with only minor refinements suggested for clarity of labeling. Transparency in data analysis was also ensured through the explanation and exemplification of code creation and the subsequent refinement of codes into dominant, larger themes. Concerning transferability, a thick description of the methodology and design of the study was provided to facilitate the judgment of its applicability to and replicability in other contexts. Finally, dependability was addressed by maintaining an audit trail of research decisions and analysis procedures, including documentation of coding iterations, theme development stages, and analytic memos, ensuring consistency throughout the study.

Regarding coding reliability, inter-rater reliability was established through independent coding conducted by a second coder. The independently generated codes were compared with those of the primary researcher, and disagreements were resolved through online discussion with the expert coder. This process yielded a satisfactory level of agreement, with a Cohen’s Kappa coefficient of 0.79. The researcher also adopted an outsider positionality during data collection and analysis, supported by reflexive practices such as journaling to monitor and mitigate potential biases.

Findings

Psycho-affective experiences of AI tutoring

Regarding EFL students’ psycho-affective experiences with AI tutors, the analysis of interview data revealed a range of positive and negative emotions depending on outcomes (Fig. 2). The linkage of each theme to SDT dimensions is also depicted but deep and analytical descriptions are reserved for the discussion section to prevent redundancy. Specifically, the participants reported experiencing six positive psycho-affective states when using AI tutors in L2 education. The most frequent positive affective-motivational states were increased ‘excitement’, ‘motivation’, and ‘engagement’. As pointed out by S18, “AI tutors can provide real-time feedback and diverse content in a few seconds. This is really exciting. I used Duolingo for learning English and my sense of excitement was tremendous”. Another student argued that “the use of AI tutors increases excitement because it is new and engaging for many students in EFL contexts” (S28). Motivation, the next affective-motivational state, was contended to “increase in light of AI tutors that engross learners in learning and technology at the same time” (S22). A student provided an example arguing that “the use of AI tutors like Duolingo and Langua added fun to grammar learning and tracking progression. This definitely increases motivation in learners” (S14). Given the engaging nature of AI tutors, it was asserted that “students are deeply engaged in classroom practices and tasks delivered by AI tools” (S8). Another respondent stated, “updates and developments in AI tutors really capture the attention of learners. They engage learners by different contents and materials asked” (S4). ‘Competence’ and ‘satisfaction’ were the next perceived positive emotions. In this regard, S29 declared, “AI tutors provide targeted practices and scaffolding, and this helps students achieve learning goals and develop a sense of competence”. In a real example, S30 stated, “I mastered the future perfect tense after constant use of AI tutors with their various practice exercises. Such tools made my grammar skills better and better”. Moreover, it was reiterated that “AI tutors can break down difficult tasks and structures based on learners’ needs and levels. It is satisfying when one observes a technology is tailoring to his/her basic needs and interests” (S40). The last experienced positive feeling pertained to the autonomy and agency granted by AI tutors. As mentioned by S11, “many AI tutors are controlled and directed by users and this creates sense of agency and autonomy in EFL students, who can use the tools for any academic purpose as they wish”. Another student supported this emotion and said, “I used GenieTutor to develop my English level. I had freedom and autonomy to pose prompts that I liked. This sharing of learning responsibility gave me a sense of agency and power to decide” (S33).Fig. 2Psycho-Affective Experiences in Adopting AI Tutors and SDT Reflections. Note. Regarding negative emotions, ‘anxiety’, ‘fear’, and ‘frustration’ were the most commonly experienced feelings by Chinese EFL students when adopting AI tutors. In the context of a pronunciation check, a student stated, “at first, Duolingo made me feel anxious because I thought it will unfairly react to and evaluate my accent, considering it as terrible” (S27). Another person concerned about the technical needs of AI tutors argued that “I did not know how to work with AI tutors and this created anxiety and fear of being judged by peers and teachers as weak” (S10). Moreover, it was maintained that “AI tutors can cause a sense of devalued and fear in students with bias algorithms” (S3). Another interviewee reported an experience of frustration when asking AI tutors a question several times. Specifically, she stated, “I was frustrated with AI because it kept sending the same error message several times. The prompt was grammatically correct and I did not know what the problem was” (S22). Moreover, S36 considered technical challenges of AI tutors “as a source of frustration in learners at beginner levels”. The fourth negative emotion was a sense of confusion, in support of which one of the students declared, “sometimes the explanations provided by AI tutors are unclear and I do not know what should I do. It is confusing when the subject or prompt is complex and disciplinary-specific” (S6). A reason for such confusion was believed to be “insufficient context provided by AI tutors and their databases” (S37). The last negative emotional experience pertained to sense of isolation. As declared by S19, “AI tutors are machines and differ from people. Over-reliance on such tools will reduce and damage students’ social skills …..it makes one isolated from others”. A student underscored the value of human interaction by saying that “AI tutors have no feeling and I personally miss real talks with real people after a prolonged period of working with AI tutors for my speaking skills” (S25). In sum, it was found that AI tutors had generated a range of positive and negative emotional states in Chinese EFL students. The most frequent positive affective-motivational states were ‘excitement’, ‘motivation’, and ‘engagement’. On the negative side, ‘anxiety’, ‘fear’, and ‘frustration’ were the most common emotions among learners.

As shown in Table 2, ‘resentment’ and ‘distrust’ were the most frequently mentioned reactions in the interviews. The limitations and errors of AI tutors were found to cause “a sense of irritation, annoyance, and resentment in students when they see their grammatically correct sentences are marked as wrong by some AI tools” (S28). Another student declared that “sometimes, AI tutors misunderstand our prompts and fail to provide correct and relevant outputs. This makes me angry, to be honest” (S11). When such problems continue, it may lead to skepticism and distrust in learners. This was highlighted in the interviews. As mentioned by S29, “I think when AI tutors make many mistakes, it makes learners feel that they are unreliable tools for L2 learning. After a time, they lose their trust in such technologies”. Another student argued that “by asking AI tutors technical and domain-specific questions, I came across irrelevant answers. So, I do not think they are actually good for gaining deep knowledge. Everything is superficial” (S13).

The next two extracted emotional responses from the interviews were ‘disengagement’ and ‘demotivation’. According to S3, “when AI tutors keep providing wrong responses, I personally become reluctant to use them anymore because it is weird at this age to see such errors”. In severe cases, it was argued that “the limitations and errors of AI tutors can even make students disengage from learning process and avoid language learning altogether” (S39). Another interviewee stated, “just the way effective AI tools can cause engagement, weak and non-functional tools can lead to disengagement and academic failure” (S30). As the last emotional response, demotivation was declared to “emerge from faulty AI tutors that provide wrong outputs and contents for L2 learning without any logic and theoretical basis” (S20). The erroneous language and feedback of AI tutors, especially in pronunciation, “may erode motivation and hinder learning because repeated mistakes cause demotivation in learners” (S6).

Discussion

This study aimed to explore Chinese EFL students’ psycho-affective experiences and responses to AI tutors and their limitations. The results of interview data analysis indicated a range of positive and negative psycho-emotional experiences in Chinese EFL students when using AI tutors. On the positive side, the participants most frequently experienced ‘excitement’, ‘motivation’, and ‘engagement’, which agree with previous studies on the positive emotional outcomes of AI adoption in EFL settings [54, 55, 57]. A justification for these feelings could be the capacity of AI tutors in fulfilling the needs and interests of EFL students, a claim supported by SDT [41]. Another reason could be the newness of AI technologies and tools for EFL students, making them excited, engaged, and motivated to renovate and advance their L2 learning approaches through AI tutors.

Three other positive emotions identified in this study were ‘competence’, ‘satisfaction’, and ‘agency’, which directly reflect the components of SDT and its basic tenet, namely needs satisfaction/fulfillment. However, compared to more frequently reported psycho-motivational states such as excitement and motivation, these experiences appeared less prominent in the dataset, which warrants further interpretation. Rather than attributing this pattern to stable personality traits of participants, it may be more appropriate to interpret these findings in relation to contextual and technological factors. One possible explanation is that the relatively higher frequency of excitement and engagement reflects a novelty-oriented response to AI tutors as emerging technologies, where initial enthusiasm may temporarily overshadow deeper forms of psychological need satisfaction, such as competence and agency. At the same time, it is also plausible that current AI tutors may not yet fully support learners’ sense of competence and autonomy in sustained and meaningful ways. For instance, while AI tutors may facilitate engagement and immediate task involvement, they may offer limited opportunities for deeper skill mastery, adaptive scaffolding, or learner-driven control over the learning process. From an SDT perspective, such limitations may help explain why competence- and agency-related experiences were reported less frequently compared to more immediate and affectively salient responses such as excitement and motivation.

On the negative side, the study revealed that ‘anxiety’, ‘fear’, and ‘frustration’ were the most common emotions among EFL learners. This is in line with the negative AI-induced emotions reported by Xin and Derakhshan [54] in their qualitative study in China. Possible explanations for experiencing such negative feelings could be students’ low AI acceptance of and readiness for technology utilization as well as their technophobia in general [3, 26, 29, 34, 47, 57]. The weaknesses and complexities involved in adopting such tools may also justify the findings. Frustration may also be due to a lack of need satisfaction by AI tutors, an idea supported by SDT. This study differed from previous studies regarding the ‘fear’, ‘confusion’, and ‘isolation’ associated with AI tutors, which may be due to inadequate AI training support and misconceptions and misjudgments of learners based on AI competence and performance in China. The mechanistic and algorithm-based nature of AI technologies can also play a role in such emotions.

Regarding emotional responses to the limitations of AI tutors, the study reported four major reactions: “resentment’, ‘distrust’, ‘disengagement’, and ‘demotivation’. These emotional responses may be interpreted within the framework of SDT as potential manifestations of unmet or frustrated basic psychological needs, particularly autonomy, competence, and relatedness, within AI-mediated L2 learning environments. From this perspective, such responses can be understood as reflecting perceived mismatches between learners’ expectations and the extent to which AI tutors support these basic psychological needs in AI-mediated L2 learning environments. More specifically, the data suggest that when learners perceive AI tutors as insufficiently responsive, overly rigid, or limited in providing meaningful pedagogical interaction, these perceptions may contribute to diminished feelings of competence and reduced sense of autonomy in regulating their own learning processes. In this sense, the reported emotional reactions can be cautiously interpreted as possible indicators of emerging motivational strain, where learners begin to experience reduced psychological alignment with the learning environment.

Another possible explanation is that these emotional responses may reflect processes of motivational depletion and emotional disengagement associated with sustained need frustration, particularly in relation to competence and autonomy support. Accordingly, it may be cautiously inferred that when AI tutors are perceived as failing to adequately support students’ basic psycho-affective needs, they may contribute to reduced motivational engagement or amotivation over time. The inherent limitations of current AI tutors in fostering higher-order cognitive engagement, such as reflection, critical thinking, creativity, and dialogic interaction, have also been identified in prior research as potential constraints on sustained learner engagement [10, 16, 60], which provides a broader pedagogical context for interpreting these findings. Within this context, the emotional responses identified in the present study may be viewed as situated reactions to perceived instructional and interactional constraints rather than fixed psychological traits of learners. Additionally, the qualitative accounts suggest that students’ negative emotional responses to AI tutors may also be linked to their reported frustration with system limitations, particularly regarding perceived inaccuracies, lack of contextual sensitivity, and occasionally inappropriate or generic feedback outputs. Such experiences appear to shape learners’ emotional distancing from AI systems, as reflected in feelings of distrust and disengagement. Finally, the gap between AI and humans in terms of interpersonal interactions and feelings can further explain such negative reactions.

Conclusion and implications

Grounded in the SDT, this inquiry explored Chinese EFL students’ emotional experiences and responses to AI tutors. The findings demonstrated different positive and negative emotional experiences associated with adopting AI tutors, as well as four major emotional reactions to their limitations and errors. In light of this research, it can be concluded that adopting AI tutors and tools is by no means an emotion-free process, but rather one that is highly driven by emotions and emotional reactions. It is also asserted that learner emotions depend on the efficacy and functionality of AI tutors. If they see positive developments in their language learning, they are more likely to experience positive emotions and react positively to such technologies. Another conclusion is that the affective aspects of AI integration into L2 education carry equal weight to pedagogical, learning, and technical aspects. The study, then, implies that emotionality is a key factor in using AI tutors in L2 education. These findings have theoretical and practical implications. They expand the components of SDT by connecting them to AI technologies and learners’ psycho-affective variables. The study extends the application of SDT to AI-mediated language-learning contexts by showing how learners’ emotional responses may be interpreted through need satisfaction and frustration. It significantly extends the application of SDT to the field of AI tutor emotion research, revealing the associative mechanisms between psychological need satisfaction/frustration and learner emotional experiences in AI technology environments, thereby providing a new theoretical perspective for understanding emotional dynamics in technology use.

EFL students, teachers, teacher educators, and AI designers can practically benefit from the outcomes of this research as well. Particularly, EFL students understand common emotional experiences and responses involved in adopting AI tutors and find suitable regulatory techniques to manage them. Their AI literacy, AI readiness, and emotional literacy may be enhanced in light of the findings. EFL teachers can develop their AI competency and skills to better engage students in classes mediated by AI tools. When using AI tutors to support instruction, teachers should proactively discuss students' emotional experiences with them, identify potential signals of need frustration (e.g., frustration, disengagement), and intervene through emotional support and peer interaction activities to compensate for AI's deficiencies in relatedness support. They can utilize emotionally supportive classroom practices to foster positive feelings when adopting AI tutors in L2 classes. They can also assist EFL students in regulating their AI-induced feelings via interactions and practical strategies, such as mindfulness practices and experience sharing with others. To equip teachers with effective skills to deal with AI-induced emotions in the classroom, teacher educators can present workshops on the emotional aspects of AI tutoring adoption. Emotion regulation strategies can be taught to EFL teachers through professional development programs. Finally, the study can be significant for AI designers, who can reflect deeply on the limitations of AI tutors and students’ emotional reactions and suffering in future updates and versions of the tools. They should attend to the ‘emotional sensitivity’ of AI tutors, incorporating mechanisms for recognizing and responding to learners’ emotional states into algorithmic design; simultaneously, optimize the friendliness and explanatory quality of error feedback to reduce frustration and distrust arising from technical limitations; furthermore, explore designs that enhance AI tutors’ social presence (e.g., more humanized conversational styles, links to virtual learning communities) to partially satisfy learners’ relatedness needs. Moreover, the linguistic accuracy of the bots and chatbots can be improved when AI developers see how negatively erroneous sentences affect users.

Limitations and suggestions for future research

Some limitations are inherent in this study, spanning from its design to its findings. First and foremost, given its qualitative nature, the researchers’ subjectivity may have influenced the data analysis and theme extraction procedures. Secondly, the use of convenience sampling introduces potential selection bias and limits the representativeness of the sample, which may affect the generalizability of the results. To address this, future research should consider employing more rigorous sampling methods, such as stratified or purposive sampling, to enhance the diversity and representativeness of participants. Thirdly, the small sample size of 40 EFL students limits the extent to which the findings can be generalized to the broader population. While the sample provided valuable in-depth insights, future studies would benefit from involving a larger and more diverse group of participants to strengthen the robustness and applicability of the results. Fourthly, the participants had previously used different AI tutors during their L2 education, which could not be controlled in this study. Each AI tool may have different emotional consequences and associated reactions. Hence, further research is suggested to work on specific AI tutors rather than all of them in a single study. Lack of tools and data triangulation is the fifth limitation of the present study. Future researchers are advised to utilize a broader range of data collection instruments, both quantitative and qualitative. The adoption of an exclusive qualitative design poses the risk of subjectivity in findings. Future research can use mixed-methods designs to obtain richer insight. Moreover, the study provided translation of interview questions unevenly, which may limit the findings as the study investigated emotional expression and the interview language may affect nuance and depth of emotional reporting. Future research could deeply investigate whether different types of AI tutors (e.g., rule-based tutors vs. generative AI tutors) differentially influence learners' emotional experiences.

Future studies can replace SDT with other theories related to emotions, such as control value theory (CVT) and the broaden-and-build theory, in the context of AI tutors. Longitudinal studies can also track the shifts and developments of emotional experiences in learners when employing AI tutors. For example, they can employ longitudinally track the dynamic evolutionary trajectory of learners' emotional experiences from initial contact with AI tutors to deep usage, as well as the causal relationships between psychological need satisfaction/frustration and emotional changes. Cross-cultural and cross-disciplinary studies can also expand this line of research by integrating data from larger communities and perspectives, helping to access a macro model of emotionality in the adoption of AI tutors in education. It is also interesting to explore how pedagogical interventions (e.g., emotion regulation strategy training) or technological optimization (e.g., enhancing AI's emotional sensitivity) may promote learners' positive emotional experiences and sustained learning engagement in AI tutor environments.
