Comparative micro‐ornamentation patterns of reptile skin: Phylogeny‐ecology debate and species‐specific variations
Authors: Melodi Yenmiş
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractThe Oberhautchen cells of reptile skin possess micro‐ornamentations that are complex structures with a wide diversity. The ongoing debate regarding the origin of these structures oscillates between phylogenetic and ecological drivers. The current study also contributes to this debate by examining micro‐ornamentations in seven lizards from three families, one worm lizard, and four snakes from three families that have epigeal, fossorial, saxicolous, or semi‐aquatic life habits. They were compared in terms of phylogeny and life habits. Additionally, the variations in different scales of the same individual and different regions of the same scale were studied. The aim of comparing phylogenetically distant species among reptiles was to understand whether micro‐ornamentation morphologies evolved convergently in species with the same life habits. The results showed that there are three cell shapes, two cell borders, five cell surfaces, and three ridges as morphological character states within the studied species. These variations might also differ significantly on different scales for an individual and even change gradually on the same scale. The ancestral form possibly has polygonal cells and regular borders with a smooth surface where the ridges are absent. It is essential to consider that convergent evolution facilitates the resolution of “problems” with similar “answers,” however, comparisons suggest that phylogenetic limitations are more decisive than habitat restrictions in shaping the diversity of reptile skin micro‐ornamentation.

INTRODUCTION

The outermost layer of the epidermis of lizards and snakes, which is in contact with the environment, is the Oberhautchen layer. This outer layer, which originates from the basal layer of the epidermis and fuses with the underlying beta cell layer after maturation, is responsible for the mechanical protection of the skin and is subject to varying degrees of wear and degradation. The Oberhautchen possesses micro‐ornamentations that are characteristic of the species (Alibardi, 1999, 2014; Alibardi & Toni, 2006; Bonfitto et al., 2025; Maderson et al., 1998; Swadzba & Rupik, 2010). Known for over 150 years (Leydig, 1873), these microstructures have been referred to by various names over time (“microornamentation” Ruibal, 1968; “microdermatoglyphics” Dowling et al., 1972; “ultradermatoglyphics” Larsen et al., 1973; “dermatoglyphics” Burstein et al., 1974; “microarchitecture” Peterson, 1984; “microsculpture” Ananjeva et al., 1991; Dujsebayeva et al., 2021; “nanogratings” Arrigo et al., 2019), and discussions about their morphology and functions continue to this day.

Scale micro‐ornamentations have been studied many times across a wide range of species in terms of their different morphological characteristics. In the literature, the morphology of these structures is generally divided into cell shape, cell boundary shape, structures on the cell surface, and ridges (Arnold, 2002; Arrigo et al., 2019; Gower, 2003; Price, 1982, 1983; Price & Kelly, 1989; Riedel & Schwarzkopf, 2022).

Species‐specific morphological differences, systematically identified through comprehensive analyses in the literature, have led to scholarly debates regarding the origin and functional significance of this structural diversity. The first hypothesis proposed was that these micro‐ornamentations diversified in accordance with phylogenetic relatedness. In contrast, an opposing view argues that these structures represent ecological adaptations that arise from convergent evolution. Some findings supporting ecological adaptation have revealed relationships between micro‐ornamentations and traits such as hydrophobicity (e.g., Webb et al., 2014), camouflage, thermoregulation (Cooper & Greenberg, 1992; Spinner et al., 2013), scale color (Dhillon et al., 2014; Doucet & Meadows, 2009), friction, dirt shedding and water resistance (Wang et al., 2020) and locomotor behavior (Renous & Gasc, 1989; Riiska et al., 2025; Tingle et al., 2024). However, in some of these studies, particularly those conducted with a large sample size, the authors found no clear relationship between scale micro‐morphology and ecological characteristics (Arnold, 2002; Arrigo et al., 2019; Price, 1982; Schmidt & Gorb, 2012). Additionally, studies have been conducted on the relationship between the microstructures covering scales in different body regions of the same individual (Stewart & Daniel, 1973, 1975), and even on the morphology of micro‐ornamentation in different regions of a single scale (Arrigo et al., 2019; Bonfitto et al., 2025; Klein & Gorb, 2014).

In the present study, 12 reptiles, 7 lizards (of families Scincidae, Anguidae, and Agamidae), one Amphisbaenid (from Blanidae), and 4 snakes (of families Natricidae, Typhlopidae, and Leptotyphlopidae) were examined in terms of scale micro‐ornamentation and its correlation with phylogeny, life habit, different body regions, and different areas of a single scale. The aim of comparing phylogenetically distant species among reptiles was to understand whether micro‐ornamentation morphologies evolved convergently in species with the same life habits. The research questions of this study are as follows: i. Do micro‐ornamentations vary across different areas of the same scale? ii. Do micro‐ornamentations vary across different body regions in a single individual? iii. Are changes in scale micro‐ornamentation in reptiles found at different taxonomic levels that exhibit varying life habits related to phylogeny or the environment to which they are adapted? In our search for answers, we used scanning electron microscopy, statistical methods, and ancestral character state reconstruction.

Dorsal body scales were used to compare the variations in micro‐ornamentations across different areas of the same scale (i.e., Dorsal Scale mid‐area = DS‐mid vs. Dorsal Scale base‐area = DS‐base) (Figure 1c,d). To compare micro‐ornamentation variations across different body regions, the mid‐areas of the dorsal body scale (DS‐mid) and tail scale (TS‐mid) were examined. Finally, for ancestral character state reconstruction, the morphological character states of the dorsal scale mid‐area (DS‐mid) were used (Figure 1e).

Scanning electron microscopy (SEM)

For scanning electron microscopy, skin samples were post‐fixed in 2.5% glutaraldehyde at room temperature for 2–8 h, washed with Sorensen phosphate buffer, dehydrated in increasing alcohol series, and dried using a Leica EMCPD300 critical point dryer. The samples were coated with gold–palladium using Leica‐EM‐ACE600 and analyzed using Thermoscientific apreo8 at Ege University Central Research Testing and Analysis Laboratory.

SEM results from scale surfaces were used to identify morphological characters such as cell shape, cell border, cell surface, and ridges (after Arrigo et al., 2019). In addition, scales were imaged saggitally, which enabled the observation and comparison of the micro‐ornamentations of the mid‐area (i.e., scale surface where no other scales in contact) and base‐area (area that normally stays under the upper scale of imbricate orientation) of the dorsal scales (Figure 1c,d).

Stereomicroscopy

Gross morphological observations of the reptilian skin and scale arrangement were documented via stereomicroscopy. Images were obtained using a Euromex DZ5040 stereomicroscope equipped with a Euromex HD II imaging system. Captured images were subsequently processed and analyzed using ImageFocus Plus V3 software (Figure 2).

Stereomicrographs of the dorsal scale mid‐areas (DS‐mid) of the 12 examined species. (Scale bars =1 mm) (Yellow arrows indicate cranial direction).

Measurements

Cell widths and lengths were measured using SEM images using ImageFocus Plus v.2.4.9. A total of 30 cells were measured from each individual: 20 from the dorsal scale (10 from the DS‐mid and 10 from the DS‐base) and 10 from the mid‐area of the tail scale (TS‐mid). In addition to these measurements, the aspect ratio of the cells (i.e., cell width/length = w/l) was calculated. Statistical analyses were performed using SPSS 25 to determine whether the cell dimensions of the mid and base areas differed significantly. Data were checked for normality using the one‐sample Kolmogorov–Smirnov test and then subjected to an independent sample t‐test.

Ancestral character state reconstruction

A total of eight loci (mtDNA: cytb, ND2, ND4, 12S, 16S, coi; nDNA: mc1r) were used for the phylogenetic analysis. The NCBI accession numbers are provided in Supplementary File 1. Mesquite was used for the analyses (vers.4.02) (Maddison & Maddison, 2025). Sequences were aligned using MAFFT (vers.7.526), trimmed using built‐in TrimAI (vers.1.5). The tree search criterion was Treelength, and subtree pruning and regrafting (SPR) were used as the rearranger (Maxtrees: 100). In addition to the 12 species in question, Sphenodon punctatus was added as an outgroup. Morphological characters of the mid‐area of the dorsal scale (DS‐mid) of each species were used for the character matrix as well as life habits. Morphological characteristics of the micro‐ornamentations of S. punctatus were deduced from the images of Peterson (1984) and Dujsebayeva et al. (2004), and life habits from Thompson et al. (1996). Morphological characters were determined following Arrigo et al. (2019).

In eight of the 11 species examined (except for Natrix natrix, in which DS‐base cell widths could not be measured, hence, NA = not applicable), a significant difference (p< 0.05) was found between the widths of DS‐mid and DS‐base cells, except for Chalcides ocellatus, Laudakia stellio, and Blanus strauchi. In terms of cell length, the DS‐mid and DS‐base areas were significantly different in 11 of the 12 species studied, except for N. natrix (p ≥0.05). Among these, the cell lengths in the DS‐mid area were larger than those in the DS‐base in six species: Heremites auratus, Anguis fragilis, Trapelus lessonae, B. strauchi, Xerotyphlops vermicularis, and Natrix tessellata. Variations among dorsal and tail scales

Comparison of cell widths between DS‐mid and TS‐mid was possible in only eight species. Among these, the widths of the DS‐mid cells in A. kitaibelii, A. fragilis, and B. strauchi were greater than those of the TS‐mid cells. In the other five species (H. auratus, M. macrorhyncha, X. vermicularis, N. natrix, and N. tessellata), the cell widths in the TS‐mid area were greater. Cells were longer in the DS‐mid in H. vittatus, B. strauchi, and X. vermicularis, as opposed to M. macrorhyncha, which had longer cells in the TS‐mid (Table 2).

Cell morphologies

The w/l index is important for determining the cell shape, that is, whether it is polygonal, wide, or large. A w/l ratio approaching 1 indicates a polygonal cell shape. If w/l is equal to or greater than 2, the cell is “wide”; if it is between 1 and 2, the cell is “large” (Figure 4).

Possible states of Oberhäutchen cell characteristics, including cell shape, cell border, cell surface, and ridge morphology (after Arrigo et al., 2019).

Within the species examined, three cell morphologies were classified into shapes, textures (cell borders and surfaces), and the presence/absence of ridges. Cell shapes can be polygonal, wide, or large, while the configurations of cell borders can be straight or interdigitating (i.e., interlocking, finger‐like projections creating a secure, alternating bond). Cell surfaces can be smooth or have holes (pits), channels (straight or labyrinth), or polygons. If present, the ridges can be regular or irregular (Figure 4). The morphological features are illustrated from a wider perspective, with stereo micrographs presented in Figure 2 and scanning electron micrographs provided in Supplementary File 2.

SEM results and w/l ratio of DS‐mid together showed that Ablepharus kitaibelii has wide cell shape, interdigitating cell borders, smooth cell surface and irregular ridges, while C. ocellatus, H. auratus, and H. vittatus have wide cells with interdigitating cell borders, smooth cell surface, and no ridges (Figure 5). A. fragilis has the same cell shape, borders, and the absence of ridges as the former species, yet it has holes on the cell surface. L. stellio and T. lessonae both have polygonal cells, particularly honeycomb, and regular cell borders with no ridges; however, while the first species has labyrinth channels on the cell surface, the latter has polygons. B. strauchi's cells are polygonal with regular borders, holes on the surface, and no ridges. X. vermicularis and M. macrorhyncha share regular cell borders, smooth cell surfaces, and the lack of ridges, but cell shapes differ between the two: the first has polygonal cells, and the latter has wide cells. Both N. natrix and N. tessellata have large cells with interdigitating cell borders and regular ridges on the surface; however, while N. natrix has straight channels on the cell surface; N. tessellata has holes (Figure 6). DISCUSSION

Micro‐ornamentations, the existence of which has been known for over 150 years, were initially imaged under light microscopes and have since been studied in detail under SEM with advancing technology (Ananjeva et al., 1991; Arnold, 2002; Arrigo et al., 2019; Dujsebayeva et al., 2021; Leydig, 1873). These structures, found to exhibit a wide variety of morphologies, have prompted researchers to investigate the reasons behind this broad spectrum. One side of the ongoing debate advocates phylogenetic proximity (Arrigo et al., 2019; Price, 1982; Schmidt & Gorb, 2012), while the other side suggests micro‐ornamentation selection based on the dominance of ecology and life habit (Cooper & Greenberg, 1992; Spinner et al., 2013; Tingle et al., 2024; Webb et al., 2014). Researchers have conducted numerous extensive studies to demonstrate which of these two sides plays the primary role in selection. The current study also contributes to this debate by examining micro‐ornamentation in species representing different taxa across a wide range of reptilian species distributed in different habitats. The results of the three main research questions of the study are discussed in this section.

Ancestral states of morphological characters

The cell shape inherited from the ancestral form is most probably polygonal. This ancestral character is conserved in 5 of the 13 terminal taxa (X. vermicularis, L. stellio, T. lessonae, B. strauchi, and S. punctatus) (Figure 8). This character, which has shifted a total of 3 times, has changed into wide cells (strap‐shaped cells of Arnold, 2002) in the common ancestral form of the Scincidae family that includes four epigeal and one fossorial species, and to large cells in the Natricidae family with two semi‐aquatic snakes. The third shift, unlike the previous two shifts that corresponded with phylogeny, occurred in the fossorial snake M. macrorhyncha, shifting from the ancestral polygonal cell to a wide cell, thus diverging from the closest terminal taxon, X. vermicularis, which shares the same life habit. Arrigo et al. (2019) categorized cell shape character into two states, polygonal and wide, the latter being when the width of the cell is bigger than twice its length, and found out that within 353 snake species studied, Natricinae members all had wide cells, as opposed to our findings. This could be the result of their choice to classify cell shape into two states rather than three, as polygonal, wide, and large while Leptotyphlopidae was found to have wide cells which our results coincide with. Typhlopidae results seem conflicted in the two studies, since we found out that the wide cells also. Arnold (2002) found that they lay at the base of the scale rather than the mid surface area. The phylogenetic tree indicates that the cell shape character has evolved differently from the ancestral character in three different taxa, two of which are conserved in all individuals of the taxon studied, whereas in M. macrorhynca, it has evolved convergently. Polygonal cell shape has also been discussed in terms of wettability, especially for its effect on convergently evolved rain‐harvesting behavior (Comanns et al., 2011; Gans et al., 1982; Schwenk & Greene, 1987), but no agreement has been reached regarding its positive or negative effects. The tree indicates that the cell shape character seems to be a relationship indicator rather than an ecological one, as was found in some studies (Arrigo et al., 2019; Price, 1982; Schmidt & Gorb, 2012).

Stochastic character mapping of Oberhäutchen cell shape. The ancestral polygonal cell shape has undergone three independent evolutionary transitions across the examined lineages.

The ancestral state of the cell border is straight, which is conserved in 6 of the 13 terminal taxa. It shifted twice in history, once at the common ancestor of the Skinks and once at Natricines (Figure 9). This history shows that both epigeal and fossorial Scincidae members that have wide cells also have interdigitating cell borders. Natricinae members with large cells also share this shifted state. M. macrorhyncha has wide cells and straight cell borders. All the other species that have an ancestral polygonal cell shape, including fossorial, epigeal, and saxicolous forms, also share an ancestral straight cell border state. Arrigo et al. (2019) proposed that interdigitating cell borders represent the ancestral condition in snakes and were subsequently lost through the evolutionary processes.

Stochastic mapping of the Oberhäutchen cell border. The ancestral regular (straight) cell border evolved independently into interdigitating borders in the skink and Natricinae lineages.

Ridges are a relatively new state considering their absence ancestrally and through history. Ridges seem to have evolved twice, independently: once as regular ridges at the Natricinae line and once as irregular ridges at epigeal Skink A. kitaibelii (Figure 10). It has been argued as an ecomorphological remark that ridges enhance the smoothness of sliding of serpentiform movement by decreasing friction (Jayne, 1986, 1988; Lillywhite & Sanmartino, 1993). Yet, this interpretation coincides neither with the present study, which shows that if the ridges are present, then they are present on dorsal scales, nor with the wide snake scale comparison of Arrigo et al. (2019), who showed that the posterior probabilities of having or not having ridges are similar.

Stochastic mapping of the ridges. The absence of the ridges represents the ancestral state, whereas regular ridges occur only in the Natricinae line.

The number of cell surface states is greater than the number of states in other characters. As the most diverse character, the cell surface has diverged among terminal taxa regardless of life habit. Accordingly, B. strauchi, A. fragilis, and N. tessellata have holes (pits) on the cell surface, while L. stellio, T. lessonae, and N. natrix each have a different state by themselves, namely labyrinth channels, polygons, and straight channels, respectively (Figure 11). The rest of the species share the smooth state of the cell surface character. The ancestral state is 50% smooth and 50% pits, which appear to have shifted at least five times throughout history. Arnold (2002) stated that the smooth surface is the ancestral (primitive) form for lacertids, which might decrease friction, but at the same time, it may compromise the camouflage by causing glare. The presence of holes on the cell surface has been debated with ecomorphologically opposing views: Arnold (2002) stated that these structures increase skin contamination, while Gower (2003) argued that these holes reduce the contact surface between water and skin, and consequently reduce wettability and contamination, particularly in aquatic reptiles. Polygonal cell surfaces and labyrinth channels have been discussed in terms of rain‐harvesting behavior, but it was determined that there is no correlation between the morphology and the behavior (Sherbrooke et al., 2007; Yenmiş et al., 2024). There are other micro‐ornamentations that have not been found within the species of the present study, such as “nanopits” in Vipers, which were found to be related to self‐cleaning (Spinner et al., 2014). In light of all available information, it can be agreed that, unlike previous characters, the cell surface character is not a useful taxonomic tool (Yenmiş et al., 2022).

Stochastic mapping of the Oberhäutchen cell surface. Smooth surface and holes are equally probable ancestral states for all examined species.

The epigeal and fossorial states are equally likely for the ancestor of all species. A convergent shift from these forms to a complete fossorial form occurred three times, in Blanidae, Anguidae, and in the line of fossorial snakes. A complete epigeal shift likely occurred twice, once at the Scincidae branch after the divergence of A. fragilis and once more for T. lessonae (Figure 12). The saxicolous state occurs only once in L. stellio. A semi‐aquatic shift seems likely to have occurred once in the Serpentes line in Natricines. Previous studies have suggested that snakes originated from terrestrial ancestors (Da Silva et al., 2018; Hsiang et al., 2015), particularly those exhibiting a fossorial lifestyle (Arrigo et al., 2019). It was stated that the ancestral squamates were highly likely to be terrestrial (Evans, 2003).

Stochastic mapping of life habits. Epigeal and fossorial states are equally probable ancestral states for all examined species.

Micro‐ornamentations on different scales and on different parts of the same scale

Gans and Baic (1977) found that some Uropeltid snakes have microstructural differences among body and tail scales, which were thought to be related to hydrophobicity and iridescence. Arnold (2002) noted that micro‐ornamentation on the tail scales of Eremiainae members is “more sculptured” than that on the base scales. In Bitis rhinoceros, black and brown scales were found to have different surface structures, which were related to hydrophobicity (Spinner et al., 2014). In the present study, it was found that cell borders, cell surfaces, or ridges do not vary among tail and dorsal scales within the same individual. This finding is consistent with Arnold's (2002) statement on Lacertinae. Only A. fragilis was found to have interdigitating cell borders on DS‐mid but regular borders on TS‐mid, which could be explained by the tail and dorsal scales being exposed to wear in different ways. However, this issue can be explained more clearly by comparing the lateral and especially the ventral scales.

Price and Kelly (1989) stated that the width of a cell varies greatly on one scale, which was also the case for Uropeltid snakes (Gower, 2003) and X. vermicularis (Yenmiş, 2025). Again, Arrigo et al. (2019), who stated that the tip of a dorsal scale has the most diverse morphology, also found that cell borders and cell surfaces change toward the tip of one scale. It was also stated that on the scales of the regenerating tail of Podarcis muralis, the crests that are inclined tips of cells change toward the border to merge and form a single crest (Bonfitto et al., 2025). The present study showed that cell shapes, surfaces, and borders might change gradually on a single scale. In eight of the 11 species studied, it was found that the widths of the cells at DS‐base were greater than those at DS‐mid. This can be explained by the hinge regions between two scales: hinge regions form the areas between scales, regardless of whether the scale arrangement is overlapping (imbricated) or not. These “joints” are softer and more irregular than the scale surfaces, and the Oberhautchen is thinner here and carries “island‐like” protrusions (Sherbrooke et al., 2007), making the surface “flexible” (Kandyel et al., 2021), thereby facilitating body movement. In addition, the varying cell sizes on a single scale can also be explained by wear dynamics: a graded‐scale surface, characterized by a rigid outer layer and a progressively more compliant interior, is thought to increase wear resistance in snake skin by effectively combining mechanical durability with flexibility (Klein & Gorb, 2012). An alternative explanation may be associated with embryonic development. For instance, in Anolis lineatopus, cell proliferation has been shown to be more restricted in the inner scale and hinge regions during scale morphogenesis; consequently, cells in these regions are larger and flatter (Alibardi, 1996). Therefore, large cells turning into wide cells that are becoming more abundant through the hinge regions might be explained by the facilitation of movement, wear resistance or developmental gradients.

CONCLUSION

Reptile scale micro‐ornamentations have complex and widely diverse morphologies. The present study, which investigates 12 reptiles, showed that the ancestral form of the studied species seems to have polygonal cells with regular borders and a smooth surface where the ridges are absent. Looking at the history, most of the character states are shared within the clade. For example, all the skinks have wide cells with interdigitating cell borders and smooth surfaces; both Agamids have polygonal cells, regular cell borders, and no ridges; likewise, both Natricinae have large cells, interdigitating cell borders, and regular ridges. These results support the idea that phylogenetic limitations are more decisive than habitat limitations (epigeal, fossorial, saxicolous, and semi‐aquatic habits) in the diversity of reptile skin micro‐ornamentation. While the results of the present study appear to favor the phylogenetic perspective in the phylogeny–ecology debate, they remain insufficient to conclusively resolve the issue. Accordingly, further studies incorporating substantially larger sample sizes are necessary to achieve a more definitive understanding. Additionally, it cannot be claimed that micro‐ornamentations evolved solely within the phylogenetic process. Convergent evolution allows solving “problems” with similar “answers.” In the case of micro‐ornamentations, the same states of characters might be in favor of similar environmental challenges. For instance, it has been demonstrated that the micro‐ornamentation differs on the scales of three snake species—aquatic, terrestrial, and semi‐aquatic‐, and these differences are related to environmentally specific mechanisms such as friction, dirt shedding, and water resistance (Wang et al., 2020). Scale microstructures in sidewinding vipers also provide an illustrative example of this phenomenon: the presence of regularly spaced micro‐pits and the loss of spine‐like micro‐projections in these snakes are indicative of convergent evolution (Riiska et al., 2025). Besides the morphological methods that have been adopted since the first discovery of micro‐ornamentations, molecular mechanisms should also be enlightened in future studies to better understand their history, structure, and function.
