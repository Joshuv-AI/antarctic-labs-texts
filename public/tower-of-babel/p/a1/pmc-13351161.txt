Candida auris Colonization in Hospitalized Patients at a Tertiary Care Center in Saudi Arabia: Clinical Characteristics, Predictors, and Outcomes
Authors: Abdullah A Awadh, Albaraa A Alsharif, Saad H Alahmari, Anmar A Bannani, Mazeed F Albogami, Assaf A Alzahrani, Abdulamajeed Y Ghazali
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
AbstractBackground Candida auris is an emerging fungal pathogen of global concern. Data on its epidemiology in the Middle East remain limited. We characterized the clinical profile, predictors, and outcomes of C. auris colonization among hospitalized patients in Saudi Arabia.MethodsA retrospective cohort study of 322 patients screened for C. auris between 2019 and 2025 at a tertiary care hospital in Riyadh. Patients were classified as C. auris positive (n = 107) or negative (n = 215). Baseline characteristics were compared using Mann–Whitney U and Fisher's exact tests. Logistic regression identified predictors of positivity.ResultsThe C. auris positivity rate was 33.2%, declining from 58.1% in 2021 to 24.8% in 2024 as surveillance expanded. Positive patients were older (median 70 vs 59 years, P < .001) and had longer hospital stays (median 124 vs 21 days, P < .001). Cardiovascular, endocrine, renal, and hematological comorbidities were significantly more prevalent. Age (per 10 years: odds ratio [OR] 1.26, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.12–1.42) and length of stay (per 30 days: OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.12–1.28) were the strongest continuous predictors. All-cause in-hospital mortality was higher in positive patients (51.4% vs 25.1%; OR 3.15, 95% CI 1.90–5.23, P < .001).Conclusions Candida auris–positive patients exhibited advanced age, multimorbidity, prolonged hospitalization, and elevated all-cause mortality. These findings support targeted screening of high-risk populations in Gulf-region healthcare facilities.

Candida auris is an emerging pathogenic yeast first identified in Japan in 2009 [1, 2]. It has since caused healthcare-associated outbreaks across more than 40 countries, prompting the World Health Organization to designate it a fungal priority pathogen [3, 4]. Candida auris demonstrates high rates of fluconazole resistance, particularly among clades I, III, and IV, with variable susceptibility to amphotericin B and echinocandins depending on geographic origin and clade [5, 6]. Its capacity to persistently colonize skin and environmental surfaces facilitates nosocomial transmission [7, 8].

Healthcare-associated C. auris infections have been associated with crude mortality rates of 30%–60% [9, 10]. However, many risk factors for C. auris colonization—including intensive care unit (ICU) admission, prolonged hospitalization, and broad-spectrum antibiotic use—overlap with those for invasive candidiasis caused by other Candida species, and attributing mortality specifically to C. auris versus underlying illness severity remains challenging [11, 12].

In the Middle East, the epidemiology of C. auris remains incompletely characterized despite documented outbreaks [13, 14]. This study aimed to identify the clinical characteristics and independent predictors of C. auris colonization and to evaluate the association between C. auris status and clinical outcomes, at a tertiary care center in Saudi Arabia.

METHODS

Study Design and Setting

This retrospective cohort study was conducted at a tertiary care hospital in Riyadh, Saudi Arabia, including patients screened for C. auris between November 2019 and May 2025. The study was approved by the Institutional Review Board of King Abdullah International Medical Research Center. Informed consent was waived due to the retrospective design.

Screening Protocol

Candida auris screening was performed as part of an active hospital surveillance program. Screening targeted patients admitted to intensive care units, those with prolonged hospitalization (≥14 days), and patients transferred from external healthcare facilities. Axillary and groin swab specimens were cultured on selective chromogenic agar (CHROMagar Candida Plus), with species-level confirmation by matrix-assisted laser desorption/ionization time-of-flight mass spectrometry (MALDI-TOF MS). Patients could undergo repeat screening on subsequent admissions; however, each patient was included only once based on their index screening result.

Study Population

Of 350 records reviewed, 28 patients with indeterminate or missing screening results were excluded, yielding an analytical cohort of 322 patients: 107 C. auris positive and 215 C. auris negative. Classification was based on culture results. All positive patients in this study represent screen-detected colonization; clinical infection (eg, candidemia) was not separately captured in the data collection instrument.

Data Collection and Variable Definitions

Data were extracted from electronic medical records. Variables included demographics, comorbidities (converted from free text to binary present/absent), healthcare exposures, laboratory parameters, length of stay (LOS), and clinical outcomes. “Prior infection” was defined as any documented microbiological infection within 90 days, including bacterial (eg, Klebsiella pneumoniae, Pseudomonas aeruginosa, Acinetobacter baumannii, Methicillin-Resistant Staphlycoccus Aureus (MRSA), and Vancomycin-Resistant Enterococcus (VRE) and fungal (eg, Candida species) infections; it was not restricted to C. auris infection. Length of stay was calculated from admission to discharge and represents total hospitalization, as the exact date of C. auris screening was not recorded. Clinical outcomes were classified as improved, died (all-cause in-hospital mortality), or other.

Statistical Analysis

Continuous variables were expressed as medians (interquartile ranges [IQRs]) and compared using the Mann–Whitney U test. Categorical variables were expressed as frequencies and percentages and compared using Fisher's exact test for all 2 × 2 tables and the χ2 test for multicategory variables. Univariate logistic regression identified predictors of C. auris positivity; continuous predictors were scaled to clinically meaningful increments (age per 10 years, body mass index [BMI] per 5 kg/m2, LOS per 30 days). Variables with P < .20 were considered for multivariate analysis. A 2-sided P < .05 was considered significant. Analyses were performed using Python 3.12 with SciPy [15] and NumPy [16].

RESULTS

Study Population and Demographics

Of 322 patients, 107 (33.2%) were C. auris positive and 215 (66.8%) were C. auris negative. The positivity rate varied by year: 58.1% (18/31) in 2021, 51.4% (18/35) in 2022, 42.9% (18/42) in 2023, and 24.8% (52/210) in 2024, reflecting expansion of surveillance to a broader patient population. Males comprised 58.7% (189/322) of the cohort, with no significant difference between groups (P = .942). Candida auris–positive patients were significantly older (median 70, IQR 58–78 years vs median 59, IQR 34–75 years; P < .001; Figure 1). Patients aged >40 years had 3-fold higher odds of positivity (odds ratio [OR] 3.08, 95% confidence interval [CI] 1.55–6.11, P < .001). Complete baseline characteristics are presented in Table 1.

Comorbidities

Cardiovascular disease (98.7% vs 81.6%, P < .001), endocrine disease (93.7% vs 82.3%, P = .024), renal disease (85.7% vs 60.0%, P = .002), and hematological disease (84.6% vs 65.2%, P = .010) were significantly more prevalent among C. auris–positive patients (Figure 2). Body mass index was higher in the positive group (median 29.2 vs 25.7 kg/m2, P = .002).

Healthcare Exposures

Prior infection within 90 days (89.2% vs 68.8%, P < .001) and ICU admission within 30 days (84.9% vs 65.2%, P = .008) were significantly associated with C. auris positivity. The majority of prior infections were bacterial, including K. pneumoniae, P. aeruginosa, A. baumannii, and multidrug-resistant organisms (MRSA, VRE, and Extended-Spectrum Beta-Lactamase [ESBL]-producing Enterobacterales), suggesting that broad-spectrum antibiotic exposure may be an underlying driver. Readmission within 30 days was less frequent among positive patients (15.0% vs 32.2%, P = .042).

Predictors of C. auris Positivity

Univariate logistic regression identified multiple significant predictors (Table 2, Figure 3). Among continuous variables, the strongest associations were age per 10 years (OR 1.26, 95% CI 1.12–1.42, P < .001), BMI per 5 kg/m2 (OR 1.21, 95% CI 1.04–1.41, P = .011), and LOS per 30 days (OR 1.20, 95% CI 1.12–1.28, P < .001). Among binary predictors, cardiovascular disease (OR 17.39, P = .005), renal disease (OR 4.00, P = .002), prior infection (OR 3.73, P < .001), endocrine disease (OR 3.18, P = .024), ICU admission (OR 3.00, P = .008), and hematological disease (OR 2.94, P = .010) were significant.

Clinical Outcomes

All-cause in-hospital mortality was 51.4% (55/107) among C. auris–positive patients compared with 25.1% (54/215) among negative patients (OR 3.15, 95% CI 1.90–5.23, P < .001; Table 4). Complications occurred in 95.2% of positive versus 64.9% of negative patients (OR 10.70, P < .001). Among C. auris–positive patients, the most common complications were septic shock, multiorgan failure, ventilator-associated pneumonia, acute kidney injury, and cardiac arrest.

Median LOS was 124 days (IQR 87–224) in the positive group versus 21 days (IQR 9–72) in the negative group (P < .001). This association likely reflects bidirectional causality: prolonged hospitalization increases nosocomial C. auris exposure, while C. auris colonization may itself prolong stay through infection control requirements and complications.

Laboratory Parameters

Hemoglobin, white blood cell count, platelet count, and absolute neutrophil count did not differ significantly between groups (all P > .05; Table 1).

DISCUSSION

This study characterizes C. auris colonization among 322 hospitalized patients at a Saudi Arabian tertiary care center. The 33.2% positivity rate, declining from 58% to 25% as surveillance broadened, is consistent with other tertiary settings with active screening programs [17, 18].

Age and LOS emerged as independent predictors. The age association (OR 1.26 per decade) aligns with the literature attributing increased susceptibility to immunosenescence and a higher comorbidity burden [19, 20]. The LOS association (OR 1.20 per 30 days) is consistent with nosocomial transmission dynamics [8, 21], though total LOS conflates prescreening exposure with postscreening hospitalization, limiting causal inference.

The strong association with prior infection (OR 3.73) likely reflects antibiotic-mediated disruption of the microbiome, although we lacked direct data on antibiotic or antifungal exposure. Most prior infections were bacterial, including multidrug-resistant organisms, consistent with reports linking multidrug resistant organism co-colonization to C. auris acquisition [22, 23].

All-cause mortality was 2-fold higher among positive patients (51.4% vs 25.1%). This is concordant with global estimates [9, 10] but should be interpreted cautiously, as higher mortality may partly reflect greater illness severity rather than direct C. auris pathogenicity [24].

Routine laboratory parameters did not differentiate between the 2 groups, reinforcing the essential role of active microbiological surveillance in detection [25]. Our findings support prioritizing screening in elderly, multimorbid patients with prolonged hospitalizations, consistent with current Centers for Disease Control and Prevention recommendations [26].

Limitations

First, the retrospective single-center design limits generalizability. Second, we could not distinguish colonization by C. auris from clinical infection. Third, antifungal susceptibility testing, clade identification, antibiotic/antifungal exposure data, and treatment information were unavailable. Fourth, total LOS could not be separated into pre- and postscreening components. Fifth, the bacteria highly resistant to antibiotics co-colonization data were not collected. Sixth, missing data reduced the effective sample size for adjusted analyses. Finally, all-cause rather than C. auris–attributable mortality was reported, and severity-of-illness scores were unavailable.

CONCLUSIONS

Candida auris–positive patients were older, had more comorbidities, longer hospitalizations, and higher all-cause mortality. Age and LOS were independent predictors. These findings support targeted screening in high-risk populations and underscore the need for prospective studies with detailed microbiological, treatment, and outcome data in the Gulf region.
