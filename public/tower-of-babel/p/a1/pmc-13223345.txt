Leveraging voice memos recorded via smartphones for qualitative data collection
Authors: Megan S. Patterson, Danny Valdez, Joshua S. Yudkin, Tyler Prochnow
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
The purpose of this paper is to discuss voice memos - where participants privately record their spoken responses to qualitative prompts on their smartphones - as a low-burden strategy for qualitative data collection. Collecting primary data has become increasingly challenging for social and behavioral scientists, both in recruiting research participants and securing quality and complete data. Qualitative data collection can pose even greater challenges due to it being time-consuming, cognitively taxing, and potentially socially undesirable for participants. We contend voice memos may be an opportune way of overcoming some of these challenges and describe our experience successfully collecting voice memo data as part of a community-based research study. We share insights on our procedures to collect voice memo data, including the survey tool and logic that could be repurposed for future studies, feasibility of collecting these data, quality of data collected via voice memos, and lessons learned from the process.

Introduction

Primary data collection has become increasingly challenging for social and behavioral scientists, particularly since the COVID-19 pandemic. 1 Researchers face obstacles including non-response bias, 2 distrust in science, 3 participant burnout, 4 and attrition in longitudinal studies.5,6 While online panel services offer a solution to recruitment challenges, they can be cost prohibitive and may yield fraudulent or nonhuman survey participation. 7 In sum, reaching appropriate sample sizes and receiving valid data from participants has become increasingly difficult, costly, and time-consuming.

Challenges to qualitative data collection

Qualitative data collection poses unique challenges beyond those affecting quantitative research. Open-ended responses require greater cognitive effort from participants, as they must formulate and articulate thoughts rather than simply select from predetermined options. 8 This increased burden can lead to shorter, less detailed responses or task abandonment altogether. Additionally, qualitative methods often address sensitive topics, such as health behaviors, trauma, or substance use, where participants may feel uncomfortable providing candid responses, particularly in settings where anonymity is compromised. 9 Traditional qualitative approaches such as focus groups introduce social dynamics that can inhibit honest disclosure; participants may be uncomfortable sharing insights due to the presence of others or discomfort with the facilitator.10,11 One-on-one interviews, while more private, require scheduling coordination and may still produce social desirability bias when participants interact directly with researchers. These challenges underscore the need for innovative qualitative data collection strategies that reduce burden, protect privacy, and facilitate authentic responses.

Voice input as a qualitative data collection strategy

There is a need for participant-centered research that includes participants as partners in the design of qualitative research endeavors to tailor innovative methodologies to overcome real-world obstacles. 12 Patnaik and colleagues 13 proposed one such approach: using voice input to respond to questions, where users call an operator and dictate data from prompts in real time, as a means of reducing respondent burden and yielding more robust open-ended responses. Building on this solution of using voice and audio to respond to questions, as opposed to delivering questions, a small number of studies have explored voice input as a mechanism for providing audible responses to survey items.

Revilla, Couper, and Ochoa 14 polled respondents from a panel study regarding their interest in voice input within web surveys and found that approximately half would like to use voice options to answer open-ended questions online. Voice memos - where participants privately record audible responses to qualitative prompts using a smartphone app - have since been evaluated as a more structured form of this approach. Schober and colleagues 15 found that voice memo responses were better quality and disclosed more sensitive information than typed responses among iPhone users. Lütters, Friedrich-Freksa, and Egger 16 similarly found that voice memos yielded more detailed answers, shorter completion times, and lower reported burden. However, they also reported a 50% dropout rate due to technical issues, and Revilla and Couper 17 found a substantial proportion of respondents unwilling or unable to complete voice recording tasks.

A critical examination of this evidence reveals important patterns regarding when voice methods may outperform or underperform text-based approaches. Voice input appears to yield richer, more candid responses, particularly for sensitive topics where participants may feel more at ease speaking privately than writing15,16 However, this potential must be weighed against meaningful trade-offs. First, technical barriers may reduce completion rates, as evidenced by dropout rates as high as 50% in prior work. 16 Second, reduced burden for some populations, such as those with lower literacy 10 or difficulty articulating complex experiences in writing, 8 may translate to increased burden for those with limited smartphone experience or privacy concerns about voice recordings. 17 Third, while recording alone affords participants privacy, voice data are inherently more identifiable than typed text.

The populations most likely to benefit from voice memo methods may include those who find typing burdensome, those uncomfortable with written expression, or participants responding to emotionally complex prompts. 9 Conversely, populations with lower digital literacy or privacy concerns may face greater barriers. 17 Importantly, the studies reporting the highest dropout rates were conducted with anonymous online panel samples, and contextual factors such as established trust between participants and the research team may meaningfully improve feasibility in community-based settings.18,19

While results from these studies are mixed, most were published before 2020, and the landscape of voice technology has shifted considerably since. Research on mobile communication practices documents the increasing normalization of voice messaging, particularly for asynchronous coordination and in contexts where typing is burdensome. 20 Industry data corroborate this trend, with estimates of over 7 billion voice notes sent daily on WhatsApp 21 and projections of voice assistant use doubling between 2020 and 2024. 22 While these industry sources are not peer-reviewed, they reflect observable shifts in communication behavior relevant to understanding participant willingness to engage with voice-based data collection methods.

Theoretical frameworks

Voice memos can be situated within several complementary qualitative methodology frameworks. First, they represent a form of asynchronous qualitative data collection, where participants provide responses at a time and place of their choosing rather than in real-time interaction with a researcher.23,24 Asynchronous methods offer participants greater flexibility, time for reflection, and reduced social pressure compared to synchronous approaches such as live interviews or focus groups. 25 Second, voice memos align with the growing body of mobile and digital data collection methodologies that leverage ubiquitous smartphone ownership to collect rich data in naturalistic settings.26,27 Mobile methods capitalize on the integration of technology into participants’ daily lives, reducing barriers to participation and enabling data collection that might otherwise be logistically impractical. 28 Third, voice memos reflect principles of participant-centered research, which prioritizes reducing respondent burden, respecting participant preferences, and tailoring methods to meet the needs of diverse populations. 12 By allowing participants to record responses privately on familiar devices, voice memos center participant comfort and autonomy while still yielding meaningful qualitative data. Taken together, these frameworks position voice memos as a theoretically grounded methodological innovation that addresses practical challenges while honoring core principles of qualitative inquiry.

Gaps in the literature

Despite the potential of voice memos for qualitative data collection, significant gaps remain. First, most existing studies predate the widespread normalization of voice messaging technology, 22 and barriers related to unfamiliarity with voice recording may be considerably lower today than when earlier studies were conducted. Second, prior high-attrition studies relied on anonymous online panels,16,17 leaving unanswered questions about whether voice memo methods would prove more feasible in community-based research contexts where trust and engagement with the research team are already established.18,19 Third, the COVID-19 pandemic has fundamentally altered data collection practices, accelerating adoption of digital and remote methodologies,1,29 yet few studies have examined voice memos as a low-burden qualitative strategy in this post-pandemic context. These gaps underscore the need for updated feasibility research on voice memo data collection in community-based setting.

The current study

Our team piloted a mixed-methods study that collected voice memo qualitative data from participants. In this study, we measured the degree of social support and recovery from substance-related addictions, where study participants were recruited from a non-profit organization aimed at leveraging group-based physical activities (e.g., CrossFit, yoga, group hikes, pickleball) to promote addiction recovery and wellbeing. 30 The purpose of this paper is to report on the feasibility and validity of voice memo qualitative data collection in a community-based sample. We will share insights from our pilot work including effective processes for voice memo data collection, challenges and limitations of data collection, changes we made to our protocols to optimize data collection, and implications of this method on future research.

Methods

Participants and recruitment

Members of a non-profit sober active community called The Phoenix, in Denver, Colorado, and Wichita, Kansas, were invited to participate in a study assessing the impact of social connections and networks on health and recovery. Prior to data collection, researchers partnered with Phoenix staff to identify and recruit Phoenix members for study participation. Community partners defined current and active membership with The Phoenix as people in the Denver or Wichita locations who (1) participated in Phoenix programming at least once in the past 30 days and (2) on average, participated in The Phoenix at least once per week. Members also had to be at least 18 years of age and own a smartphone to participate in the study. Phoenix staff identified 98 people who met these criteria across both locations, 77 of whom participated in the study (n=35 from Denver, n=42 from Wichita, response rate = 78.6%;). Ten of the 77 participated in follow-up interviews (see Procedures).

Procedures

All 98 eligible Phoenix members were emailed an invitation and link to participate in an online survey that began with an information document detailing the study’s purpose, procedures, and any risks/benefits associated with participation. Respondents had to agree to participate, and upon providing their electronic consent, were directed to a Qualtrics online survey designed to collect standard survey data and voice memo responses to three prompts.

To complete the voice memo task, participants were given instructions on how to (1) locate a voice memo application on their personal smartphone (this varied based on device type; see the Supplemental File for specific instructions per device), (2) record themselves responding to a prompt, and (3) upload recordings back into Qualtrics. Participants responded to three qualitative prompts via voice memo, which were developed in partnership with The Phoenix to understand the impact of the organization on participants’ recovery. The prompts were standardized across all participants. Please see the Supplemental File for the full voice memo task, including the three prompts. This paper reports on baseline data from an ongoing longitudinal study.

To further evaluate the participant experience with the voice memo task, we followed up with 10 participants post-data collection. Purposive sampling was employed to capture the range of experiences, including one person from each location who did not submit a voice memo, one person from Wichita who submitted their voice memo via email, and finally four people from Denver and three people from Wichita who completed the task through Qualtrics. Interviews were informal and semi-structured in nature, conducted in-person by a lead researcher at existing Phoenix program sites, making use of natural points of contact during regular programming rather than requiring participants to schedule separate sessions. Interviews focused on three areas: perceived ease of the voice memo task, preferences relative to other qualitative methods, and reasons for any difficulties encountered. Given the informal, conversational nature of these interviews and their role as a supplementary feasibility check rather than a primary data source, duration was not formally recorded. The interviewing researcher took detailed notes during each conversation and subsequently summarized key insights across all ten participants.

Audio file specifications

Participants recorded memos using native mobile applications (iPhone Voice Memos or Android Recorder). Files were captured in.m4a (AAC compression) or.wav (uncompressed PCM) formats. Following industry standards for mobile recording, these files utilized a sampling rate of 44.1 or 48 kHz. File specifications varied slightly by device operating system. The average recording length per prompt was 34.77 seconds (SD = 22.15 seconds, range = 9.00–136.02 seconds).

Data Handling

Audio files were transcribed using OpenAI’s Whisper automatic speech recognition model implemented in Python. 31 The pydub library was used to extract recording durations. Following automated transcription, a member of the research team manually reviewed the text to ensure accuracy and to perform deductive de-identification. Any names, specific geographic locations, or unique identifiers mentioned by participants were replaced with generic placeholders (e.g., [Name], [Location]). Transcripts were exported to text files and compiled into a structured dataset for analysis. Interview notes were taken on the researcher’s password-protected computer and not shared with anyone else.

Data analysis

To assess whether voice memos captured authentic reflective speech suitable for qualitative analysis, we conducted a preliminary linguistic analysis examining the distribution of parts of speech (nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs). Research on English language linguistics indicates that typical speaking patterns follow a hierarchical distribution favoring nouns and verbs; however, this distribution shifts toward greater use of adjectives and adverbs when prompts are reflective in nature. 32 We used this pattern as an indicator that participants engaged meaningfully with the prompts rather than providing superficial or incomplete responses. This analysis was conducted using Linguistic Inquiry Word Count (LIWC) following established methods for computerized text analysis. 33 While formal qualitative coding and a thematic analysis of voice memo data was not completed for this paper and represents a future research endeavor, a brief illustrative review of transcribed responses was conducted to demonstrate the qualitative richness of the data (see Results). The interviewing researcher reviewed and summarized key insights from the ten follow-up interviews.

Ethical considerations

While voice memos offer potential advantages for qualitative data collection, this approach also raises important methodological considerations related to privacy, confidentiality, and data security. Unlike text-based responses, audio recordings inherently contain identifiable information through participants’ voices, requiring careful attention to secure storage, transfer, and management of audio files. 17 Researchers must also ensure that informed consent processes clearly communicate how voice data will be handled, who will have access to recordings, and how participant confidentiality will be protected. These considerations are particularly salient when collecting sensitive information, such as experiences with substance use recovery, and should be addressed in study protocols and institutional review board applications.

In the current study, we implemented the following procedures to address these concerns. Raw audio files were transferred immediately from Qualtrics to a password-protected, university-approved encrypted storage environment. Files submitted via email were transferred to secure storage and permanently deleted from email. Access to audio files was restricted to authorized members of the research team. To mitigate the risk of data leaks associated with cloud-based transcription services, all audio was processed locally using the OpenAI Whisper model on a secure local machine. This ensured that no participant audio data was transmitted to, or stored on, external servers. Following automated transcription, a member of the research team manually reviewed the text to ensure accuracy and to perform deductive de-identification (see Data Handling).

Participants were informed of these data management procedures in the consent document. The informed consent document explicitly noted that audio recordings would contain identifiable information through participants’ voices and explained that the study was approved as confidential rather than anonymous. Participants were informed of this potential risk prior to providing consent. Master audio files are retained in encrypted storage only for the duration of the analysis phase and are scheduled for permanent deletion following the mandatory data retention period required by the Institutional Review Board. This study was approved by the Texas A&M University IRB (Protocol # IRB2023-0301D) as confidential rather than anonymous.

Results

All study participants (n=77) owned a smart phone, consistent with data from the Pew Research Center stating 90.9% of adults in the United States own a smartphone. 27 Participants’ mean age was 38.54 years (SD=10.54, range = 24–71), 53% (n=41) were male, and 75.3% (n=58) were Non-Hispanic White. Almost all participants (91%, n=70) successfully uploaded voice memo data into Qualtrics. Of the 7 (9.1%) who did not upload their data to Qualtrics, four (5.2%) emailed their voice recordings to the research team, and only three (3.9%) did not complete the voice memo task at all. Chi-square tests of independence were conducted to examine associations between task completion (those who uploaded their voice memos into Qualtrics vs. those who did not) and demographic characteristics. No significant associations were found between task completion and sex (χ2 = 0.03, p = .86), race (χ2 = 1.26, p = .87), or location (χ2 = .02, p = .89). An independent samples t-test revealed no significant difference in age between those who completed the task and those who did not (t = .032, p = .77).

To assess whether voice memos captured authentic reflective speech, we conducted a preliminary linguistic analysis examining the distribution of parts of speech. In English language linguistics, typical speaking patterns follow a hierarchical distribution of nouns (45%), verbs (25%), adjectives (10%), and adverbs (5–10%). However, this distribution shifts when prompts are reflective in nature, with greater use of adjectives and adverbs. 32 Our distribution aligned with reflective speaking styles. Two of the three prompts, which assessed the impact and feelings about the program, contained a greater presence of adverbs, followed by adjectives, verbs, and nouns. The final prompt, which measured personal success in the program, showed a similar pattern. These findings suggest that voice memos successfully captured thoughtful, reflective responses consistent with natural human speaking patterns, supporting the feasibility of this method for collecting meaningful qualitative data.32,33

To illustrate the qualitative richness of voice memo data, we conducted a brief thematic review of transcribed responses. Across participants, several prominent themes emerged regarding the program’s impact: (1) community and belonging, (2) support for recovery, (3) improvements in physical health, (4) enhanced mental health and confidence, (5) the value of a non-judgmental environment, (6) a sense of purpose through service, and (7) support for relationships affected by addiction. For example, one participant described the importance of peer connection: “Most of my friends are people that I met at the Phoenix who I spend time with every day. I have people I can call and confide in when I am struggling and need support.” Another participant reflected on the unique value of shared understanding in recovery: “There’s a comfort I think in knowing that you can go to a place where other people have the same story as you or have suffered in the same way as you. But there isn’t a requirement to talk about it. There’s just a subtle underlying of knowing.” These responses demonstrate that voice memos can capture meaningful, reflective qualitative data suitable for thematic content analysis.

Four key insights emerged from follow-up interviews. First, we learned that all 10 follow-up participants stated a preference for voice memos to both typing out their responses to open-ended questions in Qualtrics and to participating in interviews. They reported that the voice memo task was simpler and faster than typing or writing out their responses, that they appreciated the added privacy of responding to a question without having to talk to a researcher. For example, one interviewee said, “…it reminded me of when I send my sister voice memos because it’s too much to text. A lot of times just recording and sending is way easier that typing out a response.”

Second, several interviewees reported voice memos provided enhanced privacy and openness. Interviewees felt they could share more openly using this approach, especially when discussing their experience with substance use recovery, as shown in this quote: “…frankly, going over my struggles with recovery can feel embarrassing, even when a person is not being judgmental. Talking into my phone gave me leeway to just say what I needed to say.”

Third, when asked why they would prefer this method over an interview, participants appreciated the convenience and flexibility of voice memos. They preferred to complete all study protocols in one session as opposed to having to find a time to complete an interview with a researcher after survey completion. For example, one interviewee stated, “this was definitely an efficient way to participate in a study. It was nice that I could just do it on my own time, all at once, and not have to figure out scheduling a time to meet with someone for an interview. I actually did mine while out for a walk, so I didn’t have to worry about someone listening while I’m on a Zoom call or something like that.”

And lastly, we learned about technical barriers to completion. Those who did not upload their voice memos into Qualtrics stated they forgot to upload their files (i.e., they left the survey to record their responses and never went back to upload their audio files into the survey), or they had closed their browser and could not figure out how to return to their survey to upload their responses into Qualtrics. The respondent who emailed their response said, “Yeah, I realized I had hit submit on my survey before I uploaded the file. Instead of just redoing the whole survey I just decided to email [PI’s name] my recordings.”

Discussion

This paper contextualized the feasibility and potential utility of collecting qualitative data via smartphone voice memo applications. We reported results from a community-based pilot study, where the aim of the study was to assess social connections and addiction recovery. Voice memos were included as part of the methodology and were incorporated into an online survey. Based on a 91% completion rate and reports from participants that they would prefer voice memos to other forms of qualitative data collection, we strongly contend voice memos serve as a useful, innovative, and effective way to collect qualitative data from study participants. By allowing participants to record memos at their convenience on personal devices without the pressure of typing or learning a new software, we argue this method may offer a promising alternative for collecting qualitative data, particularly when reducing respondent burden is a priority. While prior research suggests voice memos may yield more detailed and candid responses than typed input,15,16 the current study did not directly compare voice memos to other qualitative methods, and future research is needed to evaluate comparative data quality across approaches.

The success of voice memo data collection in this study aligns with and advances several theoretical frameworks introduced earlier in this paper. First, the high completion rate and participant-reported preference for voice memos over both typed responses and live interviews supports the value of asynchronous qualitative methods23,24 as a means of preserving participant agency. Unlike synchronous interviews, which require participants to formulate and articulate responses in real time under social pressure, the asynchronous voice memo format afforded participants control over when, where, and how they responded, as illustrated by one participant who completed the task while on a walk. This autonomy is consistent with evidence that asynchronous methods reduce social pressure and allow for greater reflection, 25 and may explain participants’ reported sense of openness when discussing sensitive recovery experiences. Second, the finding that participants preferred voice memos to typed responses is consistent with participant-centered research principles, 12 which prioritize reducing cognitive burden and tailoring methods to participant preferences. Speaking is a lower-effort mode of expression than writing for many individuals, and the voice memo format capitalized on a familiar, everyday technology rather than requiring participants to learn new software or navigate an unfamiliar interface. Third, the integration of voice memo recording into a smartphone-based survey platform exemplifies the broader paradigm of mobile and digital qualitative methods,26,28 which leverage ubiquitous device ownership to collect rich data in naturalistic settings. Taken together, these findings suggest that voice memos are not merely a practical convenience but a methodologically grounded approach that meaningfully enhances participant agency, reduces cognitive load, and may facilitate more candid disclosure, particularly on sensitive topics, compared to more traditional qualitative data collection formats.

While previous research is sparse on voice memo data collection techniques, one study from 2018 and another from 2019 both posed concerns for voice input due to participant dropout and a large proportion of participants being unable or unwilling to complete the voice memo task. Our study did show more promising results with 91% of participants completing the task of uploading their recordings directly to the online survey, and an additional 5% submitting their recordings via email. Our success could be a result of several factors. First, everyday use of voice-based software on smartphones has increased significantly since 2019, 22 which could result in a reduction in user error. Given people are likely more comfortable now using voice memo-type software than they were even five years ago, the unwillingness and inability to complete tasks cited in prior studies16,17 may be a less glaring issue in research today. Second, our study was a community-based project, where researchers worked in collaboration with stakeholders and participants through the duration of the project. Research shows that when participants have access to and trust the investigator team, they are more likely to complete data collection tasks.18,19 Similarly, when participants in longitudinal studies experience a sense of community with fellow participants, program staff, and/or researchers, they are less likely to drop out of the study. 34 This could be why we had a lower dropout rate than previous studies, and why we had respondents find ways to submit their data even if they did not complete the task in their surveys. The few other studies that tested the feasibility of voice input were all online panel studies, where participants were anonymous to and disconnected from the research team and other participants.16,17

Finally, our linguistic analysis provided preliminary evidence that voice memos can capture authentic reflective speech. The distribution of parts of speech in participant responses aligned with patterns expected when individuals engage thoughtfully with reflective prompts, 32 suggesting that the asynchronous, self-recorded nature of voice memos did not compromise the depth or authenticity of responses.

Beyond feasibility, the presence of reflective language patterns in participant responses has implications for the quality of voice memo data as assessed against established qualitative research standards. In qualitative inquiry, credibility (i.e., the extent to which findings accurately represent participants’ lived experiences) is partly established through evidence that participants engaged authentically and thoughtfully with study prompts. 35 The linguistic distribution observed here, characterized by elevated use of adjectives and adverbs consistent with reflective rather than superficial speech, suggests that participants were genuinely processing and articulating their experiences rather than providing perfunctory responses. This is particularly meaningful given the sensitive nature of the topic, where surface-level or socially scripted responses are a credible concern. Similarly, dependability (i.e., the consistency and stability of data collection processes across participants) is supported by the standardized prompts and uniform recording procedures employed in this study, which ensured that the conditions for eliciting reflective responses were held constant. Taken together, these characteristics suggest that voice memo data collection, when implemented with clear and thoughtful prompts, has the potential to yield qualitative data that meets core standards of rigor, positioning it as a credible complement or alternative to more traditional qualitative methods.

Potential biases and limitations

Several potential biases and limitations should be acknowledged when interpreting the findings of this study. First, participants were exclusively recruited from The Phoenix, a specific recovery-oriented community organization, and therefore represent a non-random, purposive sample. Members of this organization are likely to be more motivated, engaged, and trusting of the research team than individuals in the broader population of people in recovery. Selection bias may have contributed to the high task completion rate (91%) observed in this study and limits the generalizability of findings to other community-based settings or populations without established organizational infrastructure.

Secondly, technology bias is an inherent feature of this methodology. Smartphone ownership was a required eligibility criterion, meaning that individuals without access to a smartphone were systematically excluded from participation. Although smartphone ownership is high among U.S. adults, 27 this requirement may exclude older adults, individuals with lower socioeconomic status, or those in rural areas with limited technological access. Furthermore, because participants were not screened for digital literacy or prior comfort with voice memo applications, the extent to which unfamiliarity with voice recording technology may have affected response quality or task completion for some participants remains unknown.

In addition, social desirability bias cannot be fully eliminated from this study, even given the private, asynchronous nature of voice memo recording. Although participants recorded their responses independently and without direct researcher interaction, the knowledge that recordings would be reviewed and transcribed by the research team may have influenced participants to present themselves in a socially favorable light, particularly when discussing sensitive topics such as substance use recovery. This form of bias is well-documented in self-report methods 9 and warrants consideration even when participants report feeling more comfortable speaking into their phones than interacting face-to-face with a researcher.

A few methodological limitations warrant mention. The small sample size for follow-up interviews (n = 10) limits the depth and representativeness of qualitative insights regarding participant experience with the voice memo task. While purposive sampling was used to capture a range of experiences across submission methods and locations, findings should be interpreted as exploratory and illustrative rather than definitive, particularly regarding experiences of those who encountered technical difficulties or did not complete the task. Relatedly, this pilot study lacked a formal measure of perceived task difficulty. Future studies should incorporate standardized measures of this kind. For example, Likert-scale items assessing ease of each step could better identify which aspects of the process may require additional support. Finally, the absence of a formal audio quality assessment is a limitation. While all files were successfully transcribed and our linguistic analysis supported the presence of reflective language, we did not systematically evaluate technical audio quality factors such as background noise or recording clarity. Future studies should incorporate standardized audio quality metrics and may consider providing participants with brief guidance on optimal recording conditions.

Future directions

Several promising directions emerge from this work that warrant attention in future research. First, replication in larger and more diverse samples, including older adults, individuals with lower digital literacy, non-English speakers, and populations recruited outside of established community organizations, is essential for establishing the external validity of voice memo data collection. Second, hybrid designs that pair voice memo recording with brief follow-up interviews, as piloted here on a small scale, merit further development. Such designs could preserve the low-burden, asynchronous advantages of voice memos while capturing the depth and clarification that conversational interviewing affords, particularly for complex or emotionally nuanced topics. Third, the growing availability of automated transcription tools (e.g., OpenAI Whisper, as used in this study) and computational text analysis methods, including sentiment analysis, linguistic inquiry, and natural language processing, opens new possibilities for efficiently analyzing voice memo data at scale, an avenue that future methodological work should explore systematically. Finally, while this study was conducted in the context of addiction recovery and community health, the voice memo approach is likely transferable to other sensitive research domains, including mental health, workplace wellbeing, and chronic illness, where reducing respondent burden and facilitating candid disclosure are similarly prioritized. Collectively, these directions suggest that voice memo data collection is a methodology with broad and underexplored potential across the social and behavioral sciences.

Lessons learned

It is important to share what we believe were a few lessons learned in the process of implementing a voice memo strategy for qualitative data collection. First, we involved community stakeholders in the design of the study, including which prompts to use for qualitative data collection and the user experience completing voice memos in the context of a Qualtrics survey. We learned it is important to prompt respondents to complete their survey on a smartphone or tablet at the very start of the survey due to their need to use an application on their phone or tablet to complete and upload the voice memos. We also learned that IOS, Android, and Google devices operate slightly differently, and require different sets of instructions for the respondent. We included a question in the survey for participants to indicate which kind of device they were using and leveraged display logic to give them the appropriate set of instructions for their device. We learned it is essential the online survey is set up so that the respondent can close their browser and reopen their survey later, that way if the participant accidentally closes their browser they can still return and upload their files. Additionally, learned that in the future we will provide contact information within the file upload portion of the survey, just in case participants are unable to submit their files online. Our study was already approved as confidential as opposed to anonymous, but this could require additional IRB approval due to a person’s identity being compromised if they submit data via email. We acknowledge some IRBs may require additional security measures to store voice data; however, this modification is minimal and would not affect data collection or quality.

Conclusion

Though challenges for primary data collection remain, our pilot data suggest voice recordings are an innovative and feasible way to collect qualitative data from study participants, particularly in community-based studies. Importantly, this work is a first step in addressing documented gaps in the prior literature. Earlier studies raised significant concerns about the viability of voice memo data collection, reporting dropout rates as high as 50% and substantial proportions of participants unwilling or unable to complete voice recording tasks.16,17 Our findings suggest that these barriers may be substantially reduced in community-based research contexts where trust, engagement, and technological familiarity are higher. This contribution is meaningful not only for researchers working in community health settings, but for the broader methodological landscape of qualitative inquiry.

The potential applications of voice memo data collection extend across several research contexts. In large-scale surveys with embedded qualitative components, voice memos offer a low-burden mechanism for collecting rich, open-ended data without the scheduling demands of interviews or the cognitive effort of written responses. For populations where in-person data collection is impractical, including geographically dispersed samples, individuals with limited mobility, or those in the post-pandemic landscape of remote research, voice memos may provide a flexible and accessible alternative. In longitudinal studies, where participant burden and attrition are persistent challenges, the convenience and brevity of the voice memo format may support sustained engagement over time. We also observed that our linguistic analysis was supportive of voice memos as a way to accurately capture participants’ reflections, suggesting this method can yield data of sufficient depth and authenticity for meaningful qualitative analysis. While voice memos should not replace all qualitative data collection methods as they lack the capacity for follow-up questioning characteristic of interviews and focus groups, they represent a promising and underutilized tool for collecting mixed-methods data that might otherwise be unattainable. As qualitative researchers continue to navigate the challenges of primary data collection in an increasingly digital world, voice memo methods offer a theoretically grounded, participant-centered, and practically viable path forward.
