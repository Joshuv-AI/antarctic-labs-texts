Trends in PCOS Incidence, Prevalence, and DALYs Across Five BRICS Nations, 1990–2050: A Decomposition, Age-Period-Cohort, and Bayesian Forecasting Analysis Based on GBD 2021
Authors: Mengjun Zhang, Jun Zhang, Meizhu Lin, Yijing Zheng, Sainan Chen, Yan Lin, Zhuanji Fang
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
ObjectivePolycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) burden has risen globally, yet comparative analyses across BRICS nations (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa)—representing 40% of the global population under rapid epidemiological transition—remain limited. This study aimed to assess PCOS burden in BRICS from 1990 to 2021, compare with global trends, and project outcomes to 2050 using decomposition, age-period-cohort (APC), and Bayesian methods.MethodsUsing Global Burden of Disease Study 2021 (GBD 2021) data restricted to females, we analyzed age-standardized incidence, prevalence, and disability-adjusted life year (DALY) rates (ASIR, ASPR, ASDR) with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). Das Gupta decomposition partitioned changes into population growth, ageing, and epidemiological change. APC modeling assessed temporal trends, and Bayesian APC (BAPC) forecasting projected burden to 2050. Estimated annual percentage changes (EAPCs) were calculated with 95% confidence intervals (CIs).ResultsFrom 1990 to 2021, global incident cases rose by 55.9% and prevalent cases by 89.6%, with global ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR increasing 27–28%. BRICS burden remained below global averages but showed significant upward trends. China and India had the fastest growth (EAPC for DALYs: 2.01 [1.99–2.03] and 2.06 [2.03–2.08]) versus the global average (0.81 [0.80–0.82]). Decomposition showed epidemiological change dominated in China (68–78%) and Russia (92–138%), whereas population growth predominated in Brazil (79–98%) and South Africa (66–69%). APC analysis identified the highest burden in ages 20–44, with rising period and cohort risks except in Brazil. BAPC projects global ASDR to increase from 15.46 to 20.20 per 100,000 by 2050. China shows the largest rise (13.38 to 21.39), surpassing the global level around 2045, followed by India (12.24 to 14.53). The 10–29 age group will contribute most future cases.ConclusionRising PCOS burden in BRICS, especially China and India, is driven by heterogeneous factors: epidemiological changes (China, Russia) versus population growth (Brazil, South Africa). Negative net drift in age-specific rates coexists with rising total burden, reconcilable via decomposition. These findings imply targeted strategies: lifestyle interventions for China/Russia, healthcare capacity expansion for Brazil/South Africa, integrated approaches for India, and adolescent-focused prevention across all BRICS.

Introduction

Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is one of the most common endocrine and metabolic disorders affecting reproductive-aged women, with an estimated prevalence of 10–20% worldwide.1 It is the leading cause of anovulatory infertility in women aged 15–49 and is associated with long-term complications including type 2 diabetes, cardiovascular disease, and endometrial cancer.2,3 Hyperinsulinemia, obesity, hyperandrogenism, and inflammation interact synergistically in its pathogenesis, yet the definitive etiology remains unclear, and effective mechanism-based treatments are lacking.4,5

The global burden of PCOS has risen substantially over the past three decades. The Global Burden of Disease (GBD) study has enabled systematic tracking of this trend, revealing that the burden is particularly pronounced in regions with moderate economic development.6,7 A recent GBD 2021-based analysis confirmed that global age-standardized PCOS prevalence increased by approximately 28% between 1990 and 2021, with the fastest growth observed in South Asia and East Asia.8 For example, Miazgowski et al analyzed GBD 2016 data and found that PCOS prevalence in Russia declined by 12.3% from 1990 to 2006 but rebounded by 15% thereafter.9 A more recent study focusing on China reported significant increases in both prevalence and DALYs between 1990 and 2021.10 A 2024 systematic review of 45 countries confirmed that middle-Sociodemographic Index (SDI) regions are driving the global increase in PCOS incidence and DALYs, while high-SDI regions have begun to plateau.11 These findings underscore the dynamic nature of PCOS epidemiology and the need for ongoing surveillance.

Interpretation of temporal trends in PCOS is, however, complicated by diagnostic heterogeneity. Since 1990, three major diagnostic criteria have been used in parallel: the NIH criteria (1990, requiring hyperandrogenism and oligo-anovulation), the Rotterdam criteria (2003, adding polycystic ovarian morphology), and the AE-PCOS criteria (2006, emphasizing metabolic features). The Rotterdam criteria, now most widely adopted, yield prevalence estimates approximately 1.5–2 fold higher than the NIH criteria.12,13 A 2022 meta-analysis of 31 studies (n = 45,000) reported that the choice of diagnostic criteria alone can account for up to a twofold variation in reported PCOS prevalence, underscoring the importance of standardized case definitions in temporal trend analyses.14 This evolution in diagnostic definitions may artificially inflate observed temporal trends—a limitation that GBD estimates partially address by standardizing case definitions across time and regions, but one that nonetheless warrants careful interpretation.

BRICS nations—Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa—account for approximately 40% of the global population and have experienced rapid socioeconomic transformation, with significant shifts in lifestyle patterns, urbanization, and demographic structures over recent decades. These changes may substantially influence reproductive health disorder epidemiology.15 Rapid urbanization has promoted sedentary lifestyles, increased consumption of high-calorie processed foods, and accelerated the obesity transition—all key modifiable risk factors for PCOS.16,17 Recent evidence indicates that obesity prevalence among reproductive-aged women in BRICS countries increased by 50–100% between 1995 and 2020, with the steepest rises observed in China and India.18 Concurrently, these nations face unique health system challenges, including disparities in diagnostic capabilities, variable health literacy, and inequalities in healthcare access.19 Together, these factors are driving a significant epidemiological transition within BRICS, characterized by a shift from communicable to non-communicable diseases.20 This convergence of rapid lifestyle change, demographic expansion, and health system heterogeneity makes disease-specific research focused on these countries particularly meaningful.

Despite growing global attention to PCOS, several critical knowledge gaps remain. First, no comparative multi-country analysis of PCOS burden has been conducted across all five BRICS nations; existing studies have focused on high-income Western populations or single BRICS countries. Second, the relative contributions of population growth, aging, and epidemiological changes to the rising burden have not been quantified for these nations. Third, the distinct effects of age, period, and birth cohort—crucial for distinguishing biological aging from diagnostic practice shifts or early-life exposures—remain unexplored in the BRICS context. Fourth, future burden trajectories through 2050 have not been projected.

Our study introduces three methodological innovations to address these gaps. First, we apply Das Gupta decomposition analysis to partition the total change in PCOS burden into population growth, aging, and epidemiological change, distinguishing whether rising burden is driven by demographic expansion (requiring healthcare capacity scaling) or by increases in per-capita risk (requiring lifestyle and metabolic interventions). Second, we employ age-period-cohort (APC) modeling to disentangle age effects (biological maturation), period effects (eg, diagnostic criteria updates or policy shifts), and cohort effects (eg, early-life obesity or endocrine disruptor exposure)—a distinction essential for PCOS, where rising burden could reflect true metabolic deterioration, improved diagnostic detection, or intergenerational changes. Without APC analysis, these drivers cannot be separately identified. Third, we use Bayesian APC (BAPC) forecasting to project burden trajectories to 2050 with quantified uncertainty, supporting long-term health system planning.

Accordingly, this study provides the first comparative assessment of PCOS burden across all five original BRICS nations using GBD 2021 data, integrating decomposition, APC, and BAPC forecasting to quantify country-specific drivers and project burden to 2050 with quantified uncertainty. Specifically, we (1) describe temporal trends in incidence, prevalence, and DALYs (1990–2021); (2) decompose contributions of population growth, aging, and epidemiological change; (3) quantify age, period, and cohort effects via APC; and (4) project burden to 2050 using BAPC. Our findings offer epidemiological insights to inform context-specific prevention strategies for each BRICS nation, though causal inferences cannot be drawn from this ecological analysis.

Methodologies and Materials

Data Sources and Case Definition

The GBD 2021 study systematically compiles data on 371 diseases and injuries from 1990 to 2021 across 21 regions and 204 countries and territories.21 Data are maintained by the Institute for Health Metrics and Evaluation (IHME) and publicly accessible via the Global Health Data Exchange (GHDx) platform (http://ghdx.healthdata.org).21,22 GBD aggregates data from multiple sources, including population surveillance, scientific research, administrative reports, hospital records, and health insurance claims.21

Critically, GBD estimates for PCOS are not derived from a single diagnostic framework but are harmonized from heterogeneous epidemiological sources using Bayesian mixed-effects meta-regression (DisMod-MR 2.1) and spatiotemporal Gaussian process regression (ST-GPR).23 These models standardize and calibrate raw data, addressing comparability issues across sources and imputing missing values. The GBD case definition for PCOS is based on ICD-10 code E28.2,24 which captures conditions diagnosed under any of the three major criteria (NIH 1990, Rotterdam 2003, or AE-PCOS 2006).25 This study analyzed age-standardized incidence rates (ASIR), prevalence rates (ASPR), and DALY rates (ASDR) for PCOS from 1990 to 2021 globally and in BRICS countries. As human subjects were not directly involved, ethical approval was not required.

Our analysis focuses on the original five BRICS members (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa) due to complete and consistent time-series data from 1990 to 2021. Newly added members (Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Indonesia) were excluded due to shorter or inconsistent data records, which would compromise longitudinal comparability required for APC and decomposition analyses.

Statistical Analysis

All analyses were restricted to females. For incidence, the age range was limited to 10–54 years (reproductive window); for prevalence and DALYs, all ages were included. Estimates are reported as counts and age-standardized rates per 100,000 population (ASIR, ASPR, ASDR) with 95% uncertainty intervals (UIs). Age standardization used the GBD standard population. Uncertainty intervals were propagated through all subsequent analyses via Monte Carlo simulation (1000 draws). A p-value <0.05 was considered statistically significant.

Trend Analysis (Joinpoint Regression)

We used Joinpoint regression (R package “segmented” v1.6) to identify significant changes in PCOS burden trends. The maximum number of joinpoints was set to 5. Model selection used the Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) with 4500 Monte Carlo permutations for significance testing, assuming autocorrelated errors. The estimated annual percentage change (EAPC) and average annual percentage change (AAPC) were calculated with 95% CIs. EAPC reflects segment-specific trends between joinpoints; AAPC represents the weighted average of EAPCs over the full period (1990–2021).26

Decomposition Analysis

We performed decomposition analysis following the Das Gupta method to partition the total change in incident cases, prevalent cases, and DALYs from 1990 to 2021 into three additive components: population growth, population ageing, and epidemiological change.27,28 The decomposition is order-invariant under the Das Gupta formulation, which uses sequential standardization with equal weighting. Population growth reflects change attributable to variations in total population size, assuming constant age-specific rates and structure. Population ageing captures change due to shifts in age distribution (eg, proportion of reproductive-aged women), assuming constant population size and rates. Epidemiological change represents the residual component capturing changes in age-specific disease rates from non-demographic factors (diagnostic improvements, lifestyle shifts, healthcare access, or true incidence changes). Positive values indicate contribution to burden increase; negative values indicate mitigation.

Age-Period-Cohort (APC) Analysis

We used APC modeling to estimate independent effects of age, period, and birth cohort on PCOS burden, assuming a Poisson distribution. The analysis was restricted to females aged 10–54 years (reproductive window) for the period 1992–2021 using 5-year intervals (age: 10–14, 15–19, …, 50–54; period: 1992–1996, …, 2017–2021; cohort: 1938–1942, …, 2007–2011). We repeated the APC analysis restricting the age range to 15–49 years (excluding ages <15 and >49) to assess the impact of including age groups where PCOS diagnosis is clinically challenging. The results were compared with the primary analysis (10–54 years) using net drift, period RR (2017–2021), and cohort RR. For comparability without data truncation, the primary analysis used the 1980–1984 cohort (fully captured within 10–54 years), while the sensitivity analysis used the 1985–1989 cohort (fully captured within 15–49 years). Sensitivity estimates falling within the 95% CIs of primary estimates were considered robust.

To address the identifiability problem caused by the linear dependency among age, period, and cohort, we applied the intrinsic estimator (IE) method as implemented in the R package “apc” (v14). The IE method has been previously described for APC modeling29 Reference categories were set as age 25–29 years, period 2005–2009, and cohort 1970–1974. Overdispersion was evaluated using the dispersion parameter (deviance/df); values below 1.2 indicated no significant overdispersion, supporting the appropriateness of the Poisson assumption. Model outputs included net drift (estimated annual percentage change in age-specific rates over time), local drift (age-specific annual percentage changes), and relative risks (RR) for period and cohort effects, where RR > 1 denotes a higher risk relative to the reference. The longitudinal age curve illustrated the pattern of burden changes with age. The distinctions among net drift, local drift, and other temporal trend metrics (eg, EAPC from Joinpoint regression) are summarized in Table S2. All analyses were conducted using the “apc” package (v1.4) with the IE parametrization, which constrains the sum of period and cohort effects to zero Similar APC modeling approaches have been applied in other disease burden studies.30,31

Bayesian APC (BAPC) Forecasting

We used the BAPC model implemented in the R package “BAPC” (v0.4) to project PCOS burden from 2022 to 2050. The BAPC framework, which integrates integrated nested Laplace approximations (INLA) for Bayesian inference, has been previously described.32 The analysis was performed using the existing, publicly available BAPC package without custom modification of the underlying model code. Prior specifications: Random walk of order 2 (RW2) priors for period and cohort effects, with hyperparameters selected via empirical Bayes. Convergence was assessed using the Geweke diagnostic (z-score <1.96 for all parameters). To further verify the predictive validity and robustness of the BAPC model, a strict sample-splitting backtesting verification strategy was adopted. Specifically, the complete dataset (from 1990 to 2021) was divided into a training subset (from 1990 to 2011) and a validation subset (from 2012 to 2021). By comparing the predicted values generated by the model with the actual observed values of GBD 2021 during the validation period, the mean absolute error (MAE) and root mean square error (RMSE) were used as quantitative evaluation indicators to assess the predictive performance of the model. Global population projections for 2022–2050 were obtained from GBD 2021.21 Uncertainty is reported as 95% credible intervals. Projections beyond 2035 should be interpreted with caution due to widening uncertainty.

Uncertainty Propagation and Missing Data

All GBD 2021 estimates are reported with 95% UIs generated via Monte Carlo simulation (1000 draws from the posterior distribution, with 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles defining the UI). Uncertainty was propagated through all analyses (Joinpoint, decomposition, APC, BAPC) by performing each analysis on each of 1000 draws and calculating median estimates with 95% CIs. For missing data, GBD 2021 employs DisMod-MR 2.1, which leverages available data (systematic reviews, hospital records, surveys, claims databases) and covariates (region, Sociodemographic Index) to generate complete time-series estimates.20 We used GBD-provided estimates directly without additional imputation. Additional sensitivity analyses (alternative age restrictions, reverse sequential decomposition) yielded consistent findings. No multiple-comparison corrections were applied, as analyses were prespecified and confirmatory.

All analyses were performed using R software (version 4.5.1) with the following packages and specific functions: “segmented” (v1.6, segmented() for Joinpoint), “apc” (v1.4, apc.fit() with IE method), “BAPC” (v04, bapc()), and “decompose” (v1.0, dasgupta()).

Results

Trends in PCOS Incidence, Prevalence, and DALYs (1990–2021)

The global burden of PCOS increased substantially from 1990 to 2021, with incident cases rising by 55.9% and prevalent cases by 89.6% (Table S2). Global age-standardized rates (ASRs) for incidence, prevalence, and DALYs each increased by approximately 27–28% over the study period.

Within BRICS nations, age-standardized rates remained below global averages but showed consistent upward trends. China recorded the highest age-standardized prevalence rate (ASPR) in 2021, with a growth rate (EAPC = 2.01, 95% UI: 1.99–2.03) exceeding the global average (EAPC = 0.81, 95% UI: 0.80–0.82); both were statistically significant as the uncertainty intervals did not cross zero. India showed a similar growth trajectory (EAPC = 2.06, 95% UI: 2.03–2.08). Russia maintained the lowest age-standardized burden among BRICS, while Brazil displayed the smallest increase (EAPC = 0.19, 95% UI: 0.15–0.23) (Table S1; Figures 1 and 2). Globally, population growth accounted for a 67.92% increase in incident cases, while epidemiological change contributed an additional 42.59% increase; population ageing was associated with a 10.51% reduction. A similar pattern was observed for prevalence and DALYs (Figures 4B and C). Among BRICS countries, the relative contributions of drivers varied substantially. In Brazil (78.75–98.33%) and South Africa (66.39–69.14%), population growth was the predominant factor. In contrast, epidemiological changes were the primary contributors in China (67.95–78%) and Russia (92.19–138.13%). In India, population growth and epidemiological factors contributed nearly equally. In contrast, Russia, India, China, and South Africa exhibited negative net drifts, with China’s drift being less negative than those of the other countries. BAPC Model Analysis

A BAPC model was applied to project trends in ASIR, ASPR, and ASDR for PCOS from 2022 to 2050 (Figure 6). Globally, ASDR is projected to rise steadily from 15.46 per 100,000 (95% UI: 12.98–18.21) in 2021 to 20.20 (95% UI: 16.45–24.68) by 2050. Among BRICS countries, China is expected to show the largest increase, with ASDR climbing from 13.38 (95% UI: 10.92–16.15) in 2021 to 21.39 (95% UI: 16.98–26.52) by 2050, surpassing the global level around 2045. India follows, with ASDR projected to increase from 12.24 (95% UI: 9.87–14.98) to 14.53 (95% UI: 11.45–18.02) over the same period. Brazil, Russia, and South Africa are predicted to experience relatively modest ASDR growth, with overlapping uncertainty intervals suggesting stable trends (Figures 6, S5 and S6). Sensitivity Analysis and Model Validation

To assess the impact of age range specification, we repeated the APC analysis restricting ages to 15–49 years, excluding early adolescence (before menarche) and perimenopause. As shown in Table S3, the sensitivity analysis yielded results nearly identical to the primary analysis (10–54 years). For China, the net drift changed from 2.104 (95% UI: 2.069–2.140) in the primary analysis to 2.095 (95% UI: 2.063–2.127) in the sensitivity analysis; for India, the corresponding values were 2.244 (2.200–2.288) versus 2.255 (2.215–2.294). Period RRs (2017–2021) and cohort RRs (1980–1984 for primary, 1985–1989 for sensitivity) also showed minimal changes, with all sensitivity estimates falling within the 95% UIs of the primary estimates, supporting the robustness of our APC findings. Moreover, Model validation based on split-sample back-testing demonstrated robust predictive performance of the BAPC model. All predicted incidence, prevalence, and DALY rates for the validation period (2012–2021) yielded minor prediction errors, with both MAE and RMSE values less than 0.10 (data not shown), which meets the acceptable threshold for epidemiological burden prediction models.

Discussion

This study provides the first systematic comparative assessment of PCOS burden across the original five BRICS nations using GBD 2021 data. From 1990 to 2021, the age-standardized burden of PCOS in BRICS countries remained below global averages but showed consistent upward trends. Decomposition analysis revealed heterogeneous patterns: epidemiological changes accounted for most of the increase in China (67.95–78%) and Russia (92.19–138.13%), whereas population growth predominated in Brazil (78.75–98.33%) and South Africa (66.39–69.14%). APC modeling identified the highest burden among women aged 20–44, with increasing period and cohort risks in all BRICS countries except Brazil. Bayesian projections suggest that by 2050, China will show the largest absolute increase (ASDR from 13.38 to 21.39 per 100,000), surpassing the global level around 2045, followed by India. The 10–29 age group is projected to contribute the largest share of future incident cases. As all analyses are based on ecological, model-derived data, the interpretations below should be considered associative rather than causal.

The divergent patterns across BRICS countries have distinct implications. The negative contribution of population ageing observed in several BRICS countries (eg, −10.51% globally, −29.08% in India) indicates that shifts in age distribution have mitigated the overall burden increase. Specifically, a declining proportion of reproductive-aged women or a demographic shift toward older ages beyond the typical PCOS-affected window (20–44 years) reduces the expected burden. This phenomenon is particularly relevant in countries undergoing rapid demographic transitions, such as China and Russia. A negative ageing effect does not imply that PCOS burden is decreasing in absolute terms; rather, it suggests that without this demographic shift, the burden increase would have been even larger. Values exceeding 100% in decomposition (eg, Russia 138.13%) indicate that the factor’s contribution more than fully accounts for the total increase, with other factors having a negative (mitigating) effect. In Russia, epidemiological change contributed 138.13% while population ageing contributed −38.13%, meaning that demographic shifts partially offset the burden increase driven by non-demographic factors.

The large positive contribution of epidemiological change in Russia (92.19–138.13%) suggests that non-demographic factors have been the primary drivers of the observed burden increase, especially given Russia’s relatively stable population size. A critical caveat is that “epidemiological change” is a composite residual category that cannot distinguish true increases in disease occurrence from improved diagnostic awareness, changes in coding practices, increased healthcare access, or rising obesity rates. Potential contributors include improved diagnostic awareness following post-Soviet healthcare reforms, increased screening for endocrine disorders, rising obesity prevalence, or true increases in age-specific incidence.33 Russian population-based studies have documented substantial increases in adult obesity during the 1990s–2010s. Although the exact trend among reproductive-aged women from 1995 to 2015 requires further verification, the overall pattern aligns with Eastern European trends in GBD assessments. Rising obesity burden thus represents a plausible, though not definitive, biological pathway contributing to PCOS risk.34 Similarly, the dominance of epidemiological change in China (67.95–78%) is consistent with rapid lifestyle transitions; overweight and obesity prevalence among reproductive-aged Chinese women has increased substantially, with national surveys documenting a rise from 19.9% to 27.9% among women aged 18–44 years (1992–2002).35 However, improved diagnostic awareness following economic development and healthcare expansion may also contribute substantially.

In contrast, the predominance of population growth in Brazil and South Africa suggests that per-capita PCOS risk may have remained relatively stable, with the rising burden associated primarily with the expanding number of reproductive-aged women.25 Available evidence indicates relatively stable age-standardized PCOS prevalence in these regions. A prevalence study in Salvador, Brazil reported PCOS prevalence of 8.5% (95% CI: 6.80–10.56) among women of reproductive age in primary healthcare settings.36 Globally, PCOS prevalence estimates typically range from 6.5% to 8.0% across different populations, depending on diagnostic criteria used.37 However, underdiagnosis, varying diagnostic practices, and healthcare system differences may contribute to observed stability. Additionally, cross-country differences may partly reflect heterogeneity in healthcare systems, diagnostic access, or GBD source data quality rather than true epidemiological divergence.21,25

Our APC analysis showed negative net drift (declining age-specific DALY rates) in China, India, Russia, and South Africa alongside rising age-standardized rates (positive EAPC). This apparent contradiction is resolved by recognizing that net drift reflects age-specific trends, whereas EAPC reflects trends in age-standardized rates weighted by population age structure. Negative net drift indicates that within specific age groups, DALY rates per 100,000 women have decreased. However, total burden can still rise if the population is growing or if a larger proportion of women enter high-risk age groups (a “population denominator effect”). In China and India, decomposition analysis showed that population growth and epidemiological changes offset the declining age-specific rate trends. Thus, negative net drift may reflect genuine improvements in age-specific outcomes (potentially due to better disease management), while rising age-standardized rates reflect demographic and diagnostic changes. This interpretation remains associative, as causality cannot be established with ecological data.

Our age-effect analysis revealed that PCOS burden began to rise at ages 10–14, plateaued between 20–44 years, and declined thereafter, a pattern consistent with PCOS manifesting after menarche.25,38 However, we acknowledge that ages 10–14 represent a clinically challenging diagnostic window where clinical diagnosis should be deferred until at least 2 years post-menarche to avoid overdiagnosis.38,39 A sensitivity analysis restricting ages to 15–49 years yielded nearly identical results (Table S3), supporting robustness.25,40 For projections showing high incident counts in the 10–19 age group, age at menarche is a relevant contextual variable—mean age at menarche in general populations ranges from 12 to 14 years38,39. This biological plausibility supports our projections, though GBD estimates for this age group may capture subclinical or suspected cases rather than confirmed diagnoses.25,38,40

Period effects increased over three decades in all BRICS countries except Brazil, where period risk after 2010 was lower than before 2005. An important methodological confounder is the evolution of PCOS diagnostic criteria over the study period. The Rotterdam criteria (2003), now most widely adopted, yield prevalence estimates approximately 1.5–2-fold higher than the earlier NIH criteria (1990).12 This diagnostic shift may partly explain the rising period effects observed after 2003–2009, particularly in countries that adopted the Rotterdam criteria during this period. Brazil’s lower period risk may reflect different diagnostic practice patterns, successful public health interventions, underdiagnosis, or modeling instability. Cohort effects showed women born after the mid-1980s had higher risk estimates than earlier cohorts. While our study cannot directly assess causal mechanisms, prior meta-analyses have reported associations between early-life exposure to endocrine-disrupting chemicals (EDCs) and childhood obesity with later PCOS risk, providing a biologically plausible but speculative explanation.41,42

Our BAPC projections suggest that China may surpass the global ASDR by 2045, followed by India. These forecasts are broadly consistent with BAPC-based projections by Safiri et al (2022).43 However, several caveats are essential. First, projections assume continuation of historical trends and do not account for potential future changes in diagnostic criteria, obesity prevalence, reproductive behavior, or healthcare policies. Second, widening credible intervals after 2035 indicate substantially increasing uncertainty; long-term projections should be interpreted as scenario-based estimates rather than definitive predictions. Third, the projected high incidence in the 10–14 age group (particularly in India) is driven largely by population size and demographic trends rather than clinically confirmed diagnoses, and may overestimate clinically meaningful burden in very young adolescents.

The convergence of our decomposition, APC, and BAPC results points to potential public health considerations for BRICS countries. Where epidemiological change dominates (China, Russia), interventions targeting modifiable risk factors—including obesity prevention, physical activity promotion, and early screening—may be relevant. Where population growth predominates (Brazil, South Africa), expanding healthcare infrastructure and screening services to accommodate growing at-risk populations may be a priority. For India, an integrated approach combining both dimensions may be warranted. These recommendations are offered as hypotheses based on observed associations, not as definitive policy prescriptions.44

Our findings also offer several clinical takeaways. The concentration of burden in women aged 20–44 supports routine PCOS screening in primary care for those presenting with menstrual irregularities, obesity, or infertility. The high projected burden in adolescents suggests a lower threshold for clinical suspicion, though definitive diagnosis should follow guideline recommendations (2–3 years post-menarche). Rising epidemiological-change-driven burden in China and Russia calls for heightened clinician awareness of PCOS among women with metabolic syndrome features. Finally, the projected increase across all BRICS nations underscores the need for integrated reproductive-metabolic health services bridging endocrinology, gynecology, and primary care.

This study has several strengths. It provides the first systematic comparative assessment of PCOS burden across all five original BRICS nations using harmonized GBD 2021 data, integrating decomposition analysis, APC modeling, and BAPC forecasting within a single framework to quantify country-specific drivers (epidemiological change vs. population growth) and project future burden with quantified uncertainty. Back-testing validation of the BAPC model and a sensitivity analysis of the APC model (restricted to ages 15–49 years), which yielded results nearly identical to the primary analysis, collectively support the credibility and robustness of our projections and findings. All estimates are reported with 95% uncertainty intervals propagated through each analytical step, adhering to GBD reporting standards.

Several limitations must be acknowledged. First, we included only the original five BRICS members due to data consistency requirements; recently added countries (Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, UAE, Indonesia) were excluded because of shorter or inconsistent time-series data.7 Future studies should include them as data accumulate. Second, GBD estimates are model-dependent and subject to under-diagnosis and variable data quality across BRICS health systems. The observed “epidemiological change” in China and Russia may reflect improved diagnostic awareness rather than true biological risk increases—a distinction our analysis cannot resolve. Additionally, GBD’s DisMod-MR 2.1 assumes missing data are non-informative, which may not hold universally. Third, the evolution of PCOS diagnostic criteria (NIH 1990, Rotterdam 2003, AE-PCOS 2006) represents a major methodological confounder that may artificially inflate temporal trends; GBD partially addresses this by standardizing case definitions, but residual confounding likely remains.12 Fourth, our models identify associations, not causality, and cannot elucidate underlying biological, environmental, or social mechanisms. Fifth, BAPC projections assume past trends continue smoothly; forecast uncertainty widens after 2035, and unforeseen changes cannot be accounted for.45 Sixth, PCOS is rarely diagnosed before age 14; projections for the 10–14 age group likely overestimate clinically confirmed cases.38 Seventh, the inclusion of non-reproductive age groups in APC analyses (addressed by sensitivity analysis) and the reliance on ecological data limit causal inference.8 Finally, as with all GBD-based studies, our results are subject to limitations of primary data, including underreporting, misclassification, and geographic heterogeneity.25

Conclusion

This study reveals a rising PCOS burden across BRICS nations from 1990 to 2021, with China and India showing the fastest increases. Non-demographic factors (which may include improved diagnostic awareness, rising obesity, or true increases in disease occurrence) were the primary contributors in China and Russia, whereas population growth predominated in Brazil and South Africa. Burden concentrated among women aged 20–44, with newer birth cohorts showing higher cohort-associated relative risk estimates. Projections to 2050 suggest that the 10–29 age group will account for most future increases, though long-term forecasts carry substantial uncertainty. Based on these findings, we propose context-specific policy considerations: lifestyle interventions for China and Russia, healthcare capacity expansion for Brazil and South Africa, integrated strategies for India, and adolescent health programs across all BRICS. Several caveats apply: findings are based on modeled GBD estimates and may be influenced by underdiagnosis and changing diagnostic practices; at least part of the observed increase may reflect improved disease recognition over time; and projections are conditional on continuation of historical trends. With these caveats, the findings provide useful epidemiological insights for context-specific public health planning in BRICS nations.
