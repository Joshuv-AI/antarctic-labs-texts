The Influence of Sensory Processing on Executive Function in Young Autistic Children
Authors: Jamie L. Courtland, Grace T. Baranek, Lauren DeMoss, Elizabeth Glenn, Hannah Riehl, Geraldine Dawson, Kimberly L. H. Carpenter
Published: 2026

ABSTRACT
PurposeDifferences in sensory responsivity and executive function are common in autistic individuals and impact daily functioning, however how they relate to one another in autistic children is unknown. We tested the hypothesis that differences in sensory responsivity are associated with executive function difficulties in young autistic children.MethodsSensory responses (sensory hyperreactivity; sensory hyporeactivity; SIRS—sensory interests, repetitions, and seeking behaviors) and executive function abilities were measured in 63 autistic children, ages 36 to 72 months. Sensory behaviors were assessed through caregiver reports and play-based observations, while executive function was measured via caregiver report. Generalized linear models analyzed whether sensory responsivity predicted executive function, accounting for developmental level, age, sex, ADHD, and anxiety.ResultsWhen measured via caregiver report, higher levels of SIRS behavior and sensory hyporeactivity were associated with global executive function challenges in autistic children. When measured via observational assessment, only SIRS behavior was significantly predictive of global executive function difficulties in autistic children. Exploratory subanalyses of the relationship between SIRS behavior and executive function subdomains revealed a significant relationship between higher SIRS behavior and working memory difficulties when measured by both caregiver report and observational scores.ConclusionsResults suggest that specific types of sensory response differences, namely SIRS behaviors, are associated with executive function difficulties in young autistic children, particularly in the domain of working memory. These data suggest that early supports around sensory responsivity differences could improve executive function outcomes in autistic children. In addition, these findings highlight the importance of multimodal approaches to sensory measures.

Introduction

Sensory differences and challenges with executive function are highly prevalent in autistic individuals and can significantly impact daily function, as well as the ability to benefit from intervention (Ben-Sasson et al., 2019; Boyd et al., 2010; Crane et al., 2009; Crasta et al., 2023; Demetriou et al., 2018; Granader et al., 2014; Kern et al., 2006; Leung et al., 2016; Rosenthal et al., 2013). Sensory differences have been conceptualized in multiple ways, but one common framework recognizes three main types of sensory differences, including hyporeactivity (sensory under-responsiveness, e.g. lower perception of painful stimuli), hyperreactivity (sensory over-responsiveness, e.g. aversion to loud noises or certain textures), and sensory interests, repetitions, and seeking behaviors (SIRS, e.g. spinning or gravitating to certain textures)(Ausderau et al., 2016; Ben-Sasson et al., 2019; Masters et al., 2023; Scheerer et al., 2021). Relationships among different types of sensory processes and their underlying neurobiology remain open questions. Autistic individuals often exhibit more than one sensory difference (i.e. both SIRS and hyperreactivity) and can show differences in one or more sensory systems (i.e. vision, audition, proprioception, olfaction, and gustation) (Boyd et al., 2010; Kiep et al., 2023).

Several studies have shown that autistic individuals have difficulties with executive function (EF). EF, broadly speaking, is a cognitive domain that coordinates and monitors goal-directed processes. EF encompasses multiple functions, including planning, impulse control, attention shifting, working memory, and cognitive flexibility (Cristofori et al., 2019). Research on EF in autistic individuals has shown that specific aspects of EF, such as cognitive flexibility or impulse control, are often challenges for autistic individuals (Demetriou et al., 2018; Granader et al., 2014; Lee et al., 2023; Rosenthal et al., 2013).

Great heterogeneity in EF strengths and difficulties exists among autistic individuals, even when controlling for developmental age, suggesting that there may be additional factors influencing EF abilities in autism (Kiep et al., 2023). One such factor might be sensory responsiveness differences. Bottom-up neural processing models suggest that lower-order sensory information can influence higher-order cognitive processes, including executive cognition (Kiep et al., 2023; Pastor-Cerezuela et al., 2020). A bottom-up processing framework aligns with neurodevelopmental perspectives on autism, as sensory pathways arise early in fetal development prior to higher-order structures required for executive function (Falck-Ytter & Bussu, 2023; Robertson & Baron-Cohen, 2017; Russo et al., 2025). Within this conceptual framework, alterations to sensory information processing (e.g., sensory overload) could lead to difficulty synthesizing pertinent information and performing goal-related tasks (Adams et al., 2015; Kiep et al., 2023; Stevenson et al., 2014). Structural and functional neuroimaging data further support this theory, as autistic individuals display altered connectivity in sensory networks, as well as greater activation of sensory networks during cognitive tasks compared to non-autistic individuals (Russo et al., 2010, 2025; Sahyoun et al., 2010; Soulières et al., 2009). In addition, neurophysiological analyses revealed a correlation between sensory gating difficulties and IQ in autistic individuals, suggesting a potential pre-attentive impact of sensory processing on higher-order cognitive capacities (Orekhova et al., 2008).

One of the first studies to investigate the relationship between sensory responsivity and EF in autistic individuals was conducted by Boyd and colleagues (Boyd et al., 2009). This study relied on caregiver reports of sensory features, repetitive behaviors, and executive function in children aged 6–17 years old with “high functioning” autism. They found that composite sensory features (as measured by the sensory questionnaire, SQ), were not correlated with composite EF abilities (as measured by the Behavioral Rating Inventory of Executive Function, BRIEF). However, given the study examined a broad range of developmental ages and exclusively used caregiver-report measures, whether a relationship between sensory and EF alterations existed in more homogeneous autistic populations remained an open question (Boyd et al., 2009).

Subsequent studies have found a relationship between sensory responsivity differences and EF. In a study of 4 to 16 year-old autistic children, Fernandez-Prieto et al. found that the EF subdomain of emotion regulation significantly mediated the relationship between sensory responsivity differences and externalizing behaviors (i.e., aggression) in a multiple outcome analysis (Fernandez-Prieto et al., 2021). However, similar to Boyd et al., they acknowledged that the broad age range studied could dilute possible age-based developmental effects and use of caregiver questionnaires could limit generalizability of their findings (informant-based questionnaires used: the child sensory profile-2, CSP-2, and the child behavior checklist, CBCL). A 2020 study by Pastor-Cerezuela et al. was one of the first to investigate the relationship between sensory responsivity and EF in young autistic children (4–8 years old) compared to typically developing children, as well as incorporate clinical measures of EF (Pastor-Cerezuela et al., 2020). They found that sensory response differences in autistic children (measured via total sensory scores of a teacher-reported questionnaire, the Sensory Processing Measure, SPM) predicted EF differences, such as lower levels of inhibitory control, auditory sustained attention, and short-term verbal memory (Pastor-Cerezuela et al., 2020). While their findings suggested a hierarchical relationship between sensory responsivity and EF, they acknowledged that their study was limited to teacher reports of sensory responses.

Two recent studies in autistic adults suggest that a relationship between sensory responsiveness differences and EF difficulties may persist into adulthood. Crasta et al. found that self-reported sensory differences (particularly hyporeactivity and SIRS behavior, as measured by the Adolescent/Adult Sensory Profile, AASP) significantly mediated the relationship between a clinical measure of attention and a self-reported social responsiveness measure (Crasta et al., 2023). Kiep et al. found self-reported hyporeactivity explained most of the variance in EF amongst autistic individuals (Kiep et al., 2023). Together, these studies suggest that relationships between sensory responsiveness differences and EF can present early in life and persist into adulthood.

However, thus far no study has focused on the preschool-aged autistic population or used observational measures of sensory responsivity to investigate the relationship between sensory responsiveness and EF in autistic children. The current study used multimodal assessments (caregiver reports in conjunction with clinical assessments) to investigate the relationship between specific aspects of sensory responsivity differences (SIRS, hyperreactivity, and hyporeactivity) and EF in young autistic children. Both caregiver report (Sensory Experiences Questionnaire, SEQ-3) and observational assessment (Sensory Processing Assessment, SPA) of sensory subcategories were utilized because of their difference in scope, which impacted the degree and type of sensory responsiveness detection. For instance, in SEQ-3, hyporeactivity is a broader-based construct, encompassing questions such as “my child does not respond to pain”, whereas in the SPA, hyporeactivity is primarily an orienting-based measure (Baranek et al., 2006, 2007). Therefore, while the SEQ-3 and SPA capture the same sensory constructs of interest, their distinct outputs provide a more nuanced evaluation of the relationship between sensory responsivity and EF.

Specifically, the current study sought to understand whether structured observational measures of sensory responsivity are significant predictors of caregiver-reported global executive function difficulties in a cross-sectional analysis of preschool-aged autistic children. We hypothesized that the presence of sensory responsiveness differences in autistic children would be associated with EF difficulties.

Methods

Participants

Participants were 70 children between 36 and 72 months of age diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder. Participants were recruited through community flyers and brochures, emails, social media posts, and the Duke Autism and Brain Development Research Center’s volunteer registry. A diagnosis of autism spectrum disorder was confirmed or established using the Autism Diagnostic Observation Schedule – Generic (Lord et al., 2000) and the Autism Diagnostic Interview – Revised (Lord et al., 1994). Autism severity scores ranged from levels 1 to 3. Exclusion criteria included having a known genetic disorder (e.g. Fragile X), vision or hearing problems, a significant motor impairment (e.g. cerebral palsy), chronic or acute medical illness, and having a seizure in the last year, a seizure disorder, or being on medication for seizures. One child was excluded for not meeting strict DSM-IV criteria for ASD. Six additional children were excluded from analyses due to incomplete questionnaire completion. A total of 63 autistic children were included in the analyses.

Children of the same age range who did not have a diagnosis or suspected diagnosis of autism were also recruited from the community and research registry and comprised a non-autistic comparison cohort of 45 children. Additional exclusion criteria for this group included having a biological sibling or parent diagnosed with autism spectrum disorder or developmental delay. Parents of non-autistic children were given the Strengths and Difficulties Questionnaire (SDQ) as a screening tool for internalizing and externalizing difficulties (Goodman, 1997, 2001). The SDQ contains 25 questions that form domains in emotional symptoms, conduct, hyperactivity/inattention, peer relationship problems, and prosocial behavior. Internalizing difficulties consisted of the emotional and peer relationship domains, whereas the externalizing difficulties consisted of the conduct and hyperactivity/inattention scales. Children were excluded from the non-autistic comparison cohort if they met clinical cut-off scores, or above the 90th percentile for age expectancy, in either domain.

Participant demographics are reported in Table 1. Caregivers were compensated for their time, and children received a toy at each visit to the center for their participation. All caregivers were consented to participate in the study and, when able, children provided verbal assent to the study. All study procedures were approved by the Duke University School of Medicine Institutional Review Board for research with human subjects (Pro00053510).

Observational Assessments

Cognitive Assessment

For the majority of autistic participants, general intellectual functioning was measured using the Stanford–Binet Scales of Intelligence Fifth Edition (SB5), Abbreviated Battery IQ (ABIQ; (Roid, 2003). The SB5 is a valid and reliable measurement of IQ in the general population (Roid, 2003), and the ABIQ largely reflects the full-scale IQ score for children with autism (Coolican et al., 2008). If the SB5 routing subtests were not expected to yield a valid score (e.g., inability to achieve a basal due to limited expressive/receptive language, significant behavioral interference, and cognitive level), children received the Mullen Scales of Early Learning: AGS Edition (MSEL; (Mullen, 1997). For those children receiving the MSEL, the Early Learning Composite (ELC) Standard Score was used as a measure of full-scale IQ (Mazurek et al., 2013). For children without autism, the Differential Abilities Scale (DAS) was used for children ages 3:6 – 5:11, and the MSEL was used for children 3:0–3:6, as the DAS can only yield a valid composite score for children 3:6 and older (Bishop et al., 2011; Elliott et al., 1990; Farmer et al., 2016). Because there were “floor” effects for the ELC for many children who were administered the MSEL, developmental quotients (DQ) were calculated for the purpose of controlling for developmental level in statistical analyses. These developmental quotients were calculated by dividing age equivalency, or mental age, by chronological age for all cognitive assessments.

Sensory Processing Assessment (SPA)

The SPA (Baranek et al., 2007) is a play-based behavioral observational assessment administered in a semi-structured format, approximately 20 min in duration. It is designed to assess sensory response patterns from approximately 6 months through 6 years of age. The SPA enables naturalistic observation of a child’s behavioral responses and orienting to tactile, auditory, and visual sensory modalities in social and nonsocial contexts through playful interaction with novel toys with different sensory attributes. The SPA yields construct scores in the domains of hyperreactivity, hyporeactivity, and SIRS behavior. The hyperreactivity construct consists of ratings of avoidance of novel toys and defensive responses to orienting items. The hyporeactivity construct consists of the degree of non-responding to the orienting items, while the SIRS construct measures the degree of unusual sensory interests/seeking/repetitious behaviors observed during the assessment (Boyd et al., 2010). The SPA was administered by research staff trained by the creator of the measure (GTB). Training included in-person didactic sessions around measure administration and scoring. Examiners had to achieve 90% reliability on two training videotape administrations, followed by feedback and 90% scoring reliability on two live administrations before being approved to administer the assessment. Scoring reliability and fidelity of assessment administration was maintained throughout the course of the study through monthly skills labs. Furthermore, 25% of cases were double-coded using video-recordings for reliability. Reliability of sensory constructs was calculated using the intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC). Reliability for all sensory constructs was good to excellent (Hyper = 0.85, Hypo = 0.97, SIRS = 0.72). The SPA was selected because it measures approach/avoidance behaviors (hyperresponsivity) and orienting behaviors (hyporesponsivity) rather than general sensory processing or integration and is sensitive to these responsivity patterns as early as infancy.

Caregiver Report/Interview Measures

Behavior Rating Inventory of Executive Function – Preschool Version (BRIEF-P)

The BRIEF-P (Gioia et al., 2003) is a 63-item parent report measure of executive functioning for preschool-aged children (2.0 to 5.11 years). The parent reports the frequency of behaviors on a 3-point scale: (1 = never, 2 = sometimes, 3 = often). These responses are summed and compared with normative data to create a t-score for the domains and indexes. These questions yield 5 clinical subscales (inhibit, shift, emotional control, working memory, and plan/organize), and 3 indices (Inhibitory Self-Control (ISCI), Flexibility (FI), and Emergent Metacognition (EMI). The BRIEF-P composite is based on three indexes: Inhibitory Self-Control (ISCI), Flexibility (FI), and Emergent Metacognition (EMI). An overall measure of executive functioning is captured with the General Executive Composite (GEC) score. Higher BRIEF-P scores suggest a higher level of difficulty in specific executive function domains. Validity of this scale has been established with internal consistency and test-retest reliability (α’s=0.80–0.97, r’s=0.78–0.90). This scale has been used to discriminate clinical samples, such as autism and ADHD, from nonclinical samples in different domains (Gioia et al., 2003). BRIEF-P scores have been shown to have strong correlations with laboratory-based measures of EF in preschool-aged children, supporting its use in this population (Boudreau et al., 2018; Garon et al., 2016). Of note, 7 of the 70 autistic participants did not have completed BRIEF scores, so EF analyses were performed on 63 autistic participants.

The Sensory Experiences Questionnaire (SEQ-3 )

The SEQ-3 (Baranek, 2009) is a 105-item caregiver questionnaire designed to measure children’s responses to sensory stimuli in the context of daily activities and routines. The SEQ-3 has been validated for children ages 2 –12Y and provides mean scores in the domains of (1) Sensory Hyperreactivity, (2) Sensory Hyporeactivity, (3) SIRS, and (4) Enhanced Perception (Ausderau et al., 2014; Baranek, 2009). Higher scores indicate greater sensory related behaviors (i.e. greater hyperreactivity, hyporeactivity, or SIRS). The SEQ-3 has been shown to discriminate autistic children from non-autistic children and children with other developmental delays (Ausderau et al., 2014; Baranek et al., 2006). The SEQ was selected over other measures of sensory processing, as it measures the constructs of sensory hyperresponsivity and hyporesponsivity across all sensory domains and within social and non-social constructs, which is not possible with other measures.

Preschool Age Psychiatric Assessment (PAPA)

Given high prevalence of comorbid anxiety and ADHD in autism (25–40%), DSM-IV anxiety and ADHD symptoms were assessed with the PAPA, a caregiver interview that assesses for psychopathology in young children (Egger & Angold, 2004; Khachadourian et al., 2023; M.-C. Lai et al. 2019a, b). The PAPA is based on the parent-version of the Child and Adolescent Psychiatric Assessment (CAPA), which has previously been used to assess co-occurring psychiatric disorders in autistic populations (Simonoff et al., 2008). The PAPA uses a highly structured protocol, using specific questions and probes that place the onus on the research staff throughout the interview to ensure interviewees (1) understand the question being asked; (2) provide clear information on behavior or feelings relevant to the symptom; and (3) report the symptom at a pre-specified level of severity as defined in an extensive glossary. When symptoms are reported, their frequency, duration and dates of onset are also collected, to determine whether they meet the symptom overlap and duration criteria for the various Diagnostic and Statistical Manual diagnoses. A three month “primary period” is used rather than a longer period, because shorter recall periods are associated with more accurate recall (Angold & Costello, 2000; Costello et al., 1998; Roberts et al., 2020; Simonoff et al., 2008). Diagnoses and symptom scales are generated by computer algorithms. The PAPA includes assessment of most DSM diagnostic criteria insofar as they are relevant to younger children, plus all items in the Diagnostic Classification: 0–3R (Egger & Angold, 2004). A study of the test-retest reliabilities of the PAPA concluded that the diagnostic reliability of the PAPA is on a par with those achieved by older child, adolescent and adult psychiatric interviews, with kappas ranging from 0.36 to 0.79, and test-retest intraclass correlations for DSM syndrome scale scores ranging from 0.56 to 0.90 (Egger et al., 2006). Previous studies have used the PAPA to assess co-occurring psychiatric disorders in autistic children (Hollocks et al., 2023).

Data Analysis

Statistical analyses were conducted using R Studio (version 2023.06.2, R Core Team, 2023). Chi-squared statistics and unpaired t-tests were used to compare patient demographics between autistic and non-autistic groups in Table 1 and Mann-Whitney U tests with Benjamini-Hochberg corrections for multiple comparisons were used to compare sensory response categories between autistic and non-autistic populations in Fig. 1 (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995). Correlations for Table 2 were performed using the Pearson method or Kendall rank method (if non-normal distribution) and corr function in R. Linear models were performed using the lm function of the “stats” R package (version 4.3.1, R Core Team, 2023). We utilized generalized linear models to predict executive function scores using sensory reactivity variables. Slope coefficients, standard errors, and t-values were obtained from models that included covariates of age, sex, DQ, and Vineland adaptive functioning composite scores for Tables 3 and 4, Column 1; and age, sex, DQ, Vineland adaptive functioning composite scores, anxiety, and ADHD symptom score variables for Tables 3 and 4, Column 2 and for Table 5. For Table 3, the main predictor was caregiver-reported SEQ-3 summary scores of hyperreactivity, hyporeactivity, or SIRS behavior. For Table 4, the SPA sensory scores were the main predictors- either hyperreactivity, hyporeactivity, or SIRS behavior. For Table 5, SIRS behavior was the main predictor for each model (SEQ-3 SIRS summary scores for left column, SPA SIRS scores for right column). The dependent variable was a caregiver reported measure of executive function from the BRIEF-P, the global executive composite (GEC) score, for all but Table 5, that instead predicted subdomains of executive function from the BRIEF-P. We performed bootstrap resampling (n = 1000) using the boot function in R to estimate the variability of the regression coefficient of each variable in predicting EF difficulties. The 95% confidence intervals (CI) reported in each table reflect bootstrapped analyses. To account for multiple testing, we calculated adjusted p-values for the main predictors using the Benjamini-Hochberg method (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) with an alpha of 0.05. Assumptions of generalized linear models were met. Correlations for Supplemental Tables 1 and 2 were performed using the Kendall rank method and corr function in R. Correlation strengths were defined as: 0.10–0.39 - weak correlation, 0.40–0.69 - moderate correlation, and 0.70–0.89 - strong correlation (Schober et al., 2018). For Supplemental Table 3, generalized linear models analogous to those used for Tables 3 and 4 were used on the non-ASD dataset to predict global executive function scores from sensory reactivity variables.

Results

Participant Characteristics

As can be seen in Table 1, the autistic and non-autistic groups differed significantly in terms of age, with autistic children being older by a few months on average than non-autistic children (t(99) = 2.5, p = 0.02). The ratio of males to females across groups was statistically different (X2 = 6.1, p = 0.013), with the autistic group having a larger proportion of male participants, as expected (Zeidan et al., 2022). There were also significant differences in developmental quotient (DQ) among autistic and non-autistic children, with the non-autistic children having on average higher DQ (t(99)= −9.9, p < 0.0001). There were no significant differences in the reported race or ethnicity of participants.

Expected Sensory Responsivity Differences Found in Young Autistic Children Compared to Non-autistic Children

Autistic children had higher caregiver-reported sensory scores compared to non-autistic children, as measured by the SEQ-3 (Fig. 1A). Autistic children also had higher observational sensory scores compared to non-autistic children, as measured by the SPA (Fig. 1B). As seen in Fig. 1, SEQ and SPA categories of SIRS, hyperreactivity, and hyporeactivity were significantly elevated in autistic children compared to non-autistic children. Therefore, the following analyses focus on the autistic population, however equivalent analyses for non-autistic children can be seen in Supplemental Table 2.

Correlation Between Sensory Testing Modalities

As both caregiver-reported and clinician-scored sensory response measures revealed elevated scores in autistic children, we next investigated the correlation between experimental measures for a given sensory subcategory in the autistic population. As can be seen in Table 2, SEQ-3 and SPA sensory subscores were modestly correlated with each other (SIRS-SIRS = 0.32, p = 0.001, Hyperreactivity-Hyperreactivity = 0.21, p = 0.02, and Hyporeactivity-Hyporeactivity = 0.34, p = 0.006).

Relationship Between Caregiver-reported Sensory Responsiveness Measures and Global Executive Function in Autistic Children

Given that past research supported a relationship between caregiver-reported sensory and executive measures in older or more heterogenous populations of autistic individuals, we first sought to investigate this relationship in our population of young autistic children. Using the SEQ-3 (Baranek, 2009), a caregiver report measure, we investigated whether summary scores of SEQ-3 subcategories were predictive of global EF difficulties as measured by the BRIEF-P (Gioia et al., 2003). SIRS, hyperreactivity, and hyporeactivity are all significant predictors of GEC scores when controlling for sex, age, developmental quotient (DQ), and Vineland adaptive functioning composite scores in autistic children (Table 3; Model 1). Given the high prevalence of co-occurring anxiety and ADHD in autism, we then controlled for the possibility of these symptoms contributing to the association between sensory scores and executive function (Khachadourian et al., 2023; M. C. Lai et al. 2019a, b). We ran three additional linear models with SIRS, hyperreactivity, and hyporeactivity as the predictors, and anxiety and ADHD symptom score variables (obtained from the PAPA) added as covariates along with sex, age, DQ, and Vineland adaptive functioning composite scores (Table 3; Model 2). After addition of these covariates, SIRS and hyporeactivity remained significant predictors of global executive functioning (i.e., GEC). Sensory hyperreactivity, however, was not a significant predictor when accounting for anxiety and ADHD symptoms. Of note, low to no significant correlation was seen between sensory and EF measures, with the exception of SEQ-3 hyporreactivity (which demonstrated a moderate correlation, 0.42; Supplemental Table 1). In addition, no correlation was seen between sensory measures and anxiety/ADHD scores (Supplemental Table 2). Together these correlational findings suggest low likelihood of potential circularity or confounding variables in the linear models.

Relationship Between Observational Sensory Responsivity Measures and Global Executive Function

We next tested the hypothesis that elevated SIRS, hyperreactivity, and hyporeactivity observational scores on the SPA would be associated with higher levels of caregiver reported EF difficulties on the BRIEF-P in autistic children (Table 4; Model 1). SIRS as measured in the SPA, was a significant predictor of global executive composite scores when controlling for age, sex, DQ, and Vineland adaptive functioning composite scores, but hyperreactivity and hyporeactivity were not. Similar to our SEQ-3 analyses, we then added psychiatric covariates of anxiety and ADHD to the models (Table 4; Model 2). After addition of these psychiatric covariates, SIRS behavior remained a significant predictor of global executive functioning. This suggests that the relationship between SIRS and global executive functioning is not explained by concurrent anxiety, ADHD, sex, age, DQ, nor adaptive functioning, nor the correlations between these variables in the preschool age period.

Relationships Between SIRS Behaviors and Specific Domains of EF

We performed an exploratory subanalysis of the relationship between SIRS behavior and EF subdomains, given its relationship to GEC when measured by either caregiver report or clinical observation (SEQ-3 or SPA, respectively). All models tested whether SIRS was a significant predictor of BRIEF executive function subdomains (emotional control, working memory, shifting attention (Shift), impulse inhibition (Inhibit), and planning/organizing capacity (Plan/Organize)) in the young autistic population through linear regression models in R. Models included covariates of age, sex, developmental quotient (DQ), anxiety, and ADHD symptoms. The results of these analyses are shown in Table 0. Using either sensory measure, SIRS behavior was a significant predictor of working memory scores in young autistic subjects. Otherwise, the relationship between SIRS and other EF subdomains differed based on sensory testing modality utilized, with SIRS being predictive of shifting attention and impulse inhibition scores via the SEQ-3 (left column), but not the SPA (right column), and predictive of emotional control and planning/organizing via the SPA (right column), but not the SEQ-3 (left column).

Discussion

Sensory response differences and challenges with executive function (EF) are known to be present in autistic individuals, significantly impact daily functioning, and limit the ability to engage in treatment(Ben-Sasson et al., 2019; Boyd et al., 2010; Crane et al., 2009; Crasta et al., 2023; Demetriou et al., 2018; Granader et al., 2014; Kern et al., 2006; Leung et al., 2016; Rosenthal et al., 2013). However, prior to the current study, how sensory responsivity and EF differences related to one another in young autistic children remained unclear given heterogeneity of findings thus far. Limited generalizability and reproducibility of prior results were in part driven by prior studies’ use of broad age ranges or EF levels in autistic individuals (Kiep et al., 2023), reliance on caregiver reports of sensory responses, and potential confounds from co-occurring conditions such as ADHD and anxiety (Carpenter et al., 2019; Green et al., 2012; MacLennan et al., 2020; Skaletski et al., 2024). Therefore, we aimed to identify how sensory differences, as measured by both caregiver and observational measures, related to EF in a more restricted age range of preschool-aged autistic children. We found that by either sensory method, SIRS behavior was significantly related to global executive function and independent of comorbid anxiety and ADHD in young autistic children. We also preliminarily demonstrated that this interaction may be specific to EF subdomains, including working memory. These same patterns were not seen in equivalent analyses of neurotypical children (Supplemental Table 3), suggesting that this is specific to autism.

Similar to previous findings of SEQ-3 measures in autistic individuals (Baranek, 2009; Baranek et al., 2006; Boyd et al., 2009), we found that prior to accounting for co-occurring conditions of anxiety and ADHD, elevated SEQ-3 sensory summary scores of SIRS behavior, hyperreactivity, and hyporeactivity were associated with executive function difficulties in preschool-aged autistic children. These relationships held true for SIRS and sensory hyporeactivity, even when accounting for possible confounds of co-occurring anxiety and ADHD symptoms. Expanding on these results, we explored the same relationships using an observational sensory assessment (the SPA), which also revealed associations between SIRS behavior, sensory hyperreactivity, and EF difficulties. Notably, SIRS behavior remained a significant predictor of EF challenges even when controlling for anxiety and ADHD. These findings also highlight the importance of considering co-occurring conditions that have EF and sensory features, such as anxiety and ADHD.

While the caregiver-reported and observational sensory measures yielded some different results, there was a consistent association between SIRS behavior and EF challenges. This aligns with bottom-up neural processing models, which propose sensory pathways influence higher-order networks, such as those needed for EF, due to their early neural patterning in development (Falck-Ytter & Bussu, 2023; Kiep et al., 2023; Pastor-Cerezuela et al., 2020; Robertson & Baron-Cohen, 2017; Russo et al., 2025). SIRS has been suggested to develop as early as 6 months of age, prior to communication symptoms in autistic children, supporting the notion that sensory response differences precede higher-order cognitive challenges, such as EF (Campi et al., 2024; Russo et al., 2025; Sacrey et al., 2018). Further, this suggests that there may be a distinct shared neural motif or feedforward mechanism between SIRS processes and EF in autistic children. For instance, preoccupation with stimuli may negatively impact an individual’s ability to engage with or attend to cognitively taxing processes. Alternatively, it is possible that children with poor EF tend to show more sensory seeking behaviors as a compensatory “need” or preference for this input. In line with this hypothesis, heightened sensory seeking behaviors and increased EF difficulties are seen in both ADHD and autism (Ben-Sasson et al., 2017; Dellapiazza et al., 2021; Kadlaskar et al., 2023; Kiep et al., 2023; Williams et al., 2023). However, the regulatory function of sensory-seeking behavior may differ: in ADHD, particularly hyperactive profiles, SIRS behavior helps modulate attention by enhancing the salience of stimuli, while in autism, SIRS behavior may primarily support emotion regulation (Dellapiazza et al., 2021; Little et al., 2018).

One challenge of evaluating the relationship between SIRS and EF is the potential for inadvertently capturing aspects of EF in sensory measures. Preliminary work in infants has suggested that SIRS may be related to difficulty disengaging attention, which would suggest that if an aspect of EF were included in SIRS, it would likely be the shifting attention domain (Baranek et al., 2018; Elsabbagh et al., 2013; Russo et al., 2025). In addition, other studies have identified a potential link between SIRS behavior and arousal (Donkers et al., 2013; Hudac et al., 2018; Piccardi & Gliga, 2022; Takarae et al., 2016). However, there are individuals with EF difficulties that do not demonstrate SIRS behaviors (i.e., individuals with ADHD), which would contradict the premise that SIRS is directly correlated to EF (Roberts et al., 2017; Townes et al., 2023). Therefore, it is clear that a multifaceted relationship exists between SIRS and EF which is difficult to disentangle, however this interrelatedness is not full mediation and understanding which factors impact this relationship is paramount.

In our exploratory analyses of associations between SIRS and particular subdomains of EF, higher SIRS symptoms, as measured by either sensory testing modality, were associated with greater difficulties in the domain of working memory. This is in line with previous research that has found consistent associations between SIRS and difficulties with working memory and emotional control (Boyd et al., 2009; Fernandez-Prieto et al., 2021; Iversen & Lewis, 2021; Pastor-Cerezuela et al., 2020; Sadeghi & Pouretemad, 2022). Theoretically, this may arise from differences in sensory pathways impacting the development of higher-order networks, or it may reflect real-time impacts on coincident cognitive computations. While preliminary, these data suggest a more refined interaction may exist between SIRS behavior and specific cognitive processes, particularly in the domain of working memory.

Possible explanations of differences between SEQ-3 generated models and SPA generated models include the nature of data collection; caregiver reports are theoretically more subjective and taken in the context of everyday life, whereas researcher-administered measures are more controlled and collected in a novel, less-ethologically-relevant environment. In addition, as mentioned previously, the SEQ-3 and SPA differ in how they measure sensory constructs, which impacts their thresholds for sensory responsiveness detection. For instance, in SEQ-3, hyporeactivity is a broader-based construct, encompassing questions such as “my child does not respond to pain”, whereas in the SPA, hyporeactivity is primarily an orienting-based measure (Baranek et al., 2006, 2007). Given these differences, it is not surprising that certain sensory subscores were stronger predictors of executive function challenges when analyzed by one method versus the other. However, the overall trends of each model were similar, suggesting that the measures may vary in their sensitivity, and therefore our ability to reach a threshold of detection. Supporting this theory, the correlation between SEQ-3 and SPA scores was modest at 0.3. Therefore, if we performed these analyses on a larger sample size, we may anticipate that all three sensory scores (SIRS, hyperreactivity, and hyporeactivity) may be predictive of executive challenges in young autistic children. This also holds for our exploratory subanalyses of SIRS behavior predicting EF subdomains; while SIRS was predictive of working memory (WM) challenges in both cases, the remainder of EF subcategories trended in the same direction, but did not reach a level of significance across both SEQ-3 and SPA-based analyses.

The current study should be taken in the context of certain limitations. First, our study was limited to a cross-sectional analysis, so the stability of the relationship between SIRS and EF remains an open question. Additionally, while we had multimodal assessments of sensory responsivity (i.e. observation and caregiver report), our measure of EF was based solely on caregiver report. Furthermore, while we did not find strong bivariate correlations between our caregiver-reported instruments, there remains the potential for shared rater variance amongst caregiver measures. While this does not apply to our SPA-based results, it highlights the need for multimodal analyses to avoid circularity of findings. In addition, none of these measures directly interrogate the ‘online’ neural processing underlying the connection between sensory processing and executive function difficulties, and as such, the directionality of influence between sensory behavior and executive function remains theoretical. Therefore, future studies using multimodal neurophysiological testing (such as electroencephalographic recordings, eye tracking, and noninvasive brain imaging) are needed to further refine the relationships identified between sensory processing and EF in autistic children. In addition, while caregiver-reported assessments like the PAPA are commonly employed to identify psychiatric comorbidities in young children, future work using clinician-administered observational assessments would further inform diagnostic clarity. While we controlled for DQ and adaptive functioning in our models, larger-scale studies are needed to evaluate whether additional developmental heterogeneity amongst young autistic children influences the interplay between sensory responsivity and EF. Finally, the generalizability of these findings to the broader autistic population remains unknown given that our data was limited to a study with specific inclusion and exclusion criteria. Ultimately, additional analyses should help identify more homogenous subgroups of autistic children, allowing for increased precision of clinical interventions.

In summary, our current study is one of relatively few investigations into sensory responsivity of young autistic children that utilized a researcher-administered measure rather than self- or caregiver report. Our data suggest a unique relationship exists between SIRS behavior and global executive function that is independent of anxiety and ADHD comorbidities in young autistic children. Preliminarily, this interaction may be further refined to SIRS impacting specific subdomains of EF, including working memory. Understanding the relationship between SIRS and EF difficulties is crucial given that both domains are commonly monitored throughout autistic children’s development and influence supportive treatment strategies. Uncovering the hierarchal relationship between SIRS and EF may therefore have implications in prioritizing future interventional strategies. For example, given that SIRS is often related to altered arousal states (Ausderau et al., 2016), accommodations that either modulate the sensory environment or provide opportunities that capitalize on the regulatory function of SIRS behavior may improve learning and decrease the impact of SIRS on executive abilities.
